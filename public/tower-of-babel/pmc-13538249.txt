Targeting of Acid Sphingomyelinase, a Lysosomal Membrane Protein, Using Apolipoprotein E3-containing Nanodiscs
Authors: Vidya Metkar, Vernon Gil Benedicto, Jingyuan Zheng, Angela M. Zivkovic, Nestor D. Plascencia, Thomas Gredig, Vasanthy Narayanaswami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We developed a nanodisc (ND)-based platform with recombinant acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) embedded in a bilayer of phospholipids that are circumscribed by apolipoprotein E3 (apoE3) for targeted delivery to lysosomes. We designed a fusion protein comprising essential catalytic segments of ASM with a transmembrane helix at one end (TM-ASMcat). Purified TM-ASMcat was reconstituted with phospholipids and the N-terminal (NT) domain of apoE3 to promote the formation of NDs (TM-ASMcat-ND). Physicochemical characterization revealed the presence of both TM-ASMcat (35 kDa) and apoE3NT (24 kDa) in the preparations, and formation of large complexes (400–600 kDa). Transmission electron microscopy of TM-ASMcat-ND revealed discoidal complexes (major/minor axes of 30.9 ± 11.9/8.6 ± 2.0 nm, respectively), while atomic force microscopy showed NDs ~ 14 nm high. The TM-ASMcat-ND displayed a significant ability to hydrolyze sphingomyelin at pH 5. LC-MS/MS of glioblastoma cells treated with TM-ASMcat-ND revealed a significant decrease (22.3%) in total sphingomyelin content compared to cells treated with PBS. Sphingolipidomic analysis showed a trend of decrease in specific sphingomyelin species compared to empty ND-treated cells. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed perinuclear vesicles showing colocalization of ASM with apoE3 and Lysotracker, indicative of successful targeting of TM-ASMcat-ND to the lysosomes. Taken together, our data indicate that TM-ASMcat has been embedded in ND, and that it retains catalytic activity in solution and inside the cells. Our findings bear the potential to treat ASM deficiency in Niemann-Pick disease Type A and B, which are lysosomal storage disorders characterized by the accumulation of sphingomyelin.Graphical AbstractSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00232-026-00393-4.

Introduction

Acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) (EC 3.1.4.12) is a lysosomal membrane-bound protein that hydrolyzes sphingomyelin (SM) to ceramide and phosphorylcholine. A deficiency or defect in the gene that codes for ASM (SMPD1) leads to Niemann-Pick disease Type A (NPD-A), Type B (NPD-B), and Type A/B (NPD-A/B). These are disorders that belong to a class of genetically inherited metabolic diseases collectively known as lysosomal storage diseases (LSDs), which typically involve hydrolases, and are characterized by lysosomal dysfunction (Neufeld 1991; Parenti et al. 2021; Platt 2018). NPD-A and NPD-B lead to the buildup of SM, resulting in progressive cellular dysfunction and affecting various organs such as the liver, spleen, lungs, and brain (Pinto et al. 2021; Tirelli et al. 2024); they are accompanied by plasma lipid abnormalities (Maines et al. 2022; Sun 2018). Pioneering work on the molecular genetics, biochemical, enzymological, and clinical pathological work on LSD in general, and ASM and NPD in particular, laid the groundwork for our current understanding of the etiology of NPDs (Geberhiwot et al. 2023; Schissel et al. 1996, 1998; Schuchman 2007; Schuchmann et al. 2007). This body of work paved the way to initiate a disease-modifying therapy for treating NPD, with the development of an enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) for the treatment of a small number of subjects with non-neuropathic NPD-B with recombinant ASM, Olipudase alfa, produced in Chinese hamster ovary cells (Wasserstein et al. 2015). Intravenously administered Olipudase resulted in an initial transient increase in plasma levels of SM and ceramide, both of which fell as a function of time. Several weeks post-treatment, the authors reported continued improvements (Thurberg et al. 2020; Wasserstein et al. 2018) with promising enhancement in liver function and anti-atherogenic lipid profiles, and decreased dyslipidemia. While this therapy is in practice, challenges continue to exist at the application level, and there is a parallel need to obtain a more thorough understanding of the mechanism of membrane transport and targeted delivery of ASM, and ASM-lipid interactions at the fundamental level.

ASM is a phosphodiesterase that cleaves SM, a major lipid found in membranes, to phosphorylcholine and ceramide (Scheme 1) in the acidic environment of lysosomes; it requires the presence of acidic lipids and displays a broad substrate specificity. It has the ability to cleave phospholipids such as phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylglycerol as well (Freeman et al. 1985; Oninla et al. 2014; Quintern et al. 1987). In lysosomes and endosomes, the marker acidic phospholipid bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (BMP), which is enriched in these organelles and accounts for ~ 15% of phospholipids, likely plays a role in lipid binding and membrane docking of ASM (Oninla et al. 2014).

Human ASM is a 629-amino acid (~ 70 kDa) glycosylated membrane protein that is broadly divided into an N-terminal domain (1–82), a saposin-like domain (83–165), a linker region rich in Pro (166–198), the catalytic domain (199–611), and the C-terminal domain (612–629) (Xiong et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2016). In the low-pH environment of the lysosomes, ASM bears an overall electropositive charge, and requires acidic lipids to adopt a membrane-bound active state.

In the crystal structure of ASM (PDB #5JG8) that was obtained at 2.8Å resolution (Xiong et al. 2016), the saposin domain forms a tandem series of four α-helices, which leads to a V-shaped conformation with a hinge opening that allows exposure of an inner hydrophobic surface. This domain is believed to play a role in juxtaposing the catalytic domain at the membrane surface, thereby positioning the active site residues towards the phosphodiester bond of SM that will be cleaved.

In our study, we designed an ASM construct using a minimal catalytic segment that is tethered to the phospholipid bilayer of a nanodisc (ND) via a hydrophobic transmembrane (TM) helix from a lung surfactant protein C (SPC) (Keller et al. 1991; Nilsson et al. 1998). The NDs used in this study comprise a bilayer of phospholipids and BMP circumscribed by a helical double belt of the N-terminal domain of apolipoprotein E3 (apoE3NT) (residues 1-191). ApoE3 is an exchangeable apolipoprotein typically found on large lipoprotein complexes such as the very low-density lipoprotein and high-density lipoprotein (HDL), which play a major role in lipid transport in the plasma. It serves as a ligand for the low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLr) family of proteins that facilitate cellular uptake and receptor-mediated endocytosis of lipoproteins (Hatters et al. 2006; Hauser et al. 2011; Weisgraber 1994). Receptor-mediated endocytosis culminates in the lysosomes, where the lipoprotein disintegrates completely releasing the lipids and proteins. We take advantage of this feature of apoE3 as a means for cellular entry and targeted delivery of the embedded ASM to the lysosomes. Since this process leads to complete disintegration of the lipoprotein with its cargo, it is an ideal system for targeted delivery of ASM via the LDLr to the lysosomes to treat NPD-A and NPD-B.

Materials and Methods

Design of ASM Construct

A fusion protein was engineered consisting of a modified TM helix sequence derived from SPC (FGIPSSPVLKRLLILLLLLLLILLLILGALLMGL) and a majority of the catalytic region of human ASM (residues 319–579, ASMcat), connected by a flexible (G₄S)₂ linker. The construct also bears a hexa-His-tag (H6) at the C-terminal end of ASMcat to facilitate purification. This construct, SPC-TM/(G4S)2/ASMcat/H6 will be referred to as TM-ASMcat for the sake of brevity. The sequence was codon optimized for expression in a bacterial expression system and synthesized by Eurofins (Eurofins Blue Heron, Bothell, USA) in a pUC119 plasmid, which was then sub-cloned into the pET-20b (+) plasmid with ampicillin and chloramphenicol resistance. The pET-20b(+) construct with codon optimized sequence of apoE3NT bearing a hexa-His tag at the N-terminal end was obtained from a previous study (Khumsupan et al. 2011).

TM-ASMcat and apoE3NT Overexpression, Isolation and Purification

ApoE3NT was overexpressed in E. coli BL21(DE3)pLysS Gold (Agilent) until an OD at 600 of ~ 0.6 was reached, followed by induction with 0.4 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactoside at 37 °C for 4 h, isolated and purified by immobilized metal affinity chromatography with nickel sulfate as described previously (Choy et al. 2003). TM-ASMcat was also overexpressed as above with slight modifications: (i) the isolation and purification steps were performed soon after the expression process; (ii) the cell pellet was resuspended in ice cold 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate, pH 7.8 followed by the addition of cell lysis buffer: 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, containing 150 mM NaCl (phosphate buffered saline, PBS), with 1% IGEPAL CA-630 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Ward Hill, MA), 10% glycerol, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 0.2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 0.25% soybean trypsin inhibitor with EDTA (EMD Millipore Corp., Billerica, MA), and 0.02 mM each of leupeptin and pepstatin A (RPI Research Products International, Mt. Prospect, IL); and, (iii) the imidazole used to elute the protein from the Ni-NTA column was removed using a desalting column (Cytiva PD midiTrap G-25), instead of dialyzing it out. The purified proteins were flash-frozen and stored at -80 °C until further use.

Western Blot of Purified TM-ASMcat

Purified TM-ASMcat was subjected to 10% SDS- polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by western blot using anti-ASM (SMPD-1 gene-specific ASM polyclonal antibody, catalog #PA5-95730, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (1:500) as the primary and goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP (1:10,000) as the secondary antibody. In some cases, HRP-conjugated anti-His-tag antibody (1:2500, Abcam Inc, Waltham, MA) was used for detecting TM-ASMcat.

Assembly of TM-ASMcat Nanodiscs and Empty Nanodiscs

TM-ASMcat was reconstituted with 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3 phosphocholine (POPC) (Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc.) based on the cholate dialysis method described previously (Jonas 1986; Kim et al. 2014) with several modifications. ApoE3NT (2 mg) was pre-incubated with 5x molar excess of tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) (Invitrogen) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer (pH 7.8) at 37 °C for 30 min followed by mixing with ASMcat (2 mg). To about 4 mg POPC thin film, the following were added in the presence of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer pH 7.8: 300 mM n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM), 200 mM sodium deoxycholate (NaDC) (Acros Organics, NJ), and 1 mg bis(monooleoylglycero)phosphate (S, R isomer) (ammonium salt) BMP (catalog # A85075 (Legacy 857133 C); Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc.) and the mixture was alternately sonicated and vortexed until clear. The proteins and lipids were mixed and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h with intermittent vortexing. In parallel, empty nanodiscs were prepared by an identical process except without TM-ASMcat. In both cases, the detergent was removed by Amberlite® XAD®-2 beads (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO), which allowed spontaneous assembly of NDs. The samples were subjected to density gradient ultracentrifugation at 230,000 x g for 5.5 h in an ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter Optima L-100 XP) using KBr as described previously (Kim et al. 2014). The top fractions containing the highest protein concentration were desalted using a Sephadex G-25 column (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA). The protein concentration was determined by the DC assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA).

Physicochemical Characterization of TM-ASMcat NDs and Empty NDs

The reconstituted mixtures (~ 10 µg protein) (referred to as TM-ASMcat nanodiscs (TM-ASMcat-ND) and empty nanodiscs (empty ND) were subjected to non-denaturing PAGE analysis (4–20% acrylamide gradient, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) along with standards of known molecular mass and Stokes’ radii (thyroglobulin (~ 669 kDa, 8.5 nm); ferritin (~ 440 kDa, 6.1 nm); catalase (250 kDa, 4.6 nm) and lactate dehydrogenase (140 kDa, 4.1 nm) and visualized using Instant Blue (Abcam Inc, Waltham, MA). TM-ASMcat-ND and empty ND were examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using 2% (m: m) uranyl formate (pH 4.6) as described earlier (Zheng et al. 2021). Following adsorption of samples to glow-discharged carbon-coated 200 mesh copper grids (TedPella Inc., CA, USA) the imaging was carried out using a Talos L120C electron microscope (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) at 80 kV and 36,000x magnification. About 200 particles were measured to designate the long and short diameters as the major and minor axis, respectively. The topography of the nanodiscs (~ 10 µg/ml) was imaged by atomic force microscopy (AFM) on a freshly cleaved mica surface using a Park XE7 instrument (Park Systems) in non-contact mode. A standard silicon cantilever (PPP-NCHR) with an estimated tip radius of 8 nm and a resonance frequency of 341 kHz was used. The presented images of TM-ASMcat-ND and empty ND show the surface topography on a mica surface. Image backgrounds were flattened using autoMask with nanoAFMr1 and height profiles extracted across individual nanodiscs. The hydrodynamic size distribution and polydispersity index (PDI) of TM-ASMcat-ND and empty ND were obtained by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 as described previously (Chuang et al. 2017) at 25 °C. The protein content was estimated using the Bio-Rad DC Protein assay kit (Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ).

Pull-down Assay to Verify the Presence of apoE3NT and TM-ASMcat in TM-ASMcat-ND

A pull-down assay was carried out to verify the presence of TM-ASMcat in nanodiscs. About 20 µg of TM-ASMcat-ND or empty ND was incubated with 0.5 µL of mAb1D7, an apoE-specific antibody at 4 °C for 16 h. The mixture was treated with 20 µL of Protein G plus –Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) slurry and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. Western blot analysis was conducted using anti-ASM polyclonal antibody followed by goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP secondary antibody and HRP-conjugated anti-apoE polyclonal antibody (1:2500). An anti-ASM polyclonal antibody was used for detection because association of the ASM construct with NDs may mask certain epitopes, while the antibody’s recognition of multiple epitopes ensures reliable detection.

Enzyme Activity of TM-ASMcat-ND

The sphingomyelinase activity of TM-ASMcat-ND was determined using the Amplex Red Sphingomyelinase Assay Kit (Molecular Probes Inc., USA) in a 96-well plate following the manufacturer’s instructions. This is a fluorescence-based assay that allows determination of sphingomyelinase activity at pH 7 and pH 5. The activity is expressed as relative fluorescence intensity units (RFU)/mg protein at 590 nm following excitation at 560 nm. Empty ND were used as control throughout. All assays were carried out in technical triplicates of biological triplicates unless otherwise noted and values expressed as mean ± SEM.

LDLr Binding Ability of TM-ASMcat-ND

Before applying to cells to assess the ability of the TM-ASMcat-ND to bind the cell surface localized LDLr, an initial solution-based assay was carried out using a soluble form of the LDLr represented as sLDLr. The soluble construct bearing the LDLr ligand binding domains LA3–LA6 with a c-myc epitope (sLDLr/LA3-LA6/c-myc) was a kind gift from Dr. Stephen Blacklow, Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA. As described previously, it encompasses the essential ligand binding elements of the extracellular soluble portion of the mature LDLr (Fisher et al. 2004) and has been used successfully to test the LDLr binding ability of nanodiscs (Chuang et al. 2017) using a co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay. Briefly, TM-ASMcat-ND or empty ND was incubated with 15 µg of sLDLr in PBS containing 1 mM CaCl2 at 4 °C for 1 h. The TM-ASMcat- ND/sLDLr and empty ND/sLDLr complexes were captured by co-IP with anti-c-myc antibody-linked agarose. The captured complexes were tested for the presence of (i) apoE using HRP-conjugated polyclonal apoE antibody; (ii) TM-ASMcat using anti-ASM polyclonal antibody; and (iii) sLDLr using anti-c-Myc mouse monoclonal antibody (9E10, 1:1000, Abcam Inc, Waltham, MA). Where appropriate, goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP (1:10,000) or goat anti-mouse IgG HRP (1:5,000) were used as the secondary antibody.

Immunofluorescence Analysis

To determine the LDLr binding, cellular uptake and subcellular localization of TM-ASMcat- ND, human glioblastoma (GBM) cell lines (A-172; ATCC) were used as described previously (Abeer et al. 2023; Chuang et al. 2017). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; ATCC) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 50 IU/mL penicillin and 50 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), henceforth referred as complete DMEM and grown in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. About 5 × 105 cells were seeded onto an eight-well MatTek culture well containing a cover slip. When cells reached ~ 80% confluency, the medium was replaced with 500 µl of pre-warmed 10% lipoprotein deficient serum (LPDS) and the incubation continued to induce LDLr expression for 24 h. The cells were then treated with 10 µg/ml of TM-ASMcat-ND or empty ND in LPDS at 37°C for 2 h, washed three times with pre-warmed PBS, fixed, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 at 24°C for 5 min. They were then incubated with primary antibody (mAb1D7 and polyclonal rabbit anti-ASM) at 4°C for 1 h, followed by secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor™ 488 donkey anti-mouse or Alexa Fluor™ 555 goat anti-rabbit) at 4°C for 1 h. To verify subcellular localization of TM-ASMcat-ND in lysosomes, Lysotracker yellow HCK-123 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) (465/535 nm) was used. The cells were stained with DAPI (4’,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride, Research Organics Cat # 2039D) to label the nuclei. All immunofluorescence (IF) analysis was performed as three biological replicates. In addition, the endogenous expression of ASM in GBM cells was determined in cells with no treatment, cellular autofluorescence by omitting primary and secondary antibodies and non-specific binding of secondary antibody by omitting the primary antibody. Cell images were captured using a confocal laser scanning system (Nikon AX R Confocal) using 20x or 40x objectives and analyzed using the NIS Elements Image Analysis software.

Mass Spectrometric Analysis of the Cellular Sphingolipids

GBM cells (~ 5 × 105 cells/well) were cultured as above in 6-well plates and exposed to 10 µg/ml of TM-ASMcat-ND or empty ND or PBS in LPDS at 37 °C for 2 h as above and washed three times with cold PBS. The cells were then treated with cell lysis buffer containing a cocktail of protease inhibitors as described above under the TM-ASMcat and apoE3NT overexpression, isolation and purification section, 1% Triton X-100, and 1% SDS in PBS, flash frozen in liquid N2 and shipped to the University of California San Diego Lipidomics Core for sphingolipid panel analysis. Mass spectral measurements were carried out from three biological replicates. Cell lysates were subjected to the Bradford assay for protein analysis and extracted by the BUME method as described earlier (Löfgren et al. 2016; Murawska et al. 2024) for sphingolipid analysis. Briefly, the BUME protocol is a chloroform-free lipid extraction method that involves a one-phase extraction into a 3:1 mixture of butanol: methanol (BUME) first, followed by a two-phase extraction with 3:1 mixture of heptane: ethyl acetate with 1% acetic acid. The lipid extract was analyzed by ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (Vanquish UHPLC, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a Waters Cortecs T3 C18 column (2.1 mm x 150 mm, 8 μm) with a binary solvent system as described (Murawska et al. 2024).

Sphingomyelins (SM) were identified and quantified by tandem mass spectrometry (Q-Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in MS data dependent acquisition mode using the Lipid Data Analyzer (LDA) LipidSearch software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The various cellular species of SM were expressed as pmol/mg protein using the concentration of a single internal standard for each class. The SM nomenclature used is based on the LDA decision rule set (Hartler et al. 2020). All data are expressed as normalized intensities relative to exactly measured internal standards and constitute relative abundances per mg protein. For the identification of each lipid molecular species, including its exact fatty acid composition, MS/MS foot printing techniques were used. When the MS fragmentation pattern was not conclusive, the metabolite was identified by retention time and exact molecular mass and expressed as an isobaric species. Only those metabolites that were present in at least 5 samples of the dataset were included. We report both the MS/MS confirmed validated species and the isobaric species. The latter were inferred only using the exact mass and may represent co-eluting species.

Statistical Analysis

In all studies, biological and technical triplicates were used (technical replicates represent repeated measurements of the same ND sample; biological replicates represent independently generated protein preparation and ND sample), and statistical analysis was performed using two-tailed Student’s T-test, two-way ANOVA, Welch’s T-test and Tukey’s post hoc analysis, as relevant. To evaluate the significance of differences between experimental groups, the exact P values are reported.

Results

Design of TM-ASMcat Construct

With the overall objective of transporting and delivering ASM to intracellular lysosomal compartments, residues 319–579, which encompass the enzyme’s major catalytic residues (referred to as ASMcat), were used to demonstrate the feasibility and viability of the approach. The fusion construct TM-ASMcat bearing the modified sequence of the transmembrane helix of SPC (SPC-TM) (Gustafsson et al. 2000; Nilsson et al. 1998), the (G4S)2 linker, ASMcat and the hexa-His-tag (H6) is schematically represented in Fig. 1A. The TM-ASMcat codon optimized sequence was engineered in a pUC119 plasmid, which was then sub-cloned into the pET-20b (+) plasmid with ampicillin and chloramphenicol resistance. The complete sequence of the TM-ASMcat construct is shown in Fig. 1B. Comparison of the X-ray crystal structure of ASM (residues 47-C629S) (Xiong et al. 2016) (PDB ID: 5JG8) at 2.8 Å with the model structure of TM-ASMcat generated by ColabFold 2, Fig. 1C, indicates that the overall fold is likely maintained.

It is recognized that the platform assembly process for TM-ASMcat-ND may lead to the formation of empty nanodiscs; to confirm that the TM-ASMcat was associated with the lipid bilayer, the NDs were pulled down with an anti-apoE antibody (mAb 1D7) and visualized with anti-apoE HRP and anti-ASM antibody, Fig. 4A and B, respectively. In both cases, empty ND was used as a control during the pull-down process. With anti-apoE HRP, a 24 kDa band was noted for both empty ND and TM-ASMcat-ND, while with anti-ASM antibody a 35 kDa band was noted only in TM-ASMcat-ND and not for empty ND, confirming the presence of TM-ASMcat in ND.

Having confirmed the presence of TM-ASMcat in ND, the size, geometry, and topographical information of the platforms were determined. Figure 5A and B show the TEM images of TM-ASMcat-ND and empty ND, respectively, while Fig. 5C and D show the corresponding zoomed-in versions. Empty NDs have a distribution that is closer to symmetric, with the mean and median being nearly identical.

To check if TM-ASMcat elicited catalytic activity when embedded in NDs, the Amplex Red Sphingomyelinase Assay Kit (Molecular Probes Inc., USA), a sensitive enzyme-coupled fluorometric assay, was employed. ASM activity was tested at both pH 7 and pH 5 in a 96-well plate. TM-ASMcat, TM-ASMcat-ND, or empty ND were treated with sphingomyelin and the enzyme-coupled assay reagents for 60 min at 37 °C in the presence of 50 mM sodium acetate, pH 5.0, or 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0, with 5 mM MgCl2 in the dark as described in the protocol, Fig. 7.

In parallel, sphingomyelinase from Bacillus cereus that was provided in the kit was used to ensure that the kit was working, along with a negative control that contained only the reaction buffer. The fluorescence emission was measured at 590 nm following excitation at 560 nm, corrected for background fluorescence by subtracting the corresponding values from the negative control for each point, and plotted as RFU/mg protein. At pH 5.0, lipid-free TM-ASMcat showed 18.6 ± 9.6 × 103 RFU/mg protein, while TM-ASMcat-ND showed significantly higher activity (P < 0.05), 52.4 ± 34.4 × 103 RFU/mg protein. In contrast, the empty ND showed minimal activity (689.7 ± 450.9 RFU/mg protein). Interestingly, lipid-free TM-ASMcat and TM-ASMcat-ND showed a small amount of activity (16.4 ± 3.5 × 103 and 11.8 ± 4.5 × 103 RFU/mg protein, respectively) at pH 7.0, with no significant difference in activity between the lipid-free and ND-associated states of the TM-ASMcat. The data indicate that the TM-ASMcat-ND generated under the conditions described retained significant catalytic activity and is therefore viable for use in cellular uptake studies.

LDLr Binding Ability of TM-ASMcat-ND

Prior to assessing the cellular uptake of TM-ASMcat-ND, it was important to determine if the presence of TM-ASMcat in ND affects the LDLr binding ability of apoE3NT that also serves as a scaffold for the ND. To accomplish this, we employed a soluble form of the receptor bearing essential ligand binding domains LA3-LA6 and a c-myc epitope (sLDLr/LA3-LA6/c-myc) as described previously (Benedicto et al. 2024; Fisher et al. 2004). Briefly, about 10 µg of sLDLr was incubated with 10 µg of TM-ASMcat-ND or empty ND in the presence of 2 mM CaCl2 in PBS at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by addition anti-c-myc antibody-linked agarose (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO) to capture sLDLr/ TM-ASMcat-ND or sLDLr/empty ND complexes. Figure 8 shows the western blots of the captured complexes using different antibodies to check for the individual components of the complexes: (A) use of HRP-conjugated polyclonal apoE antibody revealed a 24 kDa band indicative of the presence of apoE3NT ligand in both complexes of sLDLr with empty ND and TM-ASMcat-ND; (B) use of anti-c-myc antibody revealed a 26 kDa band indicative of the presence of the sLDLr in both lanes for complexes with empty ND and TM-ASMcat-ND; (C) use of anti-ASM polyclonal antibody revealed a 35 kDa band for sLDLr complex with TM-ASMcat-ND but not for empty ND.

Cellular Uptake and Lysosomal Localization of TM-ASMcat-ND in GBM Cells

To determine cellular uptake of TM-ASMcat-ND, GBM A-172 cells that are known to have high copy numbers of LDLr (~900,000 LDLr/cell) (Maletínská et al. 2000) were employed as described previously (Chuang et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2015) and the internalized ND visualized by IF by confocal microscopy, Fig. 9. GBM cells were treated with 10 µg/ml of TM-ASMcat-ND (Fig. 9, A – D) or empty ND (Fig. 9, E – H) as described under Materials and Methods. IF images with anti-apoE monoclonal antibody mAb1D7 and Alexa-488 conjugated secondary antibody revealed punctate green, fluorescent, vesicular structures in both treatments (B and F). IF images with polyclonal anti-ASM and Alexa-555 conjugated secondary antibody revealed red, fluorescent, vesicular structures in cells treated with TM-ASMcat-ND but not in those treated with empty ND (C and G, respectively), indicative of the presence of ASM in the former. The merged images for the two treatments are shown in D and H, respectively; they reveal a high degree of colocalization of ASM and apoE throughout the cell (Pearson’s R value = 0.81) with concentration around the nuclei (visualized using DAPI, A and E). No endogenous ASM or apoE were detected under the conditions used as seen in cells that were imaged under identical conditions with the exception that no TM-ASMcat-ND or empty ND were added (Fig. S1, Panels a and b). Similarly, no fluorescence due to non-specific binding of secondary antibodies (Fig. S1, Panels c and d) or from isotype controls (Fig. S1, Panels e and f) was observed in GBM cells. The data are indicative of cellular uptake of the exogenously added TM-ASMcat-ND likely via the LDLr mediated endocytosis pathway.

Although it is well known that the LDLr mediated endocytosis leads to delivery of apoE-containing lipoproteins to the lysosomal compartment (Hatters et al. 2006; Huang and Mahley 2014; Weisgraber 1994), we carried out further validation experiments to determine the sub-cellular localization of TM-ASMcat-ND. Specifically, colocalization assays were carried out to determine presence of TM-ASMcat in the lysosomes using LysoTracker as described under Materials and Methods. GBM cells were treated with TM-ASMcat-ND (Fig. 9, I – L) and empty ND (Fig. 9, M – P) as above followed by IF imaging with LysoTracker (J and N) or polyclonal anti-ASM and Alexa-555 conjugated secondary antibody (K and O); the merged images (L and P) indicate the presence of ASM in lysosomes in cells treated with TM-ASMcat-ND, with concentration in the perinuclear area. Taken together, the IF imaging is strongly indicative of localization of TM-ASMcat-ND in lysosomes, along with apoE3NT.

TM-ASMcat-ND decreases SM content in GBM cells

Lastly, an in-depth lipidomic analysis focusing on quantifying the SM species of GBM cells treated with PBS, empty ND, and TM-ASMcat-ND was carried out by mass spectrometry. Fig. 10 shows the change in the total validated and isobaric SM species (Panel A) and the total validated SM species (Panel B) in the cells treated with empty ND and TM-ASMcat-ND, expressed as % of levels in PBS treatment. Cells treated with empty ND show a similar amount of total SM compared to those treated with PBS, indicating that the platform bearing POPC, BMP, and apoE3NT does not alter the SM status by itself. Importantly, a significantly lower level (validated + isobaric SM) was noted (P < 0.016) upon treatment of cells with TM-ASMcat-ND, amounting to a 22.3% compared to cells treated with PBS, Fig. 10, Panel A. When viewing the validated species alone, Fig. 10, Panel B, a similar lowered value was noted (P < 0.036) amounting to 22.8%. This indicates that the ASM delivered from an external source had a notable effect on the cellular SM status.

Figure 11 illustrates the decrease of the individual validated SM species in the three sets as a bee swarm plot (Top) and as a bar graph (Bottom). A total of 105 analytes were identified as SM species, of which 36 were validated and confirmed by MS/MS data. Table 1 lists the validated SM species. A similar trend of decrease was noted in the isobaric SM species that were detected (Fig. S2). Overall, the profile of the validated species reveals dominance of very long fatty acyl chains (C18:1, C22:0, C24:0, C24:1) that appear to be typical of SM found in biological systems (Barenholz and Thompson 1999). Interestingly, of the validated species, SM d42:1_27.42 | d18:1/n24:0 and SM d42:2_25.75 | d18:1/n24:1 were the most abundant species compared to the rest of the detected species. It is noteworthy that TM-ASMcat-ND-treated cells showed a significant decrease in SM t33:2_14.59 | t14:2_n19:0 compared to empty ND treated cells (P = 0.023), though the implications of this observation are not clear at this point. Additionally, higher levels of two specific SM species were noted in cells treated with empty ND compared to PBS-treated cells (SM 41:1-26.82|d18:1 n23:0 (52%, p = 0.099) and SM t33:2_14.59|t14:2_n19:0 (61%, p = 0.022). It appears that apoE3NT may have an effect in regulating lysosomal SM levels independent of the effect of ASM. The reason for this finding is not clear; more studies are needed to understand this observation. Further, studies are also needed to assess if the externally supplied recombinant ASM displays preferential substrate specificities compared to the endogenous ASM, and the downstream effect of such preference. In the analysis, some sphingolipid species contain fatty acids of the same chain length and number of double bonds but differ in the location of the double bonds on the chains. This can lead to a difference in the elution time and is reported as two species with the same fatty acid content. At this point, the MS/MS analysis cannot resolve the position of the double bond to distinguish between these species.

Discussion

Nanodiscs are excellent platforms that have high aqueous solubility and an ability to accommodate highly hydrophobic biomolecules. They resemble physiologically generated nascent HDL in shape, size and basic composition of lipids and apolipoproteins such as apoAI and apoE3. NDs recapitulate several functional features of the apolipoproteins. The current study reports the possibility of re-purposing apoE3-containing NDs in a dual-purpose approach for delivering ASM to lysosomal membrane system: (i) employing them as a platform to insert ASM as the cargo and providing a protected environment that would shield the enzyme from immediate degradation, and, (ii) leveraging the ability of apoE3 to serve as a ligand for the LDLr, and promote receptor-mediated endocytosis of the ND bearing ASM in its entirety. Using these NDs enables targeting the cargo to the lysosomes, where its presence in a functional form is essential for degrading the accumulated SMs in NPD. Failure to degrade or partial degradation of SMs leads to their accumulation in lysosomes with severe detrimental effects to the cell. Although apoAI was the conventional scaffold for building ND platform (Bayburt and Sligar 2010; Sligar and Denisov 2021), apoE3NT was the preferred membrane scaffold protein for the current study because of its ability to bind the LDLr.

Nanodiscs are Efficient and Effective Platforms for Inserting ASMcat

The ND platform for generating TM-ASMcat-ND was constructed with well-established methodology using synthetic POPC (Bayburt et al. 2002; Chuang et al. 2017; Sligar and Denisov 2021 b), with a few modifications such as inclusion of 20% BMP by mass, using DDM and NaDC for the reconstitution process and using 1:1 (w/w) lipid: total protein ratio, with apoE3NT and TM-ASMcat present in 1:1 ratio (w/w). The LDLr binding domain of apoE3, comprising residues 1-191, served as the membrane scaffolding protein. Termed the NT domain, this segment of apoE3 is an independently folded segment bearing a 4-helix bundle, which in the presence of phospholipids, undergoes a dramatic conformational reorganization and adopts a belt-like configuration circumscribing a bilayer of phospholipids in a discoidal geometry (Chuang et al. 2020). This conformational reorganization occurs both in the intact apoE3 and in the isolated NT domain (Fisher et al. 2000; Narayanaswami et al. 2001). NDs bearing the isolated apoE3NT are stable complexes and have the ability to bind the LDLr as efficiently as those constructed with intact apoE3 (minimizing the portions essential for LDLr binding) (Innerarity et al. 1983). In the case of ASMcat, residues 319–579 not only contain a major portion of the catalytic segment but also bears epitopes that are recognized reliably and consistently by a commercially available antibody, which was essential for tracking the ASM component of NDs. After the reconstitution process, the lipid: total protein ratios were ~ 1.5:1 (w/w) for the empty ND and ~ 2:1 (w/w) for the TM-ASMcat-ND suggesting acquisition of more lipids for the assembly and integration of TM-ASMcat into the ND.

The TM helix that was engineered at the N-terminal end enabled insertion of ASMcat into the POPC/BMP bilayer of NDs. This TM helix and related SPC lipopeptides have been shown to insert into a phospholipid bilayer and adopt an α-helical structure (Gustafsson et al. 2000; Johansson et al. 1995; Nilsson et al. 1998), aligning well with the thickness of a bilayer of 4.21 nm for POPC (Pinisetty et al. 2006b) and 4.059 +/- 0.003 nm for dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (DPPC) (Drabik et al. 2020). Attenuated total reflection Fourier transform infrared analysis of SPC revealed that the peptide was oriented with its helical axis parallel to the palmitoyl chain of the DPPC (Nilsson et al. 1998; Vandenbussche et al. 1992). Our AFM analysis also reveals a thickness of ~ 4 nm for empty nanodiscs with POPC and BMP, and a much thicker layer of ~ 15 nm for TM-ASMcat-ND, suggesting insertion of the ASMcat via its TM helix. Based on the AFM findings and from studies with the lipopeptides, we predict that the TM helix in TM-ASMcat is inserted in a similar manner into the ND bilayer formed with POPC and BMP, with its helical axis oriented perpendicular to the plane of the bilayer. A schematic illustration of the ternary complex formed with the lipids (POPC and BMP), apoE3NT and TM-ASMcat is represented in Fig. 12. The (G4S)2 linker separating the TM helix from the ASMcat allows for some flexibility between the two segments, with the ASMcat likely lying parallel to the surface of the ND interacting with the phospholipid headgroups and the negatively charged BMP. In their native environment in the lysosomes, cleavage of SMs by ASM is stimulated by BMP which offers a docking site for the protein, with the saposin-like domains of ASM providing access for hydrolysis (Gallala et al. 2011; Xiong et al. 2016). The presence of BMP is a signature feature of late endosomal/lysosomal membranes within the multivesicular bodies (Kobayashi et al. 1999; Piper and Luzio 2001; Schulze et al. 2009) with its unique structure offering resistance to degradation by phospholipases and improving the stability of the membrane in the highly hydrolytic environment of the lysosomes (Gallala and Sandhoff 2011). It is noteworthy that the native BMP found in the lysosomal membrane is the S, S-(sn-2, sn-2′-diacyl)-BMP, with fatty acyl groups of varying chain length and degree of saturation (Gallala and Sandhoff 2011) differing with tissue and cell types; however, depending on the environment, and when isolated from the lysosomes, the fatty acyl chains can migrate rapidly from the sn-2, sn-2′ to the sn-3, sn-3′ positions, the latter being more thermodynamically stable. The details of the mechanism and under what circumstances this migration occurs are not clear; nevertheless, we used the commercially available S, S-(sn-3, sn-3′-diacyl)-BMP, which has the fatty acyl groups at sn-3, sn-3′, similar to that employed by other researchers (Aldosari et al. 2019; Linke et al. 2001; Xiong et al. 2016). Further, although BMP has been shown by others to aid in lipid binding of ASM (Xiong et al. 2016), in our case, the TM helix served the purpose of lipid binding/anchoring, with the negatively charged BMP providing a lysosomal membrane-like environment. Finally, since the ASM is able to use the native BMP in the destination, use of the S, S-(sn-3, sn-3′-diacyl)-BMP may not be a critical factor.

Based on established literature (Brown and Goldstein 1986; Chavan and Bhattacharjee 2025; Ebner et al. 2025; Islam et al. 2022; Li and Zhang 2019), it is anticipated that TM-ASMcat-ND would undergo dissociation from the LDLr in the early endosomes during the receptor-mediated endocytosis process, followed by particle disintegration upon exposure to the acidic, enzyme-rich environment in the lysosomes. The lumenal pH (~ 4.5–5.0) of the lysosomes activates a broad spectrum of lysosomal hydrolases, including lysosomal acid proteases and acid lipases that degrade the lipoproteins. Dissociation of the lipids of the ND is expected to release the embedded TM-ASMcat, with possible re-insertion into the intra-lysosomal membranes via the TM helix, thereby simulating the natural environment of the ASM (Xiong et al. 2016; Zhou et al. 2016). Thus, the TM helix not only serves to anchor the ASM to the ND during transit but can also likely anchor it in the destination site. In the intact ASM, the saposin domain plays a role in positioning the enzyme active site at the lysosomal membrane surface to the scissile phosphodiester bond.

Biophysical Characterization Reveals TM-ASMcat Incorporation into Nanodiscs

From non-denaturing PAGE analysis, the TM-ASMcat-ND appeared to be distributed as two discrete populations, one with a mass of ~ 440 kDa (small, with an apparent hydrodynamic diameter of 12.2 nm) and the other with a mass of ~ 700 kDa (large, with an apparent hydrodynamic diameter of 18 nm), with the empty ND showing a similar set of populations. Conversion from electrophoretic mobility to Stokes’ radius typically assumes that particles are approximately spherical. For non-spherical, discoidal particles such as NDs, non-denaturing PAGE analysis may not yield an accurate size estimation but offers reasonable estimates of populations and size ranges. TEM data further confirmed a mixed population of particles with similar major axis (averaging ~ 31 nm) for the empty ND and TM-ASMcat-ND. However, the TM-ASMcat-ND distribution appears broader with greater heterogeneity than that of the empty ND, which is more symmetric. While neither technique is able to provide a definitive size of the TM-ASMcat-ND by itself, when interpreted jointly, they define a range for the diameter for the NDs. Typically, these NDs can accommodate 4 or 6 apoE3NT per particle as reported by us and others for empty ND containing POPC or DMPC with apoE3NT or apoE4NT with particle diameter ranging from 15 to 22 nm based on non-denaturing PAGE analysis (Benedicto et al. 2024; Chromy et al. 2007; Khumsupan et al. 2011; Kim et al. 2015). The slightly larger diameter for the empty ND compared to that reported by us earlier and others may be attributed to the presence of BMP, which was unique to the current study and not used in other studies. For TM-ASMcat-ND, theoretical calculations indicate that the 440 kDa population could bear 4 apoE3NT molecules surrounding the discoidal particle as a double belt, and 1 or 2 TM-ASMcat per particle, while the 669 kDa population could bear 6 apoE3NT molecules and 2 or 3 TM-ASMcat per particle. AFM measurements further support the concept of the insertion of TM-ASMcat in a single ND with the marked increase in particle height (14.0 ± 0.4 nm versus 4.3 ± 0.1 nm for empty NDs). AFM provides more accurate measurements of particle height than the diameter because the lateral dimensions are affected by tip-convolution effects, whereas height measurements are largely independent of tip geometry. AFM tends to overestimate the actual particle size, as the tip (~ 10 nm radius) and the particle interact and the recorded image is the convolution of tip and particle. Similarly, DLS measurements overestimate particle diameter in both the empty ND and TM-ASMcat-ND, which is attributed to the hydrodynamic radius that is typically larger than the actual radius of the particle. The prominence of the larger peak in the DLS intensity distribution reflects a stronger light scattering and does not necessarily indicate a larger number of particles. This is because DLS reports intensity-weighted distributions, with larger particles scattering more light than smaller particles given that the intensity is proportional to particle diameter6 (d6).

ApoE Mediated Uptake of TM-ASMcat-ND via LDLr and Targeted Delivery to lysosomes

The TM-ASMcat-ND displayed a strong ability to hydrolyze SM indicating that embedding the enzyme did not affect the catalytic activity; the doubling in sphingomyelinase activity noted in TM-ASMcat-ND compared to lipid-free TM-ASMcat is attributed to the presence of the enzyme in its native membrane-like environment. Moreover, the presence of TM-ASMcat in the NDs does not appear to affect the ability of apoE3NT to serve as a ligand for the LDLr, which is a critical aspect of the study since we are aiming to target the cargo to the lysosomes by LDLr mediated endocytosis pathway. Since the LDLr is found in almost all cell types, it would ensure that the ASM reaches all cells in the peripheral system. We used the GBM cells to demonstrate cellular uptake as a proof of concept for targeted delivery via the LDLr. Other researchers have shown cellular uptake of recombinant human ASM of various liposomal formulations, with PC/PS/BMP/cholesterol formulations eliciting significant cellular uptake though the uptake pathway was not defined (Aldosari et al. 2019). These authors report an increased efficiency of ASM delivery to lysosomes in RAW 264.7 macrophages when supplied as a liposomal formulation compared to the lipid-free enzyme, based on the Pearson’s value (~ 0.4 for the former and random colocalization for the latter). They achieved their objective of supplying ASM in a liposomal formulation to reduce extracellular degradation, improve cellular uptake and lysosomal localization, which are important factors for consideration during ERT. In the current study, we developed a nanodisc platform that potentially provides further shielding of ASM from proteolytic degradation if injected in the plasma by virtue of the presence of the scaffold protein, apoE3NT. Further, by serving as a high-affinity ligand for the LDLr (Weisgraber 1994), apoE3NT facilitates delivery of the TM-ASMcat to the lysosomes with high Pearson’s value (0.81 for ASM colocalization with apoE3NT and 0.92 for ASM colocalization for LysoTracker in lysosomes) in GBM, indicative of increased efficiency of lysosomal delivery.

The SM lipidomics analysis provided further functional support and validated the use of this nanodisc platform bearing ASM for ERT. The decrease noted in the overall SM species content in cells treated with TM-ASMcat-ND was notable compared to that observed with empty ND. It is not known at this point if the decreases noted in SMs with fatty acyl chains C18:1 and C24:0/C24:1/C24:2/C24:3 are related to the relatively broader substrate specificity of ASM in general, or if it is due to the specific construct or cell type used in this study. It is anticipated that the profile may be altered if other segments of the ASM protein are included in the construct, an aspect that should be considered in future studies. While the current study focused on whole cell lysates, it is worthwhile to determine the lysosomal SM content using the intact protein and specific ASM-deficient cells.

Summary and Perspectives

The current study offers a fundamental understanding of the structural and functional aspects of the lysosomal membrane protein, ASM, and proof-of-concept for using apoE3NT-containing NDs to transport and selectively deliver ASM to lysosomes. It bears tremendous potential to be used for ERT to treat NPD-A, NPD-B or NPD-A/B. The mechanistic and structural insights presented in our report offer the potential for a controlled release of ASM since it is restricted by the apoE3NT scaffold that circumscribes the lipids, which may decrease the rapid and dynamic interchange of lipids that typically occurs between liposomes and plasma lipoproteins. Further, the ND offers a protective environment against protease degradation for the embedded ASM, thereby effectively increasing its bioavailability and potentially decreasing the dosage frequency. In addition to serving as a ‘belt’, apoE3NT offers a point of entry into cells by serving as a ligand for the LDLr family of receptors, which is a particularly attractive feature to facilitate delivery to neuronal sites across the blood-brain barrier, an aspect that is important for treating NPD. Currently available ERT for treating NPD-A, NPD-B or NPD-A/B appears to be promoted by cellular internalization via mannose receptors, which is facilitated by the presence of glycan moieties on ASM derived from mammalian cells (Dhami and Schuchman 2004); alternatively, it appears to be facilitated by scavenger receptors or phagocytosis when ASM is supplied as a liposomal formulation (Aldosari et al., 2019). Since ASM deficiency appears to be accompanied by defective trafficking via the mannose-6-phosphate receptor (Dhami and Schuchman 2004), our currently proposed approach may offer a viable alternative for efficient delivery to the lysosomes via the LDLr. This approach needs further refinement and optimization to assess the effectiveness in NPD Type A and B cells and in animal models. Further studies are also needed to assess the cost-effectiveness of this technology, considering that larger amounts of ASM can be produced by the bacterial compared to the mammalian expression system, and to test the immunogenicity of this formulation. Importantly, this approach can be extended for the treatment of other LSDs as well by protein engineering the deficient enzyme of interest in NDs with similar formulations.
