Determinants of Diabetes Remission and Relapse following Sleeve Gastrectomy and Gastric Bypass: A 2- and 5-Year Follow-Up Study
Authors: Beatrice Leyaro, Lyz Howie, Abdulmajid Ali, Raymond Carragher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBariatric surgery is an established treatment for obesity and has been shown to improve glycaemic control and frequently induces remission of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). However, the long-term sustainability of remission remains uncertain as relapse may occur over time. This study evaluated short- and long-term remission rates and identified factors associated with remission durability and relapse.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, which included 186 patients with obesity and type2 diabetes who underwent sleeve gastrectomy (SG) or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass between January 2009 and December 2020 at University Hospital Ayr. Diabetes remission was defined as HbA1c <6.0% without anti-diabetic medication use, assessed at 2 years (short term) and 5 years (long term). Multivariable logistic and Cox regression models were used to identify factors associated with remission and relapse.ResultsTwo- and 5-year post-surgery remission rates were 57.5% and 36.4%, respectively. Shorter duration of diabetes (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.76–0.96; p = 0.009), lower preoperative HbA1c (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.43–0.87; p = 0.005), and not using anti-diabetic medications before surgery (p < 0.05) were all associated with a higher likelihood of diabetes remission at 2 years. Of 107 patients who achieved remission at 2 years, 33.4% experienced diabetes relapse by 5 years. Age (HR 1.06; 95% CI 1.01–1.12; p < 0.015) and use of anti-diabetic medications (p < 0.05) were identified as significant factors associated with diabetes relapse.ConclusionBariatric surgery is effective in achieving early remission of T2DM; however, one-third of patients experience relapse within 5 years. A more favourable preoperative metabolic profile and younger age play a crucial role in remission durability. These findings highlight the importance of long-term follow-up and individualised patient counselling on expected diabetes outcomes after bariatric surgery.

Introduction

Obesity is a major independent and modifiable risk factor for the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The relationship between obesity and T2DM is complex and involves various metabolic, hormonal, and inflammatory mechanisms [1]. Obesity accounts for 80%–90% of the risk of developing T2DM among adults, with the prevalence of T2DM being higher in individuals with obesity compared to individuals without obesity [2]. Bariatric surgery has shown significant impacts in reducing rates of T2DM, improving cardiovascular health, and decreasing morbidity and mortality. Studies suggest that the short-term bariatric surgery can lead to T2DM remission in approximately 60%–95% of patients [3, 4].

Among surgical options, sleeve gastrectomy (SG) and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) are the most commonly performed procedures worldwide [5]. Both improve glucose homeostasis through weight-dependent mechanisms and hormonal changes such as enhanced insulin sensitivity and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) secretion [6, 7]. Nevertheless, remission is not always sustained, and a proportion of patients experience diabetes relapse after initial improvement [3].

Factors influencing diabetes remission include preoperative diabetes duration, baseline glycaemic control, medication use, and the magnitude of postoperative weight loss [8, 9]. However, evidence from randomised controlled trials studies may not fully represent outcomes in routine clinical practice. Evidence from routine clinical data in Scotland is particularly limited, despite the region’s high burden of obesity and diabetes and notable socioeconomic disparities [10–12]. Analysis using real-world clinical data from this population can provide insight into long-term diabetes remission and relapse following bariatric surgery across different patient subgroups.

In the UK, the uptake of bariatric surgery remains lower than in other European countries, with only 0.20% of eligible patients undergoing surgery [13, 14]. Within the UK, Scotland performs fewer bariatric procedures per eligible patient than England [15]. This low utilisation highlights the need to better identify which patient cohorts would have the most long-term metabolic benefit from bariatric surgery, particularly in the study geographical area, which has high levels of socioeconomic deprivation [10, 11]. This study evaluates long-term outcomes of T2DM remission and relapse following bariatric surgery in Scotland. Understanding these factors will help healthcare providers and patients make more informed decisions for optimal surgical outcomes.

Methods

Study Design, Population, and Data Sources

This was a retrospective cohort study of prospectively collected data of patients with obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m2) and T2DM who were referred to the Department of Bariatric Metabolic surgery, University Hospital Ayr, between January 2009 and December 2020 for bariatric surgery consideration. All patients who received RYGB or SG bariatric procedure were included in the study. Patients were excluded if they had less than 1-year post-surgery follow-up, received bariatric procedures outside University Hospital Ayr, or underwent revision or modification procedures.

Data were extracted from multiple sources including an electronic bariatric database in the Bariatric and Metabolic Department of the University Hospital Ayr, the Scottish Care Information (SCI) Diabetes platform (SCI-DC, Scotland) [16], Clinical Portal [17], and bariatric patients’ assessment forms (a non-electronic database). Extracted baseline data included sociodemographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, comorbidities such as hypertension and sleep apnoea, diabetes duration, use of diabetic medications, and laboratory data related to glycaemic control (glycosylated haemoglobin [HbA1c]). The number of anti-diabetic drugs used was defined as the number of different diabetes medications a patient was taking before surgery. Each distinct medication was counted separately for each patient. The total medication count was then categorised as 0, 1, 2, or ≥3. In addition, insulin use was coded separately as a binary variable (yes/no).

Follow-up data on body weight (kgs), anti-diabetic therapy status, and HbA1c were also obtained from these databases. The standard post-operative follow-up is at approximately 6 and 12 months and annually thereafter for at least 2 years [18]. Missing or incomplete records data were supplemented with the corresponding information obtained from the SCI Diabetes or Clinical Portal databases, where available.

Outcomes and Definitions

The main outcome measures were T2DM remission and relapse, defined in accordance with the standardised criteria recommended by the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery [19]. Complete diabetes remission was defined as normal measures of glucose metabolism (HbA1c <6.0%) and no use of anti-diabetic medication. This HbA1c threshold reflects a return to normal glycaemic level without glucose-lowering therapy and has been widely applied in previous bariatric surgery outcome studies [4, 9, 20]. Complete remission was assessed using available HbA1c measurements at each time point; no imputation was performed for missing values. However, because HbA1c data were not available for all patients, remission was operationally defined as the absence of anti-diabetic medication use at annual follow-up assessments, consistent with previous studies [8, 21]. Remission was assessed at both 2-year and 5-year postoperative intervals, to evaluate both short-term (2-year) and long-term (5-year) outcomes. Diabetes relapse was defined among participants who achieved remission at 2 years as the subsequent occurrence of either HbA1c ≥6.5% and/or re-initiation of anti-diabetic medication. For time-to-event analyses, among participants who achieved diabetes remission at 2 years postoperatively, follow-up for relapse began at the 2-year visit (time zero) and continued annually thereafter until relapse or censored at the end of study (online suppl. Fig S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552002) (maximum 7 years postoperatively reflecting the maximum of 5 years after remission).

Weight loss outcomes, measured as the percentage total weight loss (%TWL) and percentage excess weight loss (%EWL), 1 year post-surgery were evaluated. The %TWL was calculated as ([pre-surgery weight] – [post-surgery weight])/([pre-surgery weight]) × 100. In addition, a %TWL threshold of ≥20% was used do define optimal clinical response weight loss after bariatric surgery. The %EWL was calculated as ([pre-surgery weight postoperative weight]/[pre-surgery weight ideal body weight]) × 100 [19, 22].

Statistical Analysis

Stata statistical tools (Stata, version 18.0; Stata Corp) was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics are used to summarise baseline characteristics of the patients by diabetes remission status. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using histograms and Q-Q plots. Frequencies and percentages (%) are reported for categorical variables, mean and standard deviation for continuous variables where the data are normally distributed, and median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables where the data are not normally distributed. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients with and without 5-year follow-up. Differences in patient characteristics by remission status were assessed with t tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, or chi-square tests, as appropriate.

We examine the association between 1-year post-surgery weight loss and remission status, given that previous studies have demonstrated an association between weight loss and diabetes remission outcomes [3, 23]. Univariable logistic regression was performed to assess the association between each independent variable and diabetes remission. All clinically relevant variables such as age, sex, and bariatric procedure among others were included in the multivariable regression model regardless of their statistical significance. In addition, fully adjusted models including all variables were also performed, and the results are provided in online supplementary Tables S3–S5. Unadjusted (OR) and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. This analysis considers only preoperative factors. Statistical significance was determined using Wald test statistics, with two-sided p values.

To assess diabetes relapse following remission, the same set of variables included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis of diabetes remission was used to model the hazard of diabetes relapse. Univariable Cox regression analyses were first conducted to evaluate the association (hazard risk) of each variable with relapse. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate adjusted hazard ratios for relapse. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), no multicollinearity was detected, the mean VIF was 1.30, all of the predictor VIFs were in the range of 1 with maximum 1.62, and an assessment of the assumptions of the Cox proportional hazards model was conducted using the Schoenfeld residual-based test after fitting the cox regression model, where p > 0.05 indicates no violation of PH assumptions. Analyses were based on available data, with no imputation for missing values.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Cohort

A total of 186 patients were included in the cohort, with complete follow-up available through 3 years. The number of patients available for follow-up thereafter declined from 184 at year 4 to 156 at year 7. HbA1c measurements were not available for all patients at each time point. The proportion of missing HbA1c values at subsequent follow-up was 5.9% at year 1, 10.2% at year 2, 16.6% at year 3, 22.3% at year 4, 22.5% and at year 5, 23.2%. For the diabetes relapse analysis, follow-up was extended to 7 years (Fig. 1; online suppl. Table S1). All 186 patients were included in the post-operative 2-year diabetes remission evaluation. Among them, 107 (57.5%) patients achieved remission. For the 5-year assessment, 13 patients with less than 5 years of follow-up were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, 173 patients were analysed for 5 years diabetes remission. The proportion of patients who maintained prolonged diabetes remission at 5 years was 63 (36.4%). Complete diabetes remission was achieved by 93 patients (55.7%) at 2 years and by 40 patients (29.8%) at 5 years. A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients with and without 5-year follow-up showed no significant differences between the groups (p > 0.05) (online suppl. Table S2).

Table 1 presents the characteristics of patients stratified by remission status at 2 years post-bariatric surgery. Among patients with diabetes who underwent bariatric surgery, 107 (57.5%) achieved remission within 2 years. Patients who experienced remission had a slightly lower median age at the time of surgery compared to those who did not (49.0 years [IQR 41.0–55.0] versus 52.0 years [IQR 46.0–56.0], p = 0.014). There were no significant differences between the remission and non-remission groups regarding the proportion of patients with depression, sleep apnoea, or hypertension (p > 0.05). Similarly, deprivation status did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.471).

Association between 1-Year Weight Loss and Diabetes Remission

Greater weight loss at 1 year was significantly associated with diabetes remission at 2 years (Table 3). Each 1% increase in %TWL increased odds of remission by 5% (OR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08, p = 0.006) and achieving ≥20% TWL was associated with 85% higher odds of remission (OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.01–3.41, p = 0.045).

For 5-year remission outcomes, %TWL remained the only weight loss parameter significantly associated with remission. When analysed as continuous, each 1% increase in %TWL 1 year after surgery was associated with a 4% increase in the odds of remission (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: [1.00, 1.07], p = 0.038). However, when %TWL was categorised using a ≥20% threshold to reflect an optimal clinical response weight loss, no significant association with diabetes remission at 5 years was observed (p > 0.05).

Factors Associated with Diabetes Remission at 2 Years Post-Surgery

In the univariable logistic regression analysis, younger age, shorter duration of diabetes, lower pre-surgery HbA1c, no insulin use, and fewer anti-diabetic medications were significantly associated with higher odds of diabetes remission at 2 years post-surgery (Table 4). Each additional year of age and diabetes duration decreased odds of remission by 5% (OR = 0.95, 95% CI [0.92, 0.98], p = 0.011) and 24% (OR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.69, 0.84], p < 0.001), respectively. Patients who were using insulin before surgery had markedly lower odds of remission compared to those who were not on insulin (OR = 0.10, 95% CI [0.03, 0.27], p < 0.001). Sex, type of bariatric procedure, and deprivation status showed no associations with 2-year remission.

In the multivariable analysis, shorter duration of diabetes, lower pre-surgery HbA1c, and fewer anti-diabetic medications remained significant factors associated with remission (Table 4). Adjusted for insulin use, patient age, type of bariatric procedure, deprivation status, sex, pre-surgery HbA1c, and number of anti-diabetic medications, each additional year of diabetes duration decreased the odds of remission by 14% (OR = 0.86, 95% CI [0.76, 0.96], p = 0.009), and each one percentage increase in HbA1c decreased the odds of remission by 39% (OR = 0.61, 95% CI [0.43, 0.87], p = 0.005). Type of bariatric procedure and insulin use were not statistically significant after adjustment.

Factors Associated with Diabetes Remission at 5 Years Post-Surgery

The univariable factors associated with diabetes remission at 5 years post-surgery were similar to those observed at 2 years (Table 5). Each additional year of age and duration of diabetes decreased the odds of remission by 6% (OR = 0.94, 95% CI: [0.91, 0.98], p = 0.006) and 16% (OR = 0.84, 95% CI [0.77, 0.92], p < 0.001), respectively. Patients who were using insulin before surgery had significantly lower odds of achieving remission at 5 years (OR = 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.37], p = 0.004). Type of bariatric procedure and sex were not statistically significantly associated with remission.

In multivariable analysis, younger age, lower pre-surgery HbA1c, and a fewer number of anti-diabetic medications remained significant determinants of higher remission likelihood (Table 5). Each additional year of age decreased odds of remission by 7% (OR = 0.93, 95% CI [0.88, 0.98], p = 0.011). Compared with patients not using anti-diabetic medications, use of one anti-diabetic medication (OR = 0.14, 95% CI [0.04, 0.45], p = 0.001), two medications (OR = 0.14, 95% CI [0.04, 0.53], p = 0.04), and three or more medications (OR = 0.03, 95% CI [0.003–0.18], p < 0.001) were each associated with significantly lower odds of remission. Neither type of bariatric procedure nor insulin use was statistically significant.

Factors Associated with Diabetes Relapse following Initial Remission at 2 Years

Among patients who achieved diabetes remission at 2 years (n = 107), 36 (33.4%) experienced diabetes relapse by 5 years. In a univariable Cox regression (Table 6), older age and a greater number of pre-operative anti-diabetic medications were significantly associated with increased risk of diabetes relapse. Each additional year of age was associated 4% increased hazard of relapse (HR = 1.04, 95% CI [1.00, 1.09], p = 0.036). Compared with patients who were not using anti-diabetic medications before surgery, those using one anti-diabetic medication had nearly fourfold higher hazard of relapse (HR = 3.92, 95% CI [1.35, 11.35], p = 0.012), and those using three or more anti-diabetic medications had over fivefold higher risk of relapse (HR = 5.40, 95% CI [1.34, 21.64], p = 0.017). Other variables, including the type of bariatric procedure, sex, pre-surgery HbA1c, and duration of diabetes, were not significantly associated with relapse.

In the multivariable analysis, age and number of anti-diabetic medications remained significantly associated with relapse (Table 6). Each additional year of age increased relapse hazard 6% (HR = 1.06, 95% CI [1.01, 1.12], p = 0.015). Compared to patients who were not on anti-diabetic medications before surgery, use of one medication remained associated with an approximate fourfold higher relapse risk (HR = 4.27, 95% CI [1.45, 12.52], p = 0.008), while the associations for using two, or three or more, anti-diabetic medications were not statistically significant. Discussion

This study demonstrates that bariatric surgery is effective in inducing remission of T2DM, although remission declines substantially over time. Diabetes remission was observed in 57.5% of patients at 2 years and 36.4% at 5 years, with one-third of initial remitters experiencing relapse.

Lower preoperative HbA1c levels, a shorter pre-surgery duration of diabetes, younger age, and absence of preoperative anti-diabetic medication use were significantly associated with diabetes remission. In contrast, the type of bariatric procedure, pre-surgery use of insulin, sex, and pre-surgery BMI were not significantly associated with diabetes remission.

The short-term remission rate observed in this study is comparable with several previous studies, [21, 24, 25], but lower or higher rates have also been described, ranging from 28.8% in Douglas et al. [26] and 38.3% in Mizera et al. [27] to 72.0% in Hollanda et al. [28] and 77.1% in Stenberg et al. [8] studies, reflecting the wide variability in remission rates reported across the literature.

Similarly, the 5-year remission rate of 36.4% aligns with other long-term studies [21, 27], while remaining within the broad range of remission rates reported in the literature (25%–92.7%) [4, 29]. The variability in reported remission rates across studies may be attributed to differences in study populations, follow-up duration, and definitions of diabetes remission applied, [19, 20, 29, 30]. The use of a standardised definition of remission in the present study enhances comparability with literature [19]. While this study has provided evidence for supporting the effectiveness of MBS, the decline in remission rates over time highlights the importance of long-term patient follow-up and additional strategies, such as lifestyle interventions to sustain remission.

Consistent with previous studies, lower pre-surgery HbA1c levels, a shorter duration of diabetes, and not using anti-diabetic medications before surgery were identified as significant factors associated with diabetes remission for short term [21, 27, 31]. These factors are biologically plausible markers of preserved pancreatic β-cell function, which plays a central role in the restoration of normoglycaemia following surgery [32, 33]. Longer diabetes duration is associated with progressive β-cell failure and diminished insulin secretory capacity, thereby reducing the likelihood of achieving remission despite substantial postoperative weight loss [28, 32, 34].

In the long-term, lower preoperative HbA1c levels, younger age, and not using anti-diabetic medications before surgery remained significant factors associated with sustained remission. These results suggest that early intervention, before irreversible β-cell dysfunction and age-related metabolic decline occur, may improve the durability of diabetes remission after MBS [32]. Similar age-related effects have been reported in large Swedish population-based studies, where older patients demonstrated lower metabolic responsiveness and less favourable long-term outcomes [8].

A particularly important finding of this study relates to the distinction between insulin use and the number of anti-diabetic medications use. Insulin use was not significantly associated with remission in multivariable model; however, when the number of anti-diabetic medications use was omitted, insulin use became a significant factor. This suggests that the number of anti-diabetic medication use may provide a better overall indicator of diabetes severity, reflecting both glycaemic control and underlying β-cell reserve [8, 25]; thus, including both HbA1c and insulin use in the model may have masked their individual effects on diabetes remission. These findings align with previous studies, where insulin use and poor glycaemic control (as indicated by HbA1c) were associated with lower remission rates, but their effects were attenuated when accounting for the overall treatment burden [27]. These findings highlight the importance of considering overall treatment intensity when counselling patients undergoing bariatric procedures to optimise outcomes.

No significant association was observed between the type of bariatric procedure and diabetes remission in either the short or long term, consistent with findings from randomised trials and observational studies comparing SG and RYGB [21, 35–37]. However, some other studies have reported superior diabetes remission following RYGB compared with SG, and this difference has often been attributed, at least in part, to the greater weight loss typically achieved with RYGB [3, 38]. While our previously published analysis of the same cohort [22] found that RYGB resulted in superior weight loss relative to SG, this did not translate into a significantly higher rate of diabetes remission. This difference may be partly explained by the relatively small sample size in the current study, which may have affected the study’s ability to detect differences between procedures.

Additionally, pre-surgery BMI and sex were not significantly associated with either short- or long-term diabetes remission, and these findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies [39, 40]. The fact that not all individuals with T2DM experience remission after bariatric surgery highlights the importance of identifying reliable preoperative characteristics that can predict diabetes remission at an individual level. A personalised approach to patient selection and counselling before surgery could enhance outcomes by ensuring those most likely to benefit are appropriately identified. Moreover, the marked influence of age on long-term remission highlights the importance of early intervention as patients who undergo surgery at a younger age, before significant beta-cell deterioration occurs, are more likely to achieve and sustain diabetes remission.

In the present study among 107 patients who experienced diabetes remission at 2 years after bariatric surgery, 33.4% experienced a relapse. This rate aligns with previous studies reporting rates between 24% and 35% [23, 41–43]. Relapse was more common in older patients and those using anti-diabetic medications preoperatively, consistent with previous studies [43, 44]. These findings again supporting the concept that disease severity and reduced β-cell reserve underpin the reduced durability of remission [32, 34]. The variations in relapse across studies are likely due to differences in patient populations and inconsistencies in defining remission and relapse, with alternative timeframes employed in some studies [44, 45]. In this study, relapse was defined based both on the prescription of diabetes medications and HbA1c levels, rather than prescription alone. The applied definition may account for the higher observed relapse rate compared to previous studies, including those who did not adopt the American Diabetes Association’s definitions of diabetes remission and relapse [23, 43, 46].

Weight loss remained an important contributor to long-term remission. Percentage total weight loss (%TWL) at 1 year was associated with sustained diabetes remission at 5 years when analysed as a continuous variable, whereas achieving a ≥20% TWL a single threshold reduced statistical sensitivity [47]. Analysing %TWL as a continuous variable captures the incremental effect associated with increasing weight loss, while categorising %TWL at a single cut-off may have limited the ability to detect associations by grouping individuals with differing levels of weight loss [47]. Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that greater weight loss following metabolic surgery is strongly associated with higher rates of diabetes remission [8, 48]. Barthold et al. [48] reported that the likelihood of achieving initial diabetes remission increased progressively with %TWL, from 0 to 5% up to 20%. However, long-term studies indicated that remission may be influenced by other postoperative factors, such as recurrent weight gain, which may reduce the durability of early remission and the long-term effectiveness of initial weight loss [23, 49].

Previously published analyses of this cohort reported a mean %TWL of 18.9%, 95% CI (17.7–20.3) at 1 year post-surgery, which decreased to 13.5%, 95% CI (11.5–15.4) at 5 years [22]. This decline in %TWL highlights the challenge of recurrent weight gain and its potential role to a reduced likelihood of sustained diabetes remission over time.

Despite the high likelihood of diabetes relapse observed in this study, previous studies have indicated that MBS remains associated with substantial reductions in diabetes-related complications and improvements in quality of life [50–52]. Hence, this study supports the importance of continuing support and care for those who have had bariatric surgery to enhance the benefits of metabolic surgery.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The inclusion of both 2- and 5-year diabetes remission outcomes, alongside explicit evaluation of diabetes relapse, represents a key strength of this study. This offers clinically relevant insight into the sustainability of remission after bariatric surgery and reflects patterns of disease over time in routine clinical practice. However, there are some limitations that should be noted. First, not all patients completed the 5-year follow-up as these are routine hospital data, and this will have some effect on the results estimates. In addition, missing data particularly on HbA1c may have influenced the results observed in this study. Second, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power and contributes to wider confidence intervals observed; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with cautions. Third, as this is a single centre study the findings of the study may not be generalisable across institutions and the absence of detailed population characteristics, including ethnicity, further limits generalisability to more diverse populations. Fourth, there were no data available to the study on factors such as biochemical parameters and behavioural characteristics such as diet and physical activity, all of which are believed to influence the outcome of metabolic bariatric surgery [53, 54]. Lastly, this study reported the overall burden of anti-diabetic medication use; however, detailed information on whether these medications included newer classes such as GLP-1 agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors was not analysed. Previous studies have reported mixed results regarding the influence of GLP1 or SGLT2 inhibitors use on diabetes remission following bariatric surgery [55, 56]. Raja et al. [55] reported that pre-operative use of SGLT2 inhibitors was associated with a lower likelihood of diabetes remission after bariatric surgery. In the present study, we were unable to assess the influence of these medication classes on remission outcomes.

Conclusion

In this study of 186 diabetic patients with obesity who received bariatric surgery at Ayr University Hospital, 57.5% achieved remission 2 years post-surgery, 36.4% remained in remission at 5 years post-surgery. Among those who achieved remission at 2 years, 33.4% experienced diabetes relapse by 5 years. Factors previously associated with diabetes remission and relapse in other studies including duration of diabetes, pre-surgery HbA1c levels, use of insulin, age, and use of anti-diabetic medications before surgery were also observed in this cohort. These findings highlight the importance of continuing support and long-term follow-up after MBS. Future studies should examine postoperative therapies and strategies including pharmacological and lifestyle interventions, to improve the durability of diabetes remission following bariatric surgery.
