Inflammation and sarcopenia exhibit distinct associations with psychopathology and executive function in schizophrenia
Authors: Yee-Lam E Chan, Chih-Yuan Hung, Wei-Chung Mao, Tung-Ping Su, Wang-Sheng Lin, Hsin-Yu Yang, Mao-Hsuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Immune dysregulation and premature physical aging have been implicated in schizophrenia; however, few studies have concurrently examined inflammation, sarcopenia, psychopathology, cognitive function, and physical functioning within the same cohort. We investigated whether inflammation and sarcopenia exhibit distinct associations with clinical and cognitive outcomes in schizophrenia.Methods:This cross-sectional study recruited inpatients with schizophrenia from Suao and YuanShan branches of Taipei Veterans General Hospital. Psychopathology was assessed using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). Sarcopenia was defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 criteria. Cognitive function was assessed with the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST), 2-back, and Go/No-Go task. Circulating inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein [CRP], soluble interleukin-6 receptor, soluble interleukin-2 receptor [sIL-2R], and soluble tumor necrosis factor-α receptor type 1) were measured by ELISA. Multivariable general linear models were used to examine associations, adjusting for age, sex, education, body mass index, and medication use.Results:A total of 120 patients with schizophrenia aged 20 to 65 years were recruited; sarcopenia was present in 21 participants (17.5%), and low-grade systemic inflammation (CRP >3 mg/L) in 41 (34.2%). Patients with inflammation had higher PANSS scores and slower walking speed, and performed worse on WCST perseverative response (all p < 0.05). Patients with sarcopenia had higher PANSS total scores (74.76 vs 63.75; p = 0.014) but did not differ on cognitive measures. In multivariable analyses, CRP was positively associated with PANSS positive symptoms (B = 1.94 [unstandardized coefficient]; p = 0.009). Higher sIL-2R was associated with worse executive function (failure to maintain set), but not after multiple comparison correction. No significant inflammation-sarcopenia interactions were observed.Conclusion:These findings suggest that systemic inflammation and sarcopenia are independently associated with greater symptom severity in schizophrenia, highlighting the potential value of integrated metabolic and inflammatory assessment in clinical care.Keywords: Biomarker, Cognitive function, Frailty, Inflammation, Psychopathology

1. INTRODUCTION

Schizophrenia is a chronic, relapsing psychiatric disorder affecting approximately 0.7% to 1% of the global population and is associated with functional disability and premature mortality.1 Beyond positive and negative symptoms, patients with schizophrenia experience pervasive cognitive impairment and physical dysfunction, which together contribute to long-term functional decline and a marked social and economic burden.2 The pathophysiology of schizophrenia remains incompletely understood, underscoring the need to identify biological subtypes that may inform personalized interventions.3

Accumulating evidence has highlighted immune and inflammatory dysregulation as biological features of schizophrenia.4,5 Chronic low-grade systemic inflammation and altered cytokine signaling have been consistently reported, with meta-analyses demonstrating elevated circulating levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), soluble interleukin (IL)-6 receptor (sIL-6R), soluble tumor necrosis factor-α receptor type 1 (sTNFα-R1), and soluble IL-2 receptor (sIL-2R).6,7 Higher inflammatory burden has been associated with greater psychopathology severity and poorer clinical outcomes in patients with schizophrenia.8,9 In addition to symptom severity, inflammation has been linked to cognitive impairment in schizophrenia.10 A recent meta-analysis reported associations between peripheral inflammatory markers and deficits across multiple cognitive domains.11 However, findings remain heterogeneous, suggesting that inflammation-related cognitive impairment may be domain-specific rather than global. In particular, executive dysfunction has been proposed as a key domain associated with inflammatory processes.12,13

Parallel to immune dysregulation, schizophrenia is increasingly recognized as a disorder associated with premature physical aging.14 Sarcopenia, characterized by low muscle mass, reduced muscle strength, and impaired physical performance, has been reported at higher rates in patients with schizophrenia than in the general population.15 Beyond its physical consequences, sarcopenia may reflect reduced physiological reserve and has been proposed to confer vulnerability to adverse neuropsychiatric and cognitive outcomes.16,17 Chronic inflammation has been proposed as a key biological contributor to sarcopenia in older adults18; however, evidence linking inflammatory markers and sarcopenia in schizophrenia remains inconsistent, and few studies have examined immune markers and sarcopenia concurrently within the same cohort.19 Despite growing evidence associating inflammation with psychopathology and cognition, several critical gaps remain. First, few studies have simultaneously examined psychopathology, cognitive function, and physical performance within the same schizophrenia cohort. Second, it remains unclear whether inflammation-related clinical and cognitive features overlap with or are distinct from sarcopenia-related physical vulnerability. Third, whether sarcopenia modifies the associations between inflammatory burden and clinical or cognitive outcomes has rarely been tested.

Accordingly, the primary aim of this study was to examine the associations between systemic inflammatory burden, indexed by circulating inflammatory markers including CRP, sTNFα-R1, sIL-2R, and sIL-6R, and psychopathology and cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia. The secondary aim was to evaluate sarcopenia as a physical vulnerability phenotype and to determine whether inflammation and sarcopenia show overlapping or distinct patterns of association with clinical severity, cognitive function, and physical performance. We hypothesized that higher inflammatory burden would be associated with greater psychopathology severity and poorer physical performance, with selective associations with executive dysfunction. We further examined whether sarcopenia moderates the associations between inflammation and psychopathology or cognitive performance.

2. METHODS

2.1. Participants and study design

In this cross-sectional study, we enrolled 120 inpatients aged 20 to 65 years with schizophrenia from the chronic wards of the Suao and YuanShan branches of Taipei Veterans General Hospital between December 2023 and March 2024. Schizophrenia was diagnosed using the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview and confirmed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). Eligible participants were required to be able to speak Mandarin, read Chinese, and provide written informed consent, and were excluded if they had major systemic diseases (eg, malignancy, heart failure, severe pulmonary disease, or uremia), endocrinopathies, or current use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, or other immunomodulatory agents; individuals with known systemic or neurologic conditions likely to affect cognitive performance were also excluded. Each participant underwent a structured psychiatric interview conducted by a psychiatrist. A comprehensive medical history review was performed to screen for relevant physical illnesses. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (2023-09-009C); all participants provided written informed consent.

2.2. Clinical assessment

Demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), educational attainment, and current psychotropic medication use, were recorded for each participant. Symptom severity was evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), a 30-item clinician-rated instrument that assesses positive symptoms (7 items), negative symptoms (7 items), and general psychopathology (16 items).

2.3. Physical performance and body composition

Physical function was evaluated using walking speed and handgrip strength. These measures are commonly incorporated into frailty and sarcopenia assessments because they are feasible in clinical settings, objective, and functionally meaningful, with established associations to physical vulnerability.20 Walking speed was assessed using a 6-m walk test conducted on a straight, level walkway without turns. Participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace. Gait speed (m/s) was calculated as the distance traveled divided by the time required to complete the walk. Handgrip strength was measured using a handheld dynamometer (EH101; Camry, Zhongshan Camry Electronic Co, Ltd, China). Participants were instructed to squeeze the dynamometer with maximal effort for at least 5 seconds. The test was repeated three times using the dominant hand (all participants were right-handed), and the highest value was used for analysis.

All participants received a whole-body dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan (Lunar Prodigy instrument; GE Healthcare, Madison, WI). Total fat mass, fat‐free lean body mass, and bone mineral density were recorded. Appendicular skeletal mass was defined as the total fat‐free lean body mass from four limbs, and the relative appendicular skeletal muscle mass (RASM) index was derived from appendicular skeletal mass divided by height squared (kg/m2). Total body fat percentage was calculated as total fat mass divided by total body mass times 100.

Sarcopenia was defined according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia 2019 (AWGS 2019) consensus.21 Low muscle mass was determined using RASM derived from whole-body DXA (<7 kg/m2 for men and <5.4 kg/m2 for women). Low muscle strength was defined as handgrip strength <28 kg for men and <18 kg for women. Low physical performance was defined as usual gait speed <1.0 m/s measured using the 6-m walk test. Participants were classified as having sarcopenia if they had low muscle mass in combination with either low muscle strength or low physical performance.

2.4. Neuropsychological assessment

Executive function was assessed using the computerized Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST), a widely used measure in neurocognitive and neurobiological research. Based on the WCST manual, the following indices were derived: (1) percentage of errors (total errors divided by total trials), (2) percentage of perseverative responses, (3) percentage of perseverative errors, (4) conceptual level responses (proportion of correct responses occurring in runs of ≥3 consecutive correct trials, reflecting acquisition of the sorting principle), and (5) number of categories completed.

Attention and working memory were assessed using the computerized Test of Attentional Performance (TAP). Working memory was evaluated with the TAP n-back paradigm (2-back condition). This task assesses the ability to temporarily maintain and continuously update information in short-term memory. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly as possible when a number repeated with one intervening number (eg, 28-40-28), indicating recognition of the stimulus presented two positions earlier. Sustained attention and inhibitory control were assessed using the Go/No-Go task. Participants were instructed to press a key as quickly as possible when the “×” symbol appeared (Go trials) and to withhold responses when the “+” symbol appeared (No-Go trials). After a practice phase, the formal task was administered, and number of correct responses, commission errors, and omission errors were recorded. Commission errors were interpreted as reflecting disinhibition and impulsivity, whereas omission errors were considered markers of impaired sustained attention.

2.5. Laboratory measurements

To reduce the likelihood that elevated inflammatory markers reflected acute infection rather than chronic low-grade inflammation, CRP values were examined for extreme elevations. A CRP level >10 mg/L was prespecified as an exclusion threshold. No participants demonstrated CRP levels above this threshold. IL-6 plays a complex role in central nervous system immune signaling, with evidence supporting both neuroprotective and neurotoxic effects depending on the context.22 IL-6 signaling occurs through classical signaling, mediated by membrane-bound IL-6 receptor binding, and trans-signaling, mediated by soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R).22 Binding of IL-6 to its receptor promotes dimerization of the signal-transducing gp130 subunit and activates downstream pathways. While classical IL-6 signaling may exert mixed pro- and anti-inflammatory effects, IL-6 trans-signaling is generally considered to drive proinflammatory responses. Accordingly, circulating sIL-6R may provide additional information regarding inflammatory activity beyond IL-6 levels alone.12 To further capture immune activation relevant to T-cell signaling, serum sIL-2R was measured as a marker of immune cell activation and inflammatory burden. sIL-2R reflects activation of the IL-2 pathway and has been used as an indicator of sustained immune activation in inflammatory and immune-mediated conditions.23 TNF-α signaling is mediated primarily through TNF receptor 1 (TNF-αR1) and TNF receptor 2 (TNF-αR2). TNF-αR1 signaling has been linked to neurotoxic and apoptotic pathways, whereas TNF-αR2 is thought to play a more prominent role in modulating and limiting TNF-driven inflammatory responses.24 Soluble TNF receptors may prolong the half-life of TNF-α, and circulating sTNFα-R1 levels have been proposed as a more stable marker of TNF-related proinflammatory activity than TNF-α itself.25 Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) were used to quantify circulating inflammatory markers, including CRP, sIL-6R, sIL-2R, and sTNFα- R1. All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols, and detailed assay characteristics (eg, detection limits, sensitivity, and cross-reactivity) are provided in the Supplementary Methods, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A392. Intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation for all analytes were below 10%, consistent with acceptable assay reproducibility for ELISA-based measurements.

2.6. Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as number (percentage). The normality of inflammatory marker distributions was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and levels of CRP, sIL-2R, and sTNFα-R1 were positively skewed. Therefore, serum levels of proinflammatory markers were log-transformed to obtain normally distributed variables. Inflammatory markers were analyzed both as continuous variables and, for CRP, as a binary variable to provide complementary perspectives on inflammatory burden. The categorical definition facilitates interpretable group comparisons, whereas continuous analyses minimize information loss associated with dichotomization. Between-group comparisons were conducted to examine clinical, physical, and cognitive differences across (1) participants with and without sarcopenia and (2) participants with and without systemic inflammation, defined as CRP >3 mg/L.26 Although originally derived from cardiovascular research, the CRP >3 mg/L threshold has been widely adopted in schizophrenia studies to define low-grade systemic inflammation and identify an inflammatory subgroup associated with worse clinical outcomes.27 Student’s t test was used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables. Bivariate correlation analyses examined associations between inflammatory markers, body composition measures, physical performance (handgrip strength and walking speed), and psychopathology indices (PANSS total, positive, negative, and general scores), and WCST performance. Pearson correlation coefficients were reported. Only variables showing significant associations in bivariate analyses were entered into multivariable GLMs to evaluate independent associations after adjustment for potential confounders, including age, sex, years of education, BMI, sarcopenia status, and psychotropic medication use. In the GLMs, inflammatory markers were analyzed as continuous log-transformed variables. To assess whether sarcopenia moderated associations between inflammatory burden and clinical or cognitive outcomes, multiplicative interaction terms (sarcopenia × inflammatory marker) were added to each GLM, and the interaction p value was reported. Given the multiple comparisons involved in analyses of PANSS subscales and WCST, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

3. RESULTS

A total of 120 inpatients with schizophrenia were included. Sarcopenia, defined according to AWGS 2019 criteria, was identified in 21 patients (17.5%), and 41 patients (34.2%) met criteria for systemic inflammation (CRP >3 mg/L) (Table 1). Compared with participants without sarcopenia, those with sarcopenia were older and had a lower BMI. The sarcopenia group showed lower appendicular skeletal muscle mass and lower RASM index. Physical performance was also poorer among participants with sarcopenia, with slower walking speed and lower handgrip strength. Regarding psychopathology, participants with sarcopenia had higher PANSS total scores, higher positive symptom scores, and higher general psychopathology scores. No significant group differences were observed across the WCST, 2-back or Go/No-Go task.

In bivariate correlation analyses, CRP was negatively associated with walking speed (r = −0.20; p < 0.05) and positively associated with PANSS total score (r = 0.21; p < 0.05) and PANSS positive symptom score (r = 0.27; p < 0.01; Supplementary Table 1, https://links.lww.com/JCMA/A392). No significant associations were observed between inflammatory markers and PANSS negative or general symptoms, Go/No-Go performance, or working memory measures in bivariate analyses.

In multivariable general linear models adjusting for age, sex, education, BMI, sarcopenia status, and psychotropic medication use, higher CRP remained significantly associated with higher PANSS total score (B = 5.51; p = 0.021; Fig. 1) and higher PANSS positive symptom score (B = 1.94; p = 0.009; Table 3). After correction for multiple comparisons, only the association between CRP and PANSS positive symptom severity remained statistically significant. Sarcopenia status was also associated with PANSS total and positive scores across models (all p < 0.05). Higher sIL-2R was associated with worse performance on WCST failure to maintain set in uncorrected analyses (B = −1.70; p = 0.009); however, this association was not significant after multiple-comparison correction. No significant sarcopenia-inflammation interaction effects were observed for PANSS total score, PANSS positive symptom score, or WCST failure to maintain set (all interaction p > 0.05), indicating that sarcopenia did not significantly moderate associations between inflammatory burden and clinical or executive outcomes in this cohort.

4. DISCUSSION

In this cross-sectional study of hospitalized patients with schizophrenia, both systemic inflammation and sarcopenia were associated with greater clinical severity and poorer physical function but demonstrated distinct cognitive correlates. Patients with systemic inflammation had higher PANSS total and subscale scores and slower walking speed. They also showed poorer performance on selected WCST indices, whereas no differences were detected in working memory (2-back) or response inhibition (Go/No-Go). Sarcopenia was characterized by physical vulnerability and was similarly associated with higher PANSS scores; however, it was not associated with performance on the WCST, 2-back, or Go/No-Go tasks. Importantly, we found no evidence of multiplicative effect modification by sarcopenia on inflammation–psychopathology or inflammation–executive function associations. Our findings support independent associations of inflammation and sarcopenia with psychopathology severity, whereas evidence linking inflammation to executive dysfunction was attenuated after multiple-comparison correction.

The association between CRP and symptom severity observed in this cohort is consistent with prior evidence indicating that systemic inflammation is clinically relevant in schizophrenia. A systematic review by Orsolini et al28 reported that elevated CRP in schizophrenia is frequently associated with greater illness severity and may be most prominent during relapse phases, supporting the view that CRP captures clinically meaningful variation in disease activity across states. In addition, early work by Fan et al29 demonstrated that elevated CRP may be associated with more severe psychopathology in a subgroup of patients with schizophrenia, providing early empirical support for an immune-related phenotype within schizophrenia. Our finding that higher CRP was associated with higher PANSS positive symptom scores is also concordant with evidence linking CRP to positive psychotic symptom burden, including formal thought and language dysfunction.30 However, prior studies have reported variability regarding whether CRP is more strongly associated with positive, negative, or general psychopathology, likely reflecting heterogeneity in clinical stage (acute exacerbation vs stable/chronic illness), metabolic status, smoking behavior, and antipsychotic exposure, as well as differences in analytic approaches and confounder adjustment.31 CRP is an acute-phase reactant primarily driven by IL-6 signaling and is widely considered a marker of systemic metabolic inflammation integrating signals from peripheral immune activation. Evidence suggests that CRP may reflect global inflammatory burden linked to neurobiological processes relevant to psychosis, including alterations in dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling.32,33 Taken together, our data add to the evidence that inflammatory markers, especially CRP, may index a subgroup characterized by more severe psychopathology.

In the present cohort, inflammatory status showed signals of association with selected executive indices rather than with generalized cognitive impairment. Patients with systemic inflammation demonstrated increased WCST perseverative responses and perseverative errors, indicating greater cognitive inflexibility and difficulty shifting response strategies, whereas working memory performance on the 2-back task and response inhibition on the Go/No-Go task did not differ by inflammatory status. This pattern may be consistent with inflammation-related cognitive vulnerability preferentially affecting higher-order cognitive flexibility (set shifting) rather than working memory updating or motor response inhibition.34 Perseveration and impaired set shifting are commonly linked to dysfunction within frontostriatal circuitry supporting cognitive control, including prefrontal-striatal loops involved in rule selection and adaptive switching.35–37 Mechanistically, these observations are compatible with neuroimmune models in which inflammatory cytokines have been proposed to influence glutamatergic and dopaminergic signaling and to alter basal ganglia-prefrontal circuitry implicated in reinforcement learning and cognitive control.38–40 In multivariable analyses, higher sIL-2R was associated with worse WCST failure to maintain set in uncorrected analyses; however, this association did not remain significant after multiple-comparison correction. Accordingly, any link between inflammatory burden and executive dysfunction in this cohort should be interpreted as preliminary. sIL-2R, released from activated T lymphocytes, reflects sustained cellular immune activation.41 Elevated sIL-2R levels have been reported in schizophrenia,6 suggesting persistent T-cell-mediated immune dysregulation. T-cell-derived cytokines may influence central nervous system function through neuroimmune pathways, including modulation of microglial activity and downstream dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling relevant to cognitive control.42,43 Together, these findings raise the possibility that different inflammatory markers may relate to specific executive processes, although this requires confirmation in larger longitudinal cohorts with more comprehensive cognitive batteries.

Sarcopenia in this inpatient cohort identified a subgroup with marked impairment in physical performance and higher symptom burden, including greater PANSS severity. This pattern is consistent with evidence that sarcopenia is not uncommon in schizophrenia and may be associated with reduced functional capacity and adverse clinical profiles.15,44,45 However, sarcopenia was not associated with neuropsychological performance on the WCST, 2-back, or Go/No-Go tasks in the present cohort. This null finding contrasts with reports linking reduced physical performance to cognitive deficits in schizophrenia. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, in hospitalized settings, sarcopenia may primarily reflect physical deconditioning and reduced mobility related to long-term low activity levels and illness burden, rather than a direct marker of cognitive performance.46 Second, the relatively small sarcopenia subgroup may have limited power to detect modest cognitive differences. Third, cognitive outcomes in schizophrenia are influenced by multiple factors, including illness chronicity and motivational engagement, which may attenuate detectability of sarcopenia-related cognitive effects.47 These results suggest that sarcopenia may provide incremental clinical information regarding physical vulnerability and symptom severity, even when cognitive differences are not observed.

The prevalence of sarcopenia in our cohort (17.5%) was lower than that reported in previous studies of schizophrenia, where estimates have ranged from approximately 25% to over 50%,15,48 depending on sample characteristics. Our sample included relatively younger participants (mean age under 50 years), whereas some prior studies focused on older populations (aged 50 years or older), in whom sarcopenia is more prevalent.48 Moreover, individuals with major systemic illnesses were excluded from this study, resulting in a physically healthier cohort and potentially reducing the observed prevalence. In addition, the relatively small number of participants with sarcopenia may have limited statistical power to detect modest cognitive differences and interaction effects. Although no significant inflammation-sarcopenia interaction was observed, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of absence. Effect size estimates for group differences in psychopathology were in the moderate range (eg, Cohen d = 0.67-0.85 for PANSS outcomes), suggesting clinically meaningful differences despite the limited subgroup size. In contrast, comparisons of cognitive outcomes between patients with and without inflammation showed small-to-medium effect sizes, indicating that any potential inflammation-related cognitive effects, if present, are likely modest and would require larger samples to detect.

A key contribution of this study is the concurrent assessment of inflammation and sarcopenia, along with the direct evaluation of effect modification by sarcopenia. We did not detect significant inflammation-sarcopenia interactions for PANSS outcomes or WCST indices, indicating no evidence that sarcopenia amplifies inflammation-related symptoms or executive associations on a multiplicative scale. This result argues against a synergistic vulnerability model, in which inflammation becomes clinically relevant only after sarcopenia develops. Instead, our findings are more consistent with a framework in which immune activation and physical frailty represent parallel or partially independent pathways relating to symptom severity.49,50 Clinically, this distinction may be relevant for risk stratification: inflammatory markers may remain informative even among patients without sarcopenia, whereas sarcopenia may capture physical vulnerability that is not fully explained by circulating inflammatory measures.51,52 Nonetheless, given the limited number of sarcopenic participants in this study, modest interaction effects cannot be excluded and should be reexamined in larger samples.

The cross-sectional nature of the study raises the possibility of reverse causality. More severe psychopathology may lead to reduced physical activity or weight changes, which, in turn, could contribute to elevated inflammatory markers rather than inflammation directly influencing symptom severity or cognitive performance. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of a directional or causal relationship. Furthermore, participants were recruited from chronically hospitalized wards, where environmental factors such as structured daily routines, institutional diet, limited sunlight exposure, and reduced mobility may jointly influence inflammatory status, physical performance, and clinical severity. Such shared environmental influences may introduce setting-related confounding, potentially strengthening correlations between inflammation, physical vulnerability, and psychopathology. Simultaneously, studying a chronically institutionalized population may reduce variability in lifestyle-related factors and enable comprehensive biological and physical assessments, including standardized DXA and laboratory measurements. Accordingly, the present findings may be most representative of an institutionalized schizophrenia phenotype.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and limits conclusions regarding the temporal ordering of inflammation, sarcopenia, symptom severity, and executive dysfunction. Second, the absence of a healthy control group limits the ability to determine whether the observed levels of inflammation, sarcopenia, and cognitive performance differ from those in the general population. Third, participants were recruited from chronically hospitalized psychiatric wards, likely representing individuals with more severe illness, greater functional impairment, and reduced physical activity. These factors may influence inflammatory status, physical performance, and cognitive function. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to early-stage schizophrenia, outpatient populations, or community-dwelling patients. Fourth, although we adjusted for key covariates, residual confounding is likely, particularly in hospitalized settings where physical inactivity, limited sunlight exposure, and institutional diet may co-occur and influence inflammatory markers, physical performance, and symptom severity. Smoking status and detailed cardiometabolic factors were not assessed, despite their well-established associations with systemic inflammation. In addition, medication exposure was captured only as current use and did not account for cumulative antipsychotic dose, chlorpromazine-equivalent exposure, or anticholinergic burden. These factors may influence inflammatory markers, extrapyramidal symptoms, and physical performance. Therefore, the observed associations, particularly those involving walking speed, should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, cognitive assessment was limited to selected domains, including executive function, working memory, and inhibitory control. Other key domains of schizophrenia-related cognitive impairment, such as verbal memory, processing speed, attention, and social cognition, were not assessed. Therefore, the findings should not be interpreted as reflecting global cognitive impairment. Finally, the sarcopenia subgroup size may have reduced statistical power for interaction testing; thus, the absence of detected interaction effects should be interpreted cautiously. Future longitudinal studies should evaluate whether inflammatory and sarcopenia phenotypes predict relapse trajectories, functional outcomes, and responses to interventions targeting immune dysregulation, physical conditioning, or both.

In this cross-sectional inpatient cohort, systemic inflammation and sarcopenia were associated with greater psychopathology severity. The absence of an inflammation-sarcopenia interaction suggests that sarcopenia did not substantially moderate the observed associations between inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes in this cohort. These findings highlight the potential clinical relevance of integrating inflammatory and physical vulnerability assessments in schizophrenia, while underscoring the need for longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes to establish temporality and evaluate whether these phenotypes inform prognosis or treatment stratification.
