A General Class of Model-Free Dense Precision Matrix Estimators
Mehmet Caner, Agostino Capponi, Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract
We introduce prototype consistent model-free, dense precision matrix estimators that have broad application in economics. Using quadratic form concentration inequalities and novel algebraic characterizations of confounding dimension reductions, we are able to: (i) obtain non-asymptotic bounds for precision matrix estimation errors and also (ii) consistency in high dimensions; (iii) uncover the existence of an intrinsic signal-to-noise—underlying dimensions tradeoff; and (iv) avoid exact population sparsity assumptions. In addition to its desirable theoretical properties, a thorough empirical study of the S&P 500 index shows that a tuning parameter-free special case of our general estimator exhibits a doubly ascending Sharpe Ratio pattern, thereby establishing a link with the famous double descent phenomenon dominantly present in recent statistical and machine learning literature.



Keywords:
Precision Matrix Estimation; Double Descent; Latent Space; Hidden Factors.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we propose a general class of new estimators for precision matrices in settings with a large—and potentially diverging—number of observed variables that is dense and model-free. Hence, we neither use penalized estimation nor estimate an imposed model to generate a precision matrix estimator.

Our main contributions are as follows. We introduce a general prototype class of
consistent-non-sparse and unpenalized
precision matrix estimators based on a deep connection between matrix algebra and hidden factor analysis. Hidden factor analysis is used only for achieving consistency in high dimensions via dimension reduction. Our estimators are model-free, and do not need to estimate hidden factors.
Moreover, we derive non-asymptotic upper bounds for estimation errors that allow us to see the effects of a new complexity random variable, number of confounding factors, and signal-to-noise ratio.
Our precision matrix estimator has three broad areas of interest. First, it can be used to construct debiased estimators for parameters in high-dimensional regression models. Debiased estimators are essential for constructing valid confidence intervals in high-dimensional settings (see
Caner and
Kock 2018
,
van de Geer et al. 2014
). A key challenge in this context is obtaining an estimate of the inverse of the Gram matrix, as the sample second moment matrix is typically singular when the number of variables exceeds the sample size. Our estimator provides a viable alternative for estimating this inverse, and can be readily adapted for use in such models. Second, in network analysis, under normally distributed random outcome variables, conditional dependence between two random variables is represented as a nonzero term in the precision matrix of all outcome variables. Our method avoids imposing “artificial” conditional independence assumptions—i.e., forcing zero entries in the precision matrix that correspond to the absence of links between variables. Instead, it preserves all connections in the network, allowing some to be minor and others to be dominant. A direct application of such setting is the study of tax interactions between US states in the study of
De Paula
et al. 2024
. They impose sparsity of their social interaction matrix i.e., zero interactions between states (see equations (8) and (10) of their paper). Third, in financial econometrics, our estimator can be used where precision matrices of asset returns play a central role in tasks such as optimal portfolio allocation, portfolio variance estimation, and Sharpe Ratio calculation.
A detailed literature review and discussion of these three broad applications is provided in Section
4.4
.

1.1
Brief Overview of Estimators

We start by providing a formula for population precision matrix that can tie each variable to the other variables in a system.
Then, to bypass the high-dimensionality of the problem, our conceptual innovation is to express each variable as a linear function of
common but hidden
factors, and solve for the corresponding precision matrix estimator. In this sense, hidden factors act as confounders. We make no identifying assumptions about hidden factors, nor estimate them.

As special subcases of our general estimator, we obtain a new adaptive principal components (adaptive PCR from now on) estimator and the ridgeless regression estimator (RRE from now on). We prove consistency of both estimators when the number of outcomes exceeds the sample size.
Empirically, we find that the Sharpe Ratio of portfolios constructed using the ridge-less regression estimator exhibits a
double ascent
pattern in the S&P 500. This is significant, as it demonstrates that profitable portfolios can be formed without the need to estimate any tuning parameters.

We compare two subcase estimators: the ridge-less regression estimator (RRE) and adaptive principal component regression (PCR). In finite samples, the RRE has one fewer bias term than adaptive PCR, giving it a potential advantage. However, asymptotically the ranking may reverse, since the bias in adaptive PCR depends on the number of factors, while the bias in RRE depends on sample size. For instance, because the number of factors is typically smaller than the sample size, adaptive PCR may outperform RRE in estimating individual rows of the precision matrix.

We support these insights through simulations and empirical analysis. Specifically, we find that our ridge-less precision matrix estimator achieves a Sharpe Ratio of 0.2203 for a large portfolio of 357 S&P 500 stocks during the out-of-sample period from January 2015 to December 2023, after accounting for transaction costs. The closest competitor is the nodewise regression-based approach of
Callot
et al. 2021
, which attains a Sharpe Ratio of 0.2082.

1.2
Dimension Reduction

Why do we use a hidden factor setup? First of all, we show that precision matrix estimation is related to linear regression and that can be related to hidden factors (see Online Appendix B). Each economic or financial variable can be related to latent factors and this can be thought as confounding variables as in causality related literature such as in
Kaltenpoth and
Vreeke 2023
,
Angrist and
Pischke 2009
,
Imbens and

Also the true variables of interest may be measured with errors, and latent factors can represent the ”true” variables rather than the reported ones, especially in emerging markets. Variable such as culture, sentiment, market can be extremely valuable in certain macro, financial data sets. These hidden factors in a linear model can be used to explain international stock returns such as in Singapore, Malaysia, USA as seen in
Brooks and
Del Negro 2004
.

Another key aspect of using hidden factors is their effectiveness as a tool for dimension reduction in high-dimensional econometrics. This stems from the fact that the number of observed outcomes typically exceeds the number of underlying hidden factors. In our paper, we allow
both
the number of outcomes and the number of hidden factors to grow with the sample size. The role and significance of latent factors and the estimation of their key components, factors and loadings, have been largely extended and further linked to asset pricing in

;

Excellent theoretical and practical algorithmic results obtained in all these works usually serve as a strong indication that there is indeed a very strong rationale for utilization of latent factors. Somewhat paradoxically, while we will rely on similar factor modeling concepts and underlying intuitions that they bring along, we will be able to design new and novel precision matrix estimators without actually focusing on directly estimating factors themselves.

1.3
A Brief Discussion of Sparsity in Economics

Our proposed class of estimators is broadly applicable across a range of settings, including those where the underlying data do not exhibit sparsity. For instance,
Giannone
et al. 2021
analyze six widely used datasets in macroeconomics, microeconomics, and finance, and find that dense models consistently outperform sparse ones in terms of out-of-sample predictive accuracy. These applications include: (i) forecasting U.S. economic activity, (ii) identifying determinants of economic growth across countries, (iii) explaining the decline in crime rates across U.S. states, (iv) analyzing judicial rulings on eminent domain across U.S. circuits, (v) predicting the U.S. equity premium, and (vi) accounting for the cross-sectional variation in U.S. stock returns.

In asset pricing, the assumption of sparsity for explaining or predicting economic variables is particularly strong and has been critically examined (see p.33 and Section 3.7 of
Nagel 2021
). In that study, sparsity is imposed between the outcome variable and the predictors, effectively forcing some coefficients to be zero—even if those predictors have a non-negligible but small relationship with the outcome. This can be problematic in settings where many variables contribute modestly to predictive performance. Techniques like Lasso may shrink all small coefficients to zero, resulting in an artificially sparse model and a significant loss of predictive power.

1.4
Literature Survey

Several studies in the statistics literature have studied precision matrix estimation. An excellent survey is by
Cai
et al. 2016
. In Section 3 of their paper, they provide
two major approaches to precision matrix estimation. The first approach is neighborhood-based and closely related to nodewise regression, where a lasso regression is performed for each variable against all others. Alternatives to lasso, such as the Dantzig selector, can also be used within this framework (see
Cai and Liu 2011
,
Callot
et al. 2021
). The second major approach is based on penalized likelihood estimation. As shown by
Bertsimas
et al. 2020
, lasso-type penalties in this context can induce shrinkage bias and lead to overly sparse precision matrix estimates, with many zero entries in each row. In a penalized likelihood based setting, they reformulate the problem as a mixed-integer optimization problem, which is solvable using convex optimization techniques. This approach produces better results than lasso based approaches. Sparsity of precision matrix is used in these two main approaches.

A distinct approach, commonly used in finance and statistics, derives the precision matrix by inverting the covariance matrix using the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury (SMW) formula. In this framework, sparsity is assumed in the covariance matrix of the idiosyncratic errors, while asset returns are modeled through a factor structure (see
Fan
et al. 2013
,
Caner
et al. 2023
).

Our approach is different from the
three approaches discussed above, and provides a new way of solving the same problem. Compared to the first
two, we are not assuming exact sparsity in the precision matrix of outcomes. Compared to the last one, we are not using SMW formula to
invert the covariance matrix. This allows us to bypass imposing a structure
on the outcomes utilized in the covariance inversion. Instead we use a new
precision matrix formula without sparsity and rely on hidden factors to generate each
outcome. This effectively ensures that we do not benefit from the SMW matrix inversion formula. To be clear, we use model-free dense estimation, but consistency in high dimensions is achieved through hidden factor based dimension reduction without a need to estimate factors or identify them.

Note that nodewise regression based estimator of
Callot
et al. 2021

uses sparsity of precision matrix of outcomes. Then residual nodewise regression estimator of
Caner
et al. 2023
crucially depends on observed factors, and imposes sparse precision matrix of errors. Technique used in those papers cannot extend to hidden factors since observed factor structure in
Caner
et al. 2023
uses OLS estimation which is not applicable to more complicated estimation problems such as hidden factor structure. Note that
Callot
et al. 2021
has no factor structure and uses lasso based regression to estimate the precision matrix of outcomes.

We also tie our results to signal-to-noise ratio aspect which is not available in
Callot
et al. 2021
,
Caner
et al. 2023
papers. We show that to get consistency of our estimators, signal-to-noise ratio should be larger than number of factors. Our method can be seen as a complementary approach to nodewise regression of
Meinshausen and
Bühlmann 2006
. Nodewise regression uses an
l_{1}
norm based penalization to form the rows of the precision matrix. Doing so, the underlying assumption is the imposition of sparsity in the precision matrix which may be interpreted as a strong assumption.

An excellent paper by
Fan
et al. 2013
provides
a statistical high-dimensional analysis of covariance matrix of outcomes via hidden factors based estimation.
Similar to us, it also relies on hidden factors but uses a thresholding based principal components estimator for the covariance matrix, and then inverts this through SMW formula. The key difference is that our estimator is model-free and uses hidden factors for consistency proof only.
There are also other technical and conceptual differences that are worth highlighting.
First, our proposed class of
estimators is general and in addition to the PCR based ones it also includes the ridge-less regression estimator (RRE). Second, our signal assumption is much
weaker as we do not need the minimum signal to grow
at the same rate as the number of assets in the portfolio. Also, we allow for diverging
noise assumption (this is a notable contrast to

Fan
et al. 2013
where the noise is tied to the maximum eigenvalue of the covariance matrix of errors with assumed constant dimension
p
), which is reflective of financial markets. Moreover, as the root of our approach resembles a finite sample analysis, it also allows to explicitly tie all the estimation errors to signal-to-noise
ratio thereby making its importance in the rate of convergence estimates clearly visible. In particular, we uncover that the signal-to-noise ratio that dominates the number of
factors ensures consistency
of our precision matrix estimator.

Our paper differs from
Bing
et al. 2021
and
Bunea
et al. 2022
in a fundamental way. These papers analyze general linear class of estimators in hidden factor models in linear regression model and is only interested in risk of the estimators. Their papers consider only one outcome variable and how it can be predicted with this new general class of estimators. Theorem 3 of
Bing
et al. 2021
specifically provides a risk upper bound and this bound depends on the magnitude of coefficients in a linear system.
Our analysis on the other hand, considers the precision matrix estimation of outcome variable in linear regression via hidden factors (i.e. an application is large portfolio formation). We show how matrix algebra can be combined with latent factors to provide a new precision matrix formula in order to achieve consistency.
Then a novel idea of estimation is to use a linear linkage between outcome variables to obtain precision matrix estimator based on the matrix algebraic new formula. One of the main difficulties, theoretically, is that the estimation of the main diagonal terms in the precision matrix estimation. The proof technique and ideas in
Bing
et al. 2021
,
Bunea
et al. 2022
do not simply extend to this case. Hence, the precision matrix estimation is different than the risk analysis of a single outcome-asset. We also provide an explicit connection in our estimation error upper bounds to signal-to-noise ratio. The results in subcases are different also in a risk analysis versus precision matrix estimation.
Bing
et al. 2021
,
Bunea
et al. 2022
show that in certain very high dimensional cases, ridge-less regression (RRE) can perform better than adaptive PCR in terms of risk asymptotically. However, in case of estimation errors of rows of precision matrix errors (in
l_{2}
norm) we show adaptive PCR can perform better than RRE even in very high dimensional cases. These are due to different proofs for risk versus precision matrix estimation.

4.4
Uses of Precision Matrix Estimate

Here in this section we discuss the usage of our precision matrix estimator in three different cases of interest in economics and finance. In addition to the examples that we will discuss below, we should also add that many of our results are of independent statistical interest as well. We single out two particular features of our statistical analyses, (i) lack of exact sparsity assumption between outcomes with model-free dense estimators and (ii) an explicit connection of the estimation error to signal-to-noise ratio, as results that significantly advance the current state of the art in the relevant statistics precision matrix estimation related literature.

In financial econometrics and finance, one of the key issues is large portfolio formation. Define the number of assets
of a large portfolio as
p
, and the sample size as
n
.
Two precision matrix estimating approaches distinguished themselves in recent literature: (i) the first one that relies on the estimation and inversion of the covariance matrix (see, e.g.,
Fan
et al. 2013
and
Ledoit and
Wolf 2017
where covariance matrix is obtained via hidden factor model and shrinkage estimation, respectively);
and (ii) the second one that directly estimates the precision matrix and relies on the so-called nodewise regression approach (see, e.g.,
Callot
et al. 2021
and
Caner
et al. 2023
for nodewise regression with observed factors). We introduce a new estimator that can be used to consistently estimate the Sharpe Ratio even when
p>n
(Sharpe Ratio is one of the key practical financial metrics as it can measure return-risk ratio for a given portfolio).

The second possibility is
a simple modification of our method can be used to estimate inverted Gram matrix thereby replacing the so-called relaxed inverse typically used to get a high dimensional linear regression parameters
confidence intervals (see, e.g.,
van de Geer et al. 2014
). Our idea can also be used to form and change the debiased high dimensional estimators used in
van de Geer et al. 2014
and
Caner and
Kock 2018
. Below, we provide some details on how this can be achieved.

The third possibility for the utilization of our methodology is the network analysis. One first recalls that nodewise regression can be
tied to network analysis (see, e.g.,
Meinshausen and
Bühlmann 2006
. Given the connection between our estimator and nodewise regression, one then observes that our estimator fits
the same network structure but with an advantage of not needing to impose sparsity on the links between two nodes.

We have three examples of our estimators, and the first one is Sharpe-Ratio analysis, and the second one is the de-biased high dimensional estimator formation for the confidence intervals for structural parameters in cross section analysis, and the third one is network analysis. In this part, first, we provide a Sharpe Ratio metric that use precision matrix of asset returns. The metric is the Sharpe ratio (squared) from the maximum Sharpe Ratio portfolio.
There are other metrics as discussed heavily in
Caner
et al. 2023
for example, but the emphasis of our paper is introduction of a new class of estimators for the precision matrix not the detailed analysis of financial metrics. We also think that the analysis of financial metrics can be done in another paper with this approach, since the proofs will be non-standard and will take a larger space with very detailed analysis through several matrix norm inequalities. The metric below is chosen since the contribution of the precision matrix is very clear to the financial metric. To obtain the (squared) Sharpe ratio from the maximum Sharpe
Ratio portfolio we maximize

w_{MS}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=argmax_{w\in R^{p}}w^{\prime}\mu\quad{\mbox{s}ubject}\quad{\mbox{t}o}\quad w^{\prime}\Sigma w\leq\sigma^{2},

where
\sigma^{2}
is the variance of the portfolio. The optimal solution is

w_{MS}^{*}=\frac{\sigma\Sigma^{-1}\mu}{\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu}}.

Substituting this into Sharpe Ratio formula we have

SR_{MS}=\frac{w_{MS}^{*^{\prime}}\mu}{w_{MS}^{*^{\prime}}\Sigma w_{MS}^{*}}=\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mu}.

The estimator is:

\widehat{SR}_{MS}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\hat{\mu}^{\prime}\tilde{\Theta}^{\prime}\hat{\mu},

with
\hat{\mu}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}Y_{t}
.

This can be found in
Caner
et al. 2023
as well as several other papers in the literature, and used in practice.
The prevalent approaches in present day literature impose sparsity either on the covariance or on the precision matrix of errors, and
prove consistency results relying on those assumptions. Without exact sparsity, the proofs will not
be valid for
p>n
case. On the other hand, our methodology allows to circumvent the sparsity assumption. The second issue is that rates of convergence
are usually only tied to the number of assets, p, sample size, n, and the number of factors, K. Our methodology allows to additionally tie them to signal-to-noise ratio as well.

5
Empirical Application

The objective of the empirical application is to conduct an out-of-sample comparison among estimations. We estimate Maximum Sharpe Ratio (MSR) portfolio using estimators, and use four commonly used financial metrics: the Sharpe Ratio (SR), average return, portfolio turnover, and portfolio variance. We use data from January 1995 to December 2023, with an out-of-sample period from January 2015 to December 2023. We selected all stocks included in the S
\&
P 500 for at least one month during the out-of-sample period, ensuring the data were available for the entire 1995-2023 period, resulting in 357 stocks. The out-of-sample period includes stock market of 2022 when S
\&
P 500 index was down by 18.1 %.

5.1
Metrics

We use a rolling window set up for the out-of-sample estimation of the Sharpe Ratio following
Callot
et al. 2021
and
Caner
et al. 2023
. The sample size
n
is divided into in-sample
(1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n_{I})
and out-of-sample
(n_{I}+1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n)
. Initially, we estimate the portfolio
\hat{w}_{n_{I}}
in the in-sample period and the out-of-sample portfolio returns
\hat{w}^{\prime}_{n_{I}}y_{n_{I}+1}
. Then, we roll the window forward by one element
(2\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}n_{I}+1)
, forming a new in-sample portfolio
\hat{w}_{n_{I}+1}
and caculating out-of-sample portfolio returns
\hat{w}_{n_{I+1}}^{\prime}y_{n_{I}+2}
. This process is repeated until the end of the sample.

The out-of-sample average return and variance without transaction costs are computed as follows:

\hat{\mu}_{os}=\frac{1}{n-n_{I}}\sum_{t=n_{I}}^{n-1}\hat{w}_{t}^{\prime}y_{t+1},\hat{\Sigma}^{2}_{y,os}=\frac{1}{n-n_{I}-1}\sum_{t=n_{I}}^{n-1}(\hat{w}_{t}^{\prime}y_{t+1}-\hat{\mu}_{os})
.
We estimate the Sharpe Ratios without transaction costs:

SR=\frac{\hat{\mu}_{os}}{\hat{\Sigma}_{y,os}}

When transaction costs are present, the definition of the metrics is modified. Let
c
be the transaction cost, which we set 10 basis points as. Let
y_{P,t+1}=\hat{w}_{t}^{\prime}y_{t+1}
be the return of the portfolio
P
in period
t+1
; in the presence of transaction costs, the return is defined as

y^{Net}_{P,t+1}=y_{P,t+1}-c(1+y_{P,t+1})\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\hat{w}_{j,t+1}-\hat{w}^{+}_{j,t}|
where
\displaystyle\hat{w}^{+}_{j,t}=\frac{\hat{w}_{j,t}(1+y_{j,t+1})}{1+y_{P,t+1}}
, with
y_{j,t+1}
representing the excess return added to risk-free-rate for
j
th, and
y_{P,t+1}
representing the portfolio
P
excess return added to the risk-free rate. The adjustment made in
\hat{w}^{+}_{j,t}
is because the portfolio at the end of the period has changed compared to the portfolio at the beginning of the period.
Then, we define the mean and variance of the portfolio returns with transaction costs as:
\hat{\mu}_{os-c}=\frac{1}{n-n_{I}}\sum_{t=n_{I}}^{n-1}y^{Net}_{P,t}\mbox{ and }\hat{\Sigma}^{2}_{y,os-c}=\frac{1}{n-n_{I}-1}\sum_{t=n_{I}}^{n-1}(y^{Net}_{P,t}-\hat{\mu}_{os-c})^{2}
. The portfolio returns are replaced by the returns with transaction costs when we calculated the Sharpe Ratio with transaction costs. Finally, portfolio turnover is defined as:

\text{turnover}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\hat{w}_{j,t+1}-\hat{w}_{j,t}^{+}|.

5.2
Methods

We conduct a comprehensive empirical analysis by fixing in-sample
n_{I}=240
and considering both low-dimensional and high-dimensional cases across various portfolio sizes. Specifically, we selected 10, and 200 stocks from the pool to estimate the models with the same stocks for all windows in the low-dimensional experiments. For the high-dimensional experiment, we choose 357 (including all available stocks). We also analyzed portfolios with 25, 50, and 300 stocks, however, since these results are similar, they are not included here. We have 100 for low dimensional experiment and 325 stock high dimensional experiment in Online Appendix.

The model comparisons include several approaches from the literature. We consider the nonlinear shrinkage methods of
Ledoit and
Wolf 2017
(NL-LW) and the single-factor nonlinear shrinkage (SF-NL-LW), POET method from
Fan
et al. 2013
, and the nodewise (NW) method from
Callot
et al. 2021
, along with the feasible residual-based nodewise approach from
Caner
et al. 2023
. SF-NL-LW results are very similar to NL-LW in terms of standard deviation, so we have not included that in our Table. But these are available from authors on demand.
In particular, RNW-SF, RNW-3F, and RNW-5F refer to residual-based nodewise regressions using a single-factor model (market as the only factor), three-factor model, and five-factor Fama and French model, respectively.
Our new method introduces several variations: RRE, PCR-1F, PCR-3F, PCR-5F, PCR-7F and PCR-Adaptive. These denote the use of a RRE estimator, Principal component with fixed 1, 3, 5, 7 hidden factors, and an adaptive method to select the number of hidden factors, respectively. We use these number of factors since they are used in the empirics in the literature and seven factor is used to show large number of factors.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced a general precision matrix estimator. Our estimator is model-free and does not rely on any sparsity assumptions. Under certain conditions, our estimator exhibits double descent in the estimation error. A distinguishing feature of our analysis is that it is done for finite samples, and leads to non-asymptotic bounds for the error of precision matrix estimator. Our numerical results support our theoretical findings. We have shown that the proposed estimator compares favorably with popular existing machine learning estimator alternatives. Moreover, the Sharpe ratio produced by one of our estimators on the S&P 500 data exhibits the double ascent, which to the best of our knowledge has never obtained in other empirical studies.
