Hippocampal CA3 connectomics reveals a gradient of mossy fiber inputs and selective feedforward inhibition onto pyramidal cells
Authors: Zhihao Zheng, Changjoo Park, Eric W. Hammerschmith, Ran Lu, Szi-Chieh Yu, Marissa Sorek, Ben Silverman, Chris S. Jordan, Amy R. Sterling, William M. Silversmith, Philipp Schlegel, Gregory S. X. E. Jefferis, Forrest Collman, H. Sebastian Seung, David W. Tank
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mossy fiber (MF) connections to pyramidal cells in hippocampal CA3 are thought to participate in pattern separation, pattern completion and memory encoding, yet no large-scale neuronal wiring diagram exists for these connections. We assembled a 3D electron microscopy volume (~1 × 1 × 0.1 mm3) from mouse hippocampal CA3. By proofreading and automated segmentation, we reconstructed and classified all soma-containing neurons—including 1,815 pyramidal cells and 229 inhibitory cells—and over 55,000 MF axons. Pyramidal cells receive more numerous MF inputs along a proximodistal gradient. Some distal cells show surprisingly high convergence via relatively small terminals with fewer vesicles. Pyramidal cells share significantly more MF inputs than networks randomized by degree-preserving swap and are better approximated by networks randomized by proximity-preserving swap. We identify a feedforward inhibitory circuit motif from MFs via perisomatic interneurons that selectively target a pyramidal subtype. We demonstrated large-scale mapping across levels in the hippocampus—from circuits to cell types to vesicles. The dataset is shared through Pyr.ai, an online platform for hippocampal connectomics.

Main

The hippocampus has been implicated in episodic and spatial memory. Synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus is widely considered to be a cellular mechanism of learning and memory. To study the structural correlates of this plasticity, serial electron microscopy (EM) has been used to characterize hippocampal synapses in 3D1–7. These studies could be performed with relatively small EM volumes. However, larger volumes are required to study the connectivity of circuits in the context of the morphology and identity of the constituent neurons because synapses are distributed along axons and dendrites that span millimeters in scale. Although the sizes of 3D EM volumes for smaller model organisms and cortex have increased significantly8–11, the scale-up of synapse-resolution volumetric imaging and reconstruction for the hippocampus is still in its infancy.

Recent studies have attempted to increase the size of EM volumes in the rodent hippocampus. To study the distribution of putative entorhinal inputs, conventional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging of serial ultrathin sections was used to acquire a volume that spans two sublayers of CA1 (ref. 12) (350 × 200 × 17 μm3). The authors of previous work13 acquired a volume that encompasses all layers of CA3 with 1 mm2 sections (965 × 808 × 62 μm3). Analysis of pyramidal cell connections in that dataset revealed a high recurrent connectivity rate. In parallel, a larger EM volume of CA1 (1.1 × 0.92 × 0.143 mm3) was recently reported, along with analysis of synaptic weights across a large number of synapses14,15. These volumes are sparsely reconstructed, and the analysis is primarily focused on recurrent connectivity. Here we focus on a more complete reconstruction in the CA3 region of the mouse hippocampus, with an emphasis on MF and inhibitory inputs to pyramidal cells.

The hippocampus, classically known as the archicortex, is a multilayered structure consisting of a single layer of pyramidal somata embedded in multiple layers containing dendrites, axons and interneurons. This organization is preserved across the three major regions of the hippocampus: dentate gyrus, CA3 and CA1. Granule cells in the dentate gyrus give rise to MF axons, which provide a major excitatory input pathway to pyramidal cells of the CA3 region. The dentate gyrus is often modeled as a circuit with the computational role of pattern separation16,17, whereas CA3, owing to its extensive recurrent collaterals, is conjectured to support pattern completion18–20. However, morphological heterogeneity of single-layer CA3 pyramidal cells has been documented early on and is increasingly associated with physiological and functional heterogeneity. Along the proximodistal axis, a gradient in the properties of the CA3 pyramidal cells has been characterized in a number of anatomical, physiological, functional and behavioral studies20–24. Along the superficial–deep (radial) axis, pyramidal cells are organized into two sublayers, distinguished by the abundance of MF axons, with distinct physiological properties and proposed functions25–28. Here we aim to characterize synaptic ultrastructural correlates of these systematic variations of CA3 pyramidal cells.

Mapping the MF-to-CA3 pyramidal connectivity will inform models of how a pattern separation circuit is transformed into a pattern completion circuit in the brain29,30. MF boutons are typically large enough to be seen with light microscopy, and prior studies have quantified the number and spatial distribution of MF boutons31–33. However, current understanding of MF–CA3 connectivity relies primarily on these bouton counts along with estimates of the number of CA3 pyramidal neurons, under the assumption that each presynaptic bouton contacts only a single pyramidal cell1. Although MF terminals are typically large, they exhibit substantial morphological variability, with some being considerably smaller34,35. The one-bouton-one-CA3-cell assumption remains untested at scale. Furthermore, given the heterogeneity of CA3 pyramidal cells, it is important to study the spatial bias of their MF input, particularly along the proximodistal axis. Although MF–CA3 connectivity has typically been modeled as uniformly random, detailed mapping that captures the heterogeneity of these connections could provide a starting point to think about how connectivity variations contribute to computational functions such as pattern separation and pattern completion.

We have acquired, assembled and automatically segmented a 0.1 mm3 volume of hippocampal CA3, including neuronal and non-neuronal cells, cell nuclei and synapses. Dense segmentation is hosted on a Connectome Annotation Versioning Engine (CAVE) architecture for real-time collaborative proofreading, annotation and analysis. Additional segmentation of subcellular structures such as boutons and vesicles is demonstrated. We proofread and classified all soma-containing neurons and ~55,000 MF axons in the volume. With these reconstructions, we have characterized three features of connectivity onto CA3 pyramidal cells: spatial gradients, with proximodistal increases in MF inputs; high convergence, with a small subset of pyramidal cells receiving input from a surprisingly large number of MF axons; and cell type specificity, with perisomatic inhibitory axons targeting distinct subtypes of pyramidal cells. These findings constrain computational models of hippocampal circuits and exemplify the utility of the dataset. We are releasing the dataset as a resource to the community, and we expect other researchers will find it useful for addressing their own questions.

Results

Dataset overview

A tissue block from the dorsal hippocampus of an adult female mouse (Mus musculus, C57BL/6J, 4 months old) was stained using an established protocol (Methods) and imaged with X-ray micro-computed tomography (μCT) to locate the CA3 region (Fig. 1a–f). The CA3-containing region of the block was sectioned and imaged using our previously reported connectomic imaging pipeline featuring beam deflection TEM36 and GridTape technology (Methods). As the first large-scale acquisition using this pipeline, the CA3 volume spans 0.93 × 1.03 × 0.092 mm3 at an imaging voxel size of 3 × 3 × 45 nm (Fig. 1g). To facilitate integration with other mouse brain datasets and identify the precise anatomical location of the volume, we registered the EM dataset to a widely used template brain (the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework (CCF) version 3 (CCFv3)). The EM volume was first aligned to the x-ray µCT volume of the sample block, which in turn was registered to the CCF (Fig. 1a–d and Methods). The EM volume comprises 2,048 sections cut at 45 nm along the Z axis (spanning layers 96 to 2,143), including 39 missing sections (Extended Data Fig. 1a). The EM images were aligned and segmented with deep learning techniques (Methods). The segmented volume contains four trillion voxels at a voxel size of 18 × 18 × 45 nm3. We assessed quality by examining segmentation errors of thin axons and narrow spine necks, two challenging structures for automated reconstruction. We proofread the pyramidal and MF axons by correcting segmentation errors and then measured the lengths of axonal segments that were separated by the errors we corrected. Analysis of pyramidal axons longer than 0.5 mm shows an average axon length of 108 μm between segmentation errors (Fig. 1h and Methods) and an average of 167 μm for MF axons (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Assessments of spine attachments show two merge errors and 23 false negatives out of 441 ground-truth spines from a pyramidal cell (Extended Data Fig. 2a). Despite the thicker sections (45 nm), our segmentation accuracy is highly competitive with other contemporary mammalian connectomic datasets8,10,11. As a result, the mean proofreading rate for pyramidal axons is 11.3 mm h−1 (Extended Data Fig. 2b,c), several times faster than previous estimates8,11. The leading causes of segmentation errors are consecutive missing sections (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2d–f). We exhaustively proofread two regions spanning the largest gap, and all segments—including fine processes—could either be traced through the gap (>95%) or terminated (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). We concluded that the proofread segmentation is highly accurate in spite of the missing sections.

We have proofread and classified all neurons with somata within the volume—1,815 pyramidal cells and 229 inhibitory neurons (Fig. 1i,j)—as well as over 55,000 MF axons and 91 inhibitory axons lacking somata but with substantial axonal arbors within the volume. The proofread segments account for 40% of all voxels (Extended Data Fig. 2g). The remaining voxels consist of segmented fragments that have not yet been proofread and are generally smaller in size. We have developed a website (Pyr.ai; Supplementary Fig. 1) as the main portal for accessing the dataset, providing EM imagery, reconstructions and cell type classifications for download, programmatic access and interactive browsing.

Nucleus size distributions show that neurons have larger nuclei than non-neuronal cells, with pyramidal cells larger than inhibitory neurons (Fig. 1i). Inhibitory cells are further classified based on the layer in which the majority of their output synapses are located (Extended Data Fig. 3a–c). A hallmark of the CA3 region is the large synapses between MF boutons and CA3 pyramidal dendrites (MF–Pyr synapses), located in the soma-free stratum lucidum layer37. Indeed, filtering by size reveals that the largest synapses in the dataset are localized to this discrete layer above stratum pyramidale (Fig. 1j).

The volume spans 1 mm in the XY plane along the transverse axis, making it well-suited for study of systematic variations along this dimension. However, its range along the Z axis (92 μm) is limited by the number of sections, and the sectioning plane was measured to be on average at 44° relative to the apical dendritic shafts of pyramidal cells (Methods). As a result, neurons at different depths within the volume are truncated at different cellular compartments, such as apical dendrites, soma or basal dendrites (Extended Data Fig. 3d). The volume spans stratum radiatum, stratum lucidum, stratum pyramidale and stratum oriens, from the superficial to the deep layers (Fig. 1j).

Mapping MF inputs to CA3 pyramidal cells

The MF–Pyr synapses are physiologically unique38–41 and morphologically highly complex: each MF bouton typically envelops a cluster of densely branched dendritic spines—thorny excrescences (TEs)—located on the proximal apical dendrites1,5–7,42,43 (Fig. 2a–d). The TEs are sufficiently large such that their variability on the shaft of pyramidal cells has been documented at the light microscopy level33,44. However, individual spine heads within TEs are small and exhibit a diverse range of sizes and shapes, with spines receiving inputs from different boutons often located in close proximity. Given that the TEs are localized to the proximal apical dendrites close to the soma, we selected pyramidal cells with somata near one end of the Z axis (layers 1,400–2,143) to maximize coverage of their proximal apical dendrites within the volume. This group is referred to as ‘long apical pyramidal cells’ (636 cells) and does not imply a distinct cell type. We mapped all MF inputs to the long apical pyramidal cells, from a total of ~25,200 MF axons. Although the somata of MF axons lie outside the imaged volume, sparse anatomical tracing studies have indicated that MF axons in CA3 branch minimally and each reconstructed MF axon probably comes from a unique granule cell32. The distribution of the MF inputs is bimodal with a heavy tail (Fig. 2e). One mode comprises a population of CA3 cells (24%) that receive no or minimal MF inputs (sparsely thorny cells). The other mode consists of CA3 cells receiving 15 or more MF inputs per cell, with most having around 50 (thorny cells). This matches with previous indirect estimates for rat31. Surprisingly, a small number of CA3 cells (2%) have over 100 MF inputs per cell, with the maximum close to 300, many more than previous estimates31.

A potential concern is whether the observed distribution is confounded by truncation of the apical dendrites owing to the limited Z axis. We observed that the overall distribution and the extreme values of MF inputs are insensitive to the axial location of the nuclei (Extended Data Fig. 4a). Apical dendritic length distributions are similar across proximodistal locations (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Furthermore, a subset of long apical pyramidal cells (266 cells) have apical dendrites with side branches, which typically mark the distal extent of TEs44 (Fig. 2c,d), suggesting that most of the TEs are captured. The number of MF inputs to this subset exhibits a similar bimodal distribution (Extended Data Fig. 4c). Therefore, the observed distribution of MF inputs is not significantly biased by volume limitation.

The CA3 pyramidal cells have been classified into two morphologically distinct subtypes, thorny and sparsely thorny, distinguished by abundance of TEs25–27. In the EM volume, categorizing pyramidal cells solely by their number of MF inputs, using a threshold derived from the distribution (Fig. 2e), recapitulates this previously described division. For interactive visualizations of pyramidal subtypes, see the URLs in Supplementary Table 1. The cell bodies of the sparsely thorny cells are preferentially located deeper in the pyramidal cell layer (radial axis) and are more at the distal region (transverse axis), while thorny cells are situated more superficially within the layer (Fig. 2f). Morphologically, thorny cells have a larger nucleus and more elaborate basal dendrites than sparsely thorny cells (Fig. 2g,h). Although our quantification is limited to neurons with both somata and proximal apical dendrites within the volume, selecting cells truncated further away from the soma—thereby capturing more of the upper dendritic arbors—shows that thorny and sparsely thorny cells also differ markedly in the extent of dendritic branching (Extended Data Fig. 4d–f). These morphological distinctions and spatial preferences of two subtypes are in line with previous studies25,26.

Distal CA3 pyramidal cells receive more numerous MF inputs than proximal cells

Proximodistal heterogeneity of MF inputs to CA3 has been studied with anatomical tracing and physiological approaches23,32. We extended these findings by analyzing how the spatial distribution of pyramidal cells is related to the number of MF inputs they receive. Within the EM volume, the CA3 pyramidal cells receive an increasing number of MF inputs along the proximodistal axis (Fig. 3a). Increased sharing of MF inputs in distal pyramidal cells

The MF–Pyr connectivity in CA3 has been thought to participate in pattern separation16,17,45. To analyze their connectivity, we use theoretical methods developed for pattern separation circuits in systems such as the olfactory system and cerebellum46,47. These models relate connectivity in a two-layer circuit to the dimensionality of second-layer representations. High-dimensional representations improve associative learning, whereas low-dimensional representations facilitate generalization and increase robustness to noise47.

Two key determinants of this dimensionality are randomness and degree, referring to the number of synaptic partners per cell. Although demonstrating true randomness is inherently challenging, the extent of randomness in a circuit can be quantified through comparisons with defined null models that incorporate constraints such as degree distributions and the spatial proximity of synaptic partners.

We first compared the reconstructed MF–Pyr connectivity to a configuration model (Methods), in which the out-degree (the number of pyramidal targets each MF axon contacts) and the in-degree (the number of presynaptic MF axons each pyramidal cell receives) are preserved, while the specific connections between them are randomized. Relative to this model, the reconstructed connectivity exhibited a higher degree of shared MF inputs between pyramidal cell pairs (Fig. 4a,b). We next compared the MF–Pyr connectivity with a radius model in which each bouton was randomly connected to thorny spines within a given radius (10 µm or 50 µm radius models). The increase and clustering of common MF inputs between the observed connectivity and the models was reduced (Fig. 4a–d, Extended Data Fig. 5a and Methods). Moreover, the distribution of distances between postsynaptic sites of pyramidal cell pairs sharing MF inputs showed a peak at short distances (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). Indeed, some shared inputs originate from thorny spines of two neighboring pyramidal cells innervated by either the same MF terminal (59 out of 603 shared input pairs; Extended Data Fig. 5d) or nearby satellite terminals belonging to the same MF axon (39 out of 603 shared input pairs; Extended Data Fig. 5e). In summary, the increased sharing is largely consistent with locally randomized connectivity based on spatial proximity of synaptic partners.

The spatial gradient observed for MF inputs led us to investigate whether input sharing shows a spatial bias. Distal pyramidal cells were found to have increased input sharing (Fig. 4e) and a corresponding decrease in the expected dimensionality of activity, quantified by participation ratios (Methods). Comparison with a configuration model that preserves degrees while eliminating input sharing shows only minor differences (Fig. 4f). Therefore, increased in-degrees among distal pyramidal cells, rather than input sharing, drives the dimensionality reduction.

In addition to forming synapses on thorny spines, the MF terminals also form puncta adherentia1,5 or synaptic contacts (Extended Data Fig. 5f) on dendritic shafts of pyramidal cells. In the analysis, all connections of pyramidal cell pairs sharing two or more common MF inputs were verified to only include MF inputs onto thorny spines.

High-convergent pyramidal cells receive inputs through relatively small MF terminals

Dense segmentation at nanoscale allows us to examine not only the number but the strengths of each input, inferred from sizes of synaptic structures. The MF boutons are known to have a variety of sizes and morphologies37, with anywhere from zero to multiple filopodia extensions. We developed a method of segmenting MF boutons based on the number of neighboring vertices in 3D meshes (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6a–c) and validated its ability to segment a variety of bouton types, including en passant, terminal, doublets and boutons with filopodia (Extended Data Fig. 6d and Supplementary Table 1). This method was used to segment a majority of MF boutons (24,264, >85% of total) that are connected to the 636 long apical pyramidal cells. The distribution of bouton volumes has a wide range, from a few to over 40 μm3 (Fig. 5a), prompting us to examine their relationships with the number of synaptic vesicles. We adapted a deep learning-based cellular segmentation method to automatically identify vesicles (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 6e), given the similarity between vesicles and cell bodies from other imaging modalities. The neural network, trained on human-annotated vesicles, achieved high accuracy in predicting vesicles from 2D images of the CA3 volume. Analysis of bouton volumes shows that those originating from the same MF axon are uncorrelated (Fig. 5c). Owing to the limited volume of the dataset, each MF axon typically has only one to three boutons (Extended Data Fig. 6f), while estimates from studies in rats suggest that each MF axon, on average, forms 11 to 18 large terminals that innervate pyramidal cells34. At the postsynaptic side, individual pyramidal cells also receive inputs from boutons with a wide range of sizes (Fig. 5d). The volumes of MF boutons innervating sparsely thorny cells are smaller than those innervating thorny cells (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Paradoxically, pyramidal cells with high convergence—those with over 111 MF inputs—form synapses with MF boutons that are, on average, smaller in size (Fig. 5e–g). The inverse relationship between the number of MF inputs and bouton volume may reflect compensatory scaling of synaptic weights. Although highly convergent pyramidal cells cluster at the distal end along the transverse axis (Fig. 3b), distal pyramidal cells receive more numerous MF inputs through smaller MF boutons and fewer, smaller active zones per input (Fig. 5h and Extended Data Fig. 7b,c), matching the reported gradient of decreasing synaptic strength in response to MF stimulation23.

Cell-type-specific feedforward inhibition to pyramidal cells

The classification of pyramidal subtypes allows us to analyze cell-type-specific connectivity. We next examined inhibitory inputs onto pyramidal cells to assess whether these connections exhibit subtype specificity. Many segments with the highest numbers of output synapses in the volume were identifiable as inhibitory because they form symmetric synapses onto postsynaptic compartments such as dendritic shafts, somata and perisomatic regions (Extended Data Fig. 8). This group includes inhibitory cells with dendrites, axons and somata as well as those with truncated dendrites or somata (Extended Data Fig. 9a). From this set, we further selected axons with 200 or more output synapses onto pyramidal cells with known subtypes. We found that many inhibitory cells preferentially target either sparsely thorny cells or thorny cells, as indicated by the differing number of synapses onto each group (Fig. 6a). As thorny cells have larger nuclei than sparsely thorny cells (Fig. 2h), when the postsynaptic pyramidal cells were grouped by nucleus sizes, a preferential targeting effect in the number of synapses was also observed (Extended Data Fig. 9b). A major division of inhibitory cell types is based on their subcellular targeting region: perisomatic versus dendrite-targeting. We measured the along-the-skeleton distances from the postsynaptic sites to the center of the pyramidal cell nucleus. We then analyzed the distribution of synapse-to-soma distances for each inhibitory axon and determined the mode for each distribution. The overall distribution of modes for all inhibitory axons is bimodal, corresponding to perisomatic and dendrite-targeting groups (Extended Data Fig. 9c,d). A majority of subtype-specific inhibitory axons are found to be perisomatic (Fig. 6a). Notably, the inhibitory synapses from perisomatic inhibitory axons are distributed across many pyramidal cells (Extended Data Fig. 9e,f). The main axonal shafts of perisomatic inhibitory neurons are myelinated (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

Thorny and sparsely thorny cells occupy superficial and deep sublayers of the soma layer, respectively. The preferential targeting of either subtype by inhibitory axons could arise either from axons projecting selectively to superficial or deep sublayers, or from broadly distributed axonal innervation across the soma layer combined with subtype-specific synapse formation. Examination of axonal projection territory shows that axons of inhibitory cells that preferentially target sparsely thorny cells are predominantly localized to the deeper portion of the pyramidal cell layer, whereas those targeting thorny cells preferentially innervate the superficial portion (Fig. 6b). Consistent with a previous study34, our quantification shows that MF axons innervate interneurons primarily through filopodia and small en passant boutons (Extended Data Fig. 10a). Occasionally, filopodia contact pyramidal cells (Extended Data Fig. 10b), as previously suggested6. We find that MF innervation is selective for interneurons targeting thorny pyramidal cells (Fig. 6c and Extended Data Fig. 8b,c), showing a subtype-specific feedforward inhibition circuit. Consistent with this selective connectivity, MF axons and boutons overlap with interneurons that target thorny cells, but not with those that target sparsely thorny cells (Extended Data Fig. 10c–e). Discussion

In this study, we present a reconstructed volume from the hippocampal CA3 region (~1 × 1 × 0.1 mm), generated from 2,048 serial TEM sections collected using GridTape technology. Pyramidal cells in CA3 segregate into two sublayers, thorny and sparsely thorny25–27, with deep-layer sparsely thorny cells having fewer excrescences and smaller nuclei. We identified a proximodistal gradient of MF inputs onto thorny cells: proximal neurons received sparse but large MF boutons, whereas distal neurons received denser MF input composed of smaller boutons, along with increased input sharing in distal regions. Inhibition is also segregated, with thorny and sparsely thorny neurons targeted by distinct inhibitory axons. Finally, MF axons selectively target interneurons that innervate thorny pyramidal cells. Overall, our study characterized the organization of excitatory and inhibitory inputs to pyramidal cells in CA3.

CA3 EM datasets

A recently published 3D EM volume of CA3 (ref. 13) is comparable in size to our dataset but differs in several aspects. First, we used a 4-month-old female mouse compared to their 1-month-old male. Second, imaging and reconstruction methods differ. Our dataset is acquired with GridTape TEM, while the comparison study used multi-beam scanning electron microscopy. Although our volume has slightly more sections with greater loss, we applied deep learning-based dense reconstruction methods robust to artifacts and missing data (Methods). Third, targeted subregions differ. Proximal CA3 pyramidal cells (CA3c), targeted by the previous study13, are thought to be smaller and contain fewer recurrent collaterals than distal cells (CA3b and CA3a)48,49 in our study. Fourth, the sectioning planes differ. The comparison study sectioned more parallel to the apical dendrites, capturing fewer somata but spanning all CA3 layers. By contrast, our dataset is angled to the apical dendrites, missing parts of stratum radiatum and most of stratum moleculare but including more cell bodies in the transverse plane, allowing for analysis of systematic variations along this dimension.

Systematic variation of neuronal properties in hippocampus CA3 and CA1

Although the heterogeneity of hippocampal pyramidal cells has been well recognized50, growing evidence suggests that many neuronal properties vary in a systematic manner. One notable example is the superficial–deep axis in CA3, which exhibits consistent division in dendritic morphology26, electrophysiological properties25,51, genetic markers52, birth timing53 and physiologically assessed connectivity27. Our study extends this organizational principle to connectivity at the ultrastructural level by quantifying the number of MF inputs and revealing sublayer-specific inhibitory inputs. Furthermore, along the proximodistal axis, we identified a gradient in both the number and size of MF inputs, adding to known variations in morphology21, physiology23 and functions20,22.

In the CA1 region, superficial versus deep pyramidal cells have been studied for differences in physiology54–56, long-range entorhinal inputs57, transcriptional profiles58, birthdating59, spatial coding60,61 and inhibitory input62. Together, these findings point to a common principle of layered organization across hippocampal subfields.

Functional implication

Thorny cells fire regularly, while sparsely thorny cells show bursting activity25. This bursting was essential for initiating sharp wave events: synchronized activity patterns important for hippocampal memory function. We show that perisomatic inhibitory axons are myelinated and target pyramidal subtypes with highly distributed synapses, suggesting a role for fast, temporally precise, subtype-specific inhibition in coordinating network activity during sharp wave events63.

A functional gradient in CA3 has been described, with strong pattern separation proximally and robust pattern completion distally20,22,24. The underlying mechanism was thought to be a graded recurrent connectivity, as recurrent collaterals are more extensive distally23,48,49. Analysis of recurrent connectivity requires circuit analysis and dataset-wide segmentation of synaptic weights (for example, spine head volumes), while accounting for the limitation posed by neuron truncation, particularly of axons. This remains a topic of interest for follow-up study.

We found another proximodistal gradient in the number of MF inputs, with a few distal pyramidal cells receiving well over 200 MF inputs. Greater MF convergence may enable distal CA3 to integrate a broader range of input patterns, consistent with the larger place field sizes reported in distal compared to proximal CA3 (refs. 20,22). Using previously developed methods46, we showed that more numerous inputs in distal pyramidal cells correspond to a reduction in dimensionality of representation (Fig. 4e,f). Further modeling studies should explore the relationship between high convergence and the proposed computational roles of CA3, such as pattern completion. Directly relating functional properties of CA3 cells to the number of inputs will require combining in vivo imaging with connectomic mapping on the same cells.

Our findings showed that the two types of CA3 pyramidal cells are controlled by distinct inhibitory circuitry. Thorny cells receive feedforward inhibition driven by MF input. Feedforward inhibition is generally thought to regulate the temporal precision of action potentials64. In CA3, feedforward inhibition allows MF input to suppress excessive depolarization or repetitive burst firing of thorny cells65–67. This mechanism is thought to promote sparse firing, which is consistent with pattern separation. By contrast, inhibitory cells presynaptic to sparsely thorny cells are not driven by MF axons. Activity in CA3 has been shown to reflect both responses to external stimuli and internally generated sequences on different timescales68. Our findings suggest that thorny cells are controlled by stimulus-driven feedforward inhibition, whereas the sparsely thorny cells are controlled by internally driven inhibition from recurrent input. These results provide further support for the hypothesis that these two cell types may perform different computational functions in the hippocampal CA3.

Larger EM volume and openness

Large-scale EM imaging is dichotomized into scanning electron microscopy-based and TEM-based approaches, both of which have benefited from automated sectioning and imaging to reach millimeter-scale volumes, yet the largest volumes8,11 still contain artifacts that limit automated reconstruction. Although our dataset is modest in size (0.1 mm3), we show that improved image quality and computational methods enable dense reconstructions from a TEM volume with high efficiency and quality. Meanwhile, scanning electron microscopy volumes of comparable quality and larger in size than our volume have recently been reported10,13,14, with ongoing reconstruction. These scales, however, pale by comparison with the expansive axons of CA3 pyramidal cells, which span a large part of the longitudinal axis and densely innervate the contralateral hippocampus69. A significant scale-up of over an order of magnitude—up to a whole mouse brain—is needed to fully cover these brain-spanning neurons.

The neuroscience community will benefit from healthy competition between the two approaches and, more importantly, a continuing commitment to openness. Emulating successful community-driven connectomics platforms like EyeWire70 and FlyWire71, Pyr launches as a similarly open platform (Pyr.ai; Supplementary Fig. 1) dedicated to enabling collaborative discoveries of hippocampal connectomics.

Methods

Sample preparation

Animal

All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Princeton University. A subset of unaligned sections from this sample has been used previously36. A 4-month-old female mouse (M. musculus; C57BL/6J-Tg(Thy1-GCaMP6f)GP5.3Dkim/J; The Jackson Laboratory strain no. 028280) was housed under a reverse light cycle (lights off, 08:00–20:00 h; lights on, 20:00–08:00 h) at a temperature of 21 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 10% humidity. The mouse was transcardially perfused with a 4 °C fixative mixture of 2.5% paraformaldehyde and 1.3% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate with 2 mM CaCl2, pH 7.4. The brain was extracted and post-fixed for 36 h at 4 °C in the same fixative solution. The perfused brain was subsequently rinsed in 0.1 M cacodylate with 2 mM CaCl2 for 1 h (three times for 20 min each, 4 °C), and 300 μm coronal sections were cut on a Leica Vibratome on ice.

EM staining

The 300 μm-thick sample blocks were cut out from the right hemisphere and stained based on a modified reduced osmium (rOTO) protocol with the addition of formamide72. The tissue blocks were first en bloc stained with 8% formamide, 2% osmium tetroxide and 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide for 3 h at room temperature (20–25 °C). Subsequently, the samples were immersed in 1% thiocarbohydrazide (94 mM) at 50 °C for 50 min, followed by a second step of 2% osmium staining for 3 h at room temperature. The sample was placed in 1% uranyl acetate overnight at 4 °C, followed by lead aspartate (Walton’s, 20 mM lead nitrate in 30 mM aspartate buffer, pH 5.5) at 50 °C for 2 h. After being washed with water (three times for 10 min each, room temperature), samples proceeded through a graded acetonitrile dehydration series (50%, 75%, 90% w/v in acetonitrile, 10 min each at 4 °C, then four times for 10 min with 100% acetonitrile at room temperature). After a progressive resin infiltration series (33% resin:acetonitrile, 50% resin:acetonitrile, 66% resin:acetonitrile, 8 h each at room temperature), the sample was incubated in fresh 100% resin overnight at room temperature, and the resin was then cured in the oven at 60 °C for at least 48 h. The resin was prepared in the following steps. Solution A consisted of 10 g LX-112 (LADD Research, 21310) mixed with 11.2 g NSA (EMS, 19050), and solution B consisted of 30 g LX-112 combined with 26.4 g NMA (EMS, 19000). For the final embedding resin, 18 g of solution A (30%) and 42 g of solution B (70%) were combined, followed by the addition of 1.334 ml (2%) BDMA (EMS, 11400-25) as the accelerator. Embedded tissue blocks were then imaged by X-ray μCT (Zeiss Xradia Versa 520) to evaluate staining quality and tissue integrity. The sample was imaged with ×4 optical magnification, yielding a pixel size of 1–2 µm. The tissue block that best contained the target brain region (hippocampus CA3) was chosen for serial sectioning.

Sectioning

The tissue block spans several millimeters in the XY plane, encompassing CA1, CA2, CA3 and part of the dentate gyrus. The main distinction between CA2 and CA3 is the stratum lucidum, a cell-free layer that is difficult to discern using light microscopy. X-ray μCT imaging of EM-stained tissue blocks can resolve individual nuclei, fine blood vessels and, in this case, a slightly more translucent stratum lucidum layer immediately superficial to the soma layer. We therefore first imaged the block with μCT to precisely locate the CA3 region for subsequent serial sectioning. The resolution allows identification of the CA3 region by its comparatively sparse nuclei and unambiguous correspondence to the same structures imaged in EM (Fig. 1a–f). In addition, CA2 is defined as a small region that does not receive MF input. The pyramidal cells at the distal end of the EM dataset still have abundant TEs that receive MF input, a defining feature of the CA3 region. In summary, the entire EM volume is located within CA3.

The target sample block was first trimmed to a mesa containing the CA3 region using an ultramicrotome (Leica UC7) and a diamond trimming knife (Trim 90, EMS-Diatome). The mesa was trimmed into an oblong hexagonal shape of 4.5 mm height and 1 mm width such that the section fully covers the aperture sized 2 mm × 1.5 mm (GridTape, Luxel) and contains the target tissue at the center of the section. To facilitate adherence of the sections to the aperture on the tape during collection, the leading edge tip was trimmed to have an obtuse angle and the trailing edge tip was trimmed to have an acute angle. We combined an automated tape collecting system (ATUMtome, RMC/Boeckeler) and an ultramicrotome (UC7, Leica) to create a sectioning and collection system and further modified it to be compatible with GridTape36. The series was cut with a 4 mm wide 35° ultra Maxi Diamond Knife (Diatome). The cutting speed was set to 0.3 mm s−1 at a nominal thickness of 40–45 nm, and the speed of the tape was synchronized with this cutting rate through a closed-loop system. A total of 2,145 sections were cut. The typical cycle time of cutting and collecting one section is 20 s, resulting in a total sectioning time of about 12 h for the entire series.

TEM imaging

Imaging was done using our previously reported connectomic imaging pipeline featuring beam deflection TEM36 and GridTape technology73. We used two JEOL-1200EXII 120 kV TEMs customized with a beam deflection mechanism (Voxa), a reel-to-reel tape translation system (Voxa), lens assembly (AMT, NanoSprint50M-AV) and a high-speed CMOS camera (XIMEA, CB500MG-CM). At each stage location, the beam deflection was configured to image nine subtiles, each sized at 6,000 × 6,000 pixels with a pixel resolution of ~2.9 nm. Each image tile contains 36 megapixels (or megabytes) of data. Each set of nine tiles forms a supertile, with approximately 900 pixels of overlap between neighboring tiles within each supertile, equivalent to about 15% of the tile dimensions. The nominal overlap between subtiles of neighboring supertiles is approximately 600 pixels, equivalent to about 10% of the tile dimensions. The imaging area per section is around 1 mm × 1 mm to be in excess of the whole tissue section. A section typically comprises 440 supertiles of 20 by 22. With nine tiles per supertile, this yields a typical total of 3,960 tiles for a section. The whole dataset has over 8.5 million tile images. Imaging of the series took place over a period of about 5 months as the imaging software and hardware was being developed. The typical size of raw data for each section is ~133 GB, totaling over 280 TB of data for the whole dataset.

From the 2,145 sections, the initial portion (Z = 0–95) was severely damaged and therefore excluded from segmentation. The main series, which was aligned and segmented, spans 2,048 layers along the Z axis (Z = 96–2,143, inclusive). Within this core series, consecutive losses include one instance of five-section loss, one instance of three-section loss, three instances of two-section loss and 25 instances of single-section loss (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Most of the consecutive section losses occurred during the imaging process. The primary causes were precipitous venting of the microscope column and incomplete drying of residual water on the sections. We have since performed maintenance and modified hardware to address these issues. After alignment and segmentation, the resulting size of the volume is 1 mm × 1 mm × 92.2 µm.

Although our segmentation quality and proofreading speed are competitive in large-scale datasets, most neurons are truncated, particularly along the Z axis (Extended Data Fig. 3d). We used morphological features, such as apical side branches, to ensure complete mapping of MF inputs, and separately examined the upper apical dendrites of thorny and sparsely thorny cells whose lower apical dendrites were truncated (Fig. 2c,d). The dominant cause of segmentation errors is missing sections (Extended Data Fig. 2d), especially across a five-section gap. However, most processes can be followed across the five-section gap, although proofreading is required (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). Moreover, circuit reconstructions in other mammalian TEM volumes with a comparable number of consecutively missing sections have also been reported74.

Montaging

The processing of assembling the volume includes stitching and alignment. Stitching (that is, montaging) refers to the montaging of all image tiles within a section through the overlap between neighboring tiles. Stitching was done using a package based on an elastic spring mesh algorithm (AlignTK; https://mmbios.pitt.edu/aligntk-home). In summary, adjacent images are first registered through a linear transformation followed by a nonlinear mapping based on image correlation. Each image is represented as a grid of nodes, with pairwise mappings represented by springs connecting the nodes. Consequently, each section is represented as a large spring network, and a solution is derived through a multi-resolution relaxation algorithm. Stitching computation can generally be parallelized in two ways. First, correlation is computed between pairs of image tiles and can be distributed across multiple processes in a computing node. Second, because stitching operates on a per-section basis, each section can be independently assigned to a separate computing node for parallel processing. Processes within each node are concurrently executed using the parallel processing mechanism (mpirun) in the alignTK package. Before rendering for subsequent alignment, image tiles are contrast normalized using the gen_imaps function. Montaging of each section, when run in parallel with other sections, typically takes 4–6 h and includes pairwise correlation computation, mesh relaxation, contrast normalization, manual inspection and rendering. Montaging of the whole series takes over 4 months in total.

Alignment and segmentation

Images were aligned into a 3D stack by a vendor (Zetta AI) using published methods75–77. The vendor also pre-trained a residual symmetric U-Net78,79, with data augmentation80 to predict affinities between neighboring voxels at 16 × 16 × 40 nm3 manually annotated data derived from three mouse visual cortex datasets11. The U-Net was fine-tuned on manually annotated data from the present dataset at 18 × 18 × 45 nm3.

Segmentation used methods described previously80. The only modification was the addition of a multi-resolution approach81 to reduce merge errors between large objects caused by image defects and broken cell membranes. We generated a conservative low-resolution segmentation at 72 × 72 × 45 nm3, using a low-resolution affinity model trained on a downsampled 18 × 18 × 45 nm3 segmentation of a small cutout. We upsampled the low-resolution segmentation using nearest neighbors to 18 × 18 × 45 nm3 and used it as a constraint for the high-resolution segmentation at 18 × 18 × 45 nm3. Specifically, the upsampled low-resolution segments were treated as semantic labels, and agglomerations between fragments with different labels were forbidden. The results may contain additional split errors but no new merge errors between large objects. The final step of the segmentation pipeline treated the latest high-resolution segments as supervoxels and agglomerated them without the low-resolution label constraint. This step fixes large split errors in which fragments have different low-resolution labels. Instead, we added semantic constraints for predicted compartment labels and size heuristics to prevent unwanted mergers80.

The segmentation bounding box measures 51,421 × 57,385 × 2,048 voxels, with a voxel size of 18 × 18 × 45 nm, corresponding to a physical volume of 0.93 × 1.03 × 0.092 mm, or 0.088 mm3. Owing to irregular edges and black space in the upper left and right corners of the volume, the total number of segmented voxels is approximately four trillion.

Synapses

The vendor trained two residual symmetric U-Nets, one for synaptic cleft detection at 18 × 18 × 45 nm3 and one for presynaptic and postsynaptic mask predictions at 32 × 32 × 45 nm3 (ref. 82). Together, these models capture presynaptic specializations (active zones), the synaptic cleft and postsynaptic densities. The models were trained on manually annotated data sampled from various regions across the present dataset.

The cleft probability map was downsampled by two times, thresholded and segmented with connected components. Segments with fewer than five voxels were excluded. Presynaptic and postsynaptic cells for each cleft were assigned following previous work82.

Reconstruction pipeline

The inferences were done using a modified version of Chunkflow83, segmentation was created using Abiss84 and synaptic clefts and assignments were generated with Synaptor82. The reconstruction pipeline was orchestrated by SEURON (https://github.com/seung-lab/seuron). The segmentation and synapses were ingested into the CAVE proofreading platform for further analysis85.

Quantification of segmentation accuracy

Segmentation failure modes

For a semi-quantitative description of failure modes, we randomly chose 100 segmentation errors and attempted to attribute them to various causes (Extended Data Fig. 2d). The leading cause was consecutive missing sections. With appropriate training data augmentation, a convolutional net can detect neuronal boundaries with high accuracy in spite of missing sections78. However, accuracy plummets when several consecutive sections are missing. Other failure modes include inaccuracies of the segmentation model to identify intracellular organelles (Extended Data Fig. 2d,e) or extracellular space (Extended Data Fig. 2f). Misalignment now occurs at a rather low rate and is no longer a leading cause of segmentation errors. However, some segmentation errors may not be caused by a single factor, but rather by a ‘perfect storm’ of a failure mode in Extended Data Fig. 2d, a small misalignment and a neurite that is almost parallel to the plane of the section.

Mean axon path lengths between errors86

For each neuron, we computed the axon path length between errors by dividing the total skeleton length of the fully proofread axon by the number of proofreading edits (Fig. 1h). The mean axon path length between errors was 108 µm. As there are cases in which multiple edits were required to correct a single error, this number is a lower bound. Only pyramidal cell axons extending from the soma were used. The initial portion of axons near the soma tends to be thicker and less prone to segmentation errors. To reduce bias, we restricted analysis to pyramidal cells with axons longer than 0.5 mm.

Evaluation of continuity through missing sections

We examined the largest gap in the dataset (five missing sections, Z = 1,262–1,266 inclusive) by exhaustively proofreading nearly 500 segments in two randomly selected regions: one densely populated with axons and the other with dendrites and spines (Extended Data Fig. 1b,c). In all cases, the segments—including fine processes—could either be traced through the gap (>95%) or terminated. We concluded that our proofread segmentation is highly accurate in spite of the missing sections. Furthermore, it may be possible in the future to automate segmentation across gaps of as large as five sections, at least for data of sufficiently high resolution and contrast.

To assess the continuity of neuronal processes across this gap, we selected two regions of interest: one densely populated with axons (Extended Data Fig. 1b), and another containing a mixture of thin axons, spines and dendrites (Extended Data Fig. 1c). In both regions of interest, all neurites were exhaustively proofread in the sections immediately adjacent to the gap (Z = 1,261 and Z = 1,267). We identified 322 neurite segments in the first region of interest and 162 in the second. For each of all segments, we were able to either trace a likely continuation across the gap or determine that the segment represented a spine head terminating near the gap boundary.

Proofreading

Proofreading infrastructure

The automated segmentation was ingested into the ChunkedGraph data structure, and CAVE is used for hosting the proofreadable segmentation and annotations85.

Proofreading rate

Proofreading rate was computed by dividing the skeletal length of the final proofread neuron segment over proofreading time. To calculate total proofreading time per neuron, the timestamps of proofreading events are extracted, and the time intervals between consecutive proofreading events that are less than or equal to 1 h apart are summed for each neuron. Anything over 1 h between proofreading events was interpreted as a stop by the proofreader. When calculating the skeleton length, branches shorter than 100 µm were excluded. The proofreading rate is calculated by dividing the total skeleton length by the total proofreading time. Pyramidal axons are thinner than dendrites and more prone to segmentation errors. Therefore, only axon proofreading events and axon lengths were used to assess proofreading rate.

Registration to the Allen CCF

Registration between CA3 EM and μCT volume

To generate a transform between the EM image volume (~1 × 1 × 0.1 mm) and the larger μCT volume (~2.5 × 1.6 × 0.7 mm) of the same specimen, we manually generated a set of 13 corresponding landmarks. For this process, we first converted the μCT image stack into a neuroglancer-compatible data source (precomputed format) using the cloud-volume Python library (seung-lab/cloud-volume). Next, we marked somata and blood vessels (mostly branch points) that were uniquely identifiable in both uCT and EM. These landmarks were used to generate a thin plate spline transform between the μCT and EM image volume. The landmarks and the transform were packaged in the navis-mousebrains (navis-org/navis-mousebrains) Python package (v0.0.5 release); see the readme file for examples on how to apply. For visual inspection, we also generated a neuroglancer scene with the non-rigid (affine) part of the transform applied to the uCT dataset.

Registration between CA3 μCT volume and the Allen CCF87

We first obtained the image stack from https://alleninstitute.github.io/abc_atlas_access/descriptions/Allen-CCF-2020.html for the average template (10 μm voxel size). Next, used the ‘BigWarp’88 plugin for ImageJ (part of the Fiji distribution; https://fiji.sc) to manually place 14 landmarks on corresponding features (CA3 neuropil–tissue boundaries and cell body layers) in the μCT and CCF template volume (CCFv3). As with the CA3 EM-to-μCT transform, we packaged the landmarks and a thin plate spline transform into navis-mousebrains (see above).

Mapping Allen CCF to stereotactic coordinates

To map coordinates between Allen CCFv3 and stereotactic space, we used a 4D look-up matrix provided in a previous publication89 (ccfv3_orig_coords_all.nii.gz, downloaded from https://www.neuropedia.dk/resources). The CCFv3 template used in that publication differed in a few minor aspects from the one we used. First, we had to swap the order of axes from a lateral view (X, anterior→posterior; Y, dorsal→ventral; Z, medial–lateral) to a ventral view (X, medial–lateral; Y, anterior→posterior; Z, ventral→dorsal). Second, we had to add 1.75 mm (70 voxels at 25 μm resolution) on either end of the Y (anterior–posterior) axis. With these transformations, we were able to look up stereotactic coordinates for arbitrary points in CCF space through linear interpolation of the 4D matrix.

The EM volume’s bounding box was defined by two corner points. In EM space, the top-left-front, bottom-right-back and center coordinates are at (234.7, 300.5, 0 µm), (1409, 1445.8, 94.6 µm) and (821.6, 873.2, 47.1 µm). As a result of the registration, these map to (6,977, 3,131, 8,351 µm), (6,842, 1,758, 7,787 µm) and (6,902, 2,436, 8,073) in the Allen CCF and to (2.694, −1.466, 2.623 mm), (2.104, −1.456, 1.247 mm) and (2.415, −1.441, 1.901 mm) in stereotactic coordinates. Axes for stereotactic coordinates are X, medial–lateral; Y, anterior–posterior; and Z, dorsal–ventral.

Neuron classification

Excitatory and inhibitory neurons

Neocortical neurons can be distinguished from non-neurons largely by the size of the nucleus. In the current CA3 volume, the automated nucleus segmentation provides a list of all nuclei along with their sizes. The distribution of nucleus sizes shows several peaks that largely account for non-neuronal cells, inhibitory neurons and excitatory neurons (Fig. 1i). Cells from each peak were then used as candidates for further classification based on morphology. The classification of excitatory neurons (that is, pyramidal cells) and inhibitory neurons is based on specific features of pyramidal spines as well as their density. In general, the dendrites of pyramidal cells are densely decorated with spines (Extended Data Fig. 2a), while the dendrites of inhibitory cells are smooth. Although the hippocampus has been shown to have inhibitory cells that have spines90, their spines are typically sparser, thinner and longer than those found on excitatory neurons (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Inhibitory subtypes were further classified based on the number of output synapses in each layer (Extended Data Fig. 3c).

Pyramidal subtypes

Owing to limited range along the Z axis, different cellular components—such as the apical dendrite, nucleus (soma) and basal dendrite—are truncated to varying degrees, depending on the position of the cells within the dataset (Extended Data Fig. 3d). Therefore, different groups of thorny and sparsely thorny cells are selected for different analyses, depending on which components are fully contained within the volume.

In Fig. 2e, the goal is to enumerate all MF inputs of each pyramidal cell. Therefore, 636 ‘long apical pyramidal cells’ were selected to maximize the length of the proximal apical dendrite and are often truncated at the level of the soma or nuclei, with basal dendrites outside of the volume. The distribution of MF inputs to pyramidal cells is bimodal, with one peak near zero and another centered around 50 (Fig. 2e). A threshold of ten MF inputs—corresponding to the trough between the two peaks—was used to classify pyramidal cells as either thorny or sparsely thorny. In Fig. 2f–h, the goal was to analyze the relationship between nucleus size and the two subtypes. Therefore, a smaller subset of 240 pyramidal cells was selected that contained both relatively long apical dendrites and complete nuclei. In Fig. 6a, the goal was to analyze perisomatic inputs to pyramidal cells. Given that perisomatic, inhibitory inputs are distributed (Extended Data Fig. 9e,f), we tried to maximize the number of cells that have soma in the volume and can be identified as thorny or sparsely thorny (754 pyramidal cells). These cells were classified based on at least two of the following criteria: (1) if more than ten MF inputs were identified, the cell was classified as thorny; (2) a nucleus size threshold of 1,150 µm3, with small nuclei corresponding to sparsely thorny cells and large nuclei to thorny cells; (3) a combination of additional morphological features known to differ between thorny and sparsely thorny cells25,26 was used, including depth in the soma layer, size of the apical dendritic trunk, length of unbranched dendritic shaft and the complexity of basal dendrites (Extended Data Fig. 4d–f).

Laminar organization and spatial axes

We defined four layers in the dataset: stratum radiatum, stratum lucidum, stratum pyramidale and stratum oriens (Fig. 1f). The pyramidale was defined by the spatial distribution of pyramidal cell bodies, identified by spatial locations of the centroids of their nuclei. The lucidum was delineated by the presence of large MF–Pyr synapses, identified by thresholding aggregated synapse size at 10,000 voxels, with a voxel size of 18 × 18 × 45 nm3. We modeled the 3D curved layer boundary between the stratum lucidum and stratum pyramidale layers as a nonlinear decision boundary based on these two point distributions, using the support vector classification algorithm from the Python package scikit-learn and a nonlinear classifier with a radial basis function kernel. This nonlinear boundary effectively distinguished the spatial distributions of pyramidal cell nuclei (stratum pyramidale) and MF–Pyr synapses (stratum lucidum). The deeper region lacking pyramidal cell nuclei was defined as stratum oriens, while the superficial region lacking large MF–Pyr synapses was defined as stratum radiatum. We projected this 3D nonlinear boundary onto the XY plane to analyze the locations of the pyramidal cells along the proximodistal axis extending from the dentate gyrus side to the CA2 side. A quartic polynomial curve was fitted to the projected 2D boundary, mathematically defined by the following equation: y = 3.922 × 10−13x4 − 7.211 × 10−8x3 + 0.005003x2 − 155.2x + 1.869×106.

To quantify the spatial positions of pyramidal cell bodies, we defined two metrics: curve distance and depth. Curve distance refers to the distance along the fitted quartic polynomial curve, starting from the point of the curve that is nearest to the dentate gyrus. Curve distance was therefore used as a metric of proximodistal location. Each pyramidal cell nucleus was mapped to its nearest corresponding point on the polynomial curve. Depth was calculated as the Euclidean distance between each nucleus centroid and the closest point on the 3D boundary between the stratum lucidum and stratum pyramidale layers.

To quantify the angle between the apical dendritic shaft and the sectioning plane (that is, the Z plane), we first split the skeletons of pyramidal cells such that the thick apical shaft was isolated from the dendritic branches. For each pyramidal cell, we measured the angle of the shaft by finding the middle point of each shaft in Z and then fitting a single best-fit straight line through the middle point. The shaft’s tilt angle is the angle of the line relative to the flat XY plane. An average of angles for all pyramidal cells was calculated.

Identification of MF–Pyr cell connectivity

The MF inputs to pyramidal cells were identified based on the following criteria: localization within the stratum lucidum, envelopment of the postsynaptic structure by a large presynaptic bouton and the presence of two or more clustered thorny spines. During analysis of MF–Pyr synapses, we observed numerous contacts between MF terminals and the dendritic shafts of neighboring pyramidal cells. We considered some of these shaft contacts to be synaptic, as they displayed characteristic features such as docked synaptic vesicles, asymmetric postsynaptic densities and, in some cases, an absence of other plausible postsynaptic partners. However, we also noted that some shaft contacts probably represent a form of membrane specialization known as puncta adherentia1,5. To ensure that the analyzed connectivity reflects only MF inputs onto thorny spines, we manually inspected all MF–Pyr contacts (~2,700) that contributed to two or more shared MF inputs (Fig. 4a) and excluded any that did not meet thorny spine-based synaptic criteria.

In this work, active zones were distinguished by the following criteria that are previously established5: synaptic vesicles positioned near the presynaptic density, asymmetric pre- and postsynaptic densities and a widening of the synaptic cleft.

Bouton extraction and vesicle segmentation

We addressed the bouton extraction problem using a 3D mesh vertex classification method. First, given a MF axon, its postsynaptic pyramidal cell and the coordinates of synapses connecting these two cells, we constructed an initial bounding box around the synapse coordinates. This bounding box was subsequently enlarged by 1,000 voxels along the X and Y axes, and 400 voxels along the Z axis with a voxel size of 18 × 18 × 45 nm3. Within the enlarged bounding box, we loaded the 3D mesh representation of the MF axon including the bouton (Extended Data Fig. 6a). Each vertex of the mesh was classified based on local voxel density. For each vertex, we counted the number of neighboring vertices located within a radius of 2.5 μm. Owing to the varicosity of boutons, the distribution of vertex counts exhibited a bimodal distribution (Extended Data Fig. 6b). To determine the decision boundary for classification, we automatically identified a threshold at the minimum point between the two peaks in the bimodal distribution. Vertices with a neighbor count larger than the threshold were classified as bouton vertices (Extended Data Fig. 6c). The volume of each bouton was measured by taking the 3D convex hull of the bouton vertices and counting the number of segmentation voxels contained within the hull. Occasionally, a single MF forms multiple boutons targeting the same postsynaptic cell. If the MF axon has at least two synapses located more than 5 μm away from the centroid of all synapse coordinates, we concluded that the MF axon probably forms multiple boutons. In such cases, we determined the number of boutons by clustering the synapse coordinates in 3D space using k-means clustering (k = 2, 3 and 4). The best number of clusters was selected based on the k-means inertia score. For each synapse cluster, we constructed an initial bounding box around the cluster. If the synapse cluster contained fewer than 24 synapses, the bounding box was enlarged by 400 voxels along the X and Y axes and 160 voxels along the Z axis. Otherwise, the box was enlarged by 600 voxels along the X and Y axes and 240 voxels along the Z axis.

To demonstrate the validity of the bouton extraction, we applied it to segment boutons of various cases of the bouton shape, which include en passant, terminal, T-junction, small, medium, large, filopodial and double boutons (Extended Data Fig. 6d). The method segmented the bouton correctly in all 100 randomly selected test cases, excluding the filopodial extensions. Filopodial extensions have been reported to target GABAergic neurons34,91. Hence, we did not include extraction of filopodia in this work. The method does not apply deep learning-based techniques and can be scaled up to larger datasets with reasonable computational resources.

To segment individual vesicles from EM images, we applied the deep-learning-based cell segmentation tool called Cellpose92. Cellpose provides a pre-trained model for detecting cell boundaries, trained on extensive image datasets. Direct deployment of this model for segmenting individual vesicles was initially unsuccessful owing to differences in scale and morphology between cells and vesicles. Given that the cell diameter parameter significantly influences the segmentation accuracy, we enlarged the EM images using cubic interpolation using the resize function provided by Python’s OpenCV (cv2) package, such that synaptic vesicles have diameters around 20 pixels. Then, we fine-tuned the pre-trained Cellpose cytoplasm model and trained one additional model from scratch with 817 manually labeled synaptic vesicles (Extended Data Fig. 6e). The Cellpose software supported straightforward re-training through the PyTorch backend. We adopted a learning rate of 0.001 with a default decay strategy and momentum as implemented in Cellpose. The network could achieve the desired performance with 2,000 epochs of training on one NVIDIA GTX 1050 Ti GPU in about 2 h. We used another manually labeled set of 535 vesicles as a test set, and the accuracy of the segmentation was evaluated using precision, recall and F1 score, treating each vesicle segment as a distinct object. Objects smaller than 100 pixels were treated as dust and removed from accuracy measurements. Neuron segmentation was used as a mask to filter out any vesicle segments falling outside the neuron boundaries.

Identification of subcellular target locations of inhibitory axons

CAVE software, including CaveClient, MeshParty and Pcg_skel, was used85. Given an inhibitory axon, we first picked out all of its output synapses targeting pyramidal cells whose soma existed within the image volume. For each identified postsynaptic pyramidal cell, we generated an approximate skeleton representation. This skeletonization was accomplished by using the pcg_skel.pcg_skeleton function, whereby each level two chunk of the neuron was represented as a node and connected by edges to other level two chunks. The root_point parameter was set to the centroid of the pyramidal cell nucleus. To refine the skeleton for sufficiently high resolution, we applied linear interpolation of the skeleton nodes. The meshparty.skeleton_io.export_to_swc function was used, specifying a resample_spacing parameter of 1,000 and setting interp_kind to ‘linear’ to perform the interpolation. We then mapped each output synapse of the inhibitory axon to the nearest node of the refined skeleton based on Euclidean distance. To measure how far each synapse was located from the postsynaptic pyramidal cell body, we converted the skeleton into an undirected graph and used the networkx.dijkstra function to compute the path length from each synapse-mapped node along the skeleton edges to the centroid of the pyramidal cell nucleus.

Analysis of MF–Pyr connectivity

MF–Pyr connectivity was analyzed as a bipartite graph from MF axons to pyramidal cells. A total of 636 Pyr cells and all of their inputs (~25,200 MF axons) in the dataset were used to construct a bipartite graph, wherein only connections with three or more active zones and postsynaptic densities were included. To analyze MF–Pyr connectivity, we compared the reconstructed connectivity against two different null models, including a configuration model93 and an anatomically constrained model (10 µm or 50 µm radius models). In the MF–Pyr connectivity, MF out-degree is the number of target pyramidal cells, and the pyramidal in-degree is the number of presynaptic MF axons. The configuration model preserves both in-degrees and out-degrees but randomizes the partners93. In the radius models, each bouton was randomly connected to the dendritic spines (TEs) within a given radius (10 µm and 50 µm). MF boutons and pyramidal cell TEs were represented by the centroids of the MF–Pyr synapse coordinates. We computed pairwise Euclidean distances between all MF boutons and TEs. Temporary edges were assigned between bouton–TE pairs only if their separation was within the radius, ensuring zero connection probability beyond this radius. Within the radius, the probability of connection was uniform. The list containing these temporary edges was shuffled to randomize their order, and then each edge was evaluated sequentially. An edge was accepted only if neither the bouton nor the TE had already been connected by a previously accepted edge. Following this initial iteration, a subsequent iteration was performed to ensure that every bouton was associated with an accepted edge, preserving all MF boutons to have connections. For the configuration and radius models, 100 randomized models were sampled.

We quantified the occurrence of all bipartite motifs involving three and four neurons (Fig. 4d). The three-neuron motifs include fan-in and fan-out structures. The four-neuron motifs include fan-in, fan-out, path-of-length-three and path-of-length-four (also known as the butterfly motif). Statistics and reproducibility

To assess the statistical significance of the difference in MF input number between proximal and distal pyramidal cells, normality of the proximal and distal distributions in Fig. 3c was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk (scipy.stats.shapiro) and D’Agostino–Pearson (scipy.stats.normaltest) tests in Python. Both tests rejected the null hypothesis of normality. The distributions were then compared using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-test. The test indicated a significant difference between proximal and distal distributions (P = 1.7 × 10⁻4). A one-sided Mann–Whitney U-test further confirmed that the distal distribution was significantly larger than the proximal distribution (P = 8.0 × 10⁻5).

To examine whether the shared MF input abundance is statistically higher than chance, P values were computed for each shared MF count (k = 2,3,…) by comparing the observed counts against the null distributions. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, implemented using the statsmodels.stats.multitest Python module, was applied to adjust the P values across all k values to account for multiple comparisons. EM data were acquired from one imaged animal. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. During analysis of connectivity, whenever statistical tests were applied, distributions were assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. Randomization was used in the construction of connectivity models (radius and configuration models). Investigators were not blinded during these analyses.

All micrographs were from a large-scale EM volume that was publicly released as part of this paper (see Data Availability and links to interactive visualizations in Supplementary Table 1). As such, no independent repetitions of EM imaging were performed. Statistical analyses are based on independently sampled units (cells, boutons or synapses) within the reconstructed volume, rather than experimental replicates across preparations.
