Optimisation and validation of capture mNGS for predicting antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Qiao Lin, Xu Mei, Huimin Zheng, Jie Meng, Fusheng He, Bin Yang, Xiao Ru, Mengting Su, Danlan Wang, Ni Tan, Junyue Fang, Sha Fu, Nengtai Ouyang, Zhengfei Yang, ShanPing Jiang, Yin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntibiotic resistance critically compromises bacterial infection treatment. While antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) remains the standard for resistance assessment, its culture dependence is time-consuming. Clinical metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) offers rapid pathogen detection and antibiotic resistance gene (ARG) profiling. However, low ARG detection sensitivity and unclear genotype-phenotype correlations limit its clinical utility.MethodsWe developed capture mNGS approach with probe-based ARG enrichment and a host-attribution algorithm for precise ARG-bacteria linkage. Its ARG detection sensitivity was comparatively analysed against standard mNGS. Using phenotypic AST as reference, we then evaluated the clinical predictive value of capture mNGS-detected ARGs in a retrospective cohort from Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSMH) and an external cohort from Liuzhou Worker‵s Hospital (LWH). In addition, a prospective cohort from SYSMH was used to explore the clinical utility of ARG detection by mNGS.FindingsCompared to standard mNGS, capture mNGS significantly enhanced ARG detection sensitivity, achieving a 44-fold increase in sequencing depth. In our retrospective cohort, key resistance genes detected by capture mNGS accurately predicted phenotypic resistance: blaCTX-M achieved a sensitivity of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.86, 1.00) and specificity of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.59, 1.00) for ceftriaxone resistance prediction, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.93 (95% CI: 0.87, 0.99). BlaKPC demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.73, 1.00) and specificity of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.95, 1.00) for carbapenem resistance (AUC = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.92, 1.00). Similarly, blaOXA-23 exhibited a sensitivity of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.82, 0.99) and specificity of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.69, 1.00) for carbapenem resistance (AUC = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94, 1.00), which was externally validated in the LWH cohort. In addition, mecA showed a sensitivity of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.71, 1.00) and specificity of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.81, 0.99) for oxacillin resistance (AUC = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.87, 1.00). Whereas blaTEM/blaSHV showed higher false-positive rates for cephalosporin resistance and ErmB/ErmC showed lower sensitivity (0.6, 95% CI: 0.32, 0.84) for macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS) resistance. Capture mNGS reported results (median turnaround time (TAT): 24.71 h (IQR 22.74–41.00)) were shorter than AST (median TAT: 73.16 h (IQR 54.19–93.42)). In a prospective cohort, the time to guide antibiotic therapy based on reported positive ARGs was significantly shorter than that based on reported resistant phenotypes from AST.InterpretationThese results highlight that ARGs can be leveraged to rapidly and accurately predict bacterial resistance phenotypes with high sensitivity and specificity, thereby guiding antibiotic management in clinical practice.FundingThe National Natural Science Foundation of China, the Guangdong Science and Technology Department, Science and Technology Projects in Guangzhou.Keywords: Antibiotic resistance genes, mNGS, Bacteria, Infectious disease, Antibiotic susceptibility testing

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Clinical mNGS enables rapid and unbiased detection of pathogens and their associated ARGs. However, the complex relationship between ARG detection and resistance phenotypes remains poorly characterised in real-world clinical practice. We searched PubMed (2016–2025) using the terms “metagenomic and resistance” and “metagenomic next-generation sequencing and resistance,” identifying thirteen studies reporting ARG detection via clinical mNGS. Among them, six studies analysed ARGs in single bacterial species. Three notable investigations compared mNGS-derived ARGs with AST: one in Gram-negative bacteria (ARG-positive samples: n = 12), one in carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (ARG-positive samples: n = 46) and another in children (ARG-positive samples <55). However, neither evaluated the predictive performance of individual ARGs for resistance phenotypes. A study assessed ARG-phenotype concordance in total 27 samples but was underpowered. Collectively, these reveal a critical gap: no large-scale evidence exists to support mNGS-based ARG profiling for predicting clinical antimicrobial resistance.

Added value of this study

Clinical mNGS has limited sequencing depth, which may compromise the sensitivity of ARG detection. To address this limitation, we developed a probe-based capture mNGS approach to selectively enrich ARGs. Compared to traditional mNGS, capture mNGS achieved a 44-fold increase in sequencing depth and a twofold increase in the number of samples with detectable ARGs. We conducted a retrospective cohort study involving 2922 samples to evaluate capture mNGS-derived ARGs against phenotypic AST, without restrictions on sample type or patient population. Key ARG-phenotype predictions were validated: blaCTX for third-generation cephalosporin resistance; blaKPC and blaOXA-23 for carbapenem resistance; mecA for oxacillin resistance; and vanA for glycopeptide resistance. This represents the largest cohort to date comprehensively defining the clinical utility of mNGS-detected ARGs in predicting antibiotic resistance.

Implications of all the available evidence

Capture mNGS enables high-accuracy prediction of clinically relevant antibiotic resistance phenotypes from diverse samples. Integrating pathogen and ARG detection within a single, rapid assay (a median TAT of 24 h) could transform infection management—accelerating targeted therapy, optimising antibiotic stewardship, and improving outcomes in critically ill patients.

Introduction

Over the past century, many antibiotics have been discovered to help combat bacterial infections.1 However, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) remains a critical global health concern, with clinically notable examples including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA),2 carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE),3 carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRABA),4 and multi-drug resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa.5 It has been reported that AMR account for more than 2.8 million infections and over 35,000 deaths annually in the U.S.6 In China, AMR has been associated with approximately 600,000 deaths, including 145,000 deaths directly attributable to AMR in 2019.7

Appropriate antibiotic treatment is essential not only for effectively controlling bacterial infections but also for minimising antibiotic misuse, which prevents the development of resistance.8 Precision medicine plays a key role in this process, relying on rapid and accurate diagnostic tools to identify the causative pathogens and determine the most effective antibiotic therapy. The expression of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) is a major contributor to AMR, and their identification can provide critical insights for optimising antibiotic treatment. Due to its unbiased and comprehensive detection capabilities, along with a rapid turnaround time, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has emerged as a promising tool for detecting both pathogens and ARGs, offering significant potential to precise antibiotic treatment.9,10

Although mNGS is widely employed for pathogen identification, its application in ARGs detection for clinical antibiotic guidance is still in its infancy. While several studies have reported ARGs detection via mNGS,9,11, 12, 13 they primarily focus on specific bacterial species or involve only limited sample sizes (a few dozen cases). Comprehensive profiling of ARGs and evaluation of their clinical utility in large-scale datasets represent both the most meaningful and the most challenging.

Three main challenges hinder the clinical adoption of mNGS for ARGs detection. First, ARGs are much smaller in size compared to bacterial genomes, resulting in limited sequencing depth and coverage, which compromises the sensitivity of ARG detection. Second, the widespread occurrence of horizontal gene transfer among ARGs further complicates their accurate identification in clinically complex bacterial infections. This challenge is particularly pronounced in scenarios involving polymicrobial infections or multidrug resistance. Third, the relationship between genotypic and phenotypic antibiotic resistance is complex and influenced by various factors, including ARG expression levels, functional integrity, environmental pressures, and the synergistic effects of other resistance mechanisms.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 Consequently, the consistency between ARGs detection by mNGS and actual resistance phenotypes in clinical practice remains unclear.

In this study, we established capture mNGS, incorporating probes targeting common ARGs to enrich the ARG fragments in the library and increase their detection rates. Moreover, we developed a novel assignment algorithm to accurately link detected ARGs to their putative host. Compared to traditional mNGS (non-capture mNGS), capture mNGS demonstrated a more than 44 times depth in ARG detection. To evaluate the clinical utility of captured mNGS, we conducted a retrospective cohort from Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (SYSMH) involving 2922 clinical samples from 2112 patients, 764 samples from 616 patients detected ARG-associated bacteria, among them 416 samples underwent both capture mNGS and conventional culture-based antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). Our results demonstrated that ARGs exhibited high predictive performance for phenotypic antibiotic resistance, similar trends were also observed in an external retrospective cohort including 780 samples of 693 patients from Liuzhou Worker's Hospital (LWH). In addition, a major advantage of capture mNGS is its rapid reporting capability, which turnaround times (TATs) significantly shorter than that of AST. In a prospective cohort, we further validated that the time to antibiotic therapy guided by ARG detection was shorter than that guided by AST. Overall, our study demonstrated that capture mNGS significantly enhanced the efficiency of ARG detection, and several ARGs showed high concordance with bacterial resistance phenotypes. Given its capability to rapidly identify pathogens and ARGs, particularly in intensive care unit (ICU) settings, this approach holds strong potential to optimise antibiotic selection, improve patient outcomes, and mitigate AMR through stewardship.

Methods

Patient enrolment and data collection

From January 2022 to October 2024, patients suspected of having infectious diseases were screened for the mNGS test at SYSMH in Guangdong, China. This study was divided into two phases. In the first phase, remaining clinical samples from patients between January 2022 and June 2023 were subjected to both capture mNGS and non-capture mNGS for performance comparison. The inclusion criterion for this phase was as follows: both ARG-related pathogen and ARGs were detected in mNGS. In the second phase, samples from patients between July 2023 and October 2024 were included in a retrospective cohort. The inclusion criterion for this phase was as follows: ARG-related bacteria were identified as pathogens in the mNGS reports. The exclusion criteria was: pathogen reads <100. To externally validate the findings, an independent retrospective cohort was established at LWH in Guangxi, China. From May 2024 to September 2025, patients undergoing mNGS testing at LWH were consecutively enrolled. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the LWH cohort were identical to those applied at SYSMH.

For participants in both retrospective cohorts, we collected the clinical data of patients, including demographic characteristics, laboratory bacterial cultures and AST. Patient demographic information, including age and sex, was collected from medical records, sample types and bacterial species were obtained from both mNGS and AST reports. Only AST results corresponding to the same specimen type as mNGS and the same bacterial species identified by both culture and mNGS were included in this study. These detailed exclusion criteria and workflow are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Finally, a prospective cohort was established at SYSMH. From October 30, 2025 to December 22, 2025, only patients at ICU with either ARG positive by mNGS or phenotypic resistance identified by AST were enrolled. Specifically, mNGS reports were required to identify ARG-associated bacteria together with corresponding ARGs, while AST reports were required to demonstrate resistant phenotypes in ARG-associated bacteria. Clinical data, including diagnoses, treatments, and corresponding mNGS/AST reports, were prospectively collected. Antibiotic prescriptions and their underlying rationale were categorised based on detailed review of the patients’ medical records.

Probes design

Probes targeting microbial genomes and ARGs were designed using the HUBDesign pipeline19 with default parameters. Briefly, microbial genomes were obtained from NCBI RefSeq20 or GenBank,21 and ARG sequences were retrieved from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD).22 ARGs that are widely distributed, associated with well-established resistance mechanisms, and of clear clinical relevance14,23, 24, 25 were selected, which listed in Table 1. In the clustering phase, gene clusters with a minimum penetrance of 95% within a clade (defined as a specific genus or ARG class) were selected. Representative sequences with a minimum of 85% conservation were generated and added to pseudo-genome. In the identifying phase, up to 100 candidate 100 bp regions were selected per pseudo-genome, and 75 bp sub-sequences within these regions were generated as candidate probes. In the filtering phase, candidate probes with ≥75% identity to the human genome (GRCh38) and alignment lengths exceeding 20 bp were excluded using BLASTn.26 Finally, remaining probe regions were tiled with probes at 5 bp intervals.

Specimen collection and processing

Clinical specimens included blood, tissue, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), sputum, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), pleural fluid, synovial fluid, pyogenic fluids and urine, etc. All clinical samples were collected following standardised procedures. Blood samples were centrifuged at 1500 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C and the isolated plasma were used for DNA extraction and library construction. Urine samples was centrifuged at 1600 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the precipitate was resuspended in 1 ml of supernatant. Tissue samples were homogenised using homogeniser (TIANGEN, OSE-TH-02). For BALF, sputum, tissue, and urine samples, human DNA was removed according to the protocol described by Hasan et al.27 Briefly, samples were mixed with saponin (Sigma, 47036) with a final concentration of 0.1% and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Then it was added with Turbo DNase (Thermo Fisher, AM2238) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min.

Library construction

DNA was extracted from both collected samples and processing samples after human DNA removing using the QIAamp® UCP Pathogen DNA Kit (Qiagen, 50214). DNA libraries were constructed using the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, FC-131-1096) according to the manufacturer's protocol. DNA libraries underwent probe hybridisation and enrichment prior to sequencing for capture mNGS while DNA libraries of non-capture mNGS was subjected to sequence.

Hybrid capture of sequencing libraries

Probe hybridisation and capture were performed according to the protocol described by Metsky et al.28 Briefly, DNA libraries were co-incubated with biotinylated single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) probes at room temperature for 5 min. Following probe hybridisation, the DNA libraries were enriched and purified using streptavidin-coated beads (ThermoFisher, 88817). DNA libraries of both capture and non-capture mNGS were sequenced on Illumina NextSeq 550Dx platform, generating 40-bp single-end reads.

Quality control and removing human reads

The raw sequencing data were processed. First, Fastp (v0.23.2)29 was used to remove adaptors and filter reads, applying the following parameters: -q 30 --thread = 5. Next, human genomic reads were removed using Bowtie 230 with reference genome (hg38). The following parameters were applied: -p 10 --un-gz.

Microbial annotation

Taxonomic annotations were generated from the filtered reads, excluding the host genome, using Kraken 2 (v2.1.2)31 with default parameter. The PlusPF database (k2_pluspf_20230605), which include RefSeq genomes for archaea, bacteria, viral, plasmid, human, UniVec_Core, protozoa and fungik, was used for annotation and quantification.

ARG annotation

The CARD database22 was used as ARGs reference database. ARGs were annotated using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) tool (https://github.com/arpcard/rg)22 with default parameters. The ARGs were classified manually based on antibiotics to which they confer resistance. In this study, ARGs referring to penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems and monobactam were grouped into beta-lactam class. ARGs referring to macrolides and lincosamides were grouped into macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin (MLS) class.

ARG assignment algorithm

To identify potential host associations between ARGs and bacteria, we developed a computational workflow integrating Gaussian mixture modelling (GMM) and robust statistical validation.32 The workflow was presented using an example of Klebsiella pneumoniae, shown in Supplementary Figure S1. The analysis began with curated ARG-bacteria pairs derived from the CARD database,22 coupled with sample-specific abundance profiles for ARGs and bacterial taxa. For each candidate ARG-bacteria pair, log-transformed abundance ratios (ARG reads/bacterial reads) were modelled via GMM and optimised through expectation-maximisation (EM) to resolve multimodal distributions and identify biologically plausible clusters characterised by sufficient sample size (≥10 samples/cluster), adequate bacterial abundance (median ≥1000 reads), and significant linear correlations (Pearson/Spearman r ≥ 0.6, p ≤ 0.05) validated via robust regression (lmrob function from the robustbase R package). Observed median ratios were further constrained to <50-fold deviations from theoretical values (Ratio = ARG size/bacterial genome size, Supplementary Figure S1A) to filter implausible associations, while dynamic threshold adjustments enhanced specificity for taxa with atypical ratio ranges. Final associations were visualised through paired log–log scatterplots (ARG versus bacterial abundance), annotated with regression slopes, theoretical expectations, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with statistically validated pairs exported alongside comprehensive metrics for downstream analysis (Supplementary Figure S1B–D, Supplementary Table S1). This approach balanced sensitivity to multimodal data structures and robustness against outliers, providing a reproducible framework for deciphering ARG-host dynamics in complex microbial communities. R packages mclust (v6.1.1), robustbase (v0.99) and MASS (v7.3) were used for statistical analysis and modelling.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Remaining samples from retrospective cohort were used to validate ARG using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). A total of 32 additional clinical samples were collected, in which the detected pathogens included K. pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, A. baumannii and S. aureus. Based on the ARGs associated with these pathogens and their clinical significance, qPCR primer was specifically designed to detect blaSHV, blaTEM, blaCTX-M, blaKPC, blaOXA-23 and mecA. DNA extraction was same as capture mNGS. Then, qPCR was performed on the extracted DNA using the PowerTrack™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, A46109) according to the instructions of manufacturer. The reaction master mix, including 1 × PowerTrack™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix and primer mix, was prepared and dispensed into a 96-well plate for amplification on the Applied Biosystems® 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCR amplification was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. The thermocycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 8 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. A cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 35 was defined as positive for ARG detection. qPCR primers were listed in Table 2.

Results

Study overview

In this study, a total of 3137 clinical samples that underwent mNGS at SYSMH, Guangdong Province, China, were included. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the study was conducted in two phases: a technical evaluation phase and a retrospective cohort study phase.

The first phase, conducted from January 2022 to June 2023, included 215 samples in which ARGs were detected by either capture or non-capture mNGS. This phase was designed to assess the efficiency of ARG enrichment achieved by probe-based capture mNGS.

In the second phase, we evaluate resistance prediction performance in a real-world clinical setting. A retrospective cohort was conducted on 2922 samples obtained from 2112 patients who underwent capture mNGS testing (Table 3). Of these, 2158 samples were excluded for the following reasons: negative mNGS reports (n = 912), no bacterial pathogens identified in mNGS (n = 798), detection of bacteria not included in the ARG analysis panel (n = 300), or detection of ARG-associated bacteria with species-specific microbial read numbers (SMRN) less than 100 (n = 148). To assess the concordance between ARGs detected by mNGS and bacterial antibiotic-resistant phenotypes, AST results were collected for 764 samples in which ARG-associated bacteria were identified by mNGS. Among these, 348 samples were excluded due to negative culture results, inconsistency between cultured and mNGS-reported bacteria, or discrepancies in sample types between mNGS and AST. Ultimately, 416 samples were used to analyse the correlation between ARGs and antibiotic-resistant phenotypes. For the enrolled 764 samples, age, sex, sample types and bacterial species did not differ between samples with and without available AST results (Table 3).

Enhancing the detection efficiency of ARGs via by capture mNGS

Capture mNGS enabled targeted enrichment of pathogens of interest while simultaneously allowing broad, untargeted detection of pathogens outside the probe panel.33 Compared with non-capture mNGS, capture mNGS is more sensitive than non-capture mNGS in detecting pathogens.28,34,35 To improve the detection rates of ARGs, we developed a probe-based capture mNGS method that targeted and enriched ARG DNA (Fig. 2A). Moreover, we constructed a GMM model to resolve the host assignment of ARGs (Supplementary Figure S1B–C). To compare the detecting sensitivity of ARGs between non-capture and capture mNGS, we analysed 215 clinical samples using both methods (Fig. 2B). Notably, ARG reads had a median of 17.8 reads per million mapped reads (RPM) in the capture mNGS significantly higher than the median of 0.4 RPM observed in the non-capture mNGS, indicating that capture mNGS substantially improved the sequence depth of ARGs comparing to non-capture mNGS. Indeed, capture mNGS enabled the detection of ARGs in a significantly higher proportion of samples compared to non-capture mNGS, with ARGs identified in approximately 98% (211/215) of samples by capture mNGS, versus 42% (90/215) by non-capture mNGS (Fig. 2C). In addition, we analysed the number of ARGs detected by the two methods per sample (Fig. 2D). The number of ARG type detected by capture mNGS in 73.5% (158/215) samples was higher than that detected by non-capture mNGS, suggesting that capture mNGS is more sensitive for ARG detection. When comparing the detection of various ARG types between the two methods, capture mNGS consistently identified more ARGs than non-capture mNGS, with exception of blaGES and mefE (Fig. 2E). In particular, ErmB was detected in 55 samples using capture mNGS, approximately 4.6 times more than the 11 samples detected using non-capture mNGS. These results indicated that probe-based enrichment technology significantly enhances the sensitivity of mNGS for detecting ARGs, both in terms of the number of ARG types identified and the sequencing depth, thereby improving its reliability for use in complex clinical samples.

Prevalence of ARGs identified by capture mNGS

Next, we explored the ARGs detected by capture mNGS in a retrospective cohort study (Fig. 1). Among the 716 samples in which ARG-associated bacteria were identified and classified as pathogens, 720 ARGs were detected in 406 samples (Supplementary Figure S2). Notably, approximately 49.3% of these ARG-positive samples harboured multiple ARGs (Supplementary Figure S2). The detection rates of ARGs varied among different sample types, with BALF, sputum, and sterile body fluids (including plasma, CSF, pleural effusion, and ascites) showing detection rates above 50% (Fig. 3A). Among all identified ARGs, β-lactamase genes were the most prevalent (Fig. 3B). The prevalence of ARGs in K. pneumoniae, E. coli, A. baumannii, S. aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae exceeded 64% (Fig. 3C). Additionally, we analysed the distribution of ARGs within these bacteria (Fig. 3D). The presence of β-lactamase gene varied significantly among species. While Haemophilus influenzae and Proteus mirabilis exclusively carried extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-related genes, K. pneumoniae, E. coli, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, Enterobacter cloacae, and Serratia marcescens harboured both ESBL and carbapenemase-related ARGs (Fig. 3D). In A. baumannii, blaOXA-23 and blaTEM were the most frequently detected ARGs (Fig. 3D). Within the MLS resistance genes, ErmB was the most prevalent in S. aureus and S. pneumoniae (Fig. 3D). Moreover, MRSA -associated gene mecA was frequently detected in S. aureus (Fig. 3D). For glycopeptide resistance genes, both Enterococcus exhibited similar patterns, with vanA and vanM being the most common (Fig. 3D). Overall, the distribution of ARGs varied significantly across bacteria at the species level.

Comparison of AMR between mNGS and AST

To investigate the association between ARGs and bacterial resistance phenotypes, we compared capture mNGS results with AST results from 416 samples (Fig. 4A). Among these, 57.45% (n = 239) exhibited concordance between ARG detection and AST results. Discrepancies were observed in 22.6% (n = 94) of cases where ARGs were detected but AST indicated susceptibility, and in 19.95% (n = 83) of cases that no ARGs were detected but AST revealed resistance.

Bacterial genotypes and corresponding resistant phenotypes in each sample were displayed according to antibiotic classification (Fig. 4B–E). For third-generation cephalosporins, the presence of blaCTX-M alone or in combination with other ESBL genes was the primary driver of resistance in Enterobacteriacea (Fig. 4B). Specifically, blaCTX-M was detected in 61 of 65 Enterobacteriaceae isolates exhibiting resistance to ceftazidime and/or ceftriaxone (Fig. 4B). In contrast, the presence of blaSHV and blaTEM (90 of 146 bacteria isolates), either alone or in combination, showed a poor correlation with resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (Fig. 4B). Nevertheless, 29 of 33 blaTEM-positive A. baumanni isolates showed ceftazidime resistance (Fig. 4B). For carbapenems, resistance was predominantly mediated by blaOXA-23 and blaKPC (Fig. 4C). In A. baumannii, blaOXA-23 was detected in 36 of 39 carbapenem-resistant strains (Fig. 4C). While most Enterobacteriaceae isolates remained susceptible to carbapenems, 26 K. pneumoniae isolates exhibited carbapenem resistance, primarily associated with the presence of blaKPC (18 isolates) (Fig. 4C). Despite lacking detectable carbapenemase-related ARGs, 53.2% (58/109) P. aeruginosa isolates displayed phenotypic resistance to carbapenems. Regarding glycopeptide antibiotics, vanA was identified as the key resistance determinant in Enterococcus faecium, being present in 10 of 14 strains resistant to vancomycin and teicoplanin (14 isolates) (Fig. 4D). In MLS class, mecA, either alone or in combination with ErmB or ErmC, was detected in 16 of 22 S. aureus isolates exhibiting MLS resistance (Fig. 4E). Additionally, ErmB was identified in all five MLS-resistant S. pneumoniae isolates (Fig. 4E).

Furthermore, 32 samples were randomly selected for validation using qPCR. The concordance rates between capture mNGS and qPCR with AST were comparable, with only minor differences observed (Supplementary Figure S3). These findings highlighted the species-specific and gene-dependent nature of genotype-phenotype relationships in clinically relevant pathogens.

Evaluation of ARGs to predict bacterial resistant phenotype

To investigate whether ARGs could guide clinical antibiotic usage, we evaluated its performance using AST as the reference standard. As detailed in Supplementary Figure S4, we compared the classification outcomes of ARG detection with AST results across multiple bacteria. To ensure robustness, only ARGs that detected in at least 10 samples were included in the analysis. Based on this criterion, blaCTX-M, blaTEM, blaSHV, blaKPC, blaOXA-23, mecA, ErmB, ErmC, and vanA were selected for evaluation.

We first calculated positive predictive values (PPVs) for individual ARGs (Fig. 5A). Notably, blaOXA-23 and vanA consistently achieved a PPV of 100% across multiple antibiotics, indicating a strong correlation between their presence and phenotypic resistance (Fig. 5A). BlaCTX-M, blaKPC and mecA showed high PPVs for third-generation cephalosporins, carbapenem and oxacillin, respectively (Fig. 5A). The PPVs of individual ARGs differed across sample types (Supplementary Figure S5A). Among these, blaTEM detected in plasma showed a higher PPV for third-generation cephalosporin resistance, whereas ErmB/ErmC identified in plasma and tissue exhibited higher PPVs for MLS resistance compared with BALF.

To investigate the influence of sequencing depth on ARG prediction performance, we compared the full set of samples against the subset with SMRN >100. When all samples were included, the sensitivity and specificity of ARG-based resistance prediction were lower compared to the analysis restricted to samples with SMRN >100 (Supplementary Figure S6). This suggests that insufficient bacterial read depth compromises the accuracy of resistance inference.

We further conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, the areas under the ROC curves (AUC) for blaCTX-M, blaKPC, blaOXA-23, mecA, and vanA exceeded 0.8, with blaKPC, blaOXA-23, and mecA achieving AUCs greater than 0.9 in their corresponding antibiotic categories (Fig. 5C–G). These results highlighted the strong predictive capabilities of these ARGs in distinguishing between resistant and susceptible isolates as defined by AST.

To validate these findings, an independent external retrospective cohort from LWH was included (Table 3). Using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, 780 samples from 693 patients were enrolled to evaluate the predictive performance of ARGs (Table 3). This included 59 samples (from 54 patients) which an ARG-associated bacterium was detected by mNGS and a corresponding AST result for that same bacterium was available (Table 3). Although the sample size is relatively small, similar trends to the SYSMH cohort were observed (Supplementary Figure S7). Notably, blaOXA-23 achieved a PPV of 100%, with a sensitivity of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.62, 1.00) and a specificity of 1.00 (95% CI: 0.29, 1.00) for carbapenem resistance (Supplementary Figure S7A). Similarly, blaKPC demonstrated consistent predictive performance for carbapenem resistance (Supplementary Figure S7B). Overall, our findings demonstrated that blaCTX-M, blaKPC, blaOXA-23, mecA and vanA exhibited superior predictive performance for bacterial antibiotic resistance phenotypes, underscoring their potential clinical utility in guiding antibiotic therapy.

Clinical utility of ARG detection in guiding antibiotic decision-making

Compared with AST, mNGS offers the advantages of speed and comprehensive pathogen detection. To evaluate the difference in TATs from specimen collection to final report, we compared capture mNGS with AST across 416 samples. The median TAT for capture mNGS was significantly shorter at 24.71 h (IQR: 22.74–41.00), compared to 73.16 h (IQR: 54.19–93.42) for AST (Fig. 6A). This rapid reporting enables timely and targeted antibiotic therapy through accurate prediction of antimicrobial resistance phenotypes based on detected ARGs, which is particularly critical for managing infections in critically ill patients.

We reported a case in which ARG results informed an adjustment in antibiotic treatment for a patient with an acute pancreastitis. A 46-year-old man presented with fever and abdominal pain for over a month. Computed tomography (CT) imaging revealed acute necrotic fluid accumulation around the pancreas and in the right abdominal cavity, along with encapsulated fluid and gas, suggestive of acute necrotising pancreatitis (Fig. 6B). The patient was admitted and initially treated with piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 h). Abdominal drainage fluid was collected for mNGS. On the third day, mNGS identified E. coli and detected the blaCTX-M gene, indicating potential resistance to third-generation cephalosporins. Based on the mNGS results, the antibiotic regimen was adjusted to ertapenem (1 g every day). After 12 days of treatment, the patient experienced significant symptom relief, with resolution of fever and abdominal pain. Follow-up CT scans showed a reduction in the acute necrotic fluid accumulation in the peripancreatic and right abdominal areas, as well as a decrease in encapsulated fluid and gas, indicating resolution of the infection (Fig. 6C). This case demonstrated that adjusting antibiotic treatment based on mNGS results could effectively control bacterial infections and highlights the clinical value of ARG detection in precision medicine, leading to improved patient outcomes in bacterial infections.

Furthermore, we conducted a small prospective cohort study to evaluate the clinical utility of ARG detection by capture mNGS in antibiotic management. Patients in ICU who underwent capture mNGS testing with detected ARGs or received AST showing phenotypic resistance between October 28, 2025, and December 22, 2025, were enrolled (Fig. 6D and Table 5). In total, 92 ICU patients contributing 106 mNGS/AST reports were included in the analysis (Fig. 6D and Table 5). Changes in antibiotic prescriptions before and after the availability of mNGS or AST reports were systematically assessed. Both ARG and AST results were actively incorporated into clinical decision-making (Fig. 6E and Supplementary Figure S8). Consistent with the TAT trends observed in the retrospective analysis, ARG-guided management was associated with a significantly shorter time to precision antibiotic therapy compared with AST-guided management (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that capture mNGS–based ARG detection can facilitate earlier optimisation of antimicrobial therapy and support more timely, precision-guided antibiotic use in critically ill patients.

Discussion

In this study, we developed capture mNGS to enhance the detection rate of ARGs and comprehensively compared these ARGs with AST results to evaluate their clinical value in guiding antibiotic therapy. First, we demonstrated that capture mNGS, which employs DNA probes to target ARGs, significantly improved the detection of ARGs, achieving a 44-fold increase in read depth and more than twice the positive detection rate of ARGs compared to non-capture mNGS. Second, we scanned 2922 laboratory test results in a retrospective cohort and found that several ARGs exhibited high concordance with antibiotic resistance phenotypes. Similar trends were observed in an independent external retrospective cohort from LWH, underscoring their predictive value for resistance. Finally, the median TAT of capture mNGS was shorter than that of AST and it was verified in a prospective cohort, highlighting its advantage in rapidly obtaining both pathogen and antimicrobial resistance information. Overall, capture mNGS proved to be highly sensitive in detecting ARGs and held promise for guiding antibiotic use in clinical practice.

For clinicians, it is essential to rapidly identify infectious pathogens and assess antibiotic resistance for timely and accurate treatment decisions. Given that, extensive research has focused on using ARGs to predict bacterial resistance phenotype.36, 37, 38, 39 However, due to the complexity of gene expression and the phenotypic synergy among multiple genes, ARG presence alone does not always correlate precisely with phenotypic resistance.40,41 To address this issue, several studies have leveraged whole-genome sequencing (WGS)-derived ARG data to develop predictive models for bacterial resistance phenotypes.39,42 While these models exhibit high predictive accuracy, WGS-based technologies have limitations, including high costs, low culture positive rates, and prolonged turnaround times, making them difficult to implement in clinical practice. Additionally, WGS-based resistance models targeting specific bacterial ARGs require prior pathogen identification, further constraining their utility in real-time diagnostics. In contrast, mNGS offers high throughput, broad-spectrum pathogen detection, and superior sensitivity, making it a powerful tool for infectious disease diagnostics. Therefore, integrating ARG detection into mNGS workflows enhances its potential for predicting bacterial resistance phenotypes, thereby facilitating more precise and timely antibiotic selection in clinical settings.

However, many ARGs are located on plasmids or mobile genetic elements,43,44 making it challenges for accurate detection via mNGS. A major challenge of mNGS is its difficulty in determining the bacterial that an ARG affiliates. On the other hand, ARGs are much smaller than the microbial genome, and low-copy ARGs are susceptible to miss detection due to low sequence depth of mNGS. Increasing pathogen coverage can partially mitigate this issue through higher sequencing depth,45 host nucleic acid depletion to enhance effective microbial sequencing depth,27,46 and rigorous mNGS quality control measures, including enforcement of minimal sequencing output thresholds and exclusion of low-quality libraries. However, mNGS are typically performed at relatively modest sequencing depths to balance cost and turnaround time in routine clinical practice. To address these challenges, we implemented the following optimisations: (1) We adopted capture mNGS, a targeted enrichment technique that utilised probes to enrich ARG fragments in the library, significantly improving ARG detection sensitivity (Fig. 2A–B). (2) Sequencing is a random sampling process, detected ARGs may originate not only from pathogenic bacteria but also from colonising or commensal microbes.47 Theoretically, the ratio of bacterial to ARG reads detected by mNGS should be consistent with the size ratio of the two. To accurately assign ARGs to their affiliated pathogens, we inferred the likely pathogen host using GMM method (Supplementary Figure S1). (3) A retrospective study was conducted to evaluate the predictive performance of capture mNGS-based ARGs in bacterial resistance phenotypes (Fig. 1). These optimisations improved the sensitivity and accuracy of mNGS-based ARG detection, enhancing its potential for predicting bacterial resistance phenotypes.

In this retrospective study, we evaluated 17 ARGs, with 14 genes compared against AST as the reference standard. Our results demonstrated that blaCTX-M, blaKPC, blaOXA-23, mecA, and vanA exhibited superior predictive performance, indicating a strong correlation with phenotypic resistance. In contrast, the remaining ARGs showed relatively weak associations with bacterial resistance phenotypes. Although blaTEM and blaSHV were the most frequently detected ARGs in our study, they displayed high false-positive rates when predicting resistance to third-generation cephalosporins (Fig. 5A–B, Table 4). A previous study investigating the impact of major outer membrane porin loss in K. pneumoniae demonstrated that the absence of OmpK35 and OmpK36 led to a significant increase in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ceftazidime.48 Based on this finding, we hypothesise that blaTEM and blaSHV alone may be insufficient to confer third-generation cephalosporin resistance and that their impact may depend on mutations in outer membrane porin genes.

Unlike other bacteria, ARGs in P. aeruginosa shown poor correlation with resistance phenotype. In particular, only a limited number of samples detected blaIMP, blaVIM, blaNDM, blaKPC or blaOXA-48 affiliating to P. aeruginosa, which were excluded from the performance analysis due to statistically randomness of small sample size (Supplementary Figure S4). However, approximately 50% of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates lacked any detectable ARGs (Supplementary Figure S4). P. aeruginosa produces metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), including blaIMP, blaVIM, and blaNDM, as well as carbapenemases such as blaOXA-48-like and blaOXA-50, all of which contribute to carbapenem hydrolysis.49 In addition to β-lactamase activity, mutations in the porin protein OprD can result in loss of function and decreased permeability to carbapenems.50,51 Furthermore, regulatory genes such as mexR, nalC, and nalD modulate the MexAB-OprM efflux pump, leading to active expulsion of carbapenems and other antibiotics.52, 53, 54, 55 These findings suggest that carbapenem resistance in P. aeruginosa is multifactorial, potentially driven by mutations in OprD, mexR, nalC, and nalD. Indeed, we detected frameshift and missense mutations of these genes in a subset of isolates (data not shown). However, the relatively low sequencing depth of clinical mNGS may have compromised the sensitivity for detecting these mutations, thereby precluding the reliable identification of ARG mutants. To improve resistance phenotype prediction in P. aeruginosa, future studies should focus on developing more sensitive and rapid diagnostic methods for detecting mutations in genes such as OprD, mexR, nalC, and nalD.

There are several limitations in this study. First, only a limited set of commonly encountered ARGs was analysed, covering just 12 prevalent bacterial species with well-characterised resistance mechanisms and clinical relevance. Several ARGs could not be evaluated for predictive performance because of low detection frequency, while others showed relatively wide 95% confidence intervals, reflecting uncertainty due to small sample sizes (Table 4). A larger number of samples will be required in future studies to enable robust assessment of their performance. Second, although capture mNGS significantly improved the detection rates of both pathogens and ARGs, it remains challenging to comprehensively capture rare resistance determinants, resistance mediated by point mutations, or previously uncharacterised resistance mechanisms. WGS represents an important complementary strategy to address these gaps, particularly in cases of discordance between genotypic predictions and resistant phenotype. Comparative and pangenome analyses enable unbiased identification of resistance-associated variants beyond targeted panels, while functional validation remains essential to establish causal genotype–phenotype relationships. Furthermore, integration of WGS-derived features into artificial intelligence–based models may further enhance resistance prediction. In clinical practice, alternative techniques, such as qPCR and droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), may be more suitable for detecting resistance mediated by point mutations.

In conclusion, capture mNGS represents an advanced mNGS technology compared to uncaptured mNGS. Through targeting bacteria and ARGs and optimised ARG-to-bacteria assignment, capture mNGS enables prediction of bacterial antibiotic resistance with high sensitivity and specificity. This approach holds great potential for guiding clinicians in optimising antibiotic selection and stewardship, ultimately advancing precision medicine.
