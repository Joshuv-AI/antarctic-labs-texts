Endothelial exosomes remodel chromatin accessibility and superenhancer in cancer cells to promote liver metastatic colonization
Authors: Bei Jin, Shenglan Liu, Hongke Huang, Luo Yang, Dong Wei, Wei Dai, Jingfeng Zhou, Yun Wang, Ping Zhang, Qianyun Ye, Jingxuan Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver-organotropic metastasis occurs in cancers such as uveal melanoma (UM) and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and is a leading cause of mortality at the terminal stage. Colonization is a rate-limiting and converging step in the metastatic cascade. The drivers underlying liver colonization have largely remained elusive. In this report, we hypothesized that pioneer transcription factors (TFs) increase chromatin accessibility and superenhancer (SE) remodeling, which subsequently promote colonization-associated cellular features [e.g., proliferation, survival, cancer stem cells (CSCs), motility] by driving the transcription of the respective SE-associated genes. We tested this hypothesis and discovered that the pioneer TF KLF4 promoted chromatin accessibility and SE remodeling by ATAC-seq and ChIP-seq. Chromatin accessibility and SE remodeling significantly increased the transcription of the SE-associated genes MCL1 and RASGRP3, which consequently promoted proliferation, motility, and metastasis. Furthermore, the upregulation of KLF4 expression in UM cells was promoted by RN7SL2-containing exosomes from surrounding endothelial cells. Analysis of clinical datasets revealed that the overexpression of RIG-I, STAT1, KLF4, and RASGRP3 was individually correlated with shorter metastasis-free survival or overall survival in patients with UM and PDAC. In conclusion, the identification of the exoRN7SL2-STAT1-KLF4-SEs-RASGRP3 signaling axis may help elucidate metastatic colonization through interactions between tumor cells and the endothelial niche and may yield novel and viable therapeutic targets.Subject terms: Metastasis, Epigenetics

Introduction

Distal organ metastasis, a hallmark of malignant solid tumors, is among the direct causes of death in cancer patients at the final stage.1,2 Once metastasis to either a single organ or multiple organs occurs, there are usually limited effective therapeutic options available because of the paucity of information concerning the mechanism of metastasis and therapeutic targets. Rather than random, metastasis follows organ-specific patterns depending on the cancer type.3 Some cancers such as cutaneous melanoma, breast cancer, and lung cancer frequently metastasize to multiple target organs, while other cancers such as colorectal cancer and uveal melanoma (UM) show organ-specific colonization.3 Overall, the underlying mechanism of metastasis remains largely elusive because of enormous complexity. However, single-site organotropic metastasis [e.g., UM and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)] may offer a simplified window to elucidate the underlying mechanism of metastasis. Unlike multiple organ metastases in cutaneous melanoma, UM is a quintessential example of single-organ liver metastasis. Eighty-five percent of patients with metastatic UM experience metastasis to the sole organ of the liver, with a median survival of only 2 to 8 months.4 In addition to UM, liver metastasis occurs in 50–80% of patients with newly diagnosed PDAC.5 To date, few drivers specific to liver metastasis have been identified. Because the liver is a life-maintaining organ that performs a wide spectrum of physiological functions, such as metabolism, excretion, and body defense, elucidating the underlying mechanism of liver metastasis is highly important.

Metastasis is a complicated process that involves cancer cell migration and invasion, traveling both inwardly and outwardly across blood vessel walls, circulating in blood stream, and colonizing in the host organ.6 The migration and invasion of cancer cells precede colonization during metastasis. Thus, enhanced cell motility motivated by remodeling of the cell cytoskeleton, which is modulated by the Rho family of GTPases (e.g., RhoA, Rac1, and Cdc42), also facilitates metastatic colonization.7 Only a small set of infiltrated tumor cells [referred to as metastasis-initiating cells that hold features of cancer stem-like cells (CSCs)] can survive and eventually form macrometastases in specific and hostile host organs.1 Therefore, colonization is believed to be a rate-limiting and converging step in the metastatic cascade. Conquering colonization may be critical to win the war against extraordinarily intricate metastasis. Several lines of evidence have demonstrated that epigenetic alterations, but not genetic differences, are present between primary foci and metastatic nodules.1,8 For instance, enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) mediates metastasis in UM,9 prostate cancer,10 and breast cancer.11 However, precise epigenetic regulation during colonization remains to be fully addressed. Chromatin remodeling regulates DNA accessibility via alteration of nucleosome positioning and/or occupancy of transcription factors (TFs), and superenhancer (SE) reorganization.12 Chromatin remodeling-eliciting stemness-related TFs may presumably facilitate strengthened stemness of CSCs. Chromatin remodeling in CSCs may be triggered by the specific microenvironment of the host target organ. Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), which are proficient nonparenchymal cells and unique to liver organ, are the first microenviromental cell type that tumor cells encountered in liver.13 In addition, LSECs can secret extracellular vesicles and cytokines. Direct or indirect interactions between tumor cells and LSECs may hypothetically impact on chromatin remodeling and SE reorganization of CSCs in liver. Superenhancers (SEs) are clusters of active enhancers that are distinct from typical enhancers in terms of larger size and higher TF density and content and play a critical role in cell identity, proliferation, survival, etc.14,15 SEs promote tumorigenicity by driving the transcription of SE-associated oncogenes (e.g., XBP1, RUNX1, MYCN, PAK4, and ETS2).16–18 Disruption of SEs can preferentially inhibit the transcription of relevant oncogenes and induce regression in cancers such as ovarian cancer,16 nasopharyngeal carcinoma17 and small cell lung cancer.18 However, how SEs and which SE-associated genes confer metastatic colonization to the liver are largely unknown. Like typical enhancers, the functionality of SEs is tightly modulated by the chromatin state. In eukaryotes, nucleosomes ensure closed or silent chromatin by impeding the binding of TFs and initiating the RNA polymerase machinery. “Pioneer TFs” are capable of initiating a local opening in “closed” chromatin to create room for SEs and enhancers to execute transcription.19 FOXA1 has been demonstrated to serve as a pioneer TF to favor SEs and promote tumorigenesis in breast,20 prostate,8 and pancreatic carcinoma.21 However, which pioneer TF(s) are involved in liver metastatic colonization remains unclear.

In this report, we hypothesized that pioneer TFs implemented chromatin accessibility and SE remodeling which subsequently promoted the colonization-associated cellular features such as proliferation, survival, CSCs through driving transcription of the respective SE-associated genes. We tested this hypothesis and validated KLF4 as such a pioneer TF, and MCL1 and RASGRP3 as SE-associate genes to promote colonization. Upstream, RN7SL2-containing exosomes from surrounding endothelial cells induced RIG-I-STAT1 signaling, resulting in KLF4 upregulation in tumor cells to maintain CSCs. These findings may facilitate cracking the mechanism underlying colonization and relevant potential therapeutic targets open.

Results

The propensity for metastatic colonization of the liver is associated with SE remodeling in UM cells

To explore potential alterations in SEs in metastatic UM, we generated Mel270-Luc cell sublines with high liver-metastatic competence via three rounds of intrasplenic injection of Mel270-Luc cells in NOG mice, an approach that can effectively reflect metastatic colonization in the liver (Fig. 1a). The sublines resulting from the third round of in vivo selection were designated hepatic metastatic derivative 3 (HM3). We confirmed that compared with the parental Mel270-Luc subline, the Mel270-HM3 sublines (Subline#1, Subline#11, and Subline#15) presented significantly increased liver metastatic competence in terms of luciferase intensity, size and absolute number of surface nodules (Fig. 1b–d).

We next attempted to characterize the landscape of the transcriptome of the Mel270-HM3 sublines. Analysis of ChIP-seq data with anti-H3K27ac revealed that 563 (321–759) SE regions were increased in Mel270-HM3 cells compared with the parental Mel270-Luc cells, whereas 1030 (955–1068) SE regions were lost (Fig. 1e–h and supplementary Fig. 1a–d), indicating SE remodeling in the HM3 sublines. On the other hand, GO analysis revealed that the enriched SE-associated genes were enriched in pathways involving positive regulation of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT), extracellular exosome, negative regulation of the apoptotic process and positive regulation of cell proliferation (Fig. 1i).

CDK7 is overexpressed in uveal melanoma

CDK7 can phosphorylate the C-terminal domain (CTD) of RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) and thereby trigger transcription initiation and elongation of SE-associated genes, playing a critical role in executing SE function (i.e., transcribing SE-associated genes).22,23 Therefore, we examined the expression of CDK7 in UM cells by qRT‒PCR and Western blotting. The results revealed that compared with those in the parental Mel270-Luc cells, the levels of CDK7 in both the mRNA and protein layers markedly increased in the Mel270-HM3 sublines (Fig. 2a, b). Compared with that in parental Mel270-Luc cells, the expression of total CDK7 in the Mel270-HM3 sublines significantly increased ∼39% (ranging from 29% to 54%). Moreover, the activity of CDK7, as indicated by p-CDK7 S164 and p-CDK7 T170, significantly increased in the Mel270-HM3 sublines (Fig. 2b, c, respectively). These results indicate that both the expression and the activity of CDK7 were increased in highly metastatic Mel270-HM3 sublines.

Analysis of a cohort in the cancer genome atlas (TCGA) with clinically annotated UM24 revealed that CDK7 expression was significantly higher in patients with more aggressive UM originating from epithelioid cells than in patients with spindle-like cells and mixed cells (Fig. 2d). The presence of epithelioid or mixed cells may predict poor survival in patients with UM.4 Moreover, CDK7 overexpression was significantly correlated with short survival in patients with UM (Fig. 2e). To explore whether CDK7 is correlated with liver metastasis, we employed the GSE71729 dataset,25 which includes a considerable number of patients with liver metastasis. The results revealed that the levels of CDK7 mRNA were significantly greater in primary tumor tissues from PDAC patients with liver metastasis than in those from nonmetastatic patients (Fig. 2f), suggesting that CDK7 may play a critical role in liver metastasis.

We next performed an immunohistochemical (IHC) assay using UM specimens after validating the specificity of the antibody against CDK7 (supplementary Fig. 2a, b), revealing that CDK7 was much more abundant in human UM tissues than in normal adjacent uveal tissues (Fig. 2g–i). Additionally, a medium to high increase in CDK7 expression was observed in 89.3% of UM patients (58 out of 65) (Fig. 2h). Compared with those in ARPE-19 cells, the mRNA and protein levels of CDK7 in UM cells in all four tested lines (92.1, Mel270, Omm1, and Omm2.3) were robustly increased (Fig. 2j, k). The activity of CDK7, as indicated by p-CDK7 S164 and p-CDK7 T170, significantly increased (Fig. 2k). Furthermore, the expression of CDK7 was positively correlated with tumor thickness and the largest basal diameter of primary tumors, which are predictors of an increased risk of metastasis in patients with UM.4 In addition, it was positively correlated with TNM stage (supplementary Table 1).

The occurrence of SE remodeling and CDK7 overexpression in UM prompted us to hypothesize that SEs and their associated genes increase cellular traits (e.g., proliferation, survival, stemness, motility) and ultimately drive metastatic colonization of the liver. Therefore, blocking SEs would predict a decrease in the relevant cellular traits and metastatic colonization.

Inhibition of CDK7 by its selective inhibitor THZ1 or genetic depletion abrogates metastatic colonization

We tested the above hypothesis by employing THZ1, a covalent inhibitor of CDK7 that can effectively block SE activity.22 UM cells were exposed to THZ1 and its analog THZ1-R. Western blotting analysis revealed that THZ1 markedly inhibited the phosphorylation of the RNA Pol II CTD at Ser5/7 (initiation-related) and Ser2 (elongation-related) (Fig. 3a and supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, even at 10,000 nM, THZ1-R (40×) did not apparently decrease the phosphorylation of Ser2 or Ser5/7 of the RNA Pol II CTD (supplementary Fig. 3). These results suggest that THZ1 is specific for CDK7 inhibition.

We next assessed the impact of THZ1 on liver metastatic colonization. NOG mice inoculated with Mel270-Luc cells or Mel270-HM3 subline#1 by intrasplenic injection were intraperitoneally administered THZ1 (10 mg/kg, twice daily) (Fig. 3b). Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) monitoring with an IVIS Spectrum system revealed that compared with vehicle treatment, THZ1 treatment significantly decreased the BLI signal intensity in the liver zone (Fig. 3c, g). Anatomic observation at the end of the experiment revealed that THZ1 treatment significantly decreased the number of metastatic nodules on the liver surface (Fig. 3d, h). Histologic examination further confirmed that the number and size of metastatic lesions were markedly reduced in the livers of mice treated with THZ1 (Fig. 3e, i). IHC analysis revealed that p-CDK7 S164 was markedly decreased in liver metastasis of mice treated with THZ1, with a slight decrease in p-CDK7 T170 but no apparent alteration in CDK7 or CDK12 (Fig. 3f, j).

To specifically evaluate the effects of CDK7 on metastatic colonization, we introduced lentiviral shRNA (targeting the scramble, 3’ UTR or CDS regions of CDK7) into Mel270-Luc cells. Western blotting analysis revealed that CDK7 was specifically knocked down with shCDK7, while CDK12 remained unaltered (Fig. 3k). Mel270-Luc cells expressing scramble, shCDK7-3’ UTR, or shCDK7-CDS were intrasplenically injected into NOG mice. The BLI signals were monitored weekly by IVIS. Six weeks later, the BLI signals were significantly lower in the mice bearing Mel270-Luc cells with shCDK7 than in those bearing scramble cells (Fig. 3l). These results suggest that CDK7 promotes hepatic metastasis in UM.

Collectively, these results indicate that inhibition of CDK7 by its selective inhibitor THZ1 or genetic depletion abrogates metastatic colonization.

Pharmacological inhibition of CDK7 by THZ1 diminishes cellular proliferation and outgrowth of xenografted tumors in UM

We next determined which cellular features (e.g., proliferation, survival, CSCs, motility) of metastatic colonization were diminished by THZ1 treatment.

To assess the impact of THZ1 treatment on cellular proliferation, UM parental and Mel270-HM3 cells were exposed to THZ1 and subjected to cell viability evaluation by an MTS assay. The results revealed IC50 values of 25–250 nM against UM cells but a much higher IC50 value of >5 μM against ARPE-19 cells, suggesting a therapeutic window for THZ1 (Fig. 4a). Compared with those in parental Mel270-Luc cells, the IC50 values in Mel270-HM3 cells were reduced by 36–40% (15.08–16.74 nM vs 24.96 nM, Fig. 4a). THZ1 also significantly inhibited the clonogenicity of the four lines of UM parental cells (Fig. 4b). Notably, compared with those in parental Mel270-Luc cells, the IC50 values in Mel270-HM3 cells were reduced by 28–35% (17.06–18.62 nM vs 25.88 nM, Fig. 4b). These results support our hypothesis that CDK7-overexpressing tumor cells might have increased sensitivity to THZ1 treatment.

We next determined whether THZ1 impeded the in vivo outgrowth of UM tumors. To this end, a NOD/SCID mouse model with engraftment of Omm126 was used. Once the tumor volume reached ∼100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 8 per group) and treated with either vehicle or THZ1 (10 mg/kg, twice daily) for 14 days. The results revealed that THZ1 administration significantly suppressed UM tumor outgrowth, as reflected by decreases in tumor volume (Fig. 4c) and tumor weight (Fig. 4d). Notably, THZ1-treated mice did not experience weight loss or aberrant behaviors (data not shown), which was consistent with the findings of a previous study.23 More importantly, the in vivo antitumor activity of THZ1-mediated SE blockade was observed in the MP41 PDX model27 (Fig. 4e, f). Additionally, IHC staining for Ki67 in slides of tumors from both Omm1 xenografts and PDX tumors revealed that THZ1 treatment dramatically reduced proliferation (supplementary Fig. 4a, c). Western blotting analysis of cell lysates from these xenografted tumors revealed that THZ1 treatment dramatically decreased the phosphorylation of the RNA Pol II CTD at Ser 2, 5, and 7 (supplementary Fig. 4b, d), indicating the in vivo anti-CDK7 potency of THZ1.

MCL1 is an SE-associated survival gene in UM

Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis by Annexin V-PI double labeling revealed that exposure to THZ1 induced massive apoptosis in UM cells (Fig. 4g and supplementary Fig. 4e). Western blotting analysis of lysates from THZ1-treated UM cells revealed increases in the levels of cleaved PARP, active caspase-3 in a concentration-dependent manner (supplementary Fig. 4f) and cytosolic cytochrome c in a time-dependent manner (supplementary Fig. 4g). Notably, increased apoptosis was also evidenced by anti-active caspase-3 IHC staining in xenografted tumors from either Omm1- or MP41 PDX-bearing mice that were administered THZ1 (supplementary Fig. 4a, c).

To elucidate the mechanism of apoptosis induced by THZ1, we examined the expression of apoptosis-associated proteins by Western blotting. The results revealed a notable reduction in the expression of the pro-survival protein Mcl-1 but no appreciable change in the expression of other apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-XL, XIAP, and Survivin) (Fig. 4h). Notably, Western blotting analysis of cell lysates from xenograft tumors revealed that THZ1 treatment elicited a decrease in the expression of Mcl-1 (supplementary Fig. 4b, d).

Because ChIP-seq for H3K27ac profiling revealed that MCL1 was an SE-associated gene (Fig. 4i), we examined the transcription level of MCL1 by qRT‒PCR. As illustrated in Fig. 4j, THZ1 significantly reduced the mRNA level of MCL1. These results indicate that MCL1 is an SE-associated gene. To determine the importance of Mcl-1 in THZ1-induced apoptosis, Mel270 cells were transfected with empty vector or constructs encoding MCL1. Ectopic expression of Mcl-1 at least partially mitigated THZ1-induced apoptosis (Fig. 4k, l), whereas silencing of Mcl-1 by siRNA duplexes increased the sensitivity to THZ1, as reflected by the significantly increased levels of cleaved PARP and active caspase-3 (Fig. 4m) and the percentage of cell death in the trypan blue exclusion assay (Fig. 4n). These results suggest that blockade of SEs by THZ1 decreases the survival ability of UM cells by suppressing the SE-associated gene MCL1.

Pharmacological inhibition of CDK7 by THZ1 decreases CSC traits

Given that CSCs are believed to promote metastatic colonization,28 we next asked whether THZ1 affects the traits of CSCs. Western blotting analysis of stemness-related proteins in UM cells revealed that the levels of SLUG, the pivotal regulator of CSCs,29 were prominently decreased upon THZ1 treatment, while the levels of the other proteins remained constant (Fig. 5a). Serial replating assays of melanospheres revealed that THZ1 treatment dramatically decreased the yields of melanospheres (Fig. 5b). Accordingly, qRT‒PCR and immunofluorescence (IF) assays revealed that THZ1 also reduced the expression of SLUG at both the transcriptional (Fig. 5c) and protein levels (Fig. 5d) in the cells of the tertiary melanospheres. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the percentage of Aldefluor+ cells was significantly reduced after exposure to THZ1 (Fig. 5e). Moreover, an in vivo limiting dilution assay in NOD/SCID mice subcutaneously inoculated with Omm1 cells revealed that THZ1 dramatically decreased the frequency of CSCs (Fig. 5f and supplementary Table 2). Importantly, Western blotting analysis of cell lysates from xenograft tumors revealed that THZ1 treatment resulted in a decrease in SLUG expression (supplementary Fig. 4b, d). These results indicate that inhibition of CDK7 by THZ1 decreases the traits of CSCs.

Pharmacological inhibition of CDK7 by THZ1 decreases cellular motility in UM

We next explored whether THZ1 treatment affects cellular motility, which is a step preceding colonization. Transwell assays revealed that THZ1 treatment significantly reduced the migration and invasion ability of Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells (Fig. 5g, h).

To understand the mechanism through which THZ1 represses migration and invasion, we analyzed the organization of the cytoskeleton, which is fundamental to cellular motility.7 IF staining of polymerized actin (F-actin) using Texas Red-X phalloidin revealed that THZ1 treatment significantly decreased the fluorescence intensity of F-actin (Fig. 5i). The Rho family of small GTPases, including Rho, Rac, and Cdc42, regulates actin cytoskeletal organization and cellular motility.7 By specifically pulling down the active GTP-bound form of Rho GTPases, we found that the level of active RhoA was markedly decreased in THZ1-treated Mel270 cells (Fig. 5j). Furthermore, the activity of the RhoA/ROCK pathway was substantially inhibited (Fig. 5k). These findings suggest that THZ1 may inhibit cell motility through blocking the RhoA/ROCK pathway.

Analysis of SE-associated genes that mediate metastatic colonization

To analyze the SE-associated genes responsible for metastatic colonization, we first determined a low concentration that elicited a response and a high concentration that completely inhibited CDK7 kinase activity. Western blotting analysis of RNA Pol II CTD phosphorylation at Ser 2, Ser 5 and Ser 7 in Mel270 cells treated with THZ1 for 6 h revealed that 62.5 nM and 250 nM THZ1 met the criteria (Fig. 6a). RNA-seq analysis further revealed that exposure to the high concentration of THZ1 (250 nM) inhibited the expression of most genes, whereas treatment with the low concentration of THZ1 (62.5 nM) led to the downregulation of a subset of transcripts in a selective fashion (Fig. 6b, c). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that the SE-associated genes were significantly enriched in THZ1-treated cells at low dose compared with those in cells treated with DMSO-containing medium (Fig. 6d). These findings suggest that the transcription levels of SE-associated genes are preferentially vulnerable to CDK7 inhibition, which may explain the sensitivity of UM cells to low-concentration THZ1 treatment.

Eight candidate SE-associated genes (RASGRP3, SOS1, ID3, MET, ERBB3, BAMBI, SH3BP4, and GPR161) were obtained from the intersection based on17 (i) SEs extracted by the rank ordering of super-enhancers (ROSE) algorithm based on ChIP-seq signals; (ii) the top 15% of all expressed transcripts in Mel270 cells; and (iii) transcription sensitive to low-concentration (62.5 nM) of THZ1 treatment (≥1.5-fold change) (Fig. 6e, f). qRT‒PCR confirmed that the transcription of the 8 genes was vulnerable to THZ1 exposure in Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells (supplementary Fig. 5a). Among the 8 genes, RASGRP3 was the most abundant gene, as revealed by qRT‒PCR analysis in the four lines of UM cells vs ARPE-19 cells and in the Mel270-HM3 sublines vs the parental Mel270-Luc cells (Fig. 6g and supplementary Fig. 5b). The high levels of RASGRP3 in UM cell lines and Mel270-HM3 sublines were further confirmed by Western blotting analysis (supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Notably, Western blotting analysis of cell lysates from xenograft tumors revealed that THZ1 treatment markedly decreased RASGRP3 expression (supplementary Fig. 4b, d). Additionally, analysis of the TCGA database24 revealed that the expression of RASGRP3 was inversely correlated with overall survival in patients with UM (supplementary Fig. 5e). Gene expression omnibus (GEO) dataset30 revealed significantly higher expression of RASGRP3 in patients with metastatic UM than in patients with nonmetastatic UM (supplementary Fig. 5f).

Moreover, after verifying the specificity of the antibody against RASGRP3, we conducted an IHC analysis of UM specimens (supplementary Fig. 5g). The results confirmed that the protein levels of RASGRP3 in cancerous tissues were significantly greater than those in normal adjacent uveal tissues (supplementary Fig. 5h–j). In addition, the expression of RASGRP3 was positively correlated with tumor thickness and the largest basal diameter and with primary tumor stage and TNM stage (supplementary Table 3). Therefore, we focused on evaluating the function of the SE-associated gene RASGRP3 in driving metastatic colonization in subsequent experiments.

RASGRP3 promotes liver metastatic colonization in mice

We next determined the contribution of RASGRP3 to liver metastatic colonization. Stable transduction of a lentiviral construct encoding human RASGRP3 in Mel270-Luc cells led to a significant increase in the BLI intensity and the number of nodules on the liver surface in mice bearing intrasplenic injection of Mel270-Luc cells; and the ability of the lentiviral construct to at least partially rescue the THZ1-mediated suppression of these two parameters of liver colonization was potent (Fig. 6h–k). Taken together, these results indicate that RASGRP3 is a functionally critical SE-associated gene for liver metastatic colonization.

RASGRP3 promotes SLUG expression and CSCs

Our previous study demonstrated that SLUG is required for maintaining the stemness traits of CSCs in UM.29 Given that RASGRP3 can activate the MAPK pathway31 and the ERK/ELK-1/SLUG axis32,33 in several types of carcinomas, we tested the hypothesis that RASGRP3 might positively regulate SLUG expression and the properties of CSCs. As expected, Western blotting analysis demonstrated that silencing RASGRP3 in Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells by shRNA led to decreases in the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and ELK1 and in the level of SLUG (supplementary Fig. 6a). Furthermore, RASGRP3 knockdown dramatically decreased the transcription of SLUG, as measured by the SLUG-Luc reporter (supplementary Fig. 6b). Additionally, a ChIP assay with an anti-phospho-ELK1 antibody revealed that silencing RASGRP3 attenuated the enrichment of phospho-ELK1 at the promoter region of SLUG (supplementary Fig. 6c). More importantly, silencing RASGRP3 in UM cells resulted in a decrease in melanosphere-forming ability (supplementary Fig. 6d) and the percentage of Aldefluor+ cells (supplementary Fig. 6e). These results suggest that RASGRP3 positively regulates the ERK/ELK1/SLUG axis. Indeed, rescue experiments revealed that ectopic expression of RASGRP3 in Mel270 cells at least partially ameliorated the THZ1-mediated decrease in the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and ELK1 and SLUG expression, the serial melanosphere-forming ability and the percentage of Aldefluor+ cells (supplementary Fig. 6f–h). Collectively, these results suggest that RASGRP3 is a critical SE-associated gene for maintaining CSC traits via the ERK/ELK1/SLUG signaling axis.

RASGRP3 promotes UM cell motility

Because THZ1-downregulated transcription was enriched in genes associated with cytoskeletal organization (supplementary Fig. 7a), we investigated the role of RASGRP3 in cytoskeletal organization. IF staining revealed that RASGRP3 knockdown decreased the fluorescence intensity of F-actin in Mel270 cells (supplementary Fig. 7b). Concordantly, ranswell assays revealed that the deletion of RASGRP3 by shRNA markedly reduced the migration and invasion abilities of Mel270 cells (supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Western blotting analysis revealed that the levels of active RhoA, but not those of active Rac1 and Cdc42, were markedly lowered in RASGRP3-silenced UM cells than in scramble UM cells (supplementary Fig. 7e). Furthermore, RASGRP3 knockdown blocked the downstream activity of the RhoA/ROCK pathway (supplementary Fig. 7f).

We next explored whether RASGRP3 overexpression could reverse the THZ1-mediated inhibitory effect on motility. As anticipated, compared with empty lentiviral vector, ectopic overexpression of RASGRP3 increased the migration and invasion capacity of UM cells (supplementary Fig. 7g, h). In addition, ectopic expression of RASGRP3 reversed the abrogation of migration and invasion mediated by THZ1 (supplementary Fig. 7g, h). IF staining revealed that ectopic overexpression of RASGRP3 in Mel270 cells enhanced F-actin formation and reversed the THZ1-mediated decrease in the fluorescence intensity of F-actin (supplementary Fig. 7i). Furthermore, ectopic expression of RASGRP3 in Mel270 cells at least partially reversed the THZ1-mediated inhibitory effect on the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway (supplementary Fig. 7j).

Taken together, these stemness and motility results provide evidence that RASGRP3 functions as an SE-associated gene to promote liver colonization-associated cellular phenotypes.

Coculture with endothelial cells induces SE reprogramming

We next investigated what drove SE reprogramming in UM cells undergoing liver metastatic colonization. Because endothelial cells (ECs) are important components of the liver microenvironment,6,34 we hypothesized that the interaction between tumor cells and their surrounding ECs might induce SE reprogramming and promote colonization. This hypothesis was supported by the observation that compared with mono-cultured Mel270 cells, Mel270 cells cocultured with ECs significantly increased metastatic colonization, as evidenced by elevated BLI signals in the liver zone (Fig. 7a, b), as well as the number and size of metastatic tumor nodules in the liver (Fig. 7c, d).

To analyze the fluctuations of SEs, Mel270-Luc-GFP cells were cocultured with HUVECs for 4 days. Mel270-Luc-GFP cells were subsequently sorted by GFP-based flow cytometry and then subjected to ChIP-seq analysis with an anti-H3K27ac antibody. The results of ChIP-seq revealed that compared with those in the mono-cultured Mel270-Luc-GFP cells, most SEs (72.1%) in the Mel270-Luc-GFP cells sorted from coculture were increased, whereas only 19.8% of SEs were lost (Fig. 7e–g), indicating that the physical interaction with ECs results in SE remodeling in tumor cells.

KLF4 mediates EC-induced SE reprogramming

Because pioneer TFs play a critical role in transcriptional reprogramming, we performed a motif enrichment analysis of binding sites for TFs at gained SE regions in Mel270-Luc-GFP cells cocultured with ECs. Seven genes encoding TFs (e.g., KLF4 and ZFN384) were among the top-ranked SE regions (Fig. 7h). qRT‒PCR analysis revealed that the transcription of only KLF4 and ZNF384 among the 7 TFs was significantly increased and that ~5-fold increase in the expression of KLF4 over that of ZNF384 occurred (Fig. 7i, left). Coculture of Panc02-GFP cells with mouse primary LSECs revealed that the transcription of Klf4, Znf384, and Ascl1 was significantly increased and that the expression level of Klf4 was ~146-fold and ~7598-fold greater than those of Znf384 and Ascl1, respectively (Fig. 7i, right). Moreover, compared with that in the parental Mel270-Luc cells, the expression of KLF4 in the Mel270-HM3 sublines was strongly increased (supplementary Fig. 8a, b). Analysis of the clinical GSE27831 dataset30 revealed that KLF4 expression was significantly elevated in metastatic UM patients compared with nonmetastatic patients (supplementary Fig. 8c). Therefore, we next investigated whether KLF4 induces SE remodeling in UM cells. We analyzed the enrichment of TF binding site motifs within SE regions of the Mel270-HM3 sublines relative to those in parental cells. The results revealed significant enrichment of KLF4 binding sites in these gained SE regions (Fig. 7j), suggesting that KLF4 mediates chromatin accessibility and SE remodeling and increases metastatic potential in UM cells. To confirm this, we knocked down KLF4 in Mel270 cells by lentiviral shRNA and then carried out ChIP-seq with anti-H3K27ac (Fig. 7k). As anticipated, depletion of KLF4 led to a marked decrease in H3K27ac occupancy at gained SE regions (Fig. 7l, m).

Given that the pioneer TF KLF4 is capable of accessing silent chromatin in induced pluripotent stem cells,35 we speculated that KLF4 might also act as a pioneer TF in cancerous cells. To decipher whether KLF4 increases chromatin accessibility during liver metastasis, we manipulated KLF4 expression in CFPAC-1, which originated from a liver metastatic patient with PDAC.36 An assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) was employed to analyze open chromatin regions (OCRs) in scramble- vs shKLF4-CFPAC-1 cells (Fig. 7n) or vector- vs KLF4-overexpressing CFPAC-1 cells (supplementary Fig. 8d). Plots of the genome-wide ATAC-seq intensities revealed that approximately 80% (40,916 out of 50,143 peaks) (Fig. 7n) of the OCRs, including those of MCL1 and RASGRP3 (Fig. 7o), were reduced in KLF4-depleted CFPAC-1 cells, indicating that KLF4 knockdown potently decreases chromatin accessibility. The decreased OCRs led to reduced transcription of RASGRP3 and MCL1 in the KLF4-silenced CFPAC-1 and Mel270 cells (Fig. 7p). In contrast, ectopic expression of KLF4 increased the expression of more than 40% genes (18,632 out of 42,421 peaks), including MCL1 and RASGRP3 in the OCRs of CFPAC-1 cells, indicating that KLF4 overexpression increases chromatin accessibility (supplementary Fig. 8e, f). The transcription of RASGRP3 and MCL1 was significantly increased in KLF4-overexpressing CFPAC-1 and Mel270 cells (supplementary Fig. 8g), further suggesting that KLF4-mediated chromatin accessibility may lead to increased transcription of RASGRP3 and MCL1.

We next determined whether KLF4 was sufficient to promote liver metastasis. To this end, Mel270 cells stably overexpressing KLF4 were subjected to migration and invasion assays. We observed that ectopic expression of KLF4 significantly promoted migration and invasion (supplementary Fig. 9a, b). Consistent with these findings, forced overexpression of KLF4 in Mel270-Luc and Panc02-Luc cells significantly increased the BLI intensity in the liver zone and the number of nodules (supplementary Fig. 9c–f). Conversely, ablation of KLF4 expression in Mel270 cells by lentiviral shRNA significantly decreased migration and invasion, as evaluated by transwell assays (supplementary Fig. 9g, h). Silencing KLF4 in Mel270-Luc and Panc02-Luc cells significantly decreased the BLI intensity in the liver zone and the number of nodules (supplementary Fig. 9i–l).

These results are indicative of the prometastatic role of KLF4 in UM and PDAC.

The results revealed that STAT1 silencing obviously decreased the levels of KLF4, as reflected by the results of Western blotting and qRT‒PCR (supplementary Fig. 10a, b). A ChIP assay with anti-STAT1 indicated that STAT1 was recruited to the promoter of the KLF4 gene but not to the downstream intronic region lacking the STAT1 binding sequence and that the occupancy of STAT1 at the promoter region of KLF4 was significantly decreased in STAT1-silenced Mel270 cells (Fig. 8a).

Given that RIG-I is a cytosolic RNA pattern recognition receptor (PRR) that induces the activation of the STAT1 signaling,38,39 we reasoned that EC-derived exosomes were delivered to UM cells where RIG-I was possibly ligated by its ligand to increase the expression of STAT1 and its downstream genes KLF4 and RASGRP3, eventually leading to metastatic colonization. To test this hypothesis, conditioned medium (CM) or exosome-depleted CM from HUVECs was added to cultures of Mel270, Omm2.3, and Mel270-HM3 cells for 48 h. Western blotting and qRT‒PCR analysis revealed that CM, but not exosome-depleted CM, increased the levels of RIG-I, STAT1, KLF4, and RASGRP3 in UM cells (Fig. 8b and supplementary Fig. 10c).

Next, the exosomes purified from HUVEC CM by ultracentrifugation were verified by electron microscopy (supplementary Fig. 10d), nanoparticle tracking analysis (supplementary Fig. 10e) and Western blotting (supplementary Fig. 10f). The HUVEC-derived exosomes were then labeled with Dil (red) and added to the cultures of Mel270-GFP, Omm2.3-GFP, and Mel270-HM3-GFP cells. Confocal microscopy revealed Dil punctas in the recipient GFP+ UM cells, indicating that the labeled exosomes released by HUVECs were taken up by UM cells (Fig. 8c).

To further evaluate the role of these HUVEC-derived exosomes, migration and invasion assays in UM cells were performed. The results revealed that exposure to HUVEC-derived exosomes significantly increased migration and invasion (supplementary Fig. 10g). Notably, intravenous administration of exosomes prepared from HUVECs markedly increased liver metastatic colonization, as reflected by the nodule number and size (Fig. 8d–f).

Because the RIG-I C-terminal regulatory domain binds to the blunt-end double-stranded 5’-ppp RNA,40 we focused on the stably expressed RNAs that are capped with 5’-ppp. We next determined the components of exosomes from HUVECs by using RNA-seq and 5’-ppp RNA-seq. To identify the isolated exosomal RNA (exoRNA), we performed a series of enzymatic reactions to deplete RNA lacking 5’-ppp and subsequently constructed a library as previously described.38 The RNA classes from exoRNA-seq were compared to those from 5’-ppp RNA-seq (Fig. 8g). 5’-ppp RNA-seq revealed that only three types of RNA, mRNA, snRNA, and misc_RNA, rather than lncRNA and circRNA, were detected (supplementary Table 4). Given that endogenous 5’-ppp RNA, under conditions without viral infection, originates primarily from RNA polymerase III (POL3) transcription products and that POL3 is responsible primarily for transcribing short noncoding RNAs, including snRNA and misc_RNA,38,41 we chose the genes RN7SL2 (misc_RNA), RN7SK (snRNA), MRPS31P4 (misc_RNA), and RF00019 (misc_RNA) for further validation by qRT‒PCR. The qRT‒PCR results revealed that RN7SL2 was the most abundant gene in HUVEC-derived exosomes (Fig. 8h).

To determine the cellular origin of RN7SL2, we analyzed its expression in human endothelial HUVECs and primary murine LSECs, tumor cells (Mel270 and Omm2.3), and primary murine liver component cells [hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), and Kupffer cells (KCs)] by qRT‒PCR. The results revealed that the level of RN7SL2 was significantly greater in HUVECs (Fig. 8i) and murine primary LSECs (Fig. 8j) than in hepatocytes, HSCs, and KCs. Additionally, hypoxic exposure led to marked enrichment of RN7SL2 in exosomes from HUVECs and LSECs (Fig. 8k and supplementary Fig. 10h). We subsequently performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) to determine whether RIG-I bound to RN7SL2. We observed a significant enrichment of RN7SL2 instead of nontargeting RF00019 with anti-RIG-I antibody but not nonspecific IgG antibody upon exposure to HUVEC-derived CM (supplementary Fig. 10i) and purified exosomes (Fig. 8l), respectively. Moreover, an RNA pull-down assay confirmed that RN7SL2 bound to RIG-I but not to STAT1, KLF4, or RASGRP3 in UM cells (Fig. 8m). Together, these data reveal that the exoRNA RN7SL2 isolated from HUVECs binds to RIG-I.

After in vitro-transcribed RN7SL2 was transduced into Mel270 cells, Western blotting and qRT‒PCR analysis revealed that ectopic RN7SL2 markedly increased the expression of RIG-I, STAT1, KLF4, and RASGRP3, which was, however, at least partially ameliorated by treatment with AlkPh (supplementary Fig. 10j, k), suggesting that the 5’-ppp-mediated specificity of RN7SL2 binding to RIG-I. In parallel, the number of migrated and invaded UM cells increased after transfection with transcribed RN7SL2 but not with RN7SL2-depleted 5’-ppp (supplementary Fig. 10l). Conversely, transfection with constructs of point mutant RIG-I to disable RIG-I binding to the 5’-ppp of RN7SL2 attenuated the RN7SL2-mediated activation of the RIG-I-STAT1-KLF4-RASGRP3 signaling axis, as measured by Western blotting (supplementary Fig. 10m), as well as migration and invasion (supplementary Fig. 10n). Collectively, these results indicate that RN7SL2 activates RIG-I signaling in a specific 5’-ppp-dependent manner.

To further define the functional role of exosomal RN7SL2 in UM cells, we disrupted RN7SL2 expression with siRNA in HUVECs, then collected CM and purified exosomes (supplementary Fig. 10o). Compared with parallel cells treated with RN7SL2-containing exosomes, the culture of Mel270 cells with RN7SL2-depleted exosomes led to decreased levels of RIG-I, STAT1, KLF4, and RASGRP3 (Fig. 8n), as well as migration and invasion (supplementary Fig. 10p). The culture of the RN7SL2-depleted exosomes derived from HUVECs also decreased the levels of STAT1, KLF4, RASGRP3 and Mcl-1 in the CFPAC-1 and MIA PaCa-2 cells (Fig. 8o). More importantly, intravenous injection of RN7SL2-depleted exosomes into NOG mice that were intrasplenically inoculated with Mel270-Luc cells dramatically decreased the BLI intensity, nodule number and size of the liver (Fig. 8p–s). Taken together, these results suggest that RN7SL2-containing exosomes derived from HUVECs activate the RIG-I-STAT1-KLF4-RASGRP3 signaling axis and promote liver metastatic colonization of UM and PDAC.

Analysis of publicly available TCGA database24 revealed significant correlations between RIG-I and STAT1, between KLF4 and STAT1, and between KLF4 and RASGRP3 (supplementary Fig. 11a). Additionally, data from public GEO datasets42 and TCGA database revealed that overexpression of individual RIG-I, STAT1, KLF4, and RASGRP3 was correlated with significantly shorter metastasis-free survival and overall survival in UM patients (supplementary Fig. 11b–d). With extended analyses, similar results were obtained in publicly available datasets of PDAC patients,43 with the exception of RASGRP3, perhaps because RASGRP3 is particularly abundant in UM and is scarcely expressed in PDAC (supplementary Fig. 11e–j). Taken together, these results suggest that the RIG-I-STAT1-KLF4-RASGRP3 signaling axis may be correlated with liver metastasis in patients with UM and PDAC.

Discussion

How chromatin accessibility and SEs regulate liver colonzation has been desparately elucidated. Herein, we discovered that pioneer TF KLF4-mediated chromatin accessibility and SE remodeling, which may lead to elevated transcription of SE-associated genes MCL1 and RASGRP3, promoting proliferation, motility, CSCs, and metastatic liver colonization. KLF4 upregulation was elicited by RN7SL2-containing exosomes from surrounding niche endothelial cells. These findings of RN7SL2-STAT1-KLF4-SEs-RASGRP3 signaling axis may facilitate cracking the mechanism underlying metastatic colonization to liver open.

One highlight is the finding that SEs drive metastatic colonization to the liver. After obtaining evidence of SE remodeling, we defined the functional role of SEs in liver metastatic colonization. THZ1 is a phenylaminopyrimidine bearing a potentially cysteine-reactive acrylamide moiety. THZ1-R, a noncysteine reactive analog of THZ1, shows much lower binding affinity for CDK7 (146 nM vs 3.2 nM) than THZ1 does.44 The binding affinity of THZ1 for CDK7 is clearly 46-fold greater than that of THZ1-R for CDK7. We found that THZ1 expression significantly abrogated metastatic colonization in UM, suggesting that SE promotes metastatic colonization. The cellular basis may rely on diverse facets in which SEs exacerbate the transcription of the SE-associated genes MCL1 and RASGRP3 to increase cellular motility, CSC activity and survival in tumor cells, respectively.

Consistent with our observations, Teng et al. reported that disruption of bromodomain containing 4 (BRD4) binding to enhancers by the small molecule JQ1 abrogates liver metastatic colonization in colorectal carcinoma.45 Dong et al. reported that abrogating SEs by JQ1 potently inhibits CSC self-renewal and metastasis in squamous cell carcinoma.46 Despite the ubiquity of SEs in metastasis, nuances in these studies lie in the diverse SE-associated genes identified. For instance, BRD4 recruitment of NF-κB p65 to SEs at cancer stemness genes was proposed in Dong et al’s study, whereas the requirement of FOXA2 for the reshaping of transcription programming was proposed in Teng’s study.45,46 This discrepancy may be due to the different phenotypes and cell types used in these studies.

In this study, we concluded that RASGRP3 driven by SEs may represent an important effector in the RN7SL2-STAT1-KLF4-SEs-RASGRP3 signaling axis to mediate metastatic colonization to liver. Although RASGRP3, one of the four members of RasGTP exchange factors (GEFs) family, is overexpressed in cancers, it’s just recently demonstrated that the expression level of RASGRP3 in the UM cells and tissues harboring the mutant GNAQ/GNA1131 is much higher than that in other types of WT GNAQ/GNA11-harboring cancers such as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma,47 BRAF-mutant cutaneous melanoma,48 and breast cancer.49 Mutations in GNAQ/GNA11 in UM can render activation of PKC δ and ε which phosphorylate RASGRP3 at T133 and relay signals to MAPK to promote celluar proliferation and tumorigenicity of UM.31 Here, we newly discovered the additional functions of RASGRP3 in metastasis which elevated SLUG and activated RhoA to promote traits of CSCs and cell motility, respectively. Consistent with this, RASGRP3 was documented to promote EMT and metastasis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.47 ChIP assay with anti-phospho-ELK1 (S383) presented in this study clearly showed that the recruitment of phospho-ELK1 at the promoter of SNAI2 gene directly mediated SNAI2 transcription, while SLUG is capable of boosting CSCs in UM.29 RASGRP3-ELK1-SLUG signaling module is plausible because ERK1/2, a kind of MAP kinase, is responsible for phospho-ELK1 (S383) while RASGRP3 can activate MAPK. On the other hand, it sounds reasonable for RASGRP3 to involve activation of RhoA because RasGRPs, a GEF, can promote the release of GDP-bound Ras in order to bind GTP, yielding active state of Ras (Ras-GTP).50 For a similar reason, it’s likely that the activation of RhoA by RASGRP3 occurs at the inner plasma memberane. However, it’s worth noting that it remains to be investigated whether the anchoring the inner memberane is required for RASGRP3 to elevate SNAI2 transcription because recruitment of RASGRP3 to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane is an independent activation mechanism of RASGRP3 which is different from that by PKC δ and ε. Overexpressed RASGRP3 promotes Ras activity in gliloma cells.51 Whether RASGRP3 activate Ras activity to promote liver metastasis of UM and PDAC needs further investigation.

Of note, our results demonstrated that KLF4 impacts chromatin accessibility, in accordance with the previous reports that KLF4 is a poineer TF. Pioneer TFs are those which can scan, precisely recognize and preferentially bind to the condensed, transcriptionally silenced (closed) chromatin to endow target sites competence for recruitment of additional TFs and eventually enable a permissive state for transcriptional activity.19 Cell-type-specific gene regulation may be achieved by engagement of distinctive pioneer TFs on the genomic DNA with specific sequences and sued local opening of chromatin. In cancers of pancreas, breast, and prostate, FOXA1 has been demonstrated to act as a pioneer TF, promoting tumorigenicity and progression.20 Apart from FOXA1, GATA3/4 and SOX9, KLF4 have been revealed as pioneer TFs, irrespective of either used alone or in conjunction with the other Yamanaka factors (OCT4, SOX2, and c-MYC) in the process of fibroblast reprogramming into iPSCs.52 Linking RNA-seq analysis, we thus hypothesized, and then validated that KLF4 may function as a pioneer factor to render SE remodeling and promote liver metastatic colonization. However, it will be intriguing to further delineate what and how additional TFs were recruited the target sites to engage the local chromatin opening and SE remodeling in future.

Our results of RN7SL2-STAT1 further provided mechanistic insights into how KLF4 is elevated. Direct regulation of KLF4 by STAT1 appears to make sense not only because the promoter of KLF4 gene contains the binding sites of STAT1 but also because our confirmative results of ChIP assay which established the specific, and direct binding of STAT1 to the promoter of KLF4 gene. As predicted, knockdown of STAT1 decreased the expression of KLF4. STAT1 is a downstream molecule in the conservative pathway that relays signals of pathogen invasion engaged by recognition of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by PRRs (eg., toll-like receptors and RIG-I-like receptors).40,53 It has recently been demonstrated that DAMPs-PRRs pathway promotes cancer progression and enhances metastasis.38

Endogenous double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), often with 5’-triphosphate, are normally extensively shielded by RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to avoid recognition by PRRs under nonpathological condition.54 However, under pathological conditions (e.g., cancer), decreased interaction of dsRNA with RBPs enables dsRNA such as RN7SL1 to become unshielded and secreted via exosomes, mimicking viral RNA to activate a RIG-I-dependent inflammatory response in cancer cells.38 We found that RN7SL2 was abundantly encapsuled in exosomes derived from LSECs (Fig. 8j), indicating that intact exosomes are required to make the RN7SL2 effect. Our results revealed that RN7SL2 (DAMP) delivered by EC-derived exosomes activates RIG-I (PRR) to induce STAT1 expression, comparable with prior reports that RN7SL1, a homolog of RN7SL2, mimics viral RNA to activate RIG-I-STAT1 signaling axis in breast cancer cells and myeloid cells.38,55 Of note, extended analyses in the present study underscore that the RN7SL2-STAT1-KLF4-SEs-RASGRP3 axis may also function in PDAC. The increased expression of RIG-I, STAT1, and KLF4 are significantly correlated with progression and dismal overall survival in patients with PDAC (supplementary Fig. 11).

Curiously, our experimental results argued that RN7SL2 transferred by EC-derived exosomes induces RIG-I signaling to elevate expression of pioneer TF KLF4 and remodel SEs in UM cells, concordant with previous study that has revealed that endothelial cells, one of the most important cell types in liver microenvironment, can form supportive signals to favor tumor cells in metastatic niche.56 This may offer an additional novel modulation way for the understanding “seeds-and-soil” interaction during colonization.

In conclusion, we revealed that KLF4 induces chromatin accessibility and SE remodeling, which drive the expression of corresponding SE-associated genes (e.g., MCL1 and RASGRP3) to promote colonization-related cellular features such as proliferation, survival, and CSC activity (Fig. 8t). The upregulation of the expression of the pioneer factor KLF4 results from RN7SL2-containing exosome-based cross-talk between surrounding niche endothelial cells and tumor cells. Additionally, the validated functions of RASGRP3, such as the promotion of CSCs and motility, are added to the spectrum of functions of this protein, which are not confined to activating MAPK. These exoRN7SL2-STAT1-KLF4-SEs-RASGRP3 findings may help elucidate metastatic colonization and the interaction mode between tumor cells and the tumor niche and may yield novel and viable therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

THZ1 was purchased from Med Chem express (Shanghai, China), prepared as a stock concentration of 20 mM and stored in aliquots at −20 °C. Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Guangzhou, China), and 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was purchased from Invitrogen (Shanghai, China). The antibodies used for Western blotting analysis are listed in supplementary Table 5.

Cell culture

UM cells 92.1 (primary), Mel270 (primary), Omm1 (cutaneous metastatic), Omm2.3 (liver metastatic),26 and the murine PDAC cell line Panc0257 were grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (HyClone, Guangzhou, China) and 2 mM L-glutamine. The human embryonic kidney cell line 293T and the human primary pancreatic carcinoma cell line MIA PaCa-258 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Human adult retinal pigmented epithelium (ARPE-19) cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA) and cultured in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS. CFPAC-1 cells, a cell line derived from human liver metastasis of PDAC,36 were grown in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The molecular authentication of the cell lines 92.1, Mel270, Omm1, Omm2.3, Panc02, MIA PaCa-2, and CFPAC-1 was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) genotyping.

Coculture with HUVECs

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured with an EndoGRO-VEGF Complete Media Kit (Millipore, Burlington, MA). HUVECs were immortalized by transduction with the adenoviral E4ORF1 gene.59 The immortalized HUVECs were either incubated alone for exosome purification assays or mixed with Mel270 cells stably expressing pCDH-GFP at a ratio of 4:1 for another 4 days. GFP+ Mel270 cells in the coculture were then sorted by flow cytometry, followed by ChIP-seq and qRT‒PCR.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined by an MTS assay (Cell Titer 96 Aqueous One Solution Cell Proliferation assay, Promega, Madison, WI). In brief, cells (5000/well) were seeded in 96-well plates overnight before they were treated with DMSO (less than 0.1% v/v) or increasing concentrations of THZ1 for 68 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with MTS for another 4 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm with an automated microplate reader (Bio-Tek, Winooski, VT).

Colony formation assay

A drug-free double-layer soft agar system was used to determine the clonogenicity of UM cells. Briefly, UM cells were pretreated with various concentrations of THZ1 for 24 h (92.1, Mel270, or Omm2.3) or 48 h (Omm1). Cells (8000/well) in 0.5% soft agar were subsequently seeded into 24-well plates coated with 1% base agar and incubated for 10–14 days. The colonies (composed of >50 cells) were counted by inverted optical microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Tumor xenograft experiments

Male NOD/SCID mice weighing 20–25 g were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. (Beijing, China) and bred at the animal facility of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China). Omm1 cells (3 × 106 cells in 200 μL of PBS) were subcutaneously inoculated into the flanks of the mice. Tumor size was measured every other day, and tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: a2 × b × 0.4, where a refers to the smallest diameter and b represents the diameter perpendicular to a.9 When the tumor volume reached ~100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into two groups and treated with either vehicle (10% DMSO in D5W, i.p.) or THZ1 (10 mg/kg, twice daily, i.p.) for 2 weeks (n = 8). The mice were euthanized, and the tumors were immediately harvested, weighed, and photographed. Some of the tumor tissues were homogenized, lysed and processed for Western blotting, and the remaining tumor sections were used for IHC analysis.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model

The PDX model was established as previously described.9 Briefly, MP41 cells (1 × 107) established from patient-derived tumor xenografts27 were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of male NOD/SCID mice. The tumors (~800 mm3) were extracted and cut into small pieces of 50–60 mm3. The tumor samples were sequentially transplanted into the flanks of NOD/SCID mice for a second round of incubation. After three serial mouse-to-mouse passages, the tumor xenografts were used for experiments. Tumor specimens measured 50–60 mm3 in size were inoculated into the flanks of NOD/SCID mice. Once the tumor volume reached ~200 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into two groups and treated with either vehicle or THZ1 (n = 8) for 4 weeks. When the tumor volume in the vehicle treatment group reached ~1500 mm3, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were isolated for further analysis.

Establishing sublines with high liver-metastatic competence

Mel270-HM3 derivatives were established as follows: Mel270-Luc cells (5 × 105) were intrasplenically inoculated into NOG mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) and allowed to grow for four weeks. After the mice were sacrificed under euthanasia conditions, the livers were dissected. Metastatic nodules on the surface of the liver were removed and individually placed in each 6-cm dish supplemented with culture medium. Nodules were cut by sterile ophthalmic scissors; tumor cells were then released into the medium. After the debris was removed, the tumor cells were collected and grown in RPMI 1640 medium (10% FBS). The luciferase+ cells were kept as hepatic metastatic derivative 1 (HM1). HM1 cells were inoculated into NOG mice for another two rounds, yielding the Mel270-HM3 sublines. The molecular authentication of parental Mel270-Luc, Mel270-HM1, Mel270-HM2 and Mel270-HM3 was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) genotyping.

Histological analysis

Liver tissues with metastatic lesions were fixed in Bouin’s solution (75% saturated picric acid, 25% formaldehyde, and 5.0% acetic acid) and embedded in paraffin for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and IHC staining. Ki67 expression was analyzed with a Ki67 MaxVision kit (MaixinBiol, Fuzhou, China). The antibodies used for IHC staining were active caspase-3 (9661; Cell Signaling Technology), S164 p-CDK7 (PA5-105583; Thermo Fisher Scientific), T170 p-CDK7 (PA5-12573; Thermo Fisher Scientific), CDK7 (27027-1-A; Proteintech), and CDK12 (A6816-1-AP; Proteintech).

Limiting dilution assay in NOD/SCID mice

After pretreated with THZ1 (125 nM) for 48 h, increasing numbers of viable Omm1 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the flanks of male NOD/SCID mice (4–6 weeks, n = 6 per group). Thirty-five days later, the numbers of mice with tumors were recorded for CSC frequency calculation by L-Calc dilution software (STEM CELL Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada).9

Hepatic metastasis mouse models

Mel270-Luc cells (5 × 105) were intrasplenically inoculated into NOG mice (female, 6–8 weeks old) and allowed to grow for 4–5 weeks. Placebo or THZ1 (10 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered to randomly grouped mice (n = 5) on the day after cell inoculation for 5 weeks. Panc02-Luc cells (1 × 105) were intrasplenically inoculated into C57BL/6 mice (female, 6–8 weeks old), which were subsequently grown for 3–4 weeks. The luciferase signals were monitored weekly by bioluminescence imaging (IVIS Spectrum, PerkinElmer, Shelton, CT). The livers were isolated, fixed in Bouin’s solution, and photographed. The numbers of nodules on the liver surface were counted. The metastatic nodules in the liver were analyzed by H&E staining.

The mice were kept under specific pathogen-free conditions in the Sun Yat-sen University animal care facility. All animal studies were approved by the Sun Yat-sen University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes.

Apoptosis assay by flow cytometry

UM cells were treated with increasing concentrations of THZ1 for 24 h (92.1, Mel270 and Omm2.3) or 48 h (Omm1) or with fixed concentrations of THZ1 (92.1, Omm2.3 and Omm1 at 500 nM, and Mel270 at 250 nM) for different durations. Cells were collected for apoptosis evaluation with an apoptosis detection kit (Sigma‒Aldrich, Shanghai, China), followed by flow cytometry analysis (BD FACS LSR Fortessa, Franklin Lakes, NJ).

Western blotting analysis

For whole-cell lysates, UM cells or tumor tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) lysis buffer (1×PBS, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS). For the detection of cytochrome c, digitonin extraction buffer (10 mM PIPES, 0.015% digitonin, 300 mM sucrose, 100 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) was used to extract the cytosolic fractions. Additionally, 1×protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Indianapolis, IN), 10 mM sodium fluoride, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate, and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride were freshly added to the aforementioned buffers. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. After blocking with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) dissolved in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with antibodies (1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to IRDye 680 CW or IRDye 800 CW (1:10,000 dilution; LI-COR, Lincoln, NE) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized with an Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR).

Transfection of plasmids and small interfering RNA

UM cells in 10-cm dishes were transfected with 10 μg of plasmids or empty vector or 2 μg of in vitro transcribed RN7SL2, 10 nM specific siRNA or mock siRNA, using polyethyleneimine (Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA). Twenty-four hours later, the cells were treated with THZ1 and subjected to a trypan blue exclusion assay and Western blotting analysis. Nontargeting siRNA and siRNA against RN7SL2 (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) were transfected into HUVECs with polyethyleneimine, the medium was then replaced, and the CM was harvested at 72 h.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

After being treated with THZ1 for 24 h or exosomes for 48 h, the Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells were harvested, washed with PBS, resuspended in 200 μL of serum-free medium, and plated in the upper chamber of 24-well transwell inserts (pore size, 8 μm; Corning, NY). For the migration assay, 5000 cells per well were plated in noncoated inserts. For the invasion assay, 4 × 104 cells per well were plated in Matrigel-coated inserts. After 24 h (for migration) or 48 h (for invasion), the cells in the chambers were wiped with wet cotton swabs. Cells on the bottom of chambers were fixed and stained with 0.1% violet for 20 min. Three random fields of migrated or invaded cells were counted and photographed using an inverted microscope.

Staining and scoring of tumor tissues from UM patients

In accordance with the institutional guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki principles, 65 primary tumors from individuals with UM from 2012 to 2016 at Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center of Sun Yat-sen University were collected. Patient information is summarized in supplementary Table 6. UM patient tumor tissues were subjected to IHC staining for CDK7 (27027-1-A; Proteintech) and RASGRP3 (NBP2-4552S; Novus). Sections (4 μm thick) were incubated in a xylene bath three times and then rehydrated using gradient concentrations of ethanol to remove paraffin, after which antigen retrieval and IHC were performed using citrate buffer (pH 6.1) and a MaxVision kit (Maixin, Fuzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse monoclonal antibodies against CDK7 or RASGRP3 were used at a 1:100 dilution. Omission of the primary antibody (PBS) or mouse IgG isotype served as the negative control for the mouse monoclonal antibody. The tumor sections were scored on the basis of the percentage of tumor cells with positive staining (0: 0%; 1: 1–9%; 2: 10–40%; 3: 41–70%; 4: >70%) and the intensity of staining in positive areas (1: weak staining; 2: moderate staining; 3: strong staining). For each section, the values of the two parameters (the percentage of positive cells and predominant intensity) were multiplied, resulting in scores ranging from 0 to 12. The individuals were subsequently classified into four different groups according to the scores: negative (score: 0), low expression (scores: 1–3), moderate expression (scores: 4–8) and high expression (scores: 9–12).9

Isolation of murine primary hepatocytes and nonparenchymal liver cells

Mouse primary hepatocytes and nonparenchymal liver cells were fractionated by a two-step collagenase perfusion procedure as previously reported.60 C57BL/6 mice (male, 6–8 w) were perfused with step 1 Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) (14175500BT, GIBCO) containing HEPES (15 mM, 15630080, GIBCO), and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (15140163, GIBCO), and subsequently, step 2 was with an enzymatic solution (11995500BT, GIBCO) supplemented with 15 mM HEPES, 1% penicillin‒streptomycin, and 90 U/mL collagenase IV (17104019, Thermo Fisher Scientific). After being filtered through 70 μm nylon mesh, the cell suspensions were centrifuged at 50 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cell pellets collected were hepatocytes. The supernatants were further centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain LSECs, HSCs and KCs. LSECs and KCs were isolated through a density gradient of 50% and 25% Percoll (GE17-0891-09; Sigma-Aldrich). The LSECs were separated from KCs in the gradient interphase layer by selective adhesion to standard Petri dishes and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin‒streptomycin, and mouse VEGF (10 nM, 450-32; Peprotech).

DNA constructs and lentiviral transduction in UM cells

The full-length human RASGRP3 (NCBI reference sequence ID: NM_001139488.1) cDNA was cloned and inserted into the pTSB-CMV-MCS-SBP-3×Flag-GFPF2A-PuroR (lentivirus) construct (Transheep, Shanghai, China) with a ClonExpress MultiS One-Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Scramble (pLKO.1-puro-nontarget shRNA), human RASGRP3-specific target shRNA (pLKO.1-puro-hRASGRP3 target shRNA) and human CDK7-specific target shRNA (pLKO.1-puro-hCDK7 target shRNA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Lentiviruses were generated in 293T cells by cotransfection with the pCMV-dR8.91 and pCMV-VSVG plasmids. Medium containing viral particles were harvested at 48 h and 72 h after transfection and passed through a 0.45 μm filter. UM cells were infected with virus-containing supernatants supplemented with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) for two rounds and then incubated with puromycin (0.5 μg/mL) for approximately 7 days to establish stable clones. The sequences of the shRNAs used are listed in supplementary Table 7.

Real-time quantitative RT‒PCR (qRT‒PCR)

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) and then reversely transcribed to cDNA using a One-step RT Kit (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China). The mRNA levels of target genes were detected using a CFX96 Real-Time Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). The primers used were synthesized by Invitrogen (supplementary Table 8). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an endogenous control.

RNA-seq analysis

Mel270 cells were incubated in medium containing THZ1 at the indicated concentrations or DMSO (less than 0.1% v/v) for 6 h, after which RNA was isolated with an RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® UltraTMRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. HTSeq v0.6.0 was used to count the read numbers mapped to each gene. Transcripts with a mean expression of fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) > 1 were considered active transcripts and used for subsequent analysis.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

An EZ-ChIP Kit (EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA) was used for ChIP according to our previous reports.9 In brief, Mel270 cells (1 × 107) were crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, and the unreacted formaldehyde was quenched with glycine. The cell lysates were sonicated to shear DNA to a length of 200–500 bp and centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min. Protein G agarose (60 µL) was added to each tube to preclear the chromatin before immunoprecipitation with anti-H3K27ac antibodies (5 μg), anti-ELK-1 antibodies (5 μg), anti-STAT1 antibodies (5 μg) or rabbit IgG (negative control) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The protein‒DNA complexes underwent reverse crosslinking overnight. RNase A and proteinase K were used to digest RNA and protein, respectively. The DNA was then purified with spin columns. The DNA that was immunoprecipitated with H3K27ac antibody was used for sequencing, and the DNA fragments enriched by antibodies against ELK-1, STAT1 or IgG were subjected to qRT‒PCR using the specific primers listed in supplementary Table 9.

ChIP-seq analysis

Illumina sequencing libraries were generated using a TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Reads were mapped to the reference genome using BWA v0.7.12 and BWA mem v0.7.12. Wiggle files for gene tracks were created using MACS2, and peaks were visualized in Integrative Genomics Viewer.61 SEs were identified from all H3K27ac-marked regions using the ROSE algorithm as previously described.62 Several actions were taken to reduce the interference of typical enhancer and regulator regions. First, ChIP-seq reads were initially processed to remove adapter sequences using Cutadapt (v2.5) and subsequently aligned to the human genome build hg38 using Bowtie2 (v2.3.5.1) with default parameters.63 Significant H3K27ac enrichment peaks corresponding to certain regions were identified using MACS2 (v2.1.2) with the parameters -m 5.50,64 yielding a narrow peak bed file. The repeat sequences were removed using the peaks in the blacklist database65 (https://github.com/Boyle-Lab/Blacklist/tree/master). Second, the remaining peaks were subjected to the ROSE algorithm to analyze active enhancers and SEs.14 Enhancers were stitched, and SEs were identified using ROSE (https://bitbucket.org/young_computation/rose).66 Active typical enhancers were defined as significant H3K27ac peaks located within 2.5 kb from the nearest transcription start site (TSS). SE constituents located from 2.5 kb to 12.5 kb were stitched together and ranked by their input-subtracted H3K27ac signals in each sample. The ranked enhancers are plotted as ranks (x-axis) vs H3K27ac signals (y-axis). SEs were separated from typical enhancers by identifying an inflection point (where the slope of the cumulative signal vs. rank is 1) of the H3K27ac signal versus the enhancer rank.

ATAC-seq analysis

CFPAC-1 (pTSB-Vector or pTSB-KLF4; scramble, shKLF4#1 or shKLF4#2) cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin. After being washed with PBS, 5 × 104 cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 10 mmol/L NaCl, 3 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.5% IGEPAL CA-630, and 0.1% Tween-20)67 for 10 min. After the nuclei were collected by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min, Tn5 transposase reactions (#TD501; Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) were immediately performed at 37 °C for 30 min. The transposased DNA fragments were subsequently purified with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (#N411; Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). The purified DNA fragments were amplified using a TruePrep Index Kit V2 for Illumina (#TD202; Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library DNA was sequenced in paired-end mode with 150 bp reads on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform.

Gene Ontology and gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using the DAVID tool V6.8 (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Genes whose expression was reduced more than 1.5-fold with low-concentration THZ1 (62.5 nM) treatment at 6 h were considered to be THZ1-sensitive genes. SE-associated genes were analyzed using a similar method. GSEA was performed using GSEA v4.0.3 for Windows as previously described.22 The expression matrix was generated from the RNA-seq data of the Mel270 cells treated with the control and 62.5 nM THZ1 for 6 h. SE-associated genes were defined as “gene set database” in the Mel270 cells. The enrichment score for SE-associated genes was determined with the parameter “Metric for ranking genes” set to “log2_Ratio_of_classes” to calculate the enrichment score for SE-associated genes. The analysis yielded a normalized enrichment score (NES) of −1.0 for the SE-associated gene set, with a P value < 0.01 confirming statistically significant suppression.

Exosome RNA-seq

TRIzol reagent was used to extract exosomal RNA, and rRNAs were depleted with a RiboZero rRNA Removal Kit (Epicenter, Madison, WI). Library preparation was performed as described previously.38 In brief, a NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) was used for modification to ensure that the RNA was not fragmented prior to library preparation. One hundred-base paired-end reads were obtained on the Illumina MiSeq sequencer. To determine whether any reads mapped to viruses that can activate RIG-I, reads included in exosomal RNA were mapped to 19 virus genomes as reported in a previous study.38

5’-ppp RNA-seq

To obtain 5’-ppp RNA, Terminator 5’-Phosphate-Dependent Exonuclease (Epicenter, Madison, WI) was used to degrade 5’-monophosphate RNA in exosomal RNA, followed by treatment with 5’-polyphosphatase (Epicenter, Madison, WI) to convert 5’-ppp to 5’-p, yielding specifically ligated RNA adaptor P5_RNA to RNAs that originally have 5’-ppp. Subsequently, a primer with a 5’ random 9-mer (P7_N9) was used to synthesize cDNA, and NEBNext PCR reagents (New England, Ipswich, MA) were used to amplify the library.38

Exosome RNA-seq and 5’-ppp RNA-seq data analysis

For exosome RNA-seq, the reads were trimmed with Cutadapt v1.9 and mapped to human ribosomal RNA by using Bowtie2 (v2.1.0),63 after which the remaining reads were mapped to the GRCh38 reference genome. For 5’-ppp RNA-seq data analysis, only the first reads of the paired-end reads were utilized, and the reads were then trimmed and aligned as described for the RNA-seq analysis.

Melanosphere culture

After pretreated with DMSO or THZ1 for 24 h, Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells were inoculated into low-attachment 24-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA) with DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2% B27, 20 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF) and 10 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF). Melanospheres with >50 cells were counted using an inverted optical microscope after seven days of incubation. The melanosphere cells were subsequently collected, resuspended in the culture medium mentioned above and replated (5000 cells per well) for the secondary and tertiary rounds of culture.

Aldehyde dehydrogenase assay

Aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) activity was detected using an ALDEFLUORTM kit (Stem Cell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after exposure to the indicated concentration of THZ1 for 24 h, Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells were harvested, and ALDEFLUORTM reagent was added; subsequently, 0.5 mL of the mixture was transferred to a fresh tube containing 5 μL of DEAB reagent. After the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, the cells were pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in assay buffer and subjected to analysis of ALDH activity with a FACS LSR Fortessa flow cytometer.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

The SLUG promoter reporter construct (−908 to −2) SLUGg-pGL2 was obtained from Addgene (cat#31695). The Renilla luciferase reporter construct and dual-luciferase assay kit were from Promega (cat# E2231). Mel270 cells with stable RASGRP3 silencing, scramble-Mel270 cells, Mel270 cells with stable RASGRP3 overexpression or vector-expressing Mel270 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cotransfected with plasmids (0.5 μg) and Renilla (1 ng) using polyethyleneimine. These cells were then exposed to control or THZ1 (62.5 nM) for another 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected, and luciferase activity was determined with a dual-luciferase assay kit (Promega, Madison, WI).

Immunofluorescence assay

After pretreated with THZ1 for 24 h, the Mel270 and Omm2.3 cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 30 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with phalloidin (1:40 dilution) at room temperature for 40 min. The cells were subsequently washed with cold PBS three times to remove excess phalloidin and stained with DAPI for 10 min. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 710). To quantify the fluorescence intensity, three optical fields per experimental condition were analyzed.

Rac1, RhoA, and Cdc42 activation assays

Rac1, RhoA, and Cdc42 activation was measured with a pull-down assay as previously described.9 Briefly, UM cells (2 × 107) were lysed with ice-cold lysis buffer. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube. The lysates were subsequently incubated with 20 μg of GST-PAK1-PBD or 400 μg of GST-Rhotekin-RBD on glutathione beads for 1 h with rotation at 4 °C. The beads were subsequently washed and boiled in 2×SDS sample buffer containing dithiothreitol. GTP-bound Rac1, RhoA, and Cdc42 were examined by Western blotting with antibodies against each of these proteins.

Exosomal experiments

To isolate exosomes, CM from HUVECs was prepared in the presence of exosome-depleted FBS. Briefly, cellular debris was discarded by centrifugation at 500 × g for 10 min and 12,000 × g for 20 min. The CM was subsequently filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Steriflip, Millipore, Burlington, MA), followed by centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 90 min (Beckman Ti70, Brea, CA). The exosomal pellets were resuspended in 50 μL of PBS and subjected to electron microscopy examination. The size and quantification of the exosomes were determined with a NanoSight LM10 (NanoSight Ltd., Amesbury, Wiltshire, UK). Exosome proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer.

For exosome tracing, the purified exosomes were labeled with Dil (cat# 468495; Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China) for 10 min, washed with 20 mL of PBS, ultracentrifuged, and resuspended in PBS. Labeled exosomes were added to the Mel270-GFP, Omm2.3-GFP, and Mel270-HM3 subline#1-GFP cells for 24 h, which were subsequently visualized by confocal microscopy (Nikon C2, Tokyo, Japan). Z-stack images were acquired at 0.3 μm/45 s in axial position for 20–35 sections and analyzed by Imaris Viewer 11.0.0 (Oxford Instruments, Oxford, UK).

To extract exosome-packaged RNA, exosomes isolated from the CM of HUVECs were collected. Exosome-packaged RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent. For qRT‒PCR, exosome-packaged ncRNAs were normalized against the exogenous reference, λ polyA+ RNA (External Standard Kit, 3789; Takara, Dalian, China).

In vitro transcription

RN7SL2 and its antisense sequence were transcribed in vitro with a MEGAscript T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The secondary structure of the RNA was allowed to form in RNA structure buffer (10 mM Tris (pH 7.0), 0.1 M KCl, and 10 mM MgCl2) at 95 °C for 2 min, on ice for 3 min, and at room temperature for 30 min.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Mel270 cells (1 × 107) were subjected to RNA immunoprecipitation with a Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation kit (cat#17-700; Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Briefly, cells were suspended in RIP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and RNase inhibitor. Subsequently, the cell lysates were immunoprecipitated at 4 °C overnight with magnetic beads and incubated with 5 μg of anti-rabbit IgG or anti-rabbit RIG-I for 30 min. RNA was subsequently purified from the RNA‒protein complexes and subjected to qRT‒PCR.

RNA pull-down

In vitro transcribed RN7SL2 and its antisense sequence were biotin-labeled with a Pierce™ RNA 3’ End Biotinylation Kit (cat#20160; Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, MA), and the secondary structure was folded as described above; then, 1 mg of protein extracted from Mel270 cells was mixed together. Dynabeads M-280 Streptavidin (cat# 60210; Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) were added to the RNA‒protein complexes, which were subsequently incubated for 1 h at room temperature and then washed with RIP buffer, after which the retrieved proteins were analyzed by Western blotting.

Data analysis

The data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism Software, version 10.4.0 (San Diego, CA). Differences between two groups were analyzed with Student’s t test. Statistical analysis among multiple groups was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a post hoc test and the Tukey test. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves were analyzed with the log-rank test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
