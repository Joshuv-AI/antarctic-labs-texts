Optimal exercise modalities and doses for improving pro-atherogenic lipid profiles in patients with metabolic syndrome: a systematic review with pairwise, network, and dose–response meta-analyses
Authors: Huimin Ding, Liqun Jiang, Hyun Seo, Buongo Chun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Dyslipidemia, particularly abnormalities in pro-atherogenic lipid profiles, is a key pathophysiological feature. Background

Metabolic syndrome (MetS), characterized by central obesity, dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, and elevated blood pressure, is increasingly prevalent worldwide and has become a major public health challenge due to its strong association with cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, and all-cause mortality [1, 2]. A hallmark of MetS is dyslipidemia, which plays a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis and is reflected by abnormalities in pro-atherogenic lipid profiles [3], including elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and lipid ratios such as TC/HDL-C and TG/HDL-C, alongside reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [4]. These lipid abnormalities are key determinants of atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk and critical targets for intervention in MetS [5, 6].

Exercise is a safe, cost-effective, and widely endorsed strategy for managing MetS, consistently recommended by international guidelines [7–9]. Accumulating evidence from randomized controlled trials [10–12] and meta-analyses [13–17] shows that exercise improves insulin sensitivity, modulates visceral adiposity, attenuates inflammation, and favorably alters lipid metabolism. Yet, critical evidence gaps persist: most studies focus on conventional lipids rather than comprehensive pro-atherogenic profiles, compare only limited exercise modalities, and rarely characterize dose–response relationships needed to guide optimal prescription [18, 19].

This study aims to systematically evaluate the effects of multiple exercise modalities on pro-atherogenic lipid profiles (LDL-C, TG, TC, non-HDL-C, TC/HDL-C, and TG/HDL-C) in individuals with metabolic syndrome and to model the dose–response relationships between standardized exercise volume (METs-min/week) and these lipid profiles to identify clinically meaningful thresholds. Through this approach, the study seeks to provide more comprehensive and practice-oriented evidence to inform exercise prescription for adults with metabolic syndrome.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [20], the PERSiST guidance for sport and exercise medicine reviews [21], and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [22]. The protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251082352).

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from inception to February 17, 2025.We subsequently updated the data on September 3, 2025, and incorporated the results into the manuscript. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, covering keywords such as "metabolic syndrome," "physical activity," "cardiometabolic risk factors," and "randomized controlled trial." Full search details are provided in Additional file 1.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data using a standardized form, with inter-rater reliability assessed by Cohen’s kappa statistic. For the initial full-text screening (n=207), inter-rater agreement was excellent (κ=0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.91; Additional file 2). The two additional RCTs identified in the updated search were independently assessed by the same reviewers, and consensus was reached without disagreement.

Extracted data included: (a) study information, (b) participant characteristics, (c) intervention details, and (d) outcomes (all pro-atherogenic lipid profiles). For continuous outcomes, the mean difference (MD) of change-from-baseline was used as the effect size. If the standard deviation (SD) of change was unavailable, it was imputed using 95% confidence intervals or calculated based on a pre-specified correlation coefficient (r = 0.8) [23]. For Bayesian NMAs (Gemtc package), group mean changes, SDs, and sample sizes were used [24]; for frequentist NMAs (netmeta package), data were converted into treatment effects (TE) and standard errors (SE) [25]. Weekly exercise doses (METs-min/week) were calculated by combining frequency, session duration, and assigned metabolic equivalent values (METs) [26], standardized according to the 2024 Adult Physical Activity Guidelines [27]. All formulas are detailed in Additional file 3.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [28]. Each domain and the overall risk were rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” with disagreements resolved by consensus. Certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE approach, following GRADE guidance for NMAs, with overall certainty rated as high, moderate, low, or very low [29].

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analysis

Pairwise meta-analyses were conducted using the Gemtc [24] and netmeta [25] packages in R(R version 4.4.3). Gemtc was used to generate forest plots for effect estimation and direction. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed by I2 statistics and Cochran’s Q test [30]. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test, both implemented via the netmeta package in R [31].

NMAs

Bayesian NMAs were performed using the Generalised Mixed Treatment Comparisons (GeMTC) package with random-effects hierarchical models, executed through JAGS via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation (4 chains, 5000 burn-in, 20,000 iterations, thinning interval of 10) [32]. Non-informative priors were specified for treatment effects (Normal[0, 10,000]) and for between-study heterogeneity (Uniform[0, 5]), consistent with standard Bayesian NMA practice [33]. Convergence and model fit were evaluated using Gelman-Rubin diagnostics, trace plots, and posterior density plots [34], while residual deviance and the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) were used to assess model adequacy. Intervention ranking was performed by calculating SUCRA values. Consistency was evaluated both locally and globally: locally using node-splitting methods to compare direct and indirect evidence for specific contrasts, and globally using a design-by-treatment interaction model to detect inconsistency across the entire network [35]. Additional analyses with the netmeta package generated node-splitting inconsistency heatmaps and NMA comparison heatmaps [25]. Meta-regression was conducted to explore the impact of potential effect modifiers, including publication year, sample size, intervention duration, income level, and sex ratio [36]. Leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analyses were performed for covariates with significant or borderline associations in the meta-regression (ROB and AGE), as well as for different exercise modalities, to test the robustness of the results [37].For each exclusion, the re-estimated mean effect size, standard error, and width of the 95% Crl were systematically compared with those from the full modality-specific model. Statistical significance was defined a priori as a 95% CrI that did not include zero, and precision was further graded according to CrI width as High (<0.2), Moderate (0.2–0.5), or Low (>0.5) [38, 39].

Dose–response NMAs

We conducted Bayesian random-effects dose–response NMAs to examine the associations between METs-min/week across modalities (CAE, RT, CAREX, HIIT, MBE, and CG) and pro-atherogenic lipid profiles (LDL-C, non-HDL-C, TG, TC, TC/HDL-C, TG/HDL-C). For network linkage and dose–response estimation, dose levels were set at 0 (CG), 250, 500, 750, 1250, and 1500 METs-min/week. To capture potential non-linear relationships, three alternative model structures were fitted: Emax, quadratic polynomial, and restricted cubic splines (RCS) with knots at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of the dose distribution [40, 41]. Model fit was assessed using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), residual deviance, and the effective number of parameters, with the RCS model demonstrating the best fit. Based on this model, posterior predictive dose–response curves were generated across the range of 0–2000 METs-min/week for each intervention type. Treatment effects were further summarized within predefined dose categories (0–500, 500–1000, 1000–1500, and 1500–2000 METs-min/week) with 95% credible intervals (CrIs), and statistical significance was defined as a 95% CrI excluding zero [42]. Posterior ranking probabilities for each intervention-dose combination were also calculated and summarized. All Bayesian models were implemented in JAGS (v4.3.0) with three parallel Markov chains, each consisting of 10,000 burn-in iterations and 50,000 sampling iterations [40]. Convergence was evaluated using trace plots, posterior density distributions, and the Gelman-Rubin statistic (R-hat < 1.05). The validity of the consistency assumption was examined globally by comparing the standard consistency model with an unrelated mean effects (UME) specification, and locally through node-splitting analyses [41, 43]. Smoothed density curves were also generated to illustrate the distribution of weekly doses by modality, enabling assessment of dose clustering that could influence model precision. All Bayesian dose–response NMAs were performed in R (version 4.4.3) using the MBNMA dose package in conjunction with JAGS (v4.3.0, accessed via R2jags).

Complementary dose–response analyses, irrespective of exercise modality, were conducted to characterize the overall relationship between total weekly exercise volume and pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. Using Stata (version 17) and the drmeta package, one-stage random-effects RCS meta-regressions were fitted to METs-min/week, non-linearity was formally tested with Wald χ2 statistics, and results were expressed as mean differences (mmol/L) with 95% confidence intervals [44]. Fitted curves were then plotted across the full observed dose range.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 2375 potentially relevant records were initially identified through a comprehensive database search targeting randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on exercise interventions in patients with metabolic syndrome. After removing duplicates, 1723 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 1516 were excluded due to irrelevance to the eligibility criteria. Subsequently, the full texts of 207 articles were assessed for eligibility. An updated search conducted on September 3, 2025, yielded two additional eligible RCTs, and the statistical analyses were updated accordingly. Ultimately, 49 RCTs involving 4144 participants with metabolic syndrome were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Risk of bias

Risk of bias for all 49 included RCT was assessed using the Cochrane RoB2 tool. Most studies were judged as "low risk" in the domains of randomization process and completeness of outcome data. However, several studies were rated as "unclear" or "some concerns" in the domains of blinding and outcome measurement, indicating the potential presence of systematic bias during intervention implementation (Additional file 5: Table S2).

Comparative efficacy of exercise modalities

Pairwise and network meta-analyses

Across both pairwise and network meta-analyses, MBE showed the greatest reductions in LDL-C (Fig. 2a,b), non-HDL-C (Additional files 6–7, Figures S1-S2), TG (Additional files 8–9, Figures S3-S4), TC (Additional files 10–11, Figures S5-S6), TC/HDL-C (Additional files 12–13, Figures S7-S8), and TG/HDL-C (Additional files 14–15, Figures S9-S10), with SUCRA values ranging from 90 to 95%. CAREX and HIIT demonstrated consistent and robust effects across several outcomes, whereas CAE and RT showed more modest but favorable effects, particularly on lipid ratio indices. Notably, the superiority of MBE was sensitive to single-study influence, indicating reduced robustness for certain endpoints. Consistency, risk of bias, certainty of evidence, and sensitivity analyses

Node-splitting analyses demonstrated that direct and indirect estimates were largely consistent across all lipid outcomes, and this pattern was visually corroborated by heatmaps. Only a few isolated treatment comparisons exhibited evidence of local inconsistency (Additional file 17: Table S4, Additional file 18: Figure S11). Bayesian diagnostics confirmed adequate convergence and stable posterior distributions (Additional file 19: Figure S12). Funnel plots were generally symmetrical, and Egger’s tests yielded P > 0.05 for all outcomes, suggesting no evidence of publication bias (Additional file 20: Figure S13). GRADE assessments indicated moderate certainty for most outcomes, with some downgraded to low certainty due to risk of bias and imprecision, but none rated as very low (Additional file 21: Table S5). Meta-regression showed that publication year, sample size, intervention duration, income level, and male proportion did not significantly modify treatment effects, whereas ROB and age were identified as significant effect modifiers (Additional file 22: Table S6, Additional file 23: Figure S14). Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses confirmed that most results were robust. However, the associations of non-HDL-C and TG with ROB were sensitive to Study 4 [47], while the association between AGE and TG/HDL-C was sensitive to several studies [45, 47, 56, 62, 75] (Additional file 24: Table S7, Additional file 25: Figure S15). At the intervention level, the conclusions for MBE were strongly influenced by Study 4, as its exclusion led to the loss of significance for non-HDL-C, TC, and TC/HDL-C (Additional file 26: Table S8, Additional file 27: Figure S16).

Dose–response relationships and threshold effects

Total exercise volume dose–response NMAs

Dose–response NMAs examined the associations between METs-min/week and pro-atherogenic lipid profiles, consistently demonstrating nonlinear patterns. LDL-C reductions became significant at volumes above ~244 METs-min/week, with the greatest benefit observed at 400–600 METs-min/week (− 0.02 to − 0.28 mmol/L; Fig. 3a). TG reductions reached significance at volumes above ~510 METs-min/week, peaking at 510–855 METs-min/week (− 0.05 to − 0.10 mmol/L; Fig. 3c). Beyond these ranges, the 95% CrIs crossed the null line, indicated that improvements peaked at moderate doses, with diminishing returns at higher levels. Concentration-based lipid indices

LDL-C

A dose–response threshold for LDL-C reduction was observed at approximately 244 METs-min/week, with the greatest benefit occurring within 400–600 METs-min/week. CAREX at 500–1000 METs-min/week produced a moderate but significant reduction in LDL-C (− 0.14 to − 0.50 mmol/L), while HIIT at 1000–1500 METs-min/week achieved a larger reduction (− 5.4 to − 6.9 mmol/L). No statistically significant effects were observed for CAE, MBE, or RT. Beyond 1500 METs-min/week, the LDL-C–lowering effect plateaued, with no further improvement (Fig. 4, Additional file 31: Table S11).Fig. 4Dose–response curves for non-HDL-C across different exercise types (RCS Model)

Dose–response curves for non-HDL-C across different exercise types (RCS Model)

non-HDL-C

CAE increased non-HDL-C at 500–1000 and 1000–1500 METs-min/week (0.37–0.74 and 0.47–0.63 mmol/L, respectively). CAREX at 500–1000 METs-min/week reduced non-HDL-C by 0.04 to 0.43 mmol/L, and HIIT at 1000–1500 METs-min/week showed a reduction of 2.26 to 3.58 mmol/L. No significant effects were observed for MBE or RT (Additional file 32: Figure S18,Additional file 33: Table S12).

TG

A clear dose–response threshold for TG was observed at approximately 510 METs-min/week, with the largest reductions occurring within 510–855 METs-min/week. CAREX at 500–1000 METs-min/week was associated with a moderate but significant decrease in TG (− 0.05 to − 0.18 mmol/L), while HIIT at 1000–1500 METs-min/week produced a larger reduction (− 2.3 to − 3.1 mmol/L). No significant effects were observed for CAE, MBE, or RT. Beyond 1500 METs-min/week, the effect plateaued and no further improvements were detected (Additional file 34: Figure S19, Additional file 35: Table S13).

TC

CAREX at 500–1000 METs-min/week lowered TC by 0.15 to 0.45 mmol/L, and HIIT at 1000–1500 METs-min/week reduced TC by 5.0 to 6.5 mmol/L. No significant effects were observed for CAE, MBE, or RT (Additional file 36: Figure S20, Additional file 37: Table S14).

Ratio-based lipid indices

TC/HDL-C

CAE was associated with a paradoxical increase in TC/HDL-C within the moderate-to-high dose ranges of 500–1000 METs-min/week (0.41 to 2.80 mmol/L) and 1000–1500 METs-min/week (0.37 to 2.72 mmol/L). In contrast, MBE demonstrated a robust and clinically meaningful reduction in TC/HDL-C at 500–1000 METs-min/week (− 0.57 to − 0.28 mmol/L). No other exercise modalities exhibited statistically significant dose–response relationships across the examined intervals (Additional file 38: Figure S21, Additional file 39: Table S15).

TG/HDL-C

HIIT at 1000–1500 METs-min/week was associated with a pronounced reduction in TG/HDL-C (− 3.62 to − 2.36 mmol/L), whereas no other exercise modalities demonstrated significant dose–response relationships across the examined intervals (Additional file 40: Figure S22, Additional file 41: Table S16).

The ranking analysis indicated that, among the concentration-based lipid indices, the most likely optimal interventions were HIIT at 561 METs-min/week for LDL-C, HIIT at 672 METs-min/week for TG, HIIT at 1350 METs-min/week for non-HDL-C, and CAREX at 620 METs-min/week for TC. Taken together, the dose–response curves for the concentration-based lipid indices consistently exhibited nonlinear patterns, with the most pronounced benefits observed within 500–1500 METs-min/week. At doses exceeding 1500 METs-min/week, the effects attenuated or plateaued, and statistical significance was no longer observed. The ranking analysis further indicated that, for ratio-based lipid indices, MBE at 675 METs-min/week had the highest probability of maximizing TC/HDL-C reduction, whereas HIIT at 1000 METs-min/week ranked first for TG/HDL-C (Additional File 42: Table S17).

However, the dose–response curve for RT displayed particularly wide 95% CrIs across several outcomes. This was likely attributable to the clustering of available trials around 500–700 METs-min/week, with few data points outside this range. Such an uneven distribution restricted the model’s ability to estimate effects across the broader dose spectrum, thereby increasing statistical uncertainty and reducing the precision of RT-related estimates.

Validity, convergence and sensitivity analyses of dose–response models

The dose–response network models demonstrated good overall validity and stability. Node-splitting analyses identified only a few local inconsistencies without material impact on global estimates (Additional file 43: Table S18). All parameters achieved full convergence with stable posterior distributions (Additional file 44: Figure S23). Dose distributions demonstrated clustering of RT around 500–700 METs-min/week and CAE around 700–900 METs-min/week, whereas CAREX, HIIT, and MBE covered broader ranges (Additional file 45: Figure S24).

Exercise prescription recommendations for MetS

Based on the observed dose–response relationships across different exercise modalities and pro-atherogenic lipid profiles, and in reference to the 2024 Physical Activity Guidelines [27] for Adults and the WHO recommendations on physical activity for adults [9], this study proposes individualized exercise prescription recommendations for patients with MetS (Table 2). These recommendations may serve as a clinically actionable framework for developing personalized exercise interventions in clinical practice for MetS management. Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and dose–response NMAs is the first to evaluate the effects of exercise interventions on pro-atherogenic lipid profiles in patients with MetS and to further characterize nonlinear dose–response relationships. The main findings are as follows. First, compared with CG, exercise interventions were significantly associated with improvements in most pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. Second, dose–response analyses showed that clinically meaningful benefits for LDL-C and TG emerged at approximately 244 and 510 METs-min/week, respectively, while effects attenuated and lost statistical significance beyond ~900 METs-min/week. These thresholds are below the WHO recommendation of 600–1200 METs-min/week [9], suggesting that clinically relevant improvements can be achieved even at lower exercise volumes. Third, modality-specific patterns were observed: higher-dose HIIT and moderate-dose CAREX and MBE were significantly associated with improvements in pro-atherogenic lipid profiles, whereas RT showed wide CrIs and limited precision due to dose clustering at 500–700 METs-min/week. Finally, results appeared sensitive to ROB and age, and MBE effects were less robust, losing significance when individual studies were excluded.

Comparison with previous studies

To our knowledge, this systematic review and dose–response network meta-analysis included 49 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 4144 patients with MetS and multiple exercise modalities, making it the most comprehensive study to date in this field. Importantly, this work is the first to systematically incorporate ratio-based lipid indices such as total cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TC/HDL-C) and triglycerides to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (TG/HDL-C), thereby filling a critical evidence gap left unaddressed by previous reviews.

In the analysis of different exercise modalities, this study further revealed several clinically meaningful distinctions. Prior meta-analyses of exercise interventions in MetS populations consistently reported beneficial effects of CAE and RT on lipid outcomes [15, 18, 19, 92, 93], findings that were broadly confirmed by our pairwise and network meta-analyses. However, our dose–response analysis did not detect significant associations between RT or CAE and pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences: previous reviews primarily synthesized average intervention effects, whereas our dose–response analysis modeled exercise volume as a continuous variable. Notably, the majority of included RT trials clustered around 500–700 METs-min/week, and CAE trials around 700–900 METs-min/week. Such concentration within narrow dose ranges restricted the model’s ability to extrapolate across broader exposure levels, resulting in imprecise estimates [40, 42, 94]. Thus, although the overall benefits of RT and CAE are supported by traditional evidence synthesis, clarifying dose–response relationships and identifying optimal thresholds will require future trials that systematically investigate both lower and higher dose ranges.

Previous studies of HIIT in MetS populations have yielded conflicting results [88, 95, 96], and several reviews raised concerns about safety and adherence. Our meta-analysis indicated that HIIT was significantly associated with improvements in pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. The dose–response analysis further showed that moderate-to-high training volumes (500–1500 METs-min/week) may confer the greatest benefits. Importantly, the included trials encompassed a wide range of HIIT dosing protocols, and multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings, thereby strengthening the reliability of this evidence [37]. The potential mechanisms underlying these effects may involve enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, increased fatty acid oxidation, and improved skeletal muscle uptake of glucose and lipids, collectively contributing to favorable lipid modulation.

In the network and pairwise analyses, MBE ranked highest across all pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. This finding is partly consistent with previous systematic reviews of yoga and other mind–body exercise interventions in MetS populations, which reported improvements in lipid metabolism, particularly reductions in TG and TC [97]. Nevertheless, the dose–response model identified a statistically significant benefit only for TG/HDL-C at approximately 675 METs-min/week. This limitation may be attributable to the small number of available trials (n=6). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the overall conclusion was highly dependent on a single study (Study 4) [47], in which ROB and participant age were identified as key effect modifiers, further underscoring the fragility of current MBE evidence [22]. Therefore, the true effect of MBE in lipid management for MetS remains uncertain and warrants confirmation in larger, methodologically rigorous RCTs.

Findings for CAREX were consistent with prior meta-analyses identifying it as one of the most effective modalities for reducing cardiometabolic risk in MetS, with evidence of superior lipid improvements [19, 92]. Dose–response modeling further indicated clinically meaningful improvements within moderate dose ranges (500–1000 METs-min/week). The potential mechanisms underlying these effects include enhanced energy metabolism and fatty acid oxidation derived from CAE [98, 99], coupled with improved muscle mass, basal metabolic rate, and insulin sensitivity promoted by RT [100–102], together producing synergistic benefits across multiple metabolic pathways [103]. These findings not only corroborate and refine previous evidence but also provide a stronger foundation for optimizing CAREX prescriptions in clinical practice.

In addition, the dose–response results of this study provide important clinical insights: total weekly exercise volume demonstrated a nonlinear relationship with pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. LDL-C and TG were observed at relatively low exercise volumes (≈244 and 510 METs-min/week), below the minimum level recommended by the World Health Organization. These thresholds fall below the World Health Organization’s recommended minimum of 600–1200 METs-min/week [9], indicating that even patients with low physical activity levels can achieve substantial lipid benefits with modest exercise doses. Conversely, when weekly exercise volumes exceeded approximately 900 METs-min/week, the effects tended to plateau. This phenomenon may be driven by multiple mechanisms: first, greater exercise volumes increase energy utilization efficiency, leading to diminishing marginal benefits from further lipid oxidation [104]; second, high-dose exercise may induce compensatory hepatic lipid synthesis and elevated very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) secretion, offsetting part of the benefit [105]; and third, excessive training loads may trigger chronic stress responses and elevated cortisol levels, adversely affecting lipoprotein metabolism [106, 107].

Clinical implications

This meta-analysis provides clinically relevant evidence for exercise prescription in patients with MetS, with a particular focus on pro-atherogenic lipid profiles. Clinically meaningful improvements were observed at relatively low thresholds (approximately 244 METs-min/week for LDL-C and 510 METs-min/week for TG), equivalent to two 20-min sessions of moderate-intensity jogging or three 30-min sessions of brisk walking/cycling per week. This suggests that even patients with low baseline activity or physical limitations can achieve tangible lipid benefits through modest and attainable exercise, supporting the use of lower entry thresholds in clinical consultations to enhance motivation and adherence among patients with sedentary lifestyles.

Differences across exercise modalities further support the development of individualized exercise prescriptions. CAREX and HIIT showed the most consistent benefits at moderate doses, with CAREX demonstrating significant efficacy in improving non-HDL-C, TG, and TC within the 500–1000 METs-min/week range, and HIIT showing favorable effects at moderate-to-high volumes (≈600–1000 METs-min/week). Effects diminished beyond these levels, underscoring the need for appropriate load distribution to avoid overtraining and to ensure safety. CAE and RT showed overall positive effects, although the optimal dose–response thresholds remain undefined, highlighting the need for more systematic trials across a broader range of exercise doses. Although MBE ranked highly across several lipid outcomes, the evidence remains limited, as results were sensitive to a single trial, and further high-quality studies are needed to confirm these findings. Overall, these results underscore that structured exercise represents an effective, quantifiable, and highly adaptable intervention capable of improving pro-atherogenic lipid profiles and reducing cardiovascular risk in patients with MetS.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first systematic review and dose–response network meta-analysis to comprehensively evaluate both concentration-based lipid indices (LDL-C, TG, TC, non-HDL-C) and ratio-based indices (TC/HDL-C, TG/HDL-C) in individuals with MetS. This dual approach provided a more nuanced assessment of pro-atherogenic risk than previous reviews. By integrating pairwise, network, and dose–response meta-analyses, we were able to generate a multidimensional synthesis of exercise modality and dosage effects, identify nonlinear associations, and propose clinically actionable thresholds. The standardization of exercise dose as METs-min/week allowed comparability across diverse interventions, while multiple sensitivity analyses, RoB assessments, and GRADE evaluations further enhanced the robustness and transparency of the evidence base. Several limitations must also be acknowledged, together with the steps we took to address them.

First, data sparsity at extreme dose levels-particularly for RT and CAE-limited the precision of dose–response estimates. To mitigate this, we employed Bayesian hierarchical modeling with informative priors, which stabilizes estimates under sparse conditions, and we further performed leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to confirm the robustness of our findings.

Second, in some trials, ratio indices and METs exposure had to be indirectly estimated, which may have introduced measurement error or misclassification. To reduce this risk, we applied standardized conversion methods based on the 2024 Compendium of Physical Activities, conducted internal consistency checks across datasets, and explicitly reported all assumptions in the supplementary materials.

Third, there was inevitable heterogeneity in intervention protocols, duration, and adherence across trials. We attempted to minimize its impact by conducting pre-specified subgroup analyses, performing meta-regression with study-level covariates (e.g., age, ROB, sample size), and running ROB-based sensitivity analyses to test the stability of pooled estimates.

Finally, potential unmeasured confounders, such as background diet, medication use, or co-interventions, could not be fully excluded. Nevertheless, we restricted inclusion to randomized controlled trials, excluded studies with mixed or multicomponent interventions that would obscure attribution, and carefully extracted study-level information to reduce confounding risk.

Conclusions

This systematic review and dose–response network meta-analysis suggests that structured exercise may improve pro-atherogenic lipid profiles in patients with MetS. Clinically meaningful benefits for LDL-C and TG appeared at relatively low exercise volumes (≈244 and 510 METs-min/week), below WHO recommendations, indicating that modest activity could provide metabolic improvements. CAREX and HIIT emerged as the most promising modalities within moderate-to-high dose ranges, while MBE ranked highly but with limited and fragile evidence. CAE and RT showed overall favorable effects, although dose–response thresholds remain uncertain due to trial clustering. Taken together, these findings indicate that structured exercise represents a potentially effective and adaptable strategy, and provide preliminary dose-based targets to inform personalized prescriptions for MetS management.
