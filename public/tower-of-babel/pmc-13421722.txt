Comment on “Associations of Physical Fitness During Childhood with Arterial Structure and Stiffness in Adolescence: An 8-Year Follow-Up Study”
Authors: Kate M. Sansum, Leigh Gabel, Kurt J. Smith, Ali M. McManus
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We recently enjoyed the article by Laitinen and colleagues [1] investigating the associations between physical fitness in childhood and measures of arterial structure and stiffness in adolescence. The review by Baumgartner et al. [2] highlights that the existing literature is predominantly cross-sectional, so we were thrilled to see longitudinal analyses to further our knowledge in the area.

We were also pleased to see that the authors recognised the need to control for the effects of maturation in their . However, we wanted to raise awareness about the issues in the choice of maturational assessment method and to offer alternatives going forward. It is imperative that paediatric researchers select the most appropriate measure of maturation based on their sample and research question, yet peer reviewers in non-paediatric journals often lack the knowledge to question our choices for maturation assessment.

The rationale for selecting Tanner stages [3,4] as the assessment method for maturation is absent [1], but we assume that Tanner stages were chosen to link with the potential hormonal changes, e.g., that might influence the vascular outcomes [5]. Although Tanner stages are regularly used, they are inappropriate for mixed-sex samples, because a female who is Tanner stage 3 for breast development may not be at the same level of sexual development as a male who is Tanner stage 3 for genitalia development [6]. Furthermore, if a male and a female are both experiencing their peak height velocity (a measure of somatic maturation), the female is likely to be Tanner stage 3 for breast development, whereas the male is likely to be Tanner stages 4–5 for genitalia development [7]. In addition, some Tanner stage 1 individuals exhibit similarly elevated testosterone or follicle-stimulating hormone concentrations (measured non-invasively via urine) as higher Tanner stage individuals [8]. The use of Tanner staging may have influenced the results in Laitinen and colleagues [1], because the effects of maturation have not been appropriately controlled for, leaving residual confounding variance which could have inflated or masked associations. It is not mentioned if pubic hair staging data were available, but we would caution against using this based on the differences in the timing of pubic hair development between the sexes [3,4]. The use of pubic hair staging is more a measure of adrenarchal development, rather than complete sexual maturation [9].

When researchers have a paediatric sample with both sexes, we would advocate for the use of age in years from peak height velocity as a measure of somatic maturation to align participants. In cross-sectional studies, this can be estimated from prediction equations, such as Mirwald et al. [10] or Moore et al. [11]. In longitudinal datasets with multiple measurements around peak height velocity, it can be directly calculated for individual participants using techniques, such as SuperImposition by Translation And Rotation (SITAR) models [12].

We urge authors to review the pertinent literature for assessing maturation and encourage them to select a more appropriate assessment method going forward in their research, especially in mixed-sex samples.
