Federated Majorize-Minimization: Beyond Parameter Aggregation
Aymeric Dieuleveut aymeric.dieuleveut@polytechnique.edu, Gersende Fort gersende.fort@laas.fr, Mahmoud Hegazy mahmoud.hegazy@polytechnique.edu, Hoi-To Wai htwai@se.cuhk.edu.hk

Abstract
This paper proposes a unified approach for designing stochastic optimization algorithms that robustly scale to the federated learning setting. Our work studies a class of Majorize-Minimization (MM) problems, which possesses a linearly parameterized family of majorizing surrogate functions. This framework encompasses (proximal) gradient-based algorithms for (regularized) smooth objectives, the Expectation Maximization algorithm, and many problems seen as variational surrogate MM. We show that our framework motivates a unifying algorithm called Stochastic Approximation Stochastic Surrogate MM ( SA-SSMM ), which includes previous stochastic MM procedures as special instances. We then extend SA-SSMM to the federated setting, while taking into consideration common bottlenecks such as data heterogeneity, partial participation, and communication constraints; this yields FedMM . The originality of FedMM is to learn locally and then aggregate information characterizing the surrogate majorizing function , contrary to classical algorithms which learn and aggregate the original parameter . Finally, to showcase the flexibility of this methodology beyond our theoretical setting, we use it to design an algorithm for computing optimal transport maps in the federated setting.

Introduction

Consider the following optimization problem

\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\penalty\ W(\theta):=\left(f(\theta)+g(\theta)\right),\qquad\text{with}\quad f(\theta):=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim\pi}\left[\ell(Z,\theta)\right];

(1)

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\left(-\infty,+\infty\right]
is a proper lower
semi-continuous convex function with domain
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
measurable function such that
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[|\ell(Z,\theta)|\right]<+\infty
for any
\theta\in\Theta
and
\pi
denotes
a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. This setting covers both the
batch learning
when
\pi
is the empirical distribution of a (possibly large) data set
\{Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{N}\}
and we solve

\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ell(Z_{j},\theta)+g(\theta))

and the
online learning
when we have to process an i.i.d. stream of data

\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots\}
with the distribution
\pi
.

We are interested in the setting where
majorizing functions
can be computed on
f
, which can then be minimized. This is a framework referred to as Majorize-Minimization (MM)
(
Lange 2013
)
, or surrogate optimization
(
Lange et al. 2000
)
.
More concretely, starting from the parameter
\theta_{t}
, a standard MM algorithm constructs a surrogate
U_{t}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f(\theta_{t})=U_{t}(\theta_{t})
and
f(\theta)\leq U_{t}(\theta)
for any
\theta\in\Theta
. Then, a parameter is constructed such that
\theta_{t+1}\in\mathrm{argmin}\;U_{t}(\theta)+g(\theta)
.
Many methods can be interpreted from this point of view, e.g., gradient-based and proximal methods
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009a
;
Wright et al. 2009
)
, Expectation-Maximization (EM)
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
, boosting
(
Collins et al. 2002
;
Della Pietra et al. 2001
)
, and matrix factorization (MF) algorithms such as non-negative MF
(
Paatero and Tapper 1994
;
Lee and Seung 1999
)
or clustering
(
Jain et al. 1999
)
.

In a series of papers that inspired our work,
Mairal 2013
;
Mairal 2015
investigated the use of MM methods in large-scale machine learning. The author proposed to incrementally build the sequence of surrogates by leveraging
U_{1},\ldots,U_{t}
in constructing
U_{t+1}
. We build on this approach and demonstrate that in many examples,
the space of the surrogate functions can be parameterized by a finite-dimensional space

\mathcal{S}
; i.e., each
s\in\mathcal{S}
represents the mirror parameter characterizing the surrogate function
U(\cdot,s)
. We then perform (stochastic) incremental updates in the space of surrogate functions instead of the space of parameters
\theta
.

This paper is particularly drawn to the Federated Learning (FL) setting
(
Kairouz et al. 2021
)
, where data is shared between several agents and a central server aggregates the contributions of each agent. More concretely, we consider the extension of (
1
) to the federated setting, where
n
different
agents
collaborate to solve

\textstyle\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}f_{i}(\theta)+g(\theta),

(2)

where
f_{i}(\theta):=\expec_{\pi_{i}}\left[\ell\left(Z,\theta\right)\right]
and
\mu_{i}>0
is a weighting parameter,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}=1
. Here, for
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
, we denote by
\pi_{i}
the probability distribution of the data of agent
\#i
. In addition, for the
i
-th agent, we consider the surrogate
U:\Theta\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying: for all
\theta
, there exists
s_{\theta}^{i}\in\mathcal{S}
such that
f_{i}(\theta)=U(\theta,s_{\theta}^{i})
and
f_{i}(\cdot)\leq U\left(\cdot,s_{\theta}^{i}\right)
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

Under this setting, our main motivation is that for many problems, the surrogate function
U(\theta,s)
is linear in the mirror parameter
s
. Thus, a direct aggregation in the surrogate space corresponds to aggregation in the functional space of upper bounds. This observation enables us to solve (
2
) via aggregating the mirror parameters in a distributed manner, resulting in a MM-like algorithm for the FL setting.
On the other hand, a naive FL extension of a centralized optimization algorithm through aggregating the clients’ parameters
\theta
(e.g., FedAvg
(
McMahan et al. 2017b
)
) may not result in a stable algorithm.
From a methodological standpoint, our key message, which we further detail in
Section

3.1
, is:

In numerous FL problems, the natural way to aggregate agents’ contributions is by first aggregating on the surrogate space to construct a single surrogate function and then to minimize/majorize the common surrogate on the server

In FL, the communication between agents and the central server has been identified as a crucial bottleneck, that has been widely tackled in the gradient-based literature
(
McMahan et al. 2017a
;
Alistarh et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. Moreover, the heterogeneity between the multiple agents’ distributions often hinders convergence
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Sattler et al. 2019
)
. We leverage the stochastic surrogate MM approach in FL to propose a communication-efficient method. Following the works of
(
Mishchenko et al. 2019
)
, we incorporate control variates to handle heterogeneity. This enables us to propose a generic FL-MM algorithm. In particular cases, these algorithms can be seen as modifications or extensions encompassing multiple existing federated algorithms such as Federated EM
(
Dieuleveut et al. 2021
;
Marfoq et al. 2021
)
and Federated MF
(
Wang and Chang 2022
)
.

2
Linearly Parameterized Surrogate functions

We first consider an MM framework where the surrogate functions are linearly parameterized by focusing on problems satisfying
MM-1
and
MM-2
.

MM-1
:

there exist a convex subset
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
measurable functions
\bar{S}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\Theta\to\mathcal{S}
,

\psi:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
and
\phi:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
such
that for any
\tau\in\Theta
,
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\|\bar{S}(Z,\tau)\|\right]<\infty
and

f(\cdot)\leq f(\tau)+\psi(\cdot)-\psi(\tau)-\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\tau)\right],\phi(\cdot)-\phi(\tau)\right\rangle\qquad\text{on $\Theta$}.

(3)

MM-2
:

the application
\mathsf{T}:\mathcal{S}\to\Theta
defined by

\mathsf{T}(s):=\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(g(\theta)+\psi(\theta)-\left\langle s,\phi(\theta)\right\rangle\right),

is well-defined (i.e., there is a unique minimizer for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
) and measurable.

In words, the surrogate function given by the right-hand side in (
3
) admits a
parametric form
.

This
MM
framework encompasses many examples of optimization problems. Hereafter, we give three examples, which are detailed in
Appendix

A
.

Example 0

(Quadratic surrogate)
.

Let
f:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of

\Theta
and its gradient be
L_{f}
-Lipschitz. Assume that there exists a
measurable function
G
such that
\nabla f(\cdot)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[G(Z,\cdot)\right]
on
\Theta
. Then, for any
\rho\in\left(0,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1/L_{f}}\right]
,
MM-1
and
MM-2
are satisfied with

\psi(\theta):=\frac{1}{2\rho}\|\theta\|^{2},\qquad\phi(\theta):=\frac{1}{\rho}\theta,\qquad\bar{S}(Z,\tau):=\tau-\rho\,G(Z,\tau).

(4)

\mathsf{T}
computes

\mathsf{T}(s)=\mathrm{prox}_{\rho\,g}(s)
where
\mathrm{prox}_{\rho\,g}(s):=\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\rho g(\theta)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\|\theta-s\|^{2}\right)
is the Moreau-proximity operator of the function
\rho\,g(\cdot)
at
s

(
Moreau 1962
)
.

Example 0

(Jensen surrogate)
.

Assume that
\ell(Z,\theta)
is a function of the form

\ell(Z,\theta):=-\log\int_{\mathcal{H}}p(Z,h,\theta)\mu(\mathsf{d}h),

(5)

where
(Z,h,\theta)\mapsto p(Z,h,\theta)
is a measurable positive function, and
\mu
is a
\sigma
-finite positive measure on the measurable set
\mathcal{H}
. Assume also that
-\log p(Z,h,\theta)
can be written in the form
\psi(\theta)-\left\langle S(Z,h),\phi(\theta)\right\rangle
up to an additive term independent of
\theta
. Then under integrability conditions,
MM
-1
holds with the functions
\psi,\phi
given by
-\log p
and with

\bar{S}(Z,\tau):=\int_{\mathcal{H}}S(Z,h)\mu(\mathsf{d}h|Z,\tau),

(6)

where for any
(Z,\tau)
,
\mu(\cdot|Z,\tau)
is a probability distribution on
\mathcal{H}
defined by

\mu(\mathsf{d}h|Z,\tau):=\frac{\exp(\left\langle S(Z,h),\phi(\tau)\right\rangle)}{\int_{\mathcal{H}}\exp(\left\langle S(Z,u),\phi(\tau)\right\rangle)\,\mu(\mathsf{d}u)}\,\mu(\mathsf{d}h).

An instance of such a loss function
\ell(Z,\theta)
is the negative log-likelihood of the observation
Z
in a latent variable model when the complete likelihood
\theta\mapsto p(Z,h,\theta)
is from the vector exponential family (see e.g.
Brown 1986
).
S(Z,h)
is the sufficient statistics, and
\exp(\psi)
acts as a normalizing constant and is fully determined by
S(Z,h)
and
\phi
. The distribution
\mu(\mathsf{d}h|Z,\tau)
is the a posteriori distribution of the latent variable
h
given the observation
Z
, when the model parameter is
\tau
.

Example 0

(Variational surrogate)
.

Assume that
\ell(Z,\theta):=\mathrm{min}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\widetilde{\ell}(Z,h,\theta)
with

\qquad\widetilde{\ell}(Z,h,\theta):=\psi(\theta)-\left\langle S(Z,h),\phi(\theta)\right\rangle+\xi(Z,h);

and the optimization problem
(Z,\theta)\mapsto\mathsf{M}(Z,\theta):=\mathrm{argmin}_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\widetilde{\ell}(Z,\theta,h)
possesses a unique solution. Then under integrability conditions,
MM
-1
is
satisfied with
\bar{S}(Z,\tau):=S(Z,\mathsf{M}(Z,\tau))
.

2.1
MM as a root-finding algorithm

The first consequence of
MM1&2
is that for any
\tau\in\Theta
, there exists a
majorizing function of the objective function which equals

(f+g)
at
\tau
:

(f+g)(\cdot)\leq f(\tau)+\psi(\cdot)-\psi(\tau)-\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\tau)\right],\phi(\cdot)-\phi(\tau)\right\rangle+g(\cdot),\penalty\ \quad\text{on}\,\Theta.

Based on this property, the MM algorithm defines a
\Theta
-valued sequence by repeating two steps: given the current iterate
\theta_{t}\in\Theta
,
(i)
identify a surrogate function by computing the
mirror
parameter
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\theta_{t})\right]
;
(ii)
define the next iterate as the minimizer of the surrogate function

\theta_{t+1}=\mathsf{T}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\theta_{t})\right]\right).

(7)

This sequence satisfies
(f+g)(\theta_{t+1})\leq(f+g)(\theta_{t})

(
Lange 2013
, Chapter 12)
.

Equivalently, the MM algorithm defines a
\mathcal{S}
-valued sequence which characterizes a sequence of surrogate functions in the functional space spanned by
g+\psi
and
\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{q}
:
given
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
of the form
s_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\tau)\right]
for some
\tau\in\Theta
,
(i)
identify the
mirror parameter

\mathsf{T}(s_{t})
defined as the minimizer of the surrogate function identified by
s_{t}
;
(ii)
define the next surrogate function by computing its parameter

s_{t+1}=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(s_{t}))\right].

(8)

This sequence satisfies
(f+g)(\mathsf{T}(s_{t+1}))\leq(f+g)(\mathsf{T}(s_{t}))
.

The limiting points of the two MM methods are equivalent. Define the
mean field function

\mathsf{h}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}^{q}\qquad\mathsf{h}(s):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(s))\right]-s;

(9)

note that a root of
\mathsf{h}
is a fixed point of the iterative scheme (
8
).
Proposition

4
states that a root of
\mathsf{h}
can be used along the mapping
\mathsf{T}
to find a fixed point of the iterative scheme (
7
). Conversely, a fixed point of the iterative scheme (
7
) can be used along the mapping
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\cdot)\right]
to find a root of
\mathsf{h}
. This is the main message of
Proposition

, which is established
under the following assumptions.

2.2
A Stochastic Surrogate MM algorithm

In the large-scale learning setting considered here, the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\tau)\right]
is not explicit but stochastic oracles are available, whatever
\tau\in\Theta
; on the other hand, the minimizer
\mathsf{T}(s)
still has a closed-form expression whatever
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Thus, we propose a stochastic version of MM which replaces the deterministic fixed point algorithm by a Stochastic Approximation scheme (SA, see e.g.
Robbins and Monro 1951
,
Benveniste et al. 1990
;
Borkar 2008
) designed to solve the root of a vector field
\upsilon:\mathbb{R}^{u}\to\mathbb{R}^{u}
; it repeats
x_{t+1}=x_{t}+\gamma_{t+1}U_{t+1}
where
\{\gamma_{t},t\geq 1\}
is a sequence of positive deterministic step sizes and
U_{t+1}
is a random oracle of
\upsilon(x_{t})
. Two properties of the oracles are crucial in the convergence analysis: its conditional bias given the past of the algorithm and its conditional variance (see e.g.
Andrieu et al. 2005
;
Dieuleveut et al. 2023
). In light of
MM-1
and
MM-2
, two strategies are possible for tackling (
1
): define a
\Theta
-valued sequence targeting the roots of
\theta\mapsto\chi(\theta):=\mathsf{T}(\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\theta)\right])-\theta
; or define a
\mathcal{S}
-valued sequence targeting the roots of
\mathsf{h}
(see (
9
)). The main advantage of the second approach is to conserve the unbiasedness property of the oracle: when
\mathsf{S}
is an unbiased oracle of
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\tau)\right]
, then
\mathsf{T}(\mathsf{S})-\tau
is a biased oracle of
\chi(\tau)
- except in the specific case when
\mathsf{T}
is linear; while when
\mathsf{S}
is an unbiased oracle of
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(s))\right]
, then
\mathsf{S}-s
is also an unbiased oracle of
\mathsf{h}(s)
. Biased oracles may ruin the convergence of the algorithm and have to be controlled with convenient design of the oracles, usually inducing a higher computational cost (see e.g.
(
Dieuleveut et al. 2023
, Section IV.B.3.c)
).

This yields the
Stochastic Approximation Stochastic Surrogate
MM
(
SA-SSMM
) algorithm in
Algorithm

1
.

Data:

T_{\max}\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}
; a
\left(0,1\right]
-valued sequence

\{\gamma_{t},t\in[T_{\max}]\}
; initial value
\hat{S}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}

Result:

SA-SSMM
sequence
\{\hat{S}_{t},t\in\{0,\ldots,T_{\max}\}\}
and its mirror sequence
\{\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}),t\in\{0,\ldots,T_{\max}\}
}

for

t=0,\ldots,T_{\max}-1

do

1

Sample
\mathsf{S}_{t+1}
in
\mathcal{S}
, a random oracle for
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}))]

2

Set
\hat{S}_{t+1}=\hat{S}_{t}+\gamma_{t+1}(\mathsf{S}_{t+1}-\hat{S}_{t})

3

Algorithm 1

Stochastic Approximation Stochastic Surrogate
MM
(
SA-SSMM
)

At the
t
-th iteration, Algorithm
1
utilizes a stochastic oracle of
\mathsf{h}(\hat{S}_{t})
, the mean field function evaluated at the current iterate, defined by
\mathsf{S}_{t+1}-\hat{S}_{t}
, where
\mathsf{S}_{t+1}
approximates
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}))]
. In
online
learning
, given a new example
Z_{t+1}
, one can
set
\mathsf{S}_{t+1}=\bar{S}(Z_{t+1},\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}))
. In
large
batch learning
with
N
examples, a possible oracle is
\mathsf{S}_{t+1}=\mathsf{b}^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}_{t+1}}\bar{S}(Z_{j},\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}))

where
\mathcal{B}_{t+1}
is a mini-batch of size
\mathsf{b}
sampled at
random in
[N]
.

Since
\mathcal{S}
is convex (see
MM
-1) and
\gamma_{t+1}\in\left(0,1\right]
, we have
\hat{S}_{t}+\gamma_{t+1}(\mathsf{S}_{t+1}-\hat{S}_{t})\in\mathcal{S}
and
\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t+1})
is well-defined. Note that in the special case of constant step sizes
\gamma_{t+1}=\gamma
for all
t
, we have
\hat{S}_{t+1}=(1-\gamma)^{t+1}\hat{S}_{0}+\gamma\sum_{j=0}^{t}(1-\gamma)^{j}\hat{S}_{t+1-j}
: the iterate
\hat{S}_{t+1}
forgets the past oracles at a geometric rate. When
\gamma_{t+1}=1/(t+1)
,
\hat{S}_{t+1}
is the empirical expectation of the oracles:
\hat{S}_{t+1}=(t+1)^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{t+1}\mathsf{S}_{j}
.

2.3
SA-SSMM
on few examples and Related work

We now show that
SA-SSMM
is related to multiple stochastic algorithms in the literature.

Stochastic Gradient Descent and Stochastic Proximal Gradient Descent.

The iterative algorithm (
7
) applied to
Example

1
defines the deterministic sequence
\theta_{t+1}=\mathrm{prox}_{\rho\,g}\left(\theta_{t}-\rho\nabla f(\theta_{t})\right)
where
\mathrm{prox}_{\rho\,g}
is the proximity operator of the proper lower semicontinuous convex function
\rho\,g
. When
g\equiv 0
, it is the Gradient Descent algorithm with constant step size; and otherwise, it is the Proximal Gradient algorithm
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009b
;
Combettes and Pesquet 2011
;
Parikh and Boyd 2014
)
. Stochastic versions of the deterministic algorithm (
7
) addressing the case when
\nabla f(\theta)
is an expectation with no closed-form expression, are proposed in the literature (see e.g.
Atchadé et al. 2017
).

The iterative algorithm (
8
) applied to
Example

1
defines the deterministic sequence

s_{t+1}=s_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-\rho\nabla f(s_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}),\qquad\text{with}\quad s_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s_{t}).

The update mechanism in line
1
of

Algorithm

1
gets into
\hat{S}_{t+1}=\hat{S}_{t}-\gamma_{t+1}(\hat{S}_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-\rho G_{t+1}-\hat{S}_{t})

where
\hat{S}_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}:=\mathrm{prox}_{\rho\,g}(\hat{S}_{t})
and

Aggregate in the

Let’s consider an ideal setting where all clients are always online, there are no communication constraints, and exact expectation w.r.t.

3.2
FedMM
: a Federated Majorize-Minimization algorithm

Our idea is to develop a Federated Majorize-Minimization (
FedMM
) algorithm based on (
22
). To develop a practical algorithm, several challenges have to be overcome.

First, it is generally impossible to compute
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\cdot)\right]
, because the number of observations (on each worker) is large or even infinite; we thus have to rely on stochastic updates, for example as in
Algorithm

1
.

Second, the communication cost between workers is typically an important bottleneck. In the classical federated-optimization framework, two main solutions have been proposed to reduce this communication cost. One can either perform local iterations as in
LocalSGD

(
Stich 2019
;
Woodworth et al. 2020
)
and
FedAvg

(
McMahan et al. 2017a
)
, or reduce the communication cost either by performing a compression step
(
Alistarh et al. 2017
;
Konečný et al. 2016
)
or by sub-sampling workers at each step (referred to as Partial Participation - PP). In both situations, the data heterogeneity hinders the convergence and new algorithms have been proposed to ensure the convergence despite heterogeneity (for local iterations, see e.g.
(
Li et al. 2020
;
Karimireddy et al. 2020
)
, for compressed FL, see e.g.
(
Wangni et al. 2018
;
Karimireddy et al. 2019
;
Mishchenko et al. 2019
;
Philippenko and Dieuleveut 2021
;
Khaled and Richtárik 2020
;
Richtárik et al. 2021
)
).

In the following, we propose a unified algorithm,
FedMM
that has the following features: (i) the aggregation step is performed in the space
\mathcal{S}
; (ii) the algorithm uses stochastic oracles on
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}))\right]
, e.g., computed on a batch of examples at each iteration; (iii) Partial Participation (PP) is incorporated, and there is compression of the information computed by the active workers before communication to the central server.
In the PP setting, data heterogeneity across clients poses a significant challenge. The global fixed point
s
(where
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(s))]=s
i.e.
\mathsf{h}(s)=0
) is an average over client-specific expectations, meaning that for any individual client
i
, it does not generally hold that
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(s))]=s
. If only a subset of clients participates in an update, a naive aggregation would be biased towards the participating clients’ local fixed points, potentially destabilizing the algorithm. Similarly, lossy compression of client updates, especially if the compression error correlates with client drift from the global objective, can further exacerbate the impact of this underlying heterogeneity by distorting the aggregated information. To counteract this, (iv) control variates are introduced. These client-specific variables track and correct for the deviation of local updates from a global perspective, effectively acting as a form of client-wise variance reduction. This mechanism helps to mitigate the adverse effects of heterogeneity when employing PP or compression, as explored in works like
(
Mishchenko et al. 2019
;
Dieuleveut et al. 2021
)
.
Our approach takes its roots in
DIANA

(
Mishchenko et al. 2019
)
, an algorithm for heterogeneity mitigation for federated optimization, and in
FedEM

(
Dieuleveut et al. 2021
)
, a specific instance of the general MM framework addressed in this paper.

V_{t,i}
is subtracted. This results in the error-corrected vector
\Delta_{t+1,i}=\mathsf{S}_{t+1,i}-\hat{S}_{t}-V_{t,i}
(line
2

and sent to the server. These local subtractions are critical as they construct a more accurate update vector
before
the lossy compression step. The client’s control variate is then updated using the compressed information (line
2
), with a step size
\alpha>0
. Inactive agents (i.e. agents
\#i
with
i\notin\mathcal{A}_{t+1}
) do not perform any updates.

Finally, the central server aggregates all contributions and compensates the local subtraction of control variates by adding
V_{t}
(line
2
): it computes
H_{t+1}
, an oracle of
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t}))\right]-\hat{S}_{t}
(line
2
). Then it performs an SA-type update step on the iterate and obtains
\hat{S}_{t+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
(see line
2
) and projects back onto the space
\mathcal{S}
(line
2
). Crucially, this projection may not be performed according to the Euclidean geometry but possibly according to the geometry defined through a positive definite matrix
\mathsf{B}_{t}
, allowed to depend on the iteration index
t
and on
\hat{S}_{t}
. In our experiments, we will simply set
\mathsf{B}_{t}=\mathrm{I}
, the identity matrix, recovering the Euclidean geometry; nonetheless, our convergence result (see

) requires a finer specification of this matrix.

4
MM within FL: Related works in the framework
MM1&2

The crucial difference between
FedMM
and competing algorithms in the literature is that the aggregation steps are performed in the
\mathcal{S}
-space (see
Section

3.1
). In most federated algorithms to perform personalization, EM, or matrix factorization, aggregation steps are performed in the initial parameter space
\Theta
.

Federated Composite Optimization.

The general optimization problem (
1
), structured as
\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

f(\theta)+g(\theta)
, is a form of composite optimization.
In instances where
f
is smooth and
g
is a proper, lower semi-continuous convex function, our MM framework, as exemplified by the quadratic surrogate in
Example

1
, naturally leads to updates where the
\mathsf{T}(s)
operator becomes the proximal operator of
g(\theta)
.
In the literature on federated optimization, methods like ProxSkip/Scaffnew by
Mishchenko et al. 2022
and RandProx/RandProx-FL by
Condat and Richtárik 2023
investigate scenarios where regularization functions and their proximal operators are used primarily to enforce the consensus mechanism among clients.
These works propose performing the proximal step (which ensures consensus) periodically, rather than at every communication round, to reduce communication overhead.
While their primary focus is on consensus, this framework can be conceptually extended: the periodic server-side proximal step, which enforces consensus, shares a methodological similarity with our
FedMM
, where the
\mathsf{T}
operator (which itself could be a proximal operator for
g(\cdot)
in specific MM instances - see e.g.
Example

1
) is also evaluated centrally at the server, and not as part of the local client activity.
However, the fundamental premise of these methods, even when extended, remains distinct from our MM-based surrogate aggregation, as their core design centers on periodic randomized consensus enforcement to reduce communication overhead rather than the iterative minimization of global majorizing functions derived from client-specific surrogate parameters.

A different perspective on aggregation for composite problems is offered by
Yuan et al. 2021
.
They observe that for problems with sparsity-inducing regularizers, such as
g(\cdot)
being the
\ell_{1}
norm, if clients compute their local model updates (involving a local client-side proximal step for
g
) and these updated model parameters are then averaged in the
\Theta
-space, the resulting global model may lose its intended sparsity.
As a remedy, they propose
FedDualAvg
, where aggregation occurs in what they term the "dual space".
In the context of
\ell_{1}
-regularization, the dual variables often correspond to the subgradient of the
\ell_{1}
-norm, which directly relates to the parameters defining a particular type of surrogate or upper bound on the regularizer.
Aggregating these dual variables is thus conceptually akin to aggregating parameters in a surrogate space
\mathcal{S}
, aligning with our paper’s methodological argument against naive
\Theta
-space aggregation when a more natural, structure-preserving aggregation space exists.
Our
FedMM
framework provides a more general and direct approach by explicitly identifying and aggregating in the functional space of linearly parameterized functions.

The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm (see

The primary motivation is to facilitate federated personalization by assigning each client a distinct mixture weight and, consequently, varying their models. Similar Methodologies: Aggregation of Functional Approximations.

The principle of aggregating functional parameters extends beyond our specific Majorize-Minimization framework to the more general setting of surrogate optimization, which relaxes our MM setting by allowing surrogate functions to not necessarily be global majorizing functions. From our methodological standpoint, the key to designing federated algorithms lies in preserving the structure of the underlying surrogate-based method. An ideal federated surrogate algorithm, under perfect conditions (e.g., full client participation, no communication constraints, exact computation of
\mathsf{T}
operator), should reduce exactly to its centralized counterpart. Our linearly parameterized MM framework achieves this naturally: aggregating the surrogate parameters in the space
\mathcal{S}
is equivalent to averaging the surrogate functions themselves, ensuring the central optimization step operates on a true global surrogate.

This perspective helps contextualize other methods that follow a similar philosophy. For example, second-order (Newton-type) methods in FL, as explored by
Islamov et al. 2021
, can be viewed through this lens. Clients compute local gradients and Hessians, which are the parameters defining local quadratic approximations of the objective function. These functional parameters are sent to the server and aggregated to form a global quadratic model. Only then is the optimization step, the Newton update, is performed on the server using this aggregated information. This process is directly analogous to our approach of aggregating parameters of local majorizing functions (our surrogates) in
\mathcal{S}
-space before the central minimization step is performed using the
\mathsf{T}
operator.

Challenges in the analysis.

While
Theorem

6
provides a first result, the Federated MM framework that we introduce raises numerous theoretical challenges.

•

First and foremost, the surrogate space
\mathcal{S}
can be constrained (see, e.g.,
Examples

3
and
2
in
Appendix

E
). The updates resulting from the aggregation of the workers’ contributions may not stay within
\mathcal{S}
. We are thus required to perform a projection step. This issue is reinforced by variance reduction schemes, that often result in performing non-convex combinations (e.g., the difference of a candidate surrogate with a control variate). It is also reinforced by the compression step, the choice of the compression operator
\mathrm{Quant}
may be impactful; for example, a scalar quantization of a positive semidefinite matrix is not ensured to be a positive semidefinite matrix. Yet, these variance reduction schemes and compression steps are necessary to reduce the impact of heterogeneity and the computational cost, and helpful to accelerate convergence.

•

In order to guarantee that
\mathsf{T}(\hat{S}_{t+1})
is well defined,
FedMM
requires a projection of the point
\hat{S}_{t}+\gamma_{t+1}H_{t+1}
on
\mathcal{S}
, yielding
\hat{S}_{t+1}
. Quite often, this projection step is applied to ensure numerical stability but is not taken into account when deriving a theoretical analysis of the algorithm. On the contrary, the projection is part of our convergence analysis provided in
Theorem

6
. Nevertheless, our current analysis requires the projection to be performed w.r.t. the geometry of the matrix
\mathsf{B}(\hat{S}_{t})
which can be interpreted as a local approximation of the geometry of the mapping
s\mapsto\phi(\mathsf{T}(s))
- see
7
.

•

From an analytical standpoint, the projection introduces bias to the oracle (roughly speaking,
\mathbb{E}[\Pi(\hat{S},\mathsf{B})]\neq\Pi(\mathbb{E}[\hat{S}],\mathsf{B})
). This bias makes the analysis more complicated and degrades the convergence rate (the limit variance obtained in
Theorem

6
actually resembles the one in
(
Gorbunov et al. 2021

Federated Optimal Transport Map with an MM Approach

We now move beyond the scope of the framework characterized by
MM1&2
to show how our methodology of aggregating in the surrogate space may be generalized beyond linearly parameterized surrogates. In particular, to illustrate how our methodology may be used to design novel federated algorithms, we consider a federated version of the problem of computing parametric optimal transport (OT) maps between two measures according to the framework of
Korotin et al. 2021a
. Before presenting the federated extension and our MM-based approach, we first describe the problem in the centralized setting. Consider two measures
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
on
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and denote by
\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{X})
the set of measurable functions on
\mathcal{X}
taking values in
\mathcal{X}
, the Wasserstein-2 transport map is defined as

m^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left\{m\circ\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}\right\}\\
m\in\mathbb{M}(\mathcal{X})\end{subarray}}\expec_{\mathcal{P}}\left\{\left\lVert X-m(X)\right\rVert^{2}\right\},

(29)

where the condition
\left\{m\circ\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{Q}\right\}
indicates that the push-forward measure obtained by applying
m
to
\mathcal{P}
is equal to
\mathcal{Q}
, i.e. for any Borel set
B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, it holds that
\mathcal{Q}(B)=\mathcal{P}\left(m^{-1}(B)\right)
. In practice,
m^{*}
is computed by first solving the Kantarovich dual problem

f^{*}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}_{f\in\text{CVX}(\mathcal{X})}-\expec_{\mathcal{P}}\left[f(X)\right]-\expec_{\mathcal{Q}}\left[f^{c}(Y)\right],

(30)

where
\text{CVX}(\mathcal{X})
is the set of convex functions on
\mathcal{X}
and
f^{c}
is the convex conjugate of
f
. Under the condition that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
admit continuous probability density functions, it is known that the solution of the primal formulation may be constructed from the dual solution as
m^{*}=\nabla f^{*}

(
Villani 2021
)
. In addition, the inverse map
(m^{*})^{-1}
satisfies
(m^{*})^{-1}=\nabla\left(f^{*}\right)^{c}
. Given the structure of the problem,
Makkuva et al. 2020
aimed to find the optimal transport map as the gradient of an Input Convex Neural Network (ICNN)
(
Amos et al. 2017
)
, leading to the problem

\omega^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\omega\in\Theta}\expec_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}\left[f_{\omega}(X)+\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle-f_{\omega}(x)\}\right],

where
\expec_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}
indicates that
X\sim\mathcal{P}
and
Y\sim\mathcal{Q}
. Here,
\Theta
represents a parametric family of ICNN. Then, the transport map
m^{*}
can be approximated using the map
x\mapsto\nabla_{\!\!x}f_{\omega^{*}}(x)
.
Korotin et al. 2021a
proposes to relax the min-max formulation by using a second ICNN to parameterize the convex conjugate leading to the minimization problem

\omega^{*},\theta^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\omega,\theta\in\Theta^{2}}\left\{\expec_{\mathcal{P}}\left[f_{\omega}(X)\right]+\expec_{\mathcal{Q}}\left[\left\langle\nabla_{\!\!y}f_{\theta}(Y),Y\right\rangle-f_{\omega}(\nabla_{\!\!y}f_{\theta}(Y))\right]\right\},

(31)

where we used the fact that the convex conjugate
f^{c}
of a strictly convex function
f
satisfies

\nabla f^{c}(u)=\mathrm{argmax}_{x}(\langle u,x\rangle-f(x)).

However, a solution to (
31
) may not necessarily guarantee that
f_{\theta}
is the convex conjugate of
f_{\omega}
. Thus,
Korotin et al. 2021a
further leverage the structure of the problem and add the regularizer

R_{\mathcal{Q}}(\omega,\theta):=\expec_{\mathcal{Q}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla f_{\omega}\circ\nabla{f_{\theta}}(Y)-Y\right\rVert^{2}\right],

and thus recovering the formulation

\omega^{*},\theta^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\omega,\theta\in\Theta^{2}}\left\{\expec_{\mathcal{P}}\left[f_{\omega}(X)\right]+\expec_{\mathcal{Q}}\left[\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(Y),Y\right\rangle-f_{\omega}(\nabla f_{\theta}(Y))\right]+R_{\mathcal{Q}}(\omega,\theta).\right\}

(32)

7.1
Extension to the Federated Setting

We are interested in extending this problem to the federated setting. Consider a scenario where
n
clients, each possessing data from a distinct local distribution
\mathcal{P}_{i}
, aim to learn a single, shared optimal transport map to a common target distribution
\mathcal{Q}
. This formulation is highly relevant for federated domain adaptation. For instance, imagine a consortium of hospitals (clients) wishing to use a diagnostic AI model trained on a large, public dataset (with distribution
\mathcal{Q}
). Each hospital’s local data has a local distribution
\mathcal{P}_{i}
, which may or may not be similar to other hospitals in the consortium. The goal is to leverage the consortium data to learn a transport map that transports the hospital’s local distribution onto
\mathcal{Q}
on which the model was trained, thus improving its performance without retraining it.

In this federated context, the objective is to find a single pair
(\omega^{*},\theta^{*})
that performs well on average across all clients:

\omega^{*},\theta^{*}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\omega,\theta\in\Theta^{2}}W(\omega,\theta):=\sum_{i\in[n]}\mu_{i}l_{i}(\omega,\theta)+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\omega,\theta),

(33)

where

l_{i}(\omega,\theta):=\left\{\expec_{\mathcal{P}_{i}}\left[f_{\omega}(X)\right]+\expec_{\mathcal{Q}}\left[\left\langle\nabla f_{\theta}(Y),Y\right\rangle-f_{\omega}(\nabla f_{\theta}(Y))\right]\right\}.

When
\omega
and
\theta
parameterize an ICNN, the objective in (
33
) is differentiable in
(\omega,\theta)
, making it directly tractable by available first-order federated algorithms
(
Reddi et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. Nonetheless, we will demonstrate a more effective alternative approach, which is based on our methodology of surrogate aggregation. In particular, the central thesis of our work is that for many optimization problems, the most effective federated approach is to have clients compute local surrogate functions, aggregate these surrogates on the server to form a global surrogate, and then perform the minimization step centrally. We now demonstrate how this design philosophy can be generalized to problems, like the OT formulation above, that do not strictly fit the linearly parameterized framework of
MM1&2
.

First, we note that the objective in (
33
) decouples the data dependencies between the two sets of parameters,
\theta
and
\omega
. In particular, once
f_{\omega}
is fixed, the optimization problem for the conjugate network parameters
\theta
, only depends on the shared public distribution
\mathcal{Q}
. This structural insight allows us to interpret the proposed alternating optimization scheme as a pseudo-MM algorithm that directly applies the principles developed earlier in this paper.

To formalize this, consider the objective function for a single client
i
, which we denote as
\min_{\omega,\theta}W_{i}(\omega,\theta)
, where
W_{i}(\omega,\theta):=l_{i}(\omega,\theta)+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{Q}}(\omega,\theta)
. This local objective can be alternatively viewed as a function of
\theta
alone as it is equivalent to
\min_{\theta}\mathcal{W}_{i}(\theta)
, where
\mathcal{W}_{i}\left(\theta\right):=\min_{\omega}W_{i}(\omega,\theta)
. In particular, for an iterate
\theta_{t}
, consider the client’s local best-response
\omega_{i}(\theta_{t}):=\mathrm{argmin}_{\omega}W_{i}(\omega,\theta_{t})
. This step implicitly defines a majorizing function for the client’s true objective. Specifically, the function
U_{i,t}(\theta)\triangleq W_{i}(\omega_{i}(\theta_{t}),\theta)
is a majorizing surrogate for
\mathcal{W}_{i}(\theta)
, as it satisfies the two key properties: (1) It is tangent at the point of construction:
U_{i,t}(\theta_{t})=W_{i}(\omega_{i}(\theta_{t}),\theta_{t})=\mathcal{W}_{i}(\theta_{t})
; (2) It upper bounds
\mathcal{W}_{i}
as
U_{i,t}(\theta)=W_{i}(\omega_{i}(\theta_{t}),\theta)\geq\min_{\omega}W_{i}(\omega,\theta)=\mathcal{W}_{i}(\theta)
for all
\theta
.

Within our proposed framework, the parameter
\omega_{i}(\theta_{t})
plays a role directly analogous to the surrogate parameter
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{i}}\left[\bar{S}(Z,\theta)\right]
in the linearly parameterized setting in
MM1&2
: it is the finite-dimensional object that fully defines the client’s surrogate function
U_{i,t}(\cdot)
. Thus, analogously to
FedMM
, it is possible to develop an algorithm, which aims to aggregate
\omega_{1}(\theta_{t}),\ldots,\omega_{n}\left(\theta_{t}\right)
to construct a single surrogate at the server, which is then minimized at the server. We operationalize this approach in
Algorithm

3
, which exploits this idea to solve the federated objective (
33
).

7.2
Evaluation of Optimal Transport Map

Evaluating OT map solutions is challenging due to the high sample complexity required for accurate Wasserstein distance estimation, specifically
O(\varepsilon^{-d})
samples for
O(\varepsilon)
accuracy
(
Dudley 1969
)
, which becomes computationally prohibitive in high dimensions. To address this, we adopt the standardized benchmark methodology for evaluating continuous quadratic-cost OT maps of
Korotin et al. 2021b
.

This benchmark leverages ICNNs to construct pairs of continuous measures (
\mathcal{P}_{d},\mathcal{Q}_{d}
) where the ground truth OT map
m^{*}_{\mathrm{true}}
is analytically known. In particular, for each dimension
d
, the benchmark starts by defining the following measures:
\mathcal{P}_{d}
is a Gaussian mixture with three components, while
\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{d},\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{d}
, each a Gaussian mixture with ten components. Using two ICNNs, a push forward function
m_{\mathrm{true}}^{*}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
is learned to approximate
T^{*}\circ\mathcal{P}\approx\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left(\mathcal{Q}^{1}_{d}+\mathcal{Q}^{2}_{d}\right)
. Finally, the benchmark sets
\mathcal{Q}_{d}=T^{*}\circ\mathcal{P}
and, as such, the transport map between
\mathcal{P}_{d}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{d}
is explicitly known.

To quantify the performance of a fitted parametric transport map
m:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
against this ground truth, the benchmark employs the
L_{2}
Unexplained Variance Percentage metric (
L_{2}^{\text{UVP}}(m)
), which is formally defined as

L_{2}^{\text{UVP}}(m):=100\cdot\frac{\|m-m^{*}_{\mathrm{true}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{P}_{d})}}{\text{Var}(\mathcal{Q}_{d})},

where
\text{Var}(\mathcal{Q}_{d})
is a scalar-valued notion of the spread of
\mathcal{Q}_{d}
. We use the original implementation
2
2

2

https://github.com/iamalexkorotin/Wasserstein2Benchmark
to have
\text{Var}(\mathcal{Q}_{d})
being the
L_{1}
norm of the covariance matrix of
\mathcal{Q}_{d}
.

The
L_{2}^{\text{UVP}}
value serves as an indicator of how accurately the computed transport map
m
approximates the true optimal transport map
m^{*}_{\mathrm{true}}
.

Conclusion

This paper investigates Stochastic Surrogate MM methods that iteratively construct surrogate functions in a
parametric space
.
This principle allows us to introduce
FedMM
, a unified framework for a novel class of FL algorithms designed to address the key bottlenecks of communication and heterogeneity. We believe that this simple yet elegant idea opens up many algorithmic directions, and simultaneously poses exciting theoretical difficulties.

Finally, a point of interest is the link with Mirror Descent (MD).
Kunstner et al. 2021
showed that a (deterministic) MM - namely, the EM algorithm - can be seen as a MD algorithm. This remark strongly relies on a canonical parameterization
\phi(\theta)\equiv\theta
of the surrogate functions, see
MM-2
making the analysis simpler
(
Kunstner et al. 2021
, eq. (3),)
. Understanding the impact of the parameterization on the behavior of Federated MM is an interesting aspect left unexplored.

) which implies that the proximal operator
\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}
is firmly nonexpansive. Therefore, for all
s,s^{\prime}
, we have

\|\left(s^{\prime}-\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s^{\prime})\right)-\left(s-\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s)\right)\|^{2}\leq\|s^{\prime}-s\|^{2},

(67)

and

\left|\left\langle\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s^{\prime})-\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s),s^{\prime}-s\right\rangle\right|\leq\|s^{\prime}-s\|\,\|\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s^{\prime})-\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s)\|\leq\|s^{\prime}-s\|^{2}.

(68)

Since
\phi(\mathsf{T}(s))=\rho^{-1}\mathrm{prox}_{\rho g}(s)
, we deduce from (
67
) and (
68
) that
7
is verified with

\mathsf{B}(s):=\rho^{-1}\,\mathrm{I},\qquad v_{\min}=v_{\max}=\rho^{-1},\qquad C_{\star}:=\rho^{-2},\qquad C_{\star\star}:=\rho^{-1}.

E.2
Jensen surrogate

EM is a special case of Jensen surrogate (see
Appendix

C
).

We check
7
for EM applied to Poisson observations when
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[Z]
is explicit (see
Section

C.1
). We choose
\mathcal{S}
to be equal to the compact interval
\left[-M,0\right]
for some
M>0
. This implies that
\lambda-s\in\left[\lambda,\lambda+M\right]
. For any
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
, we write

\displaystyle\phi(\mathsf{T}(s^{\prime}))-\phi(\mathsf{T}(s))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]\left(\frac{1}{\lambda-s^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\lambda-s}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]}{\lambda-s}\frac{(s^{\prime}-s)}{\lambda-s^{\prime}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]}{(\lambda-s)^{2}}(s^{\prime}-s)+\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]}{(\lambda-s)^{2}}\frac{(s^{\prime}-s)^{2}}{\lambda-s^{\prime}}.

Hence
7
is verified with

\mathsf{B}(s):=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]}{(\lambda-s)^{2}},\quad v_{\min}:=\frac{1}{(\lambda+M)^{2}},\quad v_{\max}:=\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},\quad C_{\star}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]}{\lambda^{3}},\quad C_{\star\star}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[Z\right]}{\lambda^{2}}.

E.3
Variational surrogate

Let us consider the variational surrogate applied to Dictionary Learning / Matrix Factorization (see
Section

2.3
). We have

\phi(\theta)=\left[\begin{matrix}-\theta^{\top}\theta\\
2\theta\end{matrix}\right]

and
s=(s^{(1)},s^{(2)})\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{p\times K}
. Here,
\mathsf{B}(s)
is not an invertible
matrix so that the condition
v_{\min}>0
does not hold. To be
convinced, consider the case when
p=K=1
so that
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is equal to
(-u^{2},2u)
;
and
s:=(s^{(1)},s^{(2)})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}
. Then

{\mathsf{T}}(s)=s^{(2)}/s^{(1)}
. It is easily checked that we have

\mathsf{B}(s)=\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{s^{(1)}}&-\frac{s^{(2)}}{(s^{(1)})^{2}}\\
-\frac{s^{(2)}}{(s^{(1)})^{2}}&\frac{(s^{(2)})^{2}}{(s^{(1)})^{3}}\end{matrix}\right]=\frac{1}{s^{(1)}}\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-\frac{s^{(2)}}{s^{(1)}}\end{matrix}\right]\,\left[\begin{matrix}1\\
-\frac{s^{(2)}}{s^{(1)}}\end{matrix}\right]^{\top}.

We developed the convergence analysis under the assumption that
\mathsf{B}(s)

in invertible (with a positive minimal eigenvalue uniformly lower
bounded for
s\in\mathcal{S}
). When this assumption does not hold,
the proof has to be generalized in order to define projections and
scalar product in a space defined by the image of
\mathsf{B}(\hat{S}_{t})
. This
makes the proof more intricate, it is out of the scope of
this paper.
