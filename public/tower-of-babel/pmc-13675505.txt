Biofluorescence in polar zoarcid eelpouts: The Arctic eelpout Lycodes reticulatus (Reinhardt, 1835) and the Antarctic eelpout Pachycara brachycephalum (Pappenheim, 1912)
Authors: Thomas Juhasz‐Dora, Matthew Slater, Amirhossein Karamyar, Stein‐Kato Lindberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiofluorescence is widespread among marine fish but remains poorly documented in polar ecosystems. Using hyperspectral imaging, we report fluorescent emissions in two polar zoarcid eelpouts. A captive Antarctic eelpout (Pachycara brachycephalum) displayed uniform bright green fluorescence (~500–520 nm), whereas an Arctic eelpout (Lycodes reticulatus) showed weaker patterned emissions (~510–590 nm). Under identical illumination, the Arctic cod (Boreogadus saida) exhibited no detectable fluorescence. Although observations were limited to single individuals per species, spectral peaks substantially exceeded calibration reference values, indicating genuine fluorescent emissions. These observations represent the first reported occurrence of biofluorescence in an Antarctic teleost and expand the phylogenetic distribution of fluorescent fish to include Zoarcidae. The results also demonstrate the potential of portable hyperspectral imaging for non‐invasive detection of fluorescent traits in cold‐adapted fish.Keywords: Antarctic, Arctic, biofluorescence, hyperspectral imaging, polar fish, Zoarcidae

Biofluorescence, the absorption of light at shorter wavelengths followed by re‐emission at longer wavelengths, is widespread among marine fish and may contribute to communication, crypsis, or sensory ecology (Carr, Martin, et al., 2025; Carr, Thurman, et al., 2025; Sparks et al., 2014). Nevertheless, its distribution across high‐latitude ecosystems remains poorly understood. Surveys of Arctic fish have suggested relatively limited occurrence of fluorescence compared with tropical systems (Gruber & Sparks, 2021). To date, documented Arctic examples include the snailfish (Liparis gibbus and L. tunicatus), the American plaice Hippoglossoides platessoides (Gruber & Sparks, 2021) and the lumpfish Cyclopterus lumpus (Juhasz‐Dora et al., 2022; Juhasz‐Dora et al., 2024). No Antarctic teleost fishes have previously been reported to exhibit biofluorescence.

Zoarcid eelpouts (family Zoarcidae) are abundant benthic fish distributed throughout polar and temperate oceans (Brodte et al., 2006; Møller et al., 2005; Møller & Jørgensen, 2000). Many species are stenothermal and physiologically adapted to cold marine environments (Anderson, 1984, 1990; Hildebrandt et al., 2011; Hotaling et al., 2021; Peck et al., 2014). Despite their ecological importance in polar benthic communities, many physiological traits such as fluorescence have not been investigated in this group. The present study documents fluorescent emissions in two polar zoarcids: the Antarctic eelpout (Pachycara brachycephalum) and the Arctic eelpout (Lycodes reticulatus). The Arctic cod (Boreogadus saida) was examined for comparison. In addition, we evaluated the use of portable hyperspectral imaging for detecting fluorescence in cold‐adapted fishes maintained under laboratory conditions.

Single captive individuals of P. brachycephalum (28 cm), L. reticulatus (18 cm TL) and B. saida (27 cm TL) were imaged at the Alfred Wegener Institute (Bremerhaven, Germany). Arctic eelpout were collected during expedition HE560 to Svalbard aboard RV Heincke (August 2020; Chief Scientist: Felix Mark), while Antarctic eelpout was collected during expedition PS112 to the Antarctic Peninsula aboard RV Polarstern (March–May 2018; Chief Scientist: Magnus Lucassen). Fish were captured using a fish lift connected to a pelagic trawl. Antarctic specimens originated from scientific collections conducted in Admiralty Bay, Antarctica (PS112; 2018), whereas Arctic specimens were collected from Svalbard, Norway (HE560; 2020). Research and fisheries activities in Svalbard's territorial waters were authorised by the Norwegian Fisheries Authority (Permit Jnr. 20/5453; valid 6 August–10 September 2020), while sample collection during RV Polarstern expedition PS112 was approved by the competent German authority for Antarctic research (Umweltbundesamt, UBA; Permit II 2.8–94003‐3/410). Following collection, fish were maintained under the Alfred Wegener Institute (AWI) fish holding permit issued by the Veterinary Authority of Berlin in accordance with Paragraph 9 of the German Animal Welfare Act. Individuals were maintained in a recirculating aquaculture system at 0°C, salinity 32 ppt, and dissolved oxygen approximately 14 mg L−1. Fluorescence imaging was conducted in a 10 L aquarium using royal blue LED illumination (~445 nm; G5 XR30 Pro Radion, Ecotech Bethlehem, PA, USA). Images were captured using a digital single lens reflex (DSLR) camera (Nikon D5100 with AF‐S 60 mm f/2.8G Micro lens, Nikon, Minato City, Tokyo, Japan) fitted with a yellow barrier filter (Tiffen 62DY15 62 mm Deep Yellow 12 Filter) to capture red, green, blue (RGB) images. Hyperspectral images were recorded with a portable Specim IQ hyperspectral camera (spectral range 400–1000 nm; spectral resolution 7 nm, 512 spatial pixels; SPECIM, SPECTRAL Imaging Ltd., Oulu, Finland). The working distance between camera and aquarium was approximately 30 cm, producing a field of view of roughly 0.15 m × 0.15 m. Exposure time was set to 360 ms to maximise detection of weak emissions without saturating the sensor.

The DSLR data were analysed by applying a minimum pixel value threshold of 0.1 to remove black background pixels. Histograms of the pixel value distributions for the red, green and blue channels were then generated to assess the relative abundance of pixel intensities in each channel. Images acquired under cool white illumination were used to characterise the natural colouration of the fish, whereas images acquired under royal blue illumination were used to identify fluorescence. Under royal blue excitation, the blue channel is expected to show a strong peak corresponding to the excitation light, while fluorescence is detected as increased signal in the red and green channels in the absence of ambient red or green illumination.

Hyperspectral data were processed using the Breeze software (Prediktera, Sweden). Fish were segmented from the background using a spectral band near the excitation wavelength (483 nm), and mean spectra between 450 and 800 nm were extracted. Reflectance calibration was applied to correct sensor response characteristics.

No statistical analyses were performed on the spectral or image data because this study reports descriptive observations from single individuals of each species. Fluorescence signals are therefore presented as raw spectral measurements and comparative pixel‐value histograms without inference of population‐level significance. Both zoarcid species exhibited green fluorescent emissions when illuminated with blue excitation light (see Figure 1). Relatively uniform body fluorescence was displayed by P. brachycephalum with a spectral peak between approximately 500 and 520 nm. Weaker emissions were shown by L. reticulatus primarily along the dorsolateral region of the body, with a broader emission range extending from roughly 510 to 590 nm. In contrast, B. saida showed no detectable fluorescence under the same illumination and imaging conditions. Fluorescent signals detected in both zoarcids exceeded calibration reference values by two‐ to four‐fold, indicating genuine fluorescent emission rather than reflectance artefacts.

This study provides the first evidence of biofluorescence in the Antarctic eelpout P. brachycephalum and confirms its presence in the Arctic species, L. reticulatus. The distinct green emissions, a sharp peak (~500–520 nm) in P. brachycephalum and a broader slope (~510–590 nm) in L. reticulatus, were pronounced when contrasted with the absence of fluorescence in the Arctic cod B. saida under identical conditions. The Antarctic eelpout is the more reflective of the observed eelpout species and is skewed towards comparatively higher pixel values in histogram analyses. Nevertheless, the histograms of both specimens under royal blue illumination (Figures 2b and 3b) show that the red and green channels detect a moderately strong signal despite the absence of light of these colours in the incident illumination. The Antarctic eelpout shows a more even distribution in the green light channel, which may be due to less interference by the inner filter effect due to its higher reflectance and hence, higher translucence. This discovery expands the known biogeography of biofluorescence in marine fishes and introduces a globally distributed but understudied fish family, the Zoarcidae, into this field of research.

The interspecific variation in fluorescent signatures between the two eelpouts is particularly intriguing. The uniform, bright fluorescence of the Antarctic P. brachycephalum could be linked to environmental conditions within the Southern Ocean, where visual cues may be key in intraspecific signalling. In contrast, the weaker, patterned fluorescence in the Arctic L. reticulatus, concentrated in the head and dorsolateral regions, may serve a different function, perhaps related to intraspecific communication or a form of countershading in the more variable Arctic light environment. The lack of emission in B. saida, a pelagic and demersal schooling fish, likely reflects divergent evolutionary pressures. Its dermal structure may possess a higher density and more uniform distribution of melanosomes that suppress fluorescence for enhanced crypsis in the water column, a strategy documented in deep sea fish that effectively quench reflected light within benthopelagic and abyssal zones (Davis et al., 2020). However, the absence of fluorescence could also simply be due to a lack of the necessary fluorophores, a question that remains open to investigation.

The mechanisms responsible for fluorescence in zoarcids are also unknown. In other fish, fluorescent emissions have been associated with fluorescent proteins linked to bilirubin binding or with biliverdin‐derived pigments (Funahashi et al., 2016; Guarnaccia et al., 2021; Kumagai et al., 2013; van den Hurk, 2006). Fluorescence can also vary with physiological state, sex or ontogeny (Cohen & Summers, 2022). Assessing intraspecific variation will require relevant sample sizes to better determine the biochemical and functional basis of fluorescence in polar zoarcids. No published data exist on retinal spectral sensitivity or visual pigments in either species or in zoarcids more broadly; polar fish remain markedly understudied in visual ecology relative to reef taxa. In the absence of such data, the potential signalling or ecological functions proposed above remain hypotheses rather than established interpretations.

The present observations should be interpreted cautiously because only single individuals per species were examined. The spectral intensity of detected emissions strongly suggests that the signals represent genuine fluorescent phenomena. Additional sampling across individuals and species will be required to determine the prevalence and any ecological relevance of fluorescence in polar fish.

This study documents biofluorescence in two polar zoarcid fish and provides the first report of fluorescence in an Antarctic teleost. Portable hyperspectral imaging proved capable of detecting fluorescent emissions in cold‐adapted fishes maintained under laboratory conditions. Expanded surveys of polar fishes will be necessary to determine the distribution, mechanisms and potential ecological roles of fluorescence in these systems.
