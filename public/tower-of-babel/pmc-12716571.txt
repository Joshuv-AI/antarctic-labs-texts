Maximizing Ageing Using Volunteer Engagement (MAUVE): one health system’s journey to spread a volunteer-based intervention for acutely ill older adults – a prospective observational study
Authors: Kristina Marie Kokorelias, Nicoda Foster, Alfiya Gali, Brittany Ellis, Donald Melady, Samir K Sinha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOlder inpatients face a higher risk of delirium, falls and functional decline during hospital stays. Volunteer programmes have been shown to improve patient outcomes in single settings, but little is known about their implementation and spread across multiple care environments. This study describes the implementation and system-wide spread of Maximizing Ageing Using Volunteer Engagement (MAUVE)—a volunteer-based programme supporting older patients’ cognitive, physical and social well-being—and evaluates its impact on healthcare staff satisfaction.DesignA prospective observational service evaluation.SettingEmergency department, seven acute in-patient care units and two transitional care units within a Canadian hospital system from January to December 2019.ParticipantsOlder patients receiving care, volunteers delivering interventions and front-line nursing staff.InterventionsTrained volunteers delivered up to six types of interventions targeting patients’ cognitive stimulation, physical activity, social engagement, functional support, orientation and companionship.Outcome measuresStaff satisfaction with the MAUVE programme was measured using a structured survey administered 6 months after programme implementation. Data on patients and volunteers—including the number and type of interventions delivered, volunteer hours and patient reach—were also collected to assess feasibility and programme uptake.ResultsOver 12 months, 94 volunteers delivered 31 593 interventions to 3568 unique patients across three care settings. Front-line staff reported high satisfaction with the MAUVE programme, noting that volunteers enhanced patient care and enabled more direct patient interaction by staff.ConclusionsThe MAUVE programme is the first known volunteer-led patient engagement programme to be successfully implemented across acute, emergency and transitional care settings. This service evaluation demonstrates that structured volunteer engagement can support older patients’ well-being while enhancing staff satisfaction and enabling front-line care providers to deliver more direct care.

Introduction

Population ageing is driving a growing need for hospital-based care. Older adults (individuals aged 65 and older) already account for a disproportionately large proportion of healthcare utilisation and expenditures worldwide, through their associated higher utilisation rates of emergency departments (EDs) and hospitals compared with the rest of the population.13 Hospitalised older adults tend to be frailer, more functionally dependent and have more complex care needs (ie, mixed physical, social and mental health needs4) and experience more functional decline than the average non-hospitalised older adult.5 6 Moreover, hospitalised older adults face a higher risk of delirium, falls and functional decline during their hospital stay, which frequently results in poorer functional outcomes, longer lengths of stay and a higher likelihood of being discharged to rehabilitation and long-term care settings.710

To reduce the negative impacts of hospitalisation on frail older patients, hospitals and health systems are increasingly implementing evidence-informed interventions that focus on improving both the quality of care and hospital care environments.1113 Among some of the interventions developed and evaluated within inpatient settings, volunteer-based interventions have been demonstrated to be effective in addressing some of the negative effects of acute in-patient hospitalisations on older adults.14 15 The best-known example is the Hospital Elder Life Program (HELP), which has been implemented across North America and beyond to support admitted older patients in acute inpatient units to decrease the incidence of delirium and functional decline.16 17 In the HELP programme, volunteers are trained to provide its interventions according to the plan that a programme coordinator organises based on a review of a patient’s files.1618 Several other studies have evaluated the implementation of volunteer programmes in specific settings such as rehabilitation settings or surgical settings1822; however, most of these programmes tend to operate in a single care setting and involve interventions tailored for that specific setting and the population it serves, such as a mealtime assistance programme.23 Therefore, the generalisability of such programmes across a variety of care settings is limited. At our health system, we have designed a volunteer programme that strives to overcome these limitations by being easily implementable across multiple care settings.

From a theoretical perspective, volunteer-based programmes align with social support theories and person-centred care frameworks, offering relational and emotional benefits that extend beyond clinical care.23 Volunteers can also bridge communication gaps between patients, families and care teams, fostering a greater sense of continuity and community within hospital settings.23 These interactions can reduce stress, anxiety and feelings of loneliness, while also promoting a sense of dignity and autonomy in patients.23 By facilitating interactions and fostering trust, volunteers can help to create a more supportive and cohesive hospital environment, contributing to both patient satisfaction and staff workflow efficiency.

Sinai Health System, located in the urban core of Toronto, Canada, adapted the HELP intervention to first create its Maximizing Ageing Using Volunteer Engagement (MAUVE) programme in 2010 to improve the experience of older adults admitted to its acute care for elders (ACE) medical unit.24 By leveraging evidence of the HELP model’s effectiveness in addressing hospital-induced delirium and functional decline, the MAUVE programme was adapted to train and supervise volunteers to carry out similar interventions. Despite evidence that volunteer interventions can improve outcomes for older adults in single care settings, there exists a critical knowledge gap regarding the scalability and implementation of multicomponent volunteer programmes across multiple hospital settings within a single health system.23 The MAUVE programme addresses this gap by demonstrating a feasible approach to spread a standardised volunteer-based intervention across emergency, acute inpatient and transitional care units, while maintaining core programme components and ensuring integration with existing workflows. This study provides initial evidence on programme reach, implementation processes and staff acceptance, offering practical insights for other health systems aiming to expand their volunteer-led interventions across additional settings.

This study aimed to describe the spread and impact of the MAUVE programme across a single health system’s ED, as well as multiple acute inpatient and subacute transitional care settings and explore the influence of the MAUVE programme on staff satisfaction. The primary outcome of staff satisfaction was chosen because the MAUVE programme is a quality improvement/service evaluation project focused on implementing a volunteer-based intervention across multiple care settings. The programme’s success depends on the integration of volunteers into clinical teams, which requires active staff engagement and support. Measuring staff satisfaction provides insight into the feasibility, acceptability and sustainability of the programme, which are critical for ensuring that volunteers can deliver their interventions effectively. This approach is consistent with our previous evaluation of the MAUVE+ED programme, which demonstrated that staff engagement and satisfaction were key indicators of a successful programme implementation.24

Methods

MAUVE programme expansion

The original MAUVE programme that was implemented on the health system’s ACE Unit was coordinated by a part-time certified recreation therapist who trained and oversaw activities carried out by 30 volunteers. This has been running from 2010 to the present day (2025).

In 2017, the MAUVE programme received a charitable donation to scale and spread this initiative to other clinical settings across the health system, improve the programme’s capacity to better identify eligible patients as well as achieve high rates of staff satisfaction with increased volunteer engagement.24 This funding enabled the creation of a full-time role for the recreation therapist to enable them to oversee the programme functioning on more inpatient acute care units. With project management support, two additional part-time programme coordinators were recruited to facilitate the programme’s further expansion to the health system’s ED and subacute transitional care settings. These coordinators were a clinical nurse specialist and a communicative disorders assistant, respectively. Thus, by 2019, the MAUVE programme had expanded from one acute inpatient medical unit to seven acute inpatient medical and surgical units, the ED24 and two subacute transitional care units across Sinai Health System.

Unit staff from each potential expansion unit were engaged through their governance groups known as Unit Councils. Unit Councils are composed of volunteer staff members who facilitate conversations among key stakeholders on hospital units (eg, staff, management, physicians and ancillary services). Unit-level staff engagement facilitated the adaptation of the MAUVE programme at the unit level based on their unique culture and patient populations. Nursing staff were further engaged to support the identification of patients who were eligible and would most benefit from receiving MAUVE programme interventions. MAUVE programme coordinators further communicated with unit staff, volunteers, patients and visitors by creating bulletin boards on each expansion unit as well as a regular newsletter, to disseminate important programme information, updates and achievements.

An evaluation of the expansion of MAUVE in the ED has been reported elsewhere.24 That evaluation did not consider the spread and impact of the MAUVE programme across other healthcare settings (ie, acute inpatient and subacute inpatient transitional care units). We report our study in accordance with the SQUIRE reporting guidelines.25

This work was conducted as a quality improvement/service evaluation project within Sinai Health System, aimed at evaluating the implementation and spread of the MAUVE volunteer programme across multiple care settings. This study was not registered in a clinical trials registry because it was conducted as a quality improvement/service evaluation project within Sinai Health System, rather than as a clinical trial. The focus of the project was on evaluating the implementation and spread of the MAUVE volunteer programme across multiple care settings.

MAUVE programme description

MAUVE programme volunteers were recruited through the Sinai Health System’s Volunteer Services Department. Eligibility criteria for volunteers included successful completion of an institutional volunteer screening process (application, interview, reference checks and police clearance), strong communication skills and an expressed interest in working with older adults. Those successfully recruited for the acute inpatient and rehabilitation settings were identified through high school programmes on a seasonal basis, while volunteers for the ED were typically 18 years or older and often enrolled in university or college degree programmes related to health or social sciences.

All volunteers received comprehensive training using a full-day curriculum designed to increase volunteer knowledge of geriatric syndromes and develop their skills and competency in carrying out the programme’s specific interventions. Training content included modules on age-related changes, delirium, falls prevention, communication with cognitively impaired patients, infection control and the scope of volunteer practice. Competency was assessed through scenario-based exercises and observation by programme coordinators. Training was further tailored to include material relevant to each setting, for example, the specific characteristics of patients who would benefit from enrolment in the programme. Volunteers were required to demonstrate proficiency in safety protocols, patient interaction and documentation before being assigned to a unit.

All volunteers were further trained to complete an activity log sheet to document the patients they were able to visit and the activities they completed. These logs were reviewed weekly by programme coordinators to monitor volunteer activity, consistency and fidelity to the intervention protocols.

Patients were enrolled in the MAUVE programme if they were 65 years or older and met one or more eligibility criteria (table 1). Eligible patients were identified by programme coordinators and volunteers in consultation with nursing care providers. In the acute inpatient and subacute transitional care settings, an individualised MAUVE patient care plan was created for each patient based on their specific needs. In the ED, patients received interventions based on verbal recommendations from the programme coordinator and nursing staff. All interventions were conducted only with explicit patient consent.

Interventions delivered by volunteers were standardised across settings and included: (1) orientation and reorientation visits (eg, reminding patients of date, time and place); (2) therapeutic activities such as conversation, games or reading; (3) eating and hydration assistance (eg, offering fluids, assisting with meal setup); (4) vision and hearing support (eg, providing reading glasses, amplifiers); and (5) supervised mobility assistance (eg, walking or light exercise as directed by nursing staff).

Volunteers then used standardised paper-based data collection forms to specify the types of interventions performed and the overall time spent with patients. These forms captured encounter duration, intervention category and patient consent confirmation and were aggregated into an Excel database for analysis.

Volunteers had access to the MAUVE programme supplies in each setting, which included single-use items such as books and newspapers, personal care items, personal hearing amplifiers, reading glasses, combs, toothbrushes and games.

Data collection and analysis

Programme performance metrics on the implementation process and staff satisfaction rates were codeveloped by the programme’s executive team across all three clinical settings and briefly described below (Box 1). An Excel database was generated to track enrolled patients and volunteer interventions using forms filled out by volunteers and patient care plans. Staff satisfaction was assessed using a structured, anonymous survey administered 6 months after programme implementation. The survey included Likert scale and open-ended items assessing perceived impact on patient care, workflow, teamwork and volunteer integration. Surveys were distributed both online and in paper form across all participating units. The survey included Likert scale and open-ended items assessing perceived impact on patient care, workflow, teamwork and volunteer integration. Example survey items included statements such as ‘Volunteers positively impact patient care on my unit’, ‘Volunteers reduce staff workload’ and ‘Volunteers are well integrated into our daily routines’, which respondents rated on a 5-point agreement scale. Satisfaction surveys were conducted with nursing staff across all clinical settings (ie, ED, acute inpatient, subacute transitional) 6 months after the programme’s expansion. Descriptive analyses were performed to characterise enrolled patients and staff satisfaction rates across the three settings. Categorical variables are presented as percentages, with group comparisons made using the χ2 test.26 Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean and SD and compared between groups using one-way analysis of variance.27 A p≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.28

Results

Patient population

Data were collected between 2017 and 2019 by a research assistant. As the programme was expanding across multiple care settings, the number of volunteers and the capacity to reach patients increased. The number of programme volunteers increased from 30 to 94 across three clinical settings to accommodate coverage for the programme’s growth.

Enrolled patients were on average between the ages of 80 and 84 years (see table 2 for descriptions of participants enrolled between January and December 2019). Only sex and age were noted for the ED patients given time constraints and the urgency level of medical assessments of higher priority. With increased staffing and volunteer support, the total number of patients enrolled in the MAUVE programme grew from 223 in 2017 to 3568 in 2019 (table 3).

Mobility concerns were prevalent among patients, with 48.3% of patients in the acute inpatient setting and 60.4% of patients in the subacute transitional care settings having a history of prior falls. Almost 50% of patients in both settings also had sensory impairments, such as vision or hearing impairments. 13.4% of patients in the acute inpatient care setting and 16.3% of patients in the subacute transitional care setting were socially isolated and never had any personal visitors while hospitalised. 6.4% of patients in the acute care setting and 16.3% of patients in the rehabilitation setting were identified to be at risk of weight loss or dehydration or required mealtime assistance.

Of note, rates of delirium were only significantly different between the acute inpatient and subacute transitional care setting patient groups. 37.6% of patients in the acute inpatient setting enrolled in the programme had been diagnosed with delirium using the Confusion Assessment Method prior to being referred to the programme, compared with 9.4% in the subacute transitional care setting (p=0.0009).15

Some of the patients in the study were seen by volunteers across multiple care settings. For example, 143 patients seen in the ED by volunteers were later admitted and seen on acute inpatient units as well. Additionally, four patients were seen in both the acute inpatient and subacute transitional care settings, but not the ED. Only one patient (man) received volunteer interventions across all three care settings—starting during their initial ED visit to their eventual subacute transitional care unit stay.

Intervention delivery

Table 4 outlines the programme’s characteristics during the January to December 2019 period following its expansion across three care settings. In the ED, 14 volunteers engaged with 1801 older adults (equivalent to 15% of all individuals aged 65 and older who visited the ED during the programme’s hours of operation), to provide 4185 interventions (554.4 hours), which equated to 1.6 interventions (18.5 min) per patient during their ED visit. In the acute inpatient care settings, 65 volunteers engaged with 1724 older adults (equivalent to 21% of all individuals aged 65 and older admitted to these settings) to provide 22 393 interventions (6196.7 hours), which equated to 13.0 interventions (215.6 min) per patient during their acute inpatient stay. In the subacute transitional care setting, 15 volunteers engaged with 43 older adults (equivalent to 58% of the total number of older adults served during this time period) to provide 5015 interventions (1604.0 hours), which equated to 116.7 interventions (2238.0 min) per patient during their transitional care unit stay.

Figure 1 highlights the proportionate distribution of the interventions carried out across three clinical settings. In the acute inpatient setting, 25.4% of the interventions completed by volunteers were therapeutic activities and 31.0% were orientation activities. The proportion of vision and hearing assistance activities carried out by volunteers in the acute inpatient setting (8.7%) was higher than in the ED (1.4%) and subacute transitional care settings (0.2%). In the ED, 41.9% of the interventions completed by volunteers were therapeutic activities and 33.2% were orientation activities. The proportion of mobility assistance provided to patients in the subacute transitional care setting was higher than in the inpatient setting (7.6%) and in the ED (2.8%).

Nursing staff satisfaction

Nursing staff responses were collected in June 2019, into the 6 months following the MAUVE Programme’s full expansion. They demonstrated high levels of staff satisfaction with the programme. 82% (n=67) of the acute inpatient nursing staff and 100% of the ED (n=46) and subacute inpatient transitional care (n=15) nursing staff agreed that ‘volunteers are a part of the unit’. 25% of the acute inpatient, 69% of the ED and 87% of the subacute inpatient unit staff agreed that ‘volunteers release time to provide more patient care’. Finally, 57% of the acute inpatient, 91% of the ED and 100% of the subacute inpatient unit staff agreed that ‘volunteers enhance the care of patients’.

Discussion

This study formally evaluated the implementation and spread of a multicomponent volunteer-based intervention for older adults across three different care settings within a single health system. The MAUVE programme successfully provides interventions to many older adults in multiple care settings. Furthermore, the programme appears to have had an overall positive impact on staff satisfaction by its perceived ability to enhance patient care. This evaluation of the MAUVE programme adds to existing evidence on hospital volunteer programmes by combining a standardised, competency-based intervention model with a coordinated, health-system level implementation strategy. Unlike many prior programmes that were limited to a single unit, MAUVE used a full-day, competency-based training curriculum that was adapted to each clinical setting, standardised patient care plans and activity logs, and dedicated coordination roles (a full-time recreation therapist plus two part-time coordinators). These components created a replicable implementation package that balanced fidelity to core elements with local adaptation at the unit level, which we believe supported uptake across heterogeneous settings.

We can interpret the programme effects through an implementation science lens, using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) as a heuristic.29 Key CFIR constructs that appeared to favour adoption were leadership engagement and available resources at the health-system level, a clear implementation team with dedicated coordinators and compatibility with existing unit workflows achieved through unit-level adaptation.29 Ongoing communication with staff and routine unit engagement activities, such as bulletin boards and newsletters, likely strengthened implementation climate and staff buy-in.29 Similarly, several plausible mechanisms connect MAUVE’s design features to higher staff satisfaction. Standardised training and explicit eligibility rules reduced uncertainty about volunteer roles, visible activity logs made volunteer contributions transparent, and dedicated coordinators reduced the operational burden on front-line staff. Process metrics from our evaluation provided objective evidence of penetration, dose and sustained activity. Volunteer numbers rose from 30 to 94 and enrolled patients increased from 223 to 3568 between 2017 and 2019. Volunteers documented thousands of interventions across settings (ED: 4185 interventions with 1801 encounters; acute inpatient: 22 393 interventions with 1724 encounters; subacute transitional care: 5015 interventions with 43 encounters), and nursing staff reported high levels of programme acceptance (eg, 82% of acute inpatient staff and 100% of ED and subacute staff agreed that volunteers were part of the unit). These metrics supported claims of broad reach and sustained delivery and can be repurposed to generate standardised implementation indicators such as proportion of eligible patients reached, volunteer hours per patient and proportion of planned intervention types delivered. Recruiting from both high school and postsecondary populations and using a competency-based, tailored curriculum supported volunteer readiness to perform a range of safe, setting-appropriate activities. To strengthen causal claims, future reports should document training completion rates, maintenance of competency, and reliability of activity logs so that training fidelity can be directly linked to implementation and outcome measures.

In accordance with other studies that assessed the perspectives of clinical staff about volunteer programmes,3033 the staff participating in the MAUVE programme reported high satisfaction rates. However, in our study, the level of programme satisfaction among the acute inpatient staff was found to be lower as compared with that among the ED and subacute transitional care staff surveyed. Previous studies demonstrated that inpatient staff have negative feelings about potential overcrowding on their units, scheduling of volunteers on the units and the accessing of patient information by volunteers.34 35 Further qualitative analysis is required to determine the specific reasons for lower acute inpatient staff satisfaction scores with the MAUVE programme.

This current study demonstrates that the phased implementation of a multicomponent volunteer-based intervention across multiple care settings within a single health system is feasible. This programme may help to provide guidance for others considering instituting similar models of care. Some of the factors that have been determined to influence the rate of adoption and successful implementation of any evidence-based innovation include senior management support and the presence of clinical leadership and champions.36 The MAUVE programme was able to leverage the support of senior management and its coordinators in maintaining momentum throughout the spread process. Additionally, further engagement at the individual unit level and dissemination of information through unit bulletin boards and newsletters effectively increased knowledge and awareness about the programme among clinical staff.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s results indicate multiple strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the implementation of a multicomponent volunteer programme to support older adults across three different care settings. Our study also tracked the number of patients who transitioned across three care settings and interacted with the volunteers from the beginning. Additionally, the study quantitatively evaluated staff satisfaction with the programme in all settings.

However, there were several limitations to this study. First, this study was conducted in a large academic health system in which improving geriatric care is a key strategic priority. The programme also required substantial initial financial support and coordination from multiple departments in the health system to get up and running. Therefore, the successful implementation of this programme may not be generalisable to other centres. Second, the study was unable to collect data on most patient characteristics in the ED due to time constraints and lacked data on the uptake of the programme by the clinical staff. Moreover, we did not collect data from patients or volunteers, making us unable to comment on the programme’s impact on patient and volunteer satisfaction or outcomes. To address these gaps, we recommend that future studies incorporate more comprehensive data collection, including patient demographics, social and clinical vulnerability indicators, and measures of volunteer engagement and experience. Collecting patient-reported outcomes, such as satisfaction, perceived quality of care or functional measures, alongside volunteer-reported feedback, would allow a more complete assessment of the acceptability, feasibility and effectiveness of the programme. We also suggest implementing methods to track staff engagement and integration of volunteer activities into routine care, which would provide insight into how the programme interacts with standard workflows and identify potential facilitators or barriers to sustained implementation.

While the current study quantified staff satisfaction, future research should incorporate qualitative methods, such as interviews or focus groups, to better understand the meaning behind these data. Exploring staff experiences in greater depth could clarify how the MAUVE programme affects workflows, workload and perceptions of patient care. Furthermore, including patient-reported outcomes, such as satisfaction, perceived quality of care or functional measures, alongside volunteer-reported feedback on preparedness, engagement and satisfaction, would allow a more comprehensive evaluation of programme acceptability, feasibility and impact. Understanding how volunteers integrate with standard care processes could also identify facilitators and barriers to implementation and scalability. Collecting these multi-stakeholder perspectives using a mixed-methods approach would provide richer evidence to support causal inferences about the programme’s effectiveness and inform future adaptations and implementation strategies. We recommend that subsequent studies adopt such approaches to strengthen the understanding of volunteer-based interventions in hospital settings.

Conclusions

Preliminary data suggest that the MAUVE programme can be successfully broadened beyond acute inpatient care settings to ED and transitional care settings. MAUVE has the ability to deliver high rates of staff satisfaction across these various care settings and may result in allowing more direct patient care to be provided by front-line care providers through also leveraging the engagement of trained volunteers in the care and support of older patients.
