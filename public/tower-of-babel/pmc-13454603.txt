High frame rate action classification and state tracking in competitive sports using SlowFast dual-stream mesh structure
Authors: Mingdong Liang, Shuran Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Table tennis, as a highly dynamic competitive sport, involves rapid movements and frequent state transitions, posing a challenge to models that simultaneously capture semantic and dynamic information at high frame rates. Current motion recognition methods in high frame rate videos of competitive sports suffer from insufficient performance in complex motion classification and continuous state modeling due to the separation of semantic and dynamic features. This paper proposes a spatiotemporal fusion method based on a SlowFast (SlowFast Dual-Stream Network) dual-stream grid structure. Video frames are divided into regular grids along the spatial dimension with fixed temporal indexing. Semantic-level temporal features are extracted in the low-sampling-rate Slow branch, while fine-grained dynamic features are extracted in the high-sampling-rate Fast branch. A grid feature fusion module performs spatial alignment while preserving temporal indices and channel attention weighting, and a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) is used for state sequence modeling, achieving unified learning for motion classification and phase-state modeling, where phase-state modeling denotes the temporal evolution of action phases as an ordered latent sequence rather than spatial trajectory estimation or target-level localization across frames. Experimental results show that the proposed model outperforms representative baselines on high frame rate table tennis video benchmarks. When the dual-stream sampling ratio is 1:4, the action classification accuracy reaches 94.5% on the primary evaluation domain, exceeding TimeSformer by 0.4 percentage points and I3D by 1.5 percentage points under identical training conditions. The temporal consistency index reaches 0.93 and the state transition stability reaches 0.89, representing improvements of 0.06 over the strongest temporal baseline MS-TCN on both metrics. In multi-view cross-domain evaluation, Top-1 accuracy is maintained above 91.0% across all five camera viewpoints, while I3D and TSN degrade by up to 7.5 and 9.0 percentage points respectively under the same viewpoint shifts, confirming the robustness of the proposed spatiotemporal fusion structure under high frame rate conditions.

Introduction

High frame rate competitive sports videos contain dense action details and complex spatiotemporal dynamic information, which are important foundations for achieving accurate action recognition and state modeling. As a high-dynamic sport, table tennis has fast action rhythm and frequent state switching, which puts strict requirements on the temporal resolution and spatial resolution of video analysis models1. Accurately identifying high-frequency action sequences and continuously tracking phase-state transitions is a prerequisite for tactical pattern mining, stroke technique correction, and automated referee judgment in competitive table tennis2. In tactical analysis systems, the failure to distinguish semantically adjacent strokes such as forehand loop and forehand attack under rapid rally conditions leads to misclassification rates that render phase-level strategy extraction unreliable. In technique correction applications, the inability to model continuous state transitions across the backswing, contact, and follow-through phases within a single high-speed stroke sequence causes temporal boundaries to be estimated with errors exceeding the duration of the contact phase itself, making frame-accurate feedback to athletes impossible. Studying action recognition and continuous phase-state modeling under high frame rate conditions is of great value for improving the intelligent analysis of competitive sports3. Phase-state modeling in this study refers exclusively to the temporal evolution of action phases encoded as an ordered latent sequence, and is distinct from spatial trajectory estimation, bounding box prediction, or target-level localization across frames.

High frame rate videos display high-density variations across time, which poses significant conceptual, technical, and logistical issues when using traditional models to illustrate ordinary action transitions continuously, which often breaks down in a drift of meaning and becomes feature ambiguous. In action recognition, the differences between action features at different points in the action results in a lack of sufficient temporal consistency4–6. Existing studies suggest that single frame convolutional models are incapable of exhibiting temporal dependencies under high-speed action, the optical flow, and frame differencing representations are not robust enough to recognize spatio-temporal ambiguities leading to decreased accuracy7–9. Even in table tennis videos, instantaneous state transitions further complicate the separation of the spatio-temporal features, shifting the predominance of how the model generalizes in complex scenes10–12.

Multi-stream networks, attention mechanisms, and sequence modeling have addressed the above issues. Two-stream convolutional structures utilize optical flow branches to discriminate between static and dynamic features which increases classification performance but does not capture fine-grained changes13–15. Spatiotemporal convolutions enhance correlation through temporal expansion, but training is unstable and costly16,17. Transformer models can achieve long sequence dependency learning and improve semantic consistency, but lack sensitivity to local dynamics18,19. Recurrent networks possess temporal memory, but gradient decay remains significant in high-frequency data20,21. Existing methods have failed to couple semantic and dynamic features, limiting the unified modeling of continuous action states22,23.

To address the spatiotemporal representation discontinuity caused by the separation of semantic and dynamic features in high frame rate table tennis videos, this paper constructs a spatiotemporal fusion model based on a SlowFast dual-stream grid structure. The approach assembles dual-timescale sampling, channel-weighted fusion, spatial alignment while preserving temporal indices, and recurrent state modeling into a unified pipeline in which the feature hierarchy and optimization flow progress consistently from input to output. Multi-scale temporal features are extracted through joint sampling of the low-frequency semantic stream and the high-frequency dynamic stream. spatial alignment while preserving temporal indices is achieved through regular grid partitioning, and channel attention-weighted fusion enhances cross-stream information interaction. The fused features are input into a GRU for state sequence modeling, and a time step alignment mechanism improves state transition consistency. This structure captures high-speed dynamics while maintaining semantic stability, achieving unified optimization of action classification and phase-state modeling, and providing an effective approach for high frame rate sports video recognition. The tracking result represents phase states aligned along time and does not refer to bounding box estimation or pose trajectory inference. The overall design focuses on linking the structural components through a continuous spatiotemporal processing sequence, forming a mechanism in which the interactions among feature streams and the training objectives reinforce each other. While SlowFast dual-stream sampling, grid partitioning, channel attention, spatial alignment, GRU state modeling, and temporal consistency loss each exist as established techniques, their combination in this framework is not a parallel assembly of independent modules. The high frame rate scenario introduces a coupled failure condition in which semantic drift and dynamic noise reinforce each other across time: suppressing one without simultaneously constraining the other causes representational collapse at the fusion stage. The grid structure provides a spatially consistent anchor across frames, enabling the learnable alignment module to remap spatial content without disturbing the temporal index structure that GRU relies on for stable gradient propagation. Cross-gating between the Slow and Fast branches prevents high-frequency noise in the Fast stream from corrupting the semantic reference established by the Slow stream, which is a modulation that becomes necessary precisely when the two streams operate at a 1:4 sampling ratio and their temporal response distributions partially overlap. The temporal consistency loss constrains the GRU hidden state trajectory to follow a smooth manifold across action phases, which would not be effective without the prior spatial alignment step ensuring that the input feature positions are temporally coherent. These inter-module dependencies constitute a structural mechanism that is specific to the joint semantic–dynamic failure mode in high frame rate sports video, and cannot be reproduced by applying any subset of these components independently.

Related work

Research on action recognition in high frame rate sports videos is focusing on adjusting the structure of methods for extracting spatiotemporal features to alleviate the problem of weak inter-frame differences and feature redundancy in high dynamic scenes. The adaptive frame selection strategy proposed by Ding et al.24 strengthens the discriminative features of key frames by combining motion information screening and supervised learning, thereby improving the discrimination efficiency of action recognition models in highly redundant videos. The above methods focus on the feature purification effect brought about by the frame-level screening mechanism, thus laying the foundation for the spatiotemporal modeling of subsequent networks. Xiao et al.25 achieved high-precision human behavior classification in sports action recognition tasks by using deformable learning strategies and hybrid deep networks, and compensated for the spatial offset problem caused by action deformation through structural adaptation. This idea leaves room for the aggregation of temporal structures after solving the spatial structure instability. Ullah et al.26 fused channel and spatial attention mechanisms in the network and introduced a marginal loss function, enabling the model to obtain stronger temporal discrimination ability and more stable classification performance in high dynamic tennis sequences. These studies demonstrate meaningful progress in feature selection, spatial deformation compensation, and attention-weighted discrimination, but each operates on a single representational dimension without coupling semantic abstraction and dynamic motion capture into a shared optimization pathway. The absence of a dual-timescale encoding mechanism prevents these approaches from simultaneously suppressing semantic drift at the action level and resolving fine-grained dynamic noise at the frame level, which are two co-dependent failure modes in high frame rate competitive video that cannot be corrected by either branch alone.

Research on action phase modeling and continuous hidden state tracking has always been committed to building stable and structured temporal semantic representations in long sequences. Park et al.27 used an extended propagation network to enhance the discriminative power of frame-level features and suppressed over-segmentation through a temporal reconstruction network, thereby improving the overall stability of action segmentation. In order to express the structural dependencies between phases over a longer time span, Aziere et al.28's TCTr model unified the local representation advantages of deep convolution with the sequence modeling ability of adaptive local self-attention, thereby covering the short-term patterns and medium-to-long-term dependencies in the action evolution process. On this basis, Tian et al.29 combined a local–global Transformer with a boundary detection network and further enhanced the identification of phase turning points and the continuity of sequence structure through multi-scale features and boundary optimization. These methods advance temporal boundary precision and phase-level sequence structure, but their temporal modeling operates over uniformly sampled feature sequences that do not distinguish between semantic and dynamic timescales. Under high frame rate conditions, action phases contain concurrent slow-varying semantic transitions and fast-varying dynamic bursts that single-timescale recurrent or segmentation models cannot decompose without structural separation at the feature input stage. The joint failure of cross-stage state continuity and fine-grained dynamic discrimination in these approaches motivates the design of a framework that integrates dual-stream temporal decoupling with recurrent state modeling under a shared optimization objective.

High frame rate video preprocessing and dual-stream input construction

Ping-pong action videos, under high frame rate sampling conditions, exhibit characteristics of extremely small inter-frame motion spans but dense semantic changes. To ensure the consistency of the temporal scale and the stability of spatial representation in the model input, the preprocessing stage performs temporal normalization and independent spatial normalization on the original video sequence. The model outputs a latent phase-state sequence that reflects the temporal evolution of action phases rather than spatial movement paths, bounding box trajectories, or target localization estimates. Experimental setup

Dataset configuration

The experimental data is divided into two parts: a public set and a self-collected set to maintain balanced semantic consistency and perspective variety under high frame rate conditions. The public dataset uses the OpenTTGames51 high frame rate table tennis video collection, recorded at a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a frame rate of 120 frames per second under indoor competition conditions with fixed camera placement and controlled lighting. The eleven action categories covered are serve, push, block, forehand loop, backhand loop, forehand attack, backhand attack, forehand flick, backhand flick, footwork transition, and lateral movement, with uniform class distribution across all categories. The dataset provides 2640 samples with an average clip duration of 3.2 s per sample. All annotations originate from the official repository and define temporal boundaries at the frame level through manual inspection followed by cross-checking by independent annotators, with each boundary verified against the racket contact event and lower-limb alignment shift to ensure temporal consistency. This domain provides stable scene composition and a fixed background, which supports the extraction of core action semantics without interference from camera motion or scene change.

Single-view data does not provide enough detail for differentiating motion under complex shooting contexts, so this research expands the resources through an additional multi-view dataset. All samples were recorded using five synchronized Sony RX10 IV cameras operating at 1920 × 1080 resolution and 120 frames per second, with hardware synchronization achieved through a shared trigger signal to eliminate inter-camera temporal offset. The cameras were mounted at fixed positions under uniform indoor illumination with consistent site boundaries and no dynamic background elements. The annotations use manual frame-level marking of action boundaries followed by alignment checks to avoid temporal drift. Five viewpoints were defined, denoted V₁ to V₅, corresponding to front, side, oblique front, top and rear placements. V₁ is the main training domain and preserves detailed posture and hitting information. V₂ records lateral dynamics with partial occlusion to test semantic compression effects. V₃ introduces rotation and perspective variation. V₄ compresses motion forms under a top-down arrangement with temporal distortion. V₅ produces a mirrored semantic structure from the rear view to examine spatial symmetry effects. This dataset forms a cross-perspective validation domain for evaluating robustness and spatial alignment while preserving temporal indices under viewpoint transformations. It contains one thousand four hundred and ten samples with equal quantities across viewpoints.

The dataset suite is extended to include publicly available video datasets for badminton and tennis to evaluate cross-sport generalization under comparable preprocessing. The first dataset is VideoBadminton52, which contains seven thousand eight hundred and twenty-two video clips covering eighteen distinct badminton action categories. Videos were originally recorded under competition conditions and include annotations of racket trajectory and player positions. The second dataset is THETIS53, which contains eight thousand three hundred and seventy-four RGB video sequences from fifty-five players performing twelve different tennis shot types. All video sequences from both datasets undergo temporal resampling and spatial normalization consistent with the pipeline applied to the table tennis and multi-view data. These datasets form external validation domains for transfer testing across racket sports, enabling quantification of action recognition performance independent of table tennis–specific motion priors.

The preprocessing of the two data types runs through a single work flow for consistency. All videos undergo frame rate normalization and resampling in the temporal dimension to create frame sequences with fixed strides, while in the spatial dimension, they are center cropped and scaled normalized to maintain an equivalently sized input resolution. Video frames are all gridded into local frame blocks to remove background noise and create spatiotemporal continuity in the action region. The processed video sequences are input into the Slow and Fast branches of the dual-stream structure, respectively, in this same way, creating a unified multi-domain training input.

Regarding the data partitioning strategy, the OpenTTGames data is utilized in the main training phase, with 80% of the 2640 samples allocated to training and the remaining 20% held out as a fixed validation set, partitioned by clip identity to prevent any frame-level overlap between the two splits. The eleven action categories maintain their original distribution in both the training and validation portions without rebalancing. The multi-view supplementary set is used exclusively for cross-domain validation after main-domain training converges, and no sample from the supplementary viewpoints appears in the main-domain training split. Training and testing partitions are defined at the clip level based on view domain independence, ensuring no frame-level overlap across domains. The input frames undergo random horizontal cropping within a fixed spatial ratio, random flipping along the horizontal axis, and controlled color jittering applied through linear adjustment of brightness, contrast and saturation during training to maintain consistency in spatial statistics between augmented sequences. For the simplicity of monitoring convergence during training, the main domain OpenTTGames data is further divided into separate training and validation portions, with approximately 80% of the data allocated for training and the remainder serving as a fixed validation set. The multi-view supplementary dataset (V1–V5) is excluded from the main-domain training phase and is introduced after the main-domain model converges to conduct a restricted cross-view adaptation stage in which only the fusion layer and the GRU module are updated. The adapted parameters are then used for cross-domain evaluation to ensure consistency in viewpoint variation analysis. This avoids any information leakage between viewpoint. The complete data system forms a hierarchical layer relationship between the single semantic main domain and the multi-view supplementary domains, creating a complete spatiotemporal diversity foundation for subsequent cross-data domain experimentation. The datasets do not contain official phase segmentation, and the phase boundaries used in the experiments follow a unified manual annotation protocol that identifies the transition frame through the first observable change in racket motion and lower-limb alignment to maintain temporal consistency across viewpoints. After the main-domain training is completed, a small-scale cross-view adaptation stage is performed on the supplementary viewpoints, during which only the fusion modules and the GRU parameters are updated to maintain the stability of the low-level feature hierarchy. To extend the range of temporal and semantic conditions beyond table tennis, an additional collection of publicly available video datasets was introduced as external validation domains. The first domain consists of the Something–Something V254 resource, which provides fine-grained temporal patterns and short-range object–motion interactions recorded under varied scenes and lighting conditions. The second domain uses FineGym55, which contains structured action phases with clearly segmented temporal units. Both datasets include frame sequences with predefined labels that follow stable temporal boundaries similar to the phases defined in this study. All clips were preprocessed using the same temporal resampling and spatial normalization workflow described earlier to maintain a consistent input format across domains.

For racket-sport datasets that involve explicit striking motions, including the multi-view table tennis data, VideoBadminton, and THETIS, phase boundaries are identified through manual frame-level inspection following the same protocol. Specifically, the transition frame is defined as the first observable change in racket motion accompanied by a coordinated adjustment of lower-limb alignment, which corresponds to a semantic shift in the action execution under high-frame-rate conditions.

For datasets without explicit racket-based phase structure, such as Something-Something V2 and FineGym, the temporal segment annotations or action boundary labels officially provided by the datasets are adopted as temporal reference points. These references are mapped onto the normalized temporal grid used in this study after temporal resampling, forming surrogate phase boundaries aligned with the unified time discretization.

Model setting

The models were developed and trained in a cohesive hardware and software environment. A fixed random seed is specified for the full pipeline so that data loading order, parameter initialization, and temporal resampling remain constant across repeated training sessions, ensuring deterministic behavior under identical configurations. An NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of VRAM was utilized during the models’ training, while their operating system was Ubuntu 22.04 and the deep learning framework was PyTorch 2.2. The AdamW optimizer was selected with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10⁻4, weight decay coefficient equal to 1 × 10⁻3, a batch of 8, and 120 training epochs. When the batch size is fixed at 8, frozen Batch Normalization is used to maintain the mean and variance parameters of the inference mode of all BN layers during the training phase. This reduces the statistical fluctuations caused by small batch inputs, improves the feature stability across time steps, and ensures that the Slow branch and the Fast branch maintain a consistent normalized reference scale during the channel fusion phase. The learning rate was reduced periodically per epoch in a scheduled cosine fashion. The loss function comprised a cross-entropy term and a time consistency term, with weight ratios of 3:1, which is equivalent to having a balance coefficient α = 0.75 in the joint optimization mechanism previously presented. All parameters are initialized from a random distribution before training begins, and no pre-trained weights from external action libraries or general video models are loaded. This ensures that the convolutional coding structures of the Slow and Fast branches maintain a consistent convergence path and gradient update rhythm throughout the entire training process, avoiding the cross-domain bias introduced by external knowledge transfer from affecting dynamic modeling in high frame rate environments. Model parameters adopt Xavier uniform initialization, and convolutional layers follow fan-in distribution to stabilize gradient propagation at early training stages.

The main network structure employs an improved SlowFast dual-stream mesh architecture. The input frames all use a fixed spatial size of 224 × 224, which ensures that the two-stream structure maintains consistent spatial alignment during the convolutional coding stage, and avoids the disturbance caused by scale inconsistency to cross-branch fusion. The Slow branch has an input frame rate of 30 fps, used to capture long-range dependencies at the semantic level; the Fast branch has an input frame rate of 120 fps, corresponding to fine-grained dynamic changes in high frame rate videos. The sampling ratio between the two branches is maintained at 1:4, and a cross-gating structure is used to adjust channel weights, achieving synchronous coupling between semantics and dynamics. Three-dimensional convolutional kernels are used in the convolutional coding stage, with strides set to (1,2,2) and (1,1,1) respectively, to maintain the unity of temporal continuity and spatial resolution. Channel fusion and attention weighting are performed at the fusion layer L3, with dynamic offset sampling for alignment, and both temporal and spatial scales are normalized. The fused output is processed by a bidirectional GRU sequence modeling layer, with a hidden state dimension of 512 and a fixed temporal length of 100 discrete steps, where the step interval is determined by an equal proportional mapping of the original clip duration. The sequence step size is based on a unified discrete structure for high frame rate inputs. All videos are resampled to a fixed 100-step time grid during the preprocessing stage, aligning the time processes from different sources on a constant discrete scale, forming a stable temporal rhythm and maintaining the correspondence with the original duration. As a result, the absolute physical duration represented by each step varies across clips, while the relative temporal structure within each action sequence is preserved.

The training data uses the OpenTTGames main domain and multi-view supplementary domain defined in "High frame rate video preprocessing and dual-stream input construction". Initial training is performed using the main domain, followed by cross-domain fine-tuning in the supplementary domain. The adaptation stage updates only the parameters of the fusion layer and the GRU sequence modeling unit, while keeping all convolutional components of the Slow and Fast branches frozen to preserve the learned spatiotemporal representations from the main domain. The sample ratio of the main domain to the supplementary domain is set to 7:3. During the fine-tuning phase, only the channel fusion and GRU parameter layers are updated to ensure the stable inheritance of low-level features. The training process uses classification accuracy and temporal consistency as joint constraints. After each iteration, the mean values of Top-1 Accuracy and Temporal Consistency are calculated on the validation set to monitor the model’s convergence trend. Training is halted when the validation loss shows no improvement for fifteen consecutive epochs, and the model state with the lowest validation loss is preserved to suppress overfitting accumulation during long-sequence learning. The training loss under the joint optimization objective decreases from an initial value of approximately 2.34 at epoch 1 to below 0.41 by epoch 60, after which the rate of decrease slows and the loss stabilizes in the range of 0.31 to 0.36 between epochs 90 and 120. The cross-entropy component converges faster than the temporal consistency component: the classification loss reaches its minimum gradient region by epoch 72, while the temporal consistency loss continues to decrease gradually until epoch 98. The validation Top-1 accuracy rises steadily from 71.3% at epoch 10 to 93.1% at epoch 80, plateauing near 94.5% from epoch 95 onward with fluctuations smaller than 0.3 percentage points across the final fifteen epochs. The temporal consistency metric on the validation set follows the same trend, increasing from 0.68 at epoch 10 to 0.91 at epoch 100 and remaining stable thereafter. No divergence in the gradient norm is observed across any training run, and the variance in final metrics across the three independent runs remains below 0.5 percentage points for Top-1 accuracy and below 0.01 for temporal consistency, confirming stable convergence under the fixed random seed and AdamW schedule. All results were obtained from three independent training runs with fresh parameter initialization and reshuffled data. Final metrics are reported as the mean across runs, and their variance reflects stability under random initialization.

The comparative experiments configured Temporal Segment Network (TSN), Inflated 3D ConvNet (I3D), Expanded 3D Network (X3D), Temporal Bilinear Network (TBN), and TimeSformer models under the same data and parameter conditions. TSN uses a 2D convolutional backbone and performs average pooling in the temporal dimension; I3D is based on a 3D convolutional fully connected structure; X3D retains the advantages of 3D convolution while achieving lightweighting through layer expansion; TBN adds lightweight temporal blocks in the channel dimension; and TimeSformer learns long-range dependencies using a spatiotemporal attention mechanism. All comparative models maintained the same training epochs, optimizers, batch sizes, and learning rate scheduling strategies as the main model to ensure fairness in performance comparison.

To standardize the computational measurements across all models, a fixed clip length of one hundred frames was used, and identical resampling and spatial normalization were applied. All profiling was conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090 using a fixed driver version and stable system configuration. Parameter counts were obtained by summing all learnable tensors. GFLOPs were calculated from the forward pass of each convolution, attention layer, and recurrent unit using a fixed input size. Inference throughput was measured from the stable frame rate during continuous execution, and peak memory usage was taken from the maximum allocation recorded during the forward pass under a unified batch configuration. The SlowFast-Grid structure contains forty-two point six million parameters and requires one hundred twenty-eight point four GFLOPs for a one hundred-frame clip, with an inference throughput of one hundred thirty-six point seven frames per second and a peak memory usage of seventeen point eight gigabytes. TSN contains twenty-four point one million parameters and sixty-five point seven GFLOPs, with one hundred eighty-four point three frames per second and eleven point two gigabytes of memory. I3D contains forty-seven point nine million parameters and two hundred fourteen point two GFLOPs, with ninety-two point five frames per second and nineteen point four gigabytes of memory. X3D contains twelve point four million parameters and forty-eight point five GFLOPs, with two hundred one point four frames per second and nine point six gigabytes of memory. TimeSformer contains eighty-point two million parameters and two hundred thirty-five point four GFLOPs, with sixty-one point three frames per second and twenty-one point six gigabytes of memory. These values form the computational baseline referenced in subsequent sections.

Evaluation criteria

Model performance was evaluated from two aspects: classification accuracy and temporal consistency, to measure the quality of the joint representation of action semantics and state continuity under high frame rate conditions. Classification performance was assessed using Top-1 Accuracy as the core metric, representing the matching ratio between predicted classes and ground truth labels at the sample layer, reflecting the network’s aggregation ability in the semantic discriminative space. In high frame rate videos, the differences between action frames are subtle; the stability of Top-1 Accuracy reflects the model’s reliability in recognizing continuous action patterns.

The Temporal Consistency metric gauges the correlation between outputs over neighboring time steps, determined by taking the mean cosine similarity of latent state vectors. It can be viewed as a measure of the model’s capacity for systematic state smoothness in long-sequence scenarios. Temporal Consistency, accompanied by State Transition Stability, captures the temporal continuity of the response. State Transition Stability is measured by the variance of change rate of latent space states and provides an index of dynamic equilibrium induced by a model in the action phase, meaning that a higher value translates into a more significant effect exerted by the model to suppress temporal perturbations.

The Mean Transition Energy is useful for assessing the magnitude of dynamic response of the network under high frame rate input, serving as a measure of state update energy. It calculates the average norm of the hidden state differences allowing for consecutive time steps, and a decreasing value indicates that the evolution in the time domain is starting to converge. The Temporal Drift Index is used to measure the overall rate of offset for drift of the state, over time. The smaller the value of the index, the greater the temporal alignment of the model over the entire sequence.

In cross-domain validation, semantic deformations and occlusion differences due to viewpoint changes affect the spatial consistency of the model. Thus, each metric is evaluated independently from different viewpoints or domains. The overall performance evaluation metric, to evaluate the overall performance of the model in semantic classification, spatiotemporal modeling, and generalization across domains, is the average of the metrics for each domain. The performance evaluation system corresponds completely to the result analysis provided in the subsequent chapters, ensuring that the experimental comparisons are interpretable and consistent. All p-values were computed using two-tailed paired t-tests on metric sequences obtained from the independent runs, with standardized values used before testing to ensure numerical stability.

Impact of the spatiotemporal fusion module on action recognition accuracy

To investigate how the spatiotemporal fusion unit contributes to action recognition performance across multiple time scales in this subsection, this paper discusses the interaction of feature mechanisms through the joint application of channel attention and spatial alignment while preserving temporal indices strategies. The core principle of channel fusion, where there is cross-branch feature weighting, seeks to balance the interaction of energy distribution between semantic layers, and differences in the response of feature paths; spatial fusion facilitates dynamic alignment between streams of data through spatial reorganization under fixed temporal indexing, emphasizing continuous representation of local action changes; finally, complete fusion introduces joint channel attentions combined with spatial grid index registration under preserved temporal indices, to enable the representative: simultaneous integration of multi-level semantics, and fine-grained dynamics. The fusion process is divided into five levels: L1 performs feature interaction at the output of the initial convolutions in the Slow and Fast branches to capture low-level edges and local motion textures; L2 is located between shallow and mid-level convolutional blocks to fuse shallow semantics formed by short-term dynamics and regional semantics; L3 is set in the mid-to-high-level semantic encoding stage to enhance the spatiotemporal consistency of action pattern features; L4 performs multi-level aggregation to unify and integrate shallow dynamics and mid-to-high-level semantic features; L5 is located after the grid alignment module and before the classifier input, completing the final cross-stream alignment after global semantic integration. The sampling rate ratios are set to 1:2, 1:4, and 1:8 to simulate the feature density differences under high-frequency, balanced, and low-frequency temporal conditions. Performance of GRU state modeling in continuous motion tracking

In the sports video state modeling experiment, the study focused on the dynamic smoothness and state stability performance of the model under long-term input. The analysis examines the continuity of phase-state evolution rather than spatial motion trajectories. Three typical temporal modeling structures were used: Model 1 was a GRU network based on gated recurrent units, which has a simplified gating mechanism and strong short-term state memory capability; Model 2 was a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, with a more complex structure and a larger number of parameters, designed to capture dependencies over a longer time span; Model 3 was a CNN (Convolutional Neural Network)—Temporal model with added temporal convolutional structures, extracting dynamic features through local temporal window convolutions. In the experiment, the horizontal axis time steps t are defined on a fixed 100-step discrete time grid obtained through proportional resampling of the original high-frame-rate video sequence, and the 100 time steps are selected for analysis to balance the significance of observation length and state stability. After resampling, the original high frame rate sequence is aligned onto a unified 100-step discrete time axis, which keeps the update rhythm of the state sequence consistent in the overall structure and corresponds to the preprocessed time scale. The single-path update in the GRU state transition forms a direct gradient route across long frame chains, which reduces attenuation during backward propagation and stabilizes high-frequency temporal responses. Its compact parameterization yields a faster state refresh cycle under dense sampling, and the hidden trajectory preserves a smooth evolution pattern across extended motion phases. Using dimensionality reduction mapping, this paper built a latent space manifold to quantify the dynamic evolution of the state over time. Computational cost and practical deployment analysis

High-frame-rate motion analytics is sensitive to the resource distribution of structure density, operator arrangement, and temporal modeling paths during the inference phase. Discussion

The system demonstrates stable performance in high-frame-rate settings, yet several limitations remain visible across motion sequences. The dual-stream structure preserves fine temporal cues, though subtle wrist rotations and small-scale racket-angle transitions often fall outside the spatial sensitivity range of the fused representation. These cases produce feature ambiguity and lead to drift toward neighboring motion types with similar temporal signatures. The temporal module exhibits strong consistency in long sequences, though abrupt directional switches or partial occlusions disturb local motion continuity and weaken the alignment across adjacent frames. GRU maintains a continuous hidden-state path under dense temporal sampling, forming stable transitions across adjacent frames. Transformer-temporal spreads attention across the full sequence, which introduces abrupt local shifts in high-frame-rate data. MS-TCN and TCN-Transformer propagate temporal features through segmented convolutional layers, constraining continuity when frame-level variation is small but motion progression is continuous. GRU preserves a stable temporal trajectory suited to the fine-grained dynamics in high-frame-rate motion sequences.

The proposed framework is optimized for competitive racket sports with dense temporal sampling, and its feature distribution reflects the structural properties of this domain. Broader motion patterns with irregular tempo or non-periodic transitions require additional mechanisms to stabilize long-range dependencies. The current alignment strategy relies on mid-level feature granularity, and its scalability to low-frame-rate environments remains limited due to reduced temporal density. The temporal update mechanism of GRU stabilizes state evolution in rapid motion cycles by preserving consistent progression across dense frames. Transformer-temporal amplifies local fluctuations due to its global attention distribution, and MS-TCN and TCN-Transformer enforce phase-based boundaries that do not match the continuous transitions characteristic of high-frame-rate sequences. GRU delivers a balanced propagation pattern aligned with the temporal structure of fast competitive movements.

The spatial encoding limitation for micro-scale motion cues points toward incorporating higher-resolution grid partitioning in the Fast branch to increase the spatial sampling density within racket-hand interaction regions, which would reduce feature ambiguity in wrist rotation and contact-angle transitions without increasing the temporal stride. The instability introduced by abrupt directional switches and partial occlusions suggests that a confidence-weighted temporal smoothing layer inserted between the alignment module and the GRU input could suppress locally corrupted feature vectors without propagating their effect across the full hidden state trajectory. For cross-domain application to other high frame rate competitive sports such as badminton and fencing, the primary structural constraint is the mismatch between the fixed 8 × 6 grid resolution and the larger spatial displacement ranges characteristic of full-body striking motions. Adapting the grid resolution dynamically based on the estimated action scale at each time step, combined with a domain-specific fine-tuning stage that updates only the fusion layer and GRU parameters, forms a tractable optimization path for extending the framework to sports with structurally different motion envelopes while preserving the dual-stream temporal decoupling learned from the source domain.

Conclusion

This paper takes high frame rate competitive sports videos as the research object and addresses the spatiotemporal representation discontinuity caused by the separation of semantic and dynamic features by constructing a spatiotemporal fusion model based on a SlowFast dual-stream grid structure. Under parallel sampling of low-frequency semantic and high-frequency dynamic streams, this model achieves collaborative expression of cross-scale features through channel attention and spatial alignment while preserving temporal indices, and uses GRU to complete state sequence modeling and temporal constraint optimization. Experimental results show that when the dual-stream sampling ratio is set to 1:4, semantic and dynamic features reach the optimal coupling state, and the classification accuracy is improved to 95.4%. Under the joint optimization mechanism, Top-1 Accuracy increases from 91.3% to 94.7%, Temporal Consistency from 0.74 to 0.91, and State Transition Stability from 0.65 to 0.88. In multi-view cross-domain tests, the model achieves 94.5% accuracy in the frontal view and maintains 92.4% accuracy in the top-view, demonstrating better stability than comparative models such as I3D and TimeSformer. The research findings confirm that the method realized a successful integration of semantic and dynamic features and continues to model the temporal dimension under high frame rate conditions, significantly enhancing the consistency and robustness of action recognition and latent phase-state modeling, and provides an efficient and scalable technical pathway for high accuracy video analysis and intelligent interpretation in competitive sports contexts. The results reveal structural conditions for modeling rapid motion sequences. The alignment of receptive-field scaling with mid-level semantic stability forms a feature space that preserves phase continuity under dense temporal sampling. The recurrent module maintains stable gradient transmission across long sequences and sustains coherent state progression in fast action phases. These properties outline design principles for constructing representations adapted to high-speed behavioral dynamics.
