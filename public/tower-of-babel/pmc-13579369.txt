Silver-doped zinc oxide nanoparticles: synthesis, characterization and optimization of photocatalytic efficiencies in UV domains
Authors: S. Arfaoui, A. Mejri, S. Eichendorff, A. Mars, G. Plantard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar photo-oxidation represents an environmentally friendly approach using solar radiation as the primary energy source. Among semiconductor photocatalysts, zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles have been extensively investigated due to their favorable photocatalytic performance. However, their practical application in solar-driven processes remains limited by their low photocatalytic efficiency under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, which accounts for only approximately 5% of the solar spectrum. This limitation is mainly attributed to the rapid recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs (e−/h+). To overcome this drawback, the present study focuses on silver-doped zinc oxide (Ag/ZnO) photocatalytic materials. Owing to their high electrical conductivity, silver nanoparticles act as electron traps, thereby enhancing charge separation and reducing electron–hole recombination. ZnO and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites were successfully synthesized using a soft co-precipitation method. The structural properties of the synthesized materials were thoroughly characterized. Their functional properties, including optical behavior and photocatalytic performance, were subsequently investigated to determine the optimal operating conditions. The photocatalytic performance was evaluated under both UV and visible irradiation using photocatalytic efficiency (ρ). Different silver doping levels (0, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1 wt%) were evaluated. The results demonstrated a clear dependence of photocatalytic efficiency on the silver content. The Ag/ZnO composite containing 1 wt% Ag exhibited the highest performance, with a photocatalytic efficiency of 0.67 × 10−3 as compared with 0.4 × 10−3 for ZnO. These findings confirmed that silver doping effectively enhanced the photocatalytic properties of ZnO and improved its potential for solar-driven environmental applications.

Introduction

Photocatalysis is a promising technology for water treatment.1 Sunlight is the ideal energy source for this process. However, most photocatalysts are designed for use under ultraviolet (UV) light. UV light represents less than 5% of the solar spectrum.2 Therefore, their efficiency under real sunlight is very low. This gap between laboratory conditions and solar applications limits the practical use of photocatalysis.

Among the many catalysts used, TiO2 and ZnO appear to be the best candidates for photooxidation, particularly solar-driven photooxidation. They serve as reference materials due to the substantial body of research conducted on them as photocatalytic materials.3–5 A ZnO photocatalyst placed under the sun can absorb less than 10% of the available radiation.6 This low absorption leads to a very limited number of generated charge carriers. The reaction rate in photocatalysis is directly proportional to the photon flux. When the photon flux is low, the generation of electron–hole pairs is low, which results in the slow degradation of organic pollutants.7,8 Reaching an acceptable level of water treatment would require very long exposure times. For this reason, a photocatalyst limited to UV activation is not suitable for efficient and practical solar applications. This work was designed to overcome this barrier. Many studies have tried to enhance ZnO activation under a large spectrum of visible light. Metal doping (e.g., copper, iron, and silver), non-metal doping (e.g., nitrogen and sulfur), and hybridizing photocatalysts with other semiconductors (e.g., ZnO/TiO2 composites) have been widely reported as effective strategies.9,10 However, most of these approaches still fall short under real sunlight. One common issue is a narrow absorption window. Although BiVO4 and WO3 exhibit visible-light activity owing to their narrower bandgaps compared with that of ZnO, their absorption is still restricted to the short-wavelength region of the visible spectrum, limiting their ability to fully utilize solar irradiation.11,12 Other materials suffer from rapid charge recombination. Even when more photons are absorbed, the electron–hole pairs recombine quickly. This recombination reduces the number of active radicals available for pollutant degradation. ZnO-based photocatalysts may also suffer from photo-corrosion and stability issues during long-term operation, while the leaching of dopant ions into aqueous media has also been reported. Furthermore, several doping and heterostructure fabrication strategies often require complex synthesis methods, which are costly and difficult to scale up.13

The way a photocatalyst is made matters as much as its composition. Many reported doped ZnO materials are prepared by complex methods. Hydrothermal synthesis requires high pressure and high temperature.14 Solvothermal methods use organic solvents that are costly and toxic.15 Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) needs expensive equipment and is difficult to scale.16 Despite their effectiveness in laboratory research, these methods are not practical for large-scale water treatment. In contrast, a simple co-precipitation method, which can be performed at low temperature (i.e., room temperature) and does not require high pressure or toxic solvents, represents a more practical approach.17 This method is reproducible and easy to transfer to a pilot-scale setting. It also works with a very low silver concentration (1%). This keeps the material cost low while achieving good photocatalytic efficiency.

Numerous studies have been conducted on the use of dopants or scavengers to improve photocatalytic performance, particularly to limit charge recombination or broaden the spectral range toward the visible spectrum.4,5,9,18 Among the many dopants tested, silver—due to its chemical stability and ease of formulation—is one of the most effective and widely used dopants in the literature.10,11

A final gap in the literature concerns the amount of silver used. Most published works on Ag/ZnO use relatively high concentrations. Typical values range from 2% to 10% or even higher. These studies focus mainly on extending light absorption into the visible range.19 This is achieved through surface plasmon resonance effects.20 However, the enhancement of UV efficiency by silver doping is often overlooked. Very low silver concentrations (below 1%) are rarely studied. The mechanism under UV light is different from the mechanism under visible light.21 Under UV, silver does not act as a light absorber. Instead, it acts as an electron trap. Photogenerated electrons from the ZnO conduction band are captured by silver nanoparticles.22 This trapping prevents rapid recombination with holes. Thus, more holes remain available to produce hydroxyl radicals. This effect is valuable because it makes ZnO more effective even under the UV part of sunlight. Most studies ignore this UV efficiency enhancement by noble-metal doping.

In this study, we investigate the effect of different silver loadings (0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1 wt%) on the functional properties of Ag/ZnO nanocomposites, particularly their optical properties and oxidation capacity. The synthesized materials are thoroughly characterized to evaluate the influence of silver content on their morphological, textural, and structural properties and to ensure consistency with the characteristics reported in the literature. Phenol is chosen as the model pollutant to test real photocatalytic performance.23 Optical experiments are conducted under a solar lamp to simulate real-world application conditions. The results are analyzed using a model that expresses the dependence on the amount of absorbed photon energy rather than on irradiation time. Our approach consists of correlating the ability of the materials to absorb the maximum amount of incident irradiation with the determination of the optimal conditions for photocatalytic measurements. The photocatalytic properties are evaluated by comparing the efficiencies under identical experimental conditions. Optimal concentrations are determined for each composition, corresponding to the configuration exhibiting maximum absorbance and near-zero transmittance, i.e., absorbing up to 99% of the incident light. Our goal is to ensure optimal and comparable experimental conditions across all composites in order to maximize overall efficiency.

Experimental

Materials and reagents

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O) and silver nitrate (AgNO3) were used as precursors of zinc oxide and silver-doped ZnO nanoparticles. Both were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) (>99.99% purity). Distilled water and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) were used as the solvent and precipitating agent, respectively, during the preparation process. Ethanol was used to remove impurities formed during preparation. All reagents were of analytical grade and were used without further purification. Phenol powder (purity >99.0%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA) and was used as the model pollutant.

Synthesis process

Synthesis of ZnO and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites

Both ZnO and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites were prepared by the co-precipitation method.17 The synthesis steps are described below.

For ZnO, 11 g of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, corresponding to 0.037 mol, was dissolved in 200 mL of distilled water under continuous magnetic stirring for 30 min. Separately, 6 g of NaOH was dissolved in 200 mL of distilled water. The aqueous NaOH solution was then added dropwise into the zinc nitrate solution under continuous stirring. The resulting mixture was maintained under stirring at room temperature for 4 hours, leading to the formation of a white precipitate.

For Ag/ZnO nanocomposites, silver nitrate was introduced into the initial zinc nitrate solution with different concentrations of silver (0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1 wt%), corresponding to 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, and 0.1 g, respectively. To prepare the 1 wt% Ag sample, 11 g of zinc nitrate hexahydrate was dissolved in 200 mL of distilled water. Silver nitrate (0.1 g), separately dissolved in distilled water, was then added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min to ensure homogeneous mixing before the addition of NaOH. During the co-precipitation step, Zn2+ and Ag+ ions reacted with hydroxide ions to form Zn(OH)2 and AgOH precipitates, as represented by the following chemical precipitation reactions:Zn2+ + 2OH− → Zn(OH)2,Ag+ + OH− → AgOH (s).

Upon thermal treatment, Zn(OH)2 was converted into wurtzite ZnO, while the unstable AgOH rapidly transformed into Ag2O, which was subsequently reduced to metallic silver nanoparticles deposited on the ZnO surface.24,25 The following reactions describe the formation of ZnO (s) and metallic Ag (s):Zn(OH)2 (s) → ZnO (s) + H2O,2Ag(OH) (s) → Ag2O (s) + H2O,2Ag2O(OH) (s) → 4Ag (s) + O2 (g).

After synthesis, the suspension was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 20 min. It was then washed thoroughly with distilled water. The obtained solids were then dispersed in absolute ethanol and left undisturbed for 2 hours to minimize particle agglomeration. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged again, dried at 75 °C for 6 hours, and finally annealed at 500 °C for 2 hours, with a controlled heating rate of 5 °C min−1. This annealing step was essential to enhance the degree of crystallinity and structural stability of the ZnO phase, as well as to remove defects in the Ag/ZnO structures.26 The color of the material also changed from gray for the 0.25Ag/ZnO sample to jet black for samples of higher dopant concentrations (1Ag/ZnO). Characterization

Equipment and methods

The structure and crystallinity of the prepared materials were investigated by X-ray diffractometry (XRD). A Bruker D8-ADVANCE diffractometer equipped with a Lynxeye detector was used. The Cu Kα radiation lines (λ1 = 0.154060 nm and λ2 = 0.154439 nm) were employed. The voltage and current of the source used were 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively. Diffractograms were recorded in the 2θ range of 10 and 80°, with a goniometer speed of 0.5 s per step. The X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were collected on a Liobold RH 63. Mg X-rays were used to excite photoelectrons from Zn 2p, O 1s and the Ag 3d core level. Raman spectra were measured on a Renishaw UV-vis Raman System 1000 spectrometer using a He–Cd laser as an illumination source (325 nm). Morphological analysis was conducted by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) using a Hitachi S-4500 field-emission gun microscope. Images were acquired with a secondary electron detector. An electron beam voltage of 5 kV was applied at a working distance of 5 mm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained on a Hitachi H-8100 transmission electron microscope at 200 KV. The elemental composition and homogeneity were determined by Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using a KEVEX Si(Li) detector and Bruker software. Phenol concentration was quantified over time by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC). A Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 system equipped with a UV-vis detector was used for the analysis. Separation was carried out using a C18 column (Hypersil GOLD™, 5 µm, 100 × 2.1 mm) protected by a precolumn. The initial mobile phase composition consisted of 10% acetonitrile (Honeywell) and 90% ultrapure water. The flow rate was maintained at 0.6 mL min−1, and the detection wavelength was set at 270 nm. Before quantification, all samples were filtered through hydrophilic filters (0.22 µm, Chromafil® Xtra-PTFE-20/25). This filtration step was performed to protect the analytical column.

Granulometric analysis

Particle size distribution measurements were performed using a Mastersizer 3000 laser diffraction particle size analyzer equipped with a Hydro EV dispersion unit (Malvern PANalytical, UK). Prior to the analysis, water was selected as the dispersing medium owing to its suitability for the measurements. A refractive index of 1.33 was used for the dispersant. The instrument parameters, such as a stirring speed of 2500 tr per min, with the optical configuration defined by a blue (470 nm) and red (633 nm) laser source, were configured. The refractive index used for Ag/ZnO materials was 1.989. Approximately 20 mg of powder was gradually dispersed in 500 mL of the dispersant. This step was performed under subdued lighting to prevent agglomeration. The suspension was stirred continuously at 2500 rpm to ensure homogeneity. The measurement sequence consisted of a 5 s background measurement, followed by three consecutive sample measurements. Each sample measurement lasted 10 s. A 5 s pause was applied between measurements. The instrument was thoroughly cleaned with the dispersant between samples to prevent cross-contamination.

Assessment tools

Optical measurement process

Our approach was to assess the ability of catalysts to absorb incident photon flux using an experimental optical bench under identical conditions.4,27–29 The Ag/ZnO nanocomposites were prepared at different concentrations, ranging from 0.01 to 3 kg m−3, and were evaluated using the optical bench to study the effect of concentration variation on the absorbance capacities of suspensions. The experimental setup has been described in detail in a previous paper.30 It consists of a collimated solar simulator equipped with lens spectra between 250 and 2500 to reproduce incident sunlight (1000 W m−2), a 2 cm thick measurement cell housing the suspension solution, and a spectrophotometer-coupled light-integrating sphere, as shown in Fig. 1. The connected software generated a transmittance spectrum representing an average of 50 analyses of 100 ms for a total integration time of 5 s for each sample. The results are expressed as the evolution of the measured transmittance with wavelength, from the near-UV to the visible range, as light propagated through the cell containing the catalyst suspension.

Photograph of the optical test bench, including a 1000 W m−2 solar simulator, measurement cell housing the media, and spectrophotometer-coupled light-integrating sphere.

Assuming zero reflectivity at the measurement cell scale, absorbency properties allowed us to determine the extinction coefficient (Eext). This directly characterized the ability of each catalyst in suspension to absorb the maximum amount of irradiation. The transmission (T) is defined as the ratio of the transmitted light flux density (Is) (W m−2) to the incident light flux density (Ii) (W m−2) and expressed as a function of the concentration of the catalyst in a suspension (Csusp) and cell optical thickness (l). Refocusing on suspensions, it was then possible to identify Eext using the Beer–Lambert law, expressed by eqn (1) (ref. 31) as follows:1where Tλ corresponds to the transmittance (−). l is the cell thickness (m). Csusp is the concentration of catalyst in the cell (kg m−3). Eext is the extinction coefficient of the catalyst (m2 kg−1). Ii is the flux of incident light (W m−2). Is is the flux of light measured (W m−2).

The relationship in eqn (1), defining cell thickness and concentration as dependent variables, can be used to determine the optimal concentration for each catalyst as the configuration yielding maximal absorbance. These properties contain critical information needed in optimizing the performance of the material, such as the mass ratio of doping, as well as cell geometry (thickness), so that photons are used efficiently.29

Photocatalytic process

Photocatalytic set up

The approach adopted to evaluate the photocatalytic efficiencies of photocatalysts was to measure photocatalytic kinetics under controlled conditions. Phenol was selected as the indicator of photodegradation activity. A measuring bench was set up to evaluate phenol degradation in order to determine the photocatalytic efficiency of Ag/ZnO. Phenol photodegradation kinetics were investigated under two domains of interest for photocatalysis, i.e. ultraviolet and visible light. Our aim was to evaluate the number of photons absorbed as a function of the Ag doping percentage in ZnO from the UV to the visible range.32 To study the kinetics under UV irradiation, an experimental bench equipped with UV lamps operating in the 250–400 nm range was used, as shown in Fig. 2A. The spectrum, as shown in Fig. S1 in the SI, was representative of the spectrum emitted by the lamp within the ultraviolet wavelength range. Experiments were carried out in a series of beakers, each containing 200 mL of a phenol solution (10 mg L−1) prepared in ultrapure water, along with an Ag/ZnO concentration of 3 g L−1. The optimal concentration for each composition was established using the Eext derived from the Beer–Lambert law. This criterion ensures that the catalysts operate under optimal conditions and therefore exhibit comparable performance, meaning that they receive the maximum amount of light they are capable of absorbing throughout the volume of the beaker. The optical bench studies described in Section 3.3 allowed the determination of the concentration needed to achieve the full radiation absorption over a given thickness. These conditions were then applied in the photocatalysis experiments detailed in Section 3.4, ensuring that each catalyst particle absorbed an identical quantity of light. These optimal concentrations were subsequently established and used throughout the photocatalytic experiments, so that every catalyst absorbed the full light dose. Consequently, photocatalytic kinetics were evaluated by comparing the degradation efficiencies under these optimized conditions. Solutions were continuously stirred throughout the experiments. The lamp's distance was adjusted to provide a uniform incident flux density of 30 W m−2 at the solution surface.30

The suspensions were continuously stirred throughout the experiments.

Photocatalytic efficiency (ρ)

In most of the literature, photochemists routinely determine catalyst performance by measuring photodegradation rate or experimental kinetics. Serpone et al. introduced the concept of photocatalytic efficiency (ρ) to enable meaningful comparisons between solid photocatalysts under identical irradiation conditions. This approach ensures that the same number of incident photons is considered, regardless of the light source characteristics (e.g., flux density or spectral distribution), and that the same number of pollutant molecules is used, independent of solution volume and concentration.33 It is defined as the number of photoconverted pollutant molecules per unit volume of solution divided by the number of photons absorbed on the catalyst surface. This efficiency parameter reflects a catalyst's intrinsic ability to degrade pollutants per unit of absorbed UV light, providing a valuable tool for cross-material evaluation in photocatalytic water treatment.

The energy of a photon and the corresponding photon flux both depend on the spectral range considered. Therefore, the number of photons available for photoexcitation was determined from the incident flux density, obtained by the integration of the simulated solar spectrum across the considered spectral domain. The number of photoconverted molecules was determined from the photocatalytic kinetics obtained using the experimental setup, based on the concentration variation normalized to the unit volume. The efficiency is defined as the ratio of the variation in the number of photoconverted molecules to the number of accumulated incident photons.18 It is expressed using eqn (2) as follows:2where C0 and Ct are the initial and final concentrations of pollutant molecules. V is the volume of the measuring cell (m3). NA is the Avogadro number (mol−1). M is the molar mass of phenol (g mol−1). S is the irradiated surface (m2), and I is the flux density (W m−2). The number of photons (N) is deduced from the flux density based on the energy of a photon at the average wavelength.

Results and discussion

Structural and morphological characterizations

To establish the relationship between the nanostructural properties of the nanoparticles and their performance, a comprehensive series of characterizations was conducted. Fig. 3(a) shows the XRD patterns recorded over a 2θ range of 10°–80°, corresponding to ZnO and ZnO doped with varying Ag concentrations. These patterns offered a crystallographic fingerprint of the materials. In the case of ZnO, distinct diffraction peaks were detected at 2θ values of approximately 32.03°, 34.69°, 36.52°, 47.77°, 56.83°, 63.10°, 66.61°, 68.17°, and 69.30°, corresponding to the (hkl) values of (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112) and (201), respectively, confirming the hexagonal wurtzite crystal structure (JCPDS no. 36-1451).34 The XRD patterns of Ag/ZnO (0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 wt%) exhibited the same characteristic reflections of the hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO, with a slight decrease in peak intensities, indicating that the wurtzite framework was preserved after Ag loading. In addition, a new diffraction peak appeared at 2θ ≈ 38.36°, indexed to the (111) plane of face-centered cubic metallic silver (JCPDS no. 01-089-3722),35 and its intensity increased with the Ag loading. The existence of this reflection demonstrated that silver was present as a separate crystalline metallic phase deposited on the surface of ZnO, forming an Ag/ZnO nanocomposite, rather than being substitutionally incorporated into the ZnO lattice. Consistently, no measurable shift of the ZnO diffraction peaks was observed upon Ag loading, ruling out significant lattice substitution of Zn2+ by the relatively large Ag+ ions. No peaks attributable to Ag2O or other impurity phases were detected.

(a) XRD patterns of the ZnO and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites (0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 wt%). (b) XPS spectra and (c) resonance Raman spectra of ZnO and 1Ag/ZnO.

The XPS survey spectra of ZnO and 1Ag/ZnO were recorded to identify the elemental composition of the samples, as shown in Fig. 3(b) and S3 in the SI. In both spectra, peaks corresponding to Zn, O, and C were detected, confirming the presence of the ZnO matrix, along with a small amount of adventitious surface carbon. In the survey spectrum of the 1Ag/ZnO sample, an additional peak corresponding to Ag was observed, confirming the successful deposition of silver onto the ZnO nanoparticles. The low intensity of the Ag signal was attributed to the low loading percentage (1 wt%) of silver in the sample. No other impurity peaks were detected, indicating the high purity of both samples. Moreover, the high-resolution spectra were analyzed to gain further insight into the chemical states of the elements present. Zn was found to be present exclusively in the +II oxidation state, as shown by the Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 peaks located at 1021.73 eV and 1044.88 eV, respectively. The absence of any additional Zn 2p component upon Ag loading indicated that the chemical environment of Zn2+ in the wurtzite lattice was not modified, which was consistent with the immobilization of silver at the surface of the ZnO nanoparticles rather than within the lattice. The O 1s spectrum was found to be asymmetric and was resolved into three components. The first component, observed at 531.4 eV, was attributed to lattice oxygen (O2−) in the wurtzite structure. The second component, found near 532.8 eV, was assigned to surface hydroxyl groups and chemisorbed O2. This component was considered particularly important, as such surface oxygen species are known to participate in the trapping of photogenerated holes during photocatalysis. The chemical state of silver was examined through the Ag 3d core-level spectrum. The Ag 3d5/2 and Ag 3d3/2 peaks were detected at 367.9 eV and 373.9 eV, with a spin–orbit splitting of 6.0 eV, and were assigned to metallic Ag0 species. This finding is in full agreement with the XRD results, which revealed the (111) reflection of face-centered cubic metallic silver, confirming that silver existed as a separate metallic phase deposited on the ZnO surface rather than as Ag+ ions substituted into the ZnO lattice. When compared to bulk metallic silver (368.2 eV and 374.2 eV), the Ag 3d peaks were found to be shifted slightly toward a lower binding energy.36 This shift was not interpreted as evidence of oxidation; instead, it was attributed to electron transfer at the Ag–ZnO interface, arising from the strong electronic interaction between the metallic Ag nanoparticles and the ZnO support. Such interfacial interaction, characteristic of a Schottky-type contact, confirmed that the Ag nanoparticles were in intimate contact with the ZnO surface and was expected to promote charge-carrier separation, surface defect formation, and hydroxyl radical generation during photocatalysis.

Additional support was provided by Raman spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 3(c). For ZnO, the hexagonal wurtzite structure was confirmed through the strong E2 (low) and E2 (high) modes detected at 110.6 cm−1 and 443.1 cm−1, respectively. The presence of intrinsic defects, such as oxygen vacancies and zinc interstitials, was evidenced by the A1 (LO) mode observed at 585.1 cm−1. In the 1Ag/ZnO sample, all the characteristic wurtzite modes remained at essentially the same positions, with no measurable shift or broadening of the E2 (high) mode. Since the E2 (high) mode was highly sensitive to lattice strain and substitutional disorder, the absence of such spectral changes indicated that the ZnO crystal lattice was not perturbed by the introduction of silver into the structure.37 It was noted, however, that all Raman peak intensities were significantly reduced in the 1Ag/ZnO sample. This overall attenuation was attributed to the partial coverage of the ZnO surface by the metallic Ag nanoparticles, which screened the incident laser light and attenuated the Raman scattering signal from the underlying ZnO. These observations were fully consistent with the XRD and XPS results and collectively confirmed that the Ag/ZnO samples consisted of metallic Ag nanoparticles decorating the surface of ZnO nanoparticles, forming an Ag/ZnO nanocomposite with strong interfacial contact.

The SEM micrographs, as presented in Fig. 4, at decreasing magnifications of (a) 75 000× and (b) 22 000× show the morphology of ZnO. The images illustrate aggregated, dispersed particles of ZnO, featuring an average size of 100 nm. The particles appeared as clusters of small crystallites. No regular morphology was observed. Such agglomeration behavior is commonly reported for ZnO powders synthesized via co-precipitation methods, where strong interparticle interactions and high surface energy favor particle aggregation during drying and calcination. Fig. 5 reveals the surface morphology of all Ag/ZnO nanocomposites at various Ag concentrations (0.25 to 1 wt% Ag). At a magnification of 220 00×, ZnO showed an irregular spherical shape, whereas the doping of silver in ZnO successfully enhanced the morphology. Agglomeration was still observed.38,39 However, no major change in particle shape was caused by Ag addition. This similarity in morphology indicated that Ag was uniformly distributed in the structure of ZnO. Silver did not alter the overall nanostructure of the host material. The Ag/ZnO samples demonstrated good agreement with the observations from XRD analysis. With increasing silver concentration in 1Ag/ZnO, a slight increase in particle aggregation was noted. This may be due to the presence of silver nanoparticles on the ZnO surface.

To investigate the spatial distribution of the constituent elements, SEM-EDX elemental mapping was performed on the ZnO and 1Ag/ZnO samples (Fig. 6). The SEM image of pure ZnO (Fig. 6(a)) showed agglomerated nanoparticles, and the corresponding elemental maps revealed that zinc (Fig. 6(b)) and oxygen (Fig. 6(c)) were uniformly distributed throughout the sample, in agreement with the homogeneous ZnO wurtzite phase identified by XRD. For the 1Ag/ZnO sample (Fig. 6(d)), the Ag elemental map (Fig. 6(e)) showed that silver was homogeneously dispersed over the entire surface of the ZnO particles, with no evidence of large-scale segregation or localized Ag agglomerates. This uniform surface dispersion of the metallic Ag nanoparticles was consistent with the XRD, XPS, and Raman analysis results, confirming the presence of Ag0 at the ZnO surface.

TEM analysis was performed to examine the morphology of the 1Ag/ZnO catalyst. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the sample consists of rod-like ZnO particles with a heterogeneous size distribution, together with small, dark spherical nanoparticles (highlighted by circles) attached to the surface of the ZnO rods, which are attributed to the metallic Ag nanoparticles. This observation was consistent with the XRD, XPS, and Raman results, confirming that Ag was present as discrete nanoparticles deposited on the ZnO surface. The size distribution of the rod-like ZnO structures was quantified using ImageJ software, and the resulting histograms are presented in Fig. 7(b and c). The particle lengths ranged from 400 to 850 nm, and the widths were from 100 to 550 nm. This broad size variation was likely attributable to particle growth and aggregation phenomena occurring during the co-precipitation synthesis process.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of 1Ag/ZnO (a) and size distribution in the length (b) and width (c).

EDX analysis was used to determine the elemental composition of all samples through the detection of characteristic X-rays. The variation of the normalized Ag : ZnO mass ratio as a function of the nominal Ag loading (wt%) is plotted in Fig. 8. The raw EDX data of all materials are now provided in Fig. S4 in the SI. A progressive, nearly linear increase in the Ag : ZnO mass ratio was observed with increasing Ag loading, confirming the successful deposition of silver onto the ZnO nanoparticles in the nanocomposites. Among all samples, 1Ag/ZnO exhibited the highest silver content, in agreement with the nominal loading. This controlled Ag content played a key role in tuning the functional properties of the Ag/ZnO nanocomposites.40 A further increase in Ag doping concentration was expected to preserve the same proportional variation in the material composition.

Furthermore, granulometric analysis was performed by laser diffraction to evaluate the particle size distributions for ZnO and Ag/ZnO suspensions. Three characteristic diameter distributions were reported at Dx = 10, 50, and 90 µm, as shown in Table 1. All samples exhibited a similar distribution trend, with a gradual increase in particle size observed as the silver content increased. The Ag/ZnO nanocomposites had an average particle size of 90 µm and a relatively small size of 10–50 µm. For example, the size of Dx 10 particles increased from 2.28 µm for ZnO to only 5.74 µm for 1Ag/ZnO and from 12.5 µm to 14.8 µm in the case of Dx 50. The largest particles, represented by the Dx 90 values, exhibited an even more pronounced increase, rising from 191 µm for ZnO to 682 µm for 1Ag/ZnO. It revealed a distinct linear relationship between the average particle size of Dx 90 and the Ag doping content (wt%), as shown in Fig. S6 in the SI. This trend suggested the homogeneous incorporation and uniform size distribution of Ag nanoparticles within the ZnO matrix.41 Therefore, the morphological and granulometric analyses confirmed that silver was successfully incorporated in the ZnO surface. The uniform distribution of Ag and the increase in agglomerate size with high Ag loading were consistent with the EDX results.

Optical bandgap analysis

The optical bandgap of ZnO and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites was determined from transmittance measurements using the solar simulator. The Tauc plot method was applied,4 and the quantity (αhν)2 was plotted as a function of photon energy (hν), as shown in Fig. 9. For each sample, the linear portion of the absorption edge was identified by a systematic criterion consisting of Savitzky–Golay smoothing, followed by the selection of the contiguous points whose local slope exceeded 80% of the maximum slope below the absorption maximum. Furthermore, the bandgap was obtained by extrapolating the corresponding linear regression to (αhν)2 = 0. All fits yielded regression coefficients (R2) between 0.970 and 0.992. Considering the sensitivity of the extrapolation to the choice of the fitting window, an overall uncertainty of ±0.02 eV was associated with the reported values. The bandgap values and the corresponding absorption edge wavelengths are summarized in Table 2.

For ZnO, the bandgap was found to be 3.09 ± 0.02 eV, corresponding to an absorption edge wavelength of approximately 400 nm. This value indicates a slightly narrowed bandgap compared with the typical literature range for ZnO (≈3.20–3.37 eV),16 which is commonly attributed to structural defects, oxygen vacancies, or differences in synthesis conditions. Therefore, the observed red shift in the absorption edge may reflect defect-related states and improved light absorption in the near-UV region,42 which can be beneficial for photocatalytic applications.

Among the doped samples, 0.25Ag/ZnO exhibited the lowest bandgap, 3.03 ± 0.02 eV, i.e. a reduction of about 0.06 eV from that of pure ZnO. Although modest, this decrease exceeded the estimated uncertainty and may suggest that even a very low concentration of silver can modify the electronic structure of ZnO, either by incorporating Ag ions into the network or by forming impurity levels near the edges of the band. For 0.5Ag/ZnO and 0.75Ag/ZnO, the bandgap values were 3.07 ± 0.02 eV and 3.11 ± 0.02 eV, respectively. Both values were close to that of ZnO (3.09 ± 0.02 eV), and the differences lay within the experimental uncertainty, indicating that no significant bandgap modification occurred at these intermediate Ag concentrations. Therefore, the slight narrowing observed at 0.25% Ag was not maintained at high silver contents, consistent with the segregation of excess silver at the particle surface rather than its incorporation into the ZnO lattice. This interpretation was supported by the XRD patterns (Fig. 3(a)), where diffraction peaks of metallic Ag appeared for Ag contents ≥0.5 wt%. For 1Ag/ZnO, the value returned to 3.09 ± 0.02 eV, essentially identical to that of pure ZnO. Thus, apart from the slight narrowing observed at 0.25 wt%, the bandgaps of all doped samples remained close to that of ZnO, and the differences between them (0.02–0.08 eV) were comparable to the experimental uncertainty.

The main implication of these results is that silver doping, at the concentrations studied, leaves the optical bandgap of ZnO essentially unchanged (within ±0.02 eV), with at most a slight narrowing at the lowest Ag content. However, the main improvement in photocatalytic performance (discussed in section 3.4) was attributed more to charge separation than to bandgap narrowing alone. It should also be noted that the bandgap values presented here are derived from the transmittance measurements of suspensions, and light scattering by the particles may introduce additional uncertainty; nevertheless, the same procedure was applied consistently to all samples, so the relative comparison between them remained valid.

Optical suspension properties

The optical performance of catalysts is considered a key aspect to define the experimental conditions of the phenol photodegradation process in the near-UV to visible wavelength range. These measurements are strongly dependent on the experimental configuration (such as light characteristics, measurement cell geometry, and operating conditions). The aim of the optical tests was to experimentally determine the optimal concentration for each Ag/ZnO nanocomposite in suspension. This concentration was required to absorb >99% of incident light using the minimum amount of catalyst. The objective was to determine the number of photons effectively contributing to the initiation of photooxidation. These photons, emitted by the solar lamp at wavelengths below the catalyst's cutoff wavelength, were responsible for activating the process. The photocatalytic properties were evaluated by comparing the degradation efficiencies under identical experimental conditions.18,43

The optical properties of the suspensions were determined using an experimental optical setup, since the literature defined the optical behavior of photocatalytic media according to their specific structural characteristics, such as concentration for suspensions.44,45 An optical method was employed to define their ability to absorb the light flux density in a given configuration, which was required to ensure the optimal concentration for each Ag/ZnO catalyst in suspension. This concentration corresponded to a medium demonstrating virtually zero transmittance (i.e. 100% of incident light flux absorbed) to maximize photon utilization.43 An optical bench successfully set up in our previous work was used here to experimentally assess the transmittance of particulate media in suspension. Transmission measurements were conducted on Ag/ZnO of varying concentrations, ranging from diluted 0.01 to concentrated 3 kg m−3 in the near-UV to visible wavelength range. These concentrations were employed to differentiate between the material concentration and its ability to absorb a given quantity of photons. Fig. 10(A) and (B) show the transmittance spectra of ZnO and 0.5Ag/ZnO, respectively, at different concentrations, as a function of wavelength over a range from 350 to 700 nm. The spectra exhibited two distinct optical regimes. In the UV region (350–400 nm), both materials exhibited strong absorption, with transmittance dropping from approximately 99% in dilute suspensions (0.01 kg m−3) to about 1% in concentrated suspensions (3 kg m−3). In contrast, in the visible wavelength range (400–700 nm), the transmittance curves of ZnO and 0.5Ag/ZnO exhibited a completely different optical response. The increase in suspension concentration led to a linear increase in the variation of transmittance as a function of composition. For ZnO, the transmittance decreased with increasing concentration until it stabilized near 30%, indicating a pronounced light scattering phenomenon. For suspension concentrations from 0.1 kg m−3 to 0.5 kg m−3, the largest variations in transmittance were observed from the UV to visible wavelength range. There was a much stronger decrease in transmittance at higher concentrations in the case of 0.5Ag/ZnO. In addition, a large phase shift in transmittance curves was detected as a function of the wavelength range.31,39 These results were consistent with the literature and indicated that the pronounced variation in transmittance with wavelength was related to the distinct optical behaviors of Ag and ZnO. In the visible region, the optical response was mainly influenced by Ag species, while in the UV region, ZnO predominantly governed the absorption behavior.

Experimental transmittance measurement of (A) ZnO and (B) 0.5Ag/ZnO at different suspension concentrations (0.01 to 3 kg m−3) from near-UV to the visible wavelength range (350–700 nm).

The determination of optical properties is possible through the evaluation of key parameters known as extinction coefficients (Eext), which characterize the optimal configuration corresponding to the minimum catalyst concentration required to completely absorb the incident irradiance entering the cell. As light propagates through catalytic nanoparticle suspensions, it undergoes transmission, reflection, and absorption interactions that may alter its trajectory before complete utilization.28 This makes it necessary to define an ideal zero-transmittance configuration for each medium. Assuming negligible reflectivity at the scale of the measurement cell, the absorptivity of the catalyst suspension can be determined directly from the measured transmittance. The extinction coefficient (Eext) of each nanoparticle sample was theoretically determined by modeling the spectral transmittance measurements using the well-known Beer–Lambert law (eqn (1)). Experimentally, the methodology consists of linearizing this law. The variation of the natural logarithm of the transmittance (ln T), derived from the integration of eqn (1), is plotted in Fig. 11 for each catalyst, as a function of suspension concentrations for a fixed cell thickness. The objective of this work is to evaluate and distinguish the photocatalytic performance of catalysts depending on whether they operate under UV or visible radiation. To this end, the optical properties were evaluated across two spectral regions of interest commonly used in the literature: the UV (355–365 nm) and the visible (500–510 nm) regions. These 10 nm spectral ranges were selected to average the values obtained over a representative range and thereby smooth out measurement-related variations.30

Evolution of transmittance in the UV wavelength range from 355 to 365 nm: ZnO (Δ), 0.25Ag/ZnO (◊), 0.5Ag/ZnO (■), 0.75Ag/ZnO (▲) and 1Ag/ZnO (□).

In two spectral regions of interest, the experimental data exhibited a linear dependence on catalyst concentration, confirming the applicability of the Beer–Lambert law under these conditions. It is then possible to evaluate Eext of Ag/ZnO from near-UV (355–365 nm) to visible (500–510 nm) wavelengths. As shown in Fig. 12, the variation in the extinction coefficient (Eext) as a function of Ag doping content provides an evaluation of the influence of Ag incorporation (wt%) on the optical properties of ZnO. This trend highlighted an optimal value corresponding to a strong absorptivity capacity of the nanoparticles for Ag doping contents between 0.5 and 0.75 wt%. In the UV range, the 0.5Ag/ZnO sample exhibited the highest Eext, with Eext UV = 267 m2 kg−1. In contrast, the maximum Eext in the visible region was recorded for the 0.75Ag/ZnO composition, reaching Eext Vis = 156 m2 kg−1. In comparison, ZnO displayed significantly lower values, with Eext UV = 166 m2 kg−1 and Eext Vis = 49 m2 kg−1. Beyond this range, the extinction coefficient sharply decreased in the UV region with increasing Ag doping content, reaching a value of 120 m2 kg−1 for 1Ag/ZnO. Although the absorptivity capacity in the visible range decreased from the optimum value, it remained relatively high for 1Ag/ZnO, with an extinction coefficient of Eext Vis = 118 m2 kg−1. This value was more than 3 times higher than the value obtained for ZnO. These results confirmed the successful modification of ZnO optical properties through Ag doping, leading to a significant enhancement of its absorption capacity.

Photocatalytic activities

To determine catalyst performance under real-world irradiation, a series of photodegradation experiments were reported using photocatalytic efficiency. This parameter is directly representative of the catalyst's capacity to use effective photons. It indicated the direct dependence of the number of degraded molecules, calculated from the kinetic model, on the number of photons effectively available for the photocatalytic reaction (eqn (2)).4 The number of photons received was derived from the integration of the photon flux density for each wavelength over the spectral range considered. Efficiencies were determined for all Ag/ZnO nanocatalysts. This approach highlighted how synthesis conditions, such as Ag loading wt%, can be a key factor in photocatalytic efficiency.

Photodegradation measurements were conducted under the optimal catalyst loading and operating conditions detailed in Section 2.4.2. The kinetic results of phenol degradation for all materials were evaluated using a zero-order kinetic model as a function of the accumulated UV energy (QUV). The plots are presented in Fig. S7 in the SI. The number of photoconverted molecules was represented as a function of the number of absorbed photons received in the spectral range from 350 to 400 nm, as presented in Fig. 13. A linear dependence of the efficiency was observed for all Ag/ZnO nanocomposites at different Ag loadings (wt%), indicating that the photoconversion process was directly proportional to the number of absorbed photons. The observed linearity was maintained across all catalysts, regardless of their composition or the Ag content incorporated into the matrix. These plots were obtained under the initial reaction conditions, corresponding to a high initial phenol concentration. In this case, the influence of intermediate by-products can be considered negligible, confirming that the observed kinetics were not limited by their formation. This variation was plotted within the linear range corresponding to the initial conditions, characterized by a high concentration of target molecules and minimal byproduct formation. The efficiency was then deduced from the slope of kinetic lines for each nanocatalyst.47

Variation in the number of photoconverted molecules as a function of the number of total incident photons in the UV wavelength range from 350 to 400 nm: ZnO (Δ), 0.25Ag/ZnO (◊), 0.5Ag/ZnO (■), 0.75Ag/ZnO (▲) and 1Ag/ZnO (□).

The values of efficiency (ρ) were plotted against the Ag loading weight (wt%); they are presented in Fig. 14. The synthesized nanoparticles of Ag/ZnO exhibited significant photosensitivity under UV irradiation in the 350–400 nm wavelength range. The photocatalytic efficiency increased progressively with Ag doping content, rising from 0.25 to 1 wt%. At 0 wt% of Ag (ZnO), the efficiency started at a moderate value of 0.4 × 10−3, which reflected the intrinsic photocatalytic activity of ZnO under UV excitation. Consequently, their absorption promoted charge carriers to low excited states, resulting in the rapid recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs. At intermediate loading (0.5 wt%), efficiency increased to about 0.45 × 10−3 and continued to rise to 0.57 × 10−3 at 0.75Ag/ZnO. As anticipated, 1Ag/ZnO exhibited the highest efficiency value of 0.67 × 10−3. This progressive enhancement suggested that Ag nanoparticles began to play an effective role as electron sinks due to the formation of Schottky barriers at the Ag/ZnO interface. This facilitated charge separation by trapping photogenerated electrons from the ZnO conduction band, thereby reducing electron–hole recombination rates.48 Additionally, the possible contribution of surface plasmon resonance (SPR) from Ag nanoparticles may enhance local electromagnetic fields, improve photon utilization and promote photocatalytic reactions.49,50 Within the study range, the doping percentage for Ag/ZnO that yielded the highest efficiency was 1 (by weight). The optimal doping percentage for Ag/ZnO was 1 wt%. To evaluate the stability and reusability of the optimal 1 wt% Ag catalyst, post-reaction characterizations were carried out after two consecutive photocatalytic cycles. SEM-EDX analysis, including Ag elemental mapping, as shown in Fig. S5 in the SI, confirmed that silver remained uniformly distributed across the catalyst surface, with no evidence of leaching, dissolution, or aggregation. In line with this, the photocatalytic activity showed no significant decline over the first two cycles, indicating that the immobilized Ag species were firmly anchored and retained their functional integrity under the reaction conditions. This demonstrated the good structural stability of the 1Ag/ZnO photocatalyst under repeated use, which accounted for the retention of its photocatalytic activity upon recycling.

This indicated a significant role of metal loading in modulating charge dynamics and surface reactivity. According to the literature, Kusdianto et al. reported that the methylene blue (MB) degradation efficiency decreased when the Ag content was increased to 10 and 20 wt%. They suggested an optimal MB degradation performance at 5 wt% Ag. This behavior was explained by the synergistic effect between the electron–hole pair generation in ZnO under UV irradiation and the presence of Ag nanoparticles, which enhanced charge separation and photocatalytic activity.51 At a low doping quantity, Ag atoms were likely incorporated into the ZnO lattice through substitutional reactions, which was consistent with the present case. This structural incorporation was in agreement with the XRD analysis results. In addition, the photocatalytic results indicated a clear doping effect, as evidenced by the improved photoconversion activity in the UV range upon Ag doping. These observations agreed with studies reported by Rosset et al., highlighting similar incorporation mechanisms at low dopant contents and their positive impact on photocatalytic performance. They showed that the efficiencies obtained for Zn1−xMxO catalysts, where M represents alkaline earth metals (Ca and Mg), varied at dopant concentrations of x = 0.05 and x = 0.1.10,18,47 Yet, at high dopant contents, the dopant is generally not incorporated into the crystal lattice but tends to form surface deposits or segregated phases on the catalyst, as reported in the literature and confirmed in this work. This behavior was consistent with the findings of Wagh et al., who showed that Ag-doped ZnO exhibited an optimal photocatalytic activity at intermediate Ag loading (3–5 wt%), achieving degradation rate constants of 13 × 10−2 min−1 and 10 × 10−2 min−1 for methylene blue and rose Bengal, respectively. In contrast, at a relatively high Ag content (7 wt%), they reported a decrease in photocatalytic performance, although the material showed notable antifungal activity (45% efficiency against Bipolaris sorokiniana), highlighting that excessive doping could modify both the nature and efficiency of the catalytic response.19 A low Ag content led to true doping, which modified the structure and enhanced the photocatalytic activity. As shown in the literature, compositions with high dopant concentrations will either cause a change in the catalyst's structure—to the point of forming alloys at very high concentrations—or result in the catalyst being completely coated by the dopant. These changes are not the desired outcomes, and a compromise must be found to improve photocatalytic performance. In conclusion, Ag doping acts as an electron trap, promoting charge separation and suppressing electron–hole recombination, thereby increasing the photocatalytic efficiency with increasing Ag content.

To analyze the catalyst capacity under the visible domain, the number of photoconverted molecules was represented as a function of the number of absorbed photons received in the spectral range from 400 to 700 nm, as shown in Fig. S8 in the SI. The efficiency values were plotted as a function of Ag loading weight (wt%) under visible light irradiation in the wavelength range from 400 to 700 nm, as shown in Fig. 15. The results showed that the photocatalytic efficiency generally decreased with Ag incorporation. Pure ZnO exhibited the highest efficiency (≈0.03 × 10−3); this value remained significantly 10 times lower than that obtained under UV irradiation. This result confirmed the limited ability of ZnO to utilize visible light owing to its wide bandgap. The efficiency of photon absorption in the visible range was still very low compared with that in the UV range. It was found that photocatalytic activity was relatively high without doping. Thus, the presence of silver did not promote charge separation, either through the absorption of visible light via surface plasmon resonance or by limiting recombination. At high Ag loadings, a decrease in photocatalytic efficiency was observed.52 Consistent with the literature, high amounts of silver resulted in a decrease in photocatalytic activity, as compared with that of the undoped catalyst, which could be because the excitation level of the charges was too low to prevent their recombination.4,10,18

Despite efforts to improve the photocatalytic properties of the catalysts in the visible range, the performance of these catalysts was only slightly increased. The photons in the visible region possess lower energies than the UV photons. Consequently, their absorption promoted charge carriers to low excited states, resulting in a reduced driving force for photocatalytic reactions and a high probability of electron–hole recombination.4,10,18,33 These factors contributed to the low photocatalytic efficiency observed under visible-light irradiation. Furthermore, the present results are in agreement with studies reporting that (i) photocatalytic efficiencies under visible light are generally lower than those achieved under UV irradiation, and (ii) the effective utilization of visible light through dopant-induced bandgap engineering remains challenging due to increased charge recombination and limited charge-carrier generation. The relatively low visible-light efficiency suggested the rapid trapping of conduction-band electrons by states below the conduction band.53 Although Ag doping is found to shift the absorption band of catalysts towards the visible region, the nature of the doped species responsible for the visible-light activity and the resulting electronic modifications remain subjects of ongoing debate.

Conclusion

The development of efficient photocatalytic materials remains a major challenge for the implementation of solar-driven advanced oxidation processes dedicated to water treatment and reuse applications. Improving the photoconversion efficiency of solar radiation into reactive radical species is essential to achieve the performance levels required for practical applications, such as irrigation, urban cleaning, industrial water recycling, and groundwater recharge.

In this work, Ag/ZnO nanocomposites were successfully synthesized through a controlled co-precipitation method, with low silver-loading levels (0–1 wt%). The chosen doping strategy was designed to promote the incorporation of silver within the ZnO structure while minimizing the formation of metallic deposits on the particle surface. Structural and compositional characterizations confirmed the successful synthesis of homogeneous Ag/ZnO nanocomposites with physicochemical properties consistent with the targeted material design.

Optical characterization revealed that silver incorporation had only a limited influence on the bandgap energy of ZnO. This result suggested that Ag doping alone was insufficient to significantly extend the photoresponse of ZnO into the visible region. Nevertheless, the optical analysis provided valuable information for defining the optimal catalyst concentration in suspension, ensuring the efficient absorption of the incident radiation during photocatalytic experiments.

The photocatalytic performance of the materials was evaluated under both UV and visible irradiation using photocatalytic efficiency (ρ), defined as the number of pollutant molecules degraded per incident photon. This intrinsic performance indicator minimized the influence of experimental parameters, such as light intensity, spectral distribution, pollutant nature, and pollutant concentration, thereby enabling meaningful comparisons with literature data and between different experimental studies.

The obtained results demonstrated that photocatalytic activity under visible light remained approximately one order of magnitude lower than under UV irradiation, confirming that ZnO-based materials are still primarily activated within the ultraviolet range. Furthermore, no significant effect of silver doping on photocatalytic performance was observed under visible irradiation. In contrast, silver incorporation substantially enhanced the UV photocatalytic efficiency of ZnO, leading to an almost two-fold increase in performance. The highest photocatalytic efficiency was achieved for the material containing 1 wt% Ag, with a value of 0.67 × 10−3, as compared with 0.4 × 10−3 for undoped ZnO.

Overall, this study demonstrated that low-level silver doping constituted an effective strategy for improving charge separation and limiting electron–hole recombination in ZnO photocatalysts, resulting in enhanced UV-driven photocatalytic activity. While the limited visible-light response remains a significant challenge, the methodology proposed in this work, combining controlled material synthesis with photon-based performance evaluation, provides a robust framework for the development and comparison of next-generation photocatalysts. Future research should focus on coupling silver doping with complementary bandgap engineering approaches to improve visible-light harvesting and further increase the solar photoconversion efficiency of ZnO-based materials.
