Mitral Annular Disjunction
Authors: Shaaf Ahmad, Mili Dave, Faisal F Syed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: echocardiography, mitral annular disjunction, mitral valve prolapse, sudden cardiac death, ventricular fibrillation

Sudden cardiac arrest produces profound acute changes through myocardial stunning and injury, altered hemodynamic loading, and catecholamine excess, such that early assessment of myocardial and valvular structure and function may not reflect the baseline state. In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Srivatsav et al1 describe 4 patients aged 13 to 18 years with mitral annular disjunction (MAD) and malignant ventricular arrhythmia. In the 3 patients with moderate-to-severe early left ventricular (LV) dysfunction after cardiac arrest, MAD was not initially visualized but appeared 4-7 days later as LV systolic function recovered. In 2 patients, bileaflet mitral valve prolapse (MVP), also not initially visualized, became apparent after recovery. In the patient with preserved ventricular function, whose polymorphic ventricular tachycardia was promptly terminated by an appropriate implantable cardioverter-defibrillator shock, MAD was visible at baseline. Thus, the echocardiographic visibility of MAD, and sometimes MVP, varies with ventricular state. These findings support repeat evaluation after hemodynamic and systolic recovery and, more fundamentally, provide a clue to what MAD may represent.

MAD is commonly described as separation between the posterior mitral leaflet hinge point and adjacent ventricular myocardium. Yet it may be better understood as a dynamic mechanical expression of tissue excess and compliance at the mitral-annular-ventricular junction. These are related but distinct. Redundant leaflet tissue promotes prolapse and transmits traction through the chordae to the papillary muscles, whereas annular compliance may permit exaggerated systolic displacement and stretch of adjacent basal myocardium. Myxomatous leaflets are more extensible and less stiff than normal leaflets.2 Some studies associate MVP with generalized joint hypermobility.3 MAD is longer in young patients with connective tissue disorders than in healthy controls, and greater systolic length is associated with adverse outcomes.4

This framework reconciles MAD’s arrhythmic associations with its frequent detection without overt structural heart disease. Cardiac computed tomography identified some degree of MAD in 96% of structurally normal hearts, and UK Biobank cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) identified it in 76%; inferolateral MAD was uncommon.5,6 In MVP cohorts, MAD accompanies advanced myxomatous disease, bileaflet prolapse, and leaflet redundancy.7,8

MAD is not only more frequent but also longer with MVP, and a multicenter CMR registry found a length of at least 5 mm independently associated with arrhythmia.9,10 Greater MAD length has been associated with ventricular extracellular-volume expansion and late gadolinium enhancement.11,12

The importance of location may lie in the structures and forces with which MAD is coupled. The anterior annulus is supported by the aortomitral continuity; the posterior annulus is more mobile and directly coupled to ventricular myocardium. Posterior and inferolateral MAD place abnormal motion beside the basal inferolateral myocardium and in the force-transmission system of the leaflets, chordae, and papillary muscles. Accelerated longitudinal motion of this segment has been associated with local replacement fibrosis, MAD size, and bileaflet MVP.13

MAD and MVP frequently coexist but need not represent the same tissue abnormality. In the Norwegian cohort, 22% had MAD without MVP, and MVP itself was not associated with severe arrhythmic events.14 MVP may predominantly reflect excessive, compliant leaflet tissue, whereas MAD may more directly reflect annular compliance or mechanical uncoupling. MAD without MVP may reflect annular compliance disproportionate to leaflet excess, allowing systolic force to stretch open the junction without sufficient leaflet displacement to meet MVP criteria. Reverse remodeling has similarly unmasked MAD in an adult with nonischemic cardiomyopathy.15 In the UNC Health cohort, MAD was associated with complex or frequent ventricular ectopic activity and absence of hypertension, suggesting that loading may influence its mechanical expression.8

MAD, therefore, may represent not simply a fixed defect, but the dynamic expression of tissue properties that permit greater regional myocardial deformation. Its proarrhythmic significance may depend on location, length, circumferential extent, deformation under load, the accompanying mitral leaflet phenotype, and myocardial structural and repolarization characteristics. In this model, form determines how force is transmitted, and repeated abnormal force may alter function.

The dynamic appearance of MAD must also be distinguished from pseudo-MAD. Recent studies have defined true MAD as displacement visible in systole and diastole, and pseudo-MAD as apparent separation created only in systole by the prolapsing posterior leaflet.16 Strict application of this criterion, however, may underrecognize true, dynamically closing MAD. The displaced hinge point may be pulled from the ventricular myocardium during systole but become apposed in diastole, rendering the gap invisible. In pseudo-MAD, the posterior leaflet folds against the atrial wall and occupies the apparent disjunction space, causing its contour to be mistaken for the true annular insertion. The distinction, therefore, depends on tracking the posterior leaflet and true hinge point throughout the cardiac cycle, preferably in orthogonal views, rather than requiring a measurable gap in both phases.

The present series demonstrates state-dependent under-recognition of MAD, but not that MAD caused the arrests. The 4 cases were retrospectively selected from 35 patients with MAD, reviewed by one investigator, and imaged at different intervals under varying loading and support conditions. Three lacked prearrest echocardiography; measurements were confined to end-systole and 1 principal plane; and true vs pseudo-MAD remained unresolved. Both CMR studies showing late gadolinium enhancement followed the index arrest, so fibrosis cannot confidently be attributed to a chronic MAD/MVP substrate. Similarly, the reported genetic findings cannot be linked to the MAD or arrhythmic phenotype from the information provided.

The immediate message is to repeat structural assessment after LV recovery before concluding that a malignant ventricular arrhythmic event is idiopathic. The broader message is that recovery may restore the systolic force needed to reveal the abnormal mechanics permitted by underlying tissue redundancy and laxity, of which MAD is a dynamic expression.
