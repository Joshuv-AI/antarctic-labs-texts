Sports and exercise and changes in college students’ phubbing behavior: based on latent growth and cross-lagged models
Authors: Ai Ping Zhang, Yanhong Shao, Wanli Zang, Xinjuan Zhao, Xiaowei Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the bidirectional longitudinal relationship between sports and exercise and phubbing behavior among university students, with particular emphasis on their dynamic individuals with high initial levelss and developmental trajectories, thereby providing evidence-based interventions for promoting healthier lifestyle behaviors in this population. Using a cluster sampling approach, 689 college students (mean age = 20.34 years) were recruited from four universities in China and surveyed at three time points over one year, with 585 valid questionnaires retained for analysis. The “Physical Activity Rating Scale (PARS-3)” and the “General Scale for Phubbing Behavior (GSPB)” were employed to measure the variables. Cross-lagged regression models and parallel latent growth modeling (PLGM) were applied. Both sports and exercise and phubbing behavior exhibited significant longitudinal stability. Phubbing behavior negatively predicted subsequent levels of sports and exercise (β = -0.28), with this effect being stronger than the reverse prediction (β = -0.24). (2) Higher initial levels of phubbing behavior not only negatively predicted subsequent sports and exercise levels but also negatively predicted the rate of increase in sports and exercise over time (β = -0.35). (3) Conversely, higher initial levels of sports and exercise were significantly associated with lower levels of phubbing behavior. Sports and exercise may serve as a protective factor against the development of phubbing behavior, while excessive phubbing behavior significantly inhibits engagement in sports and exercise. Targeted interventions—such as promoting sports and exercise programs, psychological counseling, and peer-based reminder systems—are recommended to mitigate the adverse effects of phubbing behavior and foster healthier lifestyles among college students.Keywords: Sports and exercise, Phubbing behavior, Cross-lagged model, Latent growth modelSubject terms: Psychology, Health occupations

Introduction

Smartphones have improved communication convenience for college students but also pose risks of problematic use and dependence. Research shows that higher levels of sports and exercise(SE) are associated with reduced excessive mobile phone use, fewer negative emotions, and lower academic anxiety1,2. Building on this, the present study examines longitudinal associations and directional predictions between sports and exercise and phone-related problem behaviors. “Phubbing” refers to neglecting others by focusing on smartphones during face-to-face individuals with high initial levelss3. This behavior is linked to psychological issues, poorer academic performance, and social problems4,5, and shows a negative correlation with relationship quality and a positive association with academic burnout6,7. Thus, without intervention, phubbing may exacerbate academic and emotional problems and impact later development8. Given prior evidence that higher SE is linked with lower problematic smartphone use and academic stress1,2, we emphasize whether these associations with phubbing persist and show directionality across waves. To align with the aims of the present longitudinal, non-experimental design, we focus on the stability and directional predictions in the phubbing–sports and exercise relationship over time. Clarifying these longitudinal associations provides a necessary foundation for subsequent, more detailed mechanism testing.

The longitudinal induction mechanism of college students’ phubbing behavior

Given this definition, the longitudinal induction mechanisms and contextual factors of phubbing merit examination. This is especially pertinent given the behavior’s increasing prevalence among university students and its association with negative consequences such as psychological distress, academic underperformance, and social maladjustment4,5.

Grounded primarily in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT), phubbing can be understood as a behavior shaped by contextual cues (e.g., classroom norms), peer influences, and outcome expectancies, alongside self-regulatory processes9,10. In university settings, permissive learning climates and strained teacher–student individuals with high initial levelss may foster burnout and maladaptive media use11–13, while increased social media engagement further elevates phubbing prevalence14. From a habit perspective, prolonged sedentary patterns are linked to mood dysregulation and dependence-like use15,16, consistent with SCT’s emphasis on reciprocal determinism among environment, person, and behavior.

From a behavioral habit perspective, prolonged sedentary behavior is significantly associated with mood disorders and smartphone dependence15. University students who reduce physical activity due to prolonged smartphone use may experience compromised psychological well-being, further reinforcing phubbing tendencies16. Empirical evidence indicates that phubbing behavior is negatively correlated with relationship quality and positively correlated with academic burnout6,7.

Although existing research has identified several correlates of phubbing behavior, its underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. A longitudinal perspective suggests that phubbing behavior emerges from complex, long-term individuals with high initial levelss among multiple factors, and cross-sectional studies may yield biased interpretations of smartphone dependence mechanisms16. For example, peer pressure and conformity may play pivotal roles in perpetuating phubbing behavior9,10. Additionally, permissive learning environments, academic burnout, and increased sedentary behavior may collectively exacerbate phubbing through additive effects11,13,15. we test whether phubbing shows consistent rank-order stability across waves, as would be expected for routinized, contextually reinforced behaviors.

Based on this theoretical framework, we propose the following hypothesis:

H1

Among university students, phubbing/smartphone-dependent behavior will exhibit longitudinal stability, reflected by significant autoregressive paths across multiple time points.

Longitudinal characteristics of college students’ participation in SE

In this study, the term SE refers to structured, planned, and repetitive physical activities that individuals engage in during leisure time with the aim of improving physical fitness, social individuals with high initial levels, or psychological well-being. The engagement of college students in sports and exercise is influenced by a variety of theoretical frameworks and multifaceted factors. According to the Health Motivation Theory, the predominant motivations driving college students to participate in physical activities encompass promoting physical well-being, preventing diseases, enhancing body image, and fostering social individuals with high initial levels skills8,10,17. Furthermore, empirical evidence has consistently demonstrated that sports and exercise serves as an effective mechanism for emotional regulation, significantly improving mental health and enhancing self-efficacy18–21. The Self-Determination Theory further posits that sustained participation in physical activities is primarily propelled by intrinsic motivation and personal interest22.

However, research that focuses solely on a single time point may yield an incomplete or biased understanding of the dynamics underlying sports and exercise behavior. A growing body of literature indicates that the SE behavior of college students shows significant longitudinal continuity, yet it remains susceptible to influences from external environmental factors and individual characteristics. For instance, academic stress, inadequate time management skills, and family financial constraints have all been identified as potential barriers to regular exercise participation23,24. Additionally, an overemphasis on sports and exercise may lead to unintended negative consequences, such as exercise burnout, sports injuries, and imbalanced physical development25–27. Moreover, short-term overexertion in physical activity may not only fail to deliver the anticipated physical and psychological benefits but may also result in physical discomfort and emotional distress28,29. Beyond cross-sectional limitations, there are conceptual reasons to expect longitudinal continuity in SE among college students. Campus routines (e.g., course schedules, fixed training times), stable access to facilities and clubs, and peer norms can consolidate participation patterns across terms. Self-regulatory habits (planning, time allocation) also carry over from one period to the next, yielding moderate autoregressive stability in activity levels. Thus, a multiwave design enables explicit tests of whether SE remains stable over time.

To achieve a comprehensive understanding of the longitudinal continuity of college students’ sports and exercise behavior, it is imperative to adopt a longitudinal multi-time-point research perspective. This approach not only mitigates the biases inherent in single-time-point studies but also provides valuable insights into the long-term patterns and underlying mechanisms that shape college students’ exercise behavior over time.

Accordingly, we assess whether SE displays rank-order stability across waves, consistent with routinized, contextually reinforced behaviors. In this context, the current study proposes the following hypothesis (H2): College students’ sports and exercise will exhibit longitudinal continuity (i.e., significant autoregressive stability across waves), which may have implications for other behavioral patterns such as excessive screen time.

The interactive relationship between SE and college students’ phubbing behavior

For concision, references to phubbing in this subsection follow the introductory definition without restatement. Phubbing behavior among college students refers to the act of neglecting others in social individuals with high initial levelss due to a preoccupation with smartphones3. This behavior is highly prevalent in universities and can lead to psychological distress, declining academic performance, and social adaptation difficulties4. Research shows that phubbing is closely linked to smartphone dependence and may further trigger anxiety, depression, and even aggressive behaviors5.

The development of phubbing behavior is influenced by multiple factors. According to Social Cognitive Theory, group pressure, peer influence, and conformity play significant roles9,10. A relaxed academic environment, academic burnout, and dependence on online resources also contribute to excessive smartphone use11,13. Additionally, social demands in university life, such as social media individuals with high initial levelss, further increase phubbing frequency14. Prolonged phubbing behavior can weaken emotional regulation, self-efficacy, and self-control. Without timely intervention, it may lead to academic decline, psychological exhaustion, and emotional instability, ultimately affecting long-term quality of life8,30. Anchored in the same SCT-informed perspective, we consider how SE may relate to phubbing through shared contextual and self-regulatory processes.

SE is generally considered a protective behavior that helps prevent negative psychological and behavioral outcomes. It can reduce negative behaviors by improving emotional regulation, enhancing self-efficacy, and increasing self-control6,31. College students who engage in physical activities also tend to receive more social support, which alleviates psychological stress and reduces the risk of smartphone dependence32. Thus, sports and exercise may negatively predict phubbing behavior through these psychological and behavioral mechanisms.

Moreover, social compensation theory posits that when offline social or self-regulatory resources are constrained, individuals may “compensate” via increased online or media use, potentially fostering overreliance on smartphones and social media. This perspective complements SCT/SDT accounts of contextual cues and self-regulation and helps explain why phubbing may emerge under high stress and low support. Based on this, we propose Hypothesis (H3): sports and exercise will significantly and negatively predict phubbing behavior among college students in the subsequent period, meaning that more sports and exercise may reduce the frequency and intensity of phubbing behavior.

Research objectives

This study has three objectives, as specifically shown in Fig. 1. First, with the help of the latent growth modeling (LGM) structural equation model33, it explores the longitudinal multi-period trend of college students’ phubbing behavior. Second, it clarifies the longitudinal multi-period persistence of college students’ SE and analyzes the polymorphism under different groups. Furthermore, based on the examination of the relationship between cross-sectional variables, this study attempts to clarify that sports and exercise negatively predicts phubbing behavior in the next period under longitudinal multi-periods, with the help of the parallel latent growth theory model34. In the above studies, variables such as gender, age, major, place of origin, and family income were controlled respectively. Beyond prior cross-sectional work, this study integrates cross-lagged and parallel latent growth models across three waves to disentangle within- and between-person processes, testing causal direction and developmental trajectories. We also report structural validity and cross-wave stability, strengthening evidence methodologically, mechanistically, and measurement-wise.

Research methods

Research subjects

The study employed cluster sampling, selecting four universities from four Chinese provinces (Sichuan, Shandong, Henan, and Anhui). Firstly, the study established the sampling frames at the provincial and institutional levels: the provincial frame consists of the full list of the four provinces (based on list of colleges and universities released by the provincial department of education), and the institutional frame includes the full list of colleges and universities in each province. In the sampling stage, the list of colleges and universities was stratified and then one college was selected by using a computer random number table in each province’s list of colleges and universities; after entering the selected college, the survey conducted by cluster sampling according to the grade/class. A total of 689 questionnaire were distributed in three waves for this study. Exclusion criteria were pre-registered and applied to each wave:① Participants who did not provide a longitudinal match code, precluding cross-wave linkage; ② Those who left a key measure (PARS-3 or GSPB entirely blank in a given wave; ③ Those with an atypical length of time to respond (< = 2 min) or who responded in an unusually homogeneous manner (straight-lining ④ Those with extreme or contradictory responses on key items (e.g., contradictions in gender, grade) were also excluded. To maximize the use of incomplete data and potential bias due to selective attrition, a full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) approach was used to handle missing data in the longitudinal structural equation model23 Consequently, model estimation was conducted using 585 complete cases for analysis (vs. the original 689), and parameters were estimated using the FIML approach missing data on each wave.

Participant process

Prior to the survey, a work meeting was held to establish coordination mechanisms and ethical guidelines. The study followed a random cluster sampling method and adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from both school authorities and participants. Questionnaires were administered through WeChat using Questionnaire Star, with participants given 10 min to complete them anonymously. Questionnaires were administered via Wenjuanxing on WeChat. To enable longitudinal linkage while protecting privacy, we used a ‘linked-anonymity’ (pseudonymous) procedure: participants provided or were assigned a random matching code (e.g., a hashed combination of non-identifiable digits), used solely to link records across waves. The research team did not retain personally identifiable information; analyses used de-identified codes only. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the three-wave survey, and results were shared with respective universities along with intervention plans. This rigorous approach ensured ethical compliance, participant privacy, and data integrity throughout the research process. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee, Academic Committee of Leshan Normal University (approval number: LNU20230912).

Research tools

Sports and exercise scale

This study utilized the Physical Activity Rating Scale (PARS-3)24 to assess the level of SE among college students. The PARS-3 is a 3-item instrument using a 5-point Likert response format that evaluates the intensity, frequency, and duration of physical activity. This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity, with an internal consistency coefficient of 0.83 and a test-retest reliability of 0.8030. Confirmatory factor analysis for PARS-3 indicated acceptable fit (χ²/df = 8.37, CFI = 0.94, GFI = 0.98, NFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.042; see Table 2).

Phubbing behavior scale

This study employed the General Scale for Phubbing Behavior (GSPB). The revised Chinese version of the GSPB is a 15-item instrument across four dimensions: self-alienation, interpersonal conflict, nomophobia (fear of being without a mobile phone), and problem awareness35. The adaptation involved semantic adjustments of items and cross-cultural validation to fit the Chinese cultural context. The revised Chinese version of the GSPB comprises 15 items, divided into four dimensions: self-alienation, interpersonal conflict, nomophobia (fear of being without a mobile phone), and problem awareness. It uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree).

To verify the applicability of the scale, this study conducted reliability and validity analyses on the Chinese version of the GSPB. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) indicated good structural validity (χ²/df = 3.45, CFI = 0.94, RMSEA = 0.045, SRMR = 0.046; see Table 2), with factor loadings ranging from 0.68 to 0.84. Consistent with best practice, we also report loading-based reliability. McDonald’s omega (ω) for the GSPB total factor was 0.90 (T1), 0.89 (T2), and 0.89 (T3), supporting high reliability in our data. Additionally, the GSPB was significantly correlated with interpersonal relationships and self-efficacy (r = 0.41, p < 0.01), confirming its convergent validity. These results suggest that the revised GSPB is suitable for assessing phubbing behavior among Chinese college students.

To strengthen cross-cultural equivalence of the Chinese GSPB, we detail the adaptation: two bilingual researchers (measurement/health psychology backgrounds) independently conducted forward translation and semantic review; a third bilingual researcher, blinded to the original, performed back-translation; discrepancies were reconciled in a consensus meeting with two domain experts. Cultural refinements focused on classroom/peer etiquette, face-to-face individuals with high initial levels contexts, and in-class smartphone phrasing. A pilot (n ≈ 80) with cognitive interviewing informed minor revisions. Structural validity and reliability were re-examined in the main sample, with cross-wave stability assessed across three waves.

Covariates

Previous studies have shown that gender, age, place of origin, family income, and grade are related to sports and exercise; therefore, this study needs to include these variables as covariates and control for them. Among the covariates, we coded each variable with a 0 reference category for interpretability in regression/SEM: Gender (binary): 0 = male (reference), 1 = female.Place of origin (binary): 0 = urban (reference), 1 = rural. Age (categorical, 4 levels): dummy-coded with 0 = 18 years (reference); indicator dummies for 19, 20, and 21 years, respectively. Family income (categorical, 5 levels): dummy-coded with 0 = < 2,000 RMB (reference); indicator dummies for 2,000–5,000; 5,001–10,000; 10,001–30,000; and > 30,000 RMB. Grade (categorical, 3 levels): dummy-coded with 0 = freshman (reference); indicator dummies for sophomore and junior. In all multivariable models, the 0 category serves as the reference group, and coefficients for dummy indicators represent differences relative to that reference. The participants in this study were recruited from 4 universities, and random cluster sampling was conducted within different regions and schools. Therefore, we compared the correlations between different classes and school regions to determine the size of the endogeneity problem between schools. Our stepwise strategy is intentional: the Cross-Lagged Panel Model (CLPM) tests directional effects and temporal stability (H1, H2); the Latent Growth Model (LGM) estimates within-construct intercepts/slopes and individual differences; and the Parallel Latent Growth Model (PLGM) examines coupling between two latent trajectories (e.g., cross-construct intercept→slope effects, H3). These models are complementary rather than redundant and allow us to address directional and trajectory-level questions under a three-wave design. Note that all models were estimated on the same multi-wave dataset, and variables were modeled together as appropriate for each analytic aim; the PLGM was not the first point at which variables were combined. Across schools, we found no between-group differences in sports and exercise (SE) or phubbing (GSPB) at any wave. One-way ANOVAs showed non-significant mean differences across schools (e.g., T1 SE: F(3, 581) = 0.42, p = 0.74; T1 GSPB: F(3, 581) = 1.08, p = 0.36; T2 SE: F(3, 581) = 0.19, p = 0.90; T2 GSPB: F(3, 581) = 1.37, p = 0.25; T3 SE: F(3, 581) = 0.31, p = 0.82; T3 GSPB: F(3, 581) = 1.52, p = 0.21). In addition, intraclass correlations (ICCs) from empty multilevel models were small (all ICCs < 0.05), suggesting negligible school-level clustering for these outcomes.

Data analysis methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 for descriptive statistics, difference tests, and correlation analysis. Mplus 8.3 was used for all structural equation modeling, including the cross-lagged panel model (CLPM), the latent growth model (LGM), and the parallel latent growth model (PLGM). The analysis involved three steps: (1) establishing a cross-lagged effects model to address hypotheses H1 and H2, (2) constructing an LGM to observe change trends over three time periods, and (3) creating a PLGM to examine path coefficients and variable relationships, addressing hypothesis H3. Model fit was assessed using various indices (χ2/df, GFI, NFI, CFI, RMSEA) with established criteria. individuals with high initial levels terms (e.g., gender × phubbing, grade × exercise) were not included in the primary models. Missing data were handled with FIML. Cross-lagged and (parallel) growth models were estimated primarily with MLR; robust standard errors were obtained nonparametric bootstrapping (5,000 draws) for the confidence intervals. For the Bayesian sensitivity analysis, we report the posterior means and 95% CIsnot to be confused with bootstrapping) separately. The above settings and missingness handling strategy refer to Enders36. CFA loadings and all SEM path coefficients were evaluated using conventional significance thresholds: p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.00132. To support longitudinal comparisons, longitudinal measurement invariance of the PARS-3 and GSPB across T1, T2, and T3 was further examined using a stepwise CFA framework, including configural, metric, and scalar invariance models. Model comparisons were evaluated using changes in approximate fit indices, with ΔCFI and ΔRMSEA values within recommended cutoffs indicating acceptable invariance. The results supported acceptable longitudinal measurement invariance for both instruments; detailed model fit indices are provided in Appendix A.

Results

Common method bias

To address potential common method bias, Harman’s test was conducted on data from all three time periods33. Results showed the largest factor explained 19.27%, 21.46%, and 23.29% of the variance at T1, T2, and T3 respectively. As these values were below the 40% critical threshold, no significant common method bias was indicated in the study.

Cross-lagged regression analysis of sports and exercise level and phubbing behavior

Cross-lagged effects analysis using Mplus 8.3 examined the relationship between college students’ SE and phubbing behavior. The model showed good fit (χ2/df = 2.07, p = 0.04, CFI = 0.93, TLI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.02, SRMR = 0.05). Tests of temporal invariance of paths indicated that constraining autoregressive paths to equality across T1→T2 and T2→T3 did not meaningfully degrade fit (ΔCFI = 0.002, ΔRMSEA = 0.001; SB-Δχ²2= 3.84, p = 0.147). Likewise, equating the cross-lagged paths across adjacent waves was tenable (ΔCFI = 0.004, ΔRMSEA = 0.000; SB-Δχ²2= 4.26, p = 0.119). Thus, the pattern of effects was approximately time-invariant; substantive conclusions were unchanged under either specification. SE negatively predicted subsequent phubbing, and phubbing negatively predicted subsequent exercise. These cross-lagged associations are reported separately from the stability results and do not pertain to H1/H2. Phubbing behavior was found to be the stronger predictor of future SE.

To improve clarity on temporal dependencies, we note that Fig. 2 includes both stability (autoregressive) and cross-lagged paths across T1–T3. For stability, SE at T1 significantly predicts SE at T2, and SE at T2 predicts SE at T3; likewise, GSPB at T1 predicts GSPB at T2, and GSPB at T2 predicts GSPB at T3, indicating longitudinal stability. For cross-lagged effects, SE at T1 significantly predicts lower GSPB at T2, and GSPB at T1 significantly predicts lower SE at T2; these cross-lagged relations persist from T2 to T3. Regarding T1→T3 influences, the model tests (a) whether there are any retained direct paths from T1 to T3 and (b) the indirect effects from T1 to T3 via T2. In our specification, the substantive T1→T3 influence is primarily indirect through the T1→T2 and T2→T3 chains: (i) within-construct stability chains (e.g., SE T1→SE T2→SE T3; GSPB T1→GSPB T2→GSPB T3) and (ii) cross-construct chains (e.g., SE T1→GSPB T2→GSPB T3; GSPB T1→SE T2→SE T3). Accordingly, SE at T1 relates to lower GSPB at T3 mainly through SE T1→GSPB T2 and GSPB T2→GSPB T3; conversely, GSPB at T1 relates to lower SE at T3 mainly through GSPB T1→SE T2 and SE T2→SE T3. These patterns hold with and without covariates. We emphasize that phubbing showed the stronger prospective inhibition of subsequent SE.

Latent variable growth model of SE level and phubbing behavior

The latent variable growth model examines development trajectories and trends, offering advantages over traditional methods in analyzing data characteristics and addressing individual and group variations. It uses intercept and slope parameters to represent initial levels and growth rates37.

This study established a latent variable growth model to compare individuals with high initial levelss and predictive effects between sports and exercise and college students’ phubbing behavior across parallel time points. The model for SE showed good fit (x2/df = 0.76). The estimated intercept mean was 45.28 (p < 0.01). The slope mean was not significantly different from zero (Mean Slope = 0.21, p = 0.18), indicating no systematic linear increase across waves. There was significant inter-individual variability in both the intercept and slope (Intercept variance = 0.25, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001; Slope variance = 0.04, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001). The intercept–slope covariance was negative and significant, suggesting that higher initial SE levels were associated with slower subsequent change.

Although the low χ²/df value (0.76) indicates a good model fit, the low degrees of freedom may pose a risk of overfitting. To ensure the robustness of the results, this study conducted a comprehensive evaluation using multiple fit indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Additionally, the model design was based on theoretical foundations to avoid overfitting due to reduced degrees of freedom.

Discussion

This study found that college students’ participation in SE slows down with the passage of time, and individuals with high initial levels grow more slowly in the later period, which is consistent with previous research results34,37. In addition, this study also found that there are significant individual differences in both the initial level and subsequent growth rate of college students’ phubbing behavior, with groups having high initial levels exhibiting relatively slower growth rates in the later period, which is consistent with the results related to problematic mobile phone use38,39. The study confirms social compensation theory using a latent variable parallel growth model, revealing the mechanism between phubbing and SE in college students.

The mutual predictive relationship between college students’ SE level and phubbing behavior

Key findings are: (1) both exercise and phubbing show longitudinal stability; (2) they negatively predict each other with asymmetrical strengths; (3) PLGM indicates intercept–slope coupling. Through the cross-lagged regression analysis model, this study found that the level of SE in longitudinal data can significantly negatively predict phubbing behavior in the subsequent time period, and phubbing behavior can also negatively predict the level of SE in the next time period, both cross-lagged paths were significant; however, the GSPB→SE path was stronger than the SE→GSPB path (e.g., βGSPB→SE = [value], p < 0.05 vs. βSE→GSPB = [value], p < 0.05). On the one hand, some scholars have confirmed that the higher the level of college students’ sports and exercise, the higher their individual subjective initiative, the better their self-efficacy level, and their emotion regulation and willpower are higher than those of groups with lower levels, and they have lower dependence on mobile phones33. We did not directly measure putative mediators such as self-efficacy or emotion regulation; these inferences are literature-based. Future work should include these constructs and test longitudinal mediation or PLGM-mediation frameworks. The study revealed that college students who engage in sports and exercise exhibit significantly reduced phubbing behavior, which may be attributed to the enhanced emotional regulation and self-discipline resulting from physical activity6,28. Additionally, high levels of sports and exercise are generally associated with higher self-efficacy and stronger self-control, as confirmed in other studies29,33. Although research has suggested that sports and exercise may further reduce negative behaviors by improving social support and the quality of interpersonal relationships34, this mechanism was not directly verified in the current study. On the other hand, previous studies have also confirmed that phubbing behavior can predict the level of sports and exercise participation33. The lower the level of phubbing behavior observed in the previous time period of college students, the higher the degree of SE in the next time period, which may be due to the low negative self-awareness of individuals and the existence of a high degree of positive awareness and subjective self-restraint, which will drive individuals to participate in daily sports and exercise activities and further affect the shaping and persistence of SE behavior34. In addition, we found that it may be due to the approaching graduation exams and the advancement of academic difficulty of college students, the time and energy spent on SE activities are reduced, and academic burnout emotions and slack behaviors gradually intensify37, which also leads to more frequent phubbing behavior, and the main effect of phubbing behavior predicting SE is more significant. The main and secondary effects of the cross-lagged path coefficients are actually the explanation of the causal variables and independent variables. This result may be due to the increasing complexity of social interpersonal communication, the increase of social affairs, and the gradual decline of motivation, which leads to the induction of emotions such as lying, laziness, and burnout, and thus forms the result that phubbing behavior significantly negatively predicts the level of SE38.

Developmental trajectories and dynamic associations between college students’ SE levels and phubbing behavior

The discussion refrains from redefining the construct and focuses on extending theory regarding pathways and trajectories based on our findings. This study primarily employed a parallel latent growth curve model to examine the developmental trend characteristics and individuals with high initial levels mechanisms of college students’ SE levels and phubbing behavior. The results revealed that the development of college students’ mobile phone phubbing behavior levels remained stable, indicating that the formation of initial phubbing behavior has a certain impact on the development of later levels. This conclusion provides a reference for subsequent intervention and guidance on college students’ mobile phone phubbing behavior.

In the initial development stage, college students’ phubbing behavior and sports and exercise behavior showed a significant negative correlation. That is, the more severe the initial phubbing behavior, the lower the level of participation in sports and exercise during leisure time, which is consistent with the results of other scholars39. This negative correlation may be caused by the decline in individuals’ inhibitory ability, such as decreased self-efficacy, deterioration of interpersonal relationship quality, and weakening of self-control ability among college students28. When college students are subjected to external social pressures, peer verbal attacks, and academic burnout, it may trigger emotional disturbances and disorders within the individual, thereby reducing the control over mobile phone usage and exacerbating phubbing behavior29. At the same time, college students with high phubbing behavior may experience negative behaviors such as mental lethargy and cognitive function decline, which may induce individuals to feel burnout, aversion, and avoidance in their studies. This can easily lead to individuals choosing online mobile games, lying flat, and slacking behaviors, resulting in being immersed in the phubbing group33. On the other hand, the “social-psychological-physiological” model suggests34 that individuals are easily influenced by the social environment. If cognitive levels and emotional regulation cannot be effectively improved, it can easily lead to psychological-cognitive mismatches and escape behaviors, causing physiological dependence and indulgence, resulting in an increase in the level of symptoms such as lying flat, academic burnout, slacking, internet addiction, and mobile phone phubbing. Groups with high levels of sports and exercise are more likely to gain recognition and praise from others and have higher levels of social support. In contrast, Individuals with low levels of sports and exercise participation may not experience physical and mental relief, which can easily lead to individuals becoming introverted, ruminating, and experiencing misunderstanding and lack of support from others, as well as biased language, sarcasm, and negative evaluations. This leads to the emergence of individual inner fragility and avoidance behaviors, which in turn triggers mobile phone addiction and dependence behaviors37. Finally, under the culture of group identity, groups with high levels of SE participation are more likely to obtain extensive networks and high-quality friendships38. From the beginning of college life, parents and elders also constantly advise individuals to improve their exercise levels and interpersonal communication skills. The greater the expectations and pressures from parents, the more it may exacerbate individual anxiety, rumination, and emotional discomfort39. When ability levels do not meet the standards, individuals may choose to avoid and are more likely to choose to lie flat and slack off in their studies, which over time leads to the emergence of internet addiction and mobile phone phubbing behavior. This establishes an association between phubbing behavior and sports and exercise levels.

The results of this study found that groups with higher initial mobile phone phubbing behavior had slower development rates in subsequent periods. It was also found that individuals with higher initial sports and exercise participation levels had slower development rates of mobile phone phubbing behavior in subsequent periods. This provides important evidence that sports and exercise can serve as a protective factor to interfere with the development of mobile phone phubbing behavior. Resource growth theory suggests that an ideal and abundant environment is conducive to individuals’ self-growth28, while an unfavorable environment will stifle their emotions and inhibitions, erode their willpower, and ultimately affect their healthy growth. This provides a theoretical basis for explaining that moderate levels of sports and exercise can ensure healthy growth. We can consider that the level of sports and exercise can serve as an important social resource. With the progression of time, college students with high levels of sports and exercise improve their social adaptation, interpersonal relationship quality, emotional regulation ability, and inhibition expression levels, leading to a reduction or suppression of boredom, lying flat, and mobile phone dependence. This also leads to a decrease in the development rate of their mobile phone phubbing behavior. Furthermore, Chinese culture emphasizes harmony and unity, which leads college students to enter social and interpersonal relationship circles during their time in school and establish their own networks and communication methods. Participation in sports and exercise activities can provide more opportunities, and using physical activities as a channel is more conducive to improving individuals’ social support and interpersonal relationships29. The power behind this can further resolve individuals’ negative emotions33, further curbing the occurrence rate of mobile phone phubbing behavior. Based on this, In the context of a culture that values harmonious unity and sports and exercise participation, this conclusion has important reference significance for targeted prevention of college students’ mobile phone phubbing behavior. Theoretically, we extend SCT by showing how peer/academic contextual cues may shape behavioral trajectories via smartphone-use habits, illustrating reciprocal determinism. We also extend SDT by indicating links between intrinsic motivation for exercise and behavioral persistence vis-à-vis conflicts with addictive media use (self-control and need satisfaction). This tension motivates future models incorporating autonomy support and peer norms. Specifically, if school physical education workers, psychological counselors, class tutors, and school mental health stations can promptly observe the decline and deterioration of the college students’ SE levels, they can roughly predict the rise of mobile phone phubbing behavior. They can then take timely and precise measures to curb the continued deterioration of college students’ phubbing behavior through psychological counseling, exercise intervention, and peer reminders. Unlike previous behavioral interventions, promoting physical activity, investing in sports and exercise programs, and motivation-inducing models can have a more direct and convenient effect34.

Research limitations and future directions

Using a three-wave design and combining CLPM with PLGM strengthened inferences about directionality and trajectory coupling. Nonetheless, limitations remain: (1) the four-university sample may limit generalizability and entail campus-level clustering; (2) reliance on self-report without wearables or anonymized digital traces increases common-method risk; (3) three waves constrain modeling of non-linear change and short-term dynamics; (4) primary models did not include latent individuals with high initial levelss or longitudinal mediation, and key mechanism variables (e.g., self-efficacy, emotion regulation, peer norms) were not directly measured, so causal claims remain limited in an observational design. Future work should expand to more provinces and institutional types with stratified multistage sampling and multilevel models; integrate wearables and app-use logs to improve validity; add waves and consider non-linear or change-point models with experience sampling to enhance temporal resolution; incorporate mechanism variables with multi-group/latent individuals with high initial levelss and chained longitudinal mediation; and, where feasible, implement randomized or quasi-experimental interventions to strengthen causal inference.

Conclusions

Across three waves, both sports and exercise and phubbing exhibited longitudinal continuity, and they negatively predicted each other, with phubbing exerting the stronger inhibitory effect on subsequent activity. PLGM indicated that higher initial phubbing related to lower later activity levels and slower activity growth, whereas higher initial activity related to lower phubbing levels. Overall, activity may be protective, while elevated phubbing may suppress participation. These findings support early, combined programs in higher education that promote SE alongside digital hygiene, leverage peer norms, and provide tiered support to reduce phubbing risk and foster healthier behaviors. Given the bidirectional relationship and trajectory coupling observed, universities should adopt health promotion strategies that integrate SE programs with digital well-being education, encouraging balanced physical activity and responsible smartphone use among students.
