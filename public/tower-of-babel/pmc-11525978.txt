Artificial intelligence prediction of the mechanical properties of banana peel-ash and bagasse blended geopolymer concrete
Authors: George Uwadiegwu Alaneme, Kolawole Adisa Olonade, Ebenezer Esenogho, Mustapha Muhammad Lawan, Edward Dintwa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research explores the application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques to assess the mechanical properties of geopolymer concrete made from a blend of Banana Peel-Ash (BPA) and Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA), using a sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) to sodium hydroxide (NaOH) ratio ranging from 1.5 to 3. Utilizing three AI methodologies—Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS), and Gene Expression Programming (GEP)—the study aims to enhance prediction accuracy for the mechanical properties of geopolymer concrete based on 104 datasets. By optimizing mix designs through varying proportions of BPA and SCBA, alkaline activator molarity, and aggregate-to-binder ratios, the research identified combinations that significantly enhance mechanical properties, demonstrating notable international relevance as it contributes to global efforts in sustainable construction by effectively utilizing industrial by-products. The experimental results demonstrated that increasing the molarity of the alkaline activator from 4 to 10 M significantly enhanced both the compressive and flexural strengths of the geopolymer concrete. Specifically, a mixture containing 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA at a 10 M molarity achieved a maximum compressive strength of 33.17 MPa after 20 h of curing. In contrast, a mixture composed of 95% SCBA and 5% BPA at a 4 M molarity exhibited a substantially lower compressive strength of only 21.27 MPa. Additionally, the highest recorded flexural strength of 9.95 MPa (77.25% SCBA and 22.5 BPA) was observed at the 10 M molarity, while the flexural strength at 4 M was lowest, at 4.12 MPa (95% SCBA and 5% BPA). Microstructural analysis through Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (ED-SEM) revealed insights into the pore structure and elemental composition of the concrete, while Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA) provided data on the material’s thermal stability and decomposition characteristics. Performance analysis of the AI models showed that the ANN model had an average MSE of 1.338, RMSE of 1.157, MAE of 3.104, and R2 of 0.989, while the ANFIS model outperformed with an MSE of 0.345, RMSE of 0.587, MAE of 1.409, and R2 of 0.998. The GEP model demonstrated an MSE of 1.233, RMSE of 1.110, MAE of 1.828, and R2 of 0.992, confirming that ANFIS is the most accurate model for predicting the mechanical and rheological properties of geopolymer concrete. This study highlights the potential of integrating AI with experimental data to optimize the formulation and performance of geopolymer concrete, advancing sustainable construction practices by effectively utilizing industrial by-products.Keywords: Geopolymer concrete, Gene expression programing, Artificial neural networks, Adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system, Green concreteSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The increasing demand for sustainable construction materials has led to the exploration of alternative binders to reduce reliance on traditional Portland cement, which contributes significantly to global CO2 emissions. Geopolymer concrete, a promising eco-friendly alternative, utilizes industrial and agricultural waste materials to reduce carbon footprints. Among the potential waste materials, Banana Peel Ash (BPA) and Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA) have garnered interest due to their high silica and alumina content, making them suitable for geopolymerization1. However, the mechanical properties of geopolymer concrete made with BPA and SCBA blends, including compressive strength, flexural strength, and workability, can vary significantly based on the mix design and curing methods. While laboratory testing provides valuable insights into the mechanical properties of geopolymer concrete, it is time-consuming and resource intensive. Therefore, artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative tool in various fields, revolutionizing traditional methodologies and enhancing predictive capabilities. In the realm of material science and construction, AI-driven predictive models have garnered significant attention for their potential to optimize material compositions and properties2. This is particularly evident in the development of innovative materials like Banana Peel-Ash (BPA) and Sugarcane Bagasse ash (SCBA) blended Geopolymer Concrete, which offer sustainable alternatives to conventional concrete. BPA and SCBA, waste by-products from agricultural processes, have been identified as potential supplementary cementitious materials due to their abundance, low cost, and pozzolanic properties. Geopolymer concrete, which is produced by activating aluminosilicate materials with alkaline solutions, offers a promising avenue for incorporating these waste materials into concrete matrices, thereby reducing environmental impact, and promoting circular economy principles3.

Waste material blended geopolymer concrete, incorporating industrial and agricultural by-products like Banana Peel Ash (BPA) and Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA), offers significant potential for sustainable and eco-efficient construction. These waste materials serve as alternative aluminosilicate sources, reducing reliance on traditional Portland cement, which is responsible for significant CO2 emissions4. The chemical composition of BPA and SCBA, rich in silica and alumina, plays a crucial role in the geopolymerization process, forming a stable matrix that improves the mechanical properties of concrete. The use of these materials in geopolymer concrete not only promotes waste valorization but also enhances the concrete’s performance characteristics5. Studies have shown that the optimal blend of agro-industrial waste ashes, when activated by alkaline solutions, leads to improved mechanical behavior. This improvement is attributed to the formation of a denser microstructure due to the reactive silica and alumina content from the waste materials. Moreover, the incorporation of BPA and SCBA aids in refining pore structures, enhancing the durability and resistance to environmental degradation, making this geopolymer concrete a promising candidate for sustainable construction practices6.

The prediction of mechanical properties of agro-industrial waste blended Geopolymer concrete presents challenges due to the complex interactions among its constituents and processing parameters. Traditional empirical approaches often fall short in accurately capturing these intricate relationships, leading to suboptimal material designs and costly experimental iterations7. The utilization of agro-industrial waste derivatives as geopolymer precursor has been found to be a beneficial technique to obtain sustainable and eco-efficient construction materials8. In a study by Kamsuwan9, the potential of using banana peel charcoal powder (0–15%) as an additive to enhance geopolymer properties was explored. The geopolymer mix included 50% fly ash, equal parts Na2SiO3 and 12 molar NaOH, combined with river sand in a 1:2 ratio. Analysis with Scanning electron microscope (SEM) and Energy Dispersive X-Ray Analysis (EDS) showed that adding banana peel charcoal powder improved compressive and flexural strength and reduced water absorption at optimal ratios. Similarly, Akbar et al.10 carried out research aimed to reinforce sugarcane bagasse ash (SBA) based geopolymer with propylene fibers. Physical and mechanical properties of geopolymers with varying propylene fiber percentages were tested. Their results indicated that addition of propylene fibers improved flexural and tensile strength. Limiting propylene fiber content to 1% enhanced flexural and compressive strength, creating a denser microstructure. The study concludes that utilizing SBA composite reinforced with propylene fibers offers sustainable waste management alternatives, reducing environmental degradation caused by landfilling.

The integration of AI into the prediction of agro-industrial waste derivatives blended Geopolymer concrete’s mechanical properties offer several key advantages. It reduces the reliance on expensive and time-consuming experimental tests by providing rapid virtual simulations. This accelerates the material development process and facilitates the exploration of a wide range of composition and curing parameters11. Also, AI enables the identification of optimal material formulations that balance performance requirements with sustainability objectives, thus promoting eco-friendly construction practices. Additionally, AI-driven models can adapt and improve over time as they are fed with more data, ensuring continuous enhancement of predictive capabilities12. Aneja et al.13 investigated fly-ash-based geopolymer concrete (GPC) strength, with bottom ash replacing fine aggregates, using a machine learning-based ANN model. Data inputs were from literature and in-house GPC testing. GPC specimen specifications were input features to predict compressive strength, minimizing errors. Fourteen ANN models with varied backpropagation, hidden layers, and neurons were designed. Performance analysis, based on mean squared error (MSE) and coefficient of correlation (R), favored a Bayesian regularized ANN (BRANN) model for effective predicting fly ash and bottom ash-based geopolymer concrete compressive strength. Also, Khan et al.14 employ gene expression programming (GEP) soft computing techniques to derive an empirical equation for estimating the compressive strength (f′c) of geopolymer concrete (GPC) made using fly ash (FA). A comprehensive dataset of 298 experimental results is compiled from published research. Dominant parameters such as extra water added as percent of FA (%EW), plasticizer percentage (%P), curing conditions, and aggregate ratios are considered as explanatory variables. The proposed GEP equation’s accuracy and prediction capability are evaluated through parametric analysis and statistical checks, demonstrating its superiority over traditional regression equations.

The study aims to develop AI-driven predictive models such as tailored specifically for agro-industrial blended geopolymer concrete, considering the unique properties and interactions of BPA, SCBA, and geopolymer binder. By calibrating these models with experimental data and continuously refining them with additional information, researchers seek to establish reliable tools for engineers and practitioners to optimize material formulations, assess performance, and expedite the development of sustainable concrete solutions. The application of Artificial Intelligence in predicting the mechanical properties of Banana Peel-Ash and Bagasse blended Geopolymer Concrete represents a paradigm shift in material science and construction engineering. By harnessing the computational power of AI, researchers can unlock new insights, optimize material designs, and pave the way for the widespread adoption of sustainable and high-performance concrete solutions15,16.

Furthermore, the study on Artificial Intelligence Prediction of the Mechanical Properties of Banana Peel-Ash (BPA) and Bagasse blended geopolymer concrete holds significant importance across several domains. Artificial intelligence supervised machine learning algorithms

Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the simulation of human intelligence in machines, allowing them to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and language understanding21. AI systems are designed to analyze data, learn from it, and make decisions or predictions based on that data. They encompass a wide range of technologies and techniques, including machine learning, natural language processing, computer vision, robotics, and expert systems22. AI has applications across various industries and sectors, including material science, engineering, healthcare, finance, manufacturing, and agriculture. Key components of AI include machine learning, which involves training algorithms to recognize patterns and make predictions from data, and deep learning, which uses neural networks with many layers to learn from large amounts of labeled data23.

Supervised learning is a machine learning approach where a model is trained on labeled data, meaning the input–output pairs are known. The model learns to map inputs to outputs by minimizing the difference between its predictions and the actual labels. The goal is to generalize well, allowing the model to make accurate predictions on new, unseen data24. Supervised learning algorithms include linear and logistic regression, artificial neural networks (ANN), adaptive neuro fuzzy inference system (ANFIS), and gene expression programming (GEP). These methods have been used to resolve the problems of the pre-mix design of cement-additive blend optimization in concrete and soil optimization25.

Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) are computational models that emulate the structure and behavior of biological neural networks found in the brain. These networks are composed of interconnected nodes arranged in layers, which typically include input, hidden, and output layers as shown in Fig. 126. ANNs are adept at learning intricate patterns from data through a process called forward propagation, where input data undergoes transformations as it traverses through the layers. Subsequently, errors are identified and corrected via backpropagation, enabling the adjustment of network parameters to minimize discrepancies between predicted and actual outcomes27,28. The critical components of ANNs include weights, biases, and activation functions. Weights determine the strength of connections between neurons, biases allow for additional flexibility in modeling, and activation functions introduce non-linearities to the network, enabling it to capture complex relationships within the data29. ANNs have demonstrated remarkable efficacy across a spectrum of tasks, including image recognition, natural language processing, and autonomous driving. Their versatility and ability to learn from large datasets have established them as a cornerstone of deep learning, a subset of machine learning focused on hierarchical learning representations. This significance underscores their pivotal role in advancing technologies and solving complex real-world problems30.

Diagram illustrating the structure of a multilayer perceptron (MLP) with single hidden layer.

The Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) is a hybrid intelligent system that blends neural network and fuzzy logic functionalities to perform adaptive and accurate inference, especially in modeling complex and nonlinear systems31. ANFIS comprises five layers: input, fuzzy inference, normalization, consequent, and output layers. These layers work together to process input variables, apply fuzzy logic, normalize firing strengths, calculate consequents, and generate final outputs32. The parameters of the ANFIS model, including the membership functions and rule base, are adapted or learned from training data using a combination of gradient descent optimization and least squares estimation techniques33. This adaptive learning process allows the ANFIS model to adjust its parameters to better fit the training data and improve its predictive performance as shown in Fig. 2. ANFIS finds applications in diverse fields such as control systems, time series prediction, pattern recognition, and modeling complex nonlinear systems, offering a powerful solution for real-world problems34.

Gene Expression Programming (GEP) is an evolutionary algorithm conceived by Candida Ferreira, building upon the principles of Genetic Programming (GP). GEP operates by evolving populations of expression trees, which are structured combinations of functions, terminals, and constants, tailored to address particular problem domains35. The algorithm boasts several distinctive traits, including its modular gene structure, where genes are composed of segments representing functions and terminals, its utilization of hierarchical expression trees for representation, and its comprehensive approach to fitness evaluation36. Moreover, GEP employs a range of evolutionary operators such as mutation, crossover, and gene transposition to foster genetic diversity within populations, alongside selection mechanisms to determine the propagation of promising solutions. Termination criteria dictate the cessation of the evolutionary process based on predetermined conditions as shown in Fig. 3.37. The versatility of GEP is evident in its applications across diverse fields including symbolic regression, time series prediction, classification, and optimization. Its capacity to generate concise and understandable solutions underscores its effectiveness in addressing complex problems. GEP is an evolutionary algorithm that evolves mathematical expressions represented as expression trees to solve complex problems in regression, optimization, and modeling. Its modular gene structure, hierarchical representation, and evolutionary operators make it a versatile and effective tool for a wide range of applications38,39.

Research methodology

This study employs a structured methodology to predict the mechanical properties of Banana Peel-Ash and Bagasse Blended Geopolymer Concrete using Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques. The research process begins with identifying the gap in the literature: while the use of agricultural waste in geopolymer concrete is well-researched, there is a lack of robust AI-based predictive models that can optimize mix designs, especially when using unconventional materials like Banana Peel Ash (BPA) and Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA). Addressing this gap, the methodology is designed to bridge the experimental and computational aspects of material performance prediction. The methodology for predicting the mechanical characteristics of Banana Peel-Ash and Bagasse Blended Geopolymer Concrete through artificial intelligence comprises various essential stages. These encompass sample preparation, collection and mixing according to specified standards, laboratory testing to obtain pertinent data concerning geopolymer material attributes, identifying suitable input parameters, preprocessing the collected data, selecting appropriate artificial intelligence models, constructing, and training predictive models, assessing their performance, interpreting the outcomes, validating the models, refining them as necessary, and comprehensively documenting the entire process. This methodological approach is geared towards enhancing comprehension and utilization of sustainable construction materials by leveraging AI to forecast the properties of geopolymer concrete40,41.

Materials

Sugar cane bagasse ash (SCBA)

Sugar can bagasse waste are obtained as residue from sugar milling industry in Kakira Sugar Works in Jinja District, Eastern Uganda. The waste materials were burnt using the muffle furnace at 400–600 °C temperature to derive the ash samples and were sieved with standard sieve 75 μm to obtain finer particles with higher surface area to facilitate interaction and dispersion of the ash particles within the mix42.

Banana peel ash (BPA)

Banana peels were obtained from Ishaka, Bushenyi District, Uganda, dried, and burned using muffle furnace at temperature of 600–750 °C to obtain banana peel ash. The ash was then sieved using 75 μm to remove any large particles and ensure uniformity.

Cement

Portland cement was used in the experiment to prepare normal concrete which served as the control specimen for the comparison with the GPC samples and conforms to BS 12 (1978) specifications.

Alkaline solution

An alkaline activator solution was prepared by mixing sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) with Molarities of 4, 6, 8, and 10. The concentration of the activator solution and Na2SiO3 to NaOH ratio was varied from 1.5 to 3 to optimize geopolymeric reaction43.

Water

The water used in geopolymer concrete was clean, free from impurities, and suitable for drinking in accordance with BS EN 1008:2002. The amount of water used in concrete was determined by the desired workability and the water-geopolymer binder ratio specified in the mix design within the range of 0.25–0.3544.

Fine aggregates

The fine aggregate used was river sand which helps to fill the voids between coarse aggregates and help to enhance cohesion, and strength of the concrete mix. It was obtained from one of the construction sites on the campus of Kampala International University, Western Campus, Uganda and has maximum particle size of 3.18 mm. The fine aggregates were free from organic matter, clay, silt, and other contaminants in inconformity with the provisions of BS EN 12620:201345.

Coarse aggregates

The coarse aggregate used was gravel, which adds bulk to the concrete mix and enhances its strength and durability. This gravel was sourced from a construction site on the campus of Kampala International University, Western Campus, Uganda and has particle size ranging from 3.4 to 20 mm. The coarse aggregates were observed to be free from deleterious materials in inconformity with the provisions of BS EN 12620:201346.

Methods

The production of agro waste-based geopolymer concrete (GPC) involves appropriate collection and preparation of agro-industrial waste materials (BPA and SCBA). The blended waste ash samples were then mixed thoroughly with the activator solution, forming a homogeneous paste. This mixture is molded and left to cure under controlled temperatures for a duration of 24 to 48 h47,48. Adjustments in the proportions of materials and curing conditions are essential to attain specific properties, necessitating laboratory experimentation for optimal results. The hardened samples were then subjected to destructive testing to determine the mechanical properties of the geopolymer concrete produced. This process underscores the potential of utilizing agricultural waste in sustainable construction practices while highlighting the importance of precise material handling and experimentation for quality outcomes49,50.

Mix design

Utilizing the guidelines outlined in IS 10262:2009 and ACI 211:2009, the fundamental mix proportions are established. The maximum cement content provision prescribed in the IS 456 (2021) code was applied to derive the ensuing mix proportions. Mix design entailed determining the specific combination of ingredients (components) necessary to achieve desired properties in both fresh and hardened concrete as shown in Table 151. The alkaline activator for this experiment was NaOH pellets (99% purity), density, D = 2.1 g/cm3 and Na2SiO3 gel were sourced in Lagos, Nigeria. Na2SiO3 gel consists of Na2O, SiO2, and H2O with 9.4, 30.1, and 60.5% respectively52. The activated alkaline solution was prepared by (i) dissolving NaOH powder in water to obtain NaOH solutions with different molar mass (M), and (ii) mixing with Na2SiO3 solution in different concentrations. The dissolution and the mixing process are exothermic reactions (temperature ≈ 70 °C). In order to correctly activate the SCBA/BPA precursors, the solutions were prepared at least one day before mixing into GPC with SCBA-BPA blending ratio ranging from 0 to 100%53. The alkaline molarity was prepared by weighing required mass of the metallic alkaline (NaOH) by taken note of its molar mass as 40 g for 1 molar solution (M). The molar mass (40 g) will be multiplied by the number of required molar concentration (4 for 4 M, 6 for 6 M, 8 for 8 M and 10 for 10 M). Then, dissolve the measured molar mass in distilled water such that total volume of final solution is one liter (1000 cm3), and the design parameters are presented in Table 254. In the synthesis of geopolymer concrete, key factors such as the Alkali Activator Solution (AAS)/Precursor ratio (0.25–0.6), Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio (1.5–3), NaOH molarity (4–10 M), and curing time (5–20 h) significantly influence the material’s performance. These ranges, supported by literature, ensure a balance between workability, strength, and durability. The AAS/Precursor ratio affects fluidity and matrix density, while the Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio controls gel formation and mechanical strength. NaOH molarity accelerates geopolymerization, and curing time ensures full development of the polymeric structure. Optimizing these parameters leads to high-performance, sustainable geopolymer concrete.

Compressive strength test

The compressive strength test evaluates a material’s ability to withstand axial loads without failure, which is crucial for understanding its structural performance. This evaluation is essential for determining the quality and durability of geopolymer concrete in various construction applications. The geopolymer concrete mix is thoroughly combined with alkaline solutions and other materials, then poured into molds of 100 mm-by-100 mm-by-100 mm dimensions and compacted to eliminate air voids55. Once the concrete specimens are prepared and cured in a temperature-controlled environment at prescribed durations to obtain hardened samples. After curing, the hardened specimens are prepared for testing by ensuring their surfaces are smooth and level. The specimens are placed in a Universal Testing Machine (UTM), which applies compressive loads to the specimens at a constant rate until failure occurs. The compressive strength of the geopolymer concrete is calculated by dividing the maximum load sustained by the specimen at failure by its cross-sectional area56.

Flexural strength test

The flexural strength test is a standard procedure used to evaluate the strength of a material under bending or flexural loads. The test is carried out on the geopolymer concrete (GPC) to assess the material’s ability to resist bending stresses and failure. The geopolymer concrete mix is meticulously prepared by blending the mixture ingredients. Once thoroughly combined, the mixture is poured into beam molds with dimensions of 100 mm-by-100 mm-by-400 mm. To ensure the integrity and strength of the final product, the mixture in the molds is compacted to eliminate any air voids that may be present57. These specimens are cast and cured in a temperature-controlled environment at prescribed durations to obtain hardened samples. Once the specimens are prepared and cured, they are placed on a support span and subjected to a load at the center until failure occurs. The load is applied at a constant rate, typically using a Universal Testing Machine (UTM) equipped with a loading fixture designed for flexural testing58,59.

Slump test

Slump test provides a procedure used to assess the consistency and workability of fresh concrete after thorough mixing of the GPC mixture components. This test offers valuable information about the concrete’s ability to flow and deform under its own weight, which is crucial for ensuring proper placement and consolidation during construction60. During the test, a conical-shaped mold is filled with freshly mixed GPC in layers and compacted using standard methods. After filling, the mold is carefully lifted, allowing the concrete to spread and settle under its own weight. The amount of deformation, or slump, that occurs in the concrete is then measured and recorded in accordance with ASTM C143 specification. A higher slump value indicates a more fluid and workable mix, while a lower slump value suggests a stiffer and less workable mix61,62.

Chemical and morphological characterization

Chemical and morphological characterization encompasses the examination of the composition and physical structure of geopolymer concrete. This thorough analysis offers significant insights into the properties, behavior, and potential uses of the material. Through chemical analysis, the elemental composition and presence of specific compounds can be identified, shedding light on the material’s chemical properties and reactions63. Morphological analysis involves studying the physical structure, such as particle size, shape, and distribution, which influences the material’s mechanical properties and performance64.

Energy dispersive scanning electron microscopy (ED-SEM)

Energy dispersive scanning electron microscopy (ED-SEM) is an analytical technique used to characterize the elemental composition of materials at the micro- to nanoscale. In ED-SEM, an electron beam scans the sample surface, generating X-rays that are characteristic of the elements present65. The procedure involves sample preparation, instrument setup, data acquisition, elemental analysis, image acquisition, interpretation, and reporting. ED-SEM provides valuable insights into the composition and structure of materials, aiding in research and quality control across various fields66.

X-ray fluorescence (XRF)

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) is an analytical technique used to determine the elemental composition of materials by irradiating them with high-energy X-rays. The emitted fluorescent X-rays are measured to identify and quantify the elements present in the sample67. XRF analysis involves sample preparation, instrument setup, data acquisition, elemental analysis, interpretation, and reporting. It is a rapid, non-destructive method widely used in various industries for materials analysis and quality control68.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) on geopolymer concrete involves subjecting the material to controlled temperature conditions while measuring its mass loss as a function of temperature or time. This analysis provides valuable insights into the thermal stability, decomposition behavior, and composition of geopolymer concrete69. During TGA testing of geopolymer concrete, the sample is typically heated in a controlled atmosphere to prevent oxidation or other unwanted reactions. As the temperature increases, various thermal events may occur, leading to changes in the sample’s mass. These events can include dehydration, dehydroxylation, decomposition of organic additives, and the breakdown of geopolymer phases. By analyzing the TGA curves obtained from geopolymer concrete samples, identification of temperature ranges associated with different thermal processes can be achieved and characterize the kinetics of these processes70,71.

Slump and density results

The slump and density results are crucial indicators of geopolymer concrete’s workability and structural properties. The density of geopolymer concrete is crucial for its structural performance, load-bearing capacity, thermal properties, volume stability, and construction efficiency84. The experimental results shown in Figs. 5, 6 indicated an inverse relationship between the density of geopolymer concrete and the duration of heat curing, while the density increased with higher molarity of the alkaline activator solution. Mean density values of 2090 kg/m3, 2129 kg/m3, 2215 kg/m3, and 2192 kg/m3, were obtained for geopolymer concrete samples with 4, 6, 8 and 10 alkaline activator molarity respectively. Prolonged heat curing led to decreased density due to excessive moisture loss by evaporation. Conversely, higher molarity of the alkaline activator resulted in increased density due to more extensive geopolymerization and a denser microstructure85,86. The slump test results revealed an inverse relationship to both the concentration of the alkaline activator and the aggregate-binder ratio. Higher concentrations of the alkaline activator decrease the slump value by making the mix more viscous, thus reducing workability. Mean slump of 192 mm, 183 mm, 175 mm and 160 mm was obtained for alkaline molarity of 4, 6, 8 and 10 respectively. Similarly, increasing the aggregate-binder ratio also results in a lower slump because the higher aggregate content restricts the movement of the binder, leading to a stiffer and less workable mix87.

Compressive strength response

The compressive strength of geopolymer concrete is a critical parameter that determines its suitability for structural applications88. The experimental results highlight the critical factors influencing the compressive strength of geopolymer concrete, with a particular focus on the molarity of the alkaline activator, curing time, aggregate-binder ratio, and the sodium silicate-sodium hydroxide ratio as shown in Figs. 7, 8. The findings demonstrate that increasing the molarity from 4 to 10 M significantly enhances compressive strength, reaching a maximum of 33.17 MPa at 10 M with 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA, compared to a minimum of 21.27 MPa at 4 M with 95% SCBA and 5% BPA. This increase in strength can be attributed to the higher concentration of OH− ions in the alkaline solution at higher molarity levels, which promotes more efficient dissolution and reaction of the aluminosilicate source materials, leading to a stronger and denser geopolymer matrix89,90. Additionally, a curing time that allows for complete polymerization to form a stronger network within the geopolymer matrix which is crucial for achieving high compressive strength. The optimal aggregate-binder ratio of 2 ensures adequate binding within the matrix, while a SS/SH ratio of 2.5 provides the best balance for strong geopolymer formation. This aggregate-binder ratio ensures adequate binding of the aggregates within the geopolymer matrix, contributing to the overall strength. Also, Sodium silicate contributes to the formation of a more robust and cohesive geopolymer network, while sodium hydroxide aids in dissolving the aluminosilicate materials91.

Flexural strength response

The flexural strength of geopolymer concrete measures its ability to resist bending or flexural stress, which is essential for applications where tensile strength and crack resistance are critical92. The test involves preparing beam specimens, which are then subjected to a loading condition until failure, using a three-point bending test method. The experimental findings as shown in Figs. 9, 10 underscore the critical role of molarity, curing time, and specific ratio combinations in enhancing the compressive strength of geopolymer concrete. Higher molarity levels significantly boost the reactivity of the alkaline solution, facilitating the geopolymerization process and leading to a denser and more robust concrete matrix. Specifically, a molarity of 10 resulted in the highest compressive strength of 9.95 MPa, compared to a much lower strength of 4.12 MPa at a molarity of 4. This demonstrates that the concentration of the alkaline solution is directly proportional to the strength of the geopolymer concrete93,94. Additionally, extended curing times further enhance the mechanical properties by allowing more complete polymerization and stabilization of the geopolymer network. The aggregate-to-binder ratio and the sodium silicate-to-sodium hydroxide ratio are also pivotal in determining the strength of geopolymer concrete. An optimal aggregate-to-binder ratio of 2 and a sodium silicate-to-sodium hydroxide ratio of 2.5 were found to produce the highest strength response, ensuring a balanced and cohesive matrix. These ratios provide the right mix of reactive materials and structural components, which are essential for achieving maximum strength and durability by providing a dense and cohesive matrix95.

Morphological and mineralogical characterization of the geopolymer concrete

Morphological and mineralogical characterization of geopolymer concrete involves the detailed analysis of its physical structure and the identification of the minerals present in the material. This characterization is essential for understanding the material properties, performance, and durability of the geopolymer concrete126. Together, these techniques allow researchers and engineers to optimize the composition and processing conditions of geopolymer concrete to achieve desired properties and performance characteristics. Understanding the microstructure and mineralogy is essential for predicting the long-term behavior of the material, including its mechanical strength, durability, and resistance to environmental factors127.

SEM–EDX results

Scanning Electron Microscopy coupled with Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (ED-SEM) is a robust method for analyzing the microstructure and chemical composition of materials like concrete. This technique integrates SEM imaging with EDX analysis, offering detailed insights into the elemental composition and distribution within concrete samples. By combining these analyses, it is possible to correlate microstructural features with mechanical properties observed in tests such as compressive and flexural strength assessments128. This test is carried out on SCBA-BPA blended geopolymer concrete of selected optimal levels at 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA, 60% SCBA and 40% BPA both with 10 molarity and minimal levels at 95% SCBA and 5% BPA with 4 molarity in respect to the obtained strength and rheological responses as shown in Fig. 21a–c. The obtained ED-SEM analysis results reveal significant differences in their microstructures and elemental compositions, which directly influence their permeability and mechanical properties. The 52.5% SCBA + 47.5% BPA GPC sample with maximum strength response, characterized by a highly dense and compact microstructure with minimal visible pores, demonstrates a high degree of polymerization (Fig. 21a)129. EDS analysis shows a high concentration of silicon (54.04), calcium (23.48) and aluminum (10.01), suggesting a well-formed aluminosilicate network. This sample’s low porosity and high Si/Al ratio contribute to its low permeability and high mechanical strength attributed to the presence of abundance of calcium, making it suitable for applications requiring robust and durable materials. The 60% SCBA + 40% BPA GPC sample as shown in Fig. 21b exhibits a more heterogeneous structure with moderate porosity, indicating a balance between sufficient curing and some incomplete polymerization. SEM images reveal more visible pores distributed throughout the matrix, and EDS spectra show variations in the distribution of elements like calcium (61.15) and sodium (1.61) alongside Si (25.29) and Al (3.82). This sample has achieved a reasonable degree of geopolymerization but may have some weaknesses in terms of permeability and durability compared to the highly less permeable sample130. In contrast, the 95% SCBA + 5% BPA sample shows a highly porous structure with many visible pores and microcracks, indicating poor curing and high porosity as depicted in Fig. 21c. The SEM images reveal a less cohesive matrix, and the EDS spectra show a less uniform distribution of Si and Al, with higher concentrations of other elements. This sample’s high porosity and inconsistent elemental distribution highlight weaknesses in the geopolymer matrix formation, leading to lower mechanical strength, higher permeability, and reduced durability. These insights are crucial for optimizing the production and application of geopolymer concrete in various construction environments131.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) Results

TGA offers insights into the thermal stability, dehydration and decomposition behavior of the geopolymer concrete132. The test is carried out on the geopolymer concrete samples with 52.5% SCBA + 47.5% BPA, 60% SCBA + 40% BPA both with 10 molarity and 95% SCBA + 5% BPA with 4 molarity as shown in Fig. 22a–c. The TGA curve for 52.5% SCBA + 47.5% BPA GPC sample indicates a relatively stable weight profile with minimal weight loss until higher temperatures and reflecting its highly dense and less permeable nature. The initial weight loss up to 160 °C corresponds to the evaporation of physically absorbed water, which is minimal due to the low porosity. The second weight loss stage, occurring between 240 and 250 °C, can be attributed to the dehydration of chemically bound water within the geopolymer matrix. The final significant weight loss of about occurred at temperatures above 280 °C, reflecting high thermal stability with less pronounced weight loss in the higher temperature ranges, suggesting a robust structure with fewer decomposable phases133,134. Figure 22b shows more pronounced weight loss pattern in the TGA curve for 60% SCBA + 40% BPA GPC sample. The initial weight loss due to moisture evaporation was around 2–4% at lower temperatures of 160 °C reflecting more physically absorbed water due to the moderate pores. The thermogram indicated a gradual weight loss of about 6–8% starting at lower temperatures of 210–220 °C, which reflects a lower degree of geopolymerization and a less dense microstructure compared to 52.5% SCBA + 47.5% BPA GPC sample. The final weight loss at temperatures above 240 °C was approximately 10%, suggesting a moderate thermal stability. This balance between thermal resistance and weight loss makes it suitable for applications that require moderate performance under thermal stress135. Geopolymer concrete sample of 95% SCBA + 5% BPA exhibited significant weight loss during the TGA as shown in Fig. 22c. The initial weight loss due to physically bound water was around 5–7% at 100 °C reflecting a high amount of physically absorbed water due to the large number of pores. A substantial weight loss of about 8–10% occurred at temperatures as low as 130–150 °C, indicating a poorly developed geopolymer matrix with weaker chemical bonds. The total weight loss at temperatures above 200 °C was over 15%, reflecting poor thermal resistance and significant decomposition. These numerical results highlight the varying degrees of thermal stability and decomposition behavior, essential for tailoring geopolymer concrete formulations to specific environmental and performance requirements136.

Conclusion

The study successfully utilized advanced Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques such as Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS), and Gene Expression Programming (GEP) to predict the mechanical properties of geopolymer concrete blended with Banana Peel-Ash (BPA) and Sugarcane Bagasse Ash (SCBA). These AI models have provided valuable insights into optimizing the geopolymer mix design, leading to enhanced performance characteristics and sustainable concrete production.

The geopolymer mix design incorporated varying proportions of BPA and SCBA, with optimal mix ratios determined to achieve desired mechanical properties. Concrete slump tests indicated satisfactory workability, essential for practical construction applications. The results from compressive strength test reveals that increasing the molarity from 4 to 10 M raises the compressive strength from 21.27 MPa (at 4 M, 95% SCBA and 5% BPA) to a maximum of 33.17 MPa (at 10 M, 52.5% SCBA and 47.5% BPA). The higher molarity enhances the dissolution of aluminosilicates, creating a denser and stronger matrix. Similarly, the flexural strength test results indicated the highest value of 9.95 MPa achieved at 10 M (77.55% SCBA and 22.5% BPA), compared to 4.12 MPa at 4 M (95% SCBA and 5% BPA). An optimal aggregate-binder ratio of 2 and an SS/SH ratio of 2.5 were found to result in the best geopolymer formation, ensuring strong binding of aggregates and achieving maximum compressive and flexural strength.

SEM–EDS analysis provided insights into the microstructural characteristics of the concrete samples, revealing variations in porosity and elemental composition that correlated with the mechanical performance. Samples with fewer pores exhibited higher strength, confirming the critical role of microstructure in determining the material’s mechanical properties. TGA analysis further validated the thermal stability and phase composition of the geopolymer concrete, indicating key temperatures where significant weight changes occurred due to dehydration and decomposition.

Performance analysis of the AI models through loss function parameters (RMSE, MAE, MSE, and R2) showed average MSE was 1.338, RMSE was 1.157, MAE was 3.104, and R2 was 0.989 for the ANN model. For the ANFIS model, the values were 0.345 for MSE, 0.587 for RMSE, 1.409 for MAE, and 0.998 for R2. The GEP model’s performance measures were 1.233 for MSE, 1.110 for RMSE, 1.828 for MAE, and 0.992 for R2. The statistical analysis concluded that ANFIS is the best model for predicting the mechanical and rheological properties of geopolymer concrete.

In conclusion, the integration of AI techniques with experimental data has proven to be a powerful approach for predicting and optimizing the mechanical properties of BPA and SCBA blended geopolymer concrete. This innovative methodology can significantly contribute to the development of sustainable and high-performance construction materials.
