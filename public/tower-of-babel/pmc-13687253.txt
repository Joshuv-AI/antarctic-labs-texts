A novel pre–abdominal closure checklist in colorectal surgery serves as a critical defensive barrier against human errors
Authors: Akira Marui, Hiromichi Maeda, Sachi Yamaguchi, Yasuhiro Kawanishi, Masahiro Maeda, Nobuhisa Tanioka, Kazune Fujisawa, Hiroyuki Kitagawa, Tsutomu Namikawa, Ichiro Miyano, Motohiko Kume, Takashi Kawano, Satoru Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of a checklist during abdominal closure may reduce human error and improve surgical safety. To address this benefit, we evaluated the effectiveness of an institutional checklist by determining whether implementation led to additional procedures.MethodsA 12‑item pre‑closure checklist for colorectal surgery was developed and incorporated into practice in late May 2024. We retrospectively reviewed checklist findings documented in electronic medical records. Surgical outcomes were compared between 42 cases with and 409 cases without checklist use during 2020–2024.ResultsOf 45 patients, checklist information was unavailable for three. Among 42 remaining patients, additional intraoperative procedures were required in four cases following checklist use. Procedures included injury repair or hemostasis (n = 1), application of antiadhesion agents (n = 2), and repositioning of intestine or omentum to the original position (n = 1). No incidents involving retained gauze, residual tissue, or deviations from planned procedures were identified. Among four cases requiring additional interventions, two involved multiple ancillary procedures and two required extended operative duration. No significant differences were observed between groups in anastomotic leakage, complication rate, or length of postoperative hospitalization.ConclusionsThe pre-closure checklist facilitated the identification of intraoperative errors that otherwise might have been overlooked. Additional studies with a larger patient population, broader range of surgical procedures, and multiple institutions are needed to further clarify its utility.Keywords: Checklists, colorectal neoplasms, human error, patient safety, slip error

Introduction

In Japan, over 130,000 colorectal resections are performed annually [1]. The perioperative mortality rate is approximately 0.5% after low anterior resection and approximately 1% after right colectomy [2–4]. These favorable outcomes likely reflect the diligence of medical professionals and the robustness of the Japanese healthcare system. However, postoperative complications and mortality arise not only from unavoidable intrinsic factors but also from human errors. A landmark study from Australia reported that nearly half of the adverse events associated with operative procedures were preventable [5], and recent analyses from U.S. medical centers similarly attributed approximately 60% of surgical complications to human performance deficiencies [6]. Collectively, preventing human error is essential for reducing surgical complications and mortality and improving patient safety.

To reduce avoidable perioperative complications and deaths, the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines advocate the use of surgical protocols. The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist specifies that verification steps should be performed before anesthesia induction (Sign In), immediately before the skin incision (Time Out), and before the patient leaves the operating room (Sign Out) [7]. The WHO checklist has exerted its expected efficacy [8], plausibly by reducing reliance on memory, standardizing procedural steps and required actions, enhancing team communication, and mitigating errors [9]. However, because the WHO checklist intentionally focuses on essential items to ensure its applicability across a wide range of surgical specialties, there remains scope for additional refinement to address procedure‑specific requirements [10].

In abdominal surgery, the immediate systematic inspection of the entire operative field before abdominal wall closure may be beneficial, because access to the abdominal cavity would require re-laparotomy once the closure is complete. The timing of this inspection differs from that of the WHO “Sign-Out,” which is typically performed during skin closure or before the patient leaves the operating room [9]. The JONA Perioperative Safety Checklist 2024, published in December 2024 by the Japan Perioperative Nursing Association [11], appears to address this need. This checklist comprises five phases (0–4), with Phase 3 corresponding to the intraoperative period near the end of surgery, immediately before the patient leaves the operating room. The guidelines recommend confirming the placement of the drainage tubes and verifying the surgical procedure performed during Phase 3 before wound closure. Shortly before the publication of this guideline, we independently developed and introduced a pre-closure checklist specifically designed for use in colorectal surgery. Compared with the JONA checklist, our version includes more items tailored to the specific requirements of colorectal resections.

Herein, we aimed to identify the events detected through this checklist during the early phase of its introduction and evaluate whether the pre-closure checklist functions as a defensive barrier in Reason’s Swiss cheese model [12–14]. In addition, we investigated whether its implementation was associated with short-term surgical outcomes.

Methods

Endpoints and hypotheses, patients, and ethical considerations

The primary endpoint of this study was to retrospectively characterize the events documented using a pre-closure checklist. If the items in the checklist had been performed consistently before the pre-closure check, their incremental contribution to intraoperative safety would have been negligible. Conversely, if the checklist prompted the execution of additional intraoperative steps, it acted as a protective barrier against adverse events. The secondary objective was to compare the short-term surgical outcomes between procedures performed with and without the checklist.

Although the checklist was implemented in colorectal surgery, the study specifically focused on colorectal cancer surgery to ensure a homogeneous study population. This study excluded appendectomies, cecal resections, and procedures performed for non-neoplastic conditions such as perforations, diverticulitis, and fistulas. Cases initially suspected to involve malignant neoplasms that were ultimately determined to be benign following resection and lymph node dissection were included. The study period corresponded to the early phase of checklist implementation, during which both checklist-using and non-use cases were observed. The checklist was exclusively applied to surgeries substantially involving a particular surgeon. To compare short-term surgical outcomes, patients who met the same inclusion criteria and underwent surgery between January 2020 and December 2024 were included in the analysis.

This study was conducted retrospectively, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived, with an opt-out option provided. Ethical approval (approval number: 2024 − 132, Date: 09 April 2025) was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kochi Medical School, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Checklist

The checklist was developed through an email-based discussion among five surgeons who were primarily responsible for colorectal cancer surgery. During these exchanges, essential items were identified to ensure that the checklist could be completed within 1–2 min. The development process adhered to three guiding principles: (1) the checklist should focus on content relevant to colorectal surgery; (2) it should include items whose implementation substantially impacts the occurrence of complications and the success of postoperative management; and (3) it should contain the minimum number of items necessary to avoid imposing an excessive burden on the surgical workflow.

The checklist commences with items pertaining to gauze counts to facilitate coordination with the nursing staff and terminates with an item addressing the necessity of infiltration anesthesia to strengthen communication with the anesthesiologist. The checklist was drafted in Japanese and structured as a series of questions intended to be answered primarily by the surgeons. It was clarified that certain items, such as “Did you insert a drainage tube?” do not presuppose that the corresponding actions are obligatory. Although the English translation is presented in Table 1, the Japanese version of the checklist did not contain subheadings and consisted solely of text enclosed in quotation marks. Pre-closure checks were performed immediately before fascial closure in open abdominal surgery and before surgical port removal in laparoscopic or robotic surgery. Surgeons had to document the use of the checklist in electronic medical records immediately after the completion of the surgery.

Use of generative artificial intelligence

In this study, Google Translate supported the English translations. To enhance linguistic clarity and improve the sentence structure, the text was reviewed using Microsoft Copilot, a GPT‑5–based model (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). At each stage, the authors carefully evaluated the AI-generated suggestions and ensured that they were consistent with the intended meaning. Final proofreading was performed by a commercial English-language editing service.

Statistical considerations

The primary endpoint of this study was an exploratory, retrospective assessment of additional procedures prompted by pre-closure checks. Because this investigation had a unique study design, statistical hypotheses for the primary endpoints could not be established.

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records to compare the short-term outcomes between patients with and without the use of the checklist. The collected variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), disease site, surgical procedure, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, operative time, blood loss (with entries recorded as “small” assigned a value of 1 mL), postoperative complications, and length of postoperative hospital stay. Comparisons between the two groups were conducted using the chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Analysis of checklist events

During the study period, the checklist was intended for use in 45 patients and was correctly implemented and recorded in 42 (93.3%) (Table 2). For the remaining three patients, it was impossible to determine whether a pre-closure check had been performed. The mean operative time for these three cases was 254.3 min, intraoperative blood loss was 1 mL (classified as a small amount), and the mean postoperative hospital stay was 10 days. No complications of Clavien–Dindo grade 3 or higher were observed in these patients, indicating that the procedures in these three patients were clinically unremarkable compared with those performed in the other 42 patients.

None of the patients required additional intraoperative procedures related to gauze counts, retained surgical items, operative procedures, specimen handling, or drainage tube (items 1–4 and 7). However, re-evaluation of intraperitoneal nodules (item 5) was performed in one case following the pre-closure check. Regarding anti-adhesion agents (item 8), the checklist process identified additional sites for anti-adhesion agent application in two patients.

Intraoperative notes (item 9), a customary practice in which circulating or scrub nurses document intraoperative concerns on a whiteboard or drape, were used in eight surgical cases, all of which were resolved by the time the pre-closure check was initiated. Regarding hemostasis and repair confirmation (item 10), one patient required additional repair or hemostasis upon review of the surgical procedure using the checklist. In two cases, reevaluation of the hemostasis site was recommended, with no additional interventions deemed necessary. In one patient, an additional intraoperative maneuver was required to correct the position of the intestine and omentum, which is considered important for preventing postoperative intestinal obstruction (item 11).

The four events in which additional procedures were performed corresponded to items 8, 8, 10, and 11, two of which involved combinations of multiple surgical procedures. The other two cases involved rectal resection in patients with obesity and prolonged surgery. Conversely, three cases with re-evaluation involved standard colectomy without adhesions, obesity, or other complicating factors.

Short-term operative results

Three cases in which checklist use was not documented were excluded from the statistical analysis. There were no significant differences in age, BMI, proportion of patients with ASA class 3 or higher, or tumor location between the 409 cases treated before checklist implementation and the 42 cases treated with the checklist (Table 3). Although the difference was not statistically significant, the proportion of patients who underwent stoma creation without anastomosis increased after the introduction of the checklist. Nearly 10% of the surgeries in both groups involved simultaneous or combined resections, including cystectomy, para-aortic lymph node dissection, lymph node sampling, and inguinal lymph node sampling procedures. The distribution of surgical approaches differed between the two groups, with robotic surgery being more common in the checklist group.

Discussion

In this study, we retrospectively reviewed medical records to identify potential intraoperative incidents and evaluate the effectiveness of the checklist. Among the 42 surgeries reviewed, four required additional intraoperative intervention. In three additional cases, re-inspection of the surgical field was performed, although no further intervention was required. A comparison of short-term surgical outcomes showed no significantly favorable effects on the incidence of anastomotic leakage, severe postoperative complications, or length of hospital stay. Nevertheless, we claim that the checklist may function as an additional barrier to patient safety within the Swiss cheese model [12–14] of patient safety (Fig. 1).

Each surgeon functions as an essential barrier to ensure safety during surgery; however, mistakes and errors may still occur owing to the inherent complexity or prolonged duration of the procedures, as well as individual circumstances. The checklist implemented before abdominal closure provides an opportunity for surgeons to consciously reevaluate the procedural steps and is expected to serve as an additional safety barrier within the framework of surgical safety. A review of the medical records of seven cases in which additional procedures or re-evaluation were performed following the completion of the checklist yielded three important observations. First, two of the seven cases involved complex surgical procedures. Operations with high complexity, particularly those requiring multiple specialties, are inherently susceptible to changes in the operating personnel, resulting in interruptions in procedural flow. Furthermore, prolonged surgical duration increases the likelihood of surgeon fatigue, which may diminish cognitive performance and attention. These associations have been documented in the literature [15]. Only three of the 45 patients in this series required multiple major procedures, underscoring the frequency with which such events occurred. In contrast, reevaluation of the abdominal cavity was performed in three surgical cases classified as moderately difficult, a category regarded as encompassing procedures of relatively limited technical complexity by the Japanese Society of Gastroenterological Surgery (specifically, colectomy for malignant disease) [16], despite the absence of recognized risk factors such as obesity, coagulopathy, or a history of prior abdominal operations associated with severe adhesions. Although Suliburk et al. reported a case in which a telephone call precipitated a cascade of intraoperative errors [6], no comparable triggering factors could be identified in the present cases. Third, this review revealed that surgical teams often asked nurses to record intraoperative findings, events, and concerns to prevent them from being overlooked or forgotten by the surgical team. Nevertheless, none of the four events that required additional procedures were recorded in the nursing notes. Collectively, these observations indicate that the potential for unrecognized omissions persists, even within stable workflows. Accordingly, we claim that the use of a pre-closure checklist should be implemented universally rather than selectively.

This study did not identify any errors related to retained gauzes, instruments, unplanned procedural modifications, or specimens. Given that the incidence of retained intraoperative materials has been reported to be approximately 1 in 2,000 cases [17], the absence of such events in the relatively small number of surgeries examined in this study was expected. Nevertheless, all surgical team members must acknowledge the importance of these items and appropriately integrate them into checklist protocols. Once such medical errors occur, the physical burden associated with corrective procedures is substantial, and the psychological consequences for both patients and their families are profound. In addition, these incidents may lead to considerable psychological distress among medical staff [18, 19] expose individuals and institutions to media scrutiny [20, 21] and potential litigation [22], resulting in short- and long-term declines in professional performance and, in some cases, life-altering repercussions, as described by Ofri [23]. In the original version of our checklist, surgeons had to confirm the absence of gauze in the surgical field, at the surgeon’s feet, or in the drape bag. Following a recommendation from the nursing staff, this procedure was replaced with a formal gauze count, implemented as the first item on the checklist, during which the surgeon ‘must’ briefly pause the operative activity.

This study has some limitations. First, although we discussed the potential effectiveness of the checklist and the contextual factors associated with errors, the small sample size may have limited the observational power as well as the statistical power to detect significant differences in short-term outcomes. Second, the checklist was adopted voluntarily by a team with a strong interest in patient safety. Successful checklist implementation requires healthcare professionals to understand and adhere to its intended purpose; superficial or perfunctory use may not produce comparable outcomes. Conversely, in teams that are reluctant to adopt novel approaches, successful implementation and long-term sustainability may be challenging, which could limit the effectiveness of the checklist. From this perspective, future studies should investigate the sustainability of checklist use and adherence rates over time, as well as the perceptions and attitudes of multidisciplinary healthcare professionals toward the checklist.

Third, this study only focused on colorectal surgery; therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing its findings to other types of surgery. Because the checklist was newly developed, we adopted a “start small” approach [9, 24], initially implementing it in colorectal cancer surgeries performed by a specific group of surgeons. While this implementation strategy appears to have been successful, it also highlights an important limitation regarding the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, the people who implemented the checklist also comprised the research team which may have caused additional bias. Finally, although checklist results were intended to be recorded by the surgeon immediately after surgery, data were missing for three cases. While recording was generally prompt, typically on the same day as surgery, the process relied on recall. No complications were observed in these cases, and we believe the missing data are unlikely to have influenced the analysis of surgical outcomes; nevertheless, the possibility of inaccurate documentation cannot be excluded. At our institution, a modified version of the checklist has since been implemented across a broader range of diseases and surgical specialties. As prospective data are currently being collected, further evaluation is expected to provide more reliable evidence regarding its effectiveness.

We outline future directions at the end of the discussion. Although our proposed checklist appears effective in identifying slip errors, it does not allow real-time monitoring of intraoperative decision-making or procedural execution. Several surgical complications can arise because of judgment-related errors [25]. Research on the cognitive functions required for surgery [26], training to cope with interruptions [27] during surgery, cognitive support for dissection [28], and modifications to work schedules to control fatigue and cognitive load during surgery are important for improving medical safety. However, these approaches are not entirely independent. Enhancing cognitive function may increase the accuracy of the pre-closure checklist, whereas excessive fatigue may cause less attention or inconsistent checking. To achieve the goal of patient safety, we consider it necessary to evaluate the effectiveness of each approach, both individually and as a surgical safety bundle.

Conclusion

The pre-closure checklist for colorectal resection provides an opportunity for surgeons to reconfirm the details of the procedure and prompt them to take additional action. These findings suggest that a pre-closure checklist may serve as a new defensive barrier to improve surgical safety.
