Optimization of Patient Skin Dose by C-Arm Angulation in Interventional Radiology for Spinal Vascular Lesions
Authors: Tomohiro Tada, Masahiro Nishihori, Naruto Sugimoto, Takashi Mukoyama, Takashi Sakai, Takeshi Uemura, Shuji Koyama, Yoshinori Tsutsumi, Takashi Izumi, Shunsaku Goto, Shinsuke Muraoka, Issei Takeuchi, Ryuta Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAccurate assessment of a patient’s skin dose during interventional radiology (IVR) for spinal vascular lesions is challenging, particularly when the upper arms are positioned within the irradiation field. This study aimed to quantify the discrepancy between system-displayed dose values and actual skin dose, while assessing the impact of C-arm angulation on patient skin dose during spinal IVR.MethodsDosimetric measurements were performed using an adult anthropomorphic phantom. A 0.6-cc CT ionization chamber was positioned on the phantom surface at the center of the lateral C-arm irradiation field. Fluoroscopic irradiation was performed for 1 min at right anterior oblique (RAO) angulations ranging from 40° to 120° in 10° increments using a biplane angiography system. The measured skin dose was compared against the system-displayed reference air kerma at the patient reference point. Measurements were performed in triplicate and evaluated across 3 anatomical regions: the upper thoracic region (including the upper arms), lower thoracic region (excluding the upper arms), and lumbar region. Air kerma was used as a surrogate for skin dose.ResultsThe measured skin dose exceeded the system-displayed dose in all anatomical regions, with the most significant discrepancy observed in the upper thoracic region. At RAO 90°, which corresponds to a true lateral projection, the measured skin dose in the upper thoracic region was 1.68 times higher than the system-displayed dose. Conversely, smaller discrepancies were observed in the lower thoracic and lumbar regions, and the measured doses were 1.17 and 1.18 times higher than the displayed values, respectively. Modifying the C-arm angulation by 20° from RAO 90° significantly reduced the measured skin dose in the upper thoracic region, with reductions of 77.9% and 85.4% at RAO 70° and 110°, respectively. However, even with these optimizations, the measured skin dose remained 1.42 times higher than the system-displayed dose.ConclusionDuring spinal IVR, particularly in the lateral projections of the upper thoracic spine, system-displayed dose may substantially underestimate actual patient skin dose when the upper arms are included within the irradiation field. This discrepancy likely reflects increased X-ray tube output driven by automatic dose control in response to elevated tissue attenuation. Optimizing the C-arm angulation represents a practical and effective strategy for reducing patient skin dose in this anatomically challenging region.

Introduction

Technological advances in interventional radiology (IVR) have facilitated the performance of increasingly complex procedures with minimal invasiveness. However, these complex interventions often necessitate prolonged fluoroscopy times and a higher volume of radiographic acquisitions, raising considerable concerns regarding radiation-induced skin injuries. Radiation-related deterministic effects, including erythema, epilation, and chronic radiodermatitis, have been well documented following fluoroscopically guided interventions.1–5) Endovascular treatment for spinal vascular lesions, particularly spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs), involves substantial patient radiation exposure.6,7) In these procedures, lateral projections may be used to assess the spatial relationship between feeding arteries and draining veins, to confirm the position of the microcatheter or guidewire, and to reduce vessel overlap for accurate anatomical orientation. In selected cases, prolonged irradiation from the lateral C-arm may be required. The upper thoracic region poses a unique challenge, as exclusion of the shoulders and upper arms from the irradiation field is frequently difficult because of the patient’s anatomy and procedural constraints. As a result, angiography system displays often report high radiation dose values during these procedures.

Therefore, accurate assessment of the patient’s skin dose during IVR is essential for patient safety. In current clinical practice, the patient skin dose is commonly estimated using indirect dosimetry based on the system output parameters. Modern angiography systems display reference air kerma (RAK) measured at the patient reference point (PRP), a value standardized by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC 60601-2-43).8) The PRP is defined, in isocentric fluoroscopic systems, as a point located along the central X-ray beam at a distance of 15 cm from the isocenter toward the X-ray focal spot. This point is intended to approximate the location of the patient’s skin surface. The displayed RAK is therefore intended to approximate the entrance skin dose at the patient surface.8,9) However, this approach is based on the assumption that the patient’s skin surface is located near the PRP. When the irradiated body surface is positioned closer to the X-ray tube than the PRP, the displayed dose may substantially underestimate the actual skin dose.10) In spinal IVR, the upper arms are often positioned closer to the X-ray focal spot than the PRP, particularly during lateral projections of the upper thoracic spine. Under such conditions, the actual skin dose to the shoulders and upper arms may exceed the values displayed by the system. Nevertheless, quantitative data that directly compare system-displayed dose values with actual skin doses to the upper arms during spinal IVR are limited. Furthermore, practical strategies for mitigating excessive skin doses under these anatomical conditions remain unclear.

The aims of this study were twofold: to quantify the discrepancy between the measured skin dose on an anthropomorphic phantom and system-displayed RAK during spinal IVR, and to evaluate the efficacy of C-arm angulation optimization in reducing patient skin dose.

Materials and Methods

An adult anthropomorphic phantom (THRA1; Kyoto Kagaku, Kyoto, Japan), representing the torso of a standard Japanese adult male, was used in this study (Fig. 1). The phantom consisted of tissue-equivalent materials simulating soft tissue, lung, and bone, specifically Tough Water (WE-211), Tough Lung (LP-430), and Tough Bone (BE-303), respectively. Within the X-ray energy range of 20–60 keV commonly used in diagnostic imaging, the attenuation properties of these materials differ by only a few percent from those of human tissues.11,12) Accordingly, the phantom was considered suitable for evaluating X-ray absorption and scattering characteristics representative of the human body. All experiments were conducted using an under-table Artis Q biplane angiography system (Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany).

Dosimetric measurement

The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2. A 0.6-cc CT ionization chamber (Model 10X6-0.6CT; Radcal, Monrovia, CA, USA) connected to an electrometer (Accu Gold+; Radcal) was attached to the surface of the adult anthropomorphic phantom. For each evaluated angle, the chamber was positioned at the center of the irradiation field of the lateral C-arm. The lateral C-arm was rotated in 10° increments over a range of 40°–120° from the right anterior oblique (RAO). At each angulation, fluoroscopic irradiation was performed for 1 min using the standard protocol employed at our institution for the endovascular embolization of SDAVFs. Fluoroscopy was performed in normal dose mode with a pulse rate of 7.5 pulses/s, and a frame rate of 2 frames/s. The irradiation field was set to 48 cm, and the source-to-image distance/table height was kept constant across measurements. In the fluoroscopic protocol employed in this study, automatic dose control (ADC), also referred to as automatic brightness control (ABC) in some systems, was activated to maintain a predefined detector dose level at the flat panel. The system automatically adjusted exposure parameters—including tube voltage, tube current, pulse width, and copper (Cu) filtration—to achieve the preset dose per pulse under varying attenuation conditions. The specific ADC mode corresponded to the standard fluoroscopic protocol routinely used for spinal endovascular procedures at our institution.

Dose measurements were obtained at 2 locations: (1) the system-displayed dose, defined as the RAK displayed by the angiography system at the PRP, and (2) the measured skin dose, defined as the dose measured directly by the ionization chamber attached to the phantom surface at the center of the irradiation field. The measured dose was expressed as air kerma for direct comparison with the system-displayed dose. In this study, air kerma was used as a surrogate for skin dose.

The measurements were repeated 3 times at each angulation, and the mean values were calculated. Dose measurements were performed and compared across 3 anatomical regions: the upper thoracic region (including the upper arms), lower thoracic region (excluding the upper arms), and lumbar region.

At RAO 90° (true lateral projection), the measured skin dose in the upper thoracic region was 1.68 times higher than the system-displayed dose, which represents the largest discrepancy observed in this study. In contrast, the discrepancies in the regions where the upper arms were not included in the irradiation field were smaller, although underestimation by the system-displayed dose was evident. In the lower thoracic region, the measured skin dose was 1.17 times higher than the displayed value. Similarly, in the lumbar region, the measured skin dose was 1.18 times higher than the system-displayed dose.

The adjustment of the C-arm angulation resulted in a marked reduction in the measured skin dose in the upper thoracic region. Compared with RAO 90°, changing the angulation by 20° reduced the measured skin dose by 77.9% at RAO 70° and 85.4% at RAO 110°. At both RAO 70° and RAO 110°, the measured skin dose was 1.42 times higher than the system-displayed dose.

Discussion

The pronounced discrepancy observed in the upper thoracic region at RAO 90° angulation, where the measured skin dose was 1.68-fold higher than the system-displayed dose, likely reflects the combined influence of increased effective tissue thickness due to inclusion of the upper arms within the irradiation field and geometric factors related to source-to-skin distance.

When the upper arms are included in the irradiation field, the effective attenuation increases substantially because of the thickness of osseous and soft tissues in the shoulder girdle. Under ADC/ABC, the angiography system compensates for this increased attenuation by elevating X-ray tube output, resulting in a higher entrance skin dose. In addition to this system-controlled effect, the positioning of the upper arms may place the skin surface closer to the X-ray focal spot than the standardized PRP, further contributing to the discrepancy between measured and system-displayed dose. Although the present study was not designed to quantitatively isolate the individual contributions of these 2 mechanisms, the observation that discrepancies were substantially smaller in the lower thoracic and lumbar regions—where the upper arms were absent from the irradiation field—provides circumstantial evidence that the ABC-driven increase in tube output may represent the predominant contributor to the pronounced discrepancy observed in the upper thoracic region. These findings have considerable clinical implications. When the shoulders or upper arms are included within the irradiation field during procedures targeting the upper thoracic spine, reliance on the system-displayed dose may result in a substantial underestimation of the actual patient skin dose.

The influence of X-ray tube angulation on radiation exposure is well documented13–16); however, existing literature has focused almost exclusively on diagnostic and therapeutic procedures in the cardiac field, such as coronary angiography and percutaneous coronary intervention. In contrast, studies addressing the spinal region are extremely limited. Additionally, imaging conditions, patient positioning, and irradiation fields differ considerably between cardiac and spinal interventions. In cardiac procedures, X-ray tube angulations typically range from the left anterior oblique 60° to RAO 30°, and relatively small flat panel detectors are commonly used, resulting in a limited irradiation field. Consequently, the shoulders and upper arms are rarely included, and skin doses to these regions are seldom a clinical concern. Given this significant gap in the current literature, this study focused on the skin doses to the shoulders and upper arms during spinal IVR, adding a novel contribution to the literature.

In the present study, a deviation of the C-arm angulation by 20° from the true lateral projection at RAO 90° resulted in a marked reduction in the measured skin dose in the upper thoracic region, with reductions of 77.9% and 85.4% at RAO 70° and 110°, respectively. This reduction is likely attributable to the decreased overlap between the irradiation field and the shoulder region and proximal upper arms compared with the RAO 90° position, reflecting exclusion of the thick osseous and soft tissues of the shoulder girdle from the irradiation field.

Accordingly, in clinical scenarios where the shoulders and proximal upper arms cannot be excluded from the irradiation field, modification of the C-arm angulation represents a practical and effective strategy for reducing the patient’s skin dose. However, the effect of a 20° deviation from the true lateral projection on image quality was not quantitatively evaluated, and its clinical feasibility when a strict lateral projection is required remains uncertain. In spinal angiography, both frontal and lateral projections may be necessary to achieve precise anatomical orientation. Selection of C-arm angulation should therefore be individualized, balancing radiation dose reduction with procedural requirements while preserving adequate anatomical visualization for safe and effective embolization. Even after angulation adjustment, however, the measured skin dose remained higher than the system-displayed dose; at both RAO 70° and RAO 110°, the measured dose was 1.42 times the system-displayed dose, underscoring the need for caution when relying solely on system-displayed metrics. Importantly, the observed dose reduction cannot be attributed solely to geometric distance effects based on the inverse-square law. Altered C-arm angulation changes not only the source-to-skin distance but also the effective tissue thickness within the irradiation field, thereby modifying X-ray tube output through the ADC/ABC system. The measured skin dose thus reflects the combined influence of geometric and system-controlled factors.

Alternative dose-reduction techniques, such as elevating the upper arms or positioning them beneath the patient table, have also been proposed.17–21) In whole-body trauma CT, radiolucent spacers are sometimes placed between the backboard and the CT table to allow the upper arms to hang below the level of the torso, thereby reducing overlap with the irradiation field. Application of similar positioning strategies during IVR procedures may help reduce arm and shoulder overlap, even in patients unable to maintain arm elevation for extended periods, and may further decrease the patient’s skin dose. However, additional studies are required to quantify the dose reduction effects of these approaches.

This study has several limitations. First, the anthropomorphic phantom included only the proximal upper extremities up to the mid-humerus level; in this manuscript, the term “upper arms” refers to this anatomical extent. As a result, the upper arms were not included in the experimental setup for the lower thoracic and lumbar regions. To comprehensively evaluate the dosimetric impact of including or excluding the upper arms, further studies using phantoms with complete upper arms, including the forearms and hands, are warranted to more accurately replicate clinical positioning across these regions. Second, skin dose measurements were performed with the dosimeter directly attached to the surface of the phantom, and thus the measured values included the effect of backscattered radiation. However, in the lower thoracic and lumbar regions, where the upper arms were not included within the irradiation field, the discrepancy between the measured skin dose and the system-displayed dose was relatively small. Therefore, this methodological limitation was not considered to have a substantial impact on the comparative evaluation or interpretation of the results in these regions. Third, all measurements were performed using a single biplane angiography system from 1 manufacturer. Dose display characteristics and the behavior of ADC or ABC may vary depending on the manufacturer, detector technology, and system configuration. Therefore, further investigations using different angiography platforms are required to confirm the generalizability of the present findings.

Conclusion

At RAO 90° C-arm orientation, the actual patient skin dose in the upper thoracic region could be up to 1.68 times higher than that displayed by the angiography system. This study demonstrates that modification of the C-arm angulation by 20° or more represents an effective strategy for substantially reducing patient skin dose during interventional procedures targeting this anatomically challenging region.
