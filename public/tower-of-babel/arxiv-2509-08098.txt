Geometrical optics in phase space
I. Y. Dodin \aff 1,2 \corresp, N. A. Lopez \aff 3, Tingjing Xing \aff 2,4, Rune Højlund Marholt \aff 5, Valerian H. Hall-Chen \aff 4,6

Abstract
Geometrical optics (GO) is widely used for reduced modeling of waves in plasmas but fails near reflection points, where it predicts a spurious singularity of the wave amplitude. We show how to avoid this singularity by adopting a different representation of the wave equation. Instead of the physical space x x and the wavevector k k , we use the ray time τ \tau as the new canonical coordinate and the ray energy h h as the associated canonical momentum. To derive the envelope equation in the τ \tau -representation, we construct the Weyl symbol calculus on the ( τ , h ) (\tau,h) space and show that the corresponding Weyl symbols are related to their ( x , k ) (x,k) counterparts by the Airy transform. This allows us to express the coefficients in the envelope equation through the known properties of the original dispersion operator. When necessary, solutions of this equation can be mapped to the x x -space using a generalised metaplectic transform. But the field per se might not even be needed in practice. Instead, knowing the corresponding Wigner function usually suffices for linear and quasilinear calculations. As a Weyl symbol itself, the Wigner function can be mapped analytically, using the aforementioned Airy transform. We show that the standard Airy patterns that form in regions where conventional GO fails are successfully reproduced within MGO simply by remapping the field from the τ \tau -space to the x x -space. An extension to mode-converting waves is also presented. This formulation, which we call generalised metaplectic GO (MGO) offers a promising tool, for example, for reduced modeling of the O–X conversion in inhomogeneous plasma near the critical density, an effect that is important for fusion applications and also occurs in the ionosphere. Aside from better handling reflection, MGO is similar to GO and can replace it for any practical purposes.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

The geometrical-optics (GO) approach is widely used for reduced modeling of waves in plasmas and other media
(
Kravtsov & Orlov 1990
;
Tracy et al. 2014
)
. In this approach, instead of solving the wave equations per se, one converts them into Hamilton’s equations for wave rays and a first-order (in time or the ray path) differential equation for the wave envelope. Then, one calculates the ray trajectories that are relevant to the initial conditions (‘reference rays’) and solves the envelope equations on those trajectories. As an initial-value algorithm, this scheme is computationally inexpensive and has also yielded a number of spinoffs, such as beam tracing
(
Pereverzev 1998
;
Poli et al. 2018
;
Hall-Chen et al. 2022
)
and quasioptics, which can account for both transverse diffraction
(
Balakin et al. 2007
)
and mode conversion
(
Dodin et al. 2019
;
Yanagihara et al. 2021
)
.

In its standard formulation, GO is also closely related to the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) approximation, so we will use the terms ‘conventional GO’ and WKB interchangeably.

Still, the GO approach is limited in that it assumes that the characteristic scale
\smash{L}
of the medium is much larger than the local wavelength
\smash{\lambda}
. Even for high-frequency waves, such as those in the electron-cyclotron range, this assumption unavoidably breaks down near cutoffs (reflection points), where
\smash{\lambda/L}
is infinite and the conventional GO predicts a spurious singularity of the field amplitude. For example, this is an issue in modeling the O–X mode conversion near the critical plasma density, which is a part of a promising scheme for heating overdense plasmas in fusion reactors
(
Hansen et al. 1985
;
Preinhaelter & Kopecký 1973
)
and also occurs in the ionosphere
(
Mjølhus 1990
;
Eliasson & Papadopoulos 2016
)
. Since those waves experience many oscillations during the mode-conversion process, one can expect that
some
GO-like model should be possible for them, even though the conversion occurs near a cutoff. This motivates a search for alternative formulations of GO that do not rely on the smallness of
\smash{\lambda/L}
per se.

One such formulation, which has long been known, is based on Maslov’s approach
(
Maslov & Fedoriuk 1981
;
Ziolkowski & Deschamps 1984
;
Thomson & Chapman 1985
)
. The idea of this approach is based on the fact that a wave experiencing reflection in the physical space satisfies the conditions of GO in the spectral space, so one can reinstate GO locally by applying the (spatial) Fourier transform. However, applying Maslov’s method is cumbersome in that the exact moment for performing the Fourier transform is only loosely specified and thus codes have to be supervised. The more recent methods
(
Littlejohn 1985
;
Littlejohn 1986
;
Kay 1994a
;
Zor & Kay 1996
;
Madhusoodanan & Kay 1998
;
Alonso & Forbes 1997
;
Alonso & Forbes 1999
)
remedy this issue by using continuous transformations of the wavefield along ray trajectories. However, these methods are restricted in the types of solutions sought and are usually applicable only to specific equations, such as the Schrödinger equation for quantum particles. Practical modeling of waves in plasmas, especially magnetised plasmas, requires a more general framework.

The first framework reasonably fit for plasma applications, called metaplectic geometrical optics (MGO), was developed in
(
Lopez & Dodin 2019
;
Lopez & Dodin 2020
;
Lopez & Dodin 2021b
;
Lopez & Dodin 2021a
;
Donnelly et al. 2021
)
; for an overview, see
(
Lopez & Dodin 2022
)
. Its basic idea is as follows: instead of propagating the wavefield in the physical space, one propagates it, by integrating GO equations over a differentially small time, on a space that is locally tangent to the ray trajectory in the ray phase space. Then, one remaps the field to the space tangent to the reference ray at the next location on the ray and iterates. The remapping is done using metaplectic transforms (MT), a subject that we will introduce shortly (section
1.2
). An advantage of this formulation is that it can be used for any linear wave equation, including integro-differential equations that emerge in kinetic wave theory. It also readily incorporates transverse diffraction and mode conversion and has been proven workable in calculations of the field caustics within various wave models
(
Lopez & Dodin 2021b
)
. That said, the need for constant remapping of the wavefield between neighboring tangent spaces remains an inconvenience in MGO. It would be better to have a differential equation that would describe the continuous evolution of the wave amplitude on reference rays themselves, while mapping the resulting solutions to the physical space would remain an optional diagnostic. The purpose of this paper is to propose such a formulation of MGO. (Below, the term ‘MGO’ refers to the new formulation that we report here.)

1.2
Toolbox

Let us start by explaining what MTs are about. (A more detailed formulation is presented in section
3

\smash{\psi_{\hat{x}}}
on a coordinate space
\smash{x}

\smash{\hat{x}}

\smash{\hat{q}=A\hat{x}+B\hat{k}}

\smash{A}

\smash{B}

\smash{\hat{x}}
and
\smash{\hat{k}}

1.3
Synopsis

Developing MGO means answering the following questions: (i) What is the natural position operator
\smash{\hat{\tau}}
for MGO? (ii) How does one write GO equations in the
\smash{\tau}
-representation? (iii) What is the small parameter that replaces the GO parameter
\smash{\lambda/L}
? (iv) How does one map a solution from the
\smash{\tau}
-representation to the
\smash{x}
-representation? Below, we summarise how we answer these questions in the rest of the paper.

Suppose a wave is governed by
\smash{\hat{H}\ket{\psi}=0}
, where
\smash{\hat{H}}
is a Hermitian operator. (A small anti-Hermitian addition to
\smash{\hat{H}}
can be easily accommodated as a perturbation.) A systematic derivation of the conventional GO from this equation (section
4
) starts with introducing the Weyl symbol of
\smash{\hat{H}}
, denoted
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
, where the index
z
indicates that the corresponding Weyl calculus is constructed on the original ray phase-space
\smash{(x,k)\equiv\text{{\sf z}}}
. Then, one Taylor-expands
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
around the ray with
\smash{\lambda/L}
as a small parameter and converts this approximate symbol into a new operator, which serves as an approximation of
\smash{\hat{H}}
. This leads to an approximate amplitude equation on a reference ray. Assuming that
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is a scalar (vector waves are discussed below), the reference ray itself is governed by Hamilton’s equations, with
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
serving as the Hamiltonian.

In MGO, we introduce a different phase space,
\smash{(\tau,h)\equiv\text{{\sf r}}}
, which is aligned with the ray (section
5
). It turns out that the natural position operator
\smash{\hat{\tau}}
in this case is the one that is associated with the momentum operator
\smash{\hat{h}=\hat{H}}
via the canonical commutation relation
\smash{[\hat{\tau},\hat{h}]=\mathrm{i}}
;
5
5

5

Notably, this
\smash{\tau}
is also basically what
’t Hooft 2024
calls the ontological variable in a quantum-mechanical context.
i.e.
\smash{\tau}
is the ray time. We introduce the Weyl calculus on the space
r
and consider the corresponding symbol
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
. MGO envelope equations are derived much like in GO, via Taylor-expanding
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
(section
6
). However, the relevant small parameter in this case is not
\smash{\lambda/L}
but
\smash{\epsilon\doteq(\Delta x\,\Delta k)^{-1}}
, where
\smash{\Delta x}
and
\smash{\Delta k}
are the characteristic scales of the original symbol
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
along the coordinate axis and the wavenumber axis, correspondingly.
6
6

6

If
\smash{\hat{\tau}}
coincides with
\smash{\hat{x}}
, when MGO is identical to GO, then
\smash{\Delta x\sim L}
and
\smash{\Delta k\sim k\sim 1/\lambda}
, so
\smash{\epsilon}
becomes the usual GO parameter
\smash{\lambda/L}
.
This parameter can be understood as the (squared) symplectic curvature of the ray trajectory, or at least a characteristic value thereof.

How does one find
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
? In practice, one generally knows
\smash{\hat{H}}
in the
\smash{x}
-representation. This means that one knows
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
. To obtain MGO equations explicitly, one needs to express
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
though
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
. Since the mapping between
z
and
r
is nonlinear, exact symplectic invariance is not to be expected, so we derive the corresponding relation from scratch. We show that the mapping between the symbols of a given operator in any two representations is determined by the Wigner function of the MT kernel
\smash{M}
. We also show that, for the
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\mapsto\text{{\sf r}}}
transformation at small
\smash{\epsilon}
in particular, this mapping can be approximated with the Airy transform. For smooth symbols like
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
, this gives
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf r}}}(\text{{\sf r}})=H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}}(\text{{\sf r}}))+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})}
. The absence of
\smash{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)}
corrections in this formula is important, because those would have affected the envelope equation (at least, for vector waves discussed below), while
\smash{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})}
is negligible within the assumed accuracy. In other words, one can use
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf r}}}(\text{{\sf r}})\approx H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}}(\text{{\sf r}}))}
notwithstanding the lack of exact symplectic invariance. This makes the MGO envelope equation similar to the GO envelope equation, yet singularities are avoided, as
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is differentiated with respect to different variables (section
6
).

We also propose ways to approximate the MT kernel itself, which then enables mapping solutions of the MGO envelope equation to the physical space
\smash{x}
. Related problems have been traditionally attracting attention in the quantum-mechanics literature. However, they are not particularly important in the context of modeling waves in plasmas, for several reasons. First of all, possible errors caused by inaccuracy of the mapping
\smash{\psi_{\hat{q}}\mapsto\psi_{\hat{x}}}
do not accumulate along the ray trajectory, so there is no need to make this mapping particularly precise. The wavefield is propagated in MGO in a single space,
\smash{\tau}
, and mapping to the
\smash{x}
-space is just an optional diagnostic. Second, although wave simulations are traditionally expected to yield spectacular illustrations, the field in the
\smash{x}
-space does not actually matter much for practical purposes. One might not even need to leave the
\smash{\tau}
-space except for initializing the field and also calculating the final state, and those mappings may be trivial to apply. Third, even when spatial profiles are actually needed, they are not of the field per se but rather of quadratic functionals thereof. Such functionals can always be expressed through the Wigner function of the wavefield. (In fact,
any
reasonably general quasilinear calculations are expressed more naturally through Wigner functions than through fields per se; see
Dodin 2022
;
Dodin 2024
.) As a Weyl symbol, a Wigner function can be remapped from the
r
-space to the
z
-space
analytically
, using the aforementioned Airy transform or its asymptotics. We show that this allows one to easily recover the well-known WKB results, as well as the also well-known Airy profiles of wavefields near cutoffs (section
6
), which are usually considered as an epitome of GO failure. In a way, the Airy function is a cornerstone of MGO, because
any
wavefield locally looks like an Airy field in a certain representation.

These results largely extend to vector waves as well (section
7
). However, since
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is a matrix in this case, reference rays and
\smash{\hat{h}}
are constructed out of its relevant (small) eigenvalues rather than
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
per se. We also derive equations that capture mode conversion when
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is degenerate. Finally, we introduce the concept of a metaplectic resonance, which generalises the concept of the Cherenkov resonance to waves that are quasimonochromatic in their natural
\smash{\tau}
-representation but not necessarily in the physical
\smash{x}
-representation (section
8
). Applications of this theory, as well as the (potentially straightforward) extension of MGO to quasioptical beams are left to future work.

2.2
Weyl symbols

Any given operator
\smash{\hat{O}}
can be expressed as a linear superposition of non-commuting phase-space translations, i.e. combinations of the base operators
(
Tracy et al. 2014
;
de Gosson 2006
)
. Here, we present only the formulas relevant to the discussion below. First of all, one has

\displaystyle\hat{O}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\,\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x+s/2)}O(x,k)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}ks}\bra{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x-s/2)}

(19a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}r\,\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k+r/2)}O(x,k)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}rx}\bra{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k-r/2)},

(19b)

where
\smash{O(x,k)\equiv\symb\hat{O}}
is the Weyl symbol (or just ‘symbol’) of
\smash{\hat{O}}
given by

\displaystyle O(x,k)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}s\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x+s/2)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x-s/2)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ks}

(20a)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}r\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k+r/2)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k-r/2)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}rx}.

(20b)

The symbol of an operator is related to its
\smash{x}
-representation via the Fourier transform with respect to the argument difference
\smash{x-x^{\prime}}
at fixed
\smash{(x+x^{\prime})/2}
:

\displaystyle\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}k(x-x^{\prime})}\,O\left(\frac{x+x^{\prime}}{2},k\right),

(21)

and similarly for the
\smash{k}
-representation. This also leads to the following notable properties of Weyl symbols:

\displaystyle\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)}=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}k\,O(x,k),

(22a)

\displaystyle\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k)}=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}x\,O(x,k).

(22b)

An operator unambiguously determines its symbol, and vice versa. We denote this isomorphism as
\smash{\hat{O}\Leftrightarrow O}
. The mapping
\smash{\hat{O}\Rightarrow O}
is called the Wigner transform, and
\smash{O\Rightarrow\hat{O}}
is called the Weyl transform. For uniformity, we will refer to these as the direct and inverse Wigner–Weyl transform. The Wigner–Weyl isomorphism
\smash{\Leftrightarrow}
is natural in that it has the following properties:

\displaystyle\hat{1}\Leftrightarrow 1,\quad\hat{x}\Leftrightarrow x,\quad\hat{k}\Leftrightarrow k,\quad h(\hat{x})\Leftrightarrow h(x),\quad h(\hat{k})\Leftrightarrow h(k),\quad\hat{O}^{\dagger}\Leftrightarrow O^{*},

(23)

where
\smash{h}
is any function and
\smash{\hat{O}}
is any operator. For multi-component operators, the Wigner–Weyl transform is applied element-by-element. In particular, if
\smash{\hat{O}}
is a matrix operator, one finds that
\smash{\smash{\hat{O}}^{\dagger}\Leftrightarrow O^{\dagger}}
, where
\smash{O}
is the symbol matrix.

The product of two operators maps to the so-called Moyal product, or star product, of their symbols:

\displaystyle\hat{A}\hat{B}\,\Leftrightarrow\,A(x,k)\star B(x,k)\doteq A(x,k)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hat{\mathcal{L}}/2}B(x,k).

(24)

Here,
\smash{\hat{\mathcal{L}}\doteq\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}}{\partial}_{x}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}}{\partial}_{k}-\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle\leftarrow}}{\partial}_{k}\overset{{\scriptscriptstyle\rightarrow}}{\partial}_{x}}
, and the arrows indicate the directions in which the derivatives act. For example,
\smash{A\hat{\mathcal{L}}B}
is just the canonical Poisson bracket:

A\hat{\mathcal{L}}B=\{A,B\}\doteq\frac{\partial A}{\partial x}\frac{\partial B}{\partial k}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial k}\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}\equiv\frac{\partial A}{\partial\text{{\sf z}}^{\alpha}}\,\text{{\sf J}}^{\alpha\beta}\,\frac{\partial B}{\partial\text{{\sf z}}^{\beta}},

(25)

where
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\doteq(x,k)^{\intercal}}
9
9

9

We use
⊺
to denote transposition. Hence, when
z
is treated as a vector,
\smash{(x,k)^{\intercal}\equiv\text{{\sf z}}}
is understood as a column vector and
\smash{(x,k)\equiv\text{{\sf z}}^{\intercal}}
is understood as a row vector. (We ignored the difference between the two in the introduction for simplicity.) The upper and lower indices are not distinguished in this paper, but the Einstein summation convention for repeated indices is assumed. For two vectors
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{1}=(a_{1},b_{1})^{\intercal}}
and
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{2}=(a_{2},b_{2})^{\intercal}}
, one has
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{1}^{\intercal}\text{{\sf z}}_{2}=a_{1}a_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}\equiv\text{{\sf z}}_{1}\cdot\text{{\sf z}}_{2}=\text{{\sf z}}_{2}\cdot\text{{\sf z}}_{1}}
. Keep in mind, though, that
\smash{\cdot}
is not a scalar product per se and does not define a length. In particular, the expression
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\cdot\text{{\sf z}}}
is generally meaningless, because, by definition,
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\cdot\text{{\sf z}}=x^{2}+k^{2}}
yet
\smash{x^{2}}
and
\smash{k^{2}}
generally have non-matching dimensions.
and
J
is the canonical symplectic form, which satisfies
\smash{\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf J}}=-\text{{\sf I}}}
:

\displaystyle\text{{\sf J}}\doteq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{array}\right),\qquad\text{{\sf I}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{array}\right).

These formulas readily yield

\displaystyle h(\hat{x})\hat{k}\Leftrightarrow kh(x)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,h^{\prime}(x),\qquad\hat{k}h(\hat{x})\Leftrightarrow kh(x)-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,h^{\prime}(x)

(30)

(the prime denotes the derivative with respect to the argument), so

\displaystyle kh(x)\Leftrightarrow\frac{1}{2}\,(h(\hat{x})\hat{k}+\hat{k}h(\hat{x}))=h(\hat{x})\hat{k}+\frac{1}{2}\,[\hat{k},h(\hat{x})]=h(\hat{x})\hat{k}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,h^{\prime}(\hat{x}),

(31)

or, in the
\smash{x}
-representation,

\displaystyle kh(x)\Leftrightarrow-\mathrm{i}h(x)\partial_{x}-\mathrm{i}h^{\prime}(x)/2.

(32)

Also

, and so on. In particular, the symbol of a commutator
\smash{[\hat{A},\hat{B}]}
can be expressed through the so-called Moyal bracket
\smash{\{\!\{A,B\}\!\}}
:

\displaystyle[\hat{A},\hat{B}]\Leftrightarrow A\star B-B\star A\equiv\mathrm{i}\{\!\{A,B\}\!\}.

(33)

The Moyal product and the Moyal bracket are particularly handy when
\smash{\partial_{x}\partial_{k}\sim\epsilon\ll 1}
. (Sometimes,
\smash{\epsilon}
is equated with the GO parameter
\smash{\lambda/L}
, but note that
\smash{\star}
is symplectically invariant and GO is not. One might notice, though, that this
\smash{\epsilon}
is similar to the MGO parameter; cf. section

\smash{\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)}

\smash{\epsilon}

\star=\hat{1}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\mathcal{L}}/2-\hat{\mathcal{L}}^{2}/8+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).

(34)

Accordingly, the Moyal bracket can be approximated with the Poisson bracket:

\{\!\{A,B\}\!\}=\{A,B\}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})

(35)

(assuming that
\smash{A}
and
\smash{B}
are scalars or commuting matrices). Likewise, the symbol of any operator in the limit
\smash{\epsilon\to 0}
can be obtained simply by replacing
\smash{\hat{x}}
with
\smash{x}
and
\smash{\hat{k}}

\smash{k}

2.3
Wigner functions

Of particular interest in the Weyl symbol calculus are ‘density operators’

\displaystyle\hat{W}^{(\psi)}\doteq(2\upi)^{-1}\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}

(36)

for various
\smash{\ket{\psi}}
. (The factor
\smash{2\upi}
is sometimes omitted, depending on a convention.) The symbol of such an operator, called the Wigner function,

\displaystyle W^{(\psi)}\doteq\symb\hat{W}^{(\psi)},

(37)

is a real function and can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle W^{(\psi)}(x,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\psi_{\hat{x}}(x+s/2)\,\psi_{\hat{x}}^{*}(x-s/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ks}

(38a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}r\,\psi_{\hat{k}}(k+r/2)\,\psi_{\hat{k}}^{*}(k-r/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}rx}.

(38b)

(Like in the previous formulas,
\smash{2\upi}
here must be replaced with
\smash{(2\upi)^{n}}
if
\smash{x}
is
\smash{n}
-dimensional.) In particular, note the two special cases:

\displaystyle W^{(\psi)}(x,k)=\frac{1}{2\upi}\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\delta(x-x_{0}),&\ket{\psi}=\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x_{0})},\\
\delta(k-k_{0}),&\ket{\psi}=\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{k}}(k_{0})}.\end{array}\right.

Any function bilinear in
\smash{\psi_{\hat{x}}}
and
\smash{\psi^{*}_{\hat{x}}}
can be expressed through
\smash{W^{(\psi)}}
. For example, for any operators
\smash{\hat{L}}
and
\smash{\hat{R}}
, one has

\displaystyle(\hat{L}\psi_{\hat{x}}(x))(\hat{R}\psi_{\hat{x}}(x))^{*}=\int\mathrm{d}k\,{L}(x,k)\star W^{(\psi)}(x,k)\star{R}^{*}(x,k),

(41)

where
\smash{{L}}
and
\smash{{R}}
are the corresponding symbols. As a corollary, one has

\displaystyle|\psi_{\hat{x}}(x)|^{2}=\int\mathrm{d}k\,W^{(\psi)}(x,k),\qquad|\psi_{\hat{k}}(k)|^{2}=\int\mathrm{d}x\,W^{(\psi)}(x,k).

(42)

If, for a wavefield,
\smash{|\psi_{\hat{x}}(x)|^{2}}
and
\smash{|\psi_{\hat{k}}(k)|^{2}}
are interpreted as the densities of quanta in the
\smash{x}
-space and the
\smash{k}
-space, respectively, one can attribute
\smash{W^{(\psi)}}
as a quasiprobability distribution of wave quanta in phase space. The prefix ‘quasi’ is commonly added because
\smash{W^{(\psi)}}
can be negative, which is not something that one expects from a probability distribution. That said, a Wigner function averaged over a sufficiently large phase-space volume
\smash{\Delta x\,\Delta k\gtrsim 1}
is guaranteed to be nonnegative. Such a function can be understood as the spectrum of the two-point correlation function
\smash{\langle\psi_{\hat{x}}(x+s/2)\psi_{\hat{x}}^{*}(x-s/2)\rangle}
over
\smash{s}
at given
\smash{x}
and represents a local property of the field. For further details, see, for example,
(
Dodin 2022
)
.

\smash{\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)}}
. Also, by definition,

\displaystyle\hat{x}\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)}=x\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)}.

(62)

This leads to

\displaystyle(\hat{x}_{\hat{q}}M)(q,x)=xM(q,x).

(63)

Let us symbolically represent
\smash{\hat{x}_{\hat{q}}}
through the new base operators
\smash{\hat{q}_{\hat{q}}=q}
and
\smash{\hat{p}_{\hat{q}}=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q}}
as
\smash{X(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})}
. This leads to the following equation:

\displaystyle X(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})M(q,x)=xM(q,x).

(64)

Equation (
64
) is a (pseudo)differential equation for
\smash{M}
as a function of
\smash{q}
, with
\smash{x}
as a parameter. Thus, the solution of (
64
) is defined up only to an arbitrary function of
\smash{x}
and another equation for
\smash{M}
is needed to specify this function. To derive such an equation, note that
\smash{\bar{M}(x,q)\equiv\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)}\equiv M^{*}(q,x)}
can be understood as the
\smash{x}
-representation of
\smash{\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)}}
. Hence, on the one hand,

\displaystyle(\hat{k}_{\hat{x}}\bar{M})(x,q)=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}\bar{M}(x,q)=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}M^{*}(q,x).

(65)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle(\hat{k}_{\hat{x}}\bar{M})(x,q)=\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)|\hat{k}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)}=\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)|\hat{k}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)}\!{}^{*}=(\hat{k}_{\hat{q}}M)^{*}(q,x).

(66)

Thus,
\smash{(\hat{k}_{\hat{q}}M)(q,x)=\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}M(q,x)}
, or, assuming the notation
\smash{\hat{k}_{\hat{q}}=K(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})}
,

\displaystyle K(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})M(q,x)=\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}M(q,x).

(67)

Equation (
67
) can be interpreted as a Schrödinger equation (SE) for
\smash{M}
, with
\smash{x}
serving as the time variable. In this sense,
\smash{M}
is a
wave
, which we call an
\smash{M}
-wave. Likewise, in retrospect, (
64
) can be interpreted as an SE for a stationary wave with ‘energy’
\smash{x}
.

Together, (
64
) and (
67
) define
\smash{M}
up to a constant complex factor. From the normalization condition, one finds that

\displaystyle\delta(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime})|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}q\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime})|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)}\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}q\,M^{*}(q,x^{\prime})M(q,x^{\prime\prime}).

(68)

This means that, for any given
\smash{x}
,
\smash{M}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{d}s\,M^{*}(q,x+s/2)M(q,x-s/2)=1.

(69)

(This is also seen from the definition of
\smash{M}
as a representation of a normalised eigenvector.) This defines the normalization factor up to a constant phase, which can be anything depending how the eigenvectors of
\smash{\hat{q}}
are chosen relative to those of
\smash{\hat{x}}
. This choice constitutes a gauge freedom of the theory.

Similar equations apply to the inverse-MT kernel
\smash{\bar{M}(x,q)}
. That is, assuming the notation
\smash{\hat{q}_{\hat{x}}=Q(x,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x})}
and
\smash{\hat{p}_{\hat{x}}=P(x,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x})}
, one has

\displaystyle Q(x,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x})\bar{M}(x,q)

\displaystyle=q\bar{M}(x,q),

(70)

\displaystyle P(x,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x})\bar{M}(x,q)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\partial_{q}\bar{M}(x,q).

(71)

Equation (
70
) can be interpreted as an SE for a wave
\smash{\bar{M}}
, with
\smash{q}
serving as the time variable. Because
\smash{\bar{M}(x,q)\equiv M^{*}(x,q)}
, we will also call it an
\smash{M}
-wave. Also, (
70
) can be interpreted as an SE for a stationary wave with ‘energy’
\smash{q}
. Like before, one also finds

\displaystyle\delta(q^{\prime}-q^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}x\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)}\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x)|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime\prime})}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}x\,\bar{M}^{*}(x,q^{\prime})\bar{M}(x,q^{\prime\prime}),

(72)

whence

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\,\bar{M}^{*}(x,q+s/2)\bar{M}(x,q-s/2)=1.

(73)

Let us summarise our intermediate results.
\smash{M}
-waves are normalised solutions of SEs (
67
) and (
71
), which can also be written in the form (
128
):

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{M}M=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{M}\doteq K(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x},

(74a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{M}}\bar{M}=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{M}}\doteq P(x,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x})-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q}.

(74b)

Their solutions can be formally expressed as follows:

\displaystyle M(q,x)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}x\hat{k}_{\hat{q}})M(q,0),\qquad\bar{M}(x,q)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}q\hat{p}_{\hat{x}})\bar{M}(x,0).

(75)

It is generally impossible to find explicit analytic expressions for the propagators
\smash{\exp(-\mathrm{i}x\hat{k}_{\hat{q}})}
and
\smash{\exp(-\mathrm{i}q\hat{p}_{\hat{x}})}
for a given variable transformation. However, it is possible to do so for some important special cases and also asymptotically. This will be discussed later, in sections
3.5
and
4.3
.

3.3
Symplectic pseudo-measure

As a function of two variables, an
\smash{M}
-wave can be considered as a field on the two-dimensional ‘spacetime’
\smash{(x,q)}
. The variable
\smash{x}
serves as the time variable in the Schrödinger equation (
67
), so
\smash{\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}}
serves as the frequency operator and
\smash{-k}
serves as the frequency variable. Hence, MGO naturally involves Wigner functions of
\smash{M}
in the form

\displaystyle 2\upi W^{(M)}_{\text{{\sf z}}}(x,q,-k,p)\equiv\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})

(76)

(the utility of the added coefficient
\smash{2\upi}
will become clear shortly), i.e.

\displaystyle\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,M(q+s^{\prime}/2,x+s/2)\,M^{*}(q-s^{\prime}/2,x-s/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}ks}.

(77)

For the inverse transformation, the corresponding function is

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}(\text{{\sf z}},\text{{\sf y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\bar{M}(x+s/2,q+s^{\prime}/2)\,\bar{M}^{*}(x-s/2,q-s^{\prime}/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ks+\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,M^{*}(q+s^{\prime}/2,x+s/2)\,M(q-s^{\prime}/2,x-s/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}ks}

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}}).\vphantom{\int}

(78)

Like any Wigner function,
\smash{\mu}
is real, so one can also express (
78
) simply as

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}(\text{{\sf z}},\text{{\sf y}})=\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}}).

(79)

Also note that

\displaystyle\int\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})\,\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf z}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,M(q+s^{\prime}/2,x+s/2)\,M^{*}(q-s^{\prime}/2,x-s/2)\,\delta(s)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}}\int\mathrm{d}x\,M(q+s^{\prime}/2,x)\,M^{*}(q-s^{\prime}/2,x)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}}\,\delta(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=1

(80)

(here we used (
72
)) and, similarly,
\smash{\int\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})\,\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf y}}=1}
too. In summary then,

\displaystyle\int\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})\,\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf z}}=\int\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})\,\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf y}}=1.

(81)

As to be seen below (section
3.4
), these serve as measures on the
z
-space and the
y
-space, respectively (strictly speaking, pseudo-measures, because
\smash{\mu}
can be negative). Below, the arguments of
\smash{\mu}
will be occasionally omitted where they are obvious from the context.

3.4
Transformation of operators

A metaplectic transform induces a transformation of the operator representation
\smash{\hat{O}_{\hat{x}}\mapsto\hat{O}_{\hat{q}}}
, which is found as follows. Note that

\displaystyle(\hat{O}_{\hat{q}}\psi_{\hat{q}})(q)

\displaystyle=\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)|\hat{O}|\psi}

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}q^{\prime}\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})}\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})|\psi}.

(82)

Using (
8
), one can also represent this as follows:

\displaystyle(\hat{O}_{\hat{q}}\psi_{\hat{q}})(q)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}q^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime\prime}\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q)|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime})}O_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime\prime})|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})}\psi_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}q^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime\prime}\,M(q,x^{\prime})\,O_{\hat{x}}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\,M^{*}(q^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\,\psi_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}q^{\prime}\,(\hat{M}\hat{O}_{\hat{x}}\hat{M}^{\dagger})(q,q^{\prime})\,\psi_{\hat{q}}(q^{\prime}),

(83)

whence

\displaystyle\hat{O}_{\hat{q}}=\hat{M}\hat{O}_{\hat{x}}\hat{M}^{\dagger}.

(84)

Let us use the following formula for the Weyl symbol of a given operator (cf. (
20a
)):

\displaystyle O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(q,p)=\int\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q+s^{\prime}/2)|\hat{O}|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q-s^{\prime}/2)}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}}.

(85)

The subindex in the symbol’s notation refers to the fact that this symbol is defined on the phase space
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}\doteq(q,p)^{\intercal}}
. We will call it the
y
-symbol of
\smash{\hat{O}}
, and the symbol defined on the phase space
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\doteq(x,k)^{\intercal}}
will be called the
z
-symbol of
\smash{\hat{O}}
. Recall that

\displaystyle\hat{O}=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\,\ket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x+s/2)}O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(x,k)\bra{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x-s/2)}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}ks}.

(86)

Then,

\displaystyle O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q+s^{\prime}/2)|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x+s/2)}\braket{\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{x}}(x-s/2)|\mathfrak{e}_{\hat{q}}(q-s^{\prime}/2)}

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(x,k)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}ks}.

(87)

Expressing the brackets through
\smash{M}
, one obtains the following expression for the
y
-symbol in terms of the
z
-symbol:

\displaystyle O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(q,p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,M(q+s^{\prime}/2,x+s/2)\,M^{*}(q-s^{\prime}/2,x-s/2)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\times O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(x,k)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}ks},

(88)

and the inverse transformation can be written similarly. Equivalently, this result can be expressed in the following compact form:

\displaystyle O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=\int O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})\,\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})\,\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf z}},\qquad O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})=\int O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})\,\mu(\text{{\sf y}},\text{{\sf z}})\,\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf y}}.

(89)

(If
\smash{O_{\text{{\sf z}}}\equiv 1}
, then (
88
) readily yields
\smash{O_{\text{{\sf y}}}\equiv 1}
, and vice versa, by (
81
).) In particular, these allow one to recalculate the Wigner functions from one representation to another.

3.5
Examples

In this section, we present several examples of MTs that are used as building blocks of the theory presented in later sections.

3.5.1
Shift

For example, let us consider a variable transformation that is a phase-space shift by a constant vector
\smash{\Delta\doteq(\Delta_{q},\Delta_{p})^{\intercal}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{q}=\hat{x}+\Delta_{q}\hat{1},\qquad\hat{p}=\hat{k}+\Delta_{p}\hat{1}.

(90)

This corresponds to

\displaystyle X(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})=q-\Delta_{q},\qquad K(q,-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q})=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q}-\Delta_{p}.

(91)

Then, (
64
) requires that
\smash{M(q,x)=C(q)\delta(q-x-\Delta_{q})}
, where
\smash{C}
is some function. From (
67
), one finds that
\smash{(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{q}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}-\Delta_{p})M(q,x)=0}
, whence
\smash{\partial_{q}C=\mathrm{i}\Delta_{p}C}
. With (
69
) for the normalization, this gives

\displaystyle M(q,p)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Delta_{p}q}\,\delta(q-x-\Delta_{q})

(92)

(up to an arbitrary constant phase factor, which we choose equal to one), and, therefore,

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{q}}(q)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Delta_{p}q}\psi_{\hat{x}}(q-\Delta_{q}).

(93)

Notice that even at
\smash{\Delta_{q}=0}
, when
\smash{q}
is the same as
\smash{x}
, the function
\smash{\psi_{\hat{q}}}
is not the same as
\smash{\psi_{\hat{x}}}
due to nonzero
\smash{\Delta_{p}}
. Also,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Delta_{p}s^{\prime}-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}ks}\,\delta(q+s^{\prime}/2-x-s/2-\Delta_{q})\,\delta(q-s^{\prime}/2-x+s/2-\Delta_{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\Delta_{p}-p)s^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}ks}\,\delta(q-x-\Delta_{q})\,\delta(s-s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(k+\Delta_{p}-p)s}\,\delta(q-x-\Delta_{q})

\displaystyle=\delta(x-q+\Delta_{q})\,\delta(k-p+\Delta_{p}).

(94)

Then, by (
89
), one obtains

\displaystyle O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(q,p)=O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(q-\Delta_{q},p-\Delta_{p})=O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(x(q,p),k(q,p)).

(95)

Simply put, this means that
\smash{O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})}
; i.e. the symbol of an operator is invariant with respect to phase-space shifts.

3.5.2
Rescaling

Let us consider a rescaling canonical transformation:

\displaystyle\hat{q}=\hat{x}/\alpha,\qquad\hat{p}=\alpha\hat{k},

(96)

where
\smash{\alpha}
is a nonzero real constant. (The same factor appears in both equations to keep the transformation canonical.) Clearly,
\smash{M(q,x)=C\delta(q-x/\alpha)}
, where
\smash{C}
is a constant. From (
69
), one finds that
\smash{C=|\alpha|^{-1/2}}
up to an arbitrary phase, so

\displaystyle M(q,x)=|\alpha|^{-1/2}\,\delta(q-x/\alpha),\qquad\psi_{\hat{q}}(q)=|\alpha|^{1/2}\psi_{\hat{x}}(\alpha q).

(97)

3.5.3
Linear symplectic transformation

Let us also consider a linear symplectic transformation (LST), that is,

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{q}\\
\hat{p}\end{array}\right)=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&B\\
C&D\end{array}\right)}_{\text{{\sf S}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{x}\\
\hat{k}\end{array}\right),\qquad

where the (constant) coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle AD-BC=1,

(104)

i.e.
\smash{\det\text{{\sf S}}=1}
.

14

14

Additional constraints apply at
\smash{\dim x>1}
, when
\smash{A}
,
\smash{B}
,
\smash{C}

\smash{D}
are matrices. See, for example,
(
Lopez & Dodin 2019

.
This subsumes relabeling coordinates and momenta; specifically,
\smash{A=D=0}
and
\smash{B=-C=\pm 1}
corresponds to the transformation
\smash{(\hat{q},\hat{p})=(\pm\hat{k},\mp\hat{x})}
. (Note that
\smash{\hat{k}}
and
\smash{\hat{x}}
necessarily enter the latter formula with opposite signs to ensure symplecticity.) The rescaling transformation considered in section

is also subsumed as a limit (
\smash{B\to 0}
,
\smash{C\to 0}
, and
\smash{A\to 1/D}
).

Equivalently, (
3.5.3
) can be represented in the following inverted form:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{x}\\
\hat{k}\end{array}\right)=\underbrace{\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{A}&\bar{B}\\
\bar{C}&\bar{D}\end{array}\right)}_{\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{q}\\

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{A}&\bar{B}\\
\bar{C}&\bar{D}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}D&-B\\

so
\smash{\det\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}=\det\text{{\sf S}}=1}
. Then, (
64
) and (
67
) yields

\displaystyle(\bar{A}q-\mathrm{i}\bar{B}\partial_{q}-x)M(q,x)=0,\qquad(\bar{C}q-\mathrm{i}\bar{D}\partial_{q}-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x})M(q,x)=0.

(115)

With the normalization (
69
) taken into account, a straightforward calculation yields the well known result
(
Littlejohn 1986

\displaystyle M(q,x)=(2\upi\mathrm{i}\bar{B})^{-1/2}\,\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}(\bar{A}q^{2}-2xq+\bar{D}x^{2})}{2\bar{B}}\right).

(116)

As usual, an arbitrary constant phase can be added to the overall expression, depending on a convention. The phase of the square root as a function of
\smash{B}
must be chosen such that MTs remain a group. Loosely speaking, one can require that a phase-space rotation

15

15

\smash{\bar{A}=\bar{D}=\cos\alpha}
and
\smash{-\bar{C}=\bar{D}=\sin\alpha}

\smash{\alpha=\arg(\bar{A}+\mathrm{i}\bar{B})}
. Remember, though, that
\smash{\bar{A}}
and
\smash{\bar{B}}
generally have different units (or otherwise are normalised arbitrarily). Thus this expression for
\smash{\alpha}
is questionable unless
\smash{\bar{A}}
or
\smash{\bar{B}}
is zero, i.e. unless
\smash{\alpha}
is

\smash{\bar{B}}
to change by
\smash{\pm 2\upi}

\smash{\bar{B}^{1/2}}
changes by
\smash{\pm\upi}
. (For a more detailed discussion, see
(
Littlejohn 1986
)
.) Then, the MT corresponding to a complete phase-space rotation in either direction is not an identity operator
\smash{\hat{1}}
but
\smash{-\hat{1}}
.

The corresponding transformation of symbols is calculated as follows. First, note that

\displaystyle M(q+s^{\prime}/2,x+s/2)\,

\displaystyle M^{*}(q-s^{\prime}/2,x-s/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}+\mathrm{i}ks}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi|\bar{B}|}\,\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}s(q-\bar{D}x+\bar{B}k)+\mathrm{i}s^{\prime}(x-\bar{A}q-\bar{B}p)}{\bar{B}}\right),

(117)

and

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}}{2\upi|\bar{B}|}\,\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}s^{\prime}(x-\bar{A}q-\bar{B}p)}{\bar{B}}\right)\int\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{2\upi}\,\exp\left(\mathrm{i}s\left(\frac{q-\bar{D}x}{\bar{B}}+k\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\delta(x-\bar{A}q-\bar{B}p)\,\delta\!\left(k-\frac{\bar{D}x-q}{\bar{B}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta(x-\bar{A}q-\bar{B}p)\,\delta\!\left(k-\frac{\bar{D}(\bar{A}q+\bar{B}p)-q}{\bar{B}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta(x-\bar{A}q-\bar{B}p)\,\delta(k-\bar{C}q-\bar{D}p),

(118)

where we used (
104
). Then, by (
89
), one obtains

\displaystyle O_{\text{{\sf y}}}(q,p)=O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\bar{A}q+\bar{B}p,\bar{C}q+\bar{D}x)=O_{\text{{\sf z}}}(x(q,p),k(q,p)).

(119)

This shows that the symbols of operators are invariant with respect to LSTs. In particular, Wigner functions are LST-invariant. Also, for
\smash{\hat{O}=\hat{\text{{\sf y}}}}
, (
119
) gives
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{\text{{\sf y}}}=\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}\text{{\sf y}}}
and, similarly,
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}_{\text{{\sf z}}}=\text{{\sf S}}\text{{\sf z}}}
; i.e. the symbols of the base operators transform at LSTs exactly as the base operators themselves.

3.5.4
Eikonal transform

Let us also consider the ‘eikonal transform’ (this term will become clear shortly):

\displaystyle\hat{q}=\hat{x},\qquad\hat{p}=\hat{k}-\mathcal{K}(\hat{x})=\hat{k}-\mathcal{K}(\hat{q}),

(120)

which satisfies the commutation relation (
47
). Like in section
3.5.1
, one finds that

\displaystyle M(q,p)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(q)}\,\delta(q-x),

(121)

where
\smash{\theta\doteq\int\mathcal{K}(q)\,\mathrm{d}q=\int\mathcal{K}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}
. Then,
\smash{\psi_{\hat{q}}}
is given by

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{q}}(q)=\psi_{\hat{q}}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta(x)}\psi_{\hat{x}}(x),

(122)

and

). Once it is set, the remaining phase is determined by the branch of the square root and changes by
\smash{\pm\upi/2}
when
\smash{\Theta_{xq}}
goes through zero. This is the standard Maslov-index problem, which we will not revisit here, but see
(
Miller 1974
, p. 85-86)
.

One might wonder why (
166
) is not delta-shaped unlike the result in section
4.2
, which is based on similar approximations. The explanation is as follows. In section
4.2
, we used a type-2 generating function
\smash{F(x,p)}
to obtain
\smash{q=q(x,p)}
and
\smash{k=k(x,p)}
. Normally, one can invert the former to obtain
\smash{p=p(x,q)}
and
\smash{k=k(x,p(x,q))}
; then one can reformulate this as a type-1 transformation, and the results of the present section apply. However, the specific transformation in section
4.2
is singular in that, by (
147
), our specific
\smash{q(x,p)}
happens to be independent of
\smash{p}
. This makes the inversion is impossible and leads to a delta-shaped
\smash{M}
-wave (
150
).

An example illustrating (
166
) will be discussed in section
B.2
, and this formula is also useful for deriving the quantization condition for closed orbits (section
D
). However, keep in mind that the (
166
) is constructed only as the leading-order approximation and we will also need alternative approximations of
\smash{M}
-waves for our purposes (section
5.4
).

Now let us discuss how to use MTs for reduced wave modeling beyond the realm of conventional GO. This section is focused on introducing the key concepts. Specifics, such as envelope equations, and examples will be presented in section

5.1
Preliminaries

5.1.1
Tangent space

Our goal here is to construct a phase-space coordinate system aligned with the reference ray. We will assume that the ray is governed by the Hamiltonian
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
and thus satisfies
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})=0}

4.1
. (However, other Hamiltonians can be advantageous or even necessary in some cases, as will be discussed in section

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}
that satisfies
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}}_{0})=0}

\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is sufficiently smooth (the quantitative condition for this will be discussed in section

), let us consider its Taylor expansion in
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}
:

\displaystyle H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})=(\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0})\cdot\underbrace{\partial_{\text{{\sf z}}}H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}}_{0})}_{-\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf v}}}+\,\Upsilon,\qquad\text{{\sf v}}\doteq\text{{\sf J}}\partial_{\text{{\sf z}}}H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}}_{0}),

(167)

where we used
\smash{\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf J}}=-\text{{\sf I}}}
. Here,

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}
, so we will also write it as
\smash{\text{{\sf v}}=\dot{\text{{\sf z}}}_{0}}

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}

\smash{\Upsilon}
subsumes the second- and higher-order terms of the Taylor series. Albeit nonnegligible, it is small at small
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}

\smash{-(\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0})\cdot\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf v}}}

Assuming the same notation as earlier, let us adopt the operator transformation (
3.1
) in the form

\displaystyle\hat{\text{{\sf y}}}=\text{{\sf S}}(\hat{\text{{\sf z}}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0}),

(168)

where

is a symplectic matrix. As discussed in section
3.5.3
, this corresponds to the phase-space transformation
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}(\text{{\sf z}})\equiv\text{{\sf y}}_{\text{{\sf z}}}=\text{{\sf S}}(\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0})}
, or, equivalently,
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}(\text{{\sf y}})\equiv\text{{\sf z}}_{\text{{\sf y}}}=\text{{\sf z}}_{0}+\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}\text{{\sf y}}}
, where
\smash{\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}}
is a symplectic matrix that is the inverse of

. Let us represent
\smash{\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}}
through its columns
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{q}}
and
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{p}}
,

\displaystyle\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\text{{\sf s}}_{q}&\text{{\sf s}}_{p}\end{array}\right),

and assume the notation
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}=(q,p)^{\intercal}}
, as before. Then,
\smash{q}
and
\smash{p}
can be understood as the components of
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}
in the basis formed by the vectors
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{q}}
and
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{p}}
:

\displaystyle\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0}=\text{{\sf s}}_{q}q+\text{{\sf s}}_{p}p.

(170)

Also, the transformation (
168

5.1.1
) using symplecticity of

\smash{\text{{\sf S}}\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf S}}^{\intercal}=\text{{\sf J}}}
yields
\smash{\text{{\sf S}}=-\text{{\sf J}}\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}^{\intercal}\text{{\sf J}}}
(see also (
3.5.3

Let us choose
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{q}}
such that it is parallel to
v

\smash{q}
-axis is tangent to the ray,

\displaystyle\text{{\sf s}}_{q}=\text{{\sf v}}/v.

(171)

Because of this, we will call

\smash{v}
can be anything for now (but this freedom will be removed in section
5.1.2
for nonlinear coordinate transformations, where we also comment on the physical meaning of
\smash{v}
). It may be tempting to choose
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{p}}
such that it is perpendicular to
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{q}}
; however, angles are not well defined on a symplectic space for the lack of a metric.
23
23

23

Since
\smash{x}
and
\smash{k}

Instead, the only natural constraint on
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{p}}
is that
\smash{\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}}
must be symplectic, i.e.
\smash{\text{{\sf J}}=\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}^{\intercal}\text{{\sf J}}\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}}

\displaystyle\text{{\sf J}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\text{{\sf s}}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\text{{\sf s}}_{p}^{\intercal}\end{array}\right)\text{{\sf J}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\text{{\sf s}}_{q}&\text{{\sf s}}_{p}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\text{{\sf s}}_{q}\cdot\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf s}}_{q}&\text{{\sf s}}_{q}\cdot\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf s}}_{p}\\

\end{array}\right)=(\text{{\sf s}}_{q}\wedge\text{{\sf s}}_{p})\text{{\sf J}},

\smash{\text{{\sf a}}\wedge\text{{\sf b}}\doteq\text{{\sf a}}\cdot\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf b}}=-\text{{\sf b}}\wedge\text{{\sf a}}}

\smash{\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}}
must be symplectic is equivalent to the requirement that
\smash{\text{{\sf s}}_{q}\wedge\text{{\sf s}}_{p}=1}

\displaystyle\text{{\sf s}}_{p}=\text{{\sf u}}/u,\qquad u\doteq\text{{\sf v}}\wedge\text{{\sf u}}/v.

(177)

We choose

\smash{u}

\smash{\text{{\sf v}}=(a_{1},a_{2})^{\intercal}}
and
\smash{\text{{\sf u}}=(b_{1},b_{2})^{\intercal}}

\smash{\text{{\sf v}}\wedge\text{{\sf u}}=a_{1}b_{2}-a_{2}b_{1}}

Because the transformation (

\smash{\hat{H}}

\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})=\Upsilon-(\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}\text{{\sf y}})^{\intercal}\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf v}}}

\displaystyle-\text{{\sf y}}^{\intercal}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\text{{\sf s}}_{q}^{\intercal}\\
\text{{\sf s}}_{p}^{\intercal}\end{array}\right)\text{{\sf J}}\text{{\sf v}}=-\text{{\sf y}}^{\intercal}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\text{{\sf v}}\wedge\text{{\sf v}}/v\\
\text{{\sf u}}\wedge\text{{\sf v}}/u\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q&p\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\

so, in summary,
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=\Upsilon+vp}

\displaystyle\dot{\text{{\sf y}}}=\text{{\sf J}}\partial_{\text{{\sf y}}}H_{\text{{\sf y}}}\equiv\{\text{{\sf y}},H_{\text{{\sf y}}}\},

(185)

or, explicitly,

\displaystyle\dot{q}=v+\partial_{p}\Upsilon,\qquad\dot{p}=-\partial_{q}\Upsilon.

(186)

Since
\smash{\Upsilon=\mathcal{O}((\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0})^{2})}
, these lead to
\smash{\dot{q}_{0}=v}
and
\smash{\dot{p}_{0}=0}
. In this sense,
\smash{v}

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}

\smash{v}

Also note that, since
\smash{\Upsilon}
is small, the wavelength
\smash{2\upi/p}

185
)
a posteriori

\smash{x}
-representation, (

\smash{x}
-representation. In practice, this fact is often taken for granted, but it is nontrivial and requires justification. As seen from the above argument, such ‘unreasonable effectiveness’ of the ray equations is due to the existence of a symplectic transformation
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\mapsto\text{{\sf y}}}
that reinstates GO locally for any smooth
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
while conserving the Poisson bracket and the dispersion-operator symbol.

With GO reinstated in the
\smash{q}
-representation, one can also search for the wavefield in the eikonal form

for the envelope

\smash{x}
-space. If
\smash{\Upsilon}
is small

\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is mostly linear in

is a nonlinear functions of phase-space variables, so the choice of the
\smash{q}
-representation that is convenient at some

There are two ways to deal with this problem. One is to define different linear transformations at different points
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}^{(i)}}
along the ray and use MTs to map the wavefield from the
\smash{q}
-space at
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}^{(i)}}
to the
\smash{q}
-space at
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}^{(i+1)}}
(figure

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}^{(i+1)}}

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}^{(i)}}

(
Lopez & Dodin 2022

nonlinear
transformation such that the new coordinate space is aligned with the ray indefinitely (figure
1
(b)). No field remapping is needed in this case other than to initialise the field in the new coordinate space space and to output the final results back to the
\smash{x}
-space. This is the approach that we discuss below.

5.1.2
Ray path as a coordinate

Let us consider a generic ray that is curved, i.e. has
\smash{\ddot{\text{{\sf z}}}}
linearly independent from
\smash{\dot{\text{{\sf z}}}}
in the region of interest. (Straight rays can be handled as a limit; see below.) Then, at any given
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}
on the ray, the vectors

\displaystyle\text{{\sf v}}=\dot{\text{{\sf z}}}_{0},\qquad\text{{\sf u}}=\ddot{\text{{\sf z}}}_{0}

(187)

form a symplectic basis

24

24

A similar approach is used in
(
Kamran et al. 2009
)
. Also note that here we assume that

is two-dimensional for simplicity. In case of a higher-dimensional phase space

\smash{\dot{\text{{\sf Z}}}_{0}}
and
\smash{\ddot{\text{{\sf Z}}}_{0}}

\displaystyle\text{{\sf s}}_{q}=\text{{\sf v}}/v,\qquad\text{{\sf s}}_{p}=\text{{\sf u}}/u,\qquad\text{{\sf s}}_{q}\wedge\text{{\sf s}}_{p}=1,

(188)

where
\smash{u\doteq\text{{\sf v}}\wedge\text{{\sf u}}/v}

\smash{h}

Since we are interested only in the local dynamics on the ray, it will be sufficient for us to find
\smash{\text{{\sf r}}(\text{{\sf z}})}
and said PDE near a predefined generic

\displaystyle\underbrace{(x,k)^{\intercal}}_{\text{{\sf z}}}\mapsto\underbrace{(q,p)^{\intercal}}_{\text{{\sf y}}}\mapsto\underbrace{(\tau,h)^{\intercal}}_{\text{{\sf r}}}.

(189)

The first one,
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}=\bar{\text{{\sf S}}}(\text{{\sf z}}-\text{{\sf z}}_{0})}

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}=\text{{\sf z}}_{0}}

\smash{\text{{\sf y}}\mapsto\text{{\sf r}}}

47

\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\mapsto\text{{\sf y}}}
is easy to construct exactly and, for smooth rays,
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}\mapsto\text{{\sf r}}}
can also be found analytically, at least asymptotically. Below, we show how to do this step by step.

5.2
Osculating harmonic oscillator

Let us start with a simplified model, when

Strictly speaking, at
\smash{g<0}
, the dispersion curve has two branches, potentially allowing for mode conversion, i.e. energy tunneling from one dispersion surface to another (section
7.3
). However, this is largely irrelevant for us here, as our goal in this section is to construct phase-space variables around a given reference ray rather than solve a wave equation.
Hence, locally, the ray dynamics can be thought of as the dynamics of a harmonic oscillator with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=\frac{\Omega}{2}\,\big(q^{2}+(p+R)^{2}-R^{2}\big).

(210)

We call it the ‘osculating’ harmonic oscillator (OHO). The radius of the phase-space orbit of the OHO is
\smash{|R|}
. This defines the local characteristic scale of the ray orbit. Since our approximation (
190
) is generally adequate only on scales small compared to this scale, our model is applicable only when both
\smash{q}
and
\smash{p}
are much less than
\smash{R}
.

\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=0}
for various signs of
\smash{g}
and
\smash{\varepsilon}
. Darker colors mark, loosely, the areas of where the assumed model is adequate. Lighter colors mark the areas that are beyond the validity domain of the model. The arrows mark the direction of the ray propagation, which is always towards positive

\smash{|R|=|v/\Omega|}
. Dotted are the asymptotes of the hyperbolas.

The parameter
\smash{\Omega}
can be understood as OHO’s angular frequency. At
\smash{\Omega>0}
, or
\smash{\varepsilon>0}
, the phase-space trajectory is oriented clockwise, as usual. (Note that the canonical phase of the oscillator normally grows when the polar angle on the
\smash{(q,p)}
plane decreases.) At
\smash{\Omega<0}
, or
\smash{\varepsilon<0}
, the trajectory is counter-clockwise. At
\smash{\Omega=0}
, which corresponds to
\smash{\varepsilon=0}
, the trajectory is flat, i.e.
\smash{R=\infty}
. Hence, it is convenient to classify the ray trajectories in terms of their (dimensionless) ‘symplectic curvature’

\displaystyle\kappa\doteq\frac{1}{R}=\frac{\Omega}{v}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}\,\frac{|g|^{3/4}}{|\varepsilon|^{1/2}},

(211)

and
\smash{R}
will be called the symplectic radius, accordingly. (Here and further,
\sgn R\equiv\sgn\kappa=\sigma_{\varepsilon}
.) Note that the symplectic curvature can be very different from the regular curvature of a trajectory in a metric space. In particular, one can easily show that

\displaystyle\varepsilon=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}x}{\mathrm{d}k^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}{\partial x}\right)^{3}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}k}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}{\partial k}\right)^{3},

(212)

where the full derivatives are taken along the ray trajectory,
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})=0}
. Thus, at inflection points (where
\smash{\varepsilon\to 0}
),
\smash{\kappa}
is infinite, whereas the regular curvature would be zero.

Finally, notice the following. The OHO approximation (
210
) for
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
corresponds to the following approximation of
\smash{\hat{H}}
itself:
\smash{\hat{H}\approx(\Omega/2)(\hat{q}^{2}+(\hat{p}+R)^{2}-R^{2})}
. This means that, in the absence of dissipation, the wave equation (
142
) becomes the equation of a quantum Harmonic oscillator (QHO) with
\smash{\hbar=1}
. On the ray that is a circle with radius
\smash{|R|}
, the (‘quantum’) mode number
\smash{n}
of this oscillator, i.e. the action in the natural units, is

\displaystyle n=\frac{R^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}.

(213)

This readily determines applicability conditions for GO in the
\smash{q}
-representation in the region
\smash{q\ll R}
that we are interested in. Indeed, as commonly known, one can adequately approximate the dynamics of such QHO using GO at
\smash{n\gg 1}
, i.e.
\smash{R\gg 1}
. This makes
\smash{\kappa}
a natural small parameter for MGO. One might be surprised that this parameter becomes infinite at inflection points, where rays become straight and thus, supposedly, MGO should work only better. But notice that, near inflection points,
\smash{R}
evolves faster than the ray moves along the OHO trajectory per se and thus the very concept of the orbit radius becomes irrelevant. In other words, the quadratic approximation (
190
) is inapplicable in such regimes and one should either include cubic terms or simply ignore the effects produced by the quadratic terms, in which case MGO is reinstated. What matters then is not the local
\smash{R}
per se but the characteristic
\smash{R}
. We will return to this subject in section
5.4
.

5.3

There is more than one way to define the transformation

Operator transformation

As pointed out in section

Besides avoiding infinite series, the above model is advantageous in that it allows one to explicitly calculate

The corresponding solution of (

\displaystyle\bar{M}(q,\tau)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{v}}\,\delta\left(\tau-\frac{q}{v}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega q^{3}}{6v}\right).

Accordingly, the pseudo-measure (

\smash{M\equiv\bar{M}^{*}}

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}s\,M(\tau+s/2,q+s^{\prime}/2)\,M^{*}(\tau-s/2,q-s^{\prime}/2)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}hs+\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi v}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\,\delta\left(\tau-\frac{q}{v}+\frac{s-s^{\prime}/v}{2}\right)\delta\left(\tau-\frac{q}{v}-\frac{s-s^{\prime}/v}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega}{6v}\left(q+\frac{s^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\Omega}{6v}\left(q-\frac{s^{\prime}}{2}\right)^{3}-\mathrm{i}hs+\mathrm{i}ps^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\delta\left(\tau-\frac{q}{v}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon s^{3}}{24}+\mathrm{i}s(H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})-h)\right),

(221)

where we used
\smash{\Omega v^{2}=\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\mu(\text{{\sf r}},\text{{\sf y}})=\Ai_{\varepsilon}(H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})-h)\,\delta(\tau-q/v),

(222)

where we have introduced the following (real) function, with
\smash{\gamma}
as a real parameter:

\displaystyle\Ai_{\gamma}(z)\doteq\frac{1}{2\upi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}zt+\mathrm{i}\gamma t^{3}/24}=\frac{2}{|\gamma|^{1/3}}\,\Ai\left(\frac{2}{|\gamma|^{1/3}}\,\sigma_{\gamma}z\right).

(223)

Here,
\smash{\sigma_{\gamma}\doteq\sgn\gamma}
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma\to 0}\Ai_{\gamma}(z)=\delta(z).

(224)

As to be seen, the function

5.4
General case

Let us now present a formulation that is not restricted to the quadratic approximation (
190
) and also subsumes the standard WKB approximation (
153
) as a special case.

5.4.1
Operator transformation

Let us start with assuming a generic
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
in the form

\displaystyle H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})=\mathcal{H}(x/\Delta x,k/\Delta k),

(225)

where
\smash{\Delta x}
and
\smash{\Delta k}
are the characteristic scales of the system in
\smash{x}
and
\smash{k}
, respectively, and
\smash{\mathcal{H}}
is some function with order-one scales. Consider new dimensionless variables

\displaystyle X\doteq Rx/\Delta x,\qquad K\doteq Rk/\Delta k,

(226)

where
\smash{R}
is a dimensionless constant. We require that the transformation
\smash{\text{{\sf z}}\mapsto\text{{\sf Z}}\equiv(X,K)^{\intercal}}
be symplectic; then,
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf Z}}}(\text{{\sf Z}})=H_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}})}
and

\displaystyle R=\sqrt{\Delta x\Delta k}.

(227)

We will call this quantity a symplectic scale. To the extent that the quadratic approximation (
190
) is adequate at least locally,
\smash{R}
coincides with the absolute value of the symplectic radius that was introduced in section
5.2
. (For this reason, below, we occasionally mix the terms ‘symplectic scale’ and ‘symplectic radius’ and also refer to the inverse symplectic scale as symplectic curvature.)

Much like in section
4.1
, our goal now is to derive field equations that are accurate to the first (not zeroth) order in the ‘MGO parameter’

\displaystyle\epsilon\doteq R^{-2}\equiv(\Delta x\Delta k)^{-1}\ll 1.

(228)

It is similar to the GO parameter introduced in section
4.1
, but the absolute value of
\smash{k}
is now replaced with the spectral-inhomogeneity scale
\smash{\Delta k}
; i.e. the smallness of the local wavelength per se is irrelevant. To the extent that the OHO model is applicable,
\smash{\epsilon}
coincides with
\smash{\kappa^{2}}
, where
\smash{\kappa}
is the symplectic curvature introduced in section
5.2
.

Let us perform a linear canonical transformation
\smash{\text{{\sf Z}}\mapsto\text{{\sf y}}\equiv(q,p)^{\intercal}}
, as in section
5.1.1
, so that the
\smash{q}
axis is along
v
that is tangent to the ray. Then,
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})=\mathcal{H}(q/R,p/R)}
, so the ray trajectory satisfies
\smash{\mathcal{H}(q/R,p/R)=0}
. Its solution (for a given branch) can be written in the form
\smash{p=R\mathcal{P}(q/R)\equiv p(q)}
, where
\smash{\mathcal{P}}
is an order-one function that has order-one scales. (If
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is linear in
z
, then
\smash{\mathcal{P}=0}
by construction. If
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is quadratic, then
\smash{p(q)=-R+\sqrt{R^{2}-q^{2}}}
, so
\smash{p(q)\approx q^{2}/2R}
at
\smash{q\ll R}
, as in section
5.3
.) By Taylor-expanding
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})}
in
\smash{p-p(q)}
, one obtains

\displaystyle H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})\approx V(q)(p-p(q)),

(229)

where
\smash{V(q)\doteq(\partial_{p}H_{\text{{\sf y}}})_{p=p(q)}}
, so
\smash{V(0)=v}
. Like (
143
), (
229
) can be understood as Hayes’s representation of
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}}
. For GO, this representation is precise in that it leads to the correct ray equations and correctly captures the leading-order amplitude dynamics, because those are entirely determined by the first-order derivatives of the symbol. The MT that follows from (
229
) will not be exact, because
\smash{\mathcal{O}([p-p(q)]^{2})}
terms have been neglected. Still, assuming
\smash{p-p(q)=\mathcal{O}(q^{2})}
, (
229
) differs from the exact
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf y}}}}
by at most
\smash{\mathcal{O}(q^{4})}
, which is assumed negligible (at not-too-large
\smash{q}
that we are interested in locally).

Let us perform a canonical transformation
\smash{\text{{\sf y}}\mapsto\text{{\sf r}}\equiv(\tau,h)^{\intercal}}
such that
\smash{h=H_{\text{{\sf y}}}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle p(q,h)=p(q)+h/V(q).

(230)

(As a reminder:
\smash{p(q)}
is a prescribed function determined by the shape of the ray trajectory, while
\smash{p(q,h)}
is the new momentum variable
\smash{p}
expressed as a function of
\smash{q}
and
\smash{h}
.) Like in section
5.3
, to find what the new coordinate
\smash{\tau}
should be to keep the transformation canonical, we look for a generating function
\smash{F}
in the type-2 form:

\displaystyle p(q,h)=\partial_{q}F(q,h),\qquad\tau(q,h)=\partial_{h}F(q,h).

(231)

The former gives

\displaystyle F(q,h)=\int^{q}_{0}p(\tilde{q},h)\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{q},

(232)

where we set the integration constant to zero by choice. (The tilde is used to distinguish the dummy integration variable
\smash{\tilde{q}}
from the actual canonical coordinate
\smash{q}
.) Then,

\displaystyle\tau(q,h)=\int^{q}_{0}\frac{\partial p(\tilde{q},h)}{\partial h}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{q}=\int^{q}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{q}}{V(\tilde{q})}\equiv\tau(q).

(233)

In other words,
\smash{\tau}
and
\smash{h}
are the ray time and the ray energy. (By the ray energy, we mean the value of the ray Hamiltonian, not the wave energy that depends on the wave amplitude.) The corresponding operators are

\displaystyle\hat{\tau}=\tau(\hat{q}),\qquad\hat{h}=\frac{1}{2}\,(\hat{V}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{V})-\hat{V}p(\hat{q}),

(234)

where
\smash{\hat{V}\doteq V(\hat{q})}
(note, though, that
\smash{\hat{p}}
is not the same as
\smash{p(\hat{q})}
), so
\smash{[\hat{\tau},\hat{V}]=0}
and

\displaystyle[\hat{\tau},\hat{h}]

\displaystyle=[\hat{\tau},\hat{V}\hat{p}+\hat{p}\hat{V}]/2

\displaystyle=(\hat{\tau}\hat{V}\hat{p}-\hat{V}\hat{p}\hat{\tau}+\hat{\tau}\hat{p}\hat{V}-\hat{p}\hat{V}\hat{\tau})/2

\displaystyle=(\hat{\tau}\hat{V}\hat{p}-\hat{V}[\hat{p},\hat{\tau}]-\hat{V}\hat{\tau}\hat{p}+\hat{\tau}[\hat{p},\hat{V}]+\hat{\tau}\hat{V}\hat{p}-\hat{p}\hat{V}\hat{\tau})/2

\displaystyle=([\hat{\tau}\hat{V},\hat{p}]-\hat{V}[\hat{p},\hat{\tau}]+\hat{\tau}[\hat{p},\hat{V}])/2

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}((\tau(q)V(q))^{\prime}+V(q)\tau^{\prime}(q)-\tau(q)V^{\prime}(q))/2

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}V(q)\tau^{\prime}(q)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}.

(235)

6
MGO for scalar waves

6.1
Dynamics in the
{\tau}
-space

Armed with (
251
), we are now ready to formulate the MGO envelope equation. Let us return to our original wave equation (
142
), which accounts for dissipation, and rewrite it in the
\smash{\tau}
-representation:

\displaystyle(\hat{H}_{\hat{\tau}}+\mathrm{i}\hat{\Gamma}_{\hat{\tau}})\psi_{\hat{\tau}}=0.

(252)

(As earlier in this paper, we assume here that
\smash{\psi_{\hat{\tau}}}
is a scalar function, but see section
7.2
for a more general case.) Since
\smash{\hat{H}_{\hat{\tau}}=\hat{h}_{\hat{\tau}}=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\tau}}
, this leads to

\displaystyle(-\partial_{\tau}+\hat{\Gamma}_{\hat{\tau}})\psi_{\hat{\tau}}=0.

(253)

Assuming that
\smash{\Gamma_{\text{{\sf r}}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)}
, the operator
\smash{\hat{\Gamma}_{\hat{\tau}}}
can be approximated simply by its symbol on the reference ray,
\smash{\Gamma_{\text{{\sf r}}}(\tau)}
. Then, (
253

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\psi_{\hat{\tau}}=\Gamma_{\text{{\sf r}}}(\tau)\psi_{\hat{\tau}}.

(254)

Assuming that
\smash{\Gamma_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
is sufficiently smooth, (
251
) predicts that
\smash{\Gamma_{\text{{\sf r}}}(\text{{\sf r}})\approx\Gamma_{\text{{\sf z}}}(\text{{\sf z}}(\text{{\sf r}}))}
, which is a known function when the dispersion operator is given. Then, (
254
) can be readily integrated,

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{\tau}}=a\exp\left(\int\mathrm{d}\tau\Gamma_{\text{{\sf r}}}(\tau)\right),\qquad a=\text{const},

(255)

and, unlike in the GO equation (
139
), this solution exhibits no singularities. Also notably, the ray equations in the
r
-representation are particularly simple:

\displaystyle\dot{\tau}=1,\qquad\dot{h}=0.

(256)

A generalization to vector waves is discussed in section
7
. One can also generalise the MGO envelope equation to quasioptical beams that experience transverse diffraction. This can be done similarly to how quasioptical equations were derived in
(
Dodin et al. 2019
;
Yanagihara et al. 2019a
;
Yanagihara et al. 2019b
)
for conventional GO. However, presenting these calculations in detail would require introducing additional cumbersome machinery that is not specific to MGO, so we postpone discussing this subject until further publications.

Mapping solutions to the

6.2.1
General case

To calculate how the wave evolves in the original

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{x}}(x)=\int\mathrm{d}\tau M_{\hat{x}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}(x,\tau)\,\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(\tau).

Since
\smash{\hat{M}_{\hat{x}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}=\hat{M}_{\hat{x}\leftmapsto\hat{q}}\hat{M}_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}}

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{x}}(x)=\int\mathrm{d}q\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\vartheta(q)}}{\sqrt{V(q)}}\,M_{\hat{x}\leftmapsto\hat{q}}(x,q)\,\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(\tau(q)),

(258)

\smash{\vartheta(q)=\int^{q}_{0}p(\tilde{q})\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{q}}

How to approximate such an integral depends on the problem and can be done using various techniques, with accuracy requirements determined by specific applications. For example, a stationary-phase or steepest-descent methods might suffice, or one might be able to reduce (
258

(

(

Remember also that the above result is derived under the assumption that only small values of

\smash{(q,p)}

One way to do this is to numerically find the type-1 generating function

\displaystyle k=\partial_{x}\Theta(x,\tau),\qquad h=-\partial_{\tau}\Theta(x,\tau),\qquad\mathrm{d}\Theta=k\,\mathrm{d}x-h\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

(260)

Alternatively, one may notice that

\smash{\psi_{\hat{x}}}

31

7.3
Mode conversion

Suppose now that
\smash{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
is degenerate, so that more than one of its eigenvalues are
\smash{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)}
. This means that the wave can consist of multiple modes that can resonantly interact with each other, i.e. experience linear mode conversion. In this case, one can represent
\smash{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\hat{\tau}}}
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\hat{\tau}}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}\boldsymbol{a}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}},\qquad\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

(290)

where the (generally non-square) matrix
\smash{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
has the orthonormal polarization vectors
\smash{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}}
of the resonant modes (eigenvectors of
\smash{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
) as its columns,
\smash{\boldsymbol{a}}
is the vector that characterises the amplitudes of these modes, and
\smash{\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
is the nonresonant part of
\smash{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\hat{\tau}}}
. For details, see
(
Dodin et al. 2019
)
, which discusses a similar parametrization within GO.

The derivation of the envelope equation in this case is identical to that in section
7.2
up to replacing
\smash{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
with
\smash{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
(and
\smash{a}
with
\smash{\boldsymbol{a}}
), and one obtains

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}-\boldsymbol{U}+\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\Gamma})\boldsymbol{a}-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{a}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{a}=0,\qquad\boldsymbol{U}\doteq((\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\dagger})^{\prime}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Xi})_{\rm A}.

(291)

Here, the index A denotes the anti-Hermitian part, and
\smash{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
is a diagonal matrix,
\smash{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\diag\{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2},\ldots\}}
, where
\smash{\Lambda_{i}}
are the small eigenvalues of
\smash{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}}
. Also,
\smash{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\text{{\sf r}}}\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
, and
\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}=\boldsymbol{\Xi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Xi}}
, so the elements of the latter,
\smash{\mathcal{V}_{ij}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\dagger}(\partial_{h}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}})\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}}
, can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\,(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j})-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\dagger}}{\partial h}\,\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}}{\partial h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Lambda_{ij}}{\partial h}-\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\dagger}}{\partial h}\,\Lambda_{j}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{i}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{i}\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}_{j}}{\partial h}.

(292)

In the envelope equation, the coefficients
\smash{\mathcal{V}_{ij}}
appear only in combination with
\smash{\partial_{\tau}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)}
, so they are of interest only to the zeroth order in
\smash{\epsilon}
. Then, the last two terms in (
292

\smash{\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{{\sf r}}}}

\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\approx\partial_{h}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}

\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}}
is approximately diagonal, so mode coupling is determined entirely by
\smash{\boldsymbol{U}}
and
\smash{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}

\smash{\det\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}\approx\prod_{i}\partial_{h}\Lambda_{i}}

\smash{\partial_{h}\Lambda_{i}}

(293)

where

\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{N}}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{V}}^{1/2}}

8
Wave–particle interactions in MGO

8.1
Generalised Cherenkov resonance

The MT formalism also suggests a way to rethink wave–particle interactions. Consider the Cherenkov condition of the wave–particle resonance:

\displaystyle\omega=kv_{\text{p}},

(295)

where
\smash{\omega}
is the wave frequency,
\smash{k}
is the local wavenumber, and
\smash{v_{\text{p}}}
is the particle velocity. This condition is not symplectically invariant but can be generalised within MGO, if one considers particles semiclassically.
39
39

39

See
(
Ruiz & Dodin 2017b
;
Dodin & Fisch 2014
)
for how the Cherenkov condition emerges in generic wave–wave interactions and semiclassical interactions in particular.
Like any other waves, a semiclassical particle can be assigned phase-space variables
\smash{(\tau,h)}
. Suppose such a particle interacts with another (
\eg
classical electromagnetic) wave that is eikonal in these variables too, even though this wave might not propagate along the curve
\smash{h=0}
. We assume this wave in the form

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(t,\tau)=\Psi(t,\tau)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta(t,\tau)}.

(296)

Here,
\smash{\Psi}
is the envelope that is slow compared to the (real) phase
\smash{\theta}
, and the time
\smash{t}
can be considered as a parameter within our formalism. A particle can interact resonantly with such a wave if
\smash{\theta}
changes slowly, or not at all, in the particle frame:

\displaystyle 0\approx\dot{\theta}=\partial_{t}\theta+\dot{\tau}\partial_{\tau}\theta,

(297)

where the dot stands for
\smash{\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t}
.
40
40

40

Equation (
297
) subsumes (
295
) as a special case. Indeed, if the
\smash{\tau}
-axis is parallel to the
\smash{x}
-axis, one has
\smash{\dot{\tau}\partial_{\tau}\theta=\dot{x}\partial_{x}\theta=kv_{\text{p}}}
, where we used
\smash{k\doteq\partial_{x}\theta}
and
\smash{v_{\text{p}}\doteq\dot{x}}
.
Provided that the particle Hamiltonian and the wave Hamiltonian are normalized consistently, one has
\smash{\dot{\tau}=1}
(cf. (
256
)). Then, assuming the standard definition
\smash{\omega\doteq-\partial_{t}\theta}
, (
297
) leads to a
metaplectic resonance condition
:

\displaystyle\omega=\partial_{\tau}\theta.

(298)

(An alternative derivation and interpretation of this resonance condition is given in section
8.2
.) Since
\smash{\dot{\tau}=1}
, all quantities here are entirely classical, i.e.
\smash{\hbar}
is not involved. Also notice that the
\smash{\tau}
axis can be aligned arbitrarily relative to the physical-space axis
\smash{x}
. For example, a wave can resonantly interact with a particle even near a cutoff in the
\smash{x}
-space. This unifies Cherenkov acceleration with Fermi acceleration, which we define here broadly as particle acceleration caused by interaction with moving walls.

To better understand the condition (
298
), recall that
\smash{\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathrm{d}\phi/\Omega}
. As a reminder,
\smash{\phi}
and
\smash{\Omega}
are the canonical phase and frequency of particle’s OHO (section
5.2
),

\displaystyle\Omega=\left|\det\big(\partial^{2}_{\text{{\sf z}}}H_{\text{{\sf z}}}\big)\right|^{1/2}\sgn\varepsilon,

(299)

where
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
is the particle Hamiltonian,
z
is the particle phase-space variables, and
\smash{\varepsilon\doteq\ddot{\text{{\sf z}}}\wedge\dot{\text{{\sf z}}}}
.
41
41

41

Since we treat the particle as a quantum wave, its classical Hamiltonian is
\smash{\hbar H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
and its classical momentum is
\smash{\hbar k}
. However, simultaneously rescaling the energy and momentum by
\smash{\hbar}
(or any other constant factor) does not affect (
299
), so one can as well interpret
\smash{H_{\text{{\sf z}}}}
in (
299
) as the classical Hamiltonian and
z
as the classical phase space.
Then, (
298
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\phi}=\frac{\omega}{\Omega}.

(300)

For illustration, let us consider a special case when
\smash{\omega}
is a constant and the particle oscillates (nonrelativistically) in a parabolic potential, so
\smash{\Omega}
is a constant too. Then, assuming (
300
) is satisfied globally, integrating this equation over
\smash{\phi}
from
\smash{0}
to
\smash{2\upi}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\theta_{\circlearrowright}}{2\upi}=\frac{\omega}{\Omega},

(301)

where
\smash{\Delta\theta_{\circlearrowright}}
is the total change of
\smash{\theta}
on said interval. Since
\smash{m\doteq\Delta\theta_{\circlearrowright}/2\upi}
must be integer, (
301
) leads to the following condition for global wave–particle resonances:

\displaystyle\omega=m\Omega.

(302)

This result is, of course, not unexpected. However, note that (
302
) is only a special case that illustrates a more general
local
resonance condition (
300
), which does not require the wave and particle oscillations to be bounded.

8.2
Metaplectic resonance heating

The metaplectic resonance condition (
298
) also emerges naturally in plasma kinetic theory. Consider particles with a probability distribution function
\smash{f}
governed by the Vlasov equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\{H,f\}=0,

(303)

where
\smash{H}
is the particle Hamiltonian. Suppose
\smash{H=H_{0}+H_{1}}
, where
\smash{H_{1}}
is a perturbation small compared to
\smash{H_{0}}
. Then, within linear theory, one has
\smash{f=f_{0}+f_{1}}
, where
\smash{f_{0}}
is the background distribution and
\smash{f_{1}}
is of order
\smash{H_{1}}
. These functions satisfy

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{0}+\{H_{0},f_{0}\}=0,\qquad\partial_{t}f_{1}+\{H_{0},f_{1}\}=-\{H_{1},f_{0}\}.

(304)

Let us assume that
\smash{H_{0}}
is time-independent and
\smash{f_{0}}
is an equilibrium distribution
\smash{f_{0}=f_{0}(H_{0})}
. Then, in the canonical variables
\smash{(\tau,H_{0})}
, the Poisson brackets in the equation for
\smash{f_{1}}
can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\{H_{0},f_{1}\}=\partial_{\tau}f_{1},\qquad\{H_{1},f_{0}(H_{0})\}=-(\partial_{\tau}H_{1})f_{0}^{\prime}(H_{0}).

(305)

This leads to

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\partial_{\tau})f_{1}=(\partial_{\tau}H_{1})f_{0}^{\prime}(H_{0}).

(306)

Let us assume that the perturbation is an eikonal wave in
\smash{\tau}
, such that
\smash{f_{1}=\re\tilde{f}}
,
\smash{H_{1}=\re\tilde{H}}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{f}=-\mathrm{i}\omega\tilde{f},\qquad\partial_{\tau}\tilde{f}=\mathrm{i}(\partial_{\tau}\theta)\tilde{f},\qquad\partial_{\tau}\tilde{H}=\mathrm{i}(\partial_{\tau}\theta)\tilde{H}.

(307)

Hence,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}=-\frac{\partial_{\tau}\theta}{\omega-\partial_{\tau}\theta}\,\tilde{H}f_{0}^{\prime}(H_{0}),

(308)

so particles will be resonant with the wave under the condition (
298
).

As usual, the presence of a linear resonance implies a heating mechanism, which we call
metaplectic resonance heating
. A quantitative theory of such heating for specific waves is beyond the scope of this paper, but note that the concept of power dissipation is naturally extendable from the physical
\smash{x}
-space to metaplectically transformed fields. For example, as shown in appendix
E
, there is a remarkably concise formula that connects the energy dissipated by an electromagnetic wave, per volume
\smash{\Gamma}
of wave’s extended phase space, in any linear dielectric medium and plasma in particular:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{E}}{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}=\tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}).

(309)

Here,
\smash{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}}
is the symbol of the Hermitian part of the conductivity operator,
\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}}
is the average Wigner matrix of the electric field, and
\smash{\tr}
denotes the matrix trace. Traditionally,
\smash{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
is calculated analytically or semi-analytically using GO ordering. Using MGO, it should be possible to extend those calculations to regions where GO fails, cutoffs in particular.

9
Conclusions

Let us summarize. In this paper, we introduce a formulation of geometrical optics (GO) that is not limited to small
\smash{\lambda/L}
in the physical space. How do we do it? The intrepid readers who hoped to find a short answer here are referred to the synopsis in section
1.3
, which is as short as a meaningful answer can be. But here are some highlights.

As commonly known by now
(
Tracy et al. 2014
)
, the natural language of GO is the Weyl symbol calculus and metaplectic transforms (MTs).
42
42

42

In fact, one can think of the envelope dynamics itself as a metaplectic image of the dynamics of the complete wavefield, as discussed in section
4
.
But neither Weyl symbols nor MTs are inherently tied to the physical space
\smash{x}
or the spectral space
\smash{k}
for that matter. Thus, there is no fundamental reason, other than tradition, why the envelope dynamics should be calculated in the
\smash{x}
-space or the
\smash{k}
-space. Furthermore, instead of wavefields, what really matters for practical use are their Wigner functions (or even integrals thereof). As we show, those can be easily recalculated between any two representations using the Airy transform. Then, it makes sense to work in the phase-space variables in which the GO applicability conditions are satisfied best and map the solution to the
\smash{x}
-space only occasionally, when needed or convenient.

The most natural variables are the ray time
\smash{\tau}
and the ray energy
\smash{h}
. We explicitly derive the envelope equation in these variables; see section
7
for the general case. As usual, the coefficients therein are determined by the Weyl symbol of the dispersion operator, which is the same (within the assumed accuracy) as the corresponding symbol in
\smash{(x,k)}
. This means that integrating the envelope equation in MGO is just as easy as integrating the one in GO. Yet, because the derivatives in the MGO equation are taken along cutoff-free directions, the field remains non-singular. As we show, the standard Airy patterns that form in regions where conventional GO fails are successfully reproduced within MGO simply by remapping the field from the
\smash{\tau}
-space to the
\smash{x}
-space.

MGO will likely be useful, for example, for reduced modeling of the O–X conversion in inhomogeneous plasma near the critical density (work in progress). A generalization to quasioptical wave beams, which can be done mostly like in
(
Dodin et al. 2019
;
Yanagihara et al. 2019a
;
Yanagihara et al. 2019b
)
, is left to future work. Overall, MGO can replace GO for any practical purposes, because it better handles cutoffs and is a similar framework otherwise.

B.1

Strictly speaking, we are looking for an

\displaystyle\Phi=(H_{\text{{\sf y}}}(\text{{\sf y}})-h)s-\frac{\mathrm{i}s^{3}}{24}\,V^{3}(q)\,p^{\prime\prime}(q)-\frac{\mathrm{i}s^{5}}{1920}\,V^{5}(q)\,p^{\prime\prime\prime\prime}(q)+\ldots

(344)

Let us rewrite this as

\displaystyle\Phi\doteq s\Delta H+\frac{s^{3}}{24}\,\varepsilon_{2}+\frac{s^{5}}{1920}\,\varepsilon_{4}+\ldots,

(345)

where
\smash{\Delta H\doteq H_{\text{{\sf y}}}-h}
and
\smash{\varepsilon_{n}\doteq-V^{n+1}p^{(n)}}
. Note that the characteristic scales of
\smash{q}
and
\smash{p}
are both of order
\smash{R}
, and
\smash{V\sim v}
, so

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}\sim v^{3}/R\sim\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon_{4}\sim v^{5}/R^{3}\sim g\varepsilon,

(346)

where we used
\smash{g\sim\Omega^{2}\sim v^{2}/R^{2}}
. The characteristic values of
\smash{s}
that contribute to the integral (
243
) are of the order of those at which
\smash{\Phi}
is stationary, i.e. those that satisfy

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{s}\Phi=\Delta H+\frac{s^{2}}{8}\,\varepsilon_{2}+\frac{s^{4}}{384}\,\varepsilon_{4}+\ldots

(347)

Let us use this to estimate the characteristic scale of
\smash{\GAF}
with respect to
\smash{\Delta H}
. Assuming that the nonlinear, in
\smash{s}
, terms in (
345
) are not too large at values of
\smash{s}
that satisfy (
347
), one can rewrite (
243
) as follows:

\displaystyle\GAF(\Delta H,\ldots)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\bigg(1+\frac{s^{3}}{24}\,\varepsilon_{2}+\frac{s^{5}}{1920}\,\varepsilon_{4}+\ldots\bigg)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\Delta H}

(348a)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{24}\,\varepsilon_{2}\partial_{\Delta H}^{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{1920}\,\varepsilon_{4}\partial_{\Delta H}^{5}+\ldots\bigg)\frac{1}{2\upi}\int\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}s\Delta H}

(348b)

\displaystyle=\bigg(1+\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{24}\,\varepsilon_{2}\partial_{\Delta H}^{3}}_{\sim\varepsilon_{2}/\Delta H^{3}}-\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{i}}{1920}\,\varepsilon_{4}\partial_{\Delta H}^{5}}_{\sim\varepsilon_{4}/\Delta H^{5}}+\ldots\bigg)\delta(\Delta H).

(348c)

(The integral in (
348a
) can be recognised as a ‘catastrophe integral’ like those that emerge in theory of caustics; see
(
Lopez & Dodin 2022
, equation (22))
and references therein.) Then,
\smash{\GAF}
’s characteristic scale (roughly, the value of
\smash{\Delta H}
at which
\smash{\GAF}
has its first zero) is determined by

\displaystyle 1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{2}/\Delta H^{3})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{4}/\Delta H^{5})+\ldots=0,

(349)

and the values of
\smash{s}
that matter satisfy
\smash{s\sim\Delta H^{-1}}
, as seen from the derivation of (
348c
). The higher-order terms denoted by the ellipsis can be dealt with just like the third term, so they do not need to be considered separately and are ignored from now on. Then, there are two options.

First, suppose
\smash{\varepsilon_{2}\ll\varepsilon_{4}/\Delta H^{2}}
. Then, (
349
) yields
\smash{\Delta H\sim(g\varepsilon)^{1/5}}
, and
s\sim\Delta H^{-1}\sim(g\varepsilon)^{-1/5}
, which is also consistent with (
347
). The term
\smash{\propto\,\varepsilon_{2}}
is negligible when
\smash{s^{3}\varepsilon_{2}\ll 1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle 1\gg(g\varepsilon)^{-3/5}\varepsilon\sim(\varepsilon^{2/3}/g)^{3}\sim R^{4/5}.

(350)

Now, suppose
\smash{\varepsilon_{2}\gg\varepsilon_{4}/\Delta H^{2}}
. Then, (
349
) yields
\smash{\Delta H\sim\varepsilon^{1/3}}
, and
\smash{s\sim\Delta H^{-1}\sim\varepsilon^{-1/3}}
, which is also consistent with (
347
). The term
\smash{\propto\,\varepsilon_{4}}
is negligible when
\smash{s^{5}\varepsilon_{4}\ll 1}
, i.e.

\displaystyle 1\gg\varepsilon^{-5/3}g\varepsilon\sim g/\varepsilon^{2/3}\sim R^{-4/3}.

(351)

which is just the condition opposite to (
350
). In summary then,
\smash{\GAF}
is approximately equal to RAF when
\smash{R\gg 1}
.

Appendix D
Quantization condition for closed orbits

For closed orbits, the operators
\smash{\hat{\tau}}
and
\smash{\hat{h}}
can be introduced much like the angle–action operators in appendix
B
, i.e. with
\smash{\tau}
as a multi-valued function of
\smash{q}
and
\smash{p}
. Then, one can notice that, actually, multiple parts of the
\smash{\tau}
-space represent the same parts of the
\smash{q}
-space. By doing so, one effectively replaces the commonly used reflecting boundary conditions in the
\smash{q}
-space with periodic boundary conditions in the
\smash{\tau}
-space. This readily yields the familiar quantization rule for the discrete spectrum of closed orbits as follows.

First of all,
\smash{M_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}(q,\tau)}
satisfies

\displaystyle M_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}(0,\tau)\approx M_{0}\delta(\tau),

(352)

where
\smash{M_{0}\doteq[\tau^{\prime}(0)]^{-1/2}}
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{q}}(0)\approx M_{0}\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(0).

(353)

But one can also express
\smash{\psi_{\hat{q}}(0)}
through
\smash{M_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}}
after a full rotation of the ray around the closed orbit,
\smash{\tau=T}
. The corresponding MT is given by

\displaystyle M_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}(0,\tau)\approx-M_{0}\delta(\tau)\exp(\mathrm{i}\Theta_{\circlearrowright}),

(354)

where the sign change is due to the cumulative Maslov phase
\smash{\pm\upi}
that originates from the square root in (
260
) (section
4.3
)
47
47

47

Near each zero of said square root,
\smash{M}
can be approximated by the asymptotic form (
334
) (up to rescaling
\smash{\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathrm{d}\phi/\Omega}
). Then, the phase of
\smash{M_{\hat{\tau}\leftmapsto\hat{q}}}
increases by
\smash{\upi}
over a closed orbit at clockwise direction, so the phase of
\smash{M_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}}
decreases by
\smash{\upi}
. At counter-clockwise rotation, the phases have the opposite signs, but, in either case, the
\smash{M}
-wave changes sign.
and
\smash{\Theta_{\circlearrowright}}
is the phase change of
\smash{M_{\hat{q}\leftmapsto\hat{\tau}}}
around the orbit. Then,

\displaystyle\psi_{\hat{q}}(0)\approx M_{0}\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(T)\exp(-\mathrm{i}\upi+\mathrm{i}\Theta_{\circlearrowright}).

(355)

Since
\smash{\psi_{\hat{\tau}}}
is conserved on the ray, one has
\smash{\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(T)=\psi_{\hat{\tau}}(0)}
. Then, by comparing (
353
) with (
355
), one finds that
\smash{-\upi+\Theta_{\circlearrowright}}
must be equal to
\smash{2\upi n}
, where
\smash{n}
is integer; i.e.
\smash{\Theta_{\circlearrowright}=2\upi n+\upi}
. Also, from (
259
), one has
\smash{\Theta=\int(p\,\mathrm{d}q-h\,\mathrm{d}\tau)}
and
\smash{h=0}
on the ray, so

\displaystyle\Theta_{\circlearrowright}=\oint p\,\mathrm{d}q=\frac{1}{2}\oint\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf y}}\wedge\text{{\sf y}}=\frac{1}{2}\oint\mathrm{d}\text{{\sf z}}\wedge\text{{\sf z}}=\oint k\,\mathrm{d}x.

(356)

This leads to the well-known Einstein–Brillouin–Keller quantization rule
(
Stone 2005
)
,

\displaystyle\oint k\,\mathrm{d}x=2\upi\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right),

(357)

which is also known as the Bohr–Sommerfeld condition in the limit when
\smash{1/2}
is negligible compared to
\smash{n}
.

Appendix E
Dissipation power

Let us consider a wave with electric field
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}
in a linear medium with a conductivity operator
\smash{\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
. Such a wave induces the current density

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}}(t,\boldsymbol{x})=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}})(t,\boldsymbol{x})\equiv\int\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\,\underline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},t^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}(t^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})

(358)

and dissipates the energy

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{J}}}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}{}^{\intercal}\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}{}^{\dagger}\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{\rm H}\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}},

(359)

where
H
denotes the Hermitian part. (We treat
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}
as a column vector, so
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}{}^{\intercal}}
is a row vector, and
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}{}^{\dagger}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}{}^{\intercal}}
, because
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}
is real.) Using (
363
) together with (2.53a) from
(
Dodin 2022
)
, one can rewrite (
359
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{k}\,\tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}\star\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}).

(360)

Here,
\smash{\tr}
denotes the matrix trace,
\smash{\star}
is the Moyal product, and
\smash{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\equiv\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\omega,\boldsymbol{k})}
is the Weyl symbol of
\smash{\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\omega,\boldsymbol{k})\doteq\int\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega\tau-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}}\,\underline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\left(t+\frac{\tau}{2},\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{\boldsymbol{s}}{2},t-\frac{\tau}{2},\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{\boldsymbol{s}}{2}\right),

(361)

which satisfies

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t,\boldsymbol{x},-\omega,-\boldsymbol{k})=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\omega,\boldsymbol{k}),

(362)

because
\smash{\underline{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
is real by definition. Also,
\smash{\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}\equiv\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\omega,\boldsymbol{k})}
is the Wigner matrix of
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}\doteq\frac{1}{(2\upi)^{1+n}}\,\int\mathrm{d}\tau\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{s}+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}\left(t+\frac{\tau}{2},\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{\boldsymbol{s}}{2}\right)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}{}^{\dagger}\left(t-\frac{\tau}{2},\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{\boldsymbol{s}}{2}\right),

(363)

where
\smash{n\doteq\dim\boldsymbol{x}}
. By a known theorem
48
48

48

See, for example, Corollary 7 in Appendix B of
(
Ruiz 2017
)
.
, (
360
) can also be written as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}=\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{k}\,\tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}).

(364)

Assuming that
\smash{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
satisfies MGO applicability conditions for the characteristic phase-space scales, i.e. (cf. (
228
))

\displaystyle\Delta t\Delta\omega\gg 1,\qquad\Delta x\Delta k\gg 1,

(365)

one can replace (
364
) with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{E}=\int\underbrace{\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{k}\vphantom{\big\{}}_{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}\,\tr\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}\right),

(366)

where
\smash{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}}
is the local average of
\smash{\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}}
over a phase-space volume that is large compared to unity yet small compared to the scale of
\smash{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}}
. As shown in
(
Dodin 2022
)
, this averaging makes
\smash{\mathfrak{W}}
positive-semidefinite
and
a local property of a wave (as opposed to the original
\smash{\boldsymbol{W}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}}}
, which can have oscillating tails that can extend over a whole system). Then, one obtains the following phase-space density of
\smash{\mathfrak{E}}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{E}}{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}=\tr(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}).

(367)

To illustrate the connection of (
367
) with familiar results, let us consider a medium that is weakly inhomogeneous (if at all) in time and space, so that standard GO applies. Then, the wavefield can be locally represented as
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{E}}=\re(\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bar{\omega}t+\mathrm{i}\bar{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}})}
, with constant
\smash{\bar{\omega}}
,
\smash{\bar{\boldsymbol{k}}}
, and
\smash{\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}}
. This corresponds to
(
Dodin 2022
, Appendix A.2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{W}}=\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{\dagger}}{4}\,\delta(\omega-\bar{\omega})\,\delta(\boldsymbol{k}-\bar{\boldsymbol{k}})+\frac{(\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{\dagger})^{*}}{4}\,\delta(\omega+\bar{\omega})\,\delta(\boldsymbol{k}+\bar{\boldsymbol{k}}).

(368)

Then, the energy dissipated per unit spacetime volume
\smash{\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}
(‘dissipation power density’) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}\doteq\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathfrak{E}}{\mathrm{d}\Gamma}\,\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{k}=\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\bar{\omega},\bar{\boldsymbol{k}})\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}.

(369)

To the leading (zeroth) order in
\smash{(\Delta t\Delta\omega)^{-1}}
, one also has
\smash{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\rm H}=(\omega/4\upi)\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\rm A}}
, where
\smash{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\rm A}}
is the anti-Hermitian part of the symbol of the dielectric operator, or the dispersion tensor. Thus, (
369
) leads to the familiar formula
(
Stix 1992
, Sec. 4-2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{P}=\frac{\bar{\omega}}{8\upi}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\rm A}(t,\boldsymbol{x},\bar{\omega},\bar{\boldsymbol{k}})\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}.

(370)
