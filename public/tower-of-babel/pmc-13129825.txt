Silver–Histidine-Immobilized Laponite Nanoclays as Antibacterial Agents in Food Coatings for Controlling Bacterial Contamination on Green Grapes
Authors: Sutthida Sukaiem, Nutthaphol Khupsathianwong, Chanikan Sonklin, Tewarak Parnklang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, silver–histidine-immobilized Laponite nanodisks are developed for use as alternative antibacterial agents for food contact materials. The silver–histidine complex [Ag­(HHis)]+ is immobilized onto Laponite RD nanoparticles through an ion-exchange reaction. The resulting silver–histidine-immobilized Laponite nanodisks ([Ag­(HHis)]+LP) are characterized by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, transmission electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering, and zeta potential analysis to confirm the successful immobilization of the [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex onto the Laponite surfaces. The transparent antibacterial food coatings based on [Ag­(HHis)]+LP nanodisks and cellulose nanofibers (CNF) are subsequently developed. The antibacterial activities of the [Ag­(HHis)]+LP dispersion, the [Ag­(HHis)]+LP-CNF dispersion, and the [Ag­(HHis)]+LP-CNF nanocomposite films are evaluated against E. coli and S. aureus using minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) assays, Modify BSEN 1276:2019 method, and the JIS Z 2801:2010 method, respectively. The [Ag­(HHis)]+LP-CNF nanocomposite films exhibit favorable mechanical, optical, and barrier properties, and the nanocomposite coatings can be applied to green grape berries as a model fruit without altering their genuine physical appearance. Furthermore, the migration test of the coated green grape berries reveals that residual silver ions detected in the grape pulp are below the ICP-OES detection limit of 0.0076 ppm, and the coatings are fully removable with water. Notably, the optimal [Ag­(HHis)]+LP-CNF nanocomposite coating, formulated with an initial Ag+ concentration of 100 ppm, can effectively suppress bacterial contamination and extend the shelf life of green grape berries up to 21 days.

Introduction

Preserving fruit and vegetable quantity and quality after harvest poses a pressing global concern due to cross-contamination of spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms in postharvest handling, transportation, and storage processes. , Global postharvest losses of fresh produce, particularly fruits and vegetables, are estimated to be in the range of 37% to 55%, representing an economic value of approximately USD 750 billion annually. Foodborne diseases can also result in substantial medical expenses. A total of 47,780,778 cases of foodborne illness was reported, with an average cost of illness of USD 1,626 per case, amounting to an aggregated annual cost of illness of USD 77.7 billion. Therefore, reducing postharvest losses is crucial for promoting productivity and economic growth, enhancing food security and nutrition, improving the sustainability of the global food supply, and advancing the global community toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including No Poverty (SDG 1), Zero Hunger (SDG 2), Good Health and Well-Being (SDG 3), Responsible Consumption and Production (SDG 12), and Climate Action (SDG 13).

Controlling microorganisms, specifically spoilage bacteria, is one of the crucial factors for preserving fruit and vegetable quality. Various active coating formulations and packaging materials embedded with antibacterial agents have been developed to mitigate bacterial contamination in postharvest fresh fruits. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been widely employed as antimicrobial agents in food packaging due to their enhanced antibacterial activity, tunable physicochemical and biological properties, and capability to improve barrier and mechanical performance. , Antibacterial preservative composite film based on TEMPO-oxidized nanocellulose immobilized silver nanoparticles and chitosan exhibited good antibacterial properties against E. coli and S. aureus and the film was successfully applied to extend the freshness of cherry tomatoes and strawberries. Antibacterial nanocomposite films based on soluble soybean polysaccharide with in situ-generated silver nanoparticles exhibited improved thermal stability and ultraviolet light barrier properties, and the subsequently fabricated heat-sealed packaging pouches could be employed for extending the shelf life of green grapes for at least 21 days. Cellulose paper coated with bacteriocin-synthesized AgNPs was utilized for the storage of tomato fruit, maintaining a good appearance and firmness for at least 10 days. An active nanocomposite coating composed of poly­(vinyl alcohol), agar, and maltodextrin incorporated with AgNPs was applied to postharvest bananas (Musa acuminata) using a pressurized spraying method, effectively delaying banana ripening and extending their shelf life by 5 days at 25 °C. Electrospun poly­(vinyl alcohol) nanofibers incorporated with AgNPs exhibited antibacterial activity against various strains, including B. subtilis, S. aureus, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa and they could be utilized as a coating material for extending the shelf life of C. limon (lemon) fruits during 10 days of storage. Despite the superior antibacterial properties and several advantages of AgNPs, their intrinsic localized surface plasmon resonances − may inevitably alter the genuine color appearance of the base packaging materials. , The utilization of ionized silver or silver complexes , as antibacterial agents in food contact materials represents a promising alternative for mitigating color deterioration while maintaining effective antibacterial activity.

To alleviate potential health risks and comply with the limit on silver migration from packaging established by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), ionized silver and silver complexes must be encapsulated within inert carriers to minimize exposure and enable controlled release into food matrices. Nanoclays are potential candidates for use as encapsulating materials for silver-based species because of their richness in coordination chemistry, physical states, confinement effects emerging from the arrangement of their layered crystalline structures, and controlled release properties. Particularly, Laponite clay was demonstrated as an efficient silver ion storage and exhibited slow and sustained release of silver ions when compared to silver nitrate. Laponite is a synthetic smectite clay of the hectorite type with the empirical formula Na0.7[(Si8Mg5.5Li0.3)­O20(OH)4]. Laponite is nontoxic and has been classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS). It also exhibits a consistent chemical composition and superior purity compared to natural clays. Individual Laponite particles exhibit a disk geometry with an approximate diameter of 25–30 nm and a thickness of 0.92 nm. The unit cell of the Laponite particles features a sandwich structure, i.e., each sheet of six octahedrally coordinated magnesium ions is enclosed between two silicate layers, in which four tetrahedrally coordinated silicon atoms are balanced by 20 oxygen atoms and four hydroxyl groups. A charge imbalance created by the substitution of Mg2+ ions with Li+ cations within the octahedral sheet renders the planar surface of individual Laponite disks negatively charged, which is electrostatically neutralized by intercalated Na+ ions. In aqueous dispersions, the Na+ ions in Laponite are exchangeable, with a cation exchange capacity of about 50 mmol/100 g for Laponite RD, and the clay nanodisks with negatively charged surfaces are subsequently formed. Aqueous dispersions of Laponite can attain various physical states, including liquid, sol, gel, glass, and phase separation, depending on Laponite mass concentration, environmental ionic strength, and polymer addition. − Heterogeneous charge attraction, van der Waals attractive forces, and electrical double-layer repulsive forces play important roles in determining the time-dependent aging behavior and temporal structural rearrangement toward a minimum energy state of Laponite platelets. ,

Target chemical species could be protected, encapsulated, and transported by Laponite-based materials in various ways, including adsorption on the exfoliated Laponite surfaces, covalent attachment to the individual Laponite disk edges, intercalation within the Laponite crystal stacks, and encapsulation inside the intercrystal spaces formed within the microstructure of Laponite gel. , Drug molecules, such as amoxicillin, itraconazole, pilocarpine, and doxorubicin, could be encapsulated in the Laponite interlayer space by interfacial boundary ion exchange reaction with intercalated sodium ions. Aqueous Laponite nanodisk dispersions could be employed as a protective colloid and a scaffold for metallic nanoparticles. Aihara and coworkers demonstrated the utilization of cooperative binding between surface charges of layered Laponite clay and intercalated metal ions in the preparation of gold and silver nanoparticles with average diameters of 1.4–2.4 nm and 3.0–3.6 nm, respectively. Silver nanosandwiches could be fabricated on a Laponite nanodisk scaffold by adsorbing activated cationic silver onto an approximately 0.1% (w/v) aqueous clay dispersion and reducing it in situ to form approximately 1 nm thick silver plates on each side of the clay nanodisk. Not only did Laponite dispersion play a role as the protective colloid, but Huang and Yang also discovered that the clay dispersion assisted in the photoreduction process of AgNPs. Laponite immobilized AgNPs could be utilized as antibacterial agents in active food packaging, providing good antibacterial activity while maintaining low cytotoxicity and preventing excessive release of AgNPs. − Apart from metallic nanoparticles, Laponite also served as a good metal ion carrier. N. Li and coworkers employed a cationic exchange method to synthesize metal ion-exchanged Laponite (M/Lap) where M was cationic silver (Ag+), copper (Cu2+), and zinc (Zn2+). Metal ion-exchanged Laponite was subsequently incorporated into polyamide through interfacial polymerization, and the resulting thin-film nanocomposite membranes exhibited good antibiofouling ability.

Therefore, this study aims to develop a colorless and transparent antibacterial agent based on Laponite and silver complexes suitable for application in food contact materials. Laponite nanodisks were modified with a silver–histidine complex through ion exchange reactions. The silver–histidine complex was selected for this study because of its strong coordination with the histidine ligand, which helps prevent untimely deactivation of silver ions, along with its notable antimicrobial activity and low acute toxicity. Additionally, the silver–histidine complex possesses the potential to undergo ion exchange reactions, followed by subsequent intercalation within nanoclay structures. , The resulting silver–histidine-modified Laponite nanodisks were subsequently employed as active antibacterial agents in cellulose-nanofiber-based food coating materials. Even though specific regulations regarding the utilization of cellulose nanofibers as food contact materials have not been established, available evidence indicates that cellulose nanofibers pose no immediate safety concerns. , The antibacterial activity of both silver–histidine-modified Laponite nanodisks and the corresponding nanocomposite coatings was subsequently evaluated. The developed nanocomposite coatings were applied to prevent bacterial contamination in green grape berries as a model fruit. The migration of silver ions into green grape matrices was also assessed. Finally, the performance of the developed nanocomposite coatings to extend the shelf life of green grapes was investigated.

Methods

Materials and Chemicals

Laponite RD was supplied by BYK Additives & Instruments Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). Silver nitrate (AgNO3) (Fisher Chemical, 99.9%, AR grade) was purchased from Lab Valley (Bangkok, Thailand). l-Histidine (AR grade) (Acros Organics, 98%, AR grade) was obtained from Apex Chemicals Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). Ethanol (AR grade) was obtained from RCI Labscan Limited (Samutsakorn, Thailand). Carboxymethylated cellulose nanofiber (CNFs) with a diameter of 4–10 nm, a length of 1–3 μm, and a carboxyl content of 2.5 mmol g–1 was purchased from Nanografi Nano Technology (Ankara, Turkey). Glycerin (99.5%, USP/Food grade) was obtained from Chanjao Longevity Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). Tween 80 (Polysorbate 80) was obtained from Krungthepchemi Co., Ltd. (Bangkok, Thailand). Nitric acid (65%, AR grade), glacial acetic acid (AR grade), and absolute ethanol (AR grade) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). All chemicals were used as received without further purification. Dialysis tubing (Innovating Science, IS13029, 32 mm × 20.4 mm, MWCO = 12–14 kDa), fabricated from regenerated cellulose derived from cotton linters, was purchased from Aldon Corporation (New York, United States). Ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was produced by a Sartorius arium pro water system (Sartorius Lab Instruments GmbH & Co. KG, Goettingen, Germany).

Preparation of Silver–Histidine-Modified Laponite Nanoparticles

Laponite RD nanoparticles (0.0–3.0 g) were dispersed in 150 mL of ultrapure water while being vigorously agitated with a high-speed homogenizer at 10,000 rpm for at least 30 min. A silver–histidine complex solution was prepared by mixing a 25 mM l-histidine solution with a 50 mM silver nitrate solution at a volume ratio of 2:1. Subsequently, 10 mL of ultrapure water was added to the mixture and stirred at 50 °C for 30 min. The resulting silver complex solution is designated as [Ag­(HHis)]+. To modify the Laponite RD nanodisks with the [Ag­(HHis)]+ solution, the colloidal Laponite RD dispersion was added to [Ag­(HHis)]+ dropwise. The modification process was carried out at 50 °C. The mixed solution was continuously stirred for 24 h to facilitate the ion exchange process until equilibrium was reached. The resulting silver–histidine-modified Laponite nanodisks, [Ag­(HHis)]+LP, were separated by ultracentrifugation using a VWR Micro Star 30R laboratory centrifuge (VWR International GmbH, Darmstadt, Germany) at 6,000 rpm for 30 min. The centrifugate was washed with ethanol through ultracentrifugation at 6,000 rpm for 30 min to eliminate excess Ag+. The final products are designated as [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP, corresponding to the employed colloidal Laponite RD dispersion concentrations of 0.5%, 1.0%, and 2.0% (w/v), respectively. The Ag­(HHis)]+-modified Laponite RD products were stored in the dark under ambient conditions until use.

Fabrication of Silver–Histidine-Modified Laponite–Cellulose Nanofiber Nanocomposite Films

Typically, CNFs in the fine white powder form were dispersed in 100 mL of ultrapure water while stirring for 30 min to obtain a final concentration of 1.00% (w/v). Then, a plasticizer, i.e., glycerol, was added to enhance the flexibility of the formed film. Tween 80 was also added to the prepared film-forming solution to facilitate the dispersion of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP. The concentrations of glycerol and Tween 80 in the solution were 2.0% (w/v) and 0.2% (w/v), respectively. Laponite RD nanoparticles were added and optimized in the range of 0.025–1.00% (w/v). The CNF mixture was continuously stirred for another 30 min. Next, purified [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP was added to the CNF mixture. To facilitate the dispersion of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP in the CNF mixture, the solution was initially stirred with a magnetic stirrer at 200 rpm for 5 min, followed by ultrasonication with a Sonics VCX750 ultrasonic processor (Sonics & Materials, Inc., Newtown, USA) at a frequency of 20 kHz for another 5 min. The final nanocomposite film-forming dispersion was transparent and homogeneous. [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF film-forming dispersions containing Ag+ concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5.0, 10.0, 25.0, and 100.0 ppm are denoted as CL, CL2.5, CL5.0, CL10, CL25, and CL100, respectively.

The film-forming ability of the prepared dispersions was investigated using a solution-casting method on a Petri dish substrate with a diameter of 9 cm. The drying process was performed under ambient conditions for 24 h. The resulting nanocomposite film was stored in a desiccator. The nanocomposite films prepared with [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF film-forming dispersions containing Ag+ concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5.0, 10.0, 25.0, 50.0, and 100.0 ppm are denoted as f-CL, f-CL10, f-CL25, f-CL50, and f-CL100, respectively. The cellulose nanofiber film prepared from the CNF mixture is designated as f-CNF.

Ag+ Release of Silver–Histidine-Modified Laponite–Cellulose Nanofiber Nanocomposite Films

Silver ion release from [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films (2 × 2 cm2) containing Ag+ concentrations of 100 ppm (f-CL100) was evaluated. The cut nanocomposite films were placed in a dialysis bag containing 10 mL of 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) (HiMedia Laboratories Private Limited, Maharashtra, India), 3% (w/v) acetic acid, or 10% (v/v) ethanol. Acetic and ethanolic solutions were employed as food simulants. Each dialysis bag was subsequently immersed in 150 mL of 10 mM Tris-HCl under continuous stirring. At predetermined intervals, 1 mL of the external solution was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh Tris-HCl buffer to maintain the initial conditions. The silver content of the collected samples was then quantified by using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy.

Pretreatment of Model Fresh Fruits

Green grapes (Vitis vinifera L., Muscat variety) exhibiting no visible bruising or surface defects were procured from a local market and employed as model fruits. The grapes were immersed in a 100-ppm sodium hypochlorite solution for 15 min , to eliminate native surface microorganisms, thoroughly rinsed with distilled water to remove residual chemicals, and subsequently air-dried under ambient conditions for 30 min.

Residual bacterial contamination on as-obtained grapes, as-obtained grapes rinsed with ultrapure water, and pretreated grapes was analyzed. Bacterial enumeration was performed by the Food Research and Testing Laboratory (FRTL), Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University, following the U.S. FDA Bacteriological Analytical Manual (BAM, Online)2001, Chapter 3 for aerobic plate count; 2020, Chapter 4 for E. coli; and 2016, Chapter 12 for S. aureus. The total aerobic bacterial count on the as-obtained grapes, as-obtained grapes rinsed with ultrapure water, and the pretreated grapes was approximately 20–40 CFU g–1, 35–100 CFU g–1, and 10 CFU g–1, respectively. The levels of residual E. coli and S. aureus on the as-obtained grapes, as-obtained grapes rinsed with ultrapure water, and pretreated grapes were each below 10 CFU g–1. The determined levels of microorganisms in the pretreated fruits are considered “satisfactory”, according to Commission Regulation (EC) No. 2073/2005 of 15 November 2005 on microbiological criteria for foodstuffs. The as-obtained grapes and pretreated grapes were subsequently employed for further experimental investigations.

Ag+ Migration Testing in Fruits

Silver ion migration from the nanocomposite coatings into fresh fruit matrices was also investigated. Pretreated grapes were immersion-coated with [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF at Ag+ concentrations of 25 and 100 ppm, designated as Grape-CL25 and Grape-CL100, respectively. The coating process was performed by immersing the pretreated grapes in the coating solution for 30 s, followed by drying at room temperature for 2 h. The coated grapes were stored under ambient conditions (67 ± 1% RH and 31 ± 1 °C) for 1 and 7 days. After the specified storage intervals, the grape skins were peeled off, and the pulps (≈10 g) were homogenized and immersed in 20 mL of ultrapure water to extract the migrated ionic silver species. The silver content of the extracted solutions was subsequently quantified by using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy.

Characterization

The silver content in [Ag­(HHis)]+-modified Laponite nanodisks, the collected solutions from the silver ion release experiments, and the extracted solutions from the migration tests were quantified using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES) model iCAP PRO XPS instrument (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, United States). The ICP-OES limit of detection, determined from the calibration slope (f­(x) = 297.6806x + 0.5098; R2 = 0.9999) and the standard deviation of blank measurements (LOD = 3σ/slope) was 0.0076 ppm. Prior to analysis, 0.1 g portions of dried [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP samples were digested in 10 mL of 2% (w/v) nitric acid, and the final volume was adjusted to 100 mL with ultrapure water. Collected samples from the silver ion release experiments were diluted with 0.1 M HNO3 to a final volume of 10.0 mL. Extracted solutions from the migration test were filtered and diluted with 20 mL of 0.1 M HNO3 and subsequently analyzed to determine residual silver ions in grape pulp.

The crystallinity of the dried [Ag­(HHis)]+LP powders was determined by X-ray diffraction. X-ray diffractograms were obtained using a Bruker AXS Model D8 Discover X-ray diffractometer (Bruker AXS GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany), operated at 40 kV and 40 mA with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å). The dried specimens were continuously scanned over a 2θ range from 1° to 90° at a step size of 0.0200° and a time per step of 1.0 s.

The morphology of Laponite RD nanoparticles and [Ag­(HHis)]+LP nanodisks was examined using a JEOL JEM-2100 analytical transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. The samples were diluted with deionized water and dispersed using an Elmasonic P 60 H ultrasonicator (Elma Schmidbauer GmbH, Singen, Germany) at 37 kHz and 150 W for 10 min. Before TEM analysis, the diluted suspension was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and dried in a desiccator for a minimum of 24 h. The elemental composition of [Ag­(HHis)]+LP was further analyzed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using an Oxford Instruments INCAx-Act model 51-ADD0001 EDS system (LabX, Ottawa, Canada) coupled to the transmission electron microscope.

Chemical functional groups of Laponite RD nanoparticles, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks, and l-histidine were acquired by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. The [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP powders were prepared by evaporating the purified [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP gel at 50 °C in an air-circulated oven until complete moisture removal was achieved. A Nicolet iS5 FT-IR spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Massachusetts, United States), equipped with a fast-recovery deuterated triglycine sulfate (DTGS) detector, was utilized for the acquisition of ATR FT-IR spectra. A sampling probe was an iD5 single-bounce ATR accessory with a laminate-diamond crystal. For spectral acquisition, the dried specimen was gradually pressed against the flat diamond crystal IRE with a sampling diameter of 1.5 mm until the maximum allowable pressure was obtained. All ATR FT-IR spectra were collected at a resolution of 4 cm–1 with 32 coaddition scans.

Zeta potentials and hydrodynamic diameters of CNFs were measured using a Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern Panalytical Ltd., Worcestershire, United Kingdom). A constant holding temperature of 25.0 ± 0.2 °C was maintained throughout the investigation. The CNF sample, in the form of an aqueous dispersion with a mass concentration of 0.1–0.2% (w/v), was ultrasonicated at 37 kHz with a power output of 150 W for 10 min prior to the analysis.

The thickness of CNF, Laponite-modified CNF, and [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films was measured at five different positions using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo (Thailand) Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) with a measurement range of 0–25 mm and a resolution of 0.001 mm.

The appearance of the films was evaluated by capturing digital images of the coated grape samples. Grapes were immersion-coated with CNF, CL, CL10, CL25, and CL100 coating dispersions, designated as Grape-CNF, Grape-CL, Grape-CL10, Grape-CL25, and Grape-CL100, respectively. For optical characterization, film specimens (20 mm × 20 mm) were mounted in a UV/vis spectrophotometer cell, and absorbance was recorded from 200 to 800 nm using a SPECORD 210 PLUS UV–visible Spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena GmbH + Co. KG, Jena, Germany). The opacity of CNF, Laponite-modified CNF, and [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films was determined as A600/thickness (mm), where A600 is the absorbance at 600 nm.

The color of the fabricated nanocomposite films and coated grapes was evaluated using a HunterLab ColorFlex EZ 45°/0° spectrophotometer (Hunter Associates Laboratory, Inc., Virginia, United States of America). Prior to analysis, the instrument was calibrated in reflectance mode with the exclusion of specular reflection, utilizing a Reflectance Black Glass Standard (A41-1017-037 Rev. A) and an Instrument White Tile Standard (A41-1-14-635 Rev. B; CFWS3801). Color measurements were conducted based on the CIE L*a*b* color scale under D65 (normal daylight) illumination and a 10° standard observer angle. In this color space, L* represents the lightness value ranging from 0 (black) to 100 (white), a* denotes the color spectrum from green (−) to red (+), and b* indicates the color spectrum from blue (−) to yellow (+). Color measurements were obtained from three independent replicates (n = 3) and reported as the mean ± standard deviation. The CIE 1976 or CIELAB color difference ΔEab* , was calculated using the following equation: 1 ΔEab*=(ΔL*)2+(Δa*)2+(Δb*)2

For the fabricated nanocomposite films, the color difference was determined relative to the pristine CNF film with L* = 36.443, a* = 0.197, and b* = – 0.423. For the coated grapes, the color difference was determined relative to the control uncoated grape with L* = 34.823, a* = – 3.717, and b* = 8.202.

The morphology of the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films was examined using a Quanta 250 FEG analytical field-emission scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Before micrograph acquisition, all samples were coated with a thin gold layer using a JEOL JFC-1200 ion sputtering device (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

The tensile strength (MPa) and elongation at break (%) of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films were measured through tensile testing using a texture analyzer (Stable Microsystems Ltd., Surrey, UK). Rectangular specimens with dimensions of 10 mm × 50 mm were mounted on the grips with an initial gauge length of 30 mm and subjected to uniaxial extension at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/s.

Water vapor permeability (WVP) was determined gravimetrically in accordance with the ASTM E96 standard. An [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite film was cut and mounted onto glass cups containing silica gel (0% RH). The cups were then placed in a desiccator containing distilled water (100% RH). Changes in cup weight were recorded at predetermined time intervals (24, 48, 72, and 96 h). The slopes of the resulting plots were obtained by linear regression analysis (r2 > 0.99). WVP was subsequently calculated using eq : 2 WVP=ΔgΔt(xAΔP) where Δg/Δt represents the rate of the weight change (g/h), x is the sample thickness (mm), A is the permeation area (0.000121 m2), and ΔP denotes the partial pressure difference across the film (4.523 kPa at 31 °C).

Antibacterial Activity Evaluation

The antibacterial activity of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP dispersions was assessed by determining its minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) against S. aureus (ATCC 10832) and E. coli (BL21­(DE3) ECOS sonic), which represent Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively. The antibacterial assays were conducted at the Department of Biotechnology, Faculty of Applied Science, King Mongkut’s University of Technology North Bangkok. A suspension of the tested microorganisms (107 CFU mL–1) was prepared, and 5.00 μL mL–1 of this suspension was mixed with 0.05 mg mL–1 of [Ag­(HHis)]+LP in Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB). The microplate was then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, 30 μL of resazurin solution was added to each well, followed by further incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. After this period, the wells were examined for colorimetric change. A blue color indicated the absence of bacterial growth, whereas a pink or purple color signified the presence of viable cells. These observations were used to determine the MIC. To determine the MBC, the concentration corresponding to the MIC, along with the next two higher concentrations showing no visible growth, was subcultured onto Petri dishes containing TSB agar. Additionally, 20 μL aliquots from each negative well (without added resazurin) were transferred onto Mueller-Hinton Agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The MBC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no bacterial colonies were observed following incubation. An additional set of MIC experiments to evaluate [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP against E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 6538P), including an appropriate control consisting of media supplemented with [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP was also conducted by the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University.

The antibacterial activity against S. aureus (ATCC 6538P) and E. coli (ATCC 25922) of the cellulose nanofiber (CNF), CL, CL2.5, CL5.0, CL10, CL25, and CL100 dispersions was assessed using the modified BS EN 1276:2019 method. The antibacterial evaluation was provided by the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a final concentration of 106 CFU mL–1. An inoculum aliquot of 5 mL was mixed with an equal volume (5 mL) of the test formulations. The mixtures were initially incubated at 37 °C under controlled conditions of 90% relative humidity and absence of light for 24 h. Following incubation, the number of surviving bacterial colonies was enumerated. The antibacterial test was independently performed in triplicate, and the results are described as the average value of viable counts in log10 CFU mL–1 and the corresponding standard deviation. The bacterial reduction percentage (% Reduction) was calculated according to the following equation: 3 % Reduction=A−BA×100 where A and B represent the average viable counts in log10 CFU mL–1 at 0 and 24 h, respectively.

The antimicrobial activity (R) was calculated using the following equation: 4 R=Ut−At where Ut is the average number of bacterial colonies on plates in log10 CFU mL–1 of control test pieces after 24 h, and At is the average number of bacterial colonies on plates in log10 CFU mL–1 of antimicrobial test pieces after 24 h.

The antibacterial activity against S. aureus (ATCC 6538P) and E. coli (ATCC 25922) of the cellulose nanofiber film (f-CNF), the Laponite-CNF nanocomposite film (f-CL), and the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films prepared from [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF dispersions with initial Ag+ concentrations of 10 ppm (f-CL10), 25 ppm (f-CL25), 50 ppm (f-CL50), and 100 ppm (f-CL100) was assessed using the JIS Z 2801:2010 method. The film specimens were prepared by cutting portions measuring 5 cm × 5 cm. The antibacterial evaluation was provided by the Department of Microbiology, Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University. Bacterial suspensions were adjusted to a final concentration of 106 CFU mL–1. An inoculum aliquot of 0.4 mL was spread on a square tested film. The inoculated film specimens were initially incubated at 37 °C under controlled conditions of 90% relative humidity and absence of light for 24 h. Following incubation, the number of surviving bacterial colonies was enumerated. The antibacterial test was independently conducted in triplicate, and the results are reported as the average value of viable counts in log10 CFU mL–1 and the corresponding standard deviation. The % reduction and the antimicrobial activity (R) for each tested film were calculated using eqs and , respectively.

Practical Applications of Silver–Histidine-Modified Laponite–Cellulose Nanofiber Nanocomposite Coatings on Model Fruits

Pretreated grape samples were divided into four groups (200 g each): an uncoated control group and three coated groups. The coated samples were prepared by immersion in an [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coating dispersion containing initial Ag+ concentrations of 10, 25, and 100 ppm. The coating process was performed by immersing the pretreated grapes in the coating solution for 30 s, followed by drying at room temperature for 2 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of bacterial suspension (≈106 CFU mL–1) of E. coli (BL21­(DE3) ECOS sonic) or S. aureus (ATCC 10832) was surface-inoculated onto the coated grapes. The control and coated grapes surface-inoculated with E. coli are designated as Control-E and Grape-CL10E, Grape-CL25E, and Grape-CL100E, corresponding to the initial Ag+ concentrations in the coating dispersions. Similarly, the control and coated grapes surface-inoculated with S. aureus are designated as Control-S and Grape-CL10S, Grape-CL25S, and Grape-CL100S. All samples were stored at 30 °C for 7, 14, and 21 days.

Microbiological analyses of E. coli and S. aureus colonies on the control and coated grapes surface-inoculated with the corresponding bacteria were conducted after each specified storage period. A total of 50 g of control or coated grape samples were homogenized with 450 mL of Butterfield’s phosphate-buffered dilution water using a Stomacher 400 Circulator (Seward, London, U.K.), operated at 230 rpm for 2 min. The resulting suspensions were serially diluted with a sterile solution and subsequently analyzed for E. coli and S. aureus counts. Bacterial enumeration was performed by the Food Research and Testing Laboratory (FRTL), Faculty of Science, Chulalongkorn University, following the U.S. FDA Bacteriological Analytical Manual (BAM, Online)2020, Chapter 4 for E. coli and 2016, Chapter 12 for S. aureus.

Fruit Storage Study

Freshly obtained Muscat grapes were rinsed with ultrapure water to remove small debris and subsequently air-dried at room temperature for 30 min. The grapes were then immersion-coated with CNF, CL, CL10, CL25, and CL100 coating dispersions, designated as Grape-CNF, Grape-CL, Grape-CL10, Grape-CL25, and Grape-CL100, respectively. The control and coated grapes were separately placed in enclosed aseptic plastic containers and stored in a temperature-controlled chamber at 30 ± 1 °C and 68 ± 1% RH. Physical changes in the grapes were evaluated after 7, 14, and 21 days of storage. Weight loss (%) of the stored grapes was evaluated after 7, 14, and 21 days of storage.

Statistical Analysis

The tensile strength, elongation at break, water vapor permeability of CNF, Laponite–CNF, and [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films, as well as the weight loss (%) of the stored grapes, were measured and reported as mean ± standard deviation from three independent replicates. The data were subsequently analyzed to evaluate the effects of nanoclay incorporation using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc comparison test , in IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 at a 95% confidence level (p < 0.05) to assess statistical significance.

Results and Discussion

The modification of Laponite RD nanoparticles with [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes was accomplished using an ion-exchange method. The resulting [Ag­(HHis)]+LP products are shown in Figure . The [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP products appeared as clear, colorless gels following the modification. After 45 days of storage in the dark, the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP products retained their clear, colorless gel form. In contrast, [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP exhibited yellow discoloration, indicating inadequate long-term storage stability.

An inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was employed to quantify the amount of silver complexes incorporated into the Laponite particle structure. The measured total Ag+ contents for the [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP products were 10.31 ± 1.10, 14.87 ± 0.67, and 12.52 ± 1.19 mg Ag+, respectively (Figure ). These results preliminarily imply a successful intercalation of silver–histidine complexes into the Laponite structure.

A bar chart presenting the total Ag+ contents quantified by ICP-OES in Laponite nanodisks modified with [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex ions at Laponite concentrations of 0.5% (w/v) ([Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP), 1.0% (w/v) ([Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP), and 2.0% (w/v) ([Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP).

Laponite RD nanoparticles, in the form of nanodisks with a diameter of 25 nm and a thickness of 0.92 nm, were disintegrated in an aqueous dispersion with a concomitant release of interlayer sodium cations (Na+). The hydrodynamic diameter of Laponite RD, determined from dynamic light scattering, was 28.43 ± 0.78 nm (Table S1). The cation exchange capacity, i.e., CEC, of Laponite RD nanoclays was previously reported to be 53 mequiv/100 g. The [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes could be adsorbed onto the negatively charged surfaces of dispersed Laponite RD nanodisks through electrostatic interactions. The zeta potential of pristine Laponite RD (−29.75 ± 1.24 mV) increased to −14.59 ± 0.96, −17.69 ± 2.00, and −16.50 ± 2.35 mV for the purified [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP nanodisks, respectively (Table S1). This shift toward less negative zeta potential values indicates the successful immobilization of [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes onto the planar surfaces of Laponite RD. The positively charged edges of the Laponite RD nanodisks could also enhance the penetration of [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes into the interlayer space of Laponite RD nanodisks. Additionally, the hydrodynamic diameter of Laponite RD (28.43 ± 0.78 nm) increased to 102.20 ± 20.42, 107.50 ± 1.95, and 99.60 ± 13.86 nm for the purified [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP nanodisks, respectively, suggesting a self-assembly or aggregation of the [Ag­(HHis)]+-immobilized Laponite nanodisks (Table S1). This reformation of Laponite RD nanodisks might function as [Ag­(HHis)] + storage.

The theoretical molar ratios of [Ag+]:CEC of Laponite RD employed in this work for [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP were calculated to be 2.5:1, 1.26:1, and 0.63:1, respectively (Table S2). On the other hand, the experimental molar ratios of [Ag+]:CEC of Laponite RD for [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP, and [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP were determined to be 1.20:1, 0.87:1, and 0.36:1, respectively. The experimental molar ratio of [Ag+]:CEC of Laponite RD for the [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP sample suggests that unbound [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes that were not immobilized on the Laponite RD nanodisks, might remain in the final gel. This finding corroborates the observed discoloration of the [Ag­(HHis)]+0.5LP gel after 45 days of storage, potentially due to the gradual formation of silver nanoparticles via the reduction of free silver ions by l-histidine under alkaline conditions. In contrast, the experimental molar ratio of [Ag+]:CEC of Laponite RD for the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP sample closely matched the theoretical value. This result indicates the efficient intercalation of [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes into the Laponite structure with minimal processing loss. Conversely, the experimental molar ratio of [Ag+]:CEC of Laponite RD for the [Ag­(HHis)]+2.0LP sample was lower than 0.5, signifying substantial Ag+ loss during processing. Therefore, the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP sample was selected for subsequent investigations.

After modification with [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex ions, the diffraction peaks of Laponite RD nanodisks become sharper and exhibit increased intensity, indicating a more ordered structure. Notably, no diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic silver at 2θ of 38.19°, 46.18°, and 67.44°, which are attributed to (111), (200), and (220) planes of the face-centered cubic structure, respectively, were detected in the [Ag­(HHis)]+LP samples. This indicates a uniform distribution of [Ag­(HHis)]+ ions on the surfaces of the Laponite nanoparticles without the formation of metallic silver crystals during the modification process. Additionally, all silver–histidine-modified Laponite nanodisk samples exhibited intense diffraction peaks for the (001) diffraction planes and displayed quite similar XRD patterns to that of pristine Laponite RD nanoclays, indicating that the layered structure of Laponite nanodisks was still preserved after the intercalation of [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex ions.

The d-spacing of Laponite RD nanoparticles, calculated from the (001) diffraction plane, i.e., d001, at a 2θ of 6.54°, exhibited a value of 13.51 Å (Table ), indicating that Laponite RD nanoclays were in the hydrated state. Upon modification of 1.0% and 1.5% (w/v) Laponite RD with an equivalent concentration of [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex ions, the calculated d001 values increased to 14.30 and 14.14 Å, respectively. Additionally, the diffraction angles 2θ exhibited a shift to lower values of 6.18° and 6.24° for [Ag­(HHis)]+ 0.5LP and [Ag­(HHis)]+ 1.0LP, respectively. The observed shift of the (001) diffraction peak toward lower 2θ values corroborates an expansion in the d-spacing of the Laponite structure. , After subtracting the intrinsic Laponite RD layer thickness of 0.96 nm, the interlayer spacings of [Ag­(HHis)]+ 0.5LP and [Ag­(HHis)]+ 1.0LP were determined to be 0.47 and 0.45 nm, respectively. These findings partially confirm the successful intercalation of [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex ions into the Laponite RD structure via sodium ion exchange. On the other hand, [Ag­(HHis)]+ 2.0LP displayed d001 and 2θ values at the (001) diffraction plane that were comparable to those of the unmodified Laponite nanoparticles, suggesting diminished intercalation efficiency at higher Laponite concentrations at the equivalent concentration of [Ag­(HHis)]+. This may be attributed to the formation of a three-dimensional “house of cards” arrangement, leading to a reduction in the accessible surface area for ion exchange. Additionally, at higher Laponite concentrations, a greater number of adsorption sites on the Laponite nanodisks became available for [Ag­(HHis)]+ ions, leading to less densely packed and more uniformly distributed adsorption of these ions. Similar behavior was observed for silver nanoparticle-loaded Laponite RD nanoclay, in which the positively charged silver nanoparticles were highly distributed across the Laponite RD surfaces, leading to an invariable lattice parameter of the Laponite RD nanodisks following nanoparticle loading.

Laponite RD nanoparticles were modified with [Ag­(HHis)]+ complex ions to serve as a colorless antibacterial agent. The antimicrobial activity of [Ag­(HHis)]+ 1.0LP against E. coli (Gram-negative bacteria) and S. aureus (Gram-positive bacteria), which are representative bacterial strains commonly found on fruits, ,, was evaluated by determining the MIC and MBC, as summarized in Table . E. coli is an important cause of severe pediatric diarrhea in developing countries, as well as the most common cause of travelers’ diarrhea. Food-borne enterotoxin produced by S. aureus is a leading cause of food poisoning in humans. The MIC values against E. coli (BL21­(DE3) ECOS sonic) and S. aureus (ATCC 10832) were 6.25 and 12.5 ppm, respectively, while the corresponding MBC values were 25 and 50 ppm, respectively. Additionally, the MIC values against E. coli (ATCC 25922) and S. aureus (ATCC 6538P) were both 6.25 ppm. The MIC and MBC values of [Ag­(HHis)]+ 1.0LP were within the lower-intermediate range when compared with various clay minerals modified with silver nanoparticles, − ionic silver species, , and silver complexes (Table S3). Because Laponite itself has been reported to lack intrinsic antibacterial properties, the observed antimicrobial activity is primarily attributed to the immobilized [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes. The MIC and MBC results further indicate that [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP exhibits greater antibacterial activity against E. coli than against S. aureus. This observation is consistent with a previous study in which a antibacterial filter membrane was fabricated using Laponite RD as a metal-ion (Ag+, Cu2+, and Zn2+) carrier, demonstrating higher efficacy against E. coli compared with S. aureus. The comparable MIC values observed for the two E. coli strains, together with the one-dilution-step difference in MIC between the two S. aureus strains examined in the present study, indicate only a modest degree of strain-dependent antibacterial activity of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP against both E. coli and S. aureus. This limited interstrain variation may be partly attributable to the immobilization of ionic [Ag­(HHis)]+ onto Laponite RD nanodisks, rather than the utilization of silver nanoparticles, which have previously been shown to exhibit more pronounced size- and strain-dependent antibacterial effects. Furthermore, the 24-h contact period employed in this study may have further minimized apparent differences in susceptibility among the tested strains. ,

Unlike Laponite nanodisks possessing intrinsic negative surface charges, the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-modified Laponite nanodisks were immobilized with [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes on their surfaces, which may facilitate stronger electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged carboxymethyl groups grafted onto the CNFs (Table S4). These interactions are presumed to induce localized CNF aggregation, thereby further reducing the opacity of the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films. However, the opacity value of the f-CL100 film displayed a higher value than those of the f-CL10 and f-CL25 films. This increase may be attributed to light scattering arising from [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisk aggregates within the CNF matrix at higher nanoclay loadings (Figure E).

The color properties of the f-CNF, f-CL, f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films are summarized in Table . The L* parameter, representing lightness, was measured to be 36.443 ± 0.006, 36.010 ± 0.010, 22.520 ± 0.031, 23.880 ± 0.036, and 28.900 ± 0.006 for the f-CNF, f-CL, f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films, respectively. Using the f-CNF film as the reference, the ΔEab* value of the f-CL film was 0.435, indicating that the incorporation of Laponite nanoclays alone did not significantly affect the color of the pristine CNF films. Furthermore, analysis of the a* values of the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films confirmed that the inclusion of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP did not markedly alter the red-green color spectrum of the formed films. For the b* parameter, representing the yellow-blue color spectrum, only the f-CL100 film exhibited a significantly lower b* value compared with the f-CNF, f-CL, f-CL10, and f-CL25 films due to yellow discoloration. However, the ΔEab* values of the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films were all greater than 2, specifically 13.923, 12.569, and 8.607, reflecting perceptible color differences compared with the reference f-CNF film. These differences were primarily attributable to variations in the L* parameter. Nevertheless, when the developed coating formulations were applied to Muscat grapes, the differences in physical appearance between the uncoated grape and those immersion-coated with the CL10, CL25, and CL100 dispersions, designated as Grape-CL10, Grape-CL25, and Grape-CL100, respectively, were not perceptible or were barely perceptible (Figure ). These findings demonstrated that the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite coatings could be applied to real fresh fruits without causing significant alterations in their physical appearance.

Tensile strength represents a key parameter that indicates the maximum stress a film can withstand before failure. The f-CNF film exhibited a TS value of 4.04 ± 0.30 MPa (Table S10). Upon incorporation of Laponite nanoclays and [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks, a decrease in TS was observed. The f-CL, f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films exhibited TS values of 3.66 ± 0.26, 2.36 ± 0.11, 2.60 ± 0.24, and 3.26 ± 0.23 MPa, respectively. The fabricated film specimens exhibited TS values within a range comparable to those of wet carboxymethylated CNF (CM-CNF) films because the film drying process was performed under ambient conditions for 24 h without any subsequent heat treatment at elevated temperatures. A reduction in TS value was reported for films composed of CNF derived from mechanical treatments with or without enzymatic pretreatment and sepiolite or combined (sepiolite and kaolin) clay minerals, which was attributed to impaired packing between the CNFs and clay minerals as well as the intrinsic fibrillation extent of the CNFs. Furthermore, compared with TEMPO–CNFs, carboxymethylated CNFs exhibited a lower degree of fibrillation and contained fewer thin nanofibrils, resulting in the formation of a less compact matrix.

Statistically significant differences in the mean TS values were observed between f-CNF and the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 samples (Table S10). Nevertheless, the incorporation of Laponite nanoclays alone into CNFs did not cause any significant differences in their average TS values. The slight improvement in the TS values of the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 with increasing [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP loadings could be attributed to enhanced ionic interactions between the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks and the CNF matrix.

The elongation at break (EB) represents the stretching ability of a film before it eventually breaks. The f-CNF film possessed an EB value of 52.4 ± 0.9%. Following the incorporation of Laponite nanoclays, the EB value of the f-CL film increased slightly to 56.4 ± 1.8%, which was not statistically different from that of the f-CNF film (Figure B and Table S10). In contrast, the incorporation of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks into the CNF matrix led to a progressive decrease in EB with increasing Ag+ concentrations. Statistically significant differences in the mean EB values were observed between the f-CNF and f-CL films and the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films. The EB values of the f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films were 46.2 ± 1.5%, 38.5 ± 1.4%, and 35.1 ± 1.8%, respectively. This reduction in EB was likely due to the increasing rigidity arising from the electrostatic interactions of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks and the carboxymethylated groups of the CNFs, thereby restricting the flexibility and deformability of the nanocomposite matrix. Similarly, wet carboxymethylated CNF films fabricated by rehydrating dried carboxymethylated CNF films with a thickness of 50–60 μm through immersion in 1% (w/w) chloride salt solutions of Ca2+ and Fe3+ for 24 h exhibited reduced TS values from 41 ± 5% to 31 ± 2%, respectively, due to enhanced formation of cross-linking complexes provided by trivalent ions.

The water vapor permeability (WVP) of films is a critical parameter governing the effectiveness of fruit coatings in reducing moisture loss and preventing spoilage. The WVP values of the f-CNF, f-CL, f-CL10, f-CL25, and f-CL100 films were measured to be 0.481 ± 0.009, 0.445 ± 0.012, 0.476 ± 0.008, 0.428 ± 0.019, and 0.462 ± 0.009 g·mm/m2·h·kPa, respectively (Figure and Table S11). Previous studies reported WVP values of approximately 0.4 g·mm/m2·h·kPa for collagen films containing Laponite. Additionally, gelatin-Laponite nanocomposite films exhibited WVP values of approximately 0.37 ± 0.02 g·mm/m2·h·kPa. The f-CL and f-CL25 films demonstrated improved barrier properties compared with the f-CNF film. Additionally, the WVP value of the f-CNF film was significantly different from those of the f-CL and f-CL25 films. Barrier properties of the nanocomposite films could be improved by the incorporation of well-dispersed, impermeable nanoclays, partly due to the formation of more tortuous diffusion pathways. In this study, the lowest WVP value of 0.428 ± 0.019 g·mm/m2·h·kPa was observed for the f-CL25 film. However, the WVP values of the f-CNF, f-CL10, and f-CL100 films were not significantly different, indicating that the incorporation of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks at the specified initial Ag+ concentrations did not improve the barrier properties of the nanocomposite films. Invariable water permeability after the incorporation of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanoclays into CNF films might be attributed to the formation of microscale voids within the nanocomposite matrix, aggregation of stacked [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks (Figure C and E), and the increased hydrophilicity of the f-CL10 and f-CL100 films.

No migration of silver ions into grape pulp was detected for either the Grape-CL25 or Grape-CL100 samples on the first day and the seventh day after coating. Migration of ionic silver or metallic nanosilver species into food matrices is influenced by multiple factors, including the physical forms of packaging materials, e.g., nanosilver-impregnated polymeric containers or nanosilver-coated cling films, the pH of food matrices, and the conditions and durations of storage. Silver ion release from corn starch-based coating materials reinforced with green silver nanoparticles into 3% acetic acid (as a food simulant) remained below 0.0006 mg/kg of food after 10 days of storage at 60 °C. Food-touch paper has been reported to exhibit total silver releases of 0.53 ± 0.02 mg/kg in tuna meat, 0.25 ± 0.08 mg/kg in turkey meat, and 0.25 ± 0.08 mg/kg in ham after storage at 2 °C for 312 h. Preservative film materials fabricated from poly­(lactic acid) blended with silver nanoparticles exhibited silver migration levels of approximately 4 to 9.5 μg/kg after immersion in deionized water for 1 to 7 days. The migration rate of silver from packaging chitosan film decorated with silver nanoparticles synthesized from dragon fruit stems into aqueous solutions was reported to be 8.95 ± 0.25 μg/kg·dm2 and 21.96 ± 0.43 μg/kg·dm2 after 1 day and 5 days of immersion, respectively. According to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), the maximum permitted migration of silver from food-contact materials into foods, including fruit matrices, is 0.05 mg Ag/kg of food. The detected silver ion concentrations in the grape pulp from the Grape-CL25 and Grape-CL100 samples remained below this regulatory threshold. Therefore, the migration of Ag+ from the developed [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings into the real fruit matrix is minimal and may not give rise to dietary exposure or pose a toxicological concern. In the present study, the ICP-OES limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 0.0076 ppm. Accordingly, Ag in grape pulp extract was not detected above 0.0076 mg L–1 under the analytical conditions used. To provide a conservative upper-bound exposure estimate, we assumed that Ag in the edible portion would be present at the LOD level. On this basis, consumption of 200 g of grapes would correspond to a maximum Ag intake of approximately 1.52 μg/day. This value is substantially below the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) oral reference dose for silver of 0.005 mg/kg·day (i.e., 300 μg/day for a 60 kg adult). However, this is only a conservative screening-level estimate and not a substitute for standardized migration testing or a formal toxicological risk assessment.

Furthermore, when the Grape-CL100 sample was rinsed with ultrapure water 1 day after coating, no silver ions were detected in the grape pulp, whereas the rinse water contained silver ions at a concentration of 1.58 mg L–1 (Table ). These results suggest that the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings can be readily removed from the fruit surface by simple water washing, with no residual silver ions remaining within the fruit matrix. Overall, the developed [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings pose minimal to no toxicological risk and demonstrate strong practical applicability for real-fruit applications.

The f-CL100 films released Ag+ in 3% (w/v) acetic acid and 10% (v/v) ethanol, whereas the Ag+ release was markedly suppressed in buffered media during 72 h of immersion. Released silver ions from poly­(lactic acid)-polylactide/silver zeolite composites into water were determined to be 0.043 mg Ag+/kg after 24 h at 20 °C. Silver nanoparticle-poly­(L-lactic acid) nanocomposite thin films displayed a total mean cumulative silver nanoparticle release in phosphate-buffered saline of 0.174 mg/L on the first 2 days and reached the maximum value of 0.386 mg/L after 7 days. The limited release of silver ions in buffered media might be facilitated by the CNF network, whose carboxymethyl functional groups could coordinate with silver ions and enhance their retention. Similar behavior has been reported for silver nanoparticle/montmorillonite/dry-fabricated biofilm composites, where carboxyl groups within the bacterial cellulose matrix interacted with silver ions, providing a physical barrier that reduced Ag+ diffusion.

In contrast, for both 3% (w/v) acetic acid and 10% (v/v) ethanol, the release rate was relatively high during the first five h and decreased thereafter. In 10% (v/v) ethanol, Ag+ release approached a plateau after 48 h, whereas in in 3% (w/v) acetic acid, it continued to increase gradually throughout the test period. These results indicate that Ag+ release from the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films is facilitated under acidic environments. Despite the high Ag+ release value in these two food simulants, the Ag migration behavior observed in the Grape-CL100 sample did not exceed the permissible limit regulated by the EFSA. Therefore, [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite coatings could be safely applied to real fresh fruits.

To evaluate the antibacterial efficacy of the developed [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings on fresh fruit surfaces, the pretreated green grapes were immersion-coated with [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF dispersions at initial Ag+ concentrations of 10, 25, and 100 ppm. The coated grapes were surface-inoculated with E. coli and S. aureus, each at an initial concentration of approximately 106 CFU g–1, to simulate microbial spoilage on fresh fruits, considering the microbial infestation limit of 105 CFU g–1. The residual viable bacteria were quantified after 7, 14, and 21 days of storage. The viable counts of E. coli and S. aureus, expressed as log10 CFU g–1, are presented in Figure . After 7 and 14 days of storage, the control group (Control-E) exhibited viable counts of E. coli of 2.813 and 3.230 log CFU g–1, respectively (Figure A). The complete decline in the viable count of E. coli was observed in the control group after 21 days of storage. This decline is likely attributable to the intrinsic acidity of Muscat grapes, which exhibit a pH range of 2.34–4.44 after 4 to 16.9 weeks postflowering. , Additionally, a previous report also indicated that E. coli growth was strongly inhibited at pH ≤ 3.9. The developed [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings exhibited good bacteriostatic activity against E. coli because the residual E. coli counts on Grape-CL10E, Grape-CL25E, and Grape-CL100E were determined to be <1.00 log10 CFU g–1 for the entire storage duration of 21 days.

For the control group of S. aureus (Control-S), the viable counts of S. aureus were measured to be 6.68, 6.08, and 5.56 log10 CFU g–1 after 7, 14, and 21 days of storage, respectively, indicating microbial spoilage of the fruits for the entire storage duration of 21 days (Figure B). The Grape-CL10S and Grape-CL25S samples exhibited viable S. aureus counts of 5.15 and 2.79, 4.88 and 3.81, and 3.73 and 3.00, corresponding to log reductions of 1.53 and 3.89, 1.20 and 2.27, and 1.83 and 2.56 after 7, 14, and 21 days of storage, respectively. In contrast, the Grape-CL100S sample exhibited markedly low viable S. aureus counts of <1.00, < 1.00, and 1.40 log10 CFU g–1, with corresponding log reductions of >5.68, > 5.08, and 4.16 after 7, 14, and 21 days of storage, respectively. These results suggest that the developed [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coating at an initial Ag+ concentration of 100 ppm exhibited high bacteriostatic activity against S. aureus. Additionally, these results were corroborated by the antimicrobial activity of the f-CL100 films assessed with JIS Z 2801:2010 (Figure D).

Based on the previous results, the surfaces of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films, as well as the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings applied to fresh grapes, exhibited effective antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus at an optimal Ag+ concentration of 100 ppm. The intrinsic acidity of Muscat grapes might create an acidic environment, facilitating Ag+ release from the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite coatings and thus partially contributing to the antibacterial activities observed on the coated grapes. Additionally, the antibacterial activity of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks is likely activated upon direct contact with the bacterial cells. A contact-dependent mechanism has been reported for Laponite nanoparticles functionalized with the LL-37 antimicrobial peptide, which induces the combined effects of the flocculation of E. coli mediated by interactions of Laponite and lipopolysaccharide, and membrane lysis by LL-37.

The greater resistance of Gram-positive bacteria compared with Gram-negative bacteria to [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks, as demonstrated by the MIC and MBC values, the Modify BSEN 1276:2019 assay, and antibacterial assessments based on the JIS Z 2801:2010 standard, may be attributed to differences in cell wall architecture. Gram-positive bacteria possess a substantially thicker peptidoglycan layer (approximately 80 nm) reinforced with lipoteichoic acids, whereas Gram-negative bacteria contain a thinner peptidoglycan layer (approximately 8 nm) surrounded by an outer membrane enriched in a 1–3 μm lipopolysaccharide layer. Lipoteichoic acids exhibit lower affinity to Laponite when compared to lipopolysaccharide. This structural distinction likely facilitates a more effective initial contact between the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks and Gram-negative bacterial membranes. Additionally, a strong integration of Laponite into lipid bilayer membranes facilitates a controlled release of curcumin from curcumin-loaded Laponite nanoparticles. By analogy, after initial contact between [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks and bacterial membranes, subsequent binding of immobilized [Ag­(HHis)]+ complexes on Laponite nanodisks with thiol-containing proteins within the bacterial envelope may occur. Bacterial cells in contact with the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks consecutively translocate silver cations into the cytoplasm, leading to the disruption of crucial physiological functions via oxidative stress induced by ROS generation, ribosome denaturation, inhibition of key enzymes and proteins essential for ATP biosynthesis, and cell death.

Conclusions

Laponite RD nanoparticles were successfully immobilized with silver-histidine complexes via an ion-exchange reaction. The [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks appeared as a colorless, transparent gel and could be stored for up to 45 days without noticeable discoloration. The successful immobilization of the silver-histidine complex onto Laponite RD nanodisks was confirmed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, transmission electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, dynamic light scattering, and zeta potential analysis. The [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP nanodisks were subsequently utilized as an antibacterial component in food contact materials, with cellulose nanofibers serving as the film- forming matrix. The [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP dispersions exhibited MIC and MBC values of 6.25 and 25 ppm against E. coli, and 12.5 and 50 ppm against S. aureus, respectively. The surfaces of [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF nanocomposite films demonstrated effective antibacterial activity against both E. coli and S. aureus when prepared using the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF dispersions containing an initial Ag+ concentration of 100 ppm, as assessed by the JIS Z 2801:2010 standard method. The optimal nanocomposite formulation, designated as f-CL100, exhibited favorable mechanical properties, high optical transparency, water vapor permeability comparable to that of CNF films, and strong antibacterial activity. Migration testing confirmed that no detectable silver ions were present in the grape pulp of the Grape-CL100 samples after 7 days of storage, complying with the permissible limit set by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). Furthermore, the f-CL100 coatings were fully removable from the coated grape surfaces by rinsing with water, leaving a trace amount of residual silver ions in the grape pulp below the ICP-OES detection limit. The optimal [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings could be employed for preventing bacterial spoilage in green grape berries and extending the shelf life of the green grapes for up to 21 days. The intrinsic acidity of the grape surfaces facilitated the Ag+ release from the [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings, as corroborated by the Ag+ release tests in hydrophilic and acidic food simulants. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the optimized [Ag­(HHis)]+1.0LP-CNF coatings represent a promising, safe, and water-washable food contact material capable of preventing bacterial contamination of fresh fruits and extending their shelf life.
