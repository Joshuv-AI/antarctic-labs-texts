Detecting introgression from phylogenetic invariant site patterns using machine learning
Authors: Patrick F. McKenzie, Deren A. R. Eaton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseDetecting historical introgression among populations or species from genomic data is a common goal in evolutionary genetics. Most current methods fall into two major categories: network inference and admixture inference. Network inference (e.g., SNaQ) is computationally challenging and typically requires first reducing large genomic datasets into a less informative collection of inferred gene trees. In contrast, admixture inference (e.g., ABBA‐BABA tests) can accommodate enormous single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) datasets but is restricted to examining subsets of four to five samples at a time. Here, we demonstrate a new approach to evaluate SNP frequencies among quartet samples under a phylogenetic hypothesis (similar to ABBA‐BABA tests), while examining all quartet information simultaneously (similar to the network inference methods).Methods and ResultsTo do this, our method simcat trains a neural network machine learning model on coalescent simulations to discriminate between introgression scenarios based on learned SNP frequency patterns. We demonstrate the accuracy of simcat to classify introgression events from simulations, evaluate its sensitivity to variation in species tree parameters, and demonstrate its application to an empirical dataset of oak trees (Quercus ser. Virentes).ConclusionsOur approach represents a first step towards leveraging machine learning to expand phylogenetic invariants–based methods beyond the scale of quartets to a larger phylogenetic context.

Hybridization and introgression have important consequences for understanding speciation, adaptation, and biodiversity. They also pose challenges for the models used to study relationships among species. The multispecies coalescent (MSC) is a parameterized hierarchical model of the phylogenetic relationships among a set of diverged lineages, such as species or populations. It provides a framework for modeling the process of incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) as a probabilistic outcome of the coalescent process occurring among a set of structured populations as a function of a few simple parameters: the branching topology (i.e., the species tree), the population divergence times, and the effective population sizes (Degnan and Rosenberg, 2009; Edwards et al., 2016). Despite the effectiveness of the species tree inference paradigm in advancing statistical methods in demographic and phylogenetic inference (Bouckaert et al., 2014; Chifman and Kubatko, 2014), it has long been recognized that many biological datasets violate core assumptions of this model, particularly in the common case of hybrid introgression between lineages (Funk et al., 2021; Rose et al., 2021; Aguillon et al., 2022; Chan et al., 2022; Jackson et al., 2022; Suvorov et al., 2022). This has led to the development of a suite of methods that derive expectations from the MSC model as a basis for detecting historical introgression as deviations from its null expectations.

The detection of gene flow between divergent lineages (i.e., introgression) is important for understanding and studying evolution. Genomic admixture (i.e., the genomic pattern left by historical introgression) can affect the accuracy of phylogenetic inference (Leaché et al., 2014; Jiao et al., 2020) and divergence time estimation (Leaché et al., 2014; Tiley et al., 2023). It is a potential mechanism for adaptation (Suarez‐Gonzalez et al., 2018; Edelman and Mallet, 2021), including patterns of convergent trait evolution (Grant et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2021, 2023), and it may often play an important role in speciation (Ayoola et al., 2021; Edelman and Mallet, 2021; Nelson et al., 2021; Rosser et al., 2024). Hybridization also has important consequences for how we quantify and assess biodiversity, including the delimitation of species boundaries (Petit and Excoffier, 2009; Larson et al., 2014), assessment of threatened status (Rhymer and Simberloff, 1996; Ghezelayagh et al., 2025), and design of conservation strategies (Bohling, 2016; Kelly and Phillips, 2016). As the size of genomic datasets continues to grow, there is a need for scalable nonparametric methods that can accommodate large numbers of single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to detect introgression across large numbers of taxa.

Phylogenetic network and admixture inference approaches

Many methods have been developed to detect introgression among species from genomic data. As a simplification, these can generally be grouped into two major categories: network inference and admixture inference. The former involves searching a space of possible network hypotheses that are evaluated under an optimization criterion (e.g., SNaQ; Solís‐Lemus et al., 2017), whereas the latter serves as a hypothesis testing framework to evaluate evidence for one or more introgression edges given a fixed phylogenetic tree hypothesis (e.g., ABBA‐BABA tests). Network inference is more computationally challenging and thus typically requires first reducing large genomic datasets into a less informative collection of inferred gene trees (although, e.g., see Bryant et al., 2012). By contrast, admixture inference can accommodate enormous SNP datasets, but it is often restricted to examining subsets of four to five taxa at a time, making it difficult to interpret multiple non‐independent test results. There is a clear trade‐off between these two approaches in the amount of genomic information that can be analyzed versus the phylogenetic scope.

A distinct advantage of network inference methods, such as PhyloNetworks or PhyloNet (Than et al., 2008; Solís‐Lemus et al., 2017), is their decreased reliance on a fixed input species tree hypothesis. By searching for an optimal network, these methods can propose results that differ significantly from the best inferred species tree, possibly revealing unexpected relationships. Moreover, by comparing alternative network hypotheses, network inference methods have greater power than admixture inference approaches to accurately infer directionality and to distinguish among introgression events that may give rise to multiple non‐independent signatures of introgression among related taxa, a signal that is often confounding in admixture inference methods (Hibbins and Hahn, 2022).

These advantages are counterweighed by several significant drawbacks. The computational complexity of network searches both limits the size of networks that can be inferred (e.g., ≤15 taxa) and requires reducing large amounts of genomic information into topological summary statistics from inferred gene trees (Solís‐Lemus et al., 2017), introducing the potential for gene tree estimation error to bias network inference. Such biases likely derive from errors in sequence alignment and homology identification, or from concatalescence. The former are more likely when short genomic sequences are analyzed, as these contain little informative for distinguishing paralogs and inferring accurate trees (Cai et al., 2021). By contrast, the problem of concatalescence bias is more likely when longer genomic sequences are analyzed. This occurs when genomic segments span multiple recombination breakpoints, effectively concatenating distinct genealogical histories into a single gene tree that will be biased towards the dominant topology (Gatesy and Springer, 2014; McKenzie and Eaton, 2026). While the impact of concatalescence on species tree estimation has been demonstrated, its impact on network inference remains less well understood (Gatesy and Springer, 2014; Zhu et al., 2022) but is likely significant, considering that introgression edges can be supported by relatively subtle deviations in gene tree frequencies. Regardless of the extent of these biases, it is clear that by reducing genome‐scale datasets into a summary of topological discordance among error‐prone gene trees, an enormous amount of information is discarded in current network inference approaches.

An alternative approach to detecting introgression involves proposing a fixed species tree topology and examining patterns of genealogical discordance with respect to this hypothesis, which we refer to here as admixture inference (i.e., inference based on observed admixture with regard to species tree expectations). This approach greatly reduces the complexity of the inference problem, facilitating analysis of much larger sequence datasets. Because unlinked SNPs can be abundantly sampled across entire genomes, such data should in theory provide greater power to detect introgression than much smaller gene tree datasets. Examples of this approach include the ABBA‐BABA statistic (i.e., Patterson's D‐statistic) and related tests, which operate directly on SNP frequencies (Green et al., 2010; Durand et al., 2011; Chifman and Kubatko, 2014; Blischak et al., 2018; Elworth et al., 2018). The ABBA‐BABA test specifically examines quartets of taxa, measuring deviations in the frequency between ABBA and BABA site patterns, where “A” represents an ancestral allele and “B” a derived allele, with the states in order of the tips in an imbalanced four‐tip species tree: (((P1,P2),P3),P4);. The null expectation is that ABBA and BABA site patterns will occur with equal frequency in the absence of introgression if ILS is the only process generating discordance. An advantage of this approach is that it is nonparametric and topology‐based, such that its expectations (symmetries among certain site patterns) are consistent under any generalized Markov substitution model, and regardless of differences in species tree parameters (Chifman and Kubatko, 2015).

SNP‐based admixture inference approaches also have significant drawbacks. For example, although the ABBA‐BABA test is simple to understand and apply, it relies on only a subset of possible site pattern categories that could be examined, and it applies only to a subset of taxa in a dataset at a time. Consequently, the interpretability of this statistic deteriorates rapidly when applied across larger phylogenies, where many quartet tests involving the same or closely related taxa are inherently non‐independent (Eaton and Ree, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015). A single introgression event can thus yield multiple signals of admixture that can be falsely interpreted as evidence of multiple separate introgression events. Efforts to extend ABBA‐BABA–type methods to larger subsets of a tree (e.g., five taxa) partially address this problem, allowing for more accurate localization of introgression and inference of directionality (Eaton and Ree, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015; Pease and Hahn, 2015; Hibbins and Hahn, 2019; Pfeifer and Kapan, 2019). However, it remains challenging to summarize dozens or hundreds of such results across large trees to identify and distinguish the number of supported introgression events.

There is a clear trade‐off between the amount of genomic data that can be analyzed, the interpretability of test results, and the computational feasibility of existing methods for inferring introgression. Some recent work has addressed these challenges through the development of methods that are intermediate between the approaches described above. For example, the tool phynest performs phylogenetic network inference by evaluating genome‐wide SNP patterns, rather than gene trees (Kong et al., 2024). Although its implementation requires the simplifying assumptions of constant effective population sizes and a simple mutational model, it presents an exciting advance by removing biases associated with gene tree estimation. Here, we similarly introduce a new hybrid approach aimed at bridging the gap between these methodological extremes. Our approach uses a fixed species tree hypothesis, similar to existing admixture inference approaches, but evaluates entire SNP datasets across all taxa in a tree simultaneously. Thus, it addresses the interpretability problem that arises from many separate quartet‐based tests. To do this, we use machine learning to leverage the topological information contained in phylogenetic invariants, with the goal of detecting supported introgressive edges on a species tree while retaining the advantages of site pattern–based methods like ABBA‐BABA tests, which are relatively insensitive to variation in demography and which hold across a broad class of Markov substitution models.

Phylogenetic invariants and site pattern counts

Phylogenetic invariants are polynomial equations involving the expected frequencies of specific site patterns that arise from a Markov substitution process along the edges of a phylogeny (Cavender and Felsenstein, 1987; Lake, 1987; Allman and Rhodes, 2007). Different tree topologies impose distinct constraints on these frequencies, leading to different sets of phylogenetic invariants. Consequently, phylogenetic invariants can serve as a relatively simple and efficient metric for evaluating candidate tree topologies based on observed site pattern frequencies. For example, for an unrooted quartet tree with taxa P1, P2, P3, and P4, a phylogenetic invariant is the expectation that the frequency of the AATT site pattern bears a specific polynomial relationship to the frequency of the TTAA site pattern, if the true quartet topology is ((P1,P2),(P3,P4)). Under the Jukes–Cantor model (i.e., JC69) the polynomial relationship predicts the two site patterns to occur symmetrically, while under the Hasegawa‐Kishino‐Yano (HKY) model the relationship further includes potential differences in the base equilibrium frequencies (Jukes and Cantor, 1969; Hasegawa et al., 1985).

Phylogenetic invariants were long viewed as a novelty with few practical applications due to the difficulty associated with identifying a unique set of invariant patterns for any given tree topology (Felsenstein, 1991; Allman and Rhodes, 2007). However, they gained renewed interest in the genomic era as an efficient way to analyze large SNP datasets. Notable examples include the SVDquartets method for species tree inference (Chifman and Kubatko, 2014) and the ABBA‐BABA test for detecting introgression (Green et al., 2010; Durand et al., 2011). Both methods make use of well‐known phylogenetic invariants defined for quartet trees. The relationships among quartet site patterns are easy to visualize and understand in the context of the SVDquartets algorithm, which first orders all site patterns into a 16 × 16 site pattern count matrix (Figure 1A). The goal of this tool is to examine SNP frequencies among all combinations of four taxa in a dataset and infer the correct quartet tree resolution from the three possible unrooted topologies for a quartet (Figure 1B–D). Each quartet topology is associated with a different overall pattern of filling this matrix—the incorrect topologies fill more discordant‐type site patterns (e.g., ATAT) while the correct topology fills more concordant site patterns (e.g., AATT). Although these matrices contain a rich summary of the SNP dataset, in SVDquartets a matrix comparison is applied to select the topology that minimizes discordance, effectively reducing this information to a categorical result (Figure 1D).

Site patterns that are discordant with the species tree exhibit either ABBA or BABA patterns and are highlighted in green and orange, respectively. (B, C) Given a species tree hypothesis, a site pattern matrix can be generated for the three possible resolutions of each quartet sample. (D) Methods such as SVDquartets (Chifman and Kubatko, 2014) infer the most likely resolution for each quartet by analyzing phylogenetic invariants using fast matrix comparisons. (E) Here, we store and analyze a data structure termed the stacked site pattern matrix, which retains SNP frequencies across all quartets of taxa.

Phylogenetic invariant symmetries are also involved in SNP‐based tests for admixture (Elworth et al., 2019). The site patterns associated with ABBA and BABA categories in the site count matrix are highlighted in Figure 1A. The ABBA‐BABA test simply sums the numbers across these 24 cells (12 for each pattern) to evaluate the phylogenetic invariant symmetry expected (i.e., nABBA = nBABA) if the proposed species tree topology is true (i.e., no introgression exists). In theory, a broader set of phylogenetic invariant–based statistics could be developed to overcome the current sample size limitation of this approach (which is currently constrained to examining four taxa at a time), making the framework more applicable to larger phylogenetic questions (Elworth et al., 2018, 2019).

Theoretical premise

We propose that a multidimensional (i.e., “stacked”) site pattern matrix of dimension nquartets × 16 × 16 can be constructed, given a species tree hypothesis, as an informative data structure for evaluating phylogenetic invariant patterns to detect introgression on a species tree, where nquartets is the number of four‐taxon trees that can be sampled from the full tree (Figure 1E). To demonstrate that this data structure contains information about co‐varying relationships among site pattern frequencies both within and between quartet samples, we examined the stacked site pattern matrix in coalescent simulations performed on a species network with a single reticulation. Simulations were performed in ipcoal (McKenzie and Eaton, 2020) on a five‐tip imbalanced species tree with equal internal node distances, a crown height of one million generations, and a constant effective population size (N e) of 50,000. We simulated 50,000 unlinked SNPs under the JC69 substitution model in three different scenarios: without introgression, with introgression from taxon 1 to 3, and with introgression from taxon 3 to 1. Introgression occurred as a pulse of 20% migration at the midpoint of the shared interval between the two branches. We examined the change in frequency of site pattern counts in the two introgression scenarios compared to the scenario without introgression across each layer of the stacked site pattern matrices (Figure 2). Each layer in the stacked matrix represents an individual site pattern count matrix for the correct topology of a quartet from the species tree. The first pattern to emerge from these simulations is a clear example of the potential for ABBA‐BABA tests to yield false positive results when applied across quartet subsets of a species tree (Figure 2A, B, F, I). However, a second pattern also emerges at a higher level, across all five layers of a stacked site pattern matrix, which is clearly different between the two scenarios involving a different direction of introgression (e.g., Figure 2A–E). At least visually, this confirms our expectation that stacked site pattern matrices contain information across quartet subsets that may serve as a unique “fingerprint” for identifying and distinguishing between introgression scenarios on a species tree.

Introgression affects site pattern frequencies across multiple layers of a stacked site pattern matrix. For two scenarios, the first involving introgression from taxon 3 into 1 (A–E) and the second involving introgression from taxon 1 into 3 (F–J), a subtree is shown for each possible quartet subsample from a five‐tip species tree, with its site pattern count matrix below it. Matrices are plotted as a heatmap showing the deviation of site pattern counts relative to a coalescent simulation on the species tree without introgression. A single introgression event can cause a signal of admixture among many different quartet subsamples, leading to false positive inferences from ABBA‐BABA tests. For example, tests (A) and (F) yield misleading results suggesting admixture between taxon 0 and 3, due to introgression involving the outgroup. Similarly, tests (B) and (I) give misleading results caused by introgression from a sample not included in the quartet. The direction of introgression is unknowable from any individual ABBA‐BABA test. Despite these limitations of individual quartet‐based tests, a distinct pattern exists across all five layers of each stacked site pattern matrix (i.e., A–E or F–J) as a unique “fingerprint” of the introgression scenario.

Although higher‐order phylogenetic invariants exist for large trees, it is analytically intractable to derive them for every species tree hypothesis (Allman and Rhodes, 2007; Elworth et al., 2018). Considering the multidimensional signal that introgression leaves in the stacked site pattern matrix, it is likely that higher‐level invariant patterns may exist as polynomials of the frequencies within these quartet site patterns. We hypothesize that a pattern matching approach, such as a supervised machine learning classifier, can learn generalized patterns in this data structure representing such polynomials in high‐dimensional space.

Supervised machine learning methods have become increasingly popular in population genomics and phylogenetics (Schrider and Kern, 2018; Abadi et al., 2020; Bhattacharjee and Bayzid, 2020; Azouri et al., 2021). Machine learning approaches differ from classical statistical approaches in that rather than explicitly modeling the process generating the data, they are constructed naively with regard to the generative process and instead fit a model by iterating over training data and minimizing a predictive metric. Many machine learning methods are capable of fitting nonlinear patterns in data even when there are interactions among input variables, which in classical statistical approaches would need to be explicitly specified. Although machine learning approaches are still uncommon in phylogenetics, they are particularly well suited for mapping complex signals in high‐dimensional input data to categorical outcomes—such as the problem posed here of mapping SNP data to an introgressive edge that produced it.

Demonstration

To validate the potential for machine learning methods to identify introgression scenarios as a categorical result from stacked site pattern matrices, we first explored two relatively simple machine learning approaches: a supervised random forest classifier and unsupervised dimensionality reduction using t‐distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t‐SNE). Similar to the example above, we simulated two datasets involving rooted imbalanced four‐taxon and five‐taxon species trees, each with equal internal edge lengths, a crown height set to one million generations, and a constant N e of 50,000. We generated 10,000 simulated datasets on each species tree, where each simulation assigns an introgression edge randomly between two non‐sister edges to introduce a pulse of directional migration at the midpoint of the shared interval between the two edges, with the proportion sampled uniformly between 10–30%. Each dataset sampled 50,000 SNPs using the sim_snps function in ipcoal with the argument max_mutations = 1 (i.e., infinite sites).

A random forest classifier is a supervised machine learning approach that constructs multiple decision trees on random subsets of the data and features, then combines their predictions to determine a classification. Using scikit‐learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011), we fit a random forest model to flattened arrays of the stacked site pattern matrices along with categorical labels designating the source and destination edges of the introgression scenario. The data were split into training (70%) and testing (30%) components and fit with 10,000 estimators. The model trained quickly to >99.8% accuracy. A key benefit of random forest models is the opportunity to extract “feature importances” from the trained model to identify which components of the data are most predictive. An importance was assigned to every cell in the stacked site pattern matrix. In the rooted four‐taxon tree scenario, the 24 most predictive features correspond to the 24 ABBA‐ and BABA‐type site patterns (Figure 3A).

To further demonstrate identifiability of introgression scenarios, we applied unsupervised t‐SNE in scikit‐learn to embed site pattern matrices into two‐dimensional space (Figure 3B, C). The perplexity parameter was set to the number of non‐sister directional introgression scenarios: 12 in the four‐tip model and 24 in the five‐tip model. Each stacked site pattern matrix is represented by a point, and scenarios that are more similar appear closer together in space. Points are colored by the introgression scenario. At least for the relatively simple scenarios examined here (which does not yet include variation in N e), these results show a high degree of identifiability between introgression scenarios based on information in stacked site pattern matrices.

We have demonstrated that one type of supervised method (random forest) is effective for inferring introgression scenarios on a four‐taxon species tree. However, empirical datasets are likely to involve much greater complexity than our example. An inference tool built upon this framework should be able to accommodate variation in species tree parameters, including different lineage divergence times and effective population sizes, as well as variation in introgression scenarios, including the magnitude, timing, and direction of introgression. Given sufficient training data generated from coalescent simulations performed across a diversity of species trees parameterization, we expect that a machine learning model can learn generalized topology‐based patterns associated with introgression edges. Here, we introduce simcat as a method for training a neural network given an input species tree hypothesis to be able to classify introgression scenarios from large SNP datasets.

METHODS

The simcat method

Given an input species tree topology and SNP alignment, simcat can be used to perform coalescent simulations to train a neural network model on site pattern frequencies within stacked site pattern matrices to identify introgression scenarios on the input species tree. This workflow involves four simple steps: (1) create a database of training simulations for all introgression scenarios across a broad range of parameterizations on a fixed species tree topology, (2) train a supervised neural network model to link patterns from stacked site pattern matrices to categorical introgression scenarios, (3) validate the trained model accuracy, and (4) apply the model to real SNP data on the same fixed species tree topology. Example code demonstrating database simulation and model training in simcat is shown in Appendix S1 (see Supporting Information with this article). Our pipeline is implemented in the Python package simcat, available at https://github.com/pmckenz1/simcat.

Simulating training data

To generate a training database, a user defines the range of parameters over which simulations will be performed. While it is well recognized that larger training datasets yield more accurate machine learning models, the goal here is not to exhaustively generate data for every combinatorial parameter setting, but to generate a large enough dataset to be richly informative while ensuring that the empirical truth falls within the boundaries of the training data, such that the model is interpolating rather than extrapolating.

The user sets a node slider parameter that varies internal branch lengths (in generations), and then designates a root height parameter that scales total tree height; furthermore, the user defines the minimum and maximum range of uniform distributions for the N e parameters, admixture proportions, and migration timing (Figure 4A). These parameters should be set so that the biological reality is contained within their range of variation; therefore, in the absence of information one should allow them to vary broadly (e.g., a large node slider and large range of N e values). However, constraining the parameters (e.g., by divergence time estimation) improves model training. To guide the range of node slider values to select, the user might consider variance in estimated divergence times, selecting larger values if estimated divergence times display high variance. The user can also specify parameters of the substitution model, which for empirical data should be first estimated from the sequences. These parameters are all accepted by the simcat.Database class object. The user can further specify the number of simulations to perform, the number of SNPs per simulation, and whether to include introgression events between sister edges. Similar to the stepwise addition of reticulations during SNaQ's network search algorithm, to infer more than one introgression edge in simcat the user must first define one or more existing introgression edges and then generate a new database to infer the next additional introgression edge on the tree.

The simcat pipeline. (A) Users supply a species tree and hyperparameters specifying the range of variation to include in the database. These include the range of variation in N e value, in the timing and magnitude of introgression, the extent to which edge lengths should vary, and whether to include introgression between sister taxa, as well as how many SNPs to simulate and how many simulations to run. (B) Based on those input parameters, simcat automatically generates a database of labels to automatically encode simulations in the coalescent simulation program ipcoal. Simcat's Simulator class allows users to parallelize these simulations. (C) The end result after simulating is a database where each row corresponds to stacked site pattern count matrices. (D) The database is used to train a neural network using convenient helper functions. (E) The model is evaluated on training and validation data. Then, new data can be presented to the trained model to infer the location of the introgressive edge used to generate the data.

The sampled parameters of each simulation, including the introgression scenario label (source and destination edge), are stored in a hierarchical data format (HDF5) file (a container for storing large, potentially heterogeneous datasets). The training database is filled by the simcat.Simulator class object, which distributes chunks of parameter instructions from the database to simulate SNP data in ipcoal. Internally, this uses the Python packages toytree (Eaton, 2020) and msprime (Baumdicker et al., 2022) to set up species tree models and perform coalescent simulations. Generating the training database is the greatest computational bottleneck in simcat. Simulations can be massively distributed over a high‐performance computing (HPC) cluster, where separate jobs can read from the labels database and write to the site pattern matrix database concurrently. Simulations can be interrupted and/or restarted until the database is filled with the requested number of results.

Neural network training and persistence

Neural network training is performed by simcat.BatchTrain. The goal of this neural network is to marginalize over variation in the species tree parameters to isolate the signal of introgression associated with deviations from the species tree topology, matching multidimensional patterns of site frequency “fingerprints” in the stacked site pattern matrices. The model trained by simcat involves a multiple‐input neural network using the Tensorflow‐associated machine learning library Keras (https://keras.io; Abadi et al., 2015). The size of the neural network scales with the number of tips on the species tree, so that there are as many inputs as there are unique quartets on the tree. Each input is a site pattern matrix constructed from the specific quartet. To accommodate variation in empirical datasets, quartet matrices are normalized individually, such that the relative frequencies of site patterns are analyzed rather than their total counts. Normalized quartet matrices are flattened and passed to a hidden layer that is fully connected to the specific input matrix. Based on manual tuning to find an optimal trade‐off between accuracy and speed, we set the default number of nodes in these hidden layers to eight. After passing the hidden layer outputs through a dropout layer, their outputs are propagated to a single (nquartets × 8)‐node layer, which in turn feeds into an argmax‐activated output layer, for which each node corresponds to an introgression edge represented by a source and destination node ID (Appendix S2). Models are fit using the Adam optimizer and a learning rate of 0.001 (Kingma and Ba, 2014).

The BatchTrain class object automatically splits the database files into training and validation datasets in proportions specified by the user, and can accommodate loading and saving Keras models. If desired, users can manage multiple models associated with the same training database. Because the SNP database can become prohibitively large for loading into memory, simcat trains models using small batches. This is a distinct advantage of neural network models over simpler tools, like decision trees, which require all training data to be accessible at the same time.

Applying a trained model

After a neural network has been trained, the resulting model file can be distributed and loaded onto any machine. The simcat.BatchTrain class provides convenient functions for loading the model and applying it to empirical data. Using simcat scripts, a user can format SNP data, given a species tree topology, into a topologically sorted stacked site pattern matrix. Applying the trained model to this dataset is very fast, making it feasible for users to easily apply the model to make predictions across subsets of the data, such as resampled bootstrap replicates of genome‐wide SNPs or sliding windows of SNPs along chromosomes.

Evaluation

To examine the accuracy of simcat to detect introgression under scenarios where the true introgression edge placement is known, we evaluated the performance of two models trained on large databases aimed at providing predictions for one or two introgression edges on the same species tree, respectively. Using these models, we examined the impact of variation in species tree parameters and dataset quality, including variation in species tree divergence times, N e, admixture proportions, and the number of SNPs. For this evaluation, we used an eight‐tip imbalanced species tree, which has 84 possible directional non‐sister introgression edges.

We expect that if trained models have learned generalized patterns associated with admixture on a species tree topology, as opposed to learning specific patterns associated with species tree parameters, then the models should be relatively insensitive to variation in species tree parameters. We also expect that accuracy should increase with increasing numbers of SNPs, but the baseline amount of information needed to detect introgression will vary depending on the admixture proportion, level of ILS, and number of introgression edges.

To train these models, we generated two databases of simulated unlinked SNPs involving either one or two introgression edges on the eight‐tip imbalanced species tree. The first database (A) places an introgression edge between two randomly sampled non‐sister edges in every simulation, whereas the second database (B) assumes an existing introgression edge between species 2 and 4, and randomly adds a second introgression edge following the same process. The admixture proportion for each edge is drawn uniformly between 0–0.5, and the timing of introgression is drawn uniformly to occur at a time point sampled in the interval between 10–90% of the shared interval between two edges. The initial tree has equally spaced internal node heights, but each simulation first scales the tree to a root height drawn uniformly between 5e6–15e6 and applies a node sliding function that randomly shifts each node height ± 90% of its starting height. Effective population sizes are drawn uniformly between 50,000–500,000 and assigned randomly to each species tree edge. We simulated 60,000 datasets for both databases A and B, where each simulation has a unique set of parameters and contains 20,000 unlinked SNPs. We trained one model for each database using the normalized stacked site pattern frequency matrices and introgression labels.

Varying input data

To examine the impact of the number of SNPs in a dataset on the accuracy of simcat to identify the correct introgression edge placement, we simulated new SNP datasets on the eight‐tip species tree topology with equal internal edge lengths, a root height of 10e6, and one introgression edge. We generated datasets that varied in the number of SNPs between 1000–30,000 in 11 equally spaced categories and in the admixture proportion between 0.0–0.5 in 11 equally spaced categories. This was repeated across three different values of constant N e (150,000, 250,000, 350,000) and repeated 100 times for each combination of parameters (11 × 11 × 3 × 100 = 36,300 datasets). Each dataset consists of a SNP dataset simulated in ipcoal with a known introgression label.

Varying species tree parameters

To examine the effect of species tree parameters on the prediction accuracy of a trained simcat model, we simulated new SNP datasets each with high amounts of data (20,000 SNPs) but varying in species tree node heights and N e. As described above, we generated datasets across 11 evenly spaced values for N e between 50,000–500,000 and 11 equally spaced values for the admixture proportion between 0.0–0.5. This was repeated at three different values for the scaled root height (5e6, 10e6, 15e6) and across 100 replicates for each combination of parameter values to generate a total of 36,300 simulations. The highest N e and lowest root height together represent the maximum amount of ILS possible in our training database, while the lowest N e and highest root height represent the minimum amount of ILS possible in our training database. The former involves internal edge lengths of 0.7 coalescent units, while the latter involves internal edge lengths of 21 coalescent units.

Examining error types

When a model misidentifies the placement of an introgression event, it can be informative to know what the predicted alternative edge placement is. Do errors typically involve misidentifying directionality, selecting the edge just above versus below a node, or are errors much more randomly distributed? To examine this question, we generated 50 replicate simulations under each of the 84 introgression scenarios on the eight‐tip imbalanced species tree with the admixture proportion set to 0.3. For these simulations, the root height was set to 10e6 and N e to 250,000. The simulations involving one introgression edge were tested on the model trained on database A. Next, we repeated the procedure above to generate a second introgression edge on a species tree that already included an introgression edge of proportion 0.3 between species 2 and 4. In this case, we tested all remaining 83 placements for the second introgression edge. These simulated datasets involving two introgression edges were evaluated by the model trained on database B.

Empirical test

To demonstrate an application of our inference method to an empirical dataset, we used simcat to infer the placement of an introgression edge on the species tree of the American live oaks (Quercus ser. Virentes), a clade of eight oak species distributed throughout the southern United States, Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean. Previous studies have shown evidence of introgression between sympatric populations in this clade, most notably involving Quercus fusiformis Small and Q. virginiana Mill. where they overlap in Texas (Cavender‐Bares et al., 2015; Eaton et al., 2015). We downloaded the reduced‐representation (RAD‐seq) dataset from Eaton et al. (2015) (National Center for Biotechnology Information [NCBI] Sequence Read Archive [SRA] accession number: SRP055977), which includes 42 samples across eight ingroup and six outgroup species. We assembled the data in ipyrad (Eaton and Overcast, 2020) using default parameter settings, except for setting the Q. robur L. genome (Plomion et al., 2018) to be used for reference‐based assembly.

To obtain a species tree for use in simcat, we used the ipyrad window_extractor tool to construct a genome‐wide supermatrix while filtering out sites that did not map to the 12 chromosomes or had sample coverage lower than 30. A maximum likelihood tree was inferred by RAxML (Kozlov et al., 2019) from the concatenated supermatrix, along with parameters of the general time reversible (GTR) substitution model. After pruning the outgroups and binning the remaining samples into the eight ingroup clades of interest, we estimated posterior distributions of divergence times and effective population sizes on the fixed eight‐tip species tree topology from 10 replicate runs of the BPP algorithm 00 (Yang, 2015).

Using this species tree as our starting hypothesis, we generated a training database of 60,000 simulations across a range of species tree parameters. The scaled root height was allowed to vary between 179,000–537,000 generations, internal node heights were allowed to slide ±25% from their initial scaled heights, and effective population sizes were sampled uniformly between 10,000–100,000. Non‐sister introgression edges were randomly assigned in each simulation as previously described. The substitution model was set to the optimal parameters of the GTR model estimated in the RAxML analysis. Each simulated dataset contains 30,000 SNPs, a value selected to approximate a typical reduced‐representation dataset.

To prepare the empirical SNP data for analysis in simcat, we first inferred a consensus sequence for the samples in each clade to reduce the size of the data and maximize the number of sites that have base calls across all species. We then generated 100 bootstrap replicate datasets that each randomly subsample a single SNP from each restriction site–associated DNA (RAD) locus to obtain a large sample of putatively unlinked SNPs. Finally, these replicate datasets were evaluated by the trained model to infer the placement of one introgression edge.

RESULTS

Training databases

We trained neural network models to detect introgression edges from site pattern frequencies in three different training databases. The first involves one introgression edge on an imbalanced eight‐tip species tree. The second database is similar to the first but adds a second introgression edge while including the existence of an initial introgression edge between taxa 2 and 4. The third database involves one introgression edge on a different eight‐tip species tree topology based on the species tree of the American live oak clade. Each database includes 60,000 simulations, of which 57,000 were used for training and 3000 for validation. Each model was trained to >75% accuracy on the validation data (which includes simulations with very low admixture proportions where we expect some failure of detection) and were allowed to continue training until the validation loss and accuracy plateaued. For each model, this resulted in 20–50 epochs of training, with each epoch taking between 10 and 30 minutes on one 2.2 GHz CPU.

Validation

Using the model trained on database A (one introgression edge), we examined the accuracy of simcat to identify the true introgression edge on simulated datasets. The percent accuracy is shown as a contour plot across a grid of parameter settings (Figure 5A), where a correct inference is recorded as the proportion out of 100 replicates in each grid cell that identified the correct introgression edge placement (source and destination). As expected, the accuracy is lower when the admixture proportion is lower. When this value is near 0.0, the inference is essentially random among the 84 possible scenarios. However, when the admixture proportion is between 0.05–0.15, accuracy climbs to >75%, and for higher magnitudes rises rapidly to >90%. Increasing the number of SNPs improves accuracy but quickly plateaus; in the scenarios tested, 10,000 SNPs appears sufficient for accurate inference. While this number will vary among datasets, it is well within the range that can be easily achieved from reduced‐representation or whole genome sequencing.

Validation and application of simcat. (A) Contour plots show the power of simcat to infer the categorical placement of an introgression edge on a species tree across a grid of 384 parameter settings. Accuracy is generally very high above a minimum threshold for the number of SNPs and admixture proportion. Contour plots were generated using the contourf function of matplotlib. (B) When the number of SNPs is held constant (20,000), the accuracy of simcat is relatively insensitive to variation in species tree parameters. Errors are greatest at the lowest and highest N e values, representing the boundary conditions on which the neural network model was trained. (C) A “confusion trees” plot shows variation in prediction accuracy for all 84 possible introgression edge placements. In each subplot, the true introgression edge placement is shown in black and predicted placements are shown as low‐opacity colored edges for 50 replicate simulated datasets, with correct results in green, incorrect results in red, and the positions randomly jittered for improved visibility. Errors are concentrated among a few difficult inference scenarios and are typically either off‐by‐one or involve incorrect directionality. (D) For two example scenarios (source‐destination 5,9 and 7,5), accuracy is greater when the admixture proportions are high (0.3), but the errors that arise in lower admixture proportion scenarios (0.1) similarly involve off‐by‐one or directionality errors. (E) When placing a second introgression edge given an existing one (shown in gray), errors are distributed similarly as when placing the first introgression edge. (F) When applied to an empirical dataset of >22,000 SNPs from the American live oak clade, our trained model predicted an introgression edge between Quercus fusiformis and Q. virginiana, consistent with previous results from Eaton et al. (2015).

Unexpectedly, simcat performed slightly worse at the lowest N e treatment compared to the two higher N e treatments across most parameter settings (Figure 5A). Because detecting admixture should generally be easier when ILS is lower, we expect that the lower accuracy in this scenario reflects the limited size of our training database, where there are fewer simulations that include all edges having relatively low N e. Increasing the size of the training database would likely address this. Despite there being higher ILS in the tests at N e = 250,000 or 350,000, these exhibited >95% accuracy across broad parts of the parameter space.

To further investigate the impact of species tree parameters, we examined accuracy across a range of N e × divergence time parameters while keeping the number of SNPs fixed at 20,000 (Figure 5B). As before, accuracy remains low when the admixture proportion is <0.05 but increases rapidly above that. Accuracy was greatest at intermediate values of N e and lower near the extremes (50,000 and 500,000), as these were the limits on our training database. The high accuracy across the middle range of this parameter space suggests that even with our relatively small training database, relative to the possible number of combinations of N e values and divergence times that could exist on this species tree, the model has learned to broadly associate site pattern frequencies with introgression edge scenarios. Extending the lower and upper limits of the training data would presumably increase accuracy further near the edge limits here. Finally, although the number of SNPs did not have a dramatic effect among the scenarios in Figure 5A, it is clear in Figure 5B that accuracy decreases in the most extreme ILS scenarios tested, involving a root height of 5e6 and N e of 500,000. This scenario would likely benefit from a larger SNP dataset to better distinguish admixture from ILS.

Confusion trees

Using the same model trained on database A, we examined replicate simulated datasets involving introgression under all 84 scenarios to identify the most common alternative introgression edge placements (Figure 5C). Using low opacity to show many overlapping introgression edge predictions, this “confusion trees” plot conveys information for each introgression scenario in a form similar to a typical confusion matrix, which is used to show which categories are misclassified in a machine learning problem. The vast majority of scenarios recovered 100% accuracy, with errors concentrated among approximately 10% of scenarios. When simcat inferred an incorrect introgression edge, it nearly always involved one of two types of error. The first involved identifying the correct pair of edges, but the incorrect directionality; the second involved scenarios where an internal edge was the true introgressive donor, but the model misidentified its child edge as the introgressive donor.

We subselected two of the scenarios that had 100% accuracy when the admixture proportion is 0.3 and further evaluated their classification accuracy when the admixture proportion is 0.1 (Figure 5D). As expected, the error rate is higher at lower admixture proportions, but once again, these errors are distributed among alternatives that are very similar to the true scenario, involving either an error in directionality or an off‐by‐one error. We repeated this process for examining errors in the placement of introgression edges for simulations involving the model trained on database B, which assumes an existing introgression edge between taxa 2 and 4, and aims to infer a second introgression edge. The confusion trees similarly show high accuracy, with errors concentrated among just a few scenarios and involving the same two categories of error types (Appendix S3). Future work might explore these error patterns more thoroughly to identify particularly difficult network topologies. Zooming in on two scenarios repeated at lower admixture proportions (Figure 5E) shows that errors associated with the placement of a second introgression edge by simcat appear to similarly involve scenarios very close to the true scenario.

Empirical application

To apply simcat to an empirical genomic dataset, we re‐assembled and analyzed RAD‐seq data from Eaton et al. (2015) for eight species in the plant clade Quercus ser. Virentes. The assembly recovered 63,833 RAD loci from 28 samples across the eight ingroup populations of interest. The filtered supermatrix alignment of RAD loci with sample coverage above 30 included 1.14 Mbp and approximately 15% missing data. The maximum likelihood phylogeny inferred from this alignment yielded the same tree topology previously reported by Eaton et al. (2015). We split Q. fusiformis into northern and southern populations, which have putatively distinct introgressive histories (Eaton et al., 2015). After collapsing samples into subclades, divergence time estimates inferred from the BPP algorithm on the fixed species tree topology supported a posterior mean crown age for the clade of 358,017 generations.

We trained a neural network model on a database of simulations initiated on this species tree. The model trained rapidly over the first 10 epochs and then slowed, gaining approximately 1–3% accuracy per remaining epoch. We allowed the model to train for 30 more epochs, by which time it no longer improved. We were wary of overfitting but relied on the built‐in dropout nodes to prevent it. The model's accuracy on training data (incorporating dropout) never surpassed its accuracy on the validation data. After training, the model showed approximately 80% accuracy on the validation data, which included many challenging scenarios with very low admixture proportions and/or high ILS.

After calling consensus sequences within each clade and filtering to require no missing data across clades, the final filtered dataset includes 22,382 loci. After resampling this dataset to randomly include one SNP per locus, we generated 100 bootstrap replicate datasets that each include 22,382 unlinked SNPs. We analyzed these replicate datasets using our trained neural network model to obtain 100 bootstrap replicate inferences of the placement of one introgression edge. The trained model overwhelmingly supported an introgression edge from Q. virginiana into the northern population of Q. fusiformis (forward in time), with all bootstrap replicates yielding an identical prediction (Figure 5F). We queried the categorical probabilities in simcat's output layer to find, on average, a >95% probability assigned to this source/destination combination across all replicates.

CONCLUSIONS

Here, we introduced simcat, a new method for inferring the placement of introgression edges on a fixed species tree based on site pattern frequencies in SNP datasets. The theoretical basis for our approach builds upon phylogenetic invariants, polynomial equations among the frequencies of site patterns that are expected to arise given a species tree topology (Cavender and Felsenstein, 1987; Lake, 1987; Allman and Rhodes, 2007). The addition of an introgression edge to a species tree disrupts certain phylogenetic invariant expectations, which has been previously exploited in methods like ABBA‐BABA tests to detect evidence of admixture (Hibbins and Hahn, 2022). We demonstrated that such patterns can be detected in higher‐dimensional space, among more than four or five taxa at a time, by examining a stacked matrix of site pattern frequencies among all quartet samples in a species tree. Similar to ABBA‐BABA tests, a distinct advantage of this approach is the ability to analyze large SNP datasets quickly, requiring only a species tree topology hypothesis, without the need to estimate species tree model parameters. Analyzing patterns in SNP data directly, as opposed to patterns of discordance among gene trees, bypasses issues of gene tree inference error that can affect two‐step network inference methods (Xi et al., 2015; Olave and Meyer, 2020; Morales‐Briones et al., 2021).

To identify high‐dimensional polynomials in site pattern frequencies and associate them with the unique placement of introgression edges on a species tree topology, we used methods from machine learning. We first used simple approaches, including random forest and t‐SNE, to demonstrate that stacked site pattern frequencies contain rich information for classifying introgression events. To achieve increased inference power and develop a method that can scale to very large datasets, we then developed a pipeline to make predictions from a neural network model (which scales better and is able to train incrementally) trained on a database of coalescent simulations. Our goal was for the model to learn polynomial relationships among site pattern frequencies that represent unique deviations from the species tree topology associated with the existence of one or more introgression edges. With sufficient model training, we expected that such topology‐associated patterns can be learned in a (nearly) non‐parametric framework, separate from the potential impacts of variation in species tree parameters.

Although simulating a training database for a species tree topology can be time consuming, the resulting trained model can be saved and reused, and very rapidly applied to make inferences. This allows examining model predictions over many replicate analyses, such as bootstrap resampling from a genome‐wide dataset or sliding windows along a genome. Similar to bitmap (pixel‐based) image data, our stacked site pattern matrix data structure can be efficiently analyzed by a neural network, as it retains a constant size for a given number of taxa in a dataset regardless of the amount of genomic data analyzed. Currently, our simcat approach implements a stepwise inference framework that is similar to existing network inference tools (Than et al., 2008; Solís‐Lemus et al., 2017). An initial introgression edge is first inferred on a species tree, and then a subsequent analysis can infer an additional edge conditional on the existence of the first. Although not demonstrated here, the potential to detect false positive inferences of admixture, when introgression did not in fact occur, should be possible within the simcat framework by introducing a stopping criterion. One idea would be to designate a lower threshold of detectability for introgressive edge magnitude (e.g., 0.02) and to create a new “no introgression” category for scenarios that fall beneath that threshold, thus training a model to discriminate between “no introgression” and “with introgression” scenarios. This threshold could be designated as a constant value by the user, or another strategy could potentially incorporate ABBA‐BABA symmetry tests to determine this threshold, as detectability of a static admixture proportion might vary based on the amount of discordance due to ILS. Alternatively, users might first estimate a set of edges in simcat and then apply a parametric method like PhyloNet to fit a likelihood model to each fixed network until adding an edge does not significantly improve the fit (Than et al., 2008). This approach would be similar to the recommended workflow in SNaQ. Finally, one of the most significant limitations of the current simcat approach is its reliance on a starting species tree hypothesis—similar to many other admixture inference approaches. In contrast to network inference methods that explore network space, our method will fail to find optimal solutions that differ greatly from the starting tree, which can arise when introgression proportions are high (Solís‐Lemus et al., 2016). Future work might seek to relax the assumption of a fixed backbone tree in simcat.

To validate the accuracy of simcat, we examined model predictions for many simulated datasets with known introgression scenarios generated across a range of species tree parameters, admixture proportions, and dataset sizes. The trained models were highly accurate at identifying the source and destination edges involved in introgression events from among the 84 categorical possibilities on an eight‐tip species tree. One key parameter not discussed here that requires further exploration is the effect of the training database size. Our results suggest that accuracy decreased when evaluating datasets generated under parameter settings near the boundaries of what was included in our training data. Expanding these boundaries and increasing the overall database size would presumably reduce these errors. While we produced a large enough training dataset (60,000 simulations) to train each model to reasonably high accuracy, a larger dataset would likely yield even better results.

When applying simcat to an empirical SNP dataset, we recovered 100% support for an introgression edge that is consistent with results previously reported by Eaton et al. (2015), in which the authors used multiple non‐independent four‐ and five‐taxon‐based tests to localize introgression. In both cases, a strong introgression event was identified between Q. virginiana and the northern clade of Q. fusiformis, which occur in sympatry in southeastern Texas. Calculating directionality of introgression can be challenging, but our analysis and theirs both agree in supporting introgression from Q. virginiana into Q. fusiformis (forward in time). Using our trained model, this result was consistent across all 100 bootstrap resampled replicates of the SNP dataset.

We envision several directions for future research that could extend our work to develop more powerful or convenient inference tools built on a similar theoretical basis. First, we can explore additional neural network model architectures. Our model structure is simple to understand and automate, but recent work has demonstrated that more complex approaches can be more powerful in generalizing learned patterns to new unobserved datasets (Mo and Siepel, 2023). Predictions from multiple types of models can also be joined together using ensemble deep learning approaches (Cao et al., 2020). With this in mind, future work might also incorporate a convolutional neural network approach, which could help reveal spatial patterns in the data within and across layers and has been leveraged previously for population genomic inference (Flagel et al., 2019). Here, we did not attempt to identify introgression between sister taxa, as these events are not likely to leave a topology‐associated pattern; however, future approaches that accommodate multiple samples per species may be able to address this. Furthermore, future work should investigate the impact of non‐neutral evolutionary processes, such as selection, on the accuracy of simcat predictions. For example, admixture is often maladaptive and purged by natural selection, which can cause detectable patterns both locally and genome‐wide (Kim et al., 2018; Aguillon et al., 2022). The signals of these non‐neutral processes on genomic variation have been the focus of other machine learning studies, and those results could provide guidance for incorporating selection into introgression inference (Schrider and Kern, 2016; Torada et al., 2019). Similarly, future approaches might explicitly incorporate variation in substitution model parameters for generating the training dataset, as substitution model complexity misleads inference (Frankel and Ané, 2023). Finally, although our focus here has been on detecting introgression, our general framework of training a machine learning model on phylogenetic invariant patterns has the potential to apply to phylogenetic inference as well. Although it is not feasible to learn phylogenetic invariant patterns associated with all potential topologies in tree space, a simcat‐like approach could be a powerful tool for the comparison of a few candidate topologies.

Inferring species histories from genome‐scale data is an ongoing challenge. The sheer quantity of data offers the potential to fit complex parameterized models; however, given the potentially infinite size of model space, it is often necessary to first identify the topological structure of a model in order to define the parameters that must be inferred. Currently, for large trees, this problem is best addressed by species tree inference first, and subsequently by applying network or admixture inference tools. Our simcat pipeline fills a gap between existing approaches, allowing the evaluation of large SNP datasets among quartet samples under a species tree hypothesis (similar to ABBA‐BABA tests) while also examining all site pattern information simultaneously, as opposed to individually (similar to network inference methods). We believe that this and similar future approaches that blend concepts from phylogenetic theory with state‐of‐the‐art inference methods, such as neural networks, can provide efficient methods for connecting complex patterns in genomic data to the hierarchical models, such as species tree and networks, that produced them.
