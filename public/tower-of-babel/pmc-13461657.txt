Methodological and bibliographical discrepancies in “Integrating cruroplasty with laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy: a strategy to mitigate GERD symptoms in obese patients. A randomized control trial”
Authors: Feruzjon Bobosharipov
Published: 2026

To the Editor-in-Chief,

Langenbeck’s Archives of Surgery.

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent randomized controlled trial by Firdaus Che Ani et al. titled “Integrating cruroplasty with laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy: a strategy to mitigate GERD symptoms in obese patients. A randomized controlled trial” (DOI: 10.1007/s00423-025-03827-0). The authors present a highly relevant and valuable contribution to the ongoing debate regarding the management of mild hiatal laxity during bariatric procedures. However, we would like to respectfully point out a few significant methodological and bibliographical discrepancies in the article that require clarification to prevent potential misguidance of the readership.

First, regarding the subjective assessment of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) symptoms, the authors state in the Introduction: “The severity of GERD varies, with symptom scores ranging from 0 on the HR-QoL GERD scale to a maximum of 144 in severe cases, which can progress to life-threatening conditions such as malignancy [4, 5]”. This statement appears to be inaccurate. The widely validated GERD-HRQL (Health-Related Quality of Life) questionnaire, originally developed by Velanovich, utilizes a scale with a maximum score of 50 (for the 10-item heartburn scale) or 75 (when including the 6-item regurgitation scale) [1]. Furthermore, references [4] and [5] cited by the authors to support this 144-point scale are the Montreal definition and classification articles. A careful review of both Montreal consensus papers reveals that they focus strictly on a symptom-based and complication-based definition of GERD and do not propose, discuss, or validate any 144-point scoring system. We kindly ask the authors to clarify which specific instrument was utilized in their study to assess the symptoms.

Second, there is a major discrepancy concerning reference [9] in the manuscript. The authors cite this reference to support anatomical and physiological claims, stating: “The human body possesses its natural anti-reflux mechanism; acute angle of His, physiological sphincter over the gastro-oesophageal junction (GOJ)… to prevent reflux [6, 9, 10]”. Later, they cite the same reference to support surgical management claims: “Various techniques have been proposed for managing GERD in patients with obesity, including Nissen fundoplication and laparoscopic gastric banding… [3, 5, 9]”. However, reference [9] (Alayed KM et al., The effects of laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy on body mass index (BMI) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, Cureus 2024) is a retrospective cohort study focused entirely on the metabolic and weight-loss outcomes of laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy in diabetic and non-diabetic patients. It contains absolutely no information regarding anti-reflux mechanisms, Nissen fundoplication, or gastric banding.

Given the importance of accurate symptom assessment and robust bibliographical support in high-quality surgical research, we believe it is crucial for the authors to address these points. Methodological clarity is especially important for surgeons seeking to replicate the study’s protocol to optimize bariatric interventions in patients with morbid obesity and concurrent hiatal defects.

We look forward to the authors’ response and thank the editorial board for the opportunity to discuss this important topic.
