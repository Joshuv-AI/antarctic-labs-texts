Impaired collagen deposition in the absence of ERp44
Authors: Maria Pannese, Barbara Canciani, Marta Carnovali, Marco Dalla Torre, Gianpiero Gallucci, Laura Mangiavini, Massimo Mariotti, Paola Panina-Bordignon, Rob Van’t Hof, Roberto Sitia, Tiziana Anelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe biogenesis of secretory proteins proceeds under sequential quality control checkpoints operating along the exocytic pathway. Unlike other chaperones that reside primarily in the endoplasmic reticulum, ERp44 cycles through the Golgi to control the assembly of polymeric proteins and the localization of a few endoplasmic reticulum resident enzymes (ERAP1, Prx4, Ero1α, and SUMF1). To gain information about its pathophysiological role, we generated ERp44-deficient models. ERp44 KO mice are smaller than control siblings, and show skeletal malformations and delayed bone development, with reduced collagen deposition. Similar skeletal defects were also observed in ERp44 knocked down zebrafish embryos, supporting a conserved role for ERp44 in skeletal development. In cellular models, ERp44 downregulation dramatically affects collagen type 1 deposition, causing intracellular procollagen 1 accumulation. We thus conclude that the levels of ERp44 are crucial for efficient collagen deposition.Subject areas: Biochemistry, Molecular biology, Developmental biology

Introduction

Most secretory proteins are synthesized by endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-bound ribosomes. Soon after their co-translational translocation, they begin their folding under the assistance of a cohort of chaperones and enzymes.1 Stringent sequential quality control (QC) checkpoints are located along the early secretory pathway (ER, ER to Golgi Intermediate Compartment - ERGIC-, cis-Golgi), ensuring that only correctly folded and assembled proteins can proceed to downstream compartments, and finally be secreted.2 Non-native structures are instead recognized and destined to degradation.3,4 The stepwise organization of the secretory pathway, which depends on the precise localization of chaperones along the secretory compartment, is perfectly suited for timing the numerous post-translational modifications, particularly for complex proteins that must be produced in abundance.

Following a precise sequential process is particularly critical for the biogenesis collagens, which are the major structural component of the extracellular matrix. Up to 28 different collagens have been identified in mammalian cells, and together the members of this superfamily represent the most abundant proteins of vertebrate organisms. Collagen type 1 is the predominant in bone matrix and tendons. Collagens can form different extracellular polymeric structures (fibrils or networks), but they all share a common fundamental trimeric module. Collagen are synthetized on ER-bound ribosomes; in the ER, monomers are hydroxylated and glycosylated, and then assembled in triple helices (homotrimeric or heterotrimeric) with peculiar biochemical features.5,6 The collagen-specific chaperone HSP47 assists collagens type 1–5,7,8,9 preventing their intracellular aggregation. Collagen trimers are then transported to the Golgi thanks to Tango, a specific cargo receptor.10 Fibrillar collagens are synthesized as precursors with N-terminal and C-terminal pro-peptides. For collagen type 1, trimeric assembly in the ER starts from the C-terminal pro-peptides and requires the formation of specific inter-chain disulphide bonds.11 Trimerization then continues in the C-N direction.12 Once the trimer is secreted, the N- and the C-termini of fibrillar collagens are cleaved by ADAMTS-2, 3, and 14, and BMP1.13,14 Cleavage exposes the so-called telopeptides involved in triple helix covalent cross-linking by LOX15,16; this results in higher order repetitive structures. Considering the abundant demand for collagen and its challenging biosynthesis, it is no surprise that many pathology arise not only from mutations in collagen genes but also in molecules assisting their folding, assembly and transport. Osteogenesis imperfecta and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome are among the most well-known disorders primarily caused by collagen misfolding.17,18

ERp44 is a key player in the stepwise maturation of disulphide-linked, polymeric secretory proteins.19,20,21 ERp44 cycles between ER, intermediate compartment (ERGIC), and cis-Golgi.20,22,23 The more acidic pH and higher zinc concentration of post-ER stations cause conformational changes exposing the client-binding site and the C-terminal RDEL (Arg - Asp - Glu - Leu) tetrapeptide needed for ER retrieval.22,24,25 In this way, ERp44-client complexes are recognized by KDEL (Lys - Asp - Glu - Leu) Receptors, and retrieved to the ER. In the neutral and zinc-poor ER lumen, ERp44 releases the client, binds the cargo receptor ERGIC-53, and swiftly starts for another cycle.20,23 ERp44 substrates include IgM, adiponectin, and other polymeric proteins linked by inter-chain disulphide bonds.20,21,26,27,28 ERp44 attacks non-native disulphide bonds, mediating QC and oligomerization.26 Interestingly, also soluble ER resident enzymes that lack a KDEL-like motif, such as Ero1α, ERAP1, peroxiredoxin 4, and SUMF1, depend on ERp44 for their localization.19,29,30,31 Hence, ERp44 is also crucial for controlling the composition of the early secretory pathway.21 In this connection, we recently demonstrated that, by interacting specifically with KDELR3, ERp44 activates downstream cascades regulating the expression of genes controlling the biogenesis of mucins and components of the extracellular matrix.32

In this study, we describe the phenotype of mice lacking ERp44. The ERp44 knockout (KO) mice that did not die in utero suffered from retarded skeletal growth and impaired collagen deposition, phenotypes somehow reminiscent of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Similar skeletal defects were evident in zebrafish embryos after ERp44 downregulation. Moreover, in osteosarcoma and fibroblast cell lines, ERp44 silencing caused impaired collagen 1A1 (Col1A1) deposition and intracellular accumulation of procollagen 1A1 (ProCol1A1), highlighting a critical role of ERp44 levels in collagen maturation, trafficking, and extracellular assembly.

Results

Generation of ERp44 KO mice

ERp44f/f mice were generated by the Knockout Mouse Project (KOMP) removing, through in vivo Flp recombination, the gene trap cassette of the ERp44tm1a(KOMP)WTsi mouse line (as detailed in the method details, Figure S1).33 The ERp44f/f mice were crossed with a CMV-Cre transgenic line, ubiquitously expressing Cre recombinase, to delete the ERp44 exon 2 located between the LoxP sites in all tissues, including germ cells. Only a few null mice (3.6%) were generated from heterozygous (Het) breeding, fewer than expected from Mendelian inheritance. Thus, also in our model, ERp44 KO mice exhibit embryonic lethality, consistent with previous reports on other ERp44 KO mouse models.18,30

Western blot (WB) analyses confirmed the absence of ERp44 protein in KO mice. Het animals had about half the protein compared to wild-type (WT) siblings (Figure 1A). Newborns ERp44 KO were smaller than WT littermates, while Het siblings appeared normal (Figure 1B). The weight differences between ERp44 KO and WT mice increased progressively during the first 16 days after birth (Figure 1C). After 10 weeks, the weight of ERp44 KO mice was about half of the normal siblings, the ratio remaining similar at 7 months of age (Figures 1D and 1E).

ERp44 KO mice also had a shorter life-span, and naturally died around 9 months of age.

The absence of ERp44 affects skeletal development

ERp44 KO mice displayed a typical hunched-back posture (Figure 2A). Computed tomography (CT) confirmed a significantly reduced skeletal dimension (Figures 2B and 2C) and revealed pronounced upper back kyphosis (Figure 2D). Length of femur, tibia, and foot were significantly shorter in KO mice (Figure 2C). Moreover, the cranial suture of the frontal bones remained open in 2-month-old KO animals, differently from WT and Het siblings (Figure 2E, top). The cranial suture was still partially open in KO mice at 5 months of age (Figure 2E, bottom).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans revealed a significant decrease in femur thickness in 2-month-old ERp44 KO mice (Figures 3A and 3B), and higher T2-relaxation time (T2rt) signal (Figure 3C), suggesting the presence of reduced calcified bone tissue. Therefore, femurs of 11 weeks old female and male mice siblings were analyzed by micro-computed tomography (μCT) (Figure 3D). Decreased bone volume fraction and trabecular thickness, an increased trabecular separation, and a trend toward fewer trabecula were evident in ERp44 KO animals with respect to WT controls. Given the low numbers of available siblings, female and male reference mice, of the same background (C57BL6/N) and age, were used as a further control, and considered as baseline in all analyses.

Allometric analysis was performed in male and female mice siblings at different age (4, 8 weeks-old and 7 months-old), to determine whether bone dimensions change proportionately with body size (Figures S2A and S2B). All three genotypes have a linear allometric relationship, implying that the femur grows proportionately with respect to body weight (BW) (Figure S2B). ERp44 KO mice had a leaner build than the other two genotypes, especially at young age. Indeed, the ratio between the femur length (F) and the BW are higher in animals at 8 weeks of age (Figure S2C). Overall, these results indicate that KO animals do not have overtly disproportionate bones, but do not exclude systemic effects due to the lack of ERp44 in causing the skeletal phenotype.

ERp44 KO mice show a delayed skeletal development

At 4 weeks of age, hematoxylin and eosin staining showed a larger epiphyseal plate (growth plate, GP) in KO and in Het compared to control WT mice (Figure 4A). At 11 weeks of age, the femurs of ERp44 KO mice exhibited normal morphology but were reduced in overall size. Bones from Het mice displayed cartilaginous epiphyseal plate and trabecular morphology similar to WT. The epiphyseal plate rod was also observed by polarized light after picrosirius red staining, to highlight possible differences or alterations in collagen type deposition. At 4 weeks of age (Figure S3A, top), however, no differences in collagen type arrangement or deposition were evident among the three genotypes. Nevertheless, images highlighted that primary trabecular bone was thinner in Het and KO mice than in WT siblings. Analysis with polarized light of picrosirius red stained femurs of 11 weeks-old mice (Figure S3B, top) revealed delay in bone mineralization in KO, and, at a lower degree, in Het epiphyseal plate rod: green colored type 3 and type 10 collagen fibers were more evident in KO and in Het when compared to WT, indicative of delayed cartilage conversion into mineralized bone.

Bone and epiphyseal plate tissues (cartilage plus mineralized cartilage: CC) were quantified by Stevenel’s/Van Gieson (SVG) staining at 4 and 11 weeks of age (Figure 4B, details of epiphyseal growth plate component; Figures S3A and S3B, middle). Reduced amounts of bone and CC tissues were evident in ERp44 KO mice at 4 weeks of age compared to WT siblings (Figure 4D). Differently from WT animals, the bone fraction were decreased in ERp44 KO mice at 11 weeks of age in comparison with ERp44 KO younger mice (Figure 4D). Het mice had an intermediate phenotype: at 4 weeks of age Het bone percentage was similar to WT, while CC was similar to KO. At 11 weeks of age instead, CC percentage in Het was higher when compared both to WT and KO.

Next, we quantified extracellular matrix proteoglycans in the epiphyseal plate rod by Safranin-O/light green staining (Figures 4C, arrowhead and 4D; S2A and S2B, bottom). At 4 weeks of age, proteoglycans increased significantly in the Het mice, while at 11 weeks, a significant increase was observed in KO mice.

Overall, our results demonstrate a delay in reabsorption of the cartilage in the epiphyseal plate, and in its replacement with mineralized cartilage and bone, which caused delayed bone increase and growth in ERp44 KO mice, and partially also in Het siblings.

ERp44 KO mouse skin and tendons show defects in collagen deposition

To evaluate whether ERp44 KO mice display systemic defects in collagen deposition, we included in our analyses the skin, another tissue rich of collagen. Samples of skin from mice at 11 weeks of age of the three genotypes were analyzed and sections were stained by hematoxylin and eosin, Azan-Mallory Trichrome, and picrosirius red (3 images/animal and 10 measures/image). Due to the sex-related differences in mouse skin morphology,34 dermal thickness (Figure 5A, top and bottom, respectively) was evaluated separately in male and female. Regardless the gender, dermal thickness was significantly decreased in ERp44 KO mice with respect to WT and Het animals (Figure 5B).

Lowering ERp44 expression in zebrafish embryos causes skeletal defects

Based on these data, we hypothesized that the reduction in derma layer thickness and tendon collagen fibers diameter reflected defective collagen deposition, as observed in mice models bearing mutations in collagen genes, or in collagen processing enzymes.35,36 Since only a few ERp44 KO mice are born, we extended our analysis to zebrafish, another model well suited for skeletal studies.

To this end, we downregulated ERp44 expression by injecting low concentrations of Morpholinos (MOs, 10 μM) targeting the start codon of ERp44 mRNA18 in fertilized zebrafish eggs: MOs are synthetic oligonucleotides designed to inhibit translation. Standard control MOs were also used. Fertilized eggs were treated with 10 μM of MOs as a maximum dose, as higher MO concentrations caused excess embryo mortality (Figure 6A). Embryo development was followed up to 5 days post fertilization (pf). Day 5 pf embryos were fixed and stained to visualize cartilage and mineralized bones. As evident in Figures 6B and 6C, ERp44 MO-treated embryos display delayed mineralization, with a reduced number of stained vertebrae (see arrows) compared to untreated embryos (ctrl) or embryos treated with control MO (ctrl MO).

Intracellular accumulation and defective extracellular deposition of Procollagen1A1 under ERp44 downregulation

To investigate how reduced levels of ERp44 cause skeletal defects, we designed in vitro experiments downregulating ERp44 in cells producing collagen type 1, the major components of bones and tendons. SAOS-2 and BJ cells (human osteosarcoma cells and immortalized fibroblasts, respectively)37,38,39 were treated with ERp44-specific silencers (si44), or scrambled control duplexes (siC) as controls. Three days after silencing, cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence (IF) or WB. The soluble material secreted by cells in 6 h of culture was also analyzed to investigate collagen release.

Silencing reduced intracellular ERp44 levels up to ≤25% of the baseline in both cell lines (Figures S4A and S6A).

ERp44 KD caused significant accumulation of intracellular Col1A1 in SAOS-2 cells (Figure 7A). WB analyses revealed the presence of abundant uncleaved precursor as well as of some mature Col1A1 (identified by the differences in gel migration) in cell extracts. In ERp44 silenced cells, the precursor form (Pro-Col1A1) increased at the expense of the mature form. Mature form in the cell protein extracts could result from the attachment to the cell membrane of the secreted processed procollagen. IF analyses (Figure 7B) confirmed parallel decrease of extracellular fibrillary Col1 deposits, with increment of ProCol1A1 accumulation. Intracellular condensed ProCol1A1 structures were detected in almost 20% of ERp44 KD cells analyzed (Figure S5). The WB analysis of the secreted soluble material in the culture supernatants revealed only a slight decrease in extracellular Col1A1 levels (Figure S4B). No changes were detected in the total amount of secreted proteins, as visualized with SYPRO Ruby staining (Figure S4C). Thus, the decrease in extracellular Col1A1 levels does not reflect a general collapse of protein secretion in the absence of ERp44.

Similar results were obtained with BJ cells (Figure 8). Upon ERp44 KD, the increase of intracellular Col1A1 accumulation was even greater (Figure 8A), virtually all in the procollagen form. Non-reducing gels revealed the accumulation of trimers and lower molecular weight (MW) Col1A1 structures, likely representing intermediates or covalent complexes with chaperones or folding enzymes (Figure 8B). In accordance with the WB analysis, IF images also indicate intracellular accumulation of abundant Pro-Col1A1 in si44 cells (Figure 8C, right column). While long and thick Col1 fibers were visible in IF surrounding untreated cells (Figure 8C upper image, siC), much shorter and thinner Col1 fibers were detected extracellularly upon ERp44 KD (Figure 8C, bottom image). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that the downregulation of ERp44 severely affected extracellular collagen fibers (Figure 8D), with loss of the classical fibrillary structure in the KD sample. However, the total amount of secreted Col1A1 was only slightly lower (Figure S6C). Similar levels of fibronectin were found extracellularly in treated and control cells, by WB (Figure S6D) and IF (Figure S6E), confirming that ERp44 downregulation did not cause a general block in protein secretion in BJ cells (Figure S6C). The high intracellular ProCol1A1 levels thus indicate that the lack of ERp44 specifically affected Collagen1A1 trafficking, QC and assembly.

Discussion

As efficient protein secretion requires a cohort of assistants working in an orderly sequence,40 the stepwise structure of the secretory compartment provides an ideal assembly line.41,42 Only if a given step in the folding schedule is accomplished, can the product proceed to the next one. Hence, the sequential checkpoints operated by BiP and ERp44 guarantee the secretion of challenging-to-fold proteins, such as IgM, in large numbers and of high quality.20,21,26,43

To gain insight into the role of ERp44 at the physiological and organismal level, we generated a novel ERp44 KO mouse line. Our model shares similarities with previously described ERp44 deficient animals: embryonic lethality, small size, and short life-span.18,30,44 However, there are also intriguing phenotypic differences in the survivors. One line30 displayed severe hypotension caused by angiotensin II cleavage by secreted ERAP1 (a known ERp44 client). Another line instead had cardiac defects.18,44 Likely, the different genetic backgrounds offer alternative routes for complementing the absence of ERp44.

Owing to embryonic lethality and strict animal protection rules, we could analyze only a limited number of ERp44 KO animals, which sometimes limited statistical analysis. However, our results consistently point to defective collagen biogenesis in the absence of ERp44. The few KO animals that are born are always smaller than their WT littermates, the growth gap widening with age. An overt skeletal phenotype emerges, characterized by delayed bone mineralization, impaired growth, reduced derma collagen deposition, and thinner collagen fibers in tail tendons. Similarly, fertilized zebrafish eggs generate embryos with a reduced number of mineralized vertebrae when ERp44 translation is inhibited. In both mice and zebrafish, in the absence of ERp44, fewer and thinner Collagen1A1 fibers are deposited in the extracellular matrix and fibrous tissues, which could finally lead to severely delayed bone mineralization.

Overall, our in vivo data confirm that the absence of ERp44 causes skeletal defects. The most likely explanation of these phenotypic observations is that ERp44 plays an important role in controlling the biogenesis of collagens, most complex-to-fold molecules that must be produced in large amounts. This notion is supported by two human cellular models, SAOS-2 and BJ: silencing ERp44 in these cell lines severely impaired the formation of extracellular collagen type 1 fibers, and causes intracellular accumulation of Pro-Collagen1A1. The effects of ERp44 could be direct or indirect; however, they remain client specific. Indeed fibronectin, another huge molecule rich in disulphide bonds, and other secretory proteins reach their destination in ERp44 KD cells: thus, rather than a general collapse of secretory potentials, physiological levels of ERp44 are necessary for collagen trafficking and processing, as also described for polymeric IgM26,45 and adiponectin.27,28

The abundance of ERp44 in professional secretory cells points at a special role in high-rate protein secretion. Clearly certain cargoes (e.g., IgM) need its assistance more than others (e.g., IgG, fibronectin). Moreover, recent results revealed the central role of ERp44 in protein, redox, and zinc homeostasis22,23 as well as KDELR signaling32 also in non-secretory cells. Thus, the absence, or even low levels, of ERp44 are bound to have systemic long-distance, organismal consequences. Accordingly, most ERp44 KO mice die in utero, and allometric analyses reveal that at 8 weeks of age, ERp44 KO mice are thinner than control siblings. Studies with tissue-specific deletions are clearly needed.

Mechanistically, ERp44 could form mixed disulfides with ProCol1A1.46 At the proper pH and zinc concentrations, ERp44 preferentially attacks non-native disulfides.21,25,26 Since Col1A1 trimerization requires the formation of specific inter-chain disulphide bonds,11 ERp44 could be needed to isomerize non-native disulphide bonds, as described for IgM.26,45 Accordingly, different redox isoforms of Pro-Col1A1 accumulate in ERp44 KD cells. An alternative and not mutually exclusive possibility is that ERp44 controls the activity or the localization of other chaperones or enzymes essential in the collagen factories. Indeed, we recently demonstrated32 that, through specific interactions with KDELR3, ERp44 modulates the transcription of chaperones and glycosylation enzymes, important for the biogenesis of mucins and other components of the extracellular matrix.

Our findings have relevant clinical implications. The phenotype of our ERp44 KO mice may resemble that of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, in particular the kyphoscoliotic and spondylodysplastic variants.36,47 This overlap suggests a potentially broader role for ERp44 in connective tissue integrity. It will be important to explore whether alterations in ERp44 expression or function contribute to the etiology of collagen-related disorders.

Limitations of the study

As stated in the results and discussion, our studies were performed on a low number of mice, which limited statistical analyses. Nonetheless, the consistent phenotypes obtained in animal (mice and fish) and cell models (BJ and SAOS-2 cells) make us confident of the results obtained. Tissue-specific KO animals are needed to circumvent the embryonic lethality of ERp44 KO mice.

The molecular mechanisms underlying the phenotypes observed remain to be understood. ERp44 could be directly involved in collagen folding. Alternatively, it could act indirectly, controlling the activity of other collagen modifying enzymes or modulating a KDELR-dependent signaling circuit that we have recently identified.32 These issues are now under investigation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Murine model

Murine model was generated as detailed in the method details section.

Mice were housed and maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle at constant temperature (23°C). Food and water were available ad libitum. Precise details about experimental group size and composition, age and sex of the animals, genotype, and weight of the animals are indicated in the relevant sections of the results, and/or in the corresponding Figure legend.

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of IRCCS (Istituto di Ricerca e Cura a Carattere Scientifico) San Raffaele Scientific Institute and were communicated to the Italian Ministry of Health and local authorities according to Italian law (permission number 921/2015-PR and 922/2015-PR).

Zebrafish model

Danio rerio of AB strain were housed in a ZEBTEC© Bench Top System (Tecniplast, Buguggiate, VA, Italy) and maintained under standard conditions48 at 28°C. Zebrafish eggs were obtained by single pair of adults breeding and were checked for general health conditions under a light stereomicroscope.49 Experiments were performed in the Zebrafish Laboratory (IRCCS Ospedale Galeazzi-Sant'Ambrogio, GSD Foundation, Milan, Italy) according to Italian and European guidelines on research (EU Directive 2010/63/EU) with authorization by ATS Varese with Prot. No. 2019/014/DVVS/0078143, Italy. As indicated in method details and results, embryos were raised up to 5 days post fertilization and then evaluated for osteogenensis.

Cell lines

SAOS-2 cells (human osteosarcoma-derived cells, female, Saos-2-HTB-285, ATCC) and BJ cells (human immortalized foreskin fibroblast, male, BJ-CRL-2522, ATCC) were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FCS (Euroclone, Pero, Italy), 2mM L-glutamine (Euroclone, Pero, Italy) and 25 μg/ml ascorbic acid. Standard cell culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2, 90-95% relative humidity) were maintained throughout the cell culturing and during the experimental procedures, until cells were collected or otherwise processed (see method details).

Method details

Reagents

Chemicals and reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA), unless otherwise specified. Custom oligonucleotides were purchased from Metabion International AG (Planegg, Germany).

Antibodies

The following antibodies were used: mouse monoclonal anti-ERp44 36C9, that has been previously described.20 Rabbit anti-ERp44 antibody (Proteintech, Manchester, UK); mouse monoclonal anti-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA); mouse monoclonal anti-ProCol1A1 SP1D8 (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank); rabbit polyclonal anti-Col1A1 (Ab138492, Abcam, Cambridge, UK); mouse monoclonal (IgG1,K) anti-human fibronectin (R&D Systems); rabbit anti-calreticulin polyclonal (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, USA), Alexa Fluor conjugated secondary antibodies 488, 647 (Invitrogen Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA). Reagent codes and RRIDs are reported in the key resources table. Dilution and incubation conditions are detailed in the following section.

Murine model generation

The ERp44tm1a(KOMP)WTsi mouse line, (project: CSD36727) was generated by the Knockout Mouse Project (KOMP), using the KO-first strategy.33 The KO-first strategy allows the generation of conditional deletion of the gene of interest. By crossing “ERp44 KO-first” with flippase deleter mice, the KO-first cassette flanked by FRT sites was removed, and ERp44 gene function is restored, leaving 2 loxP sites flanking exon 2 of ERp44 (ERp44f/f). ERp44f/f mice were generated by KOMP. To obtain ERp44 knockout mice, we crossed ERp44f/f mice with CMV-Cre mice (B6.C-Tg(CMV-cre1Cgn/J); Strain #:006054) purchased from Jackson laboratory. The offspring were self-crossed to obtain ERp44 knockout mice, and the Cre allele was bred out of the colony before study.

Mouse genotyping

PCR analysis of genomic DNA, extracted from mouse tail, was used for genotype identification. The following primers were used:

Floxed allele: CDS-loxF (5′ GAGATGGCGCAACGCAATTAATG 3′) and CDS-Erp44-R (5′CCACCATATCAACCATGCCTTGACC 3′) amplicon size 341bp.

Wild type allele: CDS-Erp44-F (5′ AGTAGCGTCCAATGGAGTATCTGGC 3′) and CDS-Erp44-ttR (5′AGCATTCATCTCAGACAGAGCCTGG 3′) amplicon size 421bp.

PostFlp & Cre allele: CDS-Erp44-F (5′ AGTAGCGTCCAATGGAGTATCTGGC 3′) CDS-Erp44-R (5′ CCACCATATCAACCATGCCTTGACC 3′) amplicon size 659bp.

To detect Cre recombinase: CRE-FW (5′ GCCTGCATTACCGGTCGATGCAACGA 3′); CRE-R (5′ GTGGCAGATGGCGCGGCAACACCATT 3′, amplicon size 750bp.

The PCR was performed using GoTaq DNA polymerase (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) following manufacturer’s instruction. The amplicons were separated on a 2.8% agarose gel.

Morpholino ERp44 downregulation in zebrafish

To downregulated ERp44 in Zebrafish embryos, fertilized eggs were microinjected with approximately 2nL of Morpholino against ERp44, scrambled Morpholino, or demineralized water (as a control group). Microinjection was performed using borosilicate glass micro-capillaries (20 mm O.D. 15 mm I.D.; Eppendorf) and UMM-3FC micromanipulator (Narishige). Optimal morpholino concentration was determined experimentally, by increasing concentration of anti-ERp44 Morpholino, ranging from 0 μM to 1000 μM. In the experiments, a concentration of 10 μM was used for both ERp44 and scrambled Morpholinos. Embryos were raised up to 5 days post fertilization and then evaluated for osteogenensis. Morpholinos were purchased from Gene Tool LLC (Philomath, OR, USA).

Morpholino against ERp44 start codon (ATG Mo18): GATGTTGTTGAATCTTGCCAGTCT

Standard control: CTTCTTACCTCAGTTACAATTTATA

Cell culture and silencing

SAOS-2 cells (human osteosarcoma derived cells, Saos-2-HTB-285, ATCC) and BJ cells (human immortalized skin fibroblast, BJ-CRL-2522, ATCC) were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FCS (Euroclone, Pero, Italy), 2mM L-glutamine (Euroclone, Pero, Italy) and 25 μg/ml ascorbic acid.

Silencing was performed with Lipofectamine RNAiMax reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, USA) according to manufacturer’s instruction. Cells were plated in 6-well plates or 6 cm Petri dish, to have about 50-60% confluence on the day of transfection. Duplexes for ERp44 downregulation have been previously described20 (ERp44 f 5′ CAACUCUGGCAAACACUAC 3′, ERp44 rev 5′ GUAGUGUUUGCCAGAGUUG 3′); MISSION® siRNA Universal Negative Control (MERCK) was used as a control (siC). 3 μL duplexes (20 μM) were used for each well of a 6-well plate, with doubled reagent amount for 6 cm dishes. Cells were incubated overnight at 37% and 5% CO2. After 16 hours, the supernatant was replaced with 2 mL of fresh medium.

Both cell lines were analyzed 72 hours after silencing,20 and silencing efficacy was confirmed by WB analysis as described in the results.

Western blot assay

Western blot assays were performed as previously described (e.g.,19,20). Technical details are provided below. To obtain protein extract from mice, organs were solubilized in RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4), supplemented with 10 mM NEM, cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and additional 2% SDS. DNA was fragmented by sonication for 30 seconds (Fisherbrand sonifier, Marshall Scientific, Hampton, USA).

Protein concentration of lysates was determined by BCA assay, and the same amount of proteins (50 μg for sample) was loaded under reducing conditions (50mM Dithiothreitol, DTT) on precast polyacrylamide 4-12% SDS-PAGE gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltman, USA).

To obtain protein extracts from cell cultures, cells were detached by trypsinization, washed once in in ice-cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and once in PBS with 10 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) to block disulfide interchange.50 Lysis was performed with RIPA buffer, supplemented with 10 mM NEM, cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), for 15 minutes on ice (100.000 cells in 10 μL of lysis buffer). Samples were then centrifuged at 18.000g for 15 minutes at 4°C, and aliquots of the post-nuclear supernatants were loaded on SDS-PAGE (pre-cast 4-12% poly-acrylamide gradient gels) (Thermofisher, Waltham, MA), 40 μg proteins/lane; when analyzed in reducing conditions, protein samples were previously reduced with 50 mM Dithiothreitol (DTT).

Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose (0.2μm) which was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS 0.1% tween, and then decorated with primary antibodies, in PBS 0.1% Tween, 2% non-fat milk, and secondary antibodies 1:1000 in PBS, 0.1% tween. Signals were detected by laser scanning technology by FujiFilm (FLA 9000) (FujiFilm Life Science, Tokyo, Japan) or by Azure SapphireTM (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA); densitometric analysis of the images was performed with FiJi software. Actin or total protein staining (ponceau) were used for normalization.

Mouse tissue lysates: rabbit anti-ERp44 antibody (dilution 1:500, Proteintech, Manchester, UK), mouse monoclonal anti-β actin (dilution 1:2000, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA).

Cell lysates: mouse monoclonal antibody anti-ERp44 36C9 (1:1000), mouse monoclonal anti-ProCol1A1 SP1D8 (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank) 1:500; rabbit polyclonal anti-Col1A1 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) 1:500; mouse monoclonal (IgG1,K) anti-human fibronectin (R&D Systems) 1:500.

Secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor conjugated (647 or 488) 1:1000.

Secretion assay

Secretion assays were performed 72 hours after silencing. Cells were washed three times in PBS and kept in OptiMEM (serum-free rich medium, Euroclone, Pero, Italy) containing 25μg/mL ascorbic acid, for 6 hours at 37°C, 5% CO2. Culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged once (800 g for 5 minutes) to remove debris and detached cells. In parallel, cells were detached from the plate with trypsin (as described above) and counted. Volumes of supernatants corresponding to the same number of cells were precipitated in 10% Trichloroacetic Acid (TCA) (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA), resuspended in sample buffer, and loaded on 4-12% pre-cast polyacrylamide gels under reducing conditions. To analyze total secreted material, gels were then stained with Sypro Ruby (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) following manufacturer’s instructions. To analyze collagen and fibronectin secretion, proteins were transferred on nitrocellulose (0.2μm) and analyzed by Western Blot as described above. Images we acquired by Azure SapphireTM (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA) and processed with ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence analyses, SAOS-2 and BJ cells were plated on 13mm glass slides, subjected to siRNA with ERp44-specific duplexes (si44) or irrelevant scrambled controls (siC), and analyzed 72 hours later. Cells were fixed in cold (-20°C) methanol for a few seconds (for extracellular Col1A1), or in 4% buffered formaldehyde (PFA) for 10 minutes at room temperature (for ProCol1A1, fibronectin and calreticulin). Cells fixed with PFA were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 minutes at room temperature (RT), while methanol-fixed slides didn’t require further permeabilization. Slides were incubated with blocking solution (PBS containing 5% fetal calf serum, FCS) for 30 minutes at RT, and subsequently decorated with primary antibodies (diluted 1:100 in blocking solution) for up to 3 hours at RT. Afterwards, slides were incubated with secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-mouse Alexa conjugated antibodies, diluted 1:1000 in blocking solution) for 30 minutes at RT, counterstained with DAPI (1:5000 in PBS, for 5 minutes at RT) and mounted with Mowiol 4-88 (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). Images were acquired with DeltaVision Ultra microscope (GE healthcare), equipped with a 60× magnification oil lens (Olympus ×60/1.42, Plan Apo N, UIS2, 1-U2B933).

Transmission electron microscopy

Samples (mice terminal tail pieces or cells plated in 33mm diameter dishes) were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium-cacodylate buffer pH 7.4, for 1h at RT, washed three times with cacodylate buffer, and incubated with 1% osmium tetroxideand 1.5% potassium ferrocyanide in 0.1 M cacodylate for 1h on ice. After repeated washes with distilled water, en bloc staining was performed overnight at 4°C with 0.5% uranyl acetate in water.

Then, the samples were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%,70%, 80%,90%,96%, 5 min each; absolute ethanol, three washes, 10 min each), infiltrated in 1:1 ethanol/Epon 812 solution for 2 h, in 100% Epon twice for 1 h, and finally covered with a layer of Epon and polymerized in an oven at 60°C for 48 h.

A part of the sample was glued on an Epon block and sectioned with a Leica Ultracut UCT ultramicrotome. Ultrathin (70-90 nm) sections were collected on copper grids, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and finally examined with a transmission electron microscope (TALOS L120C ThermoScientific) at 120 kV.

The measurement of collagen fiber diameter was performed analyzing EM images with FiJi Software.

Micro-CT

Femurs from 11 week-old mice were dissected out and fixed for 24 hours in 4% buffered paraformaldehyde. After fixation, the bones were washed in PBS and stored in 70% ethanol until analysis. The femurs were scanned using a Skyscan 1272 μCT scanner (Bruker, Belgium) at a resolution of 4.5 μm (60 kV, 150 μA, rotation step size 0.3°, using a 0.5 mm aluminium filter). Scans were then reconstructed using Skyscan NRecon software (Bruker, Belgium). Trabecular bone was separated from cortical bone using a macro in CTAn (Bruker, Belgium), and analyzed in 200 slices in the distal metaphysis of the femurs using Skyscan CTAn software as described by van ‘t Hof et al.51 As the number of mutant animals available for the analysis was very low, the mutant mice and their littermates were compared to reference groups of age- and sex-matched wild-type mice from the same colony using one-way ANOVA. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (6.0 version).

Histology – mouse tissues

After collection, samples were rinsed in PBS, then fixed in 4% (v/v) phosphate-buffered formaldehyde (Bio-Optica Milano, Italy) overnight at RT. Samples were then dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol, cleared in xylene (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and embedded in paraffin (Bio-Optica Milano, Italy). Histological sections were then cut at a thickness of 5 μm.

Multiple stainings were carried out: a) hematoxylin and eosin staining (Histo Line, Pantigliate, MI, Italy) for comparative morphology; b) Picrosirius Red staining (Abcam Laboratories, Cambridge, UK) for evaluation of collagen types I and III; c) Stevenel’s /Van Gieson staining (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for quantification of cartilage plus mineralized cartilage (mainly collagen type II) and bone (mainly collagen type I) in the growth plate; d) Safranin-O/light green staining (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for quantification of proteoglycans of the cartilage in the growth plate; e) Azan Mallory trichrome staining (Histo Line, Pantigliate, MI, Italy) for thickness quantification of the derma layer. After staining, sections were dehydrated end cleared, and finally sealed on coverslips using Eukitt mounting medium (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Images were acquired for a, c d, e stainings with a Zeiss Axiophot (Zeiss, Baden, Wurttemberg, Germany) microscope (objectives 2.5x and 10×) equipped with a Leica DFC450 C camera (Leica, Oberkochen, Germany). Picrosirius Red staining images were acquired by the same microscope by polarized light filters.

Bone, cartilage and proteoglycan surface percentage were calculated in femoral growth plates, by using three images of one femoral growth plate for each animal, at 4 and at 11 weeks of age (n=2 animals per genotype for each time-point, 3 histological images per animal).

Dermal thickness was measured on skin sections perpendicular to the surface, and excluding the epidermal layer. Three images were analyzed for each animal, with multiple measurements performed in each image (n=3 histological images per animal; n=10 thickness measurement per image). Quantification for the growth plate percentages and dermal thickness was obtained by using ImageJ (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij Java1.54, NIH, USA) Software.

Histology – zebrafish embryos

At 5 days post fertilization, zebrafish embryos were anaesthetized with 0.01% tricaine methasulphonate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) solution, and then fixed in a 3.5% formaldehyde/0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer. A two-color acid-free staining based on Alcian Blue 8GX (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and Alizarin Red S (ARS, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was performed to stain, respectively, cartilage and bone tissue. Mineralization rate was evaluated by counting the number of mineralized vertebral bodies (N) normalized for each embryo length (L) (N/L). Embryos were examined under a light stereomicroscope (SZX-ZB7 Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and images acquired using a Discovery CH30 camera.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantifications

Quantifications were performed by ImageJ (https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.2089) or FiJi (https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.2019), as further detailed in the method details. Precise details about number and meaning of n in the different experiments can be found in the figure legends and in the results section.

Results were considered statistically significant with p < 0.05, with more details on the significance levels indicated in Figure legends.
