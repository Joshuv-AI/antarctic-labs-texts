Introduction

Polyorchidism represents an uncommon congenital anomaly of the genitourinary tract characterized by the presence of supernumerary testes, with fewer than 200 cases documented in the medical literature.1 While this structural anomaly can manifest in various clinical presentations, triorchidism remains the most frequently documented presentation, whereas bilateral duplication leading to four testes is exceedingly rare.2 Historically, epidemiological data established by Bergholz et al Indicate that approximately 65% of these anomalies are located on the left side, with a reported median patient age of 17 years.3 Although most individuals with polyorchidism are entirely asymptomatic and diagnosed incidentally during routine or unrelated clinical exams, this condition is frequently linked to pathologies such as inguinal hernia, cryptorchidism, hydrocele, testicular torsion, and a predisposition to testicular malignancies.1,4 Consequently, some investigators advocate for the prophylactic orchiectomy of supernumerary testes in adult patients, citing an estimated 4% to 7% lifetime risk of malignant transformation.5

Herein, we report an incidental finding of right-sided triorchidism in a fertile 27-year-old male who initially presented with a right inguinal hernia. This case highlights a rare anatomical variation where the supernumerary testis possessed a completely independent spermatic cord and ductal system. By documenting our successful conservative management via orchiopexy and structured follow-up, we aim to advocate for organ-preserving strategies in young, fertile patients and expand the literature on this rare anomaly.

Case Presentation

Initial Evaluation and History

A 27-year-old male presented to our department with a chief complaint of a right inguinal swelling that had been progressively enlarging over the previous few months. The swelling was associated with mild local discomfort, characterized by a noticeable increase in size and prominence upon standing or coughing, and spontaneous reduction upon lying flat (recumbency). The patient denied any history of nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or changes in bowel habits. His past medical, surgical, and family histories were entirely unremarkable, and he was not taking any regular medications He was married, reported an active sexual life, and had three children.

Physical Examination

On clinical examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable and afebrile. Abdominal examination revealed a soft, non-distended abdomen with no tenderness or palpable masses. Local examination of the right inguinal region demonstrated a soft, non-tender, reducible swelling with a positive cough impulse, consistent with a right inguinal hernia.

Scrotal examination revealed an interesting anatomical anomaly: the left hemiscrotum contained a single, normal-sized testis. However, palpation of the right side of the scrotum and the lower inguinal canal area revealed the presence of two distinct, independent, non-tender masses resembling testicular structures, both exhibiting normal consistency.

Ultrasound and Color Doppler Findings

A comprehensive scrotal and inguinal color Doppler ultrasound (US) was performed to further evaluate the anatomy. The imaging confirmed a right indirect inguinal hernia. Remarkably, the ultrasound also confirmed polyorchidism (triorchidism), demonstrating two separate testes within the right hemiscrotum:

The superior (supernumerary) right testis was smaller, measuring approximately 19×25 × 35 mm. Notably, it demonstrated multiple cystic dilatations originating from the mediastinum testis, consistent with tubular ectasia of the rete testis. The Inferior right testis was of normal size, measuring 25×29 × 43 mm, with a completely homogeneous parenchymal echotexture and no evidence of focal masses or calcifications. The left testis was normal in both morphology and size, measuring 24×35 × 60 mm [Figure 1]. Its parenchyma was homogeneous with normal, preserved vascularity on Doppler flow imaging, and no signs of calcifications or overt masses. Bilaterally, the epididymides were unremarkable, demonstrating normal size and morphology, with no evidence of significant hydrocele on either side. Vascular assessment of the left spermatic cord revealed mild venous dilatations measuring up to 2.3 mm without significant retrograde reflux during the Valsalva maneuver, consistent with a grade I left varicocele. No abnormal venous dilatations or varicoceles were detected along the right spermatic cord.

Surgical Management and Intraoperative Findings

The patient underwent an elective surgical exploration through a standard right inguinal approach. During the operation, an indirect inguinal hernia sac was identified, isolated, and high ligation was performed. In addition, surgical exploration confirmed the presence of two distinct testes on the right side [Figure 2]. The superior, smaller testis was identified as the supernumerary testis. It possessed a completely independent spermatic cord, a separate epididymis, and a separate vas deferens, with a healthy and viable blood supply. These distinct anatomical findings, together with the characteristic testicular parenchyma and preserved vascularity demonstrated on preoperative color Doppler ultrasonography, supported the diagnosis of a distinct supernumerary testis.

Intraoperative findings during right inguinal exploration. Sequential intraoperative photographs showing two separate right-sided testes encountered during surgical exploration for right indirect inguinal hernia. The superior supernumerary testis appeared smaller but viable, with preserved vascular supply and separate epididymal and vasal structures. Following assessment of viability and absence of suspicious macroscopic features, conservative management was chosen, and right orchiopexy was performed rather than orchiectomy.

The combination of characteristic testicular tissue and vascularity on ultrasonography and the distinct anatomical structures identified intraoperatively made alternative diagnoses, such as ectopic splenic tissue or splenogonadal fusion, unlikely.

Hernioplasty was successfully performed using a synthetic mesh. Given the benign ultrasound appearance, the presence of tubular ectasia, and the normal macroscopic appearance and viability of the smaller supernumerary testis, the surgical team decided against orchiectomy in order to preserve the patient’s endocrine and reproductive potential. Instead, a right-sided orchiopexy was performed, securely fixing the smaller supernumerary testis within the right hemiscrotum alongside the primary testis while avoiding twisting of its vascular pedicle.

Postoperative Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative period was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in excellent condition. The patient was counseled regarding the nature of polyorchidism and the potential risks associated with supernumerary testes, including torsion and malignant transformation. He was instructed to perform regular testicular self-examination and was scheduled for periodic clinical assessment and scrotal color Doppler ultrasound to monitor the size, echotexture, and vascularity of the intra-scrotal testes.

At the initial 3-month postoperative follow-up visit, the patient was completely asymptomatic, and scrotal color Doppler ultrasound demonstrated stable findings with preserved vascularity in all three testes, with no evidence of acute complications. A long-term follow-up plan was established for continued clinical and ultrasonographic surveillance [Table 1].

Discussion

Polyorchidism is an exceptionally rare congenital anomaly of the male genitourinary system characterized by the presence of more than two testes. Triorchidism is the most commonly reported form, and the condition is frequently associated with ipsilateral inguinal hernia, cryptorchidism, and other inguinoscrotal abnormalities.1,6 Although most reported cases have been described in children and young adults, adult presentations remain uncommon. In the present case, a 27-year-old fertile man was diagnosed preoperatively with right-sided triorchidism during evaluation of an indirect inguinal hernia. The combination of a normally positioned, viable supernumerary testis with an independent spermatic cord, epididymis, and vas deferens, together with successful conservative management by orchiopexy, makes this case clinically noteworthy.

The embryological basis of polyorchidism remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include longitudinal or transverse division of the genital ridge, incomplete degeneration of mesonephric structures, and abnormal partitioning of developing gonadal tissue during early embryogenesis. The timing and extent of this developmental division may determine the anatomical relationship between the supernumerary testis and the epididymis and vas deferens, resulting in the different anatomical variants described in current classification systems.2,4,7,8

Classification systems have been developed to standardize the anatomical patterns of polyorchidism. According to the Leung classification, supernumerary testes are categorized according to their relationship with the epididymis and vas deferens, while the Bergholz-Wenke classification further characterizes reproductive tract connectivity.2,8 In our patient, surgical exploration demonstrated a completely independent supernumerary testis with its own spermatic cord, epididymis, and vas deferens, corresponding to Leung Type D and Bergholz-Wenke Type A1. This anatomical configuration is particularly relevant because the independent vascular and ductal supply supported preservation of the supernumerary testis.

Several clinical features distinguish the present case from recently reported cases. Adult triorchidism associated with inguinal hernia has been described previously. Bayissa and Tesfaye reported a 43-year-old man with right-sided triorchidism and an incarcerated inguinal hernia; however, the supernumerary testes were atrophic and were excised.1 Similarly, Balawender et al reported a 29-year-old man with a right supernumerary undescended testis discovered during hernia repair, highlighting the complexity of deciding between orchiopexy and orchidectomy.9 In another adult case, a 48-year-old man with bilateral inguinal hernias was found to have a supernumerary testis without an epididymis or vas deferens.10 More recently, Hadj Taieb et al reported a 44-year-old man in whom a supernumerary testis was discovered intraoperatively during bilateral hernia repair and preserved with subsequent surveillance.11

Compared with these reports, our patient was younger and had a normally descended and well-vascularized supernumerary testis that was diagnosed preoperatively by Doppler ultrasonography. Importantly, the supernumerary testis possessed a completely independent spermatic cord, epididymis, and vas deferens. The patient was also fertile, being married with three children and reporting a normal sexual life. These features supported an organ-preserving approach with orchiopexy and structured postoperative surveillance [Table 2].

Modern imaging plays a central role in the diagnosis and management of polyorchidism. Scrotal ultrasonography with color Doppler is the preferred initial imaging modality because it can identify the number, location, morphology, and vascularity of the testes and their associated structures.4,6 In our patient, preoperative Doppler ultrasonography clearly demonstrated three testes, including a smaller supernumerary testis within the right hemiscrotum, with preserved vascularity and benign imaging features. These findings allowed the diagnosis to be established before surgical exploration. Doppler ultrasonography is also valuable in distinguishing testicular tissue from other inguinoscrotal masses and in assessing associated abnormalities such as hydrocele, varicocele, epididymal lesions, and suspicious testicular masses.6 Surgical exploration subsequently confirmed the imaging findings and demonstrated the independent spermatic cord, epididymis, and vas deferens of the supernumerary testis.

Although the risk of malignant transformation in supernumerary testes is considered low, it remains an important consideration in management, particularly when the testis is ectopic, undescended, dysplastic, or associated with suspicious imaging findings.6,12 Reported cases of malignancy in supernumerary testes emphasize the importance of careful assessment of testicular location, morphology, and vascularity. In contrast, the supernumerary testis in our patient was normally positioned within the scrotum, demonstrated preserved vascularity and benign sonographic features, and showed no evidence of a focal mass. These findings, together with the patient’s young age and preserved fertility, supported testis-preserving management with orchiopexy and long-term surveillance.

Management strategies for polyorchidism remain individualized according to the anatomical type, location, viability, imaging characteristics, and reproductive potential of the supernumerary testis.4,6,9 Conservative management with orchiopexy and surveillance may be considered when the supernumerary testis is scrotal, viable, structurally normal, and free of suspicious lesions. Conversely, orchidectomy may be considered when the testis is undescended, atrophic, non-viable, or associated with suspicious findings. In the case reported by Bayissa and Tesfaye, the atrophic supernumerary testes were excised during repair of an inguinal hernia,1 whereas preservation was chosen in other reported cases when the testis was considered viable and suitable for surveillance.4,11 In our patient, the combination of a normally descended and well-vascularized supernumerary testis, independent ductal anatomy, young age, and preserved fertility favored orchiopexy rather than orchidectomy.

Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, as a single case report, the findings cannot be generalized to all patients with polyorchidism. Second, long-term follow-up is still ongoing; therefore, the durability of the conservative management strategy and the long-term risk of complications cannot yet be fully assessed. Third, histopathological examination of the supernumerary testis was not performed because orchidectomy was not indicated given its benign imaging characteristics and preserved vascularity. Nevertheless, the detailed preoperative Doppler assessment, intraoperative confirmation of the independent spermatic cord and ductal structures, and postoperative surveillance provide clinically relevant evidence supporting an individualized organ-preserving approach.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rarity of right-sided triorchidism in an adult with an indirect inguinal hernia. Preoperative Doppler ultrasonography enabled accurate diagnosis and demonstrated a viable, normally descended supernumerary testis with an independent spermatic cord and ductal system. In selected young and fertile patients with viable, non-suspicious supernumerary testes, orchiopexy may provide an effective organ-preserving approach, provided that appropriate long-term clinical and imaging surveillance is maintained.
