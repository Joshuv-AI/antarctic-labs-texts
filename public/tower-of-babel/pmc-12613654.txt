Does family planning use empower women? A systematic review of the evidence
Authors: Nadia G Diamond-Smith, Lakshmi Gopalakrishnan, Salome Wawire, Jacqueline Kunesh, Jennifer Choi, Moazzam Ali, Alison M El Ayadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExtensive evidence suggests that family planning programs are second only to education in providing returns on dollars spent with improvements in long-term maternal and child health outcomes. However, no current research synthesis to date has robustly examined whether use of family planning results in increased empowerment and greater agency of women, despite theoretical, but not empirical, support that family planning influences this outcome as well. Our objective was to conduct a systematic review of the evidence on how women’s access to and/or use of family planning methods impacts their agency and empowerment, including mechanisms and effect modifiers important to this relationship.MethodsWe searched 20 global and regional databases for interventional and observational research published from January 2000 through January 2022 which reviewed the relationship between family planning use and subsequent women’s empowerment. Titles/abstracts and full-text articles were screened in duplicate, and key data were extracted using a standardized form. We evaluated risk of bias using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and rated the certainty of evidence for four outcomes (decision-making, labor force participation, wages, and schooling) using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Findings were analyzed and summarized in both tabular and narrative format.ResultsWe identified 3,170 articles after de-duplication, of which 14 quantitative and three qualitative studies met inclusion criteria. Studies were in both high income and low- and middle-income countries, with the largest proportion being from South Asia. Studies ranged from randomized controlled trials of programs at the community level to quasi-experimental studies of policies. The GRADE framework yielded low and very low confidence in the findings. Outcomes were categorized into domains using Kabeer’s framework of Resources, agency and achievements. Most studies measured the impact of family planning on resources or agency. Combining measures across all domains of empowerment, 47% had positive, 39% null, and 14% negative results.DiscussionOverall, this systematic review suggests that family planning access has a positive impact on a variety of empowerment measures across different domains of empowerment. However, the quality ratings indicate that while our synthesis suggests potential benefits of family planning for women’s empowerment, the current evidence base provides limited confidence for definitive conclusions. One of the main challenges in understanding the impact of family planning on empowerment is the diversity of conceptualizations and measures of empowerment used. In addition, the geographic coverage of studies was limited to few countries, with only one study from Latin America. Further research using a process-oriented measure of empowerment comparable across contexts could add to our understanding. In addition to being a fundamental human right, investing in family planning likely contributes to women’s empowerment in multiple domains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02146-3.Keywords: Systematic review, Contraception, Family planning, Empowerment, Agency, Economic status, Social status, Decision-making

Introduction

The relationship between women’s empowerment and reproductive health, including the use of family planning, has long been of interest to scholars, both those with an interest in reproductive rights and women’s rights. Considerable research has been conducted examining how women’s empowerment influences family planning use and fertility outcomes, with findings synthesized in multiple literature reviews; however, these studies have predominantly focused on the causal direction from empowerment to family planning use rather than the reverse [1–3]. Other reviews have explored the association between reproductive health (beyond family planning alone) and women’s empowerment; [4–7] however, these have been limited by restrictive inclusion criteria [4], narrow conceptualizations of empowerment focused primarily on economic indicators [5], focus only on measurement validation rather than causal pathways [7], or examination of only specific intervention types [6]. Prior literature includes the 1990 s Women’s Studies Project which looked at the impact of family planning on women’s lives through 26 studies in 10 countries, and resulted in a book, “Women’s Voices, Women’s lives: The Impact of Family Planning.” [8] This project looked at a range of outcomes which encompassed factors related to women’s empowerment and found evidence for impacts on relationship quality, family wellbeing, and labor force participation. While this earlier body of work offers insights from quantitative and qualitative studies to our knowledge, no recent, these were not evaluations of interventions or other study designs able to test causality. To our knowledge, no recent review has focused on the impact of family planning on a broader definition of empowerment that conceptualizes empowerment as a process and encompasses multiple domains of empowerment, as described below. Thus, the goal of this paper is to systematically review the recent evidence on how women’s use and access to family planning impacts their empowerment.

Women’s empowerment

Women’s empowerment is a complex measure, with various disciplines and thinkers over time highlighting different components. Kabeer, a preeminent scholar of women’s empowerment and its link to health, defined women’s empowerment as a dynamic process through which empowerment results in improved health and social status (Fig. 1), with women first acquiring (1) resources, which in turn facilitate women development of (2) agency, which allows women to (3) achieve desired achievements or outcomes [9]. In the 20 years since Kabeer’s foundational work, there has been a shift to incorporate the roles of broader institutional structures (including norms and relationships at the family, community, state and economic market levels) in developing agency [10]. Yount (2017) elaborates on Kabeer’s framework by describing empowerment as a dynamic process through which women gain access to resources that facilitate their independence and agency to articulate their needs, make their own decisions, and fulfill their aspirations [11]. The resources in this framework include human development resources such as education, skills development and self-efficacy; social resources such as participation in social events, access to peer and social networks, as well as access to role models beyond the traditional family set up; and economic resources/material assets including earnings, property and land. It should be noted that many scholars, including feminist scholars, see women’s empowerment as an end goal in itself, without necessarily needing to link it to achievements or outcomes [12].

Conceptual model of the pathway from contraceptive use/access to empowerment

Family planning

Aside from its potential to increase women’s empowerment, expanding access to family planning is considered essential to achieve the Sustainable Development Goals of reducing maternal mortality to 70 per 100,000 live births, neonatal mortality to 12 deaths per 1,000 live births, and universal access to sexual and reproductive health-care services by 2030 [13]. Family planning, for the context of this paper, refers to modern methods of contraception, including safe abortion services. Family planning use has been shown to positively impact long-term maternal and child health outcomes [14, 15]. For example, family planning contributes to improved childhood nutrition through enabling families to space their pregnancies to limit the likelihood of undernutrition (including stunting, underweight, wasting, and vitamin deficiencies) which collectively contribute to nearly half of all childhood deaths [16]. It also allows for prolonged breastfeeding, which is associated with better infant and maternal outcomes [16, 17].

Furthermore, unplanned or mistimed pregnancies put women and adolescent girls at increased risk of maternal and newborn adverse outcomes due to unsafe abortions, inadequate birth spacing, insufficient prenatal care, general poor pregnancy health, and high-risk pregnancies [18–22]. Among the 1.9 billion women of reproductive age (15–49 years) globally, 1.1 billion women are estimated to need a family planning method to limit or delay childbearing [23]. Of these, nearly 172 million women are not using any method and an additional 85 million are using a traditional method with limited effectiveness, despite desiring to avoid or delay pregnancy [23]. Women face barriers to family planning at various levels, including personal or interpersonal (e.g., unable to negotiate family planning with partner), systemic (e.g., lack of access to health care) or societal (e.g., sociocultural factors), many of which are related to women’s empowerment. There is a critical need for targeted interventions to support women’s ability to achieve their reproductive goals through limiting the number of children they have, timing their pregnancies, spacing their children and choosing whether to have children at all [24].

Additionally, family planning has been shown to contribute to economic development at both macro and micro levels [25]. At the macro level, family planning programs have contributed to an increase in gross domestic product (GDP) for countries and helped them save money, as well as improve services for citizens. This has community level implications for economic gains made due to family planning programs.

Conceptualization and definition of empowerment

Empowerment is a complex, multifaceted concept, especially when framed as a process including resources, agency and achievements, situated in Kabeer’s framework [9]. A critical step in understanding the association between empowerment and family planning is categorizing what types of outcomes or experiences related to women’s empowerment fall into each of these three empowerment domains. Expanding upon prior work [4, 5], and detailed below, our approach conceptualizes “resources” as including topics around labor, income/earnings, education and property rights. We conceptualize “agency” as including concepts around household decision-making, bargaining power, autonomy, mobility, and gender norms. Finally, we conceptualize “achievements” as including intergenerational health and outcomes, freedom from violence, relationship status or quality, and mental health and wellbeing. We did not include measures of women’s health in pregnancy, at childbirth, or infant/child related health status as an outcome nor did we include health care seeking behaviors or outcomes because these have been explored extensively elsewhere and were outside our definition of empowerment. We acknowledge that some of these indicators may be more or less prevalent and/or relevant in different settings (e.g., where pre-marital sex and family planning use is rare, and education ends prior to marriage, family planning use is not likely to be an important determinant of educational outcomes).

Our paper’s summary conceptual model of pathways of change through which family planning could impact women’s empowerment (Fig. 1) is based on Yount’s expansion of Kabeer’s framework. The main pathway through which family planning acts to impact empowerment is by helping a woman delay, space, or limit the number of children she has. This in turn provides her the opportunity (time) to engage in education or work (measures of resources), which could have cascading impacts on agency and achievements. Delaying, spacing and limiting childbearing could act differently on domains of empowerment. For example, delaying may be more likely to impact educational outcomes, allowing women to stay in school longer. Spacing and limiting childbearing, on the other hand, may be more likely to impact labor force outcomes directly (allowing women to return to work sooner with fewer children, for example). Since there is broad theoretical overlap in how delaying, spacing, and limiting childbearing impact empowerment, and the main purpose of this review is to understand the overall pathways rather than differentiate between these specific fertility timing mechanisms, we group delaying, spacing, and limiting together in our summarized conceptual model. Depending on the context, delaying, spacing, or limiting childbearing could impact a woman’s agency directly, by increasing or decreasing her decision making/bargaining power and improving or negatively impacting her household relationships (intimate partner violence, marriage, etc.). These potential negative or positive pathways are represented using dotted lines. Additionally, delaying, spacing or limiting childbearing could have positive or negative impacts on her self-worth or whether she is meeting familial/society expectations, again depending on the cultural context and value placed on childbearing. Delaying marriage/childbearing and spacing or limiting childbearing also has direct impacts on maternal and infant health (a form of achievement, though not the focus of this paper). However, we include them in the summarized conceptual framework because a woman’s health status could also be a pathway to facilitate her engagement in more school or work. In certain settings, access to family planning could also help a woman delay marriage, because she may be able to have sex prior to marriage without the risk of a becoming pregnant. Delaying marriage could in turn delay births directly or could allow her to be in school or work more, which could be an indirect pathway to delaying births. We do not consider other involuntary factors that could affect fertility (e.g., husband’s migration, infertility) in this analysis.

The goal of this systematic review is to explore the recent evidence for the association between family planning use and women’s empowerment. This review makes several key contributions to the literature. First, we provide the first systematic examination of the causal direction from family planning to women’s empowerment, addressing a critical gap as previous reviews have focused primarily on how empowerment influences family planning use. Second, we employ Kabeer’s comprehensive process-oriented framework to examine empowerment across multiple domains (resources, agency, achievements) rather than limiting analysis to economic indicators. Third, we synthesize evidence from both interventional and observational studies with clear temporal sequencing to establish causality. Finally, our inclusion of qualitative studies provides mechanistic insights into pathways through which family planning may influence different empowerment domains.

Methods

This systematic review examined quantitative studies and conducted a narrative synthesis of family planning interventions that aimed to improve women’s empowerment. Among quantitative studies, we included only those with a clear temporality to establish the causal relationship between family planning use and women’s empowerment. We supplemented this synthesis with eligible qualitative studies to help us understand the potential mechanisms through which family planning improves women’s empowerment. Qualitative studies provide crucial insights into the mechanisms or pathways of change through which family planning may influence empowerment, helping to strengthen causal inference by illuminating plausible biological and social processes. They help answer the ‘how’ and ‘why’ questions that support causal claims from quantitative evidence. This review is reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement and registered on the International Prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42022318696).

While our research question focuses on understanding the impact of family planning on women’s empowerment, we recognize that many included studies were observational in nature. Even those with appropriate temporal sequencing to support causal inference cannot definitively establish causation. Therefore, throughout this review, we use the term “associations” when describing study findings as a methodological precaution to accurately reflect the strength of evidence available, while maintaining our conceptual focus on understanding causal pathways.

Exclusion criteria included studies that are cross-sectional where temporality and causal direction between the use of/exposure to family planning and empowerment were not clear. We also excluded papers where family planning was a secondary component of multi-faceted interventions primarily focused on other empowerment domains (e.g., economic empowerment programs, gender training curricula, or social norm change interventions that included family planning as a minor element). Papers that included family planning but were primarily focused on another health topic (e.g., HIV) were also excluded.

Search strategy

We searched the following 20 electronic databases and registries for relevant articles: PubMed, Embase, Global Health, SCOPUS, Women’s Studies International, Sociological Abstracts, Africa Wide Information, CINAHL, Global Index Medicus, Econlit, PsycINFO, Web of Science Core Collection, IBECS, PAHO, WHOLIS, IndMED, Native Health Research Database, Clinical Trials registry, WHO International Clinical Trial Registry Platform (ICTRP), and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). The search was implemented in English; however, we did not impose language or geography restrictions as eligibility criteria. Peer-reviewed database searches were limited to articles published between January 2000 through January 2022. The search timeframe from 2000 to 2022 was selected to capture literature published after Kabeer’s foundational 1999 empowerment framework, which forms the theoretical foundation of this review, while also coinciding with increased global focus on women’s empowerment following the Millennium Development Goals. This 22-year period ensures inclusion of studies using contemporary family planning interventions and modern empowerment measurement approaches while providing sufficient temporal scope for comprehensive evidence synthesis. Using a combination of free-text terms and MeSH terms, we used search terms related to “family planning”, “contraception”, “women’s empowerment”, the many dimensions through which empowerment has been measured such as women’s autonomy, decision-making, agency, among others informed by previous systematic reviews [1, 3]. The full search strategy for all the databases are included in the Appendix 2.

To ensure a thorough comprehensive synthesis of the literature [27], we also searched 13 grey literature databases and relevant organizational websites including National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), BASENet, World Bank, EngenderHealth, Eldis, 3ie, Women’s group evidence, Packard Foundation, ICRW, UNFPA, Population Council, BMGF, and OpenGray/GrayNet. Grey literature searches included articles published from January 2000 through April 2022, followed the same temporal rationale as peer-reviewed searches (see above), with the extended end date to April 2022 accounting for longer publication timelines typical of grey literature sources. In addition to database searching, we reviewed the reference lists of included studies and related systematic reviews and conducted a forward citation search of all included studies.

We imported all citations identified from the searches into Endnote for initial de-duplication. We then imported the citations into Covidence [28], which automatically removes duplicates, for screening. Titles and abstracts were double screened following the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) selection criteria by four researchers (NDS, LG, JK, JC) in Covidence. Conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer. During the screening phase, we restricted publication language to English, Spanish, Swahili, and Hindi. For title and abstract assessment of studies published in other languages, we carried out initial (title/abstract) translation through Google Translate. To enhance consistency of inclusion and exclusion of abstracts, a 10% sample of title abstracts were screened first by all members of the review team and discussed. The full text of studies that met our inclusion criteria were then double screened by four researchers (NDS, LG, JK, JC). Conflicts that emerged were resolved by a third reviewer not directly involved in the screening or review phase.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data from included studies were independently extracted in duplicate by four researchers (NDS, LG, JK, JC). We developed a data extraction template using the PICOS framework on a Google Form (Appendix 3). We extracted data on publication details, design of the study, study setting, participant characteristics, objectives, theories and frameworks discussed by authors, intervention design, analytical methods, results, and conclusions. For quantitative studies, we extracted data on the outcomes of interest related to empowerment including indicators, relative risks, risk differences, prevalence estimates, odds ratios, percentages, etc.

Risk of bias

The quality of each of the included quantitative studies was appraised in duplicate by team members (LG, JK, and JC) using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale [29]. We resolved any conflicts by involving a third independent reviewer (AE) where necessary. The Newcastle Ottawa scale is based on three domains including selection of study groups, comparability between exposed and non-exposed groups, and ascertainment of exposure/outcomes. A study can be awarded a maximum of one point for each numbered item within the selection and outcome categories and a maximum of two points for comparability. Using the Newcastle Ottawa scale, each individual study was scored up to 11 points and a categorical ranking was assigned (0–2 = poor, 3–5 = fair; and 6–11 = good).

GRADE assessment

We used the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) to summarize the certainty of evidence for four important outcomes, including labor force participation, wages, schooling, and decision-making process as per the GRADE criteria [30]. The GRADE evidence profile covers the within-study risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, the precision of effect estimates, and the risk of publication bias. We rated the certainty of the evidence for each of our four outcomes (across the studies) as either very low, low, moderate, or high certainty of evidence, reflecting our confidence that the true effect lies close to the estimated effect from the studies. Very low certainty indicates very little confidence in the effect estimate, with the true effect likely substantially different from what studies suggest. Low certainty means limited confidence, with the true effect possibly differing from the estimate. Moderate certainty suggests moderate confidence that the true effect is close to the estimated effect, while high certainty indicates high confidence that the true effect lies close to the estimate.

Data analysis

Quantitative

Quantitative data were analyzed using a narrative synthesis approach, a useful framework when analyzing data across heterogenous studies when a meta-analysis is not possible [31]. We synthesized the narrative findings to group studies into homogenous groups based on components such as geographic setting, program components, type of family planning intervention, type of outcomes, and direction or magnitude of effect. Women’s empowerment indicators from different studies were also categorized into the three domains of resources, agency, and outcomes by using Kabeer’s women’s empowerment framework [9].

Qualitative

In the process of conducting the systematic review screening, we systematically identified qualitative papers that described the impact of family planning on women’s empowerment, meeting eligibility criteria 1–4 above. These papers were included for the purpose of helping us understand mechanisms of impact and provide potential explanations for the quantitative findings. For qualitative studies, we extracted main quotes and themes using a narrative approach and used the findings to help us better understand the findings and pathways through which family planning leads to empowerment from the quantitative studies in a descriptive way. We present these findings separately within the results section due to this systematic process.

Results

Study selection

The process of study selection is presented in Fig. 2 [32]. Database and register searches returned 11,141 papers. A total of 7,971 duplicates were removed prior to title and abstract screening, leaving 3,170 records for title/abstract screening. Of those screened, 3,145 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, with 25 reports identified as suitable for full-text screening. Separately, grey literature searches returned 1,126 papers, and manual searches (i.e., reference list review and forward citation search) returned 15 papers (Fig. 2). These were processed separately and contributed additional studies to the full-text screening phase. In total, we sought to assess 53 papers (including records from databases/registers and papers from grey literature/manual searches) for eligibility through full-text review. Full text was not available for two, leaving 51 assessed. Papers were excluded because they employed the wrong study design (n = 14), used the wrong outcome (n = 12), had the wrong intervention (i.e. not on family planning and empowerment; n = 2), examined the impact of empowerment on family planning rather than the reverse direction (n = 1).

Our GRADE assessments revealed predominantly very low to low certainty evidence across empowerment outcomes, reflecting several methodological challenges: (1) most included studies were observational with inherent risk of bias and confounding, (2) significant heterogeneity in empowerment definitions and measurement approaches across studies, (3) limited sample sizes for some outcomes leading to imprecision, and (4) potential publication bias toward positive findings. These ratings indicate that while our synthesis suggests potential benefits of family planning for women’s empowerment, the current evidence base provides limited confidence for definitive conclusions.

Of the 14 quantitative papers, six of the studies were from Bangladesh and three were from the United States (Fig. 4). Most of the included papers evaluated systems-level interventions (n = 11). One-third of intervention papers focused on evaluating icddr, b’s Matlab Maternal and Child Health and Family Planning Project in the Matlab Health and Demographic Surveillance System (n = 6), a large quasi-randomized experiment providing enhanced community-based services to women living in intervention areas [36, 40, 43–46]. The Matlab project was initiated in rural Bangladesh in 1977, and provided reproductive health (family planning) and maternal/child health services to women through a home-based care system [36]. Extensive data was collected from intervention and control villages for decades, laying the foundation for studies on the impact of family planning service provision at the community level on diverse outcomes. Five studies were natural experiments, including three evaluating the series of legal and policy facilitators of oral contraceptive pill access in the United States during the 1960s–70s (n = 3)33,35,43 and two examining national family planning program expansions in Colombia and Malaysia [33, 41].

Only one study from Kenya evaluated an intervention implanted at the health facility level, a randomized controlled trial which tested integration of family planning services into routine HIV care at the clinic level [42]. No included studies evaluated individual-level interventions.

Resources

The majority of papers (N = 8) measured some type of outcome related to Resources, with 23 resource-related outcomes measured (Table 5) [33, 34, 36, 39, 41, 43–45]. Within this category, the most common type of resource was economic (N = 18 outcomes measured), with evidence primarily from studies in Malaysia, the USA, Ethiopia, Bangladesh, and Colombia [33, 34, 36, 39, 41, 45]. Of these, five measured labor force participation across Malaysia, the USA, Ethiopia, and Bangladesh [33, 34, 36, 39, 45], and five measured earnings of some type in similar contexts [33, 34, 39, 45, 52]. Two studies from Bangladesh and Colombia measured savings [36, 41], and two studies from Ethiopia and Bangladesh examined household consumption/contribution to household income [39, 45].

After economic-related resources, the next most common type of resources measured as an outcome were related to educational resources, with two studies from Colombia and Bangladesh measuring the woman’s own educational attainment [41, 45]. Two other resource-related outcomes fell into a “other” category, namely, property rights and dowry price for marriages, both examined in Bangladesh studies [43, 44].

In summary, resources, especially economic resources, were the most frequently measured empowerment outcome across studies, and multiple types of resources were often measured within the same study, highlighting the literature’s reliance on this domain of empowerment.

Agency

A smaller number of papers (N = 6) measured an agency-related outcome, with studies conducted in Malaysia, Bangladesh, the USA, India, and Kenya [33, 37, 38, 42–44]. The most common agency outcome was related to decision-making or bargaining power, with five studies from Malaysia, Bangladesh, the USA, and India measuring these outcomes [33, 37, 38, 43, 44]. Three from Bangladesh and India measured indicators of decision-making power within the household [37, 43, 44], often using common a set of questions around decision-making around various domains, as exemplified by the Demographic and Health Surveys. One additional study from Malaysia measured co-residence with in-laws but conceptualized this as an indicator of bargaining power [33], as prior research in the South Asian context suggests that women have lower status in their household when they co-reside with their in-laws [50, 53]. Another study from the USA looked at age at marriage [35], which represented a marker of decision-making power to delay the age at marriage (assuming that women would prefer to and be able to negotiate for later ages of marriage).

Two studies from Bangladesh and India measured outcomes related to mobility [37, 44], with the Bangladesh study also including the act of head covering [44]. These studies operationalized these outcomes as reflective of women’s autonomy in dress and mobility decisions. Such restrictions and practices have been studied as complex markers of gender performance within these cultural contexts [51], although we acknowledge that these indicators should be interpreted as proxy measures of agency that are highly context-dependent rather than universal markers of empowerment. Finally, one study from Kenya measured outcomes related to gender norms (using the Gender Equitable Men Scale) [42].

In summary, similar to resources, one type of measure of agency (decision-making power) dominated the way that scholars are measuring this domain. Some more creative, and potentially culturally specific, measures of agency (mobility, co-residence, etc.) were also included in some studies.

Achievements

The fewest number of studies (N = 3) measured achievement outcomes with research conducted in the USA and Bangladesh [35, 40, 46]. One from Bangladesh measured subjective well-being of the woman [40], and two studies from the USA and Bangladesh measured achievements of the next generation (children of the woman who had access to family planning), including labor force outcomes, education, and marital status of the woman’s children [35, 46]. We conceptualized children’s empowerment outcomes as indicators of women’s agency and capacity to invest in and advocate for the next generation’s opportunities and well-being. This reflects the mother’s ability to translate her own empowerment gains into tangible benefits for her children, such as ensuring their education, health, and future prospects. While children’s outcomes are influenced by multiple household members and broader structural factors, empowered mothers are considered to be better positioned to prioritize, negotiate for, and secure resources and opportunities that enhance their children’s life chances and empowerment potential.

Despite the large number of outcomes measured, almost half of papers (N = 8) only measured outcomes from one domain of empowerment, with studies from the USA, Ethiopia, Bangladesh, Colombia, India, and Kenya [34, 37–42, 45], most often related to resources (economic empowerment). Resource and agency-related measures were included in only three studies, all from Malaysia and Bangladesh [33, 43, 44]. This suggests that the conceptualization of women’s empowerment in this literature is not comprehensive, including a lack of measuring empowerment as a process.

Impact

Distribution of directionality of associations by domain

Some papers measured more than one outcome, thus, the total outcomes discussed in this section reflect more than the total number of papers (Table 5). Overall, more papers found positive associations with empowerment outcomes (17 positive empowerment outcomes) compared to null (N = 14) or negative associations (N = 5). By empowerment domain, most interventions/exposures reported a positive association with the empowerment outcome measured (59% for Resources, 56% for agency and 40% for achievements). Negative intervention/exposure associations were reported for fewer empowerment outcomes (18% for Resources, 22% for agency and 0% for achievements). Null associations were identified for 53% of Resources, 22% of agency and 60% for achievement outcomes. We explore these results in more detail below, focusing on resource-related outcomes, negative outcomes, geography, country income classification and intervention type.

Focus on resource-related outcomes

Since the largest proportion of outcomes measured were related to resources, and these were also the most consistently commonly measured indicators across studies and regions, we explored these findings in more depth. Labor force participation (measured by five studies across Malaysia, Bangladesh, the USA, and Ethiopia) showed null associations in two studies [33, 36], positive associations in two studies from the USA and Ethiopia [35, 39], and negative associations in one study from Bangladesh [45]. This finding did not suggest a clear evidence of association between family planning and empowerment using this metric. However, wages and income, which were measured in five studies across Bangladesh, Ethiopia, and Malaysia, found positive associations in four out of the five studies [33, 39, 44, 45], with the remaining one study from Bangladesh identifying a null association [44]. This provides some evidence that family planning may have an association with women’s wages or income.

Focus on negative outcomes

Negative associations across all empowerment domain were found in only three studies, all from the Matlab study in Bangladesh [43–45]. Schultz (2009) [45] found negative associations with long term measures of women’s labor force participation. The authors hypothesized that women who have fewer children because of Matlab program participation might need to work on the land (i.e., farming) or in the household more, leading to lower labor force participation. However, women received higher wages in Matlab’s intervention arm, perhaps balancing the lower labor force participation rates in some ways. Ruthbah et al. (2020) [44] found that women in intervention communities had lower fertility and less decision-making power and fewer property rights. These authors suggested a “tradeoff in autonomy,” where women gaining economic empowerment (positive or null but in the positive direction associations are seen for other resource related outcomes) makes male decision-makers less inclined to give women property rights (which are at the discretion of male family members) or engage them in household decision-making. Peters et al. (2011) [43] found that women in the treatment arm of the Matlab study were less likely to make decisions and had to pay more for dowry than women in the control villages. Similar to the interpretation above, this author argues that this is an unintended consequence of program deployment in a setting where men still have ultimate control.

Findings were further analyzed based on other sub-categories such as geographic region (Africa vs. Asia); country income classification (HIC vs. LMIC); and intervention type (intervention vs. natural experiment).

Comparison by low- or middle-income countries (LMIC) region (Africa vs. Asia)

Of the nine papers from low-or middle-income countries, two were from Africa in Ethiopia and Kenya [39, 42] and seven were from Asia in Malaysia, Bangladesh, and India.(Fig. 3, Appendix 4) [33, 36, 37, 40, 43–46] The findings show that regionally, most null associations between family planning and empowerment were from Asian studies in Malaysia and Bangladesh (N = 13) [33, 36, 40, 44–46] compared to only one null association [42] from Kenya in Africa. A similar pattern was observed in negative associations (N = 0 from Africa and N = 5 from Asian studies in Bangladesh [43–45]). Four positive associations were identified from African studies in Kenya and Ethiopia [39, 42] and nine from Asian studies in Malaysia, Bangladesh, and India [33, 37, 44–46].

Comparison of outcomes between studies in Africa and Asia

In the agency and achievements domain, Africa had one null association [42], while Asia had one from Bangladesh [44] and two from Bangladesh [40, 46] null associations in each of the domains of agency and achievements, respectively. In the resources domain, Africa had zero positive associations while Asia had five positive associations from Malaysia and Bangladesh [33, 44, 45], with a similar pattern for negative associations (N = 0 and N = 3, respectively, all from Bangladesh [43–45]. In the agency domain, Asia had three positive associations from Malaysia and India [33, 37]with a similar pattern shown in the negative associations (N = 0 and N = 2, respectively) both from Bangladesh [43, 44].

Overall, these findings highlight limited studies from Africa, compared to Asia, reflecting the findings shown in the map. While these figures might appear to suggest that there were more negative findings in Asian studies, we interpret this instead as reflecting a difference in the number of studies across regions.

Comparison by country income classification

Overall, 11 papers were from low- or middle-income countries (LMIC) and three were from high income countries (HIC; Fig. 4, Appendix 5). By country income classification (LMIC vs. HIC), there were no null associations from HIC within any of the domains, while 14 outcomes from LMIC showed null associations in all domains (N = 9 for resources; N = 2 for agency; N = 3 for achievements) [33, 36, 40, 42, 44–46].

Comparison of outcomes between studies in high income (HIC) and low and middle income countries (LMIC)

Most of the papers that found a positive association in all domains were from LMIC (N = 7 for resources; N = 4 for agency) compared with HIC which were found to have only one paper each in the resources [34], agency [38], and achievements [35] domains. No negative associations were found in the HIC papers, compared with LMIC papers which found negative associations in three and two papers within the resources [43–45] and agency [43, 44] domains, respectively. Fewer papers from HIC compared with LMIC may have skewed this comparison.

This highlights a lack of data from HIC; however, it is interesting that all studies from HICs found positive associations. Additionally, as discussed in more detail below, the HIC studies all employed quasi experimental-designs, which are usually deemed to be lower quality in terms of interpretation of causality compared to randomized studies, yet benefit from population-level assessment.

Comparison by intervention type

Overall, nine papers from Malaysia, Bangladesh, Kenya, Colombia, and the USA [33, 36, 38, 40–45] reported on intervention studies and five on quasi-experimental studies from the USA, Ethiopia, Bangladesh, and India (Fig. 5, Appendix 6) [34, 35, 37, 39, 46]. Comparing the outcomes by type of intervention, we found that more papers with null associations were from the intervention (N = 12) category with studies from Malaysia, Bangladesh, Kenya, and the USA [33, 36, 40, 42, 44, 45], compared with the quasi-experimental category (N = 2) from Bangladesh [46]. Most of the papers analyzed found positive associations (N = 17), including twelve from the intervention category across Malaysia, Bangladesh, Colombia, the USA, and Kenya [33, 38, 41, 42, 44, 45]. Negative associations were found in five outcomes from intervention studies in Bangladesh [43–45].

Comparison of outcomes between study design

By domain, the highest number of null outcomes was in resources (N = 9) from studies in Malaysia and Bangladesh [33, 36, 44, 45], all of which were from the intervention category; compared with two outcomes (all from the intervention category) within the agency domain from Bangladesh and Kenya [42, 44], and three outcomes from the achievements domain (N = 2) outcomes from the quasi experimental category [46] and one outcome [40] from the intervention category in Bangladesh). A similar pattern was observed in the number of positive associations with outcomes, with the highest number of outcomes in the domain of resources (N = 10), most of which were from the intervention category in Malaysia, Bangladesh, and Colombia (N = 6);32,40,41,45 compared with five outcomes (three from the intervention category in Malaysia, the USA, and Kenya [33, 38, 42]) within the agency domain, and two papers from Bangladesh and the USA [35, 46], both from the quasi experimental category in the achievements domain. No papers with negative associations were identified in the quasi-experimental category, whereas such outcomes were found within the resources and agency domains in the intervention category from Bangladesh (N = 3 for resources; N = 2 for agency; N = 0 for achievements) [43–45].

Overall, most of our evidence came from intervention studies rather than natural experiments, and there is some suggestion that natural experiments from the USA, Ethiopia, Bangladesh, and India were more likely to show positive impacts compared to null or negative, while findings were mixed for intervention studies from Malaysia, Bangladesh, Kenya, Colombia, and the USA. Intervention studies mostly included randomized controlled trials or plausibly randomized controlled trials (Matlab Bangladesh), and thus the weight we put on the evidence from these studies is stronger. With this in mind, we see more null and some negative results in our intervention studies, particularly from the Bangladesh context.

Qualitative studies

While conducting our quantitative systematic review, eligible qualitative studies were identified for separate analysis to inform our understanding of the mechanisms through which family planning use and interventions impact women’s empowerment. Three papers were identified that had qualitative data on the association between family planning and empowerment: two from India [48, 49] and one from Ethiopia [47]. Two of the papers [47, 48] were exploratory and one was a mixed methods descriptive paper [49]. They were published in 2014, 2015 and 2018, respectively.

These three qualitative studies touched on all three domains of empowerment. All three of the papers mentioned some type of resource related empowerment outcome associated with family planning use. All talked about something related to income or economic stability, and one talked about the impact on schooling [47]. These papers discussed positive directionality between family planning and the resource-related outcome; however, different papers described different pathways. Pallikadavath et al. (2013), interviewing women in India, found that sterilization allowed women more autonomy within their relationships with their husbands, which the women used to work more outside the home, and earn more money which they actually had control over [49]. Yadav and Patil (2014), also in India, found that lower fertility, achieved through family planning was a pathway allowing women to invest more in their children (education and savings), as well as spend less time working and save more money [48]. Similarly, Alano and Hanson (2018), interviewing women in Ethiopia, described how family planning, by supporting women’s ability to delay, space or limit childbearing, allowed women to engage in more income generating activities, which provided financial security at the household and individual level [47]. This allowed women more financial autonomy from their husbands and less dependence. Family planning also helped women return back to school after adolescent marriages and pregnancies.

All three of the qualitative papers discussed positive empowerment outcomes in the agency domain. As mentioned above, Pallikadavath et al. (2013) discussed the autonomy that sterilization helped Indian women achieve, and described how sterilization was considered a “right of passage” which allowed women to have more joint decision-making and more mobility [49]. The pathway was one of raising household status, where young, newly married women who do “not command any authority in household decision-making” are able to ultimately gain equal status in their households with their mothers-in-law. Finally, lower, and more compressed fertility resulted in women in Ethiopia spending more time in their husband’s home, strengthening their relationships and position in that household. Thus, we gain insight into pathways through which relationship-related agency outcomes (relationship quality/stability and intimate partner violence) could be impacted by family planning. Alano and Hanson (2018) also described increased relationship quality from having fewer children, allowing couples to talk more and plan together about children and their own life aspirations [47]. Important interrelationships were identified between the domains, where, for example as described by Alano and Hanson (2018) [47], women’s economic contributions increased household economic security and allowed them to support their husbands more with work in the fields, which improved relationships.

Finally, two of the qualitative papers described achievements-related outcomes: physical health and fitness [48] and life happiness/peacefulness.52 These outcomes were achieved through the lower life burden from having fewer children. In fact, the impact on women’s empowerment of having fewer children was a crosscutting theme across all these papers. Having more children increased the amount of housework/care giving, which took time away from engaging in work or school, while also tying women more to the home (reducing agency). Having more children also increased household stress broadly, straining marital relationships, and decreasing mental and physical health. As was summed up by one woman in Ethiopia about having fewer children: “Our minds and hearts are cool and restful now.” [47].

Discussion

The goal of this review was to synthesize the recent evidence for the impact of family planning on women’s empowerment, with an emphasis on viewing empowerment as a process. Despite decades of interest in the association between family planning and women’s empowerment, and the underlying assumption that family planning somehow empowers women, we found 3,170 papers, but only 14 quantitative and three qualitative studies that rigorously evaluated the impact of family planning on empowerment-related outcomes that were framed as women’s empowerment. Of these studies, findings were mixed across empowerment domains, with most studies reporting at least one positive association alongside null or negative findings for other outcomes. Studies reporting positive associations for at least one empowerment outcome included those measuring resources such as labor force participation and wages [33, 34, 41, 44, 45], agency-related outcomes such as decision-making and mobility [33, 37, 42], and achievement outcomes including intergenerational effects [35, 46]. Overall, across all measured outcomes, 47% showed positive associations, 39% showed null associations, and 14% showed negative associations. Positive associations varied by domain: 59% for Resources, 56% for agency, and 40% for achievements. Overall, despite lack of consistent theory and measurement, and evidence quality classification as generally low, these findings suggest that family planning is positively associated with improvements in women’s empowerment.

Robustly evaluating the relationship between family planning and women’s empowerment is challenging due to the latent nature of empowerment as a concept, which is measured through a variety of proxy variables, some of which are quite challenging to reliably and validly capture (especially perhaps those related to agency, for example, gender norms, autonomy, mobility, etc.). Furthermore, if we conceptualize empowerment as a process, the expected amount of time that it may take for an intervention to influence any of these empowerment outcomes, and, more importantly, show impacts on outcomes across the domains of empowerment, is often outside the scope of one defined research study. This may have contributed to the low number of studies that met our inclusion criteria.

Almost half of the quantitative studies in this analysis came from the Matlab program in Bangladesh [36, 40, 43–46],,which, while providing valuable long-term follow-up data over several decades, showed predominantly negative or null results. This geographic concentration limits our ability to make broader claims about family planning’s impact on empowerment across diverse settings, particularly given that these studies dominated the Asian evidence base. The predominance of null or negative findings from Matlab suggests that community-health worker delivered family planning services were less effective for improving women’s empowerment compared to other delivery approaches examined in our review. However, several contextual factors may explain these findings: the Matlab program included multiple maternal and child health services beyond family planning, was implemented in a region with particularly restrictive gender norms, and was conducted decades ago using approaches that may not reflect contemporary best practices. These contextual limitations are compounded by how empowerment has been conceptualized and measured in the literature.

Most of the papers in our review, and indeed consistent with broader empowerment literature, framed empowerment as economic empowerment or “resources” (using the Kabeer framework) [9]. This included measures around income/labor force [33, 34, 36, 44, 45, 54] and education [41, 45]. The limited scope of empowerment outcomes identified suggests that the conceptualization of women’s empowerment in this literature is not comprehensive, with most studies measuring only one domain rather than capturing empowerment as a dynamic process that spans resources, agency, and achievements. This limitation reflects both the measurement challenges noted throughout our review where resource indicators are more readily quantifiable than agency or achievement measures. There are several reasons that there might be this over-reliance on economic empowerment/resources. One explanation is that these types of outcomes are relatively easier to measure. For example, wages, having a job or labor force participation, being in school or number of years of educational attainment, are easily quantifiable and defined measures. Decision-making power and mobility are hard to measure, and most agency and achievement concepts, are harder to define and measure. Additionally, measures such as wages or schooling are also more comparable across contexts, whereas other domains of empowerment may mean different things in different contexts. Another explanation could be that much of the initial focus on empowerment as a concept was related to economic empowerment or resources and led by economists. In fact, the two most similar previous reviews carried out during the timeframe of, but earlier than our systematic review had a more economic focus to their definition of empowerment, and therefore included a different set of papers (with some overlap) [4, 5]. While some change over time in the disciplinary focus of those researching women’s empowerment has occurred, a recent literature review reported that despite being conducted by multidisciplinary scholars, women’s empowerment research since Kabeer’s 1999 seminal work has been predominantly published in economics and development journals [55].

Our review identified papers that measured agency-related outcomes, such as decision-making, bargaining power, and mobility; [33, 37, 38, 43, 44] however, agency-related outcomes were more disparate than the relatively standardized economic measures (wages, employment, education), which made them harder to compare across studies. The set of items related to decision-making, which have been used globally in the Demographic and Health Surveys and have been adopted in number of other studies across the world, are one of the few validated, comparable, and standardized measures used for agency-related empowerment outcomes [56]. These, or similar measures, were used in a number of the papers from Bangladesh and India in our review [37, 43, 44]. Achievements were not measured frequently, with only three studies from Bangladesh and the USA examining this domain [35, 40, 46]. This is the least well defined and least often discussed domain of empowerment, likely due to less clear definition of what achievements really are in relationship to women’s empowerment, and the limited scope and depth of achievement outcomes limited our findings for this domain. Within our review, achievement-related outcomes identified included women’s wellbeing and intergenerational impacts; however, we excluded studies exploring the effect of family planning on maternal and child health outcomes a priori, since these rarely were defining such outcomes as a measure of empowerment. Had these been conceptualized differently within the literature (i.e., if we had included women and children’s health outcomes in our review as a measure of achievements), we would likely have had a much larger number of papers reporting on this domain.

There is clearly a need for measures of empowerment with indicators that capture all the domains of empowerment and that empowerment is a process. A unified measure capturing resources, agency, and achievements together could provide a richer understanding of how something like family planning (or any other exposure, characteristics, or experience) might influence empowerment. While these three domains of empowerment are conceptualized as a process, a longitudinal measure is not necessarily needed, as women might be empowered across domains simultaneously, or historical data (such as total education) can be integrated. Ideally, a measure that was validated across different regions would allow for cross country comparisons. However, empowerment may operate differently across different contexts. In this review, studies from different regions measured different empowerment related outcomes. This could be reflective of different conceptualizations of empowerment in those regions/contexts, different availability of validated measures in those regions, or, given the small number of papers, simple random variation based on the interest of the researchers. As found in a review on measuring women’s empowerment by Desai and colleagues, we also noted that studies from South Asia often measured women’s freedom of movement as a measure of empowerment, while this was less included in the sub-Saharan African context [57]. Desai and colleagues also mentioned that some earlier frameworks on empowerment and its association with health outcomes and fertility were examined/tested in South Asian contexts and are, therefore, best understood in that context [57]. This raises the possibility that empowerment measures may need to be contextually specific, but more research that included detailed formative and ethnographic work across diverse regions exploring multiple empowerment domains could inform if a universal measure is possible, or if a measure with slightly different items to ensure culturally appropriateness in each setting could be developed for cross-context comparison. Another idea is that a measure or a scale that weighs items differently depending on the region/cultural context could be developed. Delving into other disciplines, such as anthropologic literature, could help refine empowerment measures.

Despite the aforementioned challenges of a unified measure, some attempts have been made to develop an index that measures women’s empowerment. For instance, the SWPER (Survey-based Women’s Empowerment Index) is a reputed index that has been developed using Demographic and Health Survey data of currently married women from 34 African countries. The 15-item index is focused on three dimensions, including attitudes towards violence, decision-making authority, and social independence, and is often aggregated to study country-level differences in empowerment [58]. SWPER has recently been extended outside of Africa, finding that the constructs and measures held globally [58].

Another index used widely in the field of women’s empowerment focused on agriculture is the WEAI (women’s empowerment in agriculture index). WEAI, comprising two sub-indices, measures the empowerment, agency, and inclusion of women in the agriculture sector. WEAI was developed in the US context in 2012 to study changes in women’s empowerment resulting from America’s Feed the Future program. The WEAI is an aggregate index, often reported at the country or regional level, but is based on surveys of men and women within the same households [59]. This index comprises two subindices: the five domains of empowerment (5DE) and the Gender Parity Index. The domains of empowerment include (1) decisions about agricultural production, (2) access to and decision-making power about productive resources, (3) control of use of income, (4) leadership in the community, and (5) time allocation. The GPI sub-index measures gender disparity at the household-level about household decision-making, access to and control over resources, income, leadership, and time use [59]. These multidimensional measures collapse resources, agency, and achievements into a single score, allowing researchers to make cross-country comparisons and track advancements in women’s empowerment over time [57]. These newer measures were not used in papers included within our review, perhaps because some of these are more recently developed or developed for specific settings, and so are not as easily useable in all contexts, or they may just be less well disseminated. The Evidence-based Measures of Empowerment for Research on Gender Equality collection (EMERGE) website (https://emerge.ucsd.edu/) is a useful repository of gender empowerment measures that researchers wishing to expand their knowledge of measures of empowerment may find useful.

Another important reason to develop and use measures that incorporate multiple domains is that empowerment in one dimension may not necessarily translate into empowerment in other dimensions [60, 61]. For instance, in a few studies done in Bangladesh, women’s employment was associated with an increase in intimate partner violence [62–65]. Empirical research from developing countries also suggests that paid work as an indicator of empowerment should be examined carefully since women may resort to paid work for survival [39]. In such situations, it may be important to examine if the paid work truly empowered women or explore other dimensionalities of paid work, including work in the formal sector, full-time labor, and social protection benefits, among others [39].

Another substantial challenge that we faced in this review was the limited identification of robustly evaluated family planning interventions. Given the time frame of our review (2000–2022), most studies were publishing findings from more recent interventions (aside from those about Matlab Bangaldesh). It is therefore possible (and perhaps hoped) that as a global community, we have reached an era where it is unethical to randomize people to a family planning intervention (compared to no services), or perhaps not feasible given widespread access to family planning. Subsequently, many of the papers we reviewed relied on natural experiments, policies or program changes, or use older data but were published in this time frame. Given that more research is needed to understand if and how family planning helps support women’s empowerment, future researchers need to think carefully about designing rigorous interventions or study designs that are ethical or which cleverly leverage natural experiments.

In summary, there are several challenges moving forward in this area of study. There is a clear need for comprehensive validated measures that capture all domains of empowerment (resources, agency, and achievements), especially those that conceptualize empowerment as a process and are theory-informed. Future studies should attempt to measure across more domains of empowerment, where possible. Currently, different measures and indicators are used across studies, and relatedly, different frameworks are employed by researchers. Careful thinking about the possibility and appropriateness of a cross-cultural measure is needed given that empowerment is conceptualized and experienced differently across different regions.

Limitations

The results of our systematic review should be considered considering some limitations. First, while our team implemented a comprehensive search across 20 databases and 13 grey literature sources, it is possible that some papers that would have been eligible for inclusion into our study were missed. We mitigated this to an extent by including databases from other disciplines such as EconLit and Sociological Abstracts, to include research published by interdisciplinary researchers. Second, several quantitative studies did not provide sufficient detail on plausible mechanisms through which family planning might have influenced women’s empowerment. The geographic concentration in our evidence base limits generalizability and represents a key limitation of the current literature on family planning and empowerment. Future research should prioritize studies across diverse geographical, cultural, and programmatic contexts to build more robust evidence for policy and practice. Third, studies rarely, if at all, reported any adverse or unintended consequences of the family planning interventions, and therefore could not be assessed. Fourth, the variability in the indicators of women’s empowerment precluded us from making quantitative comparisons across studies, limiting conclusion about the magnitude of impact of family planning interventions on women’s empowerment. Fifth, while it was encouraging that we identified a robust set of papers presenting null findings, we were unable to estimate publication bias and it is possible that more null results exist but were never published.

Sixth, we implemented certainty of evidence assessment using the GRADE criteria for four outcomes following recent recommendations regarding this evaluation in the absence of meta-analysis; [66] however, these results may have limited utility due to the heterogeneity of studies. Relatedly, given the heterogenous studies in our review, selecting a quality assessment tool was challenging. We acknowledge that the Newcastle-Ottawa scale is limited by subjectivity in scoring. We sought to mitigate this through having two reviewers independently review each paper and assign scores, with conflicts resolved using a third team member. Eighth, our inclusion of children’s outcomes as empowerment measures may conflate different theoretical pathways, as family planning can improve children’s outcomes through resource reallocation mechanisms independent of women’s empowerment [67], making it difficult to distinguish genuine intergenerational empowerment transmission from household resource optimization effects. Future research should distinguish between direct empowerment outcomes, household resource effects, and intergenerational empowerment transmission using separate analytical models and mediation analysis to clarify specific mechanisms. Finally, we recognize that by a priori deciding that all papers had to use the term “empowerment” and frame their outcomes as empowerment, we have likely missed some papers that used a different framing. However, this allowed our review to present research on the impact of family planning on empowerment more broadly, overcoming limitations to other similar reviews which focused solely on economic outcomes [4, 5].

Conclusions

This systematic review presents a comprehensive summary of evidence from 2000 to 2022 about the impact of family planning interventions and use on women’s empowerment, with specific attention paid to conceptualizing empowerment more broadly across domains. Our quantitative evidence provides some evidence that family planning can lead to improvements in women’s empowerment, through diverse indicators spanning economic outcomes, decision-making power, mobility, and intergenerational effects, however results are mixed. Our qualitative evidence suggests possible pathways through which family planning access and usage can influence the various domains of women’s empowerment. In order to fully understand if and how family planning empowers women, additional studies with rigorous designs and comprehensive measures of empowerment are needed. Ensuring family planning access and usage continues to improve for women in need is critical both as a fundamental right and for supporting women’s empowerment and reproductive goals.
