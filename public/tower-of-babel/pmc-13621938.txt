Integrated Biomarker Assessment of Bifenthrin, Chlorpyrifos and Endosulfan Toxicity in Indigenous Carps (Catla catla and Labeo rohita): Oxidative Stress, Genotoxicity and Nuclear Alterations
Authors: Huma Naz, Sajid Abdullah, Tanveer Ahmed, Khalid Abbas, Muhammad Umar Ijaz, Qudrat Ullah, Shabana Naz, Rifat Ullah Khan, Luciana Rossi, Sohail Ahmad, Rasha Alonaizan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe extensive use of pesticides in agriculture has increased their runoff into aquatic ecosystems, posing serious risks to non‐target organisms. Fish are recognized as sensitive bioindicators and are widely used to evaluate the ecological impacts of pesticide contamination.ObjectiveThis study investigated the acute toxic effects of bifenthrin + chlorpyrifos (B + C) and bifenthrin + endosulfan (B + E) mixtures on antioxidant enzyme activities, DNA damage and nuclear abnormalities (NAs) in Catla catla and Labeo rohita.MethodsAcute toxicity tests were conducted to determine the 96‐h LC50 values of both pesticide mixtures. Fish were subsequently exposed to the respective LC50 concentrations for 4 days, with sampling at 24‐h intervals. Antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione S‐transferase (GST), peroxidase (POx) and catalase (CAT), were evaluated in different organs. Genotoxic effects were assessed through micronucleus (MN), NAs, damaged nuclei and genomic damage index (GDI) analyses.ResultsThe 96‐h LC50 values of the B + E mixture were 1.86 ± 0.01 and 2.84 ± 0.02 µg L−1, whereas those of the B + C mixture were 2.09 ± 0.01 and 3.41 ± 0.07 µg L−1 for C. catla and L. rohita, respectively. C. catla exhibited greater sensitivity to both mixtures. SOD and GST activities increased significantly (p < 0.05) in all organs with increasing exposure duration. POx activity generally increased with exposure time, while CAT activity increased in gills, liver and kidney but declined in brain, heart and muscle tissues. Micronuclei and other NAs increased significantly with exposure duration in both species. Genomic damage indices showed significant positive dependence on exposure duration in L. rohita.ConclusionCombined exposure to bifenthrin–chlorpyrifos and bifenthrin–endosulfan mixtures induces pronounced oxidative stress and genotoxic damage in C. catla and L. rohita, highlighting the usefulness of antioxidant and DNA damage biomarkers for monitoring pesticide contamination in aquatic environments.

Introduction

The unregulated and excessive use of pesticides in Pakistan has emerged as a serious environmental and public health concern. Over the past two decades, pesticide consumption has reportedly increased by more than 1000%, reflecting intensified agricultural dependence on chemical pest control. This rapid growth, coupled with weak regulatory enforcement and limited farmer awareness, has heightened risks of environmental contamination and occupational exposure (Rashid et al. 2022; M. I. Khan et al. 2025). Among commonly applied pesticide classes—carbamates, organochlorines, organophosphates and pyrethroids—organochlorines are particularly concerning due to their persistence, lipophilicity and long‐range environmental mobility (Sing et al. 2023). Their resistance to degradation promotes bioaccumulation and biomagnification across trophic levels, posing significant toxicological risks to aquatic organisms and humans (Sallam et al. 2018). Through agricultural runoff, rainfall and drainage systems, these chemicals ultimately enter aquatic ecosystems, where they impair ecological integrity and threaten food safety. Despite well‐documented adverse impacts, pesticides remain widely used, underscoring the urgent need for strengthened regulation, environmental monitoring and sustainable pest management strategies (Grande et al. 1994; Naz, Chatha, Ullah, et al. 2023).

Chlorpyrifos, one of the most extensively used organophosphate insecticides in agriculture, is well recognized for its pronounced aquatic toxicity. It disrupts antioxidants and immune homeostasis, triggering excessive apoptosis, lipid peroxidation and neurobehavioral impairments in exposed organisms (El‐Megid et al. 2019). Among organochlorine insecticides, endosulfan is widely applied for pest control in horticulture and field crops (Sutherland et al. 2004; Tuduri et al. 2006; Berdowska and Bandurska 2025). In fish, it primarily targets the central nervous system, inducing hyperactivity, convulsions and in severe cases, mortality. Bifenthrin, a synthetic pyrethroid characterized by enhanced photostability and strong insecticidal efficacy, is broadly used in crop protection, preconstruction termite control and stored grain management (Hougard et al. 2002; Yadav et al. 2003; Jiang et al. 2023). In addition, pyrethroids are employed in aquaculture to manage parasitic infestations, such as Lepeophtheirus salmonis in salmon farming (Pike and Wadsworth 2000; El‐Megid et al. 2019; M. S. Khan et al. 2026).

Pesticides are also reported as pro‐oxidants in various organs, altering antioxidant defences such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (POx) and glutathione S‐transferase (GST) response by the unnecessary formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), damage the protein (Nwani et al. 2015; Kreuz et al. 2016) and may also cause lipid peroxidation in the plasma membrane of the cell (Lim‐on‐Pacheco and Gonsebatt 2009). The mutagenic properties of several chemical agents can also be detected by several testing protocols, which include short‐term tests such as acute toxicity, gross chromosomal changes, micronuclei formation, analysis of sister chromatids and effects on DNA (Dar et al. 2016; Naz, Ishaque, et al. 2025). Among the different genotoxicity tests, one of the most rapid and sensitive tests is the comet assay, which detects and quantifies damage (R. Kumar et al. 2010) like alkali‐labile sites, and single‐ and double‐strand breaks in aquatic animals particularly in fish (Tice et al. 2000). Another very simple and sensitive technique is the micronucleus (MN) test widely applied in fish for evaluating the genotoxicity in aquatic environments (Talapatra and Banerjee 2007; Naz et al. 2026). Along with this, nuclear abnormalities (NAs) have also been widely applied in fish toxicology as indicators of genotoxicity. Nowadays, in aquatic toxicological research combined use of comet and micronucleus techniques is preferred to assess both the acute and chronic effects of many genotoxic substances (Ergene et al. 2007; M. S. Khan et al. 2025).

Fish are globally considered as nutrient rich food source for human beings due to their high protein. Fish not only satisfy the nutritional demands of humans but also raise our total yield (Schreiner et al. 2016; M. I. Khan et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2014). Now, a decline in fish population has been observed due to various anthropogenic and natural activities (Sing et al. 2023; Naz et al. 2021). One of the main causes of fish decline is the increased application of pesticides in crop fields (Zahan et al. 2019). Indian major carps like Catla catla, Labeo rohita and Cyprinus carpio are the native freshwater edible fish species of Pakistan. These fish species are negatively affected by aquatic pollution caused by sewage waste and agriculture runoff that fall into the rivers, resulting in different alterations in biochemical, histopathological and hematological parameters of fish, which, when taken by human caused various types of disorders in the body (Giannopolitis et al. 1977; Yaseen et al. 2024).

One of the major problems in monitoring environmental risk assessment is that insecticides are present in the form of a mixture rather than a single compound in water bodies (Sutherland et al. 2004; Nwani et al. 2015). The farmers normally apply these pesticides in the form of combination for the effective control of various pests, like insects and diseases. Most of these applied pesticides (70%) are extremely or moderately harmful to biological systems (Rashid et al. 2022; El‐Megid et al. 2019). There is a need to perform more scientific research to determine the exact concentrations and exposure periods that do not produce detrimental or trans‐genetic toxic effects in fish, and for their safe use in the future (Yaseen et al. 2024). Therefore, the present research work was designed to assess the acute toxic effects of insecticides mixtures exposure duration on antioxidant enzymes, DNA damage and nuclear anomalies in two cyprinidi fish C. catla and L. rohita.

Materials and Methods

The acute toxicity assay, including the determination of the 96‐h LC50 and lethal concentrations, was conducted using two cyprinid fish species, C. catla and L. rohita, in the Toxicology Laboratory at the University of Agriculture, Faisalabad (UAF), Pakistan. Both fish species were obtained from the Fisheries Research Farm, UAF and acclimatized to laboratory conditions for 14 days prior to the experiment.

Standard stock‐I solutions were freshly prepared by separately dissolving 1 g of each technical‐grade insecticide—chlorpyrifos (C), bifenthrin (B) and endosulfan (E)—in 100 mL of analytical‐grade methanol. The insecticide mixtures B + E (1:1) and B + C (1:1) were prepared by further diluting the respective stock‐I solutions with deionized water.

For the acute toxicity test, 10 fish of each species were separately transferred to 100‐L glass aquaria (100 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm) and exposed to the insecticide mixtures B + E and B + C for 96 h. Each treatment was conducted in triplicate. All aquaria were continuously aerated using automated air pumps. The toxicity tests were performed under constant water‐quality conditions, with pH, temperature and total hardness maintained at 7.0, 27°C and 225 mg L− 1, respectively. Fish mortality was monitored throughout the exposure period, and dead fish were promptly removed from the aquaria.

Following the acute toxicity tests, 15 fish of each species were separately exposed to the LC50 concentration of each insecticide mixture for 4 days. Sampling was conducted after 1 day of exposure. A group of fish maintained in water without insecticide mixtures served as the negative control (NC). For the positive control (PC), fish were injected intraperitoneally with cyclophosphamide at a dose of 20 µg g− 1 body weight. At each sampling point, the fish were sacrificed for subsequent analysis.

Before sacrifice, the fish were anesthetized with clove oil at a dose of 0.2 mL per 500 mL of water.

Antioxidant Defence System

After each sampling, activities of POx, SOD, CAT and phase‐II GST were determined in the brain, kidney, heart, gills, muscle and liver of both fish species.

SOD Assay

The SOD activity was checked by following the method of Giannopolitis and Ries (1977). In each cuvette, buffer (1 mL), enzyme extract (0.05 mL) and riboflavin (0.016 mL) were added, and all the cuvettes were placed in a light box containing a 30‐watt light bulb. After 12 min cuvettes were shifted into the spectrophotometer, where NACN/EDTA (0.067 mL) and NBT (0.033 mL) were added to the illuminated reaction mixture and absorbance was noted at (A560 nm) after 20 s. For the blank, buffer (1 mL) was used. The SOD activity was calculated by measuring the % inhibition of NBT.

CAT Assay

The Chance and Mehaly (1955) protocol was adopted to calculate the CAT activity. First, a buffer substrate solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mM H2O2 (0.442 mL) in 60 mM sodium phosphate buffer. In a cuvette, buffered substrate solution (1.95 mL) and enzyme extract (0.05 mL) were added and transferred into a spectrophotometer set to 0 at a wavelength of 240 nm. The reaction time was 3 min, and the absorbance was noted after interval of 1 min. The blank was phosphate buffer (60 mM).

POD Assay

The POx level was observed by following the procedure of Civello et al. (1995). For POx activity, 3 mL of buffer substrate solution containing guaiacol (750 µL), phosphate buffer (47mL, pH 6.5, 0.2 M) and H2O2 (0.3 mL) were added to a cuvette. The 0.06 mL of enzyme extract was also added and transferred into the spectrophotometer. The absorbance was observed after 3 min at 470 nm. The phosphate buffer was used as a blank.

GST Assay

The Phase‐II GST activity was noted according to the Mannervik method. Briefly, the buffer substrate solution: 980 µL of PBS (pH 6.5), 10 µL of CDNB (100 mM) and 10 µL of glutathione (100 mM) were mixed well in a beaker. The 900 µL of substrate solution was added to a plastic cuvette (1.5 mL size) and incubate it room temperature for 5 min. After that, 100 µL of enzyme sample was added to the cuvette and put it into the spectrophotometer to note the absorbance at A340 nm. Here, 100 µL of PBS was used as a blank.

Comet Assay for DNA Damage

The comet assay was performed according to the method of N. P. Singh et al. (1988) with appropriate modifications. Blood samples were collected from the caudal vein of the fish using a clean, heparinized syringe. Freshly collected blood was immediately processed to prepare single‐cell suspensions for the assay. The procedure consisted of cell lysis, alkaline unwinding, electrophoresis, neutralization, staining and microscopic analysis.

For cell lysis, the blood samples were mixed with low melting point agarose and spread onto pre‐coated microscope slides. The slides were covered with a coverslip and allowed to solidify. They were then immersed in a cold lysis solution to remove cellular and nuclear membranes and expose DNA. Following lysis, the slides were placed in an alkaline electrophoresis buffer to allow DNA unwinding and conversion of alkali‐labile sites into DNA strand breaks. Electrophoresis was subsequently performed under alkaline conditions. After electrophoresis, the slides were gently neutralized with an appropriate neutralization buffer and stained with ethidium bromide to visualize the DNA. The stained slides were examined using an epifluorescence microscope.

For each treatment and fish species, separate slides were prepared, and a total of 150 cells (50 cells per replicate) were randomly selected and scored. DNA damage was evaluated according to the comet classification system described by Grover et al. (2003), based on the percentage of DNA present in the comet tail. The comets were classified into five categories: T0, no or minimal damage (< 5%); T1, low damage (5%–20%); TII, moderate damage (20%–40%); TIII, high damage (40%–75%) and TIV, extreme damage (> 75%). The percentage of damaged cells and genetic damage index (GDI) were calculated using the following formulas: Damagedcells%=TypesII+III+IV GDI=TypeI+2TypeII+3TypeIII+4TypeIVType0+TypeI+TypeII+TypeIII+TypeIV

Micronucleus Test for Nuclear Anomalies

The micronucleus (MN) assay was performed on peripheral blood erythrocytes according to the method described by Barsiene et al. (2004). Blood samples were collected from the caudal vein of the fish, and thin blood smears were immediately prepared on clean, labelled glass microscope slides. The smears were air‐dried at room temperature and fixed in absolute methanol for approximately 10 min. The fixed slides were then stained with 5% Giemsa solution prepared in phosphate buffer and allowed to dry before microscopic examination.

Micronuclei (MNi) and NAs were identified and scored according to the criteria described by Fenech et al. (2003). Micronuclei were identified as small, round or oval, clearly separated extranuclear bodies with staining characteristics like those of the main nucleus. NAs, including blebbed, notched, lobed, vacuolated, binucleated and other morphologically altered nuclei, were also recorded.

For each treatment and fish species, 1000 erythrocytes per sample were randomly examined using a LABOMED CXR3 binocular microscope at 1000 × magnification with a 100 × oil‐immersion objective lens. The frequencies of micronuclei and NAs were expressed as the number of affected cells per 1000 cells examined. The frequency of abnormalities was calculated using the following formula:

MN(%)=NumberofcellscontainingmicronucleusTotalnumberofcellscounted×100

Statistical Analysis

The 96‐h LC50 and lethal concentrations of the insecticide mixtures for the fish species were estimated using probit analysis in MINITAB software. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All statistical analyses were performed using Statistix version 8.1. For biochemical parameters, including SOD, CAT, POX and GST activities, analysis of variance (ANOVA) under a factorial design using a linear model was performed to determine the effects of insecticide mixture, exposure duration, organ and their interactions. Where significant differences were detected, means were separated using the Student–Newman–Keuls (SNK) multiple comparison test. Differences among treatment means were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

For the comet assay, a total of 150 cells (50 cells per replicate) were randomly selected and scored using an epifluorescence microscope (N‐400M, American Scope, UK). The percentage of damaged cells and GDI were calculated based on the observed comet classes. For the micronucleus assay, the frequencies of micronuclei (MNi) and NAs were determined by scoring 1000 erythrocytes per sample using a LABOMED CXR3 binocular microscope at 1000 × magnification with a 100 × oil‐immersion objective lens. Because the genotoxicity data were non‐normally distributed, differences between groups were evaluated using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test. In addition, regression analysis was performed to determine the relationship between DNA damage/genotoxic responses and exposure duration. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

Toxicity

The mean LC50 (96‐h) values for the B + E mixture were calculated as high as 1.86 ± 0.01 and 2.84 ± 0.02 µgL−1 for C. catla and L. rohita, respectively. However, for the B + C mixture, this value was lower for C. catla and L. rohita at 2.09 ± 0.01 and 3.41 ± 0.07 µgL−1, respectively. The lethal value of B + C mixture for C. catla, and L. rohita was estimated as 3.44 ± 0.06 and 5.92 ± 0.11µgL−1, respectively. The C. catla (3.06 ± 0.04 µgL−1) showed higher sensitivity to the B + E mixture than L. rohita (4.48 ± 0.10 µgL−1) for lethal concentration (Figures 1 and 2).

In Figures 1 and 2, lethal values were computed by using probit analysis in which data on fish mortality is transforms a sigmoid dose‐response curve into a straight line, allowing for regression analysis to estimate the concentration of a substance/chemical that causes a certain percentage of fish to die (LC50). The LC50 is calculated by finding the concentration on the x‐axis that corresponds to the probit value of 50% or 100% mortality on the y‐axis.

Antioxidant Defence System

Figure 3 illustrates the alterations in the activities of SOD, CAT, POx and GST in different organs of C. catla following exposure to the B + E and B + C insecticide mixtures for different durations. Factorial ANOVA followed by the SNK test revealed significant differences (p < 0.05) in enzyme activities among the insecticide treatments, exposure durations and examined organs.

Alterations in (A) SOD, (B) CAT, (C) POX and (D) GST activities in different organs of C. catla exposed to B + E and B + C insecticide mixtures for different durations. Values represent mean ± SE. Different letters indicate significant differences among treatments (p < 0.05) according to factorial ANOVA followed by the Student–Newman–Keuls test.

SOD activity showed a significant increase (p < 0.05) in the examined organs with increasing exposure to the insecticide mixtures, indicating an enhanced antioxidant response to pesticide‐induced oxidative stress. Similarly, POX and GST activities were significantly induced (p < 0.05) following exposure, although the magnitude of the response varied among organs and exposure durations. In contrast, CAT activity showed a significant decline (p < 0.05), particularly in the heart, brain and muscle, suggesting impairment or depletion of the CAT‐mediated antioxidant defence system under insecticide stress.

Comparison between the two insecticide mixtures demonstrated that B + E produced a significantly greater alteration in enzymatic activities than B + C (p < 0.05). The magnitude of the enzymatic response also varied with exposure duration, indicating a time‐dependent effect of the insecticide mixtures. Overall, the observed elevation of SOD, POX and GST activities, together with the reduction in CAT activity, demonstrates substantial disruption of the antioxidant and detoxification systems of C. catla following exposure to the insecticide mixtures.

In the present research work, both assays were applied to assess DNA damage in erythrocytes of indigenous species, namely, C. catla and L. rohita exposed to acute pesticides mixtures. Similarly, in both fish species, MN and other NAs such as binucleated nuclei, dumbbell nuclei, blebbed nuclei, notched nuclei and de‐shaped nuclei had a positive and significant dependence on the duration of mixture exposure (Figures 9 and 10). Discussion

The mean 96‐h LC50 values for the B + E mixture were higher for C. catla and L. rohita, whereas the corresponding values for the B + C mixture were comparatively lower. Information regarding the toxicity of insecticide mixtures to fish is relatively limited. However, several studies have investigated the lethal toxicity of binary and ternary insecticide mixtures. For example, the toxicity of the B + C + E mixture has been evaluated in C. carpio and Ctenopharyngodon idella as well as in C. catla, C. mrigala and L. rohita (Ambreen and Javed 2015; Fenech et al. 2003). Ambreen and Javed () C + B, E + C and E + B, in C. carpio. The toxicity of the E + C mixture has also been investigated in C. catla and L. rohita (Kadhim and Al‐Rudainy 2012; A. Singh et al. 2023). Fish mortality following pesticide exposure is influenced by several factors, including pesticide concentration, exposure duration, fish size, habitat, developmental stage, age, nutritional status, sensitivity and species‐specific biological characteristics. Thus, even closely related species within the same genus or family may exhibit different sensitivities to pesticides (Belenguer et al. 2014; Piazza et al. 2015).

The extensive use of pesticides in agriculture to increase crop production has contributed to the occurrence of pesticide mixtures in aquatic environments (Masia et al. 2015; Bayır et al. 2023). Biochemical biomarkers are useful indicators for assessing pesticide‐induced acute and chronic effects on different organs and for evaluating the physiological status of fish (Ural 2013). In the present study, significant alterations in the activities of the antioxidant and detoxification enzymes SOD, POX, CAT and GST were observed in all examined organs of both fish species during exposure to the insecticide mixtures. The antioxidant defence system plays an important protective role against oxidative stress, and disruption of antioxidant enzyme activity can disturb the cellular redox balance. Pesticides can stimulate the generation of ROS, and the observed increase in antioxidant enzyme activities may represent a compensatory response to ROS production and detoxification (Stara et al. 2013; Masia et al. 2015). Increased activities of SOD, GST and POX have also been reported in various organs of L. rohita following pesticide exposure (Kadhim and Al‐Rudainy 2012).

The effects of CPF + END exposure duration on SOD, CAT, POX and GST activities in C. catla have also been investigated (Kadhim and Al‐Rudainy 2012; A. Singh et al. 2023). CAT activity was significantly increased in the liver, gills and kidneys but decreased in the brain, heart and muscle. Similarly, exposure to C + E and B + E mixtures resulted in significant reductions in CAT activity in neural, muscular and cardiac tissues of C. idella after 24 and 96 h of exposure (Usman et al. 2020). Siddique et al. (2020) reported increased GST activity in the brain, gills, kidneys, heart, muscle and liver of L. rohita exposed to chlorpyrifos. Liu et al. (2015) observed decreased CAT activity in the brain, spleen, kidneys and liver of Carassius auratus following short‐term exposure to triazophos. Stoyanova et al. (2020) also reported alterations in GPx and CAT activities in common carp exposed to chlorpyrifos for 72 h. Deltamethrin exposure similarly resulted in decreased CAT activity in the brain and muscle of fish (Sharma and Ansari 2013). Ucar et al. (2020) reported significant alterations in SOD, CAT and GST activities in the gills, kidneys and liver of Oncorhynchus mykiss and Salmo trutta fario. Deb and Das (2021) confirmed a concentration‐ and duration‐dependent increase in SOD, CAT and GPx activities in the gills and liver of Channa punctata following chlorpyrifos exposure. Cypermethrin also caused significant fluctuations in SOD, GPx and CAT activities in the liver, kidneys, gills and brain of Alburnus tarichi after 24, 48, 72 and 96 h of exposure (Ozok 2020). Chlorpyrifos‐induced toxicity has further been associated with impaired physiological and antioxidant responses in Caspian brown trout, C. catla, L. rohita, C. mrigala, C. carpio and O. niloticus (Tilak et al. 2004; Wang et al. 2013; Adel et al. 2017; Zahran et al. 2018).

Bifenthrin toxicity may involve disruption of sodium‐channel gating at nerve‐cell endings and interference with other ion channels, including chloride and calcium channels (Burr and Ray 2014). Endosulfan can also undergo degradation to relatively persistent metabolites, including endosulfan sulphate, ether, lactone, diol, alcohol and α‐hydroxy ether, some of which may exhibit greater toxicity than the parent compound (Awasthi et al. 2000). Organophosphate pesticides may contribute to ROS generation through multiple mechanisms. One proposed pathway involves redox cycling mediated by cytochrome P450 enzymes. Organophosphates containing a P═O group, or those metabolically converted from a P═S group, can undergo electron transfer reactions that generate superoxide anions, which may subsequently give rise to other reactive species, including hydroxyl radicals (Kovacic 2003). Another mechanism involves impairment of antioxidant defence systems, resulting in excessive accumulation of ROS within cells (Karaoz et al. 2002).

The comet and micronucleus assays are widely used and sensitive techniques for detecting DNA damage in peripheral blood erythrocytes (Mughal et al. 2010). Therefore, both assays were employed in the present study to evaluate DNA damage in erythrocytes of the indigenous fish species C. catla and L. rohita following acute exposure to pesticide mixtures. In both species, pesticide exposure resulted in changes in the percentage of damaged nuclei, GDI, micronucleus (MN) frequency and other NAs. Similar findings have been reported in C. catla exposed to CPF + END, where MN and NAs showed a positive relationship with exposure duration, whereas other NAs exhibited no significant association with exposure time (Siddiqu et al. 2020). A significant increase in MN frequency in the erythrocytes of Prochilodus lineatus following 96 h of cypermethrin exposure has also been reported (Davico et al. 2020). Hemalatha et al. (2020) observed significant increases in DNA strand breaks, MN frequency and NAs in erythrocytes of C. carpio exposed to quinalphos. Mitkovska and Chassovnikarvo (2020) reported increased frequencies of several NAs, including blebbed, notched, eight‐shaped and lobed nuclei, nuclear bridges, nuclear buds and binucleated cells in common carp exposed to chlorpyrifos. Sadiqul et al. (2016) also documented increased frequencies of erythrocytic MN and NAs in silver barb with increasing exposure duration to quinalphos. Similarly, significant DNA damage was observed in the erythrocytes of silver carp following exposure to deltamethrin (Ullah et al. 2019; Islam et al. 2019).

Ambreen and Javed (2015) reported erythrocytic DNA damage, expressed as the percentage of damaged cells and GDI, in C. carpio during the first four fortnights of exposure to a bifenthrin + chlorpyrifos mixture, followed by a decline during the fifth fortnight. A significant increase in MN frequency has also been reported in striped catfish exposed to different concentrations of Sumithion (Moore et al. 2010; Mohanty et al. 2011; Lu et al. 2013). The present findings further support the genotoxic potential of pesticide mixtures, as the END + CPF mixture caused significant nuclear damage and increased the GDI in erythrocytes of C. catla, with DNA damage showing a significant dependence on exposure duration (Fenech et al. 2003).

The genotoxic effects of these pesticides may be associated with their ability to induce ROS production (Kovacic 2003). Major ROS, including superoxide anions, hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals, can cause oxidative damage to nucleic acids, resulting in DNA strand breaks and modification of nitrogenous bases, including purines and pyrimidines (Hayat et al. 2007; Ercal et al. 2001; Naz, Ullah, et al. 2025). In addition, certain metal ions can contribute to DNA damage through Fenton‐like reactions and interactions with the polyanionic DNA backbone, thereby promoting oxidative damage (Lu et al. 2013; Naz et al. 2022; Naz, Chatha, Téllez‐Isaías, et al. 2023). Collectively, the present findings indicate that exposure to insecticide mixtures can disrupt antioxidant and detoxification systems and induce genotoxic damage in fish, with the magnitude of these effects varying according to species, tissue, pesticide combination and exposure duration.

Conclusion

The current study concluded that widespread application of insecticides is highly toxic to fish. Overall results indicate that acute exposure to insecticides mixture can induce oxidative stress and DNA damage in fish. These parameters can be successfully used for detecting and monitoring aquatic pollution caused by insecticides. This study recommends that, in order to minimize the pesticides pollution and exposure in Pakistan, there should be a need to formulate some laws that will be strictly followed for assessment and monitoring of pollution. Furthermore, there should be a complete ban on such toxic chemicals.
