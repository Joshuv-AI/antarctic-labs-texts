Maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms, parenting stress, and child psychological and cognitive outcomes among Syrian refugee mother–child dyads in Türkiye: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Metin Çınaroğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRefugee children exposed to war and forced displacement are at elevated risk for emotional, behavioral, and cognitive difficulties. However, child outcomes may be shaped not only by direct trauma exposure but also by the psychological functioning of caregivers and stress within the parent–child relationship. The present mixed-methods study examined whether parenting stress was associated with the relationship between maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms and child psychological and cognitive outcomes among Syrian refugee families living in Mardin, Türkiye.MethodsParticipants were 200 Syrian refugee mother–child dyads with children aged 6–11 years. Mothers completed measures of posttraumatic stress symptoms (PCL-5), parenting stress (PSI-SF), depressive symptoms (BDI-II), anxiety symptoms (GAD-7), and child emotional and behavioral problems (CBCL/6–18). Children completed the Stroop Color–Word Test assessing inhibitory control and selective attention. Mediation analyses were conducted using bootstrapped indirect effects while controlling for maternal depression, maternal anxiety, child age, and child gender. A purposeful subsample of 30 dyads participated in semi-structured qualitative interviews exploring trauma experiences, parenting stress, attachment-related behaviors, and child emotional and cognitive functioning.ResultsMaternal posttraumatic stress symptoms were positively associated with parenting stress, child emotional and behavioral problems, and poorer child executive functioning. Parenting stress significantly mediated the association between maternal PTSD symptoms and CBCL Total Problems, indirect effect = 0.12, 95% CI [0.058, 0.196], and also mediated the association between maternal PTSD symptoms and Stroop interference, indirect effect = 0.052, 95% CI [0.012, 0.103]. Secondary analyses demonstrated significant indirect effects for both internalizing and externalizing symptoms. Qualitative findings converged with the quantitative results and showed that maternal trauma-related distress, emotional exhaustion, irritability, and inconsistent responsiveness were reflected in children’s fear, withdrawal, dysregulation, separation distress, and attentional difficulties. Protective factors included maternal warmth, school support, supportive relationships, religious coping, and future-oriented hope.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that parenting stress may represent an important relational pathway linking maternal trauma-related distress with children’s emotional, behavioral, and cognitive functioning in refugee families. Interventions targeting refugee child mental health may benefit from simultaneously addressing caregiver trauma symptoms, parenting stress, and the broader relational environment in which children develop and recover.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05202-0.Keywords: Syrian refugees, Maternal PTSD, Parenting stress, Child mental health, Türkiye

Introduction

Forced displacement is a major global public health and developmental concern [18, 71]. By the end of 2024, an estimated 123.2 million people worldwide were forcibly displaced because of persecution, conflict, violence, human rights violations, and events seriously disturbing public order, and children constituted approximately 41% of the global refugee population [72]. Türkiye remains one of the largest refugee-hosting countries worldwide and continues to host millions of Syrians under temporary protection, making the psychological wellbeing of Syrian refugee families a critical concern for mental health research and intervention [73].

War-related displacement exposes families to multiple, cumulative stressors before, during, and after migration [35, 36]. Syrian refugee children may experience direct war-related trauma [77], loss of home [37], bereavement [22], disruption of schooling [76], social exclusion [68], economic insecurity [66], and prolonged uncertainty in the host country [61]. A review of war, displacement, and trauma in Syrian refugee children emphasized that potentially traumatic events occur across pre-migration, peri-migration, and post-migration phases and may affect cognitive functioning [81], emotion regulation, affective processing, and broader developmental outcomes [43, 48, 80]. These risks are especially important during middle childhood, when children are developing more complex capacities for emotional regulation [78], selective attention [16], inhibitory control [40], attachment representation [13], and school adaptation [45].

Although refugee children’s own trauma exposure is important, child outcomes cannot be understood independently of the caregiving environment [23]. In refugee families, parental trauma symptoms may affect children through multiple pathways, including emotional availability [65], parenting consistency [24], parent–child conflict [15], harshness [62], withdrawal [70], and reduced capacity to provide a secure base [41]. Previous research with refugee families has shown that caregiver PTSD is associated with harsher parenting and that this pathway is linked to children’s emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity, and peer difficulties [79]. Research with Syrian refugee mother–child dyads has also highlighted the importance of maternal mental health and the parent–child relationship in shaping child PTSD, depression, and externalizing outcomes [42].

Parenting stress may be a particularly important relational mechanism in trauma-exposed refugee families [20]. Parenting stress refers to stress arising within the parenting role and the parent–child system, including parental distress, dysfunctional parent–child interaction, and perceptions of the child as difficult [27]. The Turkish version of the Parenting Stress Index–Short Form has shown satisfactory reliability and validity for assessing stress in the parent–child system among Turkish-speaking mothers. In refugee contexts, parenting stress may be intensified by displacement-related poverty [77], social isolation [50], acculturation demands [3], uncertainty about the future [56], and the mother’s own trauma-related symptoms [5]. As maternal distress increases, mothers may find it more difficult to respond sensitively to children’s emotional needs, maintain consistent boundaries, or regulate their own emotional reactions during child distress [10].

However, the specific role of parenting stress in this process remains incompletely understood. While numerous studies have demonstrated robust associations between parental trauma exposure and adverse child outcomes, relatively few have examined parenting stress as a distinct mediating mechanism. Existing evidence suggests that parenting stress contributes to the transmission of trauma-related risk, but findings have been inconsistent across refugee populations because studies have differed in sample characteristics, trauma histories, and the family processes examined. In some investigations, parenting stress has emerged as an important mediator, whereas others have identified parental depression, parenting behaviors, attachment insecurity, family functioning, or post-migration living difficulties as equally or more influential pathways. These findings indicate that parenting stress should not be viewed in isolation but rather as one component of a broader network of relational and psychological mechanisms through which maternal trauma may influence child development. Consequently, examining parenting stress while simultaneously controlling for maternal depression and anxiety may help clarify its unique contribution to child emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes [30].

Maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms may also be associated with children’s emotional and behavioral difficulties [63]. Children exposed to parental trauma-related distress may show internalizing problems [74] such as anxiety [55], sadness [49], withdrawal [14], somatic complaints [28], and separation fears [53], as well as externalizing problems such as irritability [17], aggression [82], impulsivity [31], and oppositional behavior [52]. The Child Behavior Checklist is widely used to assess these domains, and Turkish validation work supports the generalizability of its measurement structure in Turkish general-population and clinical samples. Within refugee families, maternal PTSD symptoms may therefore operate not only as an individual maternal mental health problem but also as a family-level risk factor for child psychological adjustment.

Beyond emotional and behavioral outcomes, trauma and chronic stress may also affect children’s cognitive functioning [44]. Executive functions, particularly inhibitory control and selective attention, are central to children’s school functioning, emotional regulation, and adaptive behavior [34]. Among refugee children, executive functioning may also be influenced by disruptions in language development, multilingual adaptation, and interrupted educational experiences following forced displacement [29, 33, 81]. A meta-analysis of trauma-exposed youth concluded that trauma exposure is associated with lower executive functioning, suggesting that cognitive outcomes should be considered alongside emotional and behavioral symptoms in trauma-affected children [58]. The Stroop task is especially relevant in this context because it assesses the child’s ability to inhibit a dominant response and manage cognitive interference, processes that may be vulnerable to chronic stress and emotional insecurity.

Sleep-related difficulties may also represent an important pathway through which trauma and family stress affect child functioning. Refugee children commonly experience nightmares, insomnia, fragmented sleep, and altered sleep–wake rhythms following exposure to war and displacement. Increasing evidence suggests that poor sleep is not merely a consequence of trauma but may also contribute to emotional dysregulation, oppositional behavior, psychomotor agitation, impaired executive functioning, and poorer academic performance in school-age children. Chronotype and sleep quality have likewise been associated with attention, inhibitory control, and broader cognitive functioning, suggesting that sleep disturbances may partly explain the association between trauma exposure and children's behavioral and neurocognitive outcomes. Accordingly, sleep-related experiences were explored during the qualitative interviews as part of the broader assessment of children's emotional and cognitive functioning [59, 60].

Attachment theory provides an additional framework for understanding these pathways [8]. In middle childhood, children increasingly represent attachment relationships through internal working models, expectations of caregiver availability, and patterns of proximity seeking or avoidance [11]. Turkish attachment research in middle childhood supports the assessment of attachment-related anxiety and avoidance and emphasizes that children’s relationships with caregivers are linked to self-worth, anxiety, peer relationships, and life satisfaction [19, 25, 54]. Projective and story-completion approaches used in Türkiye also indicate that children’s attachment representations can be meaningfully explored through drawings, play, and narrative material [26, 47, 57]. Thus, qualitative assessment of children’s and mothers’ narratives may help explain how maternal trauma and parenting stress are reflected in children’s emotional, behavioral, cognitive, and attachment-related functioning.

The present study was conducted in Mardin, Türkiye, among Syrian refugee mother–child dyads. Using a mixed-methods design, the study examined whether maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms were associated with children’s psychological and cognitive outcomes through parenting stress. The quantitative component included 200 mother–child dyads and assessed maternal PTSD symptoms, parenting stress, maternal depression and anxiety, child emotional and behavioral problems, and child executive functioning. The qualitative component included a purposeful subsample of 30 dyads and explored mothers’ and children’s lived experiences of trauma, displacement, parenting stress, attachment-related responses, emotional regulation, and resilience.

Despite growing evidence linking parental trauma with child adjustment, relatively few mixed-methods studies have simultaneously examined parenting stress as a potential mediator of both psychological and cognitive outcomes among refugee children. Addressing this gap may improve understanding of the relational mechanisms underlying intergenerational trauma transmission.

Based on previous literature, it was hypothesized that maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms would be positively associated with parenting stress and with children’s emotional, behavioral, and cognitive difficulties. It was further hypothesized that parenting stress would mediate the association between maternal PTSD symptoms and child outcomes. The qualitative component was expected to contextualize these statistical pathways by illuminating how maternal trauma symptoms and parenting stress were experienced in daily mother–child interactions.

Methods

Study design

This study used a mixed-methods design with a cross-sectional quantitative core and an embedded qualitative explanatory component. The quantitative component examined whether maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms were associated with children’s psychological and cognitive outcomes through parenting stress among Syrian refugee mother–child dyads living in Mardin, Türkiye. The qualitative component was designed to deepen the interpretation of the quantitative findings by exploring mothers’ and children’s narratives of war-related trauma, displacement, parenting stress, attachment-related experiences, emotion regulation, and resilience. Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated at the interpretation stage, allowing statistical associations to be understood in relation to lived family experiences.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Mardin, Türkiye, among Syrian refugee families who had migrated to Türkiye following the Syrian civil war. Participants were recruited from Psychotherapy office in Mardin run by one of the Clinical Psychology master students of the author and community-based networks. Assessments were conducted face-to-face in private, quiet, and safe settings by trained clinical psychology researchers. The research procedures were structured to be trauma-sensitive, culturally responsive, and suitable for both mothers and school-aged children.

Participants

Participants were 200 Syrian refugee mother–child dyads residing in Mardin. Mothers were the biological mothers and primary caregivers of the participating children. Children were aged 6–11 years and were enrolled in formal education. This age range was selected because it allowed developmentally appropriate assessment of emotional and behavioral functioning, attachment-related experiences, and executive functioning using standardized and age-adapted procedures. All participants were able to understand and complete the study procedures in Turkish.

Mother–child dyads were eligible for inclusion if the mother had experienced war-related displacement from Syria, was currently living in Türkiye, was the child’s primary caregiver, and had lived continuously with the child since resettlement. Children were eligible if they were between 6 and 11 years of age and were able to complete the child assessment procedures. Dyads were excluded if the child had a documented neurological disorder, intellectual disability, or severe developmental condition likely to interfere with cognitive task performance. Dyads were also excluded if either the mother or child had a known psychotic disorder or severe cognitive impairment preventing valid participation, or if the child was using medication likely to substantially affect attention or cognitive performance, such as stimulant or sedative medication.

Recruitment and sampling

Participants were recruited through a psychotherapy office in Mardin. Recruitment was facilitated by a licensed clinical psychologist familiar with the local context, which supported culturally sensitive contact with families and helped establish trust. Participation was voluntary, and no financial incentives were provided.

After completion of the quantitative phase, approximately 30 mother–child dyads were purposefully selected for the qualitative component. Selection was guided by quantitative profiles to ensure variation in maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms, parenting stress, child emotional and behavioral problems, and child executive functioning. This maximum-variation strategy allowed the qualitative sample to include both high-risk and relatively resilient family profiles.

Procedure

Data collection took place between 07/2025 and 12/2025. After informed consent was obtained from mothers and assent was obtained from children, mothers completed self-report questionnaires assessing posttraumatic stress symptoms, parenting stress, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and children’s emotional and behavioral problems. Children completed an individually administered Stroop Color–Word task assessing executive functioning, particularly inhibitory control and selective attention. Quantitative assessment sessions lasted approximately 45–60 min per dyad.

For the qualitative component, mothers and selected children participated in semi-structured interviews. The mother interview focused on the child’s trauma and migration-related reactions, daily emotional and behavioral symptoms, stress coping, attachment behaviors, parenting stress, parenting style, separation and reunion behaviors, and intergenerational emotional transmission. Child interviews were developmentally adapted. Children aged 6–9 completed a projective and play-based interaction protocol including a family drawing task, a story-completion activity, and emotion-card prompts. Older children completed a semi-structured interview focused on daily life, safe places, emotional expression, mother–child relationship, separation and reunion experiences, war and migration memories, and hopes for the future.

All interviews were conducted in Turkish by trained clinical psychology researchers. Interviews were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim, and anonymized. Children were allowed to pause, skip questions, draw, play, or stop participation at any time. When signs of distress emerged, the interviewer slowed or redirected the interview and provided supportive containment.

Measures

Sociodemographic and migration-related information

Mothers completed a sociodemographic form developed for the study. The form assessed maternal age, child age and gender, maternal education, family composition, duration of residence in Türkiye, migration history, child school attendance, and relevant health or developmental history.

Maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms

Maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms were assessed using the PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5). The PCL-5 is a 20-item self-report measure assessing PTSD symptoms during the past month. Items are rated from 0, indicating “not at all,” to 4, indicating “extremely,” yielding a total score from 0 to 80 [7]. The Turkish version maps onto DSM-5 PTSD symptom clusters, including re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations, and hyperarousal, and has demonstrated good psychometric properties in Turkish samples. In the present study, the PCL-5 total score was used as a continuous indicator of maternal posttraumatic stress severity [9].

Parenting stress

Parenting stress was assessed using the Parenting Stress Index–Short Form (PSI-SF). The PSI-SF is a 36-item measure designed to assess stress within the parent–child system [1]. It includes three subscales: Parental Distress, Parent–Child Dysfunctional Interaction, and Difficult Child, as well as a Total Stress score. Higher scores indicate greater parenting stress. The Turkish version has shown satisfactory internal consistency and test–retest reliability, supporting its use for assessing parenting stress in Turkish-speaking mothers [51].

Maternal depressive symptoms

Maternal depressive symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II). The BDI-II is a 21-item self-report measure assessing depressive symptoms over the past two weeks [6]. Items are scored from 0 to 3, producing a total score from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. The Turkish validation study reported good reliability and validity and provided Turkish cut-off ranges for minimal, mild, moderate, and severe depressive symptom levels. In the present study, the BDI-II total score was used as a covariate to control for maternal depressive symptom severity [38].

Maternal anxiety symptoms

Maternal anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale (GAD-7). The GAD-7 is a 7-item self-report questionnaire assessing symptoms of generalized anxiety during the past two weeks. Items are rated from 0, indicating “not at all,” to 3, indicating “nearly every day,” yielding a total score from 0 to 21 [69]. The Turkish adaptation demonstrated good reliability, validity, and a single-factor structure. In the present study, the GAD-7 total score was used as a covariate to account for maternal anxiety symptoms [46].

Child emotional and behavioral problems

Children’s emotional and behavioral problems were assessed using the Child Behavior Checklist for Ages 6–18 (CBCL/6–18), completed by mothers [2]. The CBCL is a standardized parent-report measure in which caregivers rate children’s emotional and behavioral symptoms. Problem items are rated from 0, indicating “not true,” to 2, indicating “very true or often true.” The CBCL yields Total Problems, Internalizing Problems, and Externalizing Problems scores. The Turkish CBCL validation supported the generalizability of the CBCL measurement structure in Turkish general-population and clinical samples. In the present study, the CBCL Total Problems score was used as the primary child psychological outcome, while Internalizing and Externalizing scores were examined in secondary analyses [21].

Child executive functioning

Child executive functioning was assessed using the Stroop Color–Word Test, TBAG standardized form for children aged 6–11 years. The task assesses inhibitory control and selective attention by requiring children to name the ink color of color words under conditions of cognitive interference [32]. The task was administered individually according to standardized instructions. The primary cognitive outcome was the Stroop interference difference score, calculated as interference-condition completion time minus baseline color-naming completion time. Higher values reflected greater interference cost and therefore poorer inhibitory control. Interference-condition completion time and number of interference errors were retained as secondary Stroop indicators [39].

Child attachment-related qualitative assessment

Attachment-related experiences were assessed qualitatively through age-adapted child interviews and projective interaction tasks. For younger children, the 6–9-year protocol included a “Me and My family” family drawing task, a story-completion activity involving separation from the mother, and emotion-card prompts. These activities were designed to elicit representations of attachment figures, emotional closeness, separation and reunion themes, support-seeking behavior, and maternal responsiveness. The coding structure for this age group included attachment figures and proximity, self-positioning within the family, separation and reunion reactions, emotion recognition and regulation, abandonment anxiety, and maternal representation.

For older children, the 9–12-year semi-structured interview explored emotional expression, safe spaces, help-seeking, mother–child attachment behaviors, separation and reunion responses, war and migration memories, and future-oriented hope. The use of story-based and projective methods was consistent with Turkish attachment assessment literature, including projective story-completion approaches such as the “Incomplete Doll Family Stories Scale”, which has been used in Türkiye to examine children’s attachment representations.

Qualitative interviews with mothers

Semi-structured interviews with mothers were conducted to explore maternal perceptions of the child’s emotional and behavioral functioning, the child’s trauma and migration-related reactions, mother–child attachment behaviors, parenting stress, and parenting style. The interview guide included domains on the child’s responses during the conflict and early resettlement period, changes in sleep, eating, fear, anger, withdrawal or clinginess, the child’s coping strategies, traumatic themes in play or stories, the impact of maternal stress on the child, separation and reunion behaviors, and the mother’s hopes and fears for the child’s future.

The mother interview also included questions about parenting style and parental responsiveness, including how mothers respond when children are distressed, whether they use warmth, waiting, direction, limit-setting, or punishment, and whether stress leads to emotional withdrawal or harshness. This allowed parenting stress to be examined not only as a quantitative mediator but also as a lived relational process within the mother–child dyad. English-language versions of the semi-structured interview guides are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Quantitative data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 30 and PROCESS macro version 4. Before hypothesis testing, data were screened for accuracy, missing values, outliers, and distributional assumptions. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Internal consistency coefficients were computed for each self-report scale. Missing data patterns were examined using Little’s MCAR test. Because missingness was minimal and consistent with a missing completely at random pattern, available-case analyses were used for descriptive and correlational analyses, and mediation models were estimated using cases with complete data on the variables included in each model.Bivariate associations among maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms, parenting stress, maternal depressive symptoms, maternal anxiety symptoms, child emotional and behavioral problems, and child executive functioning were examined using Pearson correlations. If normality assumptions were violated, nonparametric alternatives were used.

The primary hypotheses were tested using mediation analyses. Maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms served as the independent variable, parenting stress served as the mediator, and child outcomes served as dependent variables. Two primary mediation models were estimated. In the first model, the CBCL Total Problems score was entered as the child psychological outcome. In the second model, Stroop interference score was entered as the child cognitive outcome. Maternal depressive symptoms, maternal anxiety symptoms, child age, and child gender were included as covariates. Indirect effects were tested using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples. Mediation was considered statistically significant when the 95% confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero. Secondary analyses examined CBCL Internalizing and Externalizing Problems as separate outcomes.

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using thematic analysis (MAXQDA) with an inductive–deductive approach. The deductive coding framework was informed by the interview guides and thematic code lists developed for the study, while inductive coding allowed new themes to emerge from participants’ narratives. Mother interview coding focused on six broad domains: trauma traces and intergenerational transmission, child psychological symptoms, attachment behaviors, parenting stress, parenting style, and protective factors or future hope.

Child qualitative data were analyzed using age-specific coding frameworks. For 6–9-year-old children, both verbal material and projective content were coded, including drawings, story completions, emotional expressions, symbolic distance, omitted figures, and representations of the mother as safe, distant, angry, or unavailable. For older children, coding focused on emotional awareness, help-seeking, safe spaces, attachment-related responses to the mother, separation and reunion themes, war and migration memories, and future identity or hope.

Two researchers independently coded an initial subset of transcripts to refine the coding framework. Discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached, and the coding guide was then applied to the remaining transcripts. Analytic memos were used to document interpretive decisions. Themes were compared across high- and low-risk quantitative profiles to identify how maternal trauma, parenting stress, and child outcomes were reflected in family narratives.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated at the interpretation stage. The qualitative data were used to explain, contextualize, and elaborate the quantitative mediation models. For example, statistical associations between maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms and parenting stress were interpreted alongside maternal narratives of emotional exhaustion, irritability, withdrawal, guilt, or overprotection. Similarly, quantitative child outcomes were interpreted in relation to children’s descriptions of fear, separation distress, avoidance, emotional suppression, help-seeking, and future hope. This integration strengthened the ecological validity of the study by linking statistical pathways to observable and narrated family processes.

Results

Preliminary analyses

Data from 200 Syrian refugee mother–child dyads were included in the quantitative analyses. Prior to hypothesis testing, all variables were screened for missing values, outliers, and distributional assumptions. Missing data were low across the main study variables, ranging from 0.0% to 2.5%. Little’s MCAR test was non-significant, χ2(84) = 89.73, p = 0.312, suggesting that missingness was consistent with a missing completely at random pattern. Because missingness was minimal, available-case analyses were used for descriptive and correlational analyses, and mediation models were estimated using cases with complete data on the model variables.

Skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable limits for maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms, parenting stress, depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and CBCL scores. Stroop error scores showed mild positive skew; however, inspection of residuals indicated that the assumption of normality was acceptable for regression-based analyses. No influential multivariate outliers were identified. The largest Cook’s distance value was 0.07, and the largest Mahalanobis distance value did not exceed the critical value for the number of predictors included in the mediation models.

Internal consistency was good to excellent for the main self-report measures. Cronbach’s alpha values were α = 0.94 for the PCL-5, α = 0.91 for the PSI-SF Total Stress score, α = 0.91 for the BDI-II, α = 0.89 for the GAD-7, and α = 0.94 for the CBCL Total Problems scale.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Mothers had a mean age of 34.71 years (SD = 6.28, range = 22–49). Children had a mean age of 8.63 years (SD = 1.55, range = 6–11). Of the children, 96 were girls (48.0%) and 104 were boys (52.0%). Families had been living in Türkiye for an average of 7.84 years (SD = 2.14). Most mothers reported primary school or less as their highest level of education (62.5%), and most families reported low household income (68.0%). War-related bereavement was reported by 46.0% of mothers.

The qualitative subsample consisted of 30 mother–child dyads selected to represent variation in maternal PTSD symptoms, parenting stress, child behavioral problems, and child Stroop performance. The qualitative subsample did not significantly differ from the remaining quantitative sample in maternal age, child age, child gender, maternal PTSD symptoms, parenting stress, or CBCL Total Problems scores.

Missing data were low across the main study variables, ranging from 0.0% to 2.5%. Little’s MCAR test was non-significant, χ2(84) = 89.73, p = 0.312. The mean PSI-SF Total Stress score was 103.48 (SD = 23.18), suggesting clinically meaningful parenting stress in a substantial proportion of the sample. The Turkish PSI-SF validation study reported satisfactory reliability for the Total Stress score and subscales, supporting its use for Turkish-speaking mothers.

Maternal depressive symptoms were also elevated. The mean BDI-II score was 22.94 (SD = 11.47), corresponding to the moderate range according to Turkish cut-off values. The mean GAD-7 score was 11.46 (SD = 5.21), and 71.0% of mothers scored at or above the Turkish GAD-7 cut-off of 8.

Children’s mean CBCL Total Problems T-score was 61.82 (SD = 10.84), indicating that many children were within the borderline or clinical range for emotional and behavioral problems. The Turkish CBCL validation supported the generalizability of the CBCL measurement structure in Turkish samples.

A mediation analysis was conducted to test whether parenting stress mediated the association between maternal PTSD symptoms and child emotional and behavioral problems. Qualitative results

The qualitative component included 30 mother–child dyads. Interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis with an inductive–deductive approach. The deductive coding framework was informed by the mother interview guide and thematic coding list, which focused on trauma traces, child psychological symptoms, attachment behaviors, parenting stress, parenting style, and protective factors.

Child interviews were analyzed using age-specific frameworks. For children aged 6–9, the protocol included drawing, story-completion, and emotion-card activities designed to explore attachment figures, emotional regulation, separation/reunion themes, and maternal representations. For children aged 9–12, the interview focused on daily life, safe spaces, emotional expression, mother–child relationship, war and migration memories, and future hopes.

Seven major themes were identified: maternal trauma traces and emotional spillover, parenting stress and reduced emotional availability, child internalizing symptoms, child externalizing and behavioral dysregulation, attachment and separation–reunion patterns, attention and executive functioning difficulties, and protective factors/resilience. Theme 1: maternal trauma traces and emotional spillover

Mothers frequently described persistent emotional effects of war, displacement, uncertainty, and loss. These experiences were often reactivated in daily parenting situations, especially when children cried, became fearful, resisted separation, or asked questions about Syria. Mothers with higher quantitative PCL-5 scores more often described feeling emotionally exhausted, easily irritated, or unable to respond calmly during child distress.

This theme contextualized the quantitative association between maternal PTSD symptoms and parenting stress. Maternal trauma symptoms appeared to affect parenting not only through general distress but also through emotional reactivation within the mother–child relationship.

Theme 2: parenting stress and reduced emotional availability

Parenting stress emerged as a central relational mechanism. Mothers described parenting under displacement-related hardship as emotionally demanding, particularly in the context of financial insecurity, social isolation, and uncertainty about the future. Some mothers reported becoming harsh or controlling when overwhelmed, whereas others described becoming silent, withdrawn, or emotionally unavailable.

This theme closely matched the mediation findings. Quantitatively, parenting stress mediated the association between maternal PTSD symptoms and child outcomes. Qualitatively, mothers described how stress reduced their patience, warmth, consistency, and capacity to provide emotional containment.

Theme 3: child internalizing symptoms

Mothers commonly described children as fearful, quiet, withdrawn, dependent on maternal proximity, or easily distressed. Sleep problems, nightmares, somatic complaints, and separation-related fears were also frequently reported. Children with higher CBCL Internalizing scores were more often described as emotionally inhibited, avoidant of trauma-related topics, or reluctant to disclose distress.

This theme aligned with the quantitative finding that maternal PTSD symptoms and parenting stress were associated with child internalizing problems.

Theme 4: child externalizing and behavioral dysregulation

A second pattern involved irritability, anger outbursts, restlessness, defiance, and difficulty following instructions. Mothers often connected these behaviors to family stress, school challenges, maternal exhaustion, or instability during migration. In several dyads, children’s dysregulation appeared to intensify when mothers described themselves as emotionally overwhelmed or inconsistent.

This theme helped explain why parenting stress was associated not only with internalizing symptoms but also with externalizing problems.

Theme 5: attachment, separation, and reunion

Attachment-related narratives varied across dyads. Some children sought physical closeness, reassurance, and repeated confirmation that the mother would return. Others avoided emotional disclosure, withdrew when the mother appeared angry or tired, or minimized their need for support. Younger children’s drawings and story completions frequently showed physical proximity to the mother, omitted family figures, symbolic distance, or themes of being left alone.

These findings helped interpret the quantitative mediation pathway by showing how parenting stress may be reflected in children’s attachment behaviors. Anxious patterns were visible in clinging, fear of abandonment, and reassurance-seeking. Avoidant patterns were visible in silence, emotional suppression, and reluctance to approach the mother during distress.

Theme 6: attention, concentration, and executive functioning difficulties

Mothers and older children described concentration problems, forgetfulness, restlessness, and difficulty completing schoolwork. These descriptions were especially common among dyads with higher parenting stress and poorer Stroop performance. Several children described becoming distracted when worried, tired, or preoccupied with family stress.

This theme contextualized the quantitative association between parenting stress and Stroop interference. Although the Stroop task measured inhibitory control directly, the qualitative narratives suggested that attentional difficulties were embedded in chronic emotional stress and family insecurity.

Theme 7: protective factors and resilience

Despite substantial adversity, some dyads showed resilient patterns. Protective factors included maternal warmth, consistent routines, school attendance, supportive teachers, religious coping, extended family support, peer relationships, and future-oriented hope. Children with lower CBCL scores and better Stroop performance were more often described as having predictable routines, supportive adult relationships, and positive school experiences.

This theme helped explain why some children showed relatively adaptive functioning despite high maternal trauma exposure.

Mixed-methods integration

Quantitative and qualitative findings converged in showing that maternal PTSD symptoms were associated with child psychological and cognitive outcomes through parenting stress. Quantitatively, maternal PTSD symptoms predicted higher parenting stress, and parenting stress predicted greater child emotional and behavioral problems as well as poorer Stroop performance. Qualitatively, mothers described emotional exhaustion, irritability, withdrawal, guilt, and inconsistent responsiveness, which appeared to shape children’s fear, silence, dysregulation, separation distress, and attentional difficulties.

The qualitative findings also elaborated the cognitive results. Children with poorer Stroop performance were often described as inattentive, easily distracted, forgetful, or preoccupied by family stress. These narratives suggest that executive functioning difficulties may be embedded in broader patterns of chronic family stress and emotional insecurity. Discussion

The present mixed-methods study examined the association between maternal posttraumatic stress symptoms and child psychological and cognitive outcomes among Syrian refugee mother–child dyads living in Mardin, Türkiye. The findings supported the hypothesized indirect pathway: maternal PTSD symptoms were associated with higher parenting stress, and parenting stress was associated with greater child emotional and behavioral problems and poorer inhibitory control. Qualitative findings converged with the quantitative results by showing that maternal trauma-related distress, emotional exhaustion, irritability, withdrawal, and inconsistent responsiveness were reflected in children’s fear, silence, dysregulation, separation distress, and attentional difficulties.

Maternal psychological burden in the refugee context

The descriptive findings indicated a high burden of maternal psychological distress. Forty percent of mothers scored above the probable PTSD threshold on the PCL-5, and the mean PCL-5 score was elevated. This is clinically meaningful when considered alongside the Turkish PCL-5 validation study, which suggested a cut-off score of 47 for PTSD screening in clinical samples and 48 for community samples. Maternal depressive and anxiety symptoms were also substantial: the mean BDI-II score fell within the moderate range according to Turkish BDI-II cut-off scores, and 71% of mothers scored at or above the Turkish GAD-7 cut-off of 8.

These findings are consistent with the broader literature showing that refugee mothers are often exposed to overlapping layers of trauma loss, displacement stress, and post-migration adversity [4, 64, 75]. In the present sample, maternal distress was not limited to PTSD symptoms alone but was accompanied by depressive and anxiety symptoms. This comorbidity is important because depression and anxiety may further reduce emotional energy, parental confidence, and sensitivity in caregiving interactions. However, the mediation models controlled for maternal depression and anxiety, indicating that maternal PTSD symptoms retained a distinct association with parenting stress even after accounting for these related forms of distress.

Maternal PTSD symptoms and parenting stress

The first major finding was that maternal PTSD symptoms were strongly associated with parenting stress. Maternal PTSD symptoms correlated with parenting stress at r = 0.56 and significantly predicted PSI-SF Total Stress in the mediation models, B = 0.58, β = 0.39, p < 0.001. This suggests that mothers with higher posttraumatic stress symptoms experienced greater difficulty within the parenting role and the parent–child relationship.

This result is consistent with prior refugee research showing that caregiver PTSD can affect parenting behavior and child mental health. Bryant and colleagues found that caregiver trauma history and post-migration difficulties were associated with PTSD, which in turn was associated with harsh parenting and child emotional and behavioral problems [12]. Similarly, research with Syrian refugee families has shown that maternal mental health and the parent–child relationship are central pathways through which the refugee environment becomes associated with child mental health outcomes [66].

The qualitative findings helped explain this association. Mothers described trauma memories, emotional reactivation, exhaustion, irritability, guilt, and difficulty remaining calm during child distress. These narratives suggest that maternal PTSD symptoms may increase parenting stress by reducing emotional availability and making ordinary parenting demands feel overwhelming. In this sense, parenting stress may represent the relational expression of maternal trauma within daily family life.

Parenting stress as a pathway to child emotional and behavioral problems

The strongest quantitative pathway involved child emotional and behavioral problems. Parenting stress significantly predicted CBCL Total Problems, B = 0.21, β = 0.45, p < 0.001. The total effect of maternal PTSD symptoms on CBCL Total Problems was significant, but the direct effect became non-significant after parenting stress was included in the model. The bootstrapped indirect effect was significant, indirect effect = 0.12, 95% CI [0.058, 0.196].

These results suggest that parenting stress may be an important mechanism linking maternal PTSD symptoms to child psychological difficulties. Although the cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions, the pattern is consistent with a family-process model in which maternal trauma symptoms affect the caregiving environment, and the caregiving environment shapes child adjustment. The finding that parenting stress was associated with both internalizing and externalizing problems further suggests that this pathway may not be limited to one symptom domain.

Secondary analyses supported this interpretation. Parenting stress significantly mediated the association between maternal PTSD symptoms and both CBCL Internalizing Problems, indirect effect = 0.104, 95% CI [0.042, 0.181], and CBCL Externalizing Problems, indirect effect = 0.093, 95% CI [0.036, 0.162]. The pathway was slightly stronger for internalizing symptoms, which is consistent with qualitative narratives describing children as fearful, silent, withdrawn, dependent on maternal proximity, or reluctant to disclose distress. However, the externalizing pathway was also significant, indicating that parenting stress was related to anger, dysregulation, restlessness, and oppositional behavior as well.

Qualitative themes provided important context for these findings. Mothers described children’s fear, sleep difficulties, somatic complaints, anger outbursts, and emotional withdrawal. Some children responded to maternal stress by clinging or seeking reassurance, whereas others withdrew or avoided approaching the mother when she was angry or tired. These attachment-related patterns are consistent with the study’s interview and coding frameworks, which focused on separation and reunion behavior, caregiver availability, emotional regulation, and representations of maternal sensitivity.

Parenting stress and child executive functioning

The second primary model extended the findings beyond psychological symptoms by examining child executive functioning. Parenting stress significantly predicted Stroop interference, B = 0.09, β = 0.22, p = 0.003, and the indirect effect of maternal PTSD symptoms on Stroop interference through parenting stress was significant, indirect effect = 0.052, 95% CI [0.012, 0.103]. This suggests that parenting stress was associated not only with children’s emotional and behavioral adjustment but also with inhibitory control under cognitive conflict.

The cognitive pathway was weaker than the pathway predicting CBCL Total Problems, but it remains theoretically important. Executive functioning develops in close interaction with emotional security, stress regulation, and environmental predictability. Trauma-exposed youth have been shown to display lower executive functioning, and chronic stress may interfere with attention, inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility. However, executive functioning in refugee children is also shaped by broader developmental and environmental influences. Disrupted schooling, interrupted language development, second-language acquisition, and the linguistic demands associated with resettlement may all influence attention, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. Previous research has highlighted the close relationship between language development and executive functioning, suggesting that refugee children's cognitive performance reflects both trauma-related experiences and language-learning challenges following forced displacement [29, 33, 81]. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted within this broader developmental context rather than attributing executive functioning differences solely to maternal PTSD or parenting stress.

Executive functioning in refugee children is also shaped by broader developmental and environmental influences beyond family stress. Disrupted schooling, interrupted language development, multilingual learning environments, and adaptation to a new linguistic context may influence attentional control, working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory processes. Previous research has highlighted that language development and executive functions are closely interconnected during childhood, and refugee children often experience educational and linguistic disruptions that may contribute to variability in executive performance. Accordingly, the present findings should be interpreted within this broader developmental context rather than attributing executive functioning differences solely to maternal PTSD or parenting stress.

The qualitative findings again supported the quantitative results. Mothers and older children described concentration problems, forgetfulness, distractibility, restlessness, and difficulty completing schoolwork. These descriptions were particularly common among dyads characterized by higher parenting stress and poorer Stroop performance. This convergence suggests that children’s cognitive difficulties may be embedded in a broader emotional and relational context rather than reflecting isolated neurocognitive weakness.

Child age was a strong predictor of Stroop performance, with older children performing better. This is developmentally expected, as inhibitory control and processing efficiency improve across middle childhood. Importantly, parenting stress remained associated with Stroop interference even after controlling for child age, suggesting that family stress may contribute to individual differences in executive functioning beyond normative developmental maturation.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

The mixed-methods design strengthened the interpretation of the findings. Quantitatively, the mediation models identified parenting stress as a statistically significant pathway linking maternal PTSD symptoms with child outcomes. Qualitatively, mothers’ and children’s narratives showed how this pathway may appear in daily life: maternal trauma memories were reactivated by child distress; mothers described exhaustion, harshness, withdrawal, or guilt; and children responded with fear, silence, anger, clinging, avoidance, or attentional difficulty.

This integration is one of the main contributions of the study. The quantitative findings demonstrate that the maternal PTSD–parenting stress–child outcome pathway is statistically meaningful, while the qualitative findings show that this pathway is relational, embodied, and contextual. Parenting stress was not simply a score on a scale; it appeared in mothers’ descriptions of being unable to remain calm, feeling overwhelmed by parenting demands, becoming emotionally unavailable, or struggling to set consistent limits. Similarly, child outcomes were not limited to CBCL or Stroop scores; they appeared in children’s attachment narratives, drawings, separation fears, emotional suppression, and school-related difficulties.

Protective factors and resilience

Despite high adversity, the qualitative findings also identified protective factors. Some families described maternal warmth, consistent routines, school attendance, supportive teachers, religious coping, extended family support, peer relationships, and future-oriented hope. These themes are important because they suggest that trauma transmission is not inevitable. Even when maternal PTSD symptoms are elevated, relational warmth, predictable caregiving, supportive school environments, and opportunities for emotional expression may buffer children against psychological and cognitive difficulties.

This finding is consistent with ecological and family-process models of refugee child mental health [67], which emphasize that risk and resilience operate across multiple systems, including the family, school, community, and broader post-migration environment. Syrian refugee research has similarly shown that the wider refugee environment may affect children indirectly through maternal mental health and parenting, while supportive relationships may reduce risk.

Clinical and practical implications

The findings have several implications for trauma-informed services with refugee families. First, child mental health interventions should include assessment of caregiver trauma symptoms. Treating child symptoms without addressing maternal PTSD, depression, anxiety, and parenting stress may miss a central relational pathway through which distress is maintained. Second, interventions should not focus only on reducing maternal symptoms but should also strengthen parenting capacity, emotional availability, and stress regulation within the mother–child relationship.

Parenting-focused interventions may be especially useful when they help mothers recognize trauma-related emotional reactivity, respond sensitively to children’s fear and dysregulation, and establish predictable routines and boundaries. In the present study, the Difficult Child and Parent–Child Dysfunctional Interaction subscales showed meaningful indirect effects, suggesting that intervention may need to address both maternal distress and the mother’s perception of the child as difficult or hard to soothe.

The cognitive findings also suggest the importance of school-based support. Children experiencing chronic family stress may struggle with attention, inhibitory control, and task completion. Teachers and school counselors should be aware that concentration problems in refugee children may reflect trauma-related family stress rather than lack of motivation. School routines, teacher support, and structured learning environments may serve as protective factors that support both emotional regulation and executive functioning.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the quantitative component was cross-sectional; therefore, causal conclusions cannot be drawn. Although the mediation models are theoretically grounded, the data cannot establish whether maternal PTSD symptoms cause parenting stress or whether child difficulties increase parenting stress and maternal distress. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify directionality. Although the mediation models are theoretically grounded, the data cannot establish whether maternal PTSD symptoms cause parenting stress or whether child difficulties increase parenting stress and maternal distress. Moreover, although bootstrapped mediation analysis is appropriate for examining indirect associations in cross-sectional data, the temporal ordering required to support causal mediation cannot be established. Therefore, the observed indirect effects should be interpreted as theory-driven statistical associations rather than evidence of causal mechanisms. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify the temporal sequence and directionality of these relationships.

Second, child emotional and behavioral problems were reported by mothers, which may introduce shared-method bias. Mothers with elevated PTSD, depression, or anxiety symptoms may perceive or report child symptoms differently. Future studies should include teacher reports, child self-reports when developmentally appropriate, and clinician-administered assessments.

In addition, maternal PTSD symptoms, parenting stress, and child emotional and behavioral problems were all assessed using maternal self-report measures. This raises the possibility of common-method variance and shared reporter bias, which may have strengthened the observed associations among these variables. Although child executive functioning was assessed using an objective neuropsychological task, future studies should incorporate multiple informants, including teachers, clinicians, fathers, and developmentally appropriate child self-reports, together with observational assessments of parent–child interactions.

Third, maternal PTSD symptoms were assessed using a self-report screening measure rather than a structured clinical diagnostic interview. Although the PCL-5 has strong Turkish psychometric support, screening scores should not be interpreted as equivalent to clinical diagnosis. Fourth, the study did not include fathers or other caregivers, although paternal mental health and broader family dynamics may also influence child outcomes.

Fifth, executive functioning was assessed primarily through Stroop indices. Although the Stroop task is a useful measure of inhibitory control and selective attention, executive functioning is multidimensional. Future work should include additional measures of working memory, cognitive flexibility, planning, and teacher-rated executive functioning. Finally, the sample was drawn from Syrian refugee families living in Mardin and able to complete study procedures in Turkish; therefore, findings may not generalize to all Syrian refugee families in Türkiye, families with limited Turkish proficiency, or refugee families in other host-country contexts.

Future directions

Future research should use longitudinal designs to examine how maternal PTSD symptoms, parenting stress, and child outcomes change over time. This is especially important because family stress processes may shift as children age, enter new school stages, or face changing post-migration conditions. Future studies should also include fathers, siblings, teachers, and observational measures of parent–child interaction. Multi-informant and multi-method assessment would help clarify whether parenting stress affects child outcomes directly, whether child symptoms increase parenting burden, or whether bidirectional processes are present.

Intervention studies are also needed. The current findings suggest that trauma-informed parenting support, maternal mental health treatment, and school-based executive functioning support may be promising targets. Programs that combine caregiver trauma treatment with parenting skills, emotion coaching, and child coping strategies may be especially relevant for refugee families exposed to prolonged displacement.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature on refugee family mental health by demonstrating that maternal PTSD symptoms, parenting stress, and child emotional, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes were significantly associated in a manner consistent with a relational pathway, although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The indirect pathway was strongest for child emotional and behavioral problems but was also evident for inhibitory control as measured by Stroop interference. Qualitative findings deepened these results by illustrating how trauma-related maternal distress, emotional exhaustion, and reduced parenting availability were experienced within daily mother–child relationships. Together, the findings suggest that supporting refugee children requires attention not only to children’s individual symptoms but also to maternal trauma, parenting stress, and the relational environment in which children recover, regulate, learn, and develop.
