Pancreatic Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm Arising Within Heterotopic Pancreas Tissue of the Stomach
Authors: Rinrada Worapongpaiboon, Kasenee Tiankanon, Benjamin L. Mazer, Saowanee Ngamruengphong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHeterotopic pancreas is a pancreatic tissue outside of its normal location. It is usually benign and asymptomatic but rarely can develop neoplasms such as intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs). A 73-year-old woman undergoing evaluation for chronic reflux was found to have a gastric submucosal lesion on esophagogastroduodenoscopy. Endoscopic ultrasound findings were inconclusive, leading to endoscopic submucosal dissection performed with a complete resection. Histopathology confirmed pancreatic heterotopia with IPMN changes and focal high-grade dysplasia. This case highlights that IPMNs with high-grade dysplasia can rarely arise in gastrointestinal heterotopic pancreas. Careful management and ongoing surveillance are essential due to potential malignant transformation.

INTRODUCTION

Heterotopic pancreas, also known as ectopic or aberrant pancreas, refers to pancreatic tissue located outside the normal pancreas with no anatomical or vascular connection to it.1 It is found incidentally in about 0.5%–14% of autopsies 1 and in approximately 1 in 500 laparotomies.2 It most commonly occurs in the stomach, duodenum, or jejunum, and typically involves the submucosal or muscular layers.3 It is usually benign and asymptomatic, but larger lesions may cause ulceration, bleeding, or obstruction, and rarely undergo malignant transformation occurring in approximately 0.7% to 1.8% of cases.4,5

Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN) is a cystic tumor of the pancreatic ductal system, marked by intraductal papillary growth, excess mucin production, and ductal dilation.6 IPMNs account for about 20% of pancreatic cystic lesions and are classified as main duct, branch duct, or mixed types according to their imaging features.7 While usually incidental, they may present with abdominal pain, pancreatitis, or weight loss.6 Though rare, IPMNs have been reported in heterotopic pancreatic tissue, including in sites such as the stomach, jejunum, and duodenum.8–10 Given their malignant potential, IPMNs, even in ectopic sites, warrant careful assessment, though data remain limited regarding their risk of malignant transformation.

In this study, we report a rare case of an incidentally discovered IPMN with high-grade dysplasia (HGD) in gastric pancreatic heterotropia.

CASE REPORT

A 73-year-old woman with a history of stroke, deep vein thrombosis, and gastroesophageal reflux disease initially underwent an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) to evaluate persistent chronic reflux. She denied alarm symptoms and had no prior EGD. She did not smoke and rarely consumed alcohol. Family history included colon cancer in the maternal grandfather and pancreatic cancer in the paternal uncle. Physical examination was unremarkable.

An EGD revealed a 2 cm hiatal hernia without reflux esophagitis. A 15 mm smooth subepithelial lesion (SEL) at the greater curvature of the antrum without the central umbilication (Figure 1) was found. Biopsy of the overlying mucosa only showed reactive gastropathy. Three months later, on endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), the lesion was well-defined, heterogeneous, and hypoechoic with scattered cystic components, measuring 15 × 12 mm (Figure 1). It was predominantly located within the third layer (submucosa), without invasion of deeper layers or adjacent structures. The initial EUS differential diagnoses included pancreatic heterotopia, neuroendocrine tumor (NET), and granular cell tumor, based on the lesion's echotexture and submucosal origin. EUS-guided sampling was deferred due to its anticipated low diagnostic yield.

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) was performed 1 month later (Figure 1), achieving en bloc removal (Figure 1). Gross specimen revealed a submucosal nodule with yellow-tan, firm, multiloculated cut surfaces. Histology demonstrated pancreatic heterotopia composed of acini and ducts extending to the deep resection margin. Areas of papillary mucinous neoplasm consistent with IPMN and focal HGD were identified within the lesion; however, no IPMN or HGD was present at the resection margins (Figure 2).

Histopathologic findings. (A) Low-power view of pancreatic heterotopia arising in gastric submucosa. (B) Flat and early papillary mucinous epithelium (gastric and intestinal types) representing changes of intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm. (C) Focal high-grade dysplasia (black arrow).

Owing to the presence of HGD in the ectopic pancreas with IPMN, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography was performed to evaluate for concurrent lesions in normal pancreatic tissue. Imaging revealed a 1.4 × 1.1 cm cystic lesion at the distal pancreatic body and smaller cysts in the uncinate process and tail, consistent with side-branch IPMNs without worrisome features.

Blood test for CA 19-9 was mildly elevated (46.8 U/mL). CA 125 and carcinoembryonic antigen were normal. The case was reviewed by a multidisciplinary pancreatic cyst group. Given the HGD and family history of pancreatic cancer, EUS and imaging surveillance was recommended. Follow-up EGD at 3 months showed well-healed scar in the antrum and no evidence of recurrence. Thereafter, the patient was scheduled for EUS/EGD and MRI/magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography at 1 year to monitor for gastric SEL recurrence and pancreatic cyst progression, respectively.

DISCUSSION

Gastric submucosal lesions have varied etiologies, including gastrointestinal stromal tumors, NETs, leiomyomas, and, rarely, heterotopic pancreas.11 On EGD, heterotopic pancreas often appears as a submucosal lesion with central umbilication, while EUS typically shows a heterogeneous submucosal or muscularis lesion with duct-like or cystic structures and no vascularity, distinguishing it from gastrointestinal stromal tumors or NETs.11,12 In our case, EGD showed a SEL, and EUS demonstrated a heterogeneous, submucosal lesion with cystic structures, raising suspicion for IPMN arising within heterotopic pancreas.

IPMN is a mucin-producing pancreatic cystic neoplasm with malignant potential, but its occurrence in heterotopic pancreas is rare, with a total of 23 cases reported. Within this group, HGD is exceedingly uncommon, with only 4 reported cases to date, including 1 in the stomach (Table 1). All HGD cases, including ours, have been diagnosed only after resection, as no endoscopic or imaging features reliably predict HGD. Histologically, they demonstrate complex papillary or cribriform growth, nuclear atypia, and frequent mitoses. By comparison, progression of ectopic IPMN to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma occurs in <2% of ectopic pancreas overall.13 While EUS or imaging may occasionally reveal worrisome morphologic features, such as cyst size ≥3 cm or thickened/enhancing walls, these findings remain nonspecific. Elevated serum CA 19-9 can also serve as an additional worrisome serum marker.13,14

No clear guidelines exist for managing IPMN in heterotopic pancreas due to its rarity. Standard recommendations guided by general pancreatic IPMN guidelines advise surveillance by MRI or EUS for small asymptomatic IPMNs.14 Resection is indicated for cysts with worrisome features.14 In our case, due to the lesion's indeterminate histology and unusually large size for incidentally found gastric ectopic pancreas, ESD was performed, leading to the diagnosis and treatment of IPMN with HGD arising in heterotopic pancreas.

This case underscores the potential for malignant transformation in heterotopic pancreas and the importance of tissue diagnosis, even in asymptomatic, incidental lesions. Close follow-up is warranted, particularly when dysplasia or relevant family history is present.
