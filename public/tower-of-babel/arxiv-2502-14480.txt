Groups with 5 nontrivial conjugacy classes of non self-normalizing subgroups are solvable
Maria Loukaki

Abstract
. For any n n nonnegative integer, a family of groups, denoted by 𝒟 n \mathcal{D}_{n} , was introduce by Bianchi et al., as the collection of all finite groups with exactly n n conjugacy classes of nontrivial, non self-normalizing subgroups. It was conjectured that 𝒟 5 \mathcal{D}_{5} consists of solvable groups with derived length at most 3 3 . In this note we verify their conjecture.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
20E07, 20E34, 20D10, 20D15

1.
Introduction

For any
n
nonnegative integer, let
\mathcal{D}_{n}
denote the family of all finite groups with exactly
n
conjugacy classes of nontrivial, non self-normalizing subgroups. These families were introduced by Bianchi et al. in
[
BCL+24
]
, and their properties regarding solvability and nilpotency have been examined. Several interesting results were established, including a complete classification of the groups lying in
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for
i=0,1,2,3
. Additionaly, it was shown that the families
\mathcal{D}_{i}
contain only solvable groups for all
i=0,1,2,3
and
4
with one exception:
A_{5}\in\mathcal{D}_{4}
. Moreover, the derived length
\mathrm{dl}(G)
of any solvable
G\in D_{i}
, for
i=0,1,\cdots,4
, is at most
2
unless
G\cong\mathrm{SL}_{2}(3)
.

In the same paper, the authors conjectured that any
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
is solvable with
\mathrm{dl}(G)\leq 3
which is confirmed by our main result:

Theorem A
.

If a finite group
G
lies in
\mathcal{D}_{5}
then
G
is solvable with derived length at most
3
.

As an immediate consequence we get the following.

Corollary 1
.

Assume
n\geq 5
and
G\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
is a solvable finite group. Then
\mathrm{dl}(G)\leq n-2
.

We follow the notation used in
[
BCL+24
]
. In particular, we denote by
\mathcal{D}(G)
the number of conjugacy classes of nontrivial subgroups of
G
that are not self-normalizing. Moreover, if
N\unlhd G
we write
\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)
for the number of
G
-conjugacy classes of nontrivial subgroups of
G
properly contained in
N
that are not self-normalizing. We also write
\varpi(G)
for the set of primes that divide the order of
G
.

2.
Proofs

Before giving the proof of our theorem we recall some useful results from

Lemma 1

If

\mathcal{D}(G/N)\leq\mathcal{D}(G)-\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)-1\leq\mathcal{D}(G)-1

Several of the results proven in
[
BCL+24
]

with

, are collected in the next proposition that we will use often.

Proposition 1
.

Assume
G
is a finite group.

•

If
G\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
if and only if
G
is cyclic of prime order. (Proposition 3.1 in
[
BCL+24
]
).

•

If
G\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
with
n\leq 3
then
G
is solvable with derived length at most
2
. (Proposition 3.3 in
[
BCL+24
]
).

•

The only non-solvable group in
\mathcal{D}_{4}
is the alternating group
A_{5}
.(Theorem 8.2 in
[
BCL+24
]
).

•

If

is a solvable group in

We will now prove our theorem, beginning with a straightforward lemma.

Lemma 2
.

If
N\unlhd G
with
\mathcal{D}(G/N)=\mathcal{D}(G)-1
then
\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)=\mathcal{D}(N)=0
and
N
is a cyclic group of prime order.

Proof.

By
1
we have
\mathcal{D}(G)-1=\mathcal{D}(G/N)\leq\mathcal{D}(G)-\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)-1
. Hence
\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)=0
and so every nontrivial proper subgroup of
N
is selfnormalizing. Thus
\mathcal{D}(N)=0
and
N
is cyclic of prime order by
1
.
∎

The next observation is useful.

Remark 1
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
and
N
be a proper normal subgroup of
G
. For any Sylow subgroup
P
of
N
, Frattini’s Lemma implies that
\mathbf{N}_{G}(P)\nsubseteq N
. Consequently, for every
p\in\varpi(N)
, the corresponding Sylow
p
-subgroup
P
of
N
is not self-normalizing in
G
. Thus,
\left\lvert\varpi(N)\right\rvert\leq 4
(since
N
itself is also not self-normalizing). If
\left\lvert P\right\rvert=p^{a}
, then in addition to
P
,
N
includes non-selfnormalizing, non-
G
-conjugate subgroups of orders
p
,
p^{2}
, …,
p^{a-1}
. As a result,
N
falls into one of the following cases:

•

N
is a
Z
-group, meaning every Sylow subgroup of
N
is cyclic. In this scenario, both
N/N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
are cyclic groups (see Theorem 5.16 in
[
Isa08
]
), implying that
N
is solvable with derived length
\mathrm{dl}(N)\leq 2
.

•

\left\lvert\varpi(N)\right\rvert=1
and thus
N
is nilpotent of order
\left\lvert N\right\rvert=p^{a}
for some prime
p
and
a\leq 5
.

•

\left\lvert\varpi(N)\right\rvert=2
and
|N|
equals one of
p^{2}q^{2},\,p^{3}q
or
p^{2}q
where
p
and
q
are distinct primes. In this case
N
is solvable.

•

\left\lvert\varpi(N)\right\rvert=3
and
\left\lvert N\right\rvert=p^{2}qr
, where
p,q,r
are distinct primes.

Any group
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
has no non-solvable proper normal subgroups, as demonstrated by the following proposition.

Lemma 3
.

If
G
is a finite group and
\mathcal{D}(G)=5
then any proper normal subgroup of
G
is solvable.

Proof.

Assume the opposite and let
N
be a proper normal non-solvable subgroup of
G
.
In view of
1
,
\left\lvert N\right\rvert=p^{2}qr
with
p,q,r
being distinct primes and one of them is
2
(or else
N
is solvable). Let
P
,
Q
, and
R
be the Sylow
p
,
q
, and
r
-subgroups of
N
, respectively. The subgroup
P_{1}
of order
p
within
P
is not self-normalizing in
N
(and in
G
). Therefore, the groups
P_{1}
,
P
,
Q
,
R
, and
N
represent the five distinct
G
-conjugacy classes of nontrivial, non self-normalizing subgroups of
G
.
Thus,
N
does not contain any other nontrivial non self-normalizing subgroup of
G
besides
P_{1}
,
P
,
Q
, and
R

and
\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)=4
.

Assume first that
q=2
and so
\left\lvert N\right\rvert=2p^{2}r
. The Sylow
2
-subgroup of
N
is cyclic, and by Corollary 5.14 in
[
Isa08
]
,
N
has a normal
2
-complement, denoted as
S
. Then
|S|=p^{2}r
and
S
is not
G
-conjugate to any of
P_{1},P,Q,R
and
N
, contradicting our assumption.

Assume now that
p=2
and so
\left\lvert N\right\rvert=4qr
. Then every Sylow subgroup of
N
of odd order is cyclic while the Sylow
2
-subgroup of
N
is of order
4
. According to Theorem A in
[
Suz55
]
we get that
N\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,s)\times K
, for some
Z
-group
K
, and
s\geq 5
because
N
is non-solvable.
Since
4qr=\left\lvert\mathrm{PSL}(2,s)\times K\right\rvert
, we conclude that
s
is an odd prime, and without loss of generality
s=q
, and
K
is trivial. Hence

4qr=|N|=\left\lvert\mathrm{PSL}(2,q)\right\rvert=\frac{q(q^{2}-1)}{2}.

Thus,
2r=\frac{q-1}{2}\cdot\frac{q+1}{2}
leads to
q=5
and
r=3
, which gives
N\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,5)=A_{5}
. Since
\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)=4
, we have
\mathcal{D}(G/N)\leq\mathcal{D}(G)-\mathcal{D}_{G}(N)-1=0
. Consequently,
G/N
is a cyclic group of prime order. If
C_{G}(N)\neq 1
and
x\in C_{G}(N)
, then
\langle x\rangle
would serve as a normal complement of
N
in
G
, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{D}(G)=5
. The
N/C
theorem implies that
G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(N)\cong S_{5}
and so
G\cong S_{5}
. But
\mathcal{D}(S_{5})=13
and this final contradiction completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We next prove that no simple group is contained in
\mathcal{D}_{5}
, while noting that, in contrast,
\mathrm{PSL}(2,8))\in\mathcal{D}_{6}
.

Proposition 2
.

Assume
G
is a non-abelian simple group
G\ncong A_{5}
. Then
\mathcal{D}(G)\geq 6
.

Proof.

In view of the work done in
[
BCL+24
]
(see
1
)
it suffices to show that there is no simple group in
\mathcal{D}_{5}
. Assume the opposite for contradiction, and let
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
be simple and non-abelian.
So
\left\lvert\varpi(G)\right\rvert\geq 3
and
2\in\varpi(G)
.

Let
p\in\varpi(G)
and
P
be any Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
. If
P
is abelian then
\mathbf{N}_{G}(P)>P
, or else
P\leq\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{N}_{G}(P))
and thus
G
has a normal
p
-complement, by Theorem 5.13 in
[
Isa08
]
. Observe now that whenever
|P|\geq p^{2}
the prime
p
adds at least
+2
to
\mathcal{D}(G)
due to the presence of subgroups of order
p
and
p^{2}
(even if
|P|=p^{2}
since
P
itself in not self-normalizing in this case). If
|P|=p
then the prime
p
contributes
+1
to
\mathcal{D}(G)
.
Corollary 5.14 in
[
Isa08
]
ensures that the Sylow
2
-subgroups of
G
are non-cyclic, contributing at least
+2
to
\mathcal{D}(G)
.

We claim that
\left\lvert\varpi(G)\right\rvert<5
. Indeed, if
p_{1},\ldots,p_{4}
are four distinct odd primes in
\varpi(G)
, each contributes at least
+1
to
\mathcal{D}(G)
, while the prime
2
contributes at least
+2
. This results in
\mathcal{D}(G)\geq 6
, contradicting our assumption.
Hence
3\leq\left\lvert\varpi(G)\right\rvert\leq 4
.

Assume first that
\left\lvert\varpi(G)\right\rvert=3
. Then
G
is a simple group whose order is divisible by three primes only and thus is isomorphic to one of the following

A_{5},\,A_{6},\,\mathrm{PSL}(2,7),\,\mathrm{PSL}(2,8),\,\mathrm{PSL}(2,17),\,\mathrm{PSL}(3,3),\,\mathrm{U}_{3}(3),\,\mathrm{U}_{4}(2),

according to
[
Her68
]
. It can easily be checked by GAP that

\mathcal{D}(A_{6})=11,\,\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(2,7))=8,\,\,\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(2,8))=6,\,\,\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(2,17))=14,\,\,\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(3,3))=38,\,\,\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{U}_{3}(3))=35
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{U}_{4}(2))=96
. As none of them lies in
\mathcal{D}_{5}
we get that

\left\lvert\varpi(G)\right\rvert=4
. As the prime
2
contributes at least
+2
in
\mathcal{D}(G)
, each one of the remaining three odd primes must contribute exactly
+1
in
\mathcal{D}(G)
. Hence

\left\lvert G\right\rvert=2^{a}pqr,

with
2\leq a\leq 3
. In particular
G
has cyclic Sylow subgroups for each odd prime.

Case 1.

a=2
and let
r<q<p
.
From Theorem on pg.671 in
[
Suz55
]
we have
G\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,p)
and so
4pqr=\left\lvert G\right\rvert=\frac{p(p^{2}-1)}{2}
.
As
3\big|(p-1)(p+1)
for every prime
p\neq 3
we get
r=3
and

12pq=\left\lvert G\right\rvert=\frac{p(p^{2}-1)}{2}.

Thus, we have
24q=p^{2}-1
with
q<p\geq 7
. Checking case by case the divisors of
24q
reveals two possibilities: either
p-1=2q
and
p+1=12
, or vice versa,
p-1=12
and
p+1=2q
. This leads to the two groups
\mathrm{PSL}(2,11)
and
\mathrm{PSL}(2,13)
. Using GAP, we find
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(2,11))=8
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(2,13))=10
. Therefore, Case 1 cannot occur.

Case 2.

a=3
and let
A
be a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
G
with
|A|=8
. Then
\mathbf{N}_{G}(A)=A
or else
\mathbf{N}_{G}(A)>A
and so

would have non self-normalizing subgroups of order
2,4
and

, leaving no room for the non self-normalizing subgroups provided by the other three odd primes.
Also
A
is non-abelian or else

to have a normal
2

Hence
A
is either isomorphic to the quaternion or the dihedral. According to the Brauer-Suzuki Theorem (see
[
BS59
]
) there is no simple group having the quaternion group as a Sylow 2-subgroup. So
A\cong D_{4}
and Theorem B in
[
Suz55
]
applies. As
\mathcal{D}(PSL(2,8)=6
we get that
G\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,s)
for some odd

and without loss may assume

with
p\geq 5
. Hence

8pqr=\left\lvert G\right\rvert=\frac{p(p^{2}-1)}{2}.

Since
3\mid(p^{2}-1)
, we can set

, leading to the equation

48q=(p-1)(p+1).

As earlier we check the divisors of

and we end up only with one possible pair

. Therefore

with
\mathcal{D}(\mathrm{PSL}(2,23))=15

We are ready now to prove that every group in
\mathcal{D}_{5}
is solvable.

Theorem 4
.

Assume
G
is a finite group with
\mathcal{D}(G)=5
. Then
G
is solvable.

Proof.

Let
G
be a counterexample, that is,
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
is non-solvable. Then

G
is not abelian and is not simple according to
2
, so let
N
be a proper normal subgroup of
G
.
By
3
,
N
is solvable, forcing
G/N
to be non-solvable. We have
\mathcal{D}(G/N)\leq\mathcal{D}(G)-1=4
and the only non-solvable group in
\mathcal{D}_{i}
for
i\leq 4
, is
A_{5}
. Consequently,

G/N\cong A_{5}
and
\mathcal{D}(G/N)=4
and so (see
2
)
N\cong C_{s}
is a cyclic group of prime order
s
. Consider the centralizer
\mathbf{C}_{G}(N)
of
N
in
G
. Since
G/N
is simple and
\mathbf{C}_{G}(N)
is normal in
G
, either
\mathbf{C}_{G}(N)=N
or
\mathbf{C}_{G}(N)=G
. The first option is impossible due to the
N/C
theorem (otherwise
A_{5}\cong G/N
would be isomorphic to a subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(C_{s})\cong C_{s-1}
). Therefore,
N
must be central in
G
, in fact,
N=\mathbf{Z}(G)
, as the previous argument applies to
\mathbf{Z}(G)
as well.

The prime
s
is different from at least one of
3,5
and without loss we may assume
s\neq 3
.
Write
\bar{A},\bar{B},\bar{C},\bar{D}
for the representatives of the four nontrivial non self-normalizing subgroups of
G/N
with orders

3,5,4
and
2
respectively. If
A,B,C,D
are their preimages in
G
then these four along with
N
form a set of representatives of the five classes of non self-normalizing subgroups of
G
with orders
3s,5s,4s,2s
and
s
respectively. If
X\in\mathrm{Syl}_{3}(A)
(and thus
X\in\mathrm{Syl}_{3}(G)
) then we clearly have
A=N\times X
and
N_{G}(X)\geq A>X
. Hence
X
is not self-normalizing and is not
G
-conjugate to any of the other five subgroups of
G
, implying
\mathcal{D}(G)\geq 6
.

This final contradiction proves that no such
G
exists and the theorem follows.
∎

The final component of our main theorem regarding the derived length of groups in
\mathcal{D}_{5}
is addressed in the next theorem.

Theorem 5
.

If
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
then its derived length
\mathrm{dl}(G)
satisfies
\mathrm{dl}(G)\leq 3
.

Proof.

Let
G\in\mathcal{D}_{5}
and assume
G
is non-abelian. As we have seen
G
is solvable, so
1<G^{\prime}<G
.
We apply
1
for
G^{\prime}
in the place of
N
, so
\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert\leq 4
.
We distinguish four cases.

Case 1:

G^{\prime}
is a
Z
-group and thus
\mathrm{dl}(G^{\prime})\leq 2
as wanted.

Case 2:

\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert=1
. Then
\left\lvert G^{\prime}\right\rvert=p^{a}
for some prime
p
and
a\leq 5
. If the derived length of
G^{\prime}
is
k+1
, it was shown by
Hall (see III.7.11 in
[
Hup67
]
) that
a\geq 2^{k}+k
. Hence
k\leq 1
and
\mathrm{dl}(G^{\prime})\leq 2
as desired.

Case 3:

\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert=2
and
\left\lvert G^{\prime}\right\rvert
is one of
p^{2}q^{2}
,
p^{3}q
, or
p^{2}q
, where
p
and
q
are distinct primes. If
\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert=p^{2}q
then
G^{\prime}
has a normal Sylow subgroup (see Theorem 1.31 in
[
Isa08
]
), so
G^{(2)}
is either trivial or abelian and thus
\mathrm{dl}(G^{\prime})\leq 2
.

If
\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert=p^{2}q^{2}
, let
P
and
Q
be Sylow
p
and
q
subgroups of
G^{\prime}
, with
P_{1}<P
and
Q_{1}<Q
subgroups of orders
p
and
q
, respectively. Then
P,P_{1},Q,Q_{1}
along with
G^{\prime}
represent the five classes of non self-normalizing subgroups of
G
. If
G^{(2)}\neq 1
, it must be one of
P
,
P_{1}
,
Q
, or
Q_{1}
, since it is normal in
G
. In any case,
G^{(2)}
is abelian, leading to
G^{(3)}=1
as required.

If
\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert=p^{3}q
let
P
,
Q
be Sylow
p
and
q
-subgroups of
G^{\prime}
and
P_{1}<P_{2}<P
subgroups of order
p
and
p^{2}
, respectively, within

. As before, the groups
P_{1},P_{2},P,Q
and

represent the five classes of non self-normalizing subgroups of
G
. Therefore,
G^{(2)}
must either be one of them or be trivial. All these groups are abelian, with the potential exception of
P
that could be an extra special group of order
p^{3}
. In this scenario, the center
\mathbf{Z}(P)
is a normal subgroup of
G
of order
p
, and any non-central element of

with order

generates another non self-normalizing subgroup of
G
that is not conjugate to
\mathbf{Z}(P)
. This leads to
\mathcal{D}(G)\geq 6
, resulting in a contradiction. Thus,
G^{(2)}
must be abelian.

Case 4:

\left\lvert\varpi(G^{\prime})\right\rvert=3
and
and
\left\lvert G^{\prime}\right\rvert=p^{2}qr
, where
p,q,r
are distinct primes.
As earlier, if
P,Q,R
are Sylow
p,q
and
r
-subgroups of
G^{\prime}
and
P_{1}<P
a subgroup of order
p
then
P_{1},P,Q,R
and

represent the five classes of non self-normalizing subgroups of

This final case completes the proof of the theorem.
