Development and assessment of a peptide vaccine against ovarian cancer utilizing nanoliposomes loaded with P53, WT1, and CA125 epitopes
Authors: Fatemeh Shariati, Mehrdad Hashemi, Maryam Peyvandi, Ali Jebali, Maliheh Entezari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to design, synthesize, and evaluate a peptide vaccine based on nanoliposomes loading multi-epitopes (PVNLME) of P53, WT1, and CA125. We selected the best epitope for each targeted protein and then, PVNLME was synthesized and characterized. Subsequently, BALB/c mice were randomly divided into two groups receiving 10 mg/ml or 100 mg/ml of PVNLME. Then, 100 µl of the vaccine were injected into each mouse every seven days for three consecutive weeks. In the fourth week, blood samples were taken, and both antibody titer and the serum level of different cytokines were measured. To further investigate, each mouse’s serum sample was exposed to the OVCAR3 cell line. Subsequently, BAX to BCL2 gene expression ratio, cell viability, and apoptosis were evaluated. Finally, the efficacy of the peptide vaccine was analyzed in humanized PDX model mice. Based on Bioinformatics analysis, a merged peptide EENLRKKGEPHHELPPKKKKCKTCQRKFSRSDHLKTKKKDTTPSMTTSHGAESSS was selected as a multi-epitope peptide. We found that the size distribution of PVNLME was 72–198 nm with a mean size of 112 nm, zeta potential of + 30 mV, and 96% peptide loading. The level of cytokines and the titer of antibodies increased with increasing doses of PVNLME. Furthermore, we showed that this vaccine can increase the ratio expression of BAX /BCL2, which promotes apoptosis. Also, there was a decrease in cell viability and an increase in apoptosis rate in both doses and exposure times. Following the administration of this multi-epitope vaccine in PDX humanized mice, a notable reduction in the tumor volume was observed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01792-2.Keywords: Multi epitope peptide, Ovarian cancer, Nanoliposomes, P53, WT1, And CA125

Introduction

Ovarian cancer ranks as the eighth most prevalent cancer among women worldwide [1]. Ovarian cancer occurs when mutated cells in the ovary start to proliferate and divide uncontrollably, eventually forming a tumor. If not detected early, cancer cells can infiltrate the surrounding tissues and potentially spread to other parts of the body. There are various types of ovarian cancer, depending on the type of cell. The most common type is epithelial ovarian cancer and the rare types of ovarian cancer are germ cell tumors (teratomas and dysgerminomas), stromal tumors (granulosa tumors), and sarcomas [2].

Typically, the treatment of ovarian cancer involves a combination of surgery and chemotherapy. While most patients initially respond well to treatment, the majority of them will experience a relapse and need further treatment. The emergence of chemotherapy-resistant cancers poses a significant challenge in managing recurrent ovarian cancer. Researchers are currently investigating novel and more effective treatments for ovarian cancer. Among novel approaches, immunotherapy is an interesting approach, that utilizes the ability of the immune system to recognize and fight cancer. The ability of the immune system to limit the growth of tumors has been known for a long time. For instance, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are tumor-specific cells presented within the ovarian tumor microenvironment and serve as a reliable prognostic factor [3]. Importantly, cancer vaccinations have shown promising results in clinical and preclinical studies [4]. Cancer vaccines stimulate immune effector cells, particularly cytotoxic T cells and NK cells, to target tumor cells with tumor-specific or tumor-associated antigens. Peptide-based vaccines, also known as epitope vaccines, utilize peptides containing tumor antigen epitopes to elicit immune responses and can be combined with other treatment options, such as chemotherapy and surgery [5]. An important step towards designing an efficient peptide-based cancer vaccine is choosing target antigens and epitopes. Tumor antigens can be detected by analyzing the tumor-overexpressed, tumor-associated antigens, and neo-antigens which are the product of somatic mutations. For ovarian cancer, the results of these analyses have introduced several tumor antigens, such as p53, FOLR1, P53, and MUC-1 [6]. Moreover, clinical trials confirmed the efficacy of some protein targets, such as HER-2/neu, WT1, CA125, and Flt3 [7].

The primary objective of this study was to identify the linear epitopes for P53, WT1, and CA125 protein targets, followed by assessing the antigenic potency, MHC binding, allergenicity, and toxicity of the selected epitopes. Subsequently, a peptide vaccine based on nanoliposomes loading multi-epitopes (PVNLME) of P53, WT1, and CA125 was synthesized and characterized. Nanoliposomes, as a peptide carrier, can be tailored in terms of size, charge, surface characteristics, and delivery mechanism, enabling them to be used for cancer vaccines [8]. The study also involved measuring the antibody titer after injection of PVNLME in BALB/c mice, analyzing serum cytokine levels, and examining their role in inducing apoptosis. In the last step, the efficacy of the PVNLME was confirmed on humanized PXD mice.

Materials and methods

Materials

The merged peptide was synthesized by Proteogenix in France. 2,3-dioleyloxy-N-[2-(sperminecarboxamido)ethyl]-N, N-dimethyl-1-propanaminium (DOSPA), 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), Annexin V, propidium iodide, Trypsin, EDTA, MTT, and DMSO were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich. DMEM, FBS, and penicillin/streptomycin were supplied by Gipco in the USA. RNA extraction buffer, cDNA synthesis kit, primers, and SYBR® Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix were obtained from the Parstus company in Iran. The Mouse antibody titration Kit and ELISA kits of interleukin 4, interleukin 6, interleukin 10, tumor necrosis factor, and interferon-gamma were purchased from BioLegend.

Methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Assessment of TP53, WT1, CA125 gene expression, survival and pan-cancer in ovarian cancer

We utilized the UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) to conduct an in-depth analysis of ovarian cancer omics data. Our investigation specifically targeted the TP53, WT1, and CA125 genes, allowing us to access a rich array of information across multiple parameters. The results we gathered provide valuable insights into sample sizes, cancer stages, and histological subtypes. This thorough analysis enhances our understanding of the roles and implications of the TP53, WT1, and CA125 genes in ovarian cancer from various clinical and molecular perspectives.

Detection of mutations in the P53, WT1, and CA125 genes and evaluation of heterogeneity

We acquired somatic mutation profiles for TCGA-COAD from the Xenabrowser database (https://xenabrowser.net/). Utilizing the R ‘maftools’ package, we visualized Mutation Annotation Format (MAF) files, concentrating on simple nucleotide variations that were processed via the varScan2 workflow for variant aggregation and masking. In addition, we computed the tumor mutation burden (TMB) and Mutant-Allele Tumor Heterogeneity (MATH) scores for tumor samples within the TCGA-COAD dataset using the ‘maftools’ package. We also investigated the relationships among highly mutated genes and P53, WT1, and CA125.

The selection of the best epitope for each protein targets

Initially, the sequence of each protein target was retrieved from the NCBI website, followed by an analysis of their different variants using the Blast algorithm. Subsequently, the FASTA sequence of the protein segments with the highest overlap was chosen and analyzed by the ABCpred (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/ ABCpred). The peptide antigenicity was determined using the Vaxijen (http://www.jenner.ac.uk/VaxiJen) server in the subsequent stage. Following that, the AllerTOP (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/) server was utilized to verify the allergenicity of the epitope sequences, and only the non-allergenic sequences were chosen. Subsequently, the ToxinPred (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxipred/) server was employed to assess the toxicity of the epitopes, and only the non-toxic sequences were selected. The VaxiTop (https://violinet.org/vaxign/vaxitop/) server was utilized to assess the epitope recognition rate by class I/II MHCs, and the peptide sequences recognized more by both class I/II MHC alleles were chosen. Subsequently, the precise location of each peptide on the 3D structure file of the desired protein targets was identified through the Uniprot website. Lastly, three peptide sequences for P53, WT1, and CA125 were selected and linked together with two KKK linkers. The final sequence was EENLRKKGEPHHELPPKKKKCKTCQRKFSRSDHLKTKKKDTTPSMTTSHGAESSS. This sequence was again checked by ABCpred, Vaxijen, AllerTOP, ToxinPred, and VaxiTop. The final 3D structure of the merged peptide was modeled by Ascalaph designer software and validated by the PROCHECK server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) UCLA-DOE LAB — SAVES v6.0.

The synthesis of PVNLME

Here, the selected peptide containing three peptide sequences for P53, WT1, and CA125 and two KKK linkers, EENLRKKGEPHHELPPKKKKCKTCQRKFSRSDHLKTKKK DTTPSMTTSHGAESSS, was synthesized using the solid phase method. Subsequently, 1 mL of PBS buffer was added to 10 mg of the final synthesized peptide and mixed for approximately 5 min. Following this, 0.5 mL of the peptide solution and 0.50 mL of cationic lipid mixture (DOSPA: DOPE, 3:1) were added and vigorously mixed using a mixer for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and 1mL of PBS buffer containing 1% alum was added to the sediment. The peptide loading percentage was determined by measuring the optical density (OD) of the supernatant at 280 nm using a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher). Furthermore, DLS (Malvern Panalytical), Zetasizer (Malvern Panalytical), and TEM (Zeiss) were used to characterize the nanoliposomes. To find the stability of PVNLME, their size and zeta potential were checked during 4 weeks at 37 °C, 25 °C, 4 °C, and − 20 °C.

Vaccination of balb/c mice

The Balb/c mice, averaging one month old and weighing approximately 10–12 g, were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran’s research center. To acclimate to their new environment, the mice were kept under normal conditions for a week. These conditions included a temperature range of 20–24 °C, a light-dark cycle of 12:12 h, suitable humidity, and constant access to water and feed. Subsequently, the mice were randomly divided into 2 main groups, receiving 10 mg/ml and 100 mg/ml of PVNLME, based on preliminary optimization work (Supplementary 1). The 100 µl of PVNLME were injected every week for three consecutive weeks. After the fourth week, blood samples were collected from the heart tissue using an insulin syringe. The serum was then separated from the blood using a centrifuge and stored at -20 °C for further analysis.

Here, other study groups were considered for better analysis, including (1) empty nanoliposomes, (2) free multi-epitope peptide, (3) free irrelevant peptide, and (4) PBS. The sample size was n = 6 mice per each group.

All animal studies were performed under the Guideline for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Biology, Faculty of Biosciences, North Tehran Branch Islamic Azad University Tehran, Iran (Approval No: IR.IAU.TNB.REC.1403.141) and ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Evaluating the antibody titer

The ELISA method was utilized to measure the antibody titer in immunized mice. The procedure involved coating the wells of a 96-well plate with the 1 mg/ml synthesized peptide, followed by the use of a 5% skim milk solution. Subsequently, the serum concentration series with dilutions of 1:10, 1:100, 1:1000, and 1:10000 were prepared and placed adjacent to the coated peptides. Mouse antibody conjugated with HRP enzyme was then added and after incubation and adding the substrate, the OD of each well was assessed at a wavelength of 630 nm. Finally, the OD of each well was compared with the OD of the control and cutoff baseline.

Analyzing the cytokines

The levels of different cytokines, including interferon-gamma, interleukin 10, interleukin 6, interleukin 4, and TNFα, were assessed after vaccination. This evaluation was conducted using the ELISA method with the BioLegend kit, adhering to the specified protocol of the kit. The absorbance of the samples was then measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using an ELISA reader. Finally, the concentration of each cytokine was individually measured by the standard curve.

OVCAR3 cancer cell line

The research was carried out using the OVCAR3 ovarian cancer cell line, sourced from the Pasteur Institute cell bank. These cells were cultivated in DMEM culture medium, supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a flask maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cell culture medium was refreshed every three days to promote cell growth. Upon reaching 70% density, the cells were detached from the flask using trypsin/EDTA enzyme, transferred to new flasks, and passaged three times to obtain a sufficient number of cancer cells. To investigate the impact of antibodies from vaccinated mice on OVCAR3 cells, the cells were enumerated using trypan blue, and around one hundred thousand cells were seeded in each plate.

MTT assay

The MTT Assay method was utilized to examine the impact of the serum of vaccinated mice on the viability of ovarian cancer cells (OVCAR3). The OVCAR3 cell line was cultured in a flask containing a DMEM culture medium. Once the cells reached the optimal level, they were isolated using preheated Trypsin/EDTA and then re-planted. After 24 h, when the cells adhered to the bottom of the plate and regained their morphology, they were treated with 50 µl of the serum of vaccinated mice and incubated for 24 and 48 h. To evaluate the cell survival at the end of each time interval, 20 µl of MTT dye solution was added to each well and the plate was incubated for 3 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was removed, and 100 µl of DMSO was added to each well. After pipetting, the OD of each well was read at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA reader. The survival percentage of the studied cells was calculated by comparing the OD obtained from the test wells to that of the control wells. Finally, the data was plotted in a graph using Excel software.

Apoptosis assay

The apoptosis assay was conducted using annexin 5 solution and propidium iodide by flow cytometry. Similar to the MTT test, the cells were initially seeded in the plate and then exposed to the serum of vaccinated mice. After 24 and 48 h, the cell suspension was prepared with trypsin, followed by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with annexin V-FITC antibody at 4 °C for 15 min in the absence of light and then propidium iodide dye was introduced. After 30 min, the flow cytometry device was utilized to calculate the percentage of apoptotic cells.

Realtime PCR

To verify that the serum of vaccinated mice induces apoptosis, the expression of the pro-apoptotic gene BAX and the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2 was evaluated. Initially, RNA extraction was performed from treated cells using the ParsTos extraction kit, adhering to the provided protocol. The RNA concentration was subsequently measured using a nanodrop device. Following this, cDNA synthesis was conducted using the ParsTos company kit, according to the kit’s instructions. For each sample, quantitative RT-qPCR was executed in duplicate utilizing the Real-Time PCR Detection Gentier 96E system, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The analysis was done on BAX2, BCL2, and GAPDH as the reference gene.

Specific primer sequences were utilized for each gene:

BAX primers: Forward: 5´CCCCCGAGAGGTCTTTTT3´ Reverse: 5´GGAGGAAGTCCAATGTCCAG3´.

BCL2 primers: Forward: 5´GAGGCAGGCAGTAGTATGGTG3´ Reverse: 5´AGGATAACGGAGGCTGGACA3´.

GAPDH primers: Forward: 5´GGGAGCCAAAAGGGTCATCA3´ Reverse: 5´AGTGATGGCATGGACTGTGG3´.

To evaluate gene expression levels, the 2^-ΔΔCt method was utilized, following Livak’s formula for calculating fold change. Expression levels were normalized against GAPDH mRNA levels.

Humanized PDX model

To generate a humanized PDX model, tumor samples were obtained from 12 patients with ovarian cancer in 2024 after providing signed informed consent, according to the Helsinki Declaration (https://www.wma.net/policies-post/wma-declaration-of-helsinki/). The clinical characterization of donor patients for PDX models is summarized in Supplementary 2. The ovarian tumors were dissected and minced into 1 mm3 fragments and were grafted subcutaneously into humanized NSG mice (Jackson Laboratory, Sacramento, CA, USA) [9, 10]. These models were approved by the Fraunhofer Institute for Cell Therapy and Immunology IZI, Germany. To validate PDX models, Stroma content, vascularization, differentiation, take rate, PDL-1 expression, TIL expression, and doubling time were analyzed (Supplementary 3).

Then, mice were randomly distributed into 6 groups receiving, (1) empty nanoliposomes, (2) free multi-epitope peptide, (3) free irrelevant peptide, (4) PBS, (5) PVNLME (10 mg/ml), and (6) PVNLME (100 mg/ml). The sample size was n = 6 mice per each group.

To take images and calculate tumor volume, they were anesthetized, killed, and then the cancer mass was removed. The Anesthesia method was isoflurane 5% and the euthanasia method was using an overdose of sodium pentobarbital intravenously under the Guideline for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). The cancer volume was determined until 5 weeks. The tumor volume was calculated as 0.5 × length × width2.

The serum levels of interferon-gamma, interleukin 10, interleukin 6, interleukin 4, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha were checked for 5 weeks in controls and vaccinated mice by ELISA technique. Also, the number of CD8+, Tregs, MDSCs, PD-1(+), CTLA-4(+), and CD45RA(+) CCR7 (-) cells were checked to find TME and Immune Profiling by flow cytometry. Also, the level of CD8, Ki-67, and cleaved caspase-3 markers were analyzed in tumor, liver, and kidney of humanized and non-humanized mice at week 5 to assess toxicity based on histopathology data, IHC.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS and Excel software were used for statistical analysis. One-way ANOVA was employed to compare different study groups with a significance level of P ≤ 0.05. It is important to note that the tests were conducted six times, with each group consisting of 6 mice, and each test was repeated twice.

Results

Bioinformatic analysis

Assessment of P53, WT1, and CA125 gene expression in ovarian cancer

Through analysis of UALCAN data, it was revealed that P53, WT1, and CA125 expression levels were significantly higher in human ovarian cancer tissues and were closely associated with the cancer’s stage (Fig. 2A-C). Higher expression levels of these genes are associated with shorter overall survival, particularly in advanced stages of ovarian cancer (Fig. 2D-F). Figures 2G-I show an expression of target genes across TCGA cancers with tumor and normal samples. The COAD dataset was sourced from the TCGA database and subsequently underwent a normalization process. We then identified the P53, WT1, and CA125 genes utilizing the Reactome database and for validation, we referenced the dataset, which comprises 199 tumor samples and 19 normal samples related to TP53, WT1, and CA125, as illustrated in Fig. 2J-L. The P53, WT1, and CA125(MUC16) play crucial roles as biomarkers in ovarian cancer. Analyzing their levels can offer valuable information on tumor characteristics, patient outlook, and potential treatment options for different types of cancer.

The characterization of final multi-epitope peptide

Nanoliposome characterization

The PVNLME was characterized using DLS, Zetasizer, and TEM techniques (Fig. 4(E and F)). It was observed that the size distribution of the PVNLME ranged from 72 to 198 nm, with an average size of 112 nm and a mean zeta potential of + 30 mV. Moreover, the size distribution of the empty nanoliposomes was from 60 to 150 nm, with an average size of 105 nm and a mean zeta potential of + 30 mV. Additionally, the loading efficiency of the selected peptides into the nanoliposomes was approximately 96% at Day 0 (Fig. 4G). The peptide loading did not significantly decreased during 5 days. Based on the stability test, the PVNLME was stable for 4 weeks (Supplementary 4). Also, the PVNLME was stable at 37 °C, 25 °C and 4 °C. It seems that the storage temperature of -20 °C leads to agglomeration.

The significant increase of interferon-gamma, interleukin 10 and interleukin 6, interleukin 4, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, was seen (P < 0.05) in the PVNLME-vaccinated PDX model (Supplementary 6). Also, a significant increase of CD8 + T cells, Treg cells, and CD45RA(+) CCR7 (-) cells was seen in PVNLME-vaccinated mice during 5 weeks. Discussion

Anti-cancer peptide vaccination is a simple method that uses specific peptides to stimulate and activate tumor-reactive T cells in the body. These peptides are presented on the surface of APC via MHC molecules and can activate CD4 + and CD8 + T cells [11]. In this study, various analyses were first conducted on the protein targets using computational bioinformatics tools. Following the determination of the final characterization of the chosen multi-epitope peptide, we proceeded to the experimental phase of the research. The initial step involved the synthesis of PVNLME. Subsequently, the antibody titer was assessed after administering PVNLME to Balb/c mice. The results demonstrated that both doses of vaccine, 10 mg/ml and 100 mg/ml, generated IgG antibodies against the targeted merged peptides. Furthermore, the antibody level remained significantly high even up to 1:10,000 dilution. Also, the serum levels of IL4, IL6, IL10, TNF, and INF were significantly increased in vaccinated mice compared to the other groups. The results of the MTT assay showed a significant decrease in cell viability along with an increase in the number of apoptotic cells in cancer cells treated with the serum of vaccinated mice. Furthermore, there was a rise in the ratio of BAX to BCL2 expression, which enhances apoptosis through the mitochondrial pathway. In the final phase, the formulated peptide was administered to humanized PDX mice. Remarkably, the volume of the cancerous tumor exhibited a substantial decrease.

Numerous research studies have been carried out in the realm of cancer treatment using immunotherapy. A study was conducted on anti-cancer immunotherapy focused on cancer-derived peptide-epitope cocktail vaccination for patients with refractory cervical and ovarian cancer [12]. Additionally, another study has evaluated the E39 peptide target in patients with ovarian and endometrial cancers. The findings suggest that the combination of E39 with GM-CSF may effectively prevent recurrence in patients at high risk [13]. Also, a study demonstrated that a folate receptor alpha peptide enhanced immune responses in patients with breast and ovarian cancer [14]. Additionally, the feasibility and good clinical effects of dendritic cell-based immunotherapy were assessed for recurrent ovarian cancer [15]. Another study used the PIR peptide to induce targeted immune responses against ovarian cancer [16]. A study identified 159 unique pentapeptides known as CA125. This suggests that immunogenicity may be associated with sequences that show low similarity to the host proteome. These findings could enhance targeted immunological strategies for diagnosing and treating ovarian cancer [17].

The P53 plays a crucial role in regulating DNA repair and cell division and has been found in a significant number of the most aggressive High-Grade Serous Ovarian Cancers (HGSOCs). In humans, TP53 mutations are strongly linked to EOCs (Epithelial Ovarian Cancers). These mutations have been found in 97% of the most aggressive HGSOCs, which make up more than two-thirds of all ovarian cancers [18, 19]. The other target is WT1, which is encoded by the specific gene located on chromosome 11’s short arm in humans. WT1 is a transcription factor with known tumor suppressor effects by regulating the transcription level of different genes which are in charge of the regulation of the cell cycle and apoptosis [20]. Additionally, this protein can also be observed in benign mesothelioma and fallopian tube epithelium [21]. The expression of WT1 in normal tissues is confined to the gonads, uterus, kidneys, mesothelium, and hematopoietic progenitor cells. This restricted expression minimizes the likelihood of side effects associated with immunotherapies aimed at targeting WT1 [22]. CA125 helps to evade anti-tumor immunity, promotes peritoneal expansion, and contributes to carcinogenesis, metastatic invasion, induced motility, and cell invasion. This marker acts as a biomarker or tumor marker. Targeting of this protein shows significant promise for tumor-based immunotherapy due to its prevalence and specificity [8–11, 13].

A study showed that the p53-SLP peptide vaccine combined with Montanide ISA-51 was safe in patients with ovarian cancer. The initial peak response was sustained above pre-immunization levels in 90% of the patients [23]. Interestingly, it has been shown that low doses of cyclophosphamide can be used to boost the immunogenicity of the p53-SLP vaccine in patients with recurrent ovarian cancer [23]. A separate investigation involved twenty-one patients with stage III, and IV of ovarian cancer who had an overexpression of the p53. These patients were divided into two groups and vaccinated with interleukin-2 and p53 peptide. It was found that both vaccination methods yielded similar specific immune responses against the p53 peptide while causing minimal toxicity [24]. One research study focusing on the WT1 gene revealed that the HLA-A*24:02-restricted peptide vaccine designed to target WT1 showed therapeutic potential for ovarian cancer. The WT1 vaccine was successful in triggering antigen-specific cellular and humoral immunity in patients with ovarian cancer who had proven to be resistant to common treatments [25]. A separate study was conducted to assess the safety of the WT1 multivalent peptide vaccine (galinpepimut-S) when combined with nivolumab in patients with WT1-expressing ovarian cancer. The vaccine demonstrated good tolerance and successfully stimulated immune responses [26]. In a particular study, the female mice were subjected to three rounds of immunization using a specific DNA-expressed L1-MUC16 [27]. Compared to DNA or mRNA vaccines, peptide vaccines offer benefits like simple production, affordability, and adaptability to various antigens. However, they come with drawbacks, including potential false reactions, limitations due to MHC compatibility, limited immune response stimulation, and the necessity for adjuvant [28].

In contrast to the majority of vaccines that typically rely on a single peptide, this study took a different approach. To address the low efficacy of single and small peptides, three epitopes from three antigens were simultaneously designed. We think this strategy aims to enhance tumor immune evasion. The selection of the multi-epitope peptide was based on maximum stimulation of the immune system and activation of CD4 + and CD8 + cells. As mentioned before, the multi-epitope peptide was meticulously chosen to target WT1, P53, and CA125. Based on the important role of these markers and analysis of data from bioinformatics resources, we found the multi-epitope peptide was a good selection. The OVCAR-3 cell line, an ovarian serous adenocarcinoma cell line, has been extensively studied and exhibits elevated levels of WT1, P53, and CA125 [29]. To ensure a more thorough and precise analysis, it is advisable to explore other cell lines associated with ovarian cancer. Upon exposure of the cell line to the serum of vaccinated mice, the change in the expression of BAX and BCL2 genes was observed. We analyzed the levels of two key factors, BAX and BCL2, which play crucial roles in the apoptosis process [29–32]. Moving forward, we propose to assess the expression of additional apoptotic genes. Clinical cancer immunotherapy offers a wide range of possibilities for combining immunotherapy with peptide vaccination. Various immunotherapy strategies are presently undergoing clinical trials, such as immunosuppressants, neutralizing antibodies, and targeting inhibitory cytokines. We think that synergistic agents, radiotherapy or chemotherapy, can be paired with immunotherapy, suggesting a potential approach for future research.

Conclusion

Here, a novel peptide cancer vaccine has been developed to trigger both humoral and cellular immune responses against three important protein targets, including WT1, P53, and CA125. We selected a merged peptide and different analyses were done, including antibody titer, cytokine level, cell viability, apoptosis, and gene expression. The efficacy of the PVNLME was investigated on humanized PDX model at the final stage. The results indicated an increase in cytokine levels and antibody titers. Furthermore, the serum of vaccinated mice led to the upregulation of BAX and downregulation of BCL2. We found that cell viability was decreased, apoptotic cell numbers were increased, and tumor volume was reduced in humanized PDX mice. These findings suggest a positive and promising future for the novel peptide vaccine to treat ovarian cancer.
