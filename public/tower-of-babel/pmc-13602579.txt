Epidemiology of food allergy in children under 3 years old in Guangdong: a community cross-sectional study
Authors: Shao-wei Chen, Zi-hui Chen, Ping Wang, Rui Huang, Jin-da Huang, Jie-wen Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence, major allergens, and associated risk factors of food allergy (FA) among children under three years old in Guangdong, China.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in 2023 across three cities (Guangzhou, Foshan, Huizhou) representing varying urbanization levels. Using multistage stratified cluster sampling, 745 children aged 0–35 months were included. Data were collected via caregiver questionnaires and with physician verification when available.ResultsThe overall prevalence of FA was 6.8%, with higher rates observed in urban Guangzhou (9.0%) compared to Foshan (6.5%) and Huizhou (5.0%). Milk (3.1%) and egg (2.1%) were the most common allergens, followed by aquatic products (1.9%). Cutaneous symptoms, particularly rash (60.6%) and pruritus (39.3%), predominated.ConclusionFA is a significant health concern among infants in Guangdong, with distinct allergen profiles and urban-rural disparities. These findings underscore the need for region-specific dietary guidance and individualized infant feeding practices to mitigate allergy risk and address nutritional concerns in allergic children.

Introduction

Food allergy (FA) has become a growing public health issue worldwide [1], particularly affecting young children [2, 3]. In China, emerging evidence suggests a rising trend in FA among the pediatric population, especially those under five years of age [4, 5]. Regional data from Guangdong Province indicate that FA prevalence in preschool children is approximately 4.0% [6], highlighting a need for further investigation into this specific geographic setting.

Common food allergens exhibit regional and age-specific variations. While shellfish such as shrimp and crab are commonly reported allergens in older children and adults in Guangdong, emerging evidence suggests that milk and egg may dominate in early childhood. In addition to genetic factors—like family history of FA or allergic rhinitis—environmental determinants such as dietary patterns, early antibiotic exposure, and indoor air pollution may be implicated in FA development.

Despite these observations, community-based studies focusing specifically on children aged 0–35 months are scarce [7]. This age group is characterized by physiological vulnerability: immature gut barriers and developing immune systems may heighten susceptibility to food allergens [3, 8]. Consequently, dietary restrictions in this population pose potential risks for nutrient deficiencies, including impaired calcium absorption [8, 9]. The present study aims to address this gap by investigating the epidemiology, predominant allergens, and associated risk factors of FA among young children in Guangdong communities.

Methods

Study design

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in 2023 across three prefecture-level cities in Guangdong Province, China: Guangzhou, Foshan, and Huizhou. These sites were selected to represent varying degrees of urbanization: Guangzhou exemplifies a major metropolitan area; Foshan represents a medium-sized urban center; and Huizhou includes extensive rural and peri-urban regions.This study was part of the China National Food Consumption Survey, supervised by the National Institute for Food Safety Risk Assessment, China. A multistage stratified cluster sampling methodology was utilized. Within each city, one district (county or township) was selected as the survey site, targeting permanent residents (residing for at least 6 months) with children under 3 years old. The sampling followed a strict stratified structure to meet the study’s requirements: a minimum of 240 children per city, with a gender ratio of 1:1 (120 males and 120 females). The sample was equally distributed across three age groups: 0–11 months (80 children, including 40 aged 0–5 months and 40 aged 6–11 months), 12–23 months (80 children), and 24–35 months (80 children), at a ratio of 1:1:1.

Data collection was conducted through household visits, during which trained interviewers administered a structured questionnaire to parents or primary caregivers. The questionnaire gathered information on demographic characteristics, feeding history, symptom profiles of suspected food allergy reactions, specific allergenic foods, and family history of allergic diseases. Physician-diagnosed food allergies when available, were further verified through medical record review. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating families prior to enrollment.

Study population

The study focused on children aged 0–35 months residing in the three target cities. The sample size was determined based on an estimated FA prevalence of 5–7% among young children in southern China. Assuming a design effect of 1.5 and a margin of error of 3%, a minimum of 240 participants per city was required, resulting in a total target sample of 720 infants and toddlers.

Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) children aged 0–35 months at the time of survey; and (2) informed consent provided by a parent or guardian. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of congenital metabolic disorders, severe immunodeficiency, or chronic gastrointestinal diseases unrelated to FA; and (2) incomplete or ambiguous responses to core questionnaire items regarding FA diagnosis or symptom description.

The final analytical sample comprised 745 participants after excluding responses with missing or inconsistent data, still exceeding the minimum required sample size of 720 and ensuring adequate statistical power.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was developed and nationally validated by the National Institute for Food Safety Risk Assessment, China, for the China National Food Consumption Survey. The identical standardized instrument was used without modification in Guangdong Province as part of the national surveillance program. Data collection encompassed four core domains: (1) Demographic and birth characteristics: Included infant age, sex, birth weight, birth length, gestational age at birth, parental education level, family income. (2) Feeding history: Assessed breastfeeding practices, age at introduction of complementary foods, types of initial complementary foods, and consumption of different formula types.(3) Allergic symptom profile: Parental reports of suspected FA reactions were captured, detailing symptoms across organ systems: cutaneous, gastrointestinal, respiratory, and other systemic reactions. The time of symptom onset post-food ingestion and duration were also recorded. (4) Potential food allergens: Parents identified specific foods suspected of triggering reactions. The list emphasized common allergens in early childhood (cow’s milk, eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, wheat) and incorporated regionally prevalent foods in Guangdong, such as shrimp, crab, shellfish, and mango.

Definition of food allergy

FA was defined based on caregiver-reported adverse reactions to specific foods in children under three years of age. Physician-diagnosed cases were verified through medical records when available. A positive response triggered detailed documentation of the suspected allergen, age at first occurrence, method of diagnosis (physician-confirmed or caregiver-identified), and allergic manifestations spanning cutaneous, respiratory, gastrointestinal, systemic symptoms and etc.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using R version 4.4.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Continuous variables were assessed for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test and expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Group comparisons utilized the Chi-square test for categorical variables, while the independent samples t-test was applied for continuous variables meeting normality assumptions. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Characteristic of included infants

A total of 745 infants were enrolled, including 51 with FA and 694 without FA. Baseline characteristics showed no significant differences in age (18.6 ± 8.2 vs. 16.7 ± 10.2 months, p = 0.195), body length (80.4 ± 8.5 vs. 77.9 ± 12.3 cm, p = 0.156), or birth weight (3.1 ± 0.6 vs. 3.2 ± 0.5 kg, p = 0.891) between groups. Sex distribution was comparable (56.9% male in FA vs. 49.0% in Non-FA, p = 0.278), and most infants were of Han ethnicity (98.8% overall, p = 0.610). Geographic distribution varied slightly, with 43.1% of FA infants from Guangzhou versus 32.1% in Non-FA (p = 0.215). Notably, complementary food introduction timing differed significantly (p = 0.002) —56.9% of FA infants started at 1–6 months versus 43.2% in Non-FA. Additionally, follow-up formula consumption was higher in FA infants (78.4% vs. 64.6%, p = 0.044) and sleep duration was shorter (12.5 ± 1.3 vs. 13.1 ± 2.2 h/day, p = 0.034) compared to Non-FA infants. Prevalence of food allergy among infants

The overall prevalence of FA among infants in Guangdong Province was 6.8% (51/745), with notable variations across demographic and geographic subgroups. Male infants exhibited a higher FA prevalence (7.9%, 29/369) compared to females (5.9%, 22/376). Age-specific analysis revealed a marked increase in FA prevalence after 6 months of age, peaking at 9.7% (14/145) in the 7 ~ 12 month group, followed by 8.9% (22/248) in the 13 ~ 24 month group. Infants aged 1 ~ 6 month group demonstrated the lowest prevalence (0.7%, 1/153). Symptoms across allergenic food categories

Cutaneous manifestations were the most frequently reported symptoms across all major allergenic food categories. As demonstrated in Fig. 3 and the accompanying table, rash was the predominant symptom, comprising 60.6% of all documented reactions, followed by pruritus at 39.3%.

Notable variations in symptom patterns were observed among different food allergens. Aquatic products predominantly elicited cutaneous reactions, with rash representing 58.8% of cases and pruritus 29.4%. Additionally, aquatic products were associated with 11.8% of orolabial symptoms and 5.9% of edema cases. Milk allergy presented with a broader symptom spectrum: while rash (45.5%) and pruritus (24.2%) remained common, a substantial proportion of cases involved diarrhea (18.2%) and vomiting (6.1%). Egg allergies similarly showed high rates of cutaneous symptoms—rash accounted for 39.3% and pruritus for 28.6%—though vomiting was also notable, occurring in 14.3% of cases. In contrast, nut and seed allergies displayed a distinct profile with a higher relative frequency of edema (17.9%) compared to other categories, alongside rash (46.4%) and pruritus (32.1%).

Discussion

This community-based cross-sectional study reveals a 6.8% prevalence of physician-diagnosed food allergy among infants and toddlers aged 0–35 months in Guangdong, China. The findings highlight milk and egg as the predominant allergens in this young cohort, which aligns with typical IgE-mediated allergy patterns [10] in early childhood but contrasts with the broader Chinese population where seafood allergens dominate. The observed peak in allergy prevalence between 7 and 24 months of age coincides with complementary feeding periods, suggesting that dietary transitions may be associated with immune sensitization. Importantly, variations in geographic prevalence, notably higher in urban Guangzhou, may reflect environmental or dietary heterogeneity across regions. These data underscore the necessity for regionally tailored dietary guidance and early allergen introduction strategies in infant health programs. The symptom profile, predominantly cutaneous, supports the clinical recognition pattern of IgE-mediated allergies and emphasizes the need for caregiver education to identify early signs of reactivity [10, 11]. From a public health perspective, this study contributes foundational data for monitoring allergy trends and formulating preventive policies specific to southern China’s pediatric population.

The findings from this investigation demonstrate a discernible disparity in food allergy prevalence between infants in Guangdong and previously reported figures for broader Chinese pediatric populations. While the observed rate of 6.8% is lower than that documented in some Western nations [12–14], it exceeds the 4.81% self-reported prevalence for Chinese children aged 0–5 years [5, 15] and the 4.0% rate specifically reported among preschool children in Guangdong [6]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors, including methodological differences in diagnosis, true increases in allergy rates over time, or unique regional dietary exposures in early life. The higher prevalence in urban centers like Guangzhou may reflect environmental influences, such as altered dietary habits, hygiene practices, or microbial exposures [16], which have been proposed as modifiers of immune development.

A striking observation in this cohort was the prominence of milk and egg allergies, which contrasts with the allergen profile commonly seen in older children and adults across China, where shellfish and seafood allergies dominate. This divergence aligns with the natural history of IgE-mediated allergies [11], where sensitivities to milk and egg often manifest in infancy and may be outgrown in later childhood, whereas shellfish and nut allergies tend to persist. The high prevalence of milk allergy (3.1%) underscores its role as a primary allergen in early life, potentially linked to the early introduction of formula. Although early introduction of complementary feeding (at 1–6 months) was significantly associated with food allergy development, the extremely low absolute number of cases (n = 1) in the 1–6 month age group limits the interpretability of this association and suggests the need for caution in generalizing this finding. This observation aligns with current hypotheses proposing that both very early and delayed introduction of allergens may confer risk, underscoring the importance of tailored, infant-specific dietary guidance.

Symptom patterns further illuminate the clinical presentation of food allergies in this young population. The predominance of cutaneous manifestations—rash (60.6%) and pruritus (39.3%)—is consistent with typical IgE-mediated reactions [11] and aligns with global data on infant food allergies. However, variations were noted across allergen categories: gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea and vomiting were more frequently associated with milk and egg allergies, whereas nut and seed reactions involved a higher proportion of edema. This suggests that the nature of the allergen may influence the immunologic pathway and subsequent clinical expression, which has implications for diagnosis and management. The absence of severe anaphylactic reactions in this cohort may reflect under-reporting, the young age of participants, or effective early avoidance by caregivers after initial mild reactions.

Several risk factors with potential associations were identified in this population. The higher use of follow-up formula among food allergy infants may indicate reverse causality, where parents switch to specialized formulas following allergic reactions, or it may suggest that certain formula compositions contribute to sensitization. Shorter sleep duration in affected infants could be either a consequence of allergic discomfort (e.g., pruritus disrupting sleep) or a marker of broader immune dysregulation influenced by sleep patterns. Genetic predisposition [17–19], as indicated by family history of allergic diseases in first-degree relatives, likely inter-played with these dietary and lifestyle factors, culminating in the development of food allergies.

From a public health perspective, these findings highlight the growing burden of food allergies among infants in southern China and underscore the need for region-specific preventive strategies. The higher prevalence in urban areas like Guangzhou suggests that urbanization and modern lifestyles may be driving factors, possibly through changes in diet, environmental exposures, or microbial diversity. The critical window for complementary feeding (around 6 months of age) represents an opportunity for targeted intervention, such as educating parents on the appropriate timing and selection of initial foods to balance tolerance induction and allergy prevention. Additionally, the nutritional risks associated with unnecessary food avoidance—particularly calcium and vitamin deficiencies in milk-allergic infants [20–22]—must be addressed through dietary guidance and possibly supplementation to ensure optimal growth and bone health.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, despite physician-confirmed diagnoses, uniform oral food challenges were not performed, potentially leading to inaccurate estimation of FA prevalence. Third, caregiver self-reported data may introduce recall bias. Fourth, no multivariable regression was conducted, and the sample size of younger age groups was relatively small. Fifth, the sample was only from Guangdong Province, restricting national generalizability.Additionally, this study adopted a multistage stratified cluster sampling design without population weighting (consistent with the national survey protocol). As this was not designed as a full provincial representative survey, strict population weighting was not applied. Therefore, the results may not reflect the overall provincial prevalence, and generalization beyond study sites should be cautious.Although the questionnaire was nationally validated, caregiver-reported reactions are subjective and do not equate to confirmed clinical diagnosis. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to track the natural course of food allergies in this population, identify predictive biomarkers, and evaluate interventions aimed at preventing sensitization through dietary modification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides timely evidence on the prevalence, triggers, and risk factors associated with food allergy among infants in Guangdong, highlighting possible positive implications of increased awareness, timely diagnosis, and tailored nutritional management in this susceptible age group.
