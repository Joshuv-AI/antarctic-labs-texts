Refractory hypokalemia in a patient on levetiracetam: An under-recognized clinical association
Authors: M. Yathwin Kanagavel, Venkata Shashank Davuluri, V. Vivek
Published: 2026

Levetiracetam is a pyrrolidone derivative, a second-generation antiepileptic medication that is used for the treatment of multiple seizure types, including focal, generalized, and myoclonic seizures, in addition to seizures associated with head injuries.[1] Levetiracetam’s mechanism of action involves binding to synaptic vesicle protein 2A (SVP2A), inhibiting calcium release from voltage-gated calcium channels, and decreasing the release of neurotransmitters from synaptic vesicles like gamma-amino butyric acid (GABA) and glutamine.[12] Levetiracetam is a widely preferred antiepileptic drug for its acceptable side effect profile and limited influence on the cytochrome P450 system, minimizing the risk of drug interactions.[12] We report an elderly male patient who developed drug-induced hypokalemia following prophylactic levetiracetam administration.

A 56-year-old man was admitted with severe traumatic brain injury following a road traffic accident, and was diagnosed with grade-3 diffuse axonal injury. He was initiated on levetiracetam (20 mg/kg/day) for seizure prophylaxis due to a suspected seizure reported at the referring center, and was gradually started on enteral feeds. Four days later, the patient developed hypokalaemia with serum potassium 2.9 mmol/L. Serum potassium at time of admission was 3.6 mmol/L. Despite intravenous and nasogastric potassium replacement, potassium values fluctuated between 2.8 and 3.1 mmol/L over the next 10 days. He subsequently developed abdominal distention and paralytic ileus, after which he was switched to parenteral nutrition. ABG showed normal pH with hypokalemia. Neurological status remained unchanged during this period. A comprehensive evaluation for hypokalemia, including renal function, thyroid profile, cortisol, and gastrointestinal loss markers, was unremarkable. Spot urine potassium was 52 mEq/L, suggesting renal potassium wasting. While serial serum magnesium levels remained within normal range, spot urine magnesium was 14 mg/dL, indicating urinary magnesium loss. Magnesium supplementation was initiated to prevent hypomagnesaemia. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain and spine revealed no structural abnormalities contributing to autonomic dysfunction. Electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated no arrhythmia or signs associated with hypokalemia. After nephrology review, the patient was started on spironolactone on day 18 without significant improvement in serum potassium levels. Given the persistent hypokalemia, absence of alternative etiologies, and temporal association with levetiracetam therapy, a drug-induced electrolyte disturbance was considered. Levetiracetam was discontinued on day-24 and replaced with lacosamide. The potassium levels normalized within 48 h of drug withdrawal and remained stable without further supplementation [Table 1]. The patient’s ileus gradually resolved, and he tolerated enteral feeds by day 31. He was subsequently discharged on day 40 with serum potassium 3.9 mmol/L.

Although levetiracetam is widely regarded as a safe antiepileptic agent with minimal systemic side effects, reports of levetiracetam-induced hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia have been described, particularly among elderly patients.[34] A real-world active comparator retrospective cohort study on levetiracetam-induced hypokalemia suggested a possible association between hypokalemia and levetiracetam usage.[2] The precise pathophysiological mechanism underlying levetiracetam-induced hypokalemia remains unclear. Proposed mechanisms, including transcellular potassium shifts and drug-induced renal tubular dysfunction causing excessive urinary potassium excretion, remain speculative.[135] While direct renal tubular toxicity has not been observed with levetiracetam, reports of renal electrolyte wasting in isolated cases raise the possibility of a functional tubular defect affecting distal potassium handling rather than overt tubular damage. Although SVP2A expression is predominantly neuronal, central modulation of neurotransmitter release may influence autonomic outflow and neurohumoral pathways involved in renal electrolyte regulation. Intracellular calcium signaling, while not directly acting on renal ion channels, plays an important regulatory role in distal nephron potassium channels, and alterations may cause functional potassium wasting. Serum magnesium, though within normal range in our patient, is an insensitive marker of total body magnesium, and ongoing renal magnesium loss may occur despite normal circulating levels. Such functional magnesium deficiency is a recognized contributor to refractory hypokalemia due to impaired inhibition of renal potassium secretion through ROMK channels. While magnesium wasting cannot be established as a primary abnormality in this case, it may have contributed to the persistence of hypokalemia. Although neuroimaging did not reveal any structural abnormalities associated with autonomic dysfunction, functional dysregulation cannot be excluded.

The relative specificity of hypokalemia reported with levetiracetam suggests a drug-specific rather than a class-wide antiepileptic effect. Other antiepileptic drugs, such as phenytoin, a voltage-gated sodium channel blocker, and sodium valproate, which enhances GABAergic neurotransmission, have not been associated with hypokalemia. Causality assessment using the Naranjo scale yielded a score of 4, classifying the association as possible. The temporal relationship, objective evidence of renal potassium wasting, and rapid resolution following drug withdrawal support a probable association under WHO-UMC criteria. Major confounding factors include the severe traumatic brain injury, which could independently predispose to electrolyte disturbances through stress-mediated catecholamine release and renal dysfunction, and autonomic dysregulation.

Given the extensive use of levetiracetam in neurosurgical and critical care settings, this adverse effect might be under-recognized. Persistent or unexplained hypokalaemia in patients undergoing levetiracetam therapy should prompt consideration of drug-induced renal potassium wasting. Early detection can prevent complications, including ileus, arrhythmias, and prolonged hospitalisation. Further studies are required to better characterize the incidence, mechanism, and predisposing factors for levetiracetam-associated electrolyte imbalances.
