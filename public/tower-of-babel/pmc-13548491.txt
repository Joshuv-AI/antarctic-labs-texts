Integrated analysis of single-cell transcriptome identifies a glial-neurovascular signaling trajectory in brain repair after ischemia
Authors: Hanyou Liu, Zhenwei Li, Ruxu Geng, Lingui Gu, Xinjie Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIschemic stroke triggers complex multicellular responses across neurons, glia, and the neurovascular unit, but the temporal coordination among these cell types remains incompletely understood. In this study, we integrated five publicly available mouse single-cell RNA sequencing datasets spanning the acute, subacute, and recovery phases after middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), together with complementary rat single-nucleus RNA-seq data, to establish a time-resolved transcriptomic framework of post-ischemic brain repair.ResultsIntegrative single-cell analysis identified candidate multicellular communication patterns linking microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocyte lineage cells, endothelial cells, and neurons during post-ischemic remodeling. Microglia exhibited an early transition from inflammatory activation toward lipid metabolic and neuroprotective programs. Oligodendrocyte progenitor cells activated maturation programs associated with remyelination, while the neurovascular unit underwent coordinated remodeling characterized by dynamic signaling interactions among astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, and neurons. Background

Ischemic stroke, a leading cause of death and long term disability worldwide, places a substantial burden on individuals and health care systems [1, 2]. Beyond the acute vascular event, ischemic stroke elicits interconnected injury and repair responses in glial, neuronal, and vascular cells in the injured brain [3, 4]. The brain experiences damage from multiple factors following occlusion, including excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, inflammation, and disruption of the relationship between neurons and vessels. Recovery from ischemic injury requires not only neuronal survival but also coordinated activity among glial, vascular, and neuronal cell populations [5, 6]. Understanding how these distinct populations communicate and adapt over time during injury and repair provides a basis for explaining the condition and for developing strategies that provide treatment.

Recent developments in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have made it possible to study brain remodeling after ischemic stroke at the level of single cells, and this allows us to describe different cell types and their states in a detailed and unbiased way. Studies using scRNA-seq show several responses in the injured brain: microglia quickly turn on inflammatory and phagocytic programs after ischemia; astrocytes change from reactive states to reparative states; oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) proliferate and differentiate to promote remyelination; and both endothelial cells and neurons exhibit transcriptional plasticity associated with vascular regeneration and synaptic remodeling [7–11]. These findings show that recovery of brain function depends on many cell types working together, but understanding of these processes is still incomplete. Most single-cell datasets cover only short time windows, usually within the first few days after ischemia. Studies based on these datasets provide separate snapshots of cell responses instead of a continuous picture from early inflammation to later repair.

To address these limitations, a framework that follows cellular changes over time and brings together data from different studies and species is needed. In this study, we combine several publicly available mouse single-cell RNA sequencing datasets that cover the acute, subacute, and recovery phases after middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from rat models was included as a complement. After quality control and correction for batch effects, a unified single-cell map was built, which includes all major cell types and their changes over time. This integrative approach enables systematic exploration of transcriptional programs and intercellular signaling networks underlying inflammation resolution, metabolic adaptation, and neurovascular remodeling in a temporally continuous manner.

This work builds a detailed map of the ischemic brain which follows cell changes over time and combines data from different datasets and species. This map shows how the states of microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, neurons, and endothelial cells change, and how communication between these cells evolves during brain repair. It gives a new view of how multiple cell types work together to support recovery. In addition, we combine time-resolved gene expression data with analyses of ligand and receptor pairs and regulatory networks. This connects events inside cells with communication between cells and provides an integrated picture of recovery after stroke.

Based on this, we suggest that recovery after cerebral ischemia is controlled by molecular programs that overlap in time rather than by clearly separate steps. We also think that resolution of inflammation, activation of metabolic and repair pathways, and restoration of vascular and brain tissue integrity are closely linked parts of one continuous process. The goal of this study is to build a map that follows, over time, how major brain cell types respond to ischemia and to identify ligand-receptor signals and gene programs that drive the shift from inflammatory states to reparative states.

Methods

Data integration and batch correction

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from five publicly available mouse MCAO datasets were obtained from the GEO database (GSE174574, GSE197731, GSE210986, GSE227651, and GSE261494) [7–11]. Detailed experimental metadata for the included public mouse MCAO datasets are summarized in Supplementary Table S1. For each dataset, Seurat objects were created and combined into a unified dataset. Cells with fewer than 200 or more than 6,000 detected genes, or with over 10% mitochondrial transcript content, were excluded to remove low-quality cells. Gene expression data were normalized, variable features were identified, and values were scaled following the standard Seurat workflow [12]. Quality control metrics were visualized as violin plots using Seurat’s VlnPlot() function to confirm the consistency of filtering thresholds across datasets.

Principal component analysis was performed for dimensionality reduction. The number of principal components used for downstream integration, dimensionality reduction, and clustering was selected based on inspection of the ElbowPlot of the leading principal components. The top 30 principal components were used to generate a two-dimensional representation by uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). To minimize inter-dataset batch effects, Harmony integration was applied using the dataset identifier as the grouping variable. The Harmony-corrected embeddings were used for dimensionality reduction, UMAP/t-SNE visualization, shared nearest-neighbor graph construction, clustering, subclustering, and cell-type annotation [13]. For computational feasibility and balanced comparison, a stratified subset of cells was sampled across dataset and cell-type combinations. Dataset-level integration Local Inverse Simpson’s Index (iLISI) was used to assess cross-dataset mixing, while silhouette-based metrics, including batch average silhouette width (batch ASW) score and cell-type ASW, were used to evaluate reduction of dataset-driven separation and preservation of biological cell-type structure.

For downstream expression-level analyses, including differential expression analysis, transcription factor activity inference, and CellChat-based ligand–receptor inference, raw count matrices or log-normalized RNA expression matrices were used as specified in the corresponding sections.

Cell-type annotation and clustering

Following batch correction, highly variable genes were identified using the variance-stabilizing transformation method, and the top 1,500 most variable features were retained for downstream analysis. Dimensionality reduction was performed using Harmony-corrected embeddings as input for both t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and UMAP.

A shared nearest-neighbor (SNN) graph was constructed based on the first 30 Harmony dimensions, and unsupervised clustering was carried out using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution parameter of 0.5. This approach resulted in the identification of transcriptionally distinct clusters across all datasets and time points: sham (non-ischemic control), 1 day, 3 days, 7 days, and 14 days after MCAO.

Cluster-specific marker genes were determined by differential expression analysis comparing each cluster to all remaining cells using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with thresholds of |log2 (fold change)| > 0.25, adjusted p < 0.05, and expression in > 25% of cells. Only genes upregulated in each cluster were retained.

Representative marker genes were visualized using heatmaps, DotPlots, and violin plots to assess their expression patterns across clusters. Based on canonical lineage markers and previously published murine brain single-cell references, clusters were annotated as microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, neurons, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, and other immune cell types [14–17]. Cell-type annotation was performed using a hierarchical strategy integrating unsupervised clustering, canonical lineage markers, cluster-enriched genes, and comparison with published murine brain single-cell references. For closely related populations, including microglia/macrophage and vascular-associated populations, annotation was based on the overall marker-expression pattern rather than a single marker gene. For lineage-level analyses, each major cell class was subsetted and reclustered using the Seurat workflow. The resulting subclusters were interpreted according to their cluster-enriched genes, canonical lineage-stage markers, temporal distribution, and functional enrichment results.

Differential expression and temporal trend analysis

To identify time-dependent transcriptional changes within each cell subtype, raw UMI counts were aggregated into pseudobulk expression profiles at the level of individual datasets and time points (sham, 1d, 3d, 7d, and 14d after MCAO). This aggregation was performed at the dataset–time point level because animal-level identifiers were not uniformly available across all integrated public datasets. Differential expression analysis was performed using these pseudobulk count-derived expression profiles. Cell subtype–time point groups containing fewer than ten cells were excluded to ensure robustness. For each subtype, genes were filtered using expression-based criteria and normalized with the TMM method in edgeR [18]. The normalized pseudobulk count data were then analyzed using the limma-voom framework, in which voom was used to model the mean–variance relationship of count-derived logCPM values before linear modeling. The primary design modeled the ordered time variable, and multiple biologically meaningful contrasts were evaluated, including combined phase comparisons (Early [1–3d] vs. 0d, Late [7–14d] vs. 0d). Because dataset and time point were partially confounded in the integrated public datasets, especially for time points represented by a single dataset, these analyses were interpreted as identifying time-associated transcriptional patterns rather than definitive dataset-independent differential expression effects. Statistical significance was determined using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, and only genes with an adjusted p < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| ≥ log₂(1.5) were retained for further interpretation.

To capture temporal expression patterns beyond pairwise contrasts, pseudobulk logCPM values were averaged for each cell subtype at each time point and converted to gene-wise z-scores across the five temporal groups. The early phase was defined as Day 1–3 and the late phase as Day 7–14. Genes were classified as EarlyUp when the early-phase z-score exceeded both baseline and late-phase scores by at least 0.4, and as LateUp when the late-phase z-score exceeded both baseline and early-phase scores by at least 0.4. Monotonic temporal trends were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation between z-scored expression and ordered post-ischemic time; genes with rho ≥ 0.6 or rho ≤ − 0.6 were classified as MonotonicUp or MonotonicDown, respectively. A transient Day 3-enriched pattern was assigned when the Day 3 z-score exceeded the other phase-level scores by at least 0.3.

Gene ontology enrichment and functional annotation

Functional annotation was performed to characterize the biological processes associated with the time-dependent gene expression changes identified in each cell subtype. For representative gene modules, such as those classified as EarlyUp or LateUp, enrichment analyses of Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were conducted using the clusterProfiler package [19, 20]. The background gene set for each analysis comprised all genes expressed within the corresponding subtype.

GO and KEGG overrepresentation tests were carried out with Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment to control the false discovery rate, and pathways with an adjusted q value < 0.05 and a minimum of five genes per term were considered significant. Enriched pathways were summarized for each subtype and temporal category, highlighting cellular programs linked to immune activation, oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, angiogenesis, and neuronal signaling depending on cell identity and post-ischemic stage.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

To characterize transcriptional state continua and asynchronous cell-state transitions within major lineages, pseudotime analysis was performed using Monocle3 [21]. This analysis was used to organize cells according to inferred transcriptional similarity and activation-state relationships, rather than to reconstruct real chronological post-injury time or lineage tracing. Because cells collected at the same post-ischemic time point may occupy different activation, stress-response, or repair-associated states, pseudotime analysis was applied to capture asynchronous cell-state transitions within each lineage.

Cells from selected major lineages, including microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, were analyzed separately after subsetting from the integrated Seurat object. Raw UMI count matrices, cell metadata, gene annotations, Seurat-derived low-dimensional embeddings, and cell-type annotations were incorporated into Monocle3 cell data sets for trajectory inference and visualization. Principal graph learning was then performed to infer lineage-specific cell-state continua. Root states were assigned based on the earliest relevant post-ischemic or homeostatic cell populations, supported by canonical lineage marker expression. For oligodendrocyte-lineage cells, OPC-enriched cells at the earliest post-ischemic stage were used to orient the trajectory. Trajectory-associated genes were identified using graph-based testing in Monocle3, and genes with q < 0.05 were considered significantly associated with the inferred trajectory. The major organization of the inferred trajectories was also inspected under alternative graph-learning settings when applicable to ensure that the observed cell-state arrangement was not driven by a single parameter choice. Trajectories were visualized using plot_cells() to show the organization of identified subclusters along inferred cell-state continua.

Cell-cell communication analysis

To investigate intercellular signaling dynamics across different cell types after MCAO, intercellular communication networks were inferred using the CellChat framework [22]. CellChat analysis was performed using log-normalized RNA expression matrices from each time group (sham, 1d, 3d, 7d, and 14d), with cell-type or subcluster annotations derived from the integrated Seurat object. The population.size = TRUE setting was used in the primary CellChat analysis to retain the contribution of post-ischemic changes in cell abundance to the population-level communication landscape. Communication probabilities were calculated based on the CellChatDB.mouse ligand-receptor interaction database. Only ligand-receptor pairs expressed in more than 10% of cells within both the sending and receiving populations were considered. Communication events involving cell groups with fewer than 10 cells were filtered to avoid unstable ligand–receptor predictions from sparsely represented populations.

For each time point, the global communication probability matrix was computed and summarized at the signaling-pathway level. Interactions were quantified by their communication strength, defined as the average probability of significant ligand-receptor engagement between sender and receiver cell populations. To assess temporal alterations in predicted communication activity, communication networks were compared across time points using normalized interaction weights, thereby identifying stage-specific gains or losses of predicted cell-cell communication following ischemic injury.

Representative pathways showing pronounced temporal variation, such as TNF and PSAP signaling among microglia and astrocytes, LAMININ signaling from astrocytes to neurons, SEMA6 signaling from microglia to neurons, PROS signaling from microglia to oligodendrocytes, and EGF signaling from endothelial cells to neurons, were selected for detailed visualization. Network and heatmap plots were used to depict the predicted directionality and relative strength of inferred communication events.

Transcription factor activity analysis

Transcription factor (TF) activity was inferred from gene expression data using the VIPER algorithm together with the DoRothEA mouse regulon database [23]. The dorothea_mm dataset was used to define TF-target interactions, and only relationships with confidence levels A-C were retained. The resulting regulon was converted into a VIPER-compatible format for single-cell analysis.

Log-normalized expression matrices were used as input after removing genes with zero expression across all cells. VIPER was run with the scale method, which estimates relative TF activity based on the enrichment of each TF’s target genes within the transcriptional profile of individual cells. The resulting TF activity scores were used for quantitative comparison of regulatory programs across cell populations and time points.

Complementary rat single-nucleus RNA-seq analysis

To assess whether selected transcriptional and intercellular communication patterns identified in the mouse MCAO analysis were also detectable in an independent rodent dataset, we analyzed single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) data from rat MCAO models. Two datasets were included: a publicly available dataset (GSE250245; Sham and Day 2) [24] and our newly generated rat dataset (Sham and Day 21). Nuclei were isolated from ischemic and contralateral hemispheres using a detergent-based protocol followed by fluorescence-activated nuclei sorting (FANS). Libraries were prepared using the 10x Genomics Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3 v3.1 platform and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Downstream analysis was performed in Seurat following the same preprocessing and integration workflow as used for mouse scRNA-seq data. Quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and batch correction were applied identically. Cell-type annotation was based on canonical marker genes and cross-species correspondence with mouse reference clusters, identifying major neuronal, glial, and vascular populations.

Data visualization and statistical analysis

All data analyses and visualizations were performed in R. Dimensional reduction and clustering plots were generated using the visualization functions implemented in Seurat, and custom plots such as violin, dot, and feature plots were created with ggplot2 [25]. Heatmaps showing gene expression, transcription factor activity, or pathway enrichment were produced using ComplexHeatmap [26]. Enrichment results and cell-cell communication networks were visualized with the plotting modules of clusterProfiler and CellChat, respectively [19, 22]. The main R packages used in this study included Seurat v4.3.0.1, harmony v0.1.1, CellChat v1.6.1, monocle3 v1.3.4, clusterProfiler v4.8.3, limma v3.56.2, edgeR v3.42.4, ggplot2 v3.4.3, and ComplexHeatmap v2.16.0.

Statistical analyses were conducted using two-sided tests, and p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Statistical significance was defined as FDR < 0.05 throughout all analyses.

Results

Integrated single-cell landscape of ischemic brain

To obtain a comprehensive cellular atlas of the ischemic brain, five publicly available single-cell RNA-seq datasets (GSE174574, GSE197731, GSE210986, GSE227651, and GSE261494) covering sham-operated and post-MCAO mouse models (1, 3, 7, and 14 days after surgery) were integrated for downstream analysis (Fig. 1A) [7–11]. After stringent quality control, cells with low gene counts or excessive mitochondrial gene expression were excluded (Fig S1A). After quality control and analytical filtering, 147,387 cells from five mouse MCAO scRNA-seq datasets were retained for downstream integration. The number of cells before final filtering and the final number of cells retained for each dataset are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

To justify the dimensionality used for integration and clustering, we examined the ElbowPlot of the leading principal components. The curve flattened after approximately the 20th-30th principal components, supporting the use of the first 30 principal components for downstream analyses (Fig. S1B). Batch effects across datasets were reduced by applying a cross-dataset integration method that aligned cells from different studies in a shared space. After correction, cells showed improved cross-dataset alignment while retaining the major biological cell-type structure (Fig. 1B). To further quantify batch correction performance, we compared integration metrics before and after Harmony correction. Dataset iLISI increased from 1.45 to 2.53 after Harmony correction, indicating improved cross-dataset mixing. Batch ASW changed only modestly, whereas cell-type ASW increased from 0.096 to 0.149, suggesting preservation of major biological cell-type structure after integration (Fig. S1C). Given the imbalance in dataset size and composition, these metrics were interpreted as supporting improved rather than complete batch correction.

The integrated UMAP visualization revealed distinct cellular clusters corresponding to major brain cell types, including endothelial cells (EC), microglia (MG), oligodendrocytes (OL), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPC), neurons (Nrn), macrophages (Mac), astrocytes (AS), neutrophils (Ntr), fibroblasts (FB), T cells (T), erythrocytes (RBC), and B cells (B) (Fig. 1C). The major cell-type assignments were further supported by a marker-gene DotPlot showing the expression frequency and scaled average expression of representative lineage-associated genes across the annotated populations (Fig. S1D). Temporal mapping of these clusters across different stages after MCAO illustrated dynamic cellular remodeling in the ischemic brain (Fig. 1D). Analysis of cell proportions showed strong remodeling of the cellular environment after MCAO (Fig. 1E). Microglia and infiltrating macrophages were markedly increased during the acute phase (days 1–3), consistent with rapid activation of innate immunity and recruitment of myeloid cells to ischemic regions. At the same time, endothelial cells and oligodendrocyte lineage cells were transiently reduced, in line with vascular damage and loss of myelin in the early phase of injury. At later stages (days 7–14), endothelial and oligodendrocyte populations showed relative recovery, indicating the onset of vascular repair and remyelination during tissue recovery. Neuronal and astrocytic fractions showed only moderate changes over time, suggesting context-dependent adjustment of neural and glial networks during the subacute and recovery phases.

Temporal activation states of microglia

Microglia are the main immune cells in the central nervous system and contribute to both injury and repair after MCAO [27]. To investigate the transcriptional heterogeneity and dynamic activation states of microglia after ischemic injury, pseudotime trajectory analysis was applied to microglial cells from the integrated MCAO dataset (Fig. 2A). Along the pseudotime axis, microglial cells were organized along an inferred activation-state continuum. Six transcriptionally distinct microglial subclusters (MG1-MG6) were identified and mapped onto the trajectory, representing a gradual transition from homeostatic to proliferative and reactive states (Fig. 2B).

The composition of each subcluster varied substantially along pseudotime and across time points after MCAO (Fig. 2C, D). Representative marker gene expression confirmed the transcriptional identity of each microglial subcluster (Fig. 2E). MG1 represented homeostatic microglia, which were mainly found in sham samples, showed high expression of P2ry12, Tmem119, and Hexb, consistent with homeostatic microglia previously described in the healthy CNS [28, 29]. MG2 appeared rapidly after injury, expressing Spp1, Lgals3, Lpl, and Apoe, resembling disease-associated microglia (DAM)-like states [7, 30]. These genes are related to phagocytosis, lipid metabolism, and tissue remodeling, suggesting an early inflammatory and metabolic activation phase. MG3 showed strong expression of proliferation genes such as Mki67 and Top2a, consistent with proliferative microglia observed during early post-stroke inflammation [17]. MG4 expressed Ly6e and Csf1r, which suggests a phenotype induced by stress and interferon, in line with microglial populations reported under inflammatory or repair-related signals [31, 32]. MG5 was enriched in ribosomal and housekeeping genes such as Rps27a, Rpl13, Eef1a1, and Cst3, reflecting a metabolically active but transcriptionally quiescent state, similar to protein synthesis associated microglia described during recovery [33]. MG6 expressed many genes involved in the cell cycle, including Uhrf1, Cdca8, Kif15, and Cenpe, representing a small cycling subset which may support local renewal of microglia after ischemic injury, as also reported in proliferative microglial niches [31, 32].

To further describe the functional programs of microglia in response to ischemic injury, GO enrichment analysis was performed on genes upregulated at early (Day 1–3), mid-late (Day 3–7) and late (Day 7–14) stages in each subcluster. In MG1, early upregulated genes were enriched in terms such as angiogenesis, wounding response, lipid transport, and phagocytosis, suggesting rapid activation of vascular and immune functions immediately after ischemic insult (Fig S2A, B). At mid-to-late stages, MG1 showed enrichment for terms associated with cell motility, endothelial migration, and Wnt/Ras-mediated signaling, in keeping with a transition toward cytoskeletal remodeling and repair-oriented motility (Fig S2C, D). These patterns indicate that MG1 microglia, although homeostatic under baseline conditions, can transiently acquire a pro-angiogenic and migratory features during early post-MCAO remodeling [34, 35].

In MG2, early upregulated genes (Fig S2E) were enriched in biological processes related to inflammation, angiogenesis, actin-cytoskeleton remodeling, autophagy, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and endothelial proliferation. This enrichment points to rapid activation of inflammatory, migratory, and pro-angiogenic programs immediately after MCAO (Fig. 2F). These results suggest that MG2 microglia promptly engage angiogenic and autophagic responses driven by inflammation that support early tissue remodeling, in line with findings from post-ischemic models [36–38]. As injury progressed, MG2 showed clear functional changes: during the later phase, it upregulated processes involved in clearance of plasma lipoproteins, endocytosis through LDL receptors, intracellular cholesterol transport, signaling through stress-activated protein kinases, and negative regulation of neuronal death and coagulation, reflecting a shift toward the control of lipid metabolic and neuroprotective adaptation. This pattern is consistent with evidence that ApoE-LDLR-dependent endocytosis shapes microglial lipid metabolism and support of neurons [39, 40]. To further delineate the transcriptional mechanisms driving these temporal transitions, TF activity in MG2 was examined across time. NR1H3 (LXRα) activity rose markedly at Day 3, followed by an increase in MAFK activity spanning Days 3 to 7 (Fig. 2G). The mid-phase rise in NR1H3 fits with its known role in promoting lipid handling, cholesterol efflux, and anti-inflammatory reprogramming in microglia during tissue remodeling after ischemia [41, 42]. The subsequent rise in MAFK activity at the subacute stage may reflect activation of oxidative-stress-responsive and phase II antioxidant pathways, as small Maf proteins have been linked to redox adaptation, although direct evidence in microglia after stroke is still limited [43]. These time-resolved TF patterns suggest that MG2 microglia integrate lipid metabolic regulation with redox adaptation to sustain vascular and neuronal protection during the sub-acute stage of MCAO.

In contrast, early and late upregulated gene profiles for MG3 (Fig S2F) showed a distinct temporal pattern. GO analysis indicated early activation of cytokine signaling, phagocytosis, blood-vessel morphogenesis, and complement-related synaptic pruning, consistent with a role in acute neuroinflammatory and vascular responses (Fig. 2H). Complement-dependent microglial pruning has been shown to shape neuronal survival and connectivity during injury responses [44, 45]. Consistent with this pattern, RELA (NF-κB p65) activity peaked on Day 1, reflecting canonical pro-inflammatory activation of microglia in ischemic brain [46], which drives cytokine release and immune recruitment [47, 48]. At later stages, MG3 upregulated genes associated with neurogenesis, axon growth, and glial differentiation, accompanied by elevated MEF2A activity around Day 3 (Fig. 2I). This combination points to the start of a regulatory or remodeling phase, as MEF2 family members have been shown to temper microglial inflammatory responses and promote neuroprotective programs [49]. These transcriptional and regulatory features of MG3 indicate a temporal shift from NF-κB–mediated inflammatory activation to MEF2-associated programs which support neuronal regeneration and structural reorganization in the post-ischemic brain. This shift marks a resolution phase of microglial activation which connects early immune responses with later reparative signaling in ischemic tissue.

Reactive-state heterogeneity and reparative transitions of astrocytes

To describe the transcriptional dynamics and intercellular interactions of astrocytes after ischemic injury, a pseudotime trajectory was reconstructed for astrocyte populations (Fig. 3A). The inferred pseudotime ordering organized astrocytes along a continuum from homeostatic-like to reactive-state-enriched populations, rather than representing real chronological post-injury time, which is consistent with previous descriptions of graded astrocyte activation following ischemia [50, 51]. Three transcriptionally distinct astrocyte subclusters (AS1-AS3) were identified within the astrocyte compartment and mapped onto this trajectory (Fig. 3B). The relative proportions of these states varied along pseudotime and across post-injury stages (Fig. 3C-D). Marker profiles further distinguished these astrocyte-associated states (Fig. 3E). AS1 was enriched for homeostatic astrocyte-associated genes, including Plpp3, Bcan, and Aqp4, consistent with baseline supportive astrocyte functions related to synaptic and metabolic homeostasis. AS2 emerged rapidly after MCAO and displayed an injury-responsive program with immune- and vascular-associated features, including C1qa, Cldn5, and Psmb8. AS3 became more prominent at later stages and expressed Hspb1, S100a10, and Tmsb10, reflecting a repair-associated reactive program related to tissue-remodeling, wound-healing, and pro-angiogenic functions, similar to astrocyte states reported to support neuronal survival and vascular recovery after stroke [52]. Together, these patterns highlighted heterogeneous astrocyte-associated transcriptional responses after ischemic injury.

To further clarify early functional transitions of astrocyte-associated states, GO enrichment analysis was applied to early (Day 1–3) upregulated genes in AS2 and AS3 (Fig S3A). The AS2 state showed early enrichment in processes including negative regulation of cell population proliferation, positive regulation of neuron apoptotic process, purine nucleotide metabolic process, cell-cell adhesion mediated by integrin, regulation of response to external stimulus, inflammatory response, regulation of cell differentiation, and intracellular iron ion homeostasis (Fig. 3F). These terms collectively indicate that AS2 rapidly engages stress- and immunity-coupled programs while modulating adhesion and metabolic demands, alongside early iron sequestration and differentiation control, features consistent with the initial phase of reactive astrogliosis after ischemic injury [50, 53]. Consistently, CellChat analysis revealed that TNF signaling networks were markedly enhanced in the early phase, predominantly between AS2 and MG2 subclusters (Fig. 3G, S3B). This finding is consistent with a potential role for TNF-associated microglia–astrocyte inflammatory crosstalk in early astrocyte reactivity [52, 54].

In parallel, AS3 displayed early enrichment for astrocyte development, astrocyte differentiation, regeneration, tissue remodeling, negative regulation of cytokine production, wound healing, positive regulation of angiogenesis, response to cytokine, and defense response (Fig. 3H). These terms suggest an early-onset, repair-associated module that coordinates matrix/tissue remodeling with cytokine-responsive and pro-angiogenic cues [55, 56]. In line with these reparative features, PSAP signaling networks were detected between astrocyte and microglial subclusters during post-ischemic remodeling, with representative interactions involving AS3 and MG5 (Fig. 3I, S3C). This pattern suggests a potential shift from early inflammatory signaling toward repair-associated glial crosstalk. PSAP signaling has recently been linked to lysosomal homeostasis and lipid recycling in glial cells, facilitating recovery in post-stroke and neurodegenerative conditions [57, 58].

Oligodendrocyte lineage maturation and glial communication

To characterize the dynamic transcriptional landscape of the oligodendrocyte lineage following ischemic injury, a pseudotime trajectory of oligodendrocyte-lineage cells was reconstructed, showing an inferred maturation-associated transcriptional continuum from OPC to mature oligodendrocytes (OLs) (Fig. 4A). Seven subclusters were identified, including two OPC clusters (OPC1-2), two pre-oligodendrocyte clusters (pre-OL1-2), and three oligodendrocyte clusters (OL1-3) (Fig. 4B).

(G) Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis based on the genes displayed in (F), highlighting biological processes related to gliogenesis, differentiation, and myelination. (H) GO enrichment analysis of genes upregulated at Day 1 in the OPC1 and OPC2 subclusters, respectively. The relative composition of these subclusters varied markedly along pseudotime and across the MCAO timeline (Fig. 4C-D). OPCs and pre-OLs were more frequent at early stages, whereas OL1-3 became progressively enriched at later stages, suggesting a differentiation sequence triggered by injury. Marker gene expression supported the major oligodendrocyte-lineage hierarchy from OPCs to pre-OLs and mature OLs (Fig. 4E). OPCs expressed Cspg4, Pdgfra, and Cnp, characteristic of progenitor states [59]. Pre-OLs showed intermediate expression of myelin-related genes such as Plp1 and Mbp, while mature OLs (OL1-3) expressed these genes at high levels, indicating advanced myelination and terminal differentiation [60].

Pseudotime-associated maturation gene expression patterns showed stepwise upregulation of myelin associated genes, including Cnp, Plp1, Mbp, and Mobp (Fig. 4F). GO analysis of these genes highlighted enrichment in gliogenesis, oligodendrocyte differentiation, axon ensheathment, and myelination processes (Fig. 4G), consistent with a maturation-associated transcriptional continuum of the oligodendrocyte lineage. This pattern is in agreement with previous transcriptomic studies that reported staged activation of myelin-related genes during the transition from OPCs to OLs after brain injury [61].

Early molecular responses of OPCs after ischemic injury were then examined. Genes upregulated at early stages were identified (Fig S4A) and subjected to GO enrichment analysis to assess their functional roles (Fig. 4H). In OPC1, these genes were enriched in biological processes related to macrophage migration, angiogenesis, and the unfolded protein response. In OPC2, they were associated with lipid metabolism, apoptotic signaling, and stress in the endoplasmic reticulum. These results suggest that different OPC subsets rapidly adopt programs related to cellular stress and survival, which is consistent with earlier reports of OPC heterogeneity and metabolic vulnerability under ischemic conditions [62].

CellChat analysis showed a strong increase of PROS signaling between microglial and oligodendrocyte-lineage subclusters during the acute phase (Day 1–3), mainly from MG2 and MG4 to OPC and pre-OL clusters (Fig. 4I, S4C), indicating active glia-glia communication that accompanies early reparative processes. Such enhanced microglia-oligodendrocyte signaling aligns with the idea that cues from microglia support OPC proliferation and remyelination after ischemic injury [63, 64], suggesting that the observed interaction may represent a conserved mechanism contributing to post-ischemic white-matter repair.

To describe temporal transcriptional dynamics within the pre-OL1 subcluster, early (Day 1–3) and late (Day 7–14) upregulated genes were identified (Fig. S4B). The early-upregulated genes were enriched in myelination, mitochondrial organization, and inflammatory response, whereas the late-upregulated genes were associated with cell morphogenesis and MAPK signaling (Fig. 4J). Consistent with these transitions, TF activity analysis showed temporal activation of Stat3, Srf, and Sox10 in pre-OL1, reflecting a coordinated regulatory program driving maturation and remyelination (Fig. 4K). Stat3 has been reported to mediate oligodendrocyte differentiation and repair induced by inflammation [65], while Srf and Sox10 are required for cytoskeletal remodeling and myelin gene expression during regeneration [66, 67].

Neurovascular signaling and endothelial remodeling

To investigate the transcriptional diversity and intercellular signaling of neurons after ischemic injury, four neuronal subclusters (Nrn1-Nrn4) with distinct transcriptional states were identified (Fig. 5A-B). Their relative proportions changed dynamically along the MCAO timeline, with Nrn2 and Nrn3 expanding during the early phase (Day 1–3), and Nrn4 increasing at later recovery stages (Day 7–14).

Marker analysis indicated that Nrn2 expressed Gad1, Gad2 and Slc6a1, consistent with GABAergic inhibitory neurons, whereas Nrn3 expressed corticocortical/excitatory markers (e.g., Bcl11b, Sox4, Satb2), aligning with glutamatergic excitatory neurons, in agreement with single-cell taxonomies of cortical inhibitory vs. excitatory classes [68, 69]. Nrn1 and Nrn4 displayed more heterogeneous transcriptional patterns, likely reflecting homeostatic and structural remodeling states, respectively (Fig. 5B).

GO enrichment analysis of early (Day 1–3) upregulated genes revealed that Nrn2 and Nrn3 were enriched in cytokine-mediated signaling, regulation of neuronal apoptosis, oxidative-stress responses, and actin-filament remodeling (Fig. 5C). These findings indicate that specific neuronal populations activate coordinated inflammatory and cytoskeletal programs during the acute phase of ischemic injury, reflecting an early neuroimmune response and synaptic reorganization process [70, 71].

CellChat analysis next revealed time-dependent intercellular communication. The SEMA6 network between microglia and neurons transiently intensified at Day 1–3, most prominently involving MG4→Nrn3 (Fig. 5D, S5B). Semaphorin family cues are well-established modulators of axon guidance and injury-related neuroimmune signaling in the adult CNS, providing biological context for interpreting these predicted early microglia-neuron interactions [72, 73]. The LAMININ signaling network between astrocytes and neurons, particularly from AS2 and AS3 to Nrn2 and Nrn3 subclusters, showed relatively stronger predicted interactions at Day 7, consistent with the notion that reactive astrocytes remodel the extracellular matrix and support neuronal stabilization during the subacute phase (Fig. 5E, S5C) [74].

To further investigate vascular-neuronal communication after ischemic injury, the heterogeneity of endothelial cells involved in intercellular signaling was examined. UMAP analysis identified five transcriptionally distinct endothelial subclusters (EC1-EC5) (Fig. 5F). EC1 and EC2 mainly represented capillary and microvascular endothelium, expressing canonical barrier and transport genes such as Cldn5 and Slc2a1. EC3 displayed a venous-like signature, while EC4 expressed arterial-associated markers such as Efnb2 and Dll4, suggesting a spectrum from capillary to large-vessel phenotypes. EC5 was enriched in genes related to cellular stress and vascular remodeling, such as Hspa1a and Klf2, indicating an activated or proliferative endothelial population that typically appears during tissue repair. These subclusters displayed distinct temporal dynamics after MCAO. EC1 and EC2 were dominant under baseline conditions, while EC4 and EC5 became more frequent during the subacute and recovery phases, in line with angiogenic remodeling after ischemia (Fig S6A-B).

To define molecular features of endothelial activation after ischemic injury, early transcriptional responses of representative endothelial subclusters were assessed. EC2 exhibited upregulation of genes associated with oxidative stress response, wound healing, and apoptotic regulation, reflecting an early adaptive and reparative phenotype (Fig S6C, D). In contrast, EC3 showed enrichment of biological processes linked to cell proliferation, immune modulation, and leukocyte differentiation, consistent with a shift toward vascular remodeling and immune-endothelial interaction during the recovery phase (Fig S6E, F). CellChat analysis revealed temporal remodeling of EGF-related signaling between endothelial and neuronal subclusters after MCAO, with relatively stronger predicted endothelial–neuronal interactions during the subacute-to-recovery phase among the subclusters detected at each time point (Fig. 5G, S5D). This temporal pattern suggests that EGF-related endothelial–neuronal communication may be associated with neuronal responses during post-ischemic recovery. Recent studies have linked EGFR signaling to neuronal remodeling and repair after stroke [75, 76], providing biological context for the potential involvement of endothelial–neuronal communication in post-ischemic remodeling.

Cross-species supportive analysis using rat single-nucleus RNA-seq

To assess whether selected transcriptional and intercellular signaling features identified in the mouse MCAO model were also detectable in an independent rodent dataset, an snRNA-seq dataset from rat brains was analyzed. This dataset included both our newly generated rat samples (Sham, Day 21) and the publicly available GSE250245 dataset (Sham, Day 2), enabling cross-timepoint integration [24] (Fig S7A). After correction for batch effects, nuclei from both datasets were well mixed in a shared space, indicating successful integration across studies (Fig S7B). The merged dataset encompassed all major brain cell populations, including excitatory neurons (ExN), inhibitory neurons (InN), oligodendrocytes (OL), oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPC), microglia (MG), astrocytes (AS), endothelial cells (EC), smooth muscle cells (SMC), and ependymal cells (EP), consistent with canonical brain cell taxonomies (Fig S7C).

UMAP visualization of the integrated data showed clear separation of neuronal, glial, and vascular compartments, with marked differences between the acute phase (Day 2) and recovery (Day 21) phases (Fig. 6A). Analysis of cell-type composition confirmed a pattern similar to that in the mouse scRNA-seq data: a transient increase in microglia and a reduction in neuronal and endothelial populations at the early time point, followed by partial recovery of vascular and neuronal cells at the later time point (Fig. 6B). These temporal changes showed partial consistency with the post-ischemic cellular remodeling patterns observed in the mouse model.

(B) Temporal variation in cell-type composition after MCAO, showing dynamic changes among neurons, glial, and vascular cells. (C) CellChat analysis of SEMA6 signaling networks between microglial (MG1-MG5) and neuronal subpopulations (ExN, InN) across different time points. (D) CellChat analysis of LAMININ signaling networks between astrocytes and neuronal subtypes

Representative intercellular signaling pathways were then examined to assess whether cell-cell communication patterns were similar across species. In line with selected mouse MCAO findings, CellChat analysis suggested that the SEMA6 signaling network between microglia and neurons was strengthened at Day 2, predominantly involving MG2-MG4 subclusters and both excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations (Fig. 6C, Fig S8A). This result supports an early microglia-neuron communication axis mediated by semaphorin signaling in the acute phase after stroke. The LAMININ signaling pathway between astrocytes and neurons was also detected from the subacute to chronic phases, with sustained signals from astrocytes to both excitatory and inhibitory neurons (Fig. 6D, Fig S8B), similar to astrocyte-dependent extracellular matrix remodeling and neuroprotective support seen in the mouse data.

Overall, despite differences in species, sequencing modality, and sampling design, the rat snRNA-seq analysis provided supportive cross-species evidence for selected transcriptional and intercellular communication patterns observed in the mouse MCAO model. The detection of microglial semaphorin-related signaling and astrocyte-derived laminin-related communication in the rat dataset supports the potential cross-species relevance of these candidate pathways, while further validation in matched experimental designs will be required.

Discussion

This study integrates multiple scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq datasets to build a map of cellular and molecular responses that follows changes over time and across species after focal cerebral ischemia. Through combining five public mouse scRNA-seq datasets, together with complementary rat data, interactions among glial, neuronal, and vascular cell groups during recovery after ischemia were described in a unified framework. The results suggest that inflammatory activation, metabolic adaptation, myelin formation, and vascular and tissue remodeling are associated with partially ordered, time-point-related patterns. These processes did not act as separate events but showed overlap in time and appeared to form a connected network potentially associated with repair-related processes.

Microglia are among the earliest responders after ischemic injury and strongly influence later tissue changes. In the present analysis, early microglia after ischemia showed high activity of TNF and NF-κB signaling, which fits with strong inflammatory activation, which promotes debris clearance and immune coordination but can contribute to neuronal injury if excessive. This pattern is in line with recent studies showing that microglia exhibit a rapid upregulation of cytokine and chemokine pathways within hours after ischemia [77]. Over the following days, the transcriptomic profile of microglia shifted toward pathways related to lipid metabolism, phagocytosis, and neurotrophic signaling. TFs such as NR1H3 (LXRα) and MEF2A showed increased activity, suggesting a metabolic transition from glycolytic to oxidative and lipid-handling states. These findings are consistent with work in experimental stroke models in which such metabolic shifts in microglia help resolve inflammation and support synaptic repair [78]. Results suggest that microglial activity after ischemia cannot be defined as simply proinflammatory or reparative but is instead temporally dependent. Therapeutic strategies that target microglia will probably need careful timing and attention to cellular context, rather than broad and continuous suppression of inflammation.

Astrocytes also showed their own dynamic transitions that appear to follow the evolution of microglial activity. In the early phase after ischemia, astrocyte populations expressed genes linked to cellular stress, responses to cytokines, and metabolic adjustment. At later stages, genes related to extracellular matrix organization, angiogenesis, and growth support became more prominent. The temporal overlap between microglial TNF signaling and astrocytic PSAP expression suggests that inflammatory signals may help to start or shape astrocyte programs that favor repair. This idea is consistent with earlier studies showing that moderate activation of immune pathways can promote astrocyte-driven remodeling and support neuronal survival [57, 79]. Prosaposin, which acts both as a lysosomal cofactor and as a secreted neuroprotective factor, has been linked to communication between glial cells and may mark the transition from mainly inflammatory activity toward regeneration. These observations support the view that resolution of inflammation and tissue repair are not sharply separated stages but develop along a continuous path, in which immune-derived signals gradually drive structural and metabolic remodeling [52, 80, 81]. Recent work has also suggested that astrocytes and microglia function together as a combined inflammatory–reparative unit during stroke recovery [82]. This continuous model may help explain why broad anti-inflammatory treatments have shown mixed results in clinical trials, as such treatments may block not only harmful inflammation but also signals that are needed for repair. The classical A1/A2 astrocyte framework should be interpreted with caution, as it represents an oversimplified binary model of reactive astrocyte biology. Reactive astrocytes after ischemic injury are better viewed as heterogeneous, context-dependent transcriptional states rather than fixed neurotoxic or reparative subtypes. Accordingly, we interpreted AS2 and AS3 as astrocyte-compartment-associated reactive states with distinct immune-, vascular-, and repair-associated features, rather than as definitive A1-like or A2-like astrocyte populations.

In addition to immune-related changes, the present findings emphasize the importance of coordination among glial cells in restoring white matter. The oligodendrocyte lineage exhibits a temporal order, including OPCs, transitional pre-oligodendrocytes, and mature myelinating cells. Transitional populations expressed both stress-related and myelin-related genes, which suggests that they act as adaptive intermediates that balance survival under stress with the start of differentiation. Similar pre-oligodendrocyte states have been reported in other demyelinating and developmental settings [64, 83, 84]. Transcriptional analysis showed enrichment of STAT3 and SOX10, both essential for oligodendrocyte maturation and myelin gene induction [65, 66, 85]. The parallel activation of these transcriptional regulators and microglial programs related to lipid handling suggests that outside signals and intrinsic differentiation mechanisms are closely aligned. These findings support the growing view that repair of white matter depends on communication among several glial cell types [86–89]. Astrocytes provide metabolic and structural support, and microglia remove myelin debris and release factors that promote differentiation. Recent studies have highlighted this type of multicellular cooperation as a central feature of regeneration in the central nervous system [90–92]. In line with the present results on glial changes after MCAO, recent work has identified the TAM receptor family (Tyro3, Axl, and MerTK) and its vitamin K–dependent ligands Protein S and Gas6 as key links between phagocytic clearance and anti-inflammatory, repair-related signaling [93]. Activation of the Gas6–TAM axis has been shown to promote microglial regulation and oligodendrocyte remyelination in both demyelinating and ischemic models, suggesting that PROS–TAM signaling may help coordinate white matter repair after stroke [94]. The present data support this idea by showing that progression of the oligodendrocyte lineage overlaps in time with microglial and astrocytic transitions, pointing to a coordinated glial network that underlies effective remyelination.

The vascular compartment also showed strong reorganization after ischemia. Distinct endothelial subclusters were more common during the recovery phase, especially those enriched for arterial markers and genes related to cell growth and proliferation. Pathways involving semaphorin, laminin, and EGF family molecules, which are known to regulate angiogenesis, remodeling of the extracellular matrix, and axon guidance, were prominent in these endothelial subsets [95, 96]. At the same time, related signals were detected in neurons and astrocytes, suggesting that growth of new vessels and stabilization of synapses proceed in parallel and are supported by reciprocal signaling among cells in the vascular and neural compartments. This pattern is consistent with the idea that the neurovascular unit functions as an integrated system rather than a simple collection of separate cell types [97]. Growing interest has focused on this type of repair centered on the neurovascular unit, as experimental enhancement of communication between endothelial cells and glia has been found to improve vascular remodeling, reduce secondary inflammation, and lead to better functional outcomes after stroke [98, 99]. The present results add to this framework by pointing to specific molecular pathways that may mediate such multicellular coordination during the subacute and later phases of recovery.

When gene expression and signaling programs from all major cell types are considered together, recovery after ischemia appears to follow a layered and overlapping pattern in time. Early stages are dominated by immune and stress responses. Intermediate stages involve regulators of metabolism, matrix remodeling, and cytoskeletal changes. Later stages are marked by activation of pathways related to myelination, angiogenesis, and maintenance of synapses. This structure supports the idea that the adult brain reuses, at least in part, developmental programs during repair, similar to what has been observed in other regenerative situations [100]. The repeated involvement of gene modules related to morphogenesis suggests that repair is guided by sequences of transcriptional waves rather than by a single, uniform pathway. Understanding this temporal order may help explain how diverse cellular processes are combined over time and may aid in the search for biomarkers that mark distinct stages of recovery.

The time-resolved framework described here may provide a conceptual basis for considering the timing of future therapeutic interventions. Although the present data are mainly descriptive, they indicate that therapies may need to match the dominant cellular programs of each phase of recovery. In the acute period, limiting excessive microglial activation and release of cytokines may reduce secondary damage. During the subacute phase, support of metabolic changes in microglia and astrocytes, for example by activating LXRα, may help resolve inflammation and stabilize the local environment. In later phases, approaches that promote oligodendrocyte differentiation and preserve vascular integrity could help maintain structural and functional recovery. These ideas underline the value of a phase-based view when planning treatment timing. A single therapy is unlikely to be equally effective at all stages of stroke recovery, and combinations of treatments that target different cellular processes at suitable time windows may produce better results. For example, stem cell-based therapies, which provide long-lasting paracrine and immune-modulating effects, could complement time-targeted interventions by supporting tissue repair across several phases of recovery [101–103].

This study also extends existing single-cell research on ischemic stroke in several important ways. Most prior analyses have focused on acute immune responses or limited time points, making it difficult to capture long-term trajectories of repair [77]. By integrating datasets across multiple post-injury intervals, we constructed a broader time-resolved view of how the ischemic brain evolves from injury toward partial recovery. The combined dataset increases statistical power, allowing detection of relatively rare but functionally relevant populations such as transitional pre-oligodendrocytes and proliferative endothelial cells. The recurrence of selected patterns across independent datasets and sequencing platforms increases confidence that these findings may reflect biologically relevant trends, although dataset-specific effects cannot be fully excluded. This integrative approach exemplifies how meta-analysis of publicly available single-cell data can reveal recurring patterns of cellular reorganization that may not be apparent in smaller, isolated studies.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this integrative analysis was limited by the incomplete overlap between datasets and post-ischemic time points. Because the included public datasets were generated by different studies, some post-ischemic stages were not evenly represented across datasets. This issue was particularly relevant for Days 7 and 14, each of which was represented by a single dataset, making biological time effects and dataset-specific effects statistically non-identifiable at these stages. Although Harmony improved cross-dataset alignment in the low-dimensional embedding used for visualization, clustering, and cell-type annotation, the integration metrics indicated incomplete dataset mixing, and Harmony does not directly remove all expression-level batch effects from the matrices used for differential expression analysis, transcription factor activity inference, or CellChat-based ligand–receptor inference. Therefore, findings associated with Days 7 and 14, including temporal communication patterns, should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Balanced prospective time-course datasets will be required to validate these candidate temporal programs. Similarly, pseudotime analysis in this study was used to organize transcriptionally related cell states within each lineage and should not be interpreted as true chronological progression or lineage tracing. Because cells collected at the same post-injury time point may occupy different activation states, incomplete alignment between pseudotime ordering and sampling time was expected and reflects cellular heterogeneity rather than a direct temporal trajectory. Use of dissociated single-cell and single-nucleus sequencing leads to loss of spatial information. Future work that combines these approaches with spatial transcriptomics or multiplex imaging will be needed to confirm where cells are located and to map signaling domains within vascular and glial networks [104]. In addition, predictions about ligand-receptor pairs and transcription factor activity are based on computational models and require experimental testing. CellChat-based estimates may also be influenced by unequal cell numbers and cellular composition across datasets and time points; targeted sensitivity analyses for representative signaling pathways are summarized in Supplementary Tables S3–S4, and pathway-level temporal changes were interpreted cautiously. The complex nature of ischemic injury, with heterogeneous lesions and variable blood supply, also complicates interpretation. Furthermore, although cross-species integration between mouse and rat increases generalizability, the human brain shows slower time courses and greater cellular diversity. Human tissue and organoid models will therefore be important for testing whether the same pathways operate in human stroke. In addition, although conservative quality-control thresholds and marker-based inspection were applied, dedicated doublet detection and ambient RNA correction were not uniformly performed across all public datasets. Therefore, residual doublets, ambient RNA contamination, or incomplete subcluster resolution cannot be fully excluded, particularly for subclusters showing mixed immune- or vascular-associated transcriptional features. These findings should be interpreted as transcriptomic state associations and require future validation using spatial or protein-level approaches. Finally, functional experiments are required to determine whether changing the identified molecular axes can truly alter outcomes.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive single-cell framework describing how inflammatory activation, glial remodeling, myelin regeneration, and neurovascular reconstruction show time-associated coordination after cerebral ischemia. The results suggest that recovery-related transcriptional programs after stroke involve a network of interacting cell types that jointly determine tissue outcome. Inflammation, metabolism, and structural repair do not occur as separate blocks but form a sequence of linked processes that unfold over time. By outlining the transcriptional and signaling structure of this multicellular system, the present work helps to generate hypotheses regarding brain repair and provides a transcriptomic basis for future mechanistic and translational studies.

Conclusions

This study provides an integrated single-cell framework for brain repair after ischemic stroke by combining multiple mouse scRNA-seq datasets with complementary rat snRNA-seq supportive analysis. The results suggest that post-ischemic recovery involves time-associated changes across microglia, astrocytes, oligodendrocyte lineage cells, endothelial cells, and neurons. Early inflammatory activation was associated with lipid metabolic adaptation, astrocyte reparative reprogramming, oligodendrocyte maturation, and neurovascular remodeling, with selected intercellular signaling patterns also detectable in the rat snRNA-seq dataset.

These findings provide a framework for understanding multicellular coordination during stroke recovery and support a phase-oriented view of therapeutic intervention. Targeting cell-state transitions and intercellular communication networks at appropriate post-injury stages may help improve the precision of restorative strategies and provide a conceptual basis for future mechanistic and translational studies.
