Mesenchymal tumors of the central nervous system: illustrative case
Authors: Michael K. Anyane-Yeboa, Jiashu Wang, Kaitlyn Quijano, Roger Fecher, Andrew Kobets
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDFET-CREB (EWSR1-ATF1) fusion–positive intracranial mesenchymal tumors (IMTs) are locally aggressive with a high recurrence rate, particularly for pediatric patients. Management is challenging because of their complex diagnostic criteria, tumor location, and limited evidence of effective adjuvant therapy. Mesenchymal tumors are commonly found in subcutaneous tissues and are rarely observed in the CNS. Intracranial mesenchymal tumors (IMTs) account for 0.3% of all soft tissue neoplasms and are typically locally aggressive with a high recurrence rate.1 The mainstay treatment for IMTs is resection with adjuvant radiation therapy and/or chemotherapy based on the biological behavior of the tumor.

Among IMTs, the FET-CREB fusion–positive IMT was recently recognized, first described in 2017 by Kao et al.2 Diagnosis is challenging given the broad histopathological spectrum of these tumors. An integrated diagnosis using morphology alongside molecular testing is increasingly essential.3,4 The suggested diagnostic criteria of IMTs include their intracranial location; variable morphological features, including spindle cells, mucin-rich stroma, hemangioma-like vasculature, and epithelioid cells in a mucin-poor collagenous stroma; and demonstration of an FET-CREB family fusion.5 However, their clinicopathological behavior, optimal diagnostic workflows, and evidence-based treatment strategies remain incompletely defined. The primary treatment for IMTs with FET-CREB fusion is a maximal safe resection. However, therapeutic decision-making may be constrained by proximity to cranial nerves (CNs), functional areas, and critical vasculature.6 Adjuvant therapy is also less effective because the tumors lie behind the tightly regulated blood-brain barrier.6,7 Consequently, treatment modalities used in extracranial mesenchymal tumors may not directly apply to IMTs.

We present a rare pediatric case of an IMT with FET-CREB fusion and multiple recurrences. This represents the 26th case in the literature and the only case requiring four staged resections due to the high-risk location of the tumor.

Illustrative Case

A 6-year-old male with a history of cerebral palsy with spastic diplegia presented with 3 weeks of vomiting, gait change, and headaches. Physical examination showed right hand weakness, facial asymmetry, and difficulty swallowing solid foods. CT revealed a right cerebellar complex and a large cystic mass extending to at least the cerebellopontine angle (CPA) (Fig. 1). Multiplanar multisequential MRI without contrast revealed a 4.6 × 3.1–cm solid cystic mass within the right posterior cerebellar fossa, with mass effect on the brainstem, pons, middle cerebellar peduncle, and right cerebellar hemisphere. Additionally, displacement and effacement of the fourth ventricle caused mild obstructive hydrocephalus. After tumor board discussion, a plan for maximal safe resection was undertaken. At this time, a gross-total resection (GTR) was not pursued due to the lack of a definitive diagnosis. The patient underwent a right retrosigmoid craniotomy with a right frontal external ventricular drain (EVD) placed to treat his hydrocephalus. Intraoperative analysis of the frozen specimen demonstrated characteristics of a poorly differentiated epithelial neoplasm with high cellularity and sheet-like architecture. Mitotic figures were identified but were not abundant (Fig. 2). The preliminary differential diagnosis included medulloblastoma and atypical rhabdoid/teratoid tumor. Given the uncertainty of the diagnosis and neuromonitoring changes with continued resection of the tumor on the brainstem surface, a decision was made to remove what was safely able to be resected and then return as needed after a diagnosis was obtained. Postoperative MRI showed a residual thick rind of enhancement along the medial aspect of the tumor with decreased leftward mass effect on the pons and medulla. The patient had a mild facial palsy, but the rest of the neurological assessment was unremarkable. His intracranial pressure was normal with patent fourth ventricle and venous sinuses. After a few days of close monitoring, the EVD was removed. Physical examination at discharge revealed no other obvious abnormalities.

After 1 month, the patient began to complain of headaches and vomiting and presented to the emergency department. MRI showed enlargement of the ventricles, concerning for hydrocephalus, and a ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed. Despite initial improvement, headaches and vomiting persisted. Examination revealed a right-sided hearing deficit, right-sided facial droop, and balance issues. Follow-up imaging 10 days later confirmed shunt function but showed significant tumor growth and cystic change around the brainstem at the right CPA, with increased effacement of the right ventricle.

Molecular testing revealed a mesenchymal fusion tumor that was diagnosed as an IMT, FET-CREB fusion–positive, without metastases. The immunohistochemical stain for marker Ki-67 showed a proliferation index of 2% (75/3107), weakly focally positive NeuN, negative EMA, negative GFAP, rare positive synaptophysin, negative nuclear beta-catenin, and positive cytoplasmic beta-catenin. This diagnosis informed the surgical team’s decision to perform a further resection for the goal of GTR.

Postoperative MRI following near-complete resection showed postsurgical changes and decreased mass effect from the tumor. Residual neoplasm was not excluded. Molecular testing revealed similar immunohistopathological characteristics to the prior specimen, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of up to 12% in hotspots. Neurological monitoring showed right-sided facial droop and CN VII palsy. Lingering facial weakness and hearing loss persisted, as these nerves were encased in the tumor and required extensive dissection. However, swallowing improved significantly, and physical and occupational therapy facilitated improvements in postural control, gait, fine motor coordination, and total body strength, leading to eventual discharge.

Despite his remarkable recovery and neurological stability, surveillance imaging 4 months later found a small recurrence of the tumor along the brainstem, and another resection was planned. The MRI study demonstrated a solid nodular enhancement within the resection cavity along the right petrous ridge extending into the right porus acusticus internus (Fig. 3). A right lateral suboccipital craniotomy with tumor resection was performed. Postsurgical imaging revealed a small area of enhancement along the right lateral pontomedullary junction that was nonspecific. Residual neoplasm was not excluded. A follow-up MRI study 3 months later showed an interval increase in a superficially located nodular enhancement (1.2 cm) abutting the right inferior cerebellar hemisphere at the junction of the sigmoid sinus and jugular foramen (Fig. 4). This was suspected to be a superficial recurrence of the lesion. The patient was without new or worsened neurological deficits. After multidisciplinary discussion, the decision was made to pursue reoperation rather than systemic chemotherapy. The patient underwent repeat surgical intervention, during which the superficially recurrent nodular lesion was completely excised. The deep residual tumor capsule adherent to the brainstem was intentionally left in situ because of the significant risk of neurological morbidity associated with further dissection. Postoperative MRI (Fig. 4) confirmed GTR of the targeted lesion. Adjuvant radiotherapy was subsequently initiated, consisting of a 6-week treatment course. The patient was discharged without new or worsened neurological deficits.

Follow-up MRI 3 months after his most recent surgery demonstrated GTR with suspected capsule encasing the CN VII/VIII complex, which was deemed too dangerous to outright remove given the surgical experience, which dictated that the tissue was absolutely adherent to the nerves and would require neurectomy to remove entirely. As the mass was thought to be encapsulated and given the further neurological risk, a decision was made to continue surveillance of the tissue, which was stable on the most recent follow-up.

Discussion

Observations

IMTs are rarely seen in the CNS.1 Our review found 25 cases of these tumors in pediatric patients reported (Table 1). Kao et al. reported the first case of a perirectal FET-CREB fusion–positive mesenchymal tumor and 4 intracranial tumors in 2017.2 From our review, the main inaugural symptoms were headache and seizures; however, patients complained of a variety of other symptoms, including abducens nerve palsy, papilledema, tongue jittering, nausea, vomiting, diplopia, loss of balance, and hydrocephalus (Table 1). The wide variety of symptoms is a challenge for diagnosing this tumor. The term “intracranial mesenchymal tumor FET-CREB fusion–­positive” was introduced to the WHO classification of CNS tumors in 2021. The diagnostic hallmark of this tumor is a genetic fusion between a member of the FET protein family (EWSR1, FUS, or TAF15) and the CREB protein family (CREB1, ATF1, or CREM). Risks of local relapse have been associated with younger age, subtotal resection (STR), and infraorbital location. FET-CREB (EWSR1-ATF1) fusion also implicated.8 These neoplasms can exhibit a variety of phenotypes and immunohistochemical markers, which makes them challenging to diagnose. In a study done with a cohort of 20 patients, all tumors expressed desmin, and most were positive for EMA and CD99. There was variable S100, synaptophysin, and CD68 staining with no immunoreactivitiy for GFAP. The Ki-67 index was generally less than 5%, with some tumors being elevated to 15%–20%. These findings are consistent with our case, which had a Ki-67 index of 2% and was negative for EMA and GFAP. In the past, without technologies like next-generation sequencing, it was difficult to identify if mesenchymal tumors had FET-CREB fusion.9–12 Thus, many tumors in the past may have been diagnosed as angiomatoid fibrous histiocytomas or intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumors instead.11 During a differential diagnosis of an intracranial neoplasm, it is important to include these tumors. With improved sequencing technology, it has become easier to screen for FET-CREB fusions, which can help narrow the differential diagnosis.10 This unique gene fusion may also be a target for targeted chemotherapy in the future. The aggressive nature of this tumor combined with its potential to occur in surgically high-risk locations, like the case described, highlights the need for targeted chemotherapy as a safer alternative.

As with many intracranial neoplasms, resection is one of the only curative treatments, although its feasibility depends on tumor location and relationship to adjacent critical structures, rather than pathology alone. In our review, the most common treatment for these tumors was resection, with 14 of 25 (56%) patients undergoing GTR and 6 of 25 (24%) undergoing STR (Table 1). None of the patients who underwent GTR received adjuvant therapy. Two of 6 (33%) patients who underwent an STR received adjuvant radiation therapy, 1 of 6 (17%) received adjuvant chemotherapy, and 1 of 6 (17%) received both. Three patients had unknown treatment. Of the 2 patients who did not undergo a resection, one patient was treated solely with focal proton beam radiotherapy, and the other patient was surveilled13 (Table 1). Of the 14 patients who received GTR, 11 (79%) had no recurrence and 3 (21%) had local recurrence of the tumor. At time of follow-up, none of these patients had died. In comparison, of the 6 patients who received STR, 1 (17%) had no recurrence, 4 (67%) had local recurrence, and 1 (17%) had metastatic disease. Of these patients, at the time of follow-up, 2 (33%) patients had died of the disease, 2 (33%) were alive with progressive disease, 1 (17%) was alive with stable disease, and 1 (17%) was alive and disease free (Table 1). Both patients who died of the disease had the EWSR1-ATF1 variant of the FET-CREB gene fusion.

In the largest cohort study to date, of 20 patients, Sloan et al. performed Kaplan-Meier analyses of overall survival and progression-free survival stratified by extent of resection.11 They found that while it was not statistically significant, STR was associated with increased risk of death and tumor recurrence. Seven of the 9 patients (78%) who received STR had local recurrence or progression, and 1 patient had metastatic spread to the thoracic spine and lymph nodes.11 Of these patients, 2 had two recurrences each, and both were alive with progressive disease at follow-up (Table 1). In the same cohort, 1 patient who received GTR had two recurrences and was disease free at follow-up (Table 1). The treatment of recurring tumors largely depends on the patient, the tumor location, and the initial treatment. Because of the rarity of this tumor, especially in pediatric patients, there have been no randomized clinical trials or established protocol, meaning management is largely extrapolated from case reports and case series.

While resection is the most common treatment for these tumors, there are nonsurgical treatment options. In many cases of STR, patients received adjuvant therapy, either adjuvant radiation therapy, chemotherapy, or both. In these cases, the chemotherapy chosen was sarcoma based and included ifosfamide with vincristine and doxorubicin, or carboplatin and etoposide.11 In addition to the use of adjuvant chemotherapy with STR, there are 2 reported cases that have shown promising results for other nonsurgical management. In a case reported by Ozkizilkaya et al., a 37-year-old woman with multiple recurrences previously treated with GTR was treated with craniospinal irradiation with protons and four cycles of octreotide/everolimus.14 At follow-up, she had stable findings. Then, in a case reported by D’Antonio et al., a 27-year-old woman was initially diagnosed with a medulloblastoma after partial resection and treated with proton beam therapy (PBT) and oral temozolomide with complete remission.1 After 18 months, there was local relapse, two metastatic lung lesions, and a lesion of the right iliac bone. Because of the tumor’s high PD-L1 expression, she was treated with nivolumab for 2 years and achieved complete remission in all sites. Six months after discontinuing treatment, there was a relapse of the lung lesion and the right iliac bone lesion. At this point the case was reevaluated and the patient was diagnosed with an FET-CREB fusion–positive IMT. The patient was started again on nivolumab but was prematurely discontinued due to stage 2 immune-mediated pneumonia. Then, she was given PBT on the iliac bone lesion and received surgical removal of the lung lesion. After, she underwent eight cycles of temozolomide and irinotecan, she achieved sustained complete remission.1 These cases illustrate the potential utility of nonsurgical treatments, including radiation therapy and immunotherapy, in primary, recurrent, and metastatic disease. Whether pediatric patients will respond similarly remains unclear and warrants further study, particularly for those in whom resection is not possible.

In comparison with the reported cases that required between one and three resections, the case presented in this paper required four resections in total. A major difference between our case and others described in the literature is the location of the tumor. Our patient’s tumor was in the right posterior cerebellar fossa, abutting CNs VII and VIII, crucial vasculature, and brainstem structures. In contrast to previously reported lesions involving the frontal lobe or superior sagittal sinus, resection in this case carried substantially higher operative risk because of the tumor’s deep CPA location. Only 2 cases reported previously were in the CPA, with one resulting in STR and stable disease, and the other requiring adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation therapy with an STR, which resulted in metastasis and death. Given the high-risk anatomy of this area, the surgical team focused on maintaining function, prioritizing preservation of hearing, swallowing, and facial movement, rather than achieving GTR. Postoperatively, the patient has some facial nerve palsy and decreased hearing; however, overall neurological function and quality of life were largely preserved. Across the literature, most patients had improved symptoms with mild complications from surgery that included CN bruising and headache recurrence (Table 1). Relative to our case, many of the described resections in the literature did not require the same degree of conservative dissection in proximity to sensitive structures. This is likely the reason that our patient required multiple staged resections and had more significant postoperative symptoms.

The variety of symptoms and diagnostic prognoses may be associated with the location of the tumors or other comorbidities, but with the small number of reported cases in pediatric patients, it is difficult to discern any patterns. However, in our small review, STR was associated with local recurrence with shorter survival. In a larger population of FET-CREB fusion–positive IMTs, including pediatric and adult patients, STR, younger age, an infratentorial tumor location, and EWSR1-ATF1 fusion were associated with a shorter progression-free survival.8 However, the sample size was small, and further investigation is needed to determine if the association between STR and recurrence in pediatric patients differs from that observed in adults. Many of the cases presented lack long-term follow-up data, further limiting treatment guidance. Recognition of the varied phenotypes of these tumors will be crucial to streamlining diagnosis. Across all the IMT cases, postoperative monitoring is essential to detect and manage tumor recurrence. Particularly for cases like ours, where the tumor location is more difficult to reach, maximal safe resections are not feasible, and the presence of residual tumor increases the risk of recurrence.

Lessons

FET-CREB fusion–positive IMT is a rare tumor characterized by mesenchymal tissue neoplasms in the intracranial space that harbor an FET-CREB fusion. Mesenchymal tumors are more commonly found in subcutaneous tissues and incredibly rare to see in the brain. These tumors have become easier to diagnose with the development of next-generation sequencing, since they can appear very similar to tumors like angiomatoid fibrous histiocytomas and intracranial myxoid mesenchymal tumors. However, they can present with a variety of symptoms, and patterns in their presentation are not well understood. Thus, it is important to consider this aggressive tumor in the differential diagnosis of intracranial neoplasms and use molecular testing to establish the diagnosis.

GTR is the preferred management strategy for these tumors, but its viability changes depending on the location of the tumor. In the case described, GTR could not be achieved in a single stage without causing devastating neurological morbidity. Management of this case was incredibly complex and shows that staged GTR with careful consideration of potential deficits is a viable treatment modality for these tumors. While this is currently the best way to manage these tumors, the unique FET-CREB gene fusion they harbor may become a molecular target for targeted chemotherapy moving forward.

Our case highlights the diagnostic challenges and surgical complexities of treating FET-CREB fusion–positive IMTs and their local aggression. We show that careful and staged resection can provide a positive outcome in cases like the one described. We emphasize the relevance of a multimodal approach to care and demonstrate how interdisciplinary postoperative surveillance leads to early detection and management of recurrences. Future efforts must prioritize the development of therapies targeting this unique gene fusion, offering a safer alternative for patients in whom resection is not viable.
