Acute subdural hematoma in spontaneous intracranial hypotension: illustrative case and review of the literature
Authors: Manou Overstijns, Jakob Straehle, Katharina Wolf, Roberto Doria-Medina, Marco Bissolo, Amir El Rahal, Niklas Lützen, Horst Urbach, Jürgen Beck, Florian Volz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSpinal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks cause spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) and are a recognized cause of chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH). In contrast, acute subdural hematoma (aSDH) without trauma as the initial manifestation of a spinal CSF leak appears to be exceedingly rare.Research questionCan a primary non-traumatic aSDH represent the first manifestation of a spinal CSF leak.Material and methodsWe report an illustrative case of a 52-year-old man presenting with a non-traumatic aSDH requiring emergency surgical evacuation together with a PRISMA-based literature review of the literature on subdural hematomas associated with SIH/spinal CSF leaks. PubMed and Embase databases were systematically screened. Diagnostic workup included cranial imaging, digital subtraction angiography, spinal MRI, and dynamic subtraction myelography.ResultsThe literature review identified 14 eligible publications. SIH-associated subdural hematomas were predominantly chronic or mixed acute-on-chronic in nature, whereas primary acute presentations were exceedingly uncommon. No documented case of a primary non-traumatic aSDH caused by a spinal CSF leak was identified. In the presented case, repeated cranial vascular imaging revealed no vascular pathology. The patient developed recurrent subdural hematomas requiring multiple surgical interventions. Subsequent spinal imaging demonstrated a ventral CSF leak at T5/6 caused by a discogenic microspur. Minimally invasive transdural surgical repair resulted in complete clinical and radiological resolution.Discussion and conclusionPrimary non-traumatic aSDH may represent the first manifestation of a spinal CSF leak. In unexplained or recurrent subdural hematomas, dedicated spinal CSF leak diagnostics should be considered, as definitive closure of the underlying leak represents the causal treatment.Keywords: Spinal CSF leak, Spontaneous intracranial hypotension, Acute subdural hematoma, Cerebrospinal fluid leak, Spinal longitudinal epidural collection (SLEC), Ventral dural tear

Introduction

Spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH) has an estimated annual incidence rate of around 4 per 100,000 and is caused by spontaneous cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) loss from a spinal CSF leak (Schievink et al., 2022). Spinal CSF leaks are categorized into three main subtypes: ventral dural tears (Type 1), lateral dural tears near the exiting nerve root sleeve (Type 2) and CSF–venous fistulas causing uncontrolled CSF outflow into the epidural venous system (Type 3) (Schievink et al., 2016). Chronic subdural hematoma (cSDH) occurs in 25.9% to 43.5% (Beck et al., 2014; El Rahal et al., 2023; Overstijns et al., 2025) of patients with SIH. Although the association between SIH and cSDH is well recognized, the development of an acute subdural hematoma (aSDH) as a primary manifestation of SIH remains exceedingly rare (Beck et al., 2014; Overstijns et al., 2025; Tanaka et al., 2016). Although the association between SIH and cSDH is well recognized, SIH-associated subdural hematomas reported in the literature are predominantly chronic, bilateral, or mixed-density lesions. Acute SDH has only rarely been described and has generally occurred in the setting of a pre-existing chronic subdural collection or following cranial intervention (Kuramae et al., 2011; Perez-Vega et al., 2020). An aSDH after an iatrogenic spinal leak has bean described, however a primary spontaneous, non-traumatic non-iatrogenic aSDH as the initial manifestation of a previously unrecognized spontaneous spinal CSF leak appears to be exceptionally rare (Sciubba et al., 2005).

We therefore present an illustrative case of a non-traumatic aSDH as the first manifestation of a spinal CSF leak requiring repeated neurosurgical intervention before definitive diagnosis and treatment of the underlying leak. In addition, we performed a systematic review of the literature on subdural hematomas associated with SIH/spinal CSF leaks to contextualize this rare acute presentation within the broader spectrum of SIH-related subdural bleeding.

Methods

Case report

Clinical, radiological, surgical, and follow-up data of the presented patient were retrospectively reviewed. The patient provided informed consent for publication. Institutional ethical approval was obtained (Ethical approval code: 24-1357_1-S1).

Review of the literature

The comprehensive review of the literature was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. PubMed and Embase databases were systematically searched for studies form 01.01.2000 until 31.03.2026 reporting subdural hematomas associated with spontaneous intracranial hypotension or spinal cerebrospinal fluid leaks using a standardized search term (Supplemental material 1). Cohort Studies, cases series, and case reports involving adult patients with SIH/spinal CSF leaks and associated subdural hematomas were included. Traumatic subdural hematomas and postoperative CSF leaks were excluded. Titles, abstracts, and full texts were screened for eligibility, and any uncertainties regarding study inclusion were resolved by discussion between two authors (MO, FV). Data regarding clinical presentation, hematoma characteristics, treatment strategies, and outcomes were extracted for qualitative analysis.

Results

Review of the literature

The literature search identified 317 records through PubMed and Embase database screening (Fig. 1). After title and abstract review, 286 studies were excluded because they did not report subdural hematomas associated with spontaneous intracranial hypotension or spinal CSF leaks. The remaining 31 articles underwent full-text assessment for eligibility. Following exclusion of 17 publications after full-text review, 14 studies were included in the final qualitative synthesis (Supplemental material 2).

Illustrative case

A 52-year-old man without pre-exisitng medical condition presented to the emergency department with sudden onset of severe, but not orthostatic, headache and nonspecific visual disturbances. There were no history or external signs of trauma. Cranial CT demonstrated a right hemispheric aSDH (coronal width of 12 mm), with severe mass effect and midline shift, prompting emergency neurosurgical admission for immediate evacuation (Fig. 2A). The patient denied antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication intake, and laboratory testing showed no evidence of coagulopathy. CT angiography demonstrated no vascular abnormality. Because of the unusual spontaneous aSDH, subsequent catheter digital subtraction angiography was performed and likewise demonstrated no aneurysm, arteriovenous malformation, or dural arteriovenous fistula. There was no imaging evidence of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis or relevant venous outflow obstruction on the available vascular imaging. There was no clinical history or cranial imaging finding suggestive of an underlying malignancy. The patient denied the intake of antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication and laboratory analysis revealed no evidence of coagulopathy. During the initial examination the patient was completely awake and conscious with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. However, he deteriorated rapidly while being transferred to the operating room and presented with a newly dilated right sided pupil just before skin incision. Immediate emergency craniotomy with hematoma evacuation was performed. Postoperatively, the pupils normalized and the patient was extubated immediately after surgery without neurological deficits. The postoperative CT showed adequate hematoma evacuation (Fig. 2B).

Ten days later, he again developed a severe headache and left-sided hemiparesis. CT imaging showed a cSDH with acute components, again with significant midline shift (Fig. 2 C). The patient underwent a second emergency craniotomy with hematoma evacuation (Fig. 2 D). Postoperatively, the hemiparesis completely resolved and he was discharged 6 days later, again without a neurological deficit. Three days later, the patient presented for the third time with a severe headache. CT imaging again showed a rightsided cSDH with moderate midline shift (Fig. 2 E). The patient underwent twist-drill craniostomy at the frontal eminence with placement of a subdural drain the same day. Postoperative CT confirmed a correct subdural position of the drain. The drain was removed the next day, and the patient remained without neurological deficits (Fig. 2 F).

Given the initial presentation of an aSDH without trauma, repeated exclusion of a vascular pathology combined with the unusual hematoma recurrence pattern, a focused spinal and cranial MRI imaging was performed. This revealed bilateral pachymeningeal enhancement, brain sagging, and small residual right sided cSDH (Fig. 3A and B) resulting in a Bern SIH score of 6 points (representing a high probability for a spinal CSF leak) (Dobrocky et al., 2019). Spinal MRT showed a “spinal longitudinal extradural CSF collection” (“SLEC positive”) (Fig. 3C and D), indicating a spinal dural tear. For exact localization of the spinal CSF leak, a dynamic digital subtraction myelography (DSM) in prone position was performed, demonstrating a ventral (Type 1) leak at T5/6 (Fig. 3 E), associated with a discogenic microspur (Fig. 3 F).

E) Dynamic Digital Subtraction Myelography (DSM) showing a ventral leak at of T5/6. F) Axial CT-Myelography following DSM shows a bony microspur.

The patient underwent minimally invasive microsurgical, transdural disconnection of fistula membranes and transdural funnels, removal of the osseous microspur and sandwich-technique closure of the ventral dural defect (Fig. 4A–D) (Häni et al., 2024) (Beck et al., 2023), (Volz et al., 2024a).

Note the deliberately oblique view directly through the retractor. B) Intraoperatve disconnection of fistula membranes (“funnels”) of the ventral leak at T5/6. C) Sandwich technique closure of the defect. D) Dorsal durotomy closure using running sutures.

Postoperatively, the patient experienced mild transient rebound hypertension headaches and a transient right-leg dysesthesia without motor deficit for two days, both of which resolved spontanously. At the six week follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic. MRI of the brain and spine showed clearly reduced brain sagging, and complete regression of the subdural hematoma and extrathecal CSF (now “SLEC negative”) (Fig. 5A–C). Postoperative CT showed the small bone defect of the hemilamina T5 right (Fig. 5D) after unilateral keyhole fenestration (Volz et al., 2024a).

A-B) 6 weeks postoperative MRI showing complete regression of subdural hematoma and obvious improvement of the brain sagging. C) Postoperative MRI of operated level T5/6 shows normal findings without relevant spinal cord compression or myelopathy, and disappearance of the Spinal Longitudinal Epidural Collection (SLEC). D) Postoperative CT showing the limited unilateral bone defect of right T5 hemilamina after the keyhole fenestration.

Discussion

The novel observation of the present case is that a primary spontaneous, non-traumatic and non-iatrogenic aSDH may represent the first manifestation of a previously unrecognized spontaneous spinal CSF leak. Although the association between SIH and subdural hematoma is well established, the available literature predominantly describes chronic, bilateral, or mixed acute-on-chronic subdural hematomas (Beck et al., 2014; El Rahal et al., 2023). Acute subdural hematoma has also been reported following iatrogenic spinal CSF leakage after dural injury during spine surgery (Sciubba et al., 2005; Lu et al., 2002). In our systematic review, we identified no previous report of a primary spontaneous, non-traumatic or non-iatrogenic aSDH occurring as the initial manifestation of a spontaneous spinal CSF leak without prior cranial or spinal intervention.

The literature review revealed that subdural hematomas associated with spontaneous spinal CSF leaks were predominantly chronic or mixed acute-on-chronic in nature. No case with an spontaneous, non-traumatic acute subdural hematoma as initial symptom was reported. Several cohort studies demonstrated that approximately 17–43% of patients with SIH develop concomitant subdural hematomas, most frequently bilateral chronic subdural hematomas (Overstijns et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2021). In a recent cohort study, among patients with SIH-related cSDH, 58.8% had type 1 ventral leaks, 15.3% had type 2 lateral leaks, and 25.9% had type 3 CSF-venous fistulas; conversely, cSDH prevalence was 31% in type 1, 22% in type 2, and 43% in type 3 leaks (Overstijns et al., 2025). Orthostatic headache remained the most common presenting symptom, reported in approximately 76–98% of patients, although atypical presentations without clear positional headache were repeatedly described (El Rahal et al., 2023), (Dehnavi et al., 2026) (D'Antona et al., 2021), (Luetzen et al., 2021).

Across the reviewed literature, recurrent, rapidly progressive, or otherwise unexplained subdural hematomas, particularly in younger patients without trauma or coagulopathy, were repeatedly emphasized as important clues to an underlying spinal CSF leak. (El Rahal et al., 2023; Overstijns et al., 2025).

Although the link between spinal CSF leaks and cSDH is well established (Beck et al., 2014; Overstijns et al., 2025; Takahashi et al., 2016), the presentation of an aSDH prior to any intervention remains very rare (Beck et al., 2014; Overstijns et al., 2025; de Noronha et al., 2003). The acute SDHs identified in our review occurred in the context of a pre-existing subdural collection, following placement or removal of a subdural drain for a pre-existing cSDH, or after a iatrogenic dural CSF leak following spinal surgery (Perez-Vega et al., 2020; Sciubba et al., 2005; Chotai et al., 2013; Wen et al., 2022).

In spinal CSF leak related cSDH, recurrence appears to depend most on whether the underlying spinal leak is definitively closed. In a surgical cohort, once the leak was surgically sealed, no cSDH recurred during follow-up, regardless of whether the hematoma had been managed conservatively or with cranial evacuation first (Overstijns et al., 2025). Surgical treatment of the spinal CSF leaks via minimally invasive approaches is a safe and effective technique that yields, in experienced centers, excellent outcomes with closure rates of around 95% and very low morbidity (Beck et al., 2023; Petutschnigg et al., 2025; El Rahal et al., 2025). By contrast, epidural blood patching can improve symptoms and sometimes control the leak, but recurrence or persistence can be as high as 48% (Hazama et al., 2023; Hashizume et al., 2013). Middle meningeal artery embolization may help reduce recurrence of non-acute SDH in general, but it does not treat the causal spinal CSF leak, although specific data on patients with spinal CSF leakage is lacking. The broader SDH literature and consensus documents describe MMA embolization as a recurrence-reducing adjunct, not a substitute for fixing the underlying SIH lesion (Siddiq et al., 2025; Maciaszek et al., 2025). In SIH-associated cSDH, this means MMA embolization may support hematoma control, but durable prevention of recurrence still depends on definitive leak closure.

The presumed pathophysiological mechanism underlying cSDH formation in patients with spinal CSF leaks is that continuous spinal CSF loss reduces intracranial CSF volume, resulting in brain sagging and traction on bridging veins, ultimately leading to subdural fluid collections and hemorrhage (Beck et al., 2014; El Rahal et al., 2023). This proposed mechanism is supported by radiological markers such as diffuse pachymeningeal enhancement, venous engorgement, all of which were present in this patient (Dobrocky et al., 2019; Luetzen et al., 2021; Miller and Nader, 2014; Yamashima and Friede, 1984). However, rupture of cortical and meningeal arteries or arterialized veins can also occur following loss of CSF volume, therefore an aSDH should not automatically argue against SIH as the underlying etiology (Miller and Nader, 2014; Rengachary and Szymanski, 1981). In patients presenting with aSDH without a history of trauma, alternative etiologies must be systematically excluded including vascular etiology (Akioka et al., 2007). These include ruptured intracranial aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, dural arteriovenous fistulas, cerebral venous pathology, coagulopathy, antithrombotic therapy, and less commonly intracranial malignancy. Although most aSDH are traumatic, non-traumatic cases have been associated with ruptured intracranial aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, dural arteriovenous fistulas, and venous hypertension (Orde, 2019; Beucler et al., 2019; Coombs et al., 2014). This case argues that spinal CSF leaks should be added to this differential diagnosis list. Especially in unusual cases or when a vascular cause has been clearly ruled out dedicated spinal CSF-leak diagnostics should be performed, including MRI of head and complete spine and potentially dynamic myelography techniques (Volz et al., 2024b).

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the available literature on SIH-associated subdural hematomas consists predominantly of retrospective series and isolated case reports, resulting in substantial heterogeneity regarding diagnostic workup, treatment strategies, and follow-up duration. Second, publication bias toward unusual or severe presentations cannot be excluded. Finally, due to the rarity of acute hemorrhagic presentations, quantitative subgroup analysis of primary aSDH was not feasible.

Conclusion

This illustrative case and literature review demonstrate that spinal CSF leaks should be considered among the differential diagnoses in patients presenting with non-traumatic or recurrent subdural hematomas, even in the absence of classical orthostatic headache. Although SIH-associated subdural hematomas are predominantly chronic in nature, rare spontaneous and non-traumatic acute presentations may occur that require emergency neurosurgical intervention. Recurrent or atypical hematoma patterns should therefore prompt dedicated spinal CSF leak diagnostics, including cranial and spinal MRI as well as dynamic myelographic techniques when indicated. While surgical evacuation of the hematoma may be necessary in symptomatic patients, definitive and durable treatment ultimately depends on successful closure of the underlying spinal CSF leak, resulting in resolution of both intracranial and spinal manifestations.
