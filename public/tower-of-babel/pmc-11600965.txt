Selection and validation of reference genes for qRT-PCR normalization in dayflower (Commelina communis) based on the transcriptome profiling
Authors: Yuejiao Yang, Gaohui Cao, Lulu Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDayflower (Commelina communis), a widely invasive weed, thrives well under a variety of abiotic stresses, including drought and herbicides, and harms the growth of crops such as maize and soybean. Gene expression in dayflower is an important but understudied area due to the lack of reliable reference genes.ResultsFifteen candidate reference genes, which are common reference genes and homologous to those used in other plants, were selected through RNA-seq datasets of dayflower. The expression stability of these screened reference genes was evaluated under three abiotic stresses (drought, herbicide and copper) and in five organs (roots, stems, leaves, flowers and seeds) using five commonly used software programs (geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, ΔCt and RefFinder). The results showed that API5 and SAND had the highest stability in stems, while SAND, EF1A and API5 had the highest stability in roots. Moreover, SAND, ALDH113 and API5 were most stably expressed under copper stress, and EF1A, SAND and API5 were most stable under drought stress. SAND was consistently the most stably expressed gene in both the organs and all samples. Notably, The SAND gene ranked among the top three in terms of stability in all abiotic treatments and in various organs. This result indicates that the SAND gene is suitable for qRT-PCR experimentation in diverse tissues and under multiple (drought, herbicide and copper) abiotic stress conditions in dayflower.ConclusionThis study identified the most stably expressed reference genes under three abiotic stresses and in five organs of dayflower, and SAND showed high expression stability under various experimental conditions, making it a reliable reference gene for gene expression analysis experiments under different conditions in dayflower. This study will enhance the precision of the qRT-PCR quantification of candidate genes related to the adaptation significance of dayflower.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05853-4.Keywords: Dayflower, Reference genes, Quantitative real-time PCR, RNA-seq datasets, Expression stability

Introduction

The dayflower (Commelina communis L.), a widespread annual herb of Commelinaceae, has drawn extensive attention for its tolerance to abiotic stress and unique heterantherous characteristics. Due to its high resistance to drought and herbicides, this weed can easily invade agricultural fields and cause yield loss in maize and soybean [1]. It is also an important accumulator of heavy metals, with its roots, stems and leaves accumulating large amounts of copper [2, 3]. In addition, the heterantherous flower of dayflower provides an interesting model for floral evolution [4]. Therefore, studying gene expression in dayflower could provide insights for weed control in agricultural fields and support the research on heteranthery.

Several techniques can be employed to study gene expression, including semiquantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), Northern blotting and in situ hybridization [5]. Among them, qRT-PCR is widely regarded as the preferred technique for the detection of gene expression due to its affordability, specificity, high sensitivity and high throughput [6–8]. However, the gene expression level can be significantly impacted by various factors, including initial material type, reverse transcription efficiency and extraction protocols [9]. To correct for these variables and ensure accurate measurements, stable reference genes must be used as internal controls under various experimental conditions [10].

The importance of using validated reference genes has been widely accepted especially in tissue samples [10, 11]. As housekeeping genes have stable expression levels in all tissues or cells and are not affected by the external environment, they are commonly used as reference genes for standardized analysis of target gene expression [12]. However, fluctuations in the transcript levels of housekeeping genes has been demonstrated in different species, tissues, organs and environmental conditions [13–15]. Therefore, to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the qRT-PCR results, stable expression reference genes for dayflower are required under specific experimental conditions.

The reference genes selected from transcriptome sequencing data have demonstrated greater stability compared to those traditionally in use [8, 16, 17]. In this study, we firstly analyzed transcriptome data of C. communis and screened out 15 candidate reference genes, which are common reference genes and are homologous to those used in other plants. These genes include Actin (ACT), Elongation Factor 1 Alpha (EF1A), Cytochrome P450 (CYP), Radical-Induced Cell Death 1 (RCD1), Ubiquitin 11 (UBQ11), SAND Family Protein (SAND), Aldehyde Dehydrogenase 113 (ALDH113), Phytochelatin Synthase 1 (PCS1), Tubulin (TUB), Ribosomal Binding Protein 2 (RBD2), Apoptosis Inhibitor 5 (API5), Histone H2A (H2A), Ubiquitin 3 (UBQ3), Protein Phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) and Alcohol Dehydrogenase 1 (ADH1). Then, we evaluated the candidates under stress treatment conditions and in different organs. The identification of stable reference genes aims to provide essential tools for future research on gene expression in dayflower, to support weed management in agriculture, and to advance studies on heteranthery-related gene regulation mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Plant material treatments and growth conditions

Seeds of dayflower (Commelina communis) were collected from the place near the West Lake Park in Changsha City, Hunan Province (112.94˚E; 28.21˚N) through local forestry bureaus. All seeds of dayflower were identified by Associate Professor Tang Lulu of Central South University. Voucher specimens (CC03051788) were deposited at the Central South University Herbarium. Seeds of dayflower were cultivated using 50% Hoagland nutrient medium in a chamber with a 12/12 h day/night cycle, an averaged temperature of 28.0 ± 1.0℃, a humidity level of 70 ± 10%, and a light intensity of 1000 µM photons m− 2 s− 1. The selection of different levels of abiotic stresses (drought, herbicide and copper) was based on the known tolerance of dayflower as a weed to drought and herbicides, as well as its capability as a copper hyperaccumulator. Four-week-old plant materials were disposed to three different abiotic stresses including drought, copper and herbicide stress. Under drought stress, roots of seedlings were placed in culture flasks containing 25 g/L mannitol solution and treated for different duration (0, 6, 12, 24 and 48 h). Under herbicide treatment, roots of seedlings were immersed in culture flasks with the application of 50% atrazine solution at different concentrations (0, 0.17, 0.35, 0.70, 1.4 and 2.8 g/L) for 48 h. Under copper treatment, roots of seedlings were placed in culture flasks containing CuSO4 at different concentrations (0, 100, 500, 1000 and 2000 µmol/L) for 48 h. Untreated seedlings served as controls. Morphological studies were conducted by measuring plant height, number of leaves and root length using a straightedge, and images of the dayflower morphology were taken by Canon 750D camera. Roots, stems, leaves, flowers and seeds were sampled in four independent replicates. Samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at − 80℃ for subsequent use.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

The samples (roots, stems, leaves, flowers and seeds) of dayflower weighing 80–120 mg were fully ground into a fine powder using the Tissuelyser® (Jingxin, Shanghai, China). Total RNAs were extracted using a FastPure Universal Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). During isolation, RNase-free DNase I digestion was used to remove genomic DNA (gDNA). The NANO-200 ultramicro nucleic acid analyzer (AoSheng, Hangzhou, China) was used for the determination of RNA purity and concentration. Only RNA samples with A260/280 ratios within the range of 1.8 to 2.2 and A260/230 ratios over 2.0 were considered suitable for cDNA synthesis. The concentration of RNA used for qRT-PCR was standardized to 100 ng/µL for all samples. The First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, Watham, MA, USA) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol with a template amount of 1 µg per sample for cDNA synthesis, and the resulting cDNA was stored at − 20℃.

Selection of candidate reference genes and design of primers

Homologues of the ACT, EF1A, CYP, RCD1, UBQ11, SAND, ALDH113, PCS1, TUB, RBD2, API5, H2A, UBQ3, PP2A and ADH1 genes were extracted from the reference transcriptomes derived from three stamen types in dayflower. RNA-seq experiments were performed using the TruSeq mRNA library construction kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) followed by sequencing on the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform. Sequencing was performed using a unique technology developed by Personalbio (Shanghai, China) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Trinity (r20140717, k-mer 25 bp) was used for de novo assembly of the transcriptome. The assembled transcriptome was subjected to a rigorous homologous gene search using the BLASTN algorithm, with gene sequences of ACT, EF1A, CYP, RCD1, UBQ11, SAND, ALDH113, PCS1, TUB, RBD2, API5, H2A, UBQ3, PP2A and ADH1 from Arabidopsis thaliana used as reference queries. The specific primer pairs were designed with PrimerBLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) using the following settings: a primer length between 18 and 25 bp, an amplicon length ranging from 80 to 150 base pairs, melting temperatures (Tm) between 55 and 60℃, and GC content between 45% and 55%.

Quantitative real-time PCR conditions

The LightCycler 96 instrument (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used for qRT-PCR analysis in a 96-well plate. The qRT-PCR reaction mixture consisted of 20 µL, comprising 10 µL of SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Watham, MA, USA, Document Connect (thermofisher.cn)), 1 µL of diluted cDNA, 1 µL each of upstream and downstream primers (20 µmol/L), and ddH2O to reach the final volume. The reaction procedures for the qRT-PCR were carried out as follows: 95 ℃ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 15 s, 60℃ for 30 s, 95℃ for 30 s and a final step of 60℃ for 30 s.

Stability assessment of reference genes

Microsoft Excel 2021 data analysis software was used to collate and summarise qRT-PCR data from all samples. Comparison of reference gene expression stability across all treatments was performed using geNorm [18], NormFinder [10], BestKeeper [19], ΔCt and RefFinder [20, 21] (https://blooge.cn/RefFinder/). The geNorm algorithm considers reference genes with M values (expression stability value) below 1.5 have stable expression, with lower M values indicating a higher level of stability. Similarly, if any primers exhibit a pairwise variation (Vn/n+1) value greater than 1.5, additional reference genes should be used for normalization. The M values and Vn/n+1 values for all reference genes were calculated using the online tool (https://seqyuan.shinyapps.io/seqyuan_prosper/). The stability of the reference genes was assessed using NormFinder, with a lower SV indicating higher stability. The BestKeeper uses the coefficient of variation (CV) and standard deviation (SD) of the cycle threshold (Ct) values to assess the stability value of a gene, with the lowest CV ± SD value indicating the highest stability. For the ΔCt method, the stability of the reference gene was evaluated by pairwise comparison of genes. The RefFinder program was used to comprehensively rank the geometric means of all candidate reference genes by four statistical analysis softwares (geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper and ΔCt methods).

Validation of reference gene stability

The reliability of the screened reference genes was validated by examining the relative expression level of the WRKY gene in dayflower leaves under drought and copper stress. The experimental design included four biological replicates. The analysis included both the most stable and unstable reference genes after normalization using RefFinder, as well as combinations of stable reference genes. The sequence of the WRKY gene was derived from the dayflower transcriptome data. The qRT-PCR amplification conditions for the target genes were the same as previously described conditions. Relative expression data were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method [22] and presented as levels of relative expression levels.

Results

Abiotic stress treatments of plant material

Dayflower individuals were subjected to three treatments: drought, herbicide and copper. In the drought treatment, there were no significant changes in leaves from 0 to 24 h, but plants turned yellow and the leaves were crumpled after 48 h (Fig. 1). In the herbicide treatments, there were few changes in plants and leaves at concentrations of 0.35, 0.7 and 1.4 g/L and at 2.8 g/L concentration plants wilted and leaves showed clear signs of intoxication. In the copper treatment, there were no significant changes in plants and leaves at concentrations of 100, 500 and 1000 µmol/L, but there was yellowing of plants and leaves at 2000 µmol/L concentration.

Figures of leaves under drought, herbicide and copper stresses

Selection of candidate reference genes and expression stability analysis

In this study, fifteen candidate reference genes (ACT, EF1A, CYP, RCD1, UBQ11, SAND, ALDH113, PCS1, TUB, RBD2, API5, H2A, UBQ3, PP2A and ADH1) were selected from the dayflower RNA-seq data. The annealing temperatures of the primers ranged from 53.3℃ to 57.8℃, and the lengths of the amplification products varied from 93 bp (PCS1) to 140 bp (H2A) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). Gene expression of 15 candidate reference genes was assessed by qRT-PCR experiments (four biological replicates and three technical replicates). Primers specificity was assessed using melting curve analysis, which showed that all target reference genes produced single peak melting curves, indicating that the primers had adequate specificity. Ct values are a direct indicator of gene expression, with lower Ct values indicating higher levels of gene expression. When analyzing gene expression under non-stress conditions in different organs (root, stem, leaf, flower and seed), the Ct values for these reference genes showed a wide range of variability from 14.51 to 38.64. Among them, EF1A (14.51–18.56) showed the least variation, followed by SAND (20.04–24.16) and ALDH113 (21.7-25.13), indicating their potential as stable reference genes in different tissues. However, H2A (29.61–38.38) and ADH1 (33.11–37.62) showed the lowest expression levels, indicating their unsuitability as reference genes (Fig. 2). Therefore, H2A and ADH1 were removed from the following experiments.

geNorm analysis

In the geNorm analyses, the M value was used as a quantitative measure of expression stability, with lower values indicating higher stability of the reference genes. In the drought-stressed roots, the M values of EF1A and TUB were the lowest, indicating that their highest stability, while the M value of PP2A was the highest, indicating that its expression to be the most unstable (Fig. 3A). In the drought-stressed leaves, the M values of all reference genes were consistently below 1.5, indicating that all reference genes were found to be available (Fig. 3B). In the drought-stressed leaves, ACT and SAND showed the most stable expression levels (Fig. 3C). In the herbicide treatment, the M values of UBQ3 in roots, stems and leaves were all greater than 1.2, indicating that UBQ3 was the least stable reference gene (Fig. 3D, E, F). In copper treatment, except for UBQ11 with an M value > 1.5 in the leaves, all other genes had values < 1.5. Therefore, all genes except UBQ11 can be considered suitable reference genes to normalize target gene expression under copper stress conditions (Fig. 3G, H, I). For the organs and all samples, SAND was consistently ranked as the most stable reference gene (Fig. 3J, K).

The expression stability was evaluated in samples from organs, in all samples, in the roots and leaves of dayflower subjected to drought, copper and herbicide. We also performed calculations to determine the optimal number of reference genes by analyzing pairwise variation (Vn/n+1) (Fig. 4). The V2/3 values remained consistently below 1.5 in drought-stressed roots, stems and leaves, as well as in copper-stressed stems, leaves and all samples. Therefore, in these cases, 2 reference genes should be used for qRT-PCR experiments. In herbicide-stressed stems and leaves, the V3/4 values were less than 1.5, and it is advisable to use 3 reference genes for qRT-PCR experiments in these cases. For copper-stressed roots and organs, the V4/5 values were less than 1.5, so qRT-PCR experiments using 4 reference genes are recommended in these cases. For herbicide stressed roots, the V5/6 values were less than 1.5, therefore it is recommended to use 5 reference genes for qRT-PCR experiments conducted on herbicide stressed roots.

The NormFinder analyses showed that SAND and API5 had the highest stability under drought stress, while SAND, RBD2 and API5 had the highest stability under herbicide stress conditions. In addition, RBD2, ALDH113 and TUB were identified as the most stable reference genes under copper stress conditions. Validation of candidate reference genes

The stability of the identified reference genes was assessed by examining the expression of the WRKY gene in leaves subjected to drought and copper stress. This analysis included normalization with SAND, EF1A, their combination and the least stable reference gene UBQ3. When SAND, EF1A and their combination were used as reference genes, the levels of WRKY expression remained constant. However, the normalization of target genes with UBQ3 showed significant variations compared to the utilization of stable reference genes. When SAND, EF1A, their combination and UBQ3 were used as the reference genes, the levels of WRKY expression were consistent at 0, 24 and 48 h under drought stress. However, the normalization results obtained using UBQ3 showed significant differences compared to those obtained using stable reference genes at 6 and 12 h under drought stress (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S4). When SAND, EF1A and their combination as well as UBQ3 were used as the reference genes, the expression levels of WRKY were relatively consistent at CuSO4 concentrations of 100 µmol/L, 500 µmol/L and 1000 µmol/L. However, the normalization results using UBQ3 compared to using the stable reference genes at CuSO4 concentrations of 2000 µmol/L showed significant variations (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

In Commelina communis, the SAND gene emerged as the most consistently stable across treatments and tissues, providing a reliable reference for gene expression studies in this species. To confirm the reliability of the screened reference genes, the expression level of the WRKY gene was examined under two abiotic conditions using SAND, EF1A and their combination, as well as the UBQ3 gene. WRKY transcription factors are one of the largest families of transcriptional regulators in plants and play a crucial role in the response to abiotic stresses [23, 24]. Under stress conditions such as drought, herbicide and CuSO4, the expression of the WRKY gene is expected to increase or remain stable [25–27]. The validation experiment expectations was as expected (Fig. 5). Therefore, the reference genes, SAND, selected in this study are considered reliable.

The SAND gene is widespread across different eukaryotic organisms, and the protein it encodes is remarkably conserved, highlighting its potential importance in essential cellular processes [28]. In general, the SAND protein is predicted to localize in the nucleus, where it may play a role in transcriptional regulation [29]. However, studies have also suggested that in some plant species, SAND protein may be localize in the cytoplasm or plasma membrane, suggesting potential versatility in function depending on the organism or cell type [28, 30]. Although the precise function of the SAND protein is not fully understood, the presence of several highly conserved domains within its structure strongly suggests involvement in gene regulation processes [28]. Specifically, these domains may enable the protein to participate in the assembly of transcription factor complexes or to bind directly to DNA, thereby contributing to the regulation of gene expression [31]. Given its fundamental and structural role in cellular activities, SAND emerges as a strong candidate for a reference gene.

The stability of the SAND gene observed in our analyses is consistent with findings in other plant species. For example, SAND has been identified as a stable reference gene in Vaccinium macrocarpon [32], Taxus spp [33] and Solanum rostratum [34]. In two sets of samples comprising different cranberry (V. macrocarpon) organs and three abiotic stress treatments (salt, alkali and drought), organs under three abiotic stress treatments (salt, alkali and drought), SAND was identified as the optimal reference gene [32]. Under all experimental conditions, SAND and GAPDH1 showed the highest stability as reference genes in Taxus spp [33]. For S. rostratum, SAND and PP2A were found to be the most reliable reference genes [34]. In addition, RefFinder showed that API5 ranked first in terms of stability in the stems of all treatments and exhibited the highest stability in the stems. EF1A and RBD2 were relatively high in all treatments and showed good stability. Based on these results, SAND is recommended to be used in combination with other stable reference genes suitable for specific experimental conditions to improve the accuracy of qRT-PCR results.

Compared to traditional reference genes such as ACT or TUB [35, 36], the SAND, API5, EF1A and RBD2 genes show improved stability in C. communis, as demonstrated by our comprehensive software analysis using geNorm, NormFinder, BestKeeper, ΔCt and RefFinder.This multi-method validation approach highlights the importance of selecting species-specific and condition-specific reference genes rather than relying solely on conventional choices. By improving the reliability of gene expression normalization in C. communis, our study provides a solid foundation for future research into stress adaptation mechanisms and genetic regulation in this species.

Conclusion

In our study, the expression stability of 13 candidate reference genes was investigated in different abiotic stress conditions and tissues of dayflower using five statistical methods. In addition, we investigated the expression pattern of the WRKY gene in dayflower leaves under drought and copper stress to further validate the reliability of the identified stable reference genes. Through screening and validation, the SAND gene was identified as a stable reference gene in different abiotic stress conditions and tissues of dayflower. The results of this study will facilitate more reliable gene expression analyses and support further functional genomics studies in this species.
