Experimental evaluation of nanobiochar and metallic oxide nanoparticles with Croton macrostachyus seed oil biodiesel on combustion and emission characteristics
Authors: Adem Siraj Mohammed, Venkata Ramayya Ancha, Samson Mekbib Atnaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiomass-derived nanoparticles offer a safer alternative to metallic oxides, yet their use in diesel–biodiesel blends remains limited. This study explores the catalytic effect of corn stover–derived nanobiochar (NBC) on the performance, combustion, and emissions of Croton macrostachyus (CMS) biodiesel, compared with Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ nanoparticles, at 2700 RPM and 0–80% load. The novelty lies in revealing NBC’s influence on CP, apparent HRR, and ID period in CMS biodiesel blends. Experiments were conducted using six fuel blends: B20 (20% CMS biodiesel + 80% diesel), B20NBC30, B20NBC50, B20NBC75 (with 30, 50, and 75 ppm NBC, respectively), B20A50 (with 50 ppm Al₂O₃), and B20T50 (with 50 ppm TiO₂). The result shows that the NBC additive reduced the NOx, CO, and HC emissions by up to 9.8%, 8.4%, and 27.3% respectively, and BSFC by 2.2% and increased BTE by 2.6% compared to the B20. Conversely, TiO₂ and Al₂O₃ were more effective in reducing HC emissions, achieving reductions of 45.9% and 29.5% respectively, while reducing BSFC and increasing BTE compared to NBC additive. In addition, B20NBC50 exhibited an average reduction in cylinder pressure (CP) of 0.5%, heat release rate (HRR) of 517.51 J/oCA with a 2.7% increment and the shortest ignition delay (ID) and advanced start of combustion (SoC) compared to the B20, B20NBC30, and B20NBC75, that makes NBC at 50 ppm as the optimum concentration. NBC reduced NOx due to its higher specific heat capacity, while Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ increased it, demonstrating NBC’s advantage and potential as a sustainable, biomass-derived alternative to metallic nanoparticles.Keywords: Nanobiochar, Biodiesel, Cylinder pressure, Heat release rate, Croton macrostachyusSubject terms: Biodiesel, Mechanical engineering, Environmental impact

Introduction

Renowned for their robust efficiency and longevity under demanding conditions, diesel engines lend themselves particularly well to the demands of heavy-duty vehicles, owing to their remarkable durability, significant torque output, and higher fuel efficiency1. However, their widespread use presents a considerable challenge, such as the inherent trade-off between high NOx and particulate matter emissions2. Given the crucial role of energy in national economies, the increasing use of petroleum diesel highlights the limited supply of fossil fuels, which necessitates a transition to renewable and sustainable alternatives for energy security3–5. The escalating global energy demand, driven by population growth, urbanization, and expanding economies (especially in developing countries), contrasts with the unequal distribution of fossil fuels, creating geopolitical risks for energy-importing nations6,7. Biodiesel presents a strong alternative due to its low sulfur content, good lubricity, and high cetane number for better ignition8–10. Blending biodiesel with conventional diesel offers reduced emissions, improved engine performance, higher cetane, less wear, and lower fuel/oil consumption11–13. The shift to renewable energy is essential to tackle environmental, economic, and geopolitical issues14,15. Renewables, including biodiesel, help mitigate climate change, promote sustainability, enhance energy security, and stimulate economic growth16,17. With advancing technology and decreasing costs, renewable energy is poised to dominate the future global energy paradigm18. Biodiesel’s benefits include production from renewable sources, enhanced combustion due to oxygen content, and good lubrication19–21. However, its higher viscosity and density, along with lower calorific value than diesel, can negatively impact engine performance, emissions, and combustion22. Other drawbacks include increased NOx emissions, poor cold-weather performance, and potential clogging requiring more frequent replacement of fuel system components23,24. These limitations can be partially mitigated by using additives like oxygenates25,26, cetane improvers27, nanoparticles28, and antioxidants29.

Nanoparticles (1–100 nm) are of interest for their distinct physicochemical properties. Incorporating them into biodiesel aims to improve combustion and reduce emissions due to their high surface area and reactivity, leading to better fuel atomization, mixing, and flame stability, thus shortening ignition delay and enhancing heat transfer30. Metal-based nanoparticles, acting as oxygen buffers, further enhance thermal conductivity and reactivity, increasing evaporation and combustion efficiency28. A key mechanism for their combustion improvement is the significant reduction of ignition delay and the facilitation of secondary atomization31. Cerium oxide (CeO2)32, Graphene oxide (GO)33, aluminum oxide (Al2O3)34, carbon nanotube (CNT)35, copper oxide (CuO)36 and titanium oxide (TiO2)37 are the most commonly used nanoparticles additives to diesel/biodiesel blended fuels38. Incorporating cerium oxide nanoparticles into biodiesel elevates the cetane number and fuel efficiency of diesel engines. Similarly, aluminum oxide nanoparticle blends in biodiesel enhance performance, reduce emissions, and accelerate the heat release rate during combustion39. Generally, the inclusion of nanoparticles in biodiesel significantly improves performance characteristics, yielding notable enhancements and minimizing emissions40,41. Specifically, aluminum oxide nanoparticles are recognized as an effective nano-additive for reducing all emission components while simultaneously boosting engine performance42. Carbon nanotubes, possessing superior thermal and chemical properties compared to metal-based counterparts, further enhance nanofluid thermal properties due to their high thermal conductivity43,44. They also act as catalysts, improving the fuel’s cetane number and burning rate45.

The addition of nanoparticles to diesel–biodiesel blends does not increase the total heat of combustion but enhances contact between fuel molecules, facilitating reactant diffusion to particle surfaces and increasing reactivity46. Despite their benefits, nanoparticle inclusion raises stability concerns, as nanofluids tend to aggregate due to their large surface areas and high surface activity47. Studies48,49 have shown that nanoparticle coagulation can lead to particle settlement and increase the risk of fuel injector clogging. This tendency can be mitigated through agitation, careful selection of surfactants, and proper addition, which reduce aggregation50. Small particle sizes and high surface energies also promote agglomeration, and high electrolyte concentrations can lower zeta potential, producing unstable nano-suspensions51. Various methods—such as sonication, stirring, shear homogenization, pH control, and surfactant addition—have been proposed to enhance nanofluid stability52. Zeta potential measurement, which quantifies the potential difference between the stationary fluid layer around dispersed particles and the bulk fluid, is commonly used to assess stability53. Large positive or negative zeta potential values indicate electrically stabilized nanofluids, while low values promote coagulation or flocculation50.

While metallic nanoparticles (NPs) have shown high efficiency in improving engine performance and reducing emissions with diesel/biodiesel blends, their release in exhaust gases poses serious health and environmental risks54–57. For instance, Hassan et al.58 reported that Nickel and Aluminum NPs increase the formation of nucleation-mode particles, which are particularly harmful to human health. Similarly, Taylor et al.59 evaluated the toxicity of metal oxide NPs such as TiO2, ZnO, Fe2O4, Al2O2, and CrO2 (30–45 nm), finding ZnO to be highly toxic, Al2O2 moderately toxic, and TiO2 and Fe2O4 slightly toxic. CrO₃ showed no toxicity at the tested concentrations. Prolonged exposure to metallic NPs has been linked to a range of health issues, including DNA damage, oxidative stress, lung inflammation, and cytotoxic effects on the respiratory, nervous, digestive, and integumentary systems56,60–63. Moreover, the uncontrolled release of these particles contributes to environmental pollution and raises serious public health concerns64–66. Effective residue control is essential, including the use of nanoparticle filtration systems such as a diesel particulate filter (DPF). However, filtration methods such as nano-membrane filters may introduce backpressure, negatively affecting engine performance51. Therefore, safer alternatives like carbon-based nanomaterials (e.g., graphite, nanobiochar) are gaining attention due to their lower toxicity and environmental compatibility67. In summary, before metallic or carbon-based nanoparticles are widely adopted in fuel technologies, their health impacts, safe concentration limits, and efficient emission control methods must be thoroughly assessed68,69.

A promising and eco-friendly alternative to metallic fuel additives is the use of biomass-derived nanoparticles in biodiesel. Nanobiochar (NBC), a carbon-rich material derived from biomass waste, offers a cost-effective and environmentally sustainable alternative to metallic additives for enhancing diesel engine performance. Nanobiochar, in particular, offers performance-enhancing benefits while minimizing toxicity concerns70. Its use not only improves fuel characteristics but also promotes sustainable biomass resource management. These bio-based additives are gaining attention for CI engines due to their low cost and wide availability. Studies on rice husk and coconut shell-derived nanoparticles have shown improved engine performance and reduced emissions. For instance, coconut shell nanoparticles enhanced performance and lowered emissions with only a slight rise in fuel consumption71, while rice husk nanoparticles increased brake thermal efficiency (BTE) and reduced brake-specific fuel consumption (BSFC), carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions72. Ardebili et al.73 demonstrated that sugarcane-derived nanobiochar significantly reduced NOx and unburned hydrocarbons (UHC) by approximately 20.5% and 25%, respectively, in a fusel oil–diesel blend. In contrast, fusel oil alone led to a 33% increase in CO emissions. Similarly, Mirbagheri et al.74 reported that nanobiochar used in a diesel–biodiesel–ethanol (DBE) blend reduced NOx by 15% at 100 ppm, lowered CO emissions from 0.03% to 0.015%, and achieved a 28% reduction in HC emissions at 125 ppm. Notably, nano-biochar outperformed graphene oxide (GO), which—while reducing UHC by 17–26%—increased NOx emissions by 4–9%. Although previous studies highlight nanobiochar’s potential to enhance engine performance and reduce emissions, they have not examined combustion parameters such as cylinder pressure (CP), heat release rate (HRR), and ignition delay (ID), nor fully characterized key physicochemical properties like surface area-to-volume ratio, zeta potential, and elemental composition, which are influenced by preparation methods. This study uniquely investigates the combustion behavior of nanobiochar-enriched CMS biodiesel blends, offering novel insights into bio-based nano-additives.

In this study, biodiesel was produced from Croton macrostachyus (CMS) seed oil, a recently introduced second-generation feedstock that grows abundantly in Ethiopia75. CMS was selected for its high oil yield of 45–53% from seeds76–79 in addition to 38.63% from its leaves as a bonus80. There is also a need to explore diversify the feedstocks for biodiesel production. The previous few investigations into CMS biodiesel in CI engines have reported notable performance and emission trends. However, there still is a knowledge gap concerning combustion and emission characteristics with different additives. For instance, Zamba and Reshad76 evaluated CMS-diesel blends (5–25%) and observed increased BSFC and EGT, with reduced BTE compared to diesel. Emissions showed elevated CO₂ and NOx, but decreased CO and HC. Mohammed et al.18 further assessed performance, combustion, and emissions across varying blend ratios and engine loads at constant speed. Results showed 33.3–51.8% lower HC emissions, shorter ignition delay, 67–102% higher NOₓ, increased BSFC and EGT, decreased BTE, and elevated HRR and peak cylinder pressure. Additionally, Mohammed et al.81 explored the combined effects of injection timing, blend ratio, and engine speed variations on performance (BTE, BSFC) and emissions (NOx, CO2), applying multi-objective optimization. They concluded that advanced injection timing with higher blend ratios at optimal speeds enhances performance while mitigating NOx emissions.

This study investigates the incorporation of NBC into CMS biodiesel–diesel blends in CI engines, a novel aspect not previously reported. The primary objective is to assess NBC’s impact on engine performance, combustion behavior, and emissions. To achieve this, experimental tests were conducted using CMS seed oil methyl ester blended with varying NBC concentrations (30, 50, and 75 ppm) and benchmarked against blends containing metallic oxide nanoparticles Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ at 50 ppm. Recognizing the potential carcinogenic risks associated with metallic oxide nanoparticles, Al2​O3 and TiO2 nanoparticles were included in this study solely for comparative purposes. They served as established benchmarks to effectively evaluate the performance and efficacy of the NBC as a fuel additive. This study contributes significantly to alternative fuel research by offering a comparative assessment of biomass-derived NBC and metallic nanoparticle additives within the same biodiesel-diesel matrix. A key novelty of this research lies in analyzing the catalytic influence of NBC on combustion parameters such as cylinder pressure (CP), heat release rate (HRR), and ignition delay (ID), and its associated impact on engine performance and emissions. These effects were systematically compared with those of Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ nanoparticles, for which such a comparison had not been previously reported. Additionally, the physicochemical characteristics of NBC, including particle size, surface area, zeta potential, functional groups, elemental composition, and morphology, were comprehensively characterized to better understand its behavior as a nano-additive in fuel systems.

Materials and methods

Equipment and materials

For this research, biodiesel derived from CMS seed oil, with physicochemical properties aligned with those reported in78, was utilized. Biochar synthesis employed corn stover biomass and a muffle furnace (Nabertherm P330), followed by the production of nanobiochar using a ball mill (NANO INTECH Ltd., KOREA). NBC underwent comprehensive characterization using a suite of analytical equipment. A Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectrometer was employed to elucidate its functional group composition while Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy-Dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX, COXEM Korea) was utilized to analyze surface morphology. The specific surface area was quantified using a surface area analyzer (Horiba SA-9600), and the nanocrystalline properties were determined via X-ray Diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’pert powder XRD system). Particle size and zeta potential were assessed using a Zetasizer nano ZS (Malvern). For comparative analysis, Aluminum oxide (Al2O3) and Titanium oxide (TiO2) nanoparticles (Nano Research Lab, India) were also characterized using SEM.

Methods

Nanoparticle Preparation and characterization

The biochar was produced from corn stover (CSV) through the process of pyrolysis using a muffle furnace (Nabertherm P330) at 550 °C for 3 h in an oxygen-free environment. The nitrogen flow rate of 3.5 l/min was used to purge oxygen and create an inert environment inside the pyrolysis furnace. The resulting biochar was ground using an ultrafine grinder to a size of 43 μm. The fine particle was continuously washed using distilled water to remove the black dye from the surface of the biochar fine powder. Then, the wet ball milling method was used to synthesize a nano biochar with ball to biochar mass ratio of 50:1 and 30 ml distilled water. The mixture of zirconia balls, biochar sample, and distilled water was added into the cylindrical jar. Then, the cylindrical jars containing the mixture were inserted into the jar holder and put on the ball mill table. The ball mill table was adjusted to work at 300 RPM for 16 h. Finally, the biochar powder mixed with the distilled water was separated from the balls. The resulting powder was also then centrifuged (Centrifuge 80-2B) at 10,000 RPM for 2 min. The supernatant with nano fraction was collected, and oven dried on petri dishes at 120 °C. Furthermore, the nanobiochar characterizations were performed using a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM), XRD, FTIR, Zeta size, and surface area analyzer.

SEM-EDX (COXEM, Korea) characterized NBC, Al2O3, and TiO2 composition/structure. Gold-coated nanoparticles were imaged by SEM for morphology/shape, crucial for the catalyst evaluation. EDX detects characteristic X-rays emitted from the sample, generating a spectrum that identifies constituent elements and their relative amounts. This elemental analysis was used to confirm the presence of the required elements as well as detect the presence of impurities that could impact catalytic performance. FTIR was also used to determine the NBC catalyst’s chemical composition, purity, and potential contaminants. To identify functional groups (within the 4000–400 cm − 1 range), FTIR provided insights into the catalyst’s properties and the mechanism of the transesterification reaction. Furthermore, the NBC surface area, a key determinant of catalytic performance, was measured using a Horiba SA-9600 analyzer. The catalyst was degassed at 150 °C for 60 min. BET theory was applied to nitrogen gas adsorption data at a constant temperature to calculate the specific surface area (m²/g), indicating the available reaction sites. Finally, the particle size and zeta potential of NBC were measured using Dynamic light scattering (DLS) Zeta sizer (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK). This technique analyzes the diffusion speed of nanoparticles undergoing Brownian motion by precisely controlling the temperature and studying the speckle pattern produced when particles are illuminated by a laser.

Test fuel preparation

The CMS seed oil biodiesel was prepared by transesterification, and its property characterization, adapted for this study, is detailed in78. The NBC was blended with 20% vol. of CMS biodiesel and 80% vol. of diesel (B20) at a concentration of 30, 50, and 75 ppm. Furthermore, the Al2O3 and titanium TiO2 nanoparticles with 50 ppm concentration each separately were used in B20 to compare with nanobiochar. The biodiesel and nanoparticle mixture underwent ultrasonication for 20 min using Soniprep 150plus (MSE, UK) to ensure thorough homogenization and uniform additive dispersion. No surfactant was used in the test fuel mixture. Following this, the resulting blend was allowed to stand undisturbed for seven days to assess its stability and monitor for any signs of component separation and coagulation. Subsequent testing was only performed after confirming the absence of phase separation. The number of fuel samples is B20 (20% biodiesel in diesel), B20NBC30 (20% biodiesel and 30 ppm nano biochar in diesel), B20NBC50 (20% diesel and 50 ppm of nano biochar), B20NBC75 (20% diesel and 50 ppm of nano biochar), B20A50 (20% diesel and 50 ppm of aluminum oxide nanoparticles), and B20T50 (20% diesel and 50 ppm of titanium oxide nanoparticles). The nanoparticle concentration was limited to a maximum of 75 ppm to prevent potential clogging of the fuel injector nozzle41. The optimal B20 blending ratio for CMS biodiesel was adopted, as established by the authors’ previous studies18. This blend demonstrated satisfactory performance and compatibility with unmodified CI engines. Experimental setup

As depicted in Fig. 2, a GUNT model CT110 engine dynamometer test setup, incorporating a CT110.23 water-cooled, naturally aspirated diesel engine was employed to analyze the engine’s experimental response. The setup utilized an asynchronous motor for engine starting and load application and was equipped with various sensors and actuators for precise measurement and control. Before the experiment, the engine was operated on pure diesel for 10 min to ensure stabilization. Experiments were conducted at a constant engine speed of 2700 RPM with variable engine loads ranging from 0 to 80%. Engine loading was capped at 80% because operating beyond this point led to instability. Combustion parameters were derived from pressure-crank angle diagrams and recorded via a data acquisition system connected to a flush-mounted piezoelectric pressure transducer in the cylinder head. A series of 100 consecutive cycles was measured during combustion analysis to reduce inter-cycle variation. Exhaust emissions were quantified using specific analyzers: HC by Infralyt smart (SAXON Junkalor GmbH, Dessau, Germany), and CO and NOx by a Seitron S500 exhaust gas analyzer (Seitron Americas Inc., Trevose, PA 19053 - USA). Results and discussion

This study systematically investigated the impact of incorporating NBC, Al2O3, and TiO2​nanoparticles into a CMS biodiesel/diesel blend (B20). Experiments were conducted in a compression ignition (CI) engine operating at a constant speed of 2700 RPM under variable loads ranging from 0% to 80%, incremented in 20% steps. The analysis encompassed a comparative evaluation of key engine performance parameters, namely BTE, BSFC, and EGT, contrasting blends with and without nanoparticle additives. Furthermore, emission characteristics, specifically CO, NOx, and HC, were assessed relative to the baseline B20 fuel. The investigation also characterized combustion attributes, including CP, HRR, ID, and SoC, comparing them against the baseline B20 operation. Data reliability was ensured by replicating each test condition three times.

Nanoparticle characterization

The nanobiochar characterization was performed to determine the surface area, zeta potential, nanosize, FTIR, and XRD analysis result was obtained. The result exhibited a surface area of 246.75 m²/gm, an average particle size of 35.11 nm, and a zeta potential of -32.4 mV, which is in agreement with the result reported in82. The properties of NBC, Al2O3, and TiO2 are shown in Table 4. NBC has a higher specific heat capacity and surface area, with a lower thermal conductivity and density compared to Al2O3 and TiO2. TiO2 and NBC exhibit good colloidal stability based on their zeta potential values, while Al2O3 shows moderate stability. NBC has the highest potential to absorb heat due to its significantly higher specific heat capacity, which could influence the combustion temperature profile. NBC, with its exceptionally high surface area, is expected to have the most pronounced catalytic effects and interactions with the fuel, followed by TiO2. Al2O3 will likely have the least. Al2O3 is expected to have the most significant direct positive impact on heat transfer enhancement due to its highest thermal conductivity.

The XRD analysis of the NBC, illustrated in Fig. 3, reveals characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ angles of 25.4° and 43.5°, corresponding to well-developed graphitic stacking (002) and the (100) reflection of disordered carbon layers, respectively – a finding consistent with previous reports83,84. Similarly, the (100) peak signifies condensed aromatic carbonized planes. These XRD patterns collectively demonstrate a low degree of crystalline organization within the biochar samples85.

XRD pattern of NBC.

The FTIR spectra presented in Fig. 4 reveal near-identical profiles for the BBC and NBC samples, exhibiting characteristic bands of biochar. Specifically, the prominent band at 1696.47 cm-1 indicates carboxylic C = O stretching vibrations, while the band at 1585.23 cm-1 is attributed to C = C stretching in aromatic and alkene structures. Phenolic C-O stretching is evident at 1085 cm-1, and aromatic C-H stretching is observed in the 694–881 cm⁻¹ region. Broadband around 3329.02 cm-1 signifies O-H stretching of carboxyl groups, likely due to hydrogen bonding between NBC particles. The high carbon content of biochar is further supported by alkene and alkane peaks at 754 cm-1 (NBC) and 744 cm-1 (BBC), which are absent in the CSV spectrum. Numerous C-H alkene bending peaks are present between 675 and 1000 cm-1 in BBC and NBC89. The absence of novel bands or significant band shifts, along with the overall spectral similarity between BBC and NBC, suggests that ball milling is primarily a mechanical process for NBC fabrication, without inducing substantial chemical alterations90. The presence of phenolic C-O stretching at 1161.53 cm⁻¹ in NBC indicates a relatively low alkalinity of the NBC82.

Combustion analysis

This investigation meticulously analyzed the combustion process at discrete crank angles, focusing on key parameters: SoC, ID, CP, and HRR. High-fidelity piezoelectric pressure transducers, integrated into the cylinder head, enabled precise measurement of cylinder pressure. These sensors were interfaced with a data acquisition system to ensure accurate pressure readings at each crank angle increment. The acquired pressure data facilitated the generation of pressure-crank angle (P-θ) diagrams, providing critical insights into in-cylinder pressure dynamics during combustion. Subsequently, the HRR was determined by applying the first law of thermodynamics to the digitized P-θ diagrams, incorporating cylinder pressure and volume data. The HRR analysis quantified the energy liberated per unit crank angle, based on the rate of pressure rise. Evaluation of the HRR offered a detailed understanding of combustion efficiency and the impact of diverse fuel blends on combustion phasing. Throughout this study, diesel engine combustion characteristics were examined across a spectrum of loading conditions for all tested fuels.

Cylinder pressure (CP)

Cylinder pressure is the maximum force exerted within the cylinder, and its magnitude is heavily influenced by the rapidity and intensity of combustion. Figure 6(a-e) shows the cylinder pressure variation for different nanoparticle additives as a function of the crank angle, based on engine load variation. As shown in Fig. 7, and Table 5, the CP increases as the engine load increases from 0% to 80%. This is because the fuel-to-air ratio increases with the load, which in turn increases the temperature within the cylinder22. The addition of NBC to B20 resulted in a decrease in CP compared to B20 alone. At 80% load operation, the highest peak CP of 56.48 bar was observed for the addition of 30 ppm NBC, followed by 56.41 bar for 50 ppm and 55.68 bar for 75 ppm, compared to 56.76 bar for B20. However, the average reduction in CP due to the addition of 30 ppm, 50 ppm, and 75 ppm of NBC was 1.4%, 0.5%, and 2.3%, respectively. B20 with 50 ppm NBC exhibited the highest cylinder pressure, closely approaching that of B20, suggesting 50 ppm to be the optimal concentration.

The metal oxide nanoparticles, such as Al₂O₃ and TiO₂, were also compared to NBC at 50 ppm. The overall result indicates that metal oxide nanoparticles exhibited a higher cylinder pressure than the NBC. Under 0%, 20%, and 40% load operation, TiO₂ resulted in a CP of 46.51 bar, 52.41 bar, and 55.06 bar, followed by Al₂O₃ with a CP of 46.42 bar, 51.32 bar, and 54.52 bar, respectively. However, under 60% and 80% load, Al2O2 exhibited the highest CP of 56.41 bar and 57.9 bar, respectively, followed by TiO2 with a CP of 56.07 bar and 57.18 bar, respectively. Therefore, compared to the base fuel of B20, Al2O2, and TiO2 have shown an average increase in CP of 1.1% and 1.4%, respectively, whereas NBC50 exhibited an average reduction in CP of 0.5%. The reason for the higher CP observed with Al2O2 and TiO2 compared to NBC is that the superior thermal conductivity of these nanoparticles enhances heat transfer between the fuel and nanoparticles, leading to improved atomization and more efficient combustion of the fuel blend. Additionally, the premixed combustion phase further optimizes heat transfer, resulting in a higher peak pressure due to nanoparticle interactions92. Furthermore, higher specific heat capacity indicates that NBC can absorb a substantial amount of heat, which could influence the combustion temperature profile and the cylinder pressure. Similar trends in CP have been observed in studies incorporating biodiesel and nanoparticles93.

Heat release rate (HRR)

The apparent HRR refers to the rate at which thermal energy is liberated during the combustion process, without accounting for simultaneous heat transfer losses. Key factors influencing HRR include fuel ignition quality, kinematic viscosity, and energy content, as these parameters directly affect the combustion characteristics and efficiency94. Figure 8(a-e) depicts the HRR variation under 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% load for all test fuels of NBC additives at 30ppm, 50ppm, and 75ppm concentration, and Al2O3 and TiO2 nanoparticles at 50ppm concentration to B20. The HRR results obtained due to the addition of NBC were compared to the base fuel of B20. The result shows that the maximum HRR of 517.51 J/oCA was obtained under 80% load for 50 ppm, followed by 508.06 J/oCA for 75 ppm and 472.88 J/oCA for 30 ppm, compared to 503.67 J/oCA for B20. Therefore, the B20NBC50 and B20NBC75 have shown a 2.7% and 0.9% increase in HRR compared to B20, whereas B20NBC30 reduced by 6.1%. However, under lower load conditions of 20%, 40%, and 60%, the addition of NBC exhibited a reduced HRR compared to B20. Furthermore, under 0% load operation, an elevated HRR was recorded for B20NBC30, which increased by 12% compared to B20. The overall result shows that a 50 ppm concentration of NBC has shown the maximum HRR, which indicates that it is the optimum NBC concentration. Therefore, it can be stated that the addition of NBC to diesel fuel facilitates enhanced atomization characteristics and leads to improved combustion performance.

Ignition delay (ID) period

The ignition delay period is the time interval between the start of fuel injection and the start of combustion, which influences combustion quality and emissions12. The variation of the ID period with the engine load for NBC, Al₂O₃, and TiO₂ addition to B20 at different concentrations is illustrated in Fig. 10. As the engine load increases, the ID period decreases, which is attributable to the increased cylinder temperature due to the increase in fuel flow rate. Among the NBC additive concentrations, 30 ppm and 50 ppm have shown an equivalent ID of 22.69°CA, followed by 22.68°CA for 75 ppm, compared to 22.73°CA for B20 under 80% load operation. Even though the ID difference among the test fuel samples is very small, it provides insight into the influence of the NBC additives compared to the B20 base fuel. Hence, the 50 ppm concentration exhibited a shorter ignition delay, which suggests better catalytic activity and is the optimum concentration. Metal oxide nanoparticles (especially Al₂O₃) exhibited a shorter ID period of 22.58°CA compared to that of TiO₂ and NBC, with 22.88°CA and 22.69°CA, respectively. This is attributed to the lower specific heat capacity and improved thermal conductivity of Al₂O₃. The catalytic activity of nanoparticles enhances the rate of oxidation reactions and consequently leads to earlier ignition95. The high thermal conductivity of nanoparticles enhances their reactivity with the fuel, leading to faster initiation of combustion. Metallic oxide nanoparticles improve the thermal conductivity of the fuel mixture, which helps to raise the temperature of the mixture and thus shorten the ignition delay96. The consequences of a shorter ignition delay are improved combustion efficiency, reduced emissions of unburned hydrocarbons, and potentially higher cylinder pressures due to more rapid combustion97.

Engine emission

Nitrogen oxide (NOx)

NOₓ formation is a complex process dependent on various factors such as engine design features, fuel properties, cylinder temperature, fuel residence and reaction time, and engine operating conditions104. Figure 15 depicts the variation of NOx emission with engine load for B20 with and without the nanoparticles. When the load increases from 0% to 20%, the NOx emission increases and reaches a maximum on account of the excess oxygen (especially in diesel with no throttle control) and long residence time that tends to increase the formation of thermal NOx. However, a further increase in load from 20 to 80% brings in a complex interplay of factors: the fuel-air mixture becomes richer, leading to less excess oxygen for NOx formation; the richer mixture slightly lowers combustion temperature due to the increased specific heat of combustion products and potential incomplete combustion zones; evaporative cooling from increased fuel spray also contributes to a temperature moderation105.

The variation of NOx emission with load for all test fuels.

At higher load, more fuel is burnt, and although some areas of combustion are less efficient or more diffusion controlled (slow burning), the overall energy will be higher resulting in a higher EGT. Moreover, peak flame temperatures would be lower or delayed, especially under fuel-rich zones that lead to less NOx. Even though biodiesel has inherent oxygen, the total air availability is lower with less oxygen in the combustion zone, which results in lower NOx despite higher total heat output and EGT. The same result is reported in106. Furthermore, if the combustion becomes faster and more complete, the residence time of the high-temperature gases reduces, which counteracts NOx formation at higher loads104. Across all loading conditions, an average of 9.8%, 6.14%, and 4.6% reduction in NOx was recorded for B20NBC30, B20NBC50, and B20NBC75, respectively. However, the addition of metallic nanoparticles such as Al₂O₃ and TiO2 to B20 has shown an increase in NOx by 1.6% and 38.18%, respectively, compared to an equivalent concentration of NBC. Compared to the metallic nanoparticles, NBC has shown reduced NOx emission, which is attributed to its thermal barrier behavior (lower thermal conductivity and high specific heat capacity) that reduces heat dissipation to the surrounding fuel and cylinder wall, retaining more heat in the cylinder and making the heat release faster, which reduces the formation of NOx. However, due to the higher thermal conductivity of metallic nanoparticles compared to NBC, the heat transfer in the flame increases the cylinder temperatures and NOx emissions .

Carbon monoxide emission (CO)

Figure 16 shows the variation of CO emission with engine load. Adding 30 ppm and 75 ppm of NBC resulted in an average increase in CO by 17.5% and 21.3%, respectively, whereas an 8.4% decrease in CO resulted from adding 50 ppm of NBC compared to B20. Therefore, the addition of 50 ppm NBC exhibited a decrease in CO emission, which can be considered the optimum NBC concentration. Furthermore, the effect of Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ nanoparticles was also compared with NBC at the same concentration of 50 ppm. The results demonstrate that TiO₂ resulted in a 72.3% increase in CO, and Al₂O₃ resulted in a 17.2% reduction in CO emission compared to the base fuel B20. Across all loading conditions, TiO₂ demonstrated higher CO emissions compared to all test fuel samples. While nanoparticles often improve combustion, under certain conditions, they can also lead to localized rich fuel-air mixtures, which can increase CO. If the dispersion of the nanoparticles in the fuel is not optimal, it could cause localized areas of incomplete combustion. Furthermore, if the fuel-air mixture isn’t perfectly homogeneous at the nanoscale, these rapid reactions can deplete the available oxygen in certain micro-zones. This oxygen depletion results in incomplete combustion in those specific areas, leading to the formation of CO95.

The variation of CO emission with load for all test fuels.

At lower loading conditions, the CO emission is higher and reduces with an increase in engine load to the minimum value, but a further increase in load increases the CO emission. The reason is that at low loads, engines often operate with relatively leaner fuel-air mixtures (excess air) and lower cylinder temperatures that hinder the complete oxidation of carbon, resulting in higher CO emissions95. However, a further increase in load increases the temperatures and fuel mixing resulting in more complete combustion and minimal CO. Furthermore, at high loads, engines often operate with richer fuel-air mixtures to maximize power output which can lead to localized oxygen deficiency within the combustion chamber, preventing complete oxidation of carbon and resulting in increased CO emissions107. Even though the temperature is high, if there is not enough oxygen available, then complete combustion cannot happen. Therefore, the CO emission curve follows a U-shaped trend, with peaks at low and high loads and a minimum at mid-load conditions, which is in agreement with the trend reported in33,39,108.

The highest hydrocarbon emission reduction due to the addition of NBC was 27.3% for 30 ppm, followed by 21.5% for 50 ppm and 16.5% for 75 ppm. In this regard, B20 with 30 ppm NBC resulted in lower HC emissions compared to B20. In addition, the effect of Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ nanoparticles was also compared with NBC at the same concentration of 50 ppm. Therefore, Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ exhibited HC emission reductions of 29.5% and 45.88%, respectively, compared to B20 due to the higher thermal conductivity of metallic oxide nanoparticles92. The study reveals that metallic oxide nanoparticles, with TiO₂ demonstrating the most pronounced effect, were more effective than NBC and Al₂O₃ in mitigating hydrocarbon emissions. A similar trend was also reported in105,109.

The variation of HC emission with load for all test fuels.

The relationship between engine load and HC emissions reveals an inverse trend at lower loads, where HC emissions are elevated. As the engine load increases towards an intermediate value, HC emissions decrease to a minimum. However, exceeding this optimal load point resulted in a subsequent rise in HC emissions. At low loads, cylinder temperatures are lower, and the relatively cool cylinder walls can quench the flame. This means the flame is extinguished near the walls, preventing complete combustion of fuel in that region, which is a major source of HC emissions at low loads93. As engine load increases, cylinder temperatures rise, creating a more favorable environment for complete combustion. This reduces quenching and improves combustion stability. At higher loads, rich mixtures and potential oxygen deficiency can cause incomplete combustion and increased HC emissions110.

Nanoparticles and their particulate emission

While stringent emission standards have progressively reduced exhaust pollutants with modern vehicle fleets, diesel engines continue to present a persistent challenge, primarily concerning NOx and particulate matter (PM)111. Conventional mitigation strategies involve exhaust gas treatment systems, such as catalytic converters and diesel particulate filters, alongside the utilization of fuel additives112. Emerging research, however, highlights the potential of nanoparticle additives for enhancing engine performance and curbing emissions113. However, a critical nuance in the application of nanoparticles is their inherent non-combustible nature, which contributes directly to the PM content in exhaust. During the high-temperature and high-pressure environment of a diesel engine combustion chamber, metallic nanoparticles can undergo various transformations. They may remain as discrete nanoparticles, aggregate into larger clusters, or become integrated within the larger soot particles formed from incomplete combustion114. Due to their small size, nanoparticles can remain suspended in the atmosphere for extended periods and be transported over long distances, potentially impacting remote ecosystems far from the emission source35. Therefore, the primary and most effective method for collecting nanoparticle emissions in a diesel engine’s exhaust system before they reach the environment is through the use of a Diesel Particulate Filter (DPF). These highly efficient post-treatment devices, typically wall-flow filters made of ceramic, physically trap solid particulate matter, including nanoparticles (often exceeding 99% efficiency for particle number), by forcing exhaust gas through porous walls where particles are captured via inertial impaction, interception, and crucially for nanoparticles115. Because of their toxicity, metallic nanoparticles can harm the environment when they are discharged into the atmosphere through the engine exhaust, compared to the biomass-derived nanoparticles35.

Comparative assessment of biomass-derived nanoparticles

The higher viscosity of biodiesel blends negatively impacts their spray behavior, resulting in poor atomization. This inadequate atomization leads to non-uniform combustion, creating localized zones of intense burning and consequently, high adiabatic flame temperatures that promote the formation of NOx116. Leveraging their unique properties, nanoparticles offer a potential solution117. Their enhanced heat transfer characteristics, improved spray behavior, and increased thermal conductivity can improve combustion quality118. By facilitating more efficient mass and heat transport during combustion, nanoparticles can mitigate the formation of localized hot spots, thereby reducing overall NOx emissions119. Furthermore, adding nanoparticles to diesel fuels introduces a micro-explosion phenomenon, significantly enhancing combustion120. This phenomenon causes rapid and intense vaporization upon exposure to high temperatures, breaking down large fuel droplets into much smaller ones, which drastically improves fuel vaporization and combustion efficiency121. Research has also indicated that nanoparticles increase the heat of evaporation while decreasing ID and combustion duration (CD), ultimately leading to higher peak CP and faster HRR38,101.

Recognizing the potential carcinogenic and toxic effects of metallic oxide nanoparticles (such as Al2O2, TiO2, CeO2, Mn2O2, Fe2O2, and CuO) when inhaled in exhaust gases, researchers have increasingly explored the use of biomass-derived nanoparticles as safer, bio-based alternatives in biodiesel, as outlined in Table 7. This shift aims to mitigate biosafety concerns associated with traditional metallic fuel additives. Several studies have investigated the impact of nanoparticle additives derived from biomass residues on engine performance and emissions. For example, Sivasaravanan et al.122 demonstrated that adding rice husk nanoparticles at a 0.1% concentration to neem oil biodiesel (B10 and B20) improved BTE, reduced BSFC, and lowered CO, HC, and NOx emissions, compared to the base fuels. These findings were corroborated by123, who observed similar improvements in BTE and reductions in CO, HC, and NOx emissions with rice husk nanoparticle addition to pongamia seed oil methyl ester. Further research by Safieddin Ardebili et al.73 explored sugarcane bagasse-derived nanobiochar as an additive in fusel oil/diesel blends using a single-cylinder diesel engine. Their findings revealed a 3% increase in engine torque and a significant reduction in NOx (21%) and Unburned Hydrocarbons (UHC) (15%). Employing Response Surface Methodology (RSM), they identified optimal engine parameters: a 60 ppm nanobiochar concentration, an engine speed of 2300 rpm, and a 10% fusel oil blend, achieving a high desirability value of 66%. Investigating diesel-biodiesel-ethanol (DBE) blends, Mirbagheri et al.74 also found that incorporating nanobiochar consistently decreased NOx emissions across all fuel blends. Increasing the nanobiochar dosage up to 100 ppm resulted in a 15% reduction in NOx, while a reduction in CO emissions from 0.03% to 0.015% was observed as the nanobiochar concentration increased across the range of 25 ppm to 125 ppm. Moreover, a maximum UHC reduction of approximately 28% was achieved with 125 ppm of nanobiochar.

The promising results observed across these studies with biomass-derived nanoparticles provided the impetus for the current research. However, recognizing the limited existing research, further investigation is warranted. Notably, previous studies primarily focused on rice husk, coconut shell, and biochar nanoparticles’ impact on engine performance and emissions, leaving a gap in understanding their effects on crucial combustion characteristics such as CP, HRR, ID, and SoC. This study aims to address this novelty by examining these combustion parameters alongside performance and emission characteristics at NBC concentrations of 30, 50, and 75 ppm in a 20% volume blend of CMS biodiesel with diesel (B20). The nanobiochar used was produced from cornstover biomass via muffle furnace pyrolysis in an oxygen-free environment, followed by wet ball milling. The inclusion of this NBC resulted in an average 0.5% reduction in CP, a 2.7% increase in HRR, a shorter ID, and an advanced SoC. Furthermore, performance parameters showed a 2.2–2.8% reduction in BSFC, a 2.6–9.5% increase in BTE, and a lower EGT compared to Al₂O₃ and TiO₂, although EGT was higher than with neat B20. Ultimately, adding NBC to B20 led to average reductions in NOx, CO, and HC emissions by a maximum of 9.8%, 8.4%, and 27.3%, respectively. When compared to Al₂O₃ and TiO₂ at an equivalent 50 ppm concentration, NBC demonstrated lower BSFC reduction, CO, NOx, and EGT. Based on these findings, a 50 ppm concentration of NBC was identified as the optimum among the tested fuels. This research concludes that NBC exhibits promising potential for reducing emissions and improving engine performance, aligning with trends reported in existing literature.

Conclusion

This study examined the effect of nanobiochar in CMS biodiesel on CI engine’s combustion, emissions, and performance characteristics, and was compared to Al2O3 and TiO2. The test was performed at a constant speed of 2700 RPM under 0%, 20%, 40%, 60% and 80% for the test fuels of B20, B20NBC30, B20NBC50, B20NBC75, B20A50, and B20T50. The NBC was synthesized from CSV biochar using the wet ball milling method and characterized to know the morphology, elemental composition, zeta potential, and particle size. Novelty lies in the detailed combustion analysis of CMS biodiesel-diesel blends, correlating heat release rate with engine outputs. The following conclusions were drawn:

The characterization of NBC revealed a high surface area (246.75 m2/g), nanoscale particle size (35.11 nm), and a zeta potential of − 32.4 mV, indicating good stability and potential for effective catalytic or additive applications.

In contrast, Al2O2 and TiO2 increased average cylinder pressure by 1.1% and 1.4%, respectively, with Al₂O₃ exhibiting a slightly shorter ID (22.58°CA) than TiO2 (22.88°CA) and NBC (22.69°CA), highlighting the differing effects of nanoparticle additives on combustion characteristics.

Limitation and scope for further work

The study was limited to a single cylinder engine under controlled laboratory conditions that may not fully replicate real-world engine performance.

The study only examined NBC at three concentrations—30, 50, and 75 ppm—without exploring a wider concentration range or evaluating its long-term dispersion stability. This narrow scope may not fully reveal the optimal dosage or potential negative effects such as nanoparticle aggregation. Similarly, the benchmarking of metal oxide nanoparticles (Al₂O₃ and TiO₂) was conducted at a single concentration of 50 ppm, which does not provide insights into how varying dosages might influence engine performance or emissions. Furthermore, the effect of nanoparticle concentration with surfactant to enhance the stability need to be tested over a higher range with long-term impacts on engine durability, injector fouling, as also the need for post-treatment emission systems evaluation which needs to be looked in to.

To optimize CMS biodiesel use, future research should examine its performance in multi-cylinder CI engines under real-world driving, specifically focusing on optimized fuel injection parameters and engine tribological assessment.
