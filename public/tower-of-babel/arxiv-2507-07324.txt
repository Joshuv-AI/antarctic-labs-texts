Chiral Cherenkov radiation at time-dependent chiral chemical potential
Kirill Tuchin

Abstract
We study the photon production process f → f + γ f\to f+\gamma in the presence of a time-dependent chiral chemical potential μ 5 \mu_{5} . Using the ultra-relativistic approximation, we demonstrate validity of the adiabatic approximation. Analytical expressions for the photon spectrum are derived for two models of the chiral magnetic conductivity b 0 ∝ μ 5 b_{0}\propto\mu_{5} : (i) b 0 ​ ( t ) = A 1 + B 1 ​ tanh ⁡ ( t / τ ) b_{0}(t)=A_{1}+B_{1}\tanh(t/\tau) and (ii) b 0 ​ ( t ) = A 2 ​ ( t / τ ) / ( 1 + t 2 / τ 2 ) b_{0}(t)=A_{2}(t/\tau)/(1+t^{2}/\tau^{2}) . It is known that for constant b 0 b_{0} , photon emission may exhibit a resonance for one photon polarization depending on the sign of b 0 b_{0} . We show that in model (i), up to two resonances may occur, depending on the sign of the asymptotic values b 0 ​ ( ∞ ) b_{0}(\infty) and b 0 ​ ( − ∞ ) b_{0}(-\infty) . No resonances are observed in model (ii). Numerical calculations are performed using parameters relevant to quark-gluon plasma, and we discuss the universality of the results.



I
Introduction

One of the most intriguing features of chiral media is the chiral magnetic effect whereby electric current is induced in the direction of the external magnetic field
Kharzeev 2006
;
Kharzeev et al. 2008
;
Kharzeev 2010
;
Kharzeev and Zhitnitsky 2007
;
Fukushima et al. 2008
. The corresponding transport coefficient
b_{0}
, known as the chiral magnetic conductivity, is proportional to the chiral chemical potential
\mu_{5}
, which reflects the net chirality in the medium. The chiral imbalance can have various sources. In quark-gluon plasma it may be induced by the parallel chromoelectric and chromomagnetic fields in the initial stage following a heavy-ion collision
Lappi and Schlichting 2018
or by a sphaleron transition at a later stage
Fukushima et al. 2008
. The phenomenological manifestation of the chiral magnetic effect is electric charge separation with respect to the collision plane of the heavy ions
Kharzeev and Zhitnitsky 2007
. Recent experimental measurements have produced encouraging signs of this charge separation
STA 2025
.

Another manifestation of the chiral magnetic effect, is the chiral Cherenkov radiation, in which a circularly polarized photon is emitted by a charges fermion
Tuchin 2018a
;
Huang and Tuchin 2018
. This process, kinematically prohibited in the empty space, becomes possible owing to the momentum supplied by the chiral magnetic current. The chiral Cherenkov radiation contributes to the parton energy loss and the jet structure, which can be directly observed in relativistic heavy-ion experiments.

The time evolution of the chiral chemical potential is governed by three processes. The first is above-mentioned sphaleron transition, a stochastic process that generates the chiral charge. At very high temperatures, the transition time is estimated to be significantly longer than the lifetime of the quark-gluon plasma
Moore and Tassler 2011
. However, at phenomenologically relevant temperatures, the time dependence may not be negligible. The second process is a graduate relaxation of the chiral chemical potential due to the inelastic processes breaking the chiral symmetry. The third process is chirality transfer to the electromagnetic field via the inverse chiral cascade
Joyce and Shaposhnikov 1997
;
Boyarsky et al. 2012
;
Hirono et al. 2015
;
Akamatsu and Yamamoto 2013
;
Tuchin 2015
;
Buividovich and Ulybyshev 2016
;
Manuel and Torres-Rincon 2015
;
Kirilin and Sadofyev 2017
. These processes impart time-dependence to the chiral chemical potential, which, in turn, impacts the chiral magnetic effect, the charge separation
Tuchin 2018b
, and the chiral Cherenkov radiation. The latter is the subject of this paper.

The chiral Cherenkov radiation is the excitation of the electromagnetic field in a chiral medium by a charged fermion. If the time-dependence of
b_{0}
is neglected, the eigenstates of the electromagnetic field are circularly polarized monochromatic plane waves with a dispersion relation dependent on the photon polarization. The transition amplitude is then proportional to the delta function, which ensures energy conservation. However, when
b_{0}
has a non-trivial time-dependence, the delta function gives way to a distribution proportional to the Fourier image of the photon amplitude. The relationship between the photon production rate and this amplitude is provided by perturbation theory.

The main goal of this paper is to compute the photon production rate, given the time-dependence of
b_{0}
. The precise form of
b_{0}
is unknown. It therefore advantageous to employ models that provide both a qualitative description of its time-evolution, and are amenable to analytical methods. Two models are examined: (i)
b_{0}=A_{1}+B_{1}\tanh(t/\tau)
and (ii)
b_{0}=A_{2}(t/\tau)/(1+t^{2}/\tau^{2})
, where
A_{1},B_{1},A_{2}
and
\tau>0
are real numbers. Model (i) describes a transition between states characterized by different asymptotic values of
b_{0}
, whereas model (ii) describes a burst of chirality with a finite duration. It will be shown that the models exhibit qualitatively distinct spectra, differing from the spectrum associated with constant
b_{0}
.

The paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
the general formalism for computing the photon production rate at the leading order of perturbation theory in the ultra-relativistic approximation is reviewed. The main formula for the transition probability is (
14
). The photon amplitude
a(t)
satisfies a second order differential equation (
3
), which is solved for a given
b_{0}(t)
. Sec.
III
lays out the benchmark results for the photon rate at constant
b_{0}
. Fig.
1
shows the corresponding photon spectrum. The exact solution to (
3
) is known only in a few relevant cases. Fortunately, all applications to relativistic heavy-ion collisions involve photons whose momenta
k
are so large that we can use the adiabatic approximation, thereby obtaining a general expression for the field amplitude in Sec.
IV
. The main results are derived in Sec.
V
and Sec.
VI
where the photon spectra for the models (i) and (ii) are obtained and analyzed. The results are exhibited in Figs.
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
. The final section summarizes and discusses the results.

II
Photon radiation probability

The electromagnetic field, in the radiation gauge
A^{0}=0
,
{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bm{A}}=0
, is described by the vector potential
{\bm{A}}
obeying the equation:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}{\bm{A}}-\partial_{t}^{2}{\bm{A}}+b_{0}(t){\bm{\nabla}}\times{\bm{A}}=0\,.

(1)

It is assumed that the chiral magnetic conductivity
b_{0}
is a given function of time unaffected by the radiation. The wave function of a photon of energy
\omega
and momentum
{\bm{k}}
is a plane wave solution to (
1
):

\displaystyle{\bm{A}}(t,{\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}a(t){\bm{\epsilon}}_{\lambda}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,,

(2)

where the polarization vector
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\lambda}
,
\lambda=\pm 1
, satisfies the condition
i\hat{{\bm{k}}}\times{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\lambda}=\lambda{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\lambda}
which implies that photons must be circularly polarized. Substituting (
2
) into (
1
) we find that the amplitude
a(t)
is governed by the equation

\displaystyle\ddot{a}(t)+\Omega^{2}(t)a(t)=0\,,

(3)

where

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}(t)=k^{2}-\lambda b_{0}(t)k\,.

(4)

The photon wave function will be normalized to one photon per unit volume as usual.

We denote the energy and momentum of the incident and final fermions as
E,{\bm{p}}
and
E^{\prime},{\bm{p}}^{\prime}
respectively. The wave function of an incident fermion with momentum
{\bm{p}}
and spin
s
reads

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(t,{\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2EV}}u_{s}({\bm{p}})e^{-iEt+i{\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}\,,

(5)

where
E=\sqrt{p^{2}+m^{2}}
. The wave function of the final fermion
\psi_{f}
is described by a similar expression. The photon radiation amplitude is given by

\displaystyle S_{fi}

\displaystyle=-ie\int\bar{\psi}_{f}{\bm{\gamma}}\cdot{\bm{A}}^{*}\psi_{i}d^{3}xdt=-ie\frac{\bar{u}_{s^{\prime}}({\bm{p}}^{\prime}){\bm{\gamma}}\cdot{\bm{\epsilon}}^{*}_{\lambda}u_{s}({\bm{p}})}{2V^{3/2}\sqrt{EE^{\prime}}}(2\pi)^{3}\delta({\bm{p}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})\tilde{a}^{*}(E-E^{\prime})\,,

(6)

where we defined:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-i(E^{\prime}-E)t}a(t)dt\,.

(7)

Given a bounded function
b_{0}(t)
, the convergence of the integral in (
7
) as assured by inserting a positive infinitesimal parameter
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-i(E^{\prime}-E-i\epsilon)t}a(t)dt+\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-i(E^{\prime}-E+i\epsilon)t}a(t)dt\,.

(8)

The photon radiation probability is then calculated as

\displaystyle dw

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda,s,s^{\prime}}|S_{fi}|^{2}\frac{Vd^{3}p^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{Vd^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}=\frac{e^{2}}{8(2\pi)^{3}E}\sum_{\lambda}|\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})|^{2}d^{2}k_{\bot}\frac{dx}{1-x}\Tr[(\not{p}+m)\not{\epsilon}^{*}_{\lambda}(\not{p}^{\prime}+m)\not{\epsilon}_{\lambda}]\,,

(9)

where
x
denotes the fraction of the incident fermion momentum carried away by the photon. Assuming that the incident fermion moves along the
z
-axis,
x=k_{z}/p_{z}
and
k^{2}=x^{2}p_{z}^{2}+k_{\bot}^{2}
, where
{\bm{k}}_{\bot}
is the photon’s transverse momentum. The product of the photon polarization vectors can be written as:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu*}_{\lambda}\epsilon^{\nu}_{\lambda}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{k^{i}k^{j}}{k^{2}}\right)+\frac{i\lambda}{2}\varepsilon^{ij\ell}\frac{k^{\ell}}{k}\,,&\mu=i,\nu=j\,,\\
0\,,&\mu\nu=0\,,\end{array}\right.

where no summation over
\lambda
is implied.

In most applications the incident fermion can be regarded as ultra-relativistic meaning that
p_{z}\gg k_{\bot},m
. In this approximation,

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=p_{z}\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{2p_{z}^{2}}\right)\,,

(12)

\displaystyle E^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(1-x)p_{z}\left(1+\frac{m^{2}+k_{\bot}^{2}}{2(1-x)^{2}p_{z}^{2}}\right)\,,

(13)

and the differential probability (
9
) becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{d^{2}k_{\bot}dx}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8(2\pi)^{3}E}\sum_{\lambda}|\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})|^{2}\frac{2e^{2}}{x^{2}(1-x)^{2}}\left[k_{\bot}^{2}(2-2x+x^{2})+m^{2}x^{4}\right]\,.

(14)

The problem of computing the photon spectrum thus reduces to determining the Fourier image (
8
) of the field amplitude
a(t)
.

III
Photon spectrum at constant
b_{0}

It is instructive to first consider the particular case of constant
b_{0}
that was discussed before
Tuchin 2018a
;
Huang and Tuchin 2018
;
Tuchin 2018c
;
Barredo-Alamilla et al. 2023
. In this case the positive frequency solution of (
3
) is

\displaystyle a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\omega}}e^{-i\omega t}\,,

(15)

where
\omega
is the photon energy given by

\displaystyle\omega=\sqrt{k^{2}+M^{2}}\approx xp_{z}\left(1+\frac{M^{2}+k_{\bot}^{2}}{2x^{2}p_{z}^{2}}\right)\,,

(16)

and we introduced a new parameter
M^{2}=-\lambda b_{0}k
. It follows then from (
7
) that

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})=2\pi\delta(\omega+E^{\prime}-E)\,.

(17)

Using (
12
) and (
13
), the argument of the delta-function can be written as

\displaystyle\omega+E^{\prime}-E=\frac{k_{\bot}^{2}+M^{2}(1-x)+m^{2}x^{2}}{2x(1-x)p_{z}}\,.

(18)

The inverse of this expression is the coherence length of the photon emission:
\ell_{\text{c}}=1/|\omega+E^{\prime}-E|
.
It signifies the distance traversed by the fermion as it radiates a photon carrying the fraction
x
of its momentum, the transverse momentum
{\bm{k}}_{\bot}
and the polarization
\lambda
.
Substituting (
18
),(
17
) into (
14
) yields the differential photon radiation rate
d\dot{w}
at constant
b_{0}

Tuchin 2018a
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\dot{w}}{dxd^{2}k_{\bot}}=\frac{e^{2}}{8\pi^{2}Ex}\left[\lambda Eb_{0}\left(1-x+\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)-m^{2}x\right]\delta\left(k_{\bot}^{2}-\lambda b_{0}Ex(1-x)+m^{2}x^{2}\right)\,.

(19)

IV
Adiabatic approximation

Eq. (
3
) cannot be analytically solved for an arbitrary function
b_{0}(t)
. However, for a sufficiently slow time-variation the solution can be obtained in the adiabatic approximation. In this section we derive a general formula for the amplitude
a(t)
based on this approximation. We first introduce an auxiliary function
R(t)
defined as

\displaystyle a(t)=e^{iR(t)}\,.

(20)

Substituting (
20
) into (
3
) we find that it satisfies the equation

\displaystyle i\ddot{R}-\dot{R}^{2}+\Omega^{2}=0\,.

(21)

The adiabatic approximation consists in treating
R(t)
as a slowly varying function such that

\displaystyle|\ddot{R}|\ll\dot{R}^{2}\,.

(22)

To develop the adiabatic expansion we introduce a bookkeeping parameter
\alpha
into (
21
):

\displaystyle i\alpha\ddot{R}-\dot{R}^{2}+\Omega^{2}=0\,,

(23)

and substitute in it the expansion

\displaystyle R=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{j}R_{j}\,.

(24)

Collecting the terms of the same order in
\alpha
we obtain equations that govern the functions
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
:

\displaystyle\dot{R}_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Omega^{2}\,,

(25)

\displaystyle i\ddot{R}_{0}

\displaystyle=2\dot{R}_{0}\dot{R}_{1}\,.

(26)

Solving these equations and plugging the result back into (
20
) yields the desired result, after setting
\alpha=1
:

\displaystyle a(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega}}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}\Omega(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\,.

(27)

The sign in the exponent corresponds to the outgoing wave as appropriate for the emitted photon. Expanding
\Omega
in the ultra-relativistic approximation
k\gg b_{0}
:

\displaystyle\Omega(t)\approx k-\frac{\lambda b_{0}(t)}{2}

(28)

we obtain the final result for the amplitude:

\displaystyle a(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k}}e^{-ikt+\frac{i\lambda}{2}\int_{0}^{t}b_{0}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}}\,.

(29)

It is valid as long as (
22
) is satisfied which is equivalent to the condition:

\displaystyle\left|\dot{b}_{0}\right|\ll k_{z}^{2}\,.

(30)

Given
b_{0}(t)
one can compute the amplitude
a(t)
using (
29
) and its Fourier transform (
8
). The latter can be carried out analytically in a number of cases, but in general one has to rely on numerical methods. The next subsection deals with two examples which can be solved analytically.

V
Photon spectrum for
b_{0}(t)=A_{1}+B_{1}\tanh\frac{t}{\tau}

In this section we consider the following model for the time evolution of the chiral magnetic conductivity:

\displaystyle b_{0}(t)=A_{1}+B_{1}\tanh\frac{t}{\tau}\,.

(31)

The initial and final values
b_{0}(t)
are
b_{0}(-\infty)=A_{1}-B_{1}
and
b_{0}(\infty)=A_{1}+B_{1}
respectively. The transition time between them is controlled by the parameter
\tau
.

V.1
Exact solution for
a(t)

Substituting (
31
) into (
4
) we obtain the function
\Omega(t)
:

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}(t)=k^{2}-\lambda kA-\lambda kB\tanh\frac{t}{\tau}\,.

(32)

The exact solution of (
3
) with
\Omega
given by (
32
) was obtained in
Bernard and Duncan 1977
. It is most readily expressed in terms of
the asymptotic values of
\Omega
as follows:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k^{2}-\lambda k(A_{1}-B_{1})}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{out}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{k^{2}-\lambda k(A_{1}+B_{1})}\,,

(34)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\Omega_{\text{out}}\pm\Omega_{\text{in}})\,.

(35)

In the ultra-relativistic approximation, we expand (
33
),(
34
) and (
35
) at large
k_{z}
:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=k_{z}-\frac{\lambda}{2}(A_{1}-B_{1})+\mathcal{O}(k_{z}^{-1})\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{out}}

\displaystyle=k_{z}-\frac{\lambda}{2}(A_{1}+B_{1})+\mathcal{O}(k_{z}^{-1})\,,

(37)

\displaystyle\Omega_{+}

\displaystyle=k_{z}-\frac{\lambda A_{1}}{2}\,,

(38)

\displaystyle\Omega_{-}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda B_{1}}{2}\,.

(39)

The solution to (
3
) describing the emitted photon, namely the one that has positive frequency at the future infinity reads:

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\text{out}}}}e^{-i\Omega_{+}t-i\Omega_{-}\tau\ln\left[2\cosh\frac{t}{\tau}\right]}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(1+i\Omega_{-}\tau,i\Omega_{-}\tau;1+i\Omega_{\text{out}}\tau;\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\right)\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k_{z}}}e^{-i\left(k_{z}-\frac{\lambda A_{1}}{2}\right)t+i\frac{\lambda B_{1}}{2}\tau\ln\left[2\cosh\frac{t}{\tau}\right]}{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(1-\frac{i\lambda B_{1}\tau}{2},-\frac{i\lambda B_{1}\tau}{2};1+ik_{z}\tau;\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\tanh\frac{t}{\tau}\right)\right)\,.

(41)

Using the Euler’s hypergeometric transformation:
{{}_{2}}F_{1}(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;z)=(1-z)^{\gamma-\alpha-\beta}{{}_{2}}F_{1}(\gamma-\alpha,\gamma-\beta;\gamma;z)
and assuming, consistently with the ultra-relativistic approximation, that
k_{z}\tau\gg 1
and
k_{z}\gg|B_{1}|
, so that
\gamma\gg\alpha,\beta
, we conclude that the hypergeometric function in (
41
) reduces to unity.

V.2
Adiabatic approximation

The adiabatic approximation (
29
) precisely reproduces the exact solution (
41
) which can be seen by substituting (
31
) into (
29
) and replacing
k\approx k_{z}
:

\displaystyle a(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2k_{z}}}e^{-i\left(k_{z}-\frac{\lambda A_{1}}{2}\right)t+i\frac{\lambda B_{1}}{2}\tau\ln\left[2\cosh\frac{t}{\tau}\right]}\,.

(42)

According to (
30
) this formula is valid when the parameters satisfy
k_{z}^{2}\tau\gg|B_{1}|
. Thus the adiabatic approximation accurately describes the time-evolution of the electromagnetic field in the ultra-relativistic limit which spares one seeking exact solutions to (
3

We proceed by calculating the Fourier image of the amplitude using (
8
), which cam be cast in the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2k_{z}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left[\left(E-E^{\prime}-k_{z}+\frac{\lambda A_{1}}{2}\right)t\right]e^{-\left(i\frac{\lambda B_{1}\tau}{2}+\epsilon\right)\ln\left[2\cosh\frac{t}{\tau}\right]}dt\,.

(43)

We used the fact that at
t\to\infty
, the infinitesimal term in the exponent becomes
-\epsilon t/\tau
as prescribed by (
8
) (up to the non-essential factor
1/\tau
that simplify redefines
\epsilon
). Employing (
36
)–(
39
) we may write (
43
) as:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\text{out}}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left[(E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{+})t\right]e^{-(i\Omega_{-}\tau+\epsilon)\ln\left[2\cosh\frac{t}{\tau}\right]}dt\,.

(44)

Performing the Fourier transform using Eq. 1.9(5) of
Bateman et al. 1954
yields:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\text{out}}}}\frac{2^{i\Omega_{-}\tau-1}\tau}{\Gamma(i\Omega_{-}\tau)}\Gamma\left(\frac{i\tau}{2}(E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{\text{in}}-i\epsilon)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{i\tau}{2}(-E+E^{\prime}+\Omega_{\text{out}}-i\epsilon)\right)\,.

(45)

Substituting into (
45
) into (
14
) furnishes the photon spectrum:

\displaystyle\frac{dw}{dxd^{2}k_{\bot}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16(2\pi)^{3}E^{2}}\sum_{\lambda}\frac{2e^{2}}{x^{3}(1-x)^{2}}\left[k_{\bot}^{2}(2-2x+x^{2})+m^{2}x^{4}\right]\frac{\tau^{2}}{4|\Gamma(i\Omega_{-}\tau)|^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left|\Gamma\left(\frac{i\tau}{2}(E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{\text{in}}-i\epsilon)\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{i\tau}{2}(-E+E^{\prime}+\Omega_{\text{out}}-i\epsilon)\right)\right|^{2}\,.

(46)

The explicit dependence of the

\displaystyle E^{\prime}-E+\Omega_{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\bot}^{2}-\lambda x(1-x)p_{z}(A_{1}-B_{1})+m^{2}x^{2}}{2x(1-x)p_{z}}\,,

(47)

\displaystyle E^{\prime}-E+\Omega_{\text{out}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{\bot}^{2}-\lambda x(1-x)p_{z}(A_{1}+B_{1})+m^{2}x^{2}}{2x(1-x)p_{z}}\,.

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}=\frac{\lambda E(A_{1}\mp B_{1})+(-1)^{\alpha}\sqrt{E^{2}(A_{1}\mp B_{1})^{2}-4\left[m^{2}+\lambda E(A_{1}\mp B_{1})\right]k_{\bot}^{2}}}{2\left[m^{2}+\lambda E(A_{1}\mp B_{1})\right]}\,,\quad\alpha=1,2\,,

(52)

provided that

\displaystyle 0<k_{\bot}^{2}\leq\frac{E^{2}(A_{1}\mp B_{1})^{2}}{4\left[m^{2}+\lambda E(A_{1}\mp B_{1})\right]}\,.

(53)

V.3
Sadden transition

The adiabatic approximation does not require
\tau
to be very large, as we saw in the previous subsection. In fact, it can be rather small as long as the condition
k_{z}^{2}\tau\gg|B_{1}|
is fulfilled. In particular,
\tau
can be much smaller than the coherence length
\ell_{c}
defined in Sec.
III
for constant
b_{0}
. In our case,
1/\tau
must be much larger than either of two expressions (
47
) and (
48
). This condition is certainly satisfied near the resonances. It also holds for not too large values of
k_{\bot}
. We can refer to such a scenario as a sadden transition. Taking the limit
\tau\to 0
in the exponent of (
42
) yields

\displaystyle a(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\text{out}}}}\left(e^{-i\Omega_{\text{out}}t}\theta(t)+e^{-i\Omega_{\text{in}}t}\theta(-t)\right)\,.

(54)

Its Fourier-transform (
7
) is

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(E-E^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\text{out}}}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{i(E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{\text{out}}+i\epsilon)t}dt+\int^{0}_{-\infty}e^{i(E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{\text{in}}-i\epsilon)t}dt\right\}

(55)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\Omega_{\text{out}}}}\left\{\frac{1}{E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{\text{out}}+i\epsilon}-\frac{1}{E-E^{\prime}-\Omega_{\text{in}}-i\epsilon}\right\}\,,

(56)

In particular, if
b_{0}
is constant, then
B_{1}=0
,
\Omega_{\text{out}}=\Omega_{\text{in}}
and (
56
) reduces to (
17
) as can be seen by using the formula

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x\pm i\epsilon}=\mp i\pi\delta(x)+P\frac{1}{x}\,.

(57)

In this section we consider a scenario in which
b_{0}
evolves in such a way that its value at distant past and future vanish. We employ the following model:

\displaystyle b(t)=\frac{A_{2}t}{\tau\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\right)}\,.

(58)

Unlike the previous model, I am not aware of the exact solution to equation (

VII
Summary

We investigated the chiral Cherenkov mechanism of photon radiation using two models of the time-evolution of the chiral magnetic conductivity
b_{0}(t)
. The first model
b_{0}(t)=A_{1}+B_{1}\tanh(t/\tau)
describes a transition of
b_{0}
between two asymptotically constant values
b_{0}(\pm\infty)
. This mechanism allows the production of only circularly polarized photons. In general, the cross sections for the two polarizations are significantly different. The main feature of the
k_{\bot}
-spectrum is the potential emergence of one or two resonances. The necessary conditions of the resonance emergence are the fulfillment of either or both of the following conditions:
\lambda b_{0}(\infty)>0
and
\lambda b_{0}(-\infty)>0
. The positions of the resonances are given by (
50
)–(
53
). The typical spectra are presented in Figs.
2
,
3
and
4
. In contrast, only a single resonance may appear when
b_{0}
is constant as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

The second model represents a pulse of chirality with a finite duration:
b_{0}(t)=A_{2}(t/\tau)/(1+t^{2}/\tau^{2})
. In this case, there are no resonances, and the two polarizations have identical spectra.

The two models investigated in this paper suggest that the time-dependence of the chiral chemical potential diminishes
the average
polarization of the chiral Cherenkov radiation as compared to the constant
b_{0}
case. The chirial Cherenkov resonance, appearing in the figures as a peak at a certain
k_{\bot}
(or two peaks at the corresponding
x
’s), gives the largest contribution to the rate and emerges if
b_{0}(\pm\infty)\neq 0
. It also determines the average polarization. The mathematical structure of the Fourier image of the amplitude
a(t)
suggests that these conclusions may hold universally. Indeed, the parameter
\epsilon
that controls the convergence of the time-integral at
t\to\pm\infty
in (
8
) also sets the resonance height, which is proportional to
1/\epsilon
. The resonance appears in any model in which
b_{0}
is finite at either remote past or future.

We implicitly assumed that the medium lifetime and spatial dimensions are much larger than any time or distance scale associated with the process
f\to f+\gamma
. This assumption might be in tension with the relativistic heavy-ion phenomenology. It would be interesting to investigate how the finite dimensions of the realistic quark-gluon plasma impact the results presented in this paper.

Our analysis focused exclusively on applications to the quark-gluon plasma. Nevertheless, the developed formalism is equally applicable to describe photon radiation emanating from cosmic rays in the background of the axion field or by electrons in Weyl semimetals
Hansen et al. 2025
.
