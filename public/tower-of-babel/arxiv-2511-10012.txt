Revisiting Koehler’s experiment of measuring the ratio of the specific heats of air by self-sustained oscillations: a more concise theoretical interpretation
Yujun Shi, Xiaoting Feng

Abstract
We revisit Koehler’s experiment, a clever modification of Rüchardt’s experiment designed to measure the ratio of specific heats of gas. The theory of self-sustained oscillations in Koehler’s experiment was provided by Koehler (1950). However, its lengthy and dense analysis may pose challenges to readers due to the complexity of the calculations. Following Koehler’s approximation for pressure changes, we explicitly present the model equations as piecewise linear differential systems and qualitatively analyze the periodic solutions from a geometric perspective. This concise and transparent approach addresses a fundamental question about Koehler’s experiment: why is the oscillation frequency nearly equal to the Rüchardt frequency? Our analysis avoids intricate calculations and will be particularly helpful for teachers and students who encounter Koehler’s experiment in general physics laboratory classes.



I
Introduction

Koehler’s experiment for measuring the ratio of specific heats
\gamma=C_{p}/C_{v}
of air (or other gases) is a modification of Rüchardt’s experiment, a classic experiment widely used in general physics laboratory courses
Koehler1951
;
Lange2000
. As shown in Fig.
1
(a), the apparatus of Rüchardt’s experiment consists of a large vessel with a vertical tube containing a closely fitting steel ball. To facilitate the discussion that follows, it is useful to briefly recall the theory of Rüchardt’s experiment. When the ball is stationary at its equilibrium position, the equilibrium pressure satisfies the equation
P_{0}=B+\frac{mg}{\pi R^{2}}
, Where
B
is atmospheric pressure,
m
and
R
are the mass and radius of the ball. When the ball is displaced from its equilibrium position and released, its rapid motion induces pressure fluctuations due to the adiabatic compression or expansion of the gas within the container. For the ball, we have

m\ddot{x}=-\pi R^{2}\cdot\Delta P

(1)

Where
x
is the displacement of the ball from its equilibrium position, and
\Delta P
is the gas pressure change in the vessel.
Considering the radiabatic equation
PV^{\gamma}=const.
,

we have

\Delta P=-\frac{\gamma P\Delta V}{V}\sim-\frac{\gamma P_{0}\Delta V}{V_{0}}=-\frac{\gamma P_{0}\cdot\pi R^{2}}{V_{0}},

(2)

where
V_{0}
is the volume of the vessel. Substituting it into Eq.(
1
), it can be seen that the motion of the ball is simple harmonic oscillation. The relationship between the period of oscillation and
\gamma
is given by

\gamma=\frac{4mV_{0}}{T^{2}P_{0}R^{4}}.

(3)

Although Rüchardt’s method has the advantages of simplicity and mechanical intuitiveness, it is not as simple as it first appears. Unavoidable frictional damping and gas leakage limit the number of oscillations, typically to only a few, preventing precise measurement of the period. In 1951, Koehler described an interesting modification involving a gas-feeding pump and introducing a small hole in the tube (as shown in Fig.
1
(b))
Koehler1951
. The ball is lifted by gas overpressure and falls back due to the pressure drop from gas leakage through the hole. By carefully adjusting the gas flow, the ball can oscillate symmetrically about the hole indefinitely. The oscillations closely approximate harmonic motion, and the relationship between the period and
\gamma
is still described by Eq.(
3
). The advantages of Koehler’s method are evident: it allows prolonged measurement of the period, thereby improving the ease and precision of measurements. Therefore, compared to Rüchardt’s experiment, Koehler’s experiment offers greater advantages for teaching purposes.

Figure 1:
(a) and (b) Schematic diagrams of Rüchardt apparatus and Koehler apparatus. (c) An image of experimental apparatus.

In China, many universities incorporate the Koehler experiment into general physics laboratory courses. However, there is limited evidence of its widespread adoption globally. A search of online resources reveals few instances of its use in English-speaking countries, particularly in American colleges and universities. Even in experimental demonstrations of the specific heat ratio of gases on YouTube, nearly all are based on Rüchardt’s design. By incorporating a simple air pump (costing less than $10), the Koehler experiment achieves sustained oscillation through a clever design, enabling more precise measurement of the gas-specific heat ratio. Thus, we argue that the Koehler experiment provides significant pedagogical advantages over the Rüchardt experiment in student laboratory teaching and has the potential for broader adoption in other regions in the future. Furthermore, even in schools that currently use the Rüchardt experiment, instructors could easily adapt the setup to implement the Koehler version or allow students to explore it as an independent project, given its simplicity. For reference, the detailed parameters of the experimental apparatus employed in this study are provided in Appendix
A
.

In courses using Koehler’s experiment, the theoretical introduction typically follows the framework of the original Rüchardt experiment. Students are generally informed that the ball’s motion approximates harmonic behavior, as described by Eq.(
3
). However, an issue arises: the supply and leakage of gas indicate that the pressure fluctuations within the container are not solely determined by the adiabatic changes in gas volume. The pressure change corresponding to the restoring force is no longer linearly proportional to the displacement of the ball. Why do these motions still closely approximate harmonic oscillations and satisfy Eq.(
3
)? In fact, the ball’s motion represents a nonlinear, self-sustained oscillation. Koehler presented a theoretical explanation for such self-sustained oscillations in another paper
Koehler1950
. Assuming the ball’s motion to be sinusoidal, he used nonlinear dynamic equations to demonstrate that the pressure fluctuations are also approximately sinusoidal, with the oscillation frequency closely matching the Rüchardt frequency. However, this analysis is indirect, with a lengthy and dense derivation, potentially posing challenges to readers given the complexity of the calculations. To our knowledge, no other studies provide a more accessible theoretical analysis of this issue. This might explain why textbooks often mention it briefly, stating, for example, that “the pressure changes caused by gas supply and leakage are relatively small, so…” without offering detailed explanations.

The following sections of this paper will revisit this issue and provide a concise theoretical interpretation of the self-sustained oscillations. The paper is organized as follows. In section 2, following Koehler’s approximation for pressure changes, we explicitly present the dynamic equations. In section 3, we dimensionless the model equations and expressed them as 3D piecewise linear differential systems, and analyzed the behavior of the systems near their fixed points in a 2D projection plane. In section 4, we qualitatively analyze the properties of periodic solutions of the system from a geometric perspective, demonstrating that the oscillation frequency in Koehler’s experiment is nearly equal to the
Rüchardt
frequency.

II
Dynamic equations of self-sustained oscillations

Let
x
and
v
denote the vertical coordinate and velocity of the ball,
P
the pressure in the vessel (assumed spatially uniform) which is a function of the ball’s coordinate
x
and time
t
. The dynamic equations are

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}=v,

(4a)

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{(P-P_{0})\pi R^{2}}{m},

(4b)

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dt}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}+\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}.

(4c)

Eq.(
4c
) shows that the rate of pressure change consists of two parts: the term
{\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}}{\frac{dx}{dt}}
caused by the adiabatic change of gas volume due to the motion of the ball, and the term
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
contributed by gas supply and leakage. For the adiabatic process of an ideal gas,

\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}=-\frac{\gamma P\pi R^{2}}{V}\sim-\frac{\gamma P_{0}\pi R^{2}}{V_{0}}.

(5)

For
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
, here we use the approximation from Koehler’s paper
Koehler1950
. The contribution to
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
due to gas supply is approximated as a positive constant

\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}_{supply}=jB.

(6)

The contribution to
\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}
due to the gas leakage is approximated as proportional to the difference in pressure between the inside and outside of the container.

\frac{\partial P}{\partial t}_{leakage}=-g(x)(P-B),\quad g(x)=\begin{cases}e+f,&\quad x\geq 0\\
e,&\quad x<0\end{cases},

(7)

The coordinate origin is defined at the center of the small hole on the tube. When
x<0
, gas escapes solely through the annular gap between the ball and the tube wall. When
x\geq 0
, gas escapes through both the annular gap around the ball and the small hole. The finite size of the small hole is neglected, resulting in a discontinuity of
g(x)
at
0
. However, this effect is minor and not crucial to the main point. By substituting Eqs. (
5
), (
6
), and (
7
) into Eqs. (
4
), the final dynamic equations are derived as

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{dt}=v,

(8a)

\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{(P-P_{0})\pi R^{2}}{m},

(8b)

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dt}=-\frac{\gamma P_{0}\pi R^{2}}{V_{0}}v+jB-g(x)(P-B).

(8c)

When the term
jB-g(x)(P-B)
is removed, the equations reduce to those describing harmonic motion in the ideal Rüchardt experiment. The Rüchardt frequency is given by
\omega^{2}=\frac{\gamma P_{0}\pi^{2}R^{4}}{mV_{0}}
(equivalent to Eq. (
3
)). The term involving the piecewise function
g(x)
introduces nonlinearity. As a result, the equations can no longer be solved analytically.

III
Preliminary qualitative analysis

We first nondimensionalize Eqs. (
8
) as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{dy}{d\tau}=u,

(9a)

\displaystyle\frac{du}{d\tau}=Ap,

(9b)

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\tau}=-\frac{1}{A}u-G(y)p+[J-G(y)(p_{0}-1)],

(9c)

where
\tau=\omega t,y=\frac{x}{R},u=\frac{v}{R\omega},p=\frac{P-P_{0}}{B},p_{0}=\frac{P_{0}}{B},A=\frac{\pi RB}{m\omega^{2}},J=\frac{j}{\omega}
, and

G(y)=\frac{g(x)}{\omega}=\begin{cases}G_{+}=(e+f)/\omega,\quad&y\geq 0,\\
G_{-}=e/\omega,\quad&y<0.\end{cases}

In mathematics, Eqs.(
9
) can be classified as piecewise linear differential systems (PWLD systems). These systems consist of two linear subsystems separated by the plane defined by
\{y=0\}
, referred to as the separation plane. They can be written in matrix form as

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{cases}M_{+}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{+}&\mathrm{if}\quad y\geq 0\\
M_{-}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{-}&\mathrm{if}\quad y<0\end{cases}

(10)

with

\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix}y\\
u\\
p\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&A\\
0&-\frac{1}{A}&-G_{+}\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
0&0&A\\
0&-\frac{1}{A}&-G_{-}\end{pmatrix}

and

\mathbf{e}_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
J-G_{+}(p_{0}-1)\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{e}_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
J-G_{-}(p_{0}-1)\end{pmatrix}.

PWLD systems are widely used to model real-world phenomena in fields such as electrical circuits, control systems, impact and friction mechanics, and biological systems. However, analyzing periodic solutions, which often correspond to steady-state responses in general PWLD systems, is notoriously challenging
Jeffrey2018
. This difficulty arises because PWLD systems autonomously switch their behavior at specific boundaries, leading to discontinuities and distinct sets of governing equations in different regions of the phase space. Accurately handling transitions across these boundaries often requires solving implicit or transcendental equations, which can be particularly challenging or even infeasible in practice. Despite these challenges, the following sections of this paper will provide an approximate analysis to demonstrate if the periodic solutions exist, specifically, solutions in which the small ball passes through the hole position once upward and once downward during a single period, then the system’s motion frequency is indeed very close to the
Rüchardt
frequency.

Analyzing fixed points (equilibrium points) yields valuable insights for dynamical systems. In both half-space
\{y\geq 0\}
and
\{y<0\}
, Eqs.(
9
) do not exhibit global fixed points. However, note that on the right-hand side of the equations, the variable
y
appears only within
G(y)
, whose constant value is piecewise determined by
y
. Thus,
y
can be treated as a parameter, allowing for an analysis of the system in the
(u,p)
projection plane for each half-space,
\{y\geq 0\}
and
\{y<0\}
.

\displaystyle\frac{du}{d\tau}=Ap,

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\tau}=-\frac{1}{A}u-Gp+[J-G(p_{0}-1)],

where
G
represents
G(y)
, with its values in the two half-space
\{y\geq 0\}
and
\{y<0\}
denoted as
G_{+}
and
G_{-}
, respectively. Let
\frac{du}{d\tau}=0,\frac{dp}{d\tau}=0
, we obtain the fixed point of this subsystem, denoted as
(u^{*},p^{*})
, given by

u^{*}=A\left[J-G(p_{0}-1)\right],\quad p^{*}=0.

Next, the local behavior around the fixed point is analyzed. Let
u=u^{*}+\eta(\tau),p=p^{*}+\xi(\tau)
, where
\eta(\tau)
and
\xi(\tau)
represent small deviations from the fixed point. Substituting these into Eqs. (
11
), the system is rewritten as

\frac{d}{d\tau}\binom{\eta}{\xi}=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
-\frac{1}{A}&-G\end{bmatrix}\binom{\eta}{\xi}.

(12)

The Jacobian matrix
\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
-\frac{1}{A}&-G\end{bmatrix}
has eigenvalues
\lambda=-\frac{G}{2}\pm i\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}}
.
Since
G
is positive and much smaller than
1
(as shown in Appendix
B
, which estimates the experimental values of parameters such as
A
,
J
, and
G
), the term
1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}
is positive. Therefore, the fixed point
(u^{*},p^{*})
is a stable focus, and any trajectory in the plane will spiral towards this point. The perturbations
\eta(\tau)
and
\xi(\tau)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\eta(\tau)=C_{1}\exp(-\frac{G}{2}\tau)\cos(\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}}\tau+\Phi),

(13a)

\displaystyle\xi(\tau)=\frac{1}{A}\frac{du}{d\tau}=-C_{1}\exp(-\frac{G}{2}\tau)\cos(\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}}\tau+\Phi-\varphi),

(13b)

where
C_{1}
,
\Phi
are undetermined coefficients related to initial values, and
tan(\varphi)=\frac{\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}}}{\frac{G}{2}}
. These results show that the perturbations in the ball’s velocity and the gas pressure are both characterized by decaying oscillations with a frequency of
\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}}\sim 1
. This indicates that in Koehler’s experiment, whether the ball is below the small hole (in half space
{y<0}
) or above it (in half space
y\geq 0
), the changes in the ball’s velocity and the gas pressure remain closely approximated by harmonic motion, with a frequency nearly identical to the Rüchardt frequency
\omega
.

It is important to note that, so far, we can only state that the oscillation frequency of the system within each subspace is close to the Rüchardt frequency. However, we cannot arbitrarily say that the frequency of the overall motion, obtained by combining the trajectories from each subspace, is also close to the Rüchardt frequency. This is because the exact duration for which the system remains in each subspace is not known. For instance, let
T_{0}
represent the Rüchardt period. If the system spends
\frac{T_{0}}{2}
in one half-space and
\frac{T_{0}}{3}
in another half-space, which may be possible, the total period of the motion will be
(\frac{T_{0}}{2}+\frac{T_{0}}{3})
, not
T_{0}
, resulting in a frequency that deviates from the Rüchardt frequency. In the following section, we will present a geometric analysis to demonstrate that, under appropriate experimental conditions, when the system alternates between two half-spaces, the overall motion period approaches
T_{0}
, and the oscillation frequency remains close to the Rüchardt frequency.

IV
Discussion on Periodic Solutions of Model Equations

In this section, we will estimate the time the system spends in each subspace from a geometric perspective in phase space and demonstrate that the overall motion period approaches
T_{0}
. We find that the original 3D PWLD system with one separation plane
\{y=0\}
can be equivalently simplified to a 2D PWLD system with a single separation straight line, which is more analytically tractable. Although this section is somewhat lengthy, it essentially describes how the system’s trajectory revolves around two alternating focal points in a periodic manner.

IV.1
Difficulties in analyzing periodic solutions of the 3D PWLD system

Let us first provide a detailed explanation of the difficulty in rigorously analyzing the periodic solutions of system (
10
). The Poincaré mapping is a powerful tool for studying periodic solutions in dynamical systems. However, for general PWLD systems, constructing a Poincaré map requires careful treatment of transitions across the switching surface within each subspace, which presents significant challenges. For system (
10
), we treat the separation plane
\{y=0\}
as the Poincaré section. As discussed previously, when the system operates in any half-space, the velocity of the ball can be represented as

u=u^{*}+\eta(\tau)=u^{*}+C_{1}\exp(-\alpha\tau)\cos(\Omega\tau+\Phi),

(14)

where
\alpha=\frac{G}{2}
,
\Omega=\sqrt{1-\frac{G^{2}}{4}}
. According to Eq. (
9
a), the expression for the coordinates of the ball can be obtained as

\displaystyle y(\tau)

\displaystyle=y_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}u(t)dt

(15)

\displaystyle=y_{0}+u^{*}\tau+\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha^{2}+\Omega^{2}}\left\{-\alpha e^{-\alpha\tau}\cos(\Omega\tau+\Phi)+\Omega e^{-\alpha\tau}\sin(\Omega\tau+\Phi)+\alpha\cos(\Phi)-\Omega\sin(\Phi)\right\}.

By solving
y(\tau)=0
, in principle, the state of the system when switching between subspaces and its running time in each subspace can be obtained. However,
y(\tau)=0
is too complex to yield analytical solutions, which complicates the construction of Poincaré mappings to analyze the periodic solution behavior of the system.

IV.2
Simplified problem: 2D PWLD system with one separation straight line

From the analysis above, it is evident that the trajectory of the system in any half-space, when projected onto the
(u,p)
plane, appears as a simple spiral around the focus. However, the switching between subspaces is controlled by the condition
y=0
in the third dimension, which presents significant challenges in solving for the switching state. To avoid this difficulty, we simplify system (
10
) by reducing its dimensionality, resulting in a purely 2D PWLD system as follows:

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{cases}M_{+}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{+}&\mathrm{if}\quad p\leq 0\\
M_{-}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}_{-}&\mathrm{if}\quad p>0\end{cases}

(16)

with

\mathbf{x}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
p\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
-\frac{1}{A}&-G_{+}\end{pmatrix},\quad M_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\
-\frac{1}{A}&-G_{-}\end{pmatrix},

and

\mathbf{e}_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
u^{*}_{+}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{e}_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
u^{*}_{-}\end{pmatrix},

where

u^{*}_{+}=A\left[J-G_{+}(p_{0}-1)\right]<0
(the ball’s motion tends towards the steady state of falling),

u^{*}_{-}=A\left[J-G_{-}(p_{0}-1)\right]>0
(the ball’s motion tends towards the steady state of rising).

IV.3
The original 3D PWLD system with one separation plane y=0

Returning to the original system described by Eqs.
10

\{y=0\}
. For the most typical and simplest periodic solution, the trajectory crosses the plane
\{y=0\}

Specifically, a periodic solution that alternates between the half-spaces
{y\geq 0}
and

In Koehler’s experiment, it is essential to adjust the gas flow rate to ensure that the small ball oscillates symmetrically about the position of the hole. In the theoretical analysis presented in Koehler’s paper
Koehler1950
, it is assumed that the small ball spends equal amounts of time above and below the hole during each oscillation cycle. This assumption is necessary for carrying out the integration required for Fourier series analysis. The analysis in our paper offers a clearer understanding of the rationale behind this requirement, avoiding the need for complex mathematical calculations.

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we revisit Koehler’s experiment and provide a concise theoretical interpretation of the self-sustained oscillations observed in the system. Without resorting to complex calculations, our analysis demonstrates that the oscillation frequency in Koehler’s experiment approximates the Rüchardt frequency. After explicitly presenting the governing equations of the model, we found that it is challenging to mathematically determine the precise parameter conditions under which the system exhibits periodic solutions close to the Rüchardt frequency. However, we prove that if such periodic solutions exist, specifically, solutions in which the small ball passes through the hole position once upward and once downward during a single period, then the system’s motion frequency is indeed very close to the Rüchardt frequency.

The self-sustained oscillations in Koehler’s experiment differ fundamentally from simple harmonic or linearly forced oscillations, representing an early nonlinear phenomenon typically encountered by undergraduates. While students can complete the experimental procedures without distinguishing between the principles underlying Koehler’s and the Rüchardt experiments, we believe that for students proficient in observation and inquiry, exploring the theoretical issues behind Koehler’s experiment can deepen their understanding of periodic motion and offer a practical scenario for studying dynamical systems.
