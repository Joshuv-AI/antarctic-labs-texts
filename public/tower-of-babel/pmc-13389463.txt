Therapeutic potential and synergistic effects of antifungal and off target drugs against clinical isolates of Cryptococcus laurentii
Authors: Isha Ajmal, Saba Sana, Nadia Mukhtar, Qaiser Farid Khan, Ali Ahmad, Muhammad Asif, Fatima Basharat, Molalign Assefa, Khalid J. Alzahrani, Khalaf F. Alsharif, Fuad M. Alzahrani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cryptococcus laurentii is an emerging opportunistic yeast pathogen associated with unpredictable antifungal susceptibility and biofilm-mediated resistance. This study investigated the therapeutic potential and synergistic interactions of standard antifungals (fluconazole, amphotericin B) and off-target drugs against clinical isolates of C. laurentii. Three clinical isolates recovered from blood (n = 2) and cerebrospinal fluid (n = 1) were confirmed by phenotypic characteristics and PCR amplification of the 18 S rRNA gene (~ 480 bp). Biofilm formation was screened using Congo red agar and quantified via crystal violet assay. Susceptibility testing employed CLSI-guided disc/well diffusion and broth microdilution methods for antifungals and 14 off-target drugs. Minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations (MBICs) were determined, and synergistic effects were evaluated using checkerboard assays (fractional inhibitory concentration index, FICI) in planktonic cells and biofilms, supplemented by coverslip biofilm reduction assay. Isolates showed moderate biofilm production (mean OD₅₉₅ = 0.32 ± 0.008), fluconazole susceptibility (mean MIC = 0.77 µg/mL), and amphotericin B resistance (mean MIC = 4.48 µg/mL). Diclofenac sodium exhibited the strongest off-target activity (mean MIC = 1.32 µg/mL). Checkerboard assays revealed synergy between fluconazole + amphotericin B (FICI = 0.5) and fluconazole + diclofenac sodium (FICI = 0.32) in planktonic cells, with additive effects against biofilms (FICI = 0.9). The fluconazole-diclofenac combination significantly disrupted biofilm matrix. Diclofenac sodium enhances fluconazole efficacy and disrupts biofilms, supporting drug repurposing as an adjuvant strategy for managing biofilm-associated C. laurentii infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51373-6.

Introduction

Cryptococcosis represents a significant systemic fungal infection with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, including meningoencephalitis, pneumonia, and disseminated disease states. This condition is predominantly caused by encapsulated yeasts within the Cryptococcus genus, which exhibit cell sizes ranging from 2 to 10 μm and are distinguished by their narrow-neck budding morphology1. The infection typically initiates through inhalation of environmental basidiospores, leading to pulmonary colonization that can escalate to severe central nervous system (CNS) involvement, especially in immunocompromised hosts. Pulmonary cryptococcosis often presents with symptoms such as fever, cough, chest pain, and dyspnea, whereas meningoencephalitis is characterized by headache, photophobia, altered mental status, and neck stiffness2. Key risk factors encompass advanced HIV infection, solid organ transplantation, prolonged corticosteroid therapy, and malignancies, with CD4 + T-cell counts below 200 cells/mm³ posing the greatest vulnerability3. On a global scale, cryptococcal meningitis impacts approximately 223,000 individuals each year, resulting in over 181,000 fatalities, yielding a mortality rate surpassing 80% in resource-constrained settings4. While C. neoformans and C. gattii continue to be the primary etiological agents, non-neoformans species are increasingly recognized in human infections. Cryptococcus laurentii, initially isolated in 1920 from fermented palm sap in the Congo, has evolved into an emerging opportunistic pathogen5. It is implicated in bloodstream infections, meningitis, peritonitis, and cutaneous lesions, particularly among immunocompromised individuals, and is often linked to indwelling medical devices owing to its pronounced biofilm-forming capabilities. Environmental sources include soil, water, wood debris, avian excreta, and various foodstuffs, with reports of its presence in extreme environments from the Antarctic to the Himalayas6,7. The pathogenesis of C. laurentii infections unfolds in four principal stages: asymptomatic latent infection, localized pulmonary disease, hematogenous dissemination, and relapse due to inadequate treatment. Virulence factors comprise a polysaccharide capsule, melanin production, extracellular enzymes (such as phospholipase, urease, and laccase), formation of titan cells, and biofilm elaboration8. Biofilms provide a protective barrier against host immune responses and antifungal agents, while titan cells evade phagocytosis and promote CNS invasion9,10. Standard treatment protocols advocate for induction therapy with amphotericin B plus flucytosine, succeeded by high-dose fluconazole for consolidation and maintenance phases. Nevertheless, adverse effects including nephrotoxicity, electrolyte imbalances, hepatotoxicity, and myelosuppression restrict their clinical utility11,12. Moreover, resistance mechanisms—encompassing biofilm-mediated barriers, efflux pump upregulation, ERG11 gene mutations, and capsule remodeling—exacerbate treatment failures13. In response to these challenges, drug repurposing has gained prominence as an efficient strategy to broaden the antifungal repertoire by leveraging approved non-antifungal pharmaceuticals. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen and diclofenac sodium, interfere with fungal prostaglandin E2 pathways, compromise membrane integrity, and destabilize biofilm structures. Similarly, statins and phenothiazines modulate ergosterol biosynthesis and efflux mechanisms, exhibiting supplementary antifungal properties14. Combination therapies amplify efficacy, minimize required dosages, and mitigate resistance development15,16. The significance of drug repurposing lies in its ability to accelerate therapeutic innovation by circumventing the protracted and costly processes of de novo drug development, thereby offering rapid, cost-effective solutions for combating resistant fungal pathogens like C. laurentii. This approach not only repurposes existing drugs with established safety profiles but also addresses the urgent need for novel strategies in settings where conventional antifungals falter due to resistance or toxicity. By highlighting the potential of off-target drugs to synergize with standard antifungals, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting repurposed agents as adjuvants, particularly in biofilm-associated infections where monotherapy often proves inadequate. Drawing parallels from similar investigations, such as those on NSAID repurposing against Candida species17. this work aims to provide foundational insights into optimizing treatment regimens for C. laurentii, an understudied yet clinically relevant pathogen.

This investigation sought to isolate and molecularly characterize clinical strains of C. laurentii, assess their susceptibility to antifungals and biofilm-forming potential, evaluate the antifungal activity of 14 off-target drugs, and examine synergistic interactions between conventional antifungals and repurposed agents in both planktonic and biofilm contexts.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

A total of 50 clinical specimens, comprising 35 blood samples and 15 cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples, were collected from immunocompromised patients between January and June 2023 at two tertiary care hospitals in Lahore, Pakistan. The samples were collected based on specimen availability and initial screening for yeast infections ensuring representation of both bloodstream and cerebrospinal infections. The patients were selected based on clinical suspicion of fungal infection and recovery of non-neoformans Cryptococcus species during routine laboratory screening. Blood specimens were aseptically drawn via peripheral venipuncture and inoculated into BACT/ALERT FA Plus aerobic culture bottles (bioMérieux, France) to facilitate microbial growth detection. CSF samples were obtained through lumbar puncture performed by a qualified neurologist under stringent sterile conditions18. Each sample was assigned a unique identifier, maintained at 4 °C during transport, and processed within 4 h of collection to preserve viability and minimize contamination risks. The culture poitive samples were transported to microbiology laboratory GCU for further experimentation. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Government College University Lahore (Approval No. GCU/IEC/2023/112), and written informed consent was secured from all participants or their legal guardians prior to sample collection. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki19.

Fungal isolation and phenotypic characterization

The collected samples were streaked onto Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA; Oxoid, UK) plates supplemented with chloramphenicol (50 mg/L) to inhibit bacterial overgrowth, and the plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 48–72 h. Colonies exhibiting cream-colored, mucoid, and smooth morphology suggestive of yeast were sub-cultured onto potato dextrose agar (PDA; HiMedia) to ensure purity and further propagation. For morphological confirmation, negative staining with nigrosine was conducted, and preparations were examined under light microscopy at 1000× magnification to verify the presence of encapsulated, ovoid yeast cells with characteristic narrow-neck budding. Biochemical profiling involved testing for urease activity on Christensen’s urea agar (incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, observing for a color change to pink), carbohydrate fermentation in phenol red broth supplemented with 1% glucose or sucrose (monitoring for acid production indicated by yellow coloration and gas formation)20.

Morphology, biolchemical profile and 18s RNA gene amplification was used to confirm C. laurentii.

Biofilm screening and quantification

Biofilm production was screened by inoculating on PDA plate containing Congo-red dye (0.02%) and incubating at 37℃ for 24–48 h23. Standardized yeast suspensions (0.5 McFarland, ~ 1–5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL) were prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4)24. One hundred microliters were added to flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene microtiter plates containing 100 µL potato dextrose broth (PDB) per well. Plates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h to allow biofilm formation. Non-adherent cells were removed by gentle washing (3×) with 200 µL sterile PBS. Biofilms were fixed with 150 µL methanol (10 min), air-dried, and stained with 150 µL of 0.1% crystal violet (30 min). Excess stain was removed by washing with distilled water. Bound stain was solubilized in 150 µL of 95% ethanol, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm using a microplate reader25. Antifungal and off-target drug susceptibility testing

Agar Disc diffusion and well diffusion method were used to assess the antifungal susceptibility pattern of yeast.

Drug selection criteria: A rational drug repositioning strategy was used as the basis of selecting off-target drugs. The compounds were shortlisted using a number of criteria: (i) already demonstrated antifungal or antibiofilm activity; (ii) known mechanisms of action that impact fungal cell membrane integrity or stress response pathways; (iii) reported or probable synergistic interactions with established antifungal agents; (iv) established clinical safety profiles; and (v) structural and pharmacological diversity. Through this approach, drugs having great translational capabilities and mechanistic activity against resistant cryptococcus isolates were selected.

Disc and well diffusion: Antifungal susceptibility was performed on Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with 2% glucose. A unform lawn was prepared using 0.5 McFarland yeast suspension. Commercial antifungal discs (fluconazole 10 µg, amphotericin B 25 µg; HiMedia, India) were applied. For off-target drugs, 6-mm wells were formed using a sterile cork borer followed by sealing with molten MHA, and filled with 100 µL of drug solution (paracetamol 100 mg, aspirin 100 mg, nimesulide 100 mg, diclofenac sodium 50 mg, ibuprofen 100 mg, domperidone 10 mg, glyceryl trinitrate 2.6 mg, atorvastatin 20 mg, amlodipine 5 mg, ramipril 5 mg, diphenhydramine 50 mg, attapulgite 100 mg, mefenamic acid 100 mg, piroxicam-β-cyclodextrin 20 mg, metronidazole 100 mg). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Zones of inhibition were measured in millimeter (mm) and interpreted using CLSI M44-A2 breakpoints for Candida spp. (applied due to lack of C. laurentii standards): fluconazole ≥ 19 mm (susceptible), 15–18 mm (susceptible-dose dependent), ≤ 14 mm (resistant); amphotericin B ≥ 15 mm (susceptible), ≤ 14 mm (resistant)26. Based on the diameter of zones of inhibition, the activity levels of the 14 non-antifungal drugs were categorized as high, moderate, or low. This helped in comparative assessment of relative antifungal activity and the identification of promising candidates for further evaluation.

MIC determination

MIC of off target drugs and antifungals aginst planktonic cells was determined by microbroth dilution method determined using 96-well flat-bottom microtiter plates (Corning, USA). Two-fold serial dilutions of each drug (100 µL) were prepared in Potato dextrose broth (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) buffered to pH 7.0. Final drug concentrations ranged from 0.019 to 100 µg/mL. Inoculum was standardized to 1 McFarland solution in Potato dextrose broth. A volume of 100 µL of inoculum were added to each well (final volume 200 µL). Each experiment was conducted with growth control (10th well, drug free inoculation) and sterility control (11th, broth medium only). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h with orbital shaking (100 rpm). Growth inhibition was evaluated visually and confirmed by spectrophotomet (OD₆₀₀) using a Multiskan GO reader (Thermo Scientific). The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration inhibiting growth of organism. All tests were performed in triplicate28. The standard antifungal agents (amphotericin B and fluconazole) were used in this study, which served as reference to compare the antifungal properties of the other tested compounds.

Minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration (MBIC)

MBIC (minimum biofilm inhibition) of antifungal and off target drugs aginst biofilm was determined using a biofilm-adapted broth microdilution method in 96-well flat-bottom polystyrene plates (Corning, USA) followed by crytal violet assay. Potato dextrose broth (100 µL) was added to each well. Drug (100 µL) was added to first well and subjected to two-fold serial dilutions up to the 10th well. The 11th well containing broth and inoculum without drug served as the positive control, while the 12th well with broth only was considered the negative control. Standardized inoculum equivalent to 0.5 McFarland solution (100 µL) was added to each well and plates were incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, wells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Biofilms was fixed with 150 µL methanol (10 min), air-dried, and stained with 150 µL 0.1% crystal violet (30 min). After destaining with 95% ethanol (150 µL) absorbance was measured at 595 nm. MBIC was defined as the lowest concentration reducing biofilm biomass by ≥ 50% compared to untreated biofilm control. All assays were performed in triplicate27.

Combination therapy

Drug combinations were evaluated by checkerboard method against planktonic cells as well as biofilms of C. laurentii. Two-fold serial dilutions of each drug were prepared in 96-well microtiter plates. Plates were inoculated with standardized fungal suspension (0.5 McFarland) and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Growth was assessed visually and by optical density using a microtiter plate reader28. The effects of drug combinations were interpreted relative to the activity of individual drugs that were tested under the same condition. Biofilm reduction assay

Sterile coverslips were placed in 24-well plates. Standardized suspension (1 mL, 0.5 McFarland) was added and incubated statically at 37 °C for 48 h to form biofilms. Medium was discarded, coverslips washed with PBS, and treated with 1 mL drug combination for 24 h at 37 °C. Coverslips were washed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, mounted, and observed under light microscope. Untreated coverslips served as positive control; shaken coverslips (150 rpm) as negative control. The samples were treated with a combination of diclofenac sodium and fluconazole and were compared with the untreated samples to evaluate reduction in biofilm formation. The cover slip-based was performed to visually observe and confirm the formation and morphological features of C. laurentii biofilms under the experimental conditions. Though this method does not offer quantitative analysis it provides assessment of biofilm surface adherence, and preliminary evaluation of the effect of drug treatments on biofilm formation29.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the mean differences in antifungal activity (ZOI, MIC, MBIC), in order to determine statistically significant effects.

Results

Isolation and identification

From the 50 clinical specimens examined, yeast growth was observed in 19 samples (38%) on potato dextrose agar following incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. Among these, 16 isolates were identified as Candida species, distributed as follows: Candida albicans (n = 5), Candida krusei (n = 3), Candida parapsilosis (n = 4), and Candida tropicalis (n = 4). The remaining three isolates, recovered from two blood samples and one cerebrospinal fluid sample, were confirmed as Cryptococcus laurentii. All three clinical isolates of Cryptococcus laurentii (CL1, CL2 and CL3) were obtained from different patients and each isolate was treated as an independent sample for analysis. These isolates exhibited characteristic colony morphology, appearing as cream-colored, smooth, slightly mucoid, and concave with rounded edges. Under light microscopy, nigrosin-stained preparations displayed elongated, encapsulated, ovoid yeast cells forming chains with narrow-neck budding. Biochemical assays further supported this identification: the positive urease test was indicated by a color change of Christensen’s medium to pink from yellowish-orange within 24 h due to ammonia production. While, fermentation tests produced a yellow broth with glucose (indicating acid production) but no color change or gas with sucrose. Molecular confirmation was achieved through polymerase chain reaction amplification of the 18 S rRNA gene, yielding a single amplicon band at approximately 480 base pairs on 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis, aligning with the expected size for C. laurentii (Figure S1).

Biofilm formation

All three C. laurentii isolates produced cream-colored colonies on Congo red agar after 48 h of incubation, indicative of moderate extracellular matrix production, as opposed to the black, dry, crystalline colonies typically associated with strong biofilm formers. The crystal violet assay was used to quantify biofilm formation. The cut-off OD (ODc) was calculated as the mean OD of the negative control plus three standard deviations (ODc = 0.11 + 3 × 0.00 = 0.11). The ODc-based biofilm classification thresholds were as follows: non-producer (OD ≤ ODc), weak (ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc), moderate (2 × ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc), and strong (OD > 4 × ODc). All isolates produced biofilms in the moderate range in relation to ODc, as shown by the mean OD595 values of 0.31 ± 0.007 for CL-1, 0.32 ± 0.008 for CL-2, and 0.32 ± 0.008 for CL-3. Applying established classification thresholds based on the control optical density (ODc = 0.11), all isolates were categorized as moderate biofilm producers. Statistical analysis via one-way analysis of variance confirmed no significant differences in biofilm biomass among the isolates (p = 0.92) (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Antifungal susceptibility

Disc diffusion assays on Mueller-Hinton agar demonstrated large zone of inhibition (ZOI) for fluconazole, ranging from 48 to 60 mm across the isolates (mean 53.9 ± 6.1 mm), confirming susceptibility according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute M44-A breakpoints adapted for Candida species (susceptible: ≥19 mm). In contrast, amphotericin B produced smaller ZOI ranging from 12 to 13 mm (mean 12.7 ± 0.6 mm), indicating resistance (resistant: ≤14 mm). Broth microdilution assays corroborated these findings, with fluconazole minimum inhibitory concentrations of 0.625 µg/mL for CL-1, 0.84 µg/mL for CL-2, and 0.84 µg/mL for CL-3 (overall mean 0.77 ± 0.12 µg/mL). Amphotericin B minimum inhibitory concentrations were 3.12 µg/mL for CL-1, 6.25 µg/mL for CL-2, and 6.25 µg/mL for CL-3 (mean 4.48 ± 1.56 µg/mL). As no specie-specific clinical breakpoints area available for C. laurentii the interpretations of the results were made on the basis of cutoff values (CVs) for C. neoformans. The CV for amphotericin B is 1 µg/mL, and for fluconazole it is 8 µg/mL, representing the upper limit of wild-type susceptibility. All three isolates were resistant to amphotericin B having MICs of 3.12–6.25 µg/mL, which is greater than the CV of 1 µg/mL, indicating that all three isolates were resistant. In contrast, all the isolates were sensitive to fluconazole with MICs between 0.625 and 0.84 µg/mL, which is much lower than the 8 µg/mL CV. Growth control and broth control were used to validate the result of experiment. One-way analysis of variance revealed no significant inter-isolate variation in susceptibility for fluconazole (p = 0.80) or amphotericin B (p = 0.50) (Table 2; Figs. 2 and 3). Normal saline was used as negative control in both experiments (no ZOI was observed in disc diffusion and growth was observed in normal saline different dilutions indicated lack of antifungal potential).

Off-target drug susceptibility

Well diffusion screening of 14 non-antifungal compounds identified diclofenac sodium as the most potent agent, producing zones of inhibition (ZOI) ranging from 24 to 26 mm (mean 25.3 ± 1.2 mm). Aspirin followed with zones of 15–17 mm (mean 16.0 ± 1.0 mm), diphenhydramine with 11–14 mm (mean 12.5 ± 1.5 mm), and ibuprofen with 10–12 mm (mean 11.0 ± 1.0 mm). The remaining 10 compounds (paracetamol, nimesulide, domperidone, glyceryl trinitrate, atorvastatin, amlodipine, ramipril, attapulgite, mefenamic acid, piroxicam-β-cyclodextrin, and metronidazole) produced no measurable ZOI, indicating negligible activity. The drugs showed antifungal activity in well diffusion were further selected for MIC determination. For diclofenac sodium, broth microdilution assays yielded minimum inhibitory concentrations of 1.56 µg/mL for CL-1, 1.56 µg/mL for CL-2, and 0.84 µg/mL for CL-3 (mean 1.32 ± 0.40 µg/mL), with no statistically significant differences among isolates (one-way ANOVA, p = 0.55). Growth control and broth control were used to validate the result of experiment. Differences in compound diffusion, solubility, or stability on agar may be the cause of the disparity between aspirin susceptibility reported by well diffusion and dilution methods. This emphasizes that results should be evaluated cautiously since disc diffusion may underestimate the activity of non-antifungal drugs. Among the 14 selected off-target drugs screened, only diclofenac sodium, aspirin, diphenhydramine, and ibuprofen produced measurable ZOI, with diclofenac sodium exhibiting the strongest activity (Table 3; Fig. 4). Normal saline was used as negative control in both experiments (no ZOI was observed in disc diffusion and growth was observed in normal saline different dilutions indicated lack of antifungal potential).

MBIC determination

Fluconazole exhibited the lowest minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations, ranging from 0.67 to 1.25 µg/mL across isolates (mean 1.05 ± 0.29 µg/mL), reflecting effective inhibition of biofilm biomass by at least 50% compared to untreated controls. Diclofenac sodium minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations were slightly higher, at 1.56–1.69 µg/mL (mean 1.60 ± 0.06 µg/mL). Amphotericin B required substantially elevated concentrations of 6.25–12.5 µg/mL (mean 8.33 ± 3.12 µg/mL), representing an approximate 8-fold increase relative to its planktonic minimum inhibitory concentrations, underscoring the enhanced resistance conferred by the biofilm state. Inter-isolate variability was not statistically significant for any drug (one-way ANOVA: p = 0.67 for fluconazole, p = 0.90 for diclofenac sodium, p = 0.42 for amphotericin B) (Table 4).

Combination therapy

Checkerboard assays for planktonic cells demonstrated that the fluconazole plus amphotericin B combination reduced individual minimum inhibitory concentrations across isolates, resulting in a fractional inhibitory concentration index of 0.5, interpreted as synergy (fractional inhibitory concentration index ≤ 0.5). The fluconazole plus diclofenac sodium pairing was particularly effective, lowering fluconazole minimum inhibitory concentrations to 0.019–0.039 µg/mL and diclofenac sodium to 0.195–0.78 µg/mL, yielding a fractional inhibitory concentration index of 0.32 (synergy). In contrast, amphotericin B plus diclofenac sodium showed no notable interaction, with a fractional inhibitory concentration index of 1.28 (indifferent; fractional inhibitory concentration index > 1.0 to ≤ 4.0). For biofilms, the fluconazole plus diclofenac sodium combination decreased minimum biofilm inhibitory concentrations to 0.84 µg/mL for fluconazole and 0.156 µg/mL for diclofenac sodium, resulting in a fractional inhibitory concentration index of 0.9 (additive; fractional inhibitory concentration index > 0.5 to ≤ 1.0). The other combinations remained indifferent (fractional inhibitory concentration index = 1.2 for fluconazole plus amphotericin B; 1.26 for amphotericin B plus diclofenac sodium). (Tables 5 and 6).

Biofilm reduction

Coverslip-based biofilms treated with the fluconazole (0.84 µg/mL) plus diclofenac sodium (0.156 µg/mL) combination for 24 h showed substantial architectural disruption under light microscopy. Untreated positive controls exhibited dense, multilayered aggregates of yeast cells embedded within a thick, cohesive extracellular matrix, forming a robust three-dimensional structure with extensive intercellular connections and minimal gaps. In treated samples, this organization was markedly altered, with sparse distribution of adherent cells, fragmentation of the matrix into discontinuous segments, reduced overall thickness (from approximately 50–70 μm in controls to 10–20 μm in treated), and increased exposure of the underlying glass surface, indicative of effective dispersal and inhibition of biofilm integrity. Negative controls, subjected to mechanical shaking at 150 revolutions per minute during formation, displayed virtually no adhesion, resulting in clean glass surfaces devoid of cellular aggregates or matrix residues (Fig. 5). These observations, consistent across triplicate experiments, provide visual evidence supporting the additive effects observed in checkerboard assays and align with enhanced antifungal penetration in disrupted biofilms.

Discussion

The rising incidence of cryptococcosis caused by non-neoformans species underscores the need to explore emerging pathogens and alternative therapeutic strategies. In this study, Cryptococcus laurentii was isolated from 6% of clinical samples, primarily blood and CSF of immunocompromised patients. Although less frequent than C. neoformans, its recovery from sterile sites aligns with prior reports of catheter-associated fungemia and meningitis30,31. Biofilm formation represents a critical virulence determinant in C. laurentii, contributing to persistence on medical devices and antifungal tolerance. All isolates produced moderate biofilms on Congo red agar and in microtiter plates. The susceptibility profiles of two antifungals amphotericin B and fluconazole were evaluated. For disc diffusion method amphotericin B showed small zone of inhibition with mean value of 13 mm. While zone of inhibition of fluconazole was 53 mm indicating sensitivity to fluconazole. These results align with findings of Nunnally et al., 2021 who demonstrated that isolates were susceptible to fluconazole. Another study conducted in 2013 by Pedroso et al., showed that there was only 36.4% agreement between disc diffusion method and broth microdilution method indicating that disc diffusion method may not be reliable for susceptibility testing in C. laurentii32,33.

The findings of this study indicated significant differences in susceptibility of two antifungals34, fluconazole showed mean MIC of 0.77 µg/ml, whereas amphotericin B had mean MIC of 4.48 µg/ml suggesting effectivity of fluconazole against planktonic cells. In 2018, Herket et al., reported fluconazole MIC values between 0.125 and 8 µg/ml, which aligns with this study35. Similarly, Kassi et al., 2016 demonstrated results that are consistent with these findings36. However, results of our study were in contrast with a study performed in 2002 by Averbuch et al., which demonstrated that C. laurentii was resistant to fluconazole. A study conducted in 2012 by Santos et al., demonstrated that high-dose of fluconazole was associated with antagonism as a result of ergosterol depletion, decreasing the binding sites of amphotericin B to fungal membranes. Other reports, however, such as one conducted by Santos et al., 2017 stated synergistic effects of amphotericin B and fluconazole particularly at optimal dosage, which align with this study37,38.

Diclofenac sodium showed notable activity against C. laurentii, with mean MIC value of 1.32 µg/ml, and mean MBIC of 1.56 µg/ml. It is also demonstrated that NSAIDs including diclofenac having activity against Candida species which are in agreement with results of this study. Alam et al., 2017 stated same results highlighting our findings and indicating diclofenac sodium could be used as an adjuvant treatment39. The findings of sousa et al., 2023 are in alignment with results of this study demonstrating diclofenac sodium has potential to disrupt biofilms and act synergistically to improve antifungal effect of combinational therapy40,41.

This study supported synergistic effect between amphotericin B, fluconazole and diclofenac sodium on C. laurentii isolates. This agrees with findings that combination of drugs with distinct mechanisms of action may act against multiple fungal pathways and limit toxicity due to reduced doses of each drug and reduce emergence of resistance42–44. The results were consistent with Zida et al., 2017 where NSAIDs increased permeability of membranes and disturbed biofilm formation, showing potential to enhance antifungal activity45. Our research supported previous findings by not only proving synergistic effect of amphotericin B and fluconazole as described by46, but also integrating addition of diclofenac sodium, adjuvant properties of which in case of antifungal compounds are growing in popularity. These findings support drug repurposing as a viable strategy to overcome resistance and biofilm barriers in C. laurentii infections. Diclofenac sodium, already in clinical use, offers a safe, cost-effective adjuvant with established pharmacokinetics. The in vitro synergy between diclofenac sodium and fluconazole presents diclofenac sodium as a promising adjuvant antifungal agent. Future studies to evaluate the pharmacokinetics and appropriate therapeutic doses will be needed to translate these results into clinical relevance. Future studies should evaluate in vivo efficacy, optimal dosing, and potential host toxicity in combination regimens.

Limitations

A significant study limitation is the small sample size (n = 3) that limits the statistical power and the strength of comparison through ANOVA. Therefore, these findings should be considered preliminary and further investigations with larger sample size are needed to validate and generalize the results.

C. laurentii was the primary focus of this study and the antifungal activity of diclofenac sodium and its interaction with conventional antifungal agents was not evaluated in other species. Therefore, this remains unclear whether these effects are unique to a specific species or broadly applicable across the Cryptococcus genus. In order to determine whether the observed effects are conserved at the genus level or restricted to C. laurentii, a bigger panel of Cryptococcus isolates should be included in the future investigations.

The observed synergistic effect of selected antifungals and off target drugs is strain-specific since only clinical isolates were included in this study. In order to confirm its general application, standard laboratory strains can be evaluated for validation. The antifungal resistance profiles of clinical isolates and wild-type isolates may be different, which could affect outcomes of the drug interaction. Triple-drug interactions were not evaluated as checkerboard analysis was limited to dual-drug combinations in order to assess of synergy. Secondly, evaluation of three drug combinations was beyond the scope of this study due to the increased experimental and analytical complexity involved. Therefore, further research is in future is required to determine any potential antagonistic or synergistic effects with triple drug combinations. To reflect physiological conditions, cultures were incubated at 37℃ but as C. laurentii is a psychotolerant yeast, a lower temperature could facilitate growth and affect recovery rates which should be considered in future studies. This study was limited to in vitro antifungal and biofilm experiments. As the study used the already approved drugs so toxicity profiling was beyond the scope of this study. But to justify the potential clinical use of these combinations, future studies on cytotoxicity and safety profiles can be perfomed.

Conclusion

This study affirms C. laurentii as an emerging threat in immunocompromised populations, marked by moderate biofilm formation and divergent antifungal susceptibilities. All three tested isolates were uniformly susceptible to fluconazole but resistant to amphotericin B. Diclofenac sodium stood out among 14 off-target drugs with robust activity (MIC 1.32 µg/mL) and potentiated fluconazole through planktonic synergy (FICI 0.32) and biofilm additivity (FICI 0.9). Biofilm reuction assays visually confirmed matrix disruption. Drug repurposing with diclofenac sodium emerges as a viable adjuvant tactic for biofilm-linked C. laurentii infections, meriting further preclinical and clinical scrutiny for safety and effectiveness.
