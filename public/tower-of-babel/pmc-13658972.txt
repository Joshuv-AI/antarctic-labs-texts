Comment on: Effect of preoperative vitamin B12 levels on postoperative outcomes in CABG patients
Authors: Mehmet Yılmaz, Hale Yılmaz, Işıl Taşöz Özdaş, Ertan Demirdaş, Muharrem Emre Özdaş, Bilgehan Savaş Öz, Celalettin Günay, Kubilay Karabacak, Murat Kadan, Cengiz Bolcal
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Taşöz Özdaş et al.[1] investigating the association between preoperative vitamin B12 levels and postoperative inotropic requirement in patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting. Identifying an easily accessible biomarker with predictive value is highly relevant for perioperative risk stratification.

The reported association between low B12 levels and increased inotropic requirement is noteworthy; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Vitamin B12 plays a key role in one-carbon metabolism and homocysteine clearance. Reduced B12 levels may lead to elevated homocysteine concentrations, decreased nitric oxide availability, increased oxidative stress, and impaired microvascular responsiveness, ultimately affecting vascular tone regulation.[2, 3] In addition, B12 deficiency has been associated with endothelial dysfunction and altered vascular reactivity, even in the absence of elevated homocysteine levels.

Low preoperative B12 levels may therefore reflect reduced “endothelial reserve”, predisposing patients to vasoplegic states and increased postoperative inotropic or vasoactive support. This may be particularly relevant in vasoplegia, where impaired vascular responsiveness to catecholamines is a key determinant of postoperative hemodynamic instability. One-carbon metabolism disorders have been linked to endothelial dysfunction through nitric oxide depletion and oxidative stress.[2, 3] Our previous work also supports this concept by demonstrating an association between homocysteine levels, folic acid metabolism, and endothelial dysfunction.[4]

However, a direct causal relationship between B12 deficiency and postoperative hemodynamic disturbances remains uncertain. The relationship between B12 levels and cardiovascular outcomes may not be linear, suggesting that B12 deficiency may reflect broader metabolic or inflammatory processes rather than acting as an isolated determinant.

The absence of homocysteine measurements and direct assessment of endothelial function limits mechanistic interpretation. In addition, potential confounding factors—including folate status, renal function, and systemic inflammation—may influence both homocysteine metabolism and postoperative hemodynamic responses.

In conclusion, studies incorporating simultaneous assessment of vitamin B12, homocysteine, and vascular reactivity may help clarify the clinical relevance of these parameters and improve perioperative risk stratification.

We thank Yılmaz and Yılmaz for their interest in our study and for their thoughtful comments.

The authors propose that homocysteine accumulation, nitric oxide depletion, and endothelial dysfunction may serve as potential mediating mechanisms underlying the association between low vitamin B12 levels and increased inotropic requirements. While we agree that these pathways are biologically plausible, we would like to clarify that our study was not designed to investigate these mechanisms. Our primary objective was to evaluate the predictive value of serum vitamin B12—a readily accessible preoperative parameter in routine clinical practice—for perioperative risk stratification, and we believe this objective was achieved.[1] Homocysteine and folate levels are not routinely measured in our CABG patient population. Given the retrospective design of our study, only parameters obtained as part of standard preoperative assessment were available for analysis. This should not be interpreted as a methodological shortcoming, but rather as a direct reflection of real-world clinical practice. Indeed, one of the strengths of our study lies in its ability to demonstrate a meaningful prognostic association using data already available in routine care, without the need for additional testing. Prospective studies incorporating homocysteine, folate, and vascular reactivity parameters would certainly be valuable in addressing mechanistic questions.

Renal function data were available and included in our analyses. Notably, the independent prognostic signal of vitamin B12 levels remained significant after controlling for renal function, further supporting its value as an independent predictor.

In conclusion, our retrospective study demonstrates that preoperative vitamin B12 level is a clinically applicable, easily accessible, and meaningful prognostic indicator in CABG patients. Elucidating the mechanistic framework underlying this association remains a subject for future prospective research.

Sincerely,
