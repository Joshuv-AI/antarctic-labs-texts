Association Between Sleep Characteristics and Anthropometric Indices Among Adults: A Cross‐Sectional Study From the PERSIAN Guilan Cohort Study
Authors: Niloufar Nezhadrekabi, Masoome Aghaei, Adeleh Isanazr, Soheil Hassanipour, Mohammadreza Naghipour, Sara yeganeh, Farahnaz Joukar, Fariborz Mansour‐Ghanaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSleep characteristics have been increasingly recognized as important determinants of obesity and metabolic health. This study aimed to investigate the association between sleep characteristics and obesity indices among adults participating in the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) Guilan Cohort Study (PGCS).MethodsThis cross‐sectional analytical study included 10,520 adults aged 35–70. Demographic, lifestyle, anthropometric, and sleep‐related data were collected using standardized questionnaires. Obesity indices included body mass index (BMI), waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR), and waist‐to‐height ratio (WHtR). Associations between sleep characteristics and obesity indices were evaluated using chi‐square tests and logistic regression analyses. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated after controlling for potential confounders.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 51.52 ± 8.90 years, and 53.5% were women. Overall, 32.7% of participants were obese based on BMI, with significantly higher prevalence among women than men (47.0% vs. 16.1%, p < 0.001). Short sleep duration (< 7 h) was reported by 43.7% of participants. In men, sleeping 7–9 h and > 9 h was associated with lower odds of BMI‐defined obesity compared with sleeping < 7 h (aOR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.64–0.88 and aOR= 0.54, 95% CI: 0.38–0.78, respectively). Longer sleep duration was also associated with lower odds of WHR‐ and WHtR‐defined obesity in men (p < 0.05). Conversely, daytime sleeping and daytime napping were associated with higher odds of central obesity among men (p < 0.05). In women, later bedtime was independently associated with higher odds of obesity, while waking between 06:00 and 09:00 was associated with lower odds of central obesity (p < 0.05).ConclusionSleep characteristics were significantly associated with general and central obesity, with notable sex‐specific differences. These findings support the integration of sleep assessment into obesity prevention and management strategies.

Introduction

Obesity has become one of the most important public health challenges worldwide, contributing substantially to the burden of cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and overall mortality [1, 2]. Its prevalence has increased dramatically over recent decades, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries undergoing rapid epidemiological and nutritional transitions. In Iran, as in many developing settings, both general and central obesity are highly prevalent and represent a major health concern across different age groups and socioeconomic status [3, 4, 5].

In addition to well‐established determinants such as dietary habits, physical activity, and genetic susceptibility, growing evidence suggests that sleep characteristics play a crucial role in the development of obesity. Sleep is a fundamental biological process that regulates neuroendocrine function, energy metabolism, and behavioral processes [6, 7]. Disruption in sleep duration, timing, and quality has been increasingly associated with metabolic dysregulation, increased appetite, body mass index (BMI), waist‐to‐hip ratio (WHR), waist‐to‐height ratio (WHtR), and weight gain [8, 9]. In particular, short sleep duration has been linked to alterations in appetite‐regulating hormones, including decreased leptin and increased ghrelin levels, which may promote hyperphagia and positive energy balance [10, 11]. Moreover, circadian misalignment and irregular sleep timing may further contribute to metabolic disturbances through impaired glucose metabolism and increased insulin resistance [12, 13].

Emerging evidence further indicates that sleep disturbances may promote obesity through multiple interconnected biological pathways, including chronic low‐grade inflammation, dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis with elevated cortisol secretion, impaired insulin sensitivity, and alterations in energy expenditure. These mechanisms may increase adiposity directly and indirectly by promoting unhealthy eating behaviors, reducing physical activity, and disrupting overall energy homeostasis [14, 15, 16].

Sleep behaviors are also closely intertwined with other lifestyle factors, particularly dietary habits. Insufficient or irregular sleep has been associated with greater consumption of energy‐dense foods, increased snacking, late‐night eating, and poorer overall diet quality, all of which may contribute to excess weight gain. Conversely, healthy dietary patterns may support better sleep quality and metabolic regulation, suggesting a bidirectional relationship between sleep and nutrition [14, 17]. Previous studies have also demonstrated sex‐specific interrelationships between sleeping habits, nutritional behaviors, and somatic health indicators, highlighting that sleep should be considered within a broader behavioral context rather than as an isolated determinant of obesity [18].

Epidemiological studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between sleep duration and obesity. While several studies have demonstrated that short sleep is associated with increased risk of obesity, others have suggested a U‐shaped association, where both short and long sleep durations are linked to adverse metabolic outcomes [19]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that sex differences may modify the association between sleep characteristics and obesity. Hormonal differences, variations in fat distribution, behavioral factors, and differences in sleep architecture between men and women may lead to distinct metabolic responses to sleep disturbances [20, 21].

In addition, most previous studies have focused primarily on sleep duration, whereas other sleep dimensions, including bedtime, daytime sleeping, and napping, have received comparatively less attention despite their potential contributions to metabolic health [14, 15, 17]. Evaluating multiple sleep characteristics simultaneously may therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of their independent associations with general and central obesity. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between multiple sleep characteristics and anthropometric indices, among adults participating in the Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (PERSIAN) Guilan Cohort Study (PGCS).

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional analytical study was conducted using data from the PGCS (2014–2017). Eligible participants included adults aged 35–70 years residing in the predefined study area [22, 23]. Participants with severe psychiatric disorders, intellectual disabilities, or acute psychiatric illnesses such as psychosis were excluded. Among the 10,520 eligible participants included in the PGCS baseline survey, individuals with complete information on sleep characteristics, anthropometric measurements, and covariates required for the present analyses were included. Participants with missing data on the outcome variables or key exposure variables were excluded using a complete‐case approach.

Data Collection

Demographic, lifestyle, clinical, anthropometric, and sleep‐related data were collected using standardized questionnaires administered by trained interviewers following the PERSIAN Cohort protocol. Information regarding age, sex, marital status, educational level, occupation, place of residence, socioeconomic status, physical activity, smoking, hookah use, alcohol consumption, drug abuse, and underlying diseases was obtained. Anthropometric measurements were performed by trained healthcare personnel using standardized protocols and calibrated equipment to minimize measurement error.

Assessment of Anthropometric Indices

Waist circumference, hip circumference, and wrist circumference were measured with a non‐elastic tape measure to the nearest 0.1 cm based on the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines. Waist circumference was measured at the level of the umbilicus, while hip circumference was measured at the widest portion of the buttocks. WHR was calculated by dividing waist circumference by hip circumference. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, WHR values ≤ 0.90 for men and ≤ 0.85 for women were considered normal [24, 25].

BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2). BMI categories were defined as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2) [26]. WHtR was also calculated as waist circumference divided by height. Participants were categorized into two groups according to anthropometric indices: individuals with normal anthropometric indices and those with abnormal anthropometric indices.

Assessment of Sleep Characteristics

Sleep‐related information was collected using the standardized sleep questionnaire incorporated into the PGCS baseline assessment. Participants were asked to report their usual sleep habits over the preceding year, including usual bedtime, average nightly sleep duration, wake‐up time, preferred wake‐up time, daytime sleeping habits, daytime napping, night shift work, leg movements during sleep, and regular use of sleeping medications.

Night sleep duration was categorized as < 7 h, 7–9 h, and > 9 h per night, consistent with commonly used categories in epidemiological studies evaluating habitual sleep duration. Bedtime was categorized as before 22:00, 22:00–24:00, and after 24:00, whereas wake‐up time was categorized as before 06:00, 06:00–09:00, and after 09:00.

Night shift work was defined as working at least 6 h between 9:00 PM and 6:00 AM during the previous year. Regular use of sleeping pills was defined as using sleep medications more than twice per week. Daytime sleeping was considered present if participants reported sleeping during the day at least three times per week. All sleep variables were self‐reported and therefore reflect habitual sleep behaviors rather than objective sleep measurements.

Statistical Analysis

Qualitative variables were summarized as frequencies (percentages), and quantitative variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences in baseline characteristics and sleep‐related variables between men and women were assessed using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and the independent‐samples t‐test for continuous variables. The crude associations between sleep characteristics and obesity indices were initially examined using chi‐square tests. Subsequently, binary logistic regression models were applied to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs and aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between sleep characteristics and BMI‐, WHR‐, and WHtR‐defined obesity. Separate logistic regression models were constructed for each obesity indicator, and analyses were performed separately among men and women to evaluate potential sex‐specific associations. Sleep characteristics were entered into regression models as categorical variables using the reference groups presented in the tables.

Covariates were selected a priori based on previous evidence regarding their associations with sleep behaviors and obesity and included age, marital status, educational level, employment status, place of residence, wealth index, physical activity, cigarette smoking, hookah use, alcohol consumption, and drug abuse. Multivariable models were adjusted for these potential confounders rather than using automated variable‐selection approaches. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 16 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL, USA). All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

The mean age of participants was 51.52 ± 8.90 years, and approximately one‐third were aged ≥ 55 years. Women constituted 53.5% of the study population (n = 5633). Most participants (n = 9527) were married (90.6%), resided in rural areas (n = 5907, 56.2%), and had an education level between 6 and 12 years (n = 4832, 45.9%). The mean physical activity score was 41.26 ± 8.88. Cigarette smoking was reported by 24.6% of participants (n = 2584), while hookah use, alcohol consumption, and drug abuse were reported by 14.4% (n = 1515), 13.3% (n = 1395), and 6.9% (n = 726) of participants, respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

Distribution of Sleep Characteristics and Anthropometric Indices

Nearly one‐half of participants (43.7%) reported sleeping less than the recommended 7 h per night, highlighting the high burden of short sleep duration in this population. Most participants went to bed between 22:00 and 24:00 (58.4%) and woke between 06:00 and 09:00 (52.2%). Daytime sleeping was common (62.2%), whereas 18.9% reported daytime napping. Night shift work and regular sleeping pill use were reported by 9.0% and 9.8% of participants, respectively (Table 1).

Women generally exhibited less favorable anthropometric profiles than men. Although one‐third of the cohort was obese according to BMI (32.7%), the prevalence was nearly threefold higher among women than men (47.0% vs. 16.1%). Even more striking, central obesity was observed in almost nine out of every 10 participants according to both WHR (88.3%) and WHtR (89.9%). The prevalence exceeded 97% among women but remained approximately 80% among men, underscoring the substantial burden of abdominal obesity in this population (Table 1).

Association Between Sleep Characteristics and Obesity Based on BMI

Overall, sleep duration showed the strongest association with obesity among men, whereas bedtime was the predominant sleep‐related factor among women. (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2). Among men, sleeping 7–9 h and > 9 h was associated with significantly lower odds of obesity compared with sleeping < 7 h. After adjustment for potential confounders, the odds of obesity decreased by 25% among men sleeping 7–9 h (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.64–0.88, p = 0.001) and by 46% among those sleeping > 9 h (OR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.38–0.78, p = 0.001). In contrast, daytime sleeping was independently associated with increased odds of obesity in men (aOR = 1.27, 95% CI: 1.07–1.51, p = 0.006). Men who woke between 06:00 and 09:00 or after 09:00 also had significantly lower odds of obesity compared with those waking before 06:00 (Table 2).

Among women, later bedtime showed a significant positive association with obesity. Compared with women going to bed before 22:00, those sleeping between 22:00 and 24:00 and after 24:00 had 26% and 44% higher odds of obesity, respectively, after adjustment for confounders. Additionally, waking between 06:00 and 09:00 was associated with increased odds of obesity among women (aOR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.03–1.29, p = 0.017) (Table 2).

Association Between Sleep Characteristics and Central Obesity Based on WHR and WHtR

Among men, longer sleep duration was consistently associated with lower odds of central obesity based on both WHR and WHtR (Supplementary Table 3 and Table 4). Men sleeping > 9 h had significantly reduced odds of WHR‐defined obesity (aOR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.46–0.80, p = 0.017) and WHtR‐defined obesity (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.44–0.76, p < 0.001) compared with those sleeping < 7 h. Waking later during the morning was also associated with lower odds of central obesity in men. Conversely, daytime sleeping and daytime napping were independently associated with higher odds of central obesity based on WHtR (Tables 3 and 4).

Among women, most sleep variables were not significantly associated with central obesity after adjustment. However, waking between 06:00 and 09:00 was associated with lower odds of both WHR‐defined obesity (aOR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.33–0.90, p = 0.018) and WHtR‐defined obesity (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.35–0.96, p = 0.034). No consistent associations were observed between sleep duration, daytime sleeping, or sleeping pill use and central obesity indices in women after multivariable adjustment (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that obesity and central obesity were highly prevalent in the study population, particularly among women. In men, longer sleep duration was consistently associated with lower odds of BMI‐, WHR‐, and WHtR‐defined obesity, whereas daytime sleeping and napping were associated with higher obesity risk. In women, later bedtime was significantly associated with higher odds of obesity, while some wake‐up time categories showed inverse associations with central obesity indices. These findings suggest that sleep behaviors may influence obesity through complex interactions between biological regulation, circadian physiology, hormonal status, and lifestyle factors rather than acting as isolated determinants of adiposity.

The high prevalence of obesity observed in this study, especially among women, is consistent with previous reports from Iran and other Middle Eastern countries, where obesity and abdominal obesity are generally more common among women than men [4, 27]. This difference may be attributed to a combination of sociocultural, behavioral, and biological factors. Reduced physical activity, dietary patterns, and changes in lifestyle during adulthood may contribute to higher obesity prevalence among women. Furthermore, sex‐specific hormonal mechanisms may influence body composition, as declining estrogen levels during the menopausal transition promote visceral fat accumulation, altered lipid metabolism, and increased cardiometabolic risk [28]. Differences in fat distribution between men and women may also modify the relationship between sleep behaviors and obesity‐related outcomes.

One of the main findings of this study was the inverse association between longer sleep duration and obesity among men. Previous studies have consistently reported that insufficient sleep is associated with increased obesity risk through multiple mechanisms, including increased ghrelin levels, reduced leptin secretion, impaired glucose metabolism, insulin resistance, inflammation, and altered cortisol regulation [8, 11, 29, 30, 31, 32]. The stronger association observed among men may reflect sex‐related differences in endocrine responses, circadian regulation, and metabolic vulnerability to sleep disruption. Testosterone regulation, visceral fat accumulation, and behavioral factors may contribute to this sex‐specific association, as sleep restriction has been shown to influence hormonal balance and energy metabolism differently between men and women [33, 34].

In contrast, no consistent association between sleep duration and obesity was observed among women after adjustment for confounding factors. This may be explained by the greater complexity of female sleep regulation, which is influenced by reproductive history, hormonal fluctuations, menopausal status, psychological factors, and social responsibilities. Recent evidence suggests that sleep quality in women is affected by a broad range of biological and reproductive determinants beyond obesity alone, emphasizing that female sleep patterns should be interpreted within a wider physiological context [35, 36].

Another important finding was the association between later bedtime and obesity among women. Previous studies have demonstrated that delayed sleep timing and circadian misalignment are associated with increased BMI, metabolic syndrome, and adverse metabolic profiles [37]. Late bedtime may contribute to obesity through disrupted circadian rhythms, altered melatonin secretion, reduced insulin sensitivity, impaired peripheral clock regulation, and increased late‐night food consumption [38, 39]. Additionally, individuals with later sleep timing may have behavioral patterns characterized by irregular meals, reduced physical activity, and greater exposure to sedentary activities, suggesting that bedtime may represent a marker of broader lifestyle behaviors rather than an independent risk factor [40, 41].

Daytime sleeping and napping were associated with increased obesity and central obesity among men. Previous studies have reported similar associations between prolonged daytime napping, metabolic syndrome, and obesity risk [42]. Although daytime sleep may contribute directly to circadian disruption and reduced energy expenditure, it may also represent an indicator of underlying health conditions, insufficient nighttime sleep, sedentary behavior, or metabolic dysfunction [14, 43]. Therefore, the observed association should be interpreted as part of a bidirectional relationship between sleep patterns and metabolic health.

The differential associations observed across obesity indicators may reflect their distinct biological characteristics. BMI represents overall adiposity, whereas WHR and WHtR better capture abdominal and visceral fat accumulation, which is more sensitive to hormonal changes, inflammation, cortisol dysregulation, and circadian disruption [1, 44]. Therefore, sleep characteristics may show stronger associations with central obesity measures than with BMI. Moreover, sleep behaviors are closely interconnected with other lifestyle factors, including dietary patterns, meal timing, physical activity, and psychological stress [45, 46]. Thus, the relationship between sleep and obesity is likely bidirectional and reflects the combined influence of multiple biological and behavioral pathways.

The strengths of this study include the large population‐based sample, assessment of multiple obesity indices, and evaluation of several sleep characteristics. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross‐sectional design prevents establishing temporal relationships or causal inference; therefore, it remains unclear whether abnormal sleep patterns contribute to obesity development or whether obesity leads to altered sleep behaviors. Second, sleep characteristics were self‐reported and may be affected by recall bias and misclassification. Third, residual confounding cannot be excluded, particularly regarding dietary habits, meal timing, physical activity intensity, psychological stress, medication use, and sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea, which may influence both sleep patterns and obesity risk. Future longitudinal studies incorporating objective sleep measurements, detailed dietary assessment, hormonal evaluations, and comprehensive behavioral data are warranted to clarify the causal pathways linking sleep characteristics with obesity and central adiposity.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated significant associations between sleep characteristics and obesity indices among adults, with notable sex‐specific differences. In men, longer sleep duration was associated with lower odds of general and central obesity, whereas daytime sleeping and napping were associated with higher obesity risk. In women, later bedtime was associated with increased odds of obesity. These findings highlight the importance of considering sleep behaviors in obesity prevention and management and support the integration of sleep assessment into clinical practice and public health strategies.
