The association of proton pump inhibitors and inflammatory bowel disease from the perspective of gut microbiota perturbation
Authors: Lizhi Hu, Chen Lai, Yong Li, Ruizheng Sun, Huixiang Yang, Xiaowei Liu, Yu Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is closely related to changes in the gut microbiota, with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) possibly playing a role. PPIs use is associated with altering gut microbiota and potentially influencing IBD onset and progression. This review explores the mechanisms by which PPIs affect gut microbiota and IBD, suggests strategies to mitigate dysbiosis, outlook on the causal relationship validation between PPIs use and IBD risk, emphasizing the broader impact of acid suppressants on gut health.Subject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic gastrointestinal disorder primarily comprising ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD)1. UC typically originates in the rectum and extends to the colon, presenting as diffuse, persistent, and nonspecific inflammation of the intestinal mucosa2. CD manifests as chronic granulomatous inflammation affecting any part of the gastrointestinal tract3. Although the exact etiology of IBD remains unclear, it is known that genetic predisposition, intestinal immune abnormality, and gut dysbiosis are directly associated with the disease4,5. The gut microbiota and its metabolites play a critical role in IBD, meanwhile, inflammation further disrupts gut microbial balance6. This bidirectional interaction underscores dysbiosis as both a hallmark and a driver of IBD progression.

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are acid-suppressive agents that irreversibly bind to the gastric mucosa’s H+/K+ ATPase proton pump. They are the first-line treatment for various gastrointestinal conditions, including peptic ulcers and gastroesophageal reflux disease, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to traditional acid-suppressive therapies7,8. PPIs have become one of the most widely prescribed long-term medications globally, however, an increasing number of adverse effects have been reported9,10. Numerous studies have confirmed PPIs’ extensive and significant impact on the gut microbiota from the oral cavity to the rectum11,12. Gut dysbiosis is a significant adverse effect of PPIs, potentially exceeding the microbiota-altering impact of antibiotics. While gastric acid suppression plays an important role, PPIs also affect the gut microbiota through multiple mechanisms.

Several studies have identified a potential association between PPIs and IBD, suggesting that PPIs may influence the onset, progression, and treatment efficacy of IBD13–15. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, many researchers speculate that dysbiosis induced by PPIs plays a critical role, given the similarities in gut microbiota changes observed in PPIs users and IBD patients. As research into the gut microbiota in patients with IBD and PPIs use advances, comprehensive reviews will be essential in determining whether PPIs affect the IBD through their impact on the gut microbiota. This review briefly outlines the epidemiological history of PPIs’ effects on IBD and the mechanisms by which PPIs influence gut microbiota. We compared changes in gut microbiota diversity and composition associated with PPIs users and IBD patients, linking these microbial and metabolic alterations to the role of PPIs in IBD. Furthermore, this review extends the discussion to the link between acid suppressants and dysbiosis-related intestinal complications, providing new insights into the broader impact and rational use of acid suppression therapy.

A brief epidemiological history of the effects of PPIs on IBD

In the 1990s, early reports suggested their utility in managing ulcerative complications associated with CD, despite limited understanding of the nature of these ulcers16. U. Heinzow et al. later reported a case of UC successfully treated with PPIs17. Over time, PPIs have been utilized in the treatment of IBD, particularly in patients with concomitant upper gastrointestinal ulcers18–21. In 2003, a small sample study indicated that PPIs might increase fecal calprotectin levels, a marker of intestinal inflammation closely associated with IBD severity22. Due to the lack of specific pharmacological mechanisms for PPIs in IBD treatment, their use has faced scrutiny. A large cohort study in 2012 involving 16,151 IBD patients found that acid-suppressive medications could influence the short-term course of IBD, with PPIs specifically associated with medication changes in UC14. Growing evidence suggests that PPIs use is associated with poorer clinical outcomes in IBD, including medication changes, unplanned hospitalizations, and surgeries, as well as increased infection rates23–25. Further research has also reported a correlation between PPIs use and elevated fecal calprotectin levels26. With the growing use of biologics in IBD treatment, several studies have highlighted the negative impact of PPIs on patients receiving infliximab or vedolizumab15,27,28.

In addition, research has produced mixed results on the link between PPIs use and the risk of developing IBD. A study investigating acid-suppressing medications and pediatric IBD risk first reported an association between PPIs use and an increased risk of IBD in children29. However, limitations in study design and sample size necessitate further validation29. Later, a multicohort analysis identified a correlation between regular PPIs use and an elevated risk of IBD (Hazard ratio:1.42, 95% confidence interval: 1.22–1.65), marking the first cohort study on adult PPIs use and IBD risk13. However, during the cohort’s follow-up period, the vast majority of new IBD diagnoses (82.2%) occurred in non-PPI users, compared to 17.8% in the regular PPIs user group. This evidence suggests that while PPIs use is a risk factor, it is more likely to increase the incidence of IBD in susceptible individuals rather than act as a decisive or primary cause. This finding is increasingly supported by related studies30. Conversely, another study found that when a 2-year lag period was implemented during exposure assessment to mitigate protopathic bias, PPIs use was not associated with an increased risk of IBD31. The differences in the above findings may be due to the heterogeneity of the population and the differences in statistical methods. As a result, establishing a definitive link between PPIs use and IBD incidence based on current epidemiological evidence remains challenging. Comparison of changes in gut microbiota between PPIs users and patients with IBD

Changes in gut microbiota diversity

Alpha and beta diversity are important indicators of the richness and compositional differences within gut microbiota. Numerous studies have indicated that PPIs usage and IBD are significant factors contributing to changes in beta diversity32–34. Regarding alpha diversity, both PPIs users and IBD patients exhibit a significant reduction in gut microbiota alpha diversity32–34. A decrease in alpha diversity is generally considered detrimental, potentially increasing the risk of infections by other pathogenic microorganisms35. Furthermore, reduced alpha diversity has been identified as a critical predictor of disease progression and poor prognosis in IBD36,37.

Changes at the taxonomic level

The specific characteristics of gut microbiota changes in IBD patients and PPIs users have been extensively summarized38,39. This review focuses on the shared and contrasting microbiota alterations in PPIs users and IBD patients (Table 1 and Fig. 3). Inconsistencies in the reported changes of certain taxa across studies may stem from variations in sample sources, population heterogeneity, and differences in detection and analytical methods.

A consistent decline in the phylum Actinobacteria has been observed in PPIs users and IBD patients12,40–42. Within this phylum, changes in the family Actinomycetaceae are contrasting, however, an increase in Actinomyces has been consistently reported in PPIs users and UC patients12,43,44. Enrichment of Actinomyces may further aggravate intestinal inflammation and immune disorders45. The decline in Bifidobacteriaceae appears to be a major contributor to the overall reduction in the phylum Actinobacteria. This decrease is more pronounced in CD patients and PPIs users12,40,42,46. Bifidobacterium, a genus recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties, includes species such as B. animalis and B. longum, which have been shown to mitigate IBD inflammation47,48. The changes in Bifidobacterium in IBD patients are not consistent40,46,49–54. B. dentium is the most abundant species in both active and inactive UC patients, as well as in active CD patients, which is typically not the dominant Bifidobacterium species in healthy individuals55. A reduction in Bifidobacterium has been observed in PPIs users12,56. However, in a study exploring the effects of various medications on the gut microbiota, the enrichment of Bifidobacterium dentium was a change specific to PPI users57.

The changes in Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria phyla are less prominent. The Bacteroidetes phylum shows a significant decrease in IBD patients, but the results in PPIs users are inconsistent32,58–60. Notably, an increase in Bacteroidales and Bacteroides has been observed in both groups12,32,40,61. Within Proteobacteria, the class Gammaproteobacteria and family Enterobacteriaceae consistently increase in both IBD patients and PPIs users, with a notable rise in Escherichia-Shigella in both groups12,40. The increase in Escherichia-Shigella is considered a hallmark of colitis development, contributing to gut microbiota dysbiosis and correlating positively with IL-6 levels62. Furthermore, changes in the abundance of Escherichia and Bacteroides are directly linked to the increased prevalence of antibiotic-resistance genes in IBD63.

The phylum Firmicutes, a dominant component of the gut microbiota, shows a consistent decline in IBD patients40,41,58,64. While most studies report a reduction in Firmicutes in PPIs users, some have detected an opposite trend32,59. Both IBD patients and PPIs users demonstrate a consistent increase in the family Enterococcaceae and a decrease in Ruminococcaceae12,32,40,49,65. Within these families, the enrichment of Enterococcus and the depletion of Faecalibacterium are considered detrimental to IBD progression, marking them as key taxa in disease development63,66,67. In contrast, the family Lactobacillaceae decreases in CD patients but increases in PPIs users12,32,40. The genus Lactobacillus is elevated in PPIs users but exhibits variable trends in IBD patients12,32,40. Known as effective probiotics for treating IBD, Lactobacillus species positively correlate with butyrate levels in the gut68,69. The family Clostridiaceae and the genus Clostridium consistently decrease in both groups, while Clostridium perfringens, a key pathogen in intestinal infections, is enriched in both IBD patients and PPIs users12,40,44,50. The family Lachnospiraceae is closely associated with adverse reactions to anti-tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) therapy and postoperative recurrence in CD patients63. Its abundance decreases in UC patients but increases in CD patients, with mixed findings in PPIs users. Blautia, a genus within Lachnospiraceae, exhibits opposite trends, increasing in UC patients and PPIs users while decreasing in CD patients40,51,53,59. Blautia is recognized as a potential probiotic70. One of the most prominent microbiota changes in PPIs users is the increased abundance of oral bacteria in the gut, including Streptococcus and Veillonella11,32,44,59,61,71–73. Similar shifts are observed in IBD patients43,51,58,74. The genus Veillonella is considered pro-inflammatory, and its levels correlate with fecal calprotectin and IBD activity fluctuations75.

Floris Imhann et al. noted consistent changes in gut microbiota due to PPIs use and Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), including a decrease in the abundance of Ruminococcaceae and Bifidobacterium, while Enterococcus, Veillonella and Lactobacillaceae showed increased abundance12. Similar changes in microbiota have also been observed in IBD patients.

Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio may be a potential marker of health76. Numerous studies have reported that in PPIs users, the F/B increased59,77. This change is consistent with the high-fat diet model, CDI, and IBD patients59,76–78.

The gut microbiota changes observed in IBD patients using PPIs are similar to those in other PPIs users but with fewer altered microbial taxa. This overlap may result from the pre-existing dysbiosis caused by IBD, highlighting the shared patterns of gut microbiota changes between PPIs users and IBD patients.

Mechanisms of PPIs affecting gut microbiota

Direct antimicrobial effects of PPIs

PPIs have broad antimicrobial activity against symbiotic bacteria, mainly exerting a bacteriostatic effect rather than a bactericidal effect79. A study found that bacterial taxa whose abundance was decreased in PPIs users exhibited slower growth rates when cultured in vitro with physiologically relevant concentrations of PPIs, whereas taxa enriched in PPIs users were minimally affected79. This suggests that PPIs can exert antimicrobial activity by selectively limiting the growth rate of certain commensal gut bacteria. These suppressed bacterial communities, including Clostridiales, Lachnospiraceae, and Ruminococcaceae, are consistently observed to be reduced in PPIs users. All three PPIs examined in the study exhibited direct antimicrobial activity, and the chemical similarity among PPIs was found to correlate with their inhibitory effects on specific commensal bacteria79. This indicates that the shared chemical structure of PPIs may underlie their capacity to selectively suppress the growth of certain gut microbial taxa. Furthermore, the activity of other microbial species, such as Helicobacter pylori and Streptococcus pneumoniae, may also be directly inhibited by PPIs, due to their similarity to human gastric H⁺, K⁺-ATPase80. PPIs may directly target and inhibit the enzyme, causing pH and ion metabolism disorders within the bacteria to exert antibacterial effects80.

Inhibit the direct and indirect effects of gastric acid

PPIs-induced alterations in the gastrointestinal acidic environment impair the dissolution of essential electrolytes and minerals, such as calcium, magnesium, and iron, from dietary sources into more bioavailable ionic forms, reducing their absorption81,82. Electrolyte and mineral absorption is closely interconnected with gut microbiota, they can also utilize these substances as nutrients and for metabolism. Consequently, fluctuations in the concentrations of minerals and electrolytes within the intestinal lumen can profoundly affect microbial homeostasis. For example, oral magnesium can enrich Bifidobacterium in the gut83. The study suggests that this may be explained by magnesium ions regulating Bifidobacterium metabolism through modulation of formate C-acetyltransferase (pflB), thereby increasing the production of indole-3-carboxylic acid (ICA), which in turn promotes the activity and enrichment of Bifidobacterium83. Similarly, iron also plays a key role in the microbial ecosystem and is an essential growth factor for many bacterial species. Therefore, iron deficiency can restrict the growth of certain pathogens such as Salmonella and Escherichia coli84. In contrast, since most members of Lactobacillus do not require iron for growth, they can proliferate vigorously even under iron-limited conditions, thereby becoming a dominant community85.

Hormones, particularly gastrointestinal hormones, are important regulators of gut function and host metabolism, and they likewise influence the gut microbiota86. In terms of hormonal changes, decreased gastric acid secretion disrupts the acidic environment of the stomach and proximal duodenum, potentially leading to hypergastrinemia and reduced secretion of somatostatin. Chronic underabsorption of calcium and magnesium and hypergastrinemia can lead to increased secretion of parathyroid hormone87. Hormonal changes also affect the composition of the gut microbiota. Gastrin can enhance gastrointestinal motility, accelerate the transit and mix of intestinal contents. This process increases the contact between dietary fiber and gut microbiota, thereby promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which in turn can further modulate the composition and function of the gut microbiota86. On the other hand, gastrin stimulates the secretion of gastric acid and pepsin, facilitating protein digestion and absorption. Many bacteria rely on metabolizing dietary proteins in the intestine to obtain nutrients for growth and metabolism, and alterations in protein digestion and absorption can affect microbial community structure88. It should be noted that the elevated gastrin levels observed in PPIs users are secondary to the drug-induced inhibition of gastric acid through a feedback mechanism. This increase in gastrin may only partially compensate for the effects of PPIs on gastrointestinal functions, such as motility, as upper gastrointestinal dysmotility is still commonly observed in PPIs users89. The impact of secondary hypergastrinemia on the gut microbiota should be distinguished from primary hormonal changes. In the latter, hormones serve as the main drivers altering gut function and consequently affecting the microbiota; in the former, the pattern of microbiota changes is primarily determined by acid suppression, with compensatory gastrin elevation potentially serving merely to partially counteract PPIs-induced dysbiosis.

The potent acid-suppressing effects of PPIs facilitate the exchange of gut microbiota across the upper and lower gastrointestinal tract. Multiple studies utilizing sequencing techniques on the gut microbiota of PPIs users have demonstrated an increase in oral-derived microbial populations, including Streptococcus and Veillonella11,90. This phenomenon is attributed to the disruption of the gastric acid barrier by PPIs, allowing oral microbes to colonize the gastrointestinal tract through digestive pathways91. Many bacteria from the Bacillus genus in the Streptococcaceae colonize the gut and ferment lactic acid, leading to an increase in the number of Veillonellaceae, which utilize lactic acid92. Moreover, PPIs demonstrate a unique ability to promote the proliferation of oral bacteria in the gut compared to histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs)11. This may be due to the strong acid-suppressing effect of PPIs. By raising the gastric pH, PPIs create a less acidic environment that is more favorable for the survival and growth of acid-intolerant bacteria. For instance, species like Streptococcus anginosus, which cannot survive in vitro at the acidic pH levels below 5, can thrive in the stomach of PPIs users90. Additionally, the inhibition of some intestinal native symbiotic bacteria by PPIs provides an ecological niche for the colonization of oral microbiota.

Suppression of gastric acid secretion by PPIs can also lead to small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO). SIBO is defined by an abnormal increase in small intestinal bacterial counts, often accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms93. Accumulating evidence indicates that PPIs use is associated with an increased risk of SIBO94–96. A meta-analysis of 19 studies including 7,055 participants, reported a significant association between PPIs use and SIBO risk97. Some studies found no such link, likely due to population heterogeneity and differences in diagnostic methods98,99. Mechanistically, PPIs may promote SIBO by reducing gastric acid-mediated bacterial clearance and by impairing gastric emptying and small intestinal motility, as suggested by findings that concomitant prokinetic therapy reduces SIBO incidence in PPI users72,100. Collectively, these data support PPIs use as an important risk factor for SIBO.

Alteration of intestinal pH

Changes in intestinal pH also directly modulate the composition and distribution of gut microbiota. The effects of PPIs on intestinal pH remain controversial. A study in rats has shown that omeprazole administration markedly increases pH levels in the stomach and small intestine101. Another study in pancreatitis patients also indicated that PPIs use increases small intestinal pH102, likely due to reduced acidity of chyme entering the intestine. Current evidence suggests that PPIs predominantly raise proximal rather than distal small intestinal pH. For instance, a 1-week trial in healthy subjects showed no significant difference in distal duodenal pH between the PPIs and placebo groups103. Whereas another study reported that PPIs reduced distal small intestinal pH in healthy volunteers104. This may explain the enrichment of Lactobacillus, which favors acidic conditions, in the intestine of PPI users12. Variability in small intestinal PH, influenced by factors such as food intake, may underlie these discrepancies. Overall, evidence supports that PPIs increase proximal small intestinal PH, thereby promoting bacterial overgrowth, consistent with the higher prevalence of SIBO among PPIs users97. The mechanisms regulating distal small intestinal pH, however, remain unclear.

Changes in colonic pH can account for 3%–67% of the variance in gut microbiota composition. Low PH ( < 5.5) enriches acid-tolerant bacteria such as Bifidobacterium, whereas neutral PH (6.5–7.5) favors Bacteroides and Enterobacteriaceae105. A study in mice reported that PPIs administration significantly increased colonic PH106. The microbial changes observed in PPIs users partly overlap with those associated with elevated colonic PH. However, similar to the small intestine, colonic pH is influenced by multiple factors, and whether colonic pH elevation is a consistent feature in PPIs users remains uncertain.

Interaction of gut microbiota

The impact of PPIs on the gut microbiota may be associated with the duration of treatment. Short-term use (e.g., 1 week) is mainly characterized by acid suppression–induced colonization of oral bacteria, while alterations in the indigenous gut microbiota remain limited11,90. In contrast, long-term administration (commonly defined as >28 days) results in more pronounced and complex changes in the resident microbiot42. These findings suggest that with prolonged therapy, secondary effects driven by interbacterial interactions become increasingly important.

The enrichment of oral microbiota and Lactobacillaceae, along with a reduction in the abundance of native butyrate-producing bacteria (such as Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae), is a notable characteristic of microbiome changes in PPI users. Enrichment of oral microbiota has been reported even in short-term PPIs users, whereas most other alterations in the gut microbiota have been documented in cohort studies of long-term PPIs use11,32. The change has also been observed in various diseases, such as CDI, cerebral infarction, liver cirrhosis, and in some patients with IBD51,92,107,108. The reduction in the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria may be due not only to the direct inhibitory effect of PPIs on native butyrate-producing bacteria but also to the proliferation of oral microbiota in the gut. The excessive colonization of oral bacteria leads to the overaccumulation of lactic acid which lowers the pH92. The interaction between the enrichment of Lactobacillaceae and the reduction in the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria remains controversial. Lactobacillaceae species can metabolize hexose through either homolactic or heterolactic fermentation. PPIs may promote the enrichment of Lactobacillus and the homolactic fermentation pathway. Homolactic fermenting species may produce more lactic acid, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of butyrate-producing bacteria109. Another possible mechanism is that the reduced abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria could shift the colonic anaerobic environment to aerobic, promoting the growth of facultative anaerobes, such as Lactobacillus, while inhibiting strict anaerobes. Additionally, lactic acid could serve as a substrate for butyrate fermentation by butyrate-producing bacteria110. Lactobacillaceae may increase lactate to offset the reduction in butyrate-producing bacteria108. Overall, this dysbiosis appears to be mutually causal.

A reduction in the abundance of Bifidobacterium and an enrichment of intestinal pathogens represented by Enterobacteriaceae are other key features observed in PPIs users. The alterations to the native gut microbiota (such as Bifidobacterium) are more commonly observed in patients on long-term PPIs therapy42,65. However, a study reported similar changes in patients with acute coronary syndrome after only 1 week of PPIs use111. This is potentially because the patients’ underlying disease had already compromised their gut microbiota homeostasis, thereby amplifying the disruptive effects of the PPIs111. Therefore, the effect of PPIs on the gut microbiota is multifactorial, and the duration of use may be just one of these dimensions. PPIs have not demonstrated any inhibitory effect on the growth of Bifidobacteriaceae and Bifidobacterium in an in vitro study79. The reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium may be associated with changes in the colonic environment. Bifidobacterium is an important anaerobic genus, and PPIs may alter the intestinal anaerobic environment and pH. In addition, hypomagnesemia is commonly observed in PPI users, and the deficiency of magnesium ions in the body may be one of the key factors inhibiting the growth of Bifidobacterium39,83. The enrichment of Bifidobacterium dentium in PPIs users may be attributed to several factors. Bifidobacterium dentium is a common member of both the oral and gut microbiota and its enrichment may be related to oral migration and colonization in the gut. Additionally, its unique acid tolerance and metabolic flexibility in utilizing different substrates may enable it to resist the intestinal environmental changes induced by PPIs112. Pathogen enrichment may occur early during PPIs use. One study found that administration of the PPIs (pantoprazole) for just 2 days significantly increased pathogen load in feces113. This may be due to disrupting the gastric acid barrier facilitates the entry of external pathogens into the gut, reaching an infective dose. With prolonged PPIs use and disruption of the gut commensal microbiota, pathogens may further accumulate in PPIs users. Multiple pathogens belong to facultative anaerobes, and the reduced abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria disrupts the intestinal anaerobic environment, thereby further promoting their competitive advantage114. The reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium and decreased butyrate levels diminish its resistance to intestinal colonization114,115. Another recent study found that PPIs promote the transfer of carbapenem-resistant genes carrying mobile genetic elements between bacteria, thereby facilitating the colonization of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae116. This enhances the ability of resistant bacteria to colonize the gut.

However, some questions remain about the mechanisms by which PPIs affect the gut microbiota. There is debate over whether PPIs increase colonic pH. The lack of expected pH changes in some studies may be due to the absence of significant acid-suppressive effects on the colon or a balance between PPIs effects and lactic acid production. Understanding these effects could clarify their impact on the gut microbiota and intestinal diseases. Additionally, it is unclear whether the colonic anaerobic environment is disrupted in PPIs users. Regarding the gut microbiota, although the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria is reduced in PPIs users, there is no significant change in the levels of butyrate and lactate in their feces117. Moreover, the abundance of Lactobacillus increases in PPIs users, but it is unclear whether this elevation under certain pathological conditions is a cause or consequence of the disease. To determine if Lactobacillus-containing probiotics benefit PPIs users, the reason for their enrichment in these individuals must first be understood. Impacts of PPIs-mediated microbiota changes on IBD

Gut microbiota

Actinobacteria phylum: Bifidobacteriaceae, Atopobiaceae and Eggerthella lenta

The species B. animalis can inhibit the TLR4/MYD88/NF-ĸB signaling pathway, which in turn suppresses the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6, while promoting the production of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10118. B.longum effectively regulates the Th1/Th2 immune cell balance and inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines119. Additionally, Bifidobacterium secretes various antimicrobial substances (such as bacteriocins) to defend against pathogen colonization115. Bifidobacterium dentium is abnormally enriched in IBD patients and has become a dominant species within the Bifidobacterium genus. Bifidobacterium dentium exhibits anti-inflammatory properties similar to other Bifidobacterium species and can alleviate inflammation and inflammatory cytokine levels in colitis mice120,121. Unlike other Bifidobacterium species, Bifidobacterium dentium does not excessively consume mucin glycans; instead, its secretions can upregulate the major mucin MUC2 and maintain goblet cell function122.

Atopobium is considered a key microbiota in the pathogenesis of CD. Its abundance is positively correlated with the severity of CD and negatively correlated with mitochondrial protein expression and the abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria123. A. parvulum can induce colitis in IL10−/− mice, whose pathogenicity may be attributed to the excessive production of H2S123. Eggerthella lenta induces Th17 cells activation by relieving inhibition of the Th17 transcription factor Rorγt, thus promoting IBD124. The enrichment of Olsenella may be beneficial, as most Olsenella species are lactic acid and acetate producers125 (Fig. 3).

Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria phylum: Bacteroidaceae and Enterobacteriaceae

Bacteroides may play a key role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. Bacteroides uniformis can improve gut microbiota dysbiosis in dextran sulfate sodium(DSS)-induced colitis mice and reduce the abundance of pathogenic Escherichia-Shigella126. The metabolic products of Bacteroides, sphingolipids, are decreased in IBD patients and negatively correlated with inflammation127. Bacteroides uniformis can also inhibit Th17 cell differentiation by increasing the levels of secondary bile acids (SBA) in DSS-induced colitis mice126. Moreover, some Bacteroides species can effectively protect epithelial integrity and promote the recovery of damaged IECs. For instance, Bacteroides fragilis strain ZY-312 can upregulate the STAT3 pathway to reduce radiation-induced damage in IECs and enhance the regeneration of goblets and stem cells128. A reduced abundance of Bacteroides in IBD patients is negatively correlated with disease activity and may serve as a marker for IBD relapse129,130. However, enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF), as a molecular subclass of the colonic symbiotic bacterium, can directly promote colitis in mice and enhance intestinal inflammation induced by DSS in mice131. Although the use of PPIs can enrich the Bacteroides genus, no studies have specifically examined the changes in ETBF in PPIs users.

In the aspect of Proteobacteria, increased Proteobacteria can cross the mucosal barrier, activating dendritic cell toll-like receptors to induce IL-23 and trigger intestinal inflammation132–134. Enterobacteriaceae are potential fecal biomarkers for IBD, enriching several opportunistic pathogens that contribute to the onset and progression of IBD41. For example, can be broadly classified into commensal and pathogenic strains. In PPIs users, the abundance of E. coli is increased, and this increase may predominantly involve pathogenic strains. A study has shown that PPIs use enhances susceptibility to infection with diarrhoeagenic E. coli135. Pathogenic E. coli plays a significant role in IBD; for example, adherent-invasive E. coli can survive and replicate within macrophages, inducing TNF-α secretion and mucus depletion, which is closely associated with CD136. However, colonization of IL-10-deficient mice with non-pathogenic E. coli has also been shown to induce colitis and stimulate inflammatory cytokine production137. These findings suggest that, in an IBD-susceptible background, nonpathogenic E. coli may also promote both the onset and progression of IBD. Oxidative stress in the intestines of IBD patients further promotes the enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae. Oxidative stress is a significant feature of IBD, characterized by high levels of reactive oxygen, leading to increased inflammation and disruption of gut microecology138. Changes in host oxidative stress promote the ability of Salmonella and enterohemorrhagic Escherichia coli to use ethanolamine as a carbon and nitrogen source, while other commensal microbiota are unable to utilize it and thus lose their competitive advantage139,140 (Fig. 3).

Firmicutes phylum: Lactobacillales, Butyrate-producing bacteria and others

In Lactobacillales, the metabolite tyramine produced by Enterococcus directly activates α2A-adrenergic receptors on intestinal stem cells, inhibiting their proliferation and intestinal epithelial repair, thereby exacerbating the progression of colitis66. Lactobacillus is commonly considered a class of probiotics. For example, Lactobacillus rhamnosus abundance increases after PPIs use, and its metabolite histamine elevates cAMP levels via histamine receptor activation, inhibiting MEK/ERK-MAPK signaling and reducing TNF-α production117,141. Lactobacillus salivarius suppresses p38 MAPK and NF-ĸB phosphorylation in ETEC K88-induced IPEC-J2 cells, lowering inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β142. Moreover, several Lactobacillus species have been found to maintain the intestinal mechanical barrier and inhibit oxidative stress in the gut143,144.

The abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria is reduced in IBD patients, primarily including members of the Clostridiaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Eubacteriaceae families. In Clostridiaceae, Clostridium perfringens is one of the few species enriched in patients with IBD and PPIs users. Clostridium perfringens is recognized as a common pathogenic bacterium causing intestinal infections in IBD patients. It exhibits toxicity to various cell types beneath the intestinal epithelial barrier, including endothelial cells, neuroblasts, and neutrophils, potentially promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines145. Moreover, Clostridium perfringens can activate neuroblasts and dorsal root ganglion neurons, contributing to abdominal pain, a primary clinical manifestation in IBD patients145.

In Lachnospiraceae, Blautia is considered a potential probiotic. It can inhibit the inflammatory progression of IBD by upregulating Treg cells and producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)146. Additionally, several Blautia species secrete bacteriocins to resist pathogen colonization70. For example, B. producta has been identified as a producer of lanthipeptides specifically inhibiting the growth of vancomycin-resistant enterococci, thereby preventing infections caused by antibiotic-resistant pathogens147. However, Blautia is also involved in the transformation and generation of SBAs70. Excessive enrichment of Blautia and subsequent SBA accumulation may be harmful to the human body. The opposing changes in Blautia abundance observed in patients with UC and CD reflect the underlying heterogeneity40,51,53. The reduced abundance of the genus Dorea in IBD patients may be related to an increased IgG immune response targeting this bacterial group148. A study comparing gut microbiota differences between remission and active phases of CD patients found that Dorea abundance was consistently reduced in both phases, independent of intestinal inflammation, which may contribute to CD progression by decreasing SCFAs production149.

In Ruminococcaceae, changes in the Ruminococcus genus in IBD patients may be complex. However, most studies have found that Ruminococcus torques and Ruminococcus gnavus are enriched in IBD patients, even those with a reduced overall abundance of Ruminococcuss40. Ruminococcus (particularly Ruminococcus torques and Ruminococcus gnavus) is an important group of mucus-degrading bacteria, which will dissolve more mucus and increase the exposure of epithelial cells to play a pro-inflammatory effect150. Ruminococcus gnavus is a key microbiota in CD, capable of synthesizing and secreting glucorhamnan to induce dendritic cells to produce TNF-α151. Additionally, its proliferation is promoted by oxidative stress caused by intestinal inflammation in IBD patients, which may be linked to its facultative anaerobic nature152. Several studies have reported a reduction in the abundance of Ruminococcus and Ruminococcus gnavus in PPI users, suggesting potential benefits of PPIs in IBD management56. However, Ruminococcus gnavus has been found to produce an antimicrobial peptide, Ruminococcin C1, which can protect against pathogenic infections such as Clostridioides difficile153. In addition, the Ruminococcus genus is an important producer of SCFAs, and its reduced abundance may be associated with decreased SCFAs production and intestinal inflammation154. Faecalibacterium represents an essential genus of bacteria that plays a key role in regulating immune homeostasis and preserving the integrity of the intestinal mechanical barrier. Faecalibacterium is positively correlated with the level of CCR6+CSCR6+DP8α regulatory T (Treg) cells in circulation, and the decrease of these Treg cells aggravates the inflammatory response of IBD155. Faecalibacterium also stimulates dendritic cells to secrete the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 while inhibiting IL-12 and interferon-gamma156. In maintaining the intestinal mechanical barrier, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii therapy reduces radiation-induced damage to colon epithelial tissue157 (Fig. 3).

Ectopic colonization of oral microbiota

The crosstalk between oral and gut microbiota is a significant factor in PPIs-induced gut microbiota alterations90. Ectopic colonization of oral bacteria in the gut is closely associated with the development and progression of IBD, with multiple oral microbiota species also enriched in the gut of IBD patients158.

A study comparing the effects of PPIs and H2RAs on oral and gut microbiota in healthy individuals revealed that PPIs significantly promote the transfer of oral bacteria to the gut and directly enhance their growth11. Considering that many factors promote the transfer of oral bacteria, the inclusion criteria for this study explicitly excluded individuals with recent use of antibiotics or probiotics, those with severe gastrointestinal or oral diseases (e.g., periodontitis), and smokers. This study also found that there was no significant difference in baseline dietary habits between the two groups. Another prospective study also controlled for these confounding factors to support the use of PPIs in promoting intestinal colonization of the oral bacteria90. Among these, Streptococcus is the most prominent. Notably, Streptococcus anginosus shows the most significant increase under PPIs influence11,90. S. anginosus is known to accumulate in the stomach, contributing to gastritis, atrophy, and gastric tumorigenesis, and can disseminate to other organs, leading to pyogenic infections159,160. Although the role of S. anginosus in IBD is unclear, it has been found enriched in the subgingival and gut microbiota of CD patients with untreated periodontitis but not in UC patients11,161. Other Streptococcus species, including Streptococcus parasanguinis, Streptococcus salivarius, and Streptococcus mitis, are also enriched in both IBD patients and PPIs users, suggesting their potential as opportunistic pathogens in IBD11,161,162.

Beyond Streptococcus, other oral microbiota influenced by PPIs also play roles in the progression of IBD. Fusobacterium nucleatum is a key bacterium implicated in IBD163. After translocating from the oral cavity to the gut, F. nucleatum can exacerbate inflammation and promote immune evasion, contributing to colorectal cancer development164. In IBD patients, F. nucleatum is commonly enriched in the gut mucosa, displaying higher invasive potential in inflamed tissues165. In murine models of DSS-induced colitis, F. nucleatum worsened colitis by increasing gut permeability and promoting immune cell differentiation into Th1 and Th17 subsets166. F. nucleatum is frequently enriched in the gut of PPIs users11. Campylobacter concisus and Klebsiella pneumoniae, associated with periodontal disease, also contribute to IBD progression163,167. Their impact on IBD is primarily mediated by disruption of intestinal epithelial tight junctions and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines163,167. Specifically, ectopic colonization of K. pneumoniae in the gut induces a surge in Th1 cells, amplifying inflammation in IBD158. PPIs are known risk factors for Campylobacter infections and promote the colonization and overgrowth of K. pneumoniae in the gut168–170. Veillonella is an important oral bacterium. This genus has pro-inflammatory effects and, when combined with Streptococcus, enhances the production of cytokines such as IL-8, IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α171.

Intestinal pathogen infection

The use of PPIs increases the risk of infections from various pathogens, such as Clostridioides difficile, Salmonella, and Escherichia-Shigella12,25,116,172. Colonization of pathogens will further mediate intestinal immune disorders. For example, the expression of IL-1β and TNF-α, important cytokines for the development of IBD, increased during Salmonella infection173. Pathogen infection may be one of the potential initiating factors of IBD. Several population-based studies have investigated the association between pathogenic infections and incident IBD. A nationwide case–control study in Sweden reported that prior gastrointestinal infections, particularly bacterial infections, increased the risk of subsequent IBD development174. Other studies have summarized that enteric infections can trigger dysbiosis, induce immunological scars, alter immune responses, and directly damage the intestinal barrier175. These mechanisms are all likely to contribute to IBD onset. However, due to inherent study design limitations, a causal relationship between enteric infections and incident IBD remains unclear. Moreover, dysbiosis in IBD patients increases susceptibility to enteric pathogens, and secondary infections may further accelerate disease progression176. Alterations in the gut microbiota have a direct impact on the colonization and reproduction of opportunistic pathogens, with CDI being one of the most important and common features25. CDI is one of the important and common pathogenic infections in patients with IBD25,174. The potential mechanisms by which CDI may promote the onset and progression of IBD are diverse177,178. The gut microbiota alterations caused by CDI overlap with those seen in IBD patients, characterized by an increase in pro-inflammatory bacteria and a decrease in beneficial bacteria38. Furthermore, an interventional study in rats found that Clostridioides difficile colonization leads to the production of toxins A and B, which bind to receptors on intestinal epithelial cells, promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β) and leukotrienes, thereby potentially facilitating the development of IBD179. Thus, the increased risk of CDI associated with PPIs use may be linked to the onset and progression of IBD.

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth

Similar to PPIs users, SIBO is frequently observed in patients with IBD, particularly CD, consistent with the greater involvement of the small intestine in CD versus UC180,181. The relationship between SIBO and IBD is likely bidirectional. Impaired gastrointestinal motility in IBD may increase SIBO risk, while SIBO itself may serve as a marker of disease activity and influence disease severity182. For example, SIBO has been associated with relapse in clinically quiescent CD, and its detection and treatment may alleviate gastrointestinal symptoms183,184. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, SIBO may exacerbate intestinal inflammation through increased bacterial load and microbial metabolites. Excessive bacterial growth is associated with an increase in serum endotoxins and bacterial components, which can promote the production of inflammatory factors185. Moreover, excessive bacterial growth also leads to the accumulation of its metabolic product, lithocholic acid, in the intestines185. This metabolic product has been found to exacerbate DSS-induced intestinal inflammation in mice and damage the intestinal mucosal barrier186. The histopathological evidence showing intraepithelial lymphocyte infiltration and mucosal immune activation in the SIBO patient’s small intestine187. Although current evidence is insufficient to establish a causal role for SIBO in IBD onset, it may contribute to disease progression. In summary, PPIs use in IBD patients may increase the likelihood of SIBO, resulting in more pronounced gastrointestinal symptoms and potentially higher disease activity.

Alterations in gut microbiota metabolites in PPIs users and their impacts on IBD

Bile acid

Dysregulation of bile acid (BA) metabolism is a key feature of IBD, characterized by increased fecal total bile acids, elevated primary bile acids (PBA), and decreased SBA188. A study suggests that PPIs may promote liver cancer by increasing BA levels189. Another study found that omeprazole could increase the deconjugation of PBA by affecting gut microbiota190. This may be due to PPIs breaking down the gastric acid barrier and increasing the diversity of bacteria in the stomach and small intestine, such as Enterococcus191,192. This bacterial overgrowth can promote the deconjugation of PBA and conversion of PBA into SBA in the gut193. Other microbiota involved in converting PBA to SBA were increased in PPIs users, such as Enterococcus, Eggerthella lenta, Escherichia coli, and Blautia producta188. Therefore, PPIs may further promote the deconjugation and conversion of PBA to SBA, raising SBA levels. SBA might inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α) and NF-κB activation by activating the TGR5 receptor194. A study has shown that SBA deficiency due to gut dysbiosis promotes the development of UC195. Thus, PPIs might reduce intestinal inflammation by increasing SBA levels. However, elevated SBA could promote colorectal cancer and may also indicate malignant progression of UC196. This may explain the increased risk of colorectal cancer in long-term PPIs users197.

Short-chain fatty acids

SCFAs are a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of IBD, characterized by a reduction in butyrate-producing gut microbiota and decreased butyrate production198. Butyrate can alleviate inflammatory responses in IBD through multiple pathways199,200. Research on the effects of PPIs on SCFAs remains controversial. One study found a significant reduction in gut butyrate levels in aged rats after 50 weeks of PPIs treatment69. In another study, 20 patients on PPIs showed a significant increase in fecal butyrate after 4 weeks, but levels normalized after 8 weeks, with no significant changes in other SCFAs117. The effects of PPIs on butyrate may depend on the user’s age and duration of use. Some studies suggest that PPIs do not have a significant impact on SCFAs201. Variability in findings may relate to the timing of PPIs use and sample heterogeneity. PPIs can influence the abundance of various SCFA-producing microbiota, with reductions observed in members of Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, Faecalibacterium, and Bifidobacteriaceae12,42,202. For example, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which decreases in PPIs users, produces butyrate that promotes anti-inflammatory effects via the IL-6/STAT3/IL-17 pathway203. Moreover, one study found that PPIs treatment in rats reduced the abundance of Lactobacillus, which correlated positively with gut butyrate levels69. However, an increase in Lactobacillus abundance has also been observed in PPIs users12,32. While some studies suggest a negative impact of PPIs on butyrate, this effect may vary across populations and is not universally observed.

Hydrogen sulfide

In the gut, both organic and inorganic sulfur sources can be metabolized by various sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) to produce hydrogen sulfide (H2S). Patients with IBD have higher levels of H2S and other sulfur-containing metabolites in their feces compared to healthy individuals204,205. Elevated H2S levels can lead to inflammation and damage of the intestinal epithelial mucosa, closely linked to the development and exacerbation of IBD206. SRB, which produce H2S by reducing sulfate, are the main producers of gut H2S207,208. Studies have found higher levels of SRB in patients with UC207. Active IBD patients often have higher SRB levels than those in remission209. Increased SRB levels have also been observed in PPIs users, with a rise in Desulfovibrio (a category of SRB) noted in systemic lupus erythematosus patients using PPIs compared to those not using them210. Another study also found that Desulfovibrio was enriched in patients with acute coronary syndrome after the use of PPIs111.This suggests that PPIs users may have a greater capacity for H2S production in the gut. However, both of these studies were conducted in specific patient cohorts with significant underlying inflammatory conditions. This change in the microbiota may result from an interaction between the drug and the underlying disease. There is currently no clear evidence to suggest this phenomenon can be generalized to all PPIs users.

Taurine is an important substrate for H2S production in the intestine210,211. It can be converted to H2S by taurine-respiring bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli), promoting intestinal inflammation and carcinogenesis212,213. Increased levels of Escherichia coli have been observed in PPIs users. The production of H2S in IBD patients is also associated with reduced levels of butyrate in the body123. Some PPIs users also show increased H2S-producing bacteria (e.g., Atopobium) alongside decreased butyrate levels12,69,214. Although changes in the abundance of several bacteria involved in H₂S metabolism have been observed in PPI users, it remains unclear whether these taxonomic shifts translate to a functional change in the overall luminal H₂S concentration. Therefore, the current evidence merely suggests that certain microbial alterations in PPIs users have the potential to promote H₂S production; this remains a hypothesis that requires direct validation in future studies.

PPIs-induced dysbiosis and adverse effects on IBD: what can we do?

Currently, there is limited research on preventing dysbiosis and adverse effects on IBD induced by PPIs. However, PPIs users should take precautions to mitigate PPIs-induced microbiota dysbiosis. Effective strategies are needed to minimize the adverse effects of PPIs in IBD patients undergoing such treatment. Based on the observed changes in gut microbiota composition and its potential impact on IBD, this review summarizes known therapeutic approaches and proposes potential strategies (Fig. 4).

Rational use and withdrawal

Although PPIs-induced gut microbiota change can also be observed in healthy individuals, dysbiosis is more likely to occur in populations with specific risk factors. Regarding duration, short-term PPIs use (2–4 weeks) generally results in smaller changes in microbial diversity and composition compared to long-term use32,42,71,90. Some studies on short-term PPIs use have even failed to detect significant taxonomic alterations in the microbiota90,215. For age, research shows that older rats exhibit more pronounced PPI-induced changes in gut microbiota composition and a greater reduction in butyrate levels69. For PPIs types and doses, different PPIs appear to have similar effects on gut microbiota57. High-dose PPI use may have a greater impact on the microbiota, although some studies have not observed this association57,215. The risk factors influencing the impact of PPIs on IBD incidence do not entirely overlap with those for microbiota dysbiosis. Studies have found that different PPIs types may have varying effects on IBD risk, while age subgroup analyses showed no significant differences13. However, research exploring the effects of PPIs duration and dosage on IBD incidence remains relatively insufficiently addressed.

Discontinuing PPIs use promptly may be the simplest approach to treat and prevent PPIs-induced microbiota dysbiosis. Multiple studies have shown that PPIs-induced alterations in the gut microbiota are reversible215,216. One study observed that reduced bacterial genera and enriched pathogenic species returned to baseline levels after discontinuing PPIs use216. In conclusion, the gut microbiota exhibits strong resilience and recovery potential once the influence of PPIs ceases. Although further exploration is needed to determine the optimal timing for discontinuing PPIs without significantly impacting the gut microbiota, establishing this concept is highly necessary.

Probiotics and prebiotics

The use of probiotics has demonstrated therapeutic benefits in both IBD patients and PPIs users. Numerous studies have reported that combining PPIs with probiotics can reduce the risk of microbiota dysbiosis217,218. A prospective study found that co-administration of probiotics with PPIs not only improved PPIs-induced dysbiosis but also promoted the colonization of specific supplemented probiotics in the gut when compared to the use of multi-strain probiotic mixtures in non-PPIs users219. This effect may be attributed to the suppression of gastric acid by PPIs, which mitigates acid-induced probiotic inactivation, thereby facilitating probiotic colonization and activity in the gut.

There are fewer studies on the effects of combining PPIs with prebiotics. One study using inulin alongside PPIs therapy found that inulin supplementation effectively alleviated PPIs-induced hypomagnesemia and related symptoms220. This may indirectly improve the adverse effects of hypomagnesemia on gut microbiota. However, the study did not investigate microbiota changes before and after treatment in PPIs users. Some studies employing synbiotics have observed benefits similar to those of probiotics alone218. However, further research is required to determine whether these benefits are attributable to the addition of prebiotics.

Oral hygiene management

Oral hygiene management is of great importance for both IBD patients and PPIs users. Even with normal oral hygiene practices, IBD patients exhibit more severe periodontal disease and a higher abundance of pro-inflammatory oral microbiota compared to healthy individuals221. Therefore, IBD patients should undergo regular dental check-ups and may require stricter oral hygiene management. In studies related to PPIs, a study in healthy volunteers found that, compared to using PPIs alone, the concurrent use of a chlorhexidine mouthwash after each meal could attenuate or even offset the enrichment of oral bacteria in the gut caused by PPIs90. This study also demonstrated in an animal model that both an increased oral bacterial load and a compromised gastric acid barrier promote the intestinal colonization of oral bacteria90. This suggests that maintaining good oral hygiene may reduce the intestinal colonization of oral bacteria during PPIs use. However, the long-term use of mouthwash may have adverse effects on the oral micro-ecosystem222. The overall effect of such oral hygiene measures in the PPI-using population remains unclear. In conclusion, for patients on PPIs therapy, maintaining standard and good oral hygiene practices, such as brushing at least twice daily, is a reasonable measure to reduce the oral bacterial load. However, the long-term net benefit of more intensive measures that could disrupt the oral micro-ecosystem, such as the chronic use of mouthwash, remains unclear in this specific population. Given the potential adverse effects on the oral microbiota, a more cautious approach is warranted when recommending such interventions for PPIs users until more definitive clinical evidence becomes available. Prospective cohort studies are needed to further validate the benefits of oral hygiene management for PPIs users and to explore whether stricter oral hygiene practices should be adopted during PPIs use.

Electrolyte and mineral supplementation

Electrolyte and mineral imbalances, including hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, and iron deficiency, have been reported in both PPIs users and patients with IBD. While not all PPIs users will experience these electrolyte and mineral imbalances, a growing body of evidence has linked PPIs use to an elevated risk for these conditions223,224. Among these, hypomagnesemia is a frequent type of electrolyte disorder in PPIs users39. A study has shown that oral magnesium chloride supplementation can significantly enrich Bifidobacterium in the gut and increase the production of ICA83. The enrichment of Bifidobacterium is beneficial for both PPIs users and IBD patients. However, another study found that ICA can inhibit CD4+ Treg differentiation and enhance CD8+ T cell function, thereby improving chemotherapy efficacy in colorectal cancer patients225. This suggests that ICA may act as a pro-inflammatory substance, with unclear implications for IBD. Hypocalcemia in PPIs users is often secondary to hypomagnesemia, and correcting magnesium deficiency should be prioritized81. Calcium supplementation may also be beneficial. Research indicates that oral calcium can exert prebiotic-like effects, promoting the enrichment of Bacteroidetes and Actinobacteria, particularly Bifidobacterium, while also protecting the colonic barrier226. The effectiveness of oral magnesium and calcium supplementation under gastric acid suppression, as well as their specific effects on the gut microbiota of PPIs users, requires further investigation.

The impact of PPIs on serum iron may still be controversial. A large, propensity-score matched cohort study of over 126,000 patient pairs, which found that PPIs users had a significantly higher risk of developing IDA compared to H2RAs users (HR: 1.761, 95% CI: 1.691–1.835)224. This finding is supported by several other studies identifying PPIs as an independent risk factor for IDA227–232. Conversely, the evidence is not entirely consistent. One long-term study (5–12 years) of patients on omeprazole and esomeprazole found no increased risk of developing IDA233. Some similar negative conclusions were reached in other studies of patients on PPIs for over 12 months, although their findings are limited by a small sample size234,235. In terms of mechanism research, the use of PPIs mainly inhibits gastric acid secretion, leading to a reduction in non-heme iron reduction and a decrease in solubility, thereby inhibiting iron absorption236. In addition, some studies have found that PPIs directly reduce iron absorption by up-regulating hepcidin and inhibiting the expression of iron transporters in the duodenum, while this effect was not observed in H2RAs237. Overall, although most studies support the association between PPIs and an increased risk of IDA, there is still significant population heterogeneity. Users of PPIs should take iron supplements under the guidance of serum iron and hemoglobin tests. The choice of iron supplementation dosage and method can be complex. Both oral and intravenous iron supplementation are considered safe and effective. However, some studies suggest that oral iron may have adverse effects on the gut microbiota, including promoting pathogenic bacteria in the Enterobacteriaceae family and reducing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus238. A recent population study found that while both oral and intravenous iron supplementation did not affect disease activity in IBD patients, oral iron had a greater impact on the microbiota compared to intravenous iron239. This included a reduction in SCFA-producing bacteria and an increase in Bifidobacterium, with CD patients showing a higher sensitivity to iron supplementation239. In PPIs users, due to gastric acid suppression, less soluble iron is available, and more iron exists in a form that is poorly absorbed as iron ions. As a result, intravenous iron is often more effective than oral supplementation. Therefore, for PPIs users, especially those with IBD or unstable gut microbiota, intravenous iron supplementation may be the preferred option.

Other potential treatments for specific microbiota

Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) scavengers could represent a novel strategy for drug development. Studies have shown that Bismuth (III)-subsalicylate acts as an H₂S scavenger, effectively reducing intestinal H₂S levels, alleviating A. parvulum-induced colitis in mice, and potentially inhibiting the proliferation of A. parvulum123. Additionally, H₂S scavengers have been reported to enhance the sensitivity of pathogenic bacteria to antibiotics, facilitating their eradication240.

Arginine supplementation may represent another potential therapeutic approach. Cardiac glycoside reductase 2 is sensitive to dietary arginine, which can enhance the suppression of the Th17 transcription factor Rorγt, counteracting Th17 cell activation induced by Eggerthella lenta124. Arginine supplementation has been shown to improve colitis in various experimental colitis mouse models and regulate gut microbiota, increasing the abundance of Bacteroidetes and decreasing the abundance of Proteobacteria241. Therefore, arginine supplementation could be a safe and simple approach for treating IBD and correcting gut microbiota dysbiosis induced by PPIs. However, further investigation is needed to explore its application in humans and its effects on PPIs users.

Butyrate supplementation could play a beneficial role in restoring PPIs-induced dysbiosis, particularly by targeting the reduction of butyrate-producing microbiota. The primary methods of butyrate supplementation include oral administration, enemas, and butyrogenic diets, all of which have been shown to reduce disease activity, lower inflammatory markers, and restore gut microbiota balance in IBD patients242. Butyrate supplementation is generally considered a safe treatment, excessive supplementation can lead to intestinal mucosal damage243. In the absence of sufficient clinical evidence on PPIs users, it may be safe and effective to supplement with fermentable dietary fibers that produce butyrate, such as sprouted barley and oat bran242. Studies have shown that β-glucan, found in oats and barley, enhances tight junction proteins and increases SCFAs production, thus improving experimental colitis, which may correspond with the mechanisms by which PPIs exacerbate experimental colitis in mice106,244.

Hypothesis, evidence, and validation of the association between PPIs and IBD onset

Due to the complexity of the relationship between PPIs and IBD, many studies have attempted to elucidate it. Current studies examining the association between PPIs use and the risk of developing IBD are primarily observational studies, with conflicting conclusions. While certain changes in gut microbiota observed in PPIs users resemble those in IBD patients and mechanisms by which these changes promote IBD onset and progression are gradually being elucidated, PPIs also exhibit potential protective and therapeutic effects on IBD through their influence on specific gut microbiota. Despite numerous studies investigating the effects of PPIs on gut microbiota composition, results remain inconsistent due to factors such as population heterogeneity, variations in PPIs dosage and duration, and comorbid conditions among subjects. Thus, solely emphasizing the harmful changes in gut microbiota to explain the association between PPIs use and increased IBD risk is insufficient. The association between PPIs and IBD risk may represent a true relationship or a spurious one resulting from statistical biases in the studies. Additionally, PPIs may influence IBD risk through mechanisms independent of gut microbiota alteration. Therefore, this review comprehensively examines the hypotheses linking PPIs use to an increased risk of IBD and explores potential biases in existing studies. It also summarizes both supporting and opposing evidence (Table 2).

Given the low incidence of IBD and the long latency between PPIs use and IBD onset, conducting human randomized controlled trials to establish a causal relationship between the two is challenging. Beyond epidemiological studies from human cohorts, experimental and translational data can assess whether PPIs-induced dysbiosis provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the development of IBD. However, there is currently a scarcity of studies that specifically link PPIs use to the development of IBD, with the gut microbiota acting as a mechanistic bridge. Our review establishes a theoretical mechanistic hypothesis, based on observed changes in the gut microbiota, to explain this link, but it requires validation with experimental data. With the advent of germ-free animal models and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) techniques, gut microbiota from various pathological conditions can increasingly be transferred into germ-free models to investigate their effects on the intestine and even the whole body. Experimental validation for the proposed association may be achieved by transplanting fecal microbiota from PPIs users into germ-free and IBD-susceptible mice and subsequently observing the severity of colitis. However, this approach demands a rigorous experimental design, including excluding other factors that affect the gut microbiota, the choice of drug type, and treatment duration, among other factors.

What is the better acid-suppressive therapy?

PPIs have become the main therapy for acid-related diseases due to their superior acid-suppressive effects and prolonged duration of action compared to H2RAs. Potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs), a newer class of drugs with stronger acid suppression, have been approved for clinical use recently245. However, the potential adverse effects of acid suppressants on the gut, particularly the gut microbiota, may have been underestimated. Acid suppressants share a common result of acid inhibition, leading to similar impacts on the gut microbiota. For instance, they promote the colonization of oral bacteria in the gut, which has been implicated in the development of colitis158. These effects are more pronounced with P-CABs due to their higher acid-suppressive potency61. Moreover, dysbiosis in PPIs users shows significant overlap with gut microbiota alterations observed in patients with colorectal cancer and CDI12. The use of H2RAs and P-CABs has also been associated with an increased risk of various gastrointestinal diseases246,247. Thus, the elevated risk of IBD observed with using PPIs may not be unique to this class but rather a shared adverse effect of acid suppressants, potentially linked to the degree of acid suppression. The ongoing development of acid-suppressive drugs with stronger effects raises concerns about the increased risk of dysbiosis and related gastrointestinal complications.

In the future, developing personalized strategies for precise gastric acid regulation may become increasingly important. Striking a balance between the benefits and risks of acid suppressant use is essential to achieve disease remission with minimal acid suppression and reduced disruption of the gut microbiota. For example, in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease, once the gastric pH exceeds 4, the duration of acid suppression becomes more critical for healing than the pH value. S. anginosus, a group of oral bacteria, is enriched in PPIs users and cannot survive at pH <590,248. Maintaining pH within an optimal range that intersects these thresholds may effectively mitigate microbiota dysbiosis in acid-suppressant users. Additionally, exploring effective monitoring indicators for pH control in acid suppressant users is crucial. Since direct measurement of gastric pH is challenging, simple and practical surrogate markers are needed. Fasting serum gastrin concentration has been proposed as a potential indicator of acid suppression efficacy in PPIs-treated patients, warranting further exploration of the relationship between gastrin levels and intragastric pH under acid suppression therapy249. Changes in specific gut microbial populations may reflect the extent of acid suppression and could serve as unique biomarkers for acid suppressant users. Moreover, different acid suppressants may have unique pharmacological effects beyond acid inhibition. For example, tegoprazan (a P-CAB) has been shown to prevent colitis by enriching Bacteroides vulgatus rather than altering pH250. Some acid suppressants may confer benefits to the gut and microbiota, further research and validation are needed to identify such agents and their potential advantages.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the influence of PPIs on the gut microbiota highlights their complex and contradictory role in the onset and progression of IBD. The relationship between PPIs and IBD seems to result from a combination of beneficial and harmful microbial changes. FMT may serve as a valuable study method in establishing the causal link between PPIs-induced dysbiosis and IBD. Not only PPIs but also other acid suppressants exhibit a similar ability to induce gut dysbiosis. The dysbiosis caused by acid suppressants may contribute to a range of intestinal disorders, including but not limited to IBD. Therefore, this highlights the need for strategies to mitigate the microbial alterations induced by these therapies. We need to maximize the therapeutic benefits of acid suppression while minimizing its adverse effects on the gut microbiota.
