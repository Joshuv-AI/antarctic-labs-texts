Amidation of Phosphonoacetate-Modified Oligonucleotides and Evaluation of the Properties of the Synthesized Oligonucleotides
Authors: Takashi Osawa, Ryusei Yamashita, Haruki Koyama, Daiki Fujisue, Satoshi Obika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemical modification of the phosphate backbone in the discovery of oligonucleotide therapeutics has recently attracted attention. However, most phosphate-backbone modifications require the synthesis of phosphoramidite building blocks, which limits rapid structural diversification and systematic structure–activity relationship studies. In this study, amidation of phosphonoacetate (PACE)-modified oligonucleotides was developed. Our modification method enables the rapid introduction of diverse substituents to the phosphate backbone of oligonucleotides, avoidance of novel phosphoramidite synthesis, and systematic functional evaluation of phosphate-backbone-modified oligonucleotides.

The number of approved oligonucleotide therapeutics, such as antisense oligonucleotides and siRNAs, has been increasing in recent years. − These drugs target RNAs present in cells for therapeutic action by forming duplexes with these RNAs; therefore, the efficacy of oligonucleotide therapeutics depends on their ability to bind to their target RNA. In addition, chemical modification of the phosphate backbone of oligonucleotides is essential for enhancing their in vivo stability. For instance, phosphorothioate (PS) modification can dramatically increase the in vivo stability of oligonucleotides and their efficacy as a therapeutic agent. Conversely, nonspecific binding of oligonucleotides to proteins due to PS modifications may induce an undesired immune response and toxicity. Therefore, the development of phosphate-backbone modifications different from PS is strongly needed for therapeutic applications of oligonucleotides.

In recent years, much research has been conducted on the development of phosphate-backbone modifications , and their therapeutic applications. Researchers investigated replacing the phosphodiester bond with modifying groups such as N3′-phosphoramidate, − N3′-thiophosphoramidate, , N-mesyl phosphoramidate, − ribonucleic neutrals, , phophotriester, , phosphoryl guanidine, − or alkyl phosphonate modifications. , The results revealed that appropriate phosphate-backbone modification of oligonucleotides can reduce the toxicity and improve cellular uptake efficiency while maintaining target RNA binding capacity and activity. − While the importance of phosphate moiety chemical modifications in oligonucleotide therapeutics has been demonstrated, it is difficult to obtain the structure–activity relationship necessary to design chemical modifications that are truly useful for oligonucleotide therapeutics because chemically modified oligonucleotides are commonly synthesized by using phosphoramidite or H-phosphonate monomers, making it difficult to prepare structurally diverse oligonucleotides.

Against this backdrop, a novel backbone modification method using a single versatile oligonucleotide intermediate would provide a very powerful methodology for discovering the correlation among the structure, activity, toxicity, and pharmacokinetics of backbone modifications of oligonucleotides. However, structural transformation using oligonucleotides is difficult because DNA and RNA are highly water-soluble and have many reactive functional groups, which limit the reactions that can be used for site-selective structural conversion. Overcoming such difficulties, several synthetic methodologies based on the oxidative coupling of amines and phosphonates, − Huisgen reaction, , amidation from N-sulfonyl phosphoramidate-modified oligonucleotides, − and modification using triazinyl phosphoramidate oligonucleotides have been developed to chemically modify the phosphate backbone from oligonucleotides. However, these methods face challenges such as a narrow substrate applicability range due to harsh reaction conditions, usage of copper reagents with low biocompatibility, or difficulty in quantitatively obtaining products due to slow reaction rates. Aiming to develop a phosphate backbone modification method with broad substrate generality, we hypothesized that the condensation of carboxylic acids with amines, which has been widely used in the field of DNA-encoded library (DEL) synthesis, − could be used to chemically modify the phosphate backbone of oligonucleotides. In this study, amidation reactions using phosphonoacetete (PACE)-modified oligonucleotides were investigated to realize a simple and convenient PACE-backbone-selective chemical modification of oligonucleotides (Figure ). We also evaluated the properties of the synthesized amidated-PACE-modified oligonucleotides, and the details are described.

The strategy shown in Figure requires activation of the carboxylic acid moiety of PACE in an aqueous solution of the oligonucleotide. It is also important that the condensing agent, which generally reacts with water as well as carboxylic acids, reacts selectively with carboxylic acids in water. Based on such a perspective, 4-(4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)-4-methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM) was selected as a water-soluble condensing agent based on the examples of the amide bond formation reaction in DNA-encoded library synthesis. , The condensation of carboxylic acids and amines with DMTMM has also been applied to the selective amidation of carboxylic acids in artificial nucleic acids modified into oligonucleotides, and we expected that the amide bond formation using DMTMM could be applied to the modification of the phosphate backbone. Therefore, amidation of a PACE-modified thymidine decamer (DNA1) using DMTMM was investigated. In this study, PACE-modified oligonucleotides were used as diastereomeric mixtures because the phosphorus atom is the chiral center in PACE. First, the examination of several buffer solutions, organic solvents, and reaction temperatures showed that DNA1 was successfully converted into the desired n-pentylamide product (DNA2) with an LC/MS yield of up to 27%, under the mixed solvent system of a 3-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazinyl]­propanesulfonic acid (EPPS) buffer (pH 7.5) and DMSO (1:1) at 60 °C for 30 min (Table S2 and Table S3). DMTMM and amines were added every 30 min after the start of the reaction (Table , Figure S5, and Figure S8). The amount of DNA2 increased with the addition of reagents (entries 1–5), and the yield of DNA2 ultimately increased to 75% by adding the reagent five times (entry 5). However, adding 300 equiv of DMTMM and 360 equiv of amine did not improve the yield of DNA2 (entry 6), which suggests that it is very effective to repeatedly add DMTMM and amine to the reaction solution in the amidation of PACE.

Next, various amidated PACEs were synthesized using PACE-modified DNA4 containing four types of nucleotides in random order and isolated under the optimal amidation conditions shown in entry 5 of Table . In this study, we investigated the substrate scope and limitations of amines using representative amines with substituents and functional groups such as aromatic amines, secondary amines, and diamines (Figure ). The yields of amidated PACE-modified oligonucleotides shown in Figure , except for DNA15, are the isolation yields after HPLC purification. As a result, the condensation reaction between n-pentylamine and DNA4 proceeded completely, successfully yielding the desired product DNA5 with a 32% isolation yield, which demonstrates that our PACE amidation is sequence-independent for oligonucleotides. Additionally, when the optimized conditions were applied to natural DNA (DNA6) with the same sequence as DNA4 linked by phosphodiester bonds, DNA6 without PACE-modification did not react (Figure S9 and Table S5), demonstrating that our method is capable of PACE-selective amidation without damaging the oligonucleotides. In addition, structurally simple pentylamine (DNA5), amides of secondary amines (DNA8 and DNA11), and amides containing functional groups such as methoxycarbonyl and hydroxyl groups (DNA12 and DNA13) were successfully obtained with moderate isolation yields. By contrast, an amide of PACE and aniline (DNA8) was isolated in nearly 70% yield, while the yield of the desired product tended to decrease when diamines were used. In particular, when 1,4-butanediamine was used, the LC/MS yield of the desired DNA15 was 9%, indicating that the amidation reaction hardly proceeded. These isolation yield trends are likely due to the pK a values of the amines. The pK a of the conjugate acid of aniline is approximately 4.5, which means that aniline is barely protonated in a buffer solution with a pH of 7.5. By contrast, the pK a values of the conjugate acids of common amines, ranging from 8 to 10, are protonated by nearly half and lose their nucleophilicity. Because diamines are particularly basic, the amidation reaction conditions may not have been suitable for obtaining diamine amides. It should be emphasized that structurally diverse backbone-modified oligonucleotides can be easily synthesized by mixing PACE-modified DNA, DMTMM, and amines, and sufficient quantities of the desired products can be obtained to evaluate the physical properties of the oligonucleotides. In addition, we attempted amidation of PACE-modified DNA16 containing the full PS modification, commonly used in oligonucleotide therapeutics, and succeeded in obtaining the desired n-pentylamide (DNA17) in a 42% isolation yield, which was slightly higher than that of DNA5 with no PS modifications. We also attempted amidation of DNA18 bearing PACE modifications at multiple positions and succeeded in obtaining the anilide (DNA19) in high yield (Scheme S1). Furthermore, our synthetic method was applied to the conjugation of functional molecules and DNA. Specifically, DNA4 was amidated with an amine having a bicyclononyne unit (BCN-NH2), and the resulting DNA20 was reacted with an azide-modified cRGD, which is a membrane-permeable peptide, via a Click reaction to successfully obtain the desired conjugate DNA21. Although the thermal instability and reactivity of bicyclooctin to nucleophiles − resulted in extremely low isolation yields of the desired amide (DNA19), these results demonstrate that our synthesis method can be used for the modification of the phosphate backbone of oligonucleotide therapeutics and will be valuable for investigating the structure–activity relationships of oligonucleotide therapeutics in the future.

The duplex-forming ability of the synthesized oligonucleotides (DNA5 and DNA7–14) with complementary DNA and RNA was evaluated by UV melting experiments. In this experiment, the duplex-forming abilities of PACE-modified DNA4 and DNA22 obtained by hydrolyzing the methyl ester in DNA12 (Scheme S2) were also evaluated. The results are summarized in Table , and the UV melting data are shown in Figures S29 and S30 in the SI. The T m values of the duplexes formed between the PACE-modified oligonucleotide (DNA4) and its complementary DNA and RNA were 53 and 52 °C, respectively. These values showed slight differences compared to the T m value of the duplex formed by natural DNA6 (54 and 53 °C). In a previous study, the duplex-forming ability of PACE-modified oligonucleotides was comparable to that of their natural counterparts, and only minor changes in T m values were observed, which is consistent with our results.

The T m values of the amidated PACE oligonucleotides (DNA5, DNA7–14, and DNA22) were comparable to or slightly lower than those of the natural oligonucleotides. In particular, T m tended to decrease as the modified amide substituents became more sterically bulky. For example, DNA7 and DNA10 modified with branched or secondary amines showed 1 °C lower T m values compared with DNA5. In addition, the T m values of duplexes formed by DNA8, modified with a planar phenyl group, were 2 °C higher than those of DNA7 modified with a cyclohexyl group. These results suggested that the presence of bulky substituents on the phosphate moiety may impair duplex formation. Furthermore, the effect of phosphate backbone modifications on the stability of duplexes was similarly observed for duplexes formed by oligonucleotides having P–N bonds. , By contrast, DNA12 and DNA22 exhibited a relatively greater decrease in T m than did the other modifications, indicating that the presence of a carbonyl group negatively affects duplex formation. These findings suggest that not only steric effects but also factors such as hydration and hydrogen bonding around the phosphate moiety may influence duplex stability. The key point is that our amidation strategy using PACE-modified oligonucleotides enabled the synthesis of a diverse set of phosphate-modified oligonucleotides with rich structural diversity. This allowed us to systematically investigate duplex-forming ability. Although the amidated-PACE-modified oligonucleotides tended to have slightly lower T m values, no significant impairment of duplex formation was observed.

The nuclease resistance of the PACE- or amidated-PACE-modified thymidine decamers (DNA23–26) was investigated by using 3′-exonuclease (Crotalus adamanteus venom phosphodiesterase, CAVP) and compared with those of natural and PS-modified oligonucleotides (DNA27 and DNA28). In this experiment, based on the results shown in Table , PACE-amides in DNA8, DNA13, and DNA14, which formed relatively stable duplexes among the synthesized oligonucleotides, were selected for the evaluation of nuclease resistance of amidated-PACE-modified oligonucleotides. In addition, DNA24–26 used in this experiment was synthesized by amidation of PACE-modified DNA23 at the 3′-end (Figure S31). The enzymatic degradation of oligonucleotides by CAVP was monitored by reversed-phase HPLC analysis, and the ratio of residual oligonucleotides was plotted over time (Figure ). As a result, while natural DNA27 was completely degraded by nuclease within 5 min, PACE-modified DNA23 showed higher stability compared to PS-modified DNA28. Under these enzyme reaction conditions, anilide-DNA24 was almost completely degraded within 10 min. Interestingly, DNA25 and DNA26 modified with amidated-PACE, which contains dimethylamino or hydroxyl groups, were significantly more stable than PS-modified DNA28, with more than 80% of DNA25 and DNA26 remaining after 60 min of reaction. These results suggest that introducing a substituent with a hydrophilic functional group into the phosphate backbone moiety may be effective in preventing recognition of oligonucleotides by nucleases. Further studies on the correlation between the structure of amidated-PACE and the nuclease resistance of oligonucleotides will be reported in the future.

In summary, we successfully developed a method for amidation of PACE-modified oligonucleotides using DMTMM as the condensing agent. This modification method enables phosphate-backbone-selective chemical modification of oligonucleotides and is applicable to PS-modified oligonucleotides, which are commonly used in oligonucleotide therapeutics. Regarding the scope and limitations of amines, our synthesis method is applicable to various primary and secondary amines with substituents and functional groups other than diamines. As the results of evaluation of the duplex-forming ability of the amidated-PACE-modified oligonucleotides, most of them did not exhibit a significant reduction in duplex-forming ability. Moreover, amidated-PACE modification with the appropriate substituents can dramatically enhance the resistance of oligonucleotides to nuclease degradation. These results demonstrated that our synthesis method could serve as a screening platform, enabling rapid chemical space exploration for oligonucleotide therapeutics. Further studies are ongoing to explore the optimal backbone modification for controlling the activity, toxicity, and pharmacokinetics of oligonucleotide therapeutics based on the chemical modification method developed in this study.
