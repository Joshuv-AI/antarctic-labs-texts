Assessment of molecular interactions and anti-inflammatory activity of fermented camel milk-derived peptides in RAW macrophages
Authors: Prashantkumar Natubhai Padhiyar, Bethsheba Basaiawmoit, Pooja M Mankad, Amar A Sakure, Arka Bhattacharya, Kanthi Kiran Kondepudi, Bipransh Kumar Tiwary, Preetam Sarkar, Prakash Koringa, Shaikh Adil, Subrota Hati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFermented camel milk is gaining attention as a functional food due to the formation of bioactive peptides during microbial fermentation. This study aimed to investigate the generation of bioactive peptides and their associated antidiabetic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities in camel milk fermented using Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus M9 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae WBS2A. The in vitro fermentation process was evaluated in relation to proteolytic activity and peptide formation under varying fermentation times and inoculum concentrations. Antidiabetic activity was assessed using α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition assays, while antioxidant potential was determined using the ABTS assay. Peptide profiles and metabolite changes were characterized using RP-HPLC, SDS-PAGE, and 2D electrophoresis, and structural modifications were analysed through FTIR and CLSM. Fermentation significantly enhanced proteolytic activity, leading to the release of low-molecular-weight peptides (3–10 kDa) associated with improved bioactivity. Fermented samples exhibited notable α-amylase (70.68%) and α-glucosidase (59.18%) inhibition, along with maximum antioxidant activity (54.97%) after 48 h. In addition, fermented camel milk reduced intracellular ROS, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and apoptosis in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages. Molecular docking analysis further suggested potential interactions between identified peptides and target enzymes, supporting the observed in vitro activities.These findings demonstrate that fermentation-derived peptides contribute to the functional properties of camel milk; however, further in vivo studies are required to confirm their bioavailability and therapeutic relevance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00726-026-03530-4.Keywords: Anti-inflammatory activity, Bioactive peptides, Fermented camel milk, LC–MS/MS, Molecular docking, Peptidomics

Introduction

Camel milk is admired for its therapeutic components, which help treat tuberculosis, asthma, and other gastrointestinal ailments. It has a better digestion and nutritional profile than that of cow’s milk. Fermentation with probiotics increases its health benefits, including vitamins, polyunsaturated fatty acids, and anti-inflammatory proteins that promote gut health and glucose metabolism (El‐Agamy 2009). Camel milk proteins follow specific patterns that differ significantly from those present in cow and human milk. This distinct immunological difference across proteins in cow and camel milk is considered an important component in physiological, dietary, and medicinal applications. Camel milk consumption has been associated with enhanced immune function, promoting overall health and reducing inflammatory responses. Furthermore, higher immunity allows for improved protection against bacterial and viral infections (Faraz 2020).

Camel milk, often referred to as the "white gold of the desert," bears a closer resemblance to human milk than other types of milk and is distinguished by its unique properties. It is characterized by low sugar and cholesterol contents and is abundant in important chemicals. Vitamin C is abundant in camel milk, which also contains protective proteins. Camel milk has a distinctive protein composition. It has an opaque white appearance, a typical milky odor, and a salty-sweet taste that varies depending on the fodder or vegetation in the grazing area. With an average viscosity of 1.72 cP and density of 1.029 g/cm3 at 20 °C, camel milk is less viscous than bovine milk with comparable dry matter under similar conditions (Baloch et al. 2018). According to Aluko (2012) statistics, Kenya produces an estimated 1.096 million tonnes of camel milk per year, whereas Somalia and Pakistan produce 0.987 and 0.944 million tonnes, respectively. The author also reported that India produces approximately 0.00827864 million tons of camel milk annually.

Bioactive peptides (BPs) are low-molecular-weight protein derivatives that can modulate diverse physiological processes and exert beneficial effects on overall human health (Al Kanhal 2010). During fermentation, lactic acid bacteria (LAB) produce proteolytic enzymes that break down milk proteins such as whey and casein, leading to the liberation of these BPs. Proteolysis of milk proteins by bacteria generates a diverse range of bioactive peptides, which are linked to several health benefits for consumers (Chaudhari et al. 2017).

Diabetes mellitus is a long-term metabolic disease characterized by persistently increased blood sugar levels. It is the outcome of either insufficient synthesis of insulin, reduced insulin action, or decreased sensitivity of build tissues to insulin. Diabetes type 1 is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system deliberately destroys pancreatic β-cells responsible for insulin synthesis. Type 2 diabetes, which is more common, develops due to an imbalance between insulin secretion and glucose uptake, leading to chronic hyperglycemia. According to Chaudhari et al. (2017), 38.4 million people in the United States suffer from diabetes, with a significant number of cases going undiagnosed and a higher prevalence observed among older adults (Munteanu and Apetrei 2021).

Antioxidants are bioactive compounds that protect the body against oxidative stress by scavenging highly reactive molecules known as free radicals, which are implicated in cellular injury, accelerated aging, and the pathogenesis of numerous diseases. These harmful molecules are naturally produced in the body during normal metabolism or arise from external factors, such as UV rays, pollution, and poor eating habits. Free radicals are neutralized by antioxidants, which reduce oxygen depletion. They contain nutrients such as C and E, proteins such as superoxide dismutase, and various plant-based compounds. Regular consumption of antioxidant-rich foods can support better health and help prevent chronic conditions linked to oxidative damage (Bhuva et al. 2025a).

Bioactive peptides released during the fermentation of camel milk using combinations of LAB and yeast have demonstrated a range of physiological effects, notably antioxidant and antidiabetic activities. These peptides scavenge reactive oxygen species, thereby lowering oxidative stress, which is a crucial element in the onset and progression of diabetes and its associated complications. Moreover, specific camel milk-derived peptides fermented with LAB and yeast have shown inhibitory effects on digestive enzymes, contributing significantly to the regulation of postprandial blood glucose levels (Sah et al. 2014). Peptide molecular weight is a critical factor influencing both bioactivity and absorption. Previous studies have demonstrated that low molecular weight peptides, particularly those below 1 kDa, exhibit enhanced biological activities such as antioxidant and antihypertensive effects. This is largely attributed to their improved ability to interact with biological targets and their efficient transport across the intestinal epithelium via peptide transport systems (e.g., PepT1). Consequently, low molecular weight peptides are considered more bioavailable and functionally effective, making them important candidates in the development of functional foods and bioactive compounds.

This study developed and analyzed antioxidative and antidiabetic proteins produced from camel milk fermented with yeast and lactic acid bacteria. The bioactive potential of these peptides was assessed via in vitro enzyme inhibitory assays, and their structural and functional characteristics were explored using molecular docking, LC–MS/MS, and RP-HPLC. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of peptides produced through camel milk fermentation and support their application in creating functional dairy-based products aimed at managing oxidative stress and metabolic disorders.

Methods and materials

Strain

The LAB culture that was employed in the present study, Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (M9, MTCC 25516) and yeast strain i.e. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (WBS2A, MG101828) from the Culture Collection of SMC College of Dairy Science, Dairy Microbiology Department, Kamdhenu University, Anand, Gujarat, India. The yeast strain was propagated in Yeast Malt Broth (HiMedia, India), whereas the LAB cultures was grown in De Man, Rogosa and Sharpe (MRS) broth (HiMedia, India). Sterilized reconstituted skim milk (RSM) with 11% TS was used for growing LAB culture, which were then kept at 5 ± 2 ºC. In a similar manner, yeast was cultured in RSM (11% TS) supplemented with @10 g/L of dextrose and kept at 5 ± 2 ºC. For the duration of the research, the transfer was made once a week.

Preparation of samples

Camel milk was obtained from Gujarat Co-Operative Milk Marketing Federation (GCMMF) Ltd., Anand, Gujarat, India. Immediately after collection, the fresh milk was passed through a sterilized muslin cloth to remove any visible impurities. It was then autoclaved at 15 pressure and 121 °C for 15 min to ensure microbial contamination. After autoclave, the milk was poured into pre-sterilized glass containers and stored under refrigerated conditions at 5 ± 1 °C until use in experimental procedures.

For culture activation, a 2% inoculum of each pure strain was added to the sterilized camel milk. Cultures of LAB were incubated for 24 h at 30 °C, whereas for two to four days, yeast cultures were incubated at 25 °C. For mixed-culture fermentation, both LAB and yeast strains were inoculated at 2% each into sterilized camel milk and incubated at 30 ℃ for varying durations of 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Subsequently, the fermented camel milk (FCM) was centrifuged at 4193 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Resultant supernatants were passed through 0.22 µm syringe filters to ensure sterility and clarity prior to analysis of their antioxidant and antidiabetic properties.

Assessment of scavenging of free radicals (%) activity

The assay was conducted according to Telagiri and Hullatti (2015). The ABTS radical solution was prepared by mixing 7.4 mM ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) with 2.6 mM potassium persulfate at a ratio of 1:0.35, and the mixture was incubated overnight in the dark in amber-colored bottles. The resulting solution was then diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to obtain the working ABTS solution. For the assay, 50 μL of sample supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of ABTS working solution and thoroughly homogenized. The absorbance was measured using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Systronics 2202, India) at 734 nm at 30 s intervals over a period of 10 min to determine the radical scavenging activity.

where, Control—absorption rate of the unfermented milk; Sample—absorption rate of the fermented milk.

Evaluation of antidiabetic properties

α-amylase inhibition

A reaction mixture containing 200 μL of different milk supernatants, 20 μL of α-amylase (2 U/mL), and 400 μL of phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 6.8) was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 200 μL of 1% soluble starch was added as a substrate, and the mixture was further incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. After incubation, 2000 μL of 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNSA) reagent was added, and the mixture was boiled for 10 min. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm as described by Ademiluyi and Oboh (2013) and Yamaki and Mori (2006).

where, Control—absorption rate of the unfermented milk; Sample—absorption rate of the fermented milk.

α-glucosidase inhibition

The reaction mixture contained 1500 μL of phosphate buffer (100 mM, pH 6.8), 100 μL of culture supernatant, and 2.5 mL of α-glucosidase enzyme solution (1 U/mL). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 500 μL of p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (p-NPG) was added as the substrate, and incubation was continued for an additional 20 min at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 μL of 0.1 M sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃). The absorbance was measured at 405 nm as described by Shai et al. (2011) and Hati et al. (2015).

where, Control—absorption rate of the unfermented milk; Sample—absorption rate of the fermented milk.

Proteolysis evaluation

Proteolytic activity was measured using the OPA assay, as described by Solanki et al. (2017). The O-phthalaldehyde (OPA) method was used to quantify proteolytic activity as described by Hati et al. (24). Lactobacillus and yeast strains were inoculated into heat-treated camel milk at a rate of 2% and incubated for 0, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. At each time point, 3 mL of fermented camel milk samples were mixed with 5 mL of 0.75% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution and vortexed thoroughly. The mixture was then filtered using Whatman No. 42 filter paper. Subsequently, 400 μL of the filtrate was mixed with 3 mL of OPA reagent and incubated at 20 °C for 2 min. The absorbance was measured to determine proteolytic activity. Results were expressed as mg/mL of peptide content.

Characterisation and purification of peptides using antidiabetic & antioxidant specifications

Growth conditions were optimized by inoculating the strain at a 2.5% inoculum level and incubating it at 30 °C for 48 h. The OPA assay was used to determine the peptide concentration (Laemmli 1970).

SDS-PAGE analysis

PAGE analysis was performed according to the methodologies outlined by Carrasco-Castilla et al. (2012) and Yang et al. (2014) to determine the molecular weights of the protein fragments. A 12% resolving gel was used for electrophoretic separation of samples of fermented camel milk to produce water-soluble extracts (WSE).

2D Gel electrophoresis

This method was used to resolve peptide spots from camel milk WSEs, following Panchal et al. (2020) considering the electrical characteristics modified according to Khakhariya et al. (2023b).

Isoelectric focusing

A 7 cm IPG strip was loaded with 125 µg of FCM protein extract (pH 3–10) as described by Kahkhariya et al. (2023b), equilibrated in two buffers, rinsed with 1 × TGS, and analyzed by SDS–PAGE.

Peptide identification and structural characterization employing RPLC–MS

Liquid chromatography

Liquid chromatography (LC) was performed using an Adhoc column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.5 µm). The samples were kept at 20 °C, and the columns were held at 40 °C. The trypsin-digested peptides from the 2D gel spots were filtered using 0.22 µm filters before being injected (20 µL). Separation was carried out using a gradient of acetonitrile at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min with water (solvent B) and formic acid at 0.1% (solvent A), utilizing the technique of Kahkhariya et al. (2023a).

Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis was conducted on an AB SCIEX QTRAP 4500 system linked to an Eksigent Ekspert Ultra-LC 100 (USA) with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Enhanced Mass Spectra (EMS) and Enhanced Product Ion (EPI) scans were used in reverse-phase liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (RPLC–MS) to identify peptides from fermented camel milk. The EMS mode targeted ions within the 350–2000 Da range, with key parameters set to 80 V for the declustering potential, 10 V for the entrance potential, and 5500 V for the ion spray voltage using a rolling collision energy ramp. EPI scans were optimized under high collision-activated dissociation (CAD) and covered the 100–2000 Da range. Information-Dependent Acquisition was used to select the top 1–3 precursor ions within a 250 mDa window, while Enhanced Resolution (ER) scans were applied to analyze isotopic distributions for improved peptide characterization. The peptide identification and validation strategy followed in this study is consistent with previously reported methodologies for fermented milk-derived peptides (Khakhariya et al. 2023a; Bhuva et al. 2025b).

Data analysis and identification of peptides

Raw mass spectral data of peptides were processed using MASCOT software to determine their sequences. The determined sequences were subsequently complemented with BIOPEP database to evaluate their potential antioxidant and antidiabetic properties.

Fractionation of peptides using RP-HPLC

A Shimadzu LC-20 RP-HPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used to separate the fractions of peptides from fermented camel milk on a Thermo UMSil Column C18(3) (5 µm, 250 × 4.6 mm). A 20 µL aliquot was administered using a Hamilton micro-injector, and elution was performed at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min at room temperature. Eluent A consisted of Water containing 0.01% TFA, while Eluent B 0.01% TFA was present in 80:20 acetonitrile: water. The peaks were monitored at 214 nm using a Shimadzu SPD-20A detector and identified following the protocol of Pipaliya et al. (2024).

Quantification of peptides via RP-HPLC

RP-HPLC was employed to extract peptides from the ultra-filtered fragments of FCM. After optimizing the fermentation parameters, WSEs were obtained by centrifugation at 4193 × g for 30 min. The extracts were then subjected to ultrafiltration (3 and 10 kDa) to obtain different peptide fractions. The antidiabetic properties of the samples were evaluated using the α-amylase inhibition (Section α-amylase inhibition) and α-glucosidase inhibition (Section α-glucosidase inhibition) assays, and their antioxidative capacity was determined as outlined in Section "Assessment of scavenging of free radicals (%) activity".

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with Fourier Transform-Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy (Alpha, Bruker, Germany) was used to examine the functional groups, recording spectra in the 4000–500 cm⁻1 range at a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 and averaging 24 scans, following the methodology of Morris et al. (2008). Proteins were labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) at a 1% concentration in Milli-Q water, as previously described. Fluorescence was excited using a laser at 540 nm, and protein structures were visualized with a 63 × objective lens.

Inflammation-inhibiting activity

Cytotoxicity assay

T25 flasks with 70–80% cell confluency was trypsinized, and approximately 1 × 105 cells per well were seeded into 96-well culture plates. The plates were incubated for 16 h at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO₂. Following this period, the cells were exposed to different dilutions of the milk sample and incubated for an additional 24 h. After incubation, 100 µL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well, and the medium was discarded. The plates were then incubated for 4 h under the same conditions. The resulting supernatant was subsequently eliminated, & Dimethyl sulfoxide was added in an amount of 100 µL to dissolve the formazan crystals. The absorbance was determined at 570 nm using a Tecan Life Science M200 PRO microplate reader.

Cell viability

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the National Center for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, Maharashtra, India. National Cell Science Center of Pune, Maharashtra, India is a Public funded Institution and they are authorized nationally to provide the cell lines for Research purpose. The cells were cultivated in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco #11,965,118) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco #A5256701) and 1% streptomycin–penicillin (Gibco #15,140,122) and maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Subculturing was performed every one to two days using T25 cell culture flasks.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production

To assess the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in RAW264.7 cells after co-treatment with LPS (1 μg/mL) and fermented milk samples, a 12-well culture plate was seeded with approximately 1 × 105 cells per well, and the cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO₂. The cells were then treated with either LPS alone (1 μg/mL) or fermented milk alone, or a combination of both for another twenty-four hours. Subsequently, the cells were gently scraped and resuspended in 20 µM DCFDA prepared in serum-free media. The suspension was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark to allow intracellular ROS detection and dye uptake. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACS Aria III flow cytometer, and analysis was performed using the FITC-A detection channel.

Pro-inflammatory cytokine (IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β) evaluation

The protocol was followed according to the ELISA kit instructions provided by Elabscience (USA).

Molecular docking

The X-ray crystallographic configurations of human bile salt-activated lipase, humanoid pancreatic α-amylase (hPAM), human maltase-glucoamylase (hMGA), and human angiotensin-converting enzyme (hACE) were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB IDs: 3BAI, 3CTT, 1F6W, and 1O8A, respectively). Protein preparation for molecular docking was performed using AutoDock 4.2.6, which included adding hydrogen atoms in idealized geometries, protonating residues, and removing all water molecules and ligands (Singh et al. 2015). PEPstrMOD was used to create the three-dimensional structure of the peptide (Jendele et al. 2019), and The PrankWeb server was used to predict the binding locations inside the protein structures (Wallace et al. 1995). Molecular docking was then conducted on the highest-ranked connecting pocket using the web server HADDOCK 2.4 with default parameters. Non-covalent interactions between the peptides and target enzymes were analyzed using LigPlot + (Steel and Torrie 1980), and the complexes for docking were visualized using PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org/pymol).

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analysed following the methodology defined by Bhuva et al. (2025b). All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3), and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A significance level of 5% was applied for all statistical evaluations. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for group comparisons, and Tukey's post hoc test was used to evaluate mean differences. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA; version 8.0).

Results

Evaluation of activity of free radical scavenging (%)

Figure 1 illustrates the antioxidative activity (scavenging of free radicals by ABTS) of camel milk (CM) Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus (M9) fermented in combination with Saccharomyces cerevisiae (WBS2A) over a 48-h incubation period. A marked and significant statistically (p ≤ 0.05) enhancement antioxidative activity was noticed as proliferation progressed. In the initial stage, ABTS radical scavenging activity was relatively low, at 3.36 ± 0.64%. However, the activity steadily increased over time, reaching a maximum of 54.97 ± 0.80% at 48 h. This upward trend highlights the potential of extended fermentation to enhance the antioxidant capacity of camel milk.

Assessment of antidiabetic activities

Figure 1 illustrates the potential of fermented camel milk as an antidiabetic agent. A significant (p ≤ 0.05) increase in all diabetes preventive measures was observed with extended fermentation. Inhibition of the enzymes α-glucosidase and α-amylase progressively improved over time, reaching maximum values of 59.18% and 70.68%, respectively, after 48 h.

Assessment of proteolysis

Figure 2 shows the camel milk's proteolytic action fermented using Saccharomyces cerevisiae (WBS2A) and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus M9. Proteolytic activity started at 2.01 ± 0.01 mg/mL at 0 h with a 1.5% inoculum and gradually increased, reaching a maximum of 6.05 ± 0.02 mg/mL after 48 h at a 2.5% inoculum. The data showed a statistically significant enhancement (p ≤ 0.05) in proteolysis using prolonged fermentation and increased inoculum concentration. Moreover, a significant interaction between incubation duration and inoculum level was observed, indicating a synergistic effect on protein hydrolysis. Among all tested conditions, after 48 h of fermentation, the maximum proteolytic activity (6.05 mg/mL) was observed at the highest inoculum level (2.5%).

Effect of inoculation rates and incubation period on proteolytic activity (mg/mL) of fermented camel milk. Note: *Values with different superscripts differ significantly (p < 0.05), Proteolytic activity (mg/mL) Mean ± SD of three replicate experiments (n = 3)

Fermentation of camel milk with a combination of KGL4 and WBS2A strains resulted in a progressive increase in proteolytic activity, starting at 2.47 mg/mL with a 1.5% inoculum at 0 h and reaching 6.40 mg/mL after 48 h with a 2.5% inoculum. The data demonstrated that proteolytic activity increased significantly (p < 0.05) with higher inoculum concentrations (1.5%, 2.0%, and 2.5%) and extended incubation periods. A significant interaction between incubation duration and inoculum level was also observed in this study.

SDS-PAGE analysis

Figure 3 shows the SDS-PAGE analysis of the WSEs obtained from FCM. Unfermented milk samples displayed a broader range of protein bands than fermented samples, indicating a higher presence of intact proteins. pecifically, unfermented camel milk showed protein bands primarily within the 15–30 kDa range, reflecting minimal degradation, whereas FCM exhibited bands spanning 15–85 kDa, indicating partial breakdown of milk proteins due to enzymatic activity. Notably, the permeate fractions did not show any discernible protein bands.

Electrophoresis of 2D gel

2D-PAGE, or two-dimensional gel electrophoresis, was employed to characterize the protein profiles of various fermented milk samples, with a particular focus on camel milk (Fig. 4). The analysis revealed a substantial number of protein spots distributed within the 15–70 kDa molecular weight range, indicating the presence of peptides and partially hydrolyzed proteins generated during fermentation. Notably, camel milk fermented with the M9 + WBS2A strain exhibited 29 distinct protein spots (Fig. 4), reflecting significant proteolytic activity and the formation of diverse peptide fragments in the fermented milk.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with Fourier Transform-Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR analysis of fermented camel milk showed notable alterations in the secondary structure of proteins (Fig. 6). A marked increase in absorbance at 1637 cm⁻1, within the amide I region, mostly associated with C= O stretching of peptide bonds, was observed, indicating proteolytic transformations compared to unfermented samples. Further enhancement in absorbance between 1620 and 1640 cm⁻1, indicative of β-sheet formation, was especially prominent in the permeate and retentate fractions of both 3 and 10 kDa molecular weights. Deconvolution and curve fitting of the FTIR spectra using Origin software enabled precise quantification of secondary structure elements. The results demonstrated a significant increase in β-sheet content along with a decrease in α-helical structures in fermented samples, supporting extensive enzymatic hydrolysis. These spectral modifications reflect conformational changes in the protein backbones due to microbial fermentation, consistent with earlier research showing that the amide I region is particularly sensitive to ancillary structural transitions. Such structural reorganizations, driven by fermentation, encourage protein chains to unfold and make it easier to produce low-molecular-weight peptides with increased bioactivity.

The microstructural properties of the peptide fractions obtained from camel milk fermented with M9 + WBS2A strains were assessed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Figure S8). The 3 kDa permeate fraction exhibited smaller, well-dispersed peptide particles, indicating efficient protein hydrolysis and the presence of low-molecular-weight peptides. In contrast, the 10 kDa permeate showed relatively larger aggregates, suggesting incomplete protein breakdown at this molecular size threshold. Additionally, the retentate fractions (both 3 and 10 kDa) revealed denser and more complex peptide networks, likely due to the retention of partially hydrolyzed or intact proteins.

Anti-inflammatory activity

The MTT assay was used to assess the viability of RAW 264.7 macrophages treated with different amounts of camel milk fermented with the M9 + WBS2A strain combination (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2.5, and 5 mg/mL). Figure 7A shows that at 0.25 and 0.5 mg/mL, fermented camel milk retained almost 100% cell viability with no cytotoxicity. However, a dose-dependent cytotoxic impact was indicated by the steady decline in viability as the concentration increased from 0.75 to 5 mg/mL.

Anti-inflammatory activity and ROS of camel milk fermented with M9 + WBS2A, A cell viability in RAW264.7 cells, B IL-1β, C TNF-α, and D IL-6 production; E Apoptotic cell population F ROS production, Data were expressed as mean ± S.E.M and analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test

The anti-inflammatory properties of fermented camel milk were evaluated Using LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. The levels of important pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly decreased after treatment with M9 + WBS2A-fermented camel milk. At 1 mg/mL, the sample effectively suppressed cytokine production, with the most pronounced reduction observed in IL-6 levels (Fig. 7B–D), demonstrating strong anti-inflammatory activity at this concentration.

Furthermore, Annexin V/PI staining revealed that LPS stimulation increased the late apoptotic cell population to 40.5%, confirming high apoptotic stress. Co-treatment with fermented camel milk reduced the late apoptotic population to 14.4% (Fig. 7E), indicating its protective effects against LPS-induced apoptosis.

The antioxidant potential of the samples was evaluated by measuring intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS). LPS exposure alone increased ROS levels by 44%, reflecting significant oxidative stress. Treatment with M9 + WBS2A-fermented camel milk reduced ROS accumulation by 12% (Fig. 7F), demonstrating strong antioxidant activity and the ability to mitigate oxidative damage.

Molecular docking

The molecular docking analysis provided insights into the potential interactions between the identified peptides and target enzymes, indicating favorable binding affinities and possible interaction sites. These findings support the in vitro enzyme inhibitory results observed in this study. However, it is important to note that docking analysis is based on in silico predictions and does not confirm actual biological activity. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as indicative of potential interactions, and further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to validate the functional efficacy of these peptides.

This study assessed the ability of bioactive peptide sequences derived from camel milk to bind to four essential metabolic enzymes. The web server HADDOCK 2.4 was used to perform the molecular docking study. Based on the docking data, the chosen peptide mostly used hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions to form stable complexes with the active sites of all target proteins.

Complexes of peptide MMSLVSLLLVGILFPTIQAKQFTK exhibited HADDOCK scores of –113.3 ± 3.3, –107.1 ± 3.7, –102.6 ± 3.8, and –3.3 ± 8.5 against human bile salt-activated lipase, human pancreatic alpha-amylase (hPAM), hMGA, and hACE, respectively. Peptide displayed the most promising docking scores against hPAM, indicating the highest binding affinity. A comprehensive representation of the binding interactions between this peptide and the target enzymes is shown in Fig. 8. Discussion

The results for evaluation of activity of free radical scavenging (%), are in line with Patel et al. (2023) found that camel milk fermented with Lactobacillus and yeast exhibited a considerable increase in ABTS radical scavenging activity during the course of the fermentation process. Initially, the ABTS activity was 4.33%, which increased to 65.28% after 48 h. Similarly, fermentation of camel milk with the KGL4 strain for 48 h at 37 °C resulted in maximum ABTS activity of 64.03% (Shori and Baba 2011). These reports support our data, indicating that extended fermentation enhances the antioxidant potential of camel milk.

In the present study, fermented camel milk exhibited stronger inhibitory effects on α-glucosidase and α-amylase than fermented cow's milk. This discrepancy could be explained by differences in the types and amounts of bioactive peptides generated during fermentation. Notably, enzyme inhibition increased progressively with longer incubation periods, suggesting that extended fermentation enhances the production of peptides with antidiabetic potential (Ayyash et al. 2018).

In fermented buffalo milk that has undergone fermentation, enzyme inhibition varied from 33.09% to 59.95% for α-glucosidase, 57.09% to 79.14% for α-amylase, and 40.75% to 63.71% for lipase. Fermented camel milk demonstrated superior efficacy, with inhibitory activities ranging from 46.27–64.55% (α-glucosidase), 61.43–81.66% (α-amylase), and 50.15–69.83% (lipase). After 48 h of incubation at 37 °C, camel milk achieved the highest recorded enzyme inhibition values across all parameters: 64.55%, 81.66%, and 69.83% for α-glucosidase, α-amylase, & lipase, respectively, outperforming milk from buffalo under the same conditions.

For assessment of antidiabetic activities, the similar trends have been reported by Shukla (2021), who assessed the antidiabetic potential of bovine and camel milk through the inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase following fermentation with Lactobacillus acidophilus DSM and Lactococcus lactis K782. Their results showed that camel milk fermented with L. lactis K782 exhibited significantly increased inhibition of α-amylase (> 40%) compared to cow's milk, with inhibition levels increasing notably over extended storage periods (P ≤ 0.05). However, bovine milk did not exhibit any notable changes. Additionally, α-glucosidase inhibition was more pronounced in both milks fermented with L. lactis K782 (30–40%) than in those fermented with L. acidophilus DSM (< 30%), although these values remained relatively stable throughout storage. The study also reported a positive correlation between α-amylase inhibition and peptide concentration (OPA values), supporting the role of bioactive peptides in mediating enzyme inhibition in vitro. These observations corroborate our findings, where enzyme inhibition increased consistently with fermentation time, highlighting the potential of microbial fermentation to enhance the antidiabetic properties of milk via the generation of functional peptides.

As reported by Khakhriya et al. (2023b), Saccharomyces cerevisiae (WBS2A) and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (M11) fermented buffalo and camel milk exhibited significant antidiabetic properties, particularly through the inhibition of key digestive enzymes (α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and lipase). These results align with the current study's findings, which also demonstrated a time-dependent increase in the antidiabetic potential of fermented camel milk. The enzyme inhibitory effects progressively intensified with longer incubation, suggesting that extended fermentation enhances the release or formation of bioactive compounds.

Maximum proteolysis (6.40 mg/mL) occurred after 48 h at the highest inoculum level, indicating that both prolonged fermentation and higher inoculum rates enhanced proteolytic activity (Patel et al. 2023). These results are consistent with our observations, showing that proteolytic activity progressively increased with longer incubation and higher inoculum concentrations, peaking at 48 h with a 2.5% inoculum.

This contrast suggests that the microbial strains used during fermentation exhibited substantial proteolytic activity, leading to extensive protein hydrolysis in the fermented product. Specifically, unfermented camel milk showed protein bands primarily within the 15–30 kDa range, reflecting minimal degradation, whereas FCM exhibited bands spanning 15–85 kDa, indicating partial breakdown of milk proteins due to enzymatic activity. Notably, the permeate fractions did not show any discernible protein bands..Our findings for SDS-PAGE analysis are consistent with those of previous studies. Patel et al. (2023) reported that SDS-PAGE analysis of CM fermented using Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus revealed clear changes in the protein profile. Camels' milk without fermentation displayed bands of proteins between 10 and 75 kDa, representing intact proteins, while fermented samples exhibited bands within the same range but with reduced intensity and diversity, indicating partial protein degradation. The permeate samples lacked detectable bands, suggesting that larger proteins were hydrolyzed into smaller peptides and removed during the filtration process. Similarly, Mine (2007) analysed WSEs from CM fermented with various strains, including M11, KGL3A, and KGL4. Unfermented CM exhibited the highest number of protein bands (approximately 10–130 kDa), indicating limited proteolysis, whereas fermented samples showed a narrower band range (10–100 kDa), and reflecting substantial protein hydrolysis by lactic acid bacteria. Interestingly, no protein bands were observed in the permeates of KGL3A and M11, implying complete hydrolysis and filtration, whereas the KGL4 permeate still displayed visible bands.

Consistent with these observations, Khakhariya et al. (2023a) and Pipaliya et al. (2024) conducted comprehensive 2D-PAGE profiling of fermented buffalo and camel milk samples. A total of 86 protein spots were detected in all fermented samples. Buffalo milk fermented with M11 + WBS2A exhibited 26 protein spots, whereas fermentation with KGL4 + WBS2A resulted in 15 spots. For camel milk, 25 spots were observed in samples fermented with M11 + WBS2A and 20 spots with KGL4 + WBS2A. These findings highlight strain-specific proteolytic patterns and suggest the generation of a diverse array of peptides during the fermentation in buffalo and camel milk.

The arrangement KILDKEGIDYWLAHK, determined by fermenting camel milk with M9 + WBS2A, showed similarity to the peptides AHK (Chen et al. 1996) and AH (Amigo et al. 2020). The peptide EYGLFQINNK matched the reported peptide YGLF (Anna et al. 2016), whereas MMSLVSLLLVGILFPTIQAKQFTK aligned with MM (Suetsuna et al. 2000). Similarly, the peptide DILKEDMPSQRYLEELHR matched multiple sequences, including LH (Amigo et al. 2020), EL (Saito et al. 2003), LHR (Huang et al. 2010), LK (Yang et al. 2018), RY and RYL (Anna et al. 2016), and YL (Nongonierma et al. 2013), as listed in the BIOPEP database. These peptide alignments support the antioxidant potential of the sequences identified in this study.

The sequence EYGLFQINNK, found in camel milk fermented with M9 + WBS2A, also showed similarity to several antidiabetic peptide fragments listed in the BIOPEP database, including GL (Hatanaka et al. 2012), EY, FQ, IN, NN, and QI (Bella et al. 1982).Similarly, the peptide KILDKEGIDYWLAHK matched reported sequences such as LA (Nongonierma et al. 2013), WL (Hatanaka et al. 2012), AH, EG, and GI (Bella et al. 1982). These alignments suggest that M9 + WBS2A-fermented camel milk contains peptides with potential antidiabetic activity.

According to Sah et al. (2014) and Patel et al. (2023), camel milk fermented and subsequently subjected to ultrafiltration (3 and 10 kDa membranes) exhibited consistently high antioxidant activity across both retentate and permeate fractions, which was significantly influenced by the microbial strains used. The 10 kDa retentate of KGL4 + WBS2A-fermented camel milk showed the highest ABTS free radical scavenging activity (69.31%), followed by M9 + WBS2A fermentation (63.56%). Similarly, in the 10 kDa permeates, KGL4 + WBS2A- and M9 + WBS2A-fermented camel milk showed notable antioxidant activities of 62.48% and 55.62%, respectively. This trend continued in the 3 kDa fractions, where KGL4 + WBS2A fermented the camel milk's retentate and permeate recorded 69.05% and 60.94%, respectively, while those fermented with M9 + WBS2A exhibited 62.72% and 56.68%.

Camel milk fermented using the KGL4 + WBS2A consortium consistently manifested the most pronounced α-amylase inhibition across all molecular weight fractions examined. Notably, in the 3 kDa permeate fraction, this strain combination exhibited a peak inhibition of 85.06%, representing the highest inhibition among all samples evaluated. The 3 kDa retentate similarly demonstrated robust inhibition efficacy, with camel milk fermented by KGL4 + WBS2A achieving 78.90% α-amylase suppression. Within the 10 kDa retentate fraction, this microbial consortium exhibited superior performance, registering substantial inhibition of 78.23%. Furthermore, the 10 kDa permeate fraction exhibited potent enzymatic inhibition, with KGL4 + WBS2A-fermented camel milk achieving 82.45% inhibition (Pipaliya et al. 2024).

According to Khakhriya et al. (2023b), the FTIR spectra of both camel and buffalo milk displayed numerous characteristic peaks within the 400–2000 cm⁻1 range. Fermentation enhanced the intensity of peaks, particularly in the 1800–400 cm⁻1 region. In fermented buffalo milk, notable absorption bands were observed at 516.75 cm⁻1, 822.25 cm⁻1, 1036.88 cm⁻1, 1120.08 cm⁻1, and 1628.67 cm⁻1. Similarly, FCM exhibited distinct peaks at 512.80 cm⁻1, 1234.85 cm⁻1, 1541.59 cm⁻1, and 1628.81 cm⁻1, reflecting the molecular changes. Sah et al. (2014) reported that FTIR spectral analysis of Gir cow milk and fermented camel milk revealed multiple characteristic peaks within the 500–4000 cm⁻1 range. Both fermented samples showed intensified spectral features between 1700 and 500 cm⁻1. Fermented camel milk exhibited distinct absorption bands at 811 cm⁻1, 850 cm⁻1, 1697 cm⁻1, 1568 cm⁻1, and 3035 cm⁻1 (Fig. 3m and 3n), whereas fermented Gir cow milk presented unique peaks at 820 cm⁻1, 1100 cm⁻1, 1293 cm⁻1, and 1492 cm⁻1, indicating differences in protein and lipid interactions during fermentation.

Morris et al. (2008) used CLSM to investigate the impact of fermentation on sheep milk. In unfermented sheep milk, proteins maintained their native structure, appearing as larger elements, whereas fermentation led to their breakdown into peptides and the formation of more aggregated and complex protein structures. This impact was ascribed to the KGL4 Lactobacillus strain's remarkable proteolytic ability. The permeate and retentate fractions treated with 3 and 10 kDa membranes were also investigated in this study. The presence of low-molecular-weight peptides following transit across the 3 kDa membrane was indicated by the notably smaller protein entities found in the 3 kDa permeate compared to the 10 kDa permeate, 10 kDa retentate, and 3 kDa retentate fractions. There was no discernible difference in the protein fractions with molecular weights over 3 kDa but below 10 kDa among the examined samples.

Molecular docking is a fundamental computational method used in structure-based drug design to predict ligand–receptor interactions at the molecular level. Predicting a ligand's ideal orientation when attached to a particular protein receptor is known as molecular docking, which analyzes their interactions using an energy-based scoring function. It is commonly used to evaluate the potential biological activity of bioactive molecules, including peptides. Molecular docking not only elucidates the binding conformation of peptides within active sites but also provides information about their mode of action and aids in the development of peptide-based therapeutics (Singh et al. 2015)..Analysis of the docked complexes indicated that the major forces stabilizing peptide–protein interactions were hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic contacts. The key hydrogen-bonded residues at the interaction interfaces are listed in Table 5. Previous studies have also demonstrated that hydrophobic interactions significantly contribute to the stabilization and enhancement of peptide–protein complex affinity, particularly for short-chain peptides interacting with metabolic enzymes (Panchal et al. 2020). Moreover, the molecular docking results correlated with the in vitro enzyme inhibition assays conducted in this study, thereby validating the docking predictions. These findings indicate that peptides isolated from camel milk are promising candidates for functional foods or therapeutic applications.

Overall, the results strongly suggest that camel milk fermented with selected probiotic strains possesses notable anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and cytoprotective properties. These beneficial effects are evident through its ability to suppress proinflammatory cytokine expression, reduce oxidative stress, and prevent apoptosis. These biofunctional properties highlight the potential of fermented camel milk as an intervention to mitigate inflammation-induced cellular damage.

In addition to the approaches used in this study, advanced omics technologies such as proteomics, peptidomics, and metabolomics can provide a more comprehensive understanding of bioactive peptide generation and functional mechanisms during fermentation. These techniques enable detailed identification and quantification of peptides and metabolites, as well as insights into their interactions and pathways. Furthermore, computational approaches, including molecular docking and related in silico tools, can complement experimental findings by predicting peptide–target interactions and supporting structure–function analysis. The integration of such omics and computational strategies may further enhance the understanding and development of functional fermented dairy products.

This study is primarily based on in vitro assays, which may not fully reflect the complex physiological conditions of the human body. The stability, bioavailability, and metabolic fate of the identified peptides can be significantly influenced by gastrointestinal digestion and absorption processes. Therefore, the observed bioactivities, including antioxidative and antidiabetic effects, should be interpreted as indicative of potential rather than confirmed therapeutic outcomes. Further studies involving simulated gastrointestinal digestion, in vivo models, and clinical validation are necessary to substantiate the efficacy and safety of these bioactive peptides.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated the biofunctional efficacy of fermented camel milk with Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus M9 and Saccharomyces cerevisiae WBS2A, highlighting its antidiabetic, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities. The fermented milk exhibited substantial inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and marked suppression of ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. SDS-PAGE and 2D electrophoresis confirmed proteolytic activity, revealing the generation of smaller peptide fragments responsible for these activities. These results highlight the potential of fermented camel milk as a source of health-promoting proteins. Nevertheless, further clinical in vivo research is required to confirm its therapeutic potential and support its application in nutraceuticals and dietary interventions.
