Pediatric submandibular gland tuberculosis: a case report
Authors: Diwan Israr Khan, Sana Jameel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis is a common systemic bacterial infectious disease usually caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. In India, there are an estimated 2.1 million cases of tuberculosis, with 16% of them being new extrapulmonary tuberculosis cases. Extrapulmonary tuberculosis may present in concurrence with a focus in the lungs or may be present primarily without pulmonary involvement. While primary submandibular tuberculosis is rare, it is even rarer in the pediatric age group. Diagnosis of this disease can be challenging due to nonspecific symptoms. Fine-needle aspiration cytology is helpful in diagnosis, but often an excisional biopsy is required.Case presentationWe present a case of a 9-year-old female patient from Aligarh, Uttar Pradesh (India) who presented with swelling in the left submandibular region without pain for the last 25 days. After diagnosis, it was found to be primary tuberculosis of the submandibular gland. The patient did not have any history of fever, cough, weight loss, loss of appetite, or other systemic symptoms but she had a family history of tuberculosis 4 years ago. On local examination, a nontender, soft-to-firm swelling of approximately 2.5 cm × 2.0 cm was detected in the submandibular region. The diagnosis was confirmed by fine-needle aspiration cytology, which revealed granulomas most likely of tubercular etiology.DiscussionTubercular involvement of salivary glands is uncommon, and primary involvement of the submandibular gland in the pediatric age group is extremely very rare. The disease typically manifests as a diffuse swelling in the submandibular region with or without systemic manifestations. Diagnosis is based on fine-needle aspiration cytology, polymerase chain reaction, or histopathological examination of the biopsied material, which reveals the presence of granulomas with or without caseous necrosis. Antituberculous treatment remains the mainstay of treatment.ConclusionThis study unveils the rare instances of tuberculosis impacting the submandibular gland among children, underscoring the importance of increased clinical vigilance. By thorough examination of clinical cases, diagnostic approaches, and treatment strategies, this study provides a valuable perspective on the unique presentation of submandibular gland tuberculosis, ultimately enhancing diagnostic accuracy and patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13256-025-05621-4.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic infectious granulomatous disease caused by air-borne bacillus Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and less frequently by other bacterium in the M. tuberculosis complex [1, 2]. It is characterized by fever, weight loss, and palmar and night sweats. The lung is the predominant site of TB. Extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) is an uncommon form of chronic infection that does not show the typical signs and symptoms of pulmonary tuberculosis. EPTB usually affects highly vascular areas such as lymph nodes, pleura, genitourinary tract, gastrointestinal system, central nervous system, bones, joints, meningitis, skin, and oral cavity. In India, it is estimated that there are 2.1 million cases of TB, of which 16% are new EPTB cases [3]. Risk factors typically include underlying conditions and pathologies that compromise the host’s immune system such as acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), leukemia, chemotherapeutic agents, prolonged steroid therapy, nutritional deficiencies, and chronic illnesses [4, 5]. EPTB usually presents itself insidiously and subtly, with a slow evolution of paucibacillary lesions or fluids. It is not always possible to access the lesions through secretions and body fluids; therefore, invasive techniques may be needed to obtain material for diagnostic investigation [6]. Maxillofacial manifestations of tuberculosis make up almost 10% of all extrapulmonary manifestations of the disease [7]. It has been reported that 60% of all cases of jaw TB occur in children under the age of 16 years. Secondary orofacial TB arises after the tuberculous infection spreads from another site through the lymphatic and hematogenous routes. Tubercular infection of major salivary glands is a rare condition and involvement of the submandibular gland with tuberculosis is even rarer. Only a few cases of submandibular salivary gland tuberculosis have been reported in the literature [8, 9]. Submandibular gland tuberculosis possibly spreads through hematogenous, lymphatic dissemination, direct spread through tuberculous lesions as in the oral cavity, and superinfection of a malignant lesion [8]. A presumptive diagnosis of tuberculous sialadenitis may be established when there is supporting evidence of tuberculosis present in other areas of the body [10]. However, in situations where no lesion can be seen elsewhere, the diagnosis is typically made via tissue culture, aspiration cytology, salivary culture, or histopathology. The material can be subjected to acid fast bacilli (AFB) staining [11]. Excisional biopsy is often required despite the usefulness of fine-needle aspiration cytology. Polymerase chain reaction for Mycobacterium tuberculosis is a reliable tool and should be performed before surgical intervention [4]. There are currently no established guidelines for the management of submandibular gland TB due to the infrequency of the disease. However, once a proper diagnosis has been established, it can be effectively treated with standard short-course antitubercular treatment (ATT). Surgery is recommended in cases of nonresponsive disease or when complications arise [8, 9, 12].

Primary tuberculosis of the submandibular gland is a rare occurrence in pediatric patients.

Here we report a case of such an event.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old female patient from Aligarh, Uttar Pradesh (India) presented with a swelling in the left submandibular region. The swelling had been there for the last 25 days but did not cause any pain. The onset of swelling was subacute. There was no history of fever, cough, weight loss, or other systemic symptoms. There were no symptoms suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis and she was in good health. The patient was immunocompetent but had a family history of tuberculosis. Her father was treated for pulmonary tuberculosis 4 years ago. She also had a history of tooth operation 5 years ago. No further treatment history was there. Her overall physical examination revealed no notable findings. Local examination detected a diffuse, nontender, soft-to-firm, bi-digitally palpable swelling of approximately 2.5 cm × 2.0 cm in the submandibular region. The skin overlying the swelling looked normal (Fig. 1). The rest of the oral cavity and oropharyngeal examination was unremarkable. The cervical lymph nodes were not palpable. On investigation, complete blood count was found to be normal except for an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 32 mm/hour. The general blood picture was also normal with normocytic normochromic, red blood cell (RBC), and white blood cell (WBC) counts within normal limits; adequate platelet count; and no immature cells abnormal cells/hemoparasite seen. Additionally, the random blood sugar, blood urea, serum creatinine, and liver function tests including the liver enzymes and serum electrolytes were all within normal limits. Her Mantoux test was positive with an induration of 14 × 14 mm. Chest X-ray did not reveal any pulmonary lesions. Ultrasonography (USG) abdomen findings were suggestive of a normal scan. Moreover, sputum for acid fast bacilli under Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative. The swelling was examined using fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC), which revealed numerous scattered well-defined discrete and a few ill-formed granulomas composed of epithelioid cells, a fair number of foreign body giant cells, and occasional Langhans giant cells surrounded by lymphocytes in the background of the polymorphous population of lymphoid cells consisting mainly small lymphocytes, a few scattered centrocytes, and centroblasts admixed with RBCs. These features were suggestive of granulomatous lymphadenitis, most likely of tubercular origin (Fig. 2).Fig. 2FNAC report suggestive of granulomatous lymphadenitis suggestive of TB

On the basis of these findings, the patient was diagnosed with tuberculosis of the submandibular gland and started on antitubercular treatment.

Discussion

Tuberculosis continues to be a major health concern. Approximately 85% of reported cases involve the lungs and the remaining cases are extrapulmonary. One such very rare site is the submandibular gland, which is affected by tuberculosis. Chaudhary et al. (2022) did a retrospective cohort study and reported that nine cases involved the major salivary glands: the parotid (n = 5), submandibular (n = 3), and sublingual (n = 1) with tuberculous involvement. Eight instances had painless swelling, the most prevalent local symptom, and every case had constitutional symptoms [13]. This condition, known as tuberculous sialadenitis, can occur both as a primary infection or in conjunction with pulmonary TB. In our patient, it occurred primarily in the absence of pulmonary TB. Although the condition is absolutely rare, primary tuberculosis of the submandibular gland should be considered as a possibility in areas where TB is common [14]. Patients with certain risk factors, such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, prolonged use of steroids, and diabetes mellitus, are associated with an increasing incidence of submandibular gland tuberculosis [7, 9]. Nutritional deficiencies, chronic illness associated with bacterial infections, poor oral hygiene, dental infection, and trauma to the gland are other predisposing factors [7, 10]. Lymphatic dissemination is considered the most common route of primary submandibular gland TB. This is because the lymphatic drainage from the oral cavity mainly occurs in the submandibular lymph nodes [8]. The most common presenting symptom is a slowly growing painless mass, with which our patient was presented. It was stated by Tauro et al. (2011) that the diagnosis of salivary gland tuberculosis requires a very high index of suspicion and could be a dilemma due to nonspecific wide range of presentation and its rarity. The wide range of presentation can be acute inflammatory lesion as suppurative sialadenitis or a chronic asymptomatic mass [11]. While laboratory abnormalities such as low hemoglobin level, mildly raised total leucocyte count, elevated ESR, and positive Mantoux test may be present in a patient with submandibular gland TB, there is no specific laboratory test to diagnose this condition per se. Differential diagnosis includes sialadenitis, submandibular lymphadenitis, dental abscess, and submandibular gland tumor [15]. Histopathological examination of the specimen is essential for differentiating it from malignancy. Observing caseous necrosis and/or granuloma in the aspiration cytology or histological examination of the biopsied material is helpful for diagnosis [16–18]. The disease in our patient was confirmed by FNAC clues compatible with TB. In a review on submandibular gland tuberculosis done by Chaudary et al., it was concluded that FNAC is diagnostic, and to improve the diagnostic yield in suspected case FNAC in conjunction with culture and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay can be used [8]. The majority of the submandibular gland TB cases are resolved with antituberculous treatment and surgery should be considered only for the lesions that do not respond well to ATT [18, 19].

This study contributes valuable insights into the rare occurrence of primary tuberculosis of the submandibular gland, particularly in pediatric patients. By documenting a well-characterized case and reviewing relevant literature, this report enhances awareness among clinicians regarding an often-overlooked differential diagnosis. Additionally, the use of FNAC as a diagnostic tool is highlighted, reinforcing its role in detecting granulomatous inflammation suggestive of tuberculosis. However, there are certain limitations to consider. The absence of a biopsy and histopathological examination prevents a more definitive confirmation of the diagnosis. Furthermore, molecular diagnostic methods such as PCR and culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis were not performed, which could have improved diagnostic accuracy. Another limitation is the lack of long-term follow-up data to assess treatment response and potential recurrence.

Conclusion

The pediatric patient in this case was presented with a subacute response. However, the source of the infection was unclear. We could not find any signs of tuberculosis in other parts of the body, so we made the diagnosis of primary submandibular gland tuberculosis on the basis of the findings of fine-needle aspiration cytology. This study elucidates the infrequent occurrence of tuberculosis affecting the submandibular gland in the pediatric population, highlighting the need for heightened clinical awareness. Through a comprehensive analysis of clinical cases, diagnostic modalities, and therapeutic interventions, this research contributes valuable insights into the distinctive manifestation of tuberculosis in this anatomical site, ultimately aiding in improved diagnostic precision and patient management.
