Impact of structural reorganization induced by buckwheat flour processing on the eating quality and starch digestibility of gluten-free noodles
Authors: Xiao-Miao Han, Zao-Zao Xie, Yang-Feng Jiang, Si-Yi Liu, Qian-Qian Yu, Su-Xi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates how flour processing-induced structural reorganization affects gluten-free noodle quality using four buckwheat flours (Endosperm flour (TBE), Whole flour (WTB), Bran flour (TBB), Ultrafine flour (WTBU)). Results indicated that conventional milling (TBE) preserved starch agglomeration and crystallinity (28%–32%), endowing flour with superior hydration and thermal stability. Conversely, ultrafine milling (WTBU) fragmented particles and disrupted crystal lattices (crystallinity reduced to 12.61%), significantly lowering thermal stability. Specifically, TBE noodles formed a dense network with highly bound water, yielding optimal hardness and sensory scores (91.6). Conversely, WTBU noodles suffered structural collapse due to excessive dissolution (15.12%). Moreover, the dense TBE-N matrix imposed steric hindrance to digestive enzymes, maximizing resistant starch (67%), whereas structural disruption in WTBU Noodles caused rapidly digestible starch to surge to 32%. Ultimately, maintaining crystalline integrity and thermal stability is essential for the synergistic optimization of both ‘texture enhancement’ and ‘digestion retardation’ in gluten-free noodles.Keywords: Tartary buckwheat, Gluten-free noodles, Crystalline integrity, Hydrothermal stability

Introduction

Tartary buckwheat (Fagopyrum tataricum), belonging to the genus Fagopyrum within the Polygonaceae family, is a dicotyledonous annual plant widely cultivated in alpine regions. Its seeds are rich in dietary fiber, protein, minerals, and various bioactive compounds (such as flavonoids and polyphenols), offering potential health benefits, including antioxidant properties and blood sugar regulation support (Zou, Wu, Ren, Hu, Peng, Zhao, et al., 2023). Driven by growing health awareness, Tartary buckwheat is transforming from a “functional grain” into a staple food: initially used as raw material for buckwheat tea, its application expanded with the development of buckwheat flour (Gao, Hou, Cang, Sangdan, Zhao, Yuan, et al., 2024). Buckwheat has been widely incorporated into various flour-based foods such as noodles, steamed buns, and baked products, becoming an important component of daily dietary consumption (Zhou et al., 2025).

Industrial production of gluten-free staple foods hinges on the milling process's precise separation of raw material characteristics to determine product quality. Based on different diversion paths (flour streams), Tartary buckwheat flour can be refined into endosperm flour (TBE), bran flour (TBB), and whole grain flour (WTB): TBE is characterized by high starch content, providing a fine texture and serving as the foundational framework for noodle physical structure; TBB integrates higher-density dietary fiber and antioxidant components, imparting physiological activity while exhibiting strong water absorption; WTB enhances nutritional density while preserving the balanced nutrition of whole grains (Zou et al., 2023).

However, the natural absence of gluten proteins in Tartary buckwheat often leads to structural looseness and limited cooking performance in gluten-free systems. To address this, ultrafine grinding technology was introduced as a physical intervention method. This process aims to enhance particle surface energy through micron-level processing, thereby optimizing the uniform dispersion of flour particles within the dough matrix. Research indicates that microscopic alterations in particle size profoundly drive water migration within dough, subsequently influencing noodle rehydration kinetics and structural integrity (Xu, Huang, Yang, Wang, Xing, Liu et al., 2021). Furthermore, differences in flour particles induced by various processing methods are directly linked to the in vitro starch digestibility and metabolic responses of the final product (Zhu, 2016; Zou et al., 2023). Therefore, systematically investigating the effects of different flour blend compositions and ultrafine processing on the quality of gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles is crucial for developing high-quality, low-glycemic-index healthy staple foods.

In the production of gluten-free noodles, the physicochemical properties of buckwheat flour exert a decisive influence on the quality of the final product. Its heterogeneity in aspects such as particle size, water absorption characteristics, and dietary fiber content directly impacts the noodles' textural properties, cooking quality, and in vitro digestion behavior (Li, Bu, Ding, & Hu, 2024). Research indicates that the composition of buckwheat flour and its processing methods can significantly alter key noodle quality indicators, including hardness, cooking loss, and starch digestion (Sofi, Ahmed, Farooq, Rafiq, Zargar, Kamran, et al., 2023; J. Wang, Li, Wang, Wang, Hong, Liu, et al., 2025). However, most existing literature focuses on whole buckwheat flour applications, with limited systematic comparisons of how different “flour fractions” (e.g., endosperm flour, bran flour, and other fractionated extracts) influence the functional properties of gluten-free noodles (Zou et al., 2023). Since different flour stream components specifically interfere with water migration within dough, these variations in water distribution further affect the efficiency of amylase access to starch granules, significantly altering the in vitro starch digestibility of the product. Therefore, investigating the effects of different flour stream sources on water migration, cooking quality, and digestive properties in gluten-free noodles not only aids in optimizing the precise utilization and production processes of buckwheat flour but also provides crucial theoretical support and practical guidance for developing gluten-free staple foods that more effectively fulfill health requirements.

Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the critical trade-off between structural disruption and functional properties during flour processing. By simultaneously comparing TBE, TBB, WTB, and WTBU, this work seeks to distinguish the respective contributions of flour composition and processing-induced structural reorganization, and to clarify how these two factors jointly influence the texture and digestion properties of gluten-free noodles. To this end, four buckwheat flour types with distinct compositional characteristics and processing histories were systematically evaluated in terms of microstructural evolution, crystallinity, and hydration behavior. These structural and physicochemical properties were further correlated with noodle quality, particularly water migration behavior (LF-NMR), eating quality, and starch digestibility. The findings are expected to deepen the understanding of structure-function relationships during buckwheat flour processing and provide practical guidance for the development of gluten-free noodles with improved sensory attributes and health benefits.

Materials and methods

Materials

Tartary Buckwheat Endosperm Flour (TBE, moisture content 13.95 ± 0.07%, crude protein 5.86 ± 0.10%, crude fat 0.90 ± 0.31%), Tartary buckwheat bran Flour (TBB, moisture content 12.60 ± 0.07%, crude protein 19.35 ± 0.11%, crude fat 2.07 ± 0.42%), Whole Tartary Buckwheat Flour (WTB, moisture content 13.52 ± 0.20%, crude protein 11.35 ± 0.01%, crude fat 1.13 ± 0.4%) and Whole Tartary Buckwheat Ultrafine Flour (WTBU, moisture content 9.50 ± 0.18%, crude protein 43.54 ± 0.1%, crude fat 7.78 ± 0.38%) were obtained from Huan Tai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., ChengDu, China. Extruded corn starch was prepared from commercially sourced native corn starch (purchased from Wuhan Xinfengshuo Trading Co., Ltd., moisture content 12.49 ± 0.05%, crude protein content 0.33 ± 0.04%, crude fat content 0.40 ± 0.20%) using an extrusion process. The specific preparation process for extruded corn starch is detailed in Supplemental 1. Potassium chloride, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; Hydrochloric acid, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.; Salivary amylase (Longkete Enzyme Preparations Co., Ltd.); Pepsin (≥400 units/mg solid), Lipase (30000u/g, Porcine Pancreatic), Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Pancreatic Enzyme (100000u/g), Shanghai Ruiyong Biotechnology Co., Ltd.; Bile Salts (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.); Glucose Assay Kit, Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Biological Engineering. It should be noted that the crude protein content in this study was determined by the Kjeldahl method, which estimates protein content based on total nitrogen. Therefore, the relatively high crude protein content of WTBU may be partly associated with the protein-rich nature of Tartary buckwheat bran, physical enrichment during superfine grinding/fractionation, and the possible contribution of non-protein nitrogen.

Structural characterization and physicochemical properties of processed flours

Microstructure analysis (SEM)

The microscopic structure of the different Tartary buckwheat flours was observed using SEM. The flour was adhered to a specially designed adhesive, gold-coated through ion sputtering, and then placed on the sample stage for observation at an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. The interface was observed at a magnification of 600 × .

Analysis of the particle size distribution (PSD)

The particle size characteristics of Tartary buckwheat flour were determined using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (NanoBrook 90Plus Zeta, Brookhaven Instruments, USA). The sample was suspended in degassed distilled water and subjected to ultrasonic dispersion for 5 min to ensure complete deagglomeration of particles. Based on the principle of dynamic light scattering (DLS), the light signals were processed using Mie scattering theory via the accompanying software to obtain the differential distribution curve G(d) and cumulative distribution curve C(d) of the samples. The D10, D50, and D90 values were recorded to evaluate the impact of processing techniques on the particle fineness and uniformity of buckwheat flour.

Hydration properties

A 0.1 g sample was accurately weighed and mixed with 4 mL of distilled water. The suspension was incubated under continuous shaking at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by incubation at 95 °C for another 30 min. After cooling to room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 4000 ×g for 20 min. The supernatant was transferred to a pre-weighed aluminum dish and dried in an oven at 105 °C until constant weight was attained. X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

The crystalline structure of the buckwheat flour was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an instrument equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (D8 ADVANCE X-ray Diffractometer, Bruker Corporation, Germany). The diffraction patterns were acquired under the following operational conditions: a voltage of 30 kV, a current of 10 mA, a scanning speed of 3°/min over a 2θ range from 4° to 40°, with a step size of 0.02°. The relative crystallinity was determined by first isolating the areas corresponding to the crystalline peaks from the amorphous halo in the XRD pattern, and then integrating these areas for calculation.

Thermo gravimetric analysis (TGA)

Precisely weigh 8 mg of the sample into an alumina crucible, with an empty crucible serving as the blank. Under an air atmosphere, heat the sample at a rate of 10 °C/min from 30 °C to 600 °C to obtain the thermogravimetric-differential thermogravimetric (TG-DTG) curve.

Preparation of gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles

The blended flour was formulated by mixing Tartary buckwheat flour (including TBE, TBB, WTB, and WTBU varieties) with extruded corn starch at a mass ratio of 65:35(w/w). This ratio was selected based on preliminary experiments and previous studies, as it provided suitable dough handling properties, cooking quality, and noodle texture for gluten-free noodle preparation.The mixed flour was then combined with distilled water (37% w/w, based on flour weight) and homogenized in a dough mixer for 4 min. The resulting dough was sealed in a plastic bag and proofed at 30 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the dough was repeatedly passed through a noodle machine to form a uniform sheet with a final thickness of 1.0 mm. Finally, the sheet was cut into noodles with dimensions of 2 mm in width and 20 cm in length. Gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles made from four blended flours labeled as TBE-N, TBB-N, WTB-N, and WTBU-N, respectively.

Sensory evaluation of gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles

Sensory evaluation was conducted in accordance with GB/T 35875–2018, with evaluation methods adapted to the color and flavor characteristics of coarse-grain noodles (see Supplementary Table 2 for details). A panel of 10 trained evaluators systematically assessed seven sensory attributes of cooked noodles.

Textural properties of gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles

The noodle samples were cooked to optimal doneness, immediately removed, and placed in cold water for 10 s to cool, then blot excess moisture from the surfaces using filter paper. Place two parallel noodle strands on the test platform for textural analysis using the P/36 R-type probe. Parameter settings: Initial gap 10 mm; pre-test, test, and post-test speeds of 1 mm/s, 1 mm/s, and 1 mm/s, respectively; compression level 75%; 10-s interval between compression cycles; trigger value 5 g.

Water migration in gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles

Cut 5 g of cooked noodles into small pieces after draining surface moisture, then place them in a 10 mL clear glass jar. The bottle mouth is then sealed with a sealing film, and the transverse relaxation time is determined using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) technology. Experimental parameters were set as follows: number of sampling points (TD) = 60,016, sampling frequency (SW) = 200 kHz, sampling interval time (TW) = 1000 ms, number of accumulations (NS) = 6, number of echoes (NECH) = 1200. CPMG decay curves were analyzed using MultiExpInv software with 107 iterations. The relaxation time and relative content of the noodles' moisture state were extracted from the curves.

In vitro digestibility characteristics of gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles

(1) Sample Preparation

Cool noodles cooked to optimal steaming time to room temperature, freeze-dry in a freeze dryer for 48 h, then grind in a pulverizer and sieve for later use.

(2) Total starch (TS)

Total starch (TS) content was determined using a total starch assay kit (Megazyme, K-TSTA-100 A) according to the manufacturer's instructions and AOAC Method 996.11. Briefly, the sample was treated with 0.2 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0), followed by enzymatic hydrolysis with thermostable α-amylase and amyloglucosidase at 50 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the glucose concentration was measured using the GOPOD method. The TS content was calculated from the released glucose and expressed on a dry-weight basis.

(3) Simulated Digestion

Digestibility was measured based on Brodkorb et al. (2019) with modifications. Place 1.0 g of the sample into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. Add simulated saliva, salivary α-amylase (100 U), and three glass beads to simulate oral chewing. Incubate at 37 °C at 220 r/min for 2 min. Adjust pH to 3.0 using 0.5 M HCl. Sequentially add simulated gastric juice, pepsin (2000 U), and lipase (60 U), incubate for 2 h; adjust pH to 7.0 with 0.5 M NaOH. Add bile salts (10 mM), simulated intestinal juice, and pancreatin (100 U) sequentially, and react for 2 h to simulate small intestinal digestion. At 0, 20, and 120 min, transfer 1 mL of the sample to centrifuge tubes. Mix with 3 mL of anhydrous ethanol to inactivate amylase. Then, take 1 mL of this mixture and centrifuge at 10,000 rpm for 5 min.

(4) Glucose Content Determination

Determine the released glucose content using a glucose assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Take 0.01 mL of the supernatant, add 1 mL of the working solution, incubate at 37 °C for 10 min, then measure the absorbance at 505 nm to calculate the glucose content. Data statistics and analysis

The data results were expressed as the mean and standard deviation of three repeated measurements. One-way analysis of variance was performed using SPSS Statistics 17.0. Tukey's test was used to analyze significant differences in the results, where p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Bar graphs and line charts were plotted using Origin2024 software.

Results and discussion

Impact of processing on the micromorphological restructuring and granular integrity of different Tartary buckwheat flours

Microstructure and particle size distribution of different buckwheat flours

The differences in flour types essentially involve the reshaping of particle microstructure through mechanical energy, a process strongly corroborated by the synergistic evolution of microstructure and particle size distribution (Fig. 1). Samples of TBE (Tartary buckwheat endosperm flour) and WTB (whole Tartary buckwheat flour) exhibited high morphological similarity. SEM images revealed that starch granules tended to remain agglomerated into irregular polygonal prisms due to natural binding forces (Zhu, 2016). This micro-level “particle clustering” directly resulted in significant macroscopic particle size fluctuations (Fig. 1A-1 & Fig. 1C-1). Notably, the extreme D90 of TBE (3864.88 nm) and the D50 of WTB (1554.14 nm) reflected the limitations of conventional milling in dissociating high-density starch aggregates (Fig. 1A-2 & Fig. 1C-2).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) curves (A), corresponding derivative thermogravimetry curves (B), X-ray diffraction patterns (C), and relative crystallinity (D) for the TBE, TBB, WTB, and WTBU samples. Means followed by different letters in each column are statistically different (p < 0.05).

In contrast, TBB (Tartary buckwheat bran flour) showed marked particle uniformity. Although surface indentations were visible on some particles due to physical contact (indicated by yellow arrows), the overall particle size distribution narrowed significantly. A narrow peak distribution was observed, with D10 and D90 closely approaching the median diameter (782.74 nm), indicating that TBB formed an ordered microstructure during grinding, which conferred stable physicochemical properties to the flours (Fig. 1B-1 & Fig. 1B-2). Conversely, WTBU (Whole Tartary buckwheat ultrafine flour) powder exhibited distinct “structural deconstruction” characteristics. During ultrafine processing, starch granules underwent high-frequency mechanical impact and intense shear forces, causing their originally distinct boundaries to blur and evolve into coarse, irregularly shaped fragments (Xiao, Zhang, & Zhang, 2022). Accompanied by a sharp decrease in D50 from the peak value of WTB to 782.74 nm (Fig. 1D-1 & Fig. 1D-2), this significant reduction signified not only the complete disintegration of starch aggregates but also implied physical damage to the starch crystalline regions (Zhao et al., 2020).

Hydration properties of different buckwheat flours

Fig. 1E and F illustrated significant differences in water absorption at 25 °C and 95 °C. These variations were governed by microstructural integrity and mechanical processing intensity. At 25 °C, TBE flour exhibited the highest absorption rate (3.04 g/g). This markedly outperformed WTBU (2.45 g/g). The results indicated that TBE retained the intact structure of natural starch granules, despite its larger macroscopic particle size. This ordered matrix effectively locked in moisture through physical entrapment (Yu, Chen, Ma, Sun, Yuan, Ju, et al., 2018). In contrast, ultrafine grinding increased the specific surface area of WTBU. However, excessive mechanical impact disrupted its water-holding structure. Consequently, hydration efficiency decreased at low temperatures. When heated to 95 °C, TBE's water absorption rate surged to 8.79 g/g, demonstrating that its undamaged starch aggregates could efficiently expand and form stable hydration networks at high temperatures (Yu et al., 2018; Z. Zhang, Zhu, Xing, Liang, Zou, Li, et al., 2023). Conversely, WTBU exhibited a limited increase to only 4.23 g/g. This phenomenon implied that the “structural deconstruction” and potential crystalline damage likely limited deep water penetration, preventing the damaged particles from absorbing sufficient water or expanding fully, unlike their intact counterparts (Xiao et al., 2022).

Regarding solubility, microstructural differences also determined processing stability. TBE maintained extremely low solubility (1.85%–2.31%) across the 25 °C to 95 °C range, owing to its micro-level “ordered aggregation”: intact starch granules resisted disintegration during water absorption and swelling, thereby minimizing solid loss. This characteristic is crucial, as TBE exhibited significant water absorption yet minimal dissolution, ensuring superior structural integrity during thermal processing. Conversely, WTBU exhibited dissolution rates reaching 31.07%, which was directly attributable to the “disordered fragmentation” caused by excessive particle size reduction. Large quantities of fragmented starch flakes and damaged cell wall material readily dissolve in hot water rather than gelatinizing, resulting in poor structural stability (Ahmed, Thomas, Mulla, & Brishti, 2024). In summary, although WTBU possessed a finer particle size, TBE achieved a superior balance of “high water absorption and low solubility” thanks to its intact microstructure. This conferred excellent processing tolerance, making it particularly suitable for applications demanding shape retention.

Thermal stability and crystallization characteristics of different buckwheat flours

Thermal stability properties of different buckwheat flours

Fig. 2A and B highlight the significant impact of processing methods on the thermal decomposition characteristics of the buckwheat flours. Both WTB and TBE samples exhibited superior thermal stability, following a distinct three-stage pyrolysis pattern. The majority of mass loss occurred during the main decomposition phase, accounting for 64.16%–65.69%, leaving minimal residue (6.65%–6.67%). These results suggested that mild processing methods, such as conventional grinding, helped maintain the ordered crystalline structure of the starch, thereby preserving its inherent thermal resistance (Vieira et al., 2021).In comparison, the thermal stability of the WTBU sample showed a significant decline. It completed its first pyrolysis stage prematurely at 134.7 °C, followed by a more dispersed and gradual main decomposition stage, resulting in only 14.31% mass loss. This reduction in thermal stability was primarily attributed to the “cavitation effect” induced by high-intensity physical fields. The intense micro-impacts and shear forces disrupted intermolecular hydrogen bonds, causing the crystalline structure to break down and form a loose, disordered structure. These structures were more susceptible to degradation at lower temperatures. Concurrently, the micronization process increased the sample's specific surface area, promoting carbonization reactions and resulting in residual carbon content as high as 11.06%—significantly higher than in mildly treated samples. These findings indicated that micronization not only disrupts crystalline structures but also accelerates carbonization reactions by increasing specific surface area, thereby substantially reducing thermal stability (Huang et al., 2021). Furthermore, TBB samples exhibited a distinct stable plateau phase (weight loss of only 4.9%) in the high-temperature zone, suggesting the potential formation of a heat-resistant crosslinked structure internally. In summary, the processing technique directly determines the thermal degradation pathway and ultimate stability of starch by reshaping its microstructure and molecular order.

Crystallization characteristics of different buckwheat flours

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis in Fig. 2C and D further elucidated the impact of processing intensity on the microcrystalline structure of buckwheat flour. The TBE, TBB, and WTB samples all exhibited distinct and sharp diffraction peaks, primarily located near 2θ angles of 16°, 18°, and 23°. These features are consistent with the typical crystalline profile of A-type starch (Sang et al., 2025). The relatively high crystallinity (28%–32%) indicated that the double-helix structure within these starch granules remained largely intact under conventional processing, preserving a tightly ordered molecular arrangement. In stark contrast, the diffraction pattern of the WTBU sample underwent a fundamental transformation. The originally sharp characteristic peaks weakened significantly or disappeared, replaced by a broad dispersive halo. Concurrently, the crystallinity dropped sharply to 12.61%. This phenomenon confirmed that the intense mechanical shear forces generated by ultrafine grinding overcame the intermolecular forces sustaining the crystalline lattice. High-intensity mechanical action disrupted the hydrogen bonds between starch molecular chains, causing the collapse and fragmentation of ordered “crystalline regions” into disordered “amorphous regions.” Consequently, this amorphization significantly altered the physical properties of the flour (Xiao et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2020).

Effects of different buckwheat flours on the cooking properties and water migration of gluten-free buckwheat noodles

Cooking properties of gluten-free buckwheat noodles

Fig. 3A-3C illustrated the profound impact of processing methods on the steaming characteristics of gluten-free buckwheat noodles. Statistical analysis indicated that the water absorption rate of WTBU-N was significantly lower than that of TBE-N, TBB-N, and WTB-N (P < 0.05). This phenomenon, though seemingly counterintuitive, was attributed to excessive starch damage caused by ultra-fine grinding. The damaged starch failed to form the stable gel network necessary for effective water retention, leading instead to structural collapse that diminished the actual water-holding capacity (Xiao et al., 2022). Regarding cooking loss, TBE-N exhibited optimal stability, achieving the lowest loss rate (10.33%). This was ascribed to its endosperm-dominant composition, which facilitated the formation of a dense, cohesive starch-protein gel matrix. In contrast, WTBU-N showed the highest cooking loss (15.12%), followed by TBB-N. This quality deterioration stemmed from two distinct mechanisms: First, the high dietary fiber content in TBB physically disrupted matrix continuity through steric hindrance, thereby weakening starch granule encapsulation (Liu, Shi, Wang, Ren, Ma, Li, et al., 2021). Second, the micro-fragmentation in WTBU resulted in a high specific surface area and water solubility index (WSI), which accelerated the diffusion and leaching of soluble solids into the boiling water. Collectively, these findings confirmed that the microstructural integrity and component distribution of raw materials directly dictated noodle cooking quality.

Water migration properties of gluten-free buckwheat noodles

T2 relaxation analysis effectively characterized the water binding and migration behaviors within the noodle matrices. Generally, shorter T2 values signified strong water confinement, whereas longer values indicated higher mobility (Wang, Xie, & Chen, 2021). In noodle systems, T2 spectra distinguished states ranging from strongly bound (T21) to highly mobile water (T24) (Fig. 3D), revealing dynamic water-starch interactions (Han & Zhu, 2024; Xu, Zheng, Cao, Yang, Xing, Zhang, et al., 2021). As shown in Table 1, TBE-N exhibited the longest T24, indicating enhanced free water mobility. This phenomenon may be explained by its higher crystallinity, which limited the number of available adsorption sites in the amorphous regions, thereby reducing water-binding capacity and increasing the proportion and mobility of relatively free water (Kaur, Oberoi, Sogi, & Gill, 2011). Conversely, WTBU-N exhibited a significantly reduced T24 (P < 0.05) (Table 1), suggesting that the mobility of relatively free water was restricted. This result may be attributed to starch damage and structural disruption caused by ultrafine grinding. The intense mechanical action disrupted particle integrity, exposed more hydrophilic groups, and increased the specific surface area of the matrix, thereby strengthening water-matrix interactions and shifting part of the relatively free water into a more restricted state (Guan et al., 2020).

Effects of different buckwheat flours on the edible quality of gluten-free buckwheat noodles

Fig. 4A showed that TBE-N demonstrated excellent sensory acceptance. It achieved a high score of 91.6, significantly outperforming other groups (P < 0.05). Conversely, WTBU-N performed the worst (score of 77.25). WTB-N and TBB-N ranked in the middle. Since color and appearance determine consumers' “first impression” (Hurling & Shepherd, 2003), all samples scored above 8.5 for color. TBE-N even reached 9.5. This indicated good color acceptance. However, WTBU-N showed a significant deficit in appearance (score of 4.25). This suggested poor structural integrity, which severely reduced initial consumer acceptance (Sun et al., 2019). The poor appearance of WTBU-N was closely related to its higher cooking loss (15.12%) and water solubility index (31.07%). Ultrafine grinding produced abundant fine particles and damaged starch, which readily leached out during cooking and adhered to the noodle surface, making it sticky, soft, uneven, and partially pasty. These changes weakened the apparent integrity and smoothness of the noodle surface, thereby explaining the low appearance score of WTBU-N.

Regarding texture properties (palatability, chewiness, and stickiness), TBE-N demonstrated superiority across all metrics. Results indicated the formation of a dense, stable starch gel network. This imparted ideal elasticity and shear resistance. Notably, WTBU-N scored lowest in chewiness (18.00), indicating insufficient binding force. Yet, its stickiness (21.25) exceeded that of WTB-N and TBB-N. This “low chewiness, high stickiness” combination suggested a loose noodle structure with excessive surface gelatinization or amylose leaching (Wang et al., 2021). In summary, TBE-N exhibited the best sensory quality. The defects in WTBU-N indicated a failure to construct a stable gel system, highlighting the need for optimization (Han & Zhu, 2024).

Textural analysis (TPA) was conducted to corroborate subjective sensory evaluation results with objective quantitative metrics. The findings revealed a high correlation between instrument-measured parameters and sensory scores. Although no significant differences were observed in elasticity among the sample groups (P > 0.05), a distinct hierarchical distribution emerged for hardness, viscosity, and chewiness (P < 0.05), with the order being TBE-N > WTB-N > WTBU-N > TBB-N. Specifically, TBE-N exhibited the strongest structural integrity (hardness 32.43 N, viscosity 22.41 N, chewiness 19.95 N), whereas TBB-N possessed the most fragile gel network (corresponding values: 10.66 N, 7.90 N, and 7.13 N). The decline in textural strength for TBB-N and WTBU-N stemmed from higher bran and aleurone residues. High levels of dietary fiber and hydrophobic polyphenols exerted a “dilution effect” and steric hindrance on the starch matrix (Jiang, Cheng, He, Zhou, & Ge, 2025). This disrupted starch-protein hydration and resulted in coarse starch granules with reduced water retention (Wang, Liu, Wang, Wang, Hong, Liu, et al., 2024). In contrast, TBE-N preserved the natural structure of buckwheat endosperm, which promoted the formation of a dense starch gel network. Additionally, changes in viscosity indicators correlated with the dissolution of amylose and small molecules during cooking (An et al., 2022).

In vitro starch digestibility and structural mechanisms of gluten-free Tartary buckwheat noodles

Fig. 5A demonstrated that processing methods significantly altered starch digestibility. This primarily manifested as a redistribution of resistant starch (RS) and rapidly digestible starch (RDS). TBE-N exhibited the strongest resistance to digestion, featuring the highest RS content (67%) and lowest RDS content (13%). In contrast, WTBU-N showed high digestibility. Its RDS rose to 32%, while RS dropped to the lowest level (49%). Notably, SDS content remained relatively stable at approximately 19%–20% across all groups. SDS refered to the starch fraction digested between 20 and 120 min during in vitro digestion (Doan, Kim, Cha, & Kim, 2025), and is jointly regulated by starch accessibility, gelatinized matrix structure, and molecular organization (Freitas et al., 2025). In this study, the same Tartary buckwheat flour/extruded corn starch ratio and identical processing conditions might have formed a similar gelatinized starch matrix, thereby maintaining a relatively stable SDS level. In contrast, RDS and RS were more sensitive to starch damage, particle size reduction, component leaching, and matrix compactness. Thus, flour stream composition and processing mainly shifted starch digestibility toward either readily digestible or resistant fractions by regulating enzyme accessibility and matrix compactness, while the intermediate SDS fraction was less affected (Zhang, Sun, Xu, Feng, Wang, Meng, et al., 2022; Xie, Zhou, Zhao, Peng, Zhou, Qian et al., 2023).

Starch components of different cooked Tartary buckwheat noodles (A). Correlation analysis among starch components and quality indicators (B). The asterisk (*) in the figure/table indicates statistical significance at P < 0.05.

Correlation analysis (Fig. 5B) further revealed the structural mechanisms underlying these differences. RS content exhibited significant positive correlations (P < 0.05) with relative crystallinity (RC), hardness, and water absorption at 95 °C. This confirmed that the high RS content in TBE-N was closely associated with the dense, ordered structure formed during its processing. This enhanced gel framework not only conferred higher macroscopic hardness to the noodles but also formed a microscopic physical barrier that effectively restricted the diffusion and substrate binding of digestive enzymes, thereby reducing the hydrolysis rate. This mechanism of “structural densification simultaneously improving texture and reducing digestibility” was corroborated by other studies on heat-treated buckwheat noodles (Wang et al., 2024). Conversely, RDS content showed a significant positive correlation with cooking loss, revealing that the high digestibility of WTBU-N stemmed from its insufficient thermal stability. Higher cooking loss suggested that its starch matrix was more prone to structural disintegration and soluble component migration under hydrothermal conditions. This disruption of structural integrity exposed more enzymatic access points, leading to increased RDS. Research on extruded buckwheat noodles further supported this perspective: solute leaching and structural degradation during cooking often correlated with an increase in in vitro digestibility (An et al., 2022; S. Zhang et al., 2022). In summary, enhancing raw material crystallinity and high-temperature hydration stability represented a critical pathway for synergistically improving gluten-free buckwheat noodles through textural reinforcement and digestion retardation.

Conclusion

This study elucidated the decisive role of microstructural reorganization and crystal integrity in regulating the quality and nutritional functionality of gluten-free buckwheat noodles. The findings indicated that the preservation of A-crystal structures and natural granule agglomeration constituted the physical prerequisite for hydrothermal stability. Conventional milling preserved these ordered structures, conferring superior hydration behavior (water absorption of 8.79 g/g, solubility of 2.31%) and a dense gel network to the raw material. This structural foundation directly translated into the outstanding sensory performance (91.6 points) and textural properties (32.43 N) of TBE noodles. In stark contrast, the lattice disruption induced by ultrafine grinding reduced crystallinity to 12.61%, triggering severe structural collapse, excessive dissolution (31.07%), and appearance degradation. Crucially, the study elucidated a distinct “structure-digestion” coupling mechanism. Correlation analysis (P < 0.05) suggested that the preserved ordered crystals likely delayed enzymatic digestion through steric hindrance, enabling TBE noodles to achieve the highest resistant starch content (RS 67%) and lowest rapidly digestible starch content (RDS 13%). Conversely, structural disruption was associated with a surge in RDS to 32% in WTBU noodles. Consequently, maintaining crystal integrity was confirmed as the critical pathway for synergistically achieving “texture enhancement” and “digestion retardation.”

Limitations of this study include the reliance on in vitro models to interpret digestive mechanisms. Future in vivo validation is essential to substantiate the physiological benefits for diabetic management. Furthermore, although the physical barrier effect was highlighted, the underlying molecular interactions between starch and non-starch matrices (e.g., proteins, polyphenols) remain to be fully characterized. Moving forward, research should extend to optimizing TBE parameters for industrial upscaling and investigating the storage stability of the noodles to broaden their commercial application.
