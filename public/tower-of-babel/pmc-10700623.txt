Study of gamma radiation shielding on tellurite glass containing TiO2 and Al2O3 nanoparticles
Authors: Floressy Juhim, Fuei Pien Chee, Asmahani Awang, Pak Yan Moh, Khairul Anuar Mohd Salleh, Sofian Ibrahim, Jedol Dayou, Amani Alalawi, MS Al-Buriahi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadiation shielding incorporates material between the radioactive source and environment to decrease exposure to hazardous radiation. It remains to be seen whether the addition of nanoparticles effectively increases the protection of tellurite glass system from further degradation under irradiation conditions. This study revealed the gamma radiation effects on tellurite glass. The tellurite glass samples were irradiated with 50 kGy and 100 kGy gamma ray, and subsequently analysed using X-ray diffractometer (XRD), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–Vis). Gamma radiation increased the creation of non-bridging oxygen (NBO) and caused colour change on TZNETi and TZNETiAl glasses. Consequently, the addition of aluminium oxides (Al2O3) was found to lower the density of glass systems. The glass samples surface roughness increased, while the optical transmission spectra decreased after 50 kGy of gamma ray irradiation. Nevertheless, the glass system maintained its transparency even after irradiation. The mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) values represented the shielding effectiveness demonstrated by the investigated glass with the addition of Al2O3. The physical, structural, optical, and radiation shielding properties showed that 69.1TeO2–20ZnO–9Na2O–1Er2O3–0.3TiO2–0.6Al2O3 (TZNETiAl) sample exhibited strong shielding properties amongst the fabricated tellurite samples.Keywords: Tellurite glass, Aluminium oxide, Radiation, Shielding, Nanoparticle, Simulation

Introduction

Nuclear radiation, such as alpha, beta, fast electron, proton, neutron, gamma rays, and x-rays is widely employed in medical, agricultural, industrial, nuclear-power plant, and scientific applications. The evolution of radiation-shielding material is fascinating in radiological applications in health, agriculture, and industry.

Transparent glass-based radiation shielding material allows engineers and technicians to visually check the radiation environment and equipment accessories [1]. To achieve this while providing radiation safety, lead (Pb) is the predominant choice as additive or dopant to glasses [2]. However, Pb presents an indirect risk to workers and patients, altering biochemical systems in the human body and causing high toxicity, especially in the manufacturing process, thus raising concern that usage of Pb poses safety and health risks [3].

Glass-doped chemical composition material is a potential Pb substitute due to its physical and optical qualities, which may be easily adjusted by composition, preparation procedures, and recyclables [3]. One of the most notable features of tellurium dioxide (TeO2) is that it is an intermediate glass-forming oxide and does not vitrify under conventional quenching settings. Furthermore, radiation damage to the tellurite glass system has drawn considerable attention in recent studies for radiation shielding applications [4].

Nanoparticles (NPs) are vast classes of material that include particulate compounds with at least one dimension of less than 100 nm [5]. The significance of this material was apparent when researchers discovered that size may impact a substance's physiochemical qualities, such as optical properties [6]. The presence of metallic nanoparticles significantly influenced the compactness and binding strength of the glass system [7]. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is introduced with silver and gold because these elements have excellent chemical stability [8]. Surface roughness characteristics gave these metallic NPs unique properties [9]. The addition of transition aluminium oxides (Al2O3) to the glass composition is desirable as modifiers throughout the glass-making process [10]. The addition of Al2O3 to glass structures improves their capabilities for optical and radiation shielding [11]. The increase in transition of Al2O3 enhances the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) values of tellurite glasses [10]. Compared to common shields, glasses with a substantially greater Al2O3 content are better photon absorbers [12].

Erbium ions (Er3+) were utilized as rare-earth ions in this study because they can generate pink colour, leading to their application in glasses [13]. Erbium is also chosen because its hexagonal structure renders it stable [14]. Furthermore, the incorporation of Er3+ ions in tellurite glass indicates the creation of network intermediates, as tellurite is a potential host for Er3+ ion incorporation and is suited for optical applications [15].

Previous studies found that NPs incorporated in erbium-doped tellurite glasses improved their absorption characteristics [8]. Based on a study conducted by Bourezgui et al. (2020), according to structural and optical studies, gamma radiation significantly impacts TiO2 performance, which depends on the application of the samples [16].

TiO2 and Al2O3 perform as a glass former or modifier in the glass formations dependent on the percentage of concentration in the glass matrix, which has attracted the attention of researchers to nanoparticle-based glasses. Additionally, the inclusion of TiO2 and Al2O3 changes the structure and optical characteristics of a glass network. This study discusses tellurite glass physical, structural, optical properties, and radiation shielding properties containing nanoparticles in detail. The mass attenuation coefficient factors (MAC), half value layer (HVL), tenth value layer (TVL), mean free path (MFP), and effective atomic number (Zeff) values of these glasses for 1.25 MeV photons were calculated using Phy-X/PSD programme.

This study aimed to examine the radiation shielding capabilities of tellurite glass doped with different nanoparticles, as well as to analyze the resulting changes in the glass's structural and optical properties upon exposure to gamma radiation.

Experimental details

This study assessed shielding performance based on the type of nanoparticles used and explained their quantitative impact on the shielding process. Furthermore, the evaluation of characterization and radiation shielding properties is used to define the effect of type of nanoparticles on shielding performance. The atomic number emerged as the most crucial factor affecting the shielding property [17]. Thus, this study focused on evaluating the shielding property in relation to nanoparticle type and atomic number. Larger particle size and particle arrangement can increase the propensity for interaction between incident radiation and the particles, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

The starting materials, obtained from Sigma Aldrich, included tellurium dioxide (TeO2, purity 99 %), zinc oxide (ZnO, purity≥99 %), sodium oxide (Na2O, purity 80 %), erbium III oxide (Er2O3, purity >99.99 %), titanium IV oxide (TiO2, with a primary particle size of 21 nm as determined by TEM, purity≥99.5 %), and aluminium oxide nanopowder (Al2O3, with a primary particle size of 13 nm as determined by TEM, purity 99.8 %). The materials were thoroughly mixed in a plastic bottle using a milling process for 30 min until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. Fabricated samples were then irradiated with Cobalt-60 source, utilizing JS10000 IR 219 tote irradiator system at MINTec SINAGAMA with an irradiation rate of 2 kGy/h. Samples were irradiated with an ionizing dose of 50 kGy and 100 kGy. The ionizing dose of 50 kGy was selected based on previous research papers [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]] that highlighted an increase in radiation's impact beyond this threshold. Additional, 100 kGy was chosen as a high dose irradiation category [18] to conduct further investigations into the properties of the glass system in this study.

The physical, structural, and optical properties of the fabricated samples were characterized to assess their pre-irradiation and post-irradiation performances. UV–Vis spectroscopy was conducted using the EZ210 model Lambda ultraviolet–visible spectrometer to obtain spectra for the proposed glasses. The optical characterization in the ultraviolet–visible range was carried out at room temperature using a CARY-60 UV–visible light spectrophotometer in the wavelength of 190–1100 nm. The structural properties of the glass system were examined using X-ray diffractometry (XRD), by using Bruker D2 PHASER Benchtop XRD scanning angle two theta ranging from 10° to 80°. Glass samples in a fine powder form are pressed in the sample holder forming a plane loaded surface to record the XRD pattern. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) measurement was carried out by using bulk glass sample. PerkinElmer Spectrum 100 was used operating at 50–60 Hz in 100–200 V. The measurement was performed in the range of 4000 cm−1 to 450 cm−1. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) assessed monolayer morphological changes before and after irradiation. Bruker Multimode AFM model was used for surface roughness characterization. The sample was placed on a holder and a cantilever moving back and forth in a tapping mode scanning the topography across the sample. The AFM images were recorded at 5 μm.

The attenuation factors were calculated using the Phy-X/PSD program and Geant4 simulations to investigate the radiation shielding capabilities of the fabricated samples. The program was used to calculate the attenuation of several energies (ranging from 1 MeV to 15 MeV) for each sample composition. Phy-X/PSD is a free software that has been shown to produce precise results that match both simulation and experimental data in recent studies [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Other than that, computations for the sample data can be performed by utilizing its composition and density. Notably, this software can deal with various radiation shielding variables. Moreover, we carried out Geant4 simulations to compare the theoretical results obtained by Phy-X/PSD program Geant4 is a powerful software toolkit used in radiation shielding simulation developed by CERN [27]. It accurately models the interactions of particles with matter, making it essential for designing effective shielding materials and structures in nuclear energy, medical, and space applications, ensuring safety and efficiency in radiation environments.

The density measurement indicates structural compaction with the incorporation of modifiers into the host matrix [28]. This can be attributed to the displacement of oxygen from its usual location, creating non-bridging oxygen (NBO) [19]. The molecules became widely spaced as the glass density decreased, resulting in a less blocked and compact structure after irradiation [18]. The density of specimens determined how much attenuated gamma radiation passes through the samples. Density is a factor that impacts the molar volume modifications. Non-bridging oxygens (NBOs) are produced in direct proportion to the molar volume [29]. The inclusion of Al2O3 replacing TeO2 reduced the glass radiation attenuation properties as the density decreased. Based on the data in Table 3, the density of both TZNETi and TZNETiAl samples decreased after being irradiated with 50 kGy.

Optical properties

Fig. 9 shows the transmission spectra before and after irradiation with different doses of gamma rays for TZNETi. This is closely related to the change in colour of the sample. The reduction in transmittance could also be seen as a consequence of loss scattering and the sample surface roughness [40]. The UV–Vis absorption spectra of Er2O3 doped glasses are also shown in Fig. 9. It consists of seven bands, each with a centre at 445, 489, 520, 545, 650, 800, and 975 nm for the unirradiated sample. These bands represent transitions from the ground state 4I15/2 of Er3+ ions to the excited states, 4F3/2, 4F7/2, 2H11/2, 4S3/2, 4F9/2, 4I9/2 and 4I11/2. The present study estimates Er3+ ions' transition energy per earlier findings [41,42]. After 50 kGy and 100 kGy, radiation decreases to six bands.

Change in percentage transmission of TZNETi before and after gamma irradiation.

Fig. 9 shows that the absorption values were slightly higher following gamma irradiation. It can be attributed to defects brought on by radiation, such as NBOs, vacancies, and an increase in free electron-hole pairs [20]. The absorption due to collisions of free electrons increases with a higher number of defects generated by irradiation.

The transmission of the unirradiated sample was 74.04 % at 720 nm. It was noted that the transmission at 720 nm decreased to 66.84 % after irradiation by 50 kGy gamma rays. This drop in transmission could be associated with the 'Compton process' releasing energetic electrons [43]. Compton scattering, the ejection of an electron from its host atom, acts as the fundamentals for the shielding process [17]. When the sample was subjected to gamma rays, the electrons produced by Compton interaction set the primary knock-on atoms in motion, as the incident radiation gained enough energy to displace atoms [44].

Structural defects, multivalent impurities can trap the generated electrons or holes and NBO, resulting in limited transmission in the visible area [45,46]. Therefore, the loss in transparency for the sample after gamma irradiation of 50 kGy was attributed to the increasing number of NBO atoms due to irradiation. This decrease indicated that glass defects arise in pairs of negative electron and positive hole centers [22]. Then, the transmission of the sample at 720 nm was 72.38 % after irradiation with 100 kGy, showing a slight change from the unirradiated transmittance. It was noted that the absorption bands were not affected by 100 kGy irradiation dose [47]. The transmittance intensity significantly reduced after 50 kGy irradiation but subsequently increased again after 100 kGy irradiation, although it remained lower than before irradiation. This behaviour indicated that tellurite glass containing TiO2 nanoparticles shields the creation of visible bands caused by gamma irradiation [48]. The addition of TiO2 had a notable impact on the parameters in this study [49].

Meanwhile, the findings also revealed that gamma ray irradiation caused a decrease in optical transmittance for TZNETiAl (Fig. 10). Additionally, the UV–Vis absorption spectra of Er2O3 the glasses showed eight distinct bands, with each having a centre at 360, 445, 489, 520, 545, 650, 800, and 975 nm for the unirradiated sample. These bands represent transitions from the ground state of 4I15/2 of Er3+ ions, to the excited states, 4G11/2, 4F3/2, 4F7/2, 2H11/2, 4S3/2, 4F9/2, 4I9/2 and 4I11/2. The number of absorption bands of Er3+ ions decreases after radiation. The absorption values were slightly higher after gamma irradiation, similar to the TZNETi.

Change in percentage transmission of TZNETiAl before and after gamma irradiation.

The transmission of unirradiated sample was 72.82 % at 720 nm for TZNETiAl. It was noted that the transmission at 720 nm decreased to 71.15 % after irradiation by 50 kGy gamma rays. Then, the transmission of the sample at 720 nm was 78.03 % after irradiated with 100 kGy, showing a drastic increase from the unirradiated transmittance. These visible band defects connected to the effect of irradiation on the base glass were predicted to be produced by gamma rays [50].

The addition of Al2O3 to the glass matrix increased the density, which resulted in increased attenuation of gamma radiation (Fig. 10). The results showed that at 715 nm, the transmission of TZNETiAl sample was 71.15 %. The transmission at 715 nm was reduced to 66.81 % for TZNETi. The transmitted beam through the glass was reduced when the gamma ray and target atoms Te and Al interacted more [51]. Furthermore, the bonding of different oxides to the base glass caused a matrix deformation and breaking of multiple links, resulting in the development of NBO bonds capable of absorbing UV–Vis light [52].

Overall, the findings revealed that gamma ray irradiation caused a general decrease in optical transmittance (Table 7) for both TZNETi and TZNETiAl at 600 nm. This wavelength showed a significant reduction in transmittance compared to the unirradiated samples. However, TZNETiAl had a higher transmission, specifically at 100 kGy of gamma radiation.

The transmittance at most Vis ranges increased initially as Al2O3 content was introduced into the glass system (TZNETiAl) and then decreased, with TZNETi having the lowest transmittance. Photons are scattered more by low Z atoms than by high Z atoms [53]. The weight fractions of low Z (Al) atoms in the glasses increased the weight fractions of lower Z atoms such as O and Na. As a result, the scattering and absorption cross-sections increase, so does the photon penetration depth in the glasses. Subsequently, Al2O3 increased the glass system's photon absorption capacity.

Fig. 9, Fig. 10 show the UV–visible transmission spectra of the two tested glasses before and after exposure to 50 and 100 kGy doses of progressive gamma radiation. Each glass' optical spectrum shows apparent optical stability against radiation doses. The effect of ionizing radiation on the glass structure, which causes atomic displacements, more free radicals, and NBO, has decreased transmittance intensity [39]. The overall findings of the optical study demonstrate that radiation has a consistent impact on the examined glasses.

Table 8 shows TZNETiAl with the highest MAC (0.05176 cm2/g) compared to the other samples, indicating a better shielding efficiency. The dominance of Compton scattering, which varies significantly depending on the atomic number and the energy, explains why TZNETiAl samples have a higher MAC [55]. The half value layer (HVL), an additional variable defining radiation penetration through a particular material, was investigated in this article. The results show that the addition of Al2O3 increased the HVL values. Several studies have discovered a correlation between HVL and heavy metal oxide concentrations [56]. This behaviour of HVL with incident photon energy is being interpreted similarly to MAC values, as previously discussed. Moreover, from Tables 8 and it is clear that there is a very good match and agreement between the results calculated by Phy-X and the results obtained by geant4 simulations. Such agreement confirms the accuracy of the obtained values for each parameter in the present work.

The mean free path (MFP) was shown further to emphasize the radiation attenuation characteristics of the samples. Thinner samples are required to block incoming photons when MFP is low. Analyzing the spatial distribution of a specific number of interactions is essential for calculating the average distance between successive interactions and determining the material that can maximize interactions within the smallest volume [57]. According to the fundamental radiation shielding principle, MFP decreases as sample density increases [58]. Despite its high density, TZNETi has a slightly lower MFP. As a result, low MFP materials will provide more interaction per unit distance, resulting in more efficient photon absorption. The greater the density of the glass specimen, the greater the possibility of radiation interaction and, consequently, radiation attenuation.

The sample's effective atomic number (Zeff) was studied in connection to their interaction with gamma rays. It describes the radiation absorption capability of the medium, with low Zeff media absorbing less radiation than other media [55]. Therefore, glass manufacturers seek to employ heavy elements, such as Te to increase the Zeff of glasses and build unique glass systems with excellent radiation shielding qualities [58]. Table 8 shows that TZNETi has the highest Zeff, whereas TZNETiAl has the lowest. Glass system with a higher percentage of TeO2 increases the Zeff value. This is because TeO2 with high atomic numbers have the ability to withstand high-energy gamma rays [59]. In addition, the use of heavy metal oxide (HMO) such as TeO2 increases the Zeff values, implying that it has interesting radiation shielding properties [60].

This enables the radiation shielding application to be evaluated by considering the significantly improved of the examined materials absorption qualities compared to those of existing materials. Conclusion

Gamma radiation was found to significantly affect the physical, structural, optical characteristics of the tellurite glass system. The results showed that the addition of Al2O3 increased the mass attenuation coefficient (MAC) and improved the radiation shielding properties of the glass. This was attributed to the decrease in density and the formation of non-bridging oxygen (NBO) bonds due to the incorporation of Al2O3, which contributed to the increased optical transmission of the samples. The simulation findings also showed that the half value layer (HVL) values increased with the addition of Al2O3. The study also revealed that the addition of Al2O3 led to lower surface roughness of the samples after gamma irradiation. Overall, the addition of Al2O3 nanoparticles causes the incident gamma radiation to be attenuated more as the collision between photons and shielding is higher. As a result, tellurite glass containing Al2O3 nanoparticles presents a materials technology that can enhance shield durability.
