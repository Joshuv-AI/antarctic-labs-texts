Gamma-glutamyltransferase and the survival of hepatocellular carcinoma patients after transarterial chemoembolization: a meta‑analysis and systematic review
Authors: Zixin Huang, Manli Zhou, Sisi Feng, Xiaomin Xiao, Baiyun Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePlentiful studies have reported that preoperative gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) had a strong relationship with the prognosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) patients treated with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). Nevertheless, due to the small sample size and contradictory conclusions, the predictive function remains indefinite. To investigate the relationship between pretreatment GGT value and clinical outcomes in HCC patients undergoing TACE, a meta-analysis including 10 articles was constructed.MethodStudies were searched in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI, and Wanfang database. Pooled Hazard Ratio (HR) or Odd Ratio (OR) with its 95% confidence intervals was calculated to evaluate the prognostic value of pretreatment GGT in HCC patients undergoing TACE.ResultTen studies with 3274 patients were included in our meta-analysis. The pooled results show that elevated pretreatment GGT has a close relationship to poorer OS (HR 1.72; 95%CI: 1.48–2.01, P < 0.00001, I2 = 56%) after TACE. Additionally, further subgroup analyses suggested that pretreatment GGT was associated with gender (male) (OR 2.02; 95%CI: 1.42–2.88; P < 0.0001), multiple tumours (OR 1.55; 95%CI: 1.18–2.03; P = 0.001), and Child-Pugh class (OR 2.09; 95%CI: 1.44–3.03; P = 0.0001).ConclusionThe result of our meta-analysis indicates that pretreatment GGT had a significant relationship with the clinical outcome in HCC patients after TACE and can serve as an useful marker for predicting overall survival.Trial RegistrationProspero number: CRD42024504715.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04321-z.Keywords: Gamma-glutamyltransferase, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Prognostic biomarker, Transarterial chemoembolization, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer, and stands as the third most common cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with an estimated number of 758,725 new cases in 2022 [1]. Over the past few decades, the incidence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has shown a progressive upward trend, becoming a significant global public health issue. Approximately 90% of HCC patients have a history of cirrhosis caused by any other risk factors, including alcohol-related liver disease, HBV or HCV infection, or metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [2]. Surgical resection and liver transplantation remain the optimal therapeutic strategies for early-stage HCC, which accounts for about 30% of diagnosed cases [3–5]. However, the majority of patients are initially diagnosed at intermediate to advanced stages when curative surgical interventions are no longer recommended. According to the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) serves as the first-line treatment for intermediate-stage (BCLC-B) patients. This includes asymptomatic individuals presenting with limited unresectable multinodular lesions, absence of vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread, and preserved hepatic function [6, 7]. The pronounced tumor heterogeneity observed in HCC results in variable therapeutic responses to TACE, with a substantial proportion of intermediate-to-advanced stage patients failing to derive clinical benefit. Low overall survival (OS) rates also reduce the quality of life in HCC patients. While existing prognostic systems such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification and Cancer of the Liver Italian Program (CLIP) scoring system have been widely adopted in clinical decision-making [8], their implementation is frequently constrained by complex multi-parameter assessments that limit practical utility. This underscores the critical need to develop validated, economically viable biomarkers capable of stratifying patients for personalized therapeutic interventions.

Gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), a membrane-bound enzyme predominantly expressed in hepatic tissue [9], plays a pivotal role in glutathione (GSH) metabolism through catalyzing the degradation of extracellular GSH and promoting cysteine recycling, thus contributing to the maintenance of cellular redox homeostasis [10, 11] Clinically, GGT serves as a sensitive biomarker of liver injury and cholestasis [9]. Emerging evidence implicates that GGT participates in tumorigenesis, progression, invasion, and resistance in different kinds of cancers, positioning it as a significant prognostic biomarker for survival [12–15]. Particularly in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), multiple observational studies and meta-analysis have mentioned the significant associations between elevated pretreatment serum GGT levels and adverse clinical outcomes following TACE [16–23]. However, the optimal threshold for GGT remains controversial, likely due to variations in study design, patient populations, sample sizes, and statistical analysis methods. Although many studies have examined GGT’s prognostic value in unresectable HCC, results have been inconsistent, possibly because of differences in treatment strategies and patient characteristics. To resolve these discrepancies, we systematically evaluated available evidence through meta-analysis to quantitatively assess the predictive capacity of pretreatment GGT levels in TACE-treated HCC populations.

Methods

Search strategy

This study was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). The present protocol was registered on PROSPERO (Register ID, CRD42024504715). PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), and Wanfang Data were systematically searched for relevant literature published from the inception of the database up to 21 January 2024, with a search strategy as follows: (“hepatocellular carcinoma” OR “liver cell carcinoma” OR “hepatoma” OR “HCC”) AND (“TACE” OR “transarterial chemoembolization”) AND (“gamma-glutamyl transferase” OR “γ-glutamyl transferase” OR “GGT” OR “γ-GT”). The studies were limited to human studies without limitation to the language of the publications. References cited in relevant articles were also screened manually.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria

Literature included in this meta-analysis should meet the following criteria: (1) cohort studies including prospective and retrospective cohorts; (2) HCC was diagnosed by pathological methods; (3) serum γ-GT level was measured before TACE treatment; (4) Hazard ratio and 95% confidence intervals for different GGT in overall survival were described in study or could be calculated by the given data. The exclusion criteria included: (1) reviews, comments, letters, conference abstract, conference paper, full text not available; (2) duplicated publication or overlapped data; (3) articles without pretreatment GGT cut-off value; (4) TACE combined with other treatment.

Data extraction and quality assessing

Two reviewers completed literature search, data extraction, and quality assessment independently, while the differences were worked out by discussion. The publication information, period of enrollment, study design, target population, study endpoints, inclusion and exclusion criteria, sample size, demographic data (age and sex), liver function, BCLC stage, overall survival, hazard ratio (mean value and 95%CI) and GGT cut-off value were required to be extracted. To ensure consistent HR directionality, all studies used the control group as the reference (HR = 1.0). When the intervention group was used as the reference in the original studies, reciprocal transformation (1/HR) was applied and the confidence intervals were inverted. Log-transformed HR values were used consistently in the combined analysis. Furthermore, in studies reporting both univariate and multivariate analyses, we prioritized the extraction of HR from multivariate analyses to better adjust for potential confounding factors, thereby improving the accuracy of assessing GGT’s prognostic value. Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used to evaluate the quality of studies. The NOS scoring criteria consist of three aspects: participant selection, comparability, and the validity of the outcomes. Studies with the NOS scores ≥ 6 were defined as high-quality studies in this meta-analysis.

Data analysis

The effect of serum GGT and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) level on overall survival (OS) was measured by using pooled hazard ratios (HR) and their 95% confidence intervals. When these statical data are not directly available in the original literature, the method reported by Tierney [24] was used to calculate them from the available data. Heterogeneity between studies was estimated by the chi-squared and I-squared tests. The random effect model was chosen to combine the data when heterogeneity was significant (I2 > 50% or P < 0.05), otherwise the fix-effect model was applied.

To elucidate the potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted a random-effects meta-regression analysis using meta package. Heterogeneity attributable to each variable was quantitatively assessed using established statistical indicators, including I², τ², and R². Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test were carried out to evaluate potential publication bias. To evaluate the contribution of each study on the pooled estimate, a sensitivity analysis employing the knockout approach was conducted. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Analyses in this meta-analysis were conducted by the Review Manager (version 5.4) and R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Literature search

The procedure of the literature search and included study were represented in Fig. 1 in the form of a PRISMA flow chat. Overall, 380 records were identified in the initial database search after excluding the duplications. Subsequently, 360 studies were removed after scanning titles, abstracts, and publication types, and 20 articles remained for full-text review. 10 articles were further excluded for no available data, and 10 studies with 3274 patients were finally included in this meta-analysis [16–21, 25–28].

The detailed characteristics of 10 studies were summarized in Table 1. The included studies were conducted in China, France, India, and Israel, which were accomplished between 2005 and 2020, all of these studies were retrospective cohort studies. In addition, the pretreatment GGT cut-off values in these studies ranged from 50 to 200 U/mL, and outcome data were reported or could be estimated through the survival curve. Under the guidance of NOS criteria, we performed the quality assessment of the included articles. As the result shown in Table 1, all studies received a score of at least 6 points, and the mean score was 7 points, suggesting that included studies were relatively high-quality according to our definition.

Meta-analysis results and sensitivity analysis

All of the included studies reported the association between pretreatment GGT and the overall survival of patients with HCC who received TACE treatment. Pooled results of 10 cohort studies showed that patients with higher pretreatment GGT had a strong relationship with worse OS (HR 1.72; 95%CI: 1.48–2.01, P < 0.00001, I2 = 56%) compared to patients with lower GGT (Fig. 2A). We further performed a meta-analysis of 6 studies investigating the association between AFP and OS. The results demonstrated that elevated pretreatment AFP levels were significantly correlated with worse survival outcomes following TACE (HR 1.82; 95%CI: 1.62–2.03, P < 0.00001, I2 = 0%). However, based on the limited available data, no statistically significant difference in HR was observed when compared to the high GGT group (Fig. 2B). Sensitivity analysis was performed to estimate the bias for the low number of eligible studies. The results displayed in Fig. 3 demonstrated that the combined data exhibited no significant change when omitting any single study, indicating that the pooled HR for OS in our study had high stability.

Subgroup analysis

In further investigation of heterogeneity, a meta-regression analysis was conducted, which revealed that the region of the studies accounted for 61.47% of the heterogeneity, while variables of mean age, GGT cut-off value, and sex ratio were unable to account for the sources of heterogeneity in this study. Subsequently, to elucidate the relationship between preoperative GGT and OS in HCC patients undergoing TACE, we conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by region of the patients. In the subgroups of North America (HR 1.36; 95%CI 1.11–1.67; P = 0.003), China (HR 1.70; 95%CI 1.44–2.01; P < 0.00001), and other areas of Asia (HR 1.97; 95%CI 1.55–2.50; P < 0.00001), statistically significant differences were observed in the pooled analysis, with a stronger association in East Asia and Other areas of Asia compared to a weaker association in North America (Fig. 4A). The low heterogeneity observed within each subgroup further substantiates the notion that region serves as a significant source of heterogeneity in this study.

Forest-plot of subgroup analysis. A subgroup analysis divided by region; B subgroup analysis divided by BCLC stage

Furthermore, in accordance with clinical guidelines identifying BCLC-B stage patients as optimal candidates for TACE, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by BCLC staging (Fig. 4B). The results demonstrated statistically significant treatment effects within both BCLC-B (HR 1.68; 95%CI 1.41–2.01; P < 0.00001; I2 = 0%) and BCLC-C (HR 2.01; 95%CI 1.59–2.53; P < 0.00001; I2 = 26%) subgroups, and the heterogeneity within each subgroup was relatively small.

Discussion

Transarterial chemoembolization is one of the most significant treatments for patients with unresectable HCC, and the outcomes have great individual differences. However, the predictive prognosis marker for TACE remains scarce, especially for cost-effective biomarkers that can easily obtained from serum. As a common biochemical index, GGT is widely recognized as a biomarker for liver fibrosis and early HCC in clinical practice. Previous studies have indicated that preoperative GGT is an independent prognosis factor in HCC patients receiving hepatectomy, RFA, chemotherapy, and combined treatment [22, 29, 30]. Several potential mechanisms of the prognostic effect of GGT have been proposed. It was reported in a study that, by elevating iron absorption, GGT contributed to DNA lesions and oncogenic-related mutations, which promoted the progression of carcinogenesis [31, 32]. On the other hand, it is revealed that GGT can promote the generation of the oxidative product, especially reactive oxygen species (ROS), which were suggested to induce EMT and promote invasion by activating the NF-κB signalling pathway [33].

Our results show that elevated preoperative serum GGT levels are associated with poor outcomes in HCC patients receiving TACE treatment (HR 1.72; 95%CI: 1.48–2.01, P < 0.00001, I2 = 56%). The combined analysis of AFP and GGT also suggests that GGT exhibits comparable prognostic predictive ability to the traditional biomarker AFP. Although the current study has data limitations that prevent a definitive assessment of the relative efficacy between GGT and AFP as prognostic markers, these findings still highlight the potential adverse effects associated with elevated GGT levels, as well as its promise as a biomarker for predicting the therapeutic response to TACE. Pooled analyses further revealed that high pretreatment GGT correlated significantly with key clinical features: multiple tumors (OR 1.55, 95%CI: 1.18–2.03; P = 0.001) and advanced Child-Pugh classification (OR 2.09, 95%CI: 1.44–3.03; P < 0.001), indicating its association with increased tumor burden and compromised hepatic functional reserve. Evidence suggests that liver functional reserve is a critical determinant of prognosis in non-surgical management, with patients exhibiting impaired reserve demonstrating suboptimal therapeutic responses [34]. Consequently, meticulous pretreatment assessment of hepatic reserve is essential for survival optimization. Given GGT’s established strong association with liver functional reserve, it demonstrates potential as a complementary biomarker for refining comprehensive liver function evaluation. Furthermore, although the number of subgroups included in this analysis was limited, the results showed that GGT exhibited a relatively consistent predictive performance across patient subgroups with different BCLC stages. These findings suggest that GGT may still hold potential as a prognostic biomarker for TACE treatment, even in less-than-ideal patient populations.

Previous studies have identified several serum biomarkers that predict TACE prognosis, including the aspartate aminotransferase-to-lymphocyte ratio index (ALRI) [35], ALBI score [36], and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [37]. These parameters can simultaneously reflect liver function status and immune response levels, and have high clinical application value. Another study has demonstrated that transient postoperative elevations in ALT and AST levels can serve as predictive indicators for the response to TACE treatment, offering new insights into the application of traditional biomarkers [38]. Although GGT reflects relatively single biological information, it has a significant correlation with complex liver function scoring systems such as Child-Pugh, suggesting that it has the potential to serve as a simple and practical alternative biomarker. It should be noted that the role of GGT in predicting the prognosis of TACE should not be viewed in isolation, but rather as an important auxiliary indicator for preoperative assessment of potential treatment benefits for patients.

In subgroup analysis, statical significance is respectively observed in subgroup North America (HR 1.36; 95%CI 1.11–1.67; P = 0.003), China (HR 1.70; 95%CI 1.44–2.01; P < 0.00001), and other areas of Asia (HR 1.97; 95%CI 1.55–2.50; P < 0.00001). The results indicate the presence of regional disparities in the association between GGT levels and prognosis, with a significantly stronger correlation observed in the Asian population compared to that in the North American population, while relevant studies involving the European population are insufficient for further evaluation. Current evidence does not establish a definitive association between γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT) levels and geographic or ethnic variations. However, multiple non-pathological factors, particularly alcohol consumption and obesity, may indirectly contribute to ethnic disparities in GGT concentrations [39]. Regional and ethnic differences in alcohol consumption patterns and obesity prevalence likely account for variations in GGT levels. Notably, higher rates of alcohol use and obesity in Western populations are associated with elevated GGT levels, which may diminish the prognostic significance of GGT in HCC patients. Furthermore, distinct etiological profiles of HCC exist across regions: viral hepatitis is the predominant cause of cirrhosis in Asia, whereas alcohol-related liver disease and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) are more prevalent in Western populations [40]. These differing underlying causes of cirrhosis result in divergent GGT expression profiles and potentially differential prognostic implications among ethnic groups. Considering that GGT is genetically regulated by the GGT1 locus and its heritability is reportedly estimated to be as high as 77% [41], the GGT1 gene polymorphisms of patients in different regions may cause variations in the correlation between GGT and the prognosis of TACE.

Nevertheless, it is noted that our meta-analysis had several limitations. The largest limitation of our meta-analysis was the inconsistency of the GGT cut-off value. It might not only lead to elevated heterogeneity but also increase the difficulty of making clinical decisions. In our included study, GGT cut-off values varied from 50 to 200 U/L and were determined by several different methods. For example, in the study of Zhang J, Chang Z, and Ventura Y, the cut-off values were determined by clinical reference interval (11–50 U/L) or reference data reported by other articles [16, 21, 27]. Log-rank survival analysis was also used in the study by Zhang J, and statistical significance was reported when the threshold was chosen between 60U/L and 300U/L [21]. Additionally, in the study of Guib B, Guo, and Patidar, the cut-off values were determined by ROC analysis, while the X-tile program was employed to determine the optimal cut-off value in the study of Le [17–20]. The results of our meta-regression analysis indicated that the cut-off value was not a primary source of heterogeneity in this study; however, it remains uncertain whether the cut-off value influences the correlation between GGT level and OS in HCC patients undergoing TACE, given that the methodology for determining the cut-off value may introduce confounding factors. Secondly, due to the lack of relevant studies, this meta-analysis only incorporated overall survival (OS) as an outcome indicator, while other commonly used indicators such as disease-free survival (DFS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) were not included in the analysis. Consequently, this study only demonstrates the predictive association between elevated preoperative GGT and poor survival rates, without being able to predict post-TACE recurrence in patients. Furthermore, the administration techniques and drug selection for TACE across the included studies lacked standardization. As most studies were retrospective in design, they may have been subject to various sources of bias, including confounding by indication, variability in imaging assessments, or unmeasured confounding factors, all of which could have influenced the study outcomes. In addition, serum GGT level can be influenced by other diseases, consisting of alcohol abuse [42], hepatobiliary disorder [9], cardiovascular disease [43], and pancreatic disease [44]. However, neither our screening criterion nor the included studies make a clear state of whether to exclude those conditions or estimate the influence of these factors on prognostic outcomes.

Considering the limitations mentioned above, the pooled HR for OS in our study may have a potential unavoidable bias. To further investigate the sources of heterogeneity among the included studies and potential publication bias, prospective, well-designed, and large-scale cohort studies were commanded to extract more convincing data, and further to concentrate on determining the optimal GGT threshold and investigating the variations in its predictive value across diverse regions and populations. Furthermore, it is necessary to further explore the clinical value of combined detection of other indicators with GGT to construct a more systematic and comprehensive prognostic evaluation system for TACE.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the result of this meta-analysis provide strong evidence that a higher pretreatment GGT has a close association with poorer prognosis for HCC patients treated with TACE, suggesting that pretreatment GGT has the potential value as a predictive marker for evaluating the prognosis of HCC patients treated with TACE.
