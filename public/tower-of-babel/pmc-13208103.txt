Insights into the seroprevalence of Toxocara spp. in Kuwait: A comparison between the general population and waste collectors
Authors: Anfal Yousef, Moh A Alkhamis, Meshal Daalah, Mohammad Al-Awadhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToxocara spp. are zoonotic nematodes transmitted primarily through environmental contamination with eggs from infected dogs and cats. Human infection is often underdiagnosed, particularly in high-income countries, and data from the Gulf region remain limited. This study aimed to investigate the seroprevalence of Toxocara spp. among the general adult population in Kuwait and to compare it with waste collectors, a vulnerable occupational group with frequent exposure to environmental contaminants and stray animals.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Kuwait. Serum samples were collected from 200 adults attending government polyclinics and 196 waste collectors recruited from residential compounds. Demographic data were obtained using structured questionnaires. Toxocara IgG antibodies were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), and statistical relationships with demographic variables were assessed.ResultsThe overall prevalence was 16%, while Toxocara IgG seroprevalence was significantly higher among waste collectors (24.5%) compared with the general adult population (7.5%) (p-value <0.01). Significant statistical relationships were observed between seropositivity and nationality (Southeast Asian), age (>40 years), and lower educational level within the general population (p-values <0.05).ConclusionsToxocara infection is present at moderate levels in Kuwait, with substantially higher seroprevalence among waste collectors, suggesting potential occupational and environmental risk despite the country's high-income status. This may also partly reflect prior exposure in endemic regions before migration. These findings highlight the need for integrated One Health surveillance incorporating human, animal, and environmental components to inform targeted prevention strategies.Keywords: Toxocara, Toxocariasis, One health, Epidemiology, Kuwait, Occupational risk

Introduction

Human toxocariasis is a global zoonotic helminth infection that remains largely neglected despite its significant public health relevance. Infection is closely linked to environmental contamination, particularly in settings with high densities of stray cats and dogs, limited parasite control measures in these animals (e.g., regular deworming), and frequent human-environment contact [1]. The lack of surveillance and limited epidemiological data in many regions have likely led to an underestimation of its true burden.

Toxocara species (spp.) are nematode helminths that can infect various mammals, serving as either definitive or paratenic hosts. Among the 26 identified Toxocara species, T. canis and T. cati are of zoonotic importance and are commonly transmitted from dogs (and other canids) and cats (and other felids) to humans, respectively. In humans, the primary transmission route is through the ingestion of embryonated eggs released by dogs or cats (the definitive hosts), which contaminate the environment, particularly the soil [2]. These eggs can be ingested via soil-contaminated food, water, fomites, or by the hand-to-mouth route, especially in children exposed to contaminated environments such as playgrounds and parks [1], [2]. Less commonly, transmission may occur through direct contact with the contaminated fur of infected definitive hosts. Another significant transmission route is through eating the encysted larvae in undercooked or raw meat from paratenic hosts, such as chickens, cattle, pigs, or sheep [2].

Humans are accidental hosts for Toxocara spp., and the parasite may remain encysted within tissues of various organs, such as the liver, kidneys, lungs, and central nervous system. This triggers a pro-inflammatory response in humans, leading to the development of symptoms associated with toxocariasis [3]. Although toxocariasis is often asymptomatic, it can manifest in four different forms. The most common, covert toxocariasis (CT), can be self-limiting and may present with mild non-specific symptoms, such as fever and abdominal and respiratory symptoms. Elevated levels of eosinophils and immunoglobulin E (IgE) are seen in CT patients, suggesting a possible link to asthma and urticaria [4]. The second form, visceral larva migrans (VLM), is often characterised by allergic responses triggered by larval migration to various organs. For example, migration to the lungs can cause asthma-like symptoms, migration to the liver can result in abdominal pain and hepatomegaly, and migration through the skin can lead to cutaneous lesions. The third form of toxocariasis, ocular larva migrans (OLM), predominantly affects the eyes of infected children due to larval migration and results in inflammation, which can impair vision and potentially lead to blindness. The last and most serious form, occurring mainly in adults, neurotoxocariasis (NT), involves larval migration to the central nervous system, causing neurological and neuropsychological symptoms [4], [5].

Globally, approximately 19% of the human population is seropositive for toxocariasis [4], [5]. Although toxocariasis is predominantly reported in tropical areas and regions with low socioeconomic status, it also has a relatively high prevalence in high-income, developed countries [[3], [4]]. The global health burden of toxocariasis was estimated at 91,714 Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALY) annually [5]. These statistics highlight toxocariasis as a public health concern that warrants greater attention. While Ma et al. have suggested that the Middle East region has the lowest global prevalence of human toxocariasis (8.2%), prevalence data of toxocariasis for many countries in the region remain limited or unavailable [[4], [6]]. However, they estimated that Egypt had the highest prevalence of toxocariasis in humans (32%), followed by Jordan and Iran [6].

Only a single study has investigated human Toxocara infection in Kuwait. The study was conducted on patients with allergic conditions, particularly those with elevated eosinophilia, and demonstrated a seropositivity of 2.5% among 400 patients tested [7]. In addition, a 2014 study reported that among 240 stray cats in Kuwait, 1.3% were infected with Toxocara cati [8]. In recent years, Kuwait has experienced a marked rise in the population of stray cats and dogs [[7], [9]]. Despite this, no formal governmental interventions have yet been implemented to mitigate the associated public health risks of this phenomenon. To date, no studies have investigated the prevalence of Toxocara spp. in other animal reservoirs, particularly in dogs, nor environmental sources in Kuwait.

This study aimed to investigate the seroprevalence of Toxocara spp. in Kuwait among the general adult population, in comparison with waste collectors, a vulnerable occupational group with frequent exposure to stray cats and dogs, as well as other environmental contaminants, potentially placing them at increased risk of infection [10]. Both groups were studied across two governorates, Kuwait City (the Capital) and Jahra, reflecting environmental conditions and population dynamics [11]. Serological detection was performed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), supported by demographic data from both populations.

Toxocariasis represents a clear One Health challenge, with transmission sustained at the human-animal-environment interface. The dearth of epidemiological research in Kuwait and neighbouring countries highlights the need to assess the current burden of Toxocara infection. In this context, baseline seroprevalence studies are essential to define population infection, identify vulnerable groups and inform evidence-based public health strategies aimed at preventing zoonotic transmission.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study to investigate the seroprevalence of Toxocara spp. infections in Kuwait. Two groups were included: waste collectors, as they represent the highest susceptible occupational group, and the general adult population. Blood samples from waste collectors were obtained in February 2023, while samples from the adult community were collected in September 2024. Demographic and exposure-related data for both groups were gathered through structured questionnaires. All sera were analysed in a single batch using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to detect Toxocara IgG antibodies.

Study area

This study was conducted in two governorates of Kuwait: Kuwait City and Jahra. Jahra is located approximately thirty kilometres from Kuwait City and is the largest of the six governorates. Unlike Kuwait City, which is the capital of Kuwait and highly urbanised, Jahra is characterised by its semi-urbanised environment and agricultural landscape, which is suitable for the circulation of pathogens like Toxocara. These governorates were selected to capture variation in socio-economic and environmental conditions, which may influence the prevalence of Toxocara infections [[10], [11]]. General populations

According to the Ministry of Health's ethical guidelines, blood samples from the general population were required to be collected in government polyclinics to ensure safety and proper handling. At the time of the study, a total of 15 and 12 polyclinics were operating in Kuwait City and Jahra, respectively. In accordance with guidance from the Ministry of Health Ethical Committee, and to avoid disruption of routine clinical services, three polyclinics were randomly selected from a list of all polyclinics in each governorate, while still ensuring geographic representation and feasibility of data collection. In Kuwait City, participants were recruited from Bneid Algar – Ibrahim Marafie Healthcare Centre, Faiha - Abdulrahman Al-Abdulmoghni Healthcare Centre, and Alyarmouk - Abdullah Al-Abdulhadi Healthcare Centre. In Jahra, participants were recruited from Saad Al-Abdullah Healthcare Centre, Alqasr Healthcare Centre, and Jahra Healthcare Centre. Within each polyclinic, systematic randomisation was applied by inviting every second individual in the waiting areas, regardless of their role (patients, accompanying persons, or staff).

Waste workers

Waste collectors were recruited from the National Cleaning Company, a government-run company working in collaboration with the Kuwait municipality to provide waste management services across the country, and the sole provider of this service in both cities. Workers are housed in designated residential compounds according to their assigned governorates. For this study, Sabhan residential complex was visited to recruit workers from Kuwait City, while the Sulaibiya residential complex was used for Jahra. Participants were selected by systematic random sampling, whereby every second worker encountered was invited to participate. Blood samples and completed questionnaires were collected on-site at the time of recruitment.

Sample size

For the general population, no Gulf-region data on Toxocara seroprevalence were available; therefore, an estimate from Jordan, a nearby country with a relatively similar population structure and cultural practices, was used, where seroprevalence was reported at approximately 11% [6]. Based on this estimate, the required sample size was calculated to be 151 participants at a 95% confidence level with a 5% margin of error. For the waste worker group, an expected seroprevalence of 13% was assumed based on data from waste collectors in Mexico [12]. Using a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 174 participants. However, the final sample size comprised 200 participants from the general population and 196 waste collectors.

Inclusion of participants required to be aged 18 years or older and residing within the designated study areas. Individuals who did not meet these criteria were excluded from participation. A total of 27 individuals from the general population and 10 waste collectors declined participation.

Sample collection

Approximately 5 ml of venous blood was drawn from all participants. For the general adult population, samples were collected by certified phlebotomists at designated polyclinics, whereas samples from the waste collector group were obtained by a Ministry of Health-assigned registered nurse at designated work sites. In a temperature-controlled box, all samples were transported to Kuwait University, College of Allied Health Sciences, the Parasitology Laboratory, for processing. Serum was separated under standard laboratory conditions and stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

Demographic data

A structured questionnaire was used to collect demographic data from all participants. The questionnaire, previously validated and used to assess Toxoplasma gondii seroprevalence among waste collectors in Kuwait [10], was adopted (with minor modifications) for the present study.

It was available in both Arabic and English and was used to obtain demographic information from participants in the general population group. The collected data included gender, age, nationality, recent travel history (defined as travel within six months or less at the time of blood collection), educational level, and recent exposure to cats or dogs. For the waste collector group, the questionnaire was provided in additional languages: Arabic, English, Bengali, and Hindi to accommodate the diverse multilingual workforce. Similar data were collected for waste workers, excluding gender, educational level and exposure to animals, as all waste workers were male, had lower educational attainment and had reported previous recent exposure to animals through their occupation.

Ethical considerations

This research was approved by the ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Kuwait University (Project No. ZN02/21 and NM01/23) and the Ministry of Health of Kuwait (Ref No. 1708/2021 and 2023/2500). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA): IgG antibodies

All serum samples were tested for Toxocara IgG antibodies using the Toxocara canis IgG ELISA kits (IBL International, Tecan, GmbH, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions. To eliminate potential cross-reactivity, additional IgG antibody tests were performed for Ascaris lumbricoides and Schistosoma mansoni using the corresponding ELISA kits from the same manufacturer (IBL International, Tecan, GmbH, Germany). All ELISA procedures were performed at the Research Core Facility (RCF), College of Medicine, Health Sciences Centre, Kuwait University.

Statistical data analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 10.6.1) was used to statistically analyse all data. Statistical relationships between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test and Fisher's exact test. A two-tailed p-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using 2 × 2 contingency tables to assess associations between potential risk factors and Toxocara seropositivity. Seroprevalence confidence intervals were calculated using the modified Wald method, and odds ratio confidence intervals were computed using the Baptista–Pike method. The reciprocal of the OR was presented in text to facilitate interpretation, where applicable.

Results

Demographic data

General population

A total of 200 adults were enrolled in the study as part of the general population group. Prior to blood collection, all participants completed a structured questionnaire capturing demographic and exposure variables, including gender, age, nationality, educational attainment, recent travel history, and contact with dogs or cats.

Females constituted the majority of participants in both governorates, representing 64.4% of the total. Overall, 55% of participants were aged 40 years or younger. Age was recorded as a continuous variable and subsequently categorised into two groups: ≤ 40 years and > 40 years, for analysis. The cutoff at 40 years corresponded closely to the median age of the study population, ensuring balanced group sizes and minimising potential age-related confounding when comparing the groups. Categorisation was applied to facilitate statistical analysis and interpretation, particularly given the sample size and the exploratory nature of the study. The sample for both cities consisted mostly of Kuwaiti nationals (72.5%), while expatriates comprised (27.5%). Additionally, the expatriate portion of the sample comprised 16% for East Asia (India, Sri Lanka, The Philippines and Bangladesh) and 11.5% from Arab ethnicities (Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Stateless/Bedoon, Bahrain, Jordan, Tunisia, Iraq and Syria). A larger proportion of participants (61.5%) reported recent travel abroad, within the past six months before sampling. In terms of education, the majority had completed high school or held a diploma (57%), followed by bachelor's degree holders (37.5%), and a small proportion holding postgraduate qualifications (5.5%). Most individuals (77%) reported no recent exposure to cats or dogs.

Waste workers

All waste collectors were males. Approximately half were older than 40 years (52%). Most participants were of South Asian origin (90%), primarily from Bangladesh, India, Nepal, the Philippines, and Pakistan. Arab workers accounted for 10%, mainly from Egypt and Yemen. Most workers (69%) reported no international travel within the previous six months of sampling. All waste collectors reported high school as their highest level of education. In terms of environmental exposure, all waste collectors reported recent contact with cats or dogs owing to the nature of their work.

Adult community population

The seropositivity of Toxocara IgG antibodies among the 200 adult community participants was 7.5% (15/200, 95% CI: 4.5–12.1%). In Kuwait City, 11% (11/100, 95% CI: 6.1–18.8%) of individuals tested seropositive, while 4% (4/100, 95% CI: 1.2–10.2%) tested positive in Jahra. Additionally, two samples, one from each governorate, yielded equivocal results. The difference in seropositivity between the two governorates was not statistically significant (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.00–8.7; p = 0.1). Therefore, results from both locations were combined for subsequent analysis and interpretation.

Seropositivity was similarly distributed between females (53%) and males (47%), with no statistically significant difference observed based on gender (p = 0.34). The highest number of seropositive cases was found among participants of Southeast Asian origin, particularly from India (80%), a statistically significant association (OR = 13.4, 95% CI: 3.9–45.6; p < 0.01). A significant relationship was observed between age and Toxocara seropositivity (OR = 3.8, 95% CI: 1.3–11.1; p = 0.03), with higher positivity among individuals aged over 40 years (73%) compared to those aged 40 years or younger (27%).

Recent travel history was not associated with significant differences in seropositivity (p = 0.23). Seven seropositive participants reported recent travel (47%), while eight had not travelled outside of Kuwait within the past six months (53%). Educational background of individuals showed a notable trend, with a higher number of positive cases among individuals holding a high school diploma or lower (87%) compared to only two cases among those with a bachelor's degree (13%). Educational level showed a significant relationship with seropositivity (OR = 5.5, 95% CI: 1.4–25; p = 0.02). Recent contact with dogs or cats was not significantly associated with Toxocara seropositivity (p = 0.5). Among the 15 seropositive participants, only two (13%) reported recent direct contact with cats or dogs.

Waste collectors

Among the 196 waste collectors enrolled, 24.5% (48/196, 95% CI: 19.0–31.0%) tested positive for Toxocara IgG antibodies across both governorates. The prevalence was 26.4% (14/53, 95% CI: 16.3–39.7%) in Kuwait City and 23.8% (34/143, 95% CI: 17.5–31.4%) in Jahra, with no statistically significant difference between the two governorates. Consistent with the analysis for the general population, subsequent evaluations of risk factors were performed on the combined dataset rather than stratified by governorate.

All waste collectors in Kuwait were male, reported only up to high-school level education, and had occupational exposure to cats or dogs. Because these three characteristics were constant across the group, they could not be assessed as independent potential risk factors in this study.

When stratified by age, workers aged ≤40 years were more frequently seropositive (60%) than those older than 40 years, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07). The majority of seropositive cases were among Southeast Asian workers, predominantly from Bangladesh (87.5%), followed by individuals from India, Nepal, and the Philippines. Only two seropositive cases (6%) were identified among Egyptian workers. A statistically significant difference was observed in Toxocara IgG seropositivity between Bangladeshi workers and all other nationalities combined (OR = 3.8, 95% CI: 1.6–9.0; p = 0.003). Most waste collectors (73%) reported no recent travel outside Kuwait within the six months prior to sampling, and travel history was not significantly associated with seropositivity (OR = 1.3, 95% CI: 0.6–2.5; p = 0.6). Adult community population

Among the 15 Toxocara-seropositive samples, 12 (80%) were positive solely for Toxocara IgG. Three samples (20%) showed concurrent seropositivity for both Toxocara and Ascaris IgG antibodies. Additionally, one sample yielded an equivocal result for Toxocara IgG but tested positive for Ascaris IgG alone.

Of the three samples positive for both Toxocara and Ascaris antibodies, two were from Kuwaiti females younger than 40 years, both with lower educational levels and no reported recent animal exposure. One of the participants reported recent travel, whereas the other did not. The third participant was a Sri Lankan male older than 40 years, with a lower educational level, and no history of recent travel or animal exposure. The sample positive for Ascaris IgG alone was obtained from a Filipino participant with recent travel to the Philippines.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the seroprevalence of Toxocara spp. infections among the adult community population and waste collectors in two governorates in Kuwait: Kuwait City and Jahra. No statistically significant geographic difference was observed between Kuwait City and Jahra in seropositivity, suggesting that both governorates share similar environmental exposures and behavioural drivers influencing infection risk. Waste collectors had a prevalence of 24.5% Toxocara IgG antibodies, significantly higher than that of the general population (7.5%) (P < 0.01). Approximately one in 13 individuals from the general population was seropositive for Toxocara, compared with one in four waste collectors.

Comparative analysis between the general population and waste collectors should be interpreted with caution. Waste collectors in Kuwait were homogeneous, consisting exclusively of non-Kuwaiti males, predominantly with low educational attainment, and all reported exposure to cats or dogs. In contrast, the general population sample was more heterogeneous, with gender diversity, a wider range of educational levels, and variable exposure histories. The observed difference in prevalence between the two groups, therefore, reflects both occupational exposure and or underlying demographic and socioeconomic factors. In addition, as many waste workers originated from countries where Toxocara is endemic, prior exposure before arrival in Kuwait cannot be excluded.

Three demographic variables had statistically significant relationships with Toxocara infections within the general adult population: nationality, age, and educational level. The highest number of seropositive individuals were those from Southeast Asian origin, specifically from India, Sri Lanka, the Philippines, and Bangladesh. In addition, the highest prevalence of Toxocara antibodies was seen among the waste collector group, who were predominantly from Southeast Asian countries, particularly Bangladeshis. These countries are generally of low socioeconomic status, a recognised risk factor for Toxocara infections [[13], [14], [15]]. Additionally, individuals with a high school or diploma level of education were significantly associated with Toxocara seropositivity within the general population. Similarly, all waste collectors in Kuwait had a low educational level. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified low educational attainment as another risk factor for Toxocara infection [[13], [14], [15], [16]].

The observed predominance of female participants in the general population sample may reflect differences in healthcare-seeking behaviour, as females are more likely to attend polyclinics for routine check-ups and other medical concerns [17]. Since recruitment in this study was conducted at polyclinics, this may have resulted in a higher proportion of female participants within this group. However, no significant association between gender and seropositivity was observed, suggesting that this imbalance is unlikely to have influenced the main outcome.

Individuals over the age of 40 were more likely to be seropositive for Toxocara within the general population. In contrast, within the waste collector group, a higher proportion of seropositive individuals was observed among those aged ≤40 years compared to those >40 years; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. While both adults and children are at risk of Toxocara transmission, multiple studies have reported higher prevalence among younger individuals, likely due to behavioural factors and greater environmental exposure, particularly in contaminated soil found in public parks, beaches, or gardens [[14], [18]]. Given that waste collectors constitute an occupationally high-risk group with frequent exposure to environmental contaminants, as well as increased contact with stray dogs and cats, it is plausible that all workers, regardless of age, had similar levels of exposure to infection due to the nature of their job. In addition, seropositivity detected by ELISA reflects previous exposure rather than recent infection, as anti-Toxocara IgG antibodies may persist for prolonged periods, possibly for years following infection [[7], [19]]. Thus, in the general population, the higher seroprevalence observed among older individuals may reflect cumulative exposure over time. Given that the age-related difference in waste collectors was not statistically significant, this finding should be interpreted with caution. This study did not include individuals under 18 years of age due to ethical limitations; future research should evaluate and compare seroprevalence in this younger population. A further limitation is that the duration of employment as a waste collector was not assessed, which may influence cumulative exposure and seropositivity.

Although travel history was not significantly associated with seropositivity, local infection in Kuwait cannot be excluded. Other studies have reported associations between Toxocara seropositivity and direct contact with cats or dogs [[16], [20], [21]]. Globally, the prevalence of Toxocara canis in dogs was estimated at 11–50% in 2020, with the WHO Eastern Mediterranean region reporting the highest rate (19%) [[5], [22]]. Risk factors for infection in dogs included stray status, male dogs, young age (under one year), and residence in rural areas [22]. In contrast, the global prevalence of Toxocara catis in cats was reported at 17%, with the highest rates observed in Africa (43%), followed by the Eastern Mediterranean region (22%). Cats that were stray (29%), young, or living in low-income areas had higher infection rates, though pet cats were also affected, albeit at lower levels (12%) [23]. Consistent with these findings, a recent study had shown that the Middle East presented with a high prevalence of Toxocara in cats from Iran (44%), Iraq (37%), and Egypt (30%) [24]. Environmental contamination represents an additional and important route of transmission, with approximately 21% of soil samples reported to be contaminated with Toxocara spp. eggs worldwide [4], [5]. Iran had high levels of soil contamination (20%), alongside substantial infection rates in both cats (26%) and dogs (16%) [6]. Within a One Health framework, stray cats and dogs, as well as owned pets, should be examined for Toxocara spp. infections through veterinary surveillance. Collecting data on deworming practices among pet owners may also provide valuable insights into transmission dynamics and support the development of targeted control measures.

In the present study, no significant association was observed between Toxocara seropositivity and self-reported cat or dog exposure among the adult community group. However, all waste collectors were occupationally exposed to multiple environmental contaminants, including frequent contact with areas inhabited by stray cats and dogs. These findings suggest that indirect exposure pathways may play an important role in transmission. Future studies should therefore focus on investigating alternative transmission routes, such as contaminated food, water, and soil [25].

Serological testing remains the first-line diagnostic approach in epidemiological studies, especially for screening immunocompetent individuals who are often asymptomatic. Such tests are favoured due to their effectiveness, speed, simplicity, and cost-efficiency. In this study, we employed ELISA, which is widely recognised as a reliable method for detecting antibodies [[6], [26], [27], [28]]. For Toxocara testing, we used a commercial assay, the IBL international ELISA kit, which has been widely applied in several published studies with reliable and reproducible results [[7], [29], [30]]. These kits have been selected to enable comparison with findings from similar studies worldwide.

False-positive results due to cross-reactivity with antigens from other helminths are a recognised limitation of ELISA testing, including potential cross-reactivity with Schistosoma spp., Ascaris lumbricoides and Trichinella spiralis [31]. In this study, 20% of Toxocara seropositive samples from the general adult population and 10% from waste collectors were also positive for Ascaris IgG antibodies. Additionally, 4% of Toxocara seropositive samples among waste collectors were also positive for Schistosoma IgG antibodies. Confirmatory testing, such as Western blot assay, can help differentiate true Toxocara infections from serological cross-reactivity or identify possible co-infections [[32], [33]]. However, as this study was designed as a population-based seroprevalence screening rather than a diagnostic investigation, further confirmatory assays were not performed, primarily due to cost restraints. Although Kuwait is considered a non-endemic country for these parasites, the possibility of local Ascaris transmission cannot be excluded, given its similar soil-mediated transmission route to Toxocara [34]. In contrast, the absence of suitable intermediate hosts, particularly freshwater snails required for Schistosoma spp. transmission, making local transmission of schistosomiasis unlikely [35]. Moreover, all seropositive participants were non-nationals originating from endemic countries, suggesting that some infections may have been acquired prior to arrival in Kuwait rather than through local transmission.

This study reports seropositivity for Toxocara IgG antibodies only, as additional antibodies, including IgM and IgG avidity, were not commercially available at the time of the study. Moreover, antibody titres may reflect a recent or past exposure; however, they cannot reliably confirm the timing or activity of infection when based on a single serum sample without subsequent follow-up testing [36].

Waste collectors have been identified as a high occupational risk group for Toxoplasma gondii in Kuwait [10]. Another parasite, with a similar transmission route to humans as Toxocara, could cause a significant human disease, particularly in pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals. Among 201 serum samples tested, 21% were positive for T. gondii IgG antibodies. In comparison with the current study findings, waste collectors are considered an occupational hazard to possibly other medically important parasites that need further investigation. A study done on waste pickers in Mexico reported a Toxocara prevalence of 13%, compared with only 1% in a control group [12]. In contrast, this present study in Kuwait demonstrated a higher seroprevalence. Occupational groups with frequent exposure to soil or animals, such as slaughterhouse workers, farmers and agricultural labourers, may also have an increased risk of Toxocara infection [37].

Several high-income countries, such as New Zealand, Japan, Denmark, Austria, Sweden, the USA and Ireland have shown comparatively low to moderate seroprevalence levels at 0.7%, 1.6%, 2.4%, 6.3%, 7%, 14% and 31% respectively. In contrast, lower socioeconomic countries, including Iran and Nepal, have documented substantially higher levels at 22% and 81%, respectively [4]. In this context, our study revealed a moderate prevalence of Toxocara IgG antibodies, with 24.5% among waste workers and 7.5% among the general adult population. Furthermore, in Kuwait City, approximately one in ten adults in the general population was infected with Toxocara. These findings suggest a possible influence of occupational and environmental exposure on infection risk, even within a high-income setting.

Within a One Health framework, population mobility and migration represent important factors influencing the spread of zoonotic parasitic infections such as Toxocara [38]. Individuals from endemic regions may carry infections acquired prior to arrival, contributing to seroprevalence in non-endemic settings. In Kuwait, where a large proportion of the workforce consists of expatriates, it is important to consider the interplay between prior exposure, occupational risks, and environmental factors. Targeted screening and improved surveillance of mobile populations may support earlier detection and better understanding of transmission dynamics.

Conclusions

Overall, Toxocara infection was moderately prevalent in the State of Kuwait, yet with notably high prevalence among waste workers. However, this may partly reflect prior exposure among waste workers originating from endemic regions, given the high proportion of migrants in Kuwait. Additionally, statistically significant relationships were identified between Toxocara seropositivity and education, age, and nationality among adults in the general population. The expansion of the geographic epidemiological map of toxocariasis beyond traditionally low-income countries illustrates the persistence of neglected tropical diseases within vulnerable populations. These findings highlight the importance of adopting the One Health approach to address the transmission of Toxocara within the community. This would acknowledge the integrated strategy of human, animal, and environmental health. Continuous investigation of parasites such as Toxocara is essential to support the effective implementation of surveillance programmes and to guide the control and prevention strategies. This includes surveillance of domestic and stray animal populations, mainly cats and dogs, alongside regular deworming coordinated by veterinarians, in addition to monitoring potential environmental contaminants such as soil, water, and food sources, which should also be emphasised as key preventive measures. A coordinated multidisciplinary framework between public health authorities, veterinary services, and environmental agencies is essential for reducing parasite burden and minimising the risk of transmission to vulnerable populations.
