Heart Rate as a Predictor of 6‐Month Pneumonia Risk in Patients With Dysphagia
Authors: Yuval Nachalon, Nogah Nativ‐Zeltzer, Yosef Kula, Anne Tran, Stephanie Reshma Warrior, Peter C. Belafsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo determine whether resting heart rate (HR) or heart rate variability (HRV) derived from finger photoplethysmography (PPG) predicts 6‐month pneumonia risk in patients with dysphagia. HRV reflects autonomic regulation of inflammation which may predispose to respiratory complications.MethodsA total of 301 patients who underwent a videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) in 2020–2021 were enrolled. Prior to VFSS, resting HR was measured using the CorSense HRV finger sensor (ELITE HRV). Additional HRV metrics, including the standard deviation of normal‐to‐normal intervals (SDNN) and the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) were collected from a subset of 153 participants. Pneumonia incidence was assessed at 3 and 6 months through phone interviews and medical record review.ResultsThe mean age was 66.3 ± 12.5 years, and 38% were female. Twenty patients (7%) developed pneumonia, with 75% of cases occurring within 3 months. Patients who developed pneumonia had higher resting HR than those who did not (83.4 vs. 74.3 bpm, p < 0.01). This association remained significant after adjusting for age (p = 0.01). Pneumonia risk increased stepwise with higher HR thresholds, with a 2.4‐fold greater risk observed at HR ≥ 82 bpm and a 2.8‐fold risk at HR ≥ 90 bpm. HRV metrics were not significantly associated with pneumonia incidence.ConclusionElevated resting HR, though within the normal range, was associated with increased pneumonia risk in patients with dysphagia. This may reflect underlying inflammation or autonomic dysfunction. Because comorbidities, medications, and VFSS severity were not accounted for, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Resting HR may represent a simple, non‐invasive tool for pneumonia risk stratification in this population, but larger studies are needed for validation.Level of Evidence2.

Introduction

Dysphagia is a well‐established independent risk factor for pneumonia. It affects more than half of nursing home residents and 20%–65% of stroke patients. Among stroke patients, those with dysphagia have up to an 11‐fold greater risk of pneumonia compared with patients without dysphagia [1, 2, 3]. Analysis of over 77 million hospital admissions has shown that patients with dysphagia experience significantly longer hospital stays (4.04 vs. 2.4 days) and higher mortality rates [4]. In a 2‐year prospective study of 689 patients with dysphagia, 22% developed pneumonia, with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (OR = 2.36), hypertension (OR = 1.82), tracheotomy status (OR = 2.96), and vallecular residue (OR = 1.88) serving as independent predictors [5].

Beyond mechanical swallowing impairment, chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation also predispose elderly individuals to pneumonia, as seen in stroke‐induced immunosuppression and altered cytokine signaling [6, 7]. These inflammatory processes are closely regulated by the autonomic nervous system, which also controls cardiovascular function [8, 9]. Given these mechanistic links between autonomic regulation, inflammation, and infection risk, identifying early, non‐invasive predictors of pneumonia risk in this population is critical for timely intervention and improved outcomes.

Resting heart rate (HR) is increasingly recognized as a biomarker of systemic stress and chronic inflammation, both of which are prevalent in patients with dysphagia and may contribute to increased pneumonia risk. Elevated resting HR has been associated with increased levels of inflammatory biomarkers, including C‐reactive protein and interleukin‐6, suggesting a potential link between autonomic dysfunction and systemic inflammation [10]. Recent evidence specifically links autonomic nervous system dysfunction to pneumonia development, with alterations in HR and its variability preceding the onset of respiratory complications by several days [11].

Heart rate variability (HRV), reflecting the variation in time intervals between consecutive heartbeats, provides insight into autonomic nervous system function and has been proposed as a biomarker of systemic inflammation and stress response. Reduced HRV has been associated with elevated inflammatory markers and may serve as an early indicator of physiological vulnerability to infection.

This study aims to investigate whether resting HR and HRV metrics, derived from finger photoplethysmography (PPG), can predict 6‐month pneumonia risk in patients with dysphagia. If validated, these measures could provide a simple, cost‐effective tool for early pneumonia risk assessment, enabling more targeted preventive interventions in this high‐risk population.

Methods

The protocol was approved by the University of California Davis Institutional Review Board (protocol #1498541) and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study. All patients undergoing a videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) at our center between the years 2020 and 2021 were offered the opportunity to participate in the study. Patients with total laryngectomy were excluded from the study.

Prior to the VFSS, subjects' HR and HRV were measured using a CorSense heart rate variability monitor (ELITE HRV, Gloucester, Massachusetts) attached to the index finger for a duration of 2 min while seated. Resting HR and HRV (Elite HRV) were measured for all participants. Additional HRV metrics (standard deviation of normal‐to‐normal intervals [SDNN] and the root mean square of successive differences [RMSSD]) were collected from a subset of 153 patients. RMSSD measures the square root of the mean of the squares of differences between successive normal heartbeats (NN intervals). It reflects short‐term HRV and is primarily influenced by parasympathetic (vagal) activity. SDNN (Standard Deviation of NN intervals) measures the standard deviation of all normal‐to‐normal (NN) intervals over a period of time and is an HRV measure that incorporates both sympathetic and parasympathetic influences. According to the manufacturer, the Elite HRV measurement is calculated by the device by applying a natural log (ln) to RMSSD and scaling to a 0–100 range, designed from a reference dataset of over 1 million recordings.

Phone follow‐up interviews were conducted 3 and 6 months following the study. During the phone interview, participants were asked to report on major changes to their health in the recent 3 months, on the occurrence of pneumonia and related hospitalization due to the pneumonia. Medical records of the patients were also reviewed for documentation of pneumonia.

Statistical Analyses

Logistic regression was conducted to identify possible predictors of pneumonia development. Spearman's rho was used to assess correlations between the metrics. Because of multicollinearity, each PPG metric was analyzed in a separate regression model. Group differences in HR between pneumonia and non‐pneumonia patients were assessed using independent samples t‐tests, and Cohen's d was reported as a measure of effect size.

Results

A total of 301 patients enrolled in the study. The mean age (±standard deviation) of the cohort was 66.3 years (±12.5) and 38% (114/301) were female. Twenty patients (7%) developed pneumonia within 6 months following the VFSS, with 15 cases (75%) occurring within the first 3 months.

Higher resting HR was significantly associated with increased risk of pneumonia. The mean HR in pneumonia cases was 83.4 versus 74.3 bpm (mean difference = 9.1 bpm, 95% CI: 3.1–15.0, p < 0.01), corresponding to a moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.61) (Figure 1). This association remained significant after adjusting for age (p = 0.01). Specifically, resting HR showed moderate negative correlation with SDNN (r = −0.248, p = 0.002), weak negative correlation with RMSSD (r = −0.162, p = 0.041), and weak negative correlation with Elite HRV (r = −0.204, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that higher HR was consistently associated with reduced HRV across metrics. Strong correlations were also observed between the various HRV metrics themselves: SDNN and RMSSD (r = 0.844, p < 0.001), SDNN and Elite HRV (r = 0.845, p < 0.001), and RMSSD and Elite HRV (r = 0.996, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that higher resting HR, measured immediately before VFSS, is associated with increased risk of 6‐month pneumonia in patients with dysphagia. The mean HR in pneumonia cases (83.4 bpm) was still within the normal range (60–100 bpm). However, even this relative elevation may be pathophysiologically significant in vulnerable patients. In medically vulnerable populations, such as older adults with dysphagia, even modest increases in resting HR have been linked to systemic inflammation and poorer outcomes. These findings add to prior research highlighting the utility of accessible clinical markers for identifying individuals at increased risk [2, 5]. The timing analysis further shows that the majority of pneumonia cases (75%) occurred within the first 3 months after VFSS assessment, suggesting that the predictive window for HR measurements is most relevant for near‐term risk stratification. Elevated resting HR has been linked to cardiopulmonary outcomes in general populations, but its association with pneumonia specifically among dysphagia patients undergoing VFSS has not previously been reported. This underscores the potential of HR to augment dysphagia‐related risk stratification.

HRV metrics (SDNN, RMSSD), measured in a subset of participants, were not significantly associated with pneumonia risk in our cohort. This may reflect age‐related changes in autonomic function, as Zulfiqar et al. [12] demonstrated that HRV metrics follow complex age‐dependent trajectories. The predictive value of HR in our study may thus stem from mechanisms independent of short‐term autonomic variability, possibly reflecting longer‐term physiological adaptations or underlying inflammatory processes. The PAS score from VFSS, which reflects mechanical risk of airway invasion, was not analyzed here. Whereas PAS reflects mechanical risk, HR may capture systemic vulnerability through inflammation and autonomic dysfunction. Future studies should test the combined predictive value of PAS and HR, as these complementary predictors may together improve prognostication.

The relationship between relatively elevated HR and increased pneumonia risk likely reflects inflammation and autonomic dysfunction. The vagus nerve plays a central role in controlling inflammation, and its dysfunction may contribute to both elevated HR and increased infection susceptibility [8]. Whelton et al. [10] found that elevated HR correlated with higher levels of C‐reactive protein, interleukin‐6, and fibrinogen across a large multi‐ethnic cohort, independent of physical activity and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. This relationship between autonomic function and inflammation suggests a potential mechanistic link between sympathetic activation and systemic inflammatory processes. Hoffmann et al. [7] further showed that dysphagia combined with low monocytic HLA‐DR expression (as a marker of immunosuppression) dramatically increased stroke‐associated pneumonia risk. These findings suggest that immune dysfunction and swallowing impairment may represent distinct but complementary pathways contributing to pneumonia. Our results are consistent with this model, as HR (potentially reflecting both autonomic and inflammatory dysfunction) may help identify at‐risk patients.

The integration of HR monitoring into dysphagia assessment offers practical advantages. It is objective, non‐invasive, and requires minimal training or equipment. If validated in controlled studies, recent advances in wearable devices could further enhance its value. Continuous vital sign monitoring detects more deterioration events than manual checks [13]. Wearable sensors perform comparably to hospital monitors even during movement [14]. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, continuous monitoring systems enabled remote patient tracking for extended periods [15]. These data highlight the potential of HR monitoring as a simple, scalable tool for early detection and surveillance.

A critical limitation of our study is that we did not control for key confounders such as cardiac and pulmonary disease, the use of rhythm‐regulating medications (e.g., beta blockers, antiarrhythmics), or dysphagia severity measured by VFSS parameters such as PAS score. Each of these factors may influence both HR and pneumonia risk, and their omission reduces internal validity.

It is also important to note that HR reflects the net balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic tone. This balance may be altered by intrinsic disease processes, pharmacologic interventions, or external vagal stimulation, yet the physiological endpoint is the same: a shift in autonomic–inflammatory status. For example, beta blockers reduce sympathetic drive and have been shown to lower pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α while restoring balance with anti‐inflammatory cytokines like IL‐10 in patients with cardiomyopathy [16]. Vagal stimulation increases parasympathetic activity and attenuates systemic inflammation through the cholinergic anti‐inflammatory pathway [17]. Conversely, cardiac disease or systemic stress may elevate HR through sympathetic activation, which is associated with increased inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP and interleukin‐6 [10]. All of these influences modulate the autonomic–inflammatory axis that governs both HR and inflammatory susceptibility. From this perspective, resting HR may function as a final common pathway integrating multiple determinants of systemic vulnerability, which could explain its predictive value for pneumonia risk across heterogeneous patient subgroups. This hypothesis, that HR integrates multiple determinants of vulnerability rather than simply reflecting confounders, requires validation. Future studies should test whether HR adds predictive value after controlling for medications, comorbidities, and dysphagia severity. Such analyses would clarify whether HR functions as a pathway‐agnostic biomarker or primarily reflects these underlying conditions.

If validated, elevated resting HR could serve as a simple clinical alert to identify dysphagia patients at higher risk of pneumonia. Future studies should also explore whether interventions targeting autonomic balance can reduce pneumonia risk in this population. HR monitoring may ultimately represent a practical, non‐invasive addition to dysphagia assessment and preventive care.

Conclusion

Elevated resting HR may help identify dysphagia patients at higher risk of pneumonia. If validated in larger studies, it could become a practical addition to clinical assessment. These findings should be viewed as preliminary, and confirmation in larger, multicenter studies is needed before clinical application.
