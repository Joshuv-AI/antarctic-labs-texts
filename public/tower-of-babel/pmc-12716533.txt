Efficacy and safety of vagus nerve stimulation for the treatment of status epilepticus in children: a study protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yulan Luo, Ling Luo, Jiarong Zeng, Ming Ou, Chunmei He, Nanxie Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStatus epilepticus, particularly in children, is a neurological emergency with significant morbidity and mortality. When standard anticonvulsant therapies fail, vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has emerged as a potential acute intervention. However, its efficacy and safety remain uncertain due to a lack of robust evidence. This systematic review aims to synthesise existing data to evaluate the role of VNS in treating paediatric status epilepticus.Methods and analysisThis protocol follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols guidelines. A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and Web of Science will be conducted from inception to 31 October 2025. Studies involving paediatric patients (0–18 years) receiving VNS for status epilepticus in acute care settings will be included. Risk of bias will be assessed using Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 and ROBINS-I tools. If feasible, random-effects meta-analysis will be performed to pool effect estimates for primary outcomes; otherwise, narrative synthesis will be conducted. Heterogeneity will be evaluated using the I² statistic. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses will explore sources of variation.Ethics and disseminationAs this study is based on published literature, ethical approval is not required. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and presentations at relevant academic conferences, contributing to evidence-based management strategies for refractory paediatric status epilepticus.PROSPERO registration numberCRD 420251153939.

Introduction

Status epilepticus is a severe neurological emergency defined as a seizure or series of seizures lasting longer than 5 min without recovery of consciousness between episodes, or a prolonged seizure activity exceeding 30 min according to earlier definitions.1 2 Status epilepticus is particularly important in paediatrics—it is one of the most common acute neurological emergencies in children, with population-based estimates of annual incidence in childhood generally reported in the low 10s per 100 000.36 The condition carries measurable short-term mortality and a risk of long-term neurological sequelae among survivors, including cognitive impairment, behavioural problems and the later development of chronic epilepsy.3 7 8 Management of status epilepticus aims to terminate seizure activity promptly to prevent irreversible neuronal injury. Current international treatment guidelines recommend a stepwise pharmacological approach, initiating with benzodiazepines as first-line agents, followed by second-line antiseizure medications such as phenytoin, valproate or levetiracetam, and escalation to anaesthetic drugs in refractory cases.9 10 However, approximately 10%–40% of paediatric cases progress to refractory status epilepticus (RSE), defined as seizures persisting despite adequate first-line and second-line therapies, and a subset further advances to super-refractory status epilepticus (SRSE), lasting more than 24 hours despite ongoing anaesthetic treatment.1114 These severe forms of status epilepticus are associated with increased risk of systemic complications, prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stays and poor neurological prognosis.15 16 In addition, the use of continuous anaesthetic infusions, while often necessary, carries significant risks such as hypotension, respiratory depression, immunosuppression and nosocomial infections.17 Thus, there is an urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies with acceptable safety profiles.

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is a neuromodulatory therapy approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for treatment of drug-resistant epilepsy in patients aged ≥4 years.18 The therapy involves intermittent electrical stimulation of the left cervical vagus nerve via an implanted pulse generator, modulating central nervous system activity through afferent vagal fibres that project to brainstem nuclei and widespread cortical networks.19 20 While the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood, preclinical and clinical studies suggest that VNS may exert antiseizure effects through modulation of the locus coeruleus, enhancement of GABAergic tone, cortical desynchronisation and reduction of neuroinflammation.21 22 Over the past two decades, there has been growing interest in VNS as a potential rescue or adjunctive therapy for status epilepticus, particularly in RSE and SRSE cases unresponsive to conventional medical management. Several case reports, small retrospective series and limited prospective cohorts have described VNS implantation during ongoing status epilepticus in both adults and children, suggesting possible benefits such as earlier seizure termination, reduced seizure burden and prevention of recurrence.2327 Importantly, reported adverse events in paediatric status epilepticus populations have generally been mild and manageable, including transient hoarseness, cough and local discomfort.28 29 Nevertheless, the available evidence is heterogeneous, with variable patient selection criteria, timing of implantation, stimulation parameters and outcome definitions. Further, most published studies involve small sample sizes, limiting statistical power and generalisability.

To date, no comprehensive systematic review has specifically evaluated the efficacy and safety of VNS for the treatment of status epilepticus in children. The existing evidence on VNS mainly focuses on the treatment of chronic refractory epilepsy in adults, but there is still a lack of high-quality evidence to support the efficacy and safety of VNS in the field of paediatric status epilepticus.3033 Given the critical prognosis of paediatric status epilepticus and the challenges in managing RSE and SRSE, synthesising the available data is essential for informing clinical practice and guiding the design of future prospective studies.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to rigorously assess the efficacy and safety of VNS in the treatment of status epilepticus in children. Through a structured synthesis of current literature, we intend to provide evidence-based insights into the therapeutic role of VNS in this urgent clinical scenario, address the existing uncertainties and highlight priorities for future research.

Methods

This study will be reported strictly in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols statement guidelines.34 35

Inclusion criteria

Study types

Due to the life-threatening nature of status epilepticus and the anticipated scarcity of evidence, a broad range of study designs will be included to capture all available data. The hierarchy of evidence will be considered during analysis and synthesis.

Primary inclusion

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs): Considered the gold standard for efficacy, if any exist.

Non-RCTs (eg, quasi-experimental studies).

Secondary inclusion (observational studies)

Cohort studies: Prospective and retrospective studies comparing outcomes between groups that received VNS and those that received usual care.

Case-control studies: Studies that compare children with status epilepticus who received VNS (cases) with those who did not (controls).

Case series: Studies reporting on a series of paediatric patients (typically ≥3 patients) treated with VNS for status epilepticus.

Individual case reports: Will be included to capture details on rare adverse events, novel stimulation parameters and unique clinical scenarios, acknowledging their inherent limitations for assessing efficacy.

Excluded study

The following study types will be explicitly excluded:

Reviews, systematic reviews and meta-analyses (their reference lists will be hand-searched for eligible primary studies).

Editorials, commentaries and letters to the editor that do not present original patient data.

Conference abstracts: Will be excluded unless sufficient details on methods, participants, interventions and outcomes are provided on request from the authors. If no additional data can be obtained, they will be excluded to avoid bias from incomplete reporting.

Preclinical studies: (eg, in vitro or animal studies).

Simulation studies.

Participant types

Inclusion criteria

Age: Paediatric patients, defined as individuals aged 0–18 years at the time of treatment.

Condition: Patients diagnosed with status epilepticus, including RSE and SRSE, as defined by the treating clinicians or international guidelines (eg, ILAE criteria).

Exclusion criteria

Age: Studies exclusively involving adult patients (≥19 years of age) will be excluded.

Condition: Studies on patients with seizures that do not meet the definition of status epilepticus (eg, isolated seizures, clustered seizures) will be excluded.

Types of interventions and comparisons

Inclusion criteria for interventions

Type of intervention: The use of an implanted, programmable VNS device.

Application of intervention: The VNS device must be used as an acute therapeutic intervention during an ongoing episode of status epilepticus. This includes: Acute activation: The manual, triggered activation of an existing, chronically implanted VNS device using a magnet swipe. Parameter escalation: The acute, purposeful adjustment of the device’s programmed stimulation parameters (eg, increasing output current, pulse width, frequency or duty cycle) by a clinician to terminate status epilepticus device status.

The VNS may be either a pre-existing, chronically implanted device or a newly implanted device as a last-resort treatment for SRSE.

Exclusion criteria for interventions

Type of device: Non-invasive or transcutaneous VNS devices will be excluded, as the mechanism of delivery and stimulation paradigm differs significantly from implanted devices.

Intervention purpose: Studies where the VNS is used exclusively for chronic, prophylactic seizure management and not explicitly for the acute termination of an active status epilepticus episode will be excluded.

Non-VNS therapies: Any other neuromodulation therapies (eg, deep brain stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, transcranial magnetic stimulation) will be excluded.

Included comparisons

Usual care (standard antiseizure medications): This is the primary comparator. It encompasses the standard pharmacological protocol for status epilepticus, such as: first-line benzodiazepines (eg, lorazepam, midazolam); second-line antiseizure medications (eg, levetiracetam, fosphenytoin/phenytoin, valproic acid); third-line/anaesthetic agents (eg, midazolam infusion, propofol, pentobarbital/thiopental coma); other non-pharmacological interventions: This may include other adjunctive therapies used in refractory cases, such as: ketogenic diet initiation, immunotherapy (eg, steroids, IVIG).

Excluded comparisons

Placebo or sham stimulation: this is not a relevant or ethical comparator in the context of a life-threatening emergency like status epilepticus.

Other established interventions not part of standard status epilepticus protocols will not be considered as direct comparators.

Types of outcomes

Primary outcome

Efficacy: Seizure cessation. Definition: The termination of clinical and/or electrographic seizure activity, leading to the resolution of the status epilepticus episode. Measurement: A dichotomous outcome (yes/no) based on clinical and/or electroencephalographic confirmation. Time point: Measured within a defined period post-VNS activation (eg, 30 min, 1 hour, 24 hours). Data will be extracted at the shortest and longest reported time points.

Secondary outcomes

Safety: Incidence of serious adverse events. Definition: Any adverse event that is fatal, life-threatening, requires or prolongs hospitalisation, or results in significant disability/incapacity, and is judged to be at least possibly related to the VNS intervention. Measurement: Dichotomous outcome (occurrence of any serious adverse event: yes/no). Specific events (eg, laryngeal paralysis, cardiac arrhythmia, aspiration pneumonia) will also be catalogued. Time point: During the acute treatment period and throughout the hospital admission.

Safety: Incidence of serious adverse events. Information sources

Electronic databases

A comprehensive and systematic search will be performed across multiple electronic bibliographic databases from their inception to 31 October 2025, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase (via Ovid), the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and Web of Science Core Collection. The search strategy, developed with the guidance of an experienced medical librarian, will use a combination of controlled vocabulary and keywords related to VNS, status epilepticus and children, with no restrictions applied to language or publication date to ensure maximal retrieval of relevant literature. The search syntax will be meticulously adapted to conform to the specific requirements of each database.

Other resources

To minimise the potential for publication bias and to identify difficult-to-locate grey literature, supplementary searches will be conducted through several additional avenues. These will include scrutinising major clinical trial registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO ICTRP for any ongoing, completed or unpublished trials, examining grey literature sources like recent conference abstracts from relevant neurology and epilepsy symposiums, and manually reviewing the reference lists of all included studies as well as pertinent review articles. Furthermore, forward citation tracking via Google Scholar will be employed to identify newer studies that have referenced key papers, and direct contact with corresponding authors of included studies may be initiated to inquire about any additional unpublished data.

Search strategy

To structure and execute a systematic search, the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, Study design) framework will guide the selection of appropriate subject headings and keywords. Selection of studies

Two investigators will independently evaluate the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records to identify and exclude citations clearly unrelated to the study topic. Subsequently, the full text of potentially eligible articles will be independently assessed by both reviewers against the predefined eligibility criteria to determine final inclusion. For non-English language publications, initial translation for relevance assessment will be performed using machine translation (eg, Google Translate). If deemed relevant, accurate translation for data extraction will be sought through professional language services. Any discrepancies between reviewers regarding eligibility will be resolved through consensus discussion, or if necessary, by adjudication from a third independent reviewer. Handling of overlapping study cohorts

We will identify multiple publications, overlapping time frames and author lists from the same institution. If cohort overlap is suspected, we will contact the corresponding authors for clarification regarding the independence of the patient populations. If overlap is confirmed, we will prioritise the publication with the largest sample size, longest follow-up or most comprehensive outcomes to avoid double counting. If publications provide unique complementary information, their relationship will be clearly described in the synthesis.

Two reviewers will independently extract data from all included studies. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer when consensus cannot be reached. The extracted data will be systematically compiled to facilitate subsequent narrative and quantitative synthesis.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies will be independently assessed by two reviewers using standardised tools appropriate to each study design. For RCTs, the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool will be used to evaluate bias across the following domains: randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result. Each study will be judged as having ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’ or ‘high risk’ of bias. For case reports and case series, we will employ the tool proposed by Murad et al,36 which assesses four domains: selection, ascertainment, causality and reporting. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through consensus or by consultation with a third reviewer. The overall findings of the risk of bias assessment will be summarised in both tabular and graphical formats, and the implications of the risk of bias on the synthesis and interpretation of the results will be explicitly discussed in the review.

Data synthesis

We will adhere to a clear hierarchy: RCTs >non-RCTs >cohort studies >case-control studies >case series >case reports. In the narrative synthesis, findings will be structured and discussed according to this hierarchy. Quantitative synthesis will only be performed if there is sufficient homogeneity among higher-level studies, and the types of studies included will be clearly stated. The data will be combined using a structured approach tailored to the nature and heterogeneity of the included studies. If studies are sufficiently homogeneous in terms of design, population, intervention and outcomes, a quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) will be performed using a random-effects model to account for clinical and methodological variations. For dichotomous outcomes (eg, seizure cessation, mortality), risk ratios with 95% CIs will be pooled, while for continuous outcomes (eg, time to seizure cessation), mean differences or standardised mean differences will be calculated. Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. If substantial heterogeneity (I² and gt; 50%) is detected, subgroup analyses (eg, by aetiology of status epilepticus, VNS parameters) or sensitivity analyses (eg, excluding high-risk-of-bias studies) will be conducted to explore its sources. Data from case reports and small case series (n<10) will not be included in any meta-analysis due to design and methodological heterogeneity, but will be synthesised narratively. Their patient characteristics, intervention details and outcomes (efficacy/safety) will be summarised in a table. All analyses will be conducted using Stata software or R-based meta-analysis packages.

For meta-analysis of dichotomous outcomes, if zero-event studies are encountered, the Peto method for pooling ORs will be preferentially used due to its performance with rare events and zero cells. If substantial effect size heterogeneity is present, a sensitivity analysis using a continuity correction with the Mantel-Haenszel method will be considered. The method used will be explicitly reported.

Assessment of heterogeneity

Heterogeneity will be assessed using the I² statistic. If I²<50% and p>0.1, we will assume low heterogeneity among the studies and use a fixed-effects model for the meta-analysis. If I²>50% or p≤0.1, we will consider substantial heterogeneity and conduct sensitivity analyses and subgroup analyses to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. If subgroup analyses fail to explain the heterogeneity, we will use a random-effects model.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis will be conducted by removing one study at a time to test the robustness of the results. If removing any study significantly affects the overall result, this will be noted and discussed.

Subgroup analysis

If sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and to assess the consistency of effects across clinically relevant categories. Predefined subgroups will include aetiology of status epilepticus (structural, genetic/metabolic, immune-mediated or unknown), age group (infants <2 years, children 2–12 years, adolescents 13–18 years), type of VNS application (preimplanted chronic device vs acutely implanted as rescue therapy), stimulation parameters (high-frequency ≥20 Hz vs low-frequency <20 Hz), and classification of status epilepticus (refractory vs super-refractory). Between-subgroup differences will be formally tested using meta-regression or interaction tests where appropriate, with the aim of enhancing the interpretability and clinical applicability of the pooled results.

Assessment of publication bias

Publication bias will be assessed using Egger’s linear regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test. If publication bias is detected, we will use the trim-and-fill method to adjust for the potential impact on the results.

Summary of evidence

The overall quality of evidence for key outcomes will be evaluated using the GRADE framework. This assessment will consider study design, risk of bias, consistency of results, directness of evidence, precision of effect estimates and risk of publication bias. The GRADE rating will start from the lowest level of evidence present (eg, ‘low’ if cohort studies and case series are included) and then be downgraded or upgraded based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Due to the focus of this study on the specific population of paediatrics and the specific context of acute intervention, indirectness may not be the main downgrading factor. However, due to the small expected sample size for inclusion in the study, imprecision (wide CI) is highly likely to become a key factor in downgrading the certainty of evidence. Two reviewers will independently perform the assessment, with disagreements resolved through discussion or third-party arbitration.

Timeline of the study

The study started on 23 October 2025 and is planned to end before 1 July 2026.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Prospects

Given the ongoing emergence of evidence in this area, we plan to maintain this as a living systematic review following its initial publication, with planned updates to the search and synthesis every 2 years to ensure its long-term relevance and impact.

Ethics and dissemination

This study will not involve patients’ personal privacy, so ethical review is not required. The results will be disseminated in a peer-reviewed journal. Following publication, the data extraction forms and analysis code generated for this systematic review will be made publicly available via a repository such as the Open Science Framework (OSF).
