Tight Phosphate Control in ESKD Patients Is Warranted: PRO
Authors: Ryan M. Song, Stuart M. Sprague
Published: 2025

CKD–mineral bone disorder (CKD-MBD) is a universal complication of CKD and ESKD. Perturbation of phosphate (Pi) metabolism is an early, central manifestation of CKD-MBD, engendering compensatory mechanisms propagating the disorder. Despite the known association between hyperphosphatemia with poor cardiovascular outcomes and overall mortality in ESKD, there has been a steady rise in mean serum Pi concentrations among patients undergoing dialysis in the United States over the past decade.1 This trend is complicated because professional guidelines have variable serum Pi targets, suggesting a possible degree of uncertainty regarding ideal Pi concentrations.1 However, it is universally recommended to achieve some degree of control. With the underlying risks of hyperphosphatemia and CKD-MBD, as well as the recent concerning trends in serum Pi concentrations, we stress the importance of stricter Pi control in ESKD rather than a conservative approach.

The pathophysiology of hyperphosphatemia in advanced CKD is well described, with the primary etiology arising from impaired urinary Pi excretion. Dietary intake eventually overwhelms excretory capabilities, leading to a net-positive Pi gain. Hyperphosphatemia itself is associated with mitochondrial oxidative stress and cell toxicity, as well as systemic inflammation and vascular calcification. High levels of Pi are highly associated with morbidity and mortality in ESKD, and in nondialysis CKD patients, higher serum Pi concentrations are associated with a faster progression of CKD.2

In response to Pi retention, fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) rises to reduce renal tubule Pi reabsorption by Klotho complex-mediated downregulation of sodium-Pi transporters and gastrointestinal absorption by suppression of 1α‐hydroxylase and decreased calcitriol.3 However, FGF23 fails to achieve its phosphaturic effect as the number of functional tubules decreases in CKD, leading to further increases in FGF23 concentrations.3,4 High levels of FGF23 have been demonstrated to enhance proinflammatory cytokines, which further worsens vascular calcification from hyperphosphatemia. Elevations in FGF23 have also independently been shown to promote hypertension, left ventricular hypertrophy, and cardiac mortality, complications that are already highly prevalent in patients with ESKD.3 This progressive increase of FGF23 is seen early in CKD, along with decreasing serum calcium concentrations and subsequent rise in parathyroid hormone (PTH), preceding even the development of overt hyperphosphatemia, again emphasizing the central role of Pi imbalance in driving the mineral disorders in CKD-MBD (Figure 1).4 The increase in PTH further contributes to Pi release from bone, worsening overall Pi balance, and stimulating further FGF23 production, thus compounding the abovementioned complications. Similarly, hyperparathyroidism itself affects morbidity with its effects on bone health, demonstrating a U-shaped relationship with bone fragility and fractures in patients with ESKD, a spectrum of disease well described as CKD-MBD and renal osteodystrophy.5 Control of Pi intake, cautious repletion of vitamin D, and modulation of the PTH receptor using calcimimetics are central in suppressing PTH concentrations and preventing the effects of hyperparathyroidism (and development of tertiary hyperparathyroidism, which itself is complex and difficult to medically manage), vascular calcification, and cardiovascular mortality.

It is important to recognize that these hormonal imbalances have significant adverse consequences on mortality and are driven by an underlying hyperphosphatemic state. In fact, it has been shown that every increase of 1 mg/dl in serum Pi was associated with an 18% increased risk of death in patients with CKD.6 Data from the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study have demonstrated overall worse cardiovascular outcomes when patients undergoing hemodialysis are hyperphosphatemic (exceeding 4.5 mg/dl) for longer periods.7 Similar outcomes are seen in studies evaluating coronary artery calcification scores, in which patients undergoing hemodialysis whose serum Pi concentrations never exceeded 4.5 mg/dl demonstrated lower progression of coronary artery calcification scores compared with those who continuously exceeded 4.5 mg/dl and also in patients undergoing hemodialysis randomly assigned to receive calcium-free Pi binders to target stricter Pi concentrations (3.5–4.5 mg/dl) compared with those in the standard Pi control group (5.0–6.0 mg/dl).8,9 Furthermore, observational data demonstrated an independent consistent increase in bone fracture risk in patients undergoing hemodialysis with a baseline serum Pi >6.1 mg/dl, suggesting an association with hyperphosphatemia with bone disease, irrespective of PTH and alkaline phosphatase.5

Although it seems logical to aggressively control serum Pi and its associated hormones, several arguments against doing so exist and should be addressed.

At present, there is a lack of high-powered outcome studies to support tighter control of serum Pi in patients with ESKD, a fact noted by the most recent Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines. Nonetheless, these guidelines do advocate for targeting normalized Pi levels due to a rapidly growing body of evidence, as delineated above, supporting the detrimental effects of hyperphosphatemia. Larger randomized control trials investigating specific Pi targets and outcomes of stricter control in patients with ESKD are already underway.1 The pathophysiology of CKD-MBD (Figure 1) and the existing literature emphasizing the clinical consequences of high FGF23 and PTH support the need for earlier strict Pi control. This is even more critical in ESKD because patients lack adequate renal function to facilitate the effects of phosphaturic hormones and are only subjected to their deleterious adverse effects.

One of the major barriers to Pi control is the need to take multiple pills with meals and overall poor patient tolerance and compliance.1 However, failure to achieve the goal represents a suboptimal strategy rather than an unattainable or undesirable target. Central to reducing total body Pi should be an emphasis on dietary Pi limitation, a strategy which benefits from patient education and dietitian/nutritionist support. Pharmacotherapy should supplement this, and pill burden can be reduced with new agents, such as tenapanor, a Pi absorption inhibitor with a distinctly different mechanism of action by inhibiting the gastrointestinal sodium-hydrogen exchanger 3 and preventing both organic and inorganic Pi absorption.10 Twice-daily tenapanor administration effectively lowers serum Pi, FGF23, and PTH, working synergistically with Pi binders, resulting in significantly better Pi control and patient compliance.10

Before the development of calcium-free Pi binders, one of the main concerns with Pi-lowering therapy was its association with arterial calcification. It is likely that this was driven by the widespread use of calcium-based Pi binders, which may promote calcification from calcium loading. On the other hand, it can be argued that as patients with ESKD lose their renal excretory capabilities and the risks of hyperphosphatemia become more profound, the gastrointestinal system becomes more paramount in Pi balance. This again emphasizes the importance of reduced dietary Pi intake and using medications limiting dietary Pi absorption.

Risks of overtreatment of hyperphosphatemia should also be addressed. Prior epidemiologic studies have demonstrated hypophosphatemia (<3.0 mg/dl) and association with increased mortality. However, this is more likely a marker of poor nutrition, a far stronger marker of mortality, and less likely to be a marker of overtreatment. Similarly, it can be argued that strict Pi control may limit necessary protein intake in ESKD. Notably, this can also be mitigated with dietary counseling, avoiding nonprotein sources of Pi and diligent use of Pi-lowering medications.

In conclusion, the overall complications associated with hyperphosphatemia and its deleterious role in the pathophysiology of CKD-MBD necessitate stricter control of hyperphosphatemia. Dietary limitation of Pi is central to achieving this goal; however, with the availability of calcium-free Pi binders and recent addition of Pi absorption inhibitors, nephrologists are equipped with additional powerful tools to achieve this effect. Such efforts will be necessary to combat the nation's latest trends toward hyperphosphatemia and prevent inevitable cardiovascular consequences and overall morbidity.
