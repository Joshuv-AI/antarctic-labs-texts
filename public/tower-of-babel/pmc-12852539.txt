Systematic review and meta analysis of the global incidence and clinicopathological characteristics of liver metastasis in patients with cervical cancer
Authors: Kimia Pakdaman, Amin Esmaeilnia Shirvani, Kasra Pakdaman, Bardia Karim, Saba Sahraian, Hossein-Ali Nikbakht, Layla Shojaie, Pouyan Ebrahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer remains a major malignancy among women, with poor survival rates below 20% despite advances in care. Patients with cervical cancer are most likely to develop hematogenous metastases in the liver, followed by the lungs and bones. Although less commonly involved than other organs, the liver is associated with the poorest prognosis among metastatic sites. This study aims to estimate the global incidence of liver metastasis in cervical cancer patients and explore its clinicopathological characteristics.MethodsThis is a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted to assess the global incidence and clinicopathological characteristics of liver metastasis in cervical cancer patients. The review followed PRISMA guidelines, and a comprehensive search was performed in Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar up to February 28, 2025. Eligibility criteria included studies that explicitly reported the incidence of liver metastases following a diagnosis of cervical cancer, and provided data on both total cervical cancer cases and cases with liver metastases. Exclusion criteria were studies lacking clear incidence data, case reports, reviews, or studies not specific to cervical cancer. The pooled incidence was calculated using a random-effects model implemented in STATA version 17, based on raw proportions. Results were presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI), and heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic.ResultsOut of an initial 7508 articles identified across five databases, 11 studies (comprising 20 records) met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis. The global pooled incidence of liver metastasis among cervical cancer patients was estimated at 1.88% (95% CI: 1.27–2.48). To explore variability among populations, we conducted subgroup analyses based on development indices. Countries with a very high Human Development Index (HDI) reported a higher incidence (2.12%, 95% CI: 1.40–2.84) than those with high (1.96%, 95% CI: 0.98–2.94) and medium (0.75%, 95% CI: 0.0–1.57) HDI levels. The incidence of liver metastasis in combination with metastases to other organs was nearly three times greater (1.76%, 95% CI: 0.99–2.53) than that of isolated liver metastasis. Furthermore, in a subgroup analysis based on histological subtype, the highest pooled incidence was observed in patients with cervical adenocarcinoma (2.36%, 95% CI: 1.35–3.37).ConclusionThis meta-analysis demonstrates that while liver metastasis in cervical cancer is relatively rare, its occurrence appears more frequently in patients with advanced disease. These results underscore the need for routine liver screening in high-risk patients and support the refinement of therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04255-8.Keywords: Cervical cancer metastasis, Liver metastasis, Uterine cervical neoplasms, Neoplasm metastasis, Incidence

Introduction

Globally, cancer is acknowledged as a significant health problem [1]. Cervical cancer, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 14.1 per 100,000 and an age-standardized mortality rate of 7.1 per 100,000, ranks as the fourth and third leading causes of cancer in women, respectively, according to the latest GLOBOCAN 2024 data [2]. Treatment of cervical cancer continues to present substantial challenges, with recurrence observed in 25–61% of cases after initial treatment [3]. Despite advancements in prevention, diagnostic, and therapeutic approaches, the prognosis for these patients remains poor [4]. Recent studies have identified key factors influencing prognosis in cervical cancer patients, such as metastasis and tumor angiogenesis [5]. At the advanced and metastatic stages of the illness, the median survival rate is about 8–13 months, and the 5-year survival rate is only about 17% [6]. One of the critical prognostic factors in these patients is the site of metastasis [7]. Among various metastatic sites in cervical cancer, the liver, although less frequently involved than the lungs or bones, is often the leading site of hematogenous spread and has been consistently associated with the poorest prognosis, serving as a critical indicator of advanced disease severity [6, 8]. The strong hepatic blood supply is a contributing factor in creating a favorable environment for liver metastasis [9]. liver metastasis in cervical carcinoma almost always occurs in the advanced stages of cancer, accompanied by local disease and extrahepatic metastases, typically associated with poor survival rates [10, 11]. Pelvic lymph node involvement increases the risk of distant metastasis, including to the liver [12]. This becomes even more significant when the occurrence of metastasis to various organs, particularly the liver, is considered a major factor in the prognosis of cervical cancer patients.

Various studies have reported differing rates of liver metastases among cervical cancer patients; however, all indicated that the incidence is less than 5% [13–15]. For instance, a 2024 study by Li et al. found that 2.29% of 7,201 cervical cancer patients developed liver metastasis [13]. Additionally, Park’s et al. study reported an incidence rate ranging from 1 to 3% based on data type. They also observed that liver metastases were more frequently associated with cervical adenocarcinoma than with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) [15]. In line with this, Tabatabaei et al. reported an incidence of 3.21% [14], while Kannan’s study cited an incidence of 1.21% for this type of metastasis [16]. These discrepancies highlight the importance of factors such as tumor type and geographical region in the development of cervical cancer liver metastases. Despite the fact that various studies have reported liver metastasis incidence rates below 5%, there remains a lack of comprehensive and complete information regarding the patterns of liver metastasis occurrence in cervical cancer patients.

Although conventional treatments for liver metastases have shown limited effectiveness and the prognosis for affected patients remains poor, data on the incidence of liver metastases originating from cervical cancer is still scarce. Furthermore, to date, no comprehensive summary has been published on the incidence of liver metastases in patients with cervical cancer. These gaps in knowledge prompted us to conduct this review to gain better insight into the characteristic patterns of this disease. Therefore, the primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to estimate the global incidence of liver metastasis in cervical cancer patients. Additionally, we aimed to explore the clinicopathological characteristics of these metastases. As part of our secondary analyses, we examined variations in incidence based on the Human Development Index (HDI), patterns of single versus multiple organ metastases, and histological subtypes of cervical cancer.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines for transparent reporting [17] (PROSPERO 2025 registration code: CRD420251041119).

Search strategy and data sources

In the search phase of this study, we employed a multi-step approach to identify relevant keywords and construct the search strategy. We then conducted comprehensive searches across Embase, PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to February 28, 2025. In addition, we reviewed gray literature for further study exploration. It is noteworthy that in this search strategy, commonly used free-text keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were employed. Furthermore, no time-based or language-based filters were applied during the search process. The key search terms included: “Liver”, “Uterine Cervical Neoplasms”, and “Incidence” (Table S1).

Study selection process

After searching all the mentioned databases, all articles were imported into EndNote software (version 2019). Using the “Find duplicate” tool in this software, duplicate studies were identified and removed. Subsequently, two researchers (K.P. and A.E.S.) independently reviewed the results from each database, performing both initial (title screening) and secondary (title and abstract screening) screenings. For studies where the two researchers disagreed, a third investigator (P.E.), who was responsible for the study design, resolved the discrepancies.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) studies that explicitly reported the incidence of liver metastases among cervical cancer patients; and (2) studies that provided both the total number of cervical cancer cases and the number of patients with liver metastases. All observational study designs, including cohort studies and cross-sectional studies, were eligible for inclusion, as long as they met the inclusion criteria. The exclusion criteria were: (1) in-vivo or in-vitro studies, case reports, case series, book chapters, conference abstracts, and reviews; (2) studies focused solely on patients with stage IVb or M1 cervical cancer; (3) studies using duplicate or overlapping datasets; and (4) studies that reported liver metastases for gynecologic cancers without distinguishing cervical cancer cases as the primary malignancy. It is noteworthy that many studies used data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program [18]. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the timeframe of data collection, the most comprehensive study addressing liver metastasis incidence in cervical cancer patients was included, while the other studies were excluded. Additionally, for studies where the full text was unavailable, we attempted to contact the corresponding authors through “Email” or “ResearchGate” to obtain the full text. Studies for which full texts could not be obtained were excluded from the analysis.

Data extraction and variables

After obtaining the final PDFs of the selected articles, two independent researchers (K.P. and A.E.S.) conducted a comprehensive data extraction using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Version 2019; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Any ambiguities encountered during the extraction process were resolved by the principal investigator (P.E.). The Excel data sheet included the following variables: first author’s name, publication year, data collection period, age range of the study population, study type, WHO region, country, Human Development Index (HDI), income level, cervical cancer stage, cervical cancer histology, and the number of liver metastases categorized as single, multiple, and total, along with their incidence rates expressed as percentages.

It should be noted that countries were classified according to the six WHO regions (Africa, Eastern Mediterranean, Europe, South-East Asia, the Americas, and the Western Pacific) [19], as well as by HDI levels [20].

Quality appraisal (risk of bias assessment)

For the quality assessment of the included studies, we utilized the Checklist for Prevalence Studies developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). This tool evaluates nine domains across different aspects of a study, including sampling methods, sample size adequacy, data analysis procedures, application of appropriate statistical methods, and the evaluation and reporting of results. Studies scoring between 5 and 6 were classified as having moderate quality, while those with scores above or below this range were considered to have low and high risk of bias, respectively [21]. Following the initial evaluation, two independent researchers (K.P. and A.E.S.) performed quality assessment separately. Any disagreements or inconsistencies that arose during the process were addressed and resolved through discussion and consensus in consultation with the team supervisor (P.E.).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed utilizing STATA software (V.17.0) (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). To address the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, the pooled incidence of liver metastases was estimated using a random-effects model following the Restricted maximum likelihood method. Effect size estimations were derived from raw (untransformed) proportions and presented along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cochran’s Q test and I2 statistics were performed to evaluate statistical heterogeneity; thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% were used to indicate small, moderate, and large heterogeneity [22, 23]. A forest plot was produced to graphically depict the pooled incidence rates and the distribution of results across individual studies. Also, there were a number of potential sources of heterogeneity to be investigated, including the publication year, the first year of data collection, the HDI classification, and the histological type of cervical cancer. A leave-one-out approach was also used to assess the robustness of the overall estimate by systematically omitting one study at a time in sensitivity analyses. Moreover, meta-regression analyses were carried out to explore the influence of continuous variables, namely publication year, HDI score, and start year of data collection, on the pooled incidence estimates. Publication bias was assessed by visually inspecting funnel plots and statistically analyzing Egger’s regression test, with a p-value below 0.05 reflecting significant asymmetry [24]. All analyses adhered to established methodological standards for meta-analyses of incidence data, ensuring that the results were rigorous, transparent, and reproducible [25].

Results

Study selection

Based on the predefined search strategies, a total of 7,508 articles were initially identified. Following the removal of 1,583 duplicate records using EndNote software, 5,925 articles proceeded to the next screening phase. After the completion of both primary and secondary screening processes, 38 articles were selected for full-text review. In total, 11 studies, corresponding to 20 individual records (based on countries, stage, and histology of cervical cancer) extracted from the Excel database, were considered eligible (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram for new systematic reviews, including database and other source searches

Study characteristics and quality appraisal

In the evaluation of the 11 included studies, the publication years ranged from 1967 to 2024, while the data collection periods spanned from 1948 to 2016. All studies employed a retrospective design and encompassed data from eight different countries. Specifically, Austria, China, India, Iran, and South Africa each contributed one record, South Korea contributed two records, and the United States America (USA) contributed four records. The study conducted by Park et al. [15] was considered as two separate records, as it utilized data from two distinct databases and reported data collection over different time periods. Most of the included records originated from the Region of the Americas (4 records), the Western Pacific Region (3 records), and the European Region (2 records). Regarding economic status and development levels, the majority of studies were classified within the “High” and “Very High” categories based on income level and HDI classifications. Regarding the type of data sources, eight studies were based on hospital-derived clinical data, while three utilized epidemiological data, including population-based databases such as SEER. With the exception of one study (Heyer et al. [26]), all other studies included patients across all stages of cervical cancer. Furthermore, quality assessment of the included articles revealed that two were found to be of low quality, six to be moderately good, and three were classified as high quality (Table 1 and Table S2).

Results of the meta-analysis

Global incidence of liver metastasis from patients with cervical cancer

Table 2 presents the pooled incidence of liver metastases, both globally and at the country level. The overall pooled incidence of liver metastases among patients with cervical cancer was estimated at 1.88% (95% CI: 1.27–2.48), based on 12 records derived from 11 independent studies encompassing a total of 47,833 cervical cancer patients worldwide. Notably, one study (Park et al., [15]) contributed two separate subgroups obtained from distinct national datasets. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the studies (Q = 135.83, I² = 94.39%).

Among the evaluated countries, Iran, based on a single record, reported the highest pooled incidence of liver metastasis at 3.21% (95% CI: 0.68–5.73). The USA (4 records) and China (1 record) ranked second and third, with pooled incidences of 2.60% (95% CI: 1.60–3.60) and 1.76% (95% CI: 1.01–2.51), respectively. Subgroup meta-analysis of liver metastasis incidence based on priori variables, single or multiple metastasis, and histology of cervical cancer

Subgroup analyses were subsequently performed based on predefined variables, including publication year, start date of data collection, risk of bias, and HDI. The results demonstrated that studies published after 2015 reported a higher pooled incidence of liver metastasis (2.06%; 95% CI: 1.58–2.54), as did studies in which data collection commenced from 2010 onward (1.89%; 95% CI: 1.47–2.30), compared to studies conducted in earlier periods. Moreover, studies classified as having a high risk of bias exhibited a lower pooled incidence (0.89%; 95% CI: 0.0–2.17) compared to those with a low risk of bias (1.43%; 95% CI: 0.43–2.43). In addition, countries categorized as having a very high HDI demonstrated a greater pooled incidence (2.12%; 95% CI: 1.40–2.84) than those with high (1.96%; 95% CI: 0.98–2.94) and medium HDI levels (0.75%; 95% CI: 0.0–1.57). Also, the pooled incidence of liver metastasis appeared slightly higher in population-based datasets (2.03%; 95% CI: 1.75–2.31) than in hospital-based studies (1.85%; 95% CI: 1.00–2.71; I² = 93.15). However, the P-value of 0.702 for between-group differences was not reach statistical significance (Table 3).

In the analysis of liver metastases based on the histological subtype of cervical cancer, adenocarcinoma of the cervix demonstrated the highest pooled incidence of liver metastasis, estimated at 2.36% (95% CI: 1.35–3.37). Additionally, SCC of the cervix showed a comparable pooled incidence of 2.03% (95% CI: 1.64–2.42), ranking second in frequency (Fig. 3).

Moreover, the global incidence of liver metastasis demonstrated a slight, yet statistically nonsignificant, upward trend over successive publication years (β = 0.002, p = 0.903). Similarly, an insignificant downward trend was noted as the start year of data collection became more recent (β = −0.012, p = 0.470). Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

After sensitivity analysis, the pooled incidence of liver metastases among cervical cancer patients did not significantly change when individual studies were excluded (Figure S2). Additionally, funnel plot assessments were conducted to evaluate the presence of publication bias regarding the primary outcome. Egger’s regression test (bias: 1.46, SE = 1.256, p = 0.244) indicated no significant evidence of publication bias in the pooled incidence of liver metastases from cervical cancer (Figure S3).

Discussion

Based on data from 47,833 patients, this study found that the incidence of liver metastases among individuals with cervical cancer was 1.88%. The incidence of isolated (single-organ) liver metastasis was calculated at 0.61%, whereas the incidence increased significantly to 1.76% when liver metastasis co-occurred alongside metastases to other organs, representing nearly a threefold rise compared to single-organ liver metastasis. From a histological perspective, cervical adenocarcinoma exhibited the highest incidence of liver metastasis, with an estimated 2.36%. Moreover, no significant difference was observed between the incidence rates of liver metastasis in patients with adenocarcinoma and those with SCC.

In this study, the incidence rate of 1.88% for cervical cancer liver metastases, aligning with rates reported in many previous investigations. Liver metastasis incidence can be as high as 14% − 35% in gastrointestinal cancer such as stomach, colon, small intestine and pancreas cancers, while it has an incidence of below 10% in non-gastrointestinal cancers such as ovary, breast and kidney [27]. In previous studies the incidence of lung and bone metastasis in cervical cancer was reported to be 4.33% and 2.42%, respectively [28, 29]. Previous studies on metastatic patterns in gynecologic cancers have typically relied either on large-scale epidemiological datasets like SEER or on smaller hospital-based data, each with inherent strengths and limitations. By incorporating both data types and conducting stratified analyses, our study offers a more balanced and comprehensive estimate. The majority of studies originated from the USA (4 records), where the pooled incidence of liver metastasis was 2.60%. A study by Liu et al., which analyzed data from the USA SEER database between 2010 and 2015, reported that 2.32% of 19,420 cervical cancer patients developed liver metastases [8]. Notably, a significant proportion of these patients also presented with concurrent lung metastases (47.3%) and bone metastases (29.7%), reflecting the widespread dissemination of disease in advanced stages [8]. Similarly, a study by Shan et al., examining SEER data from 2010 to 2014, found that among 4,176 cervical cancer patients, 84 patients (2%) developed liver metastases [30]. Interestingly, when stratified by data source, we found that studies based on epidemiological datasets (from countries such as the USA and China) reported a slightly higher pooled incidence (2.03%) with substantially lower heterogeneity, compared to hospital-based studies, which showed a marginally lower pooled incidence (1.85%) but greater heterogeneity. However, this between-group difference was not statistically significant. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The observed trend may suggest that population-based data provide more consistent and generalizable estimates, likely owing to larger sample sizes, standardized diagnostic criteria, and broader patient inclusion. Conversely, hospital-based data, often derived from single or regional centers in countries such as Austria, Germany, and Iran, may be more susceptible to local referral patterns, selection bias, and underreporting, which could contribute to variability rather than representing a true epidemiological disparity.

In this study, isolated liver metastasis ranked as the third most common site after the lungs and bones, affecting 33 patients (0.8%). Furthermore, among cases of dual-site metastasis, the combination of lung and liver involvement was the most frequent, occurring in 32 patients (0.8%) [30]. Another study utilizing SEER data from 2010 to 2016 similarly confirmed these patterns regarding liver metastasis in cervical cancer patients [6]. On the other hand, Yin et al., who analyzed data from 99 metastatic cervical cancer patients in China over a decade, reported findings that contrasted with those from the USA SEER database studies [10]. In their study, the bone was identified as the most common site of single-organ metastasis, whereas the liver remained the third most frequent site after bone and lung involvement. Regarding dual-organ metastases, the combination of lung and liver metastasis was again the most prevalent. An interesting observation emerging from the synthesis of these findings is that, according to our study, the incidence of liver metastasis increases with rising levels of the HDI, from 0.75% in countries with medium HDI to 2.12% in countries with very high HDI. This pattern may help explain why studies utilizing SEER data often report a higher incidence of liver metastasis in more developed countries. Such findings highlight the potential impact of greater access to advanced medical services and diagnostic facilities, which likely facilitates earlier detection and more effective management of liver metastases. However, it should also be acknowledged that this apparent increase in incidence among very high-HDI countries may partially reflect detection bias rather than a true biological difference. Greater availability of high-resolution imaging technologies (such as MRI and PET-CT) and more systematic follow-up protocols in high-resource settings can lead to increased identification of small or asymptomatic hepatic lesions. Consequently, while improved diagnostic capacity likely contributes to higher reported rates, these findings should be interpreted with caution and not assumed to indicate intrinsic biological variability across populations. Additionally, the liver is a favorable site for the hematogenous spread of cancer cells from distant primary tumors because of its significant blood supply from the portal vein and hepatic artery [31, 32]. While cervical cancer typically disseminates locally or to regional lymph nodes, accumulating evidence indicates that hematogenous spread can also contribute to the development of liver metastases. In our subgroup analysis, we observed that studies published after 2015 reported a higher pooled incidence of liver metastasis compared to older studies. While this might suggest a potential temporal increase, it is important to interpret these findings cautiously. One possible explanation is that more recent studies tend to have larger sample sizes, which may lead to more stable or slightly higher estimates due to improved diagnostic practices or broader patient inclusion.

In our study, the incidence of isolated (single-organ) liver metastasis was 0.61%, whereas liver metastasis occurring in combination with metastases to other organs was nearly three times higher, with a rate of 1.76%. Previous research has also emphasized the frequent involvement of multiple organs alongside liver metastases [11, 33]. Kim et al., in their analysis of 1,665 cervical cancer patients, reported 20 cases of liver metastasis, among which only one case involved isolated liver metastasis; the remaining 19 patients had either uncontrolled pelvic disease or widespread extra pelvic metastases [11]. Similarly, a study investigating small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the cervix found that among 48 patients who developed metastases, half (24 patients) exhibited multi-organ involvement at the time of first metastatic diagnosis [34]. In contrast, another study involving 54 cervical cancer patients with metastases at initial diagnosis reported that the majority (81%) had single-site metastasis [35]. Overall, across various types of cancers, the presence of liver metastases in conjunction with metastases to other organs is associated with poorer survival outcomes. According to SEER data from 2010 to 2015, the one-year survival rate for patients with liver metastases was 15.1%, compared to 24% for patients with metastases involving non-hepatic sites [27]. This study recommends regular screening for liver metastases based on its findings and comparison with the existing literature. Cervical cancer patients in the late stages are particularly at risk. Metastases to the liver, especially when they occur in conjunction with those to other organs, may indicate a poor prognosis. As a result, clinicians should be aware that liver involvement increases in patients with cervical cancer that has spread to other organs. Consequently, it is advisable to implement combined therapeutic strategies, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, more consistently in high-risk patients. These observations highlight the importance of conducting more comprehensive studies focused on identifying risk factors and assessing various clinical characteristics. Further detailed investigations could enhance early detection of liver metastasis and support the development of more effective treatment strategies. Furthermore, research efforts in resource-limited settings are crucial for better understanding liver metastasis in these populations and its underlying causes.

In our study, the incidence of liver metastasis originating from cervical adenocarcinoma was 2.36%, which was slightly higher than that observed for SCC at 2.03%. A review of previous studies has shown that the incidence of liver metastasis from cervical adenocarcinoma has ranged between 1.33 and 2.29% [13, 15], while for SCC ranging from 1.56 to 1.84% [15, 36]. Certain cervical cancer cell types, such as adenocarcinoma, appear to have a higher propensity for liver metastasis [13]. A liver metastasis from cervical cancer is often an indication of systemic disease dissemination, which indicates a poorer prognosis [6]. The increased incidence of liver metastasis in cervical adenocarcinoma may be attributed to distinct biological behavior favoring hematogenous dissemination. Adenocarcinomas often present with more developed vascular networks and higher micro vessel density, facilitating intravasation and spread through the circulation to distant organs, including the liver. In contrast, SCCs tend to rely more heavily on lymphatic routes of dissemination, which might limit distant organ metastasis such as hepatic involvement [31, 37, 38]. This mechanistic differential aligns with observed clinical patterns and supports the plausibility of the epidemiological findings. In contrast, SCC tends to spread primarily through lymphatic pathways, which may reduce the likelihood of distant organ metastases such as to the liver [39–41].

While liver metastases have been extensively studied in gastrointestinal malignancies, particularly colorectal cancer, limited research has specifically addressed the occurrence of liver metastasis in other cancer types, including cervical cancer. Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. One of the primary limitations of this analysis lies in the considerable heterogeneity observed across the included studies (I² = 94%). This variability likely reflects both methodological and clinical differences, including variations in patient characteristics (such as demographic factors or comorbidities), stage distribution at diagnosis, and the diagnostic techniques employed to identify liver metastases. Differences in imaging modalities (CT, MRI, or PET-CT), duration and intensity of follow-up, and even diagnostic thresholds across studies may have led to variation in detection rates, particularly for small or asymptomatic metastases. These factors collectively contribute to the observed heterogeneity and should be considered when interpreting the pooled incidence estimates. Moreover, the inability to perform subgroup analyses based on specific risk factors (e.g., age, stage, prior treatment) limited the granularity of our findings. Additionally, we were unable to fully assess the influence of data source on heterogeneity or potential selection bias; a more detailed subgroup analysis comparing hospital-based and population-based datasets based on every countries data could further strengthen future research. Despite these limitations, the study’s strengths include a comprehensive and systematic search strategy, rigorous inclusion criteria, and multiple subgroup and meta-regression analyses, all of which enhance the robustness and transparency of the results and provide valuable insights into the underlying sources of heterogeneity among the included studies.

Based on the limitations identified in our study, several areas warrant attention in future research. First, improved consistency in reporting key study characteristics, such as the type of data sources and the diagnostic criteria for liver metastases, would enhance the interpretability of pooled results. Second, the inclusion of clinical variables (e.g., cancer stage, treatment history, comorbidities) would facilitate more robust multivariable analyses and risk stratification. Finally, expanding research efforts to better represent low- and middle-income countries is essential to improve the global applicability and equity of the evidence base.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis indicates that although liver metastasis has a relatively low overall incidence (1.88%) among patients with cervical cancer, lower than metastases to the lungs or bones, it remains a clinically important manifestation. Incidence rates varied across countries, ranging from 0.37% to 3.21%, suggesting differences in detection and reporting practices. It is also important to acknowledge that the higher incidence observed in very high-HDI countries may partly reflect detection bias due to greater access to advanced imaging technologies and more systematic surveillance, rather than intrinsic biological differences across populations. These findings underscore the importance of continued surveillance and routine follow-up in cervical cancer patients, particularly in high-risk patients, such as those with adenocarcinoma histology or advanced disease stage, and may contribute to the optimization of screening strategies and guide appropriate clinical decision-making.
