Intraosseous hibernoma in a patient with vaginal cancer: Case report with radiologic and pathologic correlation
Authors: Ashley Zhang, Emily Nesbit, Sarah Erickson, Linda Duska, Michael Crawford, Kara Romano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Intraosseous hibernomas are rare benign soft tissue tumors most often diagnosed incidentally.•Intraosseous hibernoma imaging characteristics can mimic bone metastases, including sclerosis, PET-avidity, and heterogenous enhancement.•Bone metastases seconadary to primary gynecologic cancers are rare, but present in 1 - 16% of cases.•Appropriate recognition of this rare benign finding dramatically change staging and management.

Introduction

Intraosseous hibernomas are rare benign soft tissue tumors most commonly found intramedullary in the trunk and extremities. While the origin is unknown, these soft tissue tumors are composed of brown adipocytic differentiation and are commonly discovered incidentally, usually in the context of cancer staging or surveillance. Incidental discovery of these tumors will unsurprisingly raise the question of metastatic disease, requiring further imaging work-up and invasive biopsies. In this report, we present the case of an 85-year-old female with newly diagnosed primary vaginal cancer who was found to have a suspicious FDG-avid lesion on routine oncologic staging imaging. This interdisciplinary case will lay out the events that led to the rare diagnosis of intraosseous hibernoma, the radiologic and pathologic correlation, and the ultimate treatment plan for this patient. This case is clinically significant as failure to appropriately recognize and diagnose this rare soft tissue tumor by imaging and pathology would have led to a vastly different stage and management plan for the patient.

Case summary

An 85-year-old G4P0013 female with a medical history of hyperlipidemia, hypertension, osteopenia, and asthma presented to her routine annual Obstetrics & Gynecology appointment reporting vaginal bleeding with application of estrace cream. The patient had a history of total abdominal hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy for fibroids in 1988. She had no abnormal pap smears prior to her surgery, had not had pap smears since her surgery, and had no history of diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure. Additional past surgical history included a benign parotid tumor resection in 2011 and resection of a benign breast lesion in the distant past. On pelvic exam, a concerning yellow–brown lesion was noted on the anterior vagina and was biopsied in the office. Pathology returned on biopsy as at least high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion or vaginal intraepithelial lesion 3 (VaIN3). The patient’s general gynecologist placed a referral for the patient to be seen by Gynecologic Oncology.

At her gynecologic oncology appointment, she reported feeling well overall. She noted some beige vaginal discharge in retrospect but did not have an exact timeline for when that started. She denied systemic symptoms, such as fatigue, weight loss, or pain. On her pelvic exam, a very friable, thickened plaque of tumor was noted circumferentially from the apex to the introitus of vagina. A rectal exam revealed no mucosal involvement; however, the rectovaginal septum was noted to be thick with nodularity. Multiple vaginal biopsies were taken circumferentially. The clinical impression was invasive cancer. Pathology returned high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion or vaginal intraepithelial lesion 3 (VaIN3) with areas suspicious for invasion. HPV testing was not performed. The Gynecologic Oncologist did not recommend surgical resection due to the location of the tumor and extent of disease. Additional imaging, including a MRI Pelvis and a PET-CT, were obtained to further evaluate for definitive treatment. The patient was referred to Radiation Oncology for consideration of definitive treatment.

Imaging

MRI Pelvis showed circumferential thickening of the vagina, with focal thinning of the anterior rectal wall concerning for possible tumor invasion into the rectum. PET/CT showed a hypermetabolic lesion involving the upper portion of the vagina and vaginal cuff, as well as a sclerotic bone lesion in the left sacral wing with mild focal uptake suspicious for metastatic disease (Fig. 1). The differential diagnosis for sclerotic bone lesions on imaging include benign causes such as hemangioma, calcifying enchondroma, osteoid osteoma, bone infarct, fibrous dysplasia, and Paget disease of bone. Primary malignant causes of the lesion include metastatic disease, chondrosarcoma, lymphoma, and multiple myeloma. Diagnostic tools to evaluate bone lesions including computed tomography (CT), MRI, and PET-CT. Additional imaging was recommended by the Radiologist to further characterize the bone lesion, and MRI Musculoskeletal revealed a heterogeneously enhancing lesion with possible foci of internal fat intensity, which could represent a benign lesion versus metastatic disease (Fig. 1). Given that metastatic bone disease would change the patient’s staging and treatment plan, a CT-guided biopsy of the lesion was ordered.

Pathology

Pathology of the CT-guided biopsy revealed abundant trilineage hematopoiesis with collections of large cells with vacuolated cytoplasm and small, bland nuclei, raising the differential diagnosis of foamy macrophages versus multilocular “brown” fat. Immunohistochemical staining was performed, and the biopsy stained positive for S100 and negative for CD68, CD163, and pancytokeratin, supporting multilocular fat (Fig. 2). Treatment plan

After multi-disciplinary discussion between Radiation Oncology and Gynecologic Oncology, definitive chemo-radiotherapy with brachytherapy boost was recommended. The patient received external beam radiotherapy to the pelvis to a total dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions, at 2 Gy per fraction delivered once daily with intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), with weekly concurrent cisplatin. Pelvic exam following IMRT revealed smooth vaginal mucosa with no thickening or any nodularity in her rectovaginal septum. Repeat MRI Pelvis after IMRT confirmed her physical exam and demonstrated a complete response with a significant interval decrease in the vaginal tumor burden. Brachytherapy boost with vaginal cylinder x 5 fractions was recommended.

Discussion

In this report, we present a case of an 85-year-old female with an incidentally discovered intraosseous hibernoma in her left sacrum, that raised the question of possible metastatic disease during initial work up. Overall, the incidence of bone metastases in gynecologic cancers is quite low. The most common site with reported bone metastases is cervical cancer where the incidence is reported to range from about 2.5 % up to 16 % in more advanced stages (Gangahar et al., 2023, Han et al., 2021). In endometrial, ovarian and vulvar cancers, the incidence of bone metastases is around 1 % (Liu et al., 2009, Myslicki et al., 2019, Prieske et al., 2016). Due to the rarity of primary vaginal cancers, there are no prior studies characterizing incidence of bone metastases in vaginal cancer although extrapolating from cervical cancer data may provide a realistic estimate (Salamanna et al., 2021). In summary, a primary vaginal cancer bone metastasis in this patient would be unsuspected but not impossible, and would significantly change her management.

Intraosseous hibernomas have previously been reported to occur most frequently in young adults, although the age has been reported to range from 7 to 65 years of age (Uccella et al., 2013). Women were reported to be more commonly diagnosed than men (Uccella et al., 2013, Yahia et al., 2016). To our knowledge, our case is the oldest individual documented with intraosseous hibernoma. In prior literature, the indication for imaging where this rare tumor was discovered was cancer surveillance or staging (Uccella et al., 2013). The context in which our patient’s tumor was incidentally discovered, staging for vaginal cancer, was similar to prior cases. The location of our patient’s tumor aligned with previously reported common locations of hibernomas, which includes in the spine and pelvis (Uccella et al., 2013, Yahia et al., 2016, Zhang et al., 2019).

This case highlights a key role of a routine physical exam in an otherwise healthy elderly woman. Although cervical cancer screening is no longer indicated in the over 65 patient population, this patient’s gynecologist was able to identify an early-stage cancer prior to significant symptoms and morbidity. Further, understanding imaging characteristics and pathology of this rare diagnosis impacted the patient’s care in a clinically meaningful way.

As the use of imaging studies increases with time, more cases of intraosseous hibernoma will be incidentally diagnosed. Awareness of this rare tumor is necessary, as patients need to be appropriately counselled prior to receiving further imaging or invasive procedures. Further diagnostic work up can be the source of significant anxiety for patients, particularly in oncologic care. It is important for clinicians to be familiar with clinical and imaging characteristics of interosseous hibernomas to inform patient and provider decision making. Our case demonstrates the necessity to make clinical decisions among interdisciplinary teams, so that optimal treatment planning can be made on the patient’s behalf.
