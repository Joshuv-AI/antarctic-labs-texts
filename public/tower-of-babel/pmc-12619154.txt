Exploring the ongoing important role of meaning in life on young people’s mental health: a population-based study of the moderated mediation model
Authors: Jing Zhou, Teemu Rantanen, Timo Toikko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study explores the complex relationships between the search for meaning in life (SML), the presence of meaning in life (PML), and mental health status among diverse groups of adolescents and young adults, including students, employed, and unemployed individuals aged 14–35 years in post-pandemic urban China. Specifically, we assess the mediating role of PML in influencing mental health, and explore how age moderates this effect across these groups. Our approach includes comparing different employment statuses to further understand how these variables interact to affect mental health outcomes, highlighting the unique relations within each group.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in 2023 involving 3189 participants from Shanghai in China. Data were collected using paper questionnaires, and analyzed using multivariate statistical techniques. Descriptive statistical analysis and correlation analysis were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Mediation and moderation analyses were performed using PROCESS macro 4.2.ResultsThe results showed that PML acts as a mediator in the relationship between SML and mental health, enhancing mental health across all youth groups. Notably, the SML had a significant direct negative effect on mental health in employed youth (B = -0.123, p < 0.001), while direct relationship of lesser significance emerged in students and unemployed youth. Moderated mediation analysis further indicated that age significantly moderates these effects in the unemployed youth group (SML × Age, B = -0.058, p < 0.01), with younger individuals benefiting more from active meaning searches. Additionally, local household registration status was identified as a critical factor influencing mental health, particularly among students (B = 1.433) and employed youth (B = 2.181).ConclusionsThis study highlights the importance of meaning in life as a protective factor for mental health among urban Chinese youth. The findings suggest that tailored mental health interventions should consider socio-economic status and life stages to address the unique needs of different youth groups. Policies and programs should enhance both the search for and the presence of meaning in life to promote better mental health outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25106-9.

Introduction

Overview of meaning in life and mental health

The research topic of meaning in life (MIL) has garnered significant attention within various disciplines, including psychology, public health, and social science [1], due to its profound implications for mental health and well-being [2, 3]. Many previous studies propose that understanding the role of meaningful engagement is particularly important for adolescents and young adults to prevent mental illness and enhance life satisfaction [4]. However, the advent of the COVID-19 pandemic has shifted the paradigm, not only by introducing new psychosocial stressors, but also by altering the landscape of meaning for many young people. Preliminary studies suggest that while the constructs of MIL continue to play an integral role in mental health, the dynamics of these relationships may have evolved, due to particularly the experiences of prolonged lockdowns and global uncertainty [5–7]. Research indicates that the pandemic has profoundly affected young people’s mental health and their coping mechanisms, and the drastic changes in social interactions, employment, and daily routines have led to increased levels of anxiety and depression among young people [8, 9].

Although comparative studies are scarce, it is evident that the pandemic has had profound effects on distinct socio-economic groups, e.g., students, employed youth, and unemployed youth, each facing unique challenges that shape their experiences of MIL and mental health. Additionally, results of a previous study suggest that while young people expressed concerns about the immediate effects of COVID-19, many did not perceive these disruptions as detrimental to their long-term futures [10]. This perspective may reflect a coping mechanism where the youth focus on resilience and adaptability in the face of uncertainty, thereby reshaping their engagement with MIL.

In addition to the pandemic-induced shifts, cultural contexts significantly shape the pursuit and experience of meaning in life (MIL). Western cultures, characterized by individualistic values, prioritize personal achievement and self-fulfillment as core sources of MIL. In contrast, Eastern cultures, such as China, emphasize collectivistic values, situating MIL within community relationships and social roles. This distinction is critical, as cultural norms influence the ways in which MIL supports mental health, particularly evident during crises like COVID-19. For instance, research by Espinosa et al. [11] illustrates that in collectivistic settings, prosocial behaviors—which bolster life satisfaction and mitigate negative emotions during challenges—are more prevalent. This is juxtaposed against the stable self-concept in individualistic societies, where personal authenticity and self-expression predominate [12].

As such, there is a pressing need for empirical research to delve deeper into the current state of meaningfulness and its sustained impact on the post-pandemic mental health of young individuals. This research aims to bridge the gap in the body of literature, by providing contemporary insights into how MIL continues to influence mental health outcomes in a radically changed world. By utilizing a broad survey of young individuals aged 14 to 35 years, and including participants from diverse socio-economic backgrounds, we obtained a better opportunity to understand the complexities of this issue across a wider spectrum of youth experiences.

Meaning in life (MIL) describes the perception that one’s existence has value, purpose, and coherence [13], and embodies individual’s essential motivation and fundamental needs in life [14]. Studies have consistently highlighted the pivotal role of MIL as a crucial predictor of subjective well-being (SWB), health, and mortality [1, 13, 15–17]. Previous research has indicated that a high level of MIL can act as a protective factor against psychological problems and contribute to positive mental health outcomes during difficult times such as the COVID-19 pandemic [18–21]. In contrast, a low sense of MIL is linked to a feeling of hopelessness, a perceived lack of control over one’s life, and diminished motivation [22–25]. Further, over recent decades, numerous studies have yielded inconsistent findings on the relationship between MIL and mental health [2, 3, 26].

PML, SML, and mental health

Meaning in life encompasses a complex structure that integrates cognitive, motivational, and affective dimensions; as in, understanding oneself, pursuing purpose, and deriving emotional fulfillment, respectively [25, 27], and Frankl [14] argued the importance of the process of searching for meaning in life (SML). Steger and his colleagues [28] expanded this framework to distinctly address two dimensions that were related to each other: the presence of meaning in life (PML), where individuals perceive their lives as significant and purposeful, and the search for meaning in life (SML), involving active efforts to deepen life’s meaning [28, 29]. Additionally, the literature suggests that mental health is not solely about the absence of mental illness, but also encompasses positive aspects such as life satisfaction, self-efficacy, connectedness, and happiness [30]. This broader perspective acknowledges that mental health is an overall status, with mental well-being and mental dysfunction being interrelated components [31]. In addressing the broad concept of mental health, the present study adopts a holistic approach that encompasses both positive and negative aspect [32].

Prior studies indicate that these two aspects of meaning are associated with distinct sets of nomological networks [33]. There is consistent evidence on the positive correlations between PML and mental well-being factors across the life span [15, 18, 29, 34–39], but theoretical debates and empirical findings related to SML are more inconsistent. Some authors considered searching for meaning as a natural and beneficial aspect of life [14, 40, 41], while it might be a dysfunctional process [42] and related to undesirable psychological outcomes [4, 23, 28, 33, 40, 43, 44]. These is also a balanced view that SML could be either healthy or unhealthy, depending on the individual’s motivations for searching, or other factors (e.g., cultures or regions) [29, 45–48]. Furthermore, researchers have demonstrated that SML impacts individuals’ psychological well-being differently based on age groups [49]. The search for meaning in life could play a more protective role within younger populations rather than older populations [4, 40, 44, 47, 50], which implies a moderating effect resulting from age [2, 51].

Additionally, there are mixed findings on the interaction effect between PML and SML through quantitative analyses. Earlier studies pointed out that the two dimensions were negatively correlated with each other [15, 28, 40, 52], and stated that the absence of PML would lead to SML. Notwithstanding, adverse results were observed in the following cited studies: that SML was positively related to PML among adolescents, especially those who already possess a sense of life’s meaning [29, 36, 53–55]. Moreover, the study on emerging adults found no correlation between them [2]. This inconsistency could be explained by cultural differences. The study by Steger and his colleagues [56] compared American and Japanese individuals, and found that American participants with a high level of SML experienced low PML, while Japanese participants reported an increased PML. It may due to the dialectical and interdependent characteristics of the Asian culture, which regards the ongoing process of SML as crucial for achieving desired outcomes [56], and holds a holistic viewpoint of the world, leading to the belief that there is a connection between SML and PML [3, 55].

Overall, previous studies examined the theoretical insights into the roles of factors, both positive and negative, related to meaning in life in mental health outcomes. Despite the fact that the mechanisms of the association are unclear, some studies have suggested that perceiving PML may yield a greater effect than SML [43, 57] while other studies noted a reversal effect [58]. This indicates that SML is an essential process for maintaining the sense of meaning in life, and PML might offer resources that lessen any potentially harmful effects of SML [15, 44, 50]. Therefore, the roles of SML and PML, and their relationship between mental health outcomes require more research to examine them.

Adolescents and young adults

There is an increasing emphasis on psychological well-being and mental health for adolescents and young adults beyond traditional healthcare settings [59–61], particularly due to the profound biological, psychological, and social changes they undergo during this critical developmental stage which significantly impacts their lifelong health trajectories and socioeconomic outcomes [62, 63]. Despite its importance, the early adulthood stage often receives less attention in public health initiatives, because it is traditionally viewed as the healthiest segment of the population [64, 65]. However, the growing global youth population and the shifting focus towards prioritizing youth development [66] have highlighted the diverse mental health needs of young people, and an ongoing demand for reliable and cultural research to figure out protective factors that could help young people minimize the risk of mental problems and promote well-being, especially given the serious background of the pandemic. One such protective factor is meaning in life [36, 67]. Despite the mental challenges posed by the pandemic, more evidence is needed to determine how closely the SML and PML relate to adolescents and young adults.

The problem is similar in the Chinese context, where the most related studies focused on the undergraduates [21, 45, 68–72], one study focused on junior high school students [73], and another study focused on adolescents and emerging adults [47]. Most of these research findings are similarly inconsistent with previous reports, due primarily to the differences in mental health outcomes, study timelines (before and during the pandemic), and selection of participants.

However, the protective role of MIL is increasingly recognized as a consensus, both nationally and globally. The key issue is that further evidence is required to clarify its mechanism of action. Furthermore, adolescents and young adults encompass a wide array of individuals across various age groups, undergoing crucial developmental shifts from early adolescence through to young adulthood [61, 74]. As they progress through these pivotal stages, they encounter a range of psychological challenges. Recognizing the distinct experiences of diverse youth groups (e.g., students, employed youth, or unemployed youth) during the pandemic, the present study aims to uncover their unique sense of meaning in life and mental health. These comparisons are not only critical for understanding the varied impacts of such global crises, but also the are essential for designing mental health interventions that address the unique needs of each group. By examining these groups separately, our research seeks to provide nuanced insights into how different life circumstances influence mental health and MIL, thereby contributing to more effective, targeted support strategies.

Aims of the present study

In consideration of the adverse impacts of the pandemic on youth mental health [61, 67, 75, 76] previous evidence about the inconsistent role of SML and PML, respectively, in mental health outcomes, and the absence of research covering a broad spectrum of youth groups, the present study aims to explore the relationships between SML, PML, and mental health in the Chinese youth population after experiencing the pandemic lockdown. Although previous studies have identified associations between the variables, the mechanisms through which these variables influence mental health remain unclear. The primary objective of this study is to fill this gap by analyzing and interpreting these relationships in depth through quantitative methods.

By utilizing cross-sectional survey data and multivariate statistical analysis techniques, this research aims to provide empirical support for understanding the complex relationships between MIL and mental health. To enhance the depth and applicability of our findings, we employ multi-group comparison analysis to examine these relationships across different life situations among youth populations. Specifically, we compare three distinct groups: students, employed youth, and unemployed youth. The results are expected to offer theoretical and data-driven foundations for subsequent mental health intervention measures, tailored to address the unique needs of these diverse youth groups.

Methods

Study procedure and participants

The present study was conducted through a cross-sectional survey during April to May in 2023, almost a year after the end of the lockdown in Shanghai, China. Data were collected through paper questionnaires using a multistage stratified sampling procedure. In China, the youth are clearly defined as aged 14–35 years in the national policy “Middle- and Long-term Youth Development Plan (2016–2025)”, and we employed the definition of the age scope in the survey. Before data collection, all the participants were informed consent to the study, including the parents if the participants aged 14 to 17 through their schools. All the participants were informed about the nature of the survey (for research and policy making) and their right to refuse or quit the study when they were invited. They were asked to finish the self-report survey anonymously. This study was funded by the Shanghai Communist Youth League Committee Office for Youth Services and Rights Protection, and the research procedures have been reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of School of Law, Shanghai Lixin University of Accounting and Finance with No. C-22–6701-23-016, which was conducted in accordance with the guidance of the Helsinki Declaration.

Participants were initially categorized into three groups based on their identity characteristics within the youth population: students, employed youths, and unemployed youths. To ensure the representativeness of these groups, we utilized population estimates provided by the local Statistical Bureau. These estimates included the proportions of these youth groups, as well as further breakdowns of student subgroups, such as secondary school students, university students and vocational training students. Within each group, additional stratification was conducted based on specific characteristics, such as educational stage, nature of employment, and residency status (Hukou registration). Different areas of the city were selected for the survey to ensure comprehensive geographic coverage of the youth population. By employing these strategies, we aimed to maximize the representativeness of diverse young people in the sample, thereby enhancing the generalizability and reliability of the findings and ensuring the validity of the sample. The questionnaire included the measures of SML, PML, and mental health status, and the time taken eliciting demographic information.

Measures

Meaning in life (SML & PML)

Meaning in life (MIL) was measured through the Chinese version of Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) [28, 77], which is the most widely used questionnaire with robust psychometric properties [78]. The scale includes two 5-item subscales, SML (MLQ-S, e.g., “I am seeking a purpose or mission for my life”) and PML (MLQ-P, e.g., “My life has a clear sense of purpose”), and employs a seven-point Likert scale rated from 1 (absolutely disagree) to 7 (totally agree). The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.85 for the total scale, 0.87 for the MLQ-S, and 0.84 for the MLQ-P. Higher summed scores indicate a greater pursuit of meaning or a stronger sense of meaning presence.

Mental health

In response to the evolving understanding of mental health, our study adopted a comprehensive approach to its measurement. Aligning with the recent bodies of theoretical and empirical literature, we define mental health not merely as the absence of mental illness but as the presence of positive psychological status [30, 64, 79]. Mental health status was assessed through the self-rated mental health scale (SRMHS, 14-item), and it was demonstrated by the research group to have good reliability, as well as convergent and discriminant validity. SRMHS is a part separated from the self-rated health (SRH) measurement scale, which is a widely-used instrument in health assessment in China [32, 80–84]. SRMHS encompasses three dimensions: positive emotion, psychosocial symptom/negative emotion, cognitive function. It aligns with the prevailing consensus on the comprehensive definition of mental health. Participants were invited to indicate the frequency of their experiences during the past month. It applies a method of 1 to 5 grade scoring for each item (1 = never/very bad, 5 = always/very good). Items in the psychosocial symptom/negative emotion dimension are negative phrasing, and they need reverse scoring when calculated to be a sum score of all items. Higher summed scores denote a better mental health status. Cronbach’s α coefficient of SRMHS was 0.881.

Covariables

Prior studies have found that the variables including age, gender, Hukou, and only-child have significant relationships with mental health or meaning in life among Chinese adolescents or young people [47, 48, 85–87]. Therefore, we tested gender (0 = male, 1 = female), Hukou holder (0 = no, 1 = yes), and only-child (0 = no, 1 = yes) as covariables when conducting the statistical analysis, and examined the moderating effect of age (age in years, continuous variable), in according with our literature review, carried out previously.

Statistical analyses

The original data were inputted using EpiData 3.1, while SPSS 26.0 was used for data cleaning and statistical analysis. First, we conducted descriptive statistical tests and bivariate correlations calculation for the full sample and within the three subgroups (students, employed youth, and unemployed youth). We used independent t-tests, chi-square tests, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine group differences among demographic categories. The initial results from the skewness and kurtosis suggest that the data approximate a normal distribution (see Supplementary Table 1). Given the non-normal distribution of the variables, as indicated by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p < 0.001), Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was utilized to assess correlations between each pair of continuous variables. Concerning deviations from the normal distribution in regression analysis, previous studies showed that linear regression is reliable across any distribution for large samples [88–90]. Then, as a preliminary step we initially conducted hierarchical regression analyses to explore the relationships between the key variables. We examined the direct effect of SML and PML on mental health status, and explored the relationships and potential patterns between variables, thereby offering a foundation for predictive assessment [91].

After that, PROCESS macro 4.2 [88], a regression path analysis modeling tool, was used to examine the mediation and moderation effect with demographic variables as covariates, SML as an independent variable, mental health status as the dependent variable, PML as mediating variable, and age as moderated variable. Process Model 4 was applied to conduct mediation analyses, and to detect the size of the direct effect and the indirect effect. Then, Process Model 59 was chosen for moderation analysis to test whether the paths were moderated by age. When significant interaction effects were observed, the simple slopes method was employed to visually demonstrate the moderation. This involved plotting regression lines to depict the interaction relationships at low (mean − 1 SD), medium (mean), and high scores (mean + 1 SD) of the moderator (age). Due to the results of the Test of Homogeneity of Variances (Levene’s Test), heteroskedasticity does exist (but mainly in reference to variables of age and gender), indicating its potential to affect results. So, given the sufficiently large sample size, we chose the H0 (Huber-White) heteroskedasticity-consistent standard error estimator to provide adequate robustness. Five thousand (5,000) bootstrapping samples were used to estimate the 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals (CIs) [92], which were considered significant when zero was not included [93]. All continuous variables were standardized and mean centered before being incorporated into the mediation model, and a significance level of α = 0.05 was applied throughout the analysis.

The mediation analysis of SML, PML, and MH among distinct subgroups

We initially used hierarchical regression analysis to check the data, and the results confirmed our hypothesis that SML and PML have a significant effect on mental health status (detailed information in Supplementary Tables 2–5). Given these findings, we further investigated the role of PML using PROCESS Model 4.2, hypothesizing that PML serves as a mediator in the relationship between SML and mental health. Therefore, we conducted of PROCESSOR 4.2 Model 4 with SML as the independent variables, MH as the dependent variable, PML as the mediating variable, and demographic variables (age, gender, Hukou, and only-child status) as control variables. As shown in Supplementary Tables 2–5 and Fig. 1, The direct path from SML to PML was positive and statistically significant (p < 0.001) across all participant groups, which indicated that a greater degree of pursuit of meaning in life (i.e., more SML) always led to a stronger presence of meaning in life (i.e., enhanced PML). Importantly, the inclusion of PML in the model significantly attenuated the direct effect of SML on MH, suggesting that PML mediates this relationship by transforming the existential search into psychological well-being. This mediation effect was substantiated by the decrease in the direct effect of SML on MH when PML was included in the analysis, aligning with the criteria for statistical mediation and confirming that PML acts as a mediator rather than a moderator in this context.

As proposed in Hypothesis 2, PML acted in the role of mediation across all groups, but the effect size differed in distinct groups. Comparing the three subgroups, the direct effect of SML on MH is relatively small and not statistically significant in the student group and the unemployed youth group, suggesting that PML acted as a full mediator in these groups. The influence of SML on ML is fully channeled through PML, indicating that the psychological benefits for pursuing meaning in life are realized entirely through its ability to foster a sensed presence of meaning. However, among the employed youth group, PML acted as a partial mediator. While SML through PML significantly influenced MH positively, the process of SML itself contributes additionally to mental health outcomes; in this case, negatively. This partial mediation reflects potential stress or challenges associated with balancing the pressures of employment and the personal search for meaning.

The moderated mediation analysis of SML on MH among distinct subgroups

We investigated whether age moderated the mediation effect of PML on the association between SML and MH. It was detected that age was a significant negative predictor on mental health (at least p < 0.01). However, no moderating effect of age was observed among students and employed youth. In contrast, a distinct moderating effect was evident in the group of unemployed youth (See steps 4 and 5 in Supplement Tables 2, 3, 4 and 5). The results partially supported Hypothesis 3.

For the unemployed individuals, mental health status was significantly predicted by the interaction effect of SML and age (SML × Age, B = −0.058, p < 0.01), indicating that the positive effect of SML on MH decreases as age increases. This suggested that the influence of searching for meaning in life regarding mental health is stronger in younger than in older ones among unemployed individuals. Moreover, the interaction effect of PML and age was positive significant (PML × Age, B = 0.042, p < 0.05), indicating the influence of perceived meaning on MH increases with increasing age. Therefore, while the pursuit for meaning has a diminishing effect with increasing age, the actual possession of meaning has an increasingly positive effect as these individuals age. Regarding the conditional direct and indirect effect of SML on MH mediated by PML (Supplementary Table 6), we conducted or analysis using the Johnson-Neyman method. There was a significant indirect effect over age of “16.01” [mean age + (−8.226), B = 0.401, SE = 0.184, 95%CI = (0.039, 0.764)] (Fig. 2). Meanwhile, age also directly moderated the effect of SML on mental health status, especially for the youth aged under around 24 years [mean age + (−0.379), B = 0.220, SE = 0.089, 95%CI = (0.045, 0.396)], or above 32.79 years, [mean age + 8.463, B = −294, SE = 0.143, 95%CI = (−0.574, −0.014)] (Fig. 3). According to the results, the benefits of experiencing a sense of life’s purpose are almost universal among unemployed youth (99.52%), and become more apparent with increasing age. However, the pursuit of life’s purpose is particularly meaningful for the younger demographic, but more pronounced for those under the age of 24 years, assuming the trend holds that younger individuals benefit more.

Discussion

The findings in our study significantly provide insights into the associations between the presence of meaning in life (PML) and the search for meaning in life (SML), and how they relate to mental health among Chinese youths in the urban context. Consistently, PML was found to act as a robust protective factor, enhancing mental health across different youth subgroups. This aligns with the research by Rudaz et al. [96], which highlighted in a similar way the mediating effects of PML on mental health outcomes by enhancing psychological resilience​​.

Interestingly, SML showed a complex relationship with mental health, where it appeared beneficial in certain contexts and detrimental in other contexts. The direct influence of SML on MH was either negative or non-significant in student and unemployed youth groups, suggesting that merely searching for meaning without achieving it might not benefit mental health, and could potentially be distressing. Our findings resonate with those of Currier and his colleagues [57], who suggest that the temporal associations between SML and mental health could vary, indicating that the timing of the research process might influence its effects. The dual nature of the impact of SML aligns with the nuanced perspectives presented by Wang and Fu [87], who note that the effectiveness of the search for meaning could be contingent upon the individual’s existing mental health status, and the presence of supportive environmental factors. Additionally, our study, by comparing different youth groups (students, employed youth, and unemployed youth), has helped us to better understand and recognize these points. For instance, employed youths often face unique challenges that may complicate their engagement with SML and PML, which is related to their mental health outcomes. This aspect is crucial for understanding the full scope of how socio-economic factors intersect with personal meaning frameworks. Thus, it lays a foundation for more in-depth future research.

Mediating effects of presence of meaning in life

In our study, the search for meaning positively predicted mental health status, especially in adverse circumstances, such as unemployment, and it seems to be more effective than under other conditions. The total effects, encompassing both direct and indirect pathways, were also significant, highlighting the overarching beneficial impact of meaning in life on mental health. The direct path from SML to PML was positive and statistically significant across all youth groups, confirming that actively searching for meaning consistently leads to recognizing more meaning in life. The indirect effects of SML on MH through PML were positive and significant across the board, illustrating that the positive correlations between searching for meaning and mental health operate primarily through successfully finding meaning.

The relationship between the SML and the PML provides a compelling insight into the dynamic processes of meaning-making. Compared to the conventional understanding that SML primarily sustains an already existing sense of meaning, as proposed by Steger and his colleagues [29, 56], our findings reveal that the pursuit of more meaning would damage the mental health status of the employed youth in the Asian culture context, but it also confirms the positive relationship with the presence of meaning. So, the relationship cannot be solely attributed to cultural differences; rather, it is more closely associated with the socio-economic circumstances of the youth. Even within the same East Asian Confucian cultural context, the process of pursuing meaning can resemble a stressful event, potentially exerting negative effects on health. However, by perceiving the presence of meaning, individuals can mitigate or reverse these potential adverse impacts. Our study underscores that the mediating role of PML is significant across all demographic groups, indicating that the perception of meaning can significantly buffer against negative mental health outcomes. It suggests that the perceived presence of meaning in life is a crucial psychological asset that enhances mental health; thus, interventions aimed at enhancing PML could serve as effective therapeutic strategies, particularly in working settings where youths face various environmental and life stressors.

Age as a moderator

Age was found to moderate the relationship between the search for meaning, and the perception of meaning in life on mental health status among the unemployed youth, with younger individuals deriving more benefit from SML compared to older individuals. This finding is critical for developing age-specific interventions, and supports the work of Datu and his colleagues [49], who documented a similar age-dependent differentiation in the effects of meaning on well-being. Our study extends this research by demonstrating that these age-related differences are particularly pronounced in the context of significant life changes and challenges, such as unemployment or those differences posed by the COVID-19 pandemic. Our results suggest that interventions aimed at enhancing mental health among the unemployed youth might need to be tailored according to age. For younger individuals, strategies to help them search for meaning might be more beneficial, whereas for older individuals, enhancing and maintaining their perception of existing meaning might be more effective.

Household registration impact on youth mental health

Besides what is discussed above, the role of local Hukou status (household registration) emerged as a significant factor on the youth mental health, particularly for students and the employed youth in the urban context. The findings are consistent with recent studies [83, 97, 98], which would indicate that the Hukou system is intricately linked to social welfare, with individuals at the local level receiving more substantial government support, including healthcare, housing subsidies, and pension benefits. These benefits help reduce life stress, offering a safety net that enhances overall mental well-being. Some studies also found the institutional transition of the Hukou status could be linked to the risk of depression in later life [99] and the size of the inter-Hukou network may influence the mental health of migrants [100]. The impact of Hukou, reflecting a form of social stratification, underscores the need for policy interventions that address these disparities to improve mental health equality among Chinese youth groups.

Implications for practice and future research

By comparing groups under different socio-economic conditions and analyzing the effects across various age groups, our results hold significant implications for subsequent practice and studies. Given the significant roles of PML and SML in shaping mental health, especially highlighted during the pandemic, interventions should focus on enhancing these aspects of meaning among the youth. Educational programs and psychological interventions that promote an understanding and development of meaning could be particularly effective. Furthermore, recognizing the importance of socio-economic factors such as Hukou status in mental health interventions could lead to more tailored and culturally sensitive approaches. Through extending the dialogue on SML and PML to encompass both development and transformation, our study paves the way for future studies to examine how these dynamics operate over extended periods, and in various socio-economic contexts. This comprehensive approach is vital for crafting strategies that more effectively support individuals in their quest for a meaningful life, which is crucial for improving overall mental well-being.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study utilized a cross-sectional survey design, which inherently limits the ability to infer causality from the observed associations. Specifically, this design does not allow for the full exploration of the multifaceted causal mechanisms between meaning in life and mental health status. Second, while efforts were made to increase the heterogeneity of the sample by balancing variables such as gender, occupations, Hukou, education level, the sampling method might still limit the findings, in order to be generalizable to other cities or regions. Third, the validity of the mental health measure may need more empirical evidence to ensure its generalizability outside of China.

Future studies could benefit from employing longitudinal designs and incorporating ethnographic materials to capture dynamic interactions, and provide a more detailed causal analysis. Additionally, examining the impact of cultural factors on the perception and search for meaning could provide deeper insights into how meaning-centered interventions could be adapted for culturally diverse populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the complex relationships between the presence and search for meaning in life and mental health among the Chinese youth. Both the search for and the presence of meaning significantly correlate with mental health outcomes. This suggests that mental health interventions could be more effective if they would incorporate strategies that foster an environment conducive to exploring and finding personal meaning. Programs that encourage reflection on personal values, life goals, and meaningful engagement in community activities might be beneficial for enhancing overall well-being. Such interventions should aim to build resilience and provide the tools necessary for young individuals to navigate life’s challenges while maintaining a strong sense of purpose.

The relevance of these findings is particularly highlighted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic. Research has demonstrated that resilience mediates the relationship between meaning in life and mental well-being, which supports the notion that cultivating a sense of purpose can significantly reduce the psychological impacts of crises [101]. Similarly, a Chinese study also found that meaning in life positively influences psychological resilience, and can alleviate anxiety and depression among Chinese high school graduates [102]. These studies suggest that interventions aimed at enhancing meaning in life could be particularly beneficial during challenging times, providing crucial support for youth facing the unique pressures of the ongoing pandemic.

Our findings also underscore the importance of considering how different factors related to meaning in life influence mental health across various youth subgroups. By focusing on these factors, mental health interventions can be more effectively tailored to meet the needs of different youth groups such as students, employed youth, and unemployed youth. Mental health professionals can more effectively support the diverse pathways through which young individuals engage with meaning in their lives.

Future research should continue to explore these dynamics, aiming to develop targeted and effective mental health strategies that not only address the symptoms but also enhance the overall quality of life by fostering a deeper sense of purpose and fulfillment.
