Gut microbiota-derived Tryptophanol driven by N-Carbamylglutamate alleviates premature ovarian failure through inhibiting oxidative stress
Authors: Jinxin Zong, Yuxin Luo, Mengting Wu, Nan Wang, Yue Sui, Junjun Yang, Boqi Zhang, Chunjin Li, Xu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremature ovarian failure (POF) affects 1–5% of women under 40 years old and is characterized by granulosa cells (GCs) apoptosis and follicular atresia. However, the pathogenesis of POF is complex and lacks effective prevention and treatment strategies. N-carbamylglutamate (NCG), a bioactive substance known for its antioxidant properties; however, whether it can alleviate POF remains unclear.MethodsThis study using the cyclophosphamide (Cy)-induced POF mouse model, and demonstrated that NCG, has beneficial effects in alleviating POF symptoms and restoring the intestinal mucosal barrier through gut microbiota-metabolite crosstalk. Integrated 16 S rRNA sequencing, untargeted metabolomics, and fecal bacteria transplantation (FMT) experiments revealed that NCG induced restructuring of gut microbial communities, with enrichment of Muribaculum intestinale and concomitant elevation of its associated Tryptophan-derived metabolite, Tryptophanol.ResultsMechanistically, Tryptophanol, supplementation alleviated POF by reducing mouse GCs apoptosis and oxidative stress via inhibiting Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/ Protein Kinase B (PI3K/AKT) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, thereby restoring ovarian function and fertility, and the efficacy of NCG in alleviating POF relies on promoting Tryptophanol-mediated anti-oxidative stress.ConclusionsOur findings, highlight the “gut microbiota-Tryptophanol-ovary” axis in POF pathogenesis, and propose a potential therapy for NCG to regulate gut microbiota to restore ovarian redox homeostasis to alleviate POF.Graphical Abstract NCG alleviates POF by restoring the gut microbiota , and the intestinal barrier, and promoting the colonization of beneficial bacteria M. intestinale. The therapeutic effect of NCG is mainly attributed to the inhibition of oxidative stress and mouse GCs apoptosis by the generation of Tryptophanol, which inhibits the activation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways. Introduction

Premature ovarian failure (POF) is a non-physiological disease, as a type of ovarian dysfunction, which seriously affects women’s physical and mental health and is associated with serious consequences such as autoimmune diseases, cardiovascular diseases, psychological disorders and infertility [1, 2]. Globally, this condition affects 1%-5% of women under 40 years old, manifesting through sex hormone imbalance and accelerated follicular atresia [3]. The ovarian granulosa cells (GCs) are the main functioning cells that secrete estrogen, and maintain hormonal balance in the ovarian niche, in order to promote oocyte maturation via autocrine and paracrine mechanisms [1]. Apoptosis of the GCs in the follicles leads to follicular atrophy and atresia, ultimately leading to POF [4]. Mechanistically, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation coupled with compromised antioxidant defense mechanisms induces oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis in ovarian tissue, thereby accelerating POF progression [5]. While hormone replacement therapy (HRT) remains the clinical mainstay, its long-term application carries substantial risks including thromboembolic complications and endocrine disruption [6]. Therefore, finding therapeutic strategies targeting the regulation of GCs apoptosis and redox homeostasis restoration may represent a promising approach to extend ovarian function longevity and mitigate POF-associated pathological manifestations.

Gut microbiota acts as a regulator in a bidirectional relationship with many key organs of the host, and in regulating host health processes is beneficial, including metabolic and nutritional homeostasis, immune system maturation, and stimulation [7, 8]. Emerging evidence suggests ovarian senescence triggers gut microbiota dysbiosis, while fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from young donors to aged murine models demonstrates remarkable ovarian rejuvenation effects, including attenuated granulosa cells apoptosis and follicular atresia [9]. Despite these advances, the role of microbial communities in POF remains underexplored. The development of diseases in distal organs is influenced by the gut microbiota, which establishes an ‘gut-x axis’ through bacterial translocation, metabolic changes and systemic humoral factors [7]. The gut microbiota significantly influences intestinal barrier function, and impairment of the intestinal barrier can lead to bacterial translocation, thereby exacerbating the pathophysiology of inflammatory and metabolic disorders. Notably, small-molecule metabolites from the gut microbiota are essential in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier [10]. Therefore, targeting metabolites regulated by gut microbiota and treating POF by restoring intestinal barrier function may be a new strategy.

Among various dietary additives, N-carbamylglutamate (NCG) has emerged as a particularly promising candidate, garnering growing attention in recent biomedical research [11]. Dietary NCG supplementation enhances animal growth performance, intestinal integrity, and antioxidant status through stimulating lymphocyte proliferation and modulating cytokine synthesis [12, 13, 14]. Furthermore, NCG administration regulates amino acid and lipid metabolism in rat models [15]. However, the regulatory effect and potential mechanism of NCG on the disorder of gut microbiota in POF, especially its metabolic function, have not been explored. Therefore, the present study systematically investigated the mitigating effect of NCG on POF using the cyclophosphamide (Cy)-induced POF mice model. Cy is recognized as a drug that leads to the depletion of ovarian reserve and thus POF [16].

The present study demonstrated that NCG modified the composition and function of gut microbiota in Cy-induced POF mice, with a substantial augmentation in Tryptophanol-producing and Tryptophan-metabolizing bacteria (Muribaculum intestinale). Furthermore, it was determined that Tryptophanol supplementation could effectively alleviate POF by mitigating oxidative stress through the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways in vivo and in vitro. These findings provided theoretical and practical support for the clinical application of NCG in relieving POF by regulating gut microbiota.

Materials and methods

Animals experimental

In this research, all healthy C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology (Liaoning, China). All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the welfare of laboratory animal. Mice were raised in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal lab of Jilin University and maintained in standard environmental conditions of temperature of 22 ± 2 ℃, relative humidity 60 ± 10% and light/dark cycles 12/12. All animal experiments approved by the institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jilin University (Approval number: SY202301300).

POF model

For cyclophosphamide (Cy) induced Cy-POF model (POF), 5–7 weeks old female mice were treated with a single intraperitoneal injection of Cy (75 mg/kg, 100–200 µL). This resulted in the successful establishment of the Cy-induced POF model after 7 d [17]. The Cy was purchased from Sigma (C7397, USA), and dissolved in sterile saline.

NCG treatment experiments

For therapeutic use, NCG (purity ≥ 97%) was provided by Hunan Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Research Institute (Hunan, Changsha, China), and dissolved in sterile saline. To investigate the effect of NCG on POF mice, mice were divided into three groups: blank group (Control), model group (POF), and NCG treatment group (NCG). NCG was dissolved in sterile saline, after the POF model was successfully established, 7 weeks old female mice were gavaged with NCG (130 mg/kg, 100–200 µL) for 21 consecutive days. Control group and POF group were gavaged equal doses of sterile saline on a daily basis. After preliminary experiments, we found that 130 mg /kg NCG was the optimal concentration for gavaged therapy.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) treatment experiments

The female mice that were used for the fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) trials and fecal collection were each fed separately. Following established protocols [18], fresh fecal samples collected from the Control and NCG group were diluted in sterile saline to a working concentration of 50 mg/mL, centrifuged at 6,000 g for 10 min; the supernatant was collected, and filtered through a 100 μm polyether-sulfone filters (Merck, USA). And subjected to three additional washing/centrifugation cycles with sterile PBS to minimize potential residual drug carryover. The final pellet was resuspended in PBS and used for transplantation within 30 min. Transplantation into recipient mice was achieved by gavage with 200 µL/day of fecal-sample supernatant for 4 consecutive weeks, and in the second week, the POF model was established by intraperitoneal injection of Cy.

The FMT receptor mice was randomly divided into four groups: blank group (Control), model group (POF), POF+FMTNCG treatment group (FMTNCG), and POF+FMTCON treatment group (FMTCON) (Fig. 3A).

Muribaculum intestinale treatment experiments

To investigate the effect of Muribaculum intestinale (M. intestinale) on POF mice, M. intestinale (DSM 28989, DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms, Germany). M. intestinale cultured in MPYG Medium (HB8931, HOPEBIO, Qingdao, China) under 37 °C anaerobic conditions for 72 h. The cultures were diluted with PBS at a final concentration of 1 × 1010 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL. For pasteurization, 1 × 1010 CFU/mL M. intestinale was inactivated by being placed at 70 °C for 30 min. Previous studies have demonstrated that a daily oral dose of 1 × 1010 CFU of M. intestinale can fully colonize and regulate metabolism in vivo [19]. After the POF model was successfully established, 7 weeks old female mice were orally administered with M. intestinale or pasteurizated M. intestinale for 21 consecutive days, whereas the Control group and POF group were gavaged with the same volume of solvent.

The mice were randomly divided into four groups: blank group (Control), model group (POF), POF + M. intestinale treatment group (M. intestinale), and POF+pasteurizated M. intestinale treatment group (pasteurizated M. intestinale) (Fig. 5A).

Estrus cycle assessment

Vaginal cytology was performed daily for 8 days to monitor the estrous cycle according to previously established standards [20]. In simple terms, the vaginas of the mice were rinsed with 10 µL of 0.9% sterile saline, and the collected fluid was transferred to a microscope slide for analysis.

Litter size assessment

After the treatment experiment, the female mice were mated with adult male mice in a 2 ∶ 1 ratio to check the fertility. After 10 days, the males were removed and the fertility of the females was assessed by litter size after 35 days.

Hormone assay

According to previous experimental reports [20], we collected serum samples from mice as well as supernatants from mouse granulosa cells cultured in vitro. The levels of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (YX-E20419), luteinizing hormone (LH) (YX-E20343), and estradiol (E2) (YX-E20381) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit by Sinobestbio Biologicals (Shanghai, China).

Histological analysis

As previously described [17], tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for at least 12 h to ensure optimal tissue preservation. After the ovaries underwent paraffin embedding and were sectioned into 5 μm thick slices, H&E staining was applied, and follicle counting was conducted on every fifth section. Lastly, the total number of various follicles was then multiplied by 5 and counted. Ovarian follicles at different developmental stages were differentiated from each other as described in previous study [21].

For immunohistochemical analysis, following endogenous peroxidase activity blockade, the sections were subjected with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, following by incubation with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Antibody source and dilutions were as follows: rabbit Cleaved caspase 3 (MCE, HY-P80623, 1:300), rabbit IL-1β (Abclona, A8370, 1:100), and rabbit SOD2 (Bioworld, BS6734, 1:300).

MDA, SOD and GSH-Px detection

Quantification of the concentration of MDA (E-BC-K028-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China), SOD (S010S, Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), and GSH-Px (E-BC-K096-M, Elabscience) in ovaries were performed following the instructions in the assay kits. Specifically, ovarian samples were collected quickly, rinsed and weighed in pre-cooled sterile saline. Approximately 10 mg of ovarian tissues are homogenized on ice in pre-cooled PBS and centrifuged at 10,000 g at 4 ° C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and the total protein was determined by Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Assay Kit (Beyotime), and the results were normalized.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) detection

The ROS levels in ovaries and cell lyasates were used the ROS assay (E-BC-K138-F, Elabscience) kit. Following to the manufacturer’s protocol, the fluorescent DCFH-DA (10 µM) probe was loaded at 37 °C for 30 min.

Cell culture and treatment

According to previous reports [20], female mice (3 weeks old) were euthanised via intraperitoneal injection of pregnant Mare Serum Gonadotropin (5 IU) (PMSG, Ningbo Sansheng Biotech) followed by 48 h incubation. The ovaries were rapidly isolated under aseptic conditions. Subsequently, the ovarian tissue was placed into cell culture medium by puncturing the follicle with 1 mL of syringe needle, and the granulosa cells and oocytes were sufficiently released into the medium by repeated blowing of the tissue with a pasteurised tube. Finally, the mouse granulosa cells (GCs) obtained by isolation were placed in DME/F12 basal medium (Sigma, D8437) containing 10% inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, USA), and 1% (vol/vol) penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, CA, USA). For cell treatment experiment, mouse GCs seeded in 6-well cell culture plates (1 × 106 cells / well) until reached to 60–70% confluence at 37 °C, 5% CO₂ and 95% air.

Mouse GCs were seeded in 6-well cell culture plates (1 × 10⁶ cells/well) and cultured until reaching 60–70% confluence at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and 95% air. To establish an in vitro model of POF, cells were treated with 300 µM Cy. The protective effect of Tryptophanol was evaluated by co-treatment with 100 nM L-Tryptophanol. After 24 h of treatment, cell supernatants and cells were collected for subsequent experimental analysis. To investigate the role of specific signaling pathways, the AKT activator SC79 (MCE, 5 µg/mL) or the MAPK activator Anisomycin (MCE, 10 µM) was added to the culture medium 1 h before downstream assays. The Control group received an equal amount of solvent.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability assay was performed using cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime). Cells were inoculated into 96-well plates ( 1 × 104 cells / well) and treated with (0, 100 µM, 300 µM, 500 µM, and 1000 µM) Cy and (0, 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 µM and 10 µM) L-Trptophanol for 24 h when cells reached 60% confluence. Next, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added and incubated for 2 h. Cells were quantified and measured using an ELX 800 Universal Enzyme Labeler at 450 nm.

Cell apoptosis assay

Treated mouse GCs were resuspended via trypsin digestion, then incubated with Annexin V-FITC (Beyotime) and propidium iodide (Beyotime) for 30 min, followed by flow cytometry.

Faecal sample collection and 16 S rRNA sequencing

Based on previous literature reports [22, 23], fecal samples were collected from three groups of mice, Control, POF and NCG groups, and genomic DNA extraction was performed using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) in this study. For the V3-V4 variable region of the 16 S rRNA gene, PCR amplification was performed using specific primers 515 F (5′-AYTGGYDTAAAGNG-3′) and 806R (5′-TACNVGGTATCTAATCC-3′). After high-throughput sequencing of the amplified products by Illumina HiSeq 2000 sequencing platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), the high-quality sequences were processed using the DADA2 noise reduction process in QIIME2 2019.4 and integrated with Vsearch (v2.13.4) and cutadapt (v2.3) software to cluster sequences into operational classification units (OTUs) with a 97% similarity threshold. Subsequently, the diversity level of each sample was assessed based on the OTU abundance distribution. Species taxonomic annotation was performed using the Greengenes database (version 13.8, accessed at http://greengenes.secondgenome.com/). Sequencing was performed by Personalbio Technology Ltd. in Shanghai, China. Data analysis was performed free of charge through Personalbio’s GenesCloud online platform (https://www.genescloud.cn/) [23]. In addition, Spearman correlation analysis was completed using the OmicStudio analysis tool (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Untargeted metabolomic analyses

As previously described [18, 22, 24], samples of mouse (Control, POF, and NCG groups) feces were collected and detected to the metabolites using LC-MS system (Personalbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Vanquish UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), while mass spectrometry was conducted on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ESI ion source. Data were acquired in Full MS-ddMS2 mode, combining MS1 and data-dependent MS/MS scans. Raw data were processed through XploreMET software (v2.0, Metabo-Profile, Shanghai, China) for automated preprocessing, including baseline correction, spectral smoothing, feature extraction, and peak alignment. Multivariate statistical analyses—PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA—were employed to assess intergroup metabolic disparities. Differentially expressed metabolites were identified based on VIP > 1 and p < 0.05. Functional and pathway analyses of these metabolites were subsequently performed using MetaboAnalyst 4.0.

RNA isolation and RNA Sequencing analyses

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used to extracted total RNA from ovarian tissues of the Control, POF, and Trp groups. The RNA samples were then subjected to 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology), adhering to the standard sequencing protocol. For transcriptional-level gene expression analysis, the R packages DESeq2 and edgeR were employed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with a log2(fold change) ≥ 1.1 and a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Quantitative real-time-PCR

Reverse transcription of mRNA was performed using 1 µg total RNA with the All-in-One 5× RT MasterMix (ABM Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cDNA products were diluted tenfold with DEPC-treated water and either used immediately for qPCR analysis or stored at -20 °C until further use. SYBR green-based qPCR assays were performed with an Agilent Mx3005p (Agilent, CA, USA) thermocyclear (20 µL assay volume) in a fast two-step reaction process. Gene expression quantification was performed using the comparative 2^(−ΔΔCt) method with normalization to the GAPDH. All primers were validated and synthesized by Changchun Kumei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changchun, China) (listed in Supplementary Table S1).

Western blot (WB)

Ovarian tissue lysates were prepared in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime) containing protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Solarbio). After homogenization, lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. To remove cellular debris, protein concentration was determined using the BCA (Beyotime). The same amount of protein (12 µg) was used for subsequent experiments. 12% SDS-PAGE was used to separate the total proteins and transfer them to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibodies: rabbit Cleaved caspase 3 (MCE, HY-P80623, 1:800), rabbit BAX (Bioworld, BS61098, 1:800), rabbit BCL2 (Bioworld, BS1511, 1:800), rabbit ERK1/2 (Bioworld, BS90472, 1:1,000), rabbit p-ERK1/2 (Bioworld, BS4621P, 1:1,000), rabbit p38 (Bioworld, BS4635, 1:1,000), rabbit p-p38 (Bioworld, BS4766, 1:1,000), rabbit PI3K (MCE, HY-P80867, 1:1,000); rabbit p-PI3K (MCE, HY-P80846, 1:1,000), rabbit AKT (Bioworld, MB3288 1:1,000); rabbit p-AKT (Bioworld, BS4007 1:1,000); rabbit IL-6 (Abclonal, A0286, 1:800), rabbit IL-1β (MCE, HY-P80720, 1:1,000), rabbit Occludin (Abclonal, A2601, 1:1,000); rabbit ZO-1 (Bioworld, BS71522, 1:1,000); rabbit GPX4 (MCE, HY-P80450, 1:1,000); mouse GAPDH (Bioworld, MB001, 1:1,000); rabbit SOD2 (Bioworld, BS6734, 1:1,000). After three 5min TBST washes, membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bioworld Technology, Inc., MN, USA; anti-rabbit BS13278 and anti-mouse BS12478, 1:10,000 dilution) for 1 h at 25 °C. Following additional TBST washes (3 × 5 min), chemiluminescent signals were developed using BeyoECL star kit (Beyotime) and images were obtained with a Tanon-5200 Automated Imaging System (Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (v1.52a; National Institutes of Health, MD, USA) with GAPDH serving as the loading control.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysis with SPSS 26.0 software (IBM crop, Armonk, NY, USA). Data are expressed as the mean ± SD of three independent experiments. The data were displayed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad). Statistical differences between groups were determined using one-way or two-way ANOVA. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for faecal microbiota LEfSe analysis between two groups. The n-value details can also be found in figure legends. p < 0.05, “*” was considered significant; p < 0.01, “**” as very significant; and p < 0.001, “***” as highly significant. Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

Results

NCG treatment alleviated POF symptoms in mice

The Cy-induced POF model was employed to assess the therapeutic potential of NCG (Fig. 1A). The estrous cycle of POF mice exhibited marked disruption, characterized by a shortened estrus phase and prolonged diestrus phase, accompanied by a significant reduction in body weight at day 28. Notably, NCG treatment restored normal cyclicity and reversed the weight loss observed in the POF group (Fig. 1B-C). No significant difference was observed in the ovarian weight of mice among the Control, POF, and NCG groups (Fig. 1D). H&E and IHC staining of ovarian histology demonstrated significant irregularity in ovarian structure, with a significant reduction in the number of primary follicles, secondary follicles, antral follicles, and corpora lutea, an increase in atretic follicles, and the positive expression for Cleaved caspase 3 and IL-1β, and the positive expression was reduction for superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), an antioxidant enzyme, in the POF group (Fig. 1E-F). The levels of FSH and LH were significantly increased, and the level of E2 was significantly reduced in the POF group compared to the NCG group (Fig. 1G-I), indicating amelioration of endocrine dysfunction. Additionally, increased accumulation of ROS is one of the most crucial causes of decreased ovarian reserve, and abnormal accumulation of ROS are attributed to antioxidant system imbalance [25], we quantified ovarian ROS levels. The POF group showed significantly higher ROS accumulation compared to the Control and NCG groups (Fig. 1J). Next, we measured antioxidant enzymes including MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px, a peroxidation product in ovaries. The results indicated that the SOD and GSH-Px levels were significantly decreased, and ovarian MDA levels significantly increased in the POF group compared to the NCG group (Fig. 1K–M). To further explore the molecular mechanism underlying NCG-alleviated POF, expression of apoptosis and antioxidant-related genes and protein in mouse ovaries was detected (Fig. 1N-O). The results of qPCR and WB demonstrated caspase 3, BCL2-associated X protein (BAX), and inflammatory factors (IL-6 and IL-1β) gene and Cleaved caspase 3 protein expression were significantly increased, and SOD2, heme oxygenase-1(HO-1) protein expression were significantly decreased in the POF group compared to the NCG group. Notably, NCG treatment showed a trend toward restoring fertility in POF mice. (Fig. 1P).

Collectively, these results demonstrated that NCG treatment effectively mitigates estrous cycle irregularities, ovarian damage, abnormal changes of hormones, inflammatory responses, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and fertility impairment in POF mice.

NCG reshaped the intestinal microecology and increased the abundance of Muribaculum intestinale in POF mice

Considering the significant contribution of gut dysbiosis to the development of ovarian aging, the effects of NCG on the diversity and composition of the gut microbiota were also evaluated in POF mice using 16 S rRNA sequencing. Alpha diversity analysis revealed no significant intergroup differences in species richness (Fig. 2A). However, the beta diversity assessed by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) demonstrated distinct clustering patterns among groups (Fig. 2B), indicating structural microbiota alterations. To further assess the impact of NCG on gut microbiota composition, community abundance analysis was performed across different taxonomic levels. At the phylum level, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes of the total relative bacteria abundance dominated in all groups. At the family level, the relative abundance of Muribaculaceae decreased in the POF group compared to the Control group, while the abundance of Muribaculum intestinale (M. intestinale) increased in the NCG group. At the genus level, NCG treatment reversed POF-induced decrease of M. intestinale (Fig. 2C-E). Although there was no significant difference in Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes among the three groups, NCG treatment trend attenuated the reduction in Bacteroidetes abundance, and decreasing the Firmicutes abundance observed in the POF group. Notably, the Bacteroidetes/Firmicutes ratio was higher in the NCG group than in the POF group (Fig. 2F). LEfSe (LDA > 3) analysis indicated that NCG treatment strikingly augmented the abundance of M. intestinale in mice (Fig. 2G-H). Remarkably, Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between M. intestinale and E2 levels, as well as a significant negative correlation between M. intestinale and FSH and LH levels (Fig. 2I) following NCG treatment, suggesting that M. intestinal plays a crucial role in the protective effect of NCG against POF. The gut microbiota can influence host metabolism by interacting with host signaling pathways. Thus, in the present study, KEGG-based functions were predicted for each group of OUT with PICRUSt2 software. As shown in Fig. 2J, NCG intervention may rehabilitate the metabolism of mice with POF. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that NCG reprogrammed the intestinal microecology in POF mice.

NCG-treated fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) alleviated POF symptoms

Further FMT experiments were performed to verify that the alleviation of POF in mice was due to changes in the structure of the gut microbiota induced by NCG treatment (Fig. 3A). As hypothesized, FMT from the NCG and Control groups successfully reversed the symptoms of POF (Fig. 3B-D). The number of primary follicles, secondary follicles, and corpora lutea was significantly decreased, and the number of atretic follicles and the positive expression for Cleaved caspase 3 and IL-1β was significantly increased in the POF group compared to the FMTNCG groups (Fig. 3E-F). Additionally, the positive expression for SOD2 was decreased in the POF group. The levels of FSH and LH were significantly reduced, and the level of E2 was significantly decreased in the POF group when compared to in the FMTNCG and FMTCON group (Fig. 3G-I). The levels of ROS, MDA, SOD and GSH-Px in the ovaries were measured. The results indicated that the SOD and GSH-Px levels were significantly increased, and ovarian ROS and MDA levels significantly decrease in the FMTNCG group when compared to the POF group (Fig. 3J–M). And changes in the expression levels of antioxidant-related protein were also supported this view (Fig. 3N). The results of WB demonstrated that Cleaved caspase 3 protein expression was significantly decreased, and HO-1 protein expression was significantly increased in the FMTNCG group compared to the POF group. Subsequently, POF mice exhibited a markedly reduced fertility in comparison to the Control and FMTNCG groups (Fig. 3O). Importantly, FMTNCG treatment superior effects on POF than FMTCON treatment.

Collectively, the ability of NCG to reprogram the gut microecology and alleviate POF in a gut microbiota-dependent manner was demonstrated.

NCG reduced the intestinal barrier damage of POF mice

The intestinal mucosal barrier plays a key defensive role in protecting the host from various ingested toxins and microorganisms. Thus, the changes in intestinal barrier function in POF mice was examined. As shown in the Fig. 4A, histological examination of the distal colon specimens showed that NCG repaired mucosal injury (inflammatory cell infiltration decreased), restored crypt structure (goblet cells increased), and decreased the infiltration of inflammatory cells. Furthermore, the results of WB demonstrated IL-6 and IL-1β proteins expression were significantly decreased, and tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin) and B cell leukaemia/lymphoma 2 (BCL2) proteins expression were significantly increased in the NCG group compared to the POF group. Collectively, these results further proved that POF can cause intestinal gut disorder, and NCG can reverse this phenomenon and reduce the intestinal barrier damage of POF.

Tryptophanol mediated the benefit effect of NCG-enriched M. intestinale in mitigating POF and intestinal mucosal impairment

As shown above, NCG treatment increased the abundance of M. intestinale, therefore, whether M. intestinale plays a role in alleviating POF was further explored (Fig. 5A). M. intestinale treatment successfully reversed the symptoms of POF (Fig. 5B-D). The number of primordial follicles, primary follicles and corpora lutea was significantly increase, and the positive expression for Cleaved caspase 3 and IL-1β was decreased in the M. intestinale group compared to the POF group. Additionally, the number of atretic follicles was significantly decreased and the positive expression for SOD2 was increased in the M. intestinale group (Fig. 5E-F). The levels of FSH and LH were significantly reduced, and the level of E2 was significantly increased in the M. intestinale group when compared to the POF group (Fig. 5G-I). Additionally, the levels of SOD and GSH-Px in the ovaries significantly increased, and ovarian ROS and MDA levels significantly decrease in the M. intestinale group (Fig. 5J–M). The results of WB demonstrated Cleaved caspase 3 and BAX proteins expression were significantly decreased in the M. intestinale and Pasteurized M. intestinale group compared to the POF group (Fig. 5N). Subsequently, POF mice exhibited a markedly reduced fertility in comparison to the Control and M. intestinale groups (Fig. 5O). Combined with the current results, Pasteurized M. intestinale also have a certain mitigation effect on POF, but live M. intestinale have a better effect on POF treatment. These results suggest that NCG mitigates POF by increasing the abundance of M. intestinale.

Numerous studies indicated that metabolites produced by gut microbiota mediate host-microbiota interactions and modulate host physiology and metabolism. To investigate the association between M. intestinale-derived metabolites and host metabolism, we analyzed the fecal metabolites of different groups using non-targeted metabolomics. Firstly, positive-and negative-mode orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) demonstrated partial separation among the three groups (Fig. 6A). The results of positive- and negative-mode principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that NCG-treated group was positioned between the Control and POF groups, with its profile trending toward that of the Control group (Fig. 6B). The differential substance histograms demonstrated that 134 metabolites were upregulated and 133 metabolites were downregulated in the POF vs. Control. Conversely, 26 metabolites were upregulated and 49 metabolites were downregulated in the POF vs. NCG (Fig. 6C). KEGG analysis showed that fecal metabolites were mainly involved in the Arginine and proline metabolism, Tryptophan metabolism and Phenylalanine, Tyrosine and Tryptophan biosynthesis (Fig. 6D). Subsequently, we particularly focused on Tryptophan metabolites, because many studies indicated that some indole derivatives produced from Tryptophan metabolism by intestinal microorganisms promote metabolic benefits. Interestingly, the level of Tryptophanol was significantly decreased in the POF group (p < 0.001), but NCG treatment led to an upward trend in Tryptophanol levels (Fig. 6E). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the differential correlation between the differential microbiota in genus level of the three groups and 50 fecal pmetabolites. The results demonstrated a positive trend (p < 0.05) of correlation between M. intestinale and Tryptophanol level (Fig. 6F). These results indicated the involvement of M. intestinale in NCG-mediated Tryptophanol enrichment. Notably, there was a significant positive correlation between the levels of Tryptophanol and E2, a negative correlation between the levels of Tryptophanol and FSH and LH (Fig. 6G). Overall, these finding suggest that NCG enriched Tryptophanol in the gut through the increased abundance of M. intestinale, which could be the basis of its protective effect POF.

Since Tryptophanol is a key active molecule produced by gut microbiota through the indole pathway of Tryptophan metabolism [26], to investigate the effects of NCG on the viability of M. intestinale and its Tryptophan-metabolizing capacity, different concentrations of NCG (1, 5 10 µM) were co-cultured with M. intestinale for 24 h. The results showed that NCG treatment increased the bacterial load of M. intestinal in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6H), while Tryptophan levels in the culture supernatant also significantly increased with increasing NCG concentrations (Fig. 6I). Furthermore, Tryptophan levels in serum and ovarian tissues were measured by ELISA. The results showed that NCG treatment, FMT treatment,, and M. intestinale administration all significantly increased Tryptophan levels in serum (Fig. 6J-L) and ovarian tissues (Fig. 6M-O). Collectively, these results indicate that NCG promotes the growth and proliferation of M. intestinale, enhances its Tryptophan-metabolizing capacity, increases Tryptophan levels in both circulation and target organs, and thereby facilitates the production of its metabolite Tryptophanol.

Tryptophanol alleviated POF by inhibiting PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways in vivo and vitro

Tryptophanol, as a key of Tryptophan metabolites. Considering the may important role of Tryptophanol in regulating POF, we evaluated the effect of Tryptophanol treatment in mice. (Fig. 7A). Tryptophanol treatment successfully reversed the symptoms of POF (Fig. 7B-D). The number of primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles and corpora lutea was significantly increased, and the positive expression for Cleaved caspase 3 and IL-1β was decreased in the Trp group compared to the POF group. Additionally, the number of atretic follicles was significantly decreased and the positive expression for SOD2 was increased in the Trp group (Fig. 7E-F). The levels of FSH and LH were significantly reduced, and the level of E2 was significantly increase in the Trp group when compared to the POF group (Fig. 7G-I). Compared with the POF group, the levels of SOD and GSH-Px in the ovaries significantly increased in the Trp group, and ovarian ROS and MDA levels significantly decreased in the Trp group (Fig. 7J–M). To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the Tryptophanol reversed POF symptom, the entire transcriptional profile of the ovaries was examined and compared to that in Control POF and Tryptophanol mice via RNA sequencing analysis (Fig. S1). In comparison to the NCG group, the POF group revealed 1093 down-regulated genes and 2025 up-regulated genes with statistical significance (log2 (fold change) ≥ 1.1 or p < 0.05, Fig. S1A-B). The functions of overlapping DEG sets were individually explored by employing annotated Gene Ontology (GO) terminology and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). GO enrichment analysis revealed that the apoptotic process was enriched in three groups (Fig. S1C). Moreover, KEGG enrichment analysis and ring heatmap revealed that the primary pathways affected were those involved in PI3K/AKT, and MAPK (Fig. S1D-E). The above results indicated that the Tryptophanol had the function of regulating mouse GCs apoptosis and normal ovarian development through the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathway. Based on the RNA sequencing results, we further measured changes in the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling corresponding proteins. The expression of Cleaved caspase 3, BAX, p-p38 and p-AKT protein were significantly decreased, and BCL2 and SOD2 protein expression were significantly increased in the Trp group compared to the POF group (Fig. 7N). Subsequently, POF mice exhibited a markedly reduced fertility in comparison to the Control and Trp groups (Fig. 7O).

To investigate the protective mechanism of Tryptophanol on granulosa cells, we first established an in vitro model of Cy-induced mouse granulosa cell (GCs) injury (Fig. 8A). CCK8 assay results showed that 300 µM Cy significantly reduced cell viability, while 100 nM Tryptophanol significantly promoted cell viability. Furthermore, co-treatment with Tryptophanol and Cy markedly restored the decrease in cell viability induced by Cy (Fig. 8B–D). DCFH-DA fluorescence staining revealed that Tryptophanol treatment significantly decreased intracellular ROS levels in mouse GCs, (Fig. 8E-F). Meanwhile, MDA levels and the apoptosis rate were decreased, while SOD and GSH-Px activities were increased (Fig. 8G-J). In addition, the E2 level was significantly restored (Fig. 8K). Further Western blot analysis showed that Cy treatment upregulated the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins BAX and Cleaved caspase 3, and downregulated the expression of anti-apoptotic protein BCL2; these changes were reversed by Tryptophanol treatment (Fig. 8L). In addition, Cy treatment promoted the phosphorylation of ERK, p38, PI3K and AKT; Tryptophanol treatment significantly restored the phosphorylation levels of ERK, PI3K, and AKT (Fig. 8L). Collectively, these results suggest that Tryptophanol alleviated Cy-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis in mouse GCs by regulating the MAPK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways.

Discussion

The gut microbiota plays a vital role in host health and its composition can be modulated by nutrition, which has been linked to aspects of host metabolic diseases [27]. Imbalances in the gut microbiota composition can lead to pregnancy complications, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and cancer [28]. With the in-depth study of the mechanism of microbial-host interaction, the current research has shifted from traditional correlation analysis to accurate analysis of the function of specific bacterial species or strains, and to exploration of the mechanism [29, 30]. In view of the clinical treatment dilemma of POF, this study innovatively revealed the molecular mechanism of dietary NCG intervention in improving POF. In this study, we observed that POF induced disruption of intestinal barrier integrity and dysbiosis, in which decreased relative abundance of M. intestinale was significantly associated with imbalance of Tryptophan metabolic network. Mechanistic studies showed that NCG intervention specifically enriched M. intestinale in gut, promoted Tryptophan metabolite production, inhibited ovarian oxidative stress injury by suppressing the activation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways, thus effectively alleviating the pathological process of POF and promoting the synthesis of E2 in mouse GCs. This study constitutes the first comprehensive delineation of the gut microbiota- Tryptophan metabolism-ovarian axis in POF pathogenesis, providing mechanistic evidence for microbiota-targeted dietary interventions.

Previous studies based on animal models have confirmed that NCG has the potential to improve reproductive performance [31, 32]. Extending these findings, our investigation systematically elucidates the efficacy of NCG in mitigating POF. Notably, NCG administration significantly reversed the POF-induced estrous cycle disorder, ovarian damage, hormone imbalance and fertility defects in mice. A novel mechanistic insight emerged from our observations: ovarian tissues in POF mice were in a state of high inflammation and oxidative stress, and NCG significantly alleviated ovarian inflammation and oxidative stress, which was reported for the first time. Oxidative stress is an important cause of decreased ovarian reserve, leading to POF [33]. In various models, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of NCG have been widely reported to be beneficial to the growth of gut microbiota [34], which indirectly shows that NCG is beneficial to the growth of host probiotics.

In order to better describe the mechanism of NCG alleviating POF, this study innovatively explored the mechanism from the perspective of microbial-host interaction. 16 S rRNA sequencing analysis showed that the POF group showed changes in the structure of the gut microbiota, such as the imbalance of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B), which could be significantly restored after NCG treatment. Notably, NCG treatment significantly increased the abundance of M. intestinale. In order to verify the causal relationship between the change of gut microbiota and the therapeutic effect, the FMT technology was used to colonize the gut of NCG intervention group (FMTNCG) and control group (FMTCON) in POF mice. The results showed that the composition of FMTNCG successfully reproduced the therapeutic effects of NCG direct intervention, including reversing the estrous cycle disorder, improving the ovarian function indicators, alleviating oxidative stress and restoring fertility, confirming that the gut microbiota plays a key mediating role in NCG-mediated POF treatment.

The vicious cycle of the destruction of intestinal barrier function integrity and the ecological imbalance of gut microbiota may be the key cause of ovarian dysfunction [35]. This study found that POF model mice showed significant symptoms of intestinal barrier damage, manifested as significantly increased expression of Cleaved caspase3, IL-6 and IL-1β proteins, while NCG treatment showed the effect of maintaining intestinal barrier homeostasis, which further highlighted the potential ability of NCG to rebuild intestinal mucosal barrier to alleviate POF through gut microbiota. Furthermore, combined with the significant positive correlation between the change of M. intestinale abundance and the levels of E2 in serum (p < 0.05), according to the existing results, we speculate that M. intestinale may be the key beneficial bacteria mediating the alleviation of POF by NCG.

M. intestinale have been shown to be a beneficial bacterium. Treatment with M. intestinale and its derivative 3-HA reduced pulmonary fibrosis in mice [19]. To further verify the functional specificity of M. intestinale, this experiment adopted the strain colonization experiment. We found that M. intestinale treatment alleviated the estrous cycle disorder, ovarian damage, abnormal hormone changes, oxidative stress and fertility defects caused by POF, which provided a theoretical basis for M. intestinale as an important beneficial bacterium to alleviate POF. Interestingly, in this study, we observed that the live M. intestinale group and the pasteurized M. intestinale group exhibited inconsistent therapeutic effects across different parameters, a phenomenon worthy of further exploration. First, improvements in oxidative stress markers and Western blot results were observed in both the M. intestinale group and the pasteurized M. intestinal group, suggesting that certain heat-stable microbial metabolites or components may directly regulate oxidative stress-related signaling pathways independent of live bacterial viability and colonization. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that pasteurized bacteria retain partial immunomodulatory and [36–40]. Therefore, the reduction in oxidative stress levels under short-term intervention may be primarily attributed to the direct effects of bacterial components. Second, in contrast to the rapid response of oxidative stress markers, the estrous cycle and follicle numbers showed no significant improvement in the pasteurized M. intestinale group. This discrepancy suggests that restoration of ovarian function may require more complex mechanisms, such as sustained colonization of live M. intestinale, production of labile metabolites, or long-term regulation of the gut-ovary axis. In comparison, live M. intestinale may exert more comprehensive and sustained therapeutic effects through active colonization and dynamic metabolic activity, whereas pasteurized preparations, lacking the dynamic biological activity of live bacteria, are less effective in restoring reproductive function.

In addition, inactivated bacteria may influence the overall structure of the gut microbiota through physical or chemical means, indirectly improving the host metabolic environment. Studies have shown that pasteurized Akkermansia muciniphila (A. muciniphila) alleviates irritable bowel syndrome-like symptoms and associated behavioral disorders in mice. Pasteurization enhances the ability of A. muciniphila to reduce adiposity, improve insulin resistance, and ameliorate dyslipidemia in mice, with these improvements being significantly associated with modulation of the host urinary metabolomic profile and regulation of intestinal energy absorption [41]. Research has demonstrated that Amuc_1100, a specific protein isolated from the outer membrane of A. muciniphila, interacts with Toll-like receptor 2, remains stable at pasteurization temperatures, improves intestinal barrier function, and partially recapitulates the beneficial effects of the bacteria [42]. In the present study, the heat-inactivated bacterial components in the pasteurized M. intestinale group may retain partial metabolic activity and exert certain effects; however, the overall therapeutic efficacy was weaker than that observed in the live M. intestinale group, further highlighting the irreplaceable role of live bacteria in restoring complex physiological functions such as estrous cyclicity and follicle development. This phenomenon also reflects differences in the sensitivity of various endpoints to interventions: acute oxidative stress markers respond rapidly to bacterial components, whereas reproductive function reconstruction requires longer-term or multidimensional interventions.

Microbiome-derived metabolites are key signaling molecules in microbial community expansion and host-microorganism interactions. The definition and characterization of different microbiome-derived metabolites can help to better understand host-microbiome crosstalk and identify possible biomarkers or disease characteristics. Based on fecal non-targeted metabolomics, we noted that NCG treatment restored the microbial decomposited metabolites in POF, and the Tryptophan catabolites Tryptophanol was identified as a crucial signaling molecule involved in NCG treatment of POF. Notably, Tryptophanol levels showed significant association with the M. intestinale abundance. In addition, a notable positive correlation was found between Tryptophanol level and the E2 level, and a significant negative correlation with FSH and LH levels. Similar to a previous study, M. intestinale abundance was significantly positively correlated with 3-HA, a Tryptophan metabolite [19]. Tryptophan is the premise of various bioactive compounds and has received extensive attention due to its important impact on health [43]. In summary, our study shows that the role of NCG in alleviating POF may be related to the increased abundance of M. intestinale and the enrichment of M. intestinale -derived Tryptophanol.

Tryptophanol is a tryptophan metabolite [44], as a GCN2 agonist, it can promote cells proliferation[45], but its physiological role, especially in fertility, has not been reported. Therefore, we propose that Tryptophanol may be a key metabolite that mediates NCG to alleviate POF through the gut microbiota-ovarian axis. In the present study, Tryptophanol treatment alleviated POF-induced estrous cycle disorders, ovarian damage, abnormal hormone changes, oxidative stress and fertility defects, but the specific mechanism remains to be elucidated. Furthermore, with ovarian transcriptome sequencing technology, we found that PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways related to oxidative stress were significantly enriched. In vivo and vitro, Tryptophanol treatment significantly inhibited the activation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK, and reduced oxidative stress. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is closely related to the activation, differentiation and growth of primordial follicles in ovary, and MAPK signaling pathway is closely related to cells apoptosis and oxidative stress [46, 47]. To further validate the role of the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways in the protective effect of Tryptophanol against POF, the AKT pathway activator SC79 and the MAPK pathway activator Anisomycin were respectively added to cultured mouse GCs in vitro. The results showed that compared with the Tryptophanol group, the addition of the activators significantly attenuated the protective effect of Tryptophanol on cell viability, increased the apoptosis rate, elevated oxidative stress levels, and reduced estradiol synthesis. Western blot analysis further confirmed that the expression levels of p-AKT, p-ERK and p-p38 were increased in the activator-treated groups, and the inhibitory effect of Tryptophanol on these pathways was partially reversed. These findings indicate that activation of either the PI3K/AKT or MAPK pathway antagonizes the protective effect of Tryptophanol on mouse GCs, further demonstrating the critical mediating role of these two pathways in the ameliorative effect of Tryptophanol on POF. Consistent with in vivo results, Tryptophanol reduced ROS accumulation and promoted E2 synthesis through activation of PI3K/AKT, and MAPK in mouse GCs. The above results provide an innovative strategy for the clinical use of targeted activation of Tryptophanol in the treatment of POF (Fig. 9).

Although this model is widely used to simulate chemotherapy-associated ovarian injury, it has certain inherent limitations. First, the Cy-induced POF model primarily recapitulates the pathological process of acute ovarian injury following chemotherapy, characterized by rapid induction of follicular apoptosis and depletion of ovarian reserve. However, this model may not fully reflect the pathological mechanisms underlying chronic or age-related POF, which often involve more complex processes such as immune dysregulation, metabolic disturbances, and long-term microenvironmental changes. Therefore, the protective effects of Tryptophanol observed in this study mainly reflect its intervention in acute ovarian injury, and its applicability to chronic POF remains to be further validated. Second, as a broad-spectrum cytotoxic agent, Cy exerts effects not limited to ovarian tissue but may also impact other organs, including the intestine and liver. In this study, we focused on the effects of NCG on both intestinal and ovarian function; however, Cy may indirectly affect the ovaries through systemic metabolic or immune modulation. Accordingly, when interpreting ovarian-specific outcomes, it is important to consider the potential influence of systemic factors. Furthermore, this model does not capture the full heterogeneity of clinical POF. The etiology of POF is diverse, encompassing autoimmune factors, genetic defects, chemotherapy- or radiotherapy-induced damage, and idiopathic causes, each of which may correspond to distinct pathological mechanisms and therapeutic targets. A single Cy-induced model is therefore insufficient to fully represent all subtypes of POF.

To address these limitations, future studies should incorporate complementary models, such as autoimmune POF models, natural aging models, or genetically modified models, to more comprehensively evaluate the protective effects of Tryptophanol and its potential for clinical translation. In addition, integrating clinical samples and population-based studies will further clarify its translational relevance.

Conclusions

In summary, our finding demonstrated that NCG alleviates Cy-induced POF by restoring the gut microbiota, and the intestinal barrier, and promoting the colonization of beneficial bacteria M. intestinale. The therapeutic effect of NCG is mainly attributed to the inhibition of oxidative stress and GCs apoptosis by the generation of Tryptophanol, which inhibits the activation of PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways. This study provides new insights into the potential of NCG as a dietary supplement candidate for the treatment of POF.
