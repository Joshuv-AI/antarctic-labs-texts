Composite Coating-driven Regulation of In Vivo Magnesium Degradation and Tissue Response
Authors: TONYA D. ANDREEVA, KIM BURKHARDT, OLE JUNG, SEBASTIAN STAMMKÖTTER, SANJA STOJANOVIĆ, STEVO NAJMAN, FRANK WALTHER, RUMEN KRASTEV, MIKE BARBECK
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimMagnesium-based alloys are promising materials for fabrication of bioresorbable medical devices. The application is limited by rapid degradation and associated adverse tissue responses. In this study, nanometer-thin composite polyelectrolyte/wax (PEM/W) coatings were fabricated on magnesium-based implant prototypes to delay their degradation.Materials and MethodsCoatings were applied using a layer-by-layer technique. Cytocompatibility was assessed according to DIN ISO 10993-5 using NIH/3T3 fibroblasts and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Degradation behavior was monitored by high-resolution micro-computed tomography (µ-CT), while tissue compatibility and host responses were evaluated histologically following DIN EN ISO 10993-6.ResultsThe coatings were continuous, hydrophobic (water contact angles exceeding 100°), with sub-micrometer thicknesses and were found to improve in vitro cytocompatibility of magnesium biomaterials. In vivo evaluation using a rat subcutaneous implantation model demonstrated that the effectiveness of magnesium degradation modulation depends on the type of employed PEM. Micro-computed tomography analyses revealed that the hyaluronic acid/chitosan/wax coating provided the most robust protection over 60 days, exhibiting the lowest volume loss and superior preservation of implant geometry compared to the uncoated samples. Correspondingly, hydrogen-related gas cavity formation was reduced and temporally delayed in coated implants, indicating a more controlled degradation process. Histopathological analysis shows a moderate inflammatory response characteristic of biodegradable metallic implants, dominated by macrophages and lymphocytes. Importantly, coated implants were associated with reduced late-stage fibrosis and necrosis compared to uncoated magnesium.ConclusionOverall, composite PEM/W coatings, especially those based on natural polyelectrolytes, represent a promising surface-engineering strategy for improving the safety and performance of resorbable magnesium implants.

Introduction

Magnesium (Mg) and its alloys have emerged as a highly promising class of biomaterials for the fabrication of temporary implants. Their unique biochemical and biomechanical properties - including biodegradability, biocompatibility, and mechanical strength comparable to natural bone - render magnesium-based medical devices particularly attractive for bone regeneration and fixation, cardio stents, and other load-bearing biomedical applications (1-3). Unlike permanent materials (e.g., stainless steel or titanium), Mg alloys are designed to degrade in vivo, potentially reducing long-term implant-related complications and the need for removal surgery. These properties reduce the risk for the patient and burden, healthcare costs, and improve overall clinical outcomes (4, 5).

The clinical acceptance of magnesium implants has progressed significantly in recent decades. Early clinical experiences with Mg were reported over a century ago; however, limitations due to rapid and uncontrolled corrosion, restricted widespread clinical application (1, 6). Recent advances in alloy design, manufacturing techniques (e.g., extrusion or additive manufacturing), and surface engineering have revived interest, particularly in orthopedics, dentistry, and cardiovascular indications (7, 8). Clinical trials in Germany, China, and Korea have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of resorbable Mg implants for bone fracture fixation, with promising results for large-scale clinical translation (9, 10).

Despite the advances, rapid biodegradation and hydrogen gas evolution remain major clinical concerns (11). Uncontrolled Mg breakdown can lead to premature mechanical failure, inflammation. Gas cavity formation at the implantation site, limits the integration and long-term performance of these devices (6, 12). Therefore, innovative strategies to tailor the corrosion rate and enhance Mg biological biofunctionality are of paramount importance for clinical application.

Surface modification and coating technologies have become essential for enabling the safe and effective clinical use of magnesium implants. By controlling properties of the surface, coatings can extend functional lifetime by slowing degradation of Mg devices, regulate cellular responses, and minimizing adverse events (13-15). Various coating materials and deposition methods have been developed, with each offering specific advantages and drawbacks with respect to corrosion resistance, bioactivity, and implant stability.

Ceramic coatings - most notably those formed from calcium phosphate (CaP), hydroxyapatite (HA), tricalcium phosphate (TCP), and bioglass - mimic the mineral composition of bone and promote osseointegration (16, 17). These coatings reduce Mg corrosion, foster bone remodeling, and offer bioactive surfaces for osteoblast attachment and proliferation (18, 19). However, ceramic coatings tend to be brittle, exhibiting low tensile strength and fracture toughness. This may result in delamination, fragmentation, or the release of particulate debris, potentially triggering localized inflammatory reactions (20, 21).

Polymeric coatings, including poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), polycaprolactone (PCL), and natural polymers such as collagen and chitosan, impart greater flexibility and processability to Mg implants (22-24). These coatings can fabricate porous, biocompatible surfaces conducive to tissue integration and controlled drug delivery. Nevertheless, polymeric coatings often have lower mechanical strength compared to ceramics. Fast degradation rates or insufficient adhesion to the Mg substrate may lead to premature exposure of the underlying metal, resulting in accelerated corrosion and loss of implant integrity (25, 26).

Recent research has focused on hybrid and composite coatings that combine ceramic, polymeric, and even metallic phases to balance the degradation of each material type (27, 28). Notable strategies include multi-layered films integrating bioactive ceramics with biopolymers or the incorporation of nanoparticles to enhance corrosion-resistance and regenerative performance. Such systems may offer controlled degradation, improved cell response, and tailored drug release capabilities, but present complexities in manufacturing, reproducibility, and regulatory approval (25, 29).

By controlling degradation, coatings also modulate hydrogen evolution and osseointegration - both crucial for implant tolerance and bone healing (30). Dense coatings, like hydrothermally deposited Cap and MgP, have been shown to reduce hydrogen gas formation and maintain mechanical integrity for longer periods in vivo (31, 32).

Thus, the selection of a coating strategy for magnesium implants requires careful consideration of indication-specific requirements. In this context, polyelectrolyte (PE) multilayer (PEM) coatings represent another surface modification strategy for Mg based implants, using the layer-by-layer assembly of alternating deposition of positively and negatively charged polymers onto the implant surface (33, 34). By precisely controlling the deposition process, PEMs can form nanometer- to micrometer-thick films endowed with tunable physicochemical properties and tailored biofunctionality. PEMs can also be engineered as drug delivery systems, enabling the localized release of antibiotics, growth factors, or anti-inflammatory agents to enhance tissue healing and implant integration (33, 34). Additionally, the use of natural biopolymer layers can boost cell compatibility, promote osteointegration, and reduce biofilm formation - a major concern for implanted medical devices (33, 34). By adjusting the composition, thickness, and functionality of the PEM coating, degradation behavior and biological interactions of the underlying magnesium can be individually tailored for specific clinical requirements (33, 34). All these make PEM coatings a versatile and promising solution for controlling and optimizing the performance of implants.

PEMs have already demonstrated potential for enhancing corrosion protection and improving the biocompatibility of Mg alloy substrates; however, the reported outcomes remain inconsistent (35-37). A significant improvement in the short-term corrosion resistance and biocompatibility of bioresorbable coronary Mg stents was achieved using a layer-by-layer (LbL) assembled composite coating consisting of PEMs [polystyrene sulfonate, PSS, and poly(allylamine hydrochloride), PAH] combined with nanometer-thin wax layers (abbreviated as PSS/PAH/W) (38). This functional hydrophobic coating, which provides fluid-repellent properties, effectively delayed in vitro degradation and enhanced biocompatibility, addressing key challenges associated with Mg-based stents (38). Notably, the coating reduced the degradation rate by a factor of five compared to uncoated Mg stents. Our recent in vitro study demonstrated that applying a PSS/PAH coating to implant materials significantly reduced the inflammatory response (39). Building on these promising results, our subsequent research focused on advancing composite coating performance to further improve the safety of Mg-based implants. As an initial step, we explored the use of some synthetic PE [poly(acrylic acid) (PAA)] as well as PEs derived from natural, highly biocompatible, and non-cytotoxic substances, hyaluronic acid (HA) and chitosan (Chi), as the basis for constructing functional PAA/PAH/W and HA/Chi/W composite coatings (40).

To address concerns regarding the cytocompatibility of the hydrophobic composite PEM/W coatings, the viability of NIH/3T3 mouse fibroblasts and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) was evaluated (40). The in vivo study was designed to evaluate the biocompatibility and degradation behavior of uncoated versus PSS/PAH/W- and HA/Chi/W-coated Mg-based test prototypes using a subcutaneous implantation model in rats following a methodology described by Barbeck and colleagues (41-44). Subcutaneous implantation provides a controlled environment to assess local tissue responses, inflammatory reactions, gas formation, and degradation progression without the additional complexity of load-bearing conditions. Following explantation, micro-computed tomography (µ-CT) was performed to quantitatively assess implant degradation, including changes in implant volume, surface morphology, structural integrity and gas bubble formation. This combined in vivo-µ-CT approach enables a comprehensive assessment of the degradation kinetics and biological performance of the coatings under physiological conditions. Additionally, standardized histological and histopathological methods according to DIN EN ISO 10993-6 were applied for evaluation of the biocompatibility (41, 44-46).

Materials and Methods

Materials. Polyelectrolytes including poly(ethylene imine) (PEI, 750 kDa), polystyrene sulfonate (PSS, 70 kDa), and chitosan (Chi, 50-190 kDa, 75%-85% deacetylated) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Sodium hyaluronate (HA, 360 kDa) was purchased from Lifecore Biomedical, LLC (Chaska, MN, USA), and poly(allylamine hydrochloride) (PAH, 120-200 kDa) was sourced from Alfa Aesar [Thermo Fisher (Kandel) GmbH, Kandel, Germany]. All polyelectrolytes were used as received. PEI was dissolved in ultrapure water at a concentration of 2 mg/ml (pH 7.0). PSS and PAH were dissolved in 0.5 M NaF at 2 mg/ml (pH 7.0), while HA and Chi were dissolved in 0.5 M NaF at 1 mg/ml (pH 5.5). An aqueous wax suspension (25% w/w) was obtained from Keim Additec Surface GmbH (Kirchberg, Germany). The wax particles were paraffin-based and spherical in shape, with a melting range of 56-85 °C (according to the supplier). Dynamic light scattering measurements revealed a mean particle diameter of 65.2 ± 0.5 nm and a zeta potential of −44.8 ± 2.9 mV (38). The wax particles were resuspended in ethanol to a final concentration of 3% w/w to avoid rapid degradation of magnesium-based materials in aqueous environments during the coating process.

Mg-based implant prototypes were fabricated as cylindrical discs (6 mm in diameter, 1.3 mm in thickness) with a central hole. The discs were cut from a Mg-rod made of an alloy comprising at least 96 wt% magnesium, a minimum of 1 wt% manganese, and at least 0.5 wt% of one or more rare earth metals (patent DE 10 2009 025 511 A1, 2010.12.23, QualiMed Innovative Medizinprodukte GmbH, Winsen, Germany). Mn is commonly added (~0.3-1 wt%) to Mg-alloys to improve grain refinement, leading to improved mechanical strength. Moreover, Mn reduces corrosion by removing harmful iron impurities, which are highly cathodic and accelerate galvanic corrosion (47). Mn itself forms relatively stable intermetallic phases that are less reactive in physiological conditions. On the other side, the rare earth elements (REE) significantly slow corrosion rates and enhance passive film stability [Mg(OH)₂/REE oxide films] in physiological environments. REEs form fine, stable intermetallic phases (e.g., Mg₁₂REE) that refine the microstructure, reduce micro-galvanic coupling, and enhance uniform corrosion instead of localized pitting (48).

Silicon (Si) (100) wafers (10 × 10 mm, CrysTec GmbH, Berlin, Germany) were used as substrates for specific physicochemical laboratory characterizations of the coatings.

Preparation of composite PEM/W coatings. Prior to the coating process, all substrates were cleaned by ultrasonication - first in acetone for 1 min, followed by isopropanol for 2 minutes - and then dried under a stream of nitrogen. The magnesium discs were subsequently passivated according to the protocol described by Ostrowski et al. (37), in order to prevent early Mg degradation during the coating process.

PEM/W coatings were fabricated using the layer-by-layer (LbL) technique via manual dipping, following the protocols described by Glinel et al. (49) and Andreeva et al. (38). Two types of composite coatings were applied and tested, differing in the type of polyelectrolytes used – ((PSS/PAH)₇/Wax)₅, abbreviated as PSS/PAH/W, and ((HA/Chi)₇/Wax)₅, abbreviated as HA/Chi/W. Each coating consisted of five repeating units (building blocks), with each unit composed of a hydrophilic PEM and a hydrophobic, water-repellent wax barrier layer. The deposition of the (PSS/PAH)₇ or (HA/Chi)₇ multilayers was followed with deposition of wax particles from a suspension to build one PEM/W block. The samples were then annealed at 90°C in a laboratory oven (Heratherm, Thermo Scientific), allowing the wax particles to melt and form a uniform, continuous wax layer. The procedure was repeated until the required number of PEM/W blocks was attained. Prior to multilayer construction, substrates were primed with a layer of PEI to facilitate adsorption of the subsequent polyelectrolyte layers (50).

Identical coatings intended for in vitro and in vivo testing on Mg-based discs were also constructed on Si wafers to facilitate physicochemical characterization. All coatings were prepared under sterile conditions using sterile-filtered PE solutions (bottle-top filters with SFCA membrane, 0.45 μm pore size, Carl Roth GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany).

Physicochemical characterization of PEM/W coatings. The physicochemical properties of PEM/W coatings were systematically characterized using Si wafers coated under the same conditions as the Mg-based implant prototypes. The thickness of the PEM/W coatings were determined using a spectroscopic ellipsometer (SE800, Sentech Instruments GmbH, Berlin, Germany) at an incidence angle of 70°. Hydrophilicity was assessed by measuring the static water contact angle using a contact angle goniometer (DataPhysics Instruments GmbH, Stuttgart, Germany) with video capture system applying the Young-Laplace fitting for profile analysis. Surface topography was examined by scanning electron microscopy (Zeiss DSM 962, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany). Images were captured at an average working distance of 9 mm and an accelerating voltage of 5 kV, following sputter-coating with a 2 nm thin gold layer to enhance conductivity. All measurements were performed in triplicate at five different locations on each sample. Results are reported as mean values and standard deviation.

In-vitro cytotoxicity study of PEM/W coatings. The cytotoxicity tests were performed in accordance with DIN ISO 10993-5 as described by Andreeva et al. (38). All experiments were performed in triplicate under sterile, aseptic conditions. Appropriate positive (cytotoxic latex) and negative (non-cytotoxic polypropylene, PP) controls were included as required by the standard. Extracts of potentially cytotoxic substances were prepared from bare Mg-based implant prototypes, passivated Mg-based implant prototypes and Mg-based implant prototypes coated with PEM/W films, and the control materials. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS) served as the extraction medium (ISO 10993-12). The volume of extraction medium was adjusted to the mass and volume of each sample. Test bodies (latex, PP, Mg-based implant prototypes) were placed in small reaction vessels, covered with 500 µl DMEM, and incubated at 37°C for 24 h under agitation. The resulting extracts were used immediately for downstream biological analyses.

Liquid extracts were evaluated using a NIH/3T3 cells (CLS GmbH, Eppelheim, Germany) cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FCS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Sub-confluent cell monolayers were prepared by seeding cells in culture plates for 24 h. After discarding the spent medium, the extracts were applied directly onto the cells. As recommended, serial dilutions of each extract with fresh medium were also prepared and tested. After 24 h of incubation, cell viability was quantified using the resazurin reduction assay.

In vivo study of PEM/W coatings. This study was performed in collaboration with the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Niš, Serbia, and was approved by the Ethical committee for the protection of the welfare of experimental animals at the Faculty of Medicine, University of Niš, Serbia and the Veterinary Directorate of the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Water Management of the Republic of Serbia (approval number 323-07-01762/2019-05/9 from 01 March 2019).

According to DIN EN ISO 10993-6 standard, 72 samples were implanted subcutaneously in the rostral subscapular region of Wistar rats. A total of 72 female Wistar rats, aged 12 to 14 weeks and weighing an average of 250±20 g, were used in this study. Eight animals per Mg-based implant type and time point were euthanized at each predetermined time point (10-, 30- and 60-days post-implantation). The implanted Mg-based implants, along with adjacent tissues, were excised, fixed in 4% formalin, and further processed for µ-CT and histopathological analyses.

µ-CT analysis. The degradation behavior of the Mg-based test samples over time was accessed using high-resolution µ-CT scans using a dual-tube computed tomography system Procon ALPHA Duo (Procon X-Ray GmbH, Sarstedt, Germany) equipped with in-situ loading unit. The system was operated at a tube voltage of 68 kV and a current of 150 µA. Scans were acquired with a voxel resolution of 30 µm and an exposure time of 200 ms per frame.

The acquired raw image sequences were reconstructed into 3D objects using ImageJ software (version 1.54c, National Institutes of Health, USA). Full stacks were created and rendered into 3D using the software’s built-in tools. Calibration of the 3D stacks was achieved by referencing the known initial diameter of the Mg-discs. For quantitative volume analysis, the 3D stacks were processed using thresholding to isolate the Mg-disc from the background. The “3D Volume Calculation” plugin in ImageJ was applied to calculate the volume of the rendered grayscale 3D stacks. Threshold levels were set consistently across all samples to ensure comparability.

Histological workup and histopathological analysis. Histological analyses were conducted following previously established protocols by Barbeck et al. (40-43). In brief, tissue samples were dehydrated using a graded alcohol series, cleared with xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 3-5 µm slices. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To evaluate tissue compatibility and responses, histological analysis focused on material integration behavior within the implant beds and histopathological scoring in accordance with DIN EN ISO 10993-6 guidelines (51). Thus, several parameters were assessed to ensure safety: infiltration of granulocytes, lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages, and multinucleated giant cells, along with tissue parameters such as fibrosis, vascularization, and fatty tissue infiltration. Based on the prevalence of these cell types and tissue responses, samples were graded using the irritation/reactivity system outlined in Annex E of the DIN EN ISO 10993-6, as applied in previous in vivo biocompatibility studies of bone substitute materials (52, 53).

Statistical analyses. Raw scoring data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test to compare two independent groups. µ-CT data were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Least Significant Difference (LSD) post hoc test. Both statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Inter- (*) and intraindividual (·) statistical significance was defined as p≤0.05 (*/·p≤0.05), high significance as p≤0.01 (**/··p≤0.01), and very high significance as p≤0.001 (***/···p≤0.001). Results are presented as mean±standard deviation.

Results

In this study, a stepwise strategy was employed to develop a robust and biocompatible barrier aimed at enhancing the corrosion resistance of Mg-based implants and ensuring their safe application in biomedical settings. Coatings were prepared using two polyelectrolyte couples. Natural negative charged HA and positive charged Chi PEs were used. Synthetic negative charged PSS and positive charged PAH were applied as a second coating system. To further optimize the coating procedure chloride (Cl⁻) anions, commonly used in PEM assembly to enhance film stability but known to be detrimental to Mg substrates, were replaced with fluoride (F⁻) anions, which are significantly less corrosive. This substitution effectively mitigates Mg degradation and contributes to improved implant longevity. In addition, the structural complexity of the coatings was increased by expanding the number of building blocks from two to five, resulting in thicker and more mechanically stable multilayer architectures. The increased number of layers further enhances corrosion resistance and mechanical integrity. Overall, this comprehensive approach represents a significant advancement in the design of protective coatings for Mg-based implants, addressing critical challenges related to both biocompatibility and long-term durability.

Characterization of the composite PEM/W coatings. Both composite coatings, PSS/PAH/W and HA/Chi/W, were nano-thin, with thicknesses of 287±42 nm and 205±21 nm, respectively, as measured on Si-wafer by spectroscopic ellipsometry. The uncoated Mg discs exhibited high hydrophilicity, with an average water contact angle of 20±3°. The two basic PEMs without wax particles - (PSS/PAH)₇ and (HA/Chi)₇ - were also hydrophilic, displaying average contact angles of 55±5°. However, their respective composite coatings incorporating wax demonstrated pronounced hydrophobicity, with contact angles of 115±2° for PSS/PAH/W and 110±2° for HA/Chi/W.

SEM imaging (Figure 1) revealed that the base PEM films were smooth and uniform in appearance. In contrast, the composite PEM/W coatings exhibited increased surface roughness. Notably, the HA/Chi/W coating showed a distinct vermiculate (worm-like) surface pattern, which is characteristic of HA/Chi-based multilayers (54).

In vitro cytotoxicity of PEM/W coatings. According to ISO 10993-5, a material is considered as non-cytotoxic if its undiluted extract reduces cell viability by no more than 30%, providing a benchmark for the biological safety of medical materials. As expected, the positive control (latex) (NC) caused a strong reduction in cell viability, whereas the negative control (PP) showed no cytotoxic effect (Figure 2). Exposure to 100% extracts from uncoated Mg discs resulted in an approximately 95% decrease in cell viability, while passivated Mg discs reduced viability by about 15%. In contrast, discs coated with composite PSS/PAH/W and HA/Chi/W maintained cell viability at levels comparable to the non-cytotoxic NC, demonstrating that both coatings effectively eliminate Mg-induced cytotoxicity and substantially improve biocompatibility.

Early signs of degradation were observed at the implant edges after 10 days, and the uncoated Mg-discs showed surface irregularities indicative of the onset of corrosion. At 30 days, corrosion became more pronounced, particularly around the periphery of the implanted discs. After 60 days, substantial degradation was evident, with a loss of defined shape and noticeable reduction in volume.

Mg-discs coated with PSS/PAH/W show slightly improved protection compared to uncoated discs at 10 days post-implantation, though some edge degradation was still visible. At 30 days, more extensive material loss and surface roughening suggested the onset of coating degradation and a decline in protective performance. By day 60 post-implantation, extensive degradation was evident, demonstrating that the PSS/PAH/W coating failed to provide adequate long-term protection for the Mg implant core. The small radiopaque deposits observed at the implant periphery may represent newly mineralized peri-implant tissue. Similar radiopaque regions have been reported by Kim et al. (55), who demonstrated a correlation between high-density peri-implant areas in µ-CT images and histologically confirmed new bone formation around Mg-based implants.

In contrast, Mg-discs coated with HA/Chi/W remained mostly intact throughout the implantation period. After 10 days, the Mg-discs displayed clear, smooth edges with minimal signs of degradation. Even after 30 days, the samples maintained a relatively uniform shape with only minor degradation visible. At 60 days, the HA/Chi/W-coated discs continued to exhibit the minimal amount of degradation, retaining overall structural integrity and surface definition.

H₂ concentration in rat heart tissues peaked within 15 minutes after implantation demonstrating rapid diffusion from implant to surrounding tissues (56). According to the Henry’s law the solubility of a given gas (here H2) in a given liquid (here blood) is proportional to its partial pressure and depends on the specific Henry´s constant. This constant is very high for H2 gas. As the degradation of Mg and the accompanying hydrogen accumulation advance over time a moment comes in which the partial pressure of H2 becomes higher than those of the other gases dissolved into body fluids - oxygen (O2), nitrogen (N2), and carbon dioxide (CO2). These gases diffuse in the H2 created cavities continuously. H2 diffuses from the gas pocket to the body fluids. Kuhlmann et al. (57) analyzed the composition of the gas accumulated in the gas cavities formed after subcutaneous implantation of fast corroding rare earth element-containing Mg alloy discs into hairless rabbits, using mass spectrometry. The study reported that the subcutaneous gas cavities became clinically visible about 24 h after implantation and increase in size over implantation time. It demonstrates that aside from the relatively low amount of H2, the gas cavities contain CO2, O2, and N2.

In vivo tests: histological and scoring analyses. The histological evaluation of the peri-implant tissue revealed a moderate inflammatory reaction in all three groups over the complete study course (Figure 7). In the connective tissue (CT), the infiltrate consisted predominantly of macrophages, accompanied by lower numbers of lymphocytes and only few granulocytes, while small blood vessels were already present in the peri-implant region from day 10 to day 60 post-implantation. Notably, early fibrotic areas were already visible in the group of the uncoated Mg-discs on day 30 post-implantation (Figure 7B) but not in the groups with the coatings (Figure 7E and H). At 60 days, the reaction in all groups showed a further shift towards connective tissue maturation, with more prominent fibroblasts (Figure 7C, F and I). Fibrosis was most evident in the group of the uncoated Mg-discs with multiple fibrotic foci, while in the groups of the coated Mg-discs only localized fibrotic areas with a minimal fibrotic band were found (Figure 7C, F and I). Also, necrosis was mainly found in the group of the uncoated Mg-discs, while in the groups of the coated Mg-discs minimal numbers of necrotic cells were observed.

At 30 days post-implantation, the scoring profile remained largely comparable between groups, with the inflammatory response still dominated by macrophages and lymphocytes (Figure 8B, D). Polymorphonuclear cells and plasma cells stayed low (Figure 8A, C), and giant cells remained minor across groups (Figure 8E). Neovascularization continued at similar levels (Figure 8G), while fibrosis became more apparent compared with 10 days in all three groups (Figure 8H). Thus, the statistical analysis revealed significant intraindividual increase in fibrosis in all groups (·p<0.05 and ··p<0.01) (Figure 8H). Necrosis remained low at this intermediate time point (Figure 8F).

At 60 days post-implantation, inflammation-related scores again appeared broadly comparable, with persistently high macrophage scores and moderate lymphocyte scores in all groups (Figure 8B and D), alongside low plasma cell and low giant cell values (Figure 8C and E). Neovascularization stayed also comparable among groups (Figure 8G), and fibrosis was present in all groups with highly significantly higher values than at earlier time points, i.e., at 10 days post-implantation (··p<0.01 and ···p<0.001) (Figure 8H). Importantly, necrosis showed a difference at 60 days, with significantly higher necrosis scores in the group of the uncoated Mg-discs compared to the values in the HA/Chi/W coating group (Figure 8F).

Discussion

In this study, two nanometer-thin composite PEM/W coatings based on either natural (HA/Chi) or synthetic (PSS/PAH) polyelectrolytes were successfully fabricated on magnesium-based implant prototypes using a layer-by-layer assembly technology. The resulting coatings were continuous and hydrophobic, with thicknesses in the sub-micrometer range and water contact angles above 100°, confirming the formation of an effective water-repellent barrier. The HA/Chi/W coating exhibited a distinct vermiculate surface pattern characteristic of HA/Chi-based multilayers (54), suggesting a specific interaction between the wax component and the underlying HA/Chi structure that may influence the coating’s surface topography and functional properties.

From a biological perspective, both composite coatings markedly improved cytocompatibility in vitro. Extracts from the coated samples, maintained cell viability at levels comparable to the negative control, in contrast to the strong viability loss observed for uncoated magnesium.

The degradation behavior of Mg-based implant prototypes, with and without applied coatings, was evaluated using an in vivo rat subcutaneous implantation model followed by µ-CT analysis of the explanted biomaterials. At all implantation time points (10, 30, and 60 days), Mg discs coated with HA/Chi/W exhibited significantly greater volume retention than those coated with PSS/PAH/W, indicating superior long-term corrosion resistance. After 60 days of implantation, HA/Chi/W-coated discs retained approximately 94% of their initial volume (corresponding to a 6% volume loss), outperforming both uncoated and PSS/PAH/W-coated Mg specimens, which showed volume losses of approximately 11%. Statistical analysis confirmed significant differences between the two types of coating at all evaluated time points (p<0.05).

µ-CT analysis revealed that hydrogen gas evolution during Mg degradation leads to the formation of gas cavities at the implant site, with their temporal development strongly dependent on the applied surface coating. Uncoated and PSS/PAH/W-coated Mg discs exhibited an early peak in gas pocket volume at 10 days post-implantation, followed by a gradual decline as hydrogen diffused into the surrounding tissue. In contrast, HA/Chi/W-coated implants showed a delayed maximum in gas cavity formation at 30 days, indicating a more controlled and prolonged degradation process. This delayed and reduced gas evolution suggests that the HA/Chi/W coating effectively modulates magnesium corrosion kinetics in vivo, contributing to improved implant stability and a more favorable tissue response. Our results consistently complement the existing body of research. Data on the in vivo degradation behavior of Mg-Zn-Ca alloy demonstrated that nearly 25% of the implant volume was lost in the first 28 days of implantation in rats (58). The volume loss reached 60% after 56 days and 85% after 84 days, confirming the rapid degradation trend most of which occurred in the first 30-60 days. The authors predicted complete in vivo degradation within 15 weeks (58).

The histological and histopathological findings of the present study demonstrate that all Mg-based implant prototypes (Mg-discs) elicited a moderate inflammatory tissue response that is characteristic of biodegradable metallic biomaterials. Across all experimental groups, the early inflammatory phase was dominated by macrophages and lymphocytes, while polymorphonuclear cells, plasma cells, and multinucleated giant cells remained consistently low. This cellular profile is indicative of a physiological foreign body reaction rather than an acute or chronic inflammatory response. Comparable macrophage-driven tissue responses have been reported in previous in vivo studies on magnesium-based implants and are considered a central mechanism governing both material degradation and subsequent tissue remodeling (59, 60).

Interestingly, fibrosis increased progressively over the implantation period in all groups, reflecting normal connective tissue maturation during wound healing and biodegradation. However, marked differences were observed between uncoated and coated magnesium discs. The uncoated Mg-discs exhibited more pronounced fibrotic areas and higher necrosis scores at later time points, which can be attributed to accelerated corrosion, increased hydrogen gas evolution, and local alkalization of the peri-implant environment. These processes are known to disturb local tissue homeostasis and promote secondary tissue damage based on rapid magnesium degradation (61, 62). In contrast, both PEM/W-coatings significantly attenuated these adverse tissue reactions. In particular, the HA/Chi/W coating resulted in the lowest fibrosis and necrosis scores after 60 days, indicating a more controlled degradation behavior and improved tissue compatibility.

Overall, the histopathological scoring confirms that composite PEM/W coatings - especially the HA/Chi/W-coating - effectively modulate the host tissue response by reducing tissue damage while maintaining a physiological foreign body reaction. These findings further emphasize the importance of surface engineering strategies that combine corrosion control with the regulation of biological interactions at the implant-tissue interface (51, 63). Recently Barbeck et al. (64) demonstrated that polymer-coated biomaterials can effectively modulate local biological responses and therapeutic functionality, while Kobayashi et al. (65) highlighted the influence of biomaterial design on tissue integration and regenerative outcomes. In addition, Vera-Becerra et al. (66) underscored the importance of surgical protocols and implant handling procedures for optimizing implant performance and host tissue adaptation, highlighting the need for robust surface engineering strategies that preserve coating integrity throughout implantation.

Future studies should assess coating performance under application-relevant conditions (e.g., orthotopic or load-bearing models), extend observation periods, and further optimize coating stability and degradation kinetics to align mechanical integrity with the intended clinical indication.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that composite PEM/W coatings, particularly the HA/Chi/W system, effectively modulate the in vivo degradation of magnesium while maintaining a physiological tissue response, highlighting their potential as a surface-engineering strategy to improve the safety and performance of resorbable magnesium implants. The coatings are nanometer-thin and hydrophobic, providing a controlled barrier to corrosion. All Mg-based implants induced a moderate, macrophage-dominated foreign body reaction typical of biodegradable metals, without evidence of acute or chronic inflammation. However, uncoated Mg discs showed increased fibrosis and necrosis over time due to rapid degradation and local tissue disruption, whereas PEM/W coatings, especially HA/Chi/W, significantly attenuated these effects, indicating enhanced biocompatibility and more controlled degradation behavior.
