Recurrent myocardial infarction and adherence to secondary prevention strategies among adults with coronary artery disease in rural Aluva, South India: a nested case–control study
Authors: Neeraj Vinod Mohandas, Vijayakumar Krishnapillai, Neethu George, Avani Dinesh, Vinod Mohandas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent myocardial infarction (re-MI) remains a significant challenge in cardiovascular care, especially in low-income countries. Despite advancements in cardiac care, many patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) fail to follow the secondary prevention guidelines consistently. This study aims to determine the independent predictors of re-MI and inadequate adherence to secondary prevention strategies among adults with CAD in a rural cohort in South India.MethodsA nested case–control study with risk set sampling of controls was conducted from January 2022 to March 2022 within the ENDIRA (Epidemiology of Non-communicable Diseases In Rural Areas) Cohort in the rural part of Aluva municipality of Ernakulam district, Kerala, India. CAD patients aged 35–80 years from the ENDIRA cohort who have had one or more episodes of myocardial infarction 28 days post the index episode as confirmed by medical reports were selected as cases while those with no recurrent episodes were taken as controls. Descriptive analysis was conducted to characterise the study population and was expressed in frequencies and percentages. Regression models were used to determine the independent predictors of re-MI as well as inadequate adherence and was expressed using adjusted odds ratio along with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe study included 187 cases and 436 controls. The independent predictors of recurrent myocardial infarction included poor socio-economic status (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] 1.98; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.34–2.91), > 5 years since CAD diagnosis (AOR 1.53; 95% CI 1.06–2.22), previous history of CABG (AOR 1.75;95% CI 1.17–2.61), alcohol consumption (AOR 1.65; 95% CI 1.04–2.61), follow up under complementary and alternative medicine (AOR 2.04; 95% CI 1.03–4.01), distance to follow up hospital > 20 kms (AOR 1.85; 95% CI 1.09–3.12) and uncontrolled blood pressure (AOR 1.60; 95% CI 1.07–2.39). The CAD patients preferring private facilities for follow up were 43% less likely to develop recurrent myocardial infarction. (AOR 0.57; 95% CI 0.34–0.95).ConclusionThe determinants of recurrent myocardial infarction and inadequate adherence are multifactorial, encompassing socioeconomic, behavioural and health system factors. Strengthening equitable access to quality secondary prevention services, integrating social support, and improving continuity of cardiac care are essential to reduce recurrence and improve outcomes in rural India.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25571-2.Keywords: CAD, MI, Non-adherence, Preventive cardiology, Re-MI, Secondary prevention

Background

The global epidemiological transition marked a shift in disease patterns, with non-communicable diseases (NCDs) surpassing communicable diseases as the leading contributors to the global disease burden [1]. Owing to their chronic nature, NCDs demand sustained management through long-term therapy and lifestyle modification to prevent progression and fatal outcomes [2, 3]. Amongst NCDs, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) have emerged as the principal cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide [4]. According to the World Heart Federation, deaths from cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) have increased by 60% over the past three decades, with nearly 80% occurring in low- and middle-income countries, primarily due to coronary artery disease (CAD) and cerebrovascular accidents (CVA) [5, 6].

While developed nations have achieved declines in CAD mortality through robust health systems and preventive strategies, low- and middle-income countries, including India, are experiencing a rapidly rising CAD epidemic [7, 8]. High prevalence of risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, coupled with inadequate preventive measures, contribute to this burden [9]. Kerala, reporting the highest incidence of CAD risk factors in India, faces an impending epidemic, with post–myocardial infarction patients particularly vulnerable to recurrent events and higher mortality [10, 11].

Recurrent MI (re-MI) is defined as the myocardial infarction (MI) that occurs after 28 days following the index MI event [12]. While the incidence of re-MI was notably high in the 1980 s, advancements such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) have since reduced recurrence rates substantially in developed countries [13]. However, it’s incidence within the Indian context remains inadequately documented. Due to its prognostic implications, re-MI is one of the composite outcomes of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) studies conducted among CAD patients [14]. The mortality rate of re-MI is nearly 50% within 5 years [15]. Multiple predictive factors are indicated in re-MI, however the ones that need utmost mention are non-adherence to secondary preventive therapies [16] and old age [17].

The World Heart Federation defines secondary cardiovascular prevention is as any strategy which reduces the probability of a recurrent cardiovascular event in patients with known atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [18]. These approaches combine health education, lifestyle modifications, and medical interventions to extend patient longevity [19]. There are well established international guidelines for secondary prevention of CAD by organisations such as the American Heart Association (AHA) and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) which recommend aggressive risk factor management alongside cardioprotective therapies [20, 21]. Adherence to such guidelines can substantially enhance patients' quality of life, minimizing long-term morbidity and mortality related to CAD, and thereby reducing the socioeconomic impact of the disease [19].

Despite the known benefits, secondary prevention practices remain inconsistently applied worldwide, more so in low-income nations, where determinants and repercussions of this underutilization are inadequately characterized [16, 22]. Adequate adherence is defined as remaining attached to the prescribed medication regime for > 80% of the times [23]. A study conducted in Germany reported that only 50% of the patients having chronic diseases are adherent to their prescribed therapies at one year and only 20% take medications as directed [24]. A recent study from South India reported that the medication adherence among patients with CAD on follow up was only 56.4% [25]. As per recent reports, the cardiac rehabilitation (CR) of India is insufficient and ranks poor even among the LMICs [26].

Understanding adherence patterns among patients with recurrent MI is crucial in the Indian healthcare context, where disparities in access and continuity of care persist. To date, no studies from India have systematically explored the determinants of recurrent myocardial infarction in conjunction with adherence to secondary prevention strategies within a rural population. Hence, this nested case–control study was conducted with the objective to identify the determinants of re-MI and inadequate adherence to secondary prevention strategies among these patients in a rural cohort in the south Indian state of Kerala.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

A nested case–control study was conducted from January 2022 to March 2022 using the data from the pre-existing ENDIRA (Epidemiology of Non-communicable Diseases In Rural Areas) Cohort [27] where CAD patients from five ‘panchayats’ (Kalady, Karukutty, Manjapra, Mukkanoor and Thuravoor) consisting of 75 wards were being followed up since 2010. According to the Constitution of India, a 'panchayat' is a rural local self-governance body, and a 'ward' is a geographical division within each such body [28]. This cohort is in the rural part of Aluva municipality [28] of Ernakulam district in the south Indian state of Kerala which is representative of Kerala population in terms of mean age. The STROBE case–control checklist was used to write the report.

Definition of cases and controls

All CAD patients who have had one or more episodes of myocardial infarction 28 days post the index episode confirmed by medical records were defined as cases [12] while those with no recurrent events post the index event as per medical records were taken as controls. Controls were randomly selected by risk set sampling from the CAD patients within the study cohort. CAD was defined as per Sheridan and Crossman review [29]. The exclusion criteria included (1) stroke patients or patients in coma who are cognitively impaired and are unable to answer the questions of the interview (2) patients with auditory impairment (3) mentally ill patients (4) bedridden patients (5) patients with incomplete medical records. Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on the study by Al Saleh et al. [30] where the prevalence of recurrent myocardial infarction among CAD patients was 2.1%. The formula used was n = (Z1-α/2 + Z1-β)2 PQ × 2/d2 [Z1-α/2 = 1.96, Z1-β = 1.282, P = 2.1, Q = 97.9, d (absolute precision) = 5%] and the minimum sample size with 80% power, 95% confidence interval and 95% response rate came up to 187.

A minimum of two controls were selected from the risk set matched with respect to age (within 1 year difference) and gender at the time of the case event and this matching was maintained using conditional logistic regression. However, extra 62 eligible controls were enrolled from the cohort database during the recruitment period (till exhaustion of the resources available for the study) to increase precision for subgroup analyses and to allow for exclusions due to missing exposure data. Therefore, 187 patients with recurrent myocardial infarction and 436 patients with no recurrence were selected as cases and controls respectively.

Study procedure and study tool

The study was done by securing informed consent from each participant, followed by conducting personal interviews at the participants' residences. These interviews were carried out by study personnel in collaboration with Accredited Social Health Activist (ASHA) workers, who received training from the principal investigator prior to data collection. This approach not only facilitated direct engagement with participants but also ensured the collection of reliable data. To mitigate potential recall and information bias, the researchers employed a method of cross-verification, comparing self-reported data with medical records whenever feasible, thereby enhancing the study's data integrity.

Given the absence of a validated assessment tool for measuring adherence to secondary prevention strategies, a new tool was developed using a 4-step mini modified Delphi consensus method (Fig. 2) [31–33]. The initial version was prepared based on American Heart Association (AHA) and American College of Cardiology Foundation (ACCF) 2011 guidelines [20], existing literature as well as a validated, translated Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [34]. The further development of the assessment tool is given in detail in the supplementary material. The English version of the questionnaire is also available in the supplementary material. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was checked using Cronbach’s alpha and a score of 0.72 was found to be acceptable.

Development of the assessment tool for adherence to secondary prevention strategies-Delphi consensus method

The pre-tested, structured questionnaire was divided into seven domains:

1) basic information and sociodemographic details 2) medical history and comorbidities 3) habits including tobacco and alcohol usage, type as well as frequency 4) details of CAD medications taken, which includes the medications prescribed by the doctor, the medications which are actually consumed by the study participant along with the frequency of taking the medications per week 5) diet history 6) PHQ-9 questionnaire for depression screening 7) clinical examination details including height, weight, body mass index (BMI) and blood pressure (BP). The socio-economic status was classified into APL (Above Poverty Line) and BPL (Below Poverty Line) based on the colour of the ration card issued by the Government of Kerala [35].

Criteria for adherence to secondary prevention strategies

There are no available guidelines for adherence to secondary prevention specifically for the Indian context. Hence, the adherence to secondary prevention strategies for CAD was defined using the universally accepted 2011 guidelines of the AHA and ACCF [20] as well as published literature (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

The data collected was then entered in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Washington, USA), numerically coded and analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Missing data were assessed for pattern and extent, and where appropriate, multiple imputation was performed under the missing-at-random assumption to minimise bias. Known confounders were identified a priori based on existing literature and biological plausibility. Descriptive analysis was done to characterise the study population and was expressed in frequencies and percentages. Regression model was used to determine the independent predictors of re-MI and to identify the potential confounders of inadequate adherence to secondary prevention strategies.

Simple logistic regression model was used for all the variables to find the unadjusted odds ratio. Four separate regression models (given in the supplementary material) were used to determine the predictors of recurrence of myocardial infarction under four categories (socio-demographic factors, medical and personal history, utilization of health services and adherence to the domains of secondary prevention). All variables with a p value < 0.1 in the four independent models were taken (in order to accommodate maximum confounders into the model) for the final regression model to determine the independent predictors of re-MI. In order to determine the independent predictors of inadequate adherence to secondary prevention, the variables with a p value < 0.1 in the simple logistic regression model were taken for multivariable logistic regression. The final results from the regression models were expressed using adjusted odds ratio (AOR) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The regression coefficients were tested using the Wald statistic. A p value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

While patients and the general public were not directly involved in the initial planning and design of this study, their input was integral to its refinement. ASHA workers (trained female community health activists) of the respective wards assisted the principal investigator in data collection while feedback from the patients was incorporated into the questionnaire refinement process during the initial pilot phase of the study. The authors plan to disseminate the study findings through the ASHA workers and patient support groups to promote improved adherence to secondary prevention strategies among patients with re-MI in the region.

Results

A total of 187 cases and 436 controls were selected for the study. The mean (± SD) age of the cases and controls was 64.94 ± 8.99 years and 65.20 ± 8.37 years respectively and the difference was not statistically significant. 75.4% of the cases and 69% of the controls were males and this difference was not statistically significant as well. Among the cases, 54%, 39.6% and 47.6% were hypertensive, diabetic and dyslipidemic respectively whereas among the controls, 54.1%, 46.6% and 50.9% were hypertensive, diabetic and dyslipidemic respectively. Patients in the BPL category were 98% more likely to develop recurrence compared to those above the poverty line. Patients with CAD for > 5 years had 53% higher risk of developing recurrence compared to those with CAD ≤ 5 years. Those who underwent coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) had 75% higher risk of developing re-MI compared to those who did not. Patients with reported alcohol consumption had 65% more risk of developing re-MI compared to those with no alcohol intake. Those who preferred follow up with complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) had 2.04 times odds of recurrence compared to those who preferred a General Physician practising allopathy for follow up. Patients living > 20 kms away from the hospital had 1.85 times odds of developing recurrence compared to those living within 10 kms. Those who preferred follow up at private hospitals were 43% less likely to develop re-MI compared to those who preferred public healthcare facilities. Patients with uncontrolled blood pressure had 60% more risk of re-MI compared to those with their blood pressure under control.

Discussion

This study offers an in-depth analysis of factors contributing to MI recurrence and the challenges in adhering to secondary prevention strategies in rural populations. This study found that socio-economic status, duration of CAD, previous history of CABG, alcohol consumption, follow up under complementary and alternative medicine, proximity to healthcare facilities, uncontrolled blood pressure and facility preferred for follow up were the independent predictors of re-MI. The independent predictors of inadequate adherence to secondary prevention strategies are grouped under the following four domains: (1) CAD Medication domain: (a) diagnosis of CAD (b) primary treatment of CAD (c) previous history of CABG (2) Blood Pressure Control domain: (a) number of family members (b) socio-economic status (c) duration of CAD (d) healthcare satisfaction (3) BMI domain: (a) healthcare satisfaction (4) Physical activity domain: (a) previous history of CABG.

Recurrence of myocardial infarction

BPL patients were more likely to develop re-MI in this study which was consistent with the report of Kareem et al. where low socio-economic status had 74% increased risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) in MI patients [41]. This implies that the patients with poor socio-economic status might have decreased healthcare access, low health literacy and low medication adherence as well. The adherence to secondary prevention medications which have protective effects against the proatherogenic stimuli from initial MI decreases with time [42]. The COMMIT, SOLVD and SAVE trials have studied the short and long term recurrence rates in MI patients on CAD medications which is comparable to this study [43, 44]. The conclusions from these studies imply that with time, the myocardial injury secondary to coronary atherothrombosis may directly affect recurrence. Smolina et al. reported a 7 year risk estimate of re-MI and concluded that the probability of recurrence is greatest in the first year following index MI after which the risk continued to gradually increase in the subsequent years [45].

The patients who underwent CABG in this study had higher risk of developing recurrence probably because only complicated CADs with severe arterial occlusions undergo CABG. The early post-operative care given after the procedure should be explored further as it is crucial to the long term outcomes [46]. Litwinowicz et al. reported that postoperative MI after CABG was associated with higher mortality at short term as well as long term follow up. [46] Studies from different cohorts reported that low to moderate alcohol consumption is associated with reduced mortality risk in post MI patients but heavy drinking is associated with increased risk of acute MI [47, 48]. However, CAD patients in this study with reported alcohol consumption are more likely to develop recurrence. Samokhvalov et al. has reported an increased risk of atrial fibrillation in females consuming more than 24 g pure ethanol and in males ingesting more than 36 g daily [49]. Ilic et al. emphasis on the risk of binge alcohol consumption and myocardial infarction. [50] All these studies imply that there are detrimental effects of alcohol on MI.

This study showed that the patients preferring CAM had 2.04 times odds of recurrence compared to those who preferred a General Physician practicing allopathy for follow up. However, the study by Shafiq et al. concluded that there was no association between health status recovery post MI and CAM use. [51] The independent predictors of CAM use among patients with CAD need to be explored further. Distance has always been a barrier to follow up care throughout the world and as per Piette et al., patients living more than 20 miles from the healthcare centre had 23% more risk of death within one year of acute MI. [52] Telemedicine and community health outreach programmes can be explored as potential mitigating strategies. Patients in this study receiving treatment from private healthcare facilities were less likely to develop re-MI probably because such patients were able to afford the advanced treatment at the private hospitals. Studies report that private hospitals in Kerala are more likely to have better managerial autonomy and are in a better position to recruit and retain talent compared to the government hospitals [53]. Recent reports from the Framingham study state that uncontrolled blood pressure is a strong predictor for the development of subsequent fatal and non-fatal coronary events but there has been a gradual shift of predictors from diastolic blood pressure to systolic blood pressure and then to pulse pressure with increasing age [54].

Adherence to secondary prevention strategies

Adherence to the six domains of secondary prevention was independently studied among the cases and controls in this study. Except for blood pressure control and mental health, the adherence was generally higher among cases. This could be due to more frequent check-up advised for those who have had recurrence. The number of outpatient visits were higher for cases than for controls in this study. Pedrinelli et al. have reported that uncontrolled hypertension has been frequently associated with the recurrence of myocardial infarction. [55]

CAD medication domain

In this study, 63.1% of the cases were adherent to medication prescription which is higher compared to 56.4% adherence among controls. This study reported a lower use of all medications among cases including antiplatelets (67.3% vs 93%), statins (59.4% vs 80%), ACEi/ARBs (44.9% vs 75%) as well as Beta blockers (35.3% vs 81%) in comparison to EUROASPIRE V data [36, 56]. The better adherence among NSTEMI patients may be attributed to the health education received by the patients from the healthcare professionals as NSTEMI has poorer long term prognosis as compared to STEMI [57]. The superior adherence of patients initiated on medical therapy can again be attributed to the targeted health education given by the healthcare professionals. Those who underwent CABG had better adherence to medication intake. Undergoing a cardiac surgery is a traumatic event in anyone’s life and the realisation that the only way to lead a good quality life post-surgery is to adhere to the prescribed medications may be a possible explanation.

Blood pressure control domain

In this study, only 66.3% of the cases had controlled blood pressure which was less compared to 77.3% among the controls. Both these figures were higher compared to the Indian component of the CLARIFY registry which showed adequate blood pressure control in only 59.5% of the CAD patients [58]. The SPRINT trial reported a target systolic blood pressure < 120 mm Hg resulted in lower rates of cardiovascular events [59]. Patients who have more than four family members had inadequate blood pressure control in this study. A family-based cardiovascular intervention in India showed that structured family engagement can reduce global cardiovascular risk factors, implying that family structures can be leveraged for improvement if family support is organised [60]. The patients below poverty line in this study were more likely to have their blood pressure under control which is contrary to the findings of Anstey et al. which state that low socio-economic status was associated with poor blood pressure control. [61] The stronger reach of Kerala’s public health system especially among the lower income groups could be a possible explanation for this inversion of socio-economic and blood pressure control trend [62].

In this study, the patients diagnosed with CAD for more than five years had higher odds of inadequate adherence which was consistent with evidence that long-term patients often experience declining medication adherence over time [63, 64]. This finding underscores the need for sustained adherence support mechanisms, such as periodic counselling, structured follow-up reminders, and community-based reinforcement through ASHAs or telephonic follow-up, to maintain long-term treatment continuity in chronic cardiac care. The patients who were not satisfied with the healthcare received had greater odds of inadequate adherence because they probably do not go for follow up visits which was consistent with the results of the study by Soylemez et al. which concludes that as satisfaction with hypertensive care increases, adherence to antihypertensive medications also increase. [65]

Body mass index domain

In this study, 51.9% of the cases had their BMI in the normal range which was slightly higher compared to 48.9% among the controls. The fact that almost half of the CAD patients in both groups were not adherent to the BMI goal is concerning. However, these figures appear to be lower compared with the EUROASPIRE series of surveys (2001–2018) where only one in five were able to achieve the target [56]. Patients who were not satisfied with the healthcare received in this study had higher odds of inadequate adherence probably due to the lack of trust they have in the system. Leung et al. reported that depression, stress and previous failed weight loss attempts were associated with poor adherence to lifestyle modification. [66] These reports imply that constant motivation by the family members as well as healthcare professionals is crucial to adhere to a healthy lifestyle [67].

Smoking cessation, Physical activity and Mental health domains

In this study, 60.4% of the cases and 61.8% controls who had history of smoking reported complete cessation. Previous studies have stressed on the importance of smoking cessation as part of secondary prevention [68]. These figures were higher compared to 38.6% complete cessation in the FHS cohort reported by Duncan et al. [69] The prevalence of smoking in the state of Kerala (12.7%) is much lower compared to the national average (28.6%) which may be the reason for better adherence to smoking cessation [70].

In this study, 67.4% of the cases and 64.9% of the controls adhered to moderate physical activities as recommended. A recent multi-centric study conducted in India reported that only 27.8% of the population were moderately active while 20.3% were inactive [71]. Patients who underwent CABG in this study were more likely to have adequate physical activity which can be attributed to the health education given by the healthcare providers.

In this study, 59.4% of the cases and 72.5% of the controls had good mental health. Suffering from recurrent MI would definitely take a toll on one’s mental health. Data from studies suggest that depression may be associated with obesity [72] as well as inadequate sleep [73]. Multiple studies state that depression and anxiety are associated with increased risk of subsequent events in MI patients [74] and Abel et al. concluded depression as the strongest associated risk factor in MI for recurrence. [75] Meijer et al. reported that depression causes 13% increased risk of cardiovascular events following MI [76].

This study attempted to evaluate the adherence to secondary prevention strategies and the results could help design additional strategies to address the shortcomings of cardiac rehabilitation in India. Limitations of this study include a potential positive bias due to health education initiatives and surveys within the study cohort, which may not reflect broader rural populations in India. There is a probability of potential recall bias in the self-reported data. The new adherence tool lacks validation beyond internal consistency. Another key limitation is that data regarding exposure among recurrent MI cases were not collected prior to the recurrent event; hence, temporal association between exposures and recurrence could not be established in this study. Moreover, the adherence to secondary prevention strategies and re-MI may share unmeasured confounders such as health literacy and family support. The disruption of routine healthcare services and the potential impact on mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic as well as a subjective assessment of healthcare satisfaction may also influence the findings.

Policy and practice implications

The findings of this study underscore the need for a comprehensive, community-oriented strategy to strengthen secondary prevention of CAD in rural India. Socioeconomic disadvantage, limited access to follow-up care, and reliance on alternative medicine highlight structural gaps in continuity of cardiac care. Integrating community health workers such as ASHAs into post-MI follow-up can facilitate regular blood pressure monitoring, medication adherence checks, and patient counselling at the household level. Telemedicine-based follow-up, combined with periodic outreach clinics, may help overcome geographic barriers and support sustained engagement with qualified cardiac care providers. Additionally, context-specific health education and linkage of rural patients to social support schemes could enhance adherence to therapy and risk factor control, ultimately reducing the burden of recurrent myocardial infarction in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

The recurrence of myocardial infarction as well as the adherence to secondary prevention strategies is influenced by a multifaceted combination of individual and societal factors. To mitigate the ongoing epidemic of CAD in India, it is essential to implement strategies that bolster social support and improve healthcare access in rural areas. Additionally, addressing inadequate adherence to secondary prevention guidelines at various domains is crucial for the prevention of re-MI.
