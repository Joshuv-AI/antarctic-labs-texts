A network analysis of depressive symptoms on childhood trauma and the relationship with non-suicidal self-injury among hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents
Authors: Zhen He, Yanyun Yuan, Yiqiu Hu, Ying Cao, Yueyue Meng, Zihao Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildhood trauma exerts lasting negative effects on mental health, with hard-of-hearing adolescents particularly vulnerable compared to their hearing peers. Yet we still know little about the relationship between childhood trauma, non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and depressive symptoms in this population, especially at symptoms level.MethodsIn this study, a total of 2,008 Chinese students (hearing = 1,386; hard-of-hearing = 622) completed self-report questionnaires assessing childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI. Mediation analysis tested the indirect effect of depressive symptoms. Network analysis identified central and bridge symptoms, and directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) were used to explore potential directional pathways between symptoms.ResultsDepressive symptoms mediated the trauma–NSSI association, with a stronger effect among hard-of-hearing adolescents; For hearing adolescents, emotional abuse- and neglect-related items were central symptoms in trauma-depressive-NSSI network, whereas only an emotional neglect-related item was central for hard-of-hearing adolescents; DAGs further supported a pathway from trauma to depression to NSSI, pinpointing key symptoms driving this progression.ConclusionsOverall, this study underscores that trauma–depression–NSSI pathways vary across population at both construct and symptom levels, highlighting the importance of early identification and interventions targeting depressive symptoms to help prevent and relieve NSSI risk in trauma-exposed adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04689-x.

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is a serious public health concern during adolescence. It is characterized by the deliberate and direct infliction of harm on one’s own body tissue without suicidal intent [9]. Alarmingly, approximately 22% of Chinese adolescents reported engaging in NSSI at least once, and the majority subsequently attempted suicide [49]. Hard-of-hearing adolescents are particularly vulnerable due to communication barriers and social disadvantages linked to their hearing impairment. These challenges increase their susceptibility to psychological and behavioral problems [18]. Notably, previous research has demonstrated that adolescents with hearing loss report higher levels of depressive symptoms and face a higher risk of engaging in NSSI compared to hearing peers [37]. Given their distinct developmental environment and these observed clinical disparities, the underlying mechanisms of NSSI in hard-of-hearing adolescents may differ from those in hearing adolescents. Therefore, it is necessary to examine both populations concurrently to inform tailored prevention and intervention strategies.

The relationship between the childhood trauma and NSSI

Childhood trauma, a form of early adversity, encompasses experiences such as abuse, neglect, or other harmful events [36]. From the attachment theory, inadequate parental care during early life may disrupt the formation of secure attachments, increasing vulnerability to emotional dysregulation and maladaptive behavior [15]. Neurodevelopmental research Neurodevelopmental research further indicates that trauma exposure can lead to impairments in the prefrontal cortex and dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, both of which are critical for risk behaviors [10].

Importantly, compared with hearing pees, hard-of-hearing adolescents are more likely to experience parental neglect or abuse. Prior studies suggest that many Chinese parents, upon discovering that their child has a physical impairment, may choose to have a second child, which may reduce parental attention and increase the risk of neglect among hard-of-hearing adolescents [18]. Additionally, communication barriers may further increase the likelihood of parental neglect and rejection in this population [28]. Taken together, these findings support the conceptualization of childhood trauma as a key developmental risk factor for adolescent NSSI. Consistent with this view, hard-of-hearing adolescents in China may face an elevated risk of childhood trauma compared to their hearing peers.

The mediation role of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms are a common psychological concern during adolescence, characterized by persistent low mood, feelings of hopelessness and helplessness, and reduced motivation [35]. Emerging evidence suggests that early adverse experiences, particularly childhood trauma, are key risk factors for depressive symptoms [45].

Attachment theory posits that childhood trauma often associates with emotional disturbance [15]. Adolescents with anxious attachment exhibit heightened sensitivity to perceived rejection, increasing negative emotions, while those with avoidant attachment may suppress emotional expression or avoid seeking support, resulting in the accumulation of distress [40, 48]. Consequently, childhood trauma not only increases the risk of NSSI but also contribute to depressive symptoms.

Furthermore, experiential avoidance model suggests that adolescents may engage in NSSI to escape or suppress unwanted emotional experiences [2]. Depressive symptoms are thus a prospective risk factor for NSSI, as intense negative affect triggered by external stressors may prompt self-injury as an emotion regulation strategy [44]. In the context of childhood trauma, depressive symptoms may serve as a mediating mechanism, linking early adversity to later NSSI.

Notably, this mediation may be particularly salient in hard-of-hearing adolescents. Minority Stress Theory suggests that these individuals face chronic stressors, including social stigma and isolation, which exacerbate vulnerability to early adversity [26]. Language deprivation and communication barriers limit external resources for affect regulation, leading to intensified internalizing distress. Moreover, sensory-impaired youth often exhibit stronger associations between depressive symptoms and self-harm, compounded by thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness [12]. Consequently, depressive symptoms may function as a primary psychological factor that facilitates the transition from childhood trauma to maladaptive NSSI behaviors in this population.

The network of the childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI

Most prior research has conceptualized childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI as unitary constructs by using total scores from self-report questionnaires to assess their interrelations [46]. While this construct-level approach provides a general overview, it does not reveal which specific symptoms are central or how they interact to maintain psychopathology.

According to the network theory of mental disorders, the development and maintenance of psychological disorders are driven by direct interactions among individual symptoms rather than latent variables [5]. When symptom-level interactions become strong and mutually reinforcing, the disorder may persist. Network analysis leverages this perspective, offering a powerful tool to map complex interconnections among symptoms and identify core and bridge symptoms that may serve as intervention targets [19].

Recent network studies have identified pivotal symptoms across childhood trauma, depression, and NSSI. For example, a study examining the network structure of childhood maltreatment and depressive symptoms found that “feeling depressed” and “moody” were among the most central symptoms in the network [43]. In NSSI–depression networks, “desire to harm oneself and inability to stop” and depressive symptoms exhibited the highest strength centrality [17]. With regard to the interplay among childhood trauma, NSSI, and depressive symptoms, [24] found that “low self-esteem” and “emotional abuse” were the most central symptoms within the trauma–depression–NSSI network, with emotional abuse being the only trauma-related symptom directly linked to NSSI.

These findings suggest that analyzing symptom-level relationships can offer important insights into the mechanisms underlying psychopathology. Accordingly, the present study employs network analysis to investigate the symptom-level interrelations among childhood trauma, depressive symptoms, and NSSI.

The difference between hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents in symptom network

The structure of trauma-depressive symptoms-NSSI networks may differ across populations. Particularly in the Chinese cultural context, where carrying on the family lineage is highly valued, children with disabilities are often perceived as less capable of fulfilling traditional familial roles [21]. Many parents consequently choose to have additional children, potentially reducing attention and care for the child with a disability [22]. Sociological evidence further indicates that parents of children with disabilities invest less time and financial resources compared to parents of non-disabled children, often resulting in more severe neglect [25].

Hard-of-hearing adolescents face additional communication barriers that hinder parent–child interactions, limiting their ability to express needs and emotions effectively [47]. Research suggests that depressive symptoms in this population are more closely linked to social isolation and perceived lack of support, whereas in hearing adolescents, depressive symptoms may be more strongly influenced by interpersonal conflict or performance-related stress [41].

Taken together, cultural factors may influence the experiences of hard-of-hearing adolescents. Developmental factors also contribute to differences in emotional and behavioral responses. Communication barriers further affect how these adolescents’ express needs and emotions. Therefore, we proposed that the symptom-level network connecting childhood trauma, depressive symptoms, and NSSI may differ meaningfully between hard-of-hearing and hearing adolescents.

Current study

This study aimed to investigate the relationships among childhood trauma, depressive symptoms, and NSSI in hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents. We examined whether depressive symptoms mediate the association between childhood trauma and NSSI and whether this process differs between the two groups. At the symptom level, we aimed to identify central and bridge symptoms that play pivotal roles in these associations. We also explored potential directional pathways from trauma to depressive symptoms and NSSI to understand how early adversity may propagate through symptom networks.

Overall, the study tests the hypotheses that depressive symptoms mediate the trauma–NSSI association. Network structures differ between hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents. Certain symptoms act as key bridges linking trauma and self-injurious behavior. Directional pathways among trauma, depressive symptoms, and NSSI may reveal potential connection underlying maladaptive outcomes. By integrating construct-level and symptom-level analyses, the study provides a framework for understanding mechanisms linking early adversity to adolescent mental health and highlights potential targets for tailored interventions.

Methods

Participants and procedure

This study recruited participants using a stratified sampling strategy. The hearing participants were recruited from two middle schools in Changsha, Hunan Province. The hard-of-hearing participants were recruited from the special education school in Henan Provinces to ensure an adequate and representative sample size. Both provinces are located in Central China and share high similarities in socio-economic development, cultural values, and educational systems. All participants had normal intellectual functioning; hard-of-hearing students had passed the required entrance examinations for their respective schools. To reduce potential confounding, students with a prior clinical diagnosis of depression were excluded.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hunan Normal University (Approval No. HUNNU-20240619). Prior to data collection, informed consent was obtained from all students and their legal guardians. Trained psychology graduate students administered the surveys in classroom settings; for hard-of-hearing students, data collection was supported by certified sign language interpreters.

A total of 2,040 questionnaires were collected. After removing duplicate entries and incomplete responses, 2,008 valid questionnaires were retained (Mage = 15.77, SD = 2.02), including 1,386 from hearing students and 622 from hard-of-hearing students. The hearing sample included 682 females (49.2%) and 704 males (50.8%), while the hard-of-hearing students sample consisted of 320 females (51.4%) and 302 males (48.6%).

Measures

Childhood trauma

Childhood trauma was assessed using the short form of the childhood trauma questionnaire (CTQ; [3]. In accordance with the recommendations of the ethics committee, the sexual abuse subscale was excluded due to its sensitive nature in the school setting. As a result, the version used in this study consisted of 23 items across four subscales (items 10, 16, and 20 serving as validity-check items and not included in the scoring): emotional abuse, physical abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Never true) to 5 (Very often true). Higher scores indicate greater exposure to childhood trauma. The CTQ has been widely used in Chinese adolescent samples and has demonstrated good reliability and validity [13]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the CTQ was 0.85 among hearing students and 0.87 among hard-of-hearing students.

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item short form of the center for epidemiologic studies depression scale (CES-D-10; [1]. This self-report scale has demonstrated strong reliability and validity among Chinese populations [42]. Participants rated each item on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Rarely or none of the time) to 3 (Most of the time), with higher total scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the CES-D-10 was 0.81 among hearing students and 0.73 among hard-of-hearing students.

Non-suicidal self-injury

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) was assessed using the deliberate self-harm inventory: nine-item version (DSHI-9; [4]. This self-report measure includes 9 items assessing the frequency of specific self-injurious behaviors without suicidal intent. Each item is rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Never) to 5 (More than five times), with higher scores indicating greater frequency of self-harm. Previous research has demonstrated good psychometric properties of the DSHI-9 among Chinese adolescents [52]. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the DSHI-9 was 0.79 among hearing students and 0.95 among hard-of-hearing students.

Covariate

Previous research has consistently demonstrated significant gender differences in NSSI [32]. These differences are particularly pronounced during adolescence and may vary across regions [51]. Consequently, gender was included as a covariate in this study.

Statistical analysis

Since all data in this study were collected through self-reports, we conducted a test for common method bias before the formal analyses. The results of hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents showed that the first factor accounted for 22.51% (33.92%) of the variance, less than 40% of the critical value. In addition, we conducted a multicollinearity test to examine the appropriateness of variable selection. The results indicated that there was no serious multicollinearity in this study (VIFs < 3). Overall, these findings suggest that the data were suitable for subsequent analyses.

The analytical procedures were as follows: Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, chi-square test, and Pearson correlations to examine variable distributions and group differences. Mediation analyses using Mplus 8.3 tested the mediating role of depressive symptoms and multi-group comparisons. Model fit was evaluated following [23] recommendations, with the following criteria applied: CFI ≥ 0.90, TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and SRMR ≤ 0.08.

Symptom-level network analyses were conducted in R using graphical LASSO (Bootnet). All variables were standardized and reverse-coded where necessary. Bridge expected influence (bridge EI) was used as the primary centrality index. Bootstrapping assessed edge weight accuracy, and case-dropping subset bootstrapping evaluated stability. The EBIC tuning parameter γ was set to 0.5, with sensitivity analyses confirming the robustness of network structure. Group differences in bridge networks and global strength were examined using NetworkTools and NetworkComparisonTest (NCT). Bayesian network learning (bnlearn) was applied to explore potential directional associations among symptoms via directed acyclic graphs (DAGs).

Group differences in the mediating pathway from trauma to NSSI via depressive symptoms

Structural equation modeling with latent variables was conducted using Mplus version 8.3. Childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI were specified as endogenous latent variables, with their respective items or subdimensions treated as observed indicators. The results showed that childhood trauma significantly influenced depressive symptoms (βhearing = 0.57, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001; βhard-of-hearing = 0.54, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001), and positively influenced NSSI (βhearing = 0.48 SE = 0.04, p < 0.001; βhard-of-hearing = 0.51, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001). In turn, depressive symptoms significantly affected NSSI(βhearing = 0.23, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001; βhard-of-hearing = 0.37, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001).

The indirect effect of childhood trauma on NSSI via depressive symptoms was significant for both groups (indirect effect = 0.11 for hearing students; 0.24 for hard-of-hearing students, both p < 0.001). The total effect was 0.38 for hearing students and 0.77 for hard-of-hearing students, with the proportion of the indirect effect accounting for 28.9% and 31.2%, respectively.

The overall model demonstrated acceptable fit across both groups: χ2 = 2145.69 (1456.42), df = 248, CFI = 0.81 (0.88), TLI = 0.80 (0.86), RMSEA = 0.07 (0.08), SRMR = 0.05 (0.07), with values in parentheses corresponding to the hard-of-hearing group. Although the CFI and TLI values were slightly below the conventional cutoff of 0.90, the RMSEA and SRMR values met acceptable standards. Notably, fit indices are sensitive to sample size and model characteristics. Rigid cutoff criteria are not universally applicable. Therefore, model fit should be evaluated using multiple indices [50]. Taken together, the overall model fit was considered marginally acceptable.

Multi-group comparison of mediation paths using chi-square and wald tests

To examine whether the mediation model differed significantly between hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents, a chi-square difference test was conducted by comparing constrained and unconstrained models. The results indicated a significant difference between the two groups (Δχ2 = 130.99, Δdf = 3, p < 0.001). We further examined whether specific path coefficients differed between groups using Wald tests. The results revealed significant group differences in both direct and indirect paths from childhood trauma to NSSI (childhood trauma → NSSI: Wald χ2 = 7.34, p < 0.01; childhood trauma → depressive symptoms: Wald χ2 = 30.11, p < 0.001; depressive symptoms → NSSI: Wald χ2 = 36.30, p < 0.001).

Bridge network robustness checks

The robustness of the bridge network was evaluated by assessing the accuracy and stability of edge weights and node centrality indices. All variables were standardized prior to analysis, and items were reverse-coded where necessary. Non-parametric bootstrap procedures were used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the edge weights in both networks. The relatively narrow CIs suggest good accuracy and precision in the estimation of edge weights (see Fig. 2). Furthermore, case-dropping subset bootstrapping was conducted to assess the stability of edge weights and centrality indices. Previous research has demonstrated that node expected influence (EI) shows stronger stability and interpretability than other centrality metrics, such as closeness and betweenness. Therefore, bridge expected influence (bridge EI) was adopted as the primary index of node centrality in this study. The correlation stability (CS) coefficients for bridge strength (hearing = 0.67; hard-of-hearing = 0.67) and bridge EI (hearing = 0.67; hard-of-hearing = 0.66) indicated good stability. All CS values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.25, suggesting that the estimated bridge networks were stable and reliable [11].

Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by varying the EBIC tuning parameter γ from 0 to 0.5 to ensure our findings were not artifacts of model parsimony. Across both samples, the network topology remained highly consistent, with adjacency matrix correlations ≥ 0.99. Although the number of edges decreased slightly as the penalty increased (hearing group: 308 to 302; hard-of-hearing group: 288 to 271), the bridge strength rankings remained remarkably stable (rs = 0.99 for hearing; rs = 0.90 for hard-of-hearing). These results collectively indicate that the identification of key bridge symptoms and the overall network structure is robust across different EBIC γ.

In the network of hearing students, the symptom with the highest bridge expected influence (bridge EI) was C8 (“I thought that my parents wished I had never been born”, dimension of emotional abuse). Excluding oneself, this node showed positive associations with NSSI symptoms, and was most strongly connected to N1 (“Punching self, thereby causing a bruise”) (partial r = 0.12), suggesting that negative parental attitudes may act as a central bridge connecting trauma to NSSI behaviors.

In contrast, for hard-of-hearing students, the symptom with the highest bridge EI was D8 (“I was happy”), this node showed a negative association with trauma-related symptoms, and was most strongly linked to C13 (“People in my family looked out for each other”, dimension of emotional neglect) (partial r = 0.28), indicating that positive affect and supportive family relationships may play a protective role. Additionally, among hearing students, the average predictability across the network was 0.35, whereas among hard-of-hearing students, the average predictability was 0.54, suggesting that symptoms in the hard-of-hearing group were more strongly determined by their neighboring nodes within the network.

To further examine group differences, a Network Comparison Test (NCT) was conducted using the Holm–Bonferroni correction method. Results revealed significant differences in both network structure and global strength between the two groups (network invariance test: M = 0.52, p < 0.001; global strength invariance test: S = 2.20, p < 0.001), demonstrating that the connectivity patterns and overall association strength differed meaningfully between the two groups.

Directed acyclic graph

While mediation analysis confirmed that depressive symptoms serve as a significant mediator in the relationship between childhood trauma and NSSI, and network analysis revealed the symptom-level co-occurrence among the three constructs, both approaches are limited in inferring the directionality of associations. To address this limitation, we employed a Bayesian network learning approach to construct a directed acyclic graph. This method estimates probabilistic directional links among symptoms based on conditional dependencies, but it does not establish definitive temporal causality in cross-sectional data [27].

Notably, previously identified bridge symptoms occupied central positions in the DAGs, serving as key conduits for the propagation of risk across symptom clusters. Overall, the DAG results offer probabilistic directional insights that complement mediation and network analyses, helping to identify specific symptom chains that may underlie the progression from childhood trauma to NSSI through depressive symptoms.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships among childhood trauma, depressive symptoms, and NSSI in hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents. Hard-of-hearing adolescents reported more childhood trauma than their hearing peers, especially physical neglect. Depressive symptoms mediated the association between childhood trauma and NSSI, and this indirect effect was stronger in the hard-of-hearing group. Symptom-level analyses revealed differences in network structures: emotional abuse- and neglect-related items were central in the hearing adolescents’ network, whereas only an emotional neglect-related symptom occupied prominent positions in the hard-of-hearing network. Finally, the DAGs results suggest a directional pathway from childhood trauma to NSSI via depressive symptoms, providing insight into the mechanisms linking early adversity to self-injurious behavior.

The association of childhood trauma and NSSI among hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents

In this study, childhood trauma significantly influenced NSSI, highlighting the lasting psychological impact of early adverse experiences. Exposure to trauma during this stage may disrupt the development of secure attachments, fostering negative self-perceptions, emotional insecurity, and heightened vulnerability to NSSI [39]. Prior evidence suggests that adolescents may engage in NSSI as a maladaptive strategy to communicate distress or elicit caregiver support [7]. In the context of early trauma, such behaviors may serve as a last-resort attempt to secure attention when normal communication channels are limited.

Notably, a substantial proportion of hard-of-hearing students in this study reported experiences of physical neglect, suggesting a lack of basic physical care and insufficient parental attention to their health and well-being during early life. Lack of fundamental support may undermine a child’s sense of safety and security [33]. Especially for hard-of-hearing adolescents, physical neglect may be compounded by communication barriers, increasing the likelihood that NSSI is used to express unmet needs. These findings underscore that a supportive childhood environment is crucial for adolescent psychological well-being [45].

The mediating role of depressive symptoms in the relationship between childhood trauma and NSSI among hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents

This study further confirmed that depressive symptoms mediate the relationship between childhood trauma and NSSI. This pathway may be explained by the development of negative internal representations, such as perceived rejection or hopelessness [20], which in turn increase emotional dysregulation and vulnerability to maladaptive behaviors. However, the specific mechanism remains to be explicitly validated in future research. From the perspective of the experiential avoidance model, adolescents may engage in NSSI to cope with overwhelming negative emotions [2]. Thus, childhood abuse not only causes direct harm but also depletes internal resources, fostering emotional dysregulation, depressive symptoms, and an increased risk of NSSI [6].

Importantly, the mediating effect of depressive symptoms between childhood trauma and NSSI was significantly stronger in hard-of-hearing adolescents compared to their hearing peers. We propose that this amplified effect reflects their heightened emotional vulnerability due to barriers in communication and language development. Compared to hearing peers, barriers in language development and interpersonal communication may hinder the identification and expression of negative emotions [41]. Consequently, hard-of-hearing adolescents may have fewer adaptive outlets to process distress, increasing reliance on maladaptive coping such as NSSI.

Additionally, they often face greater social isolation, peer rejection, and reduced parental support, which may intensify depressive symptoms following trauma [37]. From a stress-sensitization perspective, repeated adverse experiences may heighten emotional reactivity, further strengthening the indirect pathway from trauma to NSSI via depressive symptoms [34]. Although communicative and neurodevelopmental factors were not directly tested as moderators, these theoretical and contextual factors likely contribute to the stronger mediation observed in hard-of-hearing adolescents [16].

Symptom-level network structure and population differences in childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI

In the trauma–depression–NSSI network of hearing adolescents, the symptoms with the highest Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) were C8 (“I thought that my parents wished I had never been born”) and N1 (“Punching self”). From a clinical perspective, these high BEI nodes function as primary mediating hubs that facilitate the reciprocal transmission of risk across distinct symptom clusters. Specifically, emotional abuse serves as a potent bridge that translates familial trauma into self-harming behaviors. In East Asian families, where parental authority is emphasized, verbal discipline can easily escalate into humiliating remarks. A frequently heard phrase in Chinese parent–child conflicts, “I regret giving birth to you” [8], represents a severe form of emotional invalidation that directly triggers NSSI as a maladaptive coping mechanism. The high BEI suggests that interventions targeting node C8, such as family therapy aimed at reducing verbal hostility, may effectively reduce the risk of NSSI.

Notably, an apparent paradox offers a key insight. In hard-of-hearing adolescents, construct-level correlations showed that emotional neglect was not associated with depressive symptoms and NSSI. However, at the symptom-level, only a specific emotional neglect item (“People in my family looked out for each other”) emerged as a bridge node. This pattern highlights why network analysis is necessary. Construct-level aggregation may obscure localized pathways, while symptom-level analysis reveals which specific experiences actually drive risk transmission. For instance, perceived lack of mutual family care is a specific aspect of emotional neglect. It directly links to depressive feelings and self-harm vulnerability. This specific association would remain undetected if we relied solely on construct-level analyses. The elevated BEI of C13 further suggests that reduced perceived familial support plays a central role in linking childhood adversity to depressive symptoms in this group.

From a cultural perspective, children are expected to continue the family bloodline. However, children with disabilities may be perceived as less able to fulfill this role, increasing their risk of receiving less emotional investment from parents [14]. Furthermore, hard-of-hearing students in this study were more likely to come from families with multiple children, suggesting a “sibling competition” context in which parental attention may be disproportionately allocated to non-disabled children [22]. This reduced emotional availability may further intensify feelings of neglect and internalizing distress among hard-of-hearing adolescents.

Further analysis revealed a distinct divergence between groups. Both emotional abuse and neglect were central in the hearing adolescents’ network, whereas only emotional neglect remained central in the hard-of-hearing group. This pattern suggests that for hard-of-hearing adolescents, the absence of emotional support, rather than overt abuse, may represent the most salient developmental risk factor. In contrast, hearing adolescents may be more influenced by both harsh parenting and emotional unresponsiveness, potentially reflecting broader socialization practices that emphasize behavioral control [38]. Additionally, the robustness of these findings is supported by the high correlation stability (CS) coefficients (0.67), which exceed recommended thresholds. By prioritizing these high-BEI symptoms, interventions can more precisely target the most influential nodes within each group’s network, thereby improving the efficiency and effectiveness of mental health strategies.

The directional connection among symptoms

The Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) reveal potential directional dependencies among symptoms, providing a probabilistic framework for understanding how risk may propagate across systems. Across both groups, a consistent directional pattern emerged, with childhood trauma symptoms directing toward depressive symptoms, which in turn directed toward NSSI, highlighting depressive symptoms as a key intermediary in this progression. While these directed edges do not establish causal relationships in this cross-sectional design, they represent probabilistic pathways that may inform clinically relevant intervention targets [27].

For hearing adolescents, a prominent pathway was observed linking negative family-related trauma cognitions (e.g., C8 “parents wished I had never been born”) to depressive symptoms reflecting diminished future orientation (e.g., D5 “felt hopeful about future”), which in turn showed a directional association with NSSI (N1 “Cutting wrists”). This pathway was predominantly negative in direction (i.e., more adverse family appraisals were associated with lower hopefulness, which in turn was linked to higher NSSI), suggesting that maladaptive cognitive appraisals of family experiences may undermine hope and increase vulnerability to self-injurious behavior. This finding aligns with cognitive models of depression, in which hopelessness functions as a proximal risk factor for self-injurious behavior [30].

In hard-of-hearing adolescents, a key pathway involved family support and emotional well-being, with C13 (“family looked out for each other”) showing a directional association with D8 (“I was happy”), which in turn was negatively associated with NSSI (N4 “Carving skin”). This pattern suggests a protective pathway, whereby stronger perceived family support is linked to greater positive affect, which may reduce the likelihood of NSSI. From an emotion regulation perspective, positive affect may buffer against maladaptive coping behaviors, whereas reduced family support may heighten emotional vulnerability [31].

Taken together, these findings highlight distinct directional pathways across groups. While these directional relationships remain exploratory, they underscore the importance of targeting family-related cognitions and emotional processes when designing interventions to prevent NSSI, particularly in vulnerable populations.

Theoretical and practical insight

This study makes several incremental contributions to the existing literature on adolescent mental health. First, it provides a rare comparative perspective by examining the differential impact of trauma on hearing and hard-of-hearing adolescents within the same cultural context, thereby addressing an important gap in existing research. Second, by employing network analysis and DAGs, this study advances beyond simple correlation and identifies probabilistic pathways through which risk may propagate across symptoms. This framework highlights the role of family-related cognitions as transdiagnostic mechanisms linking trauma, depressive symptoms, and NSSI. Importantly, network analysis offers a unique advantage over traditional correlation approaches. While construct-level correlations capture global associations between broad variables, network analysis operates at the symptom level and reveals fine-grained, item-specific pathways.

From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the value of shifting from disorder-level diagnoses to symptom-level intervention targets. The high stability and robustness of our identified bridge symptoms suggest that these nodes are reliable targets for precision intervention. For instance, in school-based mental health screenings, identifying high-risk nodes like perceived parental rejection or neglect can help counselors intervene before symptoms escalate into chronic NSSI. At the same time, given the central mediating role of depressive symptoms, interventions should prioritize reducing depressive affect as a critical entry point to disrupt the pathway from childhood trauma to NSSI. Especially for hard-of-hearing adolescents, interventions should target family communication, emotional support, and specific depressive symptoms to disrupt the pathway from childhood trauma to NSSI.

Limitation and future direction

Although this study explored the relationship among childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI through mediation effect tests and directed acyclic graphs, and found the mediating role of depressive symptoms, the cross-sectional design limits the interpretation of the findings. Meanwhile, the directed acyclic graph merely provides important preliminary evidence for the relationship between three variables [27]. We encourage future research to use longitudinal designs to verify this finding. Moreover, the sample source in this study is relatively limited. We hope to expand the sample size in future research and include hard-of-hearing students from multiple provinces in China.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the necessity of examining symptom-level pathways linking childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI across diverse adolescent populations. Compared to hearing adolescents, hard-of-hearing adolescents are more likely to experience childhood trauma and emerge depressive symptoms and NSSI. By integrating mediation modeling, network analysis, and directed acyclic graph, the findings further highlight the distinct psychological mechanisms underlying these associations in hearing and hard-of-hearing populations. This study also highlights the necessity of developing targeted intervention strategies for different populations. Importantly, intervening at crucial symptom nodes can effectively cut off the pathway linking childhood trauma, depressive symptoms and NSSI.
