Developmental manganese exposure, but not concurrent lead, alters social processing selectively in male mice
Authors: Anna Maria Tartaglione, Oyku Dinckol, Giorgia Macchioni, Ettore Meccia, Grete Francesca Privitera, Byron Fuentes, Eleonora Parlanti, Anna Pino, Alfredo Pulvirenti, Laura Ricceri, Gemma Calamandrei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExposure to multiple environmental pollutants, including heavy metals, during fetal life and early childhood, represents a major public health concern. Although the effects of exposure to a single metal are well documented, the impact of combined exposure to two or more metals remains underexplored. We investigated the neurobehavioral effects and hippocampal transcriptome changes in mouse offspring whose mothers were exposed to lead (Pb) and manganese (Mn), individually and in combination, via drinking water from two weeks prior to conception until the end of lactation. Developmental Mn exposure selectively impaired social recognition in adult males, an effect that disappeared when exposure occurred in combination with Pb exposure. Interestingly, the antagonistic interaction between Pb and Mn was further supported by biomonitoring and transcriptomic data. The latter also revealed sex-dependent vulnerability, especially to Pb, with males exhibiting a greater number of deregulated genes than females.Subject areas: rodent behavior, environmental health, biological sciences, behavioral neuroscience

Introduction

Exposure to environmental pollutants, including heavy metals, especially during fetal life and early childhood, represents a major public health concern, as even low levels, particularly in the case of lead (Pb), can interfere with typical brain development trajectories, ultimately resulting in adverse neurobehavioral outcomes in children. Furthermore, widespread exposure to these pollutants imposes a significant societal burden, resulting in substantial economic losses. For instance, the annual global cost of Pb exposure due to its detrimental effects on children’s cognitive development is estimated at approximately $1.4 trillion.1,2 Notably, the developing brain is particularly vulnerable to the effects of environmental pollutants, as many of these pollutants have been shown to reach the fetus and infants through the placenta and breast milk, respectively.3 Moreover, detoxification systems involved in the xenobiotic metabolism, as well as the blood-brain barrier, are not yet fully mature, thus providing only limited defense against the penetration of pollutants into the developing brain. Specifically, Pb exposure has been associated with cognitive and behavioral impairments, including decreased IQ scores,4,5 as well as impairments in memory, learning,6,7 and executive functioning.8,9 Pb neurotoxicity is attributed to several mechanisms of action, including ion mimicry, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress.10 Such disturbances in brain development, along with those influenced by the broader exposome, can contribute to the onset of neuropsychiatric disorders, such as attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)11,12 and schizophrenia.12,13

Unlike ubiquitous toxic metals such as Pb, manganese (Mn) is an essential trace element required for a healthy diet. Mn plays a key role in several biological processes, including metabolism, bone formation, and antioxidant and immune responses.14 Notably, a non-linear “U-shaped” dose-response relationship has been reported for Mn, where both low and high Mn exposures are associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes,15,16 thereby highlighting the need to maintain Mn exposure within an optimal range to support brain development. Moreover, Mn occupational overexposure induces adverse health outcomes, including a neurological condition known as “manganism,” which resembles Parkinson disease,17,18,19 as well as cognitive impairments.18,19

While Pb neurotoxicity in children is well established (with a current blood Pb reference value of 3.5 μg/dL),20 even though no safe threshold exists, European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) did not define a reference value (i.e., tolerable upper intake level) for Mn-induced neurotoxicity due to insufficient data from human and animal studies.21 On the basis of the observed intake data of high Mn consumers in European populations, EFSA only established safe levels of intake of 8 mg/day for adults (≥18 years, including pregnant and lactating women) and a range of 2–7 mg/day for other population groups (from 4 months to <18 years of age).21 However, the application of these safe intake levels remains limited, as the threshold at which the risk of adverse effects starts to increase is not defined, an issue that is especially critical during sensitive periods of brain development. Moreover, although health effects of exposure to a single metal, an unlikely real-life exposure scenario, have been well documented, the effects of combined exposure to two or more metals have not yet been extensively investigated. While epidemiological studies investigating the joint and interactive effects of metal mixtures, including Pb and Mn, are increasing, their findings on neurodevelopmental outcomes are inconsistent.22,23,24,25 Additionally, few existing animal studies have focused on early postnatal exposure to a Pb and Mn mixture, and only one considered exposure from conception until weaning.26 In the present study, we investigated the neurobehavioral effects and hippocampal transcriptome changes in mouse offspring developmentally exposed to Pb and Mn, both individually and in combination. To this end, female mice were exposed to environmentally relevant low levels of Pb, Mn, or their mixture during pre-conception, gestation, and lactation.

Our main finding revealed a selective impairment in social recognition in adult male mice developmentally exposed to Mn, an effect that disappeared when exposure occurred in combination with Pb. Sex-dependent vulnerability to metal exposure, particularly to Pb, was also supported by hippocampal transcriptomic profiles, which revealed a greater number of deregulated genes in males than in females.

Results

Developmental exposure to Pb and Mn, alone and in combination, differentially affects early motor and social abilities

Prenatal metal exposure did not affect the litter size, sex ratio, or mean body weight at birth, whereas developmental Pb exposure increased maternal active nursing during the first postnatal week, without affecting passive nursing or other recorded behaviors (see Figure S1). To determine the effects of exposure to Pb, Mn, or their combination on motor development and early socio-communicative abilities, we assessed offspring at postnatal day 8 (PND 8) for spontaneous motor activity and ultrasonic vocalizations (USVs) and the recognition of familiar olfactory cue at PND 11 after a brief isolation of mother from siblings. At PND 8, exposure to Pb, Mn, or their combination selectively affected locomotor responses. During the first week of postnatal life, the motor capabilities of altricial rodents develop through progressive maturation of quadrupedal locomotion that gradually replaces pivoting behavior, an immature locomotor activity pattern considered as the rodent counterpart of the human crawling. Specifically, we found that pups exposed to Mn alone exhibited lower levels of walking (overall group effect: H(3) = 8.885, p = 0.0309; Mn vs. Pb, p < 0.05, r = 0.44; Figure 1A), whereas Pb + Mn co-exposure affected pivoting behavior in a sex-dependent manner (treatment × sex interaction, F [3, 36] = 2.540, p = 0.0717; inset, Figure 1B), with Pb + Mn-exposed female pups spending more time pivoting than both Pb + Mn-exposed male pups (p < 0.01, Cohen’s d = 1.60) and control (Ctrl) females (p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 1.22). Most other motor patterns (see Figure S2) as well as USVs emitted during the 3-min test were not significantly affected by Pb, Mn, or their co-exposure (main effect of treatment: F [3, 37] = 0.899, p = 0.4509; Figure 1C). To assess the potential detrimental effects of metal exposure on early socio-communicative abilities, the pups were subjected to the Homing test at PND 11, assessing recognition of a familiar olfactory cue (i.e., sawdust from the home cage, referred to as the “home bedding area”). The latency to reach the home bedding area as well as the time spent in this area, together with simultaneous USV recordings, provide a sensitive index of nest-seeking behavior and early discrimination of socially salient cues. Pb, Mn, and Pb + Mn groups exhibited latencies to enter the home bedding area comparable to those of the Ctrl group (overall group effect: H (3) = 4.607, p = 0.2030) and emitted similar number of USVs during the test (main effect of treatment: F [3, 21] = 1.190, p = 0.3377). However, Pb-exposed mice failed to show a significant preference for the home bedding area, as the percentage of time spent there did not significantly differ from the chance level of 20% (Pb: t(11) = 2.033 p = 0.069). In contrast, both Mn- and Mn + Pb-exposed mice showed a significant preference for this area, although to a lesser extent than Ctrl mice (Mn: t(15) = 3.176, p = 0.0063; Pb + Mn: t(11) = 2.841, p = 0.0161; Ctrl: t(9) = 6.948, p < 0.0001) (Figures 1D–1F). At PND 23 and in adulthood, no effects of the exposure to Pb, Mn, or their combination were observed on the locomotor activity or anxiety-like behavior in the open-field test (see Figures S3 and S4).

Exposure to Pb or Mn alone, but not in combination, impairs novel object and social recognition, respectively, in male mice

To assess the impact of metal exposure on episodic-like declarative memory, we tested animals in the novel object recognition (NOR) test and social recognition test (SR), which exploit the innate tendency of rodents to explore novelty. NOR allows for the evaluation of recognition memory by comparing the exploration time of a novel object versus a familiar one (previously explored). Pb males exhibited a lower recognition index than Ctrl males (treatment × sex interaction, F [3, 46] = 3.450, p = 0.0240; Pb males vs. Ctrl males: p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 1.24; Figure 2A], suggesting poorer cognitive performance, an effect that was no longer evident in Pb + Mn mice.

To investigate whether metal exposure interfered with social memory recognition, the animals were subjected to the SR, which consisted of five consecutive sessions. During the first four sessions (S1–S4), they were exposed to the same familiar same-sex partner, followed by the introduction of a novel partner in the final session (S5). As the analysis revealed a main effect of sex (F [1, 41] = 9.815, p < 0.0001), the male and female mice were analyzed separately. In males, the treatment affected the response to repeated encounter with the same social partners throughout S1–S4 (main effect of treatment: F [3, 42] = 7.042, p = 0.0006), with Mn males exhibiting the highest levels of sniffing compared with all other groups (p < 0.01 vs. Ctrl, Cohen’s d = 1.90; p < 0.05 vs. Pb, Cohen’s d = 1.16; p < 0.01 vs. Pb + Mn, Cohen’s d = 1.46; Figure 2B). All treatment groups started the test (S1) with similar interaction rates. However, from S2 to S4, Mn male mice showed a disrupted habituation profile to the social cue (treatment × session interaction, F (12, [168] = 1.836, p = 0.0460), spending a similar amount of time interacting with the same partner across S2–S4. At S5, as expected, in the Ctrl, Pb, and co-exposure groups, the presentation of a novel partner elicited greater social interest than of the familiar one (S4 vs. S5: p < 0.05 for Ctrl group, p < 0.01 for Pb and co-exposure groups; Figure 2C). By contrast, the response of Mn-only mice did not increase significantly between S4 and S5 (S4 vs. S5: p = n.s.). Among females, all treatment groups exhibited the expected behavioral response in the SR (Figures 2D and 2E), showing a decrease in social interaction with same-sex partner across the four repeated session (main effect of session: F [4, 172] = 29.885, p < 0.0001; S1 vs. S4: p < 0.05; Figure 2E) and an increased interest toward the novel partner in S5 (p < 0.05 vs. S4). To exclude the possibility that the abnormal social recognition could be attributed to olfactory deficits, the male mice were assessed in the olfactory habituation/dishabituation test. The results evidenced similar patterns of habituation (Figure S5, main effect of trials: F [2, 66] = 42.165, p < 0.0001) among different exposure conditions for both non-social and social odors, with a significant preference for the latter (main effect of odors: F [4, 132] = 26.47, p < 0.0001; water, vanilla, almond vs. same- and opposite-sex odors: p < 0.01 for each pairwise comparison), indicating that the deficit observed in Mn-exposed males was specific to social interaction processing.

Hippocampal transcriptomic profiles

Exposure to Pb, Mn, and their combination induced a higher number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the hippocampus of males than in that of the females at 2 months of age (Figure 4). Specifically, in males, Pb exposure resulted in 357 DEGs versus Ctrl (75% upregulated) and 127 DEGs (38.5% upregulated) in females. Exposure to Mn alone resulted in 170 DEGs (50% upregulated) in males and 145 DEGs (51% upregulated) in females, while combined Pb + Mn exposure led to 161 DEGs (42% upregulated) in males and 114 DEGs (51% upregulated) in females. Interestingly, co-exposure to Pb and Mn resulted in a lower number of DEGs in both sexes compared with exposure to each metal individually, with all three exposure conditions sharing only a small number of DEGs (14 in males, and 12 in females), and only one DEG being common to both sexes.

These analyses confirmed a counterintuitive lack of the additive effect of Pb and Mn co-exposure, also evidenced in behavioral alterations. We also investigated DEG functions by using apposite databases, reporting, when available, the brain-related phenotypes associated with the orthologues of these genes in other species. Notably, the 700001K19Rik gene was downregulated under all three exposure conditions (Pb, Mn, and Pb + Mn) compared with Ctrl mice. Moreover, Mndal was downregulated in both Mn and Pb males relative to Ctrl mice. Smyd1 was downregulated in Mn-exposed males compared with both Ctrl and Pb-exposed mice. In contrast, D830030K20Rik and Gm3636 were upregulated in Mn-exposed males compared with both Ctrl and Mn + Pb-exposed mice.

We also performed a targeted analysis of genes involved in synaptic functions, using SynGO.27 The analysis of gene enrichment in synapse-related cellular components did not reveal statistically significant results across all experimental groups, except for Pb-exposed males. Notably, components such as postsynaptic ribosome and presynaptic ribosome were significantly enriched in the Pb-exposed males (Figure S6). Interestingly, none of these enrichment patterns were observed in the males exposed to Mn + Pb or in the females exposed to Pb alone.

Moreover, considering the link between Pb and Mn exposure and neuropsychiatric disorders such as ADHD and schizophrenia, we searched the GWAS Catalog for associations involving the human orthologues of the DEGs. The number of genes annotated for each experimental group is reported in Table S1. Genes associated with schizophrenia were those more represented in both sexes. Interestingly, one of these genes (Cwc22) was significantly downregulated in the females exposed to Pb and to Pb + Mn, as shown in the volcano plots (Figure 4B), and it is associated with both disorders.

Hippocampal expression of key metal transporters, including SLC11A2 (Dmt1), SLC39A8 (Zip8), SLC39A14 (Zip14), and SLC30A10 (ZnT10), was also evaluated. The analysis revealed an upregulation of SLC39A8, an Mn/Zn influx transporter, in the male mice exposed to either Mn (logFC = 0.3, p = 0.04 vs. Pb + Mn males) or Pb alone (logFC = 0.38, p = 0.01 vs. Pb + Mn males; logFC = 0.3, p = 0.04 vs. Ctrl males; logFC = 0.38, p = 0.01 vs. Pb females). Furthermore, downregulation of SLC39A14, a related metal cation transporter, was observed exclusively in the males exposed to Pb alone (logFC = −0.31, p = 0.01 vs. Pb + Mn males; logFC = −0.39, p = 0.002 vs. Ctrl males; logFC = −0.28, p = 0.02 vs. Pb females). These transcriptional data suggest complex interactions between Mn and Pb transport that warrants further investigation in future studies.

PCR validation of Mup3 and Smyd1 expression

Because transcriptomic analysis indicated a sex-dependent difference in Mup3 expression between the Mn-exposed males and females, PCR validation was performed, including three groups: Ctrl males, Mn-exposed males, and Mn-exposed females. The analysis of Mup3 expression did not reveal any statistically significant differences among the groups (F [2, 13] = 1.877, p = 0.1922, data not shown). Conversely, transcriptomic analysis showed that Smyd1 was downregulated in Mn-exposed males compared with both Ctrl and Pb-exposed males. Therefore, PCR validation was performed considering these three groups. The analysis of Smyd1 expression revealed a statistically significant difference between Mn-exposed and Ctrl males (p < 0.05 after post hoc comparison; F[2,15] = 3.648, p = 0.0512, data not shown).

Discussion

Preclinical research investigating combined exposure to Pb and Mn has so far focused on adults,28,29,30,31,32 as opposed to the far more susceptible neonates.26 Here, we established a developmental co-exposure model that reproduces realistic human exposure scenarios, achieving internal doses within biologically and environmentally relevant ranges. Regarding Pb, although blood lead levels (BLLs) were relatively high during the exposure phase (PND 2: 163 μg/L; PND 18: 332 μg/L), they markedly decreased thereafter, reaching 2.2 μg/L at both 2 and 10 months of age. Following the phase-out of leaded gasoline, reported BLLs in European and US populations have declined to approximately 20 μg/L.33,34 However, children living in low- and middle-income countries still exhibit elevated BLLs, ranging from 16 to 90 μg/L.35 Compared with these values, Pb concentrations measured in our developing animals at PND 2 and PND 18 were approximately 2- to 3-fold higher, representing an environmentally relevant upper exposure range. Notably, dams exhibited BLLs of 39 μg/L one week after treatment cessation, further supporting the environmental relevance of our exposure paradigm.

For Mn, typical blood concentrations in humans range from 4 to 12 μg/L.36,37,38 However, cord blood Mn concentration can reach 30–60 μg/L, up to three times higher than maternal concentrations.39 Mn concentrations detected in our animals at PND 18 were roughly 2- to 3-fold higher than those reported in children, indicating that our internal doses approximate the upper range of environmentally relevant human exposures.

It should be noted that, in toxicological studies in animals, the administered doses are often higher than typical human exposure ones to compensate for the limited statistical power inherent to small sample sizes.40 Moreover, while human exposure is often chronic and prolonged, spanning years or decades, experimental exposures in animal models are necessarily shorter to control developmental timing and capture specific windows of susceptibility.41,42

According to preclinical data on rodents exposed starting by post-weaning age28,29,30,31,32 and epidemiological evidence,19,43,44,45 we hypothesized that the combined developmental exposure to Pb and Mn could amplify the effects observed following exposure to each metal alone or potentially induce novel effects that are evident only with combined exposure to the two metals. Surprisingly, we did not observe additive or synergistic effects; instead, combined exposure to Mn and Pb mostly produced antagonistic effects, evidenced in both behavioral phenotype and hippocampal transcriptomic profile.

The single adverse effect observed in the animals exposed to both Pb and Mn was an increase in pivoting (an immature motor pattern) in neonatal female mice, which occurred at the expense of walking, a more mature locomotor response. This motor profile was also observed in the female mice exposed to Mn alone, making it more plausible that the effect was primarily driven by Mn exposure whose detrimental impact on motor functions is well documented.26,46 However, at the juvenile/adult stage, we observed no significant effects of Mn exposure, either alone or in combination with Pb, on locomotor activity. It is important to note that during the neonatal stage, when we documented motor alterations, the mice were still being exposed to metals through maternal milk. Additionally, Mn may induce subtle neurotoxic effects that are not readily detectable through assessments of gross motor functions.47,48

Our findings on the short- and long-term effects of Pb exposure alone, including mild impairments in olfactory and object recognition memory, are consistent with the broad body of literature identifying the cognitive domain as the primary target of developmental Pb neurotoxicity in both laboratory rodents49,50,51 and children.52,53,54,55 Likewise, a growing body of evidence indicates that early-life exposure to Mn adversely affects cognitive functions such as memory and executive performance.56,57,58 Interestingly, alterations in object recognition memory were not evident in the animals exposed to Mn alone or in combination with Pb, consistent with the previously reported antagonistic effects of these two metals on spatial memory.26

The most notable finding is the sex-specific effect of Mn exposure on social recognition: Mn-exposed males, but not females, exhibited persistently high levels of social investigation toward the same partner across repeated session, without the expected habituation. Additionally, they could not discriminate between the familiar and the novel partner, spending a comparable amount of time exploring each. These data suggest an impairment in social novelty recognition that was not evident under Pb and Mn co-exposure. While Mn-exposed males clearly differed from all other groups during habituation sessions with the familiar partner, a potential “ceiling” effect cannot be ruled out, as the levels of social exploration exhibited by Mn-exposed male mice were so high that further increases in response to social novelty were not detectable. However, deficits in social novelty could emerge more clearly through alternative paradigms, such as the three-chamber test, in which a familiar and a novel partner are simultaneously presented.59 The persistent investigation of an increasingly familiar stimulus, indicative of reduced behavioral flexibility, appears to represent the core social abnormality in Mn-exposed males. Behavioral flexibility, together with other components of executive functions, not assessed in this study, may help better define the behavioral domains targeted by Mn exposure in male mice.

Consistent with the behavioral alterations observed in our study, Golub et al.60 reported that male monkeys supplemented with Mn during the first four months of life exhibited increased impulsivity and more affiliative clinging behaviors during social interactions than controls. Furthermore, epidemiological studies have reported a significant association between prenatal Mn levels and behavioral disinhibition, as well as an increased risk of ADHD in children.16,58,61,62,63

Overall, our behavioral data indicate that developmental exposure to Pb and Mn alone, but not in combination, induces adverse behavioral outcomes, particularly in males. The greater vulnerability of males to the developmental exposure to Pb and Mn, especially on cognitive functions, is well documented.64,65 However, at the doses used in our study, we were unable to demonstrate the additive or synergistic effect of combined exposure on the behavioral responses assessed. Notably, the marked effects of single metal exposure in males, as well as the antagonistic effects of combined exposure, were also evident at molecular levels. In particular, exposure to a single metal led to the differential expression of a significantly higher number of genes in males than in females, especially in the Pb-exposed groups. We also observed significant enrichment of two synapse-related components, especially presynaptic and postsynaptic ribosomes, in Pb-exposed males, consistent with the well-documented effects of Pb on the synaptic structure and function.66,67 These Pb-associated gene expression changes were no longer detectable under co-exposure to Pb + Mn.

Among the DEGs with annotated functions identified in Mn-exposed males, both Mndal and Smyd1 are particularly noteworthy due to their involvement in gait and locomotor activity, as compared with Ctrl mice (https://www.mousephenotype.org/data/genes/MGI:3780953).

Of note, Smyd1 was also downregulated in Mn-exposed male mice compared with those exposed to Pb. This finding is particularly interesting considering that Mn and Pb have opposite effects on the locomotor activity. Indeed, evidence indicates that Pb exposure increases the locomotor activity,68,69 whereas Mn exposure decreases it,26,46 a pattern highlighted in our study only at the neonatal stage. The Smyd1 (SET and MYND 1 structural domains) gene is a member of the SMYD protein family, which has a methyl-transferase function, thereby playing a key role in gene expression regulation in many biological processes, including muscle formation and cancer, and the regulation of inflammation and mitochondrial energetics.70,71,72,73 By contrast, the function of Smyd1 in the brain remains largely unexplored. More recently, loss of Smyd1 has been associated with the significant deregulation of genes typically associated with neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer, Parkinson, and Huntington diseases.74 Moreover, Smyd1 expression was found to be decreased in the prefrontal cortex of both human smokers and nicotine-exposed rats. Specifically, this reduction appeared to be sex and diet dependent, with nicotine-exposed female rats exhibiting reduced Smyd1 expression only when fed a high-fat diet.75 This finding suggests that females may be less vulnerable than males, consistent with our results in which Smyd1 downregulation was observed in Mn-exposed males but not in females.

Regarding the sex-dependent effects of Mn exposure, mostly DEGs in males, compared with females, were downregulated. Among these, Mup3 (major urinary proteins 3), previously detected in mouse hippocampus,76 is particularly noteworthy due to its role in pheromonal communication77,78 and its potential link with the abnormal profile in social investigation of same-sex conspecific observed in Mn-exposed males. Although intriguing, PCR validation showed that this gene was expressed at very low levels in mice across all experimental groups, with Mn-exposed males showing levels comparable to those of Ctrl mice. These findings suggest that, although Mup3 might have potential relevance, other molecular mechanisms and brain regions are likely to play a predominant role in driving the behavioral alterations associated with Mn exposure under our experimental conditions. In particular, dopaminergic pathways, such as those involving striatum and prefrontal cortex, should be considered to investigate Mn neurotoxicity mechanisms that affect social responsiveness.79,80 A similar lack of synergistic effects from co-exposure to metals, as observed in our study and by Betharia and Maher,26 has also been reported for other metal mixtures. Co-exposure to arsenic (As) and Mn has been shown to mitigate neurobehavioral and biochemical alterations observed in mice exposed to As alone.81 Co-exposure to As and Pb was found to counteract the memory impairment induced by As alone and reduce the accumulation of each metal in the brain.82 Furthermore, some epidemiological studies have also failed to report any significant joint/synergistic effect of Mn and Pb co-exposure on infant development.16,83

Antagonist interaction between Pb and Mn was also supported by our biomonitoring data revealing tissue-specific differences in the accumulation of these two metals at 2 months of age. Indeed, the animals developmentally exposed to Pb alone exhibited significantly higher Pb concentrations in both brain and bone (10-fold increase in the bone compared with brain) than those co-exposed to both Pb and Mn. Conversely, the animals exposed only to Mn significantly accumulated more Mn in bone than those co-exposed to Pb and Mn. However, Mn levels in the brain at 2 months did not differ significantly among the exposed groups, although a trend toward higher levels relative to both Pb and Pb + Mn groups was observed. Our findings are consistent with those of Betharia and Maher (2012) who reported that Mn levels in the hippocampus and striatum were not significantly higher than those observed in Ctrl mice already five days after the exposure ended,26 further supporting the notion that brain Mn levels tend to normalize relatively quickly once exposure ceases. Another interesting finding of our work was the distribution pattern of Mn in the bone at both 2 and 10 months of age in Pb- and Pb + Mn-exposed animals. Indeed, Pb exposure, alone or in combination, led to a reduced Mn accumulation in the bone, falling below the physiological levels detected in the Ctrl group. Overall, bone Mn levels are consistent with findings from a detailed multidose rat study, which showed that Mn concentrations in the blood, brain, and bone significantly decline with age, even under conditions of elevated Mn exposure.84 This persistent decline limits the potential for true Mn accumulation (i.e., a net increase in Mn levels). Notably, bone Mn may serve as a more sensitive biomarker of recent Mn exposure than either blood or brain levels.84,85 An unexpected and intriguing aspect of our findings is the absence of detectable Mn in the bones of the Pb + Mn-exposed group, despite persistent Pb bone levels in the same animals. This may be explained by a greater efficiency of Pb, relative to Mn, in substituting for Ca2+ within the hydroxyapatite mineral matrix. If, as suggested for chronic Mn exposure,85 bone tissue also acts as primary long-term Mn body storage and Mn source after Mn developmental exposure, this lack of bone Mn could explain the absence of Mn-related effects in the Pb + Mn group whose Mn availability from bone stores appears comparable to that of Ctrl. Additionally, co-exposure may impair the transport of Mn and Pb across both intestinal and blood-brain barriers, which could result in reduced tissue accumulation, particularly in the bone.34

Further investigations are clearly needed to elucidate the nature of enhanced social response in Mn-exposed males, as this change may be attributable to the deficits in behavioral flexibility and response inhibition, which are characteristics of ADHD. Dysfunction of the dopaminergic system, recognized as a primary target of Mn exposure,86,87 is clearly implicated in these alterations. Notably, there is strong evidence that Mn accumulates in the basal ganglia nuclei, such as the striatum and globus pallidus, which have been consistently identified by neuroimaging studies as being functionally abnormal in individuals with ADHD.88,89,90,91 Further studies are required to establish whether and how concurrent developmental exposure to Pb competes with Mn in the perturbation of dopaminergic pathways, especially given the lack of a synergistic effect of Pb and Mn on social processing alterations.

Limitations of the study

In the context of the longitudinal and comprehensive behavioral assessment of Pb, Mn, and their combined exposure conducted in this study, we did not evaluate executive functions known to be affected by both Pb and Mn exposure. Assessing this cognitive domain could have provided further insight into the nature of the social processing deficits observed in Mn-exposed male mice.

Another limitation of our study was the lack of transcriptomic analyses in brain regions beyond the hippocampus, such as the basal ganglia and prefrontal cortex, which are critically involved in dopamine-mediated behaviors, including motor control and executive functioning. Additionally, we did not explore potential epigenetic mechanisms that may underlie the neurodevelopmental toxicity of Mn, Pb, and their co-exposure. Lastly, we assessed the behavioral and transcriptomic changes associated with developmental metal exposure in different cohorts, thereby limiting the possibility of performing correlational analyses between these variables. However, because the animals used for behavioral testing and RNA-seq analysis were siblings (i.e., derived from the same litter), we can reasonably assume that those subjected to behavioral testing share similar hippocampal transcriptomic profiles.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Sixty adult female outbred CD1 Swiss mice (Charles River, Italy) were randomly assigned to treatment groups (0, 100 mg/L Pb acetate, 2 g/L Mn, and 100 mg/L Pb - 2 g/L Mn mixture solutions) via drinking water two weeks before mating throughout gestation and lactation until offspring weaning [postnatal day (PND) 21]. After mating, females were inspected twice per day for a presence of a vaginal plug and in case of presence, that day marked as gestational day 0.5 (GD 0.5). On PND 2 (birth PND 0), litters were culled to 8 pups (4 males and 4 females, when possible). At weaning (PND 21), mice were housed with same-sex siblings. All animals were kept in standardized conditions (temperature 20 ± 2°C; humidity 60–70%) with access to ad libitum food and water under a reversed 12 h–12 h dark/light cycle (lights on from 7:00 p.m. till 7:00 a.m.). Total number of litters generated for each treatment group was: Ctrl = 11; Pb = 13; Mn = 16; Pb+Mn = 14. All experimental procedures were carried out under the European and Italian legislation (2010/63/EU, Dl 26/2014) and were approved by the local Ethical Committee and the Italian Ministry of Health (n. 474/2021-PR).

Method details

Metal exposures

Lead acetate (Pb(Ac)2·3H2O) and Manganese(II) chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2.4H2O) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Test solutions for animal treatment were prepared in soft tap water, slightly acidified (pH 4.(6) with acetic acid, and containing as follows: no metal salt (Ctrl), Pb acetate at a concentration of 100 mg/L (Pb only), Mn(II) chloride tetra hydrate at a concentration of 7.26 g/L (containing net 2 g/L Mn as element; Mn only) or a mixture of same concentrations of Pb acetate and Mn(II) chloride tetrahydrate (co-exposure). Acidified water ensured complete dissolution of the metal salt; single metal and mixture solutions intake levels were comparable to plain tap water. New solutions were administered every 3–4 days.

Maternal behavior

From PND 1 to PND 7, maternal behavior was daily observed for three 60 min (min) sessions, distributed across the dark phase of the diurnal cycle (at 9:00 a.m., 1:00 p.m. and 6:00 p.m.), by instantaneous sampling at an interval of 4 min (45 samples per day for each dam). The experimenter scored maternal care in the home cage without moving the cage from the mouse rack. After having assessed whether the dam was inside or outside the nest, the dam behavior was recorded, considering: active nursing (kyphosis: high-crouched nursing posture by dam’s body arched over the pups; licking and grooming: dam licking and grooming the pups mainly directed toward the anogenital region); passive nursing (supine nursing: dam lying on her side to provide maximum space for the pups; blanket nursing: dam lying flat on the pups); locomotion (dam mobile outside the nest); resting (dam lying motionless outside of nest); foraging (dam feeding); self-grooming (dam wiping, licking, combing or scratching any part of own body).

Behavioral testing

Behavioral testing was performed following a timeline, as showed in Figures 1 and 2. One male and one female from each litter underwent behavioral testing using different couple of subjects at each developmental stage (neonatal, juvenile, and adult).

Spontaneous motor activity and ultrasonic vocalization test (PND 8)

On PND 8, pups were removed from the litter and individually placed in a glass container (height: 7.5 cm; diameter: 14 cm), where they were recorded for 3 min to assess spontaneous movements and ultrasonic vocalizations as in.50 The following behavioral parameters using The Observer XT software (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands) were scored: immobility (no visible movement of the body, all four paws on container’s floor), head rising (a single rising of the head up and forward), wall climbing (forelimb placing movements on the container’s wall), nose probing (various orientation movements of the nose), pivoting (locomotor activity involving only use of forelimbs), and walking (general translocation of the body by use of four paws). Number of calls were analyzed using DeepSqueak.94

For individual identification within the litter, after behavioral testing on PND 8, pups were permanently tattooed in the paw (Ketchum permanent Tattoo Inks green paste, Ketchum Manufacturing Inc., Brockville ON Canada).

Homing test (PND 11)

In each litter, on PND 11, the same male and female pups tested on PND 8 were separated from the dam and kept for 30 min in an incubator (Elmed Ginevri 0 GB 1000, Italy) at 28 ± 1°C. Individual pups were then transferred to a polycarbonate cage (height × width × length: 15 × 21 × 42 cm) with one-fifth of the floor covered with sawdust from home cage (not cleaned in the previous five days; named as home bedding, 20%) and the rest with clean sawdust (named as clean bedding). Pups were gently placed in the middle of the area and video recorded for 3 min. Video files were subsequently analyzed using Observer XT software (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands). Latency to and time spent in the home bedding area were recorded. Values of percentage of time spent in the home bedding area greater than 20% (chance level) indicate the establishment of a nest preference.

Open field test (PND 23)

Each mouse was randomly placed in a corner of the open field (OPF) apparatus (40 × 40 × 40 cm) and video recorded for 15 min under dim light (3 lux), freely to explore the arena. The following behavioral parameters were assessed using ANY-maze software (Stoelting Co., Germany): distance traveled, time spent in central area, and latency to the first entry into central area.

Open field and Novel Object Recognition test (3 months)

Animals were tested in the OPF apparatus (40 × 40 × 40 cm) for three consecutive sessions, habituation, training, and testing. In the habituation session, mice were allowed to explore the empty apparatus for 30 min. This was followed by a 10-min training session in which mice explored two identical objects placed in opposite corners of the apparatus. After a 2- hour inter-trial interval, a 10-min test session was conducted, during which one familiar object from the training session and one novel object were presented.

Mouse-sized LEGO towers and cell culture flasks were used as objects, alternated between “familiar” and “novel” objects across animals, with their location counterbalanced. The habituation session also functions as an OPF test, and the same measures were taken as in PND 23. During both training and test sessions, the time spent in each object quadrant was recorded. The recognition index, defined as the ratio of time spent in the novel object quadrant to the total time spent in both object quadrants, was calculated as a measure of recognition memory.

Social recognition test (5 months)

Tests were performed in a cage with clean sawdust as five consecutive sessions with 15 min inter trial intervals. Each test animal was habituated to the test cage (polycarbonate cage, 33 cm × 15 cm × 13 cm) with a clean, empty wire cage (10 cm height, 10 cm bottom diameter, bars spaced 0.8 cm apart) inside for 30 min before the testing session. An unfamiliar mouse (as a stimulus partner), enclosed in the wire cage, was placed at one edge of the test cage and the test mouse was allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Using the same partner, this session was repeated three more times with 15 min intervals, and then a novel partner was introduced for 5 min.95,96 Tests were video recorded, and the total interaction time with the partners (including sniffing and rearing through the wire cage) was manually scored by a trained researcher with a silenced stopwatch. Animals used as social partners were same-sex, same-age CD1 subjects; before the experiments, these mice were habituated to the apparatus and the wire cage enclosure, 10 min per day for three consecutive days. Estrous cycle of female mice was checked 1-day prior to the experiment by vaginal smear staining,97 and only sexually non-receptive females, in metoestrous or dioestrous, were included in the experiment.

Olfactory habituation/dishabituation test (10 months)

Considering the data collected in social recognition test, only males were tested. Tests were performed in a polycarbonate cage (33 cm × 15 cm × 13 cm) with clean sawdust, and the test animal was exposed to a cotton swab introduced through the cage lid. Each test animal was habituated in the test cage for 1-h prior to the experiment. The test consisted of sequential exposure to different non-social and social odors. Each odor was presented in three consecutive trials (t1, t2, and t3) of 2 min each. Scented cotton swabs were prepared daily before the experiments, by soaking the swabs’ top to neutral and non-social odor solutions; water, vanilla extract solution (1:100), almond extract solution (1:100) or by repeatedly wiping the cotton swabs across the bottom of unfamiliar mouse (same-strain and same- or opposite sex) cage beddings98 and stored in sealed plastic bags during the day. The sequence of the odors was the following; water 1, water 2, water 3, vanilla 1, vanilla 2, vanilla 3, almond 1, almond 2, almond 3, same-sex 1, same-sex 2, same-sex 3, opposite sex 1, opposite sex 2, opposite sex 3. Olfactory responsiveness was evaluated by interaction time with the cotton swabs (sniffing and chewing) recorded by a trained researcher with a silenced stopwatch.

Sample collection

Samples for Pb and Mn biomonitoring were collected from offspring at PND 2, PND 18, 2 months, and 10 months of age, and one week after weaning for exposed dams. Blood samples were drawn in metal-free EDTA tubes (BD Royal Blue Blood Collection Tubes K2EDTA – (368381) and stored at +4°C, while whole brain samples were collected and stored at −80°C. Use of metal surgery tools and aluminum foil was minimized to prevent metal contamination during sampling.

Lead and manganese biomonitoring

For biomonitoring analyses, one male and one female per litter were included. As for blood, sample preparation and analysis were carried out using a method fully validated and accredited following the ISO/IEC 17025 guidance 213. As for brain, drying procedure was applied by using an oven at 80°C overnight, and a mineralization cycle performed via adding 3 mL of ultrapure HNO3 on the hotplate at 80 ± 3°C for 3 h. Analytical quality was assured by the repeated analysis of Certified Reference Materials (CRMs) Seronorm Trace Elements Blood (SERO AS, Billingstad, Norway) and bovine liver 185 R (Institute for Reference Materials and Measurements, IRMM, Geel, Belgium) were used for brain analysis; they have treated accordingly with the digestion and dilution procedures as samples. All samples were analyzed by using iCAP Q ICP mass spectrometry (Thermofischer, Bremen, Germany).

Transcriptomic analysis of hippocampus

For transcriptomic analyses, one male and one female from six litters per group (unhandled siblings of the pups used for behavioral testing) were euthanized at 2 months of age, and hippocampal samples were collected. To isolate RNA, frozen hippocampal tissues were disrupted in lysis buffer with a Qiagen Mixer Mill homogenizer (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) under mechanical shaking for 3 min at 20 Hz. Total RNA was subsequently isolated from tissue lysates with the Qiagen All Prep DNA/RNA purification kit following the manufacturer instructions.

Next generation sequencing experiments were performed by Genomix4Life S.R.L. (Baronissi, Salerno, Italy). RNA concentration in each sample was assayed with a Nanodrop One (Thermo Fisher) and its quality assessed with the TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies). Indexed libraries were prepared from 200 ng/ea purified RNA with Illumina Stranded mRNA Prep according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were quantified using the TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies) and and Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen Co.), then pooled such that each index-tagged sample was present in equimolar amounts, with final concentration of the pooled samples of 5 nM. The pooled samples were subject to cluster generation and sequencing using an Illumina Novaseq6000 System (Illumina) in a 2 × 100 paired-end format.

The raw sequence files generated (.fastq files) underwent quality control analysis using FastQC (http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/).

Raw fastq data have been trimmed to cut out adapter and delete low quality reads using TrimGalore(v. 0.6.9). After that, STAR(v. 2.7.10 b) aligner was employed to align the reads to the Mus musculus reference genome GRCm39 gencode release M34. After the alignment step, mapped reads have been count employing featureCounts(v.2.0.(6) software. Raw counts have been then normalized and analyzed employing R(v.4.4.(2) with packages limma and edgeR. Regarding the orthologue analyses, mouse genes have been associated with their Homo Sapiens orthologues employing HCOP [HUGO Gene Nomenclature Committee (HGNC) Comparison of Orthology Predictions]99 and Mouse Genome Information (MGI).100 We used the identified orthologues as references and employed GWAS Catalog to explore potential associations with ADHD and/or Schizophrenia. Moreover, these orthologues have been inspected employing SynGo for synapse function and gene enrichment. Enrichment dotplot has been created employing the R package ggplot2. The volcano plot have been created employing ggplot2 using a p-value of 0.01 and a logFC of |1.5|, while Venn Diagram have been produced with VennDiagram(v.1.7.(3) package using a p-value of 0.05 and a logFC of |0.6|. Finally, the expression of key metal transporters was also evaluated (see supplemental information).

Quantitative PCR

For qPCR analysis, 500 ng of total RNA for each sample were reverse transcribed (ReverTra AceTM qPCR RTMaster Mix, Toyobo Co., Ltd.) and the resulting cDNA was subject to digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR System and reagents (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.). The genes of interest were amplified using custom designed PrimeTime Mini qPCR assays by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) and analyzed on the Bio-Rad QX200 droplet reader by amplitude multiplexing using the QuantaSoft Analysis Pro software. Sequences for primes and probes used are as follows. Mup3: Forward primer CAGGAGGACAATTCTATTCC; Probe (FAM) ATGAAGCTGCTGCTGCCG; Reverse primer CTAAAGTCAGTTCCAAACACA. Smyd1: Forward primer CTACATGAAACTCTACCACC; Probe (HEX) TGTGATGAGGGCAGGGCTTA; Reverse primer TCACCAGGAGAATAGCATAG. Rpl13 (reference gene): Forward primer AAGAACTTTAAGGCTTTTGC; Probe (HEX) CAATGCCCGACTCTTTGGCA; Reverse primer CTTTTCAACATCTTGCTCTG.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The number of animals used was the minimum necessary to obtain reliable behavioral data. The sample size has been calculated using G∗Power 3.1.9.7 software, setting test power 1-β = 0.8, effect size Cohen’s d=1.5 (large effect size), and type I error probability α = 0.05/number of useful comparisons, in a two-tailed Student’s t test for independent means.

Depending on the distribution of the response variable, either Kruskal-Wallis test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures (with litters as statistical units, and male and female subjects as replicates) with treatment as between factor and time interval/trial/session (when present) as within subject-factors were performed. Post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test after ANOVA (including effects at p < 0.1101,102) and Dunn’s test after Kruskal-Wallis. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. ∗, # and ∗∗, ##, €€ denote p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively. Potential outliers were detected by Grubbs’ method available in the GraphPad software. Statistical analyses and data visualization were carried out with GraphPad Prism.
