BaCas12a3 represents a new subtype of type V CRISPR effector with collateral activity toward tRNA
Authors: Xin Li, Chenwei Wu, Jinshan Guo, Ruyi Zheng, Jingwen Kong, Lei Du, Qunxin She, Shiqi Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe CRISPR–Cas12 family encompasses diverse RNA-guided nucleases with both DNA- and RNA-targeting subtypes. They can trigger antiviral activities through either direct elimination of invading nucleic acids or activating broad collateral cleavage to induce abortive infection. Here, we report a novel type V CRISPR effector BaCas12a3 that causes growth inhibition through a unique tRNA-cleavage mechanism. Plasmid interference and western blot assays showed that BaCas12a3 induces host growth arrest without DNA damage response, suggestive of the absence of double-strand DNA breaks. Indeed, biochemical characterization of the BaCas12a3–crRNA ribonucleoprotein unraveled that the effector is an RNA-activating nuclease that cleaves the 3′ terminal CCA of tRNAs. Cryo-EM structures of BaCas12a3 reveal a conserved bilobed architecture featuring a unique tRNA-loading domain (tRLD) adjacent to the RuvC catalytic center. Structural and mutagenesis analyses show that the tRLD domain, together with a zinc ribbon domain, form a gated substrate groove. Target RNA binding induces conformational changes that open the groove and expose the RuvC active site, enabling specific tRNA 3′ end cleavage while preventing other non-specific degradation. Our findings identify the tRLD domain aside the RuvC active site responsible for the tRNA recognition in BaCas12a3, expanding the functional diversity of CRISPR immunity.

Introduction

The CRISPR–Cas (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and CRISPR-associated proteins) system constitutes the prokaryotic adaptive immunity, enabling bacteria and archaea to neutralize viral invaders through RNA-guided nucleic acid cleavage [1, 2]. These antiviral systems are broadly classified into Class 1 systems, which employ multi-subunit protein effectors, and Class 2 systems, which utilize a single-unit protein effector [2–4]. Among the three known Class 2 types, Type V CRISPR–Cas systems, which use Cas12 family nucleases as effectors, have emerged as versatile tools for genome engineering and diagnostics due to their programmable DNA/RNA-targeting activities and collateral nuclease behaviors [5–10]. Evolutionarily related to TnpB transposases, Cas12 proteins share a conserved C-terminal RuvC endonuclease domain [5, 9, 11]. To date, >16 Type V subtypes (Cas12a–Cas12o) have been characterized, most of which display dual cleavage activities: cis-cleavage of target nucleic acids and trans-degradation (collateral cleavage) of non-target nucleic acids [7, 8, 12–16].

Most Type V effectors are classified as DNA-targeting nucleases [7, 8, 10]. They target double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) as well as single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), and induce target-specific cleavage as well as collateral nucleic acid cleavage [17–19]. Nevertheless, the characterization of Cas12a2 revealed unexpected functional plasticity within the Cas12 family. The Cas12a2 effector is an RNA-guided effector capable of inducing abortive infection—a defense mechanism in which cells undergo dormancy or death to limit viral spread [20–22]. Upon recognizing an RNA target, Cas12a2 unleashes promiscuous cleavage of single-stranded RNA (ssRNA), ssDNA, and dsDNA, a mechanism that is not only distinct from the collateral nucleic acid cleavage by all other Cas12 systems but also from the ssRNA-specific collateral activity of Type VI Cas13 enzymes [8, 20, 21, 23].

Structural comparisons across Cas12 subtypes reveal diversification in the N-terminal domains, responsible for guide RNA and target nucleic acid recognition, and the C-terminal domains, which underlie functional divergence. In dsDNA targeting subtypes (e.g. Cas12a, b, e, f, j, and m), various accessory domains (e.g. TNB/NUC, BH, zinc-finger, and TSL) facilitate dsDNA unwinding and substrate loading into the RuvC catalytic sites [18, 24–28]. In contrast, RNA-targeting variants such as Cas12a2 and Cas12g retain a zinc ribbon (ZR) domain crucial for nucleic acid accommodation [21, 29]. These structural variations underscore the evolutionary adaptability of the domains associated with the RuvC domain and suggest that further mechanistic diversity within the Cas12 family remains unexplored.

We further investigated the diversity of Cas12 systems by searching for Cas12 nucleases in various metagenomic datasets, and this led to the identification of a new clade, Cas12a3, which includes numerous sequences encoded by gut bacteria. One representative, Bacteroidales Cas12a3 (BaCas12a3), was characterized in this study. We demonstrate that this Cas12 subtype possesses unique structural and functional features, including a novel uncharacterized domain. By integrating cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM), phylogenetic analysis, and biochemical assays, we provide insights into the evolutionary divergence, substrate specificity, and antiviral mechanisms of BaCas12a3. These findings expand our understanding of the antiviral repertoire of CRISPR systems.

During preparation of this manuscript, Dmytrenko et al. reported parallel findings on a homologous effector, Ba1Cas12a3, demonstrating tRNA cleavage as the immune mechanism [30]. Our independently obtained results corroborate the core mechanism while providing additional insights into Cas12a3 classification, PFS recognition mechanisms, new domain function, and structural details. These findings establish Cas12a3 as a robust and functionally differentiated type V subtype.

Materials and methods

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of Cas12a3 nucleases

We performed the initial search using the full-length amino acid sequence of Acidaminococcus sp. Cas12a (AsCpf1, UniProt: U2UMQ6) as the query. We used the PSI-BLAST algorithm (Position-Specific Iterated BLAST) from the NCBI BLAST+ suite (v2.13.0) [31, 32], with the global ocean microbiome genome catalogue (GOMC) database [33], an E-value threshold of 0.05. From the initial PSI-BLAST output of 324 sequences, we filtered hits to retain 27 sequences with identity <30% in order to focus on divergent, uncharacterized homologs. These resulting hits were used as queries for a second round of iterative BLASTp searches against the NCBI non-redundant protein database to identify additional family members, using the same E-value threshold of 0.05. One of the hits corresponded to several hypothetical proteins with amino acid lengths ranging from 1099 to 1226. One of these hypothetical protein sequences was then used in another round of iterative BLASTp searches, which yielded additional homologs annotated as either hypothetical proteins or transposases. A total of 28 Cas12a3 candidate sequences were retained for phylogenetic analysis. Among these, BaCas12a3 was one such annotated transposase identified from a gut metagenome assembled genome of a Bacteroidales bacterium isolate RGIG7249 (GenBank: JAFYNK010000087). The query and output sequences are provided in Supplementary File S1.

For the CRISPR spacer analysis, we extracted the 525 CRISPR spacer sequences from 30 Cas12a3-encoding genomic loci identified in our bioinformatic screen. We used BLASTn to align these spacers against the Viruses nt (nt_viruses) database with an E–value threshold of 0.05 [31], and against the ICEberg database for mobile genetic elements (MGEs) [34]. We also used SpacePHARER (a modular toolkit for sensitive phage–host interaction identification using CRISPR spacers) for phage searching [35]. The spacer sequences are also provided in Supplementary File S1.

To localize the RuvC domain and its conserved residues, BaCas12a3 orthologs (Supplementary File S1) were aligned using Clustal Omega [36, 37], and the BaCas12a3 structure was predicted with AlphaFold3 [38]. A multiple sequence alignment of these orthologs and related transposases (Supplementary File S1) was generated using Clustal Omega [36, 37], followed by phylogenetic analysis performed with the Neighbor-Joining method in MEGA7 and TreeViewer (v2.2.0) [39–41], and the resulting tree in Fig. 1 was visualized and annotated using tvBOT [42].

Cas12a3 is a newly identified subtype within the type V CRISPR–Cas effectors. (A) Phylogenetic tree of Cas12 subtypes and their ancestral TnpB nucleases. Distinct subtypes are color-coded. Sequences used for tree construction are provided in Supplementary File S1. (B) Gene arrangements of two representative CRISPR–Cas12a3 systems.

Plasmid construction and mutagenesis

The Bacas12a3 gene was codon-optimized for Escherichia coli expression and synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). This optimized sequence was cloned into pCDFDuet-1 vector downstream of the first T7 promoter to generate pCDFDuet-Bacas12a3. To enable co-expression of the nuclease and its cognate crRNA, a minimal CRISPR array containing five identical spacer repeats was synthesized and inserted downstream of the second T7 promoter in the pCDFDuet-Bacas12a3 backbone to generate pCas12a3. In both constructs, the nuclease and the crRNA are expressed from independent T7 promoters. For the plasmid interference assays, reporter vectors were constructed in two steps. First, a gene encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP) was inserted into the pBAD plasmid, yielding pBAD-G. Subsequently, DNA fragments containing either a motif coding protospacer flanking site (PFS) alone (as a non-targeting control) or the PFS motif-coding sequence with target sequence or the PFS alone were cloned between the araBAD promoter and the GFP gene in pBAD-G, yielding plasmids pNTarget and pTarget, respectively. Following the same approach, the plasmids pTNoPfs (containing the target but lacking the PFS-coding sequence) and pTNoPr (containing the target but lacking its promoter) were also constructed. All plasmids used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table S1, and all primers are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Specific substitutions were introduced into the target plasmid using site-directed mutagenesis. Complementary primers containing the desired mutations (Supplementary Table S2) were used for whole-plasmid PCR (polymerase chain reaction) amplification with a high-fidelity DNA polymerase (Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). The PCR product was treated with DpnI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham) at 37°C for 1 h to digest the methylated template DNA, and then circularized using a recombinase assembly mix (ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd.). The reaction was transformed into chemically competent Escherichia coli DH5α cells. Transformants were selected on LB agar plates containing streptomycin and incubated overnight at 37°C. Individual colonies were cultured, and plasmids were extracted for verification by Sanger sequencing (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Plasmid interference assay

The plasmid interference assay was performed as described previously [20]. To evaluate the function of the BaCas12a3 effector complex, we conducted two parallel assays: the nuclease-target selection (tns) assay and a modified version termed the nuclease selection (ns) assay. Electrocompetent cells were prepared as follows. For strains expressing the nuclease, the plasmid pCas12a3 (encoding BaCas12a3 and crRNA) was chemically transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). The strain was cultured in LB medium containing 34 µg/ml streptomycin to an OD₆₀₀ of ~0.25, induced with 0.05 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG), and harvested at an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6–0.8 for electrocompetent cell preparation. For strains harboring the reporter plasmids, pTarget or pNTarget was separately transformed into BL21(DE3). These strains were grown in LB medium with 34 µg/ml kanamycin to an OD₆₀₀ of ~0.6–0.8 before harvesting for competent cell preparation.

For the tns selection assays, aliquots (50 µl) of the prepared electrocompetent cells carrying pCas12a3 were mixed with 100 ng of one of the following reporter plasmids: pTarget, pNTarget, pTNoPfs, or pTNoPr. Electroporation was performed using a 1-mm cuvette (Bio-Rad) with a Gene Pulser II system (Bio-Rad) (1800 V, 200 Ω, 25 µF). Immediately after the pulse, 920 µl of SOC recovery medium containing 0.05 mM IPTG was added. Cells were recovered for 60 min at 37°C with shaking (200 rpm), serially diluted, and plated on LB agar plates containing 0.05 mM IPTG, 34 µg/ml streptomycin, 34 µg/ml kanamycin, and 0.2% L-arabinose to select for colonies carrying both the nuclease-expression plasmid and the reporter plasmid. After overnight incubation at 37°C, transformation efficiency was calculated based on colony-forming units (CFU) per microgram of input plasmid DNA.

To test whether the BaCas12a3 effector complex exhibits additional in vivo activity that could affect bacterial growth, we performed the nuclease selection assay. In this assay, 50 µl of electrocompetent cells containing either pTarget or pNTarget were mixed on ice with 100 ng of the pCas12a3 plasmid. Electroporation was performed under the same conditions described above. After recovery, cells were plated on selective LB agar containing 34 µg/ml streptomycin, 0.2% L-arabinose, and 0.05 mM IPTG (without kanamycin). Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C, colonies were counted, and transformation efficiency was calculated as described above.

To assess growth under nuclease-targeting conditions, E. coli BL21(DE3) cells were co–transformed with pCas12a3 and either the target or non–target reporter plasmid. Transformants were recovered in SOC medium supplemented with 0.2% glucose to suppress expression of the nuclease and crRNA. After overnight incubation, cells were pelleted (5 000 × g, 2 min), washed, and resuspended in LB medium to a final OD₆₀₀ of 0.1. This suspension was used to inoculate LB medium containing kanamycin (34 µg/ml), streptomycin (34 µg/ml), 0.05 mM IPTG, and 0.2% (w/v) l-arabinose, which was dispensed into 48-well plates. Growth was monitored at 37°C by measuring OD₆₀₀ every 10 min in a plate reader.

Experiments were performed in triplicate. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed Welch’s t-test with Dunnett’s T3 post-hoc correction. Comparisons with P ≥ .05 were considered non-significant (n); those with .01 ≤ P < .05 were considered significant (*); .001 ≤ P < .01, moderately significant (**); and .0001 ≤ P < .001, highly significant (***).

Protein expression and purification

The recombinant plasmid pCas12a3 was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) competent cells. Transformants were selected on LB agar plates containing streptomycin (34 µg/ml) and incubation at 37°C for 20 h. A single colony was inoculated into LB medium supplemented with antibiotic and cultured overnight. This culture was then diluted 1:100 into 1 l of fresh LB medium and grown at 37°C until OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG for 5 h at 37°C. Cells were harvested, resuspended in Buffer A (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol), and lysed by sonication. After centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane and loaded onto a Ni-NTA affinity column. The column was washed with Buffer A, followed by a wash with 8% Buffer B (Buffer A supplemented with 500 mM imidazole). Bound protein was eluted using a linear gradient of 0%–100% Buffer B. Peak fractions were pooled and concentrated. The concentrated sample was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/200 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with Buffer C (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol). Chromatography was performed on an ÄKTA purifier system (Cytiva). The main peak fractions were collected, analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and western blot, concentrated, and stored at −80°C. The same purification procedure was used for the BaCas12a3–crRNA–target RNA complex and all mutant proteins.

In vitro nucleic acid binding and cleavage assays

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays were performed to assess the binding activity of the binary complex. Reactions (10 µl) were set up in binding buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)] containing 100 nM target ssRNA, ssDNA or dsDNA, and 0–500 nM BaCas12a3–crRNA complex. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, reactions were mixed with 10× DNA loading dye (Vazyme) and resolved on 12% TBE–PAGE gels. All oligonucleotide sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

To evaluate target-activated cleavage activity, reaction mixtures (10 µl) containing 0, 50, 100, 200, or 500 nM Cas12a3-crRNA and 100 nM oligonucleotide substrate were incubated at 37°C for 60 min. For ssRNA targets, 2× RNA loading dye (New England Biolabs) was added after incubation; for ssDNA targets, 10× DNA loading dye (Vazyme) was added and the mixtures were heated at 95°C for 5 min to terminate the reaction; for dsDNA targets, 10× DNA loading dye was added directly without heating. Products were resolved by 16% TBE–urea polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and visualized using a fluorescence imaging system (Amersham ImageQuant 800, Cytiva). For collateral cleavage activity, reactions were carried out under the same conditions with 100 nM Cas12a3-crRNA, 100 nM homologous target RNA, and 100 nM fluorophore-labeled substrates (single-stranded DNA, double-stranded DNA, or single-stranded RNA) in a 10 µl volume, and analyzed following the protocol described above. As a positive control, 500 nM SuCas12a2 was used in parallel reactions under identical conditions. All oligonucleotide sequences are listed in Supplementary Tables S3. All assays were performed in three independent replicates.

For tRNA cleavage assays, three types of tRNA substrates were tested: fluorescently labeled tRNA, in vitro-transcribed tRNA, and total tRNA extracted from E. coli as described previously [43]. To generate in vitro-transcribed tRNA, tRNA genes from E. coli BL21 were transcribed with a T7 promoter sequence (5′-TAATACGACTCACTATAGGG-3′) introduced upstream of each tRNA sequence. Due to the short length of these genes (75–90 bp), each full-length sequence was amplified using two overlapping primers (∼20 bp overlap; see Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). The resulting PCR products, containing the T7 promoter, were purified by ethanol precipitation and used as templates for in vitro transcription with a commercial kit (JT101-02, TransGen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Transcription from the T7 promoter added a 5′-GGG overhang to each tRNA, which did not alter the predicted secondary structure of the transcripts. Reactions contained 1 μM BaCas12a3, 1 μM target RNA, and 1 μM of tRNA in cleavage buffer, incubated at 37°C for 60 min, and mixed with 2× RNA loading dye (New England Biolabs). Products were separated on a 16% TBE–urea polyacrylamide gel, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized using a gel imaging system (JIAPENG ZF-288, Shanghai Jiapeng Technology Co., Ltd.). For fluorescence-labeled tRNA (5′-Cy5 and 3′-Cy2) or H1/S1 truncated tRNA (3′-Cy2), reactions were performed under the same conditions, except that the loading buffer was replaced with 2× urea loading buffer (8 M urea, 5 M EDTA, and 2× TBE). Products were visualized using the fluorescence imaging system (Amersham ImageQuant 800, Cytiva). All assays were performed in three independent replicates. To assess the tRNA cleavage activity of the mutant proteins, assays were performed using a 3′-Cy2 labeled tRNA substrate. Wild-type and all mutant proteins were used at 200 nM, while the target ssRNA and tRNA substrate were each added at 100 nM. Aliquots were collected at 0, 2, 5, 10, and 20 min post-reaction and analyzed under the same conditions as those used above.

Small RNA sequencing

The BaCas12a3–crRNA complex was purified as described, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by Turbo DNase (Life Technologies) treatment to remove DNA. RNA-seq libraries were prepared and analyzed by GENEWIZ, Azenta Life Sciences on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

To map the precise cleavage sites on tRNAs mediated by activated BaCas12a3, we performed small RNA sequencing (Genewiz, China). Total tRNAs (2 μg) extracted from E. coli BL21 cells were incubated with 1 μM BaCas12a3 in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 8.0) and 10 mM MgCl₂. In the experimental group, the reaction was supplemented with 2 μM target RNA to activate BaCas12a3, whereas the control group received no target RNA. Following incubation at 37°C for 1 h, three independent reactions were mixed for paired-end small RNA sequencing. Libraries construction, sequencing, and data analysis were performed as described above. Sequencing data were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) [44]. Although standard RNA-seq has inherent technical limitations for tRNA quantification [45], we took two measures to minimize these influences. First, we ensured that approximately a dozen nucleotides at the 3′ end of tRNA can be reliably sequenced deduced from the non-targeting sample (Supplementary File S2), and we focused specifically on the very tail of the 3′ end. Second, the average counts of adjacent nucleotides of the 3′ tails, which remain stable, served as a control to profile the missing bases. To quantify cleavage efficiency at the 3′ end of tRNAs, the average read count of nucleotides at positions −6 to −8 from the 3′ terminus was calculated. The read counts of each of the first eight 3′-terminal nucleotides were then normalized to this average value to generate a score. The boundary region exhibiting low normalized scores identifies the cleaved nucleotides. These scores are displayed as a heatmap.

PFS specificity profiling

To identify PFS motifs required for BaCas12a3 interference, a high-throughput plasmid depletion assay was performed using a library of target RNA coding sequences containing a randomized 5-nucleotide (5′–NNNNN-3′) region. Complementary oligonucleotides (Target-Flank-F and Target-Flank-R) containing the random region were annealed (95°C for 5 min, followed by gradual cooling to 4°C at 0.1°C per 15 s) and ligated into a pBAD vector downstream of the araBAD promoter. The ligation product was transformed into E. coli DH5α, and plasmid DNA was extracted from >104 colonies to generate the unselected input library. For positive selection, the input library was electroporated into E. coli BL21 cells expressing BaCas12a3 and its cognate crRNA from plasmid pCas12a3. Transformants were selected on LB agar containing 0.05 mM IPTG, 0.2% (w/v) L-arabinose, kanamycin, and streptomycin. Plasmid DNA was recovered from >104 surviving colonies to generate the output library following BaCas12a3-mediated depletion of functional PFS sequences. The randomized region from both libraries was amplified and deep-sequenced. The proportion of each variant in the input and output libraries was calculated and log10–transformed. The difference between these log–values served as a quantitative measure of selection: a positive value indicates enrichment (functional PFS), while a negative value indicates depletion (impaired function). Sequence enrichment and depletion patterns were visualized as a heatmap and as sequence logos. In this analysis, a positive difference (>0) indicates that the PFS likely possesses functional activity, whereas a negative difference (<0) suggests functional impairment, with zero serving as the statistical threshold for functional classification. Sequence enrichment and depletion patterns were analyzed and visualized using TBtools [46] and sequence logos generated with WebLogo [47].

Western analysis of E. coli RecA protein

To determine whether BaCas12a3 activates the DNA damage-mediated bacterial SOS response, E. coli BL21 was co-transformed with the pCas12a3 plasmid and either a target pTarget or non-target pNTarget reporter plasmid. Transformants were recovered overnight in SOC medium supplemented with 0.2% glucose to repress nuclease and crRNA expression. Cells were harvested, resuspended in LB medium with appropriate antibiotics, and grown to an OD600 of ∼0.6. After chilling and washing, cultures were resuspended in antibiotic-free LB. Nuclease and crRNA expression was induced with 0.1 mM IPTG for 30 min, followed by induction of the target plasmid with 0.4% l-arabinose. Samples were collected immediately before and at 1 h intervals for up to 5 h after arabinose addition. Cells were pelleted, resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (8.0 g/l NaCl, 0.2 g/l KCl, 3.63 g/l Na2HPO4·12H2O, 0.24 g/l KH2PO4, pH 7.4), and lysed by sonication. Total protein concentration in the lysate supernatant was determined using the Bradford assay [48]. Equal amounts of protein from each time point were subjected to western blot analysis. RecA was monitored using an anti-RecA antibody (Abcam plc, Cambridge, UK); an antibody against the RNA polymerase α-subunit (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) served as the loading control.

Cryo-electron microscopy

The BaCas12a3–crRNA binary complex was overexpressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells containing the pCas12a3 plasmid, followed by purification via nickel-affinity and SEC. To form the BaCas12a3–crRNA–targetRNA ternary complex, the purified binary complex was incubated with a 41–nt synthetic target RNA (Tsingke) at a 1:1.5 molar ratio for 1 h at 15°C. The mixture was further purified by SEC on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with cryo–EM buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl₂, 5% glycerol).

For grid preparation, 4 µl of each complex (0.3 mg/ml for binary, 0.5 mg/ml for ternary) was applied to glow–discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 200–mesh copper grids. After 4 s blotting, grids were plunge–frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Scientific). Cryo-EM data were collected on a Glacios (Thermo Scientific) microscope operated at 200 kV. Images were acquired using EPU software at a nominal magnification of ×130 000 (calibrated pixel size 0.89 Å/px), with a defocus range of −0.6 to −1.6 µm. Movies were recorded on a Falcon 4i direct electron detector equipped with a Selectris X energy filter (10 eV slit width), at a dose rate of 10 e⁻ /px/s over 3.35 s, yielding 40 frames and a total dose of ∼40 e−/Å2. A total of 5823 and 4581 movies were collected for the binary and ternary complexes, respectively (Supplementary Figs S9 and S10).

Image processing, model building, refinement, and validation

Data processing was performed in cryoSPARC (v4.5.3) [49]. For the BaCas12a3–crRNA complex, extracted particles underwent multiple rounds of 2D classification to remove false picks. Particles from selected classes were used to generate six ab initio models. Following three rounds of heterogeneous refinement, one class (Class 1) exhibited well-defined, high-resolution features and was selected for further 3D refinement [49, 50]. The other classes showed poorly resolved density (Supplementary Fig. S9). A final set of 484 846 particles from Class 1 yielded a reconstruction at 2.91 Å resolution. For the ternary complex, a similar processing strategy was applied. A final set of 530 902 particles was refined to a global resolution of 2.87 Å (Supplementary Fig. S10). These particles subsequently underwent 3D refinement and 3D classification [49, 50], yielding five classes that were analyzed for conformational heterogeneity. Atomic models for both complexes were built de novo in Coot, using predicted structures from AlphaFold 3 as initial guides [38, 51]. The models were refined against their respective cryo-EM maps using real-space refinement in Phenix [52, 53] and validated with MolProbity [54]. Structural figures were prepared using UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.10) [55]. Atomic coordinates have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank. A detailed summary of cryo-EM data collection and processing is provided in Supplementary Table S5.

Protein expression level and circular dichroism spectroscopy analysis

To determine the overall structural components of BaCas12a3 compared with WT BaCas12a3, far-UV circular dichroism (CD) was performed using a Jasco-J1500 spectropolarimeter. In brief, 0.1 mg ml–1 of the respective protein was prepared in CD buffer (0.24 g/l KH2PO4 and 3.63 g/l Na2HPO4.12H2O, pH 7.6) and CD spectra were obtained from 250 to 190 nm using a scanning speed of 100 nm min−1 (with a 2-s response time and accumulation of two scans).

The mutant and wild-type pCas12a3 plasmids were transformed separately into E. coli BL21(DE3). Single colonies were inoculated into medium containing 34 µg/ml streptomycin and grown overnight. Cultures were diluted 1:100 into fresh medium and grown to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6–0.8, followed by induction with 0.2 mM IPTG for 3 h. Cells were harvested, resuspended in 1 ml Buffer C, and lysed by sonication. The lysate was centrifuged and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay [48], and equal amounts of total protein (11 μg) were analyzed by SDS–PAGE. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ [56]. Data are presented as mean ± s.d. from three biological replicates, and comparisons between experimental and control groups were made using two-sided Welch’s t-test.

Results

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of Cas12a3

The Cas12a3 subclade proteins were identified by sequence-based mining of metagenomic database and phylogenetic analysis of retrieved Cas12 and their homologs. These Cas12 proteins were identified in a two–step bioinformatic approach, including (i) homologous sequences were identified and retrieved from metagenomic libraries in the GOMC database using the well-characterized DNA-targeting nuclease AsCpf1 (also known as AsCas12a) as a seed query [33]; and (ii) the resulting initial hits were then filtered using an identity threshold of <30% and subsequently used as seeds for iterative BLAST searches against the NCBI non-redundant protein database [31, 32]. This approach successfully identified several distinct CRISPR loci associated with a putative cas12 gene, which was annotated as transposases or hypothetical proteins (Supplementary File S1). Phylogenetic analysis using these sequences together with representative sequences retrieved from other Cas12 subtypes revealed that these Cas12a3 sequences form an independent evolutionary clade, sharing only 10%–20% sequence identity with either Cas12a or Cas12a2 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1). This sequence divergence highlights the unique evolutionary trajectory and functional specialization of Cas12a3. We also noticed that ~45% of Cas12a3 homologs were identified in Bacteroidota derived from gut microbiome (Supplementary Fig. S1). Thus, the gut represents an extremely dense and diverse microbial ecosystem with a high prevalence of phages and MGEs [57–59], which may constitute a driven force to the functional diversification of CRISPR–Cas defense systems.

We analyzed the CRISPR arrays from Cas12a3 systems to reveal potential phage/MGE targets. Spacer sequences were extracted from 30 Cas12a3-encoding genomic loci, yielding 525 CRISPR spacer sequences (Supplementary File S1), which were then searched for homologous sequences in the nt_viruses database and the ICEberg database for MGEs [34]. In addition, SpacePHARER was used for phage searching [35]. We found that most of spacers showed no significant matches (E–value threshold of 0.05) in the nt_viruses database, likely reflecting CRISPR spacers from poorly characterized environmental and gut microbiome bacteria. No hits were identified by SpacePHARER. However, two spacers showed significant sequence homology (≥90% identity over ≥90% of the spacer length) to sequenced virus genomes, primarily from human metagenome in the nt_viruses database (Supplementary Table S4). In addition, one spacer mapped to non-phage integrative and conjugative elements from Bacteroidetes species (Supplementary Table S4), consistent with the origin of Cas12a3 systems in animal guts.

Cas12 subtypes exhibit remarkable diversity, and they are believed to have evolved from their TnpB ancestors present in the IS605 and IS607 families [7, 8, 11, 15, 60]. A unifying feature conserved across all characterized Cas12 proteins and their TnpB ancestor is the presence of an RuvC nuclease domain, which forms the catalytic core [8]. Consistent with this, AlphaFold3 structural modeling confirms that the RuvC domain of BaCas12a3 closely resembles that of the RNA-targeting Cas12a2. Notably, three catalytic amino acid residues are conserved both in the primary sequence alignment (Supplementary Fig. S2) and in their relative three-dimensional positions within the predicted structure of BaCas12a3 (Supplementary Fig. S3), strongly suggesting a shared enzymatic mechanism. Intriguingly, bioinformatic analysis revealed that ~35% of Cas12a3 homologs we identified (marked in Supplementary Fig. S1) possess an additional domain of unknown function that is completely absent from all other known Cas12 subtypes (Supplementary Fig. S2), indicative of deep internal evolutionary differentiation for Cas12a3.

Examination of the genomic loci encoding CRISPR–Cas12a3 systems shows that they also contain core adaptation genes cas1, cas2, and often cas4, and CRISPR arrays, suggesting these CRISPR systems are active in spacer acquisition [61]. This genetic architecture supports their role as functional immune systems. These Cas12a3 systems are frequently associated with accessory genes, including TPR-DUF3800 proteins or Cas12a systems (Fig. 1B). TPR domains mediate protein–protein interactions, whereas DUF3800 resembles an RNase H domain and is often linked to MGEs and defense systems [62]. Our genomic analysis showed that TPR-DUF3800 genes are present adjacent to the cas12a3 operon in ∼40% of loci, suggesting that these genes are functionally connected. We propose that they may regulate abortive infection, aid CRISPR adaptation, or sense MGE infection. Their occurrence in Cas12a2 and type III systems implies broader roles beyond Cas12a3.

Collectively, these results suggested that Cas12a3 systems may represent a new Cas12 subtype, exhibiting mechanistically diversified antiviral mechanisms. Two different groups of Cas12a3 were selected for characterization. The first is a Bacteroidales Cas12a3 (BaCas12a3) exhibiting high-level complete protein expression in E. coli. This protein carries an additional domain of unknown function and are predominantly found in gut-associated Bacteroidetes species. The other one is Roizmanbacteria Cas12a3 (RoiCas12a3), which lacks this additional domain.

BaCas12a3 induces immune activity with RNA-targeting

In order to test whether this CRISPR–Cas12a3 could be functional in E. coli, a heterologous host, we constructed an expression plasmid (pCas12a3) harboring both the BaCas12a3-encoding sequence and a CRISPR array. Plasmid-borne expression of the cas gene and CRISPR array in E. coli yielded BaCas12a3–crRNA ribonucleoprotein complexes (RNPs) that were purified by SEC (Supplementary Fig. S5). The RNA components were then extracted from the RNPs and characterized by small RNA sequencing. We found that revealed mature crRNAs containing a 19-nt repeat linked to a ∼23-nt spacer, indicating that the BaCas12a3 pre-crRNAs were processed in E. coli cells, likely by the BaCasl2a3 protein itself (Supplementary Fig. S5). We further compared the predicted binary complex of BaCas12a3 with the SuCas12a2 and FnCas12a structures (Supplementary Fig. S3). The results showed that both the REC lobe and NUC lobe of BaCas12a3 resemble those of Cas12a2, and no dsDNA-binding or DNA strand-separation domains (TNB/Nuc), which are present in Cas12a, were detected in BaCas12a3 (Supplementary Fig. S3). We therefore deduced that BaCas12a3 mediates antiviral defense through RNA targeting, similar to Cas12a2, an RNA-targeting system that executes immunity in a PFS-dependent manner [20, 21].

To validate the functional PFS motifs of BaCas12a3, we employed a PFS-depletion assay similar to Cas12a2 (Supplementary Fig. S6A). A plasmid library containing a target sequence flanked by a fully randomized 5-nt region (3′-N−5N−4N−3N−2N−1-target-5′) was transformed into E. coli expressing the BaCas12a3 effector complex (Fig. 2A). Plasmids bearing a functional PFS were depleted via CRISPR interference-mediated growth arrest, whereas those with non-functional protospacer flanking sequence variants were enriched in the surviving population. Deep sequencing of the initial and post-selection libraries (escape library) allowed comprehensive mapping of PFS preferences (Supplementary Fig. S6). Analysis of the depletion heatmap revealed that 54% (550/1024) of motifs at the PFS-coding position in the protospacer plasmid library were effectively depleted by the nuclease (Supplementary Fig. S6B). A strong preference for 5′-(U/C/G)AA at positions −1 to −3 was observed, which closely resembles the PFS of SuCas12a2 (5′-(C/U)AAA) and the non-target strand-derived PAM of DNA-targeting Cas12a (5′-(T/C/G)AA) (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S6B) [20, 63] (see also in the “Discussion” section). To validate the PFS-depleting results, PFS motifs with high depletion values were selected for testing their capability of mediating plasmid interference assays in E. coli. We found that several highly depleted PFS motifs (e.g. 5′-CAAAG-3′, 5′-GAAAG-3′, 5′-TCAAA-3′, 5′-GGAGA-3′) conferred significantly higher levels of transformation fold reduction compared to non-depleted ones (Fig. 2C). One such motif, 5′-GAAAG-3′, was selected as the optimal PFS for Cas12a3 and used in all subsequent in vivo and in vitro assays.

To gain a further insight into the antiviral defense by the BaCas12a3 system, two plasmids were employed: pCas12a3 carrying Bacas12a3 gene and a cognate mini-CRISPR array, and pTarget containing a corresponding protospacer for expressing the cognate target RNA complementary to the spacer in the mini-CRISPR array (Fig. 2D). Introduction of each plasmid into E. coli yielded two strains for interference assay: one carrying pCas12a3 and the other containing pTarget. The former was then transformed with pTarget while the latter with pCas12a3. Scoring for both plasmids in the host expressing BaCas12a3 upon pTarget transformation in the first experiment revealed >103-fold lower rate of transformation than that of a reference plasmid pNTarget (Fig. 2E). Since survivals should carry both plasmids, only bacterial cells with escape mutation of the BaCas12a3 immunity, i.e. mutation in Bacas12a3 gene, spacers, or target sequence, can form colonies on the selective plates. Thus, BaCas12a3 executes strong immunity against pTarget. Transformation of pTarget-carrying cells with pCas12a3 showed that the transformation efficiency was comparable to the occurrence of escape mutation in the first experiment (Fig. 2E), suggesting Cas12a3 operates through influencing host cell viability rather than specific plasmid targeting, as shown for Cas12a2 [20]. It is worth noting that sequencing of seven independent escape mutant colonies failed to detect any mutations in the Bacas12a3 gene, crRNA expression cassette, or target protospacer region (data not shown), indicating that the BaCas12a3-mediated immunity could be inactivated by an unknown compensatory mechanism.

To further characterize the activation of the BaCas12a3 system in E. coli, two additional plasmids were constructed: pTNoPfs (producing target RNAs with a non-functional protospacer flanking sequence 5′-AGCAC-3′), and pTNoPr (lacking a promoter for producing target RNAs), and these plasmids were used in plasmid interference assays along with pTarget (producing target RNAs with the 5′-GAAAG-3′ PFS) and pNTarget (lacking the target RNA coding sequence) (Fig. 3A). We found a drastic reduction in transformation efficiency of >1000-fold only for pTarget. In contrast, the absence of any required element (i.e. a non-functional protospacer flanking sequence, a non-target sequence, or a missing promoter) restored the transformation efficiency, demonstrating an absolute requirement for the cognate target RNA in the BaCas12a3 immune response (Fig. 3B). Growth experiments showed that, while normal growth was observed for control cultures, robust growth inhibition occurred exclusively for the culture containing both the BaCas12a3 nuclease and the complete target plasmid (Fig. 3C), consistent with the results of the plasmid interference assay (Fig. 3B).

To gain an insight into the BaCas12a3-mediated growth arrest, we performed in vitro binding and cleavage assays using purified BaCas12a3–crRNA binary complex against a panel of nucleic acid substrates, including FAM-labeled target ssRNA, non-target ssRNA, ssDNA, and dsDNA (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S7). Cleavage of nucleic acids generated fragments carrying 5′-FAM fluorophores, which were detected by fluorometer. We found that BaCas12a3 binds target RNA with high affinity (Fig. 3E). However, BaCas12a3 only exhibited marginal activity toward target ssRNA in a PFS-dependent manner, while ssDNA and dsDNA substrates remained intact even after prolonged incubation (Fig. 3E; replicate data in Supplementary Fig. S7). Moreover, BaCas12a3 did not exhibit any target RNA-activated promiscuous trans-cleavage of generic ssRNA, ssDNA, or dsDNA, which is in sharply contrast to the indiscriminate collateral activity reported for Cas12a2 (Fig. 3F) [20, 21]. Together with in vivo results shown in Fig. 3B, these results demonstrate that BaCas12a3 is a target RNA-activated nuclease that exerts strong immune activity through an as-yet-unknown mechanism.

Collateral activity toward tRNA

CRISPR–Cas12a2, along with Type III and Type VI systems, is characterized by an RNA-targeting immune response [20, 23, 64, 65]. In these systems, recognition of a cognate RNA guide activates the effector nuclease. This activation typically induces potent, indiscriminate nuclease activity that degrades cellular nucleic acids, triggering a dramatic abortive infection response to protect the bacterial population [65, 66].

Building upon the established mechanism of the related effector Cas12a2—which, upon target recognition, exhibits collateral cleavage of dsDNA, thereby triggering the RecA-dependent SOS response and genomic damage [20]—we sought to differentiate the functional mechanism of BaCas12a3. Escherichia coli BL21 co-transformed with pCas12a3 and pTarget plasmids was grown to OD600 ∼0.6 and induced to activate BaCas12a3. Samples were collected hourly for up to 5 h for western blot analysis. As a positive control, induction of the Cas12a2 system resulted in a significant increase in RecA expression, confirming the expected DNA damage response (Fig. 4A). In contrast, activation of BaCas12a3 by its target RNA did not alter RecA expression levels, indicating no detectable DNA damage during its immune reaction (Fig. 4C). This clear distinction suggests that BaCas12a3 employs an immune mechanism distinct from Cas12a2 [20].

Given the apparent restriction of collateral activity, we next tested whether tRNA could serve as a substrate for activated BaCas12a3, based on preliminary transcription analysis (data not shown). Initial electrophoretic analysis revealed a slight but consistent band shift for some tRNA species, indicating possible tRNA degradation or cleavage (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S8, red lines mark shifted bands). To determine whether the cleavage site is located at the 3′ or 5′ end, we employed fluorescently labeled tRNAThr substrates. This assay definitively demonstrated that BaCas12a3 efficiently cleaves the tRNA molecules at their 3′ end (Fig. 4E). This cleavage is highly consequential, as the integrity of the 3′ CCA terminus is essential for tRNA aminoacylation by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and for subsequent ribosomal interactions during translation [67].

To precisely map tRNA cleavage sites, we performed small RNA sequencing to specifically profile the 3′ termini of tRNAs from both RNA-targeting and non-targeting samples. The cleavage patterns were determined from in vitro assay using purified bulk tRNAs from E. coli treated with activated nucleases. Sequencing results revealed that activated BaCas12a3 induced substantial 3′-terminal cleavage, predominantly occurring between third and fourth nucleotides from the tRNA 3′ end (Fig. 4F). By cleaving off this terminal sequence, activated BaCas12a3 irreversibly inactivates the tRNA. This provides a direct mechanistic explanation for the profound growth inhibition observed in vivo upon BaCas12a3 activation (Fig. 3C). The cleavage of tRNAs effectively blocked the cellular translation, linking the targeted recognition of tRNA molecules to a fatal disruption of an essential housekeeping process and thereby executing an immune function.

Structural features of BaCas12a3

Using cryo-EM, we determined the structures of the BaCas12a3–crRNA binary complex at 2.91 Å resolution and the BaCas12a3–crRNA–targetRNA ternary complex at 2.87 Å resolution (Supplementary Figs S9 and S10; and Supplementary Table S5). Atomic models were built based on the two corresponding maps (Fig. 5). The domain organization of BaCas12a3, as revealed by these structural models, is presented in Fig. 5A. BaCas12a3 adopts the conserved bilobed architecture characteristic of the Cas12 protein family, featuring an N-terminal recognition (REC) lobe and a C-terminal nuclease (NUC) lobe, connected by a flexible linker (residues 694–709) [9]. In both complexes, the crRNA and the crRNA–target RNA duplex are positioned within the central channel formed between the two lobes (Fig. 5B and C). The REC lobe comprises the REC1, REC2, WED (wedge), and PI (protospacer-flanking site-interacting) domains. REC1 and REC2 domains primarily facilitate crRNA binding and are responsible for the initial recognition and verification of the target RNA sequence [21], while the WED and PI domains are critical for recognizing both the crRNA and the PFS (Fig. 5C). Compared to the Cas12a2 ortholog, BaCas12a3 incorporated a streamlined REC2 domain (Fig. 6A). Notably, in the binary complex of BaCas12a3, the PI domain mediates extensive interactions with the crRNA through hydrogen bonding and π–π stacking involving key residues such as Arg473, Gln476, Glu517, and Phe518 (Supplementary Fig. S11). These interactions result in an extended, ordered conformation of the crRNA 3′ region. In contrast, comparative analysis with the Cas12a2 binary complex (PDB: 8D49) reveals that neither a structurally defined PI domain nor an ordered 3′ crRNA extension is present in that structure (Supplementary Fig. S11) [21]. Despite these architectural differences in the REC2 and PI domains, the REC lobe of BaCas12a3 maintains functional parallels with SuCas12a2, as both orthologs preserve conserved mechanisms for crRNA and target RNA binding [21].

The C-terminal NUC lobe of BaCas12a3 contains the essential catalytic core and several auxiliary domains critical for its unique function. Central to this lobe is the RuvC domain (Fig. 6B), which adopts the canonical RNase H-like fold that is characteristic of Cas12 nucleases [68]. Structural comparison analysis revealed that the RuvC domains are highly similar across Cas12a, Cas12a2, and Cas12a3, and the three conserved catalytic residues (D/E/D) are strictly conserved (Supplementary Fig. S12). In BaCas12a3, the conserved catalytic triad consists of D733, E1052, and D1156, which are indispensable for magnesium ion coordination and phosphodiester bond cleavage (Fig. 6C). The Insertion domain, positioned adjacent to RuvC, exhibits distinct structural adaptations in BaCas12a3, including an N-terminal extension that wraps behind the ZR domain to provide critical structural reinforcement and stability to the entire lobe (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the RuvC domain is associated with the ZR domain incorporating two conserved zinc-finger motifs (CXXC/CXXXC), which also occurs in Cas12a2/f/e/m/g orthologs with a function of nucleic acid loading [18, 21, 27–29]. In addition, BaCas12a3 contains a unique additional domain, which is absent in all previously characterized Cas12 effectors (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S5). This domain, composed of three antiparallel β-strands and four α-helices, extends from the insertion domain to the periphery of the RuvC domain, forming a distinctive architectural feature (Fig. 6D). Spatially, the ZR domain and the uncharacterized domain are positioned on opposite sides of the RuvC domain to form a gated groove (Fig. 6D). Cas12 enzymes usually cleave nucleic acid substrates at a single RuvC active site, and the TNB (also known as Nuc or TSL) domain located adjacent to the active site facilitates the loading of the nucleic acid into the RuvC active site (Fig. 6A) [13, 25, 28, 69–71]. Based on its structural location adjacent to the RuvC catalytic center and its functional role demonstrated below, we initially referred to this domain as the nucleic acid-loading (NL) domain. Given its specific role in tRNA recognition revealed by subsequent analyses and the parallel report by Dmytrenko et al. who termed the equivalent region the tRNA-loading domain (tRLD), we adopt the term tRLD throughout the revised manuscript for nomenclature consistency [30].

Activation and allosteric gating of BaCas12a3 nuclease

Structural analysis of the binary complex revealed that the 5′ repeat region of the crRNA (19 nt) forms a pseudoknot. Comparative structural studies indicated that this 5′ region remains largely static upon target RNA binding (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Video S1). In contrast, the 3′ region of the crRNA undergoes a pronounced conformational rearrangement (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Video S1). Upon formation of the BaCas12a3–crRNA–targetRNA ternary complex, PFS recognition is mediated by residues from the PI and WED domains (Fig. 7A), while the remainder of the target RNA is primarily recognized by the RuvC and REC2 domains. Concurrently, substantial conformational rearrangements occur in the REC1 (2–65 Å), REC2 (1–68 Å), tRLD (1–25 Å), and PI (1–14 Å) domains (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Video S1). The REC2 domain rotates by ∼68 Å toward the NUC lobe; the tRLD domain undergoes a hinge-like motion that opens a positively charged groove adjacent to the RuvC catalytic site; and the ZR domain repositions to form one wall of this newly exposed channel (Fig. 7B and C; and Supplementary Video S1). Together, these movements create a gated substrate channel with surface electrostatic properties and dimensions compatible with accommodating the acceptor arm of a tRNA molecule (Fig. 8A). In contrast to the highly exposed RuvC active site observed in the Cas12a2 ternary complex (PDB: 8D4A) (Fig. 6B) [21], the tRLD domain in BaCas12a3 likely restricts substrate access, thereby limiting its collateral nuclease activity. This explains the complete lack of promiscuous collateral cleavage activity toward generic ssRNA, ssDNA, or dsDNA substrates (Fig. 3F).

RNA-targeting Cas12 subtypes utilize zinc–binding modules for nucleic acid binding, such as the 37–aa Nuc domain in Cas12g or the 47–aa ZR domain in Cas12a2 [21, 29]. BaCas12a3 employs an additional tRLD domain. The tRLD and ZR domains, positioned on either side of the catalytic site, cooperate to position the tRNA for RuvC–mediated cleavage. To elucidate how tRNA substrates are loaded into the active site and cleaved in trans, we attempted to resolve complex structures using multiple strategies, including glutaraldehyde immobilization and testing different tRNAs. However, none of these approaches succeeded, likely due to the transient or dynamic nature of this interaction. Nevertheless, a 3D classification analysis of the existing ternary complex data revealed insightful conformational details (Supplementary Fig. S10). This analysis revealed a significant and reproducible movement of the tRLD domain, which undergoes a rotational shift of ~10° relative to the core catalytic body (Fig. 7D and Supplementary Video S2). The observed “open” state likely represents a transient conformation that facilitates the initial entry and positioning of the bulky tRNA substrate. Following cleavage, a reversal of this motion—a “closing” state—may promote product release, thereby enabling catalytic turnover. Thus, the detected structural plasticity provides a compelling mechanistic framework for the tRNA substrate handling cycle of the enzyme.

To validate the functional importance of the tRLD domain and the RuvC catalytic center, we performed site-directed mutagenesis. Initial investigations focused on the conserved catalytic triad (D733, E1052, and D1156) within the RuvC nuclease domain (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Fig. 8B). To trigger immune activity, E. coli BL21 strains harboring both a mutant pCas12a3 plasmid (pCas12a3D733A, pCas12a3E1052A, or pCas12a3D1156A) (Supplementary Table S1) and the pTarget plasmid were induced with IPTG and l-arabinose, and their growth curves were monitored. The results revealed that mutations in any of the three catalytic residues nearly abolished the inhibition of bacterial cell growth (Fig. 8C). Consistent with this cellular phenotype, in vitro cleavage assays demonstrated a nearly complete loss of collateral tRNA cleavage activity for all three RuvC mutants (Fig. 8E). These results demonstrate that BaCas12a3 strictly depends on its RuvC domain for function and inhibits cell growth via a tRNA disabling mechanism, thus establishing a functional paradigm distinct from all other characterized Cas12 systems.

To further elucidate the functional role of the positively charged groove observed in our structural model, we conducted a mutational analysis. This distinctive channel is formed by the tRLD domain and adjacent regions of the REC2, RuvC, and ZR domains, creating an interface well-suited for tRNA binding (Fig. 8A). We selected six solvent-exposed positively charged residues (R379, R380, K902, R946, K1058, and K1065) lining this groove—which we suspected to be involved in substrate recognition—and substituted each with alanine. Correspondingly, five mutant pCas12a3 plasmids were generated (pCas12a3R379A-R380A, pCas12a3R946A, pCas12a3K902A, pCas12a3K1065A, and pCas12a3K1058A) (Supplementary Table S1). Moreover, we also constructed two additional mutants: one carrying multiple mutations (R379A-R380A-K902A-R946A-K1058A-K1065A) and a ΔtRLD mutant lacking partial tRLD domain. All mutants expressed at levels comparable to wild type (Supplementary Fig. S13A), and CD spectroscopy confirmed that their secondary structure contents were preserved (Supplementary Fig. S13B), ruling out gross misfolding as a confounding factor. The functional impact of these mutations was assessed using a growth curve assay, which measures immune-mediated growth inhibition upon activation by IPTG and arabinose induction. Critically, all seven mutants showed reduced growth inhibition compared with cells expressing the wild-type protein (Fig. 8D and Supplementary Fig. S14A). Mutations in the tRLD-lining basic residues (K902A and R946A) reduced immune activity without affecting RuvC integrity, providing direct functional evidence that the tRLD domain mediates tRNA substrate engagement (Fig. 8D). Consistently, the in vitro cleavage assay revealed that mutants K1058A, R379A-R380A-K902A-R946A-K1058A-K1065A, and ΔtRLD abolished tRNA cleavage activity, whereas the remaining mutants exhibited varying degrees of activity reduction (Fig. 8E and Supplementary Fig. S14B). Furthermore, we expanded our in vitro cleavage assays to include linear RNA and truncated RNA bearing a 3′ CCA terminus, and found that BaCas12a3 also cleaves these substrates at their 3′ CCA ends (Fig. 8F).

To test whether the tRLD domain is universally required for Cas12a3 function, we characterized RoiCas12a3, a representative homolog from a clade that lacks the tRLD insertion (green box, Supplementary Fig. S1). AlphaFold3-predicted structural modeling indicated that RoiCas12a3 retains the canonical Cas12 bilobed architecture but lacks the gated substrate channel observed in BaCas12a3 (Supplementary Fig. S15A; RMSD 1.2 Å over 284 pruned pairs in the conserved core). In vitro cleavage assays revealed that RoiCas12a3 cleaves target ssRNA efficiently (Supplementary Fig. S15C), in contrast to BaCas12a3. Moreover, RoiCas12a3 exhibited a high collateral RNA cleavage activity compared with BaCas12a3 (Supplementary Fig. S15D), consistent with the absence of the tRLD domain. In addition, tRNAs were degraded into a single band (Supplementary Fig. S15B), a pattern that differs from the cleavage pattern observed for BaCas12a3. These observations reinforce the conclusion that the tRLD domain is the key structural element that endows BaCas12a3 with its tRNA-directed collateral activity, and reveal evolutionary diversification within the Cas12a3 subfamily.

Discussion

The discovery of Cas12a3 expands the functional diversity of Type V CRISPR–Cas systems. While most Cas12 effectors target DNA, the emergence of RNA-targeting subtypes like Cas12a2 and now Cas12a3 underscores the remarkable evolutionary adaptability of the RuvC catalytic core [8, 9, 20, 30]. Our biochemical data demonstrate that Cas12a3 functions as a PFS-dependent, RNA-activating nuclease. To explain the strong PFS/PAM similarities among BaCas12a3, SuCas12a2, and Cas12a [20, 63], we performed a structural comparison of the PFS/PAM recognition interfaces using our BaCas12a3 ternary complex structure, the SuCas12a2 ternary structure [21], and the AsCas12a ternary structure [70] (see Supplementary Fig. S16). Structural comparison of the PI and WED domains revealed a high degree of similarity among BaCas12a3, SuCas12a2, and AsCas12a (Supplementary Fig. S16D and E). Specifically, BaCas12a3 and SuCas12a2 share conserved residues involved in PFS recognition (5′-G−1A−2A−3), including T471/T651, S469/S613, Y218/Y254, and K477/K621 (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. S16A) [21]. Thus, their PFS similarity can be readily explained by their shared evolutionary origin as RNA-targeting effectors. In contrast, when comparing Cas12a3/Cas12a2 with DNA-targeting Cas12a, the structural comparison reveals that while the overall fold of the PI and WED domains shows high similarity (Supplementary Fig. S16D and E), the recognition mechanisms differ: Cas12a recognizes the thymine bases of the PAM in the DNA duplex through minor groove interactions, with the crucial Lys607 residue forming hydrogen bonds with the thymine (−2) of the non-target DNA chain and adenine (−3) of the target strand [24, 70, 72]. Despite these mechanistic differences, the preference for A/T-rich sequences at positions −2/−3 is maintained across all three subtypes, reflecting functional constraints on PFS/PAM recognition. We also note that the residues involved in PFS recognition are conserved in the structure reported by Dmytrenko et al. (Supplementary Fig. S16C) [30].

Our data indicate that the BaCas12a3 pre-crRNAs are self-processed (Supplementary Fig. S5). Like its relatives Cas12a and Cas12a2, which process crRNAs via conserved WED-domain active sites [21, 70, 73], BaCas12a3 appears to use a similar mechanism. Despite low sequence identity, the WED and RuvC domains that coordinate the crRNA 5′ stem–loop are structurally conserved among the three enzymes. However, the catalytic residues critical for processing in Cas12a (e.g. His843, Lys852, and Lys869) are not retained in Cas12a3 or Cas12a2 (Supplementary Fig. S17) [70, 73]. Instead, Cas12a2 utilizes Lys784 and Arg785 at analogous positions [21], and we identified Arg679 in BaCas12a3 as the equivalent of Arg785. Notably, a loop bearing four positively charged residues extends from Arg679 in BaCas12a3 (Supplementary Fig. S17A), suggesting a role in processing the crRNA 5′ stem–loop. Collectively, BaCas12a3, Cas12a2, and Cas12a can recognize and process the same crRNA repeats, enabling them to functionally share CRISPR arrays when co-expressed. This shared processing machinery reflects the evolutionary connectivity among these systems and may facilitate cooperative immune defense.

BaCas12a3 induces bacterial growth arrest not through broad collateral nucleic acid cleavage, but via a collateral tRNA activity that precise cleaves at the 3′ CCA terminus. The tRNA cleavage activity of Cas12a3 shares conceptual parallels with a broader biological strategy observed in certain bacterial toxin–antitoxin systems and eukaryotic stress responses, suggesting a convergent evolutionary strategy to regulate cellular processes [74–79]. For instance, bacterial defense systems like PARIS deploy nuclease AriB to cleave specific tRNAs within their anticodon stem–loops upon phage detection, halting translation to abort infection [75, 80]. Phages counter by encoding immune-resistant tRNAs, driving a molecular arms race. While enzymes like angiogenin primarily target the anticodon loop to generate regulatory tRNA-derived fragments, cleavage at the conserved 3′ CCA terminus provides another layer of control [78, 81]. The CCA-end cleavage inactivates tRNAs by preventing aminoacylation, ensuring a robust and sustained blockade of translation [78]. Thus, tRNA cleavage serves as a fundamental host regulation strategy, utilizing both precise anticodon scission and terminal CCA inactivation to inhibit translation. The convergent evolution of tRNA-cleavage in CRISPR–Cas systems underscores the potency of disrupting translation as an antiviral strategy. It highlights how different CRISPR lineages have independently evolved effectors that, despite different ancestral structures (RuvC nuclease versus HEPN nuclease), have been selected to disrupt protein synthesis via tRNA damage [82]. Cas13a from Leptotrichia shahii cleaves tRNAs within the anticodon loop, particularly those with uridine-rich anticodons, leading to translation inhibition [82]. In contrast, BaCas12a3 targets the conserved 3′ CCA aminoacylation site. This difference in cleavage site suggests distinct modes of translational arrest: Cas13a may impair codon recognition, while Cas12a3 directly prevents tRNA aminoacylation and entry into the ribosomal cycle [82]. The depletion of functional tRNA pools by either mechanism would globally impair translation, providing a potent means to halt viral replication without the potentially mutagenic DNA damage.

The structural basis for BaCas12a3’s unique function is explained by its cryo-EM structures. The presence of the novel tRLD domain is a defining characteristic (Fig. 6). In the binary complex, the tRLD domain partially blocked the RuvC active site and contributes to a narrow groove. Target RNA recognition and PFS-dependent activation trigger large-scale conformational changes, including repositioning of the tRLD domain (Fig. 7). This opens the groove and exposes the catalytic triad, likely forming a dedicated binding channel for the tRNA substrate (Fig. 7). This “gated channel” mechanism explains the enzyme’s specificity: the architecture may sterically or electrostatically favor the structural features of tRNA over those of other nucleic acids (Figs 4D–F). This model contrasts with Cas12a2, whose more exposed active site in the ternary complex allows promiscuous substrate access (Fig. 6A and C) [30]. Cas13 enzymes undergo a distinct activation mechanism, reorienting HEPN domains to form a nonspecific RNase site that subsequently selects tRNA anticodon loops [83]. In contrast, BaCas12a3 activation opens a pre-formed, specific channel—governed by the tRLD domain (Fig. 7).

Structural superposition of our BaCas12a3 binary and ternary complexes with the corresponding Ba1Cas12a3 structures from Dmytrenko et al. (PDB: 9HLX binary; PDB: 9HM6 ternary; PDB: 9HM4/9HM5 quaternary) yielded RMSD values of 1.0 Å (binary, 821 pruned pairs), 1.0 Å (ternary, 414 pruned pairs), 0.8 Å (quaternary with tRNA, 598 pruned pairs), and 0.8 Å (quaternary with cleaved tRNA, 610 pruned pairs) (Supplementary Fig. S18) [30]. The remarkable structural concordance, despite 51.3% sequence identity (Supplementary Fig. S4), underscores the evolutionary conservation of the Cas12a3 architecture and validates our mechanistic interpretations. The same large-scale allosteric conformational changes were observed upon target RNA binding that are reported in the published study. Notably, the REC1 domain was not resolved in the Ba1Cas12a3 ternary and quaternary complex structures, whereas it was clearly resolved in our structure (Supplementary Fig. S18). The quaternary structure of Ba1Cas12a3-crRNA-targetRNA-tRNAAla reported by Dmytrenko et al. provides detailed structural insights into the molecular basis of tRNA recognition and cleavage by the tRLD and RuvC domains [30]. We found that the residues involved in tRNA binding are not fully conserved between our BaCas12a3 and the Ba1Cas12a3 from the published study, which explains the marked differences in the surface electrostatic potentials (Supplementary Fig. S19). For example, we did not find BaCas12a3 equivalents of residues of R251, N253, K885, E1116, and K902, which are involved in tRNA binding in Ba1Cas12a3 (Supplementary Fig. S19C). Moreover, an additional region (A376 to E383) is present in BaCas12a3 but absent in Ba1Cas12a3; this region contains several positively charged residues (R379, R380, and K382) that likely contribute to tRNA binding. Nevertheless, residue K1058, whose alanine substitution abolishes tRNA cleavage activity, corresponds to K1038 in Ba1Cas12a3. Other conserved residue pairs at equivalent positions include K1065/K1045, K255/K256, E736/E715, and V737/V716 (Supplementary Fig. S19C). These results indicate that different Cas12a3 nucleases might have different affinities for different tRNA species, while the conserved residues can explain the common feature of recognition and loading the 3′ CCA end into the RuvC active site.

Comparison between the results obtained with Ba1Cas12a3 by Dmytrenko et al. [30] and the homologous Cas12a3 reported in this work reveals the shared core mechanism of tRNA cleavage: RNA-activated Cas12a3 collaterally cleaves the 3′ end of cellular tRNAs to elicit bacterial growth arrest as an anti-phage immune response. Beyond this shared mechanism, our study offers additional layers of insight: (i) our work has more explicitly placed Cas12a3 within the type V phylogenetic framework, emphasizing its distinctness from both DNA-targeting Cas12a and RNA-targeting Cas12a2 (Figs 5A and 6A, and Supplementary Figs S1, S3, S12, S16, and S17); (ii) our comparison between the domain-containing (BaCas12a3) and domain-lacking (RoiCas12a3) homologs within the same clade has revealed intra-family functional diversification (Supplementary Figs S1 and S15); and (iii) we have dissected the structural determinants of substrate selectivity through mutagenesis of the tRLD groove, demonstrating that this domain gates access to the RuvC active site (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. S14). These complementary lines of insights reinforce the mechanistic model while expanding the evolutionary and functional context of Cas12a3-mediated immunity.

The immune process can be deduced as follows: upon expression, BaCas12a3 and the mature crRNA are assembled into a surveillance state that is autoinhibited for RNA cleavage (Fig. 5B); upon phage infection, the crRNA base pairs with the complementary target RNA transcript derived from the invader to scan for a compatible PFS motif, and the target RNA-guided RNA interaction induces a large conformational change to the Cas12a3 effector for opening up the substrate groove and exposing the RuvC active site (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S18); finally, the activated BaCas12a3 cleaves the 3′ CCA termini of cellular tRNAs (Figs 4 and 8), irreversibly inactivating tRNAs for aminoacylation, thereby blocking global cellular translation. Thus, the Cas12a3 immunity not only halts replication of both host and phage genomes, but also induces growth arrest to prevent the spread of the phage in the bacterial population [30].

From a biotechnology perspective, BaCas12a3 offers a unique combination of properties. Its RNA-triggered activation and highly specific tRNA collateral activity could enable novel diagnostic applications, such as specific RNA detection assays with a 3′ CCA tail cleavage readout, potentially improving sensitivity, as demonstrated by Dmytrenko et al. [30]. Furthermore, its ability to induce a potent, DNA-damage-free growth arrest may be useful for safe human gene therapy or for antimicrobial strategies that selectively trigger pathogenic bacteria inhibition upon detection of specific RNA signatures.

Together, the study reported by Dmytrenko et al. [30] and the current one have independently established Cas12a3 as a hyper-evolvable subtype of type V CRISPR–Cas systems, in which the RuvC catalytic core has been repurposed to selectively target the universally conserved 3′ CCA tail of tRNAs. This molecular strategy—disabling translation through tRNA inactivation—is functionally analogous to convergent mechanisms in toxin–antitoxin systems and Cas13a-mediated tRNA cleavage [82, 84], despite distinct evolutionary origins. Beyond its biological significance, the precise substrate specificity of Cas12a3 positions it as a promising scaffold for transcript-responsive biotechnological tools, including molecular diagnostics, conditional cell-arrest circuits, and orthogonal RNA-sensing platforms.
