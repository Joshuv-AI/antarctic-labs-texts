Optimal timing of letermovir prophylaxis after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: insights from a real-world time-varying exposure analysis
Authors: Xueyao Li, Wenhao Wang, Ruixin Li, Jieya Luo, Xin Chen, Weihua Zhai, Rongli Zhang, Jialin Wei, Donglin Yang, Yi He, Aiming Pang, Sizhou Feng, Mingzhe Han, Yigeng Cao, Erlie Jiang
Published: 2026

Letermovir (LTV) prophylaxis effectively prevents cytomegalovirus (CMV) reactivation after allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) [1], yet breakthrough or rebound infection often occurs after discontinuation at day 100 [2, 3]. Although extending prophylaxis to 200 days has proven effective in randomized trials, it may increase drug acquisition costs and may postpone CMV-specific immune reconstitution during on-treatment follow-up [4]. The optimal timing for initiating and discontinuing LTV in real-world practice remains uncertain.

We conducted a single-center, retrospective analysis of the NICHE cohort, including 284 adult allo-HSCT recipients who received LTV prophylaxis between January 2022 and November 2023. Using dynamic exposure modeling, we search the relation between initiation and discontinuation windows for preventing clinically significant CMV infection (csCMVi), and we assessed immune reconstitution parameters between days 91 and 180 post-transplantation.

A total of 284 patients who received LTV prophylaxis after allo-HSCT were included in the analysis (Fig. S1). In addition, 54 patients receiving LTV as secondary prophylaxis use were summarized separately in Table S1. The median age was 37 years (range, 16-61 years), and 59.5% were male. The majority of patients had acute myeloid leukemia or myelodysplastic syndromes (61.3%), followed by acute lymphoblastic leukemia (23.6%) and aplastic anemia (6.3%). Most patients underwent haploidentical transplantation (81.3%), and 94.4% received myeloablative conditioning. Cyclosporine-based GVHD prophylaxis was used in 83.1% of cases, and grade III–IV acute GVHD before day 100 occurred in 16.5% (Table S2).

The median initiation time of LTV was 7 days (range, 0–28 days), and the median discontinuation time was 98 days (range, 57–383 days). During prophylaxis, the cumulative incidence of csCMVi was 25.4%, with no cases of CMV end-organ disease.

Dynamic exposure modeling identified post-transplant day 14 as the optimal initiation cut-point for LTV prophylaxis (P < 0.001; Fig. 1a). Patients who initiated LTV before 14 days (n = 244) after transplantation had a significantly lower incidence of csCMVi compared with those who started between days 14 and 28 (n = 40) (23.0% vs. 40.0%, P = 0.022; Fig. 1b). Early initiation did not affect the incidence of EBV reactivation (18.0% vs. 15.4%, P = 0.687), PTLD (5.3% vs. 12.5%, P = 0.084), or neutrophil engraftment (Fig. 1c–e). Notably, 38 patients (95%) in the group initiating LTV after day 14 had started LTV before engraftment, and no delayed engraftment was observed in the remaining two patients. These findings indicate that starting LTV prophylaxis before day 14 after transplantation may provide better antiviral protection without delaying hematopoietic recovery.

For discontinuation, dynamic exposure analysis of discontinuation thresholds revealed an optimal cut-point at post-transplant day 120 (P = 0.04; Fig. 1f). When stratified by discontinuation time (≤118 days, n = 208; 119–139 days, n = 39; and >140 days, n = 37), the 119–139 group had the lowest observed csCMVi incidence compared with the ≤118 days and >140 days groups (15.4% vs. 23.1% and 48.6%, respectively), with a significant difference versus the >140 days group (P = 0.002, Fig. 1g–i). These findings suggest that discontinuation between days 119 and 139 may represent a favorable window in this real-world cohort.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified both early initiation and appropriately timed discontinuation as independent protective factors against csCMVi (Fig. 1j). Initiating LTV before 14 days post-transplantation significantly reduced the risk of csCMVi (odds ratio [OR], 0.469; 95% CI, 0.221–0.991; P = 0.047). Similarly, discontinuation between 119 and 139 days (OR, 0.176; 95% CI, 0.056–0.557; P = 0.003) or before 118 days (OR, 0.259; 95% CI, 0.115–0.583; P = 0.001) was associated with a lower infection risk compared with discontinuation beyond day 140. Donor type was also associated with csCMVi risk, with HLA-matched sibling donor transplantation showing a protective effect (OR, 0.188; 95% CI, 0.042–0.84; P = 0.029).

Compared with the 403 patients who did not use LTV, the incidence of csCMVi in the patients who used LTV was significantly lower (25.4% vs. 56.1%, P < 0.001), while the infection of EB virus (17.7% vs. 7.2%, P < 0.001) and the incidence of PTLD (6.3% vs. 2.2%, P = 0.006) were significantly higher. Clinical characteristics are described in Table S2. Compared with the non-LTV group, the LTV group showed lower peripheral blood lymphocyte reconstitution after 91-180 days after transplantation (median CD4+ T cell count, 0.07×109/L vs. 0.13×109/L, P < 0.001; median CD8+ T cell count, 0.32×109/L vs. 0.52×109/L, P < 0.001), and IgG and IgM were also decreased (median IgG, 7.77 g/L vs. 9.46 g/L, P < 0.001; median IgM, 0.48 g/L vs. 0.61 g/L, P = 0.009) (Table S3). In the LTV group, patients with csCMVi had a lower peripheral blood B cell percentage than those without infection (median B cell percentage in lymphocytes, 0.55% vs. 1.94%, P = 0.017; Table S4). Patients who developed csCMVi were more likely to experience concurrent EBV viremia (31.9% vs. 12.8%, P < 0.001), and those with dual CMV-EBV infections showed a lower B cell percentage among lymphocytes (Table S5).

Out of 284 patients in the LTV group, 14.8% of the patients (n = 42) was diagnosed with csCMVi during LTV used (Table S6), whereas 10.9% of the patients (n = 31) was diagnosed with csCMVi after LTV discontinued (Table S7). Donor CMV-seronegative status was more common in patients who developed csCMVi after discontinuation than in those who did not (25.8% vs 10.0%, P = 0.038). These findings underscore the importance of individualized prophylactic strategies that integrate both host and donor immune factors.

As a real-world, single-center retrospective study, LTV duration was not strictly predefined, resulting in unavoidable variability. However, this variability also enabled broader data exploration. Early initiation of LTV before the first 14 days significantly reduced the incidence of csCMVi compared with later initiation, without affecting neutrophil engraftment or EBV reactivation. This indicates that early prophylaxis is safe and critical for maximizing antiviral coverage during the period of profound immunodeficiency immediately following transplantation. Within the first two weeks post-HSCT, the antiviral repertoire is immature, as myeloid and NK cells dominate while adaptive lymphocytes remain scarce [5]. Providing LTV coverage during this vulnerable phase likely prevents early viral replication and mitigates the risk of subsequent reactivation.

The recommended duration of LTV prophylaxis has historically been fixed at 100 or 200 days [1, 6]. However, in our cohort, a lower incidence of csCMVi was observed among patients who discontinued LTV around day 120 ( ± 10 days), whereas a higher incidence of CMV reactivation was observed among patients who continued prophylaxis beyond this window. This finding aligns with recent studies showing that CMV-specific cell-mediated immunity typically reconstitutes around day 120 after transplantation, particularly in haploidentical or unrelated donor settings [7]. The apparent paradox - higher csCMVi risk after longer prophylaxis - may reflect delayed immune recovery due to prolonged viral suppression and reduced antigen exposure [8, 9]. Thus, extending LTV beyond approximately 140 days might provide temporary virologic control at the expense of immune competence.

This study has several limitations. As a real-world, single-center retrospective study with a relatively homogeneous cohort, mainly consisting of haploidentical related donor transplantations, its findings may be most applicable to similar transplant settings and should be generalized with caution. In addition, LTV discontinuation was not fully standardized and may have been influenced by physician discretion, and patient-related factors, introducing potential confounding. Some immune-reconstitution differences were statistically significant, but the numerical differences were small in the setting of low post-transplant immune-cell counts. Thus, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. CMV-specific immune reconstitution during days 100-140 was also not specifically assessed. Prospective randomized controlled trials incorporating dynamic changes in patient status are needed to validate these findings and to determine the optimal timing of LTV discontinuation

In conclusion, our real-world analysis suggests that initiating LTV prophylaxis before 14 days and discontinuing between days 119 and 139 post-transplantation was associated with favorable csCMVi outcomes in this real-world cohort. These findings support an individualized approach to LTV management, integrating dynamic risk assessment to optimize both antiviral efficacy and immune recovery after allo-HSCT.
