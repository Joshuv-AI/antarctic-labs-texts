High-Dimensional Analysis of Single-Layer Attention for Sparse-Token Classification
Nicholas Barnfield, Hugo Cui, Yue M. Lu

Abstract
When and how can an attention mechanism learn to selectively attend to informative tokens, thereby enabling detection of weak, rare, and sparsely located features? We address these questions theoretically in a sparse-token classification model in which positive samples embed a weak signal vector in a randomly chosen subset of tokens, whereas negative samples are pure noise. In the long-sequence limit, we show that a simple single-layer attention classifier can in principle achieve vanishing test error when the signal strength grows only logarithmically in the sequence length L L , whereas linear classifiers require L \sqrt{L} scaling. Moving from representational power to learnability, we study training at finite L L in a high-dimensional regime, where sample size and embedding dimension grow proportionally. We prove that just two gradient updates suffice for the query weight vector of the attention classifier to acquire a nontrivial alignment with the hidden signal, inducing an attention map that selectively amplifies informative tokens. We further derive an exact asymptotic expression for the test error and training loss of the trained attention-based classifier, and quantify its capacity—the largest dataset size that is typically perfectly separable—thereby explaining the advantage of adaptive token selection over nonadaptive linear baselines.



Introduction

Attention-based architectures
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
have proven in recent years to be a major driver of progress in a wide spectrum of learning tasks, ranging from language processing
(
Kenton and Toutanova 2019
;
Brown et al. 2020
)
to computer vision
(
Dosovitskiy et al. 2021
)
.
A core strength of these models is the ability of attention layers to dynamically weigh the importance of different input tokens, enabling the model to selectively focus on the most relevant information. This flexibility makes transformers particularly effective at capturing subtle patterns and features within complex, high-dimensional data, even when such information is dispersed throughout the input sequence.

Despite the ubiquity of attention-based models in contemporary deep learning practice, a rigorous theoretical understanding of their working mechanism is still in its early stages. A large body of theoretical works has focused on understanding the benefits of attention in simple solvable models
(
Geshkovski et al. 2023
;
Ahn et al. 2023
;
Von Oswald et al. 2023
;
Edelman et al. 2022
;
Hahn 2020
;
Bordelon et al. 2024
;
Bietti et al. 2023
;
Abbe et al. 2024
;
Tiberi et al. 2024
;
Troiani et al. 2025
;
Cui 2025
;
Maulen-Soto et al. 2025
)
, with particular focus devoted to single-layer architectures
(
Lopardo et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Tian et al. 2023
;
Jelassi et al. 2022
;
Lu et al. 2024
;
Rende et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2024a
;
Cui et al. 2024c
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2023
)
. Recently, a line of studies has demonstrated the advantages of attention-based architectures for
sparse
token regression tasks—settings where labels depend only on a small subset of input tokens
(
Oymak et al. 2023
;
Marion et al. 2024
;
Sanford et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Ren et al. 2024
)
. In such tasks, attention mechanisms dynamically identify and prioritize the relevant tokens, significantly enhancing learning efficiency. In contrast, fully-connected architectures require exponentially more samples
(
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2025
)
or neurons
(
Sanford et al. 2023
)
as the input sequence length grows.

In many applications, however, the
sparsity
of informative features is frequently compounded by additional challenges, notably the
weakness
and
rarity
of the underlying signals. For example, cancer diagnosis from computed tomography scans involves detecting lesions — features that are typically subtle (weakness), appear in varying locations (sparsity), and occur infrequently (rarity). All these characteristics significantly complicate the detection problem.

Motivated by scenarios of this kind, we examine a statistical classification problem in which positive samples contain weak signals embedded within a randomly selected small subset of tokens. We analyze the capability of a single attention layer to learn to adaptively identify and enhance these sparse, weak, and potentially rare signals. Specifically, our
main contributions
are as follows:

•

In the limit of large sequence length
L
, we demonstrate that an attention-based method can detect signals that are exponentially weaker in
L
than those detectable by non-adaptive linear classifiers. Our analysis uncovers regimes in which generalization is either impossible or perfect.

•

Moving from representational power to learnability, we study training at finite
L
and derive an exact characterization—down to explicit constants—of the test error and training loss for the attention model after two gradient updates, followed by full optimization of the last-layer weights, in the limit of high-dimensional token embeddings with proportionally large sample sizes. These sharp asymptotic results quantify precisely how the test error depends on the number of samples, the sequence length, and the signal strength.

•

Our analysis demonstrates that merely two gradient steps suffice for the attention model to develop meaningful internal representations. Consequently, the classifier can dynamically identify and selectively focus on the relevant subset of signal-bearing tokens—effectively amplifying the signal-to-noise ratio—and outperform non-adaptive linear classifiers.

•

To provide a complementary perspective on the advantage of attention, we characterize the capacity of the attention model, defined as the maximal dataset size that can be perfectly fit with high probability, and compare it with the corresponding capacity of baseline linear classifiers.

Related works

Expressivity of transformers and attention models —

The expressivity of attention-based architectures has been extensively studied in recent literature. In particular,
Fu et al. 2024
established that a single multi-head attention layer with fixed weights can represent a broad class of permutation-invariant functions. Using covering number arguments,
Edelman et al. 2022
;
Trauger and Tewari 2024
;
Truong 2024
observed that the complexity of bounded-norm attention models scales only weakly with sequence length, suggesting a strong inductive bias toward sparse functions dependent on only a subset of input tokens.

Sparse token regression/classification tasks —

A special class of sparse functions is studied in further detail by
Sanford et al. 2023
, who consider a sequence-to-sequence task on length
L
sequences, where outputs correspond to the average of a dynamically selected subset of
R<L
tokens. Whereas fully-connected architectures require
\Omega(L)
hidden units to represent such functions, attention models only need
\Omega(R)
and can provably learn the task via gradient-based training on the population risk
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Complementing these findings,
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2025
establish corresponding results demonstrating significant separations in terms of sample complexity. Similarly,
Marion et al. 2024
;
Duranthon et al. 2025
prove that a softmax attention layer can learn a single-token regression up to Bayes-optimal error, whereas linear attention fails, and linear regression on flattened samples performs poorly due to its inability to adapt to dynamic sparsity. Additionally, recent work by
Zhang et al. 2025
analyzes a sparse classification task where the relevant token locations are fixed across samples. Closer to our work,
Oymak et al. 2023
study a related classification task with the same single-layer attention model as the one considered here, and prove that it reaches a good accuracy after a three steps of gradient descent, outperforming linear regression with average-pooling.

Our current work builds upon and significantly extends this line of research along multiple fronts. On the technical level, we crucially extend the analysis of sparse token tasks to
arbitrary
convex losses beyond the square loss which is considered in prior works
(
Marion et al. 2024
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Oymak et al. 2023
)
. Our extension importantly includes classical loss functions such as the logistic loss, of particular relevance for classification tasks. Furthermore, while most theoretical works have focused on studying the challenges posed by signal sparsity, we further address the often concurrent hurdles of signal rarity and weakness. We demonstrate that attention mechanisms can adaptively address all three challenges by dynamically selecting informative tokens and amplifying their signals.

High-dimensional solvable models of attention —

The model considered in this work, detailed in the next section, corresponds to a simplified model of attention operating in the limit of large token embedding dimension. Such high-dimensional attention models have proven valuable as theoretical testbeds, enabling precise characterizations of learning phenomena across various settings.
Lu et al. 2024
;
Rende et al. 2024
;
Erba et al. 2024
provide sharp asymptotic expressions for the test error of linear attention. More closely related to our analysis,
Cui et al. 2024a
demonstrate that, in a sequence-to-sequence task with finite
L
inputs, a single nonlinear self-attention layer can develop a dynamic attention mechanism once a critical number of training samples is exceeded, outperforming a linear regression baseline. Our model belongs to a broader class of sequence multi-index models, initially introduced and analyzed non-rigorously using statistical physics techniques in
Cui 2025
.
Troiani et al. 2025
present a rigorous investigation for the special case of Bayes-optimal statistical inference with deep attention models, while
Arnaboldi et al. 2025
study their dynamics. Closer to our work,
Duranthon et al. 2025
provide tight asymptotic characterizations for an attention model trained on a single-location regression task. Among our contributions, our manuscript provides a fully rigorous analysis of empirical risk minimization in a classification setting.

1
Problem setup

1.1
Sparse token classification

We consider a binary classification task on
L\times d
covariates, seen as sequences of
L
tokens embedded in
d
dimensions. Positive samples contain a weak signal added to a random subset of tokens; negative signals do not display the signal. The learning task consists of discriminating samples with the signal from those devoid thereof. In a similar spirit to the sparse-token regression/classification problems studied in
Sanford et al. 2023
;
Oymak et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Marion et al. 2024
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2025
, the difficulty of the task lies in the fact that the location of the signal varies from sample to sample — consequently, any successful classifier must dynamically detect and attend to the relevant tokens.

Formally, let
\mathcal{D}=\mathinner{\left\{X_{i},y_{i}\right\}}_{i\in[n]}
be the training data where each sample
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
has rows representing token embeddings, and the labels
{y_{i}}\in\{-1,+1\}
are such that
\mathbb{P}(y_{i}=1)=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\pi\in(0,1)
. We assume that the token matrices
\mathinner{\left\{X_{i}\right\}}_{i\in[n]}
are independent and drawn from one of two probability distributions. Specifically, for negative samples (namely given
y_{i}=-1
),

X_{i}=Z_{i},

(1)

where
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
is a matrix whose entries are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. In contrast, for positive samples (
y_{i}=1
),

X_{i}=\theta v_{i}\xi^{\top}+Z_{i},

(2)

where
\xi
is a fixed signal vector with
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert}=1
,
\theta>0
is the parameter indicating the signal strength, and
v_{i}
is a random binary-valued vector indicating the location of the hidden features:

v_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbbm{1}_{1\in R_{i}}&\dots&\mathbbm{1}_{L\in R_{i}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

Importantly, the subset
R_{i}
of informative tokens is allowed to follow an arbitrary distribution on all possible subsets of
[L]
of a given fixed cardinality
|R_{i}|=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}R\in\mathbb{N}
. Equivalently,
v_{i}
follows some distribution supported on
\{x\in\{0,1\}^{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sum_{\ell}x_{\ell}=R\}
whose marginals
p_{j}=\mathbb{P}(v_{j}=1)
satisfy
\|p\|=O\left(\nicefrac{{R}}{{\sqrt{L}}}\right)
.
This assumption essentially requires that the distribution is sufficiently spread out across tokens, and is not localized on any privileged tokens. In particular, when the law of
R_{i}
is the uniform distribution on all subsets of
[L]
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}=\nicefrac{{R}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
.
Therefore, an algorithm with the capacity to generalize on the task must be able to adaptively identify the subset
R_{i}
containing the signal, if the sample is positive, in addition to learning the signal vector
\xi
. The latter point is further rendered non-trivial by the observation that in (
2
), the signal part
\theta v_{i}\xi^{\top}
is of norm
\mathcal{O}(\theta\sqrt{R})
, which is considerably weaker than the background noise term
\lVert Z_{i}\lVert=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{Ld})
when
d
and/or
L
are large — thereby making the signal hard to detect. Note that this scaling differs from that considered in
Marion et al. 2024
where both terms are comparable in size — a regime corresponding to a more easily detectable signal in the limit of large dimension
d
. Finally, the assumption
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert p\right\rVert}=O(\nicefrac{{R}}{{\sqrt{L}}})
places the task in a particularly challenging case, as the delocalization of the signal makes it difficult to pinpoint and detect.

Intuitively, the data distribution (
2
) could be interpreted as a simple model of a vision task, where each token corresponds to a patch of an input image (e.g. a computed tomography scan), and where the location of the feature
\xi
signals the presence of a certain pattern (e.g. a lesion) at the corresponding position. This pattern is sparse (
R<L
), weak (
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\theta v\xi^{\top}\right\rVert}\ll\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Z\right\rVert}
), and potentially rare (small
\pi
).

The data distribution and task is closely related in spirit to that considered in
Oymak et al. 2023
, with however two important differences. While in
Oymak et al. 2023
the signal is present in all samples, in the current work the signal is totally absent from negative samples, posing the additional challenge of rarity. In addition, the relevant tokens
R_{i}
are devoid of any noise in
Oymak et al. 2023
and contain only the clean signal. On the other hand, in (
2
) the weak signal
\xi
is corrupted by the additive noise
Z_{i}
, posing the challenge of signal weakness.

1.2
Two linear classifier baselines

We first introduce two simple linear classifiers that will serve as reference models, providing benchmarks against which the attention model—specified in the next subsection—will be evaluated.

Vectorized linear classifier —

The first baseline flattens each matrix-valued input
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
into an
Ld
-dimensional feature vector
{\rm vec}(X_{i})=[(X^{1}_{i})^{\top}\dots(X^{L}_{i})^{\top}]^{\top}
, which is then fed to a linear classifier. Explicitly, the classifier is

\mathsf{L}_{w,b}^{\rm vec}(X)=\mathrm{sign}(\langle w,f_{\rm vec}(X)\rangle+b)\quad{\rm where}\;\;f_{\rm vec}(X)={\rm vec}(X),\;w\in\mathbb{R}^{Ld},b\in\mathbb{R}.

(3)

As noted in
Marion et al. 2024
, the location of the signal within the vector would then be shifting from sample to sample due to the randomness of
R_{i}
— making it challenging for this vectorized linear classifier to pinpoint the relevant features.

Pooled linear classifiers—

A possible remedy would be to instead average the input along its first dimension, rather than flattening it. More precisely, the classifier becomes

\mathsf{L}_{w,b}^{\rm pool}(X)=\mathrm{sign}(\langle w,f_{\rm pool}(X)\rangle+b)\quad{\rm where}\;\;f_{\rm pool}(X)=\frac{1}{L}\sum\limits_{k\in[L]}X^{k},\;w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(4)

While such an average-pooling featurization bypasses the challenge of dynamically shifting signal positions, it introduces another complication. Specifically, after averaging, the norm of the signal term
\|\frac{1}{L}1_{L}^{\top}v_{i}\xi^{\top}\|=\mathcal{O}({R}/{L})
can become significantly weaker compared to the background noise term
\|\frac{1}{L}1_{L}^{\top}Z_{i}\|=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{d/L})
, especially when
R
is small and
L
is large. In other words, the averaging procedure effectively reduces the signal-to-noise ratio of the data distribution.

These intuitions will be made precise in the following section by Proposition
2
and Proposition
1
, which show that a large signal strength
\theta
is needed to counteract these limitations, in order for linear classifiers to generalize.

1.3
An attention model

Ideally, to remedy the issue of signal dilution suffered by the pooled linear classifier, a non-uniform, sample-dependent reweighting of the tokens should instead be deployed, selectively placing more weights on tokens that embed the signal. As we will discuss and formalize, such a reweighting can be readily implemented by an attention-based mechanism. This intuition motivates the principal model analyzed in this work: a single-layer attention-based architecture designed to tackle the sparse token classification task. Specifically, we consider the model

\displaystyle\mathsf{A}_{q,w,b}(X)=\mathrm{sign}\big(\langle f_{q}(X),w\rangle+b\big),\qquad{\rm with}\qquad f_{q}(X)=X^{\top}\mathsf{softmax}(\beta Xq).

(5)

This attention model
\mathsf{A}_{q,w,b}
is parameterized by two trainable weight vectors
q,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a trainable scalar bias
b\in\mathbb{R}
. In (
5
), the parameter
\beta\geq 0
represents the inverse temperature of the softmax activation. The formulation (
5
) is a simplified attention model widely studied in theoretical contexts (see, e.g.,
Oymak et al. 2023
;
Marion et al. 2024
). The representation
f_{q}(X)
can be viewed as analogous to the
[CLS]
token used for classification and readout in transformer architectures
(
Kenton and Toutanova 2019
)
. A detailed discussion connecting this simplified model with standard self-attention architectures can be for instance found in
Oymak et al. 2023
;
Marion et al. 2024
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2023
.

Dynamic reweighting and signal amplification—

An important feature of the model (
5
) is that the weight vector
w
acts not directly on the raw input
X
, but instead on the attention-based representation:

\displaystyle f_{q}(X)=\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in[L]}\frac{e^{\beta\langle X^{k},q\rangle}}{\sum\limits_{\ell\in[L]}e^{\beta\langle X^{\ell},q\rangle}}X^{k},

(6)

where each token
X^{k}
is reweighted according to the scores
e^{\beta\langle X^{k},q\rangle}
. Crucially, in contrast to the naive average-pooling (
4
) discussed in subsection
1.2
(which corresponds to the special case of
q=0_{d}
), the attention scores dynamically adapt to the input tokens. Therefore, in principle, the attention mechanism can allocate greater weight to tokens containing the signal
\xi
, thus mitigating the diminished signal-to-noise ratio described following (
4
). Such improvement occurs when the internal attention parameter
q
aligns non-trivially with the signal vector
\xi
; this alignment increases the inner product
\langle X^{k},q\rangle
and consequently enhances the attention weights (
6
) for the signal-bearing tokens. In Section
3
we formalize and rigorously prove this intuitive mechanism.

2
Optimal test errors in the limit of long sequences

Before analyzing how effectively the attention model (
5
) and the two baseline models introduced in Section
1.2
(namely, the vectorized and average-pooled linear models) perform when
trained
on the sparse classification task described in Section
1.1
, it is instructive to first determine the conditions under which these models can, in principle, learn the task. In this section, we examine the
optimal test error
of the considered hypothesis classes, measuring their intrinsic ability to represent the sparse classification problem. Formally, the optimal test error for any predictor
\hat{y}_{W}(X)
parametrized by some finite-dimensional parameters
W
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\hat{y}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\underset{W}{\inf}~\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\hat{y}_{W}]\quad{\rm where}\quad\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\hat{y}_{W}]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{P}_{X,y}\left[\hat{y}_{W}(X)\neq y\right].

(7)

The optimal test error corresponds to the smallest misclassification error achievable by the classifier, provided its parameters
W
are selected optimally. Concretely, for the vectorized (
3
) and pooled (
4
) linear classifiers defined herein,
W
is given by
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{Ld}\times\mathbb{R}
and
(w,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
respectively whereas for the attention model (
5
) one has
W=(w,q,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.
In the present section, we are thus minimizing directly the population test error with respect to the parameters. Naturally, this minimization requires oracle knowledge of the data distribution, generically unknown to the statistician — in practice, the model parameters need to be instead estimated from finite datasets. The analysis of this learnability will be the object of the following section.

The following results show that the two baseline linear classifiers discussed in subsection
1.2
necessitate, in the limit of long sequence length
L\to\infty
, a very strong signal strength
\theta
to achieve nontrivial test error.

Proposition 1

(Optimal test error of the pooled classifier)
.

Suppose that the limit

\mathsf{SNR}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{\theta R}{\sqrt{L}}

(8)

exists. Then, for the pooled classifier (
4
), the optimal test error satisfies

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool}]=\begin{cases}0&\rm{if}~~\mathsf{SNR}=\infty,\\
(1-\pi)\Phi(b^{\ast})+\pi\Phi(-b^{\ast}-\mathsf{SNR})&\rm{if}~~\mathsf{SNR}\in(0,\infty)\\
\min(\pi,1-\pi)&\rm{if}~~\mathsf{SNR}=0\\
\end{cases}.

(9)

In the above display,

b^{\ast}=-\frac{\mathsf{SNR}}{2}-\frac{1}{\mathsf{SNR}}\log(1/\pi-1)
, and
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

Proposition 2

(Optimal test error of the vectorized classifier)
.

Suppose that the limit in (
8
) exists. Then, the optimal test error of the vectorized classifier satisfies

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}]=\begin{cases}0&\rm{if}~~\mathsf{SNR}=\infty,\\
\min(\pi,1-\pi)&\rm{if}~~\mathsf{SNR}=0\\
\end{cases}.

(10)

Moreover,

\liminf_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}]>0\quad\rm{if}\ \mathsf{SNR}\in(0,\infty).

The proofs of Propositions
1
and
2
are detailed in Appendices
B.1
and
B.2
. They imply the following results:
To generalize perfectly on sparse signals
R=\Theta_{L}(1)
, both the pooled linear classifier
\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool}_{w,b}
and the vectorized linear classifier
\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}_{w,b}
require a strong signal strength
\theta=\Omega_{L}(\sqrt{L})
. If the signal is weaker, namely
\theta=o_{L}(\sqrt{L})
and thus
\mathsf{SNR}=0
, the model performs no better than the naive predictor that always outputs the majority label and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}=\min(\pi,1-\pi)
.
In the intermediate regime where
\mathsf{SNR}\in(0,\infty)
, Propositions
1
and
2
show that the optimal test error is bounded away from zero by a strictly positive number.

In sharp contrast, the attention model (
5
) can achieve perfect classification even when the embedded signal is significantly weaker, as established in the following proposition.

Proposition 3
.

Consider the attention model
\mathsf{A}
(
5
). In the limit
L\to\infty
,
R=\Theta_{L}(1)
, allowing the signal
\theta
to depend on
L
, suppose that

\liminf_{L\to\infty}\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{2\log{L}}}>1.

(11)

Then, the attention model
\mathsf{A}
achieves an optimal test error of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\mathsf{A}]=0
.

The proof of Proposition
3
can be found in Appendix
B.3
. A direct consequence of Proposition
3
is that a significantly milder signal strength of order
\theta=\sqrt{\log L}
suffices for the attention model (
5
) to perfectly learn the sparse token classification task—provided it employs optimal parameters
q,w,b
.

Finally, we highlight the important role played by the softmax nonlinearity in the attention model by considering an “approximate attention classifier”, where the softmax in (
5
) is replaced by
a
n element-wise activation function. As shown in the following proposition (whose proof can be founded in Appendix
B.4
), such “approximate attention classifiers” would still require a strong signal (in particular,
\theta=\Omega(\sqrt{L})
) for perfect classification, and thus they cannot fundamentally improve upon linear classifiers.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a continuous and non-decreasing function, such that
\varphi(0)>0
,

\lim_{x\to-\infty}\varphi(x)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}\varphi(x)=1.

(12)

Let us introduce the “approximate attention” model

\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{q,w,b}=\mathrm{sign}\left(\langle\widetilde{f}_{q}(X)),w\rangle+b\right),\qquad{\rm with}\qquad\widetilde{f}_{q}(X)=X^{\top}\varphi(Xq).

(13)

where
\varphi(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is interpreted as an element-wise application of
\varphi
.
Defining
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}]
for the optimal error achieved by the model
\widetilde{A}_{q,w,b}
. Suppose that the limit in (
8
) exists. Then

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}]=\begin{cases}0&{\rm{if}}~~\mathsf{SNR}=\infty,\\
\min(\pi,1-\pi)&{\rm{if}}~~\mathsf{SNR}=0.\end{cases}

(14)

In the large-sequence limit
L\gg 1
, Propositions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
thus conclusively show the superiority of attention-based methods for sparse token classification tasks, establishing a clear separation of their expressivity in terms of the signal strength required for perfect generalization. These results complement recent findings in regression settings, which establish separation between attention models and fully-connected networks in terms of required number of neurons
(
Sanford et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Closer to our work, for a regression task,
Marion et al. 2024
evidence a regime where the attention model achieves the Bayes-optimal test error, while vectorized linear regression displays strictly larger optimal test error. Finally,
Oymak et al. 2023
presents a similar characterization of the optimal error of the pooled and attention models, but for a noise-free data model on the relevant tokens. In contrast, we consider the case where the signal contained in the relevant tokens is corrupted by the additional noise
Z_{i}
(
2
). In addition, the results of Propositions
1
and
2
reveal a sharp phase transition—between perfect classification, nontrivial classification [where the error is strictly smaller than
\min(\pi,1-\pi)
], and trivial classification [where the error is equal to
\min(\pi,1-\pi)
], all in terms of a single signal-to-noise ratio parameter defined in (
8
).

While Propositions
1
,
2
and
3
paint a clear separation between the attention model (
5
) and the two linear baselines (
3
) (
4
) in terms of representation power and oracle test errors, they leave the question of learnability largely open. Furthermore, it is not obvious whether this clear-cut distinction, established in the large-
L
limit, still holds when the sequence length
L
is finite. Thus, a more nuanced analysis of the training at finite sample complexity and sequence length is warranted.
This is the objective of the following section.

3
Precise asymptotic analysis of the learning

In what follows, we turn to the study of the training of the three models on finite datasets, aiming to precisely characterize the learning behavior of the attention model (
5
) and the two linear classifier baselines (
3
) and (
4
) in this regime. Such exact characterizations become particularly tractable in the high-dimensional embedding limit, as demonstrated by a growing body of literature on high-dimensional attention mechanisms
Rende et al. 2024
;
Cui et al. 2024a
;
Troiani et al. 2025
;
Tiberi et al. 2024
;
Cui 2025
;
Erba et al. 2024
;
Duranthon et al. 2025
. We adopt throughout the remainder of this manuscript the following high-dimensional, finite-length scaling regime:

Assumption 1

(High-dimensional, finite-length limit)
.

We consider the limit of large embedding dimension
d
and comparably large number of samples
n
, namely
d,n\to\infty
with fixed ratio
\alpha=\nicefrac{{n}}{{d}}=\Theta_{d}(1).
The chosen scaling
n\sim d
is such that the detection of the weak signal
\xi
from the background
Z
is statistically possible
(
Lesieur et al. 2015
)
, yet not trivial. Meanwhile, the sequence length
L
, signal strength
\theta
, sparsity
R
, along with all other parameters, remain finite and fixed.

3.1
Training procedure

We now turn to the learning process. The attention model (
5
) can be trained to solve the sparse token classification task defined in subsection
1.1
by performing empirical risk minimization over the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\mathinner{\left\{X_{i},y_{i}\right\}}_{i\in[n]}
, formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{q},\hat{w},\hat{b}\in\underset{q,w,b}{\rm argmin}~~\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}}(q,w,b)

\displaystyle{\rm with}\qquad\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}}(q,w,b)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{(X,y)\in\mathcal{D}}\ell(\langle f_{q}(X),w\rangle+b;y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert w\right\rVert}^{2}.

(15)

Here,
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\times\{-1,1\}\to\mathbb{R}
is a loss function that is convex with respect to its first argument (for example, the logistic loss
\ell(z,y)=\log(1+\exp(-yz))
or the quadratic loss
\ell(z,y)=\frac{1}{2}(z-y)^{2}
). The empirical risk (
3.1
) also includes a ridge regularization of strength
\lambda
. Notably, compared to prior studies on sparse token tasks
(
Sanford et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Mousavi-Hosseini et al. 2025
;
Marion et al. 2024
;
Oymak et al. 2023
)
which only consider the squared loss, our setting extends to general convex loss functions.

A natural approach to solving the non-convex optimization problem (
3.1
) is to run gradient descent on the set of trainable parameters
q,w,b
. In fact, as demonstrated below, just
two
gradient steps are sufficient for the query weights
q
to achieve an alignment with the signal
\xi
. This alignment enables the attention model (
5
) to develop internal representations capable of effectively identifying and amplifying the hidden signal. Specifically, we consider the following training procedure:

1.

Initialization —
Partition the training data
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{0}\cup\mathcal{D}_{1}
into two disjoint sets of sizes
n_{0}
and
n_{1}=n-n_{0}
respectively. We assume
\alpha_{0}=\nicefrac{{n_{0}}}{{d}}=\Theta(1)
, and
\alpha_{1}=\nicefrac{{n_{1}}}{{d}}=\Theta(1)
. Initialize the weights of the attention model (
5
) as
w^{(0)}=q^{(0)}=0_{d}
,
b^{(0)}=0
.

2.

First gradient step on
b,q,w
—
Perform a first gradient step on each of the trainable parameters, using the training set
\mathcal{D}_{0}
with learning rates
\eta_{b},\eta_{q},\eta_{w}
:

\displaystyle b^{(1)}=b^{(0)}-\eta_{b}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}b}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(q,w,b)\big|_{b^{(0)},q^{(0)},w^{(0)}},

\displaystyle w^{(1)}=w^{(0)}-\eta_{w}\nabla_{w}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(q,w,b)\big|_{b^{(0)},q^{(0)},w^{(0)}},

\displaystyle q^{(1)}=q^{(0)}-\eta_{q}\nabla_{q}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(q,w,b)\big|_{b^{(0)},q^{(0)},w^{(0)}}.

More explicitly, we obtain the parameters

\displaystyle w^{(1)}=-\frac{\eta_{w}}{n_{0}L}\sum_{i\leq n_{0}}h_{i}(0,0,0)X_{i}^{\top}1_{L},

\displaystyle b^{(1)}=-\frac{\eta_{b}}{n_{0}}\sum_{i\leq n_{0}}h_{i}(0,0,0),

\displaystyle q^{(1)}=0_{d},

(17)

where
1_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
denotes a vector with all elements equal to
1
, and we have also introduced the shorthand notation

h_{i}(q,w,b)=\ell^{\prime}(\langle f_{i}(q),w\rangle+b;y_{i}).

3.

Second gradient step on
q
—
Note that after a first step,
q^{(1)}
remains zero — a simple consequence of the initialization
w^{(0)}=0_{d}
. For the attention model (
5
) to develop a non-trivial internal representation parametrized by
q\neq 0_{d}
, a second gradient step on
q
is thus needed:

\displaystyle q^{(2)}

\displaystyle=q^{(1)}-\eta\nabla_{q}\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}(q,w,b)\big|_{b^{(1)},q^{(1)},w^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta_{q}\beta}{n_{0}L}\sum_{i\leq n_{0}}h_{i}(0,w^{(1)},b^{(1)})X_{i}^{\top}\left(I-\frac{1_{L}1_{L}^{\top}}{L}\right)X_{i}w^{(1)}.

(18)

4.

Full training of
w,b
—
Having developed a meaningful internal representation parametrized by
q^{(2)}
, the readout weight
w
and bias
b
are finally fully updated by empirical risk minimization on the retained data
\mathcal{D}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\hat{w},\hat{b}=\underset{w,b}{\rm argmin}~~\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}(q^{(2)},w,b).

(19)

The performance of the trained attention model
\mathsf{A}_{q^{(2)},\hat{w},\hat{b}}
is measured by

its training loss and test error

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}=\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}(q^{(2)},\hat{w},\hat{b}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}=\mathbb{P}_{X,y}\left[\mathsf{A}_{q^{(2)},\hat{w},\hat{b}}(X)\neq y\right].

(20)

The primary purpose of the dataset partitioning performed in step 1—splitting the data into two subsets, used respectively for steps 2–3 and step 4—is to simplify the subsequent analysis of step 4. This partitioning ensures statistical independence between the learned query weights
q^{(2)}
and the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{1}
. Adopting a more practical viewpoint,
\mathcal{D}_{0}
can also be viewed as a
pre-training
dataset used to train the query weights
q
, which can then be frozen as the model is deployed on other datasets, with only the readout and bias
w,b
being fine-tuned on
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.
Similar stage-wise training protocols with sample splitting have previously been analyzed in the context of two-layer neural networks
Ba et al. 2022
;
Moniri et al. 2023
;
Cui et al. 2024b
;
Dandi et al. 2024
;
Dandi et al. 2023
, demonstrating how even a single gradient step on the first-layer weights can yield meaningful internal network features. Analogously, in our setting, two gradient steps on the query weights
q
are already sufficient for the attention model to develop informative internal representations. For transformer models, similar few-step analyses were conducted for instance in
Bietti et al. 2024
;
Oymak et al. 2023
, albeit without the final step of full empirical risk minimization.

We are now in a position to present our next technical results: a precise characterization of the test error (
20
) achieved by the attention model (
5
), trained using the four-stage procedure detailed in subsection
3.1
. In the following sections, we first analyze step 3—demonstrating precisely how the query weights
q^{(2)}
develop an alignment with the signal
\xi
, resulting in nontrivial attention weightings. We then examine how this learned attention mechanism leads to an improvement in the test error (
20
), as compared to the baseline linear classifiers (
4
) and (
3
) at the conclusion of step 4.

3.2
Characterization of the attention weights after two gradient steps

The first technical result of this section characterizes how, at the end of step
3
(see subsection
3.1
), the query weights
q=q^{(2)}
develop a non-zero alignment with the signal vector
\xi
. As will be discussed in a subsequent subsection, this alignment allows the attention model (
5
) to develop internal representation adapted to the task.
Before stating the result, we make the following assumption on the loss function.

Assumption 2
.

The loss function is of the form
\ell(z,y)=\tilde{\ell}(yz)
for some convex function
\tilde{\ell}(\cdot)
. This assumption is in particular satisfied by the logistic and quadratic losses on
\mathbb{R}\times\{-1,+1\}
. We further denote

C_{\mathsf{loss}}\coloneqq-y\partial_{z}\ell(z,y)|_{z=0}=-\tilde{\ell}^{\prime}(0).

(21)

For the logistic (resp. quadratic) loss, we can check that
C_{\mathsf{loss}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
(resp.
C_{\mathsf{loss}}=1
).

Theorem 1

(Characterization of the query weights
q^{(2)}
after two gradient steps)
.

Let
w^{(1)},b^{(1)}
be the readout weights and bias of the attention model
\mathsf{A}
(
5
) at the end of step 2 of the training procedure detailed in subsection
3.1
. In the asymptotic limit of Assumption
1
, the summary statistics
b^{(1)},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert w^{1}\right\rVert}
and
\langle w^{1},\xi\rangle
converge in probability to deterministic limits, given by

b^{(1)}\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{P}C_{\mathsf{loss}}\eta_{b}(2\pi-1),

(22)

while

\displaystyle\|w^{(1)}\|\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{P}\gamma_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\eta_{w}C_{\mathsf{loss}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}L}+\left(\frac{\pi\theta R}{L}\right)^{2}},

\displaystyle\langle w^{(1)},\xi\rangle\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{P}\gamma_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\eta_{w}C_{\mathsf{loss}}\frac{\theta\pi R}{L}.

(23)

Similarly, let
q^{(2)}
denote the query weights at the end of step
3
. The summary statistics
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q^{(2)}\right\rVert}
and
\langle q^{(2)},\xi\rangle
converge in probability to the limits

\displaystyle\|q^{(2)}\|\!\!\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{P}

(24)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\eta_{q}\beta}{L}\Bigg[(L-1)\gamma_{1}^{2}\left((L-1)E_{1}^{2}+\frac{E_{3}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)+\theta^{2}\left(\!\!R-\frac{R^{2}}{L}\!\!\right)\left(\gamma_{2}^{2}\frac{E_{4}}{\alpha_{0}}+2\gamma_{2}^{2}E_{2}(L-1)E_{1}\right)+\theta^{4}\gamma_{2}^{2}\left(\!\!R-\frac{R^{2}}{L}\!\!\right)^{2}\!\!E_{2}^{2}\Bigg]^{\!\!\frac{1}{2}},

(25)

and

\langle\xi,q^{(2)}\rangle\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{P}\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=-\frac{\eta_{q}\beta\gamma_{2}}{L}\left[(L-1)E_{1}+\theta^{2}(R-R^{2}/L)E_{2}\right].

(26)

Here,
E_{1},E_{2},E_{3},E_{4}
are constants whose expressions are given in Appendix
C
.

Equations (

), (

), (

), and (

) precisely characterize the parameters of the attention model (

) at the conclusion of step 3 of the training procedure described in subsection

. The detailed proof of Theorem

is provided in Appendix

. These theoretical predictions are illustrated in Fig.

The formulas in (
24
) and (

) become much more interpretable if we consider the regime where the sample complexity
\alpha_{0}
is large.

Corollary 1

(Cosine similarity)
.

In the asymptotic limit of Assumption
1
, the cosine similarity
\nicefrac{{\langle q^{(2)},\xi\rangle}}{{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q^{(2)}\right\rVert}}}
converges in probability to a limit
s_{q}
, whose absolute value admits the expansion

\displaystyle|s_{q}|=1-\frac{C}{\alpha_{0}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}\right).

(27)

The expression of the constant
C
involves
\ell,R,\theta,L,\eta_{w},\eta_{q}
, and is detailed in Appendix
C
. The sign of
s_{q}
is on the other hand given by that of

\displaystyle-\left[(L-1)+\theta^{2}R(1-\nicefrac{{R}}{{L}})\right]\pi\ell^{\prime}\left(C_{\mathsf{loss}}\eta_{b}(2\pi-1)+\frac{\eta_{w}\pi R^{2}\theta^{2}}{2L^{2}},1\right)-(1-\pi)\ell^{\prime}\left(C_{\mathsf{loss}}\eta_{b}(2\pi-1),-1\right)

(28)

and depends on all the parameters of the problem.

The above result shows that the alignment between the query weights after two gradient steps
q^{(2)}
and the signal
\xi
, as captured by the cosine similarity
\nicefrac{{\langle q^{(2)},\xi\rangle}}{{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert q^{(2)}\right\rVert}}}
, tends rapidly in
absolute value
to its maximal value of
1
as the sample complexity
\alpha_{0}
is increased, at a rate of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{0}}}
. The sign of the alignment, however, is given by an intricate but explicit condition (
28
) on all the parameters in the problem
\ell,\pi,\theta,R,L,\eta_{b},\eta_{w}
, and can in certain cases be negative — signaling that the query vector
q
detrimentally anti-aligns with the signal
\xi
. In order to avoid such a scenario, the condition (
28
) can offer some guideline for choosing the hyperparameters
\eta_{b},\eta_{w},\ell
. For example, for the logistic loss
\ell(y,z)=\log(1+\exp(-yz))
, when
\pi<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
(resp.
\pi>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
), choosing
\eta_{b}
sufficiently large (resp. negative) ensures
s_{q}>0,
namely that the query weights
q^{(2)}
properly align with
\xi
when
\alpha_{0}
grows.

As a final remark, we observe from (
23
) that after a single gradient step, the readout weight vector
w^{(1)}
already succeeds in partially learning the signal vector
\xi
, as signaled by a non-zero cosine similarity
\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1}
therewith. However, due to the vanishing query weights initialization, the attention has yet to develop meaningful internal representations, and still implements at this stage the suboptimal average-pooling (
4
), diluting the signal. As a consequence, the readout weight can only very partially retrieve
\xi
, and the overlap
\gamma_{2}/\gamma_{1}
(
23
) decays with the sequence length as
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{L}}}.
However, this initial alignment between
w^{(1)}
and
\xi
proves crucial at the later step
3
of the protocol
3.1
, allowing the query weights to develop an alignment with the signal
\xi
after a further gradient update. As discussed at the end of
1.3
, this alignment crucially enables a dynamic attention-based reweighting of the tokens (
6
), instrumental to learn the sparse token classification task.

3.3
Characterization of final test and training errors

Having described steps
1-3
of the training procedure
3.1
, we now focus on step
4
, where given
q^{(2)}
, the readout weights
w
and the bias
b
are fully trained on the held-out data batch
\mathcal{D}_{1}
.
We note that once
q
is fixed, the empirical risk minimization (
19
) amounts to training a linear model with weights
w,b
on the high-dimensional non-linear features
f_{q^{(2)}}(X)
(
6
). While the behavior of such linear classifiers is in general very well understood in the asymptotic limit of Assumption
1

(
Candès and Sur 2020
;
Liang and Sur 2022
;
Montanari et al. 2019
;
Mai et al. 2019
;
Loureiro et al. 2021
;
Mignacco et al. 2020a
)
, such works very often build on the assumption of simple (e.g. Gaussian or Gaussian mixture) data distributions. In the present case however, the features
f_{q^{(2)}}(X)
possess a highly non-trivial distribution, as they result from the non-linear attention mechanism. Fortunately, the softmax non-linearity acts only on the low-dimensional projections of the tokens along the query weights
q^{(2)}
, which can be treated separately. The idea of the proof and derivation builds from the observation, formalized in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

The feature vectors
f_{q^{(2)}}(X)
and the labels
y
admit the following statistical representation:

\left(f_{q^{(2)}}(X);y\right)\overset{(d)}{=}\left(c_{q}q^{(2)}+c_{\xi}\xi+c_{z}P_{q^{(2)},\xi}^{\perp}z;\ y\right),

where
P_{q^{(2)},\xi}^{\perp}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto the space orthogonal to
q^{(2)}
and
\xi
, and
\mathinner{\left\{c_{q},c_{\xi},c_{z}\right\}}
are scalar random variables that are independent of the isotropic Gaussian vector
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},I_{d})
. The joint law of the random variables
y,c_{q},c_{\xi},c_{z}
is given by

\displaystyle c_{q}\overset{(d)}{=}\delta_{y,1}\left(\langle g,s_{+}\rangle-\frac{\gamma\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{+}\right\rVert}z_{0}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)+\delta_{y,-1}\left(\langle g,s_{-}\rangle-\frac{\gamma\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{-}\right\rVert}z_{0}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle c_{\xi}\overset{(d)}{=}\delta_{y,1}\left(\langle\theta v,s_{+}\rangle+\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{+}\right\rVert}z_{0}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)+\delta_{y,-1}\left(\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{-}\right\rVert}z_{0}}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle c_{z}\overset{(d)}{=}\delta_{y,1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{+}\right\rVert}+\delta_{y,-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert s_{-}\right\rVert}.

(31)

Above,
z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1),g\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{L},I_{L})
and
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0_{d},I_{d})
are mutually independent Gaussian random vectors, and
s_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathsf{softmax}\big(\beta(\|q\|g+\langle q^{(2)},\xi\rangle\theta v)\big),s_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathsf{softmax}(\beta\|q^{(2)}\|g).
We recall that
\gamma=\langle q^{(2)},\xi\rangle
denotes the alignment between the query weights and the signal, characterized in Theorem
1
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is given in Appendix
A
. Lemma
1
proceeds by decomposing the input
X
into its low-dimensional projection
g
along the query weights
q^{(2)}
, which determines the non-linear softmax attention weights
s_{+},s_{-}
, and a high-dimensional orthogonal Gaussian component in which the feature retains a linear dependence. Similarly, one can decompose the weights
w
into its components
\mu_{\xi},\mu_{q}
in
{\rm span}(q^{(2)},\xi)
, and its orthogonal
d-2
dimensional component
x
. Splitting the risk minimization (
19
) on
w
into minimizations on
\mu_{\xi},\mu_{q}
and
x
allows to rewrite (
19
) as

\min_{\mu_{q},\mu_{\xi},b}\left[\phi(\mu_{q},\mu_{\xi},b)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{q}&\mu_{\xi}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\
\gamma&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{q}\\
\mu_{\xi}\end{pmatrix}\right],

(32)

where

\phi(\mu_{q},\mu_{\xi},b)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{x\in{\rm span}(q^{(2)},\xi)^{\perp}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i\in[n_{1}]}\ell(\langle c_{z,i}z_{i},x\rangle+c_{q,i}\mu_{q}+c_{\xi,i}\mu_{\xi}+b;y_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}^{2}.

(33)

The problem is thereby decoupled into a 3-dimensional
outer problem
(
32
), bearing over the bias
b
and the projections
\mu_{\xi},\mu_{q}
of the readout weight
w
in
{\rm span}(q^{(2)},\xi)
, and a high-dimensional
inner problem
(
33
), bearing over the orthogonal component
x
. Importantly, the challenging high-dimensional problem (
33
) now involves the simple covariates
c_{z,i}z_{i}
. The distribution of the latter simply corresponds to a mixture of Gaussians centered around the origin
0_{d}
, akin to those considered e.g. in
Adomaityte et al. 2023
. As a result, the inner problem is amenable to being studied using technical tools from high-dimensional probability. In our proof, detailed in Appendix (
D
), we employ the leave-one-out analysis of
Karoui 2018
. A key result that can thus be attained is that for any
\mu_{q},\mu_{\xi},b
, the inner risk
\phi(\mu_{q},\mu_{\xi},b)
converges in probability in the asymptotic regime of Assumption
1
. Plugging the resulting limit into the outer problem (
32
) in turns yields a characterization of the training loss
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
as the solution of a three-dimensional problem that explicitly depends on all the parameters of the problem. By the same token, a similar limit can be derived for the test error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}
. These results are succinctly summarized in the following theorem, while the full technical statement is deferred to Appendix
D
.

Theorem 2

(Test and training errors after step
4
)
.

The test error and training loss associated to the empirical risk minimization (
19
) converge in probability in the limit of Assumption
1
to deterministic limits
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{A}]
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}[\mathsf{A}]
, whose expression are deferred for clarity to Appendix
D
.

provides an exact characterization—precise down to explicit constants—of the test error attained by the attention model (

Baseline classifiers —

Having characterized the test error and training loss of the attention model, we now turn to the case of the two linear classifiers (
3

4

\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool}_{w,b}

\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool}_{w,b}

\displaystyle\hat{w},\hat{b}\in\underset{w,b}{\rm argmin}~~\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathcal{D}_{1}}(w,b)

In the asymptotic limit of Assumption

, the test error and training loss for the pooled (resp. vectorized) linear classifier converge in probability to limits

.

2
and
3
, let us focus on the particular case of a quadratic loss function

Corollary 2

(Ridgeless quadratic loss)
.

For a quadratic loss function
\ell(z,y)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(y-z)^{2}
, the asymptotic limits
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{A}],\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool}],\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}]
characterized in Theorems
2
and
3
admit a well-defined limit as the strength of the regularization vanishes,
\lambda\to 0
. The corresponding limits further admit the following expansion in the sample complexity
\alpha_{1}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{A}]=\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{A}]+\frac{\Delta_{\mathsf{A}}}{\alpha_{1}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\right),

(35)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool}]=\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}]+\frac{\Delta_{\mathsf{L}}}{\alpha_{1}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\right),

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}]=\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}]+L\frac{\Delta_{\mathsf{L}}}{\alpha_{1}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\right).

(37)

The constants
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{A}],\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}],\Delta_{\mathsf{A}},\Delta_{\mathsf{L}}
admit closed-form expressions which we detail in Appendices
F
.

A number of interesting conclusions can be garnered from Corollary
2
, whose proof is sketched in Appendix
F
. First, all three test errors tend to their respective
\alpha_{1}\to\infty
limit at the same
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{1}}}
rate, as the sample complexity
\alpha_{1}
is increased. Furthermore, the two linear classifiers
\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool},\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}
tend to a common limit
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}]
. This finding somewhat echoes the intuition from Proposition
2
, which already suggested that both models share similar oracle — and thus plausibly infinite sample complexity— behaviors. Further even, the leading
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{1}}}
corrections for the two models only differ by a factor
L
. This surprisingly simple relationship can be intuitively accounted for remembering that the vectorized classifier
\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}
operates in a
L
times larger dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{Ld}
, and thus the effective sample complexity is in fact
\nicefrac{{n_{1}}}{{Ld}}=\nicefrac{{\alpha_{1}}}{{L}}
. Lastly, one may naturally wonder which of the limiting test errors
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{A}],\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}]
is lower – in particular, whether the attention model always achieves a lower error provided it is given sufficient data. The answer is more nuanced, and crucially depends on the alignment
s_{q}
(see Corollary
1
) between the query weights
q^{(2)}
and the signal
\xi
achieved after step 3 of the training protocol. As shown in Fig.
5
in Appendix
D
,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{A}]>\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}]
can hold in some settings for
s_{q}
sufficiently small. In simple words, when the query weights have insufficiently aligned with the signal – e.g. as a result of insufficient data
\alpha_{0}
or bad choice of the hyperparameters
\eta_{w,b}
–, the attention suffers from a misaligned internal representation, and achieves a worse error than the simpler linear classifiers. For moderate and large
s_{q}
on the other hand,
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{A}]<\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}[\mathsf{L}]
and the attention profits from the advantage of the dynamical reweighting implemented by its internal representation.

The previous subsection compared the three models in terms of their test errors. We adopt in this subsection a complementary perspective, and analyze the
capacity

\alpha^{\star}
of the models
\mathsf{A}_{q^{(2)},w,b},\mathsf{L}^{\rm{pool}}_{w,b}
and
\mathsf{L}^{\rm{vec}}_{w,b}
, defined as the (normalized) maximal number of training samples that can typically be fitted by the models to vanishing training loss. More formally, let
\hat{y}\in\{\mathsf{A},\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool},\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}\}
be one of the three models, and let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}[\hat{y}](\alpha_{1})
designate the asymptotic training loss characterized in Theorems
2
and
3
, in the limit of vanishing regularization
\lambda\to 0
, for the logistic loss
\ell(y,z)=\log(1+\exp(-yz))
. For clarity, we make explicit the dependence of the latter on the sample complexity
\alpha_{1}
. The
capacity
of the model
\hat{y}
is then formally defined as

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star}_{\hat{y}}=\sup_{\alpha\geq 0}\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}[\hat{y}](\alpha)=0\}

(38)

For
\alpha<\alpha^{\star}_{\hat{y}}
, the training set is small enough so that it can with high probability be perfectly separated by the model and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}[\hat{y}](\alpha)=0
. At large sample complexities
\alpha>\alpha^{\star}_{\hat{y}}
, such perfect classification becomes typically impossible, resulting in a positive training loss
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}[\hat{y}](\alpha)>0
. The capacity of a model hence captures how easily it can classify samples from a given data distribution, with a higher capacity thus intuitively reflecting a higher adequacy of the model to the task. An analytical expression for the capacity can be extracted from the characterizations of the training loss
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
provided by Theorems
2
and
3
, which we report in the following Conjecture.

Conjecture 1

(Capacities)
.

The capacity of the linear classifiers
\mathsf{L}^{\rm pool},\mathsf{L}^{\rm vec}
and for the attention model
\mathsf{A}_{q^{(2)},w,b}
(with frozen query weights
q^{(2)}
characterized in Theorem
1
), admit the following expressions:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}=\max_{s\in[0,1],\mathfrak{b}}\frac{L(1-s^{2})}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi^{\prime}\left(\mathfrak{b}+\frac{\theta R}{\sqrt{L}}s+u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi^{\prime}\left(u-\mathfrak{b}\right)\right]u^{2}du},

(39)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}=\max_{s\in[0,1],\mathfrak{b}}\frac{(1-s^{2})}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi^{\prime}\left(\mathfrak{b}+\frac{\theta R}{\sqrt{L}}s+u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi^{\prime}\left(u-\mathfrak{b}\right)\right]u^{2}du},

(40)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}=\max_{m_{q},m_{\xi},\mathfrak{b}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{y,c_{z},c_{\xi},c_{q}}\left[c_{z}^{3}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{c_{z}^{2}u+y(b+c_{q}m_{q}+c_{\xi}m_{\xi})}{c_{z}}\right)u^{2}du\right]}.

(41)

We remind that the joint law of

in the expression of

is detailed in Lemma
1
, and depends in particular on
\langle q^{(2)},\xi\rangle
.

The derivation of the expressions (
39
) is detailed in Appendix
G
. Because they involve some heuristic step, we state the result as a conjecture.
The capacity of linear classifiers has been studied in a rich line of prior works
(
Candès and Sur 2020
;
Mignacco et al. 2020a
;
Loureiro et al. 2021
)
, impulsed by the seminal work of
Cover 2006
, although no analytical expressions have been to our awareness reported for the data distribution considered in the present work. Such results are on the other hand scarce for attention-based models. Conjecture
1
contributes to bridging this gap, by reporting an analytical expression for the capacity of the simple attention model (
5
).

A number of notable observations may be made regarding Conjecture
1
. First, note that the capacities of the pooled classifier
\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}
and the vectorized classifier
\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}
are related by a simple factor
L
, namely
\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}=\nicefrac{{\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}}}{{L}}
. This observation, which is reminiscent of the similar conclusion from Corollary
2
regarding the leading correction to the test errors, can once more be rationalized by the fact that the vectorized model acts in a
L-
times larger space, and thus perceives an effective sample complexity that is
L
times smaller. Yet, this remarkably simple relationship between the capacities of the two models remains perhaps unexpected. Second, both capacities depend on the parameters of the problem solely through the ratio
\nicefrac{{\theta R}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
, which captures a notion of signal-to-noise ratio. The attentive reader may remember that this ratio made an appearance already in Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
, where its
L\to\infty
limit controlled the behavior of the optimal test error. This similar observation in the context of the model capacities solidifies the status of this ratio as a central parameter of the problem, which subsumes the difficulty of the sparse token classification task for linear classifiers.

The theoretical predictions (
39
) are plotted in Fig.
3
, where they are overlayed upon numerical evaluations of the training loss
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
, revealing good agreement. In the probed setting,
\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}>\alpha_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}>\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}
, and the attention model displays a higher capacity than the pooled classifier, while the higher capacity of the vectorized classifier can again be explained from its operating in a
L-
times higher dimensional space. As we discussed above, the observation
\alpha_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}>\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}=\nicefrac{{\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}}}{{L}}
temptingly suggests that the attention model is better suited to classify the considered data distribution. Another perspective can be given, remembering that all three models boil down to logistic regression on different representations
f_{\rm pool}(X)
(
4
),
f_{\rm vec}(X)
(
3
),
f_{q^{(2)}}(X)
(
6
) : a higher capacity then intuitively signals a better separation between positive and negative classes in representation space. Importantly, we finally note that the capacity of the attention model crucially depends on the alignment
s_{q}
between the query weights
q^{(2)}
and the signal
\xi
, as characterized in Corollary
1
. We discuss in Appendix
G
how a small
s_{q}
—resulting, for instance, from insufficient pretraining data
\alpha_{0}
or bad choice of hyperparameters
\eta_{w,b}
— can cause the attention model to have a lower capacity than the pooled classifier, namely
\alpha_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}<\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}
. This echoes a similar observation at the level of the test error made in the previous subsection, and discussed in Appendix
D
.

Conclusion

The present work studies the sparse token classification task of detecting a sparse, weak, and rare signal embedded in sequential data. For long sequences, we rigorously establish a clear performance separation between linear and attention-based classifiers, showing that attention-based models require significantly weaker signals to achieve perfect generalization. For finite sequences, we provide a sharp analysis of the learning of a simple attention model in a high-dimensional limit. Specifically, our study demonstrates how merely two gradient steps are sufficient for the attention mechanism to learn meaningful internal representations, enabling the model to dynamically identify and focus on tokens containing the relevant signal. Moreover, we derive a sharp characterization of the resulting test error, quantifying precisely the performance gain achieved by the attention model relative to the linear classifier baselines. Finally, we put these results in perspective by analyzing the capacity of the three models.

Proposition 3

Consider the attention model
\mathsf{A}
(

\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}[\mathsf{A}_{q,w,b}]=0

Proof.

Let
q,w\in{\rm span}\{\xi\}
, set
\rho=\|q\|
, and without loss of generality take
\beta=1
. As
q
and

are co-linear with

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\mathsf{A}_{q,w,b}]=(1-\pi)\ \mathbb{P}\left(\langle g,s_{-}\rangle>b\right)+\pi\mathbb{P}\left(\langle g,s_{+}\rangle+\langle\theta v,s_{+}\rangle<b\right)

Set

. Recall the standard result for the maximum of iid standard Gaussians (see e.g.

\frac{\tilde{M}_{L}}{\sqrt{2\log L}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}1\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{M_{L}}{\sqrt{2\log L}}\xrightarrow{a.s.}1.

Proposition 4

Suppose

1.

\varphi(x)

2.

\lim_{x\to-\infty}\varphi(x)=0

\lim_{x\to\infty}\varphi(x)=1

3.

\varphi(0)>0

We begin with a preliminary lemma to be used in establishing the above result.

Lemma 4

Let

\inf_{\beta\geq 0}{\rm Var}(g\varphi(\beta g))=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}>0.

Proof.

Let

. Then

by assumption
(1)
of Proposition

\lim_{\beta\to\infty}g\varphi(\beta g)=\begin{cases}g,&{\rm if}\;\;g>0\\
0,&{\rm if}\;\;g\leq 0\end{cases}

. Hence, there exists some

Now, by continuity of

on the compact interval

is bounded, this can only happen if

Proof of Proposition
4
.

Throughout we assume that

\|q\|
int o
\beta

(63)

Sufficient Condition.

We begin by showing that
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}=0
when
\mathsf{SNR}=\infty
. To this end, we consider the particular choice of

for which the test error simplifies to

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{q,w,b}]=(1-\pi)\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta>\tilde{b}\right)+\pi\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta<\tilde{b}-\frac{\theta}{\sqrt{L}}\langle\varphi_{+},v\rangle-\delta_{1}\right)

(67)

By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(E_{1})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{L}}}\sum_{b\leq R}|g_{b}|\geq 1\right)=O(R/\sqrt{L})\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}0

\mathbb{P}(E_{2})\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}0

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}\leq(1-\pi)\mathbb{P}(\Delta>\tilde{b})+\pi\mathbb{P}\left(\Delta<\tilde{b}-\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{\sqrt{L}}}\sum_{b\leq R}\varphi(\theta-2\sqrt{\log L})+1\right)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1})+\mathbb{P}(E_{2})

and
\Delta
converges in distribution to a centered Gaussian random variable as

\tilde{b}=\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{2\sqrt{L}}}\sum_{b\leq R}\varphi(\theta-2\sqrt{\log L})
.

Necessary Condition.

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\ast}=\min(\pi,1-\pi)
if
\mathsf{SNR}=0
. We have

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{q,w,b}]=(1-\pi)\mathbb{P}\left(\langle w,q\rangle\Delta-\|P_{q}^{\perp}w\|\frac{\|\varphi_{-}\|}{\sqrt{L}}z>\tilde{b}\right)+\pi\mathbb{P}\left(\langle w,q\rangle\Delta-\|P_{q}^{\perp}w\|\frac{\|\varphi_{+}\|}{\sqrt{L}}z<\tilde{b}+\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}+\delta_{3}\right)

deterministically and since

and notice that

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\|\varphi_{-}\|^{2}}{L}>\frac{\varphi(0)^{2}}{4}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sum_{b\leq L}1_{\{g_{b}>0\}}}{L}>\frac{1}{4}\right)\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}1,

and so

is bounded in
[1/4,1]
in probability as

. Therefore, since

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{b=1}^{R}[\varphi(\beta(g_{b}+\theta\langle\xi,g\rangle))^{2}-\varphi(\beta g_{b})^{2}]\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}0

in probability, we have that
\mathbb{P}(|\delta_{3}|>\varepsilon/3)\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}0
uniformly over
\beta
as
\varphi
is bounded. Hence,

\sup_{\beta,w,q}\mathbb{P}(|\delta|>\varepsilon)\xrightarrow{L\to\infty}0.

Now, let us denote

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}[\widetilde{\mathsf{A}}_{q,w,b}]=(1-\pi)\mathbb{P}(U>b)+\pi\mathbb{P}(U<b+\delta).

where we rename
\tilde{b}
to

to ease notation. Observe that

\displaystyle\{U<b\}\supseteq\{U<b-\varepsilon\}\cap\{|\delta|<\varepsilon\}\supseteq\{U<b\}\setminus[\{|\delta|>\varepsilon\}\cup\{b-\varepsilon<U<b\}]

(70)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{L\to\infty}\left[\min(\pi,1-\pi)-\mathbb{P}(|\delta|>\varepsilon)-\mathbb{P}(b-\varepsilon<U<b)\right]

\displaystyle=\min(\pi,1-\pi)-\liminf_{L\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(b-\varepsilon<U<b).

(72)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(b-\varepsilon<U<b)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(v_{1}<-z<v_{2})

(74)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\{v_{1}<-z<v_{2}\}\cap\{\nicefrac{{\|\varphi_{-}\|}}{{\sqrt{L}}}>\nicefrac{{\varphi(0)}}{{2}}\})+\mathbb{P}(\nicefrac{{\|\varphi_{-}\|}}{{\sqrt{L}}}>\nicefrac{{\varphi(0)}}{{2}})

(77)

Leave-one-out analysis

A key step in the following consists in

It is straightforward to verify the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let

\tilde{r}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\tilde{f}_{i},\widetilde{x}_{i}\rangle+\epsilon_{i}=\Prox_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i,{\setminus i}};\gamma_{i}),

where
\tilde{r}_{i,{\setminus i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\tilde{f}_{i},x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}\rangle+\epsilon_{i}

\gamma_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tilde{f}_{i}^{\top}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}.

(104)

Moreover,

\widetilde{x}_{i}=x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}-\frac{1}{n_{1}}\ell^{\prime}_{i}(\tilde{r}_{i})H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}

and

\widetilde{\Phi}_{i}=\Phi^{\ast}_{\setminus i}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathcal{M}_{i}(\langle\tilde{f}_{i},x^{\ast}_{\setminus i}\rangle).

Remark 5

Let
x=\Prox(c;\gamma)

\ell^{\prime}(x)+\frac{x-c}{\gamma}=0.

(106)

D.3.2
On the boundedness of
\ell^{\prime}

A key technical difference with the closely related analysis of
Karoui 2018
lies in the assumption made therein that
\ell^{\prime}
is bounded. We would like the present results to hold for the quadratic loss in particular, which does not satisfy this assumption. The following lemma bridges this gap by showing how the optimizer of the inner problem using loss
\ell
coincides, with high probability, with that of a modified loss whose derivative is bounded.

Definition 2
.

Given
I>0
, we define the clipped loss
\ell_{\clip}(\cdot,y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
as follows:

1.

\ell_{\clip}\in C^{4}_{b}
1
1

1

Four times differentiable with continuous and bounded derivatives.
and convex

2.

\ell_{\clip}(z)=\ell(r)
for
z\in[-I,I]

3.

Letting
M=\sup_{z\in[-I,I]}|\ell^{\prime}(z)|
, we require that
\|\ell^{\prime}_{\clip}\|_{\infty}\leq 2M

4.

we further require that
\ell_{\clip}\leq\ell
.

The properties given for
\ell_{\clip}
in definition
2
can be achieved in the following manner. Consider the “bump function"

\psi(t)=\begin{cases}\exp{\left(\frac{1}{t(t-1)}\right)},&{\rm if}\;t\in(0,1)\\
0,&{\rm else}\end{cases}

and, fixing a
\iota>0
, define
\eta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
by

\eta(z)=\begin{cases}0,&\;z\leq I\\
\frac{\int_{0}^{(z-I)/\iota}\psi(t)\,{\rm d}t}{\int_{0}^{1}\psi(t)\,{\rm d}t},&z\in(I,I+\iota),\\
1,&z\geq I+\iota\end{cases}

Note that
\eta\in C^{\infty}
with bounded derivatives of all orders. Now, consider the left and right linear extensions of
\ell
,

L_{-}(z)=\ell(-I)+\ell^{\prime}(-I)(z+I),\qquad L_{+}(z)=\ell(I)+\ell^{\prime}(I)(z-I),

which allow us to define
\ell_{\clip}
as the piecewise function

\ell_{\clip}(z)=\begin{cases}L_{-}(z),&z\leq-I-\iota\\
(1-\eta(-z))\ell(z)+\eta(-z)L_{-}(z),&z\in(-I-\iota,-I)\\
\ell(z),&z\in[-I,I]\\
(1-\eta(z))\ell(z)+\eta(z)L_{+}(z),&z\in(I,I+\iota)\\
L_{+}(z),&z\geq I+\iota\end{cases}

The prescribed properties of definition

are then easily verified from basic calculus.

Lemma 6

(Clipped loss derivative)
.

Recall that

\displaystyle x^{\ast}=\argmin_{x}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{i\in[n_{1}]}\ell_{i}(\langle\tilde{f}_{i},x\rangle+\epsilon_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}.

(107)

For a given
\delta\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{2}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(\epsilon;y)\right],

\displaystyle I\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1+\mathfrak{R})\sqrt{2\ln\frac{2n_{1}}{\delta}}+1+\sqrt{\mu_{q}^{2}+\frac{\left(\mu_{q}\gamma+\mu_{\xi}\right)^{2}}{1-\gamma^{2}}}+|b|+\sqrt{L\theta^{2}}

(108)

where
\epsilon\sim c_{q}\mu_{q}+c_{\xi}\mu_{\xi}+b

\displaystyle x^{\ast}_{\clip}=\argmin_{x}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{i\in[n_{1}]}\ell_{\clip,i}(\langle\tilde{f}_{i},x\rangle+\epsilon_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x\right\rVert}.

(109)

Then, with probability at least

\displaystyle x^{\ast}=x^{\ast}_{\clip}.

(110)

Proof.

The strategy consists in controlling the supremum

\displaystyle|r_{i}^{\clip}|\leq|\Prox_{\clip}(\langle x^{\ast}_{\clip,{\setminus i}},\tilde{f}_{i}\rangle+\epsilon_{i},\gamma_{i})|+\delta^{(1)}\leq\left|\mathfrak{g}_{i}\right|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{\ast}_{\clip,{\setminus i}}\right\rVert}+\delta^{(1)}+|\epsilon_{i}|

(111)

using the contractivity of the proximal operator. We used the shorthand

\delta^{(1)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{i}|r_{i}^{\clip}-\Prox_{\clip}(\langle x^{\ast}_{\clip,{\setminus i}},\tilde{f}_{i}\rangle+\epsilon_{i},\gamma_{i})|=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{\ast}_{\clip,{\setminus i}}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell_{\clip}(\epsilon_{j};y_{j})\leq\mathfrak{R}^{2}+\delta^{(2)},

with

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{\ast}_{\clip,{\setminus i}}\right\rVert}\leq\mathfrak{R}+|\delta^{(2)}|

\displaystyle\sup_{i}|r^{\clip}_{i}|\leq(\sup_{i}|\mathfrak{g}_{i}|)(\mathfrak{R}+|\delta^{(2)}|)+\delta^{(1)}+\sup_{i}|\epsilon_{i}|.

(113)

For
n_{1}
large enough, from Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\delta^{(1)}>1]\leq\frac{\delta}{6},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|\delta^{(2)}|>1]\leq\frac{\delta}{6}.

(114)

We now need to control the term
\sup_{i}|\epsilon_{i}|
. From (
97

\mu_{\xi},\mu_{q},b

\displaystyle|\epsilon_{i}|\leq a_{\epsilon}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g_{i}\right\rVert}_{1}+b_{\epsilon}|z_{0,i}|+c_{\epsilon}

(115)

\displaystyle a_{\epsilon}=|\mu_{q}|,

\displaystyle b_{\epsilon}=\left|\mu_{q}\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}+\mu_{\xi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\right|,

We remind that all entries of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{i}|\epsilon_{i}|\geq\sqrt{a_{\epsilon}^{2}+b_{\epsilon}^{2}}h+c_{\epsilon}\right]\leq\sum\limits_{i\in[n_{1}]}\mathbb{P}\left[a_{\epsilon}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g_{i}\right\rVert}_{1}+b_{\epsilon}|z_{0,i}|\geq\sqrt{a_{\epsilon}^{2}+b_{\epsilon}^{2}}h\right].

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\sup_{i}|r^{\clip}_{i}|>(1+\mathfrak{R})\sqrt{2\ln\frac{2n_{1}}{\delta}}+1+\sqrt{a_{\epsilon}^{2}+b_{\epsilon}^{2}}\sqrt{2\ln n_{1}}+c_{\epsilon}\right]\leq\delta.

D.3.3
Concentration results

We first introduce and recall several quantities of importance in this section. For

(144)

But

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\check{\Lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\check{\Lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}F_{\setminus i}F_{\setminus i}^{\top}\check{\Lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda I_{n_{1}-1}\right)^{-1}\check{\Lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\check{\Lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}F_{\setminus i}F_{\setminus i}^{\top}\check{\Lambda}^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda I_{n_{1}-1}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda},

(145)

using the fact that similar matrices share the same operator norm. Thus

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\varepsilon\right\rVert}.

(146)

On
\varepsilon
—

We now turn our attention to
\varepsilon
. Using the closed-form expression for
\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}}
from Lemma
5
:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i,k}\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})^{2}\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2n_{1}^{3}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i,k}\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})^{2}\tilde{f}_{i}^{\top}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{j}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}\right|

(147)

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})^{2}}{2n_{1}^{3}}\tilde{f}_{i}^{\top}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\left[\sum\limits_{j\neq i,k}\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle\tilde{f}_{j}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}\right]H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}\right|

(148)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})^{2}}{2n_{1}^{3}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\sum\limits_{j\neq i,k}\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle\tilde{f}_{j}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}\right\rVert}

(149)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}^{2}}\right)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{\setminus i\setminus k}^{\top}\mathsf{D}F_{\setminus i\setminus k}\right\rVert}.

(150)

In the last step, we denoted
\mathsf{D}
the diagonal matrix with elements
\mathsf{D}_{jj}=\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{\setminus i\setminus k}^{\top}\mathsf{D}F_{\setminus i\setminus k}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{\setminus i\setminus k}\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{D}\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{\setminus i\setminus k}^{\top}F_{\setminus i\setminus k}\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathsf{D}\right\rVert}

(151)

\displaystyle\leq O_{L_{k}}({\rm polyLog}(n_{1})n_{1})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{(3)}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\sup_{j\neq i,k}|\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle|=O_{L_{k}}({\rm polyLog}(n_{1})n_{1})

(152)

Using the fact that the maximum of
n_{1}
independent standard Gaussians is
O_{L_{k}}({\rm polyLog}(n_{1}))
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i,k}\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})^{2}\langle\tilde{f}_{j},\tilde{f}_{k}\rangle\right|=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right).

(153)

The remaining two terms of
\varepsilon_{k}
can be shown to be
O_{L_{k}}(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{n_{1}}})
and so

\displaystyle|\varepsilon_{k}|\leq O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right)

(154)

Finally,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert}\leq O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right),

(155)

which concludes the proof.

∎

(156)

We called

(157)

the full Hessian.

Proof.

Lemma 10

(
\Phi^{\ast}
concentrates)
.

We have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\Phi^{\ast}]=O\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right)

(158)

Proof.

Appealing to the Efron-Stein lemma, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\Phi^{\ast}]\leq\sum\limits_{i\in[n_{1}]}\mathbb{E}\left[(F^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-F_{\setminus i}(x_{\setminus i}^{\ast}))^{2}\right]

(159)

The summand can be controlled as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(F^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-F_{\setminus i}(x_{\setminus i}^{\ast}))^{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(F_{\setminus i}^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-F_{\setminus i}(x_{\setminus i}^{\ast})\right)^{2}\right]+\frac{2}{n_{1}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{i}(r_{i})^{2}\right]

(160)

We first control the second term.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell_{i}(r_{i})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}2\left(\ell_{i}(0)^{2}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{\prime}\right\rVert}_{\infty}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i}^{2}\right]\right).

(161)

As we will later show in Remark
6
, the moments of
r_{i}
are indeed bounded, making the right hand-side
O(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{n_{1}^{2}}})
. Note that the current result is not used to reach Remark
6
, so there is no circular argument. We finally examine the first term. By the mean value theorem,

\displaystyle F^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-F_{\setminus i}(x_{\setminus i}^{\ast})=\left\langle\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell_{i}^{\prime}(\check{r}_{j})\tilde{f}_{i}+\lambda\frac{x^{\ast}+x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}}{2},x^{\ast}-x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}\right\rangle,

(162)

where
\check{r}_{j}
belongs to the (unordered) interval
(r_{j},\tilde{r}_{j,{\setminus i}})
. We now show that both terms in the scalar product are small. First, we will use the fact that the first term is close to
F_{\setminus i}(x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}})
, which is by definition of
x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}
vanishing. More precisely,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell_{i}^{\prime}(\check{r}_{j})\tilde{f}_{i}+\lambda\frac{x^{\ast}+x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}}{2}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}(\ell_{i}^{\prime}(\check{r}_{j})-\ell_{i}^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{j,{\setminus i}}))\tilde{f}_{i}+\lambda\frac{x^{\ast}-x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}}{2}\right\rVert}

(163)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{\prime\prime}_{i}\right\rVert}_{\infty}|\check{r}_{j}-\tilde{r}_{j,{\setminus i}}|+\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{\ast}-x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}\right\rVert}=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right).

(164)

Since
\check{r}_{j}\in(r_{j},\tilde{r}_{j,{\setminus i}})
,

\displaystyle|\check{r}_{j}-\tilde{r}_{j,{\setminus i}}|\leq|r_{j}-\tilde{r}_{j,{\setminus i}}|=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right)

(165)

from Theorem 2.2 of
Karoui 2018
. From Theorem 2.2. and Lemma C.2 of
Karoui 2018
, we further have
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{\ast}-x^{\ast}_{{\setminus i}}\right\rVert}=O_{L_{k}}\left(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{\sqrt{n_{1}}}}\right)
. Therefore, from Cauchy-Schwartz,

\displaystyle|F^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-F_{\setminus i}(x_{\setminus i}^{\ast})|=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right).

(166)

Putting everything together,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(F^{\ast}(x^{\ast})-F_{\setminus i}(x_{\setminus i}^{\ast}))^{2}\right]=O\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}^{2}}\right)

(167)

and

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\Phi^{\ast}]=O\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right)

(168)

from the Efron-Stein inequality, concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma 11

(
\chi
concentrates)
.

Recall
\chi=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}}}\tr[H^{-1}]
. The following concentration result holds:

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\chi]=O\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right).

(169)

Proof.

From the Efron-Stein lemma,

\displaystyle{\rm Var}[\chi]\leq\sum\limits_{i\in[n_{1}]}\mathbb{E}\left[(\chi-\chi_{\setminus i})^{2}\right],

(170)

where
\chi_{\setminus i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}}}\tr[H^{-1}_{\setminus i}]
. We recall

\displaystyle H_{\setminus i}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\tilde{f}_{j}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}+\lambda I_{n_{1}}.

(171)

Let us further decompose

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\chi-\chi_{\setminus i})^{2}\right]\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[(\chi-\tilde{\chi}_{i})^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{\chi}_{i}-\chi_{\setminus i})^{2}\right],

(172)

defining

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tr[H^{-1}_{i}],

\displaystyle H_{i}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{r}_{j,i})\tilde{f}_{j}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}+\lambda I_{n_{1}}.

(173)

We first focus on
\mathbb{E}\left[(\chi-\tilde{\chi}_{i})^{2}\right]
.

\displaystyle\chi-\tilde{\chi}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tr[H^{-1}(H_{i}-H)H^{-1}_{i}]

(174)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\left[\ell^{\prime\prime}(r_{j})-\ell^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{r}_{j,i})\right]\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\ell^{\prime\prime}(r_{i})\frac{\tilde{f}_{i}^{\top}H^{-1}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{i}}{n_{1}}

(175)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})(r_{j}-\tilde{r}_{j,i})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\ell^{\prime\prime}(r_{i})\frac{\tilde{f}_{i}^{\top}H^{-1}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{i}}{n_{1}}

(176)

where we used the mean value theorem and
\check{r}_{j}\in(r_{j},\tilde{r}_{j,i})
. Thus,

\displaystyle|\chi-\tilde{\chi}_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{n_{1}}|\langle\delta,\varrho\rangle|+O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert}_{\infty}{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\lambda^{2}n_{1}}\right).

(177)

we introduce the vectors
\delta,\varrho\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}-1}
with elements

\displaystyle\delta_{j}=(r_{j}-\tilde{r}_{j,i})

(178)

\displaystyle\varrho_{j}=\ell^{(3)}(\check{r}_{j})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}.

(179)

The latter can be controlled as
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\varrho\right\rVert}=O_{L_{k}}(\sqrt{n_{1}}{\rm polyLog}(n_{1}))
while from Lemma
9
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\delta\right\rVert}=O_{L_{k}}(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{\sqrt{n_{1}})}})
. Thus, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies

\displaystyle|\chi-\tilde{\chi}_{i}|=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right).

(180)

We now examine
\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{\chi}_{i}-\chi_{\setminus i})^{2}\right]
. From a Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{i}-\chi_{\setminus i}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}_{\setminus i}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}+\frac{1}{2n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(4)}(\check{s}_{j})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})^{2}\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}_{\setminus i}H^{-1}_{i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}

(181)

for some
\hat{s}_{j}\in(\tilde{r}_{j,i},\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})
.
From Lemma C.4 of
Karoui 2018
,
|\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}}|=O_{L_{k}}(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{\sqrt{n_{1}}}})
, from which it follows that the second term is
O_{L_{k}}(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{n_{1}}})
. The objective is now to approximate
H_{i}
in the first term by the
\tilde{f}_{i}-
independent Hessian
H_{\setminus i}
, to unravel all statistical dependencies on
\tilde{f}_{i}
. The correction is

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}_{\setminus i}(H^{-1}_{i}-H^{-1}_{\setminus i})\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{(3)}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\sup_{j\neq i}|\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}}|\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\tilde{f}_{j}\right\rVert}^{2}}{\lambda n_{1}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{i}^{-1}-H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\right\rVert}

(182)

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{(3)}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\sup_{j\neq i}|\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}}|\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\frac{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\tilde{f}_{j}\right\rVert}^{2}}{\lambda^{3}n_{1}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{i}-H_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}.

(183)

But

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert H_{i}-H_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\hat{t}_{j})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\tilde{f}_{j}\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}\right\rVert}\leq\sup_{j\neq i}\left|\ell^{(3)}(\hat{t}_{j})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\right|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\hat{\Sigma}_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}

(184)

where
\hat{\Sigma}_{\setminus i}
is the empirical covariance of the features, excluding the
i-
th. Putting everything together yields

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-1}_{\setminus i}(H^{-1}_{i}-H^{-1}_{\setminus i})\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}\right|=O_{L_{k}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right).

(185)

Thus, going back to the original objective,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{\chi}_{i}-\chi_{\setminus i})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})(\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}\right)^{2}\right]+O\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}^{2}}\right)

(186)

Leveraging the closed-form expression of
\tilde{r}_{j,i}-\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}}
, the first term can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\{\tilde{f}_{j}\}_{j\neq i}}\left[\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{j}\right\rVert}^{4}\mathbb{E}_{g}\left[g^{4}\right]\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

(187)

using Minkovski’s inequality;
g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in the expression above. Note that, introducing the vector
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}-1}
with elements
h_{j}=\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\nicefrac{{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}}{{n_{1}}}

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{j}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}h^{\top}F_{\setminus i}H^{-1}_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda n_{1}^{2}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert h\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}.

(188)

But
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert F_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}=O_{L_{k}}(\sqrt{n_{1}}{\rm polyLog}(n_{1}))
, and

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert h\right\rVert}\leq\sqrt{n_{1}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{(3)}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\sup_{j\neq i}\left|\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}\right|\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{(3)}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n_{1}}}\sup_{j\neq i}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\tilde{f}_{j}\right\rVert}^{2}=O_{L_{k}}({\rm polyLog}(n_{1})\sqrt{n_{1}}).

(189)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum\limits_{j\neq i}\ell^{(3)}(\tilde{r}_{j{\setminus i}})\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H_{\setminus i}^{-1}\tilde{f}_{i}\frac{\tilde{f}_{j}^{\top}H^{-2}_{\setminus i}\tilde{f}_{j}}{n_{1}}\right)^{2}\right]=O\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}^{2}}\right).

(190)

To complete the proof, we need control of
\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})^{4}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}
, which is provided by the proof of Lemma (
9
), where we established that
\ell^{\prime}(\tilde{r}_{i,i})=O_{L_{k}}(1)
.
∎

D.3.4
Limiting residual distributions

It now remains to ascertain the law of
\tilde{r}
, which we describe in the following lemma.

Lemma 12

(Limiting distribution of
\tilde{r}_{i{\setminus i}}
)
.

The leave-one-out residual admit the simple representation

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{i,{\setminus i}}=\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right)

(191)

with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
independently from
\epsilon_{i},c_{z,i}
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{i{\setminus i}}-\epsilon_{i}=\left\langle\tilde{f}_{i},\nicefrac{{x_{\setminus i}}}{{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}}}\right\rangle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}

(192)

and
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tfrac{1}{c_{z,i}}\left\langle\tilde{f}_{i},\nicefrac{{x_{\setminus i}}}{{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}}}\right\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Furthermore, from the proof of proposition C.7 of
Karoui 2018
,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}^{2}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x^{\ast}\right\rVert}^{2}+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right)+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right)

(193)

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right).

(194)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}=\nu\sqrt{1+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right)}=\nu+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right),

(195)

using the inequality
|\sqrt{1+x}-1|\leq|x|
in the last step. Finally,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{2}\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}-\nu\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}-\nu\right)^{2}\right]=O\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{n_{1}}\right),

(196)

in other words

\displaystyle Z\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert x_{\setminus i}\right\rVert}-\nu\right)=O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right).

(197)

∎

Lemma 13

(Limiting distribution of
\tilde{r}_{i,i}
)
.

Setting
\chi_{E}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}[\chi]
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{i,i}=\Prox(\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z;c_{z,i}^{2}\chi_{E})+O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right).

(198)

Proof.

Let us introduce the shorthands
\delta_{r}=\tilde{r}_{i{\setminus i}}-\epsilon_{i}-c_{z,i}\nu Z
and
\delta_{\chi}=\gamma_{i}-c_{z,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right]
. From Lemma
5
,

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{r}_{i,i}-\Prox(\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z;c_{z,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right])\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\Prox(\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z+\delta_{r};c_{z,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right]+\delta_{\chi})-\Prox(\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z;c_{z,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right])\right|

(199)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+c_{z,i}^{2}\check{\chi}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\check{r})}\delta_{r}+\frac{\ell^{\prime}(\check{r})}{1+c_{z,i}^{2}\check{\chi}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\check{r})}\delta_{\chi},

(200)

using the two-variable mean value theorem, and eliciting the derivatives of the proximal function.
\check{r},\check{\chi}
are on the line between the points
(\tilde{r}_{i{\setminus i}}-\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z+\delta_{r},c_{z,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right]+\delta_{\chi})
and
(\tilde{r}_{i{\setminus i}}-\epsilon_{i}+c_{z,i}\nu Z,c_{z,i}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right])
. From Lemma
13
,
\delta_{r}=O_{L_{2}}(\nicefrac{{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}}{{\sqrt{n_{1}}}})
. For the second term

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{\ell^{\prime}(\check{r})}{1+c_{z,i}^{2}\check{\chi}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\check{r})}\delta_{\chi}\right|\right]\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\ell^{\prime}\right\rVert}_{\infty}|\delta_{\chi}|

(201)

But

\displaystyle|\delta_{\chi}|\leq|\gamma_{i}-c_{z,i}\chi|+c_{z,i}|\chi-\mathbb{E}\left[\chi\right]|=O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right)

(202)

One thus reaches

\displaystyle\frac{\ell^{\prime}(\check{r})}{1+c_{z,i}^{2}\check{\chi}\ell^{\prime\prime}(\check{r})}\delta_{\chi}=O_{L_{2}}\left(\frac{{\rm polyLog}(n_{1})}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\right).

(203)

Putting everything together, one thus reaches that

(204)

Lemma 15

By the construction of the Hessian matrix

can be explicitly expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}=(1-\pi)\Phi\left(\frac{(2\pi-2)(1+\pi\mathcal{X}^{2})}{2\pi\mathcal{X}(1-\pi)}\right)+\pi\Phi\left(\frac{2\pi-1+\pi\mathcal{X}^{2}(1-\pi)}{2\pi\mathcal{X}(1-\pi)}\right).

(316)

This incidentally corresponds to the residual error achieved by the pooled classifier trained with ridgeless quadratic loss (
287
), since for the considered data distribution
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{X}=\nicefrac{{\theta R}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
. We also furthermore have a similar correspondence at the level of the summary statistics, namely
b^{\infty}=\hat{b}^{\infty},\nu^{\infty}=\nicefrac{{\mu_{2}^{\infty}}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
, where
\hat{b}^{\infty},\nicefrac{{\mu_{2}^{\infty}}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
are defined for the pooled model in (
285
). Furthermore, the leading order corrections are related by a simple factor
L
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test},\mathsf{vector}}-\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test},\mathsf{pool}}-\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}}=L+o(1).

(317)

Note that a consequence of Remark
9
is that in the
\alpha\to\infty
limit, for ridgeless regression with a quadractic loss, the pooled and vectorized models converge to the same solution, in the sense that the weights of the vectorized model correspond to that of the pooled model stacked
L
times. Both models furthermore yield the same limiting test error. Let us also comment that
Arnaboldi et al. 2025
also observe a similar speed up between related flattened and pooled models learning from sequential data, in a related task, in terms of weak recovery time. The result of Remark
9
instead bears on the coefficient of the leading asymptotic correction in terms of sample complexity.

Remark 10
.

We note that the joint limit
\alpha_{1},L\to\infty,\mathfrak{b}=\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{L}}=O(1),\mathcal{X}=O(1)
can also be analyzed, and is simply given by equations (
307
) setting

\displaystyle\chi=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{b}-1}

(318)

Study of the
\alpha_{1}\to\infty
residual error

We now examine the behaviour of the residual error
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}
with the signal-to-noise ratio
\mathcal{X}
. We first examine the case
\mathcal{X}\to\infty
. In this limit,

\displaystyle b^{\infty}=-1+o(1),

\displaystyle(\nu^{\infty})^{2}=\frac{4}{\mathcal{X}^{2}}+o\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{X}^{2}}\right)

(319)

The residual error then decays to zero as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}\asymp\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\mathcal{X}^{2}}{8}}}{\mathcal{X}}.

(320)

In the opposite limit of small signal
\mathcal{X}\to 0
,

\displaystyle b^{\infty}=2\pi-1+o(1),

\displaystyle(\nu^{\infty})^{2}=4\pi^{2}(1-\pi)^{2}\mathcal{X}^{2}+o(\mathcal{X}^{2}).

(321)

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{test}}^{\infty}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{X}\to 0}\min(\pi,1-\pi).

(322)

These limiting errors stand in coherence with Proposition
2
.

Derivation of Conjecture

As a corollary of Theorem
2
, we derive in the appendix the
capacity
of the three considered models, namely the largest number of samples that can typically be perfectly classified, up to vanishing training error. The corresponding separability threshold was characterized in the seminal work of
Cover 2006
, and revisited in many later works, e.g.
Gardner and Derrida 1988
,
Krauth and Mézard 1989
,
Candès and Sur 2020
,
Mignacco et al. 2020a
. Note that at the level of the representations
f_{\rm vec}(\cdot),f_{q}(\cdot),f_{\rm pool}(\cdot)
, the capacity intuitively reflects how well the representations separate positive and negative samples in feature space, with a larger separability thresholds signaling more markedly separated classes.

Definition 3

(Separability threshold)
.

Consider the empirical risk minimization problem (
19
) for the attention model, or the related problem for the linear classifier models, with logistic loss
\ell(z;y)=\ln(1+e^{-yz})
and vanishing regularization
\lambda=0^{+}
. As stated in Theorem
2
, the training loss converges in probability in the considered asymptotic limit to a limit
\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}
. We define the separability threshold
\alpha^{\star}
of the model as

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star}=\sup~\{\alpha\geq 0~|~\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{train}}=0\}.

(323)

A closed-form characterization of the separability threshold
\alpha^{\star}
can be heuristically derived from Theorem
2
for each of the three models. We first provide the characterization for the vectorized classifier.

Conjecture 2

(Separability threshold for the vectorized classifier)
.

The separability threshold for the vectorized classifier is equal to

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}=\max_{s\in[0,1],\mathfrak{b}}\frac{L(1-s^{2})}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi^{\prime}\left(\mathfrak{b}+\mathcal{X}s+u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi^{\prime}\left(u-\mathfrak{b}\right)\right]u^{2}du}

(324)

We have used the shorthand
\mathcal{X}=\nicefrac{{\theta R}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
.

Proof.

First note that the following identity follows from Proposition
5
, and most conveniently seen from the rewriting of Remark
8
:

\displaystyle\nu^{2}-\frac{L}{\theta^{2}}m^{2}=\frac{\alpha}{L}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell^{\prime}(z^{\ast}+b,y)^{2}\right]\chi^{2}

(325)

for any given
b
.
We assume that the loss function is of the form
\ell(z,y)=\tilde{\ell}(yz)
, and satisfies
\lim_{z\to\infty}\tilde{\ell}(z)=0
, while being convex. We assume
\tilde{\ell}
to be decreasing, with a monotonically increasing and negative derivative satisfying
\lim_{z\to\infty}\tilde{\ell}^{\prime}(z)=0^{-}
. We denote
\kappa=-\lim_{z\to-\infty}\tilde{\ell}^{\prime}(z)
, which we assume to be finite. Note that all those assumptions are satisfied in particular by the logistic loss function.
We again assumed all token locations are symmetric, leading to a solution
m_{k}=m
for all
k\in[L]
. Introducing the cosine similarity
s=\nicefrac{{\sqrt{L}m}}{{\theta\nu}}\in[0,1]
, and the normalized quantities
\gamma=\nicefrac{{\chi}}{{\nu}},\mathfrak{b}=\nicefrac{{b}}{{\nu}}
, and introducing the random variable
u=\tilde{\ell}^{\prime}((z^{\ast}+b)y)

\displaystyle 1-s^{2}=\frac{\alpha}{L}\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[u^{2}\right].

(326)

But
z^{\ast}-\delta_{y,1}Rm-\nu z+\chi yu=0
by definition of the proximal operator, and
z^{\ast}=y\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)-b
, while
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Furthermore,
u\in(-\kappa,0)

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-2\int\limits_{-\kappa}^{0}\left[\pi\Phi\left(\frac{\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)-b-Rm+\chi u}{\nu}\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi\left(\frac{\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)+b+\chi u}{\nu}\right)\right]udu

(327)

\displaystyle=-2\int\limits_{-\kappa}^{0}\left[\pi\Phi\left(\frac{\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)}{\nu}-\mathfrak{b}-\mathcal{X}s+\gamma u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi\left(\frac{\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)}{\nu}+\mathfrak{b}+\gamma u\right)\right]udu.

(328)

Following
Mignacco et al. 2020b
we aim to determine the necessary conditions on
\alpha
such that there exists a solution satisfying
\nu=\infty,\gamma=\infty
– which should hold for a solution achieving zero training loss. We conjecture the following limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma,\nu\to\infty}\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[u^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi\left(-\mathfrak{b}-\mathcal{X}s-u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi\left(\mathfrak{b}-u\right)\right]udu

(329)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi^{\prime}\left(\mathfrak{b}+\mathcal{X}s+u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi^{\prime}\left(u-\mathfrak{b}\right)\right]u^{2}du

(330)

where we remind that
\Phi^{\prime}
is simply a standard Gaussian density. Then, a necessary condition for the existence of a solution with
\nu=\infty,\gamma=\infty
is the existence of an
s\in[0,1]
so that

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{L(1-s^{2})}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi^{\prime}\left(\mathfrak{b}+\mathcal{X}s+u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi^{\prime}\left(u-\mathfrak{b}\right)\right]u^{2}du}

(331)

∎

Note that the pooled classifier can be mapped to a special case of the vectorized classifier, formally evaluating the expression for the vectorized classifier for
L,R\to 1,\theta\to\nicefrac{{\theta R}}{{\sqrt{L}}}
. Leveraging this connection yields the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3

(Separability threshold for the pooled classifier)
.

The separability threshold for the pooled classifier is equal to

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{pool}}^{\star}=\max_{s\in[0,1],\mathfrak{b}}\frac{(1-s^{2})}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left[\pi\Phi^{\prime}\left(\mathfrak{b}+\mathcal{X}s+u\right)+(1-\pi)\Phi^{\prime}\left(u-\mathfrak{b}\right)\right]u^{2}du}=\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{vec}}^{\star}}{L}.

(332)

Finally, a similar characterization can be conjectured from Theorem
2
for the attention model.

Conjecture 4

(Separability threshold for the attention model)
.

The separability threshold for the pooled classifier is equal to

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{A}}^{\star}=\max_{m_{q},m_{\xi},\mathfrak{b}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{y,c_{z},c_{\xi},c_{q}}\left[c_{z}^{3}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Phi^{\prime}\left(\frac{c_{z}^{2}u+y(b+c_{q}m_{q}+c_{\xi}m_{\xi})}{c_{z}}\right)u^{2}du\right]}

(333)

Proof.

The derivation proceeds in close likeness to that for the vectorized classifiers. First observe that from Theorem
2
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\nu^{2}=\alpha\chi^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}^{2}\ell^{\prime}(z^{\ast}+c_{q}\mu_{q}+c_{\xi}\mu_{\xi}+b,y)^{2}\right]

(334)

Introducing the normalized quantities
\gamma=\nicefrac{{\chi}}{{\nu}},\mathfrak{b}=\nicefrac{{b}}{{\nu}},m_{q}=\nicefrac{{\mu_{q}}}{{\nu}},m_{\xi}=\nicefrac{{\mu_{\xi}}}{{\nu}}
, and introducing the random variable
u=\tilde{\ell}^{\prime}((z^{\ast}+c_{q}\mu_{q}+c_{\xi}\mu_{\xi}+b)y)
, this identity can be compactly rewritten as

\displaystyle 1=\alpha\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}^{2}u^{2}\right].

(335)

But
z^{\ast}-c_{z}\nu z+c_{z}^{2}\chi yu=0
by definition of the proximal operator, and
z^{\ast}=y\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)-b-c_{q}\mu_{q}-c_{\xi}\mu_{\xi}
, while
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}^{2}u^{2}\right]=-2\int_{-\kappa}^{0}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}^{2}\Phi\left(\frac{\frac{\tilde{\ell}^{-1}(u)}{\nu}+c_{z}^{2}\gamma u-y(\mathfrak{b}-c_{q}m_{q}-c_{\xi}m_{\xi}}{c_{z}})\right)udu\right].

(336)

Then,

\displaystyle\lim_{\gamma,\nu\to\infty}\gamma^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}^{2}u^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}\Phi\left(c_{z}^{2}u-\frac{y(\mathfrak{b}-c_{q}m_{q}-c_{\xi}m_{\xi})}{c_{z}}\right)udu\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[c_{z}^{3}\Phi^{\prime}\left(c_{z}u-\frac{y(\mathfrak{b}-c_{q}m_{q}-c_{\xi}m_{\xi})}{c_{z}}\right)u^{2}du\right],

(337)

which concludes the derivation.
∎

The theoretical prediction of Conjectures
2

3

4
are contrasted with numerical experiments in Fig.

\alpha^{\star}_{\rm vec,pool}
for the vectorized and pooled classifiers are related by a factor
L
. The latter can be rationalized by the fact that the vectorized classifiers operates in
\mathbb{R}^{Ld}
, while the pooled classifier acts on the smaller space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Moreover, observe that while the threshold
\alpha^{\star}_{\rm A}
for the attention model lies for large query/signal alignment
\gamma

\alpha^{\star}_{\rm pool}
, it becomes smaller for small values of
\gamma
(see Fig.

, right). This temptingly suggests the intuitive interpretation that when the internal representation of the attention is misaligned with the signal, the attention model displays a smaller capacity than the simple pooled linear classifier. This conclusion echoes a similar observation at the level of the residual errors, see the discussion of Fig.

and its discussion in Appendix
D
