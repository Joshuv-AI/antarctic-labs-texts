Identification of epileptic hippocampal sclerosis related genes through bulk and single-nucleus RNA sequencing datasets
Authors: Jincheng Wang, Zhongyu Zhou, Die Wu, Yiqiao Zeng, Hanyu Huang, Qigang Zhou, Fan Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHippocampal sclerosis is one of the most common pathological alterations in drug-refractory epilepsy. However, the exact causes and molecular mechanisms of its formation remain unknown.MethodsWe constructed a hippocampal sclerosis score (HSS) based on the collective expression of selected hub genes harvested from multiple transcriptomic datasets. We also evaluated immune infiltration and related hallmark signaling pathways to verify the connection between immune cells and hippocampal sclerosis pathogenesis.ResultsBased on three machine learning models and differential expressed genes (DEGs) from multiple RNA-seq datasets, four intersecting genes were identified (PSD4, CFAP47, TMEM156 and P2RY13). We found significant infiltration of immune cells via the CIBERSORT algorithm, particularly in myeloid cell features. Diagnostic nomogram was established to predict the incidence of hippocampal sclerosis. Single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) data elucidated the distinct signaling pathways of microglia based on HSS.ConclusionWe excavated an HSS model from multiple bulk RNA and snRNA-seq datasets. This model was used to evaluate the likelihood of developing hippocampal sclerosis and reveal potential factors such as signaling changes and immune infiltrations during hippocampal sclerosis formation.

Introduction

Epilepsy affects 70 million people worldwide, and is a complex neurological disorder with an elusive etiology involving intricate molecular mechanisms and distinct pathological features characterized by neuronal loss, gliosis, and granule cell dispersion in the hippocampus region [1–3]. Nearly 30% of patients with epilepsy fail to manage their seizure activities with medications [1, 4]. Epileptic hippocampal sclerosis may stem from various factors, including congenital defects, brain trauma, infections, toxins, or other neurological disorders. The precise mechanism underlying epileptic hippocampal sclerosis remains unclear, but several studies suggest that neuronal damage, glial cell activation, inflammatory responses, and aberrant signaling pathways may all play a role [5, 6]. This pathology likely exerts a significant influence on the occurrence and progression of epilepsy, as it alters the structure and function of the hippocampus, disrupting normal communication between neurons [7, 8].

In epilepsy, glial cells may undergo abnormal changes, disrupting the electrochemical balance between neurons and causing an imbalance of excitatory and inhibitory signals. Aberrant activation and proliferation of glial cells in the brain, as well as impaired regulation of extracellular ion balance by glial cells, are associated with the occurrence and progression of epilepsy [8]. Abnormal activation of glial cells can lead to changes in the release of neurotransmitters and neuromodulators, such as glutamate, glutathione, and acetylcholine [9–11]. Alterations in the levels of these substances can cause abnormal neuronal firing and synaptic transmission, thus promoting the development of epilepsy. Furthermore, glial cells can regulate neuronal excitability by releasing extracellular ions such as potassium, sodium, chloride, and calcium. Compromised glial cell regulation of extracellular ion balance can result in ion concentration disturbances and trigger abnormal neuronal firing [12–14].

Bioinformatics analysis has become an invaluable tool in disease research, helping to identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Recently, several studies have utilized bioinformatics approaches to gain deeper insights into various diseases. For instance, Wang et al. used integrated bioinformatics methods to investigate kainic acid-induced model of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) [5]. Other research has also focused on identifying key genes associated with epilepsy. Despite these efforts, there has been limited exploration of diagnostic biomarkers for epileptic hippocampal sclerosis through bioinformatics analysis.

In conclusion, glial cells are involved in the progression of epilepsy. Their aberrant activation, proliferation, regulation of neurotransmitters, and modulation of extracellular ion balance can affect neuronal excitability and inhibition, thus promoting the occurrence and progression of epilepsy and hippocampal sclerosis. However, further research and exploration are needed to understand the specific mechanisms and effects involved.

Methods

Ethics statement

All procedures concerning animal care and treatment were carried out in accordance with the protocols approved by the ethical committee of Nanjing Medical University (protocol number: IACUC-2202017).

Animal and tissue collection

Male C57BL6/J mice (23–25 g) were used in this study for epilepsy model. All mice were housed in 12 h/12 h light/dark cycle environment which temperature was maintained at 24℃±0.5℃. Food and water were placed for mice ad libitum. Mice were randomly divided into Control and KA (Kainic acid) groups. The EEG of mice from different group was monitored to confirm spontaneous recurrent seizure (SRS) before hippocampus collection.

Data acquisition and processing

The bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Three RNA-seq datasets (GSE190451, GSE197015 and) were included in this study. RNA-seq data were analyzed as fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM) values. For dataset GSE256068, only 62 hippocampal sclerosis samples and 14 control samples were used in subsequent analysis. All FPKM expression values were normalized as log2(FPKM + 1). Human TLE and control cortex snRNA-seq data was downloaded from GEO under accession number GSE190452 [15, 16]. We performed and integrated transcriptomic data of 47, 773 cells from 4 patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) and 4 control cortices of the same age, under Seurat package based on the official tutorial from https://satijalab.org/seurat/. All the above data are available for download from the GEO database. This study does not require ethical approval or patient informed consent.

Identification of differentially expressed genes in RNA-seq

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined through DEseq2 R package with a threshold of |log2FC|>0.0585 and p < 0.05 in GSE256068. Heatmap and volcano plots of screened DEGs were depicted using ggplot2 pheatmap packages in R studio. And further DEGs narrowing conjoint analysis with GSE256068, GSE190451, GSE197015 set a threshold of |log2FC|>1 and p < 0.05.

Gene function and enrichment analysis

Up-regulated DEGs were selected for further functional analysis. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) were conducted to evaluate the function of upregulated genes under adjusted p < 0.05 requirements using ClusterProfiler R package. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted to estimate the change in signaling pathways according to expression alteration of selected genes [17, 18].

WGCNA module identification

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was conducted to uncover the potential gene networks in hippocampal sclerosis. The signed adjacency co-expression matrix was created by robustly correlating genes pairwise, followed by hierarchical clustering to identify gene modules. Crucially, module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated to capture the overall expression patterns of each module. Next, the module membership (MM) of genes was determined by correlating their expression with ME values. Hub genes were selected based on correlation values within each module.

Machine learning model

Machine-learning feature selection and modeling were performed using three complementary approaches. First, we applied LASSO-penalized logistic regression with the glmnet package (family set to binomial), using three-fold cross-validation to tune the penalty parameter lambda and retaining predictors with nonzero coefficients at the minimum cross-validated classification error. Second, we used SVM-RFE with a radial basis kernel and ten-fold cross-validation to rank and iteratively eliminate features, selecting the subset at the minimum cross-validated error. Third, we trained a random forest classifier with one thousand trees using an R implementation; the number of variables tried at each split was tuned by ten-fold cross-validation and features were ranked by permutation importance, with the subset at the minimum cross-validated error retained. The final hippocampal sclerosis–related genes were defined as the intersection of the features selected by all three methods.

Establishment of a prognostic nomogram.

Establishment of a prognostic nomogram

Intersected genes were obtained from three different RNA-seq DEGs (two human and one mouse epilepsy samples) and WGCNA module genes. A prognostic nomogram was established to predict the likelihood of progression into hippocampal sclerosis. The ROC curve was used to evaluate the efficacy of the predictive model and each prognostic gene in it.

Analysis of single-nucleus RNA sequencing data

Seurat R package was used in snRNA-seq analysis. Low-quality cells characterized by expressing higher than 20% of mitochondrial or hemoglobin-related genes and genes expressed less than 3 cells or cells expressing less than 200 genes were filtered in this study. The top 2000 genes with the highest variance were for normalization and principal component analysis (PCA). Batch effect was eradicated by harmony of all samples used in this study. A total of ten clusters (Oligodendrocyte, Oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC), Excitatory neuron, Inhibitory neuron, Microglia, Astrocyte, Endothelial cell, Macrophage, T cell and Pericyte) were identified by tSNE reduction method and their unique gene markers. The expression of the prognostic gene marker was integrated using AddModuleScore function and cells were dichotomized using the median of the HSS: values at or above the median were classified as “high” and those below the median as “low”. Finally, CellChat R package was used to decrypt the ligand-receptor communication between targeted cell types and other cell types [19].

Electrode implantation and micro infusion of kainic acid

Adult mice (23–26 g) were treated with ketamine (100 mg/kg, diluted in 0.9% saline) for analgesia and anesthesia prior to surgery. A guide cannula (RWDSUS304, stainless steel, OD 0.41 mm/ID 0.25 mm,8.5 mm in length) was located above the dentate gyrus of right hippocampus(coordinates from bregma: AP = + 2.8 mm, ML=−3.2 mm, and D=−1.4 mm below dura).A electrode with screw cap was implanted into the prefrontal cortex (coordinates from bregma: AP = + 0.5 mm, ML = + 1.2 mm, and D=−2.0 mm below dura) as reference. Another electrode with screw cap was placed into left entorhinal cortex(coordinates from bregma: AP=−2.8 mm, ML=−2.7 mm, and D=−2.9 mm below dura) with a straight electrode targeting the dentate gyrus of left hippocampus(coordinates from bregma: AP=−2.8 mm, ML=−3.2 mm, and D=−1.4 mm below dura).After a recovery of 7 days, a 10 mm-long inner cannula was inserted into the right dentate gyrus through the guide cannula to a depth of 2.9 mm below the dura. Either kainic acid (0.25 µg in 0.5 µl PBS) or vehicle (0.5 µl PBS) was injected into the right dentate gyrus at a rate of 167 nl/min. After completion of injection, the inner cannula was additionally left in the right dentate gyrus for 3 min. Subsequently, mice were individually housed for the first electroencephalogram (EEG) telemetry at least 7 days after surgery. All mice were treated with dexamethasone to prevent inflammation and penicillin to prevent infection.

Western blotting

Brain tissues were dissected and lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime), followed by sonication. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and proteins were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were then separated using 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with primary antibodies, including CFAP47 polyclonal antibody (20837-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), P2Y13 antibody (sc-517219, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:1000), and recombinant anti-GAPDH antibody (GB15004-100, Servicebio, 1:500). The membranes were washed three times and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL system (Tanon, 180–5001), and signal intensities were quantified with ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen™, 15596018CN) and reverse transcribed using HiScript® III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, R323) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative analysis was performed using SYBR™ Green I (Invitrogen, S7563). Gene expression levels were normalized to hippocampal reference genes. Immunofluorescence staining

Mice were deeply anesthetized and perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C, paraffin-embedded, and coronally sectioned at 4 μm through the hippocampus. Sections were blocked/permeabilized in PBS containing 3% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 (30 min, room temperature). Primary antibodies (GFAP (CST, #12389, 1:500), Iba1 (CST, #78060, 1:500), NeuN (CST, #12943,1:1000) were applied overnight at 4 °C. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with species-appropriate Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibodies (1 h, room temperature), counterstained with DAPI, and mounted in antifade medium. Images were acquired on the same fluorescence microscope with fixed exposure settings.

Another human TLE RNA-seq dataset (GSE190451 3 normal samples vs. 3 TLE samples) and Kainic acid-induced mouse hippocampus epilepsy model RNA-seq dataset (GSE197015) underwent the same screening protocol. The analysis found a total of 1,681 DEGs in GSE256068, 9,370 DEGs in GSE190451, 3,331 DEGs in GSE197015 (p < 0.05, |log2FC|>1). Heatmap and volcano plot were used to display the upregulated and downregulated genes between two groups (Fig. 1a, c). Then, we used these screened DEGs for gene ontology functional enrichment analysis (GO) by ClusterProfiler R package. Results are shown in biological process (BP), molecular function (MF) and cellular component (CC). CellChat identifies communication patterns between high/low HSS microglia and other cell types during hippocampal sclerosis formation

CellChat R package was conducted to analyze cellular communication depicting ligand-receptor patterns, which were further categorized into many secreting signaling pathways, including NRG, PTN, BMP, PDGF, FGF, SPP1, IGF, PSAP, TGFβ, VISFATIN, CSF, SEMA3, WNT and ANGPT (Fig. 8a, b). Network centrality analysis of inferred SPP1 signaling pathways identified that microglia are not only the source of SPP1 but also can be the target. High score microglia are the predominant sender and receiver of the signal, indicating its role in positive feedback regulation, and sustaining activation during hippocampal sclerosis (Fig. 8c-e). These findings are consistent with the known pivotal role played by microglia in initiating inflammation during epileptogenesis [20, 21]. Moreover, CellChat also predicted that microglia may have contributed to endothelial cell alteration, blood-brain barrier insult in epilepsy, and to T cell recruitment and activation too [22, 23]. In addition to SPP1 signaling, TGFβ also presented significant changes in CellChat analysis. Network centrality analysis confirmed that both high and low score microglia are prominent influencers controlling the communications (Fig. 8g). Importantly, elevated expression of TGFβ in microglia and its role in different diseases has long been reported [24, 25]. TGFβ can originate from microglia with different scores, and it may also act on itself. Interestingly, astrocytes also appeared to participate in this reaction (Fig. 8f). All of the signaling pathways were primarily forced by TGFβ1(TGFβR1 + TGFβR2) ligand-receptor pair (Fig. 8h). Communication patterns function of CellChat also found that a large portion of outing microglia signaling is characterized by pattern #1, which represents multiple pathways, including PDGF, SPP1, IGF, TGFβ, WNT (Fig. 8i). On the other hand, the communication patterns of targeted cells (Fig. 8j) showed that incoming microglia signaling is predominated by pattern #2 which include SPP1, TGFβ and CSF. Immunofluorescent validation of hippocampal sclerosis in KA-induced mouse epilepsy model

Compared with controls (Fig. 9a, b), the hippocampus 30 days after kainic acid (KA) shows a canonical hippocampal-sclerosis profile: GFAP markedly increases with hypertrophic, fibrous astrocytosis; Iba1 intensifies with a shift from ramified to enlarged, retracted-process microglia; NeuN-positive neurons are reduced across CA1–CA3 and the dentate region, with a thinned, discontinuous pyramidal layer and disorganized somatic alignment; and parvalbumin (PV)-positive inhibitory interneurons are substantially diminished, with weakened layer-specific labeling. Biologically, the triad of reactive gliosis, principal-neuron loss, and PV-interneuron depletion indicates glia–neuron imbalance, persistent neuroinflammation, and impaired inhibitory tone—structural substrates for network hyperexcitability and spontaneous recurrent seizures. Gene expression validation in KA-induced refractory epilepsy model

To validate the expression changes of genes identified in our predictive model in refractory epilepsy hippocampal sclerosis (HS), we established a kainic acid (KA)-induced mouse model of refractory epilepsy with HS and confirmed spontaneous recurrent seizures (SRS) via electroencephalography (EEG) monitoring (Fig. 10a, c). Hippocampal tissues from HS mice and age-matched controls were collected for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis. qPCR results revealed significantly upregulated mRNA expression of CFAP47 (p < 0.0001) and P2RY13 (p < 0.001) in HS samples compared to controls. Conversely, Psd4 mRNA levels showed no statistically significant difference between groups (p > 0.05), while Tmem156 mRNA trended towards downregulation in HS (p < 0.05). To further investigate protein expression, Western blot analysis was performed for CFAP47 and P2RY13. Consistent with mRNA findings, protein levels of both CFAP47 (p < 0.05) and P2RY13 (p < 0.05) were significantly increased in HS hippocampal tissues. These results validate the accuracy of our previously proposed predictive model and highlight the potential roles of CFAP47 and P2RY13 in HS pathogenesis (Fig. 10b, d, e). Discussion

Hippocampal sclerosis is a common pathological change in the development of epilepsy, characterized by neuronal loss, aberrant activation of glial cells (astrocyte and microglia) [3, 7, 8]. Although many signaling pathways and molecules have been shown to play important roles in the development of epilepsy, there is still no accurate description of the mechanism of hippocampal sclerosis formation and how different cell components may function during such process still remains unveiled [26, 27]. In our research, we are committed to discovering new biomarkers related to hippocampal sclerosis formation in epilepsy, which might be used as potential drugs and therapeutic targets for epilepsy treatment. Most previous studies have focused on molecular alterations in epilepsy, whereas our research further differentiates pathological changes in epilepsy by comparing drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) with common epilepsy subtypes. This approach enables the identification of molecular signatures specific to the pathological progression of epilepsy.

This study aims to discover new genes associated with hippocampal sclerosis in epilepsy. By analyzing three different transcriptome sequencing datasets (including human hippocampal sclerosis samples and KA-induced mouse model of refractory epilepsy), we first screened for differentially expressed genes in these datasets. Through WGCNA analysis and three machine learning models, we further narrowed down the intersecting genes that are most relevant to hippocampal sclerosis formation, to perform subsequent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis, aiming to distinguish the functions and interactions of cells expressing these genes. Finally, we found four genes associated with hippocampal sclerosis in epilepsy: P2RY13, TMEM156, CFAP47 and PSD4. We then established a diagnostic nomogram based on these four highly selective genes to predict the occurrence of hippocampal sclerosis.

The current study’s gene selection process, based on univariate AUC thresholds (> 0.96), may have overlooked critical multivariate interactions that impact model performance. While univariate analysis efficiently identifies individual biomarkers, it does not account for dependencies between variables, such as the potential functional interplay between P2RY13 and TMEM156. In our model, linearly combining these genes without testing interaction terms likely diluted their synergistic effect, resulting in suboptimal performance. Therefore, we will demonstrate their significance in HS through further verification.

In addition, we also conducted an analysis of immune cell infiltration using CIBERSORT and found that various immune cells play important roles in the development of hippocampal sclerosis such as macrophages, T cells, and NK cells under different polarization states. According to RNA-seq data, we found that in the biological process of GO analysis, glial cell proliferation and extracellular matrix reorganization play key roles in the progression of hippocampal sclerosis. Based on previous reports, gliosis is a typical pathological change in hippocampal sclerosis in epilepsy, but its causal relationship with the occurrence of sclerotic formation is unclear. Some literature reports have shown that regulating the astrocyte proliferation can cause spikes in EEG waveforms in mice, indicating that pathological changes in astrocytes may be one of the causes of hippocampal sclerosis [7, 28]. Through further analysis with WGCNA, we found that biological behavior is more focused on regulating inflammatory cytokines production, immune system activation, and response of different immune cells, which is consistent with CIBERSORT results. Moreover, astrocyte activation and proliferation might also be the consequence of long-term inflammatory immune microenvironment [29–31]. However, the specific mechanism has not been fully elucidated.

Cilia and flagella associated protein 47 (CFAP47) encodes a protein involved in flagellar formation and sperm motility [32]. In the CFAP47 mutant mouse model, adult male mice exhibited infertility, reduced sperm motility, and abnormalities in flagellar morphology and movement [32]. Based on analysis of snRNA-seq data, we found that CFAP47 is mainly expressed in astrocytes in the central nervous system, and its expression in refractory epilepsy groups is significantly higher than that in normal temporal lobe cortex. Besides, there is no clear manifestation of CFAP47 function in astrocytes. Accumulating evidence indicates that interactions between CFAP47 and proteins such as WDR87 play critical roles in cell cycle regulation and migration mechanisms. By modulating the motility components of flagella and mitochondria, CFAP47 supports cytoskeletal organization and microtubule assembly, thereby governing cellular motility [33]. In line with these findings, our scRNA-seq data from hippocampal sclerosis (HS) reveal that CFAP47 is significantly upregulated in a specific astrocyte subpopulation. Given the pivotal role of astrocytes in epileptogenic HS, where astrocyte excessive proliferation and activation drive glial scar formation—a process closely linked to cytoskeletal remodeling and proliferative gene expression—we hypothesize that CFAP47 contributes to HS pathogenesis by regulating astrocyte cytoskeletal dynamics, migration, and proliferation. The specific function may need to be further confirmed by experiments.

P2Y receptors are a class of G-protein-coupled receptors that respond to nucleotides such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and other nucleotides [34]. They play important roles in various physiological processes, including neurotransmission, inflammatory responses, and platelet aggregation [6, 35, 36]. In the nervous system, P2Y receptors regulate the functions of neurons and glial cells, significantly impacting the pathophysiology of epilepsy [6]. The P2Y receptor family includes several subtypes: P2Y1, P2Y2, P2Y4, P2Y6, P2Y11, P2Y12, P2Y13, and P2Y1 [37, 38]. According to the results of snRNA-seq analysis, P2RY13 is specifically expressed in microglia. Epileptic seizures are often accompanied by neuroinflammatory responses [5, 21]. P2RY13 in microglia mediates inflammatory signaling pathways, which may contribute to the progression and severity of epilepsy [36, 39]. Chronic inflammation in the CNS is known to exacerbate neuronal hyperexcitability and seizure susceptibility. The role of P2Y receptors (including P2RY13) in regulating the release of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) from microglia emphasizes their role in inflammatory responses [36]. Therefore, this not only suggests the functional impact of P2RY13 on microglia but also highlights the important role chronic inflammation may play in hippocampal sclerosis formation. Unlike the classical microglial marker P2RY12, both P2RY13 and P2RY12 exhibited relatively high levels of mRNA expression. However, under physiological conditions, the protein level of P2RY13 is relatively low [36]. This suggests that if subsequent validation shows significant changes in its protein levels in hippocampal sclerosis, it could confirm its critical role in the pathological process. Furthermore, literature reports indicate that antagonists of P2Y receptors, mainly P2RY13, can reduce seizure frequency and provide therapeutic benefits [39]. Thus, P2RY13 antagonists hold potential as therapeutic targets for epilepsy.

The Pleckstrin and Sect. 7 domain containing 4 (PSD4) gene encodes a protein that is involved in cellular signaling and membrane trafficking processes [40]. The exact relationship between PSD4 and epilepsy is still under investigation, but several mechanisms could potentially link PSD4 to the pathophysiology of epilepsy. Proper functioning of synaptic vesicle trafficking is essential for neurotransmitter release and synaptic plasticity. Dysregulation of proteins involved in vesicle trafficking, such as PSD4, could lead to abnormal synaptic transmission and increased neuronal excitability, contributing to seizure activity [41]. Dysregulation of cellular signaling and trafficking pathways can influence neuroinflammatory responses. Since inflammation is a known contributor to epileptogenesis, the aberrant function of PSD4 could modulate the inflammatory environment within the brain, affecting seizure susceptibility [42]. The relationship between PSD4 and microglia is still elusive, but several potential roles may be identified. PSD4, through its GEF activity and interaction with ARF GTPases, could influence the activation state of microglia, which includes transition between resting and activated states [43, 44].

Transmembrane protein 156 (TMEM156) is predicted to be an integral component of the membrane. For the function of this gene and its role in epilepsy, many experiments are needed for further verification and exploration.

However, this study has several limitations. It is true that our findings require further validation through additional experimental research to confirm our findings. The genes we selected are based on the transcriptome sequencing results from hippocampus and temporal lobe samples, which are not easily obtained in routine examinations. To more accurately evaluate the model’s reliability and confirm its relationship with hippocampal sclerosis formation, we also need to analyze additional data on peripheral blood and other clinical manifestations, followed by confirming their connections.

Conclusions

Based on the present results, four genes (TMEM156, CFAP47, PSD4 and P2RY13) associated with hippocampal sclerosis formation have good predictive value for epileptic sclerotic changes and may be explained as the cause of hippocampal sclerosis. However, the results of this study still need to be validated by larger-scale basic experiments and clinical research in the future to further confirm our findings.
