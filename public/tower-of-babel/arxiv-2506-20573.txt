LARP: Learner-Agnostic Robust Data Prefiltering
Kristian Minchev, Dimitar I. Dimitrov, Nikola Konstantinov

Abstract
The widespread availability of large public datasets is a key factor behind the recent successes of statistical inference and machine learning methods. However, these datasets often contain some low-quality or contaminated data, to which many learning procedures are sensitive. Therefore, the question of whether and how public datasets should be prefiltered to facilitate accurate downstream learning arises. On a technical level this requires the construction of principled data prefiltering methods which are learner-agnostic robust , in the sense of provably protecting a set of pre-specified downstream learners from corrupted data. In this work, we formalize the problem of L earner- A gnostic R obust data P refiltering (LARP), which aims at finding prefiltering procedures that minimize a worst-case loss over a pre-specified set of learners. We first instantiate our framework in the context of scalar mean estimation with Huber estimators under the Huber data contamination model. We provide a hardness result on a specific problem instance and analyze several natural prefiltering procedures. Our theoretical results indicate that performing LARP on a heterogeneous set of learners leads to some loss in model performance compared to the alternative of prefiltering data for each learner/use-case individually. We explore the resulting utility loss and its dependence on the problem parameters via extensive experiments on real-world image and tabular data, observing statistically significant reduction in utility. Finally, we model the trade-off between the utility drop and the cost of repeated (learner-specific) prefiltering within a game-theoretic framework and showcase benefits of LARP for large datasets.

Contributions

In this work we study the problem of prefiltering public data with the goal of protecting a wide specification of learners, proposing a framework of
Learner-Agnostic Robust data Prefiltering (LARP)
. Formally, the objective is to find a prefiltering, which computes a subset of an initial contaminated dataset, so as to minimize a learner-agnostic notion of risk, defined as the worst-case (maximal) loss of a set of pre-specified downstream learners trained on the prefiltered data. Differently from classic robust learning, in LARP we optimize over prefiltering procedures, instead of learners, with the best prefilter generally depending on the given specification of learners.

We first instantiate LARP in the context of scalar mean estimation with Huber contamination.
We provide an example learning problem which highlights that non-trivial bounds on the learner-agnostic risk necessarily depend on the specified set of learners.
Then we prove an upper bound on the learner-agnostic risk over any set of Huber estimators, achieved by a prefiltering procedure inspired by popular methods for outlier removal based on quantiles.
The bound contains a term reminiscent to standard results in robust statistics, featuring the data corruption ratio and the dataset size, as well as an additional term that features the dependence on the learner set.
We also observe similar behaviour of the learner-agnostic risk in empirical evaluation of three mean estimation prefiltering procedures.

Our theoretical results indicate that the learner-agnostic risk generally becomes larger with increased heterogeneity of the specified learner set, leading to a loss in utility compared to the alternative of prefiltering data for each learner/use-case individually (learner-specific prefiltering).
We term the average utility reduction across the learner set as the
price of learner-agnostic prefiltering
and study it via extensive experiments on the Adult
[
3
]
and CIFAR-10
[
36
]
datasets, with label noise and spurious correlations data corruptions.
Finally, we model the trade-off between the price of LARP and the cost of repeated (learner-specific) prefiltering within a game-theoretic framework and showcase benefits of LARP for large datasets.

3
Framework

In this section we formalize the problem of finding learner-agnostic robust prefiltering (LARP) procedures.
First, we define the environment of the problem, which consists of the contamination model and the set of downstream learners.
Then we move on to present the main objective of LARP. We then discuss differences with classic robust learning and we motivate and define a notion of price of learner-agnostic prefiltering.

Contamination model

We consider the general data contamination model from classic robust learning.
In the canonical setting of learning theory, one is interested in learning a property
\theta\in\Theta
of a distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
.
However, instead of i.i.d. data from the distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
, in the robust setting one assumes that the data is independently drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
, but an arbitrary
\epsilon
-fraction of the points are contaminated according to some assumed contamination model.
We refer to
\epsilon\in[0,1/2)
as the contamination ratio and we let
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\theta}
denote the contaminated distribution.

Downstream learners

Each learner is a function
l:\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}
that takes a finite sample
S
and produces a hypothesis
\hat{h}\vcentcolon=l(S)
.
For example, in the context of scalar mean estimation, we have
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
, whereas for classification we have
\mathcal{H}=\left\{h:\mathcal{X}\to\left\{0,\ldots,c-1\right\}\right\}
.
We denote the set of downstream learners as
\mathcal{L}
.

Learner-agnostic robust prefiltering

We study data prefiltering mechanisms, which filter the data
before it is presented to downstream learners
.
Specifically, consider an
\epsilon
-contaminated dataset
S
from the target distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
.
Given this dataset, a prefiltering procedure is a function
F:\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}^{n}
satisfying
F(S)\subseteq S
.
In other words, the dataset is mapped to one of its subsets
S^{\prime}
.
The prefiltered subset
S^{\prime}
is then presented to the set of downstream learners, where each element
l\in\mathcal{L}
produces a hypothesis
l(S^{\prime})
.
The performance of each hypothesis is then measured using a risk function
R(l(S^{\prime}),\theta)
.

Our goal is to design prefiltering procedures that protect all learners in
\mathcal{L}
.
Formally, we seek to minimize a notion of
learner-agnostic risk

R_{agn}(F)
over a set of possible prefiltering procedures
\mathcal{F}
:

\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}}R_{agn}(F),

where

R_{agn}(F)\vcentcolon=\max_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R(l(F(S)),\theta).

(1)

For brevity, we denote
R_{l}(F)\vcentcolon=R\left(l\left(F\left(S\right)\right),\theta\right)
.
Classic examples of risk functions include the squared loss
R(\hat{h},\theta)=(\hat{h}-\theta)^{2}
for scalar mean estimation, or population risk
R(\hat{h},\theta)=\mathbb{P}_{(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}(\hat{h}(X)\neq Y)
for classification tasks.
Note that the learner-agnostic risk presented in
Equation

1
is a random variable since it depends on the random sample
S
.
In this work, we focus on high-probability bounds for
R_{agn}
in our theoretical results.

Analyzing the learner-agnostic risk

Although our setup is concerned with producing accurate hypotheses, similarly to classical robust learning, there are two important conceptual differences.
We can formally define robust learning as finding
\argmin_{l}R\left(l(S),\theta\right)
over all possible learners
l:\cup^{\infty}_{n=1}\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}
.
The main difference with our framework is that in the LARP problem the learner set
\mathcal{L}
is fixed, and instead minimization happens over the prefiltering procedure
F
.
Thus, the optimal procedure may vary significantly according to the properties of the downstream learners.
This can be further highlighted by rewriting
Equation

1
as

\displaystyle R_{agn}(F)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\max_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R_{l}(F)=\min_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R_{l}(F)+\left(\max_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R_{l}(F)-\min_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R_{l}(F)\right).

(2)

The first term
\min_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R_{l}(F)
denotes the best possible error that can be achieved by any learner in the set.
Under the assumption that at least one learner in
\mathcal{L}
is reasonable, this resembles the target objective of classic robust learning.
The second term encapsulates the hardness that arises from the heterogeneity between the learners.
In the general case, this term is positive and induces additional losses not present in standard robust frameworks. In Section
4
we study the objective
R_{agn}(F)
in further detail for the case of scalar mean estimation.

Price of learner-agnostic prefiltering

The second term in
Equation

2
indicates that applying LARP for larger learner sets may lead to worse downstream performance. This is intuitive, as prefiltering a public dataset with the goal of protecting the performance of
multiple learning procedures
is intuitively harder than prefiltering the dataset
for a specific learner only
(e.g. prefiltering performed by anyone using the dataset for their own use-case).

To quantify the loss in utility resulting from such accuracy drop, we introduce a measure of “price of learner-agnostic prefiltering”. Formally, we call an instance of LARP
learner-specific prefiltering for
l
if
\mathcal{L}=\left\{l\right\}
.
We want to compare the across-learners performance of a prefiltering procedure
F\in\mathcal{F}
selected by LARP on a learner set
\mathcal{L}
, to the performance of the learner-specific optimal prefiltering procedures
F^{\ast}_{l}=\argmin_{F\in\mathcal{F}}R_{l}\left(F\right)
for each
l\in\mathcal{L}
.
Note that
R_{l}(F)\geq R_{l}(F_{l}^{\ast}),\forall F\in\mathcal{F},l\in\mathcal{L}
, which leads to each learner losing utility which we describe using a function
\mathcal{U}_{red}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

2
2

2

For fixed
R_{1},R_{2}
, the value
\mathcal{U}_{red}(R_{1},R_{2})
is the reduction in learner
l
’s utility if their risk is
R_{1}
instead of
R_{2}
.
.
Then we define
the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering
of a prefiltering procedure
F
as

P\left(F\right)\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{\lvert\mathcal{L}\rvert}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{U}_{red}\left(R_{l}(F),R_{l}(F_{l}^{\ast})\right).

(3)

Despite the loss in utility, LARP can still be beneficial as it is intuitively “cheaper” to prefilter a dataset once, than for each learner individually. Therefore, in
Section

5
we measure the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering on real data and study incentives for performing LARP over learner-specific prefiltering.

4
LARP for scalar mean estimation

In this section we present a theoretical analysis of LARP for the problem of scalar mean estimation.
We begin by discussing a particular instance of the framework that highlights the additional loss incurred by LARP compared to classic robust learning, drawing parallels to the decomposition presented in
Equation

2
.
Afterwards, we present guarantees for a prefiltering procedure inspired by popular rules for outlier-removal based on quantiles.
Finally, we empirically study three prefiltering mechanisms for the task of Gaussian mean estimation.

4.1
Setup

In this section we focus on scalar mean estimation within the Huber contamination model.

Huber contamination

We assume the standard Huber contamination
[
28
]
, in which the
\epsilon
-contaminated sample
S
is drawn i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\theta}=(1-\epsilon)\mathcal{D}_{\theta}+\epsilon Q,

where
Q\in\mathfrak{Q}
is a noise distribution.
Note that since
Q
is unknown in practice any upper bound on
R_{agn}
should hold for all possible
Q\in\mathfrak{Q}
.

Huber estimators

The set of learners
\mathcal{L}
is a set of Huber estimators
[
28
,
57
,
50
]
, which are parametrized by a scalar
\delta>0
.
They are defined as
\hat{\theta}_{\delta}(S)\vcentcolon=\argmin_{\hat{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}}\sum_{x\in S}H_{\delta}(x-\hat{\theta})
, where the Huber loss
H_{\delta}
is defined as

H_{\delta}(x)\vcentcolon=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}x^{2}&\text{for $\lvert x\rvert\leq\delta$}\\
\delta\left(\lvert x\rvert-\frac{1}{2}\delta\right)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In the cases where
\delta\to 0
and
\delta\to\infty
, the Huber estimator converges to the sample median and the sample mean respectively.
The intermediate values of
\delta
present the tradeoff between robustness and sample efficiency.
We denote the set of the parameters of the given Huber learners as
\Delta\vcentcolon=\left\{\delta:H_{\delta}\in\mathcal{L}\right\}
.

Finally, we define a risk function that measures the square distance between the estimate and the true mean:
R\left(l(S^{\prime}),\theta\right)\vcentcolon=(l(S^{\prime})-\theta)^{2}
.

4.2
Hardness of learning with high heterogeneity

The heterogeneous behaviour of the downstream learners can lead to high losses, no matter the prefiltering.
To show this we provide a lower bound on the learner-agnostic risk, by considering a specific example of a target dataset, noise, and learner set.

Lemma 4.1
.

There is an instance of LARP with specified
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}=Ber(1/2)
and fixed
\mathcal{L}
such that: 1) there exists a prefiltering with
\min_{l\in\mathcal{L}}R_{l}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, but 2) for all prefiltering procedures,
R_{agn}=\Omega(1)
.

We refer to
Section

A.1
for the construction, proof and a more thorough discussion.
This lower bound arises due to the presence of learners that are inefficient for the instance. Therefore, while the first term in
Equation

2
can be made small, this will cause the second term to be large.
This is a strong indicator that any non-trivial bound on the learner-agnostic risk needs to be a function of the learner set
\mathcal{L}
.

4.3
Prefiltering mechanism based on quantiles

We now prove feasibility of LARP in the context of a scalar mean estimation for a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
, by providing a prefiltering procedure with provable upper bounds on its learner-agnostic risk.
This procedure is inspired by a popular informal rule for marking the tails of a sample as outliers
[
41
]
.
This can be adapted to define the following outlyingness measure:

Q\left(x,\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right\}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\left\lvert\min\left\{i:X_{(i)}\geq x\right\}-\frac{n}{2}\right\rvert.

Based on that, we can define a quantile prefiltering procedure as

F^{q}_{p}(S)\vcentcolon=\left\{X\in S:Q(X,S)<p\right\}.

Sensible values for the hyperparameter
p
are in the range
\left(0,1/2\right)
.
The following result provides guarantees for the learner-agnostic risk of
F^{q}_{p}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that the target distribution is
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}=\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
, and that
\epsilon<2/7
.
Let
F_{p}
be the quantile prefiltering procedure with any
p\in(0,1/2)
.
Then, if
n\geq\Omega(\log(1/\delta_{0}))
, with probability
1-\delta_{0}
the downstream Huber learners with parameter set
\Delta
produce mean estimates
\hat{\theta}_{\delta}
such that

\max_{\delta\in\Delta}\lvert\hat{\theta}_{\delta}-\theta\rvert^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\epsilon^{2}+\log(1/\delta_{0})/n\right)\sigma^{2}+\max_{\delta\in\Delta}\delta^{2}\right).

(4)

The first term in
Equation

4
is reminiscent of upper bounds in classic robust mean estimation (see e.g.
[
14
]
) and increases with the corruption rate
\epsilon
and decreases with the sample size. The second term depends solely on the learner set and is always nonnegative.

We note that small values of
\delta\approx 0
yield estimators close to the sample median, which is known to enjoy guarantees similar to the first term in
Equation

4

[
14
]
. Therefore, if all estimators in
\mathcal{L}
use a small value of
\delta
(i.e. all estimators are “good” for the considered learning problem), the bound is comparable to those in standard robust mean estimation. However, if some estimators use a large
\delta
, they are suboptimal for the problem and the bound is also larger as
\max_{\delta\in\Delta}\delta^{2}
is large.
This aligns with the arguments presented in
Section

4.2
, since any non-trivial bound on
R_{agn}
must also take into account the set of downstream learners.

on

5
Price of learner-agnostic prefiltering

Previous results show that performing LARP on a non-trivial set of learners leads to a reduction of downstream performance compared to learner-specific instances.
The average reduction over the learner set can be measured through the notion of price of learner-agnostic prefiltering as defined in
Section

3
.
In this section we measure the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering for different real-world tasks, with various types of data contamination.
We conduct ablation studies to investigate the dependence on contamination ratio, learner heterogeneity and dataset size.
Finally, we present a theoretical argument for the benefits of learner-agnostic prefiltering for large datasets.

5.1
Price of learner-agnostic prefiltering on real-world data

In this section, we quantify the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering on datasets with inherent noise.
Specifically, we conduct experiments in both the tabular and image classification settings, using two realistic noise models: i)
Uniform Label Noise
, which mimics scenarios where annotation is unreliable; and ii)
Shortcuts
[
24
]
, which simulates scenarios where training data contains spurious correlation features irrelevant to the labels, e.g. textual information embedded in the CT image patches.
In all cases, the price
P
is computed using the utility function
\mathcal{U}_{red}(R_{1},R_{2})\vcentcolon=(R_{1}-R_{2})/R_{2}
, that measures the relative increase in classification risk when learner-agnostic prefiltering is used.

Datasets

For image experiments we use CIFAR-10
[
36
]
, splitting its train set into train (80%) and validation (20%) sets and using the original test set.
Tabular experiments utilize Adult
[
3
]
, randomly split into train (64%), validation (16%), and test (20%) sets.
The validation sets are utilized to select the hyperparameters (Adult), and perform early-stopping (CIFAR-10). In all experiments, we apply the noise to the train and validation sets, but evaluate on clean data to measure learner generalization.

Label noise

In label noise experiments, we use the model accuracy as our risk metric
R
,
\epsilon=30\%
uniform label noise on all classes, and prefilter up to
p\%
of the train and validation sets.
Motivated by the practical observation that complex models tend to learn useful features first before overfitting to noisy data, the prefiltered samples were selected by maximizing the loss of chosen overparametrized models (ResNet-9
[
27
]
on CIFAR-10, and 2 layer fully connected NN on Adult) trained on the combined train and evaluation sets with early-stopping.
We found this procedure effective in practice, resulting in
>75\%
of corrupted samples being filtered at the default value of
p=25\%
.

Shortcuts

For tabular data, we inject noise by maximizing the integer feature “education-num” in
85\%
of the data for the class corresponding to the label “
>
50K”, leading to
\epsilon\approx 21\%
contamination rate due to class imbalance.
For image data, we set the color of a
3\times 3
patch in the lower right corner of
85\%
of the data in classes 0,2,4 according to their label, resulting in
\epsilon=25.5\%
contamination rate.
Based on the observation that models learn shortcut data quickly, in both cases we prefilter the top-
p
percent of lowest-loss datapoints based on CNN (CIFAR-10) and Random Forests (Adult) models from the respective learner sets, but trained on the combined train and validation sets. We verify that this results in
>85\%
of prefiltered shortcut data in practice at the default
p=25\%
.
We use the macro-F1 score
[
40
]
as our risk metric
R
due to the class imbalance of the prefiltered datasets.

Learner sets

We evaluate LARP on sets of models with different noise robustness levels, which can intuitively be seen as the worst-case heterogeneity, as models with high robustness will achieve optimal risk under little-to-no prefiltering, while models with low robustness will achieve optimal risk under heavy prefiltering.
As we train very large number of models, for CIFAR-10 we opt to use convolutional networks (CNNs) with an architecture (See
Section

B.2
) optimized for fast training that still maintains good accuracy.
As we observe that L2 regularization increases the robustness to noisy labels in practice, but is also susceptible to underfitting, in the label noise setting, we generate our set of models
\mathcal{L}
by varying the CNNs’ L2 regularization parameter in the range
[3e{-3},2e{-2}]
.
For shortcut experiments we regularize the effect of the shortcuts on the training explicitly by reducing the gradient contribution of the shortcut patch pixels by a factor in the range
[1e{-6},1]
.

5.2
Benefits from learner-agnostic prefiltering

In our theory and experiments, we have observed that LARP leads to a loss in utility resulting from an average accuracy drop of the learners, compared to learner-specific prefiltering. Nevertheless, prefiltering large datasets for each learner individually (i.e. performing learner-specific prefiltering) is intuitively impractical, as the very process of prefiltering can be costly.

In this section we study a game-theoretic model that captures this trade-off between the price of LARP and the cost of repeated prefiltering. In our setting, each learner can either choose learner-specific prefiltering and pay the full cost of preprocessing the dataset, or take part in learner-agnostic prefiltering and split said cost with other learners.
We provide a theoretical result which provides a sufficient condition under which all learners are incentivized to participate in LARP.

Definition

Assume that we conduct the prefiltering setup as presented in
Section

3
.
We consider a game in which each of the downstream learners
l\in\mathcal{L}
is a player trying to maximize their utility.
Each learner
l
has utility
U^{l}
that is a function of their risk
R_{l}(F)
, as well as the cost they pay for the prefiltering procedure.
We model the total cost of prefiltering a dataset of size
n
as
Cn^{\alpha}
for some constants
C>0,\alpha\geq 1
.

Each learner selects an action
a_{l}\in\left\{0,1\right\}
indicating whether they want to take part in a learner-specific or learner-agnostic procedure respectively.
If learner
l
plays
a_{l}=0
, they receive individual prefiltering, which costs
Cn^{\alpha}
, and receive risk
R_{l}(F^{\ast}_{l})
.
Let
U^{l}_{spec}
denote their final utility in this scenario.

On the other hand, if learner
l
plays
a_{l}=1
, then they participate in learner-agnostic prefiltering with other players.
Then, all learners can distribute the cost
Cn^{\alpha}
of prefiltering among them according to a vector
(p_{l})_{l\in\mathcal{L}}
, i.e.
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}p_{l}=Cn^{\alpha}
.
In this case, learner
l
loses
\mathcal{U}_{red}\left(R_{l}(F),R_{l}(F_{l}^{\ast})\right)
utility due to reduced performance, but they also reduce their cost of prefiltering from
Cn^{\alpha}
to
p_{l}
.
Then the utility for player
l
becomes
U^{l}_{agn}=U^{l}_{spec}-\mathcal{U}_{red}\left(R_{l}(F),R_{l}(F_{l}^{\ast})\right)+Cn^{\alpha}-p_{l}
.
These utilities are tightly connected to the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering defined in
Equation

3
.
The following result provides a sufficient condition for the benefit of LARP over learner-specific prefiltering.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that in the aforementioned setup, the dataset size
n
satisfies

n>\left[\frac{\lvert\mathcal{L}\rvert}{C(\lvert\mathcal{L}\rvert-1)}P(F)\right]^{1/\alpha}.

Then, there is a payment scheme
(p_{l})_{l\in\mathcal{L}}
such that
U^{l}_{agn}\geq U^{l}_{spec}\text{ for all }l\in\mathcal{L}
.
In other words, no learner is incentivized to opt out of the learner-agnostic prefiltering scheme.

Furthermore, this condition holds for sufficiently large
n
as long as
P(F)
grows as
o(n^{\alpha})
.
Harking back to
Figure

2
, we observe indications of sublinear growth across all setups.
This gives us an empirical confirmation of the overall benefit from LARP.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the problem of prefiltering public data with learner-agnostic guarantees.
We presented a formal framework for the problem and highlighted differences to classic robust learning.
We presented a specific instance of the framework that showcases the need for guarantees that depend on the learner set.
We then proved upper bounds on the achievable maximum risk for the task of mean estimation for scalar Gaussian random variables.
We also conducted an empirical analysis on real-world tabular and image data in order to measure the effect of learner heterogeneity on downstream performance.
Finally, we argued about the benefits of LARP on large datasets.

Limitations and future work

We see our work as an initial step towards understanding how prefiltering datasets can provide reliability guarantees for inference and learning procedures applied on top of them.
We consider generalizations of the results presented in
Section

4
to regression and classification tasks, as well as the design of provably optimal prefiltering procedures, to be an exciting direction for future work.

Additional details for experiments on mean estimation

We conduct an experiment on an instance of LARP in the context of mean estimation with

\theta=0

The sample size we set is

We simulate Huber contamination by using a set of noise distributions
\mathfrak{Q}=\left\{\mathcal{N}(m,1):m\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

We can use symmetry arguments to restrict the space to
m\geq 0

The first prefiltering is defined as in
Section

4.3
.
The second prefiltering that we study is based on the famous “three-sigma edit” rule .
Intuitively speaking, given a sample
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, we can measure the “outlyingness” of a single observation as
t_{i}=(x_{i}-\overline{x})/s
and then removing all points for which
\lvert t_{i}\rvert>3
.
This measure is also known as the z-score of the observation.
Motivated by this, we study the sample z-score function as

Z\left(x,\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\right)\vcentcolon=\left\lvert\frac{x-M(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})}{SD(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})}\right\rvert

where the sample mean
M(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and the sample standard deviation
SD(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
are defined as

\displaystyle M(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{n}

\displaystyle SD(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(X_{i}-M(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\right)^{2}}.

Then, we can define the z-score prefiltering mechanism as

F^{z}_{l}\left(S\right)\vcentcolon=\left\{X\in S:Z\left(X,S\right)<l\right\}.

Note that this mechanism has a hyperparameter
l
that can be selected in order to apply a “
l
-sigma edit” rule on a dataset.

The third prefiltering mechanism is based on Stahel-Donoho outlyingness (SDO), which can be defined as

SDO\left(x,\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right\}\right)\vcentcolon=\frac{\lvert x-Med(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\rvert}{MAD(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})},

where
Med(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
and
MAD(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
are defined as the sample median and the median absolute deviation respectively.

\displaystyle Med(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}X_{(k)},\text{ if $n$ is odd, }k=\frac{n+1}{2}\\
\frac{X_{(k)}+X_{(k+1)}}{2},\text{ if $n$ is even, }k=\frac{n}{2}\end{cases}

\displaystyle MAD(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

\displaystyle=Med\left(\left\{\lvert X_{i}-Med(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\rvert\right\}\right).

This induces the SDO prefiltering procedure, defined as

F^{sdo}_{p}(S)\vcentcolon=\left\{X\in S:SDO(X,S)<p\right\}.

The hyperparameter
p
that can take values in the range
(0,\infty)
, with similar interpretation as in the “
p
-sigma edit” rule with different measures for location and scale.

In

Finally, we repeat this process 8 times in order to get the expected value and standard error of

For

In the experiment on

B.2
Additional details for real-world data experiments

For Adult dataset, we consider seven different algorithms, as described in
Section

5.1
.
Then, we generate two sets of fixed hyperparameters for each of them using random search, resulting in 14 different models that form the learner set.
The robust version of each model is performed by training on the noisy train set and then evaluating on the noisy validation set.
The objective of the respective hyperparameter search is to maximize the validation metric, either acuracy or F1 depending on noise.
The brittle version of each model is performed by training two instances of the same model.
For the first instance we prefilter training and validation sets together, then train on the train set and evaluate on the validation set.
The second instance is trained and evaluated on untouched noisy train and validation sets respectively.
The objective of the hyperparameter search is to maximize the difference between the two evaluation metrics.
We follow this procedure once for each type of noise.
The resulting hyperparameters are shown in
Table

3
for the shortcut setting and
Table

2
for the setting of label noise.

For the CIFAR-10 dataset, we use a custom CNN that we present in
Table

1
.
All instances of our model are trained using Adam optimizer with learning rate
1e{-3}
and batch size
128
.

For experiments in the CIFAR-10 with label noise, prefiltering is done by training the standard Resent-9 architecture using batch size 128 and Adam optimizer with learning rate
1e{-3}
for 7 epochs.
For experiments with shortcuts the model we use for prefiltering is our custom CNN, trained using batch size 128 and learning rate
1e{-3}
for 5 epochs.

In all experiments we calculate the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering as follows.
First, we conduct multiple runs where we test our prefiltering procedure with different values of the prefiltering hyperparameter
p

In each run we train all models on the prefiltered train and validation datasets and evaluate on clean test dataset.
Finally, for each run we compute the best learner-specific risk over all

Appendix D
Measuring price of learner-agnostic prefiltering in the mean estimation setup

While
Lemma

5.1
holds in the general setting, verifying the given condition requires knowledge of
P(F)
, which can be measured only after the prefiltering is conducted.
However, in the setting of mean estimation we can use the upper bound from
Theorem

4.2
as a surrogate measure for downstream performance.
This allows us to provide a provable guarantee on the benefit of LARP that only uses the initial parameters of the instance.

Assume that we conduct the LARP instance presented in
Section

4
, with
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
.
We denote the set of Huber parameters
\Delta=\left\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{N}\right\}
where without loss of generality
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}\leq\ldots,\leq\delta_{N}
.
Then we can calculate the price of learner-agnostic prefiltering explicitly:

P(F)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}_{red}\left((\epsilon^{2}+\log(1/\delta_{0})/n)\sigma^{2}+\delta_{N}^{2},(\epsilon^{2}+\log(1/\delta_{0})/n)\sigma^{2}+\delta_{i}^{2}\right).

(6)

We note that in this setting
P
depends only on the learner set
\mathcal{L}
through the set of hyperparameters
\Delta
.

Lemma D.1
.

Assume that the utility reduction function
\mathcal{U}_{red}
can be represented as

\mathcal{U}_{red}(x,y)=\mathcal{R}(y)-\mathcal{R}(x),

where the function
\mathcal{R}
represents general utility from achieving particular risk.
Furthermore, assume that the function
\mathcal{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz, i.e. we have

\lvert\mathcal{R}(x)-\mathcal{R}(y)\rvert\leq L\lvert x-y\rvert,

for all
x,y>0
.
Then, if in addition to the assumption to
n\geq\Omega\left(\log\left(1/\delta_{0}\right)\right)
, the dataset size
n
satisfies

n\geq\left(\frac{LN}{C(N-1)}\left(\max_{\delta\in\Delta}\delta^{2}-\min_{\delta\in\Delta}\delta^{2}\right)\right)^{1/\alpha},

then, there is a payment scheme
(p_{i})^{i=1}_{N}
such that, with probability
1-\delta_{0}
over the randomness of the noisy sample, we have
{U^{(i)}_{agn}\geq U^{(i)}_{spec}}
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that
\delta_{1}\leq\delta_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\delta_{N}
.
This implies that
R_{u}^{(1)}\leq R_{u}^{(2)}\leq\ldots\leq R_{u}^{(N)}
.
If every learner decides to do individual prefiltering, their utility will be

U^{(i)}_{spec}=\mathcal{R}(R_{u}^{(i)})-Cn^{\alpha}.

On the other hand, we can see that the learner-agnostic risk when all learners participate in the prefiltering and also the payments are
(p_{i})^{i=1}_{N}
is

U^{(i)}_{agn}=\mathcal{R}(R_{u}^{(N)})-p_{i}.

Thus, the difference in the utilities can be calculated as

\displaystyle U^{(i)}_{spec}-U^{(i)}_{agn}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}(R_{u}^{(i)})-Cn^{\alpha}-\mathcal{R}(R_{u}^{(N)})+p_{i}

\displaystyle\leq L\lvert R_{u}^{(i)}-R_{u}^{(N)}\rvert-Cn^{\alpha}+p_{i}

\displaystyle=L\left(\delta_{N}^{2}-\delta_{i}^{2}\right)-Cn^{\alpha}+p_{i}.

Now, recall that
\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}=Cn^{\alpha}
by definition.
Let us set

p_{i}\propto Cn^{\alpha}-L\left(\delta^{2}_{N}-\delta^{2}_{i}\right).

Then we have the inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}Cn^{\alpha}-L\left(\delta^{2}_{N}-\delta^{2}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq NCn^{\alpha}-NL\left(\delta^{2}_{N}-\delta^{2}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq NCn^{\alpha}-\left(N-1\right)Cn^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq Cn^{\alpha},

where the last inequality follows from our assumption that

n\geq\left(\frac{LN}{C(N-1)}\left(\delta^{2}_{N}-\delta^{2}_{1}\right)\right)^{1/\alpha}.

Hence we have that
p_{i}\leq Cn^{\alpha}-L\left(\delta_{N}-\delta_{i}\right)
and hence
U^{(i)}_{spec}-U^{(i)}_{agn}\leq 0
, as desired.
