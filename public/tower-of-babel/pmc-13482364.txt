Trustworthy and ethical intrusion detection for healthcare internet of medical things using reinforcement learning and governance rules
Authors: Ibrahim Adabara, Bashir Olaniyi Sadiq, Aliyu Nuhu Shuaibu, Yale Ibrahim Danjuma, Venkateswarlu Maninti, Mutebi Joe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealthcare Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environments require intrusion detection systems that are accurate, safe, proportionate, and auditable. Conventional intrusion detection metrics such as accuracy and F1-score quantify classification performance but do not indicate whether automated response actions are clinically safe or operationally acceptable. This study proposes a governance-aware intrusion response framework for healthcare IoMT systems. The framework integrates a Random Forest flow-based detector, a Deep Q-Network triage agent, and an ethical rule engine informed by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. The detector provides probabilistic and uncertainty-aware evidence, the triage agent selects one of four response actions, and the rule engine constrains unsafe actions through explicit governance rules, fallback decisions, and audit logs. The framework was evaluated using CIC-IoMT 2024 for in-domain assessment, a stratified CSE-CIC-IDS2018 sample for domain-shift stress testing, simulated Clinical Load Index sensitivity analysis, independent decision-quality metrics, and temporal streaming replay. In-domain DQN triage achieved a weighted F1-score of 0.978. Governance reduced benign blocking from 25.3% to 17.3%, showing that rule oversight reduced one form of potentially disruptive automated response. Corrected cross-domain evaluation showed substantial performance degradation, with CAS retention scores of 0.282 for the Random Forest baseline and 0.341 for the DQN triage model. In temporal streaming replay, performance declined after the transition to shifted traffic; however, the governed DQN produced lower mean decision harm cost than the unconstrained DQN under shifted replay windows, reducing harm cost from 0.502 to 0.434. These findings suggest that ethical rule oversight can improve selected decision-safety outcomes under controlled, uncertain, and shifted conditions. Introduction

The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) is increasingly embedded in healthcare delivery through interconnected bedside monitors, infusion pumps, wearable sensors, imaging platforms, and hospital telemetry devices. These systems support continuous monitoring, remote diagnostics, and data-driven clinical decision-making, making reliable device communication an essential part of modern healthcare infrastructure1–3. As IoMT adoption expands, healthcare networks become more dependent on heterogeneous devices that exchange high-frequency clinical and operational data across wireless, wired, and cloud-connected environments4,5.

This dependence creates a cybersecurity problem that differs from ordinary enterprise security. In conventional enterprise systems, a network disruption may primarily affect data access, business continuity, or administrative services. In healthcare IoMT environments, the same disruption may affect patient monitoring, device coordination, clinical workflow, and the availability of time-sensitive information used by clinicians2,6. IoMT devices are also exposed to threats such as denial-of-service attacks, reconnaissance, spoofing, botnet activity, and protocol misuse, which can compromise confidentiality, integrity, and availability in safety-critical settings7,5,8.

Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) are therefore an important defense mechanism for healthcare networks. Machine learning and deep learning methods have improved IDS performance in IoT and IoMT environments by learning flow-level patterns associated with benign and malicious traffic9–11. However, accurate detection does not automatically imply a safe response. A false positive may produce unnecessary alerts, increase analyst workload, or contribute to alarm fatigue. More seriously, an automated blocking action applied to benign IoMT communication may interrupt device connectivity and affect clinical continuity. Similarly, excessive escalation of low-risk events may burden security teams and reduce attention to urgent threats12,13.

These risks indicate that IDS decisions in healthcare should be governed not only by detection confidence but also by safety, proportionality, auditability, and operational context. This is particularly important when reinforcement learning is used for adaptive intrusion response, because an agent may learn actions that optimize a reward function without fully reflecting clinical constraints or institutional governance requirements14,15. Governance-aware IDS design is therefore needed to ensure that automated responses are constrained by explicit rules, interpretable fallbacks, and audit logs aligned with responsible AI principles16,17.

Conventional IDS evaluation commonly relies on accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. These metrics are useful for measuring whether a model correctly classifies network traffic, but they do not evaluate the safety of the action selected after detection. Conventional IDS metrics indicate whether traffic is classified correctly, but they do not show whether the selected response action is clinically safe, proportionate, auditable, or operationally acceptable.

This limitation is important in healthcare IoMT environments because two IDS models may achieve similar classification performance while producing substantially different operational consequences. A model that frequently blocks benign device traffic may pose a higher clinical risk than a model with a comparable F1-score but less disruptive response behavior. Likewise, a system that escalates too many low-risk events may increase operational burden without improving patient or network safety. The problem addressed in this study is therefore not only how to detect IoMT intrusions, but how to govern response actions so that automated decisions remain safe, transparent, and proportionate under uncertainty, workload variation, and domain shift.

Existing IoMT IDS studies mainly optimize classification accuracy rather than response safety. Many recent studies have improved detection using tree-based models, deep learning, feature selection, ensemble learning, and explainable AI approaches18–20,11. Although these approaches improve the ability to identify malicious traffic, they often treat detection as the final objective. In healthcare IoMT settings, however, the operational action that follows detection is equally important because actions such as blocking or escalation may affect clinical workflow.

Reinforcement learning-based IDS methods can support adaptive intrusion response by learning action policies in dynamic environments21,22. However, reinforcement learning approaches often rely on reward functions that emphasize attack mitigation without embedding explicit governance constraints. This can produce decisions that are technically effective but clinically inappropriate, especially when model confidence is low, traffic conditions shift, or operational workload is high23,15. For healthcare IoMT systems, adaptive response policies require governance boundaries that can override unsafe or disproportionate actions.

Healthcare IDS evaluation also rarely measures action-level safety under clinical workload variation and domain shift. Existing evaluations frequently report classification performance on benchmark datasets, but fewer studies assess benign blocking, attack allowance, unnecessary escalation, decision harm cost, or governance override behavior. Domain-shift testing is particularly important because IoMT-trained models may degrade when exposed to traffic distributions that differ from the training environment24,25. Similarly, clinical workload may alter the acceptable cost of blocking, alerting, or escalation, yet this operational context is rarely incorporated into IDS evaluation26,27.

This study makes six contributions. First, it proposes a governance-aware intrusion response framework for healthcare IoMT environments by integrating a Random Forest flow detector, a Deep Q-Network triage agent, and ethical rule oversight. Second, it formalizes an ethical rule engine with explicit triggers, fallback actions, rule priorities, and audit logging, drawing on responsible AI governance principles and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework16,17. Third, it introduces an independent decision-quality evaluation strategy that goes beyond circular rule conformance by measuring benign blocking rate, attack allow rate, unnecessary escalation rate, override rate, and mean decision harm cost. Fourth, it evaluates Clinical Load Index sensitivity using alternative weighting schemes for intensive care load, emergency demand, and time-based operational stress. Fifth, it corrects the domain-shift evaluation using stratified CSE-CIC-IDS2018 sampling and a revised Cross-Domain Adaptation Score based on F1 retention. Sixth, it adds a temporal streaming replay experiment to partially address dynamic validation concerns by evaluating system behavior across sequential in-domain and shifted traffic windows.

This study is a controlled offline simulation using public cybersecurity datasets and simulated or context-derived workload signals. It does not claim validation in a live hospital network, real-time clinical deployment, or direct integration with operational medical devices. The Clinical Load Index is used as a proxy for workload-sensitive governance testing rather than as a clinically validated hospital workload predictor. The CSE-CIC-IDS2018 experiment is used as a domain-shift stress test and should not be interpreted as evidence that an IoMT-trained model can be deployed directly in hospital networks without local calibration and validation. Accordingly, the framework should be understood as a methodological contribution to governance-aware IoMT intrusion response that requires further evaluation in semi-live testbeds, healthcare cyber ranges, and real clinical network environments.

Related work

Machine learning and deep learning have become central to intrusion detection in IoT and IoMT environments because they can learn complex traffic patterns that are difficult to capture using static signatures. In healthcare networks, IDS models have been developed using tree-based classifiers, ensemble methods, deep neural networks, and hybrid architectures to detect abnormal behavior in medical device traffic10,11,5. Recent IoMT studies have also explored explainable XGBoost, feature selection, stacked deep learning, and deep learning-based IDS designs to improve detection performance in connected healthcare environments28,18,9,19.

These studies show that high classification performance is achievable on benchmark datasets. For example, IoMT and healthcare IDS research commonly reports high accuracy, precision, recall, or F1-score when models are trained and tested under controlled conditions28,9,20. However, the operational meaning of a detection decision is less frequently examined. In a healthcare IoMT setting, the action taken after detection may be as important as the classification result itself. A technically correct alert may still be operationally disruptive if it increases alert burden, while an incorrect block may interfere with benign medical device communication. Existing IDS studies therefore provide strong evidence for classification capability, but comparatively less evidence on whether automated response actions are clinically safe, proportionate, or auditable.

Reinforcement learning has been proposed as a promising approach for adaptive cybersecurity because it can learn policies that respond to changing threats and network states. Deep reinforcement learning has been applied to cyber defense, intrusion detection, and multi-stage attack response, including approaches based on DQN, partially observable Markov decision processes, and multi-agent reinforcement learning26,21,14. In IoT and IoMT contexts, reinforcement learning is attractive because network conditions, attack strategies, and device behaviors may change over time, requiring response policies that are more flexible than static threshold rules29,15.

Despite this potential, reinforcement learning introduces safety concerns when used for automated intrusion response. An RL agent optimizes a reward function, but the reward function may not fully represent clinical priorities, device criticality, workload pressure, or institutional governance constraints. This is especially important in IoMT environments, where the same response action may have different consequences depending on whether the affected device supports routine monitoring or critical patient care. Without explicit governance constraints, a learned policy may select actions that reduce cyber risk while increasing operational or clinical risk. This limitation motivates the use of rule-based oversight to constrain reinforcement learning decisions before they are executed.

Trustworthy AI governance requires that automated systems are not only technically effective but also safe, accountable, explainable, and aligned with human and institutional values. In healthcare, these requirements are particularly important because AI-enabled decisions may affect patient safety, clinical workflow, and professional accountability30–32. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework provides a structured approach for identifying, measuring, managing, and governing AI-related risks, including risks associated with reliability, safety, transparency, accountability, and harmful misuse17. Subsequent work has emphasized that AI risk management requires maturity models, operational governance, and continuous oversight rather than abstract ethical statements alone33,34.

Cross-layer agentic AI governance is also relevant to cybersecurity systems that combine autonomous reasoning, adaptive planning, and action execution. Recent work on trustworthy agentic AI systems emphasizes that autonomous agents introduce governance and security challenges that cannot be addressed through static policies alone. Instead, agentic systems require cross-layer safeguards that connect architecture, threat modelling, oversight, auditability, and ethical governance. This perspective supports the need to treat IoMT intrusion response as a governed socio-technical process rather than a purely technical classification task35.

In cybersecurity, ethical governance is needed because automated systems may select defensive actions that have real operational consequences. Blocking, isolating, or escalating traffic may protect systems from attack, but these actions may also disrupt legitimate service, increase workload, or shift responsibility to human operators. Recent discussions of AI-driven cybersecurity decision-making highlight the need to address proportionality, accountability, explainability, and human oversight when autonomous or agentic systems are used in security operations36,16. Context-aware IDS research in vehicles and IoT systems further shows that security decisions can be improved when the system considers operational context rather than relying only on raw detection output37–39. These ideas support the need for a healthcare IoMT IDS that combines detection accuracy with explicit ethical rule constraints and auditability.

Explainability is essential for intrusion detection systems used in high-risk environments because security analysts and clinical stakeholders must understand why a system generated an alert, blocked a flow, or escalated an event. Explainable AI methods such as SHAP, LIME, feature importance, and model-specific interpretation have been applied to IDS and healthcare cybersecurity to support transparency and forensic analysis40–42. In IoMT settings, explainability is particularly important because false positives and automated interventions may affect clinical continuity. A model that produces accurate predictions but provides no interpretable basis for its actions may be difficult to trust or audit in safety-critical environments.

Human-centered decision-making also requires that automated systems provide actionable rationales rather than only prediction labels. Feature-importance analysis can show which traffic characteristics influence detection, while decision-path logs can show how detector confidence, uncertainty, clinical workload, and governance rules contribute to the final response action. Audit logs are, therefore, a practical mechanism for linking technical detection to accountable decision-making. This is consistent with the broader direction of responsible healthcare AI, where explainability, human oversight, traceability, and institutional governance are treated as prerequisites for safe deployment31,17. For IoMT intrusion response, explainability should therefore include both model-level interpretation and action-level justification.

Broader interdisciplinary work also supports the need for contextual and human-centered reasoning in AI-assisted decision-making. The authors in43 proposed a human-centered explainable AI framework for healthcare, emphasizing that AI systems should assist, interact with, and collaborate with human professionals rather than replace situated clinical judgement43. Similarly, Peter and Ibrahim argued that complex reasoning and innovation cannot always be reduced to linear analytical models, highlighting the value of unconventional and systems-oriented modelling when dealing with complex human-centered processes44. These perspectives strengthen the rationale for treating IoMT intrusion response as a governed decision-support process that combines model evidence, operational context, human oversight, and auditable action justification rather than relying only on classification accuracy.

Existing research has improved IDS classification performance in IoT and IoMT environments, and reinforcement learning has created new opportunities for adaptive intrusion response. However, fewer studies jointly evaluate detection, action selection, ethical override, clinical workload sensitivity, explainability, and domain-shift stress in healthcare IoMT environments. This gap is important because healthcare IDS performance cannot be judged only by whether traffic is classified correctly. It must also be evaluated by whether the resulting action is safe, proportionate, auditable, and robust under operational uncertainty. The present study addresses this gap by combining Random Forest detection, DQN-based triage, governance rule oversight, independent action-level metrics, Clinical Load Index sensitivity analysis, explainability outputs, and corrected domain-shift evaluation.

Materials and data sources

Dataset overview

This study used three data sources to evaluate the proposed governance-aware intrusion response framework. CIC-IoMT 2024 was used as the primary in-domain healthcare IoMT dataset for detector training, DQN triage training, and in-domain evaluation. CSE-CIC-IDS2018 was used as an external domain-shift stress-test dataset to evaluate how the trained models behave when exposed to a different traffic distribution. Clinical workload context was represented using simulated workload signals inspired by operational indicators available in MIMIC-IV Demo and related clinical data resources. These signals were used only for controlled governance sensitivity analysis and temporal replay experiments, not for clinical prediction or live hospital validation49–52 See table 2.

CIC-IoMT 2024

CIC-IoMT 2024 was used as the primary healthcare IoMT dataset because it was designed for multi-protocol security assessment in Internet of Medical Things environments49. The dataset includes WiFi and MQTT traffic patterns that reflect common communication modes in connected healthcare systems. In this study, the WiFi/MQTT subset was used because it provides flow-level records suitable for training supervised intrusion detection models and for replay-based DQN triage simulation.

Each flow contains 45 numeric features describing protocol behavior, packet statistics, timing characteristics, flag counts, and traffic-rate patterns. The labels include benign flows and multiple attack categories representing adversarial behaviors relevant to healthcare IoMT networks. For supervised detection, the original labels were retained for multiclass detector training where applicable. For triage and governance evaluation, labels were also mapped into a binary representation, where benign traffic was assigned to class 0, and all attack categories were assigned to class 1. This binary mapping enabled direct evaluation of action-level outcomes such as benign blocking, attack allowance, escalation, and harm cost. Figure 1. CIC-IoMT dataset overview.

Overview of CIC-IoMT 2024 WiFi/MQTT flow data and attack categories used for in-domain training and evaluation.

CSE-CIC-IDS2018

CSE-CIC-IDS2018 was used as a deliberate domain-shift stress-test dataset rather than as a healthcare IoMT dataset. The dataset contains enterprise-scale network traffic and multiple attack types and has been widely used in IDS research for evaluating detection models under heterogeneous traffic conditions50,53,25. Its traffic distribution differs from CIC-IoMT 2024, making it suitable for testing how a model trained on healthcare IoMT traffic behaves when exposed to non-IoMT network patterns.

The corrected domain-shift experiment used a stratified balanced sample of 200,000 flows, consisting of 100,000 benign and 100,000 attack flows. This sampling strategy was adopted to avoid one-class evaluation and to ensure that shifted-domain performance reflected both benign specificity and attack sensitivity. Because CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and CIC-IoMT 2024 use different feature schemas, a harmonization procedure mapped compatible CSE-CIC-IDS2018 variables into the 45-feature IoMT input space. Features without reliable correspondence were imputed using CIC-IoMT training-set means. A feature-mapping report was retained to make this process transparent and to support reproducibility. The resulting evaluation is interpreted as a cross-domain stress test, not as evidence of direct deployment readiness.

Clinical workload context

Clinical workload was represented through a simulated Clinical Load Index used to test whether governance behavior remained stable under different operational stress assumptions. The index was inspired by hospital workload concepts such as intensive care occupancy, emergency demand, and time-of-day operational pressure, which are commonly represented in clinical and critical care datasets such as MIMIC-IV and related PhysioNet resources51,54,27. In this study, the index was not used as a validated hospital workload predictor. It was used as a controlled contextual signal for sensitivity analysis and temporal replay.

Ethical and data-use considerations

All data sources used in this study were public, de-identified, or simulation-derived. CIC-IoMT 2024 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 are publicly available cybersecurity datasets used for network intrusion detection research50,49. Clinical workload context was derived as a simulated or aggregate operational context and did not involve patient-level prediction, diagnosis, or intervention. No live patient data, direct human-subject interaction, real hospital network traffic, or operational medical device integration was used. The study, therefore, represents controlled offline experimentation and does not constitute clinical deployment validation.

Governance-aware intrusion response framework

System overview

The proposed framework separates intrusion detection, action selection, governance oversight, and audit logging into a sequential decision pipeline. Each network flow is first processed by a Random Forest detector trained on the CIC-IoMT 2024 feature space. The detector produces class probabilities, from which the maximum prediction probability and entropy are extracted as confidence and uncertainty indicators. These values, together with selected scaled flow features, form the state representation for the Deep Q-Network (DQN) triage agent. The DQN then proposes one of four response actions: allow, alert, block, or escalate.

The proposed action is not executed directly. Instead, it is passed to an ethical rule engine inspired by the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which evaluates the action using detector confidence, uncertainty, simulated clinical load, and contextual risk. If the proposed action violates a governance rule, the rule engine replaces it with a predefined fallback action and records the decision rationale. The final action is then forwarded for execution, while the original action, final action, triggered rule, confidence values, and contextual variables are stored in an audit log. This design reflects the need for trustworthy intrusion response systems that are not only adaptive but also explainable, accountable, and constrained by safety-oriented governance principles See fig 2.

Random Forest detector

The implemented detection module is a Random Forest classifier. Random Forest was selected because it is suitable for tabular network-flow data, provides strong performance on IDS benchmarks, supports efficient CPU-based inference, and provides model-level feature importance for explainability. These characteristics are relevant to healthcare IoMT settings, where intrusion detection must operate under computational constraints while remaining interpretable to security analysts and clinical stakeholders. Tree-based models have also been widely used in IoT and IoMT intrusion detection because of their robustness to heterogeneous feature distributions and their ability to model nonlinear relationships in traffic data18,10,5.

For each flow, the detector outputs a probability distribution over the learned classes. This output is used as evidence rather than as a direct response command. The highest-class probability provides a confidence estimate, while entropy over the probability distribution provides an uncertainty estimate. These two quantities support downstream triage by allowing the DQN and governance layer to distinguish between high-confidence threats, uncertain flows, and low-risk traffic.

DQN triage agent

The triage module is implemented as a Deep Q-Network that selects an operational response action from the detector output and compact flow context. Reinforcement learning is appropriate for this task because intrusion response involves sequential decision-making under uncertainty, where actions may carry different security and operational costs21,14. However, the DQN is used as a triage component rather than an unconstrained autonomous responder. Its proposed action remains subject to ethical rule evaluation before execution.

Action space

The DQN triage agent selects from four response actions:

The allow action permits the flow to continue without intervention and is appropriate for benign or low-risk traffic. The alert action notifies the monitoring system without interrupting communication, making it suitable for uncertain or low-confidence anomalies. The block action interrupts the flow and is reserved for high-risk traffic because incorrect blocking may disrupt legitimate IoMT communication. The escalated action forwards the event for higher-priority review and is used when uncertainty, risk, or contextual sensitivity makes automated resolution inappropriate. This action space reflects the principle that healthcare intrusion response should be proportional to both cyber risk and potential clinical disruption12,17.

Reward function

The DQN reward function was designed to encourage correct threat response while penalizing actions that may create operational harm. The implemented reward matrix assigns a high reward to blocking attacks and allowing benign traffic, while penalizing missed attacks, benign blocking, and unnecessary escalation. This formulation reflects the asymmetric risk structure of healthcare IoMT intrusion response, where allowing an attack and blocking benign medical device communication can both be harmful, but in different ways see table 3.

Ethical rule engine

The ethical rule engine evaluates the DQN-proposed action before execution. It applies explicit governance rules based on model confidence, attack probability, prediction uncertainty, simulated clinical load, and contextual risk. These rules are designed to prevent unsafe automation, reduce clinically disruptive blocking, and support human review when uncertainty or operational pressure is high. Evaluation metrics

The evaluation framework was designed to assess both predictive performance and action-level safety. Conventional IDS metrics were used to measure detection quality, while governance and decision-quality metrics were used to evaluate whether the selected response actions were proportionate, auditable, and operationally acceptable. This distinction is important because high detection accuracy alone does not show whether an automated response is safe in a healthcare IoMT environment11,17.

Detection metrics

Detection performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, weighted F1-score, and macro F1-score. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified flows across all test samples. Precision measures the proportion of predicted attacks that were truly malicious, while recall measures the proportion of actual attacks that were detected. The F1-score provides the harmonic mean of precision and recall and is useful when both false positives and false negatives are important. Weighted F1-score accounts for class support and therefore reflects performance under imbalanced class distributions, whereas macro F1-score gives equal weight to each class and is useful for assessing whether performance is consistent across benign and attack categories. These metrics are commonly used in IoT and IoMT intrusion detection studies and provide a baseline for comparison with existing IDS work18,9,45.

Independent decision-quality metrics

To evaluate action-level consequences independently of the rule engine, four additional decision-quality metrics were computed: Benign Blocking Rate, Attack Allow Rate, Unnecessary Escalation Rate, and Mean Decision Harm Cost. These metrics use ground-truth labels and executed actions to quantify the operational consequences of the response policy.

Benign Blocking Rate (BBR) measures the proportion of benign flows that were blocked. This metric is important in healthcare IoMT because blocking benign device communication may disrupt clinical monitoring or device coordination.

Attack Allow Rate (AAR) measures the proportion of attack flows that were allowed. A high AAR indicates unsafe under-response because malicious traffic is permitted to continue.

Unnecessary Escalation Rate (UER) measures the proportion of escalated events that corresponded to benign flows. This metric reflects potential operator burden and alert fatigue.

Mean Decision Harm Cost (HC) summarizes the expected operational harm of the executed actions using a predefined cost matrix. The cost function assigns higher penalties to clinically or operationally harmful actions, such as allowing attacks or blocking benign traffic See table 5.

Temporal replay metrics

Temporal streaming replay was evaluated using windowed weighted F1-score, windowed mean decision harm cost, governance override rate, and per-flow latency. Windowed F1-score measures detection and triage performance within each replay segment. Windowed harm cost measures the operational consequence of decisions across time. Governance override rate measures the proportion of DQN-proposed actions modified by the ethical rule engine. Per-flow latency measures the average processing time required to generate a final governed action. These metrics were computed separately for the DQN without governance and the governed DQN to assess whether rule-based oversight changed action behavior under sequential in-domain and shifted-domain traffic.

Experimental setup

Hardware and software

All experiments were conducted on a CPU-only workstation running Windows 11 Pro with an 11th Gen Intel Core i7-1185G7 processor, 16 GB RAM, integrated Intel Iris Xe graphics, and a 477 GB solid-state drive. No dedicated GPU acceleration was used. The experimental environment was managed using Anaconda and JupyterLab with Python 3.10. PyTorch was used for the DQN triage agent, scikit-learn for preprocessing and Random Forest modelling, and NumPy and Pandas for numerical and tabular data processing. Matplotlib was used for visualization. Random seeds were fixed across NumPy and PyTorch to improve reproducibility. CPU-only execution was retained to reflect a lightweight experimental setting suitable for evaluating whether the framework could operate under constrained computing conditions commonly associated with edge-oriented IoT and healthcare cybersecurity environments21,18.

Detector training

The supervised detector was implemented as a Random Forest classifier trained on the CIC-IoMT 2024 WiFi/MQTT feature space. The raw dataset contained 45 numeric flow-level features after removal of the label column. These features were standardized using a StandardScaler fitted only on the CIC-IoMT training data to prevent information leakage from the test set. The same scaler was reused during DQN state construction and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 domain-shift evaluation to ensure consistent feature transformation.

To manage memory and runtime on the CPU-only workstation, the Random Forest was trained using a reproducible random subset of 200,000 CIC-IoMT training flows. The trained detector and scaler were saved as a reusable model bundle and subsequently loaded by the DQN, governance, and domain-shift evaluation notebooks. The detector produced class-probability distributions for each flow. These probabilities were not used as direct response actions; instead, the maximum class probability and probability entropy were extracted as confidence and uncertainty indicators for the DQN triage state. The Random Forest design was selected because tree-based models are effective for tabular IDS data, provide efficient CPU inference, and support feature-importance analysis for explainability10,5.

DQN training

The DQN triage agent was trained in an offline replay environment using CIC-IoMT flows and binary benign/attack labels. The state representation contained 12 dimensions: maximum detector probability, entropy of the detector probability distribution, and the first ten scaled flow features. The agent selected one of four actions: allow, alert, block, or escalate. The DQN architecture consisted of two fully connected hidden layers with 64 units each and ReLU activations, followed by an output layer corresponding to the four response actions.

The DQN was trained on 80,000 CIC-IoMT samples, while 20,000 samples were reserved for evaluation. Training was conducted for 200 episodes using a batch size of 256, a discount factor of 0.99, and the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of . A replay buffer with a capacity of 200,000 stored transition tuples and a target network was synchronized periodically to stabilize temporal-difference learning. Exploration followed an ε-greedy strategy, with ε decaying from 1.0 to 0.05 during training. This design allowed the agent to explore response actions early in training while gradually shifting toward exploitation of learned Q-values. The resulting trained DQN was saved and reused for in-domain evaluation, governed policy assessment, and domain-shift testing. Reinforcement learning was used to support adaptive triage, but the learned action was not executed without governance review because unconstrained RL policies may produce unsafe or disproportionate actions in safety-critical settings14,15.

Domain-shift protocol

Domain-shift evaluation was conducted using CSE-CIC-IDS2018 as an external stress-test dataset. This dataset was not treated as a healthcare IoMT dataset; rather, it was used to examine how the trained system behaves when exposed to a substantially different traffic distribution. The corrected evaluation used a stratified balanced sample of 200,000 flows, consisting of 100,000 benign flows and 100,000 attack flows. This sampling strategy ensured that both benign specificity and attack sensitivity were measurable.

The CSE-CIC-IDS2018 features were harmonized into the 45-feature CIC-IoMT input schema used by the trained Random Forest and DQN. Of the 45 target IoMT features, 43 were mapped from compatible CSE-CIC-IDS2018 fields or derived protocol statistics. The remaining two features were imputed using CIC-IoMT training-set means from the fitted scaler. A feature-harmonization report was retained to make the mapping transparent and reproducible. Because the source and target datasets differ in capture environment, feature semantics, and traffic distribution, the results are interpreted as cross-domain stress testing rather than evidence of direct deployment validity25,24 See table 6.

Clinical Load Index sensitivity protocol

Clinical workload sensitivity was evaluated using a simulated Clinical Load Index rather than live hospital workload data. The index combined normalized intensive care load, emergency demand, and time-of-day operational stress. To test whether governance outcomes depended strongly on a single weighting choice, four weighting scenarios were evaluated: equal weights, ICU-heavy, emergency-room-heavy, and time-stress-heavy. This analysis was designed to address the sensitivity of the ethical rule engine to clinical workload assumptions, while acknowledging that the Clinical Load Index is a proxy signal and not a clinically validated workload model.

Temporal streaming replay protocol

A temporal streaming replay experiment was conducted to partially address dynamic validation concerns. The replay simulated sequential processing of network flows across 24 windows. Windows 1–12 contained in-domain CIC-IoMT traffic, while windows 13–24 contained shifted CSE-CIC-IDS2018 traffic. Simulated clinical load increased across the replay windows to test governance behavior under rising operational pressure.

For each window, the DQN without governance and the DQN with governance were evaluated using windowed weighted F1-score, mean decision harm cost, override rate, and per-flow latency. The comparison was designed to determine whether ethical rule oversight changed action behavior during the temporal transition from in-domain to shifted traffic. This experiment does not replace live hospital validation, but it provides a controlled temporal stress test of policy stability, governance intervention, and decision harm cost under sequential distribution shift.

DQN triage performance

The DQN triage agent was evaluated on 20,000 in-domain CIC-IoMT test flows. In the binary flagging view, where alert, block, and escalate are treated as positive security interventions, the DQN achieved an accuracy of 0.983 and a weighted F1-score of 0.978. Attack recall was 1.000, indicating that the agent did not allow attack flows in this in-domain evaluation. The action distribution showed that the DQN selected block most frequently, followed by escalate, alert, and allow. Specifically, allow was selected for 126 flows (0.63%), alert for 174 flows (0.87%), block for 16,227 flows (81.14%), and escalate for 3,473 flows (17.37%).

The governance-oriented evaluation produced an Ethical Compliance Rate of 0.990 and a False Escalation Rate of 0.021. These values indicate high rule conformance and a low proportion of unnecessary escalations among all escalation actions. However, ECR is interpreted only as rule conformance and not as independent evidence of ethical correctness, because it depends on the rule engine used to evaluate the actions See fig 4.

Discussion

This study evaluated a governance-aware intrusion response framework for healthcare IoMT environments by combining Random Forest detection, DQN-based triage, and ethical rule oversight. The results show that high IDS accuracy is necessary but insufficient for safety-critical deployment. The detector baselines achieved strong in-domain classification performance, with the Random Forest model reaching a weighted F1-score of 0.998. However, classification performance alone did not indicate whether the final response action was clinically safe, proportionate, or operationally acceptable. This supports the broader argument that healthcare cybersecurity systems require evaluation beyond conventional predictive metrics12,17.

The DQN triage agent achieved strong in-domain intervention performance, with a weighted F1-score of 0.978 and attack recall of 1.000. However, the unconstrained DQN blocked 25.3% of benign flows, which may represent a clinically disruptive behavior if such decisions were applied to real IoMT communication. After applying the ethical rule engine, benign blocking decreased to 17.3%. This reduction shows that governance rules can constrain selected harmful actions, especially where automatic blocking may be disproportionate. The improvement was not universal, as the governed DQN produced a higher in-domain mean harm cost under the independent cost matrix. This finding indicates a measurable trade-off between reducing direct blocking and increasing less disruptive actions such as alerting or escalation.

The Clinical Load Index sensitivity analysis showed stable but modest variation across workload weighting schemes. Mean decision harm cost remained within a narrow range across equal-weight, ICU-heavy, emergency-heavy, and time-stress-heavy settings. This suggests that the governance layer was not highly dependent on a single workload weighting choice in the controlled simulation. However, the small effect size also indicates that the Clinical Load Index should be interpreted as a proxy for workload-sensitive governance testing rather than as a validated clinical workload model.

The corrected domain-shift evaluation showed substantial degradation when models trained on CIC-IoMT 2024 were evaluated on the stratified CSE-CIC-IDS2018 stress set. The Random Forest CAS retention score was 0.282, while the DQN triage CAS retention score was 0.341. These values indicate limited cross-domain retention. The DQN retained a slightly higher proportion of its in-domain F1-score than the Random Forest baseline, but the absolute shifted-domain performance remained weak. This finding is important because it shows that cross-domain transfer from healthcare IoMT traffic to enterprise-style traffic remains difficult, even after feature harmonization.

Temporal streaming replay provided a more dynamic stress test. After the transition from in-domain CIC-IoMT traffic to shifted CSE-CIC-IDS2018 traffic, the F1-score decreased substantially for both governed and unguided DQN policies. However, under shifted replay windows, the governed DQN produced a lower mean decision harm cost than the unconstrained DQN, reducing the shifted-phase cost from 0.502 to 0.434. This suggests that ethical rule oversight may provide its clearest benefit under distributional stress, where unconstrained learned policies are more likely to produce harmful response patterns.

A key methodological issue in governance-aware AI evaluation is the risk of circularity. If a system is constrained by a rule engine and then evaluated only by whether it follows the same rules, the resulting metric mainly measures internal rule conformance rather than independent ethical quality. For this reason, the Ethical Compliance Rate was interpreted in this study as a rule-conformance metric only. The observed ECR of 0.990 shows that the final actions were highly consistent with the implemented governance logic, but it does not independently prove that the decisions were ethically correct.

To address this limitation, the evaluation included independent action-level metrics that were not used to define rule conformance. These included benign blocking rate, attack allow rate, unnecessary escalation rate, override rate, and mean decision harm cost. These metrics evaluate the operational consequences of the final action, rather than whether the action agrees with the rule engine. This distinction is important for trustworthy AI evaluation because governance should be assessed not only by internal compliance but also by its effects on safety, accountability, and operational burden. The results show that governance reduced benign blocking but increased some alerting and escalation costs in the in-domain setting, demonstrating that independent metrics are necessary to reveal trade-offs that ECR alone would hide.

The added value of the governance layer should be interpreted as selective and context-dependent. The results do not support a claim that governance improves every metric in every condition. In the in-domain policy ablation, the detector-only baseline achieved the lowest mean harm cost, and the governed DQN had a higher harm cost than the unguided DQN because the cost matrix penalized additional alerting and escalation. This demonstrates that ethical oversight may introduce operational costs when it converts aggressive actions into more cautious or human-review-oriented responses.

The main value of governance is therefore not universal performance improvement, but risk constraint. The ethical rule engine reduced benign blocking, converted low-confidence blocks into alerts, redirected high-criticality blocks toward escalation, and logged all interventions. These effects are important in healthcare IoMT environments, where preventing an unsafe automated action may be more important than maximizing a single performance score. The temporal replay results further indicate that governance became more valuable after the domain shift, where the governed DQN reduced mean harm cost relative to the unconstrained DQN. This supports the view that explicit governance constraints are most useful when learned policies operate under uncertainty, workload pressure, or unfamiliar traffic distributions.

The framework incorporated explainability at both model and action levels. At the model level, Random Forest feature importance identified the traffic characteristics most influential for in-domain detection. Inter-arrival time, packet rate, source rate, header length, and TCP flag-related features were among the most important predictors. This is consistent with IDS research showing that temporal, rate-based, and protocol-level features are informative for detecting anomalous network behavior.

At the action level, representative decision paths showed how detector confidence, entropy, simulated clinical load, device criticality, and governance rules influenced the final response. These logs provide a practical audit trail by recording the DQN-proposed action, final governed action, triggered rule, and relevant contextual variables. This form of action-level explanation is important because healthcare stakeholders may need to understand not only why a flow was classified as suspicious, but also why it was blocked, alerted, or escalated. Such auditability aligns with responsible AI principles that emphasize traceability, transparency, and human oversight in high-risk systems.

The corrected domain-shift results show that direct transfer from IoMT traffic to enterprise-style traffic remains difficult. Although the CSE-CIC-IDS2018 sample was harmonized into the 45-feature IoMT input space, shifted-domain performance degraded sharply. This finding is not unexpected because CSE-CIC-IDS2018 differs from CIC-IoMT 2024 in traffic source, protocol distribution, attack composition, feature semantics, and capture environment. Domain-shift degradation has also been noted in IDS research using CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and related datasets, where dataset-specific structure can strongly affect model generalization.

The corrected CAS retention score provides a clearer interpretation than the earlier formulation because it decreases when the shifted-domain F1 decreases. In this study, the low CAS values indicate substantial loss of retained performance. The DQN triage model had slightly higher retention than the Random Forest baseline, but its shifted-domain behavior was overly conservative, flagging almost all flows. This result should be interpreted as evidence of limited generalization and conservative over-flagging, not as evidence of robust cross-domain deployment. Future work should therefore incorporate domain adaptation, calibration monitoring, local retraining, and validation on healthcare-specific external datasets before any deployment claim is made.

The proposed framework is best understood as a decision-support architecture for hospital security operations rather than as an autonomous, deployment-ready IDS. In a hospital security operations center, such a system could help separate detection from response, allowing a detector to provide probabilistic evidence, a triage agent to propose an action, and a governance layer to constrain unsafe responses before execution. The audit log could support post-incident review by documenting why an action was selected or overridden.

The framework also supports human oversight by reserving escalation for uncertain or context-sensitive cases. This is important in healthcare cybersecurity because some decisions require institutional judgment, especially when device criticality, clinical workload, or patient-care continuity may be affected. The use of explicit fallback actions and decision logs may also help align IDS operations with responsible AI governance expectations, including transparency, accountability, and proportionality.

These implications should be interpreted cautiously. The experiments were conducted offline using public datasets and simulated workload signals. The results do not establish safety in live hospital networks or real medical device environments. Instead, they show that governance-aware evaluation can reveal action-level risks that classification metrics alone do not capture, and that ethical rule oversight can reduce selected harmful behaviors under controlled conditions. Further validation in semi-live testbeds, healthcare cyber ranges, and operational IoMT environments is required before practical deployment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, all experiments were conducted using offline public datasets and replay-based simulations. The framework was not evaluated on live hospital traffic, operational medical devices, or a real healthcare security operations environment. Although CIC-IoMT 2024 provides a relevant benchmark for IoMT intrusion detection, public datasets cannot fully reproduce the heterogeneity, temporal variability, device dependencies, emergency conditions, and human interventions that characterize real hospital networks.

Second, the Clinical Load Index was used as a simulated contextual signal for governance sensitivity analysis. It was inspired by hospital workload indicators such as intensive care load, emergency demand, and time-of-day operational stress, but it was not clinically validated as a workload prediction model. The results, therefore, show how the governance layer behaves under controlled workload assumptions, not how it would perform under measured real-time hospital workload. Future work should validate workload-sensitive governance using operational data and expert input from clinical and security teams.

Third, device criticality was simulated or rule-based rather than obtained from a real hospital asset inventory. In practice, the clinical importance of an IoMT device depends on its function, patient dependency, ward context, redundancy, and current care pathway. A realistic deployment would require integration with hospital asset-management systems and clinical risk classification processes.

Fourth, CSE-CIC-IDS2018 was used as an enterprise-domain stress dataset and is not a healthcare IoMT dataset. Its traffic patterns, protocol distributions, attack composition, and feature semantics differ from CIC-IoMT 2024. This difference was intentional for stress testing, but it limits the interpretation of the cross-domain results. The domain-shift experiment should therefore be viewed as an external robustness probe rather than evidence of deployment readiness in hospital networks.

Fifth, feature harmonization between CIC-IoMT 2024 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 required mapping compatible variables into the IoMT feature space and imputing features without reliable correspondence. Although the mapping process was documented, imputation may reduce semantic fidelity and contribute to performance degradation under shift. Future studies should evaluate the framework on external healthcare IoMT datasets with more directly comparable feature schemas.

Sixth, the DQN triage agent became highly conservative under shifted-domain testing by flagging nearly all flows. This behavior preserved attack sensitivity but reduced benign specificity and indicates limited generalization outside the in-domain training distribution. The result supports the need for domain adaptation, calibration monitoring, and external validation before any real deployment.

Seventh, the governance rules were expert-designed and require institutional validation. Although they were grounded in principles of safety, proportionality, explainability, and accountability, different hospitals may require different thresholds, fallback actions, device-criticality categories, and escalation policies. The rule engine should therefore be treated as a configurable governance prototype rather than a universal policy.

Finally, the corrected Cross-Domain Adaptation Score is a performance-retention metric, not a complete robustness measure. It summarizes how much weighted F1-score is retained under shift, but it does not capture all dimensions of trustworthiness, including calibration stability, adversarial robustness, latency, operator workload, or clinical acceptability. For this reason, CAS was interpreted alongside independent action-level metrics, harm cost, override analysis, and temporal replay results rather than as a standalone measure of deployment robustness.

Future work

Future work should evaluate the proposed framework in semi-live healthcare cybersecurity testbeds that combine real IoMT devices, emulated hospital network traffic, and controlled attack scenarios. Such testbeds would provide stronger evidence of runtime stability, latency, governance behavior, and response safety than offline replay alone. They would also allow evaluation of how the framework behaves under realistic device communication patterns, network congestion, clinician interventions, and adversarial adaptation.

Further work should integrate real IoMT device inventories and asset-management records. In the present study, device criticality was simulated using a rule-based approach. In a practical hospital environment, device criticality should be derived from validated asset registers, device function, ward location, patient dependency, redundancy level, and clinical risk classification. This would allow governance rules to distinguish between routine devices, monitoring systems, and life-critical equipment more accurately.

Hospital cyber ranges and healthcare-specific digital twins should also be developed to test governance-aware IDS behavior under controlled but realistic operational conditions. These environments could support safe experimentation with denial-of-service attacks, reconnaissance, spoofing, lateral movement, and device-level communication failures without exposing real patients or hospital operations to risk. Such testbeds would also help evaluate whether ethical rule thresholds remain appropriate under emergency surges, device failures, and mixed benign–malicious traffic.

The Clinical Load Index should be validated using real hospital operational data and expert clinical review. Future studies should examine whether ICU occupancy, emergency demand, staffing pressure, ward-level congestion, and time-of-day workload can be combined into a clinically meaningful governance signal. This validation should involve clinical, cybersecurity, and hospital operations stakeholders to ensure that workload-sensitive rules reflect actual institutional risk and not only simulated assumptions.

Human-in-the-loop evaluation is also required. The framework should be tested with security analysts, biomedical engineers, and clinical operations teams to assess whether alerts, escalations, override explanations, and audit logs are understandable and useful. Such an evaluation would help determine whether the system reduces cognitive burden or creates new forms of operational complexity. It would also support refinement of fallback actions, escalation thresholds, and explanation formats in line with responsible AI governance expectations.

Future work should incorporate adaptive calibration and domain adaptation. The corrected domain-shift results showed that direct transfer from CIC-IoMT 2024 to CSE-CIC-IDS2018 caused substantial degradation. This indicates the need for calibration monitoring, drift detection, local retraining, and adaptation methods that can adjust detector confidence and DQN behavior when traffic distributions change. Federated and privacy-preserving learning may also be explored to support multi-site adaptation without centralizing sensitive hospital data.

External multi-site validation is essential before deployment claims can be made. The framework should be evaluated across different hospitals, device types, network configurations, attack scenarios, and operational policies. Multi-site testing would help determine whether the governance rules generalize across institutions or require local customization. Such validation would also provide stronger evidence on robustness, fairness, auditability, and clinical acceptability in real healthcare cybersecurity environments.

Conclusion

This study presented a governance-aware intrusion response framework for healthcare IoMT systems. The framework combined Random Forest-based flow detection, DQN triage, and NIST AI RMF-inspired ethical rule oversight to separate intrusion classification from response governance. The revised evaluation showed that governance rules actively modified DQN actions, reduced selected harmful decisions such as benign blocking, and lowered mean decision harm cost during temporal domain-shift replay. However, corrected cross-domain testing also showed substantial performance degradation when models trained on CIC-IoMT 2024 were evaluated on the stratified CSE-CIC-IDS2018 stress set. These findings indicate that governance-aware intrusion response can improve selected decision-safety outcomes under controlled conditions, but the framework is not yet deployment-ready. The study should therefore be interpreted as a controlled offline methodological contribution that requires further validation in semi-live healthcare cybersecurity testbeds, hospital cyber ranges, and real IoMT environments.
