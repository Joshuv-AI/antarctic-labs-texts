Nonlinear Stability in the Two-Fluid Model, v1
Alexander López-de-Bertodano, Alejandro Clausse

Abstract




I
Abstract

The 1-D Two-Fluid Model (TFM) promises a powerful and computationally cheap platform for simulating multi-fluid flow phenomena. However, runaway Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities plagued previous approaches, necessitating aphysical regularizations. We demonstrate a novel physics-based approach, using a simple turbulent viscosity model to provide nonlinear stabilization, compatible with inertial coupling. We develop a set of analytical and numerical tools to investigate the resulting dynamics, including turbulent cascades, chaos, and formation of churn or slug flow. Our approach opens up a wide range of new capabilities for the TFM by capturing the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability physically.

II
Introduction

The Two-Fluid Model (TFM) is a continuum approach to fluid dynamics, originally conceived for mixture flows in up to 3 dimensions
Landau 1941
. It subsequently found wide use in 1-D interfacial flows
Ishii and Hibiki 2011
, with applications in the design of nuclear reactors, geothermal plants, condensers, heat exchangers, rocket engines, and so on.

In problems that may be reduced to one dimension (pipe flows), the TFM has one main advantage, computability. However, dimensional reduction constitutes a substantial approximation and introduces two key weaknesses:

1.

Higher-Dimensional Structure.
3-D flow and interfacial structure may be baked into a 1-D TFM on an empirical basis, but cannot be derived from it. The sacrifice of this capability is the fundamental trade-off of simplification to 1-D. Modalities that seek to retain full predictive power by moving to higher dimension (e.g. Volume of Fluid or Level Set) incur a corresponding computational cost.

2.

Badly Behaved Local Instabilities.
Historically, the TFM has been subject to unruly local wave behavior resulting from the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability. Short wavelength components grow too quickly, meaning solutions become dominated by noise or cease to exist altogether. The model is, in such a case,
ill-posed
.

These two issues are not really separate. The real culprit of ill-posedness is a failure to appropriately account for higher-dimensional structure throughout the development of the Kelvin-Helmholtz, not the instability itself.

It is important that we state precisely what we mean by ill-posedness. In prior work on the TFM
Gidaspow 1994
, three ideas have often been regarded as equivalent and interchangeable: (1) the presence of complex linear eigenvalues, (2) ellipticity, and (3) “ill-posedness”.

The original work on well-posedness by Hadamard
Hadamard 1902
analyzed the wave and Laplace equations. For these simplest cases, the three ideas do perfectly intersect. An initial value (Cauchy) problem is well-posed on the
hyperbolic
wave equation with its constant, real eigenvalues. It is ill-posed on the
elliptic
Laplace equation with its constant, imaginary eigenvalues.

In more complex systems, however, these equivalences break down. Dispersive systems
Whitham 1999
need not be cleanly elliptic or hyperbolic. In some TFMs, eigenvalues even shift between complex and real based on wavelength. Signs also matter. Positive imaginary eigenvalues, corresponding to wave growth, are workable if confined to a finite range of wavelengths. Negative imaginary eigenvalues are no issue whatsoever.

Throwing out a system of PDEs because it has complex eigenvalues is like throwing out a system of ODEs because it has any kind of fixed point other than oscillatory centers. Many famous dynamical models (predator-prey, Lorenz, Van der Pol) would need to be classed as “ill-posed”.

For the purposes of the present analysis, we therefore adopt the following definition: a well-posed system has unique solutions (“possible et determiné”), and an ill-posed system lacks them. We are now free to analyze the linear dynamics on their own terms, as well as the nonlinear effects that may cause a TFM to be well- or ill-posed more generally.

The problem of ill-posedness has reared its head repeatedly in the development of the TFM, and to combat it a slough of fixes have emerged
Dinh et al. 2003
. These regularizations are universally unsatisfactory, either proving too weak or involving aphysical forces.

The most convenient and widespread approach in simulation has been to employ “coarse” (large node) meshes, sidestepping low-wavelength Kelvin-Helmholtz behavior through a combination of numerical viscosity and the exclusion of sub-mesh length scales. Premier codes like RELAP
Pokharna et al. 1997
rely on coarse meshes. This methodology makes the correspondence between numerical solutions and experimental or analytical results dependent on the level of mesh refinement used.

We maintain that, to be of predictive value, numerical solutions of a TFM must converge up to arbitrarily fine mesh resolution, via the application of physical forces only.

In pursuit of such a TFM, M. López-de-Bertodano et al. pioneered the use of surface tension
López-de Bertodano et al. 2017
. Surface tension introduces a short-wavelength cutoff to linear perturbation growth, but further work
Fullmer et al. 2014

Vaidheeswaran et al. 2019
showed that this cutoff is not sustained in numerical solutions when transitioning to a nonlinear regime. Surface tension is a beginning but not the end; we explore its role thoroughly in Sections
IV
and
V
.

It has been previously held (see e.g.
Pokharna et al. 1997
) that numerical approaches to the TFM could not practically handle mesh scales where higher-order terms become strong enough to constrain wave growth. This limitation no longer stands.

Inertial coupling (IC), which may be used to model conservative aspects of higher-dimensional structure, is a fully effective stabilizer for runaway short waves
Clausse and López-de Bertodano 2021

Clausse and López-de Bertodano 2024
. In some regimes, however, it is more appropriate to leave the IC at least partially unstable in order to capture Kelvin-Helmholtz wave growth. In such cases, it is necessary to introduce nonlinear dissipation to bound the amplitude of the fluctuations.

Our novel approach to the ill-posedness problem is inspired by the thesis of W. Fullmer
Fullmer 2014
, who originated the idea of using a simple eddy model to represent turbulent dissipation around a fluid-fluid interface. The power of this approach lies in allowing viscosity to scale with the relative velocity across the interface.

We present a methodology for the construction, analysis, and numerical solution of well-posed, Kelvin-Helmholtz unstable TFMs. We well-pose these cases through a combination of linearly and nonlinearly stabilizing higher-order forces. We hope that this work will open the door to an even broader class of well-posed, physically predictive models for a variety of applications.

IV.1
Pure Advection

Assuming incompressibility (Eq.
8

We omit system oscillations from the analysis by setting

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\alpha+\partial_{x}(

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}W+\alpha j)=0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}W+\partial_{x}(\tfrac{1}{2}

All in all, the linear ill-posedness problem is caused by runaway growth of short wavelengths. This result seems counter-intuitive given the fact that the local wave energy (cf. Eq.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}\equiv\int T_{\text{local}}\,dx=\int\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}W^{2}\,dx

\mathcal{F}_{\text{local}}=(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)\,T_{\text{local}}

(28)

However, the lowest-order energy perturbation term:

\Delta\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}=\int\tfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}W^{2}(\Delta\alpha)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}(\Delta W)^{2}\,dx

remains constant in time despite growing perturbations so long as
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}<0
.

Negative
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha)

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}\geq 0

\alpha

Inertial coupling in the TFM equations results from energy not reflected in the averaged flow variables

\Gamma(\alpha)
, whose reciprocal
\mathcal{Q}(\alpha)
is key to understanding the stability of local wave modes.

In some regimes (stratified flow in particular) sufficient stabilizing inertia may not be forthcoming. Furthermore, wherever one sets
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
to be positive, inertial wave growth does not occur. Over-application of this approach rids the TFM of predictive power over Kelvin-Helmholtz-type phenomena along with ill-posedness. To retain all possible physics, inertial instability should be retained and incorporated.

IV.2
Viscosity

Since using inertia to tame local wave growth is not desirable in every case, it is natural to look toward dissipative mechanisms as well. Dissipation of the relative motion comes from interfacial drag and viscosity. However, the interfacial drag includes no derivatives, so its contribution to
\omega
does not scale in
k
. It is therefore uncompetitive with the inertial growth term at short wavelengths – see Eq.
171
. Viscosity, having a second derivative, contributes to the dispersion with a promising
k^{2}
scaling.

We thus define a viscous force term in simple form:

\partial_{t}W+\partial_{x}(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2}+W\!j)=\ldots+\tilde{\nu}\,\partial_{x}^{2}W

(30)

where
\tilde{\nu}
is the effective viscosity, a constant. This form describes the contribution of the real viscous forces to the linearized equations if there is no inertial coupling (cf. Eq.
12
). If inertial coupling is included, it may only be approximate.

We then introduce the following notation for dispersion frequencies:

\omega=\bar{\omega}\pm\sqrt{\Delta}

(31)

where, with viscosity:

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)k-\frac{i\tilde{\nu}k^{2}}{2}

(32)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}W^{2}k^{2}}{2}-\frac{\tilde{\nu}^{2}k^{4}}{4}

Despite the decay term in
\bar{\omega}

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}<0

\lim_{k\to\infty}\text{Im}(\omega)=\frac{\mathcal{Q}|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}|W^{2}}{2\tilde{\nu}}

(33)

Repeating the process used to obtain Eq.

-norm of the linear response to an allowable interfacial perturbation:

L_{2}[\Delta W]\!\sim\!\sum_{k}^{\infty}k^{-2p}\!\sinh^{2}(t)

(34)

Though the viscous model is not linearly ill-posed, it allows for unchecked growth of arbitrarily high-

17

\tilde{\nu}_{\text{num}}=\Delta x(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)

where
\Delta x
is the mesh size. The key difference is that now the

\partial_{t}\alpha+\partial_{x}(\mathcal{Q}W+\alpha j)=\tilde{\nu}_{\text{num}}\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha

(36)

The result of numerical viscosity is to modify the base dispersion frequency:

\bar{\omega}=(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)k-i\,\tilde{\nu}_{\text{num}}k^{2}

(37)

\Delta=\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}W^{2}k^{2}

(38)

The new, artificial term in
\tilde{\nu}_{\text{num}}
can constrain wave growth effectively, though only insofar as
\Delta x

IV.3
Gravity and Surface Tension

Dissipative forces fail to stabilize the linear model. That leaves us with the conservative forces, gravity and surface tension.

Gravity parallel to the channel (

V
Nonlinear Stability

We now wish to analyze solutions over longer times, where nonlinear interactions between wavelengths become important and amplitude dependence emerges. We begin with numerical solutions to gain intuition, and gradually elucidate the observed dynamics by analytical means. The resulting insights will allow us to construct a nonlinearly well-posed model.

We construct our initial conditions somewhat differently than in the purely linear test case. Instead of probing individual wavelengths, we perturb the interface with a Gaussian pulse:

\Delta\alpha(x,t=0)=\varepsilon e^{-x^{2}/2s^{2}}

(43)

where
\varepsilon
is a small finite magnitude and
s
is the pulse width. We select:

\varepsilon=10^{-5}\qquad s^{2}=5\,\text{mm}^{2}

(44)

The Gaussian has a smooth Fourier series, which enhances the readability of the nonlinear response. We continue to utilize periodic boundary conditions in order to allow moving waves enough time to develop at minimum computational cost.

V.1
Breakdown of the Linear Approximation

We begin by testing our minimal linearly well-behaved model, i.e. the one with uncoupled inertia and surface tension only (Sec.
IV.3
).

One may understand the essence of this mode-to-mode transfer by extending the linear approximation of the equations to second order. Take an equilibrium state vector and a perturbation:

\Psi_{0}\equiv(\alpha_{0},W_{0});\,\psi\equiv(\Delta\alpha,\Delta W)

Writing the perturbation in terms of its Fourier transform:

\psi(x,t)=\sum_{k}\widetilde{\psi}(k,t)\,e^{ikx}

\mathbf{M}_{1}

The extension to 2nd-order, however, involves a convolution between wavelengths:

\partial_{t}\widetilde{\psi}(k,t)\approx...+\mathbf{M}_{2}\sum_{k^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}(k-k^{\prime},t)\,\widetilde{\psi}(k^{\prime},t)

where

Suppose for now that higher-order contributions are negligible. The convolutive structure of Eq.

In the present case, nonlinear growth of increasingly short waves creates narrowing spikes in

\alpha

If growth occurs via the convolution of low-

It is worth asking whether the time to this blowup increases significantly as the mesh is refined. Perhaps if the system has more modes to pour energy into, it can hang on for longer times – perhaps for arbitrarily long. Fortunately from a computing perspective, the trend is the opposite. Blowup times asymptote downward (see Fig.

This minimal model is ill-posed, not instantly, but after a finite time. Some other mechanism is required to tame this
nonlinear
ill-posedness.

Continuing to analyze the issue via Fourier series is possible but difficult; at the end of this Section we will use a perturbative expansion to model the interactions among a finite ladder of modes. First, however, we develop heuristics to understand the effects of high-order forces in the highly nonlinear regime.

V.2
Characteristics and the Black Box Method

In developing our analytical methods, we look to simpler PDE systems. A family of nonlinear wave equations with analytical solutions descends from the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation
Korteweg and de Vries 1895
:

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2})+\sigma\partial_{x}^{3}u=0

(51)

and the Burgers equation
Burgers 1948
:

\partial_{t}u+\partial_{x}(\tfrac{1}{2}u^{2})=\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u

(52)

Many approaches to these problems involve some variation of the characteristic method, meaning the assumption of solutions in terms of one combined variable:

\theta=x-v_{c}t

(53)

where
v_{c}
is the characteristic velocity. The one-soliton solution to the KdV equation assumes a globally constant
v_{c}
, while the pre-shock solution of the inviscid Burgers problem utilizes
v_{c}(\theta)=u(x=\theta,t=0)
; i.e. constant velocity along characteristics.

The nature of the TFM seriously complicates the application of this methodology. The linearized problem generates candidate characteristic velocities given by
\frac{w(k)}{k}
, but usually two of them. They can be complex as well as
k
-dependent.

It is unclear how these challenges may be overcome in an analytical approach to the general nonlinear problem. However, inspiration may be found in the principal dispersion term:

\bar{\omega}=(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)k

(54)

which appears in every relation in Section
IV
. We name the factor multiplying
k
the “eigenvelocity”:

v_{\text{eig}}\equiv(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)

(55)

The eigenvelocity is constant in
k
and controls the kinetic energy flux of local waves (Eq.
202
).

To make use of these properties, we construct a thought experiment where, due to piecewise constant flow variables, the eigenvelocity temporarily dominates momentum and energy transport. We start by taking some inverse inertial function
\mathcal{Q}(\alpha)
with a local maximum
\mathcal{Q}_{max}
at void fraction
\alpha_{max}
and select two surrounding values of
\alpha
yielding equal
Q
:

\alpha_{-}<\alpha_{max}<\alpha_{+}\qquad\mathcal{Q}(\alpha_{-})=\mathcal{Q}(\alpha_{+})<\mathcal{Q}_{max}

(56)

We may select
\alpha_{-}
and
\alpha_{+}
such that:

\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\alpha_{-})>0>\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\alpha_{+})

(57)

We then define:

\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\equiv\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\alpha_{+})-\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\alpha_{-})<0

(58)

See Fig.
5
for an illustration.

Suppose now that at each side of a flow region length
\delta x
the void fraction is given by
\alpha_{-}
and
\alpha_{+}
sequentially. Also set the relative momentum density at each side of the region to be equal,
W_{-}=W_{+}
, as shown in Figure
6
. We do not specify the conditions inside the region, making it a “black box”.

We construct the case so that no gradients exist outside the box:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\alpha(x<0)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}\alpha(x>\delta x)=0

(59)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}W(x<0)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}W(x>\delta x)=0

that is,
\alpha
and
W
are piecewise constant.

Figure 6
:
Illustration of flow conditions either side of the “black-box” region.

A constant
j
acts just as a Galilean frame shift. Working in the frame
j=0
, we have:

\displaystyle v_{\text{eig}-}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\alpha_{-})W_{-}

(60)

\displaystyle v_{\text{eig}+}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}(\alpha_{+})W_{+}

Assuming
W_{-}=W_{+}>0
(the generalization to
W<0
is not difficult), we therefore find:

v_{\text{eig}-}>0>v_{\text{eig}+}

(61)

so the eigenvelocity flows into the black-box region from both sides.

This bunching up of the “characteristics” has asymmetric effects on the flow variables. We have constructed this example so that the mean void fraction within the region:

\bar{\alpha}\equiv\frac{1}{\delta x}\int_{0}^{\delta x}\alpha\,dx

(62)

remains constant in time:

\frac{d\bar{\alpha}}{dt}=\frac{1}{\delta x}\left[(\mathcal{Q}W)_{-}\,-(\mathcal{Q}W)_{+}\right]=0

(63)

The same may not be said for the mean relative momentum:

\overline{W}\equiv\frac{1}{\delta x}\int_{0}^{\delta x}W\,dx

(64)

Considering only the effect of the advective term (Eq.
9
), this quantity is set to grow as long as
\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
is negative:

\frac{d\overline{W}}{dt}=\frac{1}{\delta x}\left[(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2})_{-}\!-(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2})_{+}\right]=-\frac{\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2}}{\delta x}

(65)

where we have dropped the
+/-
subscripts on the background
W
. Without any conservative forces, the mean energy density is given by (cf. Eq.
2
):

\overline{T}\equiv\frac{1}{\delta x}\int_{0}^{\delta x}\tfrac{1}{2}\!\mathcal{Q}W^{2}\,dx

(66)

Using Eq.
201
, we find that this quantity also grows:

\frac{d\overline{T}}{dt}=\frac{1}{\delta x}\left[(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\!\mathcal{Q}W^{3})_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}}\scalebox{0.8}[1.0]{$-$}\,(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\!\mathcal{Q}W^{3})_{+}\right]=\scalebox{0.9}[1.0]{$-$}\frac{\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\!\mathcal{Q}W^{3}}{\delta x}

(67)

This accumulation of momentum and energy around a localized change in
\alpha
explains the growth of momentum “spikes” as seen in Figure
3
(b)/(c). We may also predict a narrowing of the spikes over time because of the inward flow of the eigenvelocity.

The energy and momentum growth rates diverge as
\delta x\rightarrow 0
, an effect somewhat analogous to the divergence of
\text{Im}(\omega)
as
k\rightarrow\infty
in the linear regime under pure advection. However, the behavior of the force terms in this highly nonlinear test case is not at all similar.

Surface tension, for instance, has no limiting effect on the growth of
\overline{W}
as long as the flow conditions around the black box remain piecewise constant:

\frac{d\overline{W}}{dt}=\;...+\left.\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha}{[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}]^{3/2}}\right|_{0}^{\delta x}=\>...+0

(68)

nor does it contribute to the energy flux (see Eq.
206
):

\mathcal{F}_{\text{local},\sigma}(x=0)=\mathcal{F}_{\text{local},\sigma}(\delta x)=0

(69)

despite its effectiveness as a linear limiter.

We now consider a typical viscosity term (Eq.
12
):

\partial_{t}W=...+\partial_{x}\!\left(\nu\partial_{x}W\right)

(70)

Regardless of the type of viscosity (kinematic or turbulent) we find that the dissipative effect on the average relative momentum is 0:

\int_{0}^{\delta x}\!\partial_{x}\!\left(\nu\partial_{x}W\right)\,dx=\nu\left.\partial_{x}W\!\right|_{0}^{\delta x}=0

(71)

although the average energy is dissipated (Eq.
199
):

\int_{0}^{\delta x}\!\mathcal{Q}W\,\partial_{x}\!\left(\nu\partial_{x}W\right)dx\approx-\mathcal{Q}\!\int_{0}^{\delta x}\!\nu(\partial_{x}W)^{2}\,dx

(72)

neglecting the effect of the variation of
\mathcal{Q}
over the region.

It seems that viscosity would tend to “spread”
W
out within the black box, reducing energy but conserving total momentum.

Granted, the piecewise constant boundary conditions are not likely to remain untouched. Viscosity and surface tension would eventually carry significant gradients to the boundaries, breaking the simplicity of the thought experiment.

Nevertheless, the dissipation of relative flow energy via viscosity gives some hope that runaway growth may be stopped. In order to make a complete analysis of this effect, assumptions about the behavior of the flow variables within the black-box region are necessary.

V.3
Shock-Spikes and Turbulent Limiting in the Nonlinear Limit

Figure 7
:
Rough, presumptive forms of the flow variables within the black-box region.

The numerical results in the inviscid case (Fig.
3
) give support to the straightforward assumption that as runaway growth starts within the black box,
\alpha
takes the form of a narrow shock, and
W
the form of a narrow spike. See Figure
7
for an illustration.

We call the peak value of
W
, assumed to occur roughly at the center of the region,
W_{max}
. The difference between the peak and the background momentum density is labeled
\Delta W
:

\Delta W\equiv W_{max}-W_{\pm}

(73)

As an additional shorthand we define the difference in
\alpha
across the region:

\Delta\alpha\equiv\alpha_{+}-\alpha_{-}

(74)

We may estimate spatial derivatives of the flow variables at the peak using these deltas and the length scale
\delta x
. For example, the gradient of
\alpha
is roughly:

\left.\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial x}\right|_{\text{peak}}\!\approx\frac{\Delta\alpha}{\delta x}

(75)

Its second derivative is roughly 0, but the third derivative comes out to:

\left.\frac{\partial^{3}\alpha}{{\partial x}^{3}}\right|_{\text{peak}}\!\approx-\frac{\Delta\alpha}{\delta x^{3}}

(76)

The gradient of
W
is assumed to be 0, but its second derivative is significant:

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}W}{{\partial x}^{2}}\right|_{\text{peak}}\!\approx-\frac{\Delta W}{\delta x^{2}}

(77)

Plugging Eqs.
75
-
77
into the
W
-equation (Eq.
9
) yields an approximation for the growth rate of the spike. We handle the key terms one by one.

The advective term comes out to:

\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}_{W}=\partial_{x}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2}\right)\approx\tfrac{1}{2}W_{max}^{2}\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\,\delta x^{-1}

(78)

using:

\partial_{x}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}\partial_{x}\alpha\approx\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}\Delta\alpha\ \delta x^{-1}\approx\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\,\delta x^{-1}

(79)

To approximate the surface tension term, we first expand:

\displaystyle F_{\sigma,W}=\sigma\!H\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\!\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha}{[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}]^{3/2}}\!\right)=

(80)

\displaystyle\sigma\!H\!\left(\!\frac{\partial_{x}^{3}\alpha}{[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}]^{3/2}}-\frac{3H^{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha)^{2}\partial_{x}\alpha}{[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}]^{5/2}}\!\right)

Since
\left.\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha\right|_{\text{peak}}\approx 0
, the second part vanishes. Additionally, keeping
\delta x
sufficiently small:

H\partial_{x}\alpha\approx H\Delta\alpha\,\delta x^{-1}\gg 1

(81)

Thus, the denominator of the first part simplifies:

[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}]^{\frac{3}{2}}\approx(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{3}

(82)

leaving:

F_{\sigma,W}\approx\sigma\!H\!\frac{\partial_{x}^{3}\alpha}{(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{3}}\approx-\sigma\!H\!\frac{\Delta\alpha\,\delta x^{-3}}{(H\Delta\alpha\,\delta x^{-1})^{3}}

(83)

Performing the final cancellations reveals a quantity independent of
\delta x
:

F_{\sigma,W}\approx-\sigma H^{-2}\Delta\alpha^{-2}

(84)

Finally, we have the viscosity term, for which we take the IC-free form (Eq.
12
) as representative:

F_{\text{visc},W}=\partial_{x}(\nu\partial_{x}W)=\nu\partial_{x}^{2}W+\partial_{x}\nu\cdot\partial_{x}W

(85)

The second term vanishes assuming
\left.\partial_{x}W\right|_{\text{peak}}=0
, so:

F_{\text{visc},W}=(\nu_{k}+\nu_{t})\,\partial_{x}^{2}W

(86)

where
\nu_{k}
is the kinematic viscosity and
\nu_{t}
is the turbulent viscosity. From Eq.
14
:

\nu_{t}=l_{m}|u_{r}|=l_{m}\mathcal{Q}\alpha^{-1}(1-\alpha)^{-1}W

(87)

where we have dropped the absolute value bars just for the case
W>0
. Then:

F_{\text{visc},W}\approx-\!\left(\nu_{k}+\frac{l_{m}\mathcal{Q}W_{max}}{\alpha(1-\alpha)}\right)\frac{\Delta\!W}{\delta x^{2}}

(88)

where
\alpha
and
\mathcal{Q}
are taken at the location of the
W
-peak.

We now combine the above terms as follows:

d_{t}W_{max}=-\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}_{W}+F_{\sigma,W}+F_{\text{visc},W}

(89)

The advective term
-\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}_{W}
drives growth. In taking
\delta x
closer and closer to zero (a narrower and narrower shock), it diverges as
\delta x^{-1}
.
F_{\sigma,W}
, having no dependence on
\delta x
, becomes irrelevant for narrow shocks. This fact may be surprising given the effectiveness of surface tension as a linear well-poser, but it explains its breakdown in the nonlinear regime (Sec.
V.1
). It is the viscous force,
F_{\text{visc},W}
, that contributes effective nonlinear limiting scaling with
\delta x^{-2}
.

We may consider momentum spikes to be well-behaved if, above some bounding
W_{max}
, they always tend down:

\frac{d_{t}W_{max}}{W_{max}}\leq 0

(90)

For a narrow shock the surface tension is negligible, so Eq.
90
is equivalent to:

\frac{\partial_{x}\mathcal{A}_{W}}{W_{max}}\leq\frac{F_{\text{visc},W}}{W_{max}}

(91)

or roughly:

\tfrac{1}{2}W_{max}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|\,\delta x^{-1}\!\,\leq(\nu_{k}+\nu_{t})\Delta\!W\,W_{max}^{-1}\,\delta x^{-2}

(92)

In the simple case
\Delta\!W\approx W_{max}
we arrive at:

W_{max}\leq 2(\nu_{k}+\nu_{t})|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|^{-1}\delta x^{-1}

(93)

The limiting behavior depends on the type of viscosity. With kinematic (constant) viscosity only, the inequality reduces to:

W_{max}\leq 2\nu_{k}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|^{-1}\delta x^{-1}

(94)

showing the wrong kind of threshold. Above a certain bound
{W_{max}>2\nu_{k}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|^{-1}\delta x^{-1}}
one obtains super-exponential growth instead of stable limiting.

The difficulty presented by Eq.
94
appears even worse when considering the integrated momentum of the spike:

p_{spike}\propto W_{max}\,\delta x

(95)

which must only exceed a value proportional to
\nu_{k}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|^{-1}
to cause runaway growth.

With the viscosity outcompeted in this way, the dynamics quickly become dominated by the advective term:

d_{t}W_{max}\approx\tfrac{1}{2}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|\,\delta x^{-1}W^{2}_{max}

(96)

This simple ODE is solved by:

W_{max}(t)\approx\frac{W_{max}(0)}{1-\tfrac{1}{2}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|W_{max}(0)\,\delta x^{-1}\,t}

(97)

exhibiting divergence at time:

t_{blowup}\approx\frac{2\delta x}{|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|W_{max}(0)}

(98)

Finite divergence times in the ultra-nonlinear regime explain the blowup behavior observed in Section
V.1
. We may verify those results against Eqs.
97
-
98
quantitatively. Instead of following
W_{max}
to infinity numerically, we check the time it takes to go from one finite value to another. Selecting
W_{max}(0)=2\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}
, and with:

|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|=2\Delta\alpha\approx 0.16\qquad\delta x=1/4000\,\text{m}

(99)

Eq.
97
predicts that the time for
W_{max}
to reach
3\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}}
should be
5.2\cdot 10^{-4}
s. The code with
\Delta x=1/4000\,
m gets
5.9\cdot 10^{-4}
s (see Fig.
3
c for rough confirmation). These findings are in approximate agreement.

Turbulent viscosity, as we have formulated it, appears to avert this blowup issue. Leaving out the kinematic viscosity, we expand the stability condition Eq.
93
:

W_{max}\leq\frac{2l_{m}\mathcal{Q}_{max}W_{max}}{\alpha_{max}(1-\alpha_{max})}|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|^{-1}\delta x^{-1}

(100)

or, equivalently:

\delta x\leq\frac{2l_{m}\mathcal{Q}_{max}}{\alpha_{max}(1-\alpha_{max})|\Delta\!\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}|}

(101)

In the turbulent model the peak height disappears from the inequality entirely. We see that spikes are unconditionally stable below a certain
width
that varies directly with the turbulent mixing length
l_{m}
.

This very different stability behavior is due to the proportionality between viscosity and relative momentum:

\nu_{t}\propto|u_{r}|\propto|W|

(102)

which the concept of turbulent viscosity has allowed us to introduce.

It may then be that all shock-spikes grow and narrow until they reach the turbulent
\delta x
threshold, after which they dissipate. What remains is to test this hypothesis numerically.

V.5
Perturbative Series Analysis

We now embark on a rigorous analytical treatment of the Fourier mode interactions in an inertially unstable TFM. This approach provides a degree of insight into the preceding results, but we leave it for last because it is outside our present scope to pursue it to the point of decisive conclusions about nonlinear stability. For that purpose, the heuristic analysis of Sec.
V.3
is much more economical.

Our numerical results show that low-
k
perturbations in the TFM excite higher-
k
harmonics. Eq.
50
illustrates the general mathematical structure underlying these cross-wavelength interactions, but to analyze them in more detail, we test a perturbative series solution:

\displaystyle\Delta\alpha=\varepsilon\!\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}e^{i\theta}+\widetilde{\alpha}_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}1}e^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}i\theta^{*}}\right)+

(105)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\!\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}e^{2i\theta}+\widetilde{\alpha}_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}2}e^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}2i\theta^{*}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle\Delta W=\varepsilon\!\left(\widetilde{W}_{1}e^{i\theta}+\widetilde{W}_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}1}e^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}i\theta^{*}}\right)+

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\!\left(\widetilde{W}_{2}e^{2i\theta}+\widetilde{W}_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}2}e^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}2i\theta^{*}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

where
\varepsilon
is a perturbative parameter such that
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.
\widetilde{\alpha}_{\pm j}
and
\widetilde{W}_{\pm j}
denote magnitudes of order-
j
perturbations. Real solutions require:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}j}^{\,*}

(106)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{j}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W}_{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}j}^{\,*}

The phase
\theta
is defined as:

\theta\equiv kx-\omega t

(107)

where
\omega
may be complex:

\omega=\text{Re}(\omega)+i\,\text{Im}(\omega)

(108)

meaning that the phase also has an imaginary part:

\text{Im}(\theta)=-\text{Im}(\omega)\cdot t

(109)

hence the necessity of the complex conjugate of
\theta
in minus-mode exponents (Eq.
105
) to construct real-valued solutions. We could equivalently write:

\displaystyle\Delta\alpha

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\,\text{Re}\!\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}e^{i\theta}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\,\text{Re}\!\left(\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}e^{2i\theta}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

(110)

\displaystyle\Delta W

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\,\text{Re}\!\left(\widetilde{W}_{1}e^{i\theta}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\,\text{Re}\!\left(\widetilde{W}_{2}e^{2i\theta}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})

but the expanded form is more useful in understanding the emergence of nonlinear corrections to
\omega
.

Perturbing the PDEs around some equilibrium and substituting in this hypothetical solution (Eq.
105
) allows for the grouping of perturbations by order (power of
\varepsilon
) and wavelength (power of
e^{i\theta}
). Grouping together the first order (
\varepsilon^{1}
) terms results in linearization; supplying
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}
yields two solutions for the linear frequency
\omega_{0}
and the coefficient
\widetilde{W}_{1}
.

At second order one obtains secondary coefficients
\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}
and
\widetilde{W}_{2}
. At third order, the terms multiplying
e^{3i\theta}
yield the tertiary coefficients, but interestingly there are also 3rd-order terms multiplying
\varepsilon^{3}e^{i\theta+2\text{Im}(\omega)t}
: nonlinear products of perturbations in
\varepsilon^{2}e^{2i\theta}
and
\varepsilon e^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}i\theta^{*}}
.

In Stokes’ original application of the series method
Stokes 1847
,
\omega
is purely real, so these cross terms are fully accounted for by a correction to
\omega
:

\omega=\omega_{0}+\varepsilon^{2}\omega_{2}+...

(111)

such that the time derivatives on the left-hand side of the PDEs yield:

\partial_{t}\Delta\phi=\varepsilon\widetilde{\phi}_{1}e^{ikx}\partial_{t}e^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}i\omega t}=(\varepsilon\omega_{0}+\varepsilon^{3}\omega_{2}+...)\widetilde{\phi}_{1}e^{i\theta}

(112)

for
\phi=
either field variable,
\alpha\text{ or }W
.

In Stokes’ case, the above frequency correction of order
\varepsilon^{3}
perfectly balances contributions in
\varepsilon^{3}e^{i\theta+2\text{Im}(\omega)t}
from the nonlinear RHS terms because
\text{Im}(\omega)=0
. In our case, however, a constant
\omega_{2}
cannot compensate the additional factor
e^{2\text{Im}(\omega)t}
. This factor is meaningful; it describes the time-dependence of the perturbation amplitudes given mode growth or decay:

\varepsilon(t)\rightarrow\varepsilon e^{\text{Im}(\omega)t}

(113)

The frequency correction depends on
\varepsilon^{2}
, therefore matching with a factor of
e^{2\text{Im}(\omega)t}
. Luckily, for small enough perturbations we may approximate:

e^{\text{Im}(\omega)t}\approx e^{\text{Im}(\omega_{0})t}

(114)

at least eliminating the difficult circular dependence of
\omega_{2}
on itself. In the stricter limit
t\ll\text{Im}(\omega_{0})^{\scalebox{0.5}[1.0]{$-$}1}
we may ignore the time-dependent factor altogether:

e^{\text{Im}(\omega)t}\approx 1

(115)

We work under this assumption first.

We construct an unstable model (
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}_{0}<0
) with surface tension and turbulent viscosity, exactly like the first model in Section
V.4
. We use the turbulent viscosity (Eq.
14
) sans local averaging to ease the analysis. We do not account for kinematic viscosity or other forces.

The nonlinear corrections have no dependence on
j
, which acts merely as a Galilean frame shift. We take
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{0}=0
for simplicity, and we assume the effect of higher derivatives of
\mathcal{Q}
(
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}
,
\mathcal{Q}^{(4)}_{0}
, etc.) is negligible. We assume that the simple form of the viscous force (Eq.
12
) is valid. We furthermore take
\alpha_{0}
, appearing in the turbulent viscosity (Eq.
87
), equal to
1/2
. Again, we do not consider the effect of local averaging of the turbulent viscosity.

This system is governed by three dimensionless groups; the inertial instability strength:

N_{\text{in}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}_{0}|}{\mathcal{Q}_{0}}}

(116)

the Weber number:

\text{We}\equiv\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{0}W_{0}^{2}H}{\sigma}

(117)

and the turbulent Ohnesorge number:

\text{Oh}\equiv\frac{\nu_{T}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}_{0}\sigma\!H}}=\frac{\mathcal{Q}_{0}}{\alpha_{0}(1-\alpha_{0})}\cdot\frac{\ell_{vis}|W_{0}|}{\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}_{0}\sigma\!H}}

(118)

Finally, the frequencies (growth rates) can be stated in terms of a scaling parameter:

\omega_{\text{norm}}\equiv\frac{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}_{0}|\mathcal{Q}_{0}W_{0}^{2}}{\sqrt{Q_{0}\sigma\!H}}

(119)

We select some
k=\kappa k_{\text{cut}}
as our principal perturbation wavenumber, where
k_{\text{cut}}
is the wavenumber of the linear growth cutoff (see Eq.
42
):

k_{\text{cut}}=|W_{0}|\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}_{0}|}{2\sigma\!H}}

(120)

and
0<\kappa<1
.

For this
k
we compute
\omega_{0}
and
\widetilde{W}_{1}
in the growth and decay modes:

\displaystyle\omega_{0}-jk

\displaystyle=\frac{i\kappa\omega_{\text{norm}}}{4}(-\text{Oh}\,\kappa\pm\bar{\Delta}^{\frac{1}{2}})

(121)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,W_{0}N_{\text{in}}(\omega_{0}-jk)}{\kappa\omega_{\text{norm}}}\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}

where:

\bar{\Delta}\equiv\text{Oh}^{2}\kappa^{2}-4\kappa^{2}+4

(122)

We then use the
+
(growth) mode values to compute secondary amplitudes:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\sqrt{2}N_{\text{in}}}{2\kappa}\frac{F_{1}(\text{Oh},\kappa)}{(-\text{Oh}^{2}+6)\kappa+\text{Oh}\bar{\Delta}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{2}

(123)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{W_{0}N_{\text{in}}^{2}}{2\kappa}\frac{F_{2}(\text{Oh},\kappa)}{(-\text{Oh}^{2}+6)\kappa+\text{Oh}\bar{\Delta}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{2}

There are multiple ways to satisfy the 3rd-order conditions in
e^{i\theta}
. We choose two frequency corrections, one for each variable:

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{\alpha}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i\omega_{\text{norm}}N_{\text{in}}^{2}}{4}\frac{F_{3}(\text{Oh},\kappa)}{(-\text{Oh}^{2}+6)\kappa+\text{Oh}\bar{\Delta}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{2}

(124)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{W}=

\displaystyle i\omega_{\text{norm}}\left(\frac{N_{\text{in}}^{2}}{4}\frac{\text{We}\cdot F_{4}+F_{5}}{F_{6}}-6\,\text{Oh}\,\kappa^{2}\right)\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{2}

The functions
F_{n}(\text{Oh},\kappa)
are listed in Appendix
E
.

From third order onward, the symbolic algebra becomes very cumbersome. However, at second order we may already reap insights on three separate lines:

1.

Elaboration of the nonlinear cascade mechanism identified in Subsection A.

2.

Nonlinear waveforms, given by the higher-order amplitudes.

3.

Nonlinear time evolution, and potential limiting, governed by the amplitude-dependent frequency corrections.

Concerning the first point: we find that components of order
n
grow as
e^{n\text{Im}(\omega)t}
. This means that growth from a low-
k
“source” mode not only feeds into higher-
k
modes, but the resulting nonlinear growth can also be far faster.

\omega_{2}^{W}
increases.

This increased growth is unlikely to be the end of the story. Higher order corrections to
\omega^{W}
may be stabilizing. We have also assumed that the feedback of amplitude growth or decay over time is negligible (Eq.

), making the present results applicable only at very short times.

In effect, we have so far solved for amplitudes of the form:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}(0)\,e^{|j|\text{Im}\left(\omega^{\alpha}\right)t}

(132)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W}_{j}(0)\,e^{|j|\text{Im}(\omega^{W}\!)t}

However, evolving amplitudes mean evolving
\omega
’s, making simple exponentials à la
e^{\text{Im}(\omega)t}
an insufficient description. One can get around this problem by seeking to state only the time derivatives of the amplitudes:

\displaystyle\frac{d\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}}{dt}\!(t)

\displaystyle=|j|\,\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}\,\text{Im}\!\left[\omega^{\alpha}(\widetilde{\alpha}_{1})\right]

(133)

\displaystyle\frac{d\widetilde{W}_{j}}{dt}\!(t)

\displaystyle=|j|\,\widetilde{W}_{j}\,\text{Im}\!\left[\omega^{W}\!(\widetilde{\alpha}_{1})\right]

where the
\omega
’s are allowed to evolve flexibly. Most of the foregoing analysis stays intact up to this point.

Now, however, parameterizing the
\omega
’s in terms of just
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}
becomes unjustified, as the multiple growth rate corrections cause previously “constant” amplitude ratios to evolve. Once a sufficient degree of nonlinearity enters in, variability of
\widetilde{W}_{1}/\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}
will invalidate even the linear step of the analysis.

One is left with the prospect of considering the time dependence of each amplitude in terms of every other amplitude individually, for example:

\displaystyle d_{t}\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}

\displaystyle=C^{\alpha_{1}}_{\alpha_{1}}\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}+C^{\alpha_{1}}_{W_{1}}\widetilde{W}_{1}+C^{\alpha_{1}}_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{1}^{*}}\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{*}+C^{\alpha_{1}}_{\alpha_{2}W_{1}^{*}}\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}\widetilde{W}_{1}^{*}

(134)

\displaystyle+C^{\alpha_{1}}_{W_{2}\alpha_{1}^{*}}\widetilde{W}_{2}\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}^{*}+C^{\alpha_{1}}_{W_{2}W_{1}^{*}}\widetilde{W}_{2}\widetilde{W}_{1}^{*}+\dots

The
C
’s are obtained by plugging Eq.
105
into the PDEs and collecting terms in
e^{i\theta}
only (not
e^{2i\theta}
etc.) to match the wavelength that goes with
\widetilde{\alpha}_{1}
. Now that the time dependence resides in the amplitudes,
\theta\equiv kx
.

Constructing an equation like Eq.
134
for each amplitude
\widetilde{\alpha}_{j}
or
\widetilde{W}_{j}
up to an order
j_{\text{max}}
results in a system of nonlinear ODEs.

What this approach gains in generality, it loses in usefulness. Even at low order, the ODEs may only be approachable numerically. One could think of them as a finite element discretization of the PDEs on a Fourier basis set. With anything like sufficient resolution, they would become unwieldy.

The results obtained with the simpler perturbation method, assuming constant
\omega
’s and ratios between amplitudes, still support the rest of the theoretical and numerical analysis overall. Disagreements occur where the perturbation method is weakest, i.e. in predicting limiting behaviors over long times.

Appendix A
Derivation of Force Terms

The derivation of the flux-variable TFM equations using the variational method is carried out in
Clausse and López-de Bertodano 2021
, from which we take our advective terms. This method may also be used to derive the surface tension and gravity forces. We include these conservative forces in our present mechanistic derivation, but both approaches give identical results.

We constitute the forces on each fluid,
i
, as follows:

\displaystyle F_{i}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}\rho_{i}g_{x}-D_{i}-(-1)^{i}D_{\text{int}}+V_{i}

(135)

\displaystyle-\partial_{x}(\alpha_{i}P_{i})+P_{\text{int},i}\,\partial_{x}\alpha_{i}

Gravitational acceleration parallel to the channel,
g_{x}
, supplies a body force proportional to the mass of each fluid. For the wall drag on each fluid,
D_{i}
, we use a simple drag law:

D_{i}\equiv\frac{f_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i}}{2A}\rho_{i}|u_{i}|u_{i}

(136)

where
f_{i}
is the Fanning friction factor for fluid
i
,
A
is the cross-sectional area of the channel, and
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is the wall perimeter in contact with fluid
i
. For a rectangular channel in stratified flow:

\mathcal{P}_{i}=w+2\alpha_{i}H

(137)

where
w
is the channel width. However, under a threshold
\alpha_{D}
, we supply limiting:

\mathcal{P}_{i}(\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{D})=\alpha_{i}(w+2H)

(138)

so that
\mathcal{P}_{i}
goes to zero, avoiding infinite rigidity. We take
\alpha_{D}=0.02
where not otherwise specified.

Fluid-fluid interfacial drag is given by:

D_{\text{int}}\equiv\frac{f_{\text{int}}\mathcal{P}_{\text{int}}}{2A}\rho_{D}|u_{r}|u_{r}

(139)

The interfacial Fanning friction factor
f_{\text{int}}
will be reconstituted into the interfacial drag coefficient
C_{D}
in the final equations. The drag density
\rho_{D}
should be chosen based on the extent to which cross-sectional velocity variations are expected within each fluid. Similarly to the
\mathcal{P}_{i}
’s, the interfacial perimeter
\mathcal{P}_{\text{int}}
will be (effectively) set to 0 at extreme
\alpha
’s – see Eq.
166
.

The viscous force on each fluid:

V_{i}=\alpha_{i}\,\partial_{x}\tau_{\text{visc},i}

(140)

is driven by the gradient of the viscous stress
\tau_{\text{visc},i}
, which is given by:

\tau_{\text{visc},i}=\rho_{i}\nu\,\partial_{x}u_{i}

(141)

The viscosity
\nu
(taken equal between the two fluids for simplicity) is split into two components:

\nu=\nu_{k}+\nu_{t}

(142)

where
\nu_{k}
is the kinematic viscosity and
\nu_{t}
is the turbulent eddy viscosity.

We estimate the eddy viscosity with a simple mixing length model. The equation for time-averaged turbulent momentum flux near a boundary:

\tau_{t}=\beta\,l_{m}\Delta u\frac{du}{dy}

(143)

is due to Prandtl
Prandtl 1925
, who was loyal and sympathetic to the Nazi regime
Epple 2002
.
y
is the coordinate normal to a boundary,
l_{m}
is the mixing length,
\Delta u
is the transverse eddy speed (assumed roughly equal to the difference in bulk velocity across a
y
-distance of
l_{m}
) and
\beta
is the area fraction occupied by eddies.

This flux is defined in the
y
direction, but assuming that eddy structure around boundaries is isotropic in
x
(the coordinate aligned with bulk flow) and
y
(the boundary-normal coordinate), we may generalize a local eddy viscosity:

\nu_{t}=\beta\,l_{m}\Delta u

(144)

describing momentum diffusion in both the
x
and
y
directions. Since
l_{m}
is an undetermined parameter, we simply condense
\beta
into it:

\nu_{t}\rightarrow l_{m}\Delta u

(145)

Consider fluid-fluid interfaces to act like boundaries, at least insofar as they generate substantial transverse velocity gradients. The scale of
\Delta u
near an interface is then given by
|u_{r}|
(the difference between the average velocities of the fluids) along with dimensionless quantities that may again be folded into
l_{m}
. Taking the cross-sectional average yields:

\bar{\nu}_{t}=\bar{l}_{m}|u_{r}|

(146)

We drop the averaging bars from here on out and treat
l_{m}
as a constant, yielding Eq.
14
:

\nu_{t}=l_{m}|u_{r}|

(147)

This is one of the turbulent viscosity models proposed by Fullmer
Fullmer 2014
.

With these considerations, the viscous force on each phase becomes:

V_{i}=\rho_{i}\alpha_{i}\partial_{x}\!\left[\left(\nu_{k}+l_{m}|u_{r}|\right)\partial_{x}u_{i}\right]

(148)

Under certain unstable inertial couplings, a TFM with the above turbulent viscosity can exhibit very slow convergence, or even momentum spike blowups like those observed in an inviscid model (Sec.
V.1
). In other words, the model can be ill-posed.

Unfavorable behavior occurs when destabilizing effects from the second part of the viscous stress, seen by expanding Eq.
140
:

V_{i}=\alpha_{i}\rho_{i}\!\left[\nu\partial_{x}^{2}u_{i}+\partial_{x}\nu\cdot\partial_{x}u_{i}\right]

(149)

hinder or outweigh the stabilization provided by the first part. The dependence of
\nu
on the relative velocity (Eq.
147
), as much as it is crucial to nonlinear well-posing, provides the gateway. If the inertia
\Gamma
leads to overly large variations of
u_{r}
across a shock-spike (Sec.
V
), good behavior is not guaranteed.

For this reason, we introduce “local averaging” of Eq.
147
over a preset length
\delta_{m}
:

\nu_{t}(x)\equiv l_{m}\int_{x-\delta_{m}}^{x+\delta_{m}}|u_{r}|\,\frac{dx}{2\delta_{m}}

(150)

The physical reasoning is that, given a turbulent length scale
l_{m}
, the momentum diffusion caused by eddies must be influenced by the flow conditions across a similar length
\delta_{m}
, seeing as the eddies have a finite length.

This averaging tamps down on destabilizing effects coming from the second term in Eq.
149
by smoothing out overly sharp gradients in
u_{r}
as they contribute to
\partial_{x}\nu
. It somewhat weakens the nonlinear stabilization from the first part, but still basically maintains the all-important proportionality
\nu_{t}\propto|W|
(Eq.
102
). Linear damping is unaffected.

The argument is not that these turbulent viscosity models are particularly exact, but that they are simple, physical, and effective.

The average bulk pressure on each phase,
P_{i}
, acts on an area proportional to
\alpha_{i}
; the resulting force density is the gradient
\partial_{x}(\alpha_{i}P_{i})
. In stratified flow, on a finite volume of length
\delta x
, the pressure at the interface of phase
i
(
P_{\text{int},i}
) acts over an area:

\delta a=w\delta x\sqrt{1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}}

(151)

The component of the interfacial pressure acting in the
x
direction is:

(P_{\text{int},i})_{x}=P_{\text{int},i}\cdot\frac{H\partial_{x}\alpha}{\sqrt{1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}}}

(152)

The resulting force density is shown to be:

\frac{\delta F}{A\delta x}=(P_{\text{int},i})_{x}\,\frac{\delta a}{H\!w\delta x}=P_{\text{int},i}\,\partial_{x}\alpha_{i}

(153)

A hydrostatic approximation for the pressures across each phase gives:

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle=P_{\text{int},1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{1}g_{y}H(1-\alpha)

(154)

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle=P_{\text{int},2}-\tfrac{1}{2}\rho_{2}g_{y}\alpha

The surface tension results in a pressure jump across the interface. Call the height of the interface
h_{\text{int}}=H\alpha
. Then its curvature is:

\kappa_{c}=\partial_{x}^{2}h_{\text{int}}\left(1+h_{\text{int}}^{2}\right)^{-3/2}

(155)

and the pressure jump:

\Delta P_{\text{int}}=\sigma\kappa_{c}

(156)

ergo:

P_{\text{int},1}=P_{\text{int},2}+\sigma\!H\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha\left[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}\right]^{-3/2}

(157)

These relations are valid only for stratified flow, and substantial modifications may be necessary when extending to other flow regimes. For example, in bubbly flow of uniform radius
r_{b}
, the pressure jump becomes constant:

\Delta P_{\text{int}}=\sigma\kappa_{c}=\frac{2\sigma}{r_{b}}

(158)

leading the surface tension terms in both the mixture and relative momentum equations to vanish. The absence of a high-order ST term necessitates linear stabilization via the inertial coupling.

Any of 4 pressures (
P_{i}
,
P_{\text{int},i}
) may be chosen as the reference pressure
P
, although this choice must be respected when supplying boundary conditions. We choose:

P=P_{\text{int},1}

(159)

This choice has no effect on the
g_{y}
and
\sigma
terms in the relative momentum equation, but it does affect their form in the mixture momentum equation.

We now demonstrate combining the forces into the mixture and relative momentum equations. We use the fact that the mixture momentum may be stated:

\rho_{m}j-\Delta\rho J=(1-\alpha)\rho_{1}u_{1}+\alpha\rho_{2}u_{2}

(160)

which is just the sum of the phase momenta. With no IC, the term under the time derivative in the relative momentum equation simplifies to:

W-\Delta\rho j=\Gamma_{0}J-\Delta\rho j=\rho_{2}u_{2}-\rho_{1}u_{1}

(161)

which is the difference of the phase momenta divided by their respective void fractions.

Thus the force terms must be combined as follows:

\displaystyle F_{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{2}}{\alpha}-\frac{F_{1}}{1-\alpha}

(162)

\displaystyle F_{m}

\displaystyle=F_{1}+F_{2}

where
F_{W}
is the relative momentum force, and
F_{m}
is the mixture momentum force. We assume that the forces derived without inertial coupling remain applicable generally. Some assumption of this type must be made to include non-conservative forces, which cannot be derived by the variational method.

Carrying out Eq.
162
yields the RHS’s of Eqs.
9
and
16
. We draw attention to two terms; first, the interfacial drag:

\displaystyle F_{\text{int},m}

\displaystyle=0

(163)

\displaystyle F_{\text{int},W}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{\text{int}}\mathcal{P}_{\text{int}}}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)A}\rho_{D}|u_{r}|u_{r}

The “equal and opposite” actions cancel in the mixture equation. For the relative momentum equation, we define a simplified drag coefficient:

C_{D}\equiv\frac{f_{\text{int}}\mathcal{P}_{\text{int}}H}{2\alpha(1-\alpha)A}\Rightarrow F_{\text{int},W}=\frac{C_{D}\rho_{D}}{H}|u_{r}|u_{r}

(164)

For stratified flow in a rectangular channel at intermediate void fractions:

\mathcal{P}_{\text{int}}\approx w\Rightarrow\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\text{int}}}{A}\approx\frac{1}{H}

(165)

so we may simplify:

C_{D}=\frac{f_{\text{int}}}{2\max\left[\alpha(1-\alpha),\alpha_{D}\right]}

(166)

where the
\max
condition avoids infinite rigidity. We set
\alpha_{D}=0.02
where not otherwise specified.

Secondly, we examine the effect of viscosity:

\displaystyle F_{\text{visc},m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}V_{i}

(167)

\displaystyle F_{\text{visc},W}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\tfrac{(-1)^{i}}{\alpha_{i}}V_{i}

Based on Eq.
148
, we may rewrite
F_{\text{visc},W}
:

F_{\text{visc},W}=\partial_{x}\!\left[\nu\partial_{x}(\rho_{2}u_{2}-\rho_{1}u_{1})\right]

(168)

Without inertial coupling (Eq.
161
), this term simplifies:

F_{\text{visc},W}=\partial_{x}\!\left[\nu\partial_{x}(W-\Delta\rho j)\right]

(169)

and assuming incompressibility (Eq.
8
):

F_{\text{visc},W}=\partial_{x}\!\left(\nu\partial_{x}W\right)

(170)

In practice, we extend the use of Eqs.
169
-
170
to general inertial couplings to avoid destabilizing effects caused by the appearance of other gradients.

Appendix C
Numerical Methods

For the purpose of this analysis, the Fourier transform of a function
f(x)
is defined as:

\mathcal{F}[f](k)\equiv\frac{1}{\ell}\int_{0}^{\ell}e^{-ikx}f(x)\,dx

(186)

assuming a finite periodic domain. The inverse transform is then defined:

\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\widetilde{f}](x)\,=\!\sum_{k=2\pi/\ell}^{\infty}e^{ikx}\widetilde{f}(k)

(187)

On a finite set of mesh points with separation
\Delta x
, we can approximate Eq.
186
via:

\mathcal{F}[f](k)\approx\frac{1}{\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-ikx_{i}}f(x_{i})\,\Delta x

(188)

Numerical solutions are carried out via explicit 2nd-order time-marching on a finite difference method. All variables are regarded as being located at the center of evenly sized mesh-cells of length
\Delta x
.

Advective flux terms are discretized on a per-equation basis according to the upwinded flux-limiter (FL) method, a very succinct description of which may be found in
Roe 1986
. This method is conservative and up to second-order accurate in space, mixing in first-order upwind differencing in the vicinity of sharp gradients to avoid spurious oscillations.

A variety of 2nd-order differencing methods may be used as a base for the FL. Though Lax-Wendroff is common in problems of pure advection, it is not easily applicable to problems with additional force terms. We use simple center differencing instead.

Taking as an example the
\alpha
-equation (Eq.
6
), one supplies to the flux limiter the advected variable
\alpha
, the flux
\alpha u_{2}=\alpha j+\mathcal{Q}W
, and the upwinding velocity, given by the partial derivative of the flux w.r.t. the advected variable, in this case
j+\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W
. This velocity, shared by the void and relative momentum equations, is just the eigenvelocity
v_{\text{eig}}
discussed in Section
V.2
.

The flux limiter takes a pre-specified limiter function (
\phi
) of the ratio (
r
) of node-to-node deltas of the advected variable to determine the levels of 1st-order upwind and 2nd-order central differencing applied to each face flux. The face upwinding velocity is determined by averaging the velocities at the two adjoining nodes. Though many limiters exist, we find that the simple minmod limiter:

\phi(r)=\max\!\left[0,\min(1,r)\right]

(189)

works best in our case. The combined flux is given by:

F=\phi(r)F_{\text{2nd-order}}+\left(1-\phi(r)\right)F_{\text{1st-order-upwind}}

(190)

We discovered it was necessary to include the surface tension within the same FL framework for numerical stability. We view the
W
-equation ST term (Eq.
9
) as supplying an added momentum “flux” (stress) equal to:

\Phi_{\sigma}=\sigma H\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\alpha}{[1+(H\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}]^{3/2}}

(191)

Obtaining the variation of this flux w.r.t. the local flow variables is complicated by the presence of
\alpha
’s derivatives; some notion of a functional derivative may be used to do so in the future. We ignore its potential contribution to the upwinding velocity in the present study.

An open end in the present numerical approach is that it is not precisely suited to the unstable two-equation system. A canonical multi-variable FL method linearizes and diagonalizes a system of conservation laws
LeVeque 1992
along the lines of Sec.
IV
/Appx.
B
. The flux limiter is then implemented on each “eigen-equation”. This methodology is intended for purely hyperbolic systems where the resulting characteristic velocities are always real. No bespoke methods for advective terms resulting in complex eigenvalues were found.

It is also true that the present numerical approach is not Galilean invariant; adding a total velocity to the system generally results in additional numerical dissipation. A moving-mesh scheme along the lines of
Springel 2010
may be applied to resolve this issue in the future.

We implement a method to ensure that entropy-violating shocks do not propagate. This failure case occurs when the characteristic (upwinding) velocity crosses 0 across a single-node shock, and there are roughly equal fluxes either side of it
Harten et al. 1976
. If the characteristic velocities point inward, one has correctly rendered a stable shock. If they lead outward, the shock should be smoothed out over time, producing a “rarefaction wave”. The numerical method leaves the shock untouched because it lacks information about the intermediate region.

To address this issue, we make a targeted fix to the differencing of the advective term in the
\alpha
equation. Whenever
v_{\text{eig}}
crosses from negative to positive between two nodes
j
and
j+1
, instead of taking the flux at the adjoining face to be some combination of the node-centered fluxes:

F_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\beta F_{j}+(1-\beta)F_{j+1}

(192)

with
\beta
dictated by the flux limiter, and the flux at each node given by:

F_{j}=\alpha_{j}u_{2_{j}}

(193)

we instead average the sub-components of the flux individually:

F_{j+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j+1}}{2}\frac{u_{2_{j}}+u_{2_{j+1}}}{2}

(194)

We find that this “entropy fix” both dissipates spurious shocks and avoids their formation.

We observe light spurious noise in the present method when the characteristic velocity sits close to 0 over a significant part of the domain. Such cases usually only occur when the model is extremely dissipative.

It is not necessary to create a special modeling regime for regions where
\alpha
is near 0 or 1. We instead make a simple change to the basic flux limiter method that ensures
\alpha
remain between
0
and
1
very closely.

We find that, as long as
\lim_{\alpha\to 0\,\text{or}\,1}\!\mathcal{Q}=0
, overshoots of
\alpha
beyond 0 or 1 tend to be isolated to single nodes at the top (or bottom) of shocks. They are also self-limiting or even self- correcting. This self-correction can be enhanced if the sign of the upwinding velocity at the overshoot node is imposed on both of the adjacent face fluxes. This simple approach makes no change to the underlying equations, keeps the numerical method completely conservative, and brings overshoots down from
10^{-2}
or more to about
10^{-4}
.

Second derivatives appearing in the viscous term and ST flux are evaluated according to the simple 2nd-order discretization:

(\partial_{x}^{2}\phi)_{i}\equiv\frac{\phi_{i+1}-2\phi_{i}+\phi_{i-1}}{\Delta x^{2}}

(195)

where
\phi
stands in for
\alpha
or
W
. The square of the first derivative in the denominator of the ST flux is discretized as the average of the squares of the forward and backward differences:

(\partial_{x}\alpha)^{2}_{i}\approx\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i}}{\Delta x}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}}{\Delta x}\right)^{2}\right]

(196)

This averaging scheme is second-order accurate, and preferable to center-differencing insofar as it does not return 0 if the forward and backward differences cancel, better limiting the action of the surface tension in the presence of large gradients.

Remaining first derivatives in the buoyant (
g_{y}
) term:

g_{y}\Delta\rho H\partial_{x}\alpha

(197)

and the viscous cross term:

\partial_{x}\nu\cdot\partial_{x}W

(198)

are center-differenced.

We totally separate the discretization of the time and space derivatives. We opt for an explicit Runge Kutta method to iterate forward in time. Of the wide selection available, we choose the second-order midpoint method for its simplicity and good performance per computation.

We make this choice despite the findings of Gottlieb and Shu
Gottlieb et al. 2001
. They showed that for purely advective problems, Heun’s method is the optimal 2nd-order Strong Stability Preserving (SSP) method. On TFM problems involving surface tension and turbulent viscosity, Heun’s method does tolerate higher timesteps than the midpoint method without solutions numerically “blowing up”, but they instead become noisy and differ significantly from converged solutions. Excepting such results, we find that Heun’s method only increases the “allowable” timestep length by
\sim 1\%
. We judge that the value of this slight speed-up does not outweigh the cost of filtering bogus solutions.

Gottlieb and Shu’s optimal 3rd-order method increases the allowable timestep by
\sim 20\%
, nonetheless failing to justify its more than
50\%
higher computational cost.

Appendix D
Energy Conservation and Flux

To demonstrate energy conservation w.r.t. local transport we take the time derivative of the “local energy” (Eq.
27
):

\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}=\int_{0}^{1}\tfrac{1}{2}\partial_{t}(\mathcal{Q}W^{2})\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2}\partial_{t}\alpha+\mathcal{Q}W\partial_{t}W\,dx

(199)

then using the force-free equations (Eq.
17
) we replace:

\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}\!=\!-\!\int_{0}^{1}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2}\partial_{x}(\mathcal{Q}W+\alpha j)+\mathcal{Q}W\partial_{x}(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W^{2}+Wj)\,dx

(200)

If
\partial_{x}j=0
, combining terms reveals the conservation law:

\dot{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}=\left.-(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{Q}W^{2}\right|^{1}_{0}

(201)

We may therefore write the energy flux:

\mathcal{F}_{\text{local}}=(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}W+j)\,T_{\text{local}}

(202)

Including the surface tension force means one must consider the surface energy (Eq.
45
). The time derivative of this energy, along with the force term in
