Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis reveals differential molecular mechanisms underlying resistance of native and invasive Phytolacca americana against Spodoptera litura herbivory
Authors: Feiran Jia, Huiyi Lu, Baoya He, Yupeng Geng, Yi Wang, Danfeng Liu, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn response to herbivory from insects, plants have evolved complex defense strategies. Alien plants often escape control by specialist insects in their introduced range. This release from herbivory pressure may lead to a reallocation of the defensive strategies of alien plants, thereby promoting their invasiveness. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying this resource reallocation process remain unclear. In the present study, we used Phytolacca americana as a model species, and combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses to investigate the molecular basis of its invasion success under the herbivory by Spodoptera litura.ResultsHerbivore assays with S. litura revealed that constitutive resistance was comparable between native and invasive P. americana, while induced resistance was significantly lower in invasive P. americana. In the native population, differentially expressed genes and differential abundance metabolites upon S. litura herbivory were mainly enriched in α‑linolenic acid metabolism, the primary pathway for jasmonic acid (JA) formation, whereas in the invasive population, they were predominantly enriched in biosynthesis of amino acids. Notably, the invasive population maintains the synthesis of certain defense metabolites like isoquercitrin, suggesting a defense strategy reconfiguration rather than a complete loss.ConclusionsCollectively, these findings suggest that invasive P. americana may employ a strategic resource reallocation from induced defense to growth metabolism, while JA-mediated induced defense was activated in native P. americana. Our results provide support for the Shifting Defense Hypothesis and advance the mechanisms of plant invasion adaptation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09599-z.Keywords: Herbivory resistance, Native and invasive Phytolacca americana, Transcriptome-metabolome integration

Introduction

Plants are exposed to diverse biotic and abiotic stresses throughout their life cycle, with insect herbivory being one of the primary biotic stresses [1]. In response to insects’ damage, plants have evolved multiple defense strategies, encompassing resistance and tolerance [2]. Resistance refers to the ability of plants to reduce or avoid insect damage, and tolerance is the ability of plants to endure the damage or detrimental agents to which they are exposed [3], and there is a trade-off between them [4]. Plant resistance is divided into constitutive resistance and induced resistance. Constitutive resistance is an inherent characteristic of plants and is determined by their genotype, while induced resistance is often elicited by mechanical damage and herbivory [5]. Similarly, trade-offs also exist between these two forms of resistances [6].

The invasion of alien plants has severely affected the structure and function of ecosystems [7], even posing a serious threat to economy [8]. Thus, for the effective prevention and management of plant invasions, it is especially crucial to thoroughly elucidate their invasion mechanisms. Upon invading novel habitats, invasive plants typically escape control by their native natural enemies, especially specialist herbivores (Enemy Release Hypothesis, ERH) [9]. Their herbivory resistance traits tend to shift from defense against specialist herbivores to enhanced resistance against generalist herbivores (Shifting Defense Hypothesis, SDH) [10, 11], or reallocate resources from defense to growth and reproduction (Evolution of Increased Competitive Ability, EICA) [12]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that invasive plants exhibit lower constitutive resistance compared to their native counterparts, whereas their induced defense is higher than that of native plants [3, 13]. Nevertheless, current research on the impacts of insect herbivory on plant invasions remains lacking in systematic investigations [14, 15], particularly at the molecular and metabolic levels [16, 17].

Transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis has been widely applied in plant resistance research, successfully identifying key defense genes and metabolites, and elucidating the molecular and metabolic mechanisms underlying plant resistance against herbivory [18]. However, few studies have investigated resistance and omics differences between invasive plants and their native counterparts in response to generalist insect herbivory [19].

Phytolacca americana is a perennial herb native to North America. Since its first discovery in Hangzhou, China in 1935, it has rapidly spread to multiple regions across the country, inflicting severe harm on the local ecosystem [20, 21]. Studies have investigated the differences in two types of resistance (constitutive resistance and induced resistance) between invasive populations and native populations of P. americana following insect herbivory [22]. However, at the molecular level, research on the mechanisms underlying the formation of these resistance differences remains relatively scarce. In our field surveys, Spodoptera litura were observed to conduct herbivory on P. americana leaves, leaving holes and scars. Furthermore, its chewing herbivory effectively elicits plant defense signaling pathways, thereby inducing a cascade of defense responses [23]. Therefore, S. litura was selected as the insect material in this study.

To investigate the differences in constitutive and induced resistance between native and invasive populations of P. americana, as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms, we examined gene expression profiles and quantified metabolite accumulation in both native and invasive P. americana plants following herbivory by the generalist herbivore S. litura. This approach not only reveals the contrasting anti-herbivore strategies between invasive and native populations of P. americana but also provides novel insights into the molecular and ecological mechanisms underlying plant invasion. Furthermore, the findings may offer a theoretical basis and identify potential molecular targets for the environmentally sustainable management of invasive plants.

Results

Constitutive and induced resistance

To assess the resistance of P. americana against S. litura, the constitutive and induced resistances were determined based on individual larval weight gain following herbivory on detached leaves (constitutive) or intact plants (induced) from both native and invasive populations. Resistance assays showed that constitutive resistance levels were comparable between invasive and native plants of P. americana, with no significant difference (P = 0.999) (Fig. 1). In contrast, induced resistance exhibited opposite trends and differed significantly between the two populations (P < 0.001). Specifically invasive plants displayed weaker induced resistance (-172.25 ± 38.23), whereas native plants showed stronger induced resistance (327.11 ± 90.35) (Fig. 1). These results suggested that invasive P. americana plants maintain constitutive resistance at a level similar to their native conspecifics but exhibit a reduced capacity to activate induced defenses upon S. litura herbivory, which may represent a distinctive defense strategy associated with its invasion success.

Transcriptomic analysis of P. americana in response to S. litura herbivory

To explore the differential responses of genes and transcription factors in invasive and native plants of P. americana following S.litura herbivory, transcriptome sequencing was conducted in this study. A total of 18 samples were sequenced, yielding 137.09 Gb of clean data. The clean data for each sample exceeded 6 Gb, and the percentage of Q30 bases was above 93%, indicating that the data quality met the requirements for subsequent analysis. The total raw reads and trimmed reads for each sample are provided in Table S1. Through quantitative PCR (qPCR) validation of seven randomly selected genes, we found that the qPCR results showed similar trends with the transcriptome sequencing data (Fig. 2, Table S2, Table S3), thereby confirming the reliability of the transcriptomic data for subsequent analyses.

To identify a high-confidence regulatory relationship, we applied stringent correlation filters to the Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values of DEGs and transcription factors (TFs). This approach substantially refined the candidate sets by decreasing the number of considered DEGs from 2,290 to 320 and the number of TFs from 574 to 289. The most dramatic refinement was observed for DEGs in the invasive P. americana between 0 h and 48 h of herbivory, where their number decreased from 1,227 to 175 (Fig. 4A).

KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analysis revealed that the genes within this core network were predominantly associated with defense-related metabolic and nutrient-related biosynthetic pathways. Among the DEGs, several specific pathways, including alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation, and the MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase) signaling pathway were enriched across multiple comparison groups. Meanwhile, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism and valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation were consistently significantly enriched (P < 0.01) in all detected groups. In addition, two broad categories, including metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, were consistently and significantly enriched across all comparison groups (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A). Collectively, these results indicate that native and invasive P. americana plants present distinct gene expression patterns in response to S. litura herbivory. Functional enrichment analysis further reveals that the core network genes are mainly enriched in defense-linked metabolic and nutrient biosynthetic pathways.

Metabolomic analysis of P. americana in response to S. litura herbivory

Metabolomic analysis of P. americana leaves following S. litura herbivory revealed significant reprogramming in metabolite accumulation. PCA showed that PC1 (explaining 38.02% of the variance) and PC2 (17.52% of the variance) were major factors shaping the metabolic profiles, with clear separation among treatment groups (Fig. S1B).

From a total of 1,327 detected metabolites, 154 were identified as differential abundance metabolites (DAMs) in response to herbivory. The response dynamics, however, differed markedly between the two populations. Analysis of DAMs at the same herbivory time points between the two populations revealed dynamic changes in both number and composition over time. The number of DAMs was the highest when comparing the two populations at the same time point. With prolonged herbivory by S. litura, the number of all DAMs increased in the native P. americana plants, whereas in the invasive P. americana plants, DAMs showed a more complex pattern of both up- and down-regulation, though up-regulation was predominant (Fig. 3B). Overall, the number of DAMs was lower in the invasive plants compared to the native plants. Before herbivory, 27 DAMs were identified between the invasive and native plants. This number increased to 87 at 24 h post-infestation and decreased slightly to 80 at 48 h (Fig. 4B). Among these comparisons, 15 DAMs were consistently differential across all time points, while the numbers of unique DAMs in each comparison group were 2, 40, and 37, respectively (Fig. 4B).

Enrichment analysis of DAMs also identified several specific pathways, including isoflavonoid biosynthesis, D-amino acid metabolism, and biosynthesis of amino acids, in multiple metabolomic comparison groups (Fig. 5B). Consistent with the transcriptomic findings, the broad categories metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites were detected in all metabolomic comparisons. However, unlike the transcriptomic data, significant enrichment of these two pathways was observed only in direct comparisons between the two populations (Fig. 5B). Taken together, metabolomic results showed that after P. americana were damaged by S. litura, the accumulation of DAMs exhibited significant differences between invasive and native P. americana with the extension of herbivory time. Among them, the number of DAMs in invasive P. americana was higher than that in native P. americana. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DAMs in invasive P. americana were significantly enriched in resistance-related pathways and primary metabolite biosynthesis pathways, while DAMs in native P. americana were mainly concentrated in resistance-related pathways.

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analysis of P. americana in response to S. litura herbivory

Using integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic data, we conducted a comparative analysis between the native and invasive P. americana upon S. litura herbivory. Integrated KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the metabolome of the native P. americana population was primarily enriched in the alpha‑linolenic acid metabolism pathway, whereas its transcriptome was involved in a broader range of defense‑related pathways. In the invasive population, both DAMs and DEGs were widely enriched across multiple metabolic pathways, which could be broadly categorized into three major classes: flavonoid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism. Through stringent correlation filtering and subsequent removal of components without direct biochemical links, we constructed a consolidated pathway map centered on core metabolic processes. This refined core regulatory network ultimately comprised 50 key DEGs and 32 pivotal DAMs, which consistently distinguished the two populations (Fig. 6; Table S4; Table S5).

Metabolites sustaining primary metabolism and growth, such as tryptophan, serotonin, glutamine, aspartate, valine, UDP (Uridine Diphosphate)-glucose, and succinate, were consistently more abundant in the invasive P. americana upon herbivory. In contrast, histamine was slightly more abundant in the native P. americana (Fig. 7A). These metabolic shifts may presumably be driven by correspond changes at the transcriptional level for several key pathways. The elevated accumulation of metabolites such as UDP-glucose, valine, glutamate, and succinate in the invasive plants could be associated with the concordant upregulation of their biosynthetic genes, including those encoding enzymes for UDP-glucose synthesis (EC 5.1.3.2, EC 2.4.1.13), valine (EVM0006240), glutamate (EVM0020332, EVM0011398, EVM0006028), and succinate (EVM0013675, EVM0024573) (Fig. 7A).

In addition to the coordinated changes described above, several key genes with consistently high expression in both populations emerged as potential metabolic hubs. For instance, gene EVM0013675, encoding a putative isocitrate dehydrogenase (EC 4.1.3.1) that catalyzes the conversion of isocitrate to succinate, exhibited sustained high expression in both populations upon insect herbivory. Similarly, gene EVM0004566, annotated as a fructose-bisphosphate aldolase (EC 4.1.2.13) involved in the conversion of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate to dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, showed consistently high transcript levels across populations following herbivory by S.litura. Meanwhile, gene EVM0025204, which putatively facilitates the conversion of dopaquinone to tyrosine and was highly expressed in the invasive P. americana at 24 h post-herbivory, may contribute to the enhanced primary metabolic capacity observed in this population. The coordinated high expression of these genes, particularly those involved in central carbon and energy metabolism (gene EVM0013675 and EVM0004566), aligns with the metabolomic evidence of enhanced primary metabolism in the invasive population (Fig. 7B). Meanwhile, several key metabolites highlighted the complexity of metabolic regulation beyond transcription. For metabolites such as isoquercitrin accumulation was higher in the invasive P. americana even in the absence of a parallel upregulation in their immediate biosynthetic genes (Fig. 7B).

Beyond these pathways, the phytohormone jasmonic acid (JA) itself exhibited distinct accumulation dynamics between populations. JA levels surged rapidly (0–24 h) in native P. americana after herbivory, whereas its accumulation in the invasive plants were delayed and more gradual (24–48 h) (Table S4). Due to its primary role as a signaling molecule and the lack of direct substrate-enzyme links within the consolidated metabolic map, JA was not retained in the final network of directly interacting molecules. Nonetheless, this divergent temporal pattern highlights a fundamental difference in the timing of defense signaling initiation.

Discussion

Herbivore bioassays showed a clear divergence in defense strategies between the native and invasive P. americana (Fig. 1). While constitutive resistance remained comparable, induced resistance was significantly lower in the invasive P. americana (Fig. 1). This pattern aligns with the core prediction of the SDH, which posits that invasive plants, released from specialist herbivores, are able to shift their defense strategies. Alternanthera philoxerophora showed that its invasive populations exhibited increased constitutive resistance but decreased induced resistance against S. litura, similar to our observation of a diminished induced defense response in its invasive range [3]. However, the evolutionary trajectory of defense traits is not uniform. For instance, invasive populations of Lespedeza cuneata displayed a contrasting pattern, with increased induced resistance coupled with decreased constitutive resistance [24]. This contrast underscores the context-dependent nature of plant invasion strategies. Silene latifolia has been reported lower overall resistance but superior growth in invasive populations following insect herbivory, highlighting a growth-defense trade-off consistent with EICA [25].

The invasive P. americana exhibited a pronounced metabolic shift towards growth. It accumulated significantly higher levels of primary metabolites, including key amino acids (e.g., glutamine, aspartate, valine), sugars (e.g., UDP-glucose), and organic acids (e.g., succinate) (Fig. 7A; Table S4). The invasive P. americana accumulates significantly higher levels of serotonin than the native conspecifics (Fig. 7A), and serotonin has been widely reported to increase plant tissue palatability [26]. This metabolic flux shift aligns with the prediction of the SDH, whereby invasive plants tend to withdraw resources from high-nitrogen-cost induced defenses and reallocate them into primary metabolism to support growth, the redirected nitrogen may contribute to elevated serotonin production [27]. Many of these amino acids serve as essential nitrogen sources for protein synthesis and growth. Their coordinated accumulation, supported by the upregulation of biosynthetic genes (e.g., EVM0006240 for valine; EVM0013675 for succinate) (Fig. 7B; Table S5), may be associated with a strategic reallocation of nitrogen resources, as further supported by a recent study on Ageratina adenophora, which revealed that invasive populations, in contrast to native ones, accumulated significantly higher levels of multiple amino acids in response to herbivory by the specialist herbivore Procecidochares utilis [28]. Similar results also found in other invasive species that enhance amino acid synthesis under nitrogen enrichment [29]. Rather than predominantly invested in nitrogen-rich, induced defense compounds like PIs, nitrogen is efficiently assimilated into growth-supporting metabolites. The concomitant elevation in sugars and organic acids may provide the necessary carbon skeletons and energy for this enhanced nitrogen assimilation, illustrating a coordinated reprogramming of both carbon and nitrogen metabolisms [30].

The observed reduction in induced resistance in invasive P. americana is presumably underpinned by a fundamental molecular reprogramming. Transcriptomic profiling indicated a critical divergence in phytohormone regulation. In the native P. americana, herbivory triggered a rapid, strong, and sustained upregulation of the JA signaling pathway, which is critical for induced and systemic plant defenses [31, 32]. In contrast, the invasive P. americana exhibited a markedly attenuated JA pathway response. At 24 h post-infestation, the increase in JA content in native plants was 3.40-fold greater than in invasive plants (Table S4). Even at 48 h, when JA levels in both populations converged, the induced resistance remained significantly lower in the invasive P. americana plants, indicating a putatively compromised JA-mediated defense program.

The molecular basis for the reduced JA response and enhanced growth metabolism in the invasive P. americana is partially speculated to be attributable to conserved hormonal crosstalk. Auxin signaling appears to antagonize JA-dependent defense. Additionally, it may contribute to the population-specific metabolic differences identified here [33]. Such growth-defense trade‑off may be further modulated by dynamic crosstalk between gibberellin and JA signaling, in which the core interaction between DELLA and JAZ proteins presumably consolidates such metabolic divergence across populations [34]. Combined with the defense-growth balance, a signaling shift toward auxin predominance is plausible for the invasive population. This hypothesis is consistent with the observed accumulation of tryptophan, an essential precursor for the major auxin indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) [35]. However, no direct correlation was found between tryptophan biosynthetic gene expression and its accumulation, which may be attributed to enzyme pleiotropy or pathway flux redirection [36]. Future studies directly quantifying hormone dynamics are needed to test this hypothesis. This strategy involves strategic reconfiguration, not a complete abandonment of defense. The invasive P. americana maintains comparable constitutive resistance and accumulates defense-related compounds such as isoquercitrin, revealing a strategic reconfiguration rather than a complete abandonment of defensive capacity [37]. As a flavonoid glycoside with anti-herbivore properties derived primarily from carbon skeletons, isoquercitrin may represent a more “cost-effective” defensive investment compared to nitrogen rich PIs [38, 39], potentially imposing a lower metabolic burden on growth.

Nevertheless, several improvements can be made in future investigations. First, to facilitate subsequent full-length gene cloning and functional verification, we set a relatively stringent threshold of FPKM > 100 for gene screening. This filtering criterion inevitably eliminates some moderately expressed genes, which may also perform vital functions in the pathways of plant growth and anti-herbivore defense. Second, further integration of multi-omic datasets from more populations will facilitate rigorous comparisons of phenotypic variation across native and invaded geographic regions. Third, the functions of all identified candidates require systematic and comprehensive validation, with potential applications for invasive species management.

Conclusion

Herbivore resistance test suggested that invasive P. americana had similar constitutive resistance to native P. americana, whereas the former exhibited attenuated induced resistance. Transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses indicated that primary metabolites and the genes responsible for their biosynthesis were significantly enriched in invasive P. americana after herbivory by S. litura, while defense-related metabolic pathways, including JA signaling, were activated in native P.americana. These findings may provide tentative evidence for the SDH and EICA hypotheses and lend further molecular support.

Materials and methods

Plant and insect materials

Seeds of P. americana were collected from Baiwangshan Park (40°1′N, 116°15′E), Haidian District, Beijing, China (invasive) and Wayne County (42°15′N, 83°40′W), Michigan, USA (native) in late September 2022. Sampling was permitted when conducted complying with local legislation. The DNA identification of the samples was conducted by us, based on the nuclear ribosomal ITS2 region [40], all voucher specimens were deposited at the Institute of Biodiversity Science, Yunnan University, China. Experimental plants were cultivated at the Institute of Biodiversity Science (24°49′N, 102°51′E), Yunnan University, China. Seeds were treated with concentrated sulfuric acid for 6 min and then placed on 1% agar (w/v) medium and then germinated in a growth chamber (Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd, China) under controlled conditions of 24 ± 2 ℃ and a photoperiodic cycle of 14 h light :10 h dark for 7 days. After germination, the seedlings were transplanted into rectangular plastic boxes with cover (length: 21.5 cm, width: 14 cm, height: 10 cm) and cultured in the same growth chamber as mentioned above for two weeks. Then, these seedlings were transplanted to round plastic pots (top diameter: 18 cm; height: 18 cm) filled with cultivated soil (Yunnan Highland Wetland Resources Comprehensive Research Institute Co., Ltd China) and grown in a common garden (24 ± 2 ℃, 14 L: 10D) for 45 days.

S. litura eggs were purchased from Jiyuan Baiyun Industrial Co., Ltd., Henan Province, China. and hatched under the same growth chamber (27 ℃/25 ℃, 14 L: 10 D). Newly hatched larvae were used for subsequent herbivory resistance experiments. Meanwhile, third-instar S. litura larvae were purchased from the same commercial supplier and subjected to 12 h starvation prior to treatments for transcriptomic and metabolomic sampling.

Constitutive and induced resistance of P. americana against S. litura

For the constitutive resistance assay, 10 plants per population with uniform growth were used. Four rectangular covered plastic boxes (length: 21.5 cm, width: 14 cm, height: 10 cm) were prepared for each population and treated as four replicates. Approximately 150 mL of 1% (w/v) agar was poured into each box, and after solidification, a layer of absorbent paper was placed on top. One fully expanded leaf was cut at the base of the petiole using scissors, then flattened on absorbent paper in the corresponding plastic box. Sixteen newly hatched S. litura larvae were inoculated onto each leaf using a fine brush carefully. The larvae underwent 10 days herbivory. Throughout this period, fresh leaves were regularly and uniformly replaced by harvesting four leaves daily from a single dedicated plant of the same population, ensuring one leaf per replicate. This procedure was designed to maintain a consistent and equivalent leaf supply across all four replicates. During leaf replacement, the larvae were gently transferred to the new leaves with a fine brush. After 10 days of herbivory, the weight of each larva in a plastic box was recorded individually using an electronic balance (OLABO, Shangdong, China), and the reciprocal of the individual larval weight was used to represent constitutive resistance, lower larval weight indicates stronger resistance [41].

For induced resistance, 16 newly hatched larvae S. litura were inoculated per plant, evenly distributed on three fully expanded leaves [42]. After inoculation, the entire plant was covered with a mesh cover to prevent larval escape. Four plants per population were used. After 10 days of herbivory, the weight of the individual larvae on each plant was measured using an electronic balance as described above. The reciprocal of the larval weight was used to represent total resistance; induced resistance is defined as total resistance minus constitutive resistance [43].

Transcriptomic sequencing

Nine invasive and 9 native P. americana plants were infested with six third-instar larvae per plant and confined with mesh bags. Leaf samples were collected at 0 h, 24 h, and 48 h after the onset of larval herbivory, and stored at -80℃ until further analysis. Each replicate consisted of three plants per population, with three biological replicates in total.

Total RNA was extracted from each replicate using a RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, RNA identification and quantification were performed using a Nano Drop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). After this, mRNA was purified with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads, and the purified mRNA was fragmented into small segments. Qualified RNA was used for library construction, which involved mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, PCR amplification, and final library preparation [44]. RNA-Seq was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate paired-end (PE) reads [45]. After aligning the clean reads to the reference genome using HISAT, gene alignment counts were calculated with feature Counts and normalized as FPKM values. DESeq2 and GSEA were employed to analyze differential gene expressions and perform enrichment analysis. The screening criteria for DEGs and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were at least a two-fold change in expression (P < 0.05). For further screening, DEGs with FPKM ≥ 100 and TFs with FPKM ≥ 10 were retained and subjected to correlation analysis [46]. Only pairs with a significant Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC > 0.8, P < 0.05) were retained [47]. Subsequent statistical analysis was performed to identify the KEGG pathways enriched by the remaining DEGs and TFs.

qPCR analysis

To further validate the reliability of the transcriptomic data, seven randomly selected DEGs were subjected to absolute qPCR. Primers (Table S6) were designed with SnapGene (SnapGene 6.1.0) according to transcriptome sequences. Target fragements of each gene were ligated into the pEASY-Blunt Simple cloning vector (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and the resulting recombinant plasmids were used as quantitative standards. QPCR assays were performed in a 20 µL reaction volume using the SuperReal PreMix Plus Kit (SYBR Green; Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) on an ABI QuantStudio 7 Flex system (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). Samples from the two populations collected at different time points were tested. Each sample was run in three technical replicates. The thermal cycling program was set as follows: 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 32 s [46].

Metabolomic determination

Samples for metabolomic profiling were collected in accordance with the same procedure as those for transcriptomic sequencing. Biological samples were freeze-dried, ground into powder, extracted with pre-cooled 70% methanolic aqueous internal standard extract, vortexed intermittently, centrifuged, filtered, and stored for UPLC-MS/MS (Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography-Electrospray Ionization-Tandem Mass Spectrometry) analysis [48]. Sample extracts were analyzed using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system equipped with an Agilent SB-C18 column. The mobile phase consisted of water (containing 0.1% formic acid) and acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid) with gradient elusion. The gradient was initiated at 95% water and 5% acetonitrile, linearly increased to 5% water and 95% acetonitrile within 9 min, held for 1 min, then returned to the initial ratio in 1.1 min, followed by re-equilibration for 2.9 min. The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min, column temperature was 40 °C, and injection volume was 2 µL, 2-chlorophenylalanine was used as an internal standard to verify the stability of the analytical procedure. The column effluent was directed to an ESI-QTRAP-MS for detection. ESI source parameters included source temperature 500 °C, ion spray voltage 5500 V (positive ion mode)/-4500 V (negative ion mode), ion source gas I (GSI) 50 psi, gas II (GSII) 60 psi, curtain gas (CUR) 25 psi, and high collision-activated dissociation (CAD); QQQ scans were performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with medium nitrogen collision gas, optimized decluttering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for individual transitions, and period-specific MRM transition monitoring based on eluted metabolites [49].

DAMs were screened based on Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values from the multivariate orthogonal partial least squares-discriminate analysis (OPLS-DA) model and P-values from the univariate Student’s t-test, retaining those with VIP ≥ 1 and P < 0.05 [50]. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed on the retained metabolites, and pairs with a significant Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC > 0.8, P < 0.05) were retained. Identified metabolites were annotated via the KEGG Compound database, mapped to the KEGG Pathway database, and pathways containing significantly regulated metabolites were subjected to MSEA with significance determined by hypergeometric test P-values.

Integrated analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics

The correlations between DEGs and DAMs were assessed using PCC. Prior to correlation analysis, a preliminary screening was performed: only DEGs with FPKM values greater than 100 and DAMs with peak areas greater than 500,000 were retained. Significantly associated gene-metabolite pairs (PCC > 0.8, P < 0.05) were selected for subsequent analysis. Following correlation analysis, a further screening step was implemented to filter the retained DEGs and DAMs, focusing on retaining only those with direct pairwise correlations to form the candidate molecular set for subsequent pathway construction. To gain a system-level view of the altered metabolic pathways, an integrated KEGG pathway map was constructed. First, all DEGs and DAMs mapped to the top 30 enriched KEGG pathways were extracted like Luo et al. [51]. These components were used to generate a composite pathway diagram that amalgamated the relevant sections from each individual KEGG pathway map [52]. Specifically, any molecule (gene or metabolite) that was not directly connected within a KEGG-defined biochemical reaction step to another molecule from the DEGs/DAMs list was removed from the diagram. This process yielded a final, streamlined core pathway network containing only directly interlinked DEGs and DAMs [53]. Finally, gene expression (FPKM values) and metabolite abundance (peak areas) for elements within this core network were extracted. To ensure clarity in visualization, metabolites with uniformly low peak areas across all samples were excluded. Heatmaps were generated based on this refined dataset to display the expression and accumulation patterns of the core molecular components.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (4.4.2). Two-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the population and resistance type and their interaction on the resistance index. After a significant interaction was detected, Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test was conducted for multiple pairwise comparisons among all combined groups. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 and adjusted P-values were reported to account for multiple resting. Bar charts were constructed using Origin (2019b).
