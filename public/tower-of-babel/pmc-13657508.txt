Exploring overweight and obesity among Pakistani university students: a nationwide prevalence and knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) assessment – a cross-sectional study
Authors: Omar Irfan, Salman Muhammad Soomar, Mohammad Aadil Qamar, Muhammad Bin Hammad, Babar Irfan, Muhammad Awais, Muhammad Omer Sultan, Abdul Waheed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Obesity, as a global epidemic disease, is widely distributed across all age groups and genders. Overweight and obesity are on the rise in low- and middle-income countries, with around 50% of the population being overweight or obese. Pakistan ranks tenth out of 188 countries worldwide in the prevalence of obesity. This study aims to investigate the knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding overweight and obesity among Pakistani undergraduate students.Methods:A cross-sectional survey was conducted from January to March 2025 among Pakistani undergraduate students. A validated questionnaire was disseminated online and in person, and the responses were recorded anonymously. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.Results:Of the 1086 participants, 164 (15.10%) were overweight, 42 (3.87%) were obese, and 10 (0.92%) were severely obese. Around 72% of the participants knew about obesity, with nearly half reporting an obese person in the family (n = 510, 47.89%), and having an obese person in the family was significantly associated with obesity among the participants (n = 130, 61.61%; P < 0.001). Males (n = 116, 54.21%) were more likely to be overweight or obese than females (n = 98, 45.79%; P < 0.001). Adults aged 26–30 years had a 4.73-fold higher risk of obesity (95% CI, 1.83–7.99; P = 0.001) compared with younger adults.Conclusion:This study highlighted an alarming trend of overweight and obesity among Pakistani undergraduate students, with associations observed between obesity and factors such as family history, age, gender, and unhealthy lifestyle practices.

Introduction

Obesity, as a global epidemic disease, is widely distributed among all age groups and genders[1]. In adults, the World Health Organization (WHO) defines a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 25–30 as “overweight” and a BMI greater than 30 as “obese”[2]. Overweight and obesity are risk factors for many of the leading causes of mortality globally, including cardiovascular diseases, stroke, diabetes, and cancer[3]. The 2021 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study estimated that obesity was an attributable risk factor for nearly 10% of total deaths in 2019[4,5]. Globally, from 1990 to 2022, both the number of deaths attributable to obesity and the prevalence of obesity have more than doubled[6]. Overweight and obesity are on the rise in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which now face a so-called double burden of malnutrition. With around 50% of the population being overweight or obese, Pakistan ranks tenth out of 188 countries for population prevalence of obesity worldwide[7]. As per the World Obesity Federation, around 5.4 million Pakistani school-aged children will be obese by 2030[7]. Unfortunately, Pakistan has yet to implement a national initiative or policy to reduce overweight and obesity in the population.

A recent WHO/Europe report described alarming disparities in the health of young people across the WHO European Region, highlighting unhealthy lifestyles including fatty diets and low physical activity, and rising rates of overweight and obesity, which are well-established risk factors for non-communicable diseases (NCDs), such as heart disease and diabetes[8]. Being overweight at a younger age affects immediate health and is associated with a higher risk and earlier onset of various NCDs. A cross-sectional survey, which assessed the prevalence of overweight and obesity and their associated factors among university students from 22 countries, reported that 22% of the participants were overweight or obese (24.7% men and 19.3% women)[9]. The literature demonstrates a paucity of data from Pakistan on the prevalence and risk factors of overweight and obesity overall, with few studies previously reporting a high prevalence (4%–16%) of obesity[10,11]. From a public health perspective, it is vital to synthesize up-to-date population-based morbidity data, especially in younger age groups, to plan and implement health promotion interventions in educational institutions and communities[12].

As current global statistics report an alarming prevalence of overweight and obesity across all age groups worldwide, especially in developing countries that face the public health burden of the double burden of malnutrition, it is critical to assess the prevalence and risk factors of overweight and obesity in the younger population. Recent global evidence further underscores the rapidly escalating burden of overweight and obesity, particularly among young adults and student populations. Contemporary systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated that the prevalence of overweight and obesity among university and medical students is increasing worldwide, with pooled estimates suggesting that nearly one-quarter of students are affected, reflecting a concerning upward trend over time[13]. Similarly, updated global and regional analyses indicate that obesity continues to rise sharply in LMICs, driven by urbanization, dietary transitions, and sedentary lifestyles, thereby exacerbating the double burden of malnutrition[14]. Evidence from South Asian populations also highlights a growing prevalence among younger age groups, emphasizing the need for timely, context-specific epidemiological data to guide prevention strategies[15]. Despite these advances, there remains limited recent multi-regional evidence on knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAPs) related to obesity among university students in Pakistan, further justifying the need for the present study.

Previous KAP studies on obesity conducted in Pakistan have largely been limited to single institutions, specific provinces, or selected urban populations, thereby restricting the generalizability of their findings. While these studies provide useful localized insights, they do not sufficiently account for the country’s broad sociodemographic and regional variances. There is a paucity of comprehensive national data on KAP patterns across provinces and population groups. Therefore, the current cross-sectional survey aimed to assess the prevalence of obesity and related KAPs among university students in Pakistan to help public health bodies identify younger groups who may be candidates for possible health interventions.

Methods

The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist[16] was used to enhance comprehensibility and ensure the completeness of our study. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[17,18].

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among the undergraduate university students at Pakistani educational institutions from 1 January 2025 to 1 March 2025, to assess the prevalence of overweight and obesity and the KAP regarding obesity and its risk factors.

Study population

The targeted population included adult Pakistani undergraduate university students who were at least 18 years of age. This study recruited participants from multiple provinces across Pakistan; however, the non-probability purposive sampling limited representativeness despite broad geographic coverage. The study’s goal was to strengthen external validity and generalizability of the findings to the larger Pakistani population by adding respondents from varied socioeconomic backgrounds.

Eligibility criteria

Undergraduate students enrolled in recognized Pakistani universities who were aged 18 years or older and able to provide informed consent were included, and both medical and nonmedical students were eligible. Postgraduate students and individuals unwilling to provide consent were excluded.

Sample size and sampling strategy

A non-probability purposive sampling approach was employed to recruit undergraduate students from multiple provinces in Pakistan. Although this strategy facilitated broad geographic inclusion and the feasibility of a cross-regional survey, it inherently carries a risk of selection bias and limits the strict representativeness of the overall university population.

The sample size was calculated using the formula 32 (15.20).

Considering the confidence level as 5% and power 80%, the Z2(a/2) was 1.96, 42.88% proportion/prevalence (P = 0.43, 1 − P = 0.57) of obesity among university students in Pakistan was used as reported previously[19], with a design effect of 1 and a precision of 2% (0.02). The calculated sample size was 1251. The final sample size was 1375 after adding 10% to account for non-response. The achieved sample size was 1086.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the prevalence of overweight and obesity, defined according to WHO BMI classification criteria. For analytical purposes, the overweight, obese, and severely obese categories were grouped as “obese,” while the underweight and normal BMI categories were grouped as “non-obese.” Secondary outcomes included the assessment of KAPs related to obesity, as well as the identification of sociodemographic and lifestyle factors associated with obesity.

Independent variables

Covariates included socio-demographic and clinical characteristics. Moreover, the prevalence and KAP related to obesity were further explored in the study.

Data-collection tool

The questionnaire was structured using the KAP framework, a widely utilized public health model that evaluates awareness (knowledge), perceptions (attitudes), and health-related behaviors (practices). This framework allows the assessment of whether adequate knowledge translates into favorable attitudes and healthier behaviors, thereby identifying gaps relevant to targeted intervention strategies. Knowledge scores were calculated by assigning one point to each correct response and zero to incorrect or “don’t know” responses. Scores were categorized as “good knowledge” (≥75% correct), “moderate knowledge” (50%–74%), and “poor knowledge” (<50%). Attitude and practice domains were assessed using Likert-scale items, with composite scores categorized into positive/negative attitudes and adequate/inadequate practices based on median-split thresholds.

Data collection and management

A self-administered questionnaire, designed by adapting previously validated questionnaires, was formulated as a tool for data collection[11,20,21]. In-person and online media were used to gather responses based on non-probability convenience sampling. The survey was distributed online using platforms such as Google Forms. The participants had to fill out a consent form before proceeding to answer the questionnaire. The questionnaire was in both English and Urdu. The 45-question survey was broken down into categories covering medical history, anthropometric measures (anthropometric data, including height and weight, were self-reported by participants), sociodemographic traits, knowledge of obesity, and attitudes and behaviors related to obesity. Sections of the questionnaire included open-ended questions, Likert-scale items, and multiple-response options per question.

Validity and reliability of the questionnaire

A pilot study involving 20 undergraduate students was conducted to assess the clarity, relevance, and comprehension of questionnaire items. Minor linguistic and structural modifications were made accordingly. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.86), indicating good reliability. Construct validity was further supported by domain-wise factor coherence, and internal consistency remained acceptable across KAP subdomains (α > 0.70). Convergent validity was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients between the domain scores, supporting acceptable construct validity of the instrument.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while skewed variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables, including the prevalence of obesity and related characteristics, were reported as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to examine associations between independent categorical variables and obesity status, gender, study program, and BMI. For multivariable analysis, a binary logistic regression model was employed to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), given the categorical nature of the outcome variable (obese vs non-obese). Multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values <5 indicating acceptable levels. Model adequacy and goodness-of-fit were assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1).

Results

Of the 1086 participants, the median (IQR) age was 22 (20–26) years, and the majority were female (n = 627, 58.27%) and currently enrolled in a medical-related degree program (n = 753, 69.34%). The majority of the participants were from Sindh Province (n = 721, 67.01%). Most participants were living with family (n = 767, 70.63%). Of the 1086 participants, the majority had a healthy weight (n = 686, 63.17%), 164 (15.10%) were overweight, 42 (3.87%) were obese, and 10 (0.92%) were severely obese (Fig. 1). Discussion

Summary of findings

The present study examines the prevalence and KAP regarding overweight and obesity among undergraduate university students in Pakistan. The findings revealed that one in every five students was either overweight or obese. The study showed adequate knowledge about overweight and obesity and their associated risk factors among the participants. Among the unhealthy lifestyle choices, having picky eating habits, eating desserts two to six times in the past 7 days, and surfing the internet and playing video games were commonly reported. Increasing age, being male, having an obese family member, and having breakfast on more than 5 days per week were significant correlates of being overweight or obese. The observed association between frequent breakfast consumption and obesity contradicts existing literature. This may reflect reverse causality, where individuals with obesity adopt healthier eating patterns, or reporting bias. Residual confounding, including dietary composition and total caloric intake, may also explain this finding.

Comparison of findings

The rise in obesity rates across all age groups worldwide highlights its growing significance as a public health issue. Previous studies on university students (18–26 years) also report a high prevalence of overweight and obesity. At a global level, studies from Colombia reported a prevalence of 12.4%–16.7%[22], Mexico had an even higher prevalence of 31.6%[23], while in Saudi Arabia it was as high as 47.9%[24]. In Africa, the highest prevalence was found in Egypt (25.3%–59.4%)[25,26], followed by South Africa (10.8%–24%)[27,28]. Regionally, among Asian countries, the prevalence was also high – Bangladesh (20.8%)[29], China (2.9%–14.3%)[30], India (11%–37.5%)[31,32]. In Pakistan, a wide range in the prevalence of overweight (9.0%–19.0%) and obesity (2.4%–17.0%) has been reported among university students (18–26 years)[33–39]. Our findings are consistent with previous studies, with the prevalence of overweight at 15.10% and obesity at 3.87%. However, of particular concern is the high prevalence of underweight individuals observed in our study (16.94%). These concerning findings highlight the double burden of obesity and malnutrition that Pakistan continues to face.

Recent systematic reviews and global analyses provide further context for interpreting these findings. A 2024 meta-analysis reported that approximately 24% of medical students globally are overweight or obese, with a consistent increase observed across study years, reinforcing the growing vulnerability of student populations[13]. Additionally, emerging evidence highlights that obesity trends are accelerating in LMICs due to rapid lifestyle transitions, including increased consumption of energy-dense diets and reduced physical activity[14]. Regional data from South Asia similarly demonstrate rising obesity prevalence among young populations, aligning with the patterns observed in this study[15]. These updated findings support the notion that obesity among university students is not only a localized concern but also part of a broader global public health challenge, thereby strengthening the relevance and timeliness of the present study.

Our study further demonstrated a good level of knowledge and a positive attitude toward obesity among participants. These findings are consistent with previous research among undergraduate students. For example, a study conducted in Malaysia found that 71.5% of students recognized BMI as an indicator of obesity, and 88.6% acknowledged the link between obesity and related diseases[40]. Although participants demonstrated generally adequate knowledge regarding obesity, this did not consistently translate into healthier lifestyle practices. This discrepancy suggests the presence of behavioral, environmental, or structural barriers influencing health-related decision-making, which warrants further investigation through longitudinal or mixed-method research designs.

Despite having adequate knowledge and a generally positive attitude toward obesity, our findings showed that students exhibited unhealthy lifestyle habits, with the majority frequently consuming desserts and fast food. Moreover, the most common leisure activities reported were browsing the internet and playing video games. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. A study of 244 medical students from Lahore, reporting an obesity prevalence of 21%, found that 80.7% of students did not participate in any sports activities in college. The daily median time spent watching television or working on the computer was 120 minutes, and only 28.7% of students had regular physical activity [34]. Additionally, our study found that male sex was associated with higher rates of obesity, although the published literature reports mixed results[41,42].

The health consequences of obesity in young adulthood can include physical and psychosocial comorbidities[43,44]. These often persist and worsen into adulthood. Managing these conditions in young adults is particularly challenging, as symptoms may be minimal or health may not be prioritized due to competing life demands[45]. The presence of negative lifestyle habits within an educated and health-aware population is particularly concerning. The exclusion of uneducated youth in Pakistan from this study also creates an opportunity for future studies targeting this specific population to explore the risk exposures and behaviors that might have been different from those identified among our participants[46,47].

Cultural dietary practices in Pakistan, including high consumption of carbohydrate-rich and fried foods, may contribute to observed patterns of obesity. Additionally, socioeconomic disparities affect access to healthy food options and opportunities for physical activity. Gender differences observed in this study may reflect sociocultural norms affecting mobility, dietary behaviors, and health awareness. These findings can be interpreted through behavioral frameworks such as the Health Belief Model, where knowledge alone may not translate into behavior change due to perceived barriers and social norms. Furthermore, the Social Ecological Model highlights the role of interpersonal, institutional, and environmental factors influencing obesity-related behaviors.

Public health implications and future directions

Our results indicate that young males and students with an obese family member are more likely to be obese than their counterparts. The identification of these high-risk groups helps educators and policymakers in devising educational content and policies more focused on these groups to reduce overall obesity prevalence. The Pakistan Multi-Sectoral Nutrition Strategy (PMNS) 2018–2025, which was developed by the Ministry of Planning, Development & Reform in collaboration with the World Food Program, provides a framework to address malnutrition, including obesity. It emphasizes multi-sectoral collaboration to improve nutrition across population groups, with a focus on the most marginalized and disadvantaged [48]. Public health initiatives in Pakistan have faced several challenges, including government instability, a lack of funding, and a lack of government interest, which have hindered their successful implementation.

Introducing education related to stress management, cognitive behavioral therapy, and access to healthier food options is needed to ensure that both the mental and physical well-being of undergraduate students are supported. Lastly, a strong correlation among physical inactivity, a sedentary lifestyle, and obesity has been highlighted, given the prevalence of technology[49]. Implementation of behaviors at the micro-level, such as limiting screen time, promoting active leisure (e-sports tournaments paired with physical activities and on-campus challenges), and active transportation (cycling, walking) both within university campuses and to and from them, is needed to promote a healthier lifestyle[50].

Given that this study found a higher percentage of obesity in participants who had an obese family member, the extension of health promotion to families is imperative, considering that family practices related to cooking and eating behaviors influence obesity among our participants. Furthermore, the introduction of food labeling related to contents, nutrition assistance programs, balanced meals, and discouragement of unhealthy foods is needed as one of the ways to counter obesity at the national level[51]. In 2016, Chile enacted the Law of Food Labeling and Advertising to combat childhood obesity. This law mandated front-of-package warning labels on foods high in calories, sugar, sodium, or saturated fat[52]. Effective 1 March 2023, and as advocated by the Pakistan National Heart Association (PANAH), the national government increased the excise tax on carbonated drinks from 20% to 13% and also introduced a 10% tax on sweetened juices and concentrates, aiming to tackle the rising consumption of high-sugar drinks[53].

Moreover, future research incorporating qualitative methodologies grounded in established behavioral frameworks, such as the Health Belief Model or the Social Ecological Model, may provide deeper insight into the cultural, familial, and environmental determinants influencing obesity-related behaviors. Such approaches could inform the development of tailored, context-specific intervention strategies.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides a comprehensive and timely outlook on the KAP of Pakistani undergraduate students regarding obesity. Firstly, the use of robust data collection tools, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86, and advanced statistical analysis methods, such as multivariable regression, ensure that the identification of high-risk groups in this study is based on statistical strength. The large and diverse sample, spanning different age groups and regions of Pakistan, makes the results more generalizable.

Apart from its strengths, a few limitations should be noted while interpreting the study findings. First, the cross-sectional design meant that a temporal or causal relationship could not be established. Second, the predominance of participants from Sindh (approximately 67%) and medical disciplines (approximately 70%) may have introduced selection bias and limited extrapolation to the broader university population. The predominance of medical students may also have led to overestimation of knowledge levels compared to the general student population. Anthropometric measurements were self-reported, introducing potential information bias. Participants may underreport weight or overreport height due to social desirability or recall bias, potentially resulting in underestimation of BMI and misclassification across weight categories. Such misclassification may attenuate true prevalence estimates and weaken observed associations between obesity and related factors. The use of non-probability purposive sampling limits the ability to draw fully generalizable conclusions about all Pakistani university students. Additionally, the disproportionate representation of participants from Sindh province may have influenced regional comparability. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolating to the broader national student population. Self-reported data can lead to under- or overestimated values, which result in inaccurate associations derived from the study. Medical training likely enhances awareness of obesity definitions, risk factors, and complications, potentially inflating overall knowledge scores compared to the general university population. Consequently, the observed KAP levels may not fully represent students from non-medical academic disciplines. Many of the participants were young and in their most productive years; therefore, the prevalence of obesity was also low.

Conclusion

The study shows that one in five undergraduate students at Pakistani universities is either overweight or obese. Notable associations were observed between obesity and factors such as family history, age, gender, and lifestyle practices. The presence of adequate knowledge did not translate into consistent attitudes and practices. Targeted health education and intervention programs directed toward high-risk groups identified in this study are warranted. Further studies are recommended to identify additional high-risk strata in the population for evidence-based targeted interventions.
