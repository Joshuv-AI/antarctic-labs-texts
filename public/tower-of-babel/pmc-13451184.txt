Seroincidence of Brucella spp. infection among humans and livestock in a pastoralist setting in Northern Kenya
Authors: Athman Mwatondo, Mathew Muturi, James M Akoko, Richard Nyamota, Daniel Nthiwa, Stephen Gichuhi, Marianne W Mureithi, Bernard Bett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrucellosis is one of the most widespread zoonoses globally. The disease is associated with significant human illnesses and livestock production losses, particularly among pastoralist communities. Despite its recognition as a significant zoonotic disease, reliable estimates of the health burden of brucellosis in humans and animals are limited. This study aimed to determine the annual seroincidence of Brucella spp. in humans and animals in a pastoralist community and to explore risk factors associated with exposure. We conducted a one-year, household-based longitudinal study in Isiolo County, northern Kenya. Households within a 20 km radius of Kinna town were eligible for enrollment if they owned at least one livestock species (cattle, goats, or sheep). All consenting humans and five animals per species were included. Quarterly blood sampling was performed, and Brucella immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies were detected using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA). A structured questionnaire was administered to collect data on potential risk factors for human and animal exposures. An incident case was defined as any human or animal that seroconverted during follow-up. Counts of incident cases were used to estimate exact incidence rates, with human- and animal-years at risk serving as the denominators. The data were analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards model, which allowed for type I right censoring. Crude and adjusted hazard rate ratios were estimated using univariable and multivariable models. The study enrolled 814 individuals (median age, 21.5 years; range, 2–90 years). A total of 1938 animals were recruited, comprising 687 cattle (35.5%), 622 sheep (32.1%), and 629 goats (32.4%). We identified 25 human incident cases over 435 person-years, resulting in an incidence rate of 0.057 (95% CI 0.035–0.080). The standard multivariable Cox regression analysis (with right censoring) revealed significant associations with age (p = 0.005), placenta removal (p = 0.031), and the occurrence of an incident case in livestock (p = 0.026). We identified 22 animal incidents across 1143 animal years, yielding an annual incidence of 0.019 cases per year (95% CI 0.011–0.027). Incidence was higher among younger animals and goats. The population-based incidence of Brucella spp. in humans and livestock indicates active transmission in this pastoralist community. Age, placenta removal, and household livestock infection were independently associated with human seroconversion, while goats and younger animals had significantly higher exposure rates than other livestock species and older animals, respectively. This highlights the human–livestock interface as a key pathway for transmission. Introduction

Brucellosis is one of the most widespread zoonoses globally, and it is associated with significant human illnesses and livestock production losses, particularly in endemic areas1,2. The main Brucella species that can infect livestock and humans are B. abortus, B. Melitensis, B. suis, and B. Canis3,4. The disease primarily affects domestic animals and wildlife species. Human infections occur through direct contact with infected animals, eating or drinking contaminated animal products, or inhaling aerosolized Brucella3,5. Consumption of unpasteurized milk or milk products is identified as the leading cause of human brucellosis6,7.

The clinical presentation of human brucellosis is highly variable and nonspecific, with symptoms varying depending on the stage of the disease and the specific organs or systems involved8,9. The common symptoms include undulating fevers, chills, night sweats, arthralgia, headache, fatigue, anorexia, myalgia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and weight loss9. The variable clinical presentation and prolonged incubation period present a challenge to a timely and accurate diagnosis of the disease10. Additionally, these nonspecific symptoms overlap clinically with those of other common febrile illnesses, such as malaria, leading to under-recognition and misdiagnosis of brucellosis and contributing to delayed treatment and continued transmission9.

Additionally, up to 40% of infected individuals in high-risk zones may present with asymptomatic or subclinical disease, which may delay the diagnosis and treatment of such individuals to facilitate rapid recovery and help prevent complications or sequelae11,12. Abortions, reproductive failure, and infertility characterize the disease in animals13,14. Infected animals often do not fully recover but instead develop subclinical, subacute, or chronic infections. These animals may intermittently shed bacteria through their milk and birth products, even in the absence of clinical signs, contaminating the environment and posing a risk to other animals and humans14.

Despite its global impact, accurately quantifying the burden of human and animal brucellosis remains a challenge15. Current estimates suggest an annual burden ranging from 1.6 to 2.1 million new human cases, significantly higher than previous estimates of 500,000 cases yearly1,16. Asia and Africa carry the heaviest burdens, accounting for approximately 1.2 to 1.6 million and 0.5 million cases, respectively. However, the reliability of these estimates is compromised by reliance on inadequate and incomplete data sources, such as reports to public health authorities or research studies of limited scope17,18.

Brucellosis is prioritized for control and elimination as a public health threat in Kenya19. The disease is particularly prevalent in the semi-arid or arid agro-ecological zones, where pastoralism is the primary livelihood20. Prevalence studies conducted in these high-risk zones have reported varying exposure levels: 10%-54% in apparently healthy individuals and an average of 8.2% in livestock in East Africa, with fluctuations from 3.4% to 22.3% observed over time and across geographic regions21–25.

Cross-sectional seroprevalence studies provide valuable insights into cumulative exposure and disease burden, particularly in endemic settings where brucellosis may persist as a chronic infection; however, they capture only a single time point and cannot distinguish recent from past infections or characterize incidence, seroconversion, and evolving risk factors15,26. Longitudinal designs address these limitations by enabling assessment of disease incidence, seasonal variation, and transmission dynamics, while supporting identification of predictors of infection. Due to cost constraints, few longitudinal studies have been conducted in Kenya27–30. Most human studies are conducted in health facilities, relying on individuals seeking medical care; therefore, they may miss asymptomatic or subclinical cases, particularly in endemic areas11,12.

To address these gaps and enhance our understanding of the burden of brucellosis in humans and livestock in Kenya, we conducted a household-based longitudinal linked study in a community setting in Kinna, Isiolo County, northern Kenya. This study aimed to determine the annual incidence of exposures to Brucella spp. in humans and animals, and to explore risk factors for seroconversion. The goal was to provide more reliable estimates of the disease burden, enhance understanding of transmission dynamics, and inform strategies and interventions for brucellosis control.

Study methods

Study site

This study was conducted in Kinna Ward in Isiolo County, northern Kenya (Fig. 1). The area was purposively selected as part of a more extensive investigation of co-infections with Rift Valley fever virus, Brucella spp., and Coxiella burnetii in humans and animals (including wildlife). Previous studies in the area indicate a high prevalence of Brucella spp. in humans and animals, with a seroprevalence of 47.2% (39.9–54.9) in humans and 12.7% (10.3–15.4) in livestock in 202124,30. Additionally, knowledge of brucellosis has been found to be low to moderate in the study setting, with gaps in understanding transmission, recognizing clinical signs, and implementing preventive practices31–33.

Map of Kenya and Isiolo county showing sampling sites around Kinna settlement (The underlying map layer used to generate this figure was obtained from https://www.diva-gis.org/gdata).

Kinna is a rural township in Garbatula Subcounty, Isiolo County, with a projected human population of 12,178 as of 202234. The area has a hot and dry climate for most of the year, with a mean annual temperature of 29 °C and an average annual rainfall of 580 mm, ranging from 350 to 600 mm. Long rains typically occur from March to May, while short rains occur from October to December. Pastoralism and small-scale businesses are the mainstay of the people within and around Kinna township35,36. The area is served by Kinna Health Centre, the main public health facility.

Study design

We conducted a one-year prospective, household-based longitudinal cohort study to estimate the seroincidence of Brucella spp. and identify associated risk factors in humans and livestock.

Sample size calculation in humans

For both humans and livestock, the sample size calculation was based on the methods by Lwanga and Lemeshow to estimate an incidence rate with specified relative precision37

where: Z value for 95% confidence level = 1.96, α = significance level = 0.05, ε is the relative precision = 10%, giving a minimum sample size of 385 humans. A priori, a design effect of 1.5 and a 10% loss to follow-up were accounted for, resulting in a minimum sample size of 643 participants.

Sample size calculation in livestock

The initial animal sample size was determined using the same formula as for humans, yielding a total of 385 animals. The sample size was multiplied by a design effect of two to account for cluster sampling. The sample size was adjusted by 50% to account for loss to follow-up, resulting in a minimum sample size of 1152 cattle, sheep, and goats. The proportion of each species in the sample size was determined by the average population of each species in herds in Isiolo County, obtained from the cross-sectional study24,38.

Recruitment of humans and livestock cohorts

We selected households for enrollment within a 20 km radius of Kinna town. A household was defined as a family using a common cooking area. Households were eligible for enrollment if they owned at least one livestock species (cattle, goats, or sheep).

We applied a two-tier inclusion approach to identify and recruit study humans and livestock (cattle, sheep, and goats) from participating herds. First, a sampling frame of livestock-owning households in each village was developed with guidance from village elders, including information on livestock species and herd sizes. Households were then randomly selected from this sampling frame, as described by Muturi et al.39.

Human participants were eligible if they had resided in the study area for at least six months and were older than two years. Children under 2 years of age were excluded due to sampling challenges and community preferences regarding participation of very young children. Each household member was assigned a personal identification (ID) number. A baseline structured questionnaire was administered to the head of each household or their proxy to collect information on family structure, livestock ownership, demographic characteristics, and exposure variables for each household member who consented and was eligible. Potential risk factors assessed included animal contact, handling of birthing materials, consumption of milk and other raw animal products, livestock management practices, and herd health history. The questionnaire was developed based on established brucellosis risk factors reported in the literature and refined for the local context. It was pretested before implementation and administered via face-to-face interviews by trained study staff to ensure consistency and data quality. A blood sample was then collected from each study participant.

Five animals per species (sheep, cattle, and goats) were selected from each herd using systematic random sampling, except in herds with fewer than five animals, where all animals were included. Animals younger than one month were excluded due to sampling constraints and community preferences regarding the handling of very young animals. All selected animals were tagged with pre-printed ear tags, and a blood sample was collected from each.

Follow-up of recruited humans and livestock

Households were visited every 3–4 months for repeat sampling until the end of the 1-year follow-up period. During each visit, the household head and all recruited individuals consented to the administration of a semistructured questionnaire and the collection of blood samples for serological testing. The questionnaire gathered data on individual demographics, roles in livestock care, consumption and handling of animals and animal products, and health-seeking behaviour.

Livestock recruited into the study were followed for 1 year and sampled concurrently with the human participants. All recruited animals underwent clinical examination at each visit; to assess their general health status. Blood samples were collected, and a questionnaire was administered to the household head to obtain data on potential risk factors.

To improve retention in the study, a qualified veterinary officer was based in the study area, and two clinical officers were stationed at the local health facility. The veterinary officer responded to farmers’ requests to examine animals, provide treatment, and offer veterinary advice. In addition, the clinical officers provided free consultations at the health facility, including diagnosis and treatment for brucellosis.

Sample collection, handling, and transport

Qualified and trained personnel collected samples from humans and animals. Blood samples were collected and processed using the same methods as reported in a related study implemented in the same area 24. Each follow-up sampling round took approximately 3–4 weeks . To preserve sample integrity, blood samples collected from humans and livestock were stored immediately after collection in barcoded vacutainers placed in cool boxes with ice packs (4–8 °C) during fieldwork. Samples were transported daily to local health and veterinary laboratories for serum separation and temporary storage at − 20 °C. A backup generator was available at the field laboratory to maintain uninterrupted freezer storage during power outages. Serum samples were subsequently transported every 5 days under maintained cold chain conditions (− 20 °C) using motorized freezers to the International Livestock Research Institute laboratories in Nairobi for serological analysis.

Laboratory testing

The sera samples from this study were screened for anti-brucella antibodies using the same approach described in our previous study24. Briefly, all livestock sera samples were tested using the ID Screen Brucellosis Serum Indirect Multispecies kit (Grabels, France), which detects anti-brucella IgG antibodies in bovine, ovine, caprine, and porcine samples. Briefly, all livestock serum samples were tested using the ID Screen® Brucellosis Serum Indirect Multispecies kit (Grabels, France), which detects anti-Brucella IgG antibodies in bovine, ovine, caprine, and porcine samples. This assay has been widely applied in the region and has demonstrated high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in field settings24,40,41. All human serum samples were screened for anti-Brucella IgG antibodies using IBL-America IgG enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (IBL-America, Minneapolis, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions42. Briefly, the samples were diluted (1:100) using the kit-supplied sample diluent, loaded onto antigen pre-coated microtitre plates, and incubated for 1 h. The microtiter plates were washed, and the kit-supplied, ready-to-use conjugate was added. A 30-min incubation was then performed. Lastly, the plates were washed before the para-nitrophenyl Phosphate (pNPP) substrate was added, and then incubated for 30 min. The conjugate–substrate reaction was then terminated using a stop solution supplied by the kit. The ODs were recorded at 405 nm against a substrate blank, and 630 nm was used as a reference wavelength to correct the background fluorescence. All incubations were performed at 37 °C; wash steps preceded both conjugate and substrate additions, and all sera and controls were run in duplicate. The OD results interpretations and validations for each test plate were done as previously described24.

Data management and statistical analysis

All questionnaires were stored in the ODK application, and data were entered electronically. All data were stored in a secure database at the ILRI data center, accessible only to the principal investigator and co-investigators.

Data were downloaded into Microsoft Excel, cleaned, and imported into R statistical software, version 4.3.2, for analysis43. Descriptive analyses were performed to calculate the seroprevalence of Brucella spp. At each sampling interval, the CrossTable command in the gmodels package33 and the epiR package34 were used to calculate its 95% confidence interval (CI).

We developed a code to process data and identify new cases. Its output was a data frame with multiple records per subject, containing subject-, household-, and area-level characteristics. First, a dummy variable was generated to determine whether a subject was positive at the time of recruitment. This was used to implement left censoring. Secondly, another dummy variable was created to determine how long each subject was at risk. The time-at-risk for subjects exposed during the follow-up period ended when they were exposed to the pathogen. For those who remained unexposed, their time-at-risk extended to either the end of the follow-up period or the time they were lost to follow-up. Lastly, subject-specific time-at-risk (in years) was calculated as the difference between the recruitment date and the end-of-follow-up date.

The total number of new cases of Brucella spp. observed during the follow-up period, as well as the total human and animal time at risk, were used to derive incidence rates and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) using the epi.conf function from the epiR package44. These rates were further stratified by species, sex, and age.

We used Cox proportional hazard models to identify risk factors for Brucella spp. incidence in humans and animals45,46. It was implemented using the coxph function47, assuming that the event time occurred at the end of a sampling interval48. The model included household ID as a random-effects variable to account for potential clustering of observations at the household/herd level. The proportional hazards assumption was tested for each covariate and for the overall model using the cox.zph function.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of (a) loss to follow-up and (b) misclassification of the exposure on the observed baseline Brucella incidence and the robustness of the estimates of the Cox proportional hazards model. The best and worst-case analysis approach was used to determine the impact of censoring. For analyses on loss to follow-up, the best-worst case scenario approach was used. The best-case scenario assumed that censored participants remained uninfected throughout the study period, whereas the worst-case scenario assumed exposure occurred immediately after dropout.

Two alternative exposure scenarios were tested in the worst-case analysis. The most conservative and extreme scenario assumed that all the human and animal subjects that dropped out were exposed upon exiting the study. The other approach used estimates from a seroprevalence study conducted earlier in the same study area as exposure probabilities24. The binomial distribution was therefore used to identify subjects classified as exposed (1) or non-exposed (0).

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the Institutional Research Ethics Committee at the International Livestock Research Institute (reference number: ILRI-IREC2020-07) and the KNH-UoN Ethics and Research Committee (Approval number: P535/07/2021). Household heads provided written consent for livestock sampling, and each adult participant provided written informed consent for human sampling. For children aged 13–17, written assent forms and parental/guardian permissions were obtained; for those under 13, only written parental/guardian permissions were obtained. The human and animal sampling and data collection were performed in accordance with the standard operating procedures and guidelines outlined in the ethical approval. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org), in line with the editorial policies of Scientific Reports.

Results

Descriptive results

The study enrolled 814 participants from 140 households, with a median household size of five people (range 1–16) and a median age of 21.5 years (range 2–90). Recruitment was conducted in March/April 2022 (530 participants, 65%) and June/July 2022 (284 participants, 35%). Males comprised the majority, with 481 participants (59.1%). Most participants were primarily involved in off-farm activities (465; 57.1%), and most had not completed formal education (534; 65.6%).

The prevalence in humans was 35.6% (95% CI 32.3–39.1) at enrolment, and it steadily declined to 17.6% (95% CI 14.8–20.8) in the final sampling (Table 1).

A total of 1938 animals were recruited into the study. Of these, 1521 (78.5%) were recruited in March and April 2022, and 417 (21.5%) in July 2022. The animals comprised 687 cattle (35.5%), 622 sheep (32.1%), and 629 goats (32.4%). Female animals made up the majority, at 1638 (84.5%). The seroprevalence in livestock ranged from 3.8% (95% CI 3.0–4.8) at enrolment to 2.5% (95% CI 1.6–3.3) in the final sampling. Species-level prevalence was highest in cattle at 4.4% (95% CI 3.6–5.3), followed by goats at 2.8% (95% CI 2.0–3.5), and lowest in sheep at 0.7% (95% CI 0.4–1.2). The prevalence in livestock showed minimal fluctuations over time (Table 1).

By the last sampling, the overall loss to follow-up in livestock was 43.9% (850/1938), with the highest loss observed in sheep at 50.8% (316/622). Loss in goats was 45.8% (288/629), while cattle had the lowest loss at 35.8% (246/687). In humans, the loss to follow-up was minimal at 14.0% (114/814).

Incidence of Brucella spp. exposure in humans

Overall, a total of 25 new human cases from 21 households were observed over 435 person-years. This resulted in an incidence rate of 0.057 (95% CI 0.035–0.080), equivalent to 6 new human exposures per 100 person-years. The incidence rate was higher in males (0.07; 95% CI 0.04–0.12) than in females (0.04; 95% CI 0.01–0.07). It was also higher among those working on farms (0.09; 95% CI 0.06–0.15) than those employed off-farm (0.02; 95% CI 0.01–0.05).

When stratified by age, the incidence was higher in adults over 30 years (0.12; 95% CI 0.07–0.19) than in younger individuals (of < 30 years). Notably, the incidence was higher in those with no formal education and decreased with higher levels of education (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis findings

Sensitivity analyses showed that the parameter estimates of the Cox proportional hazards model remained consistent across the scenarios that were used to analyse the potential effect of censoring. and diagnostic misclassification, indicating that the primary findings were robust (Supplementary Tables S1–3).

Discussion

This study estimated the incidence of Brucella spp. exposure in humans and livestock using a One Health framework to characterize transmission at the human–livestock interface. To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal study in northern Kenya to simultaneously estimate seroincidence in humans and multiple livestock species within the same households. Incidence was moderately high in both populations, with human rates approximately threefold higher than those in livestock. Age, involvement in the removal of retained placenta in livestock, and the presence of an incident case in livestock within a household were identified as significant predictors of seroincidence in humans, underscoring the value of integrated human–animal surveillance for understanding transmission in pastoralist settings.

Over one-third of participants were seropositive at baseline; however, this proportion declined steadily during follow-up, whereas livestock prevalence remained relatively stable, suggesting persistent circulation of the pathogen within animal populations. The decline in human seroprevalence may reflect seasonal variation in transmission49. The study coincided with a severe drought characterized by high temperatures and low precipitation, environmental conditions known to influence brucellosis occurrence49,50. Reduced milk production and breeding may have lowered consumption-related and direct-contact risks, while migration in search of pasture and water likely reduced herd sizes, thereby lowering human exposure risk. Larger herd sizes have previously been identified as a risk factor for Brucella seroprevalence due to increased opportunities for transmission24,51. Migration-related herd reductions may therefore have contributed to lower exposure risk, particularly among household members not directly involved in animal care, and may partly explain why herd size was not associated with seroconversion in this study. Collectively, these findings highlight the value of longitudinal designs in capturing transmission dynamics that may follow seasonal patterns even in endemic settings49,52.

Although the study area has historically reported high Brucella prevalence24,30,33, the observed incidence of 5.7 per 100 person-years indicates ongoing, but relatively stable, transmission, consistent with endemic persistence. Notably, cumulative incidence exceeded clinical estimates by more than 50-fold, likely reflecting methodological differences between our population-based serological approach and facility-based surveillance that primarily captures symptomatic infections27,53. This suggests that reliance on clinical surveillance alone may substantially underestimate the true burden of brucellosis in endemic regions and highlights the need for integrated surveillance strategies that capture both clinical and subclinical infections and address high-risk exposures at the human–livestock interface.

We observed a threefold higher incidence in humans than in livestock, consistent with prior prevalence studies from Isiolo and neighboring Marsabit counties24,54. This pattern likely reflects intense human–animal contact in pastoralist systems, where exposure occurs through direct handling of livestock and consumption of contaminated animal products. Shared consumption of unpasteurized milk from a single infected animal may facilitate within-household transmission, potentially explaining the observed association between incident animal cases and human seroconversion. This co-occurrence suggests household-level clustering and is epidemiologically consistent with shared exposure pathways at the human–livestock interface reported in previous studies15,24,54. Collectively, these findings highlight households as critical targets for interventions in endemic settings.

In multivariable analysis, increasing age, removal of retained placenta in livestock, and residence in households with incident animal cases were significantly associated with human seroconversion. The higher risk observed among adults likely reflects cumulative participation in high-risk activities, including animal husbandry and handling of animal products. In pastoralist communities, adults are more likely to consume unpasteurized milk and assist with animal births, both recognized pathways for Brucella transmission3,15,22. In contrast, younger children may have lower exposure risk, potentially reflecting household feeding practices, such as the consumption of boiled milk and cooked foods, as well as limited participation in livestock-related activities55,56. Handling placental materials known to harbor high concentrations of Brucella represents a well-established occupational hazard, particularly when performed without appropriate protective measures57,58.

Incidence was highest among goats and younger animals. Goats are recognized as highly susceptible to Brucella melitensis, and their shorter reproductive cycles may facilitate transmission. Although adult animals often exhibit higher seroprevalence due to cumulative exposure20,24. The elevated incidence observed in young stock likely reflects immunological susceptibility to primary infection following initial contact with contaminated environments59. These findings suggest that young animals may serve as useful sentinels for detecting ongoing transmission in endemic settings.

Camels were not included in this study despite evidence linking them to higher Brucella prevalence, particularly when co-herded with infected small ruminants60. Given the importance of camel milk during dry periods, the camel milk value chain may be a significant pathway for human exposure61–63. The influence of camels on transmission dynamics in this setting, therefore, warrants further investigation.

Limitations

One limitation of our study is the inherent variability in the sensitivity and specificity of serological assays such as ELISA, particularly in endemic settings. Combined with potential cross-reactivity with closely related pathogens, this may increase the risk of false-positive results. The absence of confirmatory testing using more specific assays, such as competitive ELISA (cELISA), may have influenced the estimated seroprevalence. Sensitivity analyses showed that the effect estimates remained consistent under plausible diagnostic assumptions.

Our study also experienced loss to follow-up, particularly among livestock. Nearly half of the goats and sheep were lost during follow-up, compared with approximately one-third of cattle and humans, reflecting typical dynamics in pastoralist systems, where small stock are frequently sold or consumed while cattle are retained longer. Because attrition may introduce bias if related to infection risk, we calculated animal-time at risk using only the period during which animals remained in the cohort, and we increased the sample size to offset differential losses. Sensitivity analyses produced effect estimates comparable to those from the primary analysis, suggesting that attrition did not meaningfully influence the study’s conclusions.

Finally, this study was conducted within a geographically restricted region of northern Kenya over a one-year period. As a result, the findings may not be generalizable to national populations or to settings with substantially different risk profiles for Brucella infection in humans and livestock. Additionally, transmission dynamics may vary across years, potentially limiting the temporal generalizability of the results.

Conclusion and recommendations

Conclusion

This study provides the first population-level estimates of Brucella seroincidence in humans and livestock in northern Kenya, demonstrating ongoing transmission in pastoralist settings. Associations with animal exposures and household infection highlight the importance of the human–livestock interface, while higher incidence among goats and young animals suggests their potential value as sentinel populations for monitoring transmission. Linking human and animal investigations of brucellosis seropositivity further enhances collaboration between human and animal health practitioners.
