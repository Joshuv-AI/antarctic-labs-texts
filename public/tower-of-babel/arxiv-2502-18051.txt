Unified χ 2 \chi^{2} discriminators for gravitational wave searches from compact coalescing binaries
Sanjeev Dhurandhar

Abstract
Gravitational wave (GW) signals of astrophysical origin are typically weak. This is because gravity is a weak force, weakest among the four forces we know of. In order to detect GW signals, one must make differential measurements of effective lengths less than thousand’th of the size of a proton. In spite of the detectors achieving extraordinary sensitivity, the detector noise typically overwhelms the signal, so that GW signals are deeply buried in the data. The challenge to the data analyst is of extracting the GW signal from the noise, that is, first deciding whether a signal is present or not then if present, measuring its parameters. However, in the search for coalescing compact binary (CBC) signals, short-duration non-Gaussian noise transients (glitches) in the detector data significantly affect the search sensitivity. Chi-squared discriminators are therefore employed to mitigate their effect. We show that the underlying mathematical structure of any χ 2 \chi^{2} is a vector bundle over the signal manifold 𝒫 {\cal P} , that is, the manifold traced out by the signal waveforms in the Hilbert space of data segments 𝒟 {\cal D} . The χ 2 \chi^{2} is then defined as the square of the L 2 L_{2} norm of the data vector projected onto a finite-dimensional subspace 𝒮 {\cal S} (fibre) of 𝒟 {\cal D} chosen orthogonal to the triggered template waveform. Any such fibre leads to a χ 2 \chi^{2} discriminator and the full vector bundle comprising the subspaces 𝒮 {\cal S} and the base manifold 𝒫 {\cal P} constitute the χ 2 \chi^{2} discriminator. We show that this structure paves the way for constructing effective χ 2 \chi^{2} discriminators against different morphologies of glitches. Here we specifically demonstrate our method on blip glitches, which can be modelled as sine-Gausians, which then generates an optimal χ 2 \chi^{2} statistic for blip glitches.

However, the data in the ground-based detectors is neither Gaussian nor stationary; non-Gaussian and non-stationary features can be produced by various components of the detector itself or by the environment. In this article, we will only consider CBC signals emitted by a pair consisting of either black holes or neutron stars. Further, the signals can be adequately modeled by using either post-Newtonian approximation methods, perturbation techniques, or numerical relativity. In the case of adequately modelled signals, the preferred data analysis technique is matched filtering. However, the signals depend on several parameters, such as the masses, time of coalescence, etc., and then it becomes necessary to employ a bank of templates densely covering the parameter space. II
The context

In this section we describe the matched filtering technique and how it is employed in the detection of CBC signals. We then discuss the statistical method of deciding on detection and significance. The treatment here follows the frequentist approach. Currently, Bayesian methods are in vogue. However, we do not discuss them here. In fact, gravitational wave astronomy is in its infancy, and it seems somewhat premature to choose priors; we have to rely on our knowledge gained from electromagnetic astronomy, and it could give a misleading picture. The gravitational wave universe could be far different from the universe as we know it from other astronomical windows.

II.1
The matched filtering paradigm

Consider a data train
x(t)
defined over a time interval
[0,T]
. The data trains form an infinite dimensional vector space, which we call
{\cal D}
. The data trains as vectors in
{\cal D}
will be denoted in boldface
x(t)\longrightarrow{\bf x}
. Let
n(t)
be the noise in the detector, a stochastic process defined over
[0,T]
; we assume that it has an ensemble mean zero and is stationary in the wide sense, that is, its first two moments are stationary - they do not depend on the absolute time. A given noise realisation is a vector
{\bf n}\in{\cal D}
- it is a random vector. We denote its power spectral density (PSD) by
S_{h}(f)
as is prevalent in the literature; the subscript
h
has been introduced, indicating the detector’s signal channel. The signal is the gravitational wave strain, denoted by
h(t)
. We also state that
S_{h}(f)
is a one-sided PSD - the negative frequencies are folded onto positive frequencies - it is defined only for positive frequencies. Now we consider two data trains
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
; their scalar product is written conveniently in the Fourier domain. Let
\tilde{x}(f)
and
\tilde{y}(f)
be the Fourier representations of
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
, then the scalar product is given by:

({\bf x},{\bf y})=4\Re\int_{f_{\rm lower}}^{f_{\rm upper}}~df\frac{\tilde{x}^{*}(f)\tilde{y}(f)}{S_{h}(f)}\,,

(1)

where the integration is carried out over the band-width
[f_{\rm lower},f_{\rm upper}]
. This construction makes the space
{\cal D}
into a Hilbert space - a
L_{2}
space with measure
d\mu\equiv df/S_{h}(f)
. Then
{\cal D}=L_{2}([0,T],\mu)
. This scalar product can be used to normalise vectors in
{\cal D}
;
\|{\bf x}\|=+\sqrt{({\bf x},{\bf x})}
.

We think of a signal
{\bf s}
as just an amplitude
A
multiplying a normalized waveform
{\bf h}
, where
\|{\bf h}\|=1
; thus,
{\bf s}=A{\bf h}
. The data vector, which we denote by
{\bf x}
, in additive noise is then
{\bf x}={\bf s}+{\bf n}
when a signal is present, while in the absence of a signal, it is just noise, i.e.,
{\bf x}={\bf n}
. The match
c
(correlation) is the scalar product between the data
{\bf x}
and a (normalized) template
{\bf h}
, that is,
c=({\bf x},{\bf h})
.
Eq.(
1
) of the scalar product turns this operation into a matched filter because,

c=4\Re\int_{f_{\rm lower}}^{f_{\rm upper}}~df~\tilde{x}^{*}(f)\tilde{q}(f)\,,

(2)

where
\tilde{q}(f)={\tilde{h}}(f)/S_{h}(f)
where we have the usual
L_{2}
scalar product in Eq. (
2
). The quantity
\tilde{q}(f)
is in fact the matched filter. We remark that
c
is a surrogate statistic of the likelihood ratio in Gaussian noise.

s(t)
, while the middle panel shows the signal embedded in the noise
x(t)=s(t)+n(t)
, where the amplitude of the signal has been reduced, so that it is not apparent in the data. The bottom panel shows the matched filter output
c
which has a peak, in spite of the weak signal. If the peak crosses a preset threshold, we then say we have detected a signal.

.

II.2
Maximum likelihood detection

First consider the simple situation of a binary hypothesis. Let
p_{0}({\bf x})
is the probability density function (pdf) when the signal is absent, that is,
{\bf x}={\bf n}
(hypothesis
H_{0}
) and
p_{1}({\bf x})
is the pdf when the signal is present, that is,
{\bf x}={\bf s}+{\bf n}
(hypothesis
H_{1}
). Detection is decided by computing the likelihood ratio
\Lambda({\bf x})=p_{1}({\bf x})/p_{0}({\bf x})
and comparing its maximum with a threshold
\Lambda_{0}
, which in turn depends on the false alarm probability
P_{F}
that we can tolerate. In order to achieve this, the space
{\cal D}
is partitioned into

{\cal D}=\mathcal{R}+\mathcal{R}^{c}
, where the superscript ”
c
” denotes the complement. The false alarm and detection probabilities are defined as:

P_{F}(\mathcal{R})=\int_{\mathcal{R}}p_{0}({\bf x})~d{\bf x}\,,~~~~~~~~~P_{D}(\mathcal{R})=\int_{\mathcal{R}}p_{1}({\bf x})~d{\bf x}\,.

(3)

The region
\mathcal{R}
is found by the Neyman-Pearson lemma
[
14
]
, which maximises the detection probability for a given false alarm probability. The likelihood ratio must satisfy
\Lambda({\bf x})\geq\Lambda_{0}
, and
\Lambda_{0}
chosen so that
P_{F}=\alpha
, where
\alpha
is the false alarm probability that we can tolerate, usually
\alpha<<1
. This sets the threshold
\Lambda_{0}
. Detection is announced if
\Lambda({\bf x})>\Lambda_{0}
.

However, we are not in this situation because the signal depends on several parameters; that is, the signal is one among a family of signals, and then we have a composite hypothesis
H_{1}
. We must, therefore, use maximum likelihood detection. In Gaussian noise, we can replace the likelihood ratio by the surrogate statistic
c
as it is a monotonic function of
c
. In searching for signals, the statistic
c
is maximized over template parameters and compared with a threshold based on
P_{F}
. In the simplest case, the signal is a function of the masses
m_{1},m_{2}
and kinematical parameters, namely, the coalescence time
t_{c}
(in Fig.
2
the peak occurs at
t=t_{c}
) and the coalescence phase
\phi_{c}
. In practice, for the parameters
t_{c},\phi_{c}
, the templates (normalised waveforms) need to be only defined at
\phi_{c}=0
and
\phi_{c}=\pi/2
, and for
t_{c}=0
. This is because the search over these parameters can be done efficiently using quadratures for
\phi_{c}
and the fast Fourier transform algorithm for
t_{c}
. However, the search over the mass parameters requires a densely sampled discrete bank of templates so that the chance of missing out on a signal is small
[
15
,
16
]
. A typical template bank is shown in Fig.
3
for masses in the range
1M_{\odot}\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq 100M_{\odot}
and
m_{1}+m_{2}\leq 100M_{\odot}
.

Figure 3:
In the template bank shown, the ranges of the masses
m_{1},m_{2}
are as follows:
1M_{\odot}\leq m_{1},m_{2}\leq 100M_{\odot}
,
m_{1}+m_{2}\leq 100M_{\odot}
and
m_{2}\leq m_{1}
. The bank consists of about a million templates.

We only require templates with
m_{2}\leq m_{1}
, because the signal waveform is symmetric in
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. The density of templates is governed by the maximum mismatch permitted. The match between two normalised templates
{\bf h}_{\alpha}
and
{\bf h}_{\beta}
is just the scalar product
({\bf h}_{\alpha},{\bf h}_{\beta})={\mathcal{M}}
say. The mismatch is defined as
1-{\mathcal{M}}
. It is customary to choose the mismatch to be
\sim 3\%
, which means a possible loss of about 10 % in the detection of sources as the number of sources depends on the volume (if
\epsilon
is the mismatch, then the fraction of signals lost is
1-(1-\epsilon)^{3}\simeq 3\epsilon
).

III
What is a
\chi^{2}
discriminator?

As mentioned in the introduction, the data contain non-stationary and non-Gaussian features which are called glitches. These interfere with our maximum likelihood methods; we need to distinguish between triggers produced by signals and those by glitches. For this purpose, we use
\chi^{2}
discriminators. We first describe the general mathematical formalism and then use it to target specific morphologies of glitches.

III.1
Constructing a generic
\chi^{2}
discriminator

We begin with an arbitrary set of linearly independent vectors
{\bf h}_{\alpha}\in{\cal D}
, where
\alpha=1,2,...,p
. The
\chi^{2}
statistic is constructed by taking the differences between the expected and observed correlations. The expected correlations are obtained when the data are just the signal. The observed value of the correlation of the data
{\bf x}
with the chosen vector
{\bf h}_{\alpha}
is just given by:

c_{\alpha}^{o}({\bf x})=({\bf x},{\bf h}_{\alpha})\,

(4)

We now define the expected correlations
c^{e}_{\alpha}
. We consider the situation of the perfect match between the signal and the normalised trigger template
{\bf h}_{0}
, and so
{\bf s}=A{\bf h}_{0}
for some amplitude
A=({\bf s},{\bf h}_{0})
. Then, the correlation of the data
{\bf x}
with the template
{\bf h}_{0}
is
just
c=({\bf x},{\bf h}_{0})
and its average value is
\langle c\rangle=({\bf s},{\bf h}_{0})
, because
\langle({\bf n},{\bf h}_{0})=0
.
We also have the relation,

({\bf s},{\bf h}_{\alpha})~=~(A{\bf h}_{0},{\bf h}_{\alpha})~=~A({\bf h}_{0},{\bf h}_{\alpha})~=~({\bf s},{\bf h}_{0})~({\bf h}_{0},{\bf h}_{\alpha})~=~\langle c\rangle({\bf h}_{0},{\bf h}_{\alpha})\,.

(5)

In the above equation, we replace the average value of the correlation
c
, namely,
\langle c\rangle
by
c
itself. This gives us the expected correlation:

c_{\alpha}^{(e)}({\bf x})=({\bf x},{\bf h}_{0})~({\bf h}_{0},{\bf h}_{\alpha})\,.

(6)

We take the difference between the expected and observed correlations:

\Delta c_{\alpha}({\bf x})=c_{\alpha}^{o}({\bf x})-c^{e}_{\alpha}({\bf x})\equiv({\bf x},\Delta{\bf h}_{\alpha})\,,

(7)

where we have defined the projected vectors
\Delta{\bf h}_{\alpha}
as,

\Delta{\bf h}_{\alpha}={\bf h}_{\alpha}-({\bf h}_{\alpha},{\bf h}_{0})~{\bf h}_{0}\,.

(8)

We readily verify that
(\Delta{\bf h}_{\alpha},{\bf h}_{0})=0
for every
\alpha
and therefore
\Delta{\bf h}_{\alpha}
are also orthogonal to the signal
{\bf s}
. Then
{\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha}
span a
p
-dimesional subspace of
{\cal D}
. We call this subspace as
{\cal S}
. Then the generic
\chi^{2}
for an arbitrary collection of vectors
{\bf h}_{\alpha}
is just the square of the
L_{2}
norm of the projected data vector
{\bf x}
on
{\cal S}
.

We note the following properties of the linear functional
\Delta c_{\alpha}
:

\Delta c_{\alpha}({\bf s})=0,~~~~~~~~\langle\Delta c_{\alpha}({\bf n})\rangle=0\,.

(9)

Secondly, the r.v.s
\Delta c_{\alpha}
are Gaussian if the noise is Gaussian because they are linear combinations of Gaussian variables, as seen from Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
). However, we cannot right away take the sum of squares of the
\Delta c_{\alpha}
to construct a
\chi^{2}
because (i) they could, in general, be correlated and, moreover, (ii) they may not have unit variance. But these problems can be easily remedied. To construct the
\chi^{2}
, we first require the following covariance matrix:

C_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\langle\Delta c_{\alpha}({\bf n})\Delta c_{\beta}({\bf n})\rangle~=~(\Delta{\bf h}_{\alpha},\Delta{\bf h}_{\beta})\,.

(10)

The proof of the second equality is given in
[
17
]
. The
\chi^{2}
discriminator is then given by:

\chi^{2}~=~\Delta c_{\alpha}~[C^{-1}]^{\alpha\beta}~\Delta c_{\beta}\,.

(11)

By choosing an orthonormal basis of
{\cal S}
, one can explicitly show that in Gaussian noise, the
\chi^{2}
defined in Eq. (
11
) is the sum of squares of independent standard normal variables and hence the so defined statistic is
\chi^{2}
distributed with
p
degrees of freedom
[
10
]
.

To extract maximum benefit from the generic
\chi^{2}
, it is intuitively clear that the
{\bf h}_{\alpha}
should be chosen in the direction of the glitches, so that they project heavily on
{\cal S}
.

III.2
The underlying mathematical structure

Recall that the space of data trains over a time segment
[0,T]
is the Hilbert space
{\cal D}=L_{2}([0,T],\mu)
, where
\mu
is a measure defined conveniently in the Fourier domain by
d\mu=df/S_{h}(f)
, with
S_{h}(f)
being the one-sided noise PSD. The
\chi^{2}
discriminator is defined so that its value for the signal is zero, and for Gaussian noise, it has a
\chi^{2}
distribution with some number of degrees of freedom. In practice, this is of the order of a few tens to a hundred for a CBC search. In principle,
{\cal D}
is infinite-dimensional because it is a space of functions. However, actually, since the data are sampled at some finite rate, it becomes a finite-dimensional space but with a large number of dimensions; typically, a data train contains points of the order of
\sim 10^{6}
or more.

Consider a single waveform
{\bf h}
. Now choose a finite-dimensional subspace
{\cal S}({\bf h})\subset{\cal D}
of dimension
p
normal to
{\bf h}
, defined by:

{\cal S}({\bf h})=\{{\bf x}\in{\cal D}~|~({\bf x},{\bf h})=0\}\,.

(12)

Note that we are selecting vectors
{\bf x}
from a plethora of such vectors so that they form a
p
dimensional vector space. Then we claim that the
\chi^{2}
pertaining to the waveform
{\bf h}
is just the square of the
L_{2}
norm of an arbitrary data vector
{\bf x}
projected onto
{\cal S}
(we drop the
{\bf h}
in order to avoid clutter). Specifically, we decompose the data vector
{\bf x}\in{\cal D}
as,

{\bf x}={\bf x}_{{\cal S}}+{\bf x}_{{\cal S}^{\perp}}\,,

(13)

where
{\cal S}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
{\cal S}
in
{\cal D}
defined as:

{\cal S}^{\perp}=\{{\bf x}\in{\cal D}~|~({\bf x},{\bf y})=0,~~~~\forall~~{\bf y}\in{\cal S}\}\,.

(14)

We may write
{\cal D}
as a direct sum of
{\cal S}
and
{\cal S}^{\perp}
, that is,
{\cal D}={\cal S}\oplus{\cal S}^{\perp}
.
The quantities
{\bf x}_{{\cal S}}
and
{\bf x}_{{\cal S}^{\perp}}
are projections of
{\bf x}
into the subspaces
{\cal S}
and
{\cal S}^{\perp}
, respectively.

Then the statistic
\chi^{2}
is just,

\chi^{2}({\bf x})=\|{\bf x}_{{\cal S}}\|^{2}\,.

(15)

Now choose any orthonormal basis of
{\cal S}
say
{\bf e}_{\alpha},~~\alpha=1,2,...,p
so that
({\bf e}_{\alpha},{\bf e}_{\beta})=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
, where
\delta_{\alpha\beta}
is the Kronecker delta. We easily verify the following properties:

1.

For a general data vector
{\bf x}\in{\cal D}
, we have:

\chi^{2}({\bf x})=\|{\bf x}_{{\cal S}}\|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}|({\bf x},{\bf e}_{\alpha})|^{2}\,.

(16)

2.

Clearly,
\chi^{2}({\bf h})=0
, because
{\bf h}
is orthogonal to
{\cal S}
and so the projection of
{\bf h}
into the subspace
{\cal S}
is zero or
{\bf h}_{{\cal S}}=0
.

3.

Now let us take the noise
{\bf n}
to be Gaussian with zero mean. Therefore, we obtain the following:

\chi^{2}({\bf n})=\|{\bf n}_{{\cal S}}\|^{2}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}|({\bf n},{\bf e}_{\alpha})|^{2}\,.

(17)

Observe that the random variables
({\bf n},{\bf e}_{\alpha})
are independent and Gaussian, with mean zero and variance unity because
\langle({\bf e}_{\alpha},{\bf n})({\bf n},{\bf e}_{\beta})\rangle=({\bf e}_{\alpha},{\bf e}_{\beta})=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
(see
[
17
]
for proof). Thus
\chi^{2}({\bf n})
has a
\chi^{2}
distribution with
p
degrees of freedom.

The
\chi^{2}
statistic so defined, satisfies the essential criteria of being zero on the signal and is distributed
\chi^{2}
for Gaussian noise. We call this the
unified

\chi^{2}
or the
generic

\chi^{2}
.

One can think of the
p
-dimensional subspace
{\cal S}
as ”attached” to each point of the signal manifold - we, therefore, have the structure of a vector bundle. The unified
\chi^{2}
is a function on the base manifold, which is the signal manifold or the parameter space we had denoted by
{\cal P}
. Although, in principle, there is an enormous choice in selecting
{\cal S}
and, therefore, the
\chi^{2}
discriminator, physical and practical considerations limit the choice. Here, we mention two such important considerations:

•

The normal spaces
{\cal S}
chosen must be such that the projection of the glitches on
{\cal S}
is large. Note that a glitch occurring in a data train is a vector in
{\cal D}
, denoted by
{\bf g}
. Specifically, if
{\bf g}
is a glitch, then as before decomposing
{\bf g}={\bf g}_{{\cal S}}+{\bf g}_{{\cal S}^{\perp}}
, we must have
\chi^{2}({\bf g})=\chi^{2}({\bf g}_{{\cal S}})\gg p
. This will distinguish the glitch from the signal and Gaussian noise. Since this is not a straightforward proposition, special constructions are required to select the subspaces with this property. Glitches shaped like sine-Gaussians are ubiquitous in detector data, and an optimal
\chi^{2}
to rule out such glitches has been constructed
[
18
]
. Our analysis shows a lot of freedom in selecting
{\cal S}
, which should be advantageous in constructing better
\chi^{2}
discriminators.

•

It is desirable that the computational cost of evaluating the
\chi^{2}
is not too large - that is, the discriminator is computationally efficient.

III.3
The traditional
\chi^{2}
discriminator

The traditional method, due to Bruce Allen
[
9
]
, involves partitioning the frequency band
[f_{l},f_{u}]
into
p
sub-bands with the following properties: The frequency band
[f_{\rm lower},f_{\rm upper}]
is decided by the bandwidth in which the detector operates and the signal in which it has appreciable power. We form the subintervals
I_{\alpha}=[f_{\alpha-1},f_{\alpha}],~\alpha=1,2,...,p
, such that
f_{0}=f_{\rm lower}
and
f_{p}=f_{\rm upper}
and there is equal signal power in the sub-bands. Explicitly for the binary inspiral signal (Newtonian waveform), we have
{\tilde{h}}(f)=N_{h}f^{-7/6}
where
N_{h}
is a normalisation constant. We then normalise the signal by setting:

\displaystyle\langle{\bf h},{\bf h}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\int_{f_{\rm lower}}^{f_{\rm upper}}~\frac{|{\tilde{h}}(f)|^{2}}{S_{h}(f)}~df\,,

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4N_{h}^{2}\int_{f_{\rm lower}}^{f_{\rm upper}}~\frac{df}{f^{7/3}S_{h}(f)}~\equiv~1\,,

by choosing
N_{h}
appropriately.

The
f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{p-1}
are so chosen that the above integral over
I_{\alpha}
equals
1/p
, that is,

4\int_{f_{\alpha-1}}^{f_{\alpha}}~\frac{|{\tilde{h}}(f)|^{2}}{S_{h}(f)}~df=\frac{1}{p},~~~~~~~~~~~k=1,2,...,p\,.

(19)

Given these definitions, we can state the Bruce Allen
\chi^{2}
statistic in the following way. In the Fourier domain, we define the functions
{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha}(f)
as:

\displaystyle{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\tilde{h}}(f),~~~~f\in I_{k}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0~~~~~{\rm otherwise}\,.

Then the quantities
{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha}
possess the following properties:

{\tilde{h}}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha},~~~~~~\langle{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha},{\tilde{h}}_{\beta}\rangle=\frac{1}{p}\delta_{\alpha\beta},~~~~~~\langle{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha},{\tilde{h}}\rangle=\frac{1}{p}\,.

(21)

We have omitted the argument
f
to avoid clutter (and there is a slight abuse of notation for the scalar product). We can describe the above procedure in geometric language in vector form. We have the correspondence
{\tilde{h}}(f)\longrightarrow{\bf h}
and
{\tilde{h}}_{\alpha}(f)\longrightarrow{\bf h}_{\alpha}
where we have represented functions by vectors. For a template (normalised)
{\bf h}
and a data vector
{\bf x}
, the correlation is
c=({\bf x},{\bf h})
. We assume that the signal matches the template perfectly. The Bruce Allen test consists of splitting the template vector
{\bf h}
into
p
frequency bands equal in power and checking for consistency. This translates Eq. (
21
) to:

{\bf h}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}{\bf h}_{\alpha},~~~~~~~~~({\bf h}_{\alpha},{\bf h}_{\beta})=\frac{1}{p}\delta_{\alpha\beta},~~~~1~\leq~\alpha,~\beta~\leq p\,.

(22)

Then from Eq. (
8
), we have
{\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha}={\bf h}_{\alpha}-\frac{{\bf h}}{p}
and
\Delta c_{\alpha}=({\bf x},{\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha})
.

We observe that
({\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha},{\bf h})=0
for each
\alpha
so that the subspace spanned by
{\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha}
is orthogonal to
{\bf h}
. This is precisely
{\cal S}
. We further see that
\sum_{\alpha=1}^{p}{\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha}=0
so that the dimension of
{\cal S}
is less than
p
. Since this is the only linear relation
\dim({\cal S})=p-1
. Further we compute that,

({\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha},{\bf\Delta h}_{\beta})=-\frac{1}{p^{2}}
for
\alpha\neq\beta
, implying that the
{\bf\Delta h}_{\alpha}
are not orthogonal.

We can however exhibit an orthonormal basis of
{\cal S}
:

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{p}{2}}\left({\bf\Delta h}_{1}-{\bf\Delta h}_{2}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{p}{6}}\left({\bf\Delta h}_{1}+{\bf\Delta h}_{2}-2{\bf\Delta h}_{3}\right)\,,

\displaystyle{\bf e}_{p-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{p}{p(p-1)}}\left({\bf\Delta h}_{1}+{\bf\Delta h}_{2}+...+{\bf\Delta h}_{p-1}-(p-1){\bf\Delta h}_{p}\right)

Thus, we have explicitly established that the traditional
\chi^{2}
(i) has
p-1
degrees of freedom because it is the sum of the squares of
p-1
standard independent Gaussian random variables, and (ii) it is also a unified
\chi^{2}
.

IV
The optimised
\chi^{2}
for sine-Gaussians and blip glitches

The construction described here is general and applicable to any family of glitches that can either be modelled or to a collection of samples, preferably of common morphology picked out from the data
[
19
]
. Here, we apply our formalism to the family of glitches, which are shaped like sine-Gaussians. These are the so-called blip glitches. The reason for this choice is that empirically, a large subset of noise transients in gravitational-wave strain data of LIGO and Virgo detectors has been found to project strongly on sine-Gaussians, including the types that trigger CBC templates
[
10
]
. In order to improve the sensitivity of CBC searches, we specifically target these glitches, which are a major source of reduced sensitivity. However, our algorithm can be straightforwardly adapted to replace sine-Gaussians with any other relevant glitch morphology.

Below we detail the steps in obtaining an optimal
\chi^{2}
(see
[
18
]
for details).

•

Sample the parameter space of sine-Gaussians densely so that the sample is representative of the blip glitches. We call this subspace as
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
(subscript
{\cal G}
for glitches). Here the metric defined on the parameter space of glitches helps in uniformly sampling the parameter space.

•

Since the subspace
{\cal S}
should be orthogonal to the trigger template
{\bf h}
, we remove the component parallel to
{\bf h}
from each of the sample vectors spanning
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
. Thus if
{\bf v}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
, then we define
{\bf v}_{\perp}={\bf v}-({\bf v},{\bf h}){\bf h}
. By construction, the
{\bf v}_{\perp}
are orthogonal to
{\bf h}
. The space spanned by these clipped vectors, namely
{\bf v}_{\perp}
, we call
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
.

•

In practice,
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
or equivalently
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
, contains a large number of vectors, few hundreds to thousands. If we used all these vectors to construct
{\cal S}
, its dimension would be large as well; these would be the number of degrees of freedom of the
\chi^{2}
, leading to high computational cost. Therefore we apply the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to the vectors of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
to obtain a subspace of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
, that is the best fit low dimensional approximation to
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
.

The best lower-dimensional approximation to a vector space of
n
dimensions is defined as follows: given
n
linearly independent vectors that span a vector space

\mathcal{V}
, the best
k
-dimensional,
k<n
, approximation to
\mathcal{V}
is the subspace
\mathcal{W}
, such that the sum of squares of the norm of the projections of all the
n
vectors onto
\mathcal{W}
is maximum.

How does one construct
\mathcal{W}
? This can be achieved by using the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem
[
20
]
. First one applies the SVD to the vectors that span
\mathcal{V}
. Then, one chooses the right singular vectors corresponding to the largest
k
singular values, then the subspace spanned by these vectors is precisely
\mathcal{W}
. The dimension
k
is chosen by applying a cut-off to obtain the desired level of approximation. The subspace
{\cal S}
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
is generated in this way - it is the best
p
dimensional approximation to
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
. We also have the desired property that
{\bf h}
is orthogonal to
{\cal S}
since
{\cal S}\subset{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
.

We start by sampling the space of sine-Gaussians in order to obtain
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
.

IV.1
The sine-Gaussian waveform and the metric

We begin by describing a sine-Gaussian waveform. Fig.
4
depicts the general form of this type of glitch.

We now proceed to design an optimal
\chi^{2}
for blip glitches. It is therefore important to select appropriate vectors in the parameter space occupied by the glitches.
A recent work
[
18
]
demonstrates how transient bursts represented by sine-Gaussian waveform can be vetoed with the help of an optimal
\chi^{2}
from the GW data. Since the blip glitches are also known to have a time-domain morphology similar to the sine-Gaussian waveforms, we use these waveforms as the vectors to generate
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
and further construct the optimized sine-Gaussian
\chi^{2}
- the Opt
\chi^{2}
. To allow for an arbitrary phase in the noise transient, we use a complex-valued sine-Gaussian waveform, which is more convenient. In the time domain the sine-Gaussian waveform can be defined as,

\displaystyle\psi(t;t_{0},f_{0},Q)=

\displaystyle A\exp{\bigg(-\frac{4\pi^{2}f_{0}^{2}}{Q^{2}}(t-t_{0})^{2}\bigg)}

(23)

\displaystyle\times\exp{[-i2\pi f_{0}(t-t_{0})]}\,.

where
t_{0}
is the central time,
f_{0}
the central frequency,

Q
the quality factor and
A
the amplitude. The parameters describing the glitches apart from the amplitude are
t_{0},f_{0}
and
Q
and we will consider these three parameters to define the parameter space of sine-Gaussians. We have dropped the phase
\phi_{0}
because the match is defined as the absolute value of the scalar product
[
21
]
, and so it does not appear anywhere in the computations - in effect, we have put it to zero. In order to populate the parameter space, we require a metric. This is achieved by considering two neighboring normalised
sine-Gaussian waveforms,
\psi_{1}(f;t_{0},f_{0},Q)
and

\psi_{2}(f;t_{0}+dt_{0},f_{0}+df_{0},Q+dQ)
. A metric may then be introduced on this space as a map from the differences in the parameters of these waveforms to the fractional change in their match:

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{4\pi^{2}f_{0}^{2}}{Q^{2}}\bigg)dt_{0}^{2}+\bigg(\frac{2+Q^{2}}{4f_{0}^{2}}\bigg)df_{0}^{2}+\bigg(\frac{1}{2Q^{2}}\bigg)dQ^{2}

(24)

\displaystyle-\bigg(\frac{1}{2f_{0}Q}\bigg)df_{0}dQ.

The above metric (
24
) can be reduced to its diagonal form by using the transformations,

\omega_{o}=2\pi f_{0},~~~~~\nu=\frac{\omega_{o}}{Q}\,.

(25)

In the new coordinates,
(t_{0},\omega_{0},Q)
, the metric takes the form:

ds^{2}=\nu^{2}dt_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\nu^{2}}d\omega_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\nu^{2}}d\nu^{2}.

(26)

In comparison to the metric in Ref.
[
18
]
, this metric has no
\omega_{0}^{2}
term multiplying
dt_{0}^{2}
. This results from accounting for the arbitrary phase of the sine-Gaussian waveform. As mentioned in Ref.
[
22
]
, a CBC template is triggered with a time lag
t_{d}
after the occurrence of a glitch, i.e., after
t_{0}
. The time
t_{d}
is given by
[
22
]
, which for the parameters considered is essentially
\tau_{0}
, where,

\tau_{0}=\frac{5}{256\pi f_{0}}(\pi{{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm chirp}}f_{0})^{-5/3}.

(27)

Here, the quantity
{{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm chirp}}
is called the chirp mass
{{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm chirp}}=(\mu M^{2/3})^{3/5}
, where
\mu
and
M
are the reduced and the total mass of the binary system, respectively, and
\tau_{0}
is called the Newtonian chirp time. Because of this relation between
t_{0}
and
\omega_{0}
, the metric given in Eq.(
24
) is reduced to one in two parameters. In the new parameters,

z=(\omega_{0}{{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm chirp}})^{-5/3}\quad{\rm and}\quad y=\ln(\nu/\rm{rad/sec})\,,

(28)

the following metric results:

ds^{2}=\Bigg[\frac{2^{-14/3}}{Q^{2}}+\frac{9Q^{2}}{100z^{2}}\Bigg]dz^{2}+\frac{1}{2}dy^{2}.

(29)

For most of the parameter space we consider, the metric given in Eq. (
29
) applies, and our sampling of the parameter space based on this metric is valid to a good approximation. Fig.
5
shows the sampling of the parameter space for a match of
80\%
in the coordinates
(f_{0},Q)
and
(z,y)
.

IV.2
Obtaining
{\cal S}
: the degrees of freedom of the
\chi^{2}

Fig.
5
displays the sampling of the parameter space, which gives a collection of vectors which span the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
on which the blip glitches are expected to have a substantial projection. However, as mentioned before, the SVD cannot be applied directly to the vectors in
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
. The input matrix, which we denote by
{\bf M}
needs to be first prepared. We briefly describe the procedure here since the full details can be found in
[
18
]
. The following are the salient steps required:

•

The sine-Gaussians have central time
t_{0}=0
and they need to be appropriately time-shifted with respect to the time of occurrence of the trigger.

•

The vectors need to be
clipped
by subtracting out the component parallel to the template as described at the beginning of this section. The clipped and time-shifted sine-Gaussians span the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
of
{\cal D}
.

•

The usual SVD algorithm uses the Euclidean scalar product. However, we have a weighted scalar product given by Eq. (
1
); a scalar product that is inversely weighted by the PSD
S_{h}(f)
. We therefore
whiten
each row vector by dividing each Fourier component of the row vector by
\sqrt{S_{h}(f)}
.

Now, we have a collection of vectors which we use to form the row vectors of
{\bf M}
. This is the matrix to which the SVD algorithm is applied. After the SVD, we have to finally modify the output of the SVD, namely, the right singular vectors, which must then be
unwhitened
. This is done by multiplying by the factor
\sqrt{S_{h}(f)}
. The unwhitened singular vectors are orthonormal in the weighted scalar product.

We now briefly describe the SVD algorithm
[
23
]
. The SVD decomposition of the input
M\times N
matrix
{\bf M}
is written as a product of three matrices:

\displaystyle{\bf M}={\bf U}~\Sigma~{\bf V}^{\dagger}\,,

(30)

where
{\bf U}
is the
M\times r
matrix of left singular vectors,
\Sigma
is an
r\times r
square diagonal matrix consisting of singular values
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},...,\sigma_{r}
. The singular values
\sigma_{k},~k=1,2,,,,,r
are arranged in descending order of magnitude. Finally,
{\bf V}^{\dagger}
is the
r\times N
matrix of right singular vectors. The matrix
{\bf V}^{\dagger}
is the Hermitian conjugate of
{\bf V}
. Both left and right singular vectors are normalised and appear as column vectors in both matrices
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
. Invoking the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem we obtain the best
p
-dimensional approximation to
{\mathcal{V}}_{\perp}
, which
states that the first
p
singular vectors span the best-fit
p
-dimensional subspace of
{\bf M}
. The cut-off
p
is chosen by using Frobenius norm
[
24
]
of the matrix
{\bf M}
, namely,

\displaystyle\|{\bf M}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}|a_{ij}|^{2}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sigma_{k}^{2}\,.

(31)

If we set the accuracy level to 90
\%
, then we choose
p<r
such that
\sum_{k=1}^{p}\sigma_{k}^{2}~\gtrsim~0.9~\|{\bf M}\|_{F}^{2}
. We obtain
{\cal S}
as the linear span of the first
p
right singular vectors
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},...,{\bf v}_{p}
; they form an orthonormal basis of
{\cal S}
. This implies that the sum of squares of projections of the row vectors of
{\bf M}
on
{\cal S}
add up to more than 90
\%
of the full value of the sum of squares of all the vectors
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2},...,{\bf v}_{M}
. If a glitch vector is close to the span of the row vectors
{\bf v}_{i},~i=1,2,...,p
, its square of the norm of its projection onto
{\cal S}
will tend to be large, which will result in a large
\chi^{2}
. We have also succeeded in reducing the number of degrees of freedom of the
\chi^{2}
to
p
, which is usually much less than the dimension of
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
. Typically, for the ranges of parameters
f_{0},~Q,~{{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm chirp}}
, etc., considered here, the number of vectors spanning
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
is about a few hundred
while
p<10
.

VI
Concluding Remarks

Here we have presented a general framework for generic
\chi^{2}
discriminators. These discriminators have the mathematical structure of a vector bundle. Then, the
\chi^{2}
statistic is just the
L_{2}
norm of the data vector projected onto a subspace of the space of data vectors orthogonal to the trigger template. Since the trigger could occur anywhere in the parameter space, we are dealing with a collection of subspaces attached to each point of the signal manifold - a smooth choice leads to a smooth vector bundle. The
\chi^{2}
can be viewed as a non-negative real valued function on sections of the vector bundle. Apart from this mathematical elegant construction, the important practical application that emerges from this formulation is the enormous flexibility available in the
\chi^{2}
discriminators. This flexibility could be exploited for
tuning
the
\chi^{2}
; it can discriminate more decisively against frequently occurring glitches or glitches which pose difficulties to the search algorithms.

One such morphology is that of blip glitches which can be modelled as sine-Gaussians. We have therefore constructed a
\chi^{2}
statistic that is optimally effective in discriminating BBH signals from sine-Gaussian glitches
[
18
,
26
]
. In previous works signal-based
\chi^{2}
discriminators have been formulated that have succeeded in identifying noise artifacts in the data (see
[
26
]
and references therein). However, in the recent past, they have been found wanting in high-mass BBH searches. This awareness has led to new proposals for discriminators which would improve on BBH search sensitivities. For the first time, reference
[
10
]
evolved the appropriate mathematical formalism for geometrically understanding existing
\chi^{2}
discriminators and constructing new ones.

There are several steps, which we summarise below, required to arrive at the final goal. We considered a family of sine-Gaussian waveforms with physically relevant parameters. We then uniformly sampled this family of sine-Gaussians with the help of a metric so that it is adequately represented. The space spanned by this set of sampled sine-Gaussians we have called
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
. It turns out that the number of sampled glitch vectors is too large, and consequently, the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
spanned by them is high dimensional. A
low-dimensional approximation to
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
is sought in order that the computational costs for the
\chi^{2}
remain in control. The
best possible low-dimensional approximation to
{\mathcal{V}}_{{\cal G}}
is obtained by invoking the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem, and this is
achieved with the help of the SVD algorithm. We ensure that the associated subspace obtained for the
\chi^{2}
is
orthogonal to the trigger template by appropriately projecting out the components of the glitch vectors parallel to the
trigger template. The above steps lead to the required optimal
\chi^{2}
discriminator - the Opt
\chi^{2}
. Our results on real data from the O3 run of the LIGO detectors show that the Opt
\chi^{2}
performs significantly better than the other
\chi^{2}
s. For more details see
[
26
]
. Current work in progress
[
19
]
uses real blip glitches extracted from detector data, instead of the sine-Gaussian model to construct the
\chi^{2}
. Additionally, this work also targets more than one glitch morphologies.
