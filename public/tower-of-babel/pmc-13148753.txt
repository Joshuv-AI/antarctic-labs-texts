Intraoperative Agents for Hemostasis and Dural Reconstruction in Neurosurgery
Authors: Youssef E. Sibih, Alexander F. Haddad, David J. Caldwell, Alexander A. Aabedi, John K. Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Intraoperative hemostasis and dural closure are critical components of neurosurgical procedures, directly affecting operative time, blood loss, neurological outcomes, and the risk of cerebrospinal fluid leak, infection, and reoperation. Over the past 2 decades, there has been rapid expansion in the availability of hemostatic adjuncts, dural substitutes, and liquid sealants. Despite widespread adoption, product selection is frequently guided by surgeon preference and institutional practice rather than a detailed understanding of material composition, mechanism of action, and product-specific limitations. This qualitative review provides a neurosurgery-focused, product-level comparison of commonly used intraoperative hemostatic agents, dural substitutes, and dural sealants, with emphasis on composition, mechanism of action, indications, advantages, limitations, safety considerations, and cost. A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, United States Food and Drug Administration device summaries, manufacturer instructions for use, and key clinical trials and observational studies. Products were categorized into 3 groups: intraoperative hemostatic adjuncts, dural substitutes and graft materials, and liquid dural sealants and adhesives. The review encompasses gelatin-based products, oxidized regenerated cellulose, collagen-based agents, flowable gelatin-thrombin matrices, topical thrombin preparations, autologous and nonautologous dural grafts, and fibrin- and polyethylene glycol-based sealants. Selection of these materials should be guided by anatomic considerations, patient-specific factors, and best available evidence. This review serves as a practical reference to support informed intraoperative decision-making in neurosurgical practice.

Intraoperative hemostasis and watertight dural closure are foundational objectives in intracranial and spinal neurosurgery, as failure of either can lead to postoperative hematoma, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, infection, pseudomeningocele, revision surgery, and increased healthcare costs.1-5 Over the past 2 decades, the number of commercially available hemostatic agents, dural substitutes, and liquid sealants has expanded substantially, encompassing products with distinct material properties, biological activity, and degradation profiles.6-8 However, this proliferation has complicated intraoperative decision-making, and product selection is often driven by surgeon preference or institutional practice rather than comparative effectiveness data.9

Neurosurgical procedures occur in constrained environments where even modest bleeding or product-related mass effect may result in neurological injury. These risks are amplified by direct communication with the subarachnoid space and proximity to eloquent neural and vascular structures.10-12

In this review, we provide an evidence-based, neurosurgery-focused comparison of hemostatic adjuncts, dural substitutes, and liquid sealants, emphasizing higher level clinical evidence where available. We synthesize these data into practical, scenario-driven selection frameworks and identify key knowledge gaps to inform future comparative and cost-effectiveness research.

METHODS

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials for studies published between 1940 and 2025. Intraoperative Hemostatic Adjuncts

Gelatin-Based Agents

Gelfoam (Pfizer), a porcine gelatin sponge available since 1945, represents one of the most widely used hemostatic agents in neurosurgery.13 Gelfoam provides a physical matrix promoting platelet aggregation and activating the intrinsic coagulation pathway.13 The porous matrix promotes clot formation by facilitating platelet adhesion and trapping red blood cells.6 Importantly Gelfoam itself has no intrinsic hemostatic properties and requires the patient's normal coagulation cascade to be effective.8 In practice, Gelfoam is often saturated with topical thrombin, so the sponge provides both a matrix and rapid enzymatic clot formation; In addition, it can be soaked with papaverine (a phosphodiesterase inhibitor that relaxes vascular smooth muscle) to provide local vasodilation and mitigate arterial spasm during cerebrovascular surgery. Complete absorption occurs within 4 to 6 weeks. Studies confirm sustained efficacy of Gelfoam for craniotomy hemostasis, although rare imaging findings may mimic abscess and lead to unnecessary intervention.6,14 Multiple studies support the safety and efficacy of Gelfoam for neurosurgical hemostasis without increased infection or foreign body reaction risk. Rare inflammatory complications, including aseptic meningitis, have been reported but typically resolve with conservative management.15,16 Despite these considerations, Gelfoam remains one of the most cost-effective hemostatic agents, with typical costs of $5 to $15 per unit depending on size and form17 (Table 1).

Flowable Gelatin-Thrombin Matrices

Floseal (Baxter Healthcare) is a flowable hemostatic matrix consisting of bovine-derived gelatin granules combined with human recombinant thrombin. Approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 1999, it is widely used because of ease of application and rapid hemostasis effect.18,19 The gelatin matrix provides a scaffold for clot formation, whereas the thrombin directly converts fibrinogen to fibrin, and the flowable consistency allows application to irregular surfaces and hard-to-reach areas.20,21 Complete absorption typically occurs within 6 to 8 weeks, although serial imaging studies have demonstrated that Floseal can cause significant mass effect and enhancement in the early postoperative period, and in some cases can potentially lead to cyst formation.22

Numerous studies support the efficacy of Floseal in neurosurgery. In a large prospective series spanning cranial, craniospinal, and spinal procedures, Gazzeri et al23 reported rapid bleeding control after application, with hemostasis achieved within minutes in most cases and only a small proportion requiring reapplication. Transsphenoidal surgery studies similarly report reliable hemostatic control with no procedures aborted because of hemorrhage.24 Floseal costs approximately $300 to $500/5-10 mL kit, making it significantly more expensive than traditional agents.20

Surgiflo (Ethicon) is a similar flowable gelatin-thrombin matrix. The product contains gelatin granules and human thrombin (1000 IU/mL), at a lower concentration than Floseal, with the gelatin component derived from porcine skin collagen.25-27 A propensity score-matched lumbar spine study reported no differences between Floseal and Surgiflo in transfusion rates, operative time, blood loss, or postoperative hemoglobin.28 However, Surgiflo required a greater number of units per case and was associated with an additional cost of $102.45 per surgery, corresponding to an estimated annual savings of $102,450 per 1000 cases with Floseal.28

Oxidized Regenerated Cellulose

Oxidized regenerated cellulose (ORC) has been used since the 1950s and remains widely used in neurosurgery.29 The Surgicel family (Ethicon) includes several formulations: Original (loosely woven fabric, easily torn to size), Fibrillar (fibrous form that conforms to irregular surfaces), Nu-Knit (tightly woven fabric with greater tensile strength), and structured non-woven fabric (absorbable hemostat with improved conformability).30 ORC products achieve hemostasis through a physical matrix, acidic pH-induced vasoconstriction, and intrinsic cellulosic acid properties, functioning even in coagulopathic patients unlike gelatin products.31,32 In cranial surgery, ORC is variably left as a thin layer to line postoperative tumor cavities after hemostasis to tamponade diffuse oozing.23 Practice differs across centers because the acidic microenvironment can irritate tissue and contribute to local tissue reaction.33 ORC is typically absorbed within 1 to 2 weeks, faster than gelatin-based products, with the acidic degradation products absorbed systemically and excreted renally.34 Rare mass lesions and inflammatory reactions, including intracranial textiloma, have been reported with ORC use.35,36 The acidic nature of ORC can cause tissue irritation and has been associated with nerve injury when used near neural structures.37,38 Several case reports describe cauda equina syndrome after epidural use of Surgicel, likely because of chemical injury or compression.39,40 ORC can interfere with bone healing and should be removed from bone surfaces before closure; the acidic environment inhibits osteoblast function and can delay fusion in spinal surgery.41 Foreign body reactions to Surgicel are less common than with gelatin products.35 Surgicel products range from $15 to $60 depending on size and formulation, making them cost-effective options.30

Collagen-Based Hemostats

Microfibrillar collagen hemostats are derived from purified bovine collagen and have been used in neurosurgery since the 1970s.42 Examples include Avitene (Davol), Helistat (Integra), and Instat (Ethicon). Collagen fibers activate platelets through direct contact, triggering the release of coagulation factors and initiating the clotting cascade.43 The fibrillar structure promotes platelet adhesion and aggregation. Unlike ORC, microfibrillar collagen has a neutral pH and does not cause tissue irritation.44,45 Complete absorption typically occurs within 8 to 12 weeks, with the collagen broken down by collagenases.45

In a rat neurosurgical model, microfibrillar collagen and gelatin-based sealants achieved rapid hemostasis but were both associated with granuloma formation, suggesting similar foreign-body reaction potential.46 Another concern is embolization if applied to actively bleeding vessels, particularly in cardiac and vascular surgery, although this risk seems minimal in neurosurgery given lower venous pressure and topical rather than intravascular application.46-48 Microfibrillar collagen products cost approximately $80 to $150/unit.

Topical Thrombin and Antifibrinolytics

Topical thrombin preparations are available as both human recombinant (Recothrom) and bovine-derived (Thrombin-JMI) formulations. These products directly convert fibrinogen to fibrin, bypassing the coagulation cascade.49 Thrombin is rarely used alone in neurosurgery but is commonly combined with other hemostatic agents.50 Bovine thrombin has been associated with the development of antibodies against coagulation factors, particularly Factor V, leading to coagulopathy.51 This has led to preference for human recombinant thrombin in many institutions. Intravascular injection of thrombin can cause catastrophic thrombosis and must be avoided.52

Tranexamic acid (TXA), an antifibrinolytic that inhibits plasminogen activation and fibrin clot breakdown, is used by systemic and topical routes. Intravenous TXA is typically administered intraoperatively and, in select cases, continued postoperatively to reduce perioperative blood loss and may lower the risk of postoperative hematoma formation.53,54 In spinal surgery, a meta-analysis demonstrated that TXA reduces intraoperative and postoperative blood loss and length of stay, with higher postoperative hemoglobin levels compared with controls.53 In intracranial tumor surgery, TXA has been shown to reduce intraoperative blood loss without increasing venous thromboembolism or seizure activity.54 Because patients with malignancy and other procoagulant syndromes may have elevated baseline thrombotic risk, systemic TXA is generally used with individualized risk assessment and standard thromboprophylaxis practices.

Bone Hemostasis

Bone wax, composed of beeswax, paraffin, and isopropyl palmitate, has been used since the 1900s for controlling bone bleeding through mechanical tamponade.55 However, bone wax is not absorbed and can cause foreign body reactions, interfere with bone healing, and increase infection risk. Bone wax remains inexpensive, typically $5 to $10 per unit. Ostene (Ceremed), an absorbable alkylene oxide copolymer, provides equivalent hemostasis with complete absorption within 48 hours.56 Studies demonstrate similar efficacy to bone wax and no interference with bone healing.56 Ostene costs $150 to $200.

Dural Substitutes and Graft Materials

The ideal dural substitute should provide watertightness, biocompatibility, tensile strength, ease of suturing, infection resistance, and minimal adhesion formation. No product satisfies all criteria, necessitating careful selection based on clinical scenario.

Autologous Grafts

Pericranium remains the gold standard for dural reconstruction given excellent biocompatibility, strength, and availability at the surgical site. Large series report CSF leak rates of 1.4% with no graft-related infections when combined with fibrin sealant.57 Limitations include size constraints imposed by craniotomy extent and potential inadequacy for large defects. Fascia lata provides larger grafts with comparable outcomes but requires surgical site harvest and may cause wound complications, seroma, and donor site pain.58,59 Temporalis fascia offers a convenient autologous option during pterional approaches but limited size restricts applicability60 (Table 2).

Allograft and Xenograft Substitutes

DuraGen (Integra) is an acellular collagen matrix derived from bovine Achilles tendon that has been processed to remove cellular components and reduce immunogenicity.61 The material consists of type I collagen arranged in a three-dimensional matrix that is cross-linked to provide mechanical strength and slow degradation. It is available in various sizes and thicknesses (0.5 and 1.0 mm), is suturable, and can be used as an onlay graft or sutured in place. The collagen matrix serves as a scaffold for host cell infiltration and neovascularization, with bovine collagen replacement by host tissue typically over 6 to 12 months.61

Clinical series show complication rates comparable to autologous grafts while significantly reducing operative time by avoiding fascia lata harvest. Across cranial, spinal, and posterior fossa procedures, CSF leak and infection rates are low and similar between graft types.62-64

Comparative studies, including propensity score-matched analyses, support the efficacy of collagen matrix dural substitutes, demonstrating reduced CSF leak rates without increased infection risk. Operative efficiency advantages include shorter duraplasty and overall operative time compared with autologous grafts and sutured alternatives. Complication rates are low and comparable to other dural reconstruction techniques, whereas cost remains mid-range among commercially available substitutes.62,64-66

AlloDerm

AlloDerm (LifeCell) is an acellular dermal matrix derived from donated human skin that has been processed to remove cellular components while preserving the collagen matrix and basement membrane.67,68 The material consists of human collagen with preserved basement membrane complex, and the processing removes all cellular antigens, making it immunologically inert.

Evidence regarding allograft dural substitutes in Chiari decompression is mixed. Although single-center series have reported lower CSF leak-related reoperation rates with AlloDerm, meta-analytic data suggest higher overall complication and reoperation rates for allografts compared with autologous duraplasty.69,70

AlloDerm seems to have fewer foreign body reactions than xenograft materials, likely because of its human origin. However, it is significantly more expensive ($800-$1500 per graft) and limited by tissue donation.

Dura-Guard

Dura-Guard (Baxter) is a suturable bovine pericardium-based dural substitute treated with glutaraldehyde for cross-linking and sterilization, providing excellent tensile strength and handling characteristics. The material is derived from bovine pericardium and treated with glutaraldehyde for cross-linking and sterilization.71 A clinical series of 35 patients demonstrated that glutaraldehyde-fixed bovine pericardium provides excellent tensile strength and suturability. In this cohort, the material was easily sutured to achieve a watertight closure using standard suture material, with favorable handling characteristics, low elasticity, and minimal antigenicity or toxicity.72 Dura-Guard costs approximately $300 to $450 per graft.

Durepair

Durepair (Integra) is a suturable dural substitute derived from fetal bovine dermis, processed to retain its native collagen matrix structure while removing cellular components to minimize immunogenic response.71 The acellular collagen matrix is designed to serve as a scaffold for host tissue ingrowth and remodeling. Durepair is indicated for use as a dural patch graft when primary closure cannot be achieved and can be sutured or placed as an onlay graft.71 The material offers favorable handling properties, conforming readily to irregular defects, and its porous architecture facilitates cellular infiltration and neovascularization. Durepair costs approximately $450 to $750 per graft.

AmnioFix

AmnioFix (MiMedx) is a suturable dehydrated human placental allograft derived from an amnion layer rich in various collagen types along with growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular matrix proteins.73 The membrane exerts anti-inflammatory, proepithelialization, antifibrotic, and antimicrobial effects while exhibiting low immunogenicity.73 In neurosurgery, it has been applied to augment dural closure in craniotomies, transsphenoidal approaches, decompressive craniectomies, and spinal dysraphism repair.74-76 A clinical series of 155 cranial cases reported a 0.6% complication rate with zero CSF leaks, supporting its favorable safety profile.74 AmnioFix costs approximately $750 to $2000 per graft.

Synthetic Substitutes

Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene dural substitutes offer anti-adhesion properties that are particularly advantageous when future reoperation is anticipated. Clinical series demonstrate reduced adhesions, improved reoperative dissection, and shorter operative times compared with cases without barrier placement. Importantly, synthetic dural substitutes have not been associated with increased infection risk and may reduce CSF leak rates in large retrospective cohorts.77-79 Synthetic substitutes cost $350 to $700.

Liquid Dural Sealants

Liquid dural sealants are applied over primary dural closure or dural grafts to achieve watertight closure and prevent CSF leakage.80 Even with meticulous suturing, microscopic gaps in closure can lead to CSF leaks, particularly in high-pressure situations or anatomically challenging regions such as the skull base or ventral spine.80,81 Sealants provide an additional barrier against CSF egress and can significantly reduce postoperative CSF leak rates (Table 3).

The ideal dural sealant should provide rapid polymerization, high burst pressure resistance, biocompatibility without neurotoxicity, complete absorption, ease of application, and minimal swelling or mass effect. As with hemostatic agents and dural substitutes, no current product satisfies all these criteria completely. Dural sealants can be classified by composition: (1) fibrin-based sealants, which mimic final steps of coagulation cascade (Tisseel, Evicel); (2) fibrin-collagen patches, which combine fibrin with collagen matrix (TachoSil); (3) polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based synthetic hydrogels, which are PEG polymers (DuraSeal, DuraSeal Exact, Adherus); (4) albumin-based adhesives, consisting of albumin cross-linked with glutaraldehyde (BioGlue); and (5) collagen-PEG hybrids (Hemopatch).

Fibrin-Based Sealants

Tisseel (Baxter) and Evicel (Ethicon) contain human fibrinogen and thrombin, mimicking the final coagulation step.82,83 Polymerization occurs within 3 to 5 minutes with complete absorption by fibrinolysis within 1 to 2 weeks. TachoSil is a fibrin-coated equine collagen patch that provides combined hemostatic and sealing properties. Biomechanical testing has demonstrated high burst pressures ranging from 41 to 74 cm H2O, placing it among the strongest available dural sealants.84 TachoSil has demonstrated effective CSF leak control, combining structural reinforcement from its collagen matrix with biological dural sealing from fibrin components. It is generally well tolerated with minimal mass effect.85 Cost ranges $300 to $700 per kit.

PEG-Based Synthetic Hydrogels

PEG-based sealants such as DuraSeal (Integra), approved in 2005, are synthetic hydrogels that form a watertight barrier through in situ polymer cross-linking.81 Among available sealants, DuraSeal has demonstrated the highest burst pressures on biomechanical testing (45.5-82 cm H2O).84 In randomized spinal surgery studies, low-swell PEG hydrogels have demonstrated high rates of watertight dural closure, but have also been associated with impaired dural regeneration and increased inflammatory complications.81 DuraSeal can swell approximately 50% within 24 hours, creating significant mass effect concerns.86In addition, there have been cases of paralysis reported with DuraSeal use.87 Lower swelling PEG formulations, including DuraSeal Exact and CraniSeal, may offer improved safety profiles while retaining similar indications.88 Cost is $400 to $650 per kit.89

Adherus Autospray

Adherus is a polyethylenimine-based synthetic sealant delivered by an automated spray applicator, enabling controlled application in anatomically constrained surgical fields. In comparative testing, Adherus demonstrated burst pressures of 31 cm H2O, consistent with mid-range sealant performance. Under physiological conditions in both cranial and spinal models, samples maintained dural adherence for 72 hours, exceeding that of alternative sealants.84 As a PEG-based synthetic hydrogel, Adherus shares class-related limitations, including potential inhibition of dural regeneration and inflammatory tissue responses, although neurosurgery-specific complication data remain limited.84

Albumin-Based Adhesives

BioGlue (CryoLife) is a surgical adhesive composed of bovine serum albumin cross-linked with glutaraldehyde, forming a rapidly polymerizing bond within 20 to 30 seconds that provides immediate watertight dural closure.90 BioGlue has shown effective dural sealing. Prospective and comparative studies have demonstrated low CSF leak rates with BioGlue use in cranial and spinal dural repair, including favorable early postoperative outcomes compared with fibrin sealants.90,91 Despite these reports, BioGlue has been shown to elicit a pronounced inflammatory reaction, characterized by acute pyogenic inflammation and chronic granulomatous foreign-body response.92 In addition, the Food and Drug Administration issued warnings about neurosurgical use, and some institutions prohibit BioGlue contact with neural tissue.90 BioGlue ranges between $200 and $400 per kit.

Hemopatch

Hemopatch is a hybrid sealant composed of a bovine collagen matrix functionalized with N-hydroxysuccinimide-PEG, enabling hemostasis and tissue sealing through protein-reactive bonding.93 Biomechanical testing demonstrates moderate burst pressure, and clinical studies report reduced CSF leak and fistula rates compared with standard closure. Hemopatch costs $300 to $500 per sheet.84,94,95

Limitations

Limitations include predominance of retrospective studies, single-institution series, and heterogeneous patient populations, which constrain comparisons across materials and procedures. Biomechanical performance metrics rely on ex vivo or animal models that may not reflect in vivo behavior. In addition, cost analyses are variably reported and institution-specific.

CONCLUSIONS

Intraoperative hemostasis and dural closure are critical determinants of neurosurgical outcomes. Traditional agents such as Gelfoam and Surgicel remain effective and cost-efficient for routine use, whereas flowable matrices provide advantages in challenging bleeding at higher cost. Autologous grafts remain the standard for dural reconstruction when available, with collagen-based allografts offering comparable efficacy without harvest morbidity, and synthetic materials providing value in revision settings. Among sealants, fibrin-based products have the most established safety profile, whereas PEG-based hydrogels require careful anatomic selection because of swelling-related risks. Optimal product selection balances efficacy, cost, anatomic constraints, and anticipated reoperation, with clear documentation to aid postoperative imaging interpretation. Despite widespread use, important evidence gaps remain, underscoring the need for comparative effectiveness studies as surgical adjunct materials and techniques continue to evolve.
