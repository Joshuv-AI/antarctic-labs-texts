Adverse Reactions to Blood Transfusion in Pediatric Cardiac Surgery: A Retrospective Cohort Study at a Single Center
Authors: V. Nithin Krishna, Mamatha Munaf, Vinu Rajendran, R. Amita, Divya P. Jacob, Sowmya Ramanan, Debasish Gupta, Shrinivas Vitthal Gadhinglajkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:Blood transfusion is an integral part of pediatric cardiac surgery. Our study intends to analyze the incidence of transfusion reactions in pediatric cardiac surgery patients. Secondary objectives were to identify the common type of transfusion reaction and the factors that increase the incidence of transfusion reaction.Design:Retrospective cohort study.Setting:University-level hospital.Participants:Children who underwent cardiac surgery between January 2014 and October 2024 and received blood transfusions.Interventions:Nil.Measurements and Main Results:Data on blood transfusion were gathered from the institute’s database and the Transfusion Reaction Register in the Department of Transfusion Medicine. Patients were evaluated based on age, gender, diagnosis, clinical history, surgical procedures and transfused blood components. Among 13,482 transfusions, 24 cases of transfusion reactions were identified. The reaction rate per 1000 units of transfusion was highest for PRBCs (24.9 per 1000 units of transfusion), followed by FFP ( 3.14 per 1000 units of transfusion). Patients with a history of prior blood transfusion had a 3.02 times higher likelihood of experiencing a transfusion reaction compared to those without prior transfusions (Odds ratio = 3.02, 95% confidence interval: 1.35–6.76). Allergic transfusion reaction was the most prevalent, accounting for 70.8% of cases.Conclusion:The incidence of blood transfusion reactions among pediatric cardiac surgery patients is 0.18%. Transfusion of PRBCs was the most frequent cause of reactions in this group, followed by that of FFPs.

INTRODUCTION

Blood transfusion is an integral part of pediatric cardiac surgery.[1] In children, owing to a lesser blood volume compared to the adult population, hemodilution is inevitable without adding blood to the priming volume.[2] Platelet dysfunction and dilution of coagulation factors lead to a nearly universal need for blood component transfusion in neonates who undergo complex congenital cardiac surgeries.[3] Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, reexploration, redo surgeries, intraoperative injury to cardiac chambers, prolonged duration of surgery, coagulopathy, and sepsis can warrant multiple or even massive transfusion in this population which can lead to adverse events to blood transfusion.[4] Conditions, like the truncus arteriosus (DiGeorge syndrome), have syndromic associations and require specialized irradiated blood products.[5]

Multiple studies have analyzed the transfusion practices, blood conservation strategies, duration of blood storage, and the postoperative infection potential of transfusion in pediatric cardiac surgery.[678] However, limited data exist regarding the transfusion reactions. Guo et al. studied the transfusion reactions in general pediatric population retrospectively.[9] It was a single-center retrospective study where they identified increased transfusion reactions in patients with hematological system abnormalities. Since the patient population comprised of general pediatric patients, the indication for transfusion was hematological malignancy or a medical disorder. The study cannot be extrapolated to pediatric cardiac surgery patients whose indications, physiology, and mechanism of bleeding differ widely from the study cohort.

To our knowledge, as no published studies report hemovigilance-confirmed acute transfusion reaction incidence restricted to pediatric cardiac surgery patients, our study primarily aimed to identify the incidence of transfusion reactions in this group.[9] The secondary objectives were to identify the type of transfusion reaction that is predominant in children undergoing cardiac surgery and to identify the factors that can increase the risk of transfusion reaction in this population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted in our university-level hospital, performing nearly 350 to 450 pediatric heart surgeries per year. After Institute Ethics Committee clearance (SCT/IEC/2351/ER/JANUARY/2025), the electronic medical records of the Institute were accessed and the total number of patients under 18 years of age, operated for congenital heart disease in this duration, was identified. Transfusion Reaction Register in the Department of Transfusion Medicine was analyzed to identify the patients under 18 years of age who underwent pediatric cardiac surgery and developed transfusion reaction perioperatively till discharge between January 2014 and October 2024. This gave the cohort of patients who developed transfusion reactions (transfusion reaction group). The patients who developed transfusion reaction, as per the registry, were manually separated from the total number of operated children to give the cohort of patients who did not develop blood transfusion reactions (no transfusion reaction group). Acute transfusion reaction, in our study, was defined as any adverse event or undesirable response to blood or blood component transfusion that developed within 24 h of transfusion of blood and blood products, which was confirmed by detailed clinical evaluation and immunohematological analysis.[10] Recognized adverse events to blood transfusion, such as transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion-associated dyspnea (TAD), and febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction (FNHTR), were defined as per the National Healthcare Safety Network Biovigilance Component Hemovigilance Module Surveillance Protocol.[10] Specific parameters, like age, gender, details of cardiopulmonary bypass, history of previous transfusions, type of cardioplegia, age and volume of transfused products, additive solutions in the blood products, history of allergy, and immunosuppression, were analyzed and compared between the groups. The patient identification details were not collected or used in any way.

Role of investigators

Passive reporting is followed for reporting transfusion reactions. Investigators collected the data of Transfusion Reactions → Data were analyzed. The case definitions and imputability assessments were carried out by Transfusion Medicine experts at the time of each reaction. The investigators did not have any role in determining the case definitions or assigning imputability.[10]

Imputability criteria

Imputability is classified into

Definite (certain)

Clear evidence beyond reasonable doubt that the reaction is due to the transfusion.

Probable (likely)

A causal relationship is probable; the reaction cannot be reasonably explained by another cause.

Possible

The reaction may be due to transfusion, but other causes are also equally plausible.

Unlikely (doubtful)

Evidence suggests that the reaction is more likely explained by causes other than transfusion.

Excluded (not related)

Conclusive evidence that the reaction is unrelated to transfusion.

Adjudication was performed by the senior most-Professor in charge of the Blood Centre, in accordance with the Hemovigilance document guidelines.

Statistics

Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage and were analyzed by the Chi-square test. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Confidence interval (CI) for proportions was calculated by Clopper–Pearson method. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, calculated by Fisher’s exact P-test. Data analysis was performed using Jamovi 2.5 3.

RESULTS

A total of 5682 patients aged <18 years underwent cardiac surgery and received blood transfusion in our institute between January 2014 and October 2024. Twenty-four of them developed transfusion reactions, while 5658 children had an uneventful blood transfusion [Figure 1]. A total of 13,482 blood transfusions were needed. On an average, 2.37 units of blood components were transfused per child. The incidence of transfusion reaction was 0.18% per transfusion unit (Unit level incidence = 24/13,482 = 0.18% [95% CI 0.11%–0.27%]) and 0.42% per patient (Patient level incidence = 24/5682 = 0.42% [95% CI 0.27%–0.63%]). There was no statistically significant association between gender and the likelihood of transfusion reactions [Table 1]. Children between 5 and 10 years of age constituted the majority in the reaction group (37.5%), compared to 18.8% in the nonreaction group. This difference was statistically significant, with a P = 0.038, suggesting this age group as a potential risk factor for transfusion reactions. The diagnoses associated with transfusion reactions showed significant variability. Conditions, like D-TGA (33.3%), VSD (16.7%), and TA + VSD + PS (8.3%), were in higher proportions in the reaction group compared to the nonreaction group. Diagnoses, such as ASD, TOF, and TAPVC, were common in the nonreaction group and had lower proportions in the reaction group. The comparison yielded a statistically significant P = 0.001, highlighting the role of specific diagnoses in influencing the transfusion reaction risk [Table 2]. Among types of surgery, total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) surgery accounted for 37.5% of the transfusion reactions. Other surgeries like intracardiac repair and total anomalous pulmonary venous connections repair, though more common in the nonreaction group, were significantly less in proportion in the reaction group. The comparisons point toward the influence of surgery type on transfusion reaction rates, with a P = 0.001 [Table 3].

Around 62.5% of patients with transfusion reactions received Del Nido cardioplegia, 25% received blood cardioplegia, and 12.5% had no cardioplegia. In the nonreaction group, the distribution was 60%, 31.8%, and 8.2%, respectively. There was no significant relationship between the type of cardioplegia used and the occurrence of transfusion reactions (P = 0.629). Among patients with transfusion reactions, 95.8% underwent on-pump surgery compared to 91.8% in the no-reaction group. There was no significant association between on-pump procedures and the occurrence of transfusion reactions (P = 0.475).

A history of previous transfusion was present in 54.2% of patients with transfusion reactions, compared to 28.1% in those without reactions. Previous transfusion was defined as receiving any blood or blood component transfusion any time from birth. There was a strong association between previous transfusions and the risk of transfusion reactions (P = 0.005). Odds ratio (OR) of developing transfusion reaction in patients who received prior transfusion compared to those who did not was 3.02 (OR = 3.02, 95% CI: 1.35–6.76) [Figure 3]. Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) was the most frequently implicated component (50% of the transfusion reactions), followed by packed red blood cells (PRBC) (33.3%) and leucoreduced packed red blood cells (8.3%). Other components, like cryoprecipitate and leucoreduced platelets, were less common (4.2% each). These findings point toward specific components being more likely to provoke transfusion reactions, though no statistical test was performed. Around 75% of the patients developed allergic transfusion reaction, 12.5% of patients developed FNHTR, and 8.3% had TAD. Imputability assigned was definite for all allergic transfusion reactions and FNHTR and possible for both cases of TAD. All patients with transfusion reactions (100%) received massive transfusion. Massive transfusion was defined as the replacement of more than one blood volume of transfusion within 24 h or 50% of the patient’s total blood volume within 3 h.[11] No patients in this group had a history of transplantation, immunosuppression, or allergies. Additionally, only 12.5% received blood products with additives like saline adenine glucose mannitol, compared to 87.5% without additives. These factors provide descriptive insights but could not be statistically analyzed.

DISCUSSION

Acute blood transfusion reactions are adverse events that occur within 24 h of administration of blood and blood products. It can vary in intensity from a febrile reaction to life-threatening anaphylaxis.[12] Pediatric cardiac surgery warrants blood and blood component transfusion owing to inherent hemodilution during the cardiopulmonary bypass, quickly reaching transfusion thresholds with less loss of blood, dilutional coagulopathy, platelet dysfunction after cardiopulmonary bypass, inherent presence of coagulation inhibitors and immature coagulation pathway in neonates, and prolonged duration of some of the cardiac surgery procedures.[3] Hanson et al. analyzed datasets from the hospitals in the United States and reported that nearly all the patients undergoing pediatric cardiac surgery, especially those in the univentricular pathway, received multiple transfusions.[13] Our study agrees with the observation since, on average, 2.37 units of blood components were transfused per patient during our study period.

This is the first study to evaluate the incidence of transfusion reactions in pediatric cardiac surgery. We found an incidence of 0.18%. The study by Kumar et al. reported transfusion reactions in 0.2% of the hospitalized patients in the United States, while Guo et al. reported an incidence of 1.35% among the general population of pediatric patients.[91415] Acute transfusion reactions are often reported to vary between 0.5% and 3%.[9]

Significant influence of age (χ2 = 8.433, P = 0.038) and prior history of blood transfusion (n0 = 13, 54.2% vs n = 1591, 28.1%; χ2 = 8.003, P = 0.005) was seen in our study. Children between 5 and 10 years of age constituted 37.5% of the transfusion reaction group. This age group is very often when staged univentricular/biventricular procedures are completed. This might indirectly mean they have already received transfusions during the previous surgery, which could have caused sensitization and further reactions, which aligns with the previous studies.[91617] Rather than the effect of age, the redo nature of surgery and previous transfusion might be a confounding factor here. In accordance with this supposition, nearly one-third of the patients underwent TCPC in the transfusion reaction group and more than 70% of these patients had a complex congenital heart disease. There was a significant association between the development of transfusion reaction and the diagnosis (P = 0.001) and the type of surgical procedure (P = 0.001).

A parameter that had no significant association with transfusion reaction was surgeries conducted on cardiopulmonary bypass. Cardiopulmonary bypass is proven to cause a pro-inflammatory response and an anti-inflammatory response to compensate the same.[18] Surgeries performed under cardiopulmonary bypass is associated with a significant increase in the postoperative transfusion requirement.[19] Though transfusion reaction in this group is not yet studied, both the immune response and increased transfusion requirements after the cardiopulmonary bypass are expected to increase the postoperative transfusion reactions theoretically. However, we could not find any significant difference. The number of patients who underwent off-pump procedures was 4.2% (n = 1) in the reaction group and 8.2% (n = 462) in the nonreaction group. The inadequate number of off-pump cases in the reaction group might be influencing our observation. It is also possible that rather than the number of blood products transfused, prior sensitization is influencing the development of transfusion reaction, though further studies are needed to comment any further.

The reaction rate per 1000 units of transfusion was highest for PRBCs (24.9 per 1000 units of transfusion), followed by FFP (3.14 per 1000 units of transfusion). Study by Yune and O’Brien identified highest rate of transfusion reaction in patients receiving platelets, followed by RBCs and cryoprecipitate.[20] Only 4.2% of the transfusion reactions in our study population was after the transfusion of platelets. This could be because infants <1 year of age had significantly lesser transfusion reaction in our study. Most of the platelets were transfused after cardiopulmonary bypass as per standard institute practice was to neonates or young infants. In addition, leucoreduced platelets are often provided for transfusion in children, which could have reduced the event in our cohort. Analysis of transfusion reaction over 7 years in a multi-organ transplant center has identified FFP as the major cause of transfusion reaction, followed by RBCs.[21] The predominance of FFP reactions could be due to the presence of plasma proteins that are immunogenic, especially in sensitized individuals.[20] Predominant type of transfusion reaction in our patients was allergic transfusion reaction. This agrees with other studies.[202223]

Statistical analysis could not be carried out to assess the influence of immunosuppression, history of transplantation, or known allergies owing to zero event frequency in the no-reaction group. The greatest challenge while confirming transfusion reactions in pediatric cardiac surgery is the confounding effect of cardiopulmonary bypass which can cause hematuria, elevated airway pressures or elevated body temperature after the surgery, use of medications that might trigger an allergic response like antibiotics or protamine, effect of the disease process in the findings pertaining to the transfusion reaction like elevated airway pressure, and delayed recognition owing to the patient being fully covered till drapes are removed at the end of the surgery. However, all the transfusion reactions are confirmed by a detailed clinical assessment, and immunohematological workup before confirmation and the imputability assignment and are hence highly reliable [Figure 4, Table 5]. The drawback of a retrospective study is inherent in our study, though extensive documentation and data backup has allowed us to accurately retrieve the needed information. All patients are closely monitored during cardiac surgery and any changes in hemodynamics, increase in airway pressure, increase in temperature or hemolysis, evident as urinary discoloration is actively monitored for following transfusion of every unit of blood or blood products. As patients are under drapes during the surgery with cardiac surgery itself effectively confounding the hemodynamic changes that can possibly be due to blood transfusion, under reporting could be a possible drawback of our study. Other study limitations include potential selection bias, misclassification, confounding by indication (complex cases get more transfusions) and center-specific practices (single high-volume tertiary center) that can limit external validity. Cardiopulmonary bypass can alter the normal physiological assumptions of transfusion reactions (febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reaction, hemolytic transfusion reactions, TACO, TRALI) and administration of drugs with allergic potential, like antibiotics or protamine, can confound the assessment (allergic transfusion reactions). With only 24 events, our study is underpowered to detect small to moderate associations.
