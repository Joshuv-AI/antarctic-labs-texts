Prediction of remaining useful life for electronic equipment based on online PINN
Authors: Feng Han, Bo Mo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs critical components of spacecraft systems, electronic equipment requires accurate remaining useful life (RUL) prediction to ensure reliable operations. Traditional physics-based and data-driven methods are limited by poor generalization and substantial data demands, respectively. While Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) integrate physical laws to enhance accuracy, they remain susceptible to performance degradation under shifting operational conditions due to catastrophic forgetting. This paper proposes a novel online PINN framework integrated with continual learning to dynamically adapt to new degradation patterns. Validated on NASA’s IGBT dataset, our method reduces RUL prediction error to 27.2% of a traditional LSTM model and achieves an online update time of less than 800ms/cycle. These results demonstrate a significant improvement in robustness and accuracy, providing a superior solution for RUL estimation of aerospace power electronics under extreme environments.Keywords: Aerospace, remaining useful life; Physics-Informed neural networks; Continual learningSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The prognostics of remaining useful life (RUL) for spacecraft electronic equipment constitutes a critical technological foundation for ensuring mission success in aerospace engineering. As the central nervous system of modern spacecraft, onboard electronics operate under extreme orbital conditions characterized by intense radiation fluxes (± 2 × 10^12 protons/cm²·s), drastic thermal cycling (-180 °C to + 150 °C), and prolonged zero-maintenance requirements (typically > 15 years)1. The U.S. Defense Advanced Re-search Projects Agency (DARPA) reports that over 68% of spacecraft anomalies originate from electronic system degradation, with power electronics accounting for 42% of these failures2. Current RUL prediction methodologies predominantly adopt two paradigms: physics-of-failure models based on accelerated life testing (ALT) and data-driven approaches utilizing telemetry analytics. The former leverages parametric degradation equations (e.g., modified Arrhenius-Eyring hybrid models3 but struggles with multi-physics coupling effects in actual space environments. The latter, empowered by machine learning architectures like CatBoost and DBSO-optimized networks, faces challenges in physical interpretability and operational condition adaptability. Figure 1 shows the real-time monitoring data of 8 key voltage channels on the integrated flight control power board, which not only reflects the dynamic response characteristics of the integrated flight control assembly under harsh working conditions, but also serves as an important basis for predicting the remaining life of the system. In the aviation industry, the gradual aging of the integrated flight control assembly can lead to increased power losses and rising operating temperatures, ultimately potentially causing catastrophic failures.

The Insulated Gate Bipolar Transistor(IGBT), as a high-performance power semi-conductor switching device, has found extensive applications in the aerospace sector due to its fast switching speed, high current-carrying capacity, and low switching losses. In spacecraft power systems, IGBTs play critical roles in solar array maximum power point tracking (MPPT) and lithium-ion battery charge-discharge management, where their high-voltage capabilities (up to 6.5 kV) ensure efficient energy conversion under extreme orbital conditions. For electric propulsion systems like ion thrusters, IGBT-based inverters enable kHz-level switching to generate precise high-voltage pulses for plasma acceleration, as demonstrated in NASA’s Deep Space 1 mission. The device’s radiation-hardened variants (e.g., SiC-IGBT hybrids in ESA’s BepiColombo Mercury probe) address unique space challenges including cosmic ray-induced single-event effects and − 150 °C to + 150 °C thermal cycling. In attitude control subsystems, IGBT-driven brushless DC motors achieve micro-radian-level precision in reaction wheel assemblies, while commercial constellations like Starlink leverage their compact HiPak modules for high-density power distribution. However, the harsh space environment imposes stringent reliability requirements - typical spacecraft demand 15-year operational lifespans with zero maintenance, making RUL prediction paramount.

Currently, RUL estimation methods are mainly divided into two categories: physics-based prediction techniques and data-driven prediction techniques4– 5. Physics-based methods typically start from the internal working mechanisms of the relevant system, establishing mathematical models that reflect the physical laws of system performance degradation. For example, under the premise of unchanged storage conditions and failure mechanisms, Tao Xueqing and others initially analyzed the magnitude of stress applied during accelerated storage, considered the interaction of multiple load factors, and modified the Arrhenius and Eyring models to establish a model for converting product life under accelerated and natural conditions6. However, the obtained physical models are often not precise enough, and the modeling process can easily lose the nonlinear relationships between parameters, leading to increased errors.

Data-driven RUL assessment methods learn the mapping relationship between input and output from historical aging data of electronic device output characteristics. These methods are primarily divided into probability statistical methods and machine learning methods. Among them, machine learning methods first select several historical data as training samples and then use training algorithms to predict failures after training. Liu Jiacheng utilized NASA’s publicly available aging experiment data for IGBT life prediction, analyzing and comparing the accuracy of model predictions7; Chen Changji et al., based on data feature extraction, employed the Differential Brainstorm Optimization (DBSO) algorithm to optimize the CatBoost model for fault diagnosis8. Machine learning methods avoid the complex establishment of traditional mathematical models and the acquisition of expert experience. However, they require complete historical data from the initial use to the final failure of the observed object; otherwise, the credibility of the prediction results may be reduced.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) integrate physical constraints into neural networks by embedding governing principles within the loss function9. This approach ensures that network training aligns with fundamental physical laws rather than relying purely on data-driven optimization10– 11, leading to more physically meaningful predictions. Initially introduced for solving mathematical equations such as ordinary and partial differential Eqs12. –13, PINNs have also been explored in power electronics for parameter estimation. Building upon parameter estimation, the application of PINNs has been extended to prognostics and health management in adjacent fields. However, their potential application in predicting the RUL of power electronic devices is still an area requiring further investigation.

Beyond physics-informed and continual learning approaches, recent studies have explored graph neural networks (GNNs) for fault detection and out-of-distribution recognition. For instance, the GCI-ODG framework leverages graph convolution and information propagation to detect complex industrial faults, while the EGN-OOD framework employs energy-based graph neural networks to improve out-of-distribution fault detection performance. These methods demonstrate the versatility of GNN-based detection in capturing relational dependencies among signals. Although promising, they primarily focus on graph-based representations rather than integrating physics constraints and continual learning, which is the focus of this work.

However, a trained neural network model aligns with the degradation patterns found in historical data. When operational conditions shift, these patterns also change. Due to the nature of deep learning, retraining a life assessment model with new degradation data can degrade predictive performance14– 15, a challenge known as catastrophic forgetting. Additionally, gathering all past degradation data for retraining is costly and often impractical16. Continual learning enhances a model’s ability to adapt to new tasks over time, mitigating catastrophic forgetting while maintaining learning efficiency.

Related works

IGBT RUL

Model-based methods establish mathematical relationships between a device’s lifespan and its influencing factors. These models predict the failure cycle count (Nf) of IGBT devices, often attributing failure to plastic strain induced by thermomechanical mismatches in packaging layers19. The Coffin-Manson model, which focuses on temperature fluctuations (ΔT), provided the basis for later developments20. The Nor-ris-Landzberg equation extends this by factoring in temperature cycling frequency (f)21. The Bayerer model integrates five physics-based parameters22: peak junction temperature (T(j, max)), heating duration (tₒₙ), DC current (I), bond wire diameter (D), and blocking voltage (V). Additionally, cumulative damage models employing rainflow counting techniques are frequently used alongside analytical models for failure estimation23. In addition to mainstream analytical models, there are also physical lifespan models based on energy hypotheses24.

Neural networks (NNs) have been utilized for IGBT RUL prediction, demonstrating slightly better performance than comparable methods like Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS). To enhance input quality, precursor signal data underwent preprocessing, with Principal Component Analysis applied to extract key time-domain features before being fed into feedforward neural networks. More advanced deep learning techniques have also been explored, including Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) networks, which have been employed for RUL estimation in IGBTs25. While LSTMs offer improved accuracy over traditional model-based methods, they demand larger training datasets. Additionally, attention mechanisms have recently been introduced in IGBT RUL prediction for the first time.

PINN

PINNs have been applied in power electronics. Zhao et al. utilized PINNs for parameter estimation in DC-DC buck converters26. Zhang et al. leveraged PINN-based physical modeling to assess the impedance of voltage source converters27. Wu et al. introduced AutoPINN, integrating AutoML with PINNs for automated model design, demonstrating improved performance in parameter estimation for power electronic converters28. Chen et al. developed the PI-LSTM method, combining PINNs with LSTMs to enhance parameter estimation in DC-DC buck converters29.

Hybrid methods incorporate physical knowledge into data-driven modeling, combining the strengths of both model-based and data-driven approaches. This integration enhances predictive capabilities, making hybrid techniques a promising direction. Busca et al.30 introduced a particle filter-based approach that embeds crack propagation physics to estimate the RUL of IGBT modules. Arias Chao et al.31 developed a hybrid frame-work inspired by PINNs, demonstrating improved RUL prediction for turbofan engines with reduced training data compared to purely data-driven methods. However, their approach did not tackle the challenge of catastrophic forgetting. More recently, Shen et al.32 proposed an embedded physical information network for blade crack detection, incorporating dynamic multi-level credibility into the training process to enhance interpretability and stability. While this study highlights the potential of embedding physics into deep learning for fault diagnosis, it remains limited to offline settings and does not address the continual adaptation problem that our work focuses on.

Continual learning

Several approaches have been developed to preserve past knowledge and mitigate catastrophic forgetting. Li et al. established knowledge distillation in continual learning through their Learning Without Forgetting (LWF) framework, enabling cross-task stability during sequential model training33. This technique remains a cornerstone for continual learning, spawning diverse algorithmic extensions such as Dhar et al.’s attention distillation loss for preserving inter-task feature relationships34. Douillard et al. introduced ensemble output distillation loss to limit feature changes35. Rusu et al. created “Progressive Networks,” which merge networks corresponding to different tasks into one36. Abati et al. introduced task-specific gating modules within convolutional layers to selectively activate neurons for learning new tasks37. However, such approaches introduce the stability-plasticity dilemma, where a continual learning system must balance retaining prior knowledge with adapting to new information.

Continual learning mimics human learning and aims to develop efficient models capable of continuous learning across a series of tasks. It mainly addresses two issues: solving the inherent problem of catastrophic forgetting in neural network design and improving the generalizability of training models. This ensures models have both plasticity (the ability to learn new knowledge) and stability (the ability to retain old knowledge), thus solving the stability-plasticity dilemma.

This work belongs to the hybrid methods for IGBT RUL estimation. It incorporates physical rules into network training and introduces continual learning to adapt to changing working environments, thereby advancing current data-driven NN methods. Recently, Shen et al.38 proposed an unsupervised mixed-up differential replay mechanism with the concept of a “forgetting frontier” for blade crack propagation monitoring. Their work demonstrates the effectiveness of continual learning in mitigating catastrophic forgetting in industrial fault detection tasks. However, it remains focused on unsupervised anomaly detection rather than online RUL prediction, which is the main goal of our study.

Federated learning

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a promising decentralized training paradigm for RUL prediction, as it enables collaborative model training across multiple clients while preserving data privacy. Xu et al.39 proposed an adaptive sampling-based ensemble FL framework for aircraft engine RUL prediction, which dynamically adjusts client participation based on data quality. Their method effectively improves prediction accuracy and robustness under heterogeneous data distributions.

Building upon this line of research, several recent studies have further extended FL to address key challenges in industrial prognostics. Cai et al.40 developed FedCov, a trustworthy FL framework that converts continuous RUL values into discrete categories to mitigate client heterogeneity. By incorporating a converter module and uncertainty estimation, FedCov alleviates model drift issues and achieves state-of-the-art performance on the C-MAPSS benchmark. Liu41 proposed a federated learning framework with prototype learning and physical information fusion for turbine blade RUL prediction. This approach integrates sensor monitoring data with digital twin-based physical degradation data, and leverages SHAP analysis to enhance model interpretability. Guo et al.42 introduced FedRUL, which adopts an edge–cloud collaboration mechanism combining local convolutional autoencoders (CAEs) with a global encoder and RUL predictor. This design addresses the computational and data limitations of edge clients while ensuring model confidentiality. Chen et al.43 presented RUL-FLTNP, which integrates FL with Taylor-expansion-based network pruning to produce lightweight RUL predictors. Their method alternates pruning and recovery in local clients, enabling compact and efficient models suitable for deployment on edge devices.

These advances highlight the versatility of FL in addressing privacy, heterogeneity, interpretability, and deployment efficiency in decentralized RUL prediction. However, most existing FL-based methods remain focused on distributed learning and data privacy, without considering the issue of catastrophic forgetting in dynamically evolving operating environments. This limitation further motivates our proposed framework, which combines PINNs with continual learning to achieve online RUL prediction under non-stationary conditions.

Methodology

Considering the harsh operational conditions of space missions, where components are exposed to extreme thermal and mechanical stresses, accurate RUL prediction models are indispensable. Therefore, this paper utilizes a complex network model to handle degradation data and predict RUL. The entire process can be divided into several key steps and components, as shown in Fig. 2. First, the model receives real degradation data as input, representing the actual observed degradation of the equipment. Subsequently, the encoder processes the input data. The encoder’s function is to compress and encode the input data, extracting important features known as feature values.

Structure of networks.

Once the feature extraction is complete, part of the feature values are transmitted to the decoder. The decoder’s task is to reconstruct the predicted degradation data using these feature values. After generating the RUL predictions, these data are sent to the discriminator and the physical model. The discriminator compares the predicted results with the actual data, yielding a loss value to ensure the accuracy and validity of the data. The physical model uses actual physical laws and empirical predictions to validate the predicted degradation data, obtaining a physical model loss value. This ensures that the predictions are meaningful not only on a data level but also on a physical level.

Another part of the process involves using the network model to handle the feature values and generate RUL predictions, which are then compared to the actual RUL data. The network model adopts an encoder-decoder structure, extracting feature values from the input degradation data and generating predictions. These predictions are then analyzed and validated using the RUL prediction network. Finally, the accuracy and reliability of the predictions are ensured through the discriminator and the physical model. The model uses a 3-layer BiLSTM encoder (256 units/layer) and a decoder with LSTM + 2 dense layers (128→64 units), integrating multi-head attention (8 heads), and the Dropout rate is 0.3.

Framework overview

Our proposed online PINN framework integrates four core components to address the challenges of RUL prediction under dynamic operational conditions: (1) an attention-based LSTM encoder-decoder network for temporal feature extraction and RUL prediction, (2) a discriminator module for ensuring prediction quality through adversarial training, (3) physics-informed regularization incorporating semiconductor degradation constraints, and (4) a continual learning mechanism enabling online adaptation without catastrophic forgetting.

The framework operates in two phases: an initial offline training phase using historical degradation data, followed by an online adaptation phase where the model continuously updates as new operational data becomes available. The attention-based LSTM processes time-series degradation signals and generates initial RUL predictions. The discriminator validates prediction quality by distinguishing between real and predicted degradation patterns. Physical constraints ensure predictions conform to semiconductor failure physics through monotonicity and boundary conditions. Finally, continual learning enables dynamic adaptation while preserving historical knowledge through the DAARN architecture.

The integration of these components creates a unified optimization objective that balances data-driven accuracy, physical consistency, and adaptive capability, specifically designed for the demanding requirements of aerospace power electronics monitoring.

In this paper, we aim to fully utilize the measured priors by using measurement results as inputs to the discriminator. Since the two domains carry different types of information, directly merging them could lead to confusion. Instead of the Conditional Generative Adversarial Network (CGAN) approach that combines conditional information with feature vectors, we propose a Y-shaped structure to integrate information from the image and measurement domains separately. Physical degradation mechanisms in IGBT devices

To establish rigorous theoretical foundations for our physics-informed constraints, we first examine the fundamental degradation mechanisms governing IGBT lifetime. IGBT failures primarily occur through two interconnected physical processes: bond wire degradation and solder fatigue, both driven by thermomechanical stress cycling.

Bond wire degradation follows the Coffin-Manson relationship, where thermal expansion coefficient mismatches between aluminum bond wires (αAl ≈ 24 × 10⁻⁶/K) and silicon chips (αSi ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶/K) generate plastic strain accumulation. This process is inherently unidirectional and irreversible, as each thermal cycle contributes additional microstructural damage through grain boundary sliding and void nucleation. Results and discussion

Experimental setup

This paper evaluates the performance of PINN-based RUL estimation through experiments using NASA’s complete IGBT degradation aging dataset. Two baseline bottom-layer Recurrent Neural Networks ​​(RNNs), vanilla RNN and LSTM, are considered in the PINN approach. The evaluation protocol systematically assesses recurrent architectures through dual testing paradigms: intra-device evaluation trains and tests models on identical hardware configurations to measure learning capacity, while cross-device generalization employs distinct devices for training versus testing to quantify adaptation potential. For baseline analysis, vanilla RNN performance is quantified under both paradigms, whereas LSTM generalization metrics are exclusively reported on unseen device data. Concurrently, the experimental framework incorporates ablation studies to isolate the effects of two PINN regularization constraints on both model convergence dynamics and ultimate prediction fidelity.

During network training, we use the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) algorithm, an enhanced version of the stochastic gradient descent optimization method. Mean Square Error (MSE) and 2 score are used as evaluation metrics, commonly used standards for measuring regression problem performance. The 2 score is a statistical measure that represents the proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that can be predicted from the independent variable(s).

The hyperparameter equilibrates residual prediction errors and monotonicity regularization terms, maintaining commensurate loss contributions through empirical calibration. Experimental configurations allocated a weighting coefficient of 0.1 to the monotonic constraint for optimal gradient equilibrium.

The IGBT dataset and pre-processing

This benchmark dataset, sourced from NASA Prognostics Center of Excellence’s public repository, features the IRG4BC30KD power semiconductor module (600 V/15A, TO220 package) from International Rectifier44. Experimental data were acquired through controlled thermal cycling tests employing pulsed gate excitation, with comprehensive monitoring of operational parameters including gate-emitter potential (V_ge), collector-emitter voltage (V_ce), and collector current (I_ce).

Figure 5 illustrates the characteristics of the preprocessed IGBT degradation dataset used in our experiments. The experimental results identify transistor latch-up as the primary failure mechanism, characterized by collector-emitter current surge and V_ce voltage collapse. Although latch-up initiates thermal runaway, a temperature threshold controller prevents physical damage by terminating tests when exceeding preset limits, preserving device functionality post-failure to simulate operational failure conditions.

IGBT degradation data preprocessing results.

The experimental protocol employed pulsed gate excitation on the IGBT, generating synchronized square-wave patterns in collector-emitter voltage (V_ce). Raw measurements underwent cycle-synchronous averaging, compressing each waveform cycle into single representative samples. Subsequent z-score normalization (µ = 0, σ = 1) eliminated amplitude dependencies, followed by exponential moving average (EMA) filtering with temporal weighting coefficients emphasizing recent measurements, contrasting with equal-weight simple moving average (SMA) approaches to enhance neural network convergence.

Mechanistic analysis of component contributions

The systematic performance improvements observed in our ablation studies can be attributed to specific computational mechanisms within each component:

Attention mechanism enhancement (0.69→0.78): The attention mechanism addresses the temporal heterogeneity inherent in IGBT degradation processes. Unlike uniform sequence weighting in standard RNNs, attention dynamically prioritizes time steps corresponding to critical degradation events (e.g., thermal stress peaks, current surge episodes). This selective focus enables the model to capture non-linear degradation acceleration phases that are crucial for accurate RUL prediction. The 13% accuracy improvement reflects the model’s enhanced ability to distinguish between gradual wear-out processes and sudden degradation events.

Discriminator integration (0.78→0.80): The discriminator provides adversarial feedback that regularizes the generator’s prediction distribution, preventing mode collapse to trivial solutions. Specifically, it enforces distributional consistency between predicted and observed degradation patterns, ensuring that the model captures the full spectrum of degradation variability rather than converging to mean predictions. This mechanism is particularly valuable for IGBT datasets where degradation patterns exhibit significant inter-device variability due to manufacturing tolerances and operational stress variations.

Physics-Informed constraints (0.80→0.82): The integration of monotonicity and boundary constraints acts as a physics-based regularizer that constrains the solution space to physically realizable trajectories. This regularization effect becomes more pronounced in data-sparse regions where purely data-driven models might extrapolate to non-physical solutions. The 2.5% improvement demonstrates that physics-informed constraints provide crucial guidance when historical data alone is insufficient to constrain the prediction space.

Continual learning optimization (0.82→0.92): The substantial 12% improvement from continual learning mechanisms reflects the model’s ability to leverage both historical knowledge and new operational patterns simultaneously. The DAARN architecture’s dual-branch design enables selective knowledge preservation while allowing adaptive plasticity, effectively expanding the model’s representational capacity without interference between old and new learning patterns.

Discussion on State-of-the-Art methods

While our experimental comparison focuses on traditional RNN and LSTM baselines, it is important to acknowledge recent advances in RUL prediction using Transformer architectures and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Transformer-based models have shown promise in sequence modeling tasks due to their self-attention mechanisms and parallel processing capabilities. However, their successful application typically requires large-scale training datasets (> 10^5 samples). The NASA IGBT dataset used in this study contains complete lifecycle data for only four devices, making direct application of standard Transformers potentially prone to overfitting.

Similarly, Graph Neural Networks have demonstrated effectiveness in capturing complex relationships in multi-sensor systems. However, GNN approaches primarily excel in scenarios with rich relational structures between multiple components or sensors. Our focus on single-device IGBT degradation monitoring, combined with the limited multi-device relational information in the available dataset, makes GNN architectures less directly applicable to this specific problem context.

Furthermore, both Transformer and GNN approaches typically operate in offline training paradigms and lack the integrated physics-informed constraints that are crucial for semiconductor degradation modeling. Our framework’s unique combination of physical constraints, continual learning capability, and online adaptation specifically addresses the dynamic operational challenges in aerospace applications that these advanced architectures do not inherently handle. Future work will explore hybrid architectures that combine self-attention mechanisms with our physics-informed continual learning framework while addressing the data scarcity challenges through physics-guided data augmentation strategies.

Mechanism-level performance analysis

The superior performance of our PI-LSTM framework stems from three synergistic mechanisms that address fundamental limitations in traditional RUL prediction. The physics-informed constraints create a regularized solution space that prevents the model from converging to mathematically valid but physically meaningless predictions, particularly critical when extrapolating beyond training data ranges. The attention-enhanced LSTM architecture enables selective temporal focus on degradation-critical events, allowing the model to distinguish between normal operational variations and genuine degradation signals that traditional uniform weighting approaches cannot capture. Most importantly, the continual learning mechanism addresses the dynamic nature of real-world degradation processes by enabling knowledge accumulation rather than replacement, allowing the model to leverage both historical patterns and newly observed degradation behaviors simultaneously. This multi-mechanism integration creates a robust prediction framework that maintains both mathematical rigor and physical consistency while adapting to evolving operational conditions.

Computational efficiency analysis

The time efficiency evaluation demonstrates the practical feasibility of our online learning strategy. Initial offline training requires approximately 45 min on a standard GPU (NVIDIA RTX 3080), comparable to baseline LSTM training times. However, the key advantage lies in online adaptation: each continual learning update cycle completes in 763ms ± 127ms, enabling real-time model adaptation without disrupting operational monitoring. Single RUL prediction inference takes 12ms ± 3ms, suitable for integration with spacecraft telemetry systems operating at 1–10 Hz sampling rates. The DAARN continual learning mechanism adds only 15% computational overhead compared to static inference, making it highly practical for resource-constrained aerospace applications.

Conclusion

This study validates the significant advantages of the PI-LSTM model based on Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINN) in predicting the RUL of industrial equipment. Experimental results demonstrate that the PI-LSTM model exhibits highly accurate and stable prediction capabilities across multiple test devices, particularly adept at handling dynamic changes during equipment operation. Compared to traditional RNN and LSTM models, PI-LSTM not only accurately tracks the variations in actual RUL but also effectively captures equipment degradation patterns, offering a reliable solution for predictive maintenance of industrial equipment. This method reduces the mean square error of RUL prediction to 27.2% of traditional LSTM (p < 0.01, t-test), and the online update time is less than 800ms/cycle, extending the average service life of IGBT to 12,300 cycles (± 5.7%).

Furthermore, we investigated the impact of continual learning mechanisms on model performance, highlighting their crucial role in enhancing the model’s adaptability in long-term monitoring and dynamic environments. Future research can further optimize the model architecture. Transformer architecture demonstrates advantages in sequence modeling, but its successful application often relies on large-scale training data (requiring > 10 ^ 5 sample sizes). The NASA dataset used in this study only contains full lifecycle data for four devices, and directly applying standard Transformers may lead to overfitting. Future work will explore: (a) data augmentation strategies based on physical constraints, such as degradation trajectory generation guided by thermodynamic equations; (b) Hybrid architecture design combines self attention mechanism with PINN differential constraints to enhance generalization ability under limited data. In particular, we aim to extend the model’s application to high-radiation and multi-excitation mechanical environments typical of space missions, validating it with real-world aerospace operational data. Specifically, we plan to implement transfer learning techniques where pre-trained Transformers on large-scale industrial degradation datasets will be fine-tuned using our physics-informed loss functions, combined with synthetic data generation through Monte Carlo simulations of IGBT thermal cycling processes. Additionally, we will investigate federated learning approaches to aggregate knowledge from multiple spacecraft missions while preserving data privacy, enabling model training on distributed small-sample datasets across different orbital environments.

The practical implementation of our framework in spacecraft operations can be achieved through integration with existing telemetry systems, where the sub-800ms online update capability enables real-time RUL monitoring during mission-critical phases. Ground-based mission control centers can utilize the physics-informed predictions to optimize maintenance scheduling and component replacement decisions, while the continual learning mechanism ensures model adaptation to unique orbital degradation patterns without requiring frequent ground-based retraining interventions.
