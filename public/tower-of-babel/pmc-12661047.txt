Interval-aware optimal control of PMSG-based wind energy conversion systems via piecewise Chebyshev inclusion
Authors: Navid Razmjooy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Due to the ever-increasing demand for wind energy, control strategies must be developed that can efficiently extract energy while ensuring system stability. Nevertheless, with significant excursion uncertainties like parametric variation, unmodeled dynamics, and environmental variations jeopardizing control performance and system reliability, the Wind Energy Conversion System (WECS) is subject to significant interval uncertainties. In this paper, a new Piecewise Chebyshev Inclusion Method (PCIM) is proposed for optimal control of Permanent Magnet Synchronous Generator (PMSG)-based WECS with uncertainty awareness under bounded parameter uncertainties. The method reformulates the finite-horizon Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) optimal control problem with interval-valued system matrices into Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI), thus enabling robust closed-loop control design. The proposed approach significantly reduces overestimation in propagating intervals with 98.5% accuracy in state bounding while consuming 60% less computational time than the Monte Carlo Method (MCM) by harnessing piecewise Chebyshev polynomial approximations. The simulation results show that the PCIM controller exhibits a 15% faster settling time and 32% lower Integral Absolute Error (IAE) under ± 15% parameter uncertainties as compared to conventional interval methods, namely, Natural Interval Method (NIM), Centered Interval Method (CIM), Taylor Interval Method (TIM), and Monte Carlo Method (MCM). The LMI-based formulation is also found to be real-time feasible with 10.2 s of solution time for a 10-second horizon, hence scalable for high-order WECS models. The results demonstrate the efficiency and robustness of the proposed method and highlight the practical possibility for application in modern wind energy systems.

Introduction

The wind energy source is both geographically spread and decentralized, and is a renewable energy source. Although this energy source is becoming more and more part of the contemporary power systems, its unpredictable nature and instabilities do not allow it to run permanently. In the past twenty years, the increasing demand for renewable energy has stimulated significant expansion of wind energy systems (WES) development.

With technological developments, WESs can attain power capacities of megawatts, which makes them a possible substitute for traditional energy sources. But with the growth in size and the extent of WESs, the energy conversion efficiency is an essential issue. Specific component designs-blades, gearboxes, and generators like complex control strategies, are critical towards maximizing energy conversion.

However, control strategies that are based on the principal goal of maximizing power and efficiency might neglect other important factors, including fatigue loads and severe weather conditions that can shorten the lifespan of the components and lead to high costs of operation. They would therefore require an adequate control strategy to stabilize the power output and reduce the fatigue loads.

Traditional control methods, such as Proportional-Integral (PI) control1, have been widely used in WESs, but they have limitations due to the uncertain nature of the system. The use of linear control techniques, such as the Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR)2, has been proposed as an alternative to traditional PI control3, but they may not be effective in dealing with non-linear systems. The development of non-linear control techniques, such as Fuzzy Logic Control (FLC)4 and Sliding Mode Control (SMC)5, has shown promise in addressing the non-linear nature of WESs. However, these techniques have their own limitations, such as the need for prior knowledge of the system and the potential for chattering.

Liu et al.6 developed a constant Stochastic Model Predictive Control (SMPC) method for an uncertain Wind Energy Conversion System (WECS) to ensure the stability and practicality of WECS under varying points of operation during the entire operation areas. A Luenberger observer has been utilized to predict model plant mismatch suggested via a linearization procedure, once a control structure based on the basis of a tube has been employed to tackle the issue of stochastic disturbance of wind speed. The practicality SMPC model has been ensured by employing artificial steady targets, once the consistency of WECS has been supported by adjusting the objective function and lengthening the terminal limitation. The efficacy of the suggested technique has been validated by conducting studies and simulations in diverse contexts.

Liu et al.7 suggested an SMPC method free from offset that had the purpose of achieving the rated power tracking free from offset, while the safety of WECS has been ensured throughout the procedure within the full load area. A Luenberger observer has been utilized, which was suggested by the linearization procedure, while an SMPC on the basis tube has been employed to address the issues relevant to stochastic disturbances of wind speed to meet the limitations of the probability. The replication findings, comprising the investigation carried out on fatigue, aerodynamics, structures, and turbulence (FAST), have been presented to validate the efficiency of the suggested technique using diverse contexts.

Shalbafian and Ganjefar8 has suggested a kind of nonlinear optimum second-order sliding mode controller by the use of Homotopy Perturbation Method (HPM) for maximizing the power of wind and minimizing the mechanical stress on the driving train. To create the nonlinear optimum controller, HPM was utilized for calculating the estimated solution of the fractional discrepancy HJB (Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman) formula. The nonlinear optimum controller has been integrated with a second-order sliding mode controller to reduce chattering and enhance robustness. The suggested controller ensured secure operation of wind turbines using uncertainties and effectively balanced the goals of maximizing power of wind capture while minimizing mechanical loads through reduced control input. To assess the performance of the suggested controller, its outcomes have been compared with those of several present control strategies in two distinct contexts. The findings demonstrated that the suggested controller represented the desired performance outcomes.

Mebkhouta et al.9 made a method that uses LQR and nonlinear control (Backstepping). The backstepping control has been utilized to represent a better efficiency utilizing stability conditions and a nonlinear strategy; however, it had several pitfalls. For this reason, optimal control LQR was utilized to overcome the issues related to the backstepping. Additionally, the combination of LQR control and Backstepping yielded a robust system that performed well even in challenging scenarios throughout wind system operations. It also demonstrated favorable efficacy regarding stability, chattering, and other evaluation metrics, contributing to the generation of high-quality energy. In summary, merging LQR control with Backstepping resulted in a more effective, adaptable, and efficacious control system that enhanced performance while minimizing the complexity of computation.

Liu et al.10 suggested a random MPC technique with a robust and probabilistic tube to find the high-precision power tracking of the output that could surpass the rated value. The current state was confined within a sturdy tube that was centered around a probabilistic tube, which ensured recursive practicality by reducing state error to the least amount. Meanwhile, the nominal state was directed toward the target point by creating a probabilistic tube based on the nominal stochastic system. As a result, the proposed tube-based stochastic MPC technique effectively mitigated the random wind speed disturbances, ensuring the safety of Wind Energy Conversion Systems (WECS) operation in areas with high wind speed. A range of virtual plant simulations, comprising tests from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) concerning fatigue, aerodynamics, structures, and turbulence (FAST), have been conducted to demonstrate the efficiency of the suggested controller across various contexts.

The majority of available methods address stochastic or norm-bounded uncertainties, and not many of them ensure bounds in interval uncertainties more realistic model in which the parameters are known only to be within a range of values. The novel Piecewise Chebyshev Inclusion Method (PCIM) of optimal control of PMSG-based WECS in the context of interval uncertainties is proposed in this paper. The algorithm uses Chebyshev polynomial approximation to create a tight control on system trajectories, and restructures the optimal control problem to a manageable Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI) problem structure to design robust closed-loop controllers. Piecewise Chebyshev inclusion for dynamic systems

For the time-domain analysis of WECS pieces, the classical Chebyshev inclusion method is extended to a piecewise formulation (PCIM). It divides the time horizon \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[{t}_{0},{t}_{f}\right]$$\end{document} into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} subintervals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[{t}_{k},\:{t}_{k+1}\right]$$\end{document}.

The state and control trajectories are approximated separately using Chebyshev polynomials, and the inclusion bounds are calculated independently within each subinterval.

This piecewise approach: Helps eliminate error accumulation over long horizons, Treats time-varying uncertainties (e.g., wind speed fluctuations), Allows development of an efficient LMI-based reformulation of the optimal control problem The PCIM seems particularly suitable for WECS, where system parameters such as air density \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:$$\end{document}, inertia \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{r}$$\end{document}, generator inductances \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{d}$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{q}$$\end{document} vary within known bounds and should be incorporated into a real-time control.

Piecewise Chebyshev inclusion method (PCIM) for optimal control of WECS

The interval parameter uncertainties pose a challenge for the optimal control of WECS due to the inherently nonlinear and time-varying nature of the system. In the case of the classical type finite-horizon LQR design, the solution to the nonlinear DRE becomes intractable if the system matrices are interval valued. This section introduces a novel framework, PCIM, which reformulates the uncertain optimal control problem into a convex optimization problem via LMIs, facilitating robust closed-loop control of PMSG-based WECS.

Problem formulation

Finite-horizon LQR under interval uncertainty

Consider the WECS nonlinear model described in Sect. 2, which is linearized around an operating point. Although, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:A$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B$$\end{document} are interval matrices, the solution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(t\right)$$\end{document} becomes an interval-valued matrix function, and direct integration leads to severe overestimation due to the wrapping effect.

Core innovation: Chebyshev expansion of the riccati solution

The main clue of PCIM is to approximate the Riccati solution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(t\right)$$\end{document} over the time horizon using piecewise Chebyshev polynomial interpolation to provide high accuracy along with minimal overestimation.

By dividing the time horizon \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[{t}_{0},{t}_{f}\right]$$\end{document} into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N$$\end{document} sub-intervals, i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[{t}_{k},{t}_{k+1}\right],k=0,\dots\:,N-1$$\end{document}. By considering each sub-interval, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is approximated as follows:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(t\right)\approx\:\sum\:_{i=0}^{d}\:{c}_{i}^{\left(k\right)}{T}_{i}\left(\frac{2t-{t}_{k}-{t}_{k+1}}{{t}_{k+1}-{t}_{k}}\right),\:t\in\:\left[{t}_{k},{t}_{k+1}\right]$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{i}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document} are Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{c}_{i}^{\left(k\right)}$$\end{document} are symmetric matrix coefficients.

This approximation allows the interval-valued Riccati equation to be replaced by a set of pointwise LMI constraints at the Chebyshev nodes, significantly reducing computational complexity and overestimation.

Using the Schur complement, this inequality is transformed into an LMI. Optimization framework

The PCIM-based optimal control problem is formulated as:37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{minimize\:}\text{t}\text{r}\text{a}\text{c}\text{e}\left(P\left({t}_{0}\right)\right)$$\end{document}

This is a convex semi-definite program (SDP) and can be efficiently solved using LMI solvers such as MOSEK or SDPT3 (for example via YALMIP in MATLAB).

Once \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is computed, the robust optimal control law is:39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{u}^{\text{*}}\left(t\right)=-{R}^{-1}{B}^{T}\left({\xi\:}_{\text{m}\text{o}\text{m}}\right)P\left(t\right)x\left(t\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\xi\:}_{\text{nom\:}}$$\end{document} is the nominal parameter value. The controller remains stable for all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\xi\:\subset\:{\Xi\:}$$\end{document} due to the robustness of the LMI solution.

Simulation and practical analysis

Here, such an exhaustive simulation study is provided for the proposed Piecewise Chebyshev Inclusion Method (PCIM) that is to be applied to the PMSG-based Wind Energy Conversion System (WECS) under parameter intervals uncertain. This is modeled along with its nonlinear dynamic system modeled for air density (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:\in\:\left[\text{1.15,1.35}\right]\:kg/{m}^{3}$$\end{document}), rotor inertia (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}_{r}\in\:\left[0.9{J}_{{r}_{0}},1.1{J}_{{r}_{0}}\right]$$\end{document}), and generator inductances (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{d},{L}_{q}\in\:\left[0.95{L}_{0},1.05{L}_{0}\right]$$\end{document}).

The control objectives serve to bring about regulation in terms of generator speed and torque while minimizing the exertion of control and ensuring robustness. The PCIM is compared against a nominal LQR controller designed for the same system but without uncertainty consideration. All simulations are conducted over 10 s horizon, and with a timestep of 1 s, hence all results are presented in per-unit (pu) values for consistency.

Comparative results

This section provides a comparison analysis of the Piecewise Chebyshev Inclusion Method (PCIM) with some other interval methods and also the Monte Carlo method. In the meantime, the Natural Interval Method (NIM)17 defines a simple method to resolve the interval problems with a computationally expensive process which is not also accurate for complicated problems. the Centered Interval Method (CIM)18 improves upon NIM by offering more accurate results, but computationally expensive process again. The Taylor Interval Method (TIM)19 is a more advanced technique that uses Taylor series expansions to solve interval problems.

All the methods are considered equally for an adjustable PMSG-based Wind Energy Conversion System with the same interval uncertainties for parameters like air density, rotor inertia, and generator inductance. A benchmark reference trajectory is constructed by simulating the nominal (deterministic) WECS model using a high-fidelity Runge-Kutta solver. Each method’s accuracy is evaluated as an average relative deviation from this reference, while computational time considers both model formulation and solution time through the YALMIP toolbox with the SDPT3 solver.

All aforementioned performance parameters were analyzed from the perspective of accuracy, maximum error, feasibility rate, and computation time throughout a 10-second simulation horizon. The scalability of the PCIM with increasing order of the system is also assessed. PCIM results are seen to provide the greatest accuracy and complexity among the methods considered, providing excellent prospects for use in real-time robust control for WECS.

Interval containment rate (ICR)

The interval methods need to ensure that the actual or nominal system performance does not exceed the anticipated limits at the entire admissible uncertainties. This is measured by ICR, which quantifies how often time steps exist; the high-fidelity nominal trajectory (simulated with precise parameters and no uncertainty) falls within the PCIM-computed interval envelope.

PCIM attains ICR = 100% as illustrated in Table 3, which mathematically rigorously validates its vertex-based LMI formulation of linearized WECS dynamics (Sect. 3.3). Monte Carlo using 1,000 samples, by contrast, has only 80% ICR because it cannot sample all of the elements, whereas NIM and CIM have either over-conservatism (NIM) or under-coverage (CIM). Such a 100% ICR is confirmation that PCIM provides hard, non-probabilistic guarantees that are required to execute safety-critical WECS.

Normalized mean absolute deviation (NMAD)

Although containment is a safe technique, the fidelity is monitored by NMAD, which is the time-average error between the interval midpoint and the nominal reference and is expressed as a ratio of the reference range (0.5–1.0.5.0 pu). The NMAD = 1.5 in PCIM is much lower than NIM (4.2%), CIM (3.1%), TIM (2.3%), and MCM (5.0%) as in Table 3. The small deviation here validates the fact that the bounds of PCIM are not merely only safe, but are also centered very close to the desired trajectory, which reduces the amount of conservatism that is needless. Because of this piecewise Chebyshev approximation, which does not propagate the wrapping effect, it better aligns the midpoint of the interval to nominal dynamics than classical interval arithmetic does.

Worst-case bound width ratio (WBR)

Bound tightness is important regarding the efficiency of control and actuator wear. WBR is the ratio of the width of the envelope containing the interval:45\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{W}\text{B}\text{R}=\underset{t\in\:[0,T]}{\text{m}\text{a}\text{x}}\:\left(\frac{{x}_{\text{u}\text{p}}\left(t\right)-{x}_{\text{l}\text{o}\text{w}}\left(t\right)}{\left|{x}_{\text{r}\text{e}\text{f}}\left(t\right)\right|}\right)\times\:100\text{\%}$$\end{document}

PCIM has WBR = 3.0% at t = 0.5 s (peak transient) compared to NIM (9.8%), CIM (7.5%), TIM (5.2%) and MCM (12.1%) which has much broader bounds (Table 3). Importantly, the initial 98.5% accuracy can now be reinterpreted to mean 100%-WBR/2 = 100 − 1.5 = 98.5% which is the percentage of the reference signal that is not absorbed by uncertainty-induced spread.

The tripartite accuracy evaluation in Table 3 addresses the uncertainty of the initial claim of 98.5% accuracy by grounding the performance in three different underlying, interpretable dimensions: ICR, NMAD, and WBR. PCIM is the only method to do all three without compromising precision, implying that it can perform perfect containment, which is inaccessible to sampling-based (MCM) and classical interval (NIM, CIM) approaches.

Conclusions

This study presented a new Piecewise Chebyshev Inclusion Method (PCIM) for robust optimal control of Permanent Magnet Synchronous Generator (PMSG)-based Wind Energy Conversion Systems (WECS) under interval parameter uncertainties. The limitation of the classical interval analysis techniques was overcome using this method and the Chebyshev polynomial approximations so as to minimize the wrapping effect and provide tighter bounds on the system trajectories. By reformulating the finite-horizon Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problem using the convex Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI) framework, the PCIM allows the design of a closed-loop optimal controller that is both robust and computationally tractable. Simulation results testify that the PCIM achieves a 98.5% accuracy in state bounding and reduces computation time by 60% compared to the Monte Carlo Method (MCM) while being superior in error bounds and feasibility to the NIM, CIM, and TIM. It has a 15% faster settling time and 32% lower IAE under ± 15% parameter variations, thus proving the excellent robustness and efficiency of the controller. However, the present approach assumes bounded but non-stochastic uncertainties, which may not capture the real-life scenario where uncertainties possess both probabilistic and interval characteristics. The method has worked well in simulations, but it is yet to be tested for real-time performance on hardware platforms. Concerning future works, there are three main directions: (1) extending PCIM to incorporate hybrid stochastic-interval uncertainties for more realistic modeling; (2) implementing the controller on a real-time digital simulator (RTDS) or hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) platform to verify its practical feasibility; and (3) investigating applicability on high-fidelity models like the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) 5 MW turbine to evaluate scalability in large-scale systems. Advancing in this direction would further consolidate the path from theoretical robust control to industrial deployment concerning current wind energy systems.
