Primary cardiac ewing sarcoma presenting as an infiltrative myocardial mass: a rare case with long-term survival
Authors: Ahmad Khajeh-Mehrizi, Mehdi Karimi, Masoud Mortezazadeh, Bahar Amiri, Hamidreza Zarei, Hana Saffar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEwing sarcoma (ES) is a malignant small round-cell tumor that typically arises in the bones or soft tissue of children and young adults. Although most cases involve the skeletal system, extraskeletal presentations are less common, especially involving visceral organs. Primary cardiac ES is exceptionally rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature.Case presentationA 27-year-old male presented with symptoms including cough, dyspnea, fever, anorexia, night sweats, and weight loss. Clinical and imaging evaluations revealed a mass anterior to the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), which was ultimately diagnosed as primary extraskeletal cardiac ES. The patient was treated with a comprehensive multimodal approach, incorporating surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Remarkably, he survived for 36 months after diagnosis, surpassing the survival durations reported in most previous cases.Conclusion and key clinical messageA prolonged survival outcome was demonstrated in this rare case of cardiac ES through aggressive multimodal therapy. Although rare, ES should be included in the differential diagnosis of intracardiac tumors, especially in younger patients presenting with systemic symptoms, to facilitate timely diagnosis and intervention.

Introduction

Extraskeletal Ewing’s sarcoma (ES) is a rare malignant round-cell tumor that primarily affects young adults, with a slight male predominance [1, 2]. It typically occurs in the long bones, pelvis, chest wall, and spine [3]. Metastatic spread frequently involves the lungs, pleura, skeletal system, and bone marrow [4]. It can occur in both children and adults and may present either as a primary cardiac tumor or as a metastasis from other sites [5–8]. Clinical presentations often include symptoms of congestive heart failure and cardiac tamponade, such as cough, chest discomfort, and dyspnea. Less commonly, patients may experience arrhythmias, thromboembolism, and myocardial ischemia [5].

Primary cardiac ES is exceptionally rare, with only a limited number of confirmed intracardiac cases described in the literature [5, 6, 9–11]. Fewer than 20 cases have been reported overall, spanning both pediatric and adult populations, and only a small subset originates within the cardiac chambers rather than extending secondarily from adjacent structures. A case report study summarized the pediatric experience, highlighting that true intracardiac ES, including those involving the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) or pulmonary valve, remains exceedingly uncommon [5]. Reported cases include tumors arising from the right atrium [6], right ventricle [9], and those invading or obstructing the RVOT and pulmonary valve region [10, 11]. Collectively, these reports underscore the extreme rarity of cardiac-origin ES, particularly those involving the RVOT, with a small number of well-documented cases exhibiting such localization.

Due to the rarity of such cases, diagnosis and management are often challenging. Imaging techniques such as chest computed tomography scan (CT-scan) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are essential for detecting masses within the heart chambers or surrounding areas [6, 7]. Definitive diagnosis requires biopsy, and according to immunohistochemistry, about 95% of ES show high, diffuse membrane expression of CD99 [12]. Management typically involves a multimodal approach, combining surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [3, 13]. Prognosis varies significantly, with 5-year survival rates ranging from 70% in non-metastatic cases to 15–30% in metastatic disease, and long-term follow-up is recommended because of high rates of metastasis and recurrence [3, 13].

Because of the extreme rarity of cardiac ES, treatment strategies are primarily informed by case reports and small case series. In this report, we describe a 27-year-old male with extraskeletal ES originating in the heart, presenting with cough, dyspnea, and clinical features consistent with cardiac tamponade.

Case presentation

In July 2022, a 27-year-old man presented with symptoms including cough, difficulty breathing, loss of appetite, occasional fever, night sweats, and weight loss, all of which had progressively worsened over the past few months. On physical examination, his weight was 57 kg, height 176 centimeters, and his body mass index (BMI) was calculated at 18.4 kg/m², indicating an underweight status. Vital signs included a pulse rate of 110 beats per minute, blood pressure of 80/60 mmHg, and a respiratory rate of 36 breaths per minute. Cardiac auscultation revealed a mid-systolic murmur best heard at the second left intercostal space. The remainder of the physical exam was unremarkable, with no peripheral edema, cyanosis, or clubbing observed.

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory evaluations, including complete blood count (CBC), blood biochemistry, urinalysis (UA), and arterial blood gas (ABG), were within normal limits. However, chest radiography revealed a massive pericardial effusion along with a large mediastinal mass. A chest CT-scan showed multiple epicardial seeding-like nodules and a soft tissue mass measuring 107 × 56 mm in the anterior mediastinum. The mass appeared to invade the infundibulum and epicardium, suggesting a cardiac origin (Fig. 1).

Further assessment with positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) revealed a hypermetabolic soft tissue mass located inferior to the pulmonary artery bifurcation, extending caudally to the level of the left atrium, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 13.0. The mass obliterated the intervening fat planes. Additional metabolic activity was observed around the prosthetic pulmonary valve (SUVmax = 5.7). Small, hypodense lesions were also identified adjacent to the right side of the aortic root, the right atrium, and on the left side of the aortic root, with SUVmax = 4.8. No evidence of distant hypermetabolic metastasis was detected (Fig. 2).

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a mass anterior to the RVOT and a reduced ejection fraction of approximately 35–40%. Cardiac MRI revealed a mass centered on the RVOT with direct involvement of the infundibulum and contiguous extension toward the pulmonary valve region. There was a marked reduction in the caliber of the RVOT, compatible with functional obstruction. The patient required urgent surgical intervention due to progressive hemodynamic compromise from a mass involving the right ventricle and pericardial space, with imaging and clinical findings consistent with impending obstruction. A preoperative biopsy was considered but not feasible given the patient’s instability and the tumor’s anatomic location, and would have delayed definitive management. A significant amount of bloody pericardial effusion was drained, and the patient underwent complete tumor resection, along with RVOT reconstruction and pulmonary valve replacement (PVR). Imaging and intraoperative assessment supported a primary intracardiac origin of the tumor. The mass was centered within the right ventricular myocardium with contiguous epicardial involvement. There was no radiologic or intraoperative evidence of a distinct mediastinal primary.

Differential diagnosis

Considering the clinical presentation and imaging findings, the differential diagnosis encompassed a range of malignant cardiac tumors and metastatic lesions. Angiosarcoma was a primary consideration given its propensity to present with pericardial effusion and involvement of the right heart structures, which aligned with the observed findings. Leiomyosarcoma was also considered, particularly due to its potential to originate within the cardiac or mediastinal soft tissues, although it is less common in young adults. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma is a high-grade, broad-spectrum sarcoma that can involve any cardiac chamber and may mimic other sarcomas on imaging. Primary cardiac lymphoma, though rare, often presents as an intracardiac mass or diffuse infiltration and may mimic thrombus or sarcoma. ES was included in the differential because of the patient’s relatively young age and the tumor’s location near the mediastinum and cardiac structures, which, although rare, has been documented in the literature. Additionally, metastatic disease from primary tumors such as lymphoma, lung cancer, or other extracardiac malignancies was evaluated, especially given the presence of multiple nodules and widespread tissue involvement seen on imaging.

To distinguish among these possibilities, definitive diagnosis relied heavily on histopathological examination and immunohistochemical profiling of the tissue obtained through biopsy or surgical resection. These analyses are crucial for accurately identifying the tumor type, determining its origin, and guiding subsequent treatment decisions. The combination of histopathology and immunohistochemistry provides detailed cellular and molecular insights that are essential for differentiating primary cardiac tumors from metastases and for identifying specific tumor subtypes such as ES.

Final diagnosis

Gross pathological examination revealed three lobulated masses measuring 10 × 9 × 6 cm, 4 × 3.5 × 2 cm, and 2 × 2 × 1 cm with white, fleshy, necrotic, and hemorrhagic cut surfaces. Histopathological analysis showed sheets, nests, and papillary arrangements of uniform small neoplastic cells with finely stippled chromatin, inconspicuous nucleoli, and scant clear cytoplasm, separated by fibrous septa. Atypical mitotic figures and foci of necrosis were observed. Cytology of the pericardial effusion revealed a hemorrhagic background without definitive malignant cells (Fig. 3A and B).

Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated strong membranous positivity for CD99, which is characteristic of small round cell sarcomas, most notably ES. Additional positive markers, including NKX2.2 and FLI-1, supported this diagnosis. Conversely, markers such as TdT, desmin, leukocyte common antigen (LCA), S100 protein, cytokeratin AE1/AE3, and MyoD1 were negative, helping to exclude other differential diagnoses (Fig. 3C and D). The absence of nuclear expression of mismatch repair proteins suggested a low likelihood of microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status.

Given the rarity of primary extraskeletal cardiac ES and the potential for metastasis from another site, comprehensive imaging, including repeat PET-CT, abdominal CT, and bone scan, was performed. No evidence of distant disease was found, leading to the final diagnosis of primary extraskeletal cardiac ES.

Therapeutic interventions

Following the surgical resection that was not curative due to the extensive infiltration of the tumor, the patient underwent RVOT reconstruction and pulmonary valve replacement to restore cardiac function. Postoperatively, a chemotherapy regimen specific to sarcoma, based on established guidelines for ES, was initiated. The chosen protocol was VAC/IE, which involves alternating cycles of Vincristine, Actinomycin-D, and Cyclophosphamide with Ifosfamide and Etoposide (VAC/IE). Given the cardiologist’s assessment and concerns regarding the patient’s reduced ejection fraction, anthracyclines, such as doxorubicin, were avoided to minimize cardiotoxicity, and Actinomycin-D was incorporated into the chemotherapy regimen instead. To mitigate the risk of hemorrhage associated with certain chemotherapeutic agents, Mesna (sodium 2-mercaptoethane sulfonate) was also prescribed for uroprotection. The patient was on anticoagulation therapy, receiving two 5 mg tablets of warfarin on even-numbered days and 1.5 tablets on odd-numbered days, with regular monitoring of INR levels.

After completing nine cycles of chemotherapy at three-week intervals, follow-up cardiac MRI showed persistent, large, infiltrative tissue surrounding the posterior aspect of the ascending aorta and the aortic root. The mass extended from just below the pulmonary artery bifurcation up to the roof of the left atrium, which was partially filled by the tumor and measured approximately 37 by 57 mm axially. Given these findings, the multidisciplinary team reviewed the case comprehensively and agreed that the addition of radiotherapy could improve local control. Consequently, they decided to proceed with targeted radiotherapy while continuing the VAC/IE chemotherapy regimen in order to maximize the chances of achieving tumor remission (Fig. 4).

Outcome and Follow-up

Follow-up PET-CT imaging performed after chemotherapy, aimed at assessing treatment response, demonstrated complete resolution of the previously identified mass located above the pulmonary artery bifurcation in the aortopulmonary region. No pericardial effusion was observed. Additionally, the prosthetic pulmonary valve showed no significant hypermetabolic activity, with an SUVmax of 4.0 (Fig. 4). The patient was considered as a candidate for heart transplantation; however, he was deemed ineligible due to the presence of a metallic valve. The transplant committee recommended an autologous stem cell transplant to consolidate the response, but it was ultimately declined due to the patient’s prosthetic metal valve, which necessitates warfarin use and poses a high bleeding risk during post-transplant thrombocytopenia Currently, the patient remains in remission and is under regular follow-up with periodic CT scans and MRI investigations.

This case represents the first documented instance of primary extraskeletal cardiac ES, with the patient surviving 36 months after diagnosis and surgical resection, in addition to adjuvant therapies. Notably, aside from a local recurrence at the previous surgical site, there has been no evidence of widespread metastasis to other organs or tissues.

Discussion

Primary extraskeletal cardiac ES is exceedingly rare, with only a few cases documented in the medical literature among primary cardiac tumors. Reports of such cases date back to early descriptions, with Higgins et al. in 1994 reporting the first known instance of a primary cardiac ES involving a 13-year-old boy who presented with cardiac tamponade and died during surgery. His tumor measured 8 × 8 cm and involved the right and left ventricles. Since then, only sporadic cases have been reported, underscoring the rarity and diagnostic challenges associated with this entity [9].

The initial assessment of suspected cardiac tumors usually starts with a chest radiograph, which helps identify abnormal cardiac shapes or mediastinal masses. Advanced imaging techniques, such as CT-scan and MRI, are crucial for accurately characterizing cardiac masses, including their location, extent, and tissue characteristics. To rule out metastatic disease, a comprehensive metastatic workup is essential at diagnosis, involving PET-CT, total-body CT-scan, and isotope bone scans to aid in staging and detecting distant metastases [14].

Cytology can be unrevealing in cardiac tumors due to sampling limitations and the heterogeneity of tumor histology. In our case, a negative cytology result did not diminish the diagnostic confidence, which was ultimately confirmed with ES. Clinicians should consider proceeding to tissue diagnosis when imaging and clinical suspicion remain high, even in the presence of negative cytology results. Histopathological examination, coupled with immunohistochemical profiling, remains essential for definitive diagnosis. ES cells typically express markers such as CD56, CD99, and Vimentin, aiding in distinguishing this tumor from other small round cell neoplasms. The use of immunohistochemical markers ensures accurate diagnosis and informs treatment planning [6]. Molecular confirmation of ES can be provided by detecting the Ewing sarcoma RNA-binding 1 – Friend leukemia integration 1 (EWSR1-FLI1) fusion transcript (most commonly t(11;22)(q24;q12)) using FISH, RT-PCR, or targeted sequencing. The presence of an EWSR1-FLI1 fusion supports the diagnosis of ES and can aid in identifying atypical presentations [15]. In our case, immunohistochemical profiling (CD99 strong membranous, NKX2.2, and FLI-1 positivity) was diagnostic, and molecular testing for EWSR1 rearrangement was not performed due to financial constraints. Nevertheless, the combined morphological and immunophenotypic profile is highly concordant with ES.

Standard treatment modalities include surgical resection, which is often feasible, although complete excision may be difficult due to the tumor’s location and infiltration of surrounding structures. Surgery is generally followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy, aiming to minimize residual disease and prevent micrometastasis. Chemotherapy regimens usually align with protocols for skeletal ES, incorporating agents such as vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, and etoposide—collectively known as VAC/IE. However, the aggressive nature of cardiac ES and its proximity to vital structures often pose significant surgical challenges. Literature suggests that multimodal approaches, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, are associated with improved outcomes. However, the overall prognosis for primary extraskeletal cardiac ES remains poor, with a median survival often around 12 months [5, 6, 16].

In recent reports, some adult patients have achieved favorable long-term survival with upfront chemotherapy followed by surgical resection and, in certain cases, heart transplantation. Nevertheless, heart transplantation is often limited by the presence of prosthetic devices such as metallic valves, which complicate the procedure and increase risks. For instance, Cho et al. in 2015 [17] documented a successful case of heart transplantation following chemotherapy in an adult with ROVT ES. In our case, the presence of a metallic pulmonary valve prosthesis precluded transplantation, underscoring the challenges related to existing cardiac devices. Despite these limitations, the multidisciplinary team emphasized the importance of comprehensive, tailored treatment, utilizing all available modalities to improve patient outcomes [17].

Other cases highlight the heterogeneity of presentation and management. For example, a 14-year-old boy was treated with intensive chemotherapy, radical surgery with negative margins, and subsequent chemoradiotherapy, resulting in survival beyond 24 months [10]. Another patient, a 51-year-old man with a large pericardial mass extending to the pulmonary artery and ventricles, underwent extensive surgical and chemoradiotherapy protocols, also demonstrating prolonged survival [18]. These cases exemplify the importance of early diagnosis, aggressive multimodal treatment, and individualized management strategies.

The case we report involves a 27-year-old Iranian male presenting with cough and dyspnea, ultimately diagnosed with primary extraskeletal cardiac ES. After surgical resection and receiving a chemotherapy regimen consistent with ES’s protocol, along with palliative radiotherapy, he remains alive and well 36 months after diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of considering rare neoplasms in differential diagnoses, particularly for intracardiac masses, and highlights the potential for successful management despite the aggressive nature of such tumors.

It is noteworthy that a significant strength of our case lies in our access to comprehensive laboratory investigations and a highly skilled, multidisciplinary team, which collectively enhances diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy. Molecular confirmation of ES was not performed due to financial constraints, as the detection of EWSR1 rearrangements was not feasible. While the combination of classic immunohistochemical features and morphology strongly supported the diagnosis, the absence of molecular testing represents a limitation.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the critical importance of an integrated, multimodal treatment approach—combining surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy—for managing rare and aggressive cardiac tumors like extraskeletal ES. Early diagnosis and prompt, tailored interventions are paramount to improving survival outcomes. Healthcare providers should remain vigilant for rare neoplasms in the differential diagnosis of cardiac masses, as timely recognition can significantly influence treatment success. Increasing awareness and reporting of such cases are essential to advancing understanding and optimizing future management strategies for this highly challenging condition.
