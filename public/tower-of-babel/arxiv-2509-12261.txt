An Adaptive CMSA for Solving the Longest Filled Common Subsequence Problem with an Application in Audio Querying
Marko Djukanović, Christian Blum, Aleksandar Kartelj, Ana Nikolikj, Günther R. Raidl

Abstract
This paper addresses the Longest Filled Common Subsequence (LFCS) problem, a challenging NP-hard problem with applications in bioinformatics, including gene mutation prediction and genomic data reconstruction. Existing approaches, including exact, metaheuristic, and approximation algorithms, have primarily been evaluated on small-sized instances, which offer limited insights into their scalability. In this work, we introduce a new benchmark dataset with significantly larger instances and demonstrate that existing datasets lack the discriminative power needed to meaningfully assess algorithm performance at scale. To solve large instances efficiently, we utilize an adaptive Construct, Merge, Solve, Adapt (CMSA) framework that iteratively generates promising subproblems via component-based construction and refines them using feedback from prior iterations. Subproblems are solved using an external black-box solver. Extensive experiments on both standard and newly introduced benchmarks show that the proposed adaptive CMSA achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming five leading methods. Notably, on 1,510 problem instances with known optimal solutions, our approach solves 1,486 of them—achieving over 99.9% optimal solution quality and demonstrating exceptional scalability. We additionally propose a novel application of LFCS for song identification from degraded audio excerpts as an engineering contribution, which uses real-world energy-profile instances from popular music. Finally, we conducted an empirical explainability analysis to identify critical feature combinations influencing algorithm performance, i.e., the key problem features contributing to success or failure of the approaches across different instance types are revealed.



1
Introduction

Strings are fundamental data structures in many programming languages, widely used to model sequences such as DNA, RNA, protein molecules, and even time series. A string
s
is defined as a finite sequence of characters drawn from a (typically finite) alphabet
\Sigma
. A key concept in analyzing similarities between strings is a
subsequence
—a sequence derived from a string by deleting zero or more characters without changing the order of remaining characters. Detecting structural similarities among multiple strings is central to both stringology and molecular biology. One of the most widely used tools for this purpose is the identification of
common subsequences
, which can help uncover relationships among sequences and reveal meaningful biological or structural patterns, such as mutations. Advances in genome sequencing have further enabled the analysis and reconstruction of genomic data using such techniques. A core optimization problem in this context is the
Longest Common Subsequence
(LCS) problem
[
1
]
, defined as follows: Given a set of input strings, find a longest sequence that is a subsequence of each of the input strings. The LCS problem has broad applications in computational biology
[
2
]
, file plagiarism detection, data compression
[
3
,
4
]
, text editing
[
5
]
, and spatial data analysis, such as identifying road intersections from GPS traces
[
6
]
. Practical applications include file comparison tools like the Unix
diff
command
[
7
]
and revision control systems such as
Git
.

To solve the LCS problem when the number of input strings
m
is fixed, polynomial-time algorithms based on dynamic programming (DP) have been proposed
[
8
]
. However, their practical applicability remains limited due to the rapidly growing computational complexity in respect to the number of strings and the strings’ lengths. To address this, several advanced metaheuristics have been developed to solve LCS variants more efficiently in practice. State-of-the-art methods are based on effectively exploring the state space graph
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, combined with carefully crafted, problem-specific search guidance strategies. These methods have been tested on both artificial and real benchmark datasets, revealing the strengths and drawbacks of these approaches on various instance distributions.
In parallel, exact approaches have advanced through the use of best-first search frameworks and their extensions to hybrid anytime algorithms
[
12
]
. Over the past two decades, practical variants of the LCS problem have gained increasing attention in the research community. Notable examples include the longest common arc subsequence problem
[
13
]
, the repetition-free LCS problem
[
14
]
, and the constrained LCS problem
[
15
]
, among many others.

More recently, research efforts have turned toward understanding gene mutations and reconstructing genomic data, aiming to uncover the evolutionary history of molecular sequences. This goal is formalized by the
Longest Filled Common Subsequence
(LFCS) problem, which is inspired by another real-world application, the scaffold filling problem
[
16
,
17
]
from genomic data reconstruction. The LFCS problem (LFCSP) was introduced by Castelli et al.
[
18
]
. Briefly, the problem involves two sequences: the original sequence and one that requires repair. Additionally, a multiset of symbols is provided, which can be used for the repair. The goal is to insert some (or all) of these symbols into the second, shorter, sequence to maximize its similarity to the original sequence. The application of LFCSP can clearly extend towards a broader range of research areas involving sequential data that may suffer from information loss, such as audio querying or corrupted text recovery
[
19
]
.

Concerning the methodological appraoches to solve the LFCSP from the literature, in
[
18
]
the authors proposed two
\frac{3}{5}
-approximation algorithms and gave a formal proof on the APX-hardness of the LFCSP, even when the first input string contains at most two occurrences of each symbol from the alphabet
\Sigma
. This result was established via a reduction from the maximum independent set problem on cubic graphs. The second notable work by Mincu and Popa
[
20
]
presented diverse solving approaches including an Integer Linear Programming (ILP) formulation, a randomized sampling-based method, and a local search (LS) strategy based on the classical hill-climbing. Their comparative computational study was conducted on a set of randomly generated instances.

Several limitations of this earlier works remain unaddressed:

1.

Scalability
: Existing benchmark instances are limited to small and medium-sized problems, all of which can be solved efficiently by the ILP model in a matter of seconds. As a result, the practical scalability and limitations of these methods have not been fully thoroughly explored. Moreover, the current literature lacks real-world problem instances, or at least instances with characteristics appearing in real-world applications.

2.

Heuristic Potential
: The heuristic methods developed stand far from leveraging advanced search operators or learning mechanisms commonly used in modern optimization, leaving room for significant methodological improvements for solving the tackled problem.

3.

Explainability
: There is a lack of systematic analysis to explain the performance of algorithms based on instance characteristics or internal algorithmic behaviors—a growing area of interest in optimization research.

In this article, we address these gaps through the following contributions:

1.

A state-of-the-art ILP-based metaheuristic approach is proposed for solving the LFCSP. The method is based on an adaptive variant of the
Construct, Merge, Solve, Adapt
(CMSA) framework
[
21
,
22
]
.

2.

Two new benchmark datasets are introduced. The first one consists of dozens of synthetic instances which are significantly larger than the instances from the existing benchmarks. This allows for a deeper investigation into the scalability and robustness of both existing and new solving approaches. The second dataset comprises realistic LFCSP instances based on discretized energy profiles from music recordings, illustrating a way the LFCSP approach supports audio fingerprinting in challenging, low-fidelity query scenarios.

3.

An explainable AI analysis is performed using the SHAP (
SHapley Additive exPlanations
) appraoch
[
23
]
to reveal how each instance feature influences the performance of each method. This provides novel insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different approaches across varying instance types.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, a formal problem definition is given and some notations are introduced. Brief descriptions over the existing approaches from the literature are given in Section
3
. Section
4
provides the details on the newly developed approach to solve the LFCSP. A thorough experimental analysis is provided in Section
5
. Section
6
pursues a case-study in the context of audio querying and fingerprinting. Section
7
is devoted to the performance analysis of all approaches explained through the eye of instance features. In Section
8
, we conclude the work and outline for promising future research questions.

3
Approaches from the Literature

In the following, we briefly explain the existing approaches for solving the LFCSP, which we will consider in our experimental comparisons. As corresponding implementations were not freely available, nor were they provided to us by the authors, we re-implemented all these approaches from scratch.

3.1
Approximation Algorithms

Castelli et al.
[
18
]
described two approximation algorithms for the LFCSP. As both provide the same approximation factor of 3/5, and as, during experimentation, we observed no statistically significant difference between the results of the two approaches, we limit ourselves to providing the details of one of them here.

The basic workflow of the algorithm, henceforth called
Approx
, is as follows.

1.

Determine an LCS of
A
and
B
. Let
R_{1,\mathrm{a}}
denote the set of positions in
A
matched by the alignment of the LCS.

2.

Remove all positions of
R_{1,\mathrm{a}}
from
A
, and denote the resulting string by
A^{\prime}
.

3.

By applying the procedure
GreedyMatch
as described below, compute a set
R_{1,\mathrm{i}}
of positions of
A^{\prime}
of maximal size that matches
\mathcal{M}
by insertion.

4.

Let
R_{1}=R_{1,\mathrm{a}}\cup R_{1,\mathrm{i}}
be the set of resulting positions returned by the algorithm, representing the LFCSP solution.

Procedure
GreedyMatch
(A^{\prime},\mathcal{M})
works as follows:

1.

Set
\tilde{A}=\varepsilon
,
R_{1,\mathrm{i}}=\emptyset

2.

Proceed letter-by-letter from left to right over string
A^{\prime}
and check if the current symbol
A^{\prime}[i]=\alpha
is free/available to be matched, i.e., appended to
\tilde{A}
.

3.

If yes (
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}
is confirmed), append
\alpha
to
\tilde{A}
(
\tilde{A}\leftarrow\tilde{A}\cdot\alpha
), remove
\alpha
from
\mathcal{M}
(
\mathcal{M}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}\setminus\{\alpha\}
), and add
i
to
R_{1,\mathrm{i}}
. Otherwise, skip the letter and move to the next letter of
A^{\prime}
;

4.

When all letters of
A^{\prime}
were considered, the algorithm terminates by returning
\tilde{A}
and the set of positions
R_{1,\mathrm{i}}
.

3.2
A Randomized Greedy Construction Method

The following randomized greedy solution construction was proposed by Mincu and Popa
[
20
]
.

1.

Select a subset of symbols from
A
to match as many symbols as possible from
\mathcal{M}
. Let the positions of these matched symbols in
A
be denoted by
R_{1,\mathrm{a}}
. For instance, if
A
contains three
\texttt{a}\in\Sigma
symbols and
\mathcal{M}
contains four, all three
a
symbols in
A
(and their positions) are subsequently matched. Conversely, if
A
has four
a
symbols and
\mathcal{M}
has three, then three out of the four
a
positions in
A
are randomly selected to match between symbols.

2.

Obtain string
A^{\prime}
by removing all selected positions in
R_{1,\mathrm{a}}
.

3.

Determine an LCS for
A^{\prime}
and
B
; the quality of the obtained solution equals to LCS(
A^{\prime},B
)
+|A|-|A^{\prime}|
.

Mincu and Popa apply this randomized construction
n_{\mathrm{rand}}=10000
times and finally return a best obtained solution, and so do we in our experimental comparison.

3.2.1
Local Search Methods

The following local search method has also been proposed by Mincu and Popa
[
20
]
.

1.

For a fixed
k
, we consider all consecutive substrings of size
k
of string
A
, i.e.,
A[1,k]
,
A[2,k+1]
, etc. obtained by windowing sequence
A
.

2.

For each such substring, we take all possible combinations of letters (
2^{k}

. For the remaining (non-matched) parts of sequences
A
and

3.

In each major iteration, up to

candidate solutions are therefore considered and evaluated.

The authors refer to the algorithm as S
k
, and the two settings

and

are considered in the evaluation. We will do the same in our experimental comparisons.

3.3
Integer Linear Programming Model

Mincu and Popa
[
20
]
further modeled the LFCSP by an ILP.
Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the set of all matchings between input strings
A
and
B
, i.e.,
(i.j)\in\mathcal{R}
iff
A[i]=B[j]
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,m
. Two sets of binary decision variables are used:

1.

x_{i,j}
for
(i,j)\in\mathcal{R}
:
x_{i,j}=1
means that the letter
A[i]=B[j]
is included in the LFCS, otherwise it is not.

2.

y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
:
y_{i}=1
means that
A[i]
is included in the solution as a symbol that is filled in, or in other words,
A[i]
is deleted from
A
for obtaining
A^{\prime}
in the deletion-based interpretation of the LFCSP.

The ILP can now be stated as:

\displaystyle\max\

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{R}}x_{i,j}

(2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.\ }

\displaystyle x_{i,j}+x_{k,l}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall(i,j),(k,l)\in\mathcal{R}\ \text{if in conflict}

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,j}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall j=1,\ldots,m

(4)

\displaystyle y_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{i,j}\leq 1

\displaystyle\forall i=1,\ldots,n

(5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,\ldots,m\mid A[i]=\sigma}y_{i}\leq|\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}|

\displaystyle\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{M}

(6)

\displaystyle x_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\mathcal{R}

(7)

\displaystyle y_{i}\in\{0,1\}

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n

(8)

We say that two matchings
(i,j),(k,l)\in\mathcal{R}
are
in conflict
iff they are different and
(i\leq k\ \wedge\ j\geq l)\vee(i\ \geq\ k\wedge\ j\ \leq l)
holds, i.e., they cannot both appear in a common subsequence.

Inequalities (
3
) ensure that the selected matchings are without conflicts and thus form a proper subsequence of both input strings.
Constraints (
4
) enforce that each symbol in
B
is matched with at most one symbol in
A
.
On the other hand, constraints (
5
) ensure that each symbol from
A
, if part of the solution, is either matched with a symbol in
\mathcal{M}
or matched with at most one symbol from
B
. Constraints (
6
) permit to match symbols
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}
to symbols from
A
only up to
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}
times.

A
that match appropriate symbols from
\mathcal{M}
. In Example
1
, the selected positions

and

, the set of non-conflict pairs is given by the set

. Hereby, an LFCSP solution

induces the solution components

4
A New Hybrid ILP/Metaheuristic Approach for the LFCSP

The ILP from the last section performs well on small- to medium-sized instances, roughly up to
n=80
, see
[
20
]
. We build on this and set out to develop an ILP-based metaheuristic capable of efficiently solving significantly larger instances. A promising frameworks for this purpose is the Construct, Merge, Solve, and Adapt (CMSA) approach originally proposed by Blum et al.
[
21
]
. Since its introduction, CMSA has achieved state-of-the-art results in a wide range of problem domains, including bioinformatics
[
24
]
, the field of software engineering and the product line of software
[
25
]
, planning
[
26
]
, packing and routing
[
27
]
, among others. For more information on CMSA and its variants, the reader is referred to the recent survey paper
[
28
]
. The core idea of CMSA is to iteratively generate promising subproblems that are solved using an external solver, which—in our case—shall be an ILP solver applied to a model based on the previously presented one. Over successive iterations, the components that form these subproblems are adapted based on their historical contribution to high-quality solutions. This is achieved through an aging mechanism, where promising components are retained longer in the search process, while less useful components are discarded rather quickly. This dynamic adjustment enables the approach to continuously refine the search space and improve solution quality over time.

In each major iteration, CMSA performs four main steps, as already indicated by the title of the algorithm. The
construction
phase is responsible for a typically rather fast generation of a set of diverse solutions of reasonable quality.
The
merge
phase defines a sub-instance of the problem of restricted size that considers only solution components of an initially empty pool plus the newly generated solutions’ components.
The
solving
phase, solves this sub-instance with a time limit.
Finally, the adapt phase takes care of awarding or penalizing solution components and preparing the pool for the next iteration. The model that is usually used is the
aging
model, i.e., each component that appears in the new incumbent solution is awarded by resetting its age to zero, while the age of all other components is incremented. Components whose age exceed a threshold are removed from the pool.

To improve the robustness of CMSA—particularly its sensitivity to parameter tuning across different instance sizes—several variants have been proposed in the literature. Among them, the
self-adaptive
CMSA (or Adapt-CMSA) has demonstrated enhanced performance on various problems, including influence maximization in social networks
[
29
]
and graph partitioning
[
30
]
. For a comprehensive overview of CMSA and its variants, we refer to
[
22
]
.
In contrast to the basic CMSA, the self-adaptive variant eliminates the need for a manually parameterized adapt phase.
Instead, the components of a sub-instance are retained only during the iteration in which they are generated.
The algorithm dynamically adjusts key parameters such as the number of generated solutions (
n_{\mathrm{a}}
) and a similarity threshold (
\alpha_{\mathrm{bsf}}
) that govern the balance between intensification and diversification. This parameter adaptation is driven by the solution obtained during the solve phase of each iteration. At the end of each iteration, components from the best-so-far solution are passed to the subsequent iteration which, combined with newly generated solutions, will serve to create a new subproblem. The size of the sub-instance in the next iteration is controlled by the value
\alpha_{\mathrm{bsf}}
—the larger
\alpha_{\mathrm{bsf}}
, the larger is the diversity in the components of newly generated solutions. This mechanism allows the algorithm to continuously refine its focus based on accumulated search experience, leading to improved scalability and robustness across diverse problem instances.

4.1
The Self-Adaptive CMSA

This section provides with details on the self-adaptive CMSA designed to solve the LFCSP. Before diving into these, remember from Section
3.3
that a possible solution
s
(being a subsequence of
A
) to a problem instance
\mathcal{I}=(A,B,\mathcal{M})
is obtained by determining the set of positions matched between
A
and
\mathcal{M}
to generate
s
by deleting these from
A
, and by evaluating
s
through the identification of the matched positions between
s
and
B
(obtained by determining the LCS between
s
and
B
). Note that a solution
s
was previously denoted by
A^{\prime}
.

The merge procedure and subproblems
. A core step in the design of a CMSA is to define what the solution components are. Suppose
S
is a set of diverse solutions to an LFCSP instance
I=(A,B,\mathcal{M})
. For each
s\in S
, we make use of the following notations:

1.

\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}\}
a set of positions of
A
whose corresponding symbols are found in
\mathcal{M}
. These positions (resp. symbols) are those that, when removed from
A
, generate the solution string
s
.

2.

\texttt{Match}_{B}^{s}=\{(p_{1}^{s},q_{1}^{s}),\ldots,(p_{u}^{s},q_{u}^{s})\}
,
0\leq u\leq\min\{|s|,|B|\}
a set mutually non-conflicting matching positions (i.e., pairs) between string
s
and
B
. This set results from solving the LCS problem between
s
and
B
. Again, two (different) components
(i,j)
and (
k,l
) are in conflict iff either
i\leq k\ \wedge\ j\geq l
or
i\geq k\ \wedge\ j\leq l
, following Section
3.3
.

In other words, a solution
s
is completely determined by these two sets of components, i.e. actions that correspond to decision variables
x
and
y
following their meaning in the ILP model.

A subproblem can be derived from the solution set
S
by merging the respective sets of matchings:

\mathcal{C}(S)=(\texttt{Match}^{S}_{B},\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{S})
, where
\texttt{Match}^{S}_{B}:=\bigcup_{s\in S}\texttt{Match}_{B}^{s}
and
\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{S}:=\bigcup_{s\in S}\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s}
.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{i}=\{j\mid(i,j)\in\texttt{Match}^{S}_{B}\}
represents
all positions in
B
that are matched (in at least one of the solutions
from
S
) to the position
i
of
A
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and let
\mathcal{C}^{j}=\{i\mid(i,j)\in\texttt{Match}^{S}_{B}\}
represents all positions in
A
that are matched (in at least one of the solutions from
S
) to the position
j
of
B
,
j=1,\ldots,m
.

We can now formulate the ILP model for the subproblem to find a LFCSP solution with the restriction that only solution components from
\mathcal{C}(S)
are allowed (this can be seen as a task of finding an optimal recombination between the given solution components). Boolean decision variables
x_{i,j}
are now only defined for
(i,j)\in\texttt{Match}^{S}_{B}
, and variables
y_{i}
only for
i\in\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{S}
. We refer to this subproblem formulation as ILP(
\mathcal{C}(S)
), which is given by

\displaystyle\max\

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\texttt{{Match}}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{S}}y_{i}+\sum_{(i,j)\in\texttt{Match}_{B}^{S}}x_{i,j}

The solution construction procedure
. We basically apply the randomized greedy approach from
[

]
in the CMSA’s construction phase. However, this approach was changed for a stronger randomization and thus diversification that is controlled by a bias parameter. The resulting method is shown in the form of pseudo-code in Algorithm

and works as follows.

First, the solution
s_{\mathrm{bsf}}
—in terms of the corresponding solution components—is generated employing the randomized greedy approach from Section
3.2
(with a setting of
n_{\textrm{rand}}=1
). These components are further copied to
\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s_{\mathrm{mutate}}}
and
\texttt{Match}_{B}^{s_{\mathrm{mutate}}}

\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s_{\mathrm{bsf}}}

A
that matches the same symbol not already included in
\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s_{\mathrm{mutate}}}

\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s_{\mathrm{mutate}}}
(see Line

\texttt{Match}_{A\mathcal{M}}^{s_{\mathrm{bsf}}}

and
\mathcal{M}

Note that an additional termination criterion is employed in the solving phase: We noticed that the time spent by the solver to prove optimality was much larger than the time spent on finding high-quality solutions in general. 5
Experimental Evaluation

In this section, a thorough experimental analysis between 4 known literature approaches to solve the LFCSP and our approach has been performed. More precisely, our
Adapt-Cmsa
is compared against the following approaches:

1.

An approximation algorithm, described in Section
3.1
, labeled by
Approx
;

2.

The randomized greedy approach, described in Section
3.2
, labelled by
RandSample
;

3.

The local search method, described in Section
3.2.1
, labelled by
Ls
. Two settings of this algorithm are executed depending on the value of parameter
k
(referring to the neighborhood size):

(a)

the setting with
k=2
, therefore labelled by
Ls
2;

(b)

the setting with
k=4
, therefore labelled by
Ls
4;

4.

ILP method, described in Section
3.3
, labelled by
Ilp
.

All algorithms are implemented in
C
++ and compiled under GCC 11.4.0. All experiments are executed in single-threaded mode on a server equipped with an Intel Xeon E5-2640 v4 processor with 2.40 GHz, allocating 32 GB of memory per each run. For all literature-based approaches, the algorithms are run to completion, except for
Ilp
and our
Adapt-Cmsa
, both of which are subject to a time limit of 600 CPU seconds. The
Ilp
model is solved using the general-purpose solver
Cplex
, under version 12.8. For
Adapt-Cmsa
, a custom
IncumbentCallbackI
is implemented to enforce an additional termination condition—specifically, the algorithm immediately stops when a new incumbent solution is found during the execution of the
Cplex
solver.

5.1
Problem Instances

Two sets of synthetic instances are generated. The first set, denoted as
Small
, consists of the instances generated by the procedure as introduced in
[
20
]
. For each combination of sequence length
n\in\{16,32,48,64,80\}
and alphabet size
|\Sigma|\in\left\{\frac{n}{8},\frac{n}{4},\frac{n}{2}\right\}
, 100 instances are generated using the instance generator as described in the next paragraph. In contrast to the small- and medium-sized instances, a second benchmark set,
Large
, consists of significantly larger instances—approximately an order of magnitude larger in size. Using the same instance generator, 10 instances are generated for each combination of
n\in\{200,400,500,600,800,1000\}
and
|\Sigma|\in\{4,20\}
. These alphabet sizes are chosen to reflect more realistic scenarios: RNA sequences are those with an alphabet of size 4, while protein sequences are modelled with an alphabet of size 20. In total, 1,620 problem instances are generated, spanning 27 distinct instance groups.

Instance Generator
. The instance generator, originally proposed in
[
20
]
, operates as follows. Each instance
I=(A,B,\mathcal{M})
is created by first generating a random string
A
over alphabet
\Sigma
. The sequence
B
is then derived from
A
by iterating through its symbols and applying a modification operation with a probability of 50%. The modifications are selected uniformly between three possible operations: duplication, deletion, or substitution with another symbol. After applying these operations, sequence
B
is randomly partitioned into segments of maximum length
n
/8, and more than 30% of these segments are randomly discarded. The remaining segments are concatenated to form the final string
B
, while the discarded segments are stored in
\mathcal{M}
.

These synthetic problem instances and source codes can be downloaded from a GitHub repository at
https://github.com/markodjukanovic90/LFCSP_public/tree/main
.

A Case Study: Audio Fingerprinting

Audio fingerprinting is a widely used technique for identifying songs based on short, potentially noisy audio excerpts. A canonical system is Shazam
[
33
]
, which extracts prominent spectral peaks from a song and hashes pairs of them into compact fingerprints. These are stored in a large-scale database and enable fast, noise-tolerant matching even for low-quality recordings.
Other robust alternatives include Panako
[
34
]
and Audfprint
[
35
]
, among others. These methods generally rely on exact or approximate matching of robust audio features and assume at least partial preservation of time-local structure.

When audio is recorded using low-end microphones or under challenging conditions such as high noise levels, lossy compression, or variable latency, much of the structure typically relied upon by fingerprinting systems may be lost or severely degraded. While established systems like Shazam and its variants are highly effective in many practical settings, they are not designed to operate with extremely fragmented or partially observed data. In such scenarios, alternative formulations like the LFCSP approach offer a different perspective. Rather than competing directly with conventional fingerprinting, LFCSP represents one of several directions that could be further explored to address highly degraded or incomplete audio inputs.

To compare songs meaningfully, a variety of signal representations have been proposed:

1.

Spectrograms: A basic but powerful representation computed using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), showing how amplitudes are distributed across frequencies over time.

2.

Mel-Spectrograms: Spectrograms warped onto the Mel scale to better reflect human perception of pitch and loudness.

3.

MFCCs: Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients summarize the spectral envelope of a sound and are popular in speech and speaker recognition.

4.

Learned Embeddings: Models such as OpenL3
[
36
]
, VGGish
[
37
]
produce high-level embeddings from audio using deep learning trained on large datasets. These embeddings capture abstract musical or semantic properties.

5.

Energy Profiles: A simplified yet informative representation used in this work is the energy profile, where consecutive parts of audio (seconds in our study) are converted into discretized energy levels (e.g., one of 10 bins from
\{0,..,9\}
). This results in a one-dimensional symbolic sequence that captures loudness and dynamics, and is robust to time stretching and pitch shifts.

LFCSP in the context of audio footprinting is defined over three components:

1.

Sequence
A
(candidate song),

2.

Sequence
B
(noisy audio query),

3.

Multiset
\mathcal{M}
of additional symbols derived from
B
but without known temporal positioning.

In this application, the solution to LFCSP is used to identify a song from a known database of high-quality recordings, i.e., a set of candidate sequences
As=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots\}
based on a noisy or incomplete energy profile
B
(evidence), supplemented with additional unordered energy information
\mathcal{M}
, which acts as a histogram of symbols with unknown temporal positions.

In a practical system, identification would typically proceed in two phases. The first phase would quickly eliminate vast majority of songs from a large-scale database (potentially millions of entries) by comparing coarse global features, such as energy histograms. Model Explainability Analysis

To provide a more comprehensive comparison of the algorithms, we apply explainable algorithm performance prediction. This analysis is conducted on both the
Small
and
Large
benchmark sets. Each dataset is partitioned into training and test set scenarios, with 25% of the problem instances randomly selected for testing. For each algorithm, a separate single-target regression (STR) model is trained using four instance features to predict its final solution quality. To evaluate the contribution of these features, we employ the
SHapley Additive exPlanations
(SHAP) method
[
38
]
. SHAP provides both global feature importance—indicating the overall impact of each feature on the model’s predictions across the entire dataset—and local feature importance—reflecting the influence of each feature on individual instance predictions. By computing feature importance for each algorithm-specific model, we enable a comparative analysis of how instance characteristics affect predicted performance across the different algorithms.

This analysis aims at highlighting similarities and differences in algorithm behavior with respect to specific instance characteristics. To achieve this, we employ SHAP waterfall plots—a visualization tool that decomposes a model’s prediction for a single instance into the individual contributions of each feature. In a SHAP waterfall plot, the
y

x

Red bars indicate features that positively influence the prediction (i.e., increase the output), while blue bars represent features that negatively impact the prediction (i.e., reduce the output). The plot begins at the base value, which is the expected value of the model’s output over the entire dataset. Feature contributions are then sequentially added or subtracted from this base value, culminating in the final predicted value shown at the top.

8
Conclusions and Future Work

This paper addressed the Longest Filled Common Subsequence (LFCS) problem, a combinatorial optimization problem with practical relevance in bioinformatics, particularly for identifying gene mutations and reconstructing genomic data. To effectively solve this problem, we utilized a variant of the robust metaheuristic framework known as Construct, Merge, Solve, Adapt (CMSA). Our proposed variant incorporates several tailored components. In the construction phase, we adapted a randomized algorithm from the literature to generate diverse solutions within a parameterized neighborhood of the current best solution. The generated solution components are then merged efficiently to build a promising subproblem, which is solved using an Integer Linear Programming (ILP) technique. At subsequent iterations, new subproblems are dynamically generated based on the quality of the obtained solution, automatically adjusting the number of solutions to be constructed in each iteration as well as the value of the parameter that controls the level of diversification between the solutions. To evaluate the scalability and robustness of our approach relative to five existing methods from the literature, we introduced an additional synthetic dataset comprising 120 large-scale problem instances. These instances are approximately an order of magnitude larger than those previously studied, allowing for a more thorough and realistic assessment of algorithmic performance under practical conditions. A comprehensive experimental evaluation has demonstrated the effectiveness of our CMSA-based approach, which consistently outperformed all competing methods in terms of solution quality across all datasets. This superiority is further confirmed by the number of instances for which the approach was successful in identifying optimal solutions. Specifically, out of 1,510 problem instances solved to optimality, our method successfully solved 1,486 problem instances–substantially more than the second-best approach. In addition to synthetic problem instances, a set of real-world problem instances is generated. These are used in a case-study analysis involving audio fingerprinting, demonstrating the reliability of LFCSP in identifying proper songs within a noisy audio query scenario. To further investigate the relationship between instance features and algorithmic performance across all six approaches, an explainable AI technique is applied, specifically the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP). This analysis identified key combinations of features that significantly influence algorithm performance. For our CMSA-based method, the most influential feature was the length of the first input sequence (
n
), followed by the cardinality of the multiset
\mathcal{M}
(
k
). Interestingly, the length of the second input sequence (
|B|
) had a largely negligible effect on performance, particularly on the large synthetic benchmark set. This observation suggests that the ability to effectively identify a substantial number of matchings between elements of the first sequence and those of the multiset represents a critical set of decisions for producing high-quality solutions.

For future work, the ability and robustness of our approach in practical gene data reconstruction scenarios could be exploited. Furthermore, several variants of CMSA from the literature offer a huge potential for further performance enhancements. In particular, the reinforcement learning-based CMSA method proposed by Reixach et al.
[
40
]
avoids traditional greedy decision making during solution construction, potentially improving solution diversity and adaptability. Another avenue involves refining the current ILP formulation, which could lead to improved solution quality within the CMSA framework. Moreover, integrating insights from various machine learning models tailored to the problem domain presents an exciting research direction
[
41
]
. For example, a trained model could inform better symbol deletion strategies by identifying promising symbols and respective positions for a quick omission when constructing high-quality solutions.
