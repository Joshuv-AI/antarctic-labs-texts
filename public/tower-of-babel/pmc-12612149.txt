Influence of obstacle aspect ratio on proton exchange membrane fuel cell performance with numerical and sensitivity analysis
Authors: Mohammadhadi Maghsoudniazi, Iman Khazaee, AmirReza Ghiabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Efficient proton exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) operation demands optimization of both flow field geometry and operating parameters to balance mass transport and reaction kinetics. This study quantifies how the aspect ratio (AR) of rectangular flow obstacles interacts with temperature, pressure, and humidity to influence cell performance. A three-dimensional finite-volume CFD model of a full PEMFC, incorporating gas diffusion layers, catalyst layers, and flow channels, was used to solve the coupled transport of species, heat, and current. Rectangular obstacles with AR from 0 (no obstacle) to 1 were placed in the cathode and anode channels, and a design-of-experiments sensitivity analysis was performed over operating conditions: temperature 333–363 K, pressure 1–4 atm, and anode/cathode relative humidity 0–100%. The results reveal that mid-sized obstacles (AR ≈ 0.25–0.50) deliver the largest improvements in power density across all conditions, primarily by enhancing oxygen convection and reducing concentration overpotentials. In particular, AR≈0.25–0.50 configurations provided approximately 18–20% higher power output across a wide range of temperatures and humidity levels on anode and cathode sides, relative to the baseline (AR = 0) case. Peak performance occurred at about 2 atm: under these moderate pressures the obstructed channels achieved maximal power output. However, at 4 atm the trend reversed: diffusion limitations dominated and the obstacle-free design achieved the highest power density. Furthermore, the AR≈0.25 design promoted uniform water distribution and mitigated local flooding, yielding roughly 15–20% gains in performance across the humidity spectrum. These findings map the optimal operating regime and provide guidelines for tuning flow field obstacle geometry to maximize PEMFC performance under realistic conditions.

Introduction

Climate change and the growing demand for clean energy have positioned renewable technologies as a key strategy for reducing greenhouse-gas emissions and ensuring reliable power supply worldwide1,2. Among these, fuel cells have attracted particular attention because they directly convert chemical energy into electricity with high efficiency and produce only water as a by-product. Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs), in particular, offer high electrochemical conversion efficiency3 and rapid dynamic response2, making them well suited for both transportation and distributed-generation applications.

A PEMFC stack consists of alternating membrane-electrode assemblies (MEAs) and bipolar plates (BPs). In addition to conducting electrical current, the bipolar plates separate hydrogen and oxidant streams, uniformly distribute reactant gases over the catalyst layers, and manage both water and thermal loads4,5. Because each stack contains many cells, BPs account for roughly 70% of the stack’s mass and approximately 30–45% of its cost3,4. Accordingly, optimizing the material selection and geometric design of the bipolar plates is crucial to reducing weight and cost while preserving optimal cell performance5.

Importance of flow-channel geometry

The flow-channel geometry in bipolar plates governs reactant distribution and water evacuation within the cell. A well-designed channel network ensures uniform current density and prevents localized flooding or membrane dry-out; conversely, poorly designed channels can induce excessive pressure drop and nonuniform oxygen distribution, degrading both efficiency and power output6. Consequently, modifying channel shapes or introducing obstacles can enhance mass transport and thereby increase the cell’s maximum current and power capabilities7.

Structural innovations in channel geometry

In recent years, researchers have proposed a broad range of novel flow-field designs for PEMFC enhancement:

Inserting rectangular obstacles alongside straight channels drives reactant gas into the gas diffusion layer beneath the ribs, enhancing catalytic reaction rates. Studies demonstrate that symmetric side-blockages can boost power density by up to 10%7,8. Khazaee’s combined numerical and experimental work showed that varying obstacle shape and arrangement can improve cell efficiency by up to 15%9. Parmar et al. investigated the effect of channel aspect ratio (height to width) and found that values below 0.5 offer an optimal trade-off between electrical resistance and mass transport10. Havaej reported that a periodic converging–diverging (variable-cross-section) flow field yields over 10% higher net power compared to a straight channel11. Zhang and Bai introduced a tapered-obstruction pattern that amplifies lateral flow components, markedly improving mass transport12. Mancusi et al. demonstrated that a gently diverging channel enhances throughflow velocity and liquid–water removal efficiency13. Chen et al. showed that increasing the wave amplitude to 0.8 mm while reducing the wavelength to 4 mm raises the maximum power at 0.6 V by approximately 35%14. Zhou and colleagues evaluated both “opposite” sinusoidal patterns and fully three-dimensional sinusoidal designs, finding significant gains in gas-distribution uniformity and water removal15,16. Valentín-Reyes et al. simulated interdigitated channels—where flow passages dead-end under porous ribs—and reported reduced pressure drop alongside very uniform oxygen distribution at high current densities17. Li et al.’s combined experimental–numerical study of a fully three-dimensional flow field found as much as an 18% increase in peak power density18. Ramin et al. presented trap-shaped expansions that create local reactant reservoirs, optimizing oxygen and water distribution at the cathode19. Carcadea et al. conducted large-scale CFD analyses showing that reducing channel width by 20% can improve flow uniformity by up to 12%, albeit at the expense of higher pressure drop20,21. Fan et al. proposed multi-plate and integrated cathode configurations that enhance oxygen delivery to the catalyst layer, achieving net-power gains of 4–7.5%22.

Sensitivity analyses and operating conditions

PEMFC performance also depends strongly on operating parameters. Key studies include:

Kanani et al. used response-surface methodology to optimize pressure, temperature, and humidity, finding that pressures up to 2 bar and inlet humidity around 80% maximize performance23. Chen et al. examined sensitivity under varying electrical loads, highlighting the dominant influence of anode-side humidity over cathode-side humidity on cell output24. Wang et al. conducted a broad parametric study showing that simultaneous increases in temperature and pressure are the primary drivers of efficiency gains and pressure-drop reduction25. Carcadea et al. (2018) further analyzed combined effects of channel dimensions and operating conditions, reporting up to 15% improvement in power density when geometry and temperature are co-optimized21.

1.4 Research gap and study objective

Although diverse flow-field configurations have been explored, no systematic evaluation has yet been performed of rectangular obstacle aspect ratios—defined as obstacle width relative to channel width—across a continuous range from 0 to 1 in discrete increments, nor in both anode and cathode channels simultaneously. Identifying the optimal aspect ratio that balances enhanced mass transport against increased pressure drop is critical for guiding future bipolar-plate designs.

The present work fills this gap by employing a fully three-dimensional CFD model to investigate five discrete obstacle aspect ratios (0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1) in both anode and cathode flow fields. The geometry—comprising bipolar plates with structured flow channels, gas diffusion layers, catalyst layers, and a polymer electrolyte membrane—was built in SolidWorks 2024 and meshed in ANSYS Workbench 2023R2. Steady-state, isothermal, two-phase simulations were carried out under identical operating conditions, with additional sensitivity analyses over varying pressures, temperatures, and inlet humidities. Ultimately, our results identify the obstacle aspect ratio that maximizes mass-transport enhancement while minimizing pressure penalties, providing practical guidance for next-generation bipolar-plate design in real-world PEMFC applications.

Modeling and simulation

This section provides a detailed overview of the simulation modeling process. Initially, a geometric model is constructed and the associated control equations are formulated. Building upon these foundations, three-dimensional multiphase numerical models are subsequently developed and implemented.

Geometric model

As noted earlier, a PEMFC comprises two bipolar plates (BP) and membrane electrode assemblies (MEA). Each MEA typically consists of five main components: the cathode and anode, which each include a gas diffusion layer (GDL) and a catalyst layer (CL), with a proton exchange membrane (PEM) positioned between the cathode and anode catalyst layers3.

Numerical procedures and boundary conditions

Three-dimensional models were developed in SolidWorks 2024, with mesh generation carried out in ANSYS Workbench 2023R2. Subsequent CFD simulations in ANSYS Fluent 2023R2 employed second-order numerical schemes to minimize discretization errors and accurately resolve flow and heat transfer phenomena. The SIMPLE/SIMPLER algorithm was implemented to couple pressure and velocity fields, ensuring robust convergence (residual set to 1 × 10−5 with F-cycle iterations and BCGSTAB stabilization).

Operating conditions were maintained at 1 atm (101,325 Pa) and a gas temperature of 353 K. The anode was supplied with 100% pure hydrogen, while the cathode received air humidified to 100%, with future work planned to assess the effects of anode humidity. Structured meshes, validated by mesh independence tests, ensured computational efficiency without compromising accuracy.

Key boundary and initial conditions were succinctly defined—including fixed inlet mass flow rates, no-slip walls, zero-initialization of primary variables, and standardized reference pressures—to guarantee continuity and stability throughout the domain. Additionally, material properties (e.g., diffusion coefficients and reaction kinetics) were calibrated against experimental and literature data to reflect realistic operating scenarios.

Grid independency and validation

In all cases, structured meshes were used given the simplicity of the straight channel geometry. For the tapered channel in the modified model, five distinct mesh configurations were tested (see Fig. 2a) to ensure mesh reliability. Results and discussion

As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the polarization and power density curves for the baseline (no obstacle) configuration and for cathode channels equipped with obstacles at aspect ratios (AR) of 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, and 1 were obtained at 353 K and atmospheric pressure. Introduction of obstacles up to AR ≈ 0.25–0.50 markedly mitigates concentration overpotentials by enhancing mass transport and more uniform oxygen distribution within the reactive zone. At high voltages (V > 0.7 V), where kinetic and ohmic losses dominate, the impact of AR on performance is negligible; however, in the mid-to-low voltage range (0.3–0.6 V), the operating current density increases by over 30%, and the peak power density rises from 0.2978 W cm−2 (baseline) to 0.3576 W cm−2 at AR = 0.25, while only exhibiting marginal (≤ a few percent) declines for AR = 0.50–0.7537.Fig. 3(a) polarization (left axis) and power density (right axis); (b) maximum power density (left axis) and pressure drop (right axis).

(a) polarization (left axis) and power density (right axis); (b) maximum power density (left axis) and pressure drop (right axis).

Figure 3b shows despite this mass-transport enhancement, obstacles incur a hydrodynamic penalty. Under an operating voltage of 0.3 V, the cathode channel pressure drop increases from 7.4 Pa (no obstacle) to 8.10 Pa at AR = 0.25, remains essentially constant for AR = 0.50–0.75, and then slightly decreases at AR = 1. This behavior suggests that beyond a certain AR, flow redistribution behind the obstacles becomes more uniform, limiting additional pressure losses—consistent with previous numerical and experimental studies attributing excessive AR to flow saturation and reduced pumping efficiency38,39.

A combined electrochemical and hydrodynamic analysis indicates that AR ≈ 0.25–0.50 constitutes the optimal design window for cathode anode channels obstacles, wherein significant gains in power density and reduced concentration overpotentials are achieved at an acceptable pressure-drop penalty. In contrast, AR = 1.00, despite comparable pressure losses, yields slightly lower power output. These findings align with reports in leading fuel-cell journals and underscore the necessity of balancing mass-transport benefits against hydrodynamic losses to maximize PEMFC performance9.

Quantitative analysis of the velocity difference (ΔV) and maximum velocity (Vmax) within the cathode channel in Fig. 4 reveals that the incorporation of obstacles with increasing aspect ratio (AR) significantly enhances mixing and attenuates boundary-layer thickness. At an operating voltage of 0.3 V, ΔV rises from approximately 0.0056 m/s in the unobstructed baseline configuration to 0.0586 m/s at AR = 1.0, while at 0.4 V it increases from 0.0215 m/s to 0.0412 m/s. Concurrently, Vmax escalates from 1.26 m/s to 1.50 m/s at 0.3 V and from 1.29 m/s to 1.48 m/s at 0.4 V. These local velocity enhancements mitigate oxygen concentration overpotentials within the active region, corroborating Zhao et al.37, who demonstrated that elevated velocity differentials alongside improved reactant distribution yield higher current densities. However, Yin et al.38 noted that the increased Vmax and ΔV at AR = 1.0 incur a pronounced pressure-drop penalty, which can compromise pump-system efficiency. Consequently, an AR range of approximately 0.25–0.75 is recommended as optimal, achieving a favorable balance between mass-transport augmentation and acceptable hydrodynamic losses—a conclusion consistent with Jung et al.39, who reported that further AR increases provide only marginal mass-transport gains at the expense of substantial pressure-drop costs.Fig. 4Velocity difference and maximum velocity in cathode channels under different aspect ratio.

Velocity difference and maximum velocity in cathode channels under different aspect ratio.

In Fig. 5a, which presents the H2 mass-fraction contours at the cathode CL/GDL interface, it is evident that as the aspect ratio (AR) increases from the baseline configuration to AR = 1.0, the decrease in H2 concentration from inlet to outlet becomes more uniform at both 0.3 V and 0.4 V. Quantitatively, the mass-fraction difference of H2 in Fig. 5c between the inlet and outlet channels at 0.3 V rises from 0.1792 in the baseline case to 0.2625 at AR = 1.0, and at 0.4 V from 0.1388 to 0.193. This augmented concentration difference reflects a higher rate of reactant consumption along the channel, indicating improved cathodic reaction efficiency.Fig. 5(a) H2 mass fraction distribution at GDL/anode CL interface; (b) O2 mass fraction distribution at GDL/cathode CL interface; (c) H2 and O2 mass fraction drop at channels.

(a) H2 mass fraction distribution at GDL/anode CL interface; (b) O2 mass fraction distribution at GDL/cathode CL interface; (c) H2 and O2 mass fraction drop at channels.

Figure 5b depicts the O2 mass-fraction contours, showing a trend analogous to that observed for H2. With increasing AR, the O2 distribution becomes more homogeneous, yielding a more uniform consumption profile throughout the cathode channel. In Fig. 5c specifically, the O2 mass-fraction difference between inlet and outlet at 0.3 V increases from 0.0825 in the baseline to 0.11355 at AR = 1.0, and at 0.4 V from 0.058 to 0.08, which demonstrates enhanced oxygen utilization along the flow path. These improvements in reactant transport distribution directly contribute to the optimized electrochemical performance illustrated in Fig. 5. In particular, the elevated concentration gradients and consumption rates in designs with higher AR underscore the pivotal role of the obstructed channel geometry in strengthening mass transport at the cathode and promoting a uniform reaction rate. Consequently, configurations featuring greater obstruction achieve superior electrochemical performance by leveraging more effective reactant utilization.

In Fig. 6a the molar-concentration profile of O2 along the cathode channel reveals that, at the channel inlet, the O2 concentration in the baseline (no-obstacle) case is approximately 0.00305 kmol/m3. When an obstacle is introduced at AR = 0.25, this inlet concentration rises slightly to 0.00306 kmol/m3, and for AR values of 0.50–0.75 it further increases to 0.00312 kmol/m3. At the channel outlet, the O2 concentration increases from 0.00688 kmol/m3 (baseline) to 0.00716 kmol/m3 for AR = 0.25, ranges between 0.00676–0.00699 kmol/m3 for AR = 0.50–0.75, and reaches 0.00727 kmol/m3 at AR = 1.0. These observations indicate a reduction in concentration losses due to enhanced mixing and the elimination of thick boundary-layer zones37,38. Simultaneously, the water (H2O) molar-concentration profile along the same channel shows an inlet value of about 0.00577 kmol/m3 in the baseline configuration. With AR = 0.25–0.50, this inlet concentration remains in the range of 0.00577–0.00575 kmol/m3, while for AR = 0.75–1.00 it spans 0.00576–0.00595 kmol/m3. At the outlet, the baseline H2O concentration is approximately 0.00330 kmol/m3; in contrast, AR = 0.25–0.50–0.75–1.00 yield outlet values of 0.00330, 0.00338, 0.00345, and 0.00351 kmol/m3, respectively. This trend demonstrates improved liquid removal and mitigation of local flooding40. Collectively, these data confirm that intermediate AR values (≈ 0.25–0.50) establish an optimal balance between enhanced oxygen distribution and adequate water drainage; this balance minimizes both concentration losses and hydrodynamic pressure drop while maintaining superior electrochemical performance9.Fig. 6(a) molar-concentration profile of O2 and H2O along the cathode channel; (b) molar-concentration profile of H2 along the cathode channel.

(a) molar-concentration profile of O2 and H2O along the cathode channel; (b) molar-concentration profile of H2 along the cathode channel.

Figure 6b the hydrogen (H2) molar-concentration profile along the anode channel exhibits a slight decrease from approximately 0.03246 kmol/m3 at the inlet to roughly 0.03067–0.03085 kmol/m3 at the outlet, regardless of the obstacle aspect ratio (AR = 0.00 to 1.00). The minimal variation in H2 concentration across all AR cases indicates that the introduction of obstacles in the cathode channel does not significantly influence the hydrogen distribution in the anode. This behavior arises because hydrogen transport under the chosen operating pressure and temperature remains efficient, and the primary mass-transport limitation resides on the cathode side. Consequently, while cathode obstacles yield marked improvements in oxygen concentration profiles and overall electrochemical performance, their impact on anode-side hydrogen concentration is essentially negligible40.

The temperature distributions at the cathode catalyst-layer/GDL interface for operating voltages of 0.3 V and 0.4 V are presented in Fig. 7a. As the obstacle aspect ratio (AR) increases, the volumetric heat-generation zones due to electrochemical reactions become increasingly localized, and the peak temperature shifts toward the channel center. At 0.3 V (higher current density), the unobstructed baseline case exhibits a relatively uniform and lower temperature field (~ 353.5–354.3 K). In contrast, for AR ≥ 0.25, the combined effect of higher local current densities and reduced convective heat removal results in a peak temperature of approximately 354.6–354.7 K. At 0.4 V (lower current density), all configurations maintain an overall lower temperature range (~ 353.5–354.0 K), yet intermediate AR values (0.25–0.75) demonstrate a more uniform thermal profile and attenuated temperature peak compared to AR = 1.0. This trend in elevated peak temperatures at higher AR closely correlates with the local current-density distributions shown in Fig. 7b and the previously discussed concentration-overpotential patterns, indicating that obstacles intensify local current-density—and thus heat production—while intermediate AR values (≈ 0.25–0.50) provide enhanced mixing that mitigates excessive thermal accumulation.Fig. 7(a) temperature distributions at the cathode CL/GDL (b) current density distributions at the wall terminal cathode.

(a) temperature distributions at the cathode CL/GDL (b) current density distributions at the wall terminal cathode.

The water-content contours at the mid height of membrane (Fig. 8) for an operating voltage of 0.3 V reveal that the AR = 0.25 configuration exhibits the highest liquid-water saturation near the channel outlet, where the contour levels reach approximately 2.90–2.95. In contrast, other AR cases show lower water-content values at this location. This observation indicates that obstacles with a low aspect ratio (≈ 0.25) reduce the local evaporation rate and vapor-phase transport near the outlet; in other words, the mass flux of water vapor from the GDL into the bulk flow is weakened, resulting in increased localized water accumulation. Similar numerical findings by Tiss et al.41 demonstrated that small-scale obstacles can induce local water buildup in specific regions. Therefore, AR = 0.25 appears to strike a balance between sufficient convective mixing for oxygen delivery and restrained local evaporation, ultimately yielding the highest water saturation at the membrane surface.Fig. 8Water-content contours at the mid height of membrane.

Sensitivity analysis

As shown in Fig. 9a, At 333 K, introducing obstacles consistently reduces peak power density: compared to the unobstructed case (0.6317 W cm−2), AR = 0.25 yields –3.4% (0.6100 W cm−2), AR = 0.50 –4.3% (0.6042 W cm−2), AR = 0.75 –5.3% (0.5980 W cm−2) and AR = 1.00 –3.9% (0.6070 W cm−2). Conversely, at 343 K the mid-range obstacles markedly boost performance: AR = 0.25 gives + 34.6% (0.5074 W cm−2), AR = 0.50 + 37.3% (0.5174 W cm−2), AR = 0.75 + 40.4% (0.5289 W cm−2) and AR = 1.00 + 34.6% (0.5072 W cm−2) over the baseline of 0.3769 W cm−2. This trend confirms that moderate aspect ratios enhance ORR kinetics and oxygen transport under intermediate thermal conditions. At higher temperatures (353–363 K), the relative gains narrow but remain positive: in the 353 K case, AR = 0.25–0.75 improve power density by + 20.2% (0.3576 W cm−2), + 18.1% (0.3518 W cm−2) and + 18.0% (0.3515 W cm−2), respectively, while AR = 1.00 yields + 16.1% (0.3456 W cm−2) versus 0.2978 W cm−2. At 363 K, AR = 0.25–0.75 deliver + 15.9% (0.2866 W cm−2), + 14.6% (0.2834 W cm−2) and + 15.4% (0.2856 W cm−2), whereas AR = 1.00 offers + 12.4% (0.2781 W cm−2) over 0.2474 W cm−2. These results indicate that AR≈0.25–0.50 strikes the optimal balance between mass-transport enhancement and pressure-drop penalty across the temperature range, maximizing performance gains without excessive hydraulic losses.Fig. 9Sensitivity of PEMFC power density to obstacle aspect ratio (AR = 0–1) under varying (a) temperatures, (b) pressures, and (c) anode/(d) cathode relative humidities.

Sensitivity of PEMFC power density to obstacle aspect ratio (AR = 0–1) under varying (a) temperatures, (b) pressures, and (c) anode/(d) cathode relative humidities.

As depicted in Fig. 9b, Under atmospheric pressure (1 atm), introducing a mild obstruction (AR = 0.25) elevates peak power density from 0.2978 W cm−2 to 0.3576 W cm−2 (+ 20.2%), principally by enhancing convective mixing and mitigating oxygen concentration overpotentials without imposing prohibitive hydraulic losses. Mid-range obstructions (AR = 0.50–0.75) sustain similar gains of + 18.1% (0.3518 W cm−2) and + 18.0% (0.3515 W cm−2), respectively, while a channel (AR = 1) still achieves + 16.1% (0.3456 W cm−2). At 2 atm, this advantage magnifies—AR = 0.25 delivers + 66.6% (0.8815 vs. 0.5293 W cm−2), and even AR = 1.00 maintains + 62.4% (0.8590 W cm−2)—reflecting that moderate aspect ratios optimally balance improved reactant transport against the incremental pressure-drop penalty. In contrast, at elevated pressures (3–4 atm) the benefit of obstruction vanishes as frictional losses dominate. At 3 atm, AR = 0.25 yields only a + 0.1% gain (0.8634 vs. 0.8624 W cm−2), whereas AR ≥ 0.50 provokes –19% to –20% declines. At 4 atm, any barrier degrades performance relative to the unobstructed case (0.9134 W cm−2)—AR = 0.25: –14.7%; AR = 0.50–0.75: –38.5%; AR = 1.00: –37.8%—indicating that under high reactant partial pressures, the marginal mixing gain cannot offset the steep rise in pressure-drop losses. Consequently, a smooth channel (AR = 0) is preferable for high-pressure operation.

Figure 9c illustrates anode-side relative humidity sensitivity: under anode-side relative humidity (RH) ranging from 0 to 100%, introducing a mild obstruction (AR = 0.25) consistently enhances peak power density by 17–20% versus the unobstructed baseline: specifically, at RH = 0% it rises from 0.2504 to 0.2937 W cm−2 (+ 17.3%), at RH = 25% from 0.2631 to 0.3105 W cm−2 (+ 18.1%), at RH = 50% from 0.2768 to 0.3288 W cm−2 (+ 18.8%), at RH = 75% from 0.2878 to 0.3446 W cm−2 (+ 19.7%) and at RH = 100% from 0.2978 to 0.3576 W cm−2 (+ 20.1%). Mid-range aspect ratios (AR = 0.50–0.75) still deliver significant gains of + 14–18%, whereas a channel with abstacles (AR = 1) yields more modest improvements of + 11–16%, reflecting that moderate obstacles most effectively balance enhanced hydrogen distribution and minimized pressure-drop penalties. These trends indicate that AR ≈ 0.25 provides the optimal compromise for water management and reactant transport under varying anode humidification.

Similarly, Fig. 9d shows cathode-side relative humidity sensitivity: introducing a mild obstruction (AR = 0.25) in the cathode flow-field consistently boosts peak power density by + 18–20% across all relative humidities: from + 21.9% at RH = 0% (0.1884 → 0.2297 W cm−2) down to + 20.1% at RH = 100% (0.2978 → 0.3576 W cm−2). Mid-range aspect ratios (AR = 0.50–0.75) still deliver substantial gains of + 15–17% (e.g. + 15.6% and + 16.5% at RH = 50%), whereas a channel with abstacles (AR = 1) yields more modest improvements of + 13–16%, indicating that excessive obstruction begins to incur pressure-drop penalties that partially offset mass-transport benefits. Mechanistically, AR ≈ 0.25 optimally perturbs the boundary layer to enhance O2 convection into the GDL and membrane catalyst layer while facilitating water evacuation, thereby minimizing both concentration overpotentials and local flooding. Higher ARs, by contrast, increase hydraulic resistance and can trap condensed water in pore networks, which marginally erodes performance gains.

In summary, obstacle aspect ratios of approximately 0.25–0.50 achieve the best compromise among oxygen-transport enhancement, acceptable pressure-drop penalties, and effective water management over a broad spectrum of temperatures (343–353 K), pressures (1–2 atm), and relative-humidity conditions (0–100%). Under low-temperature or high-pressure scenarios, larger obstacles generate excessive pressure losses that negate kinetic and mass-transport improvements; conversely, small obstacles (AR ≈ 0.25) introduce sufficient flow perturbation and balanced water-oxygen distribution to ensure optimal cell performance.
