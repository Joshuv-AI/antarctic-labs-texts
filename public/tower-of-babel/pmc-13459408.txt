Parent views on a two-step consent process used in emergency neonatal research in the delivery room in Eastern Uganda
Authors: Alice Nakiyemba, Agnes Napyo, Martha Muduwa, John M Ssenkusu, Francis Okello, Cornelia Hagmann, Judith Namuyonga, Adam Hewitt-Smith, Kate Loe, Grace Abongo, Denis Amorut, Julius Wandabwa, Peter Olupot-Olupot, Kathy Burgoine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObtaining ethically valid consent for delivery room research from parents facing the imminent birth of a sick or preterm infant is particularly challenging. While deferred consent has been used successfully in some delivery room studies - especially in low-risk observational research - its application is more complex in settings with high neonatal mortality and in the context of interventional trials. In such contexts, alternative approaches such as two-step consent – where initial verbal consent is obtained at enrolment, followed by full written informed consent after maternal and neonatal stabilisation – have been proposed. This study explores the acceptability of this approach.MethodsThis qualitative study from March 2023 and February 2024, was nested within a pilot randomised controlled trial of immediate continuous positive airways pressure (CPAP) in the delivery room for very low birthweight infants (< 1500 g) at a government hospital in Uganda. Focus group discussions were held with 6–12 purposively selected mothers, fathers, and other caregivers of infants enrolled in the trial, with a total of three FGDs conducted. Groups were mixed by caregiver type. Data were analysed inductively using a thematic approach, allowing codes and themes to emerge directly from the data. Transcripts were coded using NVivo 12 to facilitate analysis and explore shared and divergent perspectives across participants.ResultsMothers generally viewed the two-step consent process positively. Verbal consent was seen as sufficient for an initial decision despite limited understanding, while written consent was considered more appropriate once the mother and infant were stabilised. Mothers valued the autonomy that the initial verbal consent gave them. Digital recording was acceptable to all, and the use of a script supported consistency. Fathers wished to be informed, but mothers emphasised their capacity to decide independently, while many preferred fathers to be present.ConclusionDelivery room trials in low-resource settings involve complex ethical and practical challenges related to obtaining informed consent in time-sensitive situations. In this study, the two-step consent process was found to be acceptable to parents and caregivers, supported by collaboration with the community advisory board. Further research should continue to refine consent approaches to ensure they remain ethically robust, practically feasible in emergency settings, and acceptable to parents across diverse cultural and clinical contexts.Trial registrationStudy is registered on Pan African Clinical Trials Registry (PACTR) PACTR202208462613789. Introduction

Randomised controlled trials are the gold standard for evaluating clinical interventions, and obtaining informed consent is a fundamental ethical requirement for their conduct. In neonatal trials, consent is typically provided by parents on behalf of their infants. When randomisation is required soon after birth or in the delivery room, obtaining ethically valid prospective consent can present a major challenge in conducting neonatal trials [1].

Preterm birth is often rapid and unpredictable, and in such situations, time is critical. Seeking consent at the time of delivery has challenges. Mothers may be in pain, recovering from birth or receiving treatment, and both parents may experience considerable distress. Therefore, approaching families for trial participation and obtaining valid, informed consent under these circumstances can be challenging [1]. They may not be able to engage in a comprehensive consent procedure, while lengthy discussions can delay treatment allocation and compromise infant outcomes. Consequently, traditional written informed consent is not always feasible in trials involving preterm infants, especially in the delivery room setting. As such, alternative or complementary approaches are necessary to ensure equitable inclusion while maintaining ethical standards.

One alternative is to use antenatal consent for women identified as at risk of preterm birth. Although this offers an important opportunity to support informed decision-making in advance, this approach has several challenges. Firstly, many infants identified as high risk antenatally may not ultimately require enrolment at birth, which can lead to inefficiencies in recruitment processes and the use of limited research staff in resource-constrained settings [2]. Secondly, even when consent is appropriately targeted to at-risk women, antenatal discussion of potential interventions may contribute to parental anxiety regarding complications that may ultimately never occur. Thirdly, restricting consent solely to the antenatal period excludes infants born unexpectedly or precipitously, as is common in preterm birth. Such infants are often the most critically ill and stand to benefit most from advances in care, and their exclusion may introduce selection bias [3–6]. Importantly, however, the possibility that an anticipated event may not occur does not negate the ethical value of offering antenatal information and consent, which can support respect for maternal autonomy when appropriately implemented within a risk-stratified approach.

In response to the challenges of obtaining informed consent in emergency settings, deferred consent - seeking parental consent as soon as reasonably possible after enrolment of their infant - has increasingly been adopted in delivery room studies [7–12]. This approach allows infants to receive potentially beneficial interventions without delay, while still giving parents the opportunity to decide on their infant’s continued participation in trial procedures and contribution to data collection. Compared to antenatal consent, deferred consent increases the number of mothers and their infants eligible for delivery-room trials, improves recruitment rates, facilitates the inclusion of higher-risk infants with greater morbidities, and reduces selection bias [13–16]. Consequently, the final study sample is more representative and generalisable [17].

Although some researchers initially expressed concern that deferred consent might undermine trust between parents and researchers, experienced investigators have reported that parents were generally receptive to the approach [18]. In fact, parents have reported that deferred consent supported their informed decision-making and enhanced their trust in the research process [18]. However, parental acceptance may vary depending on the perceived risk and nature of the intervention, which may not align with the clinical urgency of the situation. Even in life-threatening neonatal contexts, some studies involve interventions that are part of standard care or carry minimal incremental risk, while others introduce greater uncertainty, influencing how parents interpret and accept enrolment. Trials of investigational medicinal products present further challenges, such as additional safety concerns and short timelines available to commence therapy - however, deferred consent has also been used successfully in such clinical trials [19]. For instance, a UK survey of parents’ whose children were enrolled in meningitis or meningococcal septicaemia trials found that most parents accepted enrollment without prior explanation; however, two-thirds wanted to be informed as soon as their child had stabilised [20].

Despite its advantages, deferred consent still raises ethical concerns when parental consent cannot be obtained after randomisation, particularly in cases where infants die before consent is sought. These situations can lead to the loss of valuable data and introduce bias, disproportionately affecting critically ill infants. Furthermore, seeking consent from bereaved parents can add to their emotional distress [20–23]. This partly explains why deferred consent is more commonly accepted for minimal-risk observational studies than for interventional delivery room trials [24, 25]. Nonetheless, a UK survey among bereaved parents whose children had participated in research found that most parents still wished to be informed after their child’s death, although this process was considered to require a sensitive and individualised approach [20].

In high-mortality settings such as Uganda, where the neonatal mortality remains high and most deaths occur within the first 24 h, implementing deferred consent can become even more challenging. In Uganda, parental consent for children’s participation in healthcare and research is governed by national ethical guidelines requiring permission from a parent or legal guardian for individuals under 18 years, alongside age-appropriate assent where applicable. In practice, decision-making is often influenced by broader family structures, with fathers and extended relatives playing an important role. Seeking consent from bereaved parents in these contexts has been considered potentially harmful, as it may exacerbate emotional distress and lead to feelings of guilt or self-blame for having allowed their child to participate in research [21]. In recognition of this, a trial comparing treatment of children requiring fluid resuscitation in three African countries employed a modified approach: verbal consent was obtained from parents at enrolment, followed by full written consent after the child was stabilised. For children who died before the consent process could be completed, parents were not approached for deferred consent and ethical approval was obtained to waive full consent [21]. This approach was acknowledged to balance the ethical responsibilities of protecting both participants and researchers, and to avoid delays in starting treatment [21]. In high-income settings, some neonatal trials have also employed a two-stage consent pathway: oral consent during the time-critical period followed by written consent later [17, 26–28].

The Delivery Room Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (DR-CPAP) trial was a single-centre pilot feasibility and acceptability trial of immediate CPAP among preterm infants with a birthweight of 800–1500 g at a referral hospital in Uganda [29]. Infants were randomised to one of two arms: either the application of CPAP +/- oxygen in the delivery room within 15 min of birth, or supplementary oxygen at delivery when indicated. During the design of the DR-CPAP trial, investigators, community representatives, and parents of preterm infants engaged in discussions on consent methodology. Preterm birth constituted a neonatal emergency, and delays caused by conventional consent procedure were considered unacceptable [29]. We also recognised that most mothers delivered without prior antenatal contact with the referral hospital, often presenting from a wide geographical area, and therefore antenatal screening was not feasible. Furthermore, many mothers arrived in which, while not diminishing their legal capacity to provide consent, may limit their ability to engage in detailed or prolonged consent discussions. It was therefore concluded that full consent prior to enrolment was neither feasible nor ethically appropriate for our trial. Drawing on the principles outlined above and the experiences of the previous Ugandan trails of severely ill children that had successfully implemented modified deferred consent [21, 30], a two-step consent process, incorporating verbal consent during labour followed by full written consent after delivery, was adopted. This approach aimed to balance the ethical imperative of informed parental-decision making with the practical need to initiate potentially lifesaving intervention within 15 min of birth. By providing parents with early information and a sense of autonomy through verbal consent, the DR-CPAP trial sought to improve both parental understanding and the acceptability of participation in this time-critical context.

The aim of this study was to explore the experiences and perspectives of parents regarding this two-step consent process to inform the development of context-appropriate, ethically robust consent procedures for delivery-room research in low-resource settings.

Methods

Study design

We carried out a qualitative study between 29 March 2023 and 5 February 2024 to explore the acceptability of a two-step consent process in a pilot randomised control trial of immediate CPAP in the delivery room for very low birth weight (VLBW, < 1500 g) infants. The study was nested in the DR-CPAP trial —a single-centre pilot feasibility and acceptability trial of immediate CPAP among infants with a birthweight of 800–1500 g [29]. Infants in the DR-CPAP trial were randomised to one of two arms: either the application of CPAP +/- oxygen in the delivery room within 15 min of birth, or supplementary oxygen at delivery when indicated. The qualitative study examined the experiences of mothers and caregivers, including grandmothers, aunts, and fathers of VLBW infants enrolled in the DR-CPAP trial through focus group discussions (FGDs). The study was conducted in line with the Consolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [31].

Study setting

This study was conducted in Mbale Regional Referral Hospital (MRRH), a government hospital in Eastern Uganda serving a population exceeding 4.5 million people. There are up to 10,000 deliveries in MRRH every year, and there is a dedicated Level 2 neonatal unit (NNU) that admits approximately 2,500 neonates annually, including around 450 VLBW infants. The in-patient mortality rate for VLBW infants is 26.5% [21]. All neonates enrolled in the DR-CPAP study received Level II neonatal care as defined by the Ugandan National Clinical Protocols for Managing Small and Sick Newborns.

The DR-CPAP two-step consent process

The two-step consent process involved the following two steps:

Initial verbal consent, including agreement to randomisation, was sought from the mother and/or the father if a preterm delivery was anticipated during active labour or in obstetric conditions requiring urgent delivery.

Full written informed consent was subsequently obtained from the mother and/or father after both the mother and infant had been stabilised and once eligibility criteria were confirmed.

Verbal consent was obtained by a specially trained researcher. Mothers and/or fathers were provided with a concise verbal explanation of the DR-CPAP trial and given the opportunity to opt into the trial. The verbal consent script is included as Supplement 2. Before commencing the consent script, permission was sought from the mother and/or father to audio-record the process. If consent was granted, the entire verbal consent interaction was audio recorded. Audio files were securely stored in a dedicated folder on an encrypted server at the research institute, with access restricted to authenticated and authorised personnel only. The use of audio recording for verbal consent was proposed and endorsed by our Patient and Participant Involvement (PPI) group. Audio recordings were not routinely transcribed but will be securely stored for five years. Transcription will be undertaken only if required, for example in response to concerns about the consent process.

For those infants who met all the inclusion and exclusion criteria, written, informed consent was obtained when the infant had been stabilised; treatment had been initiated; and the mother had recovered and was able to receive, evaluate and discuss the information. Full informed written consent was always obtained within 24 h of birth. This process was conducted by either the Research Midwife or Trial Medical Officer. All mothers were given a patient information sheet in their preferred language, outlining the details of the trial. For those who are unable to read, the information sheet was read aloud, and a second person was always present during the informed consent process to ensure transparency and support. All mothers were encouraged to ask questions and seek clarification before signing the consent form. The right of the mother to decline participation without providing a reason was fully respected. Participant information sheets were also made available for the mother’s partner or next of kin. Each participant either personally signed or provided a thumb print on three copies of the most recently approved version of the consent form prior to the initiation of any further study-specific procedures. A copy of the signed consent form was given to the participant, one copy was placed in the medical records, and a copy was retained for the Investigator Site File.

The patient information sheet and the consent form were translated into the three main local languages (Ateso, Lumasaaba, Luganda), and the appropriate version was provided based on the mother’s stated preference. All study information was explained in her preferred language, with opportunities to ask questions. Participation was voluntary, and mothers were informed they could withdraw at any time without affecting their infant’s care.

For participants who were unable to read, information was delivered orally in their preferred language in the presence of a friend, family member or independent witness. The patient information sheet was read aloud verbatim, and consent was documented by thumbprint. For deaf participants who communicated via a relative using sign-language, this approach was accepted, and participation proceeded following informed consent.

For mothers and/or fathers who initially provided verbal consent but were unwilling or unable to provide written informed consent within 24 h of delivery, the infant was excluded from the trial. In such cases, no further data were collected, any data previously collected were destroyed and the infant was replaced in the trial. Regardless of participation, all infants continued to receive standard care in the neonatal unit for the duration of their hospital stay.

Participants and sampling

Focus group discussion (FGDs) were conducted with mothers and caregivers of infants enrolled in the trial. Each FGD comprised between 6 and 12 participants, with a minimum threshold of six to ensure the reliability of findings. Participants were purposively selected to capture a range of perspectives, including mothers, fathers, other care givers, various modes of delivery and parents of infants enrolled in both the control and intervention arms of the study. FGDs were convened once enough eligible participants had been identified.

The principle of data saturation guided the conclusion of the FGDs [32]. An iterative process of sampling and analysis was employed, continuing until no new themes or codes emerged from the data, indicating that saturation had been reached.

Consent

Participant information sheets and consent forms were prepared in English and the three main local languages (Ateso, Lumasaaba, Luganda) and provided in the participant’s preferred language. For those unable to read, the information was read aloud. Participants were encouraged to ask questions before providing consent, and their right to decline participation without giving reasons was respected.

Data collection

A topic guide was developed by the research team for the FGDs and was modified according to the preliminary findings (Supplement 3). Data were collected in either a local language or English by a trained interviewer in a neutral location. Data were collected by two researchers: a female nurse with a PhD in qualitative research and extensive experience in qualitative methods (AgN) and a second female researcher, a medical officer who underwent training with the lead qualitative researcher (MM). AgN had no prior relationship with the participants prior to the study and MM was the trial coordinator and had interacted with the participants during their involvement in the main DR-CPAP trial. The interview guide was initially piloted on a small sample to ensure the ease of understanding and suitability of the questions. Limited changes were made after the pilot, such as language and phrasing. Mothers and caregivers were invited to participate after discharge from the neonatal unit. Interviews were held in the research institute away from the neonatal unit. Each FGD lasted from 60 to 120 min. The participants were asked to provide written consent prior to the interviews.

Data analysis

We analysed interview data using an inductive thematic approach, allowing codes and themes to emerge directly from the data rather than applying a pre-existing framework [33]. This approach was chosen to capture participants’ experiences of the consent process in their own terms, without constraining responses to predefined categories.

Focus group discussions included mothers, fathers, and aunts, and data were analysed together rather than stratified by participant type. Thematic analysis aimed to identify shared and divergent perspectives across caregiver groups, with attention paid to differences in views where they emerged.

Focus group discussions were audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Recordings were transcribed verbatim in Microsoft Word in the original language of discussion. Where discussions were conducted in local languages, transcripts were subsequently translated into English by trained research assistants fluent in both English and the respective local language. All transcripts were reviewed manually by at least two of four authors (AgN, AlN, MM, KB), and initial codes were generated systematically across the dataset. Codes were then collated into potential themes and iteratively refined through team discussion.

Analysis was conducted in parallel with data collection, allowing emerging finding to inform recruitment until data saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new themes were identified. Once data collection was complete, all transcripts were re-examined in Nvivo by at least two authors (AgN, AlN, MM, KB) to confirm coding consistency. Final themes and subthemes were agreed through collaborative discussion and consensus.

Themes and sub-themes

Verbal consent

Adequacy of information provided

Mothers shared their perceptions of whether the verbal consent provided sufficient understanding of the study. They reported that the verbal consent gave them an adequate - though necessarily limited - understanding of the study. They described the information as a suitable representation of the trial, which could later be expanded upon during the full informed consent discussion. The subsequent written consent process was viewed as an opportunity to revisit the information and formally confirm what they had already agreed to during the challenging circumstances of labour.

“For me this verbal communication was so easy to understand. I understood it quickly, so that by the time the written consent came I had already understood the verbal, so it did not disturb me a lot.” (FGD 1, R9).

The limited time available for obtaining verbal consent restricted the amount of information that could be communicated compared with the more comprehensive written informed consent process. Nevertheless, all mothers reported that the information provided verbally was sufficient to support their decision-making at that time.

“It was just enough for me with my condition, it was enough.” (R8, FGD 2).

“The doctor explained to me everything, and I understood and accepted. (R9, FGD3)

Mothers also acknowledged that, at the time of verbal consent, their primary focus was on their own wellbeing and that of their infant.

“As long as you explain the key issues, all information is not relevant at that time. The key issue is saving the baby.” (FGD 2, R2).

Perceptions of duration

Whilst most mothers perceived the duration of the verbal consent process as brief, they also considered it adequate given the circumstances and context.

“The questions and time they gave us was enough because they gave us time to decide.” (R6, FGD 3).

“It was a short period just after I had arrived. It was short but I understood what the doctor was saying.” (FGD1, R9).

For some mothers, however, even the brief duration of the verbal consent was perceived as too long when they were in active labour and experiencing contractions.

“For me I saw that the time was too much because I was in a lot of pain.” (R10, FGD 1).

Even among mothers whose preterm labour was successfully halted and who did not proceed to deliver immediately, the verbal consent process and its duration were generally perceived as adequate.

“That time was enough because he explained to me, and I understood. I spent a week in HDU before my operation, but we were in touch. So, for me the time I was given was enough.” (R9, FGD 1).

Acceptability of recording

Our community advisory board advised that recording the verbal consent process would be acceptable and would strengthen the integrity of the procedure. Mothers also supported this approach, noting that the recording fostered a sense of trustworthiness, reassurance, and accountability.

“Due to the recording, it (verbal consent) was enough. Because in that condition, you would not read and understand anything. But you just take it like it is. Because the recording, you know it’s at least the truth, they are going to help the baby. By that time, it’s enough.” (R5, FGD 2).

Maternal willingness during labour

All mothers interviewed expressed willingness to provide verbal consent while in labour. Despite the inherent vulnerability of this period, many reported feeling able to make an autonomous decision.

“I was happy because they would come and talk with me, they would come and tell me, they would just tell you and not force you. They would tell you to take your time to answer. If I didn’t want something I would say I don’t want. That’s why I say I was happy; they took care of my baby.” (R2, FGD 3).

“For me my decision was from the bottom of my heart, I decided for myself.” (R9, FGD 1).

Many mothers expressed positive views about the verbal consent process. Despite being in labour, the information provided before delivery was still appreciated, and many reported that it was possible to listen adequately between contractions. However, most mothers acknowledged that it was challenging to understand everything at this time.

“It depends in the condition they find you in. They may find you when you are not so badly off and can understand what they are saying so they give you enough time. But when they find you in the labour suite, I think of the things being explained to you may pick some or maybe not because they may be telling you and it (contraction) comes. So, it helps a lot when they come back and explain to you again (written consent) because during that time of contractions you will not understand everything.” (R7, FGD 1).

“Yeah, I just needed to answer and let the nurse go as long as I understood what it was all about.” (R5, FGD 2).

Some mothers reported feeling obliged to provide verbal consent given the circumstances. They appreciated the opportunity to provide written informed consent at a later stage, recognising that completing this process during labour would not have been feasible or fully valid.

“Now like for me, if you have told me that I am not okay and my pregnancy is not yet due, if you tell me like that, I’ll think about it and see that you are going to help me so let me accept because I cannot care for the baby until they survive.” (R4, FGD 1).

“My decision, I just accepted because of the pain I was in at that time (verbal consent). Later, after explaining to me (written consent), is when I accepted with one heart. During the other time (labour) I can’t lie to you that I was sure about my decision, I just accepted.” (FGD 1, R7).

Written consent

Importance and necessity

Overall, mothers expressed strong support for the two-step consent process. They appreciated receiving essential information during labour, while also valuing the opportunity to complete the full written informed consent process at a later, more appropriate time.

“I think that you love me so much, you care a lot for me that’s why you came twice.” (R10, FGD1).

“Because the first time we are not understanding ourselves, that’s why you come again the second time to explain to us.” (R8, FGD 1).

Although verbal consent was welcomed, many mothers acknowledged the importance of having the opportunity to receive full information and provide their comprehensive written consent.

“That verbal one, the information was enough at the time. It is done at a time when the pains are too much, so you just accept anything that comes. So, still I needed that written consent because you never know, at times you may get verbal, but the written is not the same as verbal. But after time we were given that written one, you go through and decide very well.” - (FGD 2, R5).

Timing of post-delivery

When asked whether they would have preferred to receive the full information provided during the written consent process at an earlier stage, all mothers responded negatively, citing fatigue and intensity of contractions as limiting factors.

“At that moment I would not have had the energy to read through the written consent papers.” (FGD 2, R5).

“You can’t read with that pain. You can’t read anything. The pain was too much. What they did was okay because at first, I just accepted. Then when I reached there (neonatal unit), I’ve seen what is happening. So, it is right for me to sign.” (FGD2, R1).

Most mothers felt that having the opportunity to provide full written consent after recovering from the birth of their infant allowed them to make an informed decision, which they believed would not have been possible during labour.

“Because I’d recovered and I was able to read, to understand everything. It was okay. And it was the time to make the final decision in a sober mind.” (FGD 2, R8).

Many mothers felt that that the volume of information provided during the full written informed consent process would have been overwhelming if it had been delivered during the time they were in active labour.

“The situation you are in at that moment (labour) is not good and you can’t concentrate and listen very well to understand. It’s not possible. It’s better to do it after.” (R9, FGD3).

“It wouldn’t have been possible (during labour) because the information was bulky and needed time.” (FGD 1, R9).

Mothers were given 24 hours after the delivery to provide full written informed consent. While most considered this timeframe sufficient, those who had undergone a caesarean section expressed a preference for a longer period. Nonetheless, they also emphasised the importance of obtaining consent as early as possible.

“24 hours are enough except for those ones who had a caesarean. You know the second day you feel tired, but however the earlier the better.” (R8, FGD 2).

“Yes, we deserve 24 hours, it’s enough for us to decide and its quick enough to help us.” (FGD, R6).

Correlation between verbal and written consent

Although no parents changed their minds regarding their infant’s participation during the written informed consent process, many appreciated having the opportunity to do so, had they wished.

“I was happy because I was given a chance to decide what I want and what I don’t want, I had a chance to say no.” (FGD 3, R5).

Paternal involvement

Role in verbal consent

Culturally, fathers are often expected to make family and household decisions. While most mothers felt it would be ideal for the father to present during the verbal consent process, they also reported feeling capable of making the decision independently.

“It is good for the father of the baby to be there so they can all agree regarding the condition of the baby. But all the same, if one parent is present, they decide. Because I was present, I decided to participate, and he has no problem about it.” (FGD 2, R3).

“It would be good for the father to be present but if he is far away, then he can’t be able. I see no problem if the father is not present because the process of consent is intended to help our child so there is no problem.” (R4, FGD3).

Fathers expressed a desire to be informed about the research but emphasised that the mother remains the primary decision-maker regarding her baby.

“I found a research nurse educating my wife, she called me and asked me to read through, but I was too busy. I told her my wife knows how to read. She told me to finish what I was doing so she could explain to me for only 2 minutes, I allowed her to read through and then I referred her back to my wife.” (FGD 1, R3, father).

“It is very important for me also to know because at the time when she delivers and the doctors find that she has had a child which is too small or too soon, it helps me also to understand.” (R1, FGD 1).

Discussion

The immediate care of sick or preterm infants in the delivery room is fundamental for improving both survival and long-term health outcomes. This is especially true in low-resource-settings, where the neonatal mortality remains unacceptably high, and most deaths occur within the first 24 h after birth. Consequently, delivery room studies are essential to identify effective interventions and improve care practices in these vulnerable populations. To our knowledge, this is the first study exploring parental perspectives on consent for a neonatal trial in a low-income country, as well as the first study to provide insight into perceptions of a two-step consent approach in delivery room research within such settings. However, the implementation of clinical trials is frequently hindered by the difficulty of obtaining valid informed consent in time-critical and emotionally distressing circumstances, which pose significant ethical and practical challenges. Mothers are often in active labour, and parents may be overwhelmed by distress due to the imminent or actual birth of a preterm or critically ill infant [34]. These factors complicate the consent process and require careful consideration to balance the urgency of care with respect for parental autonomy. In the postnatal period, parents have more time and are in a relatively less stressful environment, thus enabling them to make more fully informed decisions.

The mothers in our trial expressed unanimously that it would not have been possible for them to provide full written consent during labour and that once recovered from the birth of their infant they could make an informed decision. In studies from high-income countries, providers using deferred consent for delivery room research have generally reported positive parental perceptions [17, 35]. In an Australian study of parental opinions of deferred consent in neonatal research found that 89% of parents of infants enrolled into a research study using deferred consent considered it to be acceptable, and half would not have preferred the option of prospective consent due to the stressful situation they were in [35].

In a pilot randomised controlled trial of extremely preterm infants where consent was obtained either during labour or within 4 h of delivery, parents reported that a simultaneous verbal explanation of the study by the research team was a key factor in their decision-making [36]. This highlights the importance of verbal communication during these sensitive situations. Some studies have further adapted the deferred consent approach further to improve the parental autonomy by including a prospective oral consent and a written deferred consent [17, 21, 28]. The two-step consent process has been shown to both inform parents about study participation and enhance their satisfaction while safeguarding their autonomy [28]. In our study, most mothers greatly valued the additional step of verbal consent during labour, as it provided insight into the trial and a sense of autonomy, thereby supporting more informed decision-making at the time of full written consent. Some previous studies have raised concerns that the use of deferred consent alone may negatively affect the parent-healthcare worker relationship [28]. In our study, parents found the two-step consent process to be acceptable within the context of neonatal emergency research, which may offer a potential alternative approach in this setting. Similarly in the ALBINO study in Germany, most parents preferred two-step consent over deferred consent, with one-quarter of parents reporting that deferred consent only without prior information might negatively affect their trust [28]. These findings support the inclusion of a verbal consent step in studies considering the use of deferred consent.

Mothers in the DR-CPAP trial felt that the information portrayed during the verbal consent was appropriate both in content and in duration during the time of labour. This is like other studies using two-part consent where healthcare workers felt that the verbal consent minimized any unnecessary burden to mothers already potentially in distress [17]. Some mothers in our study expressed that they were unable to understand a lot during the verbal consent process, a concern that healthcare workers in other studies have had [17]. However, despite this, they still had an awareness and limited understanding of the trial, that appreciated when they were later approached for full written consent.

An important finding from this study is that some mothers reported feeling obliged to provide verbal consent given the circumstances of enrolment. This raises important ethical concerns regarding voluntariness in high-acuity neonatal settings, where parents are often distressed and may perceive limited real opportunity to refuse participation. From a bioethics perspective, this highlights the tension between respect for autonomy and the realities of vulnerability and dependence inherent in emergency care contexts in low-resource settings. Even in the absence of overt coercion, such circumstances may create a sense of implicit obligation, potentially compromising the voluntariness of initial consent.

In this context, the second stage of written informed consent is therefore ethically important, as it provides a structured opportunity to reaffirm participation once the immediate emotional and clinical pressure has reduced. This deferred process helps to restore and strengthen autonomy by allowing parents to revisit their decision with greater clarity and less perceived urgency. Ensuring that participants are explicitly reminded that refusal or withdrawal will not affect care is essential to safeguarding genuine informed consent in such settings.

In the DR-CPAP trial, mothers were given 24 h after the birth of their infant to provide full written informed consent. While most considered this timeframe sufficient, mothers who had undergone a caesarean section expressed a need for additional time. Similarly, in a study involving parents of preterm infants, participants indicated that obtaining consent within 4 h of birth was too soon and suggested that deferred consent could be a suitable alternative [36].

In the DR-CPAP trial, the way staff presented the verbal consent process and interacted with parents played a key role in helping them feel at ease, supported in making their own decision, and reassured that their voices were respected and valued. Other studies have suggested that the attitude and nature of the healthcare workers were important to mothers during the verbal consent process, with staff who were warm, friendly, kind and reassuring being important considerations [27]. When utilising this approach, the information provided during the verbal consent process must be focused to utilise the limited time available [17]. To address this, a standardised verbal consent script was used in our study. However, other studies adopting this approach describe providing a summary of the study and an opportunity to ask questions, however no standard script has been used. Other studies show that this can lead to concerns that it is comprehensive and detailed enough [17]. Thus, preparing a verbal script, as we did in the DR-CPAP trial can help ensure that the salient points are communicated, and no key information is omitted.

A previous study of emergency paediatric interventions in Uganda identified the absence of the household decision maker - normally the father - as a challenge [21]. This contrasts with the DR-CPAP trial, where most mothers and fathers suggested that the mother had the ultimate decision over her infant’s enrolment. This difference may be due in part to the age of the child and the relationships that the child has.

One of the strengths of the DR-CPAP trial protocol, was the prior engagement of the preterm community advisory board in the design and development of the consent process. This involvement ensured that the approach used during a time-critical and emotionally intense period was acceptable to parents as well as religious and cultural leaders, which likely contributed to its continued acceptability among families enrolled in the trial. One of the concerns about our two-step consent was the validity of the verbal consent if the information is not adequate or is poorly understood [17, 27, 28]. In other studies that have used verbal consent, clinicians have expressed concern regarding the lack of a legal document to prove consent as their only record was their documentation and signature in the patient notes [17]. Acknowledging these concerns, we tried to address this through undertaking audio recording of the verbal consent and having the research assistant sign the verbal consent form. Although other studies using two-step consent process have had the clinician taking the verbal consent complete a form [17, 26], we did not come cross other studies where the verbal consent was audio recorded. In our study using a simple dictaphone to record the consent and verbal consent process helped mitigate the risk of not having enough evidence should it be required.

One of the strengths of the DR-CPAP trial protocol, was the prior engagement of the preterm community advisory board in the design and development of the consent process. This involvement ensured that the approach used during a time-critical and emotionally intense period was acceptable to parents as well as religious and cultural leaders, which likely contributed to its continued acceptability among families enrolled in the trial. Similarly in the Cord Pilot Trial, the successful implementation of a two-stage consent process was developed in consultation with, and endorsed by, parent representatives [17, 26]. These experiences highlight the importance of engaging parents and communities in the design of research studies - particularly regarding consent procedures - to foster mutual trust and ensure ethically sound practices [1]. One limitation of this study is that we did not interview healthcare workers or research staff to explore their perspectives on the consent process. Including their views could have offered additional insights into the contextual and procedural factors that contributed to the success of the two-step consent approach.

Conclusion

Balancing the complex ethical issues of consent with representative and timely recruitment in a delivery-room trial can be challenging. For clinical trial data to be representative of the population being studied, delivery room research must consider alternative consent pathways. The two-stage consent process is a practical and ethically supported approach in neonatal emergencies when prospective consent is not feasible. Like deferred consent, it offers considerable advantages over antenatal consent - faster recruitment rates, more representative samples, earlier completion times, and translation to clinical care sooner. This method had a positive reception from parents of infants in the DR-CPAP trial, where we showed that the provision of minimal key information about the trial at recruitment was adequate and more appropriate approach than full written consent at this time. Parents’ views should always be carefully considered when incorporating alternative consent processes into trials, and this is likely to be context specific depending on the social, religious, and cultural constructs. Early engagement with patient and public partners is key in the development and design of a context specific consent process and to ensure the consent resources are both appropriate and adequate. Digital recording of the verbal consent process together with a script to support the process helped address the challenges of documentation of verbal consent and consistent information that other trials using two-step consent have highlighted. Consent options should be tailored to the specific study and setting, which is best informed through consultation with community advisory boards.

Alternative consent processes such as the one we describe, should not routinely replace prospective informed consent but can support recruitment when the traditional pathway is not feasible. Further research is needed to identify procedures that are both ethically valid and acceptable to parents across diverse cultural and clinical contexts. In our trial, the two-step consent process proved to be an acceptable approach with its success likely attributed in part to the careful development and involvement of the community advisory board.
