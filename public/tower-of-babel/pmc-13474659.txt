RNA-Seq analysis reveals early defense activation and responses associated with resistance to Fusarium thapsinum stalk infection in sorghum
Authors: Kinde Ketema, Ramasamy Perumal, Christopher R Little, Sandeep Marla, Sanzhen Liu, Diriba Chere, Yemane Girma Belaineh, Terry Felderhoff, P V Vara Prasad, Tesfaye Tesso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFusarium stalk rot in sorghum is caused by Fusarium thapsinum and affects sorghum production globally. In this study, we investigated genome-wide transcriptional responses associated with resistance to sorghum stalk rot. Transcriptomes of two sorghum genotypes, a resistant (SC599) and a susceptible (BTx3042) genotype, were analyzed at 24 and 48 h post-inoculation (hpi) with F. thapsinum at flowering. A total of 24 RNA-seq libraries were generated. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) ranged from 360 to 6,178 (FDR < 0.05), with earlier and stronger transcriptional responses observed in the resistant genotype. Key defense-related genes, including nucleotide-binding site leucine-rich repeat (Sobic.005G226400), pathogenesis-related proteins (Sobic.001G401200), disease resistance-response genes (Sobic.009G021300), wall-associated kinase (Sobic.008G170800 and Sobic.002G249600), and flavonoid synthase (Sobic.004G050200) genes, were upregulated in SC599 at 24 hpi, whereas expression of these genes was lower or delayed in BTx3042. Gene ontology and KEGG analyses revealed enrichment of defense responses, flavonoid biosynthesis, and signal transduction at 24 hpi in the resistant genotype, whereas the susceptible genotype showed delayed activation of stress-related pathways. These results highlight the importance of early and coordinated activation of defense responses and provide insight into the temporal regulation of resistance mechanisms in sorghum against F. thapsinum and may provide a framework for future studies to understand the genes and pathways underlying the molecular mechanisms of Fusarium stalk rot resistance in sorghum.Keywords: RNA-Seq, Fusarium thapsinum, Sorghum, Transcriptome, Stalk rot

Introduction

Plant innate immunity is a complex and multifaceted system that enables plants to perceive and respond to pathogen invasion. It involves two layers of defense. The first layer, pattern-triggered immunity (PTI), involves the recognition of conserved microbial molecules, called microbe- or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs/PAMPs), by surface-localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) [1]. PTI can halt the ingress of most microbes, but some pathogens secrete effector proteins to suppress PTI [2]. Effector-triggered immunity (ETI) is the second defense against these adapted pathogens. ETI is activated through intracellular nucleotide-binding site leucine-rich repeat (NBS-LRR) receptors [3]. It involves the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), transcriptional reprogramming, hormone signaling, and localized cell death [3]. Localized cell death is often associated with resistance to biotrophic pathogens. However, necrotrophic pathogens can exploit host cell death to promote infection, a process often described as effector-triggered susceptibility (ETS) [4–6]. Therefore, the timing and regulation of these defense responses are important for determining disease outcome and differences between resistant and susceptible genotypes in sorghum–Fusarium thapsinum interactions, where early defense responses and their regulation may influence resistance or susceptibility [7, 8].

Growing climate adversity has increased the effects of biotic and abiotic stresses on field crops. Sorghum (Sorghum bicolor [L.] Moench) is a heat and drought-tolerant crop grown worldwide as a staple food, animal feed, and biofuel feedstock [9]. However, sorghum is vulnerable to several fungal stalk rot pathogens that cause lodging, particularly under drought conditions [10]. Fusarium thapsinum Klittich, Leslie, Nelson et Marasas, sp. nov. and Macrophomina phaseolina (Tassi) Goid are the dominant fungal pathogens causing Fusarium stalk and charcoal rot, respectively [11, 12]. Fusarium spp. are often described as hemibiotrophic pathogens that undergo an initial biotrophic phase and then switch to a necrotrophic phase as the infection progresses [13, 14]. They cause various crop diseases, including collar, stalk, and root rots; seedling blight; grain mold; ear and kernel rot; and Fusarium wilt [10]. F. thapsinum is the predominant and virulent pathogen causing Fusarium stalk rot [15]. It affects sorghum production by interfering with grain filling, causing lodging and thus resulting in reduced grain yield and quality, with losses reaching up to 100% when disease infection is severe [16]. In addition, it affects biomass yields and quality in sweet and forage sorghums [17, 18]. In Kansas, grain losses due to stalk rot may reach 50% during drought seasons, with a statewide annual average loss of about 4% [19, 20].

Despite significant sorghum breeding progress in the United States [21], stalk rot diseases yield losses persist due to a combination of factors, namely poor stalk strength and climate change-induced post-flowering drought stress [19, 22, 23]. The mechanisms of resistance and host-pathogen interactions in Fusarium stalk rot remain poorly understood. Labor-intensive artificial inoculation and disease-scoring methods, along with environmental stress-driven factors and the opportunistic nature of Fusarium pathogens, further complicated breeding for disease resistance and slowed progress. Despite these challenges, different genetic sources of resistance were identified [24]. To further enhance our understanding, next-generation sequencing (NGS) can identify the genetic architecture of host resistance and elucidate its molecular basis [25–27]. Transcriptome analysis, which compares transcript expression between samples, can provide insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms of F. thapsinum resistance in sorghum [28, 29].

RNA-seq has been used to study the molecular responses of sorghum to various abiotic stresses, including nitrogen, drought, and chilling [25, 26, 30–32], as well as biotic stresses [8, 33–37]. However, only a few such studies have investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying sorghum stalk rot diseases, including charcoal rot and Fusarium stalk rot [38–40]. These studies identified genes and pathways responding to the disease and provided insights into disease resistance mechanisms. For instance, Sharma et al. [38] used RNA-seq to examine the roles of defense genes, including chitinase and stilbene synthase, in sorghum infected with M. phaseolina. They found that chitinase and stilbene synthase expression was higher in the resistant than the susceptible genotype, suggesting that these genes play roles in disease response. Bandara et al. [39] conducted RNA-seq analysis of sorghum infected with M. phaseolina and found that the infection activated host cell wall-degrading enzyme-encoding genes. Funnell-Harris et al. [40] reported increased expression of monolignol biosynthesis genes in response to stalk rot infection by F. thapsinum and M. phaseolina in bmr lines compared to the wild-type sister line. These studies have contributed to understanding the molecular mechanisms of sorghum stalk rot disease resistance and have identified potential breeding targets. Despite previous studies identifying genes associated with stalk-rot pathogens, the temporal dynamics of transcriptional responses and the molecular basis of differences between resistant and susceptible genotypes during early infection remain poorly understood.

We hypothesized that Fusarium stalk rot resistance in sorghum is associated with earlier and coordinated activation of defense-related genes and pathways in resistant genotypes compared to susceptible genotypes. The objectives of this study were: (i) to identify differentially expressed genes between resistant (SC599) and susceptible (BTx3042) sorghum genotypes following inoculation; (ii) to characterize key biological pathways and processes associated with resistance and susceptibility; and (iii) to evaluate temporal differences in transcriptomic responses during early stages of infection.

Materials and methods

RNA-Seq experimental design and greenhouse experiment

Sorghum inbred lines SC599 and BTx3042 were used for transcriptome analysis. These lines were selected based on previous studies by Tesso et al. [15, 22, 41] and Bandara et al. [39], which reported that SC599 is resistant to Fusarium stalk rot, whereas BTx3042 is susceptible. They were grown in a greenhouse using a Sun Gro nursery potting mix (Sun Gro Bellevue, WA, USA), each genotype in 12 pots (19 L) from August - December 2021.

The experimental design included three biological replicates, two treatments (F. thapsinum inoculated and control), and two sampling times at 24 and 48 h post-inoculation (hpi), a total of 24 samples. These time points were selected based on previous reports to capture early transcriptional responses associated with the perception and activation of host defense upon pathogen invasion [38, 42].

The pure culture of F. thapsinum was obtained from the Row Crops Pathology Laboratory, Kansas State University (KSU), Manhattan, Kansas, USA. The isolate, ‘FT-TX1-LWN’, used to produce inoculum was originally collected from sorghum tissue in College Station, Texas, in 2010 and retrieved from long-term storage at 80 °C. Due to the challenges of morphological differentiation within the Fusarium fujikuroi species complex, the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF1-α) gene was sequenced to confirm isolate identity. The gene was amplified using EF-1 forward and EF-2 reverse primers (Table S1). PCR was performed in a Multigene OptiMax thermocycler (Labnet International, Inc., NJ, USA) following a protocol modified from O’Donnell et al. [43]. Briefly, the reaction consisted of 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30s, annealing at 62 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Sequence analysis using NCBI BLAST [44] and the FUSARIOID-ID databases [45] showed 100% identity to 25 database accessions and 99.85% identity to one accession, respectively (Table S1).

For inoculation, a suspension culture was prepared in potato dextrose broth and incubated for 3 days. The suspension was filtered to separate the mycelial mass from the liquid inoculum. The concentration was adjusted to 5 × 104 conidia ml− 1 by diluting with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. Six plants of each genotype were inoculated with F. thapsinum, and the remaining six served as mock-inoculated controls with PBS solution. Studies have shown that moisture stress during flowering and grain filling enhances the development of stalk rot symptoms and disease severity in sorghum [15, 46]. Therefore, mild water stress was induced at flowering before inoculation to mimic dry-field conditions, under which Fusarium stalk rot infection and severity increase, thereby facilitating disease development to capture genotypic differences in resistance. 15 days after flowering, both solutions were applied to the base of the stalk, ~ 10 cm above the soil surface.

RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and sequencing

Tissue samples (~ 1 cm2) were collected from the inoculated plants 3 cm above or below the inoculation site at 24 and 48 hpi. The samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C until RNA extraction. Approximately 100 mg of each ground sample was used for RNA extraction with the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA). The quantity and quality of the extracted RNA were assessed using a Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Genomics, Santa Clara, CA, USA). 24 cDNA libraries were constructed at the Integrated Genomic Facility at KSU following the Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA protocol. Poly-A mRNAs were enriched using oligo-dT beads. Each library was indexed (Table S2), pooled, and sequenced on the NextSeq500 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at the Genome Sequencing Facility at the University of Kansas Medical Center using a single-end 1 x 75 bp read configuration.

Quality control, read alignment, and quantification

Sequence data processing was performed on the BeocatHPC cluster at KSU.

Raw reads were evaluated for quality parameters using FastQC v.0.11.9 [47] and quality and adapter trimming were performed using Trimmomatic v.0.39 [48], with standard parameters for single-end reads (ILLUMINACLIP:2:30:10, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:60). Quality was re-evaluated with FastQC before alignment.

Reference genome indexing and read alignment were performed using STAR v 2.7.10a [49], utilizing the NCBI Sorghum bicolor v3.1.1 reference genome (BTx623 v3.1.1) and annotation file v3.53 [50, 51] with default alignment parameters except a few modifications for intron length (--alignIntronMax 50000) and mismatch thresholds (--outFilterMismatchNmax 6; --outFilterMismatchNoverLmax 0.06). The output BAM file was sorted by coordinate and indexed for downstream gene quantification.

FeatureCounts [52] quantified gene-level read counts using S. bicolor annotation file v3.53 [51] and grouped reads based on their gene_id attribute in the GTF file.

MultiQC [53] was run to generate the combined summary report, including percentage of mapped reads to indicate the overall quality and sequencing accuracy.

Read counts were converted to transcripts per million mapped reads (TPM) in R [54] to visualize the expression profile. Heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 V3.3.0 R package and the heatmap3() function with TPM values [55, 56].

Differential expression analysis

The DESeq2 R package [57] was used for differential gene expression (DEG) analysis using the Wald test for pairwise comparisons between control and pathogen (F. thapsinum)-inoculated samples of each genotype (within) and between genotypes at 24 and 48 hpi. Eight pairwise comparisons were made and assigned a short comparison code (Table 1).

DESeq2 uses raw count data and internally accounts for library size differences during normalization [57]. For downstream filtering, normalized counts were obtained with the normalized=TRUE argument. Genes with low expression were removed by retaining those with a mean normalized count ≥ 10 across all samples using the following code: dds[rowMeans(counts(dds, normalized=TRUE)) > = 10,]. Statistical significance was determined using the Wald test with a Benjamini–Hochberg multiple-testing correction, and genes with an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significant. The family and annotations of the genes were determined based on Phytozome v13 sorghum (BTx623) annotation [50, 58].

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate global patterns of variation among RNA-seq samples. Raw count data were normalized using the variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) implemented in the DESeq2 package [57] in R. PCA was performed on the transformed expression matrix using the prcomp() function in R, based on all expressed genes that passed the filtering criteria described above. The first two PCs were used to visualize sample relationships. PCA plots were generated using the ggplot2 package, with samples annotated by genotype, treatment, and time point.

Gene ontology, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were conducted to functionally classify DEGs and identify enriched biological processes, molecular functions, cellular components, and metabolic pathways [59, 60] using the web-based ShinyGO v0.61 tool [61]. Significant DEGs with an absolute log2 fold change of at least 2 (|log2FC| ≥ 2), corresponding to a fold change of at least 4, were filtered and used as input for enrichment analysis. Overrepresentation analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test, and p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction; both were considered significantly enriched when FDR < 0.05. GO annotations were obtained using AmiGO 2 [62], and KEGG pathway data were retrieved from the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp) [60, 63]. KEGG pathway visualization was performed using Pathview [64]. The number of genes associated with each GO term (nGenes) and the total number of genes annotated to each term (Pathway Genes) were used to calculate enrichment statistics.

Results

Sequence data processing, quality control and aligned read quantification

Quality assessment of RNA-Seq data generated from 24 libraries, representing F. thapsinum susceptible (BTx3042) and resistant (SC599) genotypes at two time points (24 and 48 hpi), indicated that the libraries met standard quality thresholds, with no sequence flagged as poor quality, and no significant adapter contamination (Tables S3a; Figure S2). On average, 97.2% of reads were retained after trimming, resulting in 1.38 billion high-quality reads across all samples (Table S3b). The read length ranged from 60 to 75 bp, with an average of ~ 74 bp. Over 88% of the reads were aligned to the sorghum reference genome, and ~ 64.8% of the reads were assigned to annotated genomic features, while the remaining reads were unassigned due to ambiguity or lack of a feature (Table S3c).

Clustering pattern of DEG in response to F. thapsinum inoculation

After filtering genes with low expression (mean normalized counts ≥ 10 across all samples), a total of 22,220 gene counts were retained for downstream DEG analysis and used across all pairwise comparisons (Table 1).

In the PCA analysis, the first two principal components explained 62% (PC1) and 17% (PC2) of the total variance. Samples were separated by genotype along PC1, while treatment effects were evident within each genotype along PC2, and this clustering pattern was consistent across both time points (24 and 48 hpi), suggesting genotypes and treatments were the main sources of transcriptional variation in the dataset (Fig. 1).

In BTx3042, 360 DEGs were significant, of which 73 were upregulated and 287 were downregulated at 24 hpi (Table 1; Table S4a). Among the 73 significantly upregulated genes in BTx3042 at 24 hpi, log-fold change (LFC) ranged from 0.5 to 4.3 (Table 1; Table S4a). Only a subset of these genes met the stricter |log2FC| ≥ 2 threshold used for downstream Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis (Table 1). Several defense-related genes, including pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins, cytochrome P450s, leucine-rich repeat (LRR) proteins, and chalcone/stilbene synthases, were strongly downregulated in BTx3042 at 24 hpi (Table S4a). In contrast, more significant DEGs were detected in BTx3042 at 48 hpi (BC48 -vs- BP48) than at 24 hpi (BC24 -vs- BP24), with 2,593 upregulated and 2,413 downregulated genes out of 5,006 significant DEGs (Table 1; Table S4b).

In SC599, 3,106 DEGs were observed (SC24 -vs- SP24), compared to 360 DEGs in BTx3042 at 24 hpi and (Table 1; Fig. 2A and D; Tables S4a-4c). Of these, the majority of DEGs in SC599 at 24 hpi were upregulated, including genes annotated as PR proteins, LRRs, NB-ARCs, dirigent-like proteins (DIRs), chalcone/stilbene synthases, cell wall-associated kinases (WAKs), and various peroxidases (Table S4c). At 48 hpi, SC599 (SC48 -vs- SP48) had a higher proportion of downregulated than upregulated DEGs (Table 1; Table S4d).

Both up- and downregulated DEGs were co-expressed in response to inoculation across the lines (Fig. 2; Tables S4e and S4f). Samples collected from both lines at 24 hpi (BC24 -vs- BP24 and SC24 -vs- SP24) shared nine upregulated DEGs (Fig. 2A; Table S4e). The remaining 3,056 DEGs in SC599 and 64 in BTx3042 were uniquely upregulated (Fig. 2A). In contrast, 2,273 DEGs in BTx3042 and only 292 in SC599 were exclusively expressed at 48 hpi (BC48 -vs- BP48 and SC48 -vs- SP48) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, 51 downregulated DEGs were shared at 24 hpi, whereas 397 were shared at 48 hpi in both genotypes (Fig. 2C and D; Table S4f).

When the inoculated samples from both genotypes were compared at 24 hpi (BP24 -vs- SP24) and 48 hpi (BP48 -vs- SP48), a higher number of genes showed lower expression in the resistant genotype at 48 hpi (Fig. 2E and H). Genes with negative LFCs in the resistant line corresponded to higher expression levels in the susceptible genotype.

At 24 hpi (BP24 -vs- SP24), a greater number of R-gene families, including NB-ARC, CC-NBS-LRR, and LRR, as well as cytochrome P450 family genes, were upregulated, whereas fewer were downregulated (Tables S4e and S4f). In contrast, at 48 hpi (BP48 -vs- SP48), these gene families showed higher expression in BTx3042.

The proportion of shared upregulated DEGs between control and pathogen-treated samples was 1.9% at 24 hpi and 31.0% at 48 hpi (Fig. 2E and F). Among the downregulated DEGs, 15.7% at 24 hpi and 28.9% at 48 hpi were shared between treatments (Fig. 2G and H). Differences in DEGs were also observed between the control treatments of the genotypes at each time point.

Defense, perception, and signaling in the upstream cell wall and membrane-bound DEGs

At the gene family level, the DIR gene family (also known as the disease resistance-responsive (DRR) gene) expression was generally lower in F. thapsinum inoculated samples than mock-inoculated controls in both genotypes (Fig. 7A; Tables S4b and S4c). However, one DIR gene, Sobic.009G021300, was significantly upregulated in SC599 at 24 hpi (log2FC = 20.8) and 48 hpi (log2FC = 7.2) (Fig. 7B; Tables S4c and S4d). Additionally, two WAK genes (Sobic.008G170800 and Sobic.002G249600), functionally associated with DIR, were also upregulated in SC599 at 24 hpi (Fig. 7B; Table S4c).

Several peroxidase (SbPrx) genes were differentially expressed in both genotypes and time points. Multiple genes located in the chromosome 2 peroxidase cluster showed differential expression (Fig. 7B). Sobic.002G391900 (SbPrx4.1; LFC>|-20|) showed strong downregulation in BTx3042 at 24 hpi and was also downregulated in SC599 at 48 hpi (Fig. 7B; Tables S4a, S4c, and S4d). Sobic.002G416600 (SbPrx46) was downregulated in BTx3042 at 24 hpi, whereas Sobic.002G010700 (SbPrx30) was upregulated in SC599 at 24 hpi and in BTx3042 at 48 hpi (Fig. 7B; Tables S4a to S4c).

Calcium-binding proteins also showed differential expression between genotypes (Fig. 7A). In BTx3042 at 24 hpi, calcium-related genes identified in Table S4a were consistently downregulated, including Sobic.009G083600, Sobic.002G199500, and Sobic.001G383700. In SC599, calcium-related genes showed increased expression at 24 hpi, including Sobic.002G411900, Sobic.009G242700, and Sobic.001G383700 (Fig. 7B ; Table S4c). At 48 hpi in SC599, calcium-related gene expression was variable, with both upregulated and downregulated genes observed (Table S4d).

Increased early expression of nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat proteins

In BTx3042 at 24 hpi, 7 genes annotated as NB-ARC/NBS-LRR were differentially expressed, all of which were downregulated (Table S4a). At 48 hpi, 19 such genes were differentially expressed in BTx3042, including 12 upregulated and 7 downregulated genes (Table S4b). The downregulated NBS-LRR genes in BTx3042 at 24 hpi included Sobic.005G047800, Sobic.005G128000, Sobic.005G150700, Sobic.008G184900, and Sobic.008G174100 and those upregulated at 48 hpi included Sobic.008G117600 and Sobic.009G204300 (Fig. 8A and B; Table S4b).

Differential regulation and functional analysis of PR proteins in the downstream defense cascade

Four PR-10 genes were downregulated at 24 hpi in BTx3042, including Sobic.001G400700, Sobic.001G400800, Sobic.001G400900, and Sobic.001G401300 (Fig. 8B; Table S4a). In SC599, PR-related expression was mixed rather than uniformly downregulated. Sobic.001G400900 was downregulated at 24 hpi, whereas Sobic.001G401200 was upregulated at both 24 and 48 hpi, and Sobic.001G400700 was downregulated at 48 hpi (Tables S4c and S4d).

PR-5 genes were also differentially expressed. Sobic.003G097200 and Sobic.003G097000 were upregulated in SC599 at 24 hpi and in BTx3042 at 48 hpi (Fig. 8B; Tables S4b and S4c). Sobic.009G019100 was downregulated in BTx3042 at 24 hpi (Table S4a).

Several CHS and STS genes were differentially expressed in response to inoculation. In BTx3042 at 24 hpi, 4 CHS/STS genes on chromosome 5 – Sobic.005G136200, Sobic.005G136300, Sobic.005G137000, and Sobic.005G137200 – were downregulated (Table S4a). In SC599, Sobic.005G137000 was upregulated at 24 hpi, whereas it was downregulated in BTx3042 (Fig. 8B; Tables S4a and S4c). In the comparison between inoculated genotypes at 24 hpi, Sobic.005G136200 also showed higher expression in SC599 (Table S4e).

Sobic.004G050200 was downregulated at 24 hpi and upregulated at 48 hpi in BTx3042, whereas it was upregulated at both 24 and 48 hpi in SC599 (Fig. 8B; Tables S4a to S4d).

Discussion

RNA-seq has been widely used to investigate plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [32–35]. In sorghum, several transcriptomic studies have identified genes and pathways associated with stalk rot resistance [38, 39]. In this study, we examined how transcriptional responses differ over time between a resistant genotype (SC599) and a susceptible genotype (BTx3042) [15] following inoculation with F. thapsinum to understand how the timing and coordination of these responses relate to resistance.

SC599 responded with earlier and more coordinated transcriptional activation of defense genes, whereas BTx3042 showed a delayed response. However, some DEGs were shared between resistant and susceptible genotypes, with limited overlap at 24 hpi and were shared between resistance and susceptible genotypes.

These results indicate that differences in pathway activation timing, rather than specific pathways themselves, were likely associated with resistance. Similar patterns have been reported in sorghum and other systems, where earlier activation of defense responses has been linked to improved resistance [8, 38].

Temporal dynamics of host response to F. thapsinum

This study focused on early host responses at 24 and 48 hpi following inoculation. At 24 hpi, SC599 activated genes associated with signaling, cell wall processes, and defense. In contrast, BTx3042 displayed a weaker response at this stage, with relatively few genes upregulated. However, BTx3042 exhibited a much larger transcriptional response by 48 hpi. This suggests that the susceptible genotype can mount a defense response. Such a delay reduces the effectiveness of the defense at a stage when restricting pathogen establishment is critical [35, 36].

Integration of perception, signaling, and defense responses

At 24 hpi, gene expression in the resistant genotype involved perception, signaling, and downstream defense responses.

In terms of perception, strong induction of the dirigent-like (DIR) gene Sobic.009G021300, together with upregulation of wall-associated kinase (WAK) genes (Sobic.008G170800 and Sobic.002G249600), reflects that SC599 responded rapidly to cell wall-derived signals. DIRs are involved in lignin and lignan biosynthesis and have been linked to cell wall structure reinforcement [65, 66], while WAKs function as receptors that detect damage or modifications to the cell wall [67–69]. Activation of these genes in SC599 suggests that its defense system can detect F. thapsinum early.

Enrichment for calcium ion binding and protein kinase activity among upregulated DEGs in SC599 at 24 hpi indicated that intracellular signaling pathways were also activated early. Calcium fluxes and kinase cascades are components of plant immune signaling, and connect perception to transcriptional reprogramming [2, 70]. Because genes associated with these processes were enriched earlier in SC599 than in BTx3042 support the idea that signal transduction was more rapidly initiated in the resistant genotype.

Further downstream, several NBS-LRR genes, including Sobic.002G104400, Sobic.002G050900, and Sobic.005G226400, were upregulated in SC599 at 24 hpi. These resistance gene families are involved in pathogen recognition and the activation of defense responses [71–75]. Likewise, specific PR genes showed increased expression in SC599.

ROS-related processes and oxidative regulation

Reactive oxygen species (ROS)-related pathway genes showed clear differences between the two genotypes. In SC599 at 24 hpi, DEG enrichment for processes such as hydrogen peroxide catabolism, oxidoreductase activity, and peroxidase activity indicates active regulation of oxidative responses. ROS functions to play dual roles in plant defense as signaling molecules and as contributors to antimicrobial activity [76–78].

The presence of ROS-related pathways, both for production and scavenging, is important to maintain the balance of ROS accumulation. Both processes were activated in SC599, which indicated controlled oxidative homeostasis. ROS contributes to signaling and defense but can induce host senescence and cell death if allowed to accumulate unchecked. The coordinated regulation observed in SC599 aligns with a system that uses ROS effectively without causing excessive stress.

Secondary metabolism, flavonoid pathways and physiological support of defense

Genes involved in flavonoid biosynthesis also showed different expression timing between the two genotypes. In SC599, genes like Sobic.005G137000 (SbCHS1; chalcone synthase) and Sobic.004G050200 (SbDFR3; dihydroflavonol 4-reductase) were triggered earlier and, in some cases, stayed high over time. In BTx3042, similar genes were either downregulated early or upregulated later.

Flavonoids play roles in plant defense, including antimicrobial activities, antioxidant functions, and involvement in signaling [79–81]. Early activation of flavonoid pathways in SC599 indicated that secondary metabolite production could be part of the initial defense response rather than a result of F. thapsinum infection. Previous research in sorghum has shown that flavonoid accumulation helps confer resistance against fungal pathogens [8, 38, 82].

SC599 showed early enrichment of processes related to carbohydrate metabolism, transport, and water movement. These functions maintain cellular homeostasis and provide the energy to sustain defense responses [83]. In contrast, similar pathways were enriched later in BTx304. Stalk rot pathogens disrupt vascular tissues, which affects transport and overall plant physiology [15, 84].

Sorghum Fusarium stalk rot: Quantitative disease resistance and a study-based defense framework

Quantitative disease resistance (QDR) is a durable mechanism that reduces disease severity caused by various pathogens through the combined action of multiple genes or loci with moderate effects, where resistance traits are continuously distributed, ranging from susceptibility to enhanced resistance [85–87]. QDR has proven effective against necrotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens, offering broad-spectrum resistance [69, 88, 89]. The broader components of QDR include upstream perception, signaling, and defense, as well as downstream signaling and defense response [90].

The overall pattern of gene expression observed in this study supports a quantitative resistance model. Therefore, we proposed a hypothetical resistance framework to Fusarium stalk rot that involves elevated expression of QDR-related genes in resistant lines, regulating molecular responses to infection (Fig. 9). Rather than relying on a single major gene, resistance in SC599 was associated with coordinated regulation of multiple gene families, including DIR, WAK, NBS-LRR, PR, and flavonoid biosynthesis genes. This interpretation is consistent with previous reports showing that stalk rot resistance in sorghum is polygenic [24].

Hypothetical model of sorghum plant defense mechanisms against infection by F. thapsinum. The model is composed of perception, signaling, and defense response layers [90]. During upstream perception, the plant recognizes pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) through pattern recognition receptor (PRRs) proteins such as wall-associated kinases (WAKs) and receptor-like kinases (RLKs) in the cell wall and plasma membrane. Signaling is performed by mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades or via the calcium signaling pathway. Finally, downstream responses are directed by transcription factors and protein kinases, which activate specific defense responses including genes for pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins, ROS production, oxidative stress protection, and secondary metabolites such as flavonoids (e.g., CHS, DFR3)

The model presented in Fig. 9 summarizes these observations within the hierarchical framework of perception, signaling, and defense responses. In SC599, early responses at 24 hpi include activation of perception-related genes (DIR and WAK), signaling components (calcium-binding proteins and kinases), and downstream defense genes (NBS-LRR, PR, and flavonoid biosynthesis genes). In BTx3042, similar processes are present but are delayed and become more prominent at 48 hpi.

By incorporating specific genes identified in this study such as Sobic.009G021300 (DIR), Sobic.008G170800 (WAK), Sobic.005G226400 (NBS-LRR), and Sobic.004G050200 (SbDFR3) the model reflects features of the sorghum-F. thapsinum pathosystem.

Limitations and validation

The RNA-seq data were generated with biological replicates and analyzed with stringent statistical thresholds (FDR < 0.05), supporting the overall reliability of the observed expression patterns. However, independent validation of selected DEGs using qRT-PCR was not performed and should be considered a limitation. Future work should include targeted validation of key candidate genes, such as DIR, WAK, NBS-LRR, and CHS/DFR family members, as well as functional studies to clarify their roles in resistance to F. thapsinum.

The results reported here were based on early host responses at 24 and 48 hpi following inoculation. We did not directly monitor fungal development, so the interpretation is limited to host transcriptional changes rather than to specific stages of pathogen growth. Future studies integrating transcriptomics with analyses of infection progression would provide a more comprehensive understanding of host–pathogen interactions.

Conclusions

Fusarium stalk rot caused by F. thapsinum is a major constraint to sorghum production under favorable environmental conditions. In this study, transcriptomic analysis revealed clear differences in the timing and magnitude of gene expression between the resistant (SC599) and susceptible (BTx3042) genotypes at 24 and 48 hpi.

Resistance in SC599 was associated with earlier and more coordinated activation of genes related to pathogen perception, signaling, and defense responses, whereas BTx3042 exhibited a delayed transcriptional response. Several gene families, including DIR, WAK, NBS-LRR, PR, and flavonoid biosynthesis-related genes, were differentially expressed, supporting a coordinated and multi-layered defense response in the resistant genotype.

These findings support a model of quantitative resistance involving the combined action of multiple genes and pathways rather than being controlled by a single major gene. Notably, the results further emphasize that the timing of defense activation is an important feature associated with resistance.

This study provides a set of candidate genes and pathways associated with early defense responses to F. thapsinum infection. To advance the field, it is imperative that future studies rigorously validate these genes and define their functional roles in resistance through targeted approaches.
