Single-Cell and Spatial Transcriptomics Across Populations With Perianal Fistulizing Crohn’s Disease Shows Proinflammatory CD4+ Macrophage Involvement in Disease Progression
Authors: Sushma C. Maddipatla, Sachith Munasinghe, Anne Dodd, Christopher Tastad, Rachel Moss, Hong Yin, Shanta Murthy, Abhishek Dash, Vasantha L. Kolachala, David J. Cutler, Judy Cho, Subra Kugathasan, Jason D. Matthews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsPerianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease is a severe morbidity with rectal involvement and a higher prevalence in African Americans compared with European Americans. In this study, we sought to define the distinguishing cellular and molecular features between inflamed rectal Crohn’s disease with and without perianal fistula across populations that give new insight into disease severity or progression.MethodsRectal mucosal biopsies were obtained for single-cell sequencing from 50 individuals (262k cells); 18 with Crohn’s disease of the rectum with fistula, 25 with Crohn’s disease of the rectum without fistula and any other perianal complications, and 7 from individuals with no past or present mucosal disease (controls). Spatial transcriptomics were conducted and analyzed by Sopa and Squidpy, whereas immunofluorescence was processed with QuPath. Patient-derived rectal organoids were used to test inflammatory signaling.ResultsA major distinction between inflamed Crohn’s disease of the rectum with and without fistula was the decreased phagocytic signature, antigen processing profile, and unique morphologic structures of proinflammatory CD4+CD68+ macrophages. In fistulizing Crohn’s disease, these macrophages formed large aggregates in the lamina propria near epithelial crypts and appeared to disrupt cellular networks by engaging in signaling with other cell subtypes. At the population level, the most significant differences in cellular pathways were detected between African Americans and European Americans with inflamed Crohn’s disease of the rectum and fistula, and between patients with and without fistulizing disease among African Americans.ConclusionsDuring inflamed Crohn’s disease of the rectum with fistula, CD4+ macrophages alter cellular networks in the lamina propria while forming large cell aggregates, likely mediating complex interactions with T cells and possibly microbial antigens that are involved in the more severe pathologic conditions associated with fistula tract formation and progression.

Summary

This study shows that hyperactive immune responses in T cells and changes in epithelial cell function are associated with macrophage-mediated antigen processing in perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease.

What You Need to Know

Background

Perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease is anatomically characterized by having rectal involvement. Our results suggest that T cells that are associated with changes in crypt epithelial cell-specific MHC class II activity and CD4+ macrophage behavior in the lamina propria.

Impact

These findings implicate T-cell signaling and macrophage antigen processing as potential clinical targets to be explored in future studies. Mitigating these changes in cellular function could protect the epithelial cells and restore their normal function, a critical step in mucosal healing.

Future Directions

These findings should motivate deeper investigations into factors stimulating interferon pathways in the rectal mucosa and the different aspects of epithelial cell–macrophage Dcrosstalk during Crohn’s disease.

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the gastrointestinal tract, commonly affecting the terminal ileum and proximal colon.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 CD affecting the distal colon, particularly the rectum, is associated with severe and aggressive complications such as perianal fistulizing CD. The presence and anatomic location of the deep fistula tracts with external opening into the perineal area distinguish this extreme phenotype,5,6 setting it apart from the stricturing luminal disease that is often found in severely inflamed (IF) small intestinal CD.2

Perianal fistulizing CD is highly prevalent in African American (AA) populations, with worse outcomes compared with those with European American (EA) ancestry.8 Although CD has many known genetic associations,9,10 there have not been any associations detected for the perianal fistulizing CD phenotype, suggesting that cellular and/or environmental factors are likely to play a more prominent role in promoting perianal complications.11 Our previous studies support epithelial cell dysfunction playing a role in perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease, including epithelial cell pathways associated with metabolism, proliferation, and goblet cell function.12,13 These are consistent with previous reports that have pointed to changes in rectal epithelial cells that are involved with forming the fistula tract through mechanisms like epithelial-to-mesenchymal transitioning.14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 Recently, CD14+ myeloid cells and their crosstalk with inflammatory fibroblasts were also functionally implicated in perianal fistulizing CD, confirming immune–stromal involvement in the pathogenesis of this phenotype.21 Thus, further investigations into the cellular mechanisms driving this phenotype in the rectum are warranted.

To that end, in this study, we compared CD of the rectum with and without perianal fistula along with non-inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) controls across ancestral populations at the single-cell level to determine the distinguishing molecular and cellular features among the phenotypes while gaining insight into the interplay of the epithelial, immune, and stromal compartments and their potential impact on the progression of CD of the rectum towards fistula development. We identified transcriptional and morphologic changes pointing to differences in CD4+/CD68+ macrophages, changes in the activity of the stromal compartments, and disruption of crosstalk between epithelial cells, along with clear distinctions in the immune pathways and cytokine profiles between CD of the rectum with and without fistulizing disease. Some of these differences reside at the population level, whereas others showed differences in the cellular networks connecting mucosal neighborhoods. Our results indicate that specialized proinflammatory macrophages play a role in the pathology of fistula development in CD of the rectum that is involved in a type of antigen processing not observed in CD of the rectum without fistula.

Integrative Single-Cell Portal for Crohn’s Disease of the Rectum and Perianal Complications

Subjects with CD of the rectum with and without perianal fistulizing disease were recruited for the study, and their rectal mucosal biopsies were analyzed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) (Figure 1A–C). Roughly one-half of the patient cohort were of EA ancestry, with the remaining having primarily (∼45%) AA and ∼7% Asian ancestry (Figure 1B). About 40% of the cohort had an inflammatory phenotype (10 CD of the rectum without fistula and 7 CD of the rectum with fistula) with visibly active disease at the time of endoscopic biopsy (Figure 1B and Figure 2). Mucosal Pathways, Cellular Composition, and Gene Expression in Inflamed Crohn’s Disease of the Rectum With and Without Fistula

Subsequent single-cell analysis across the CD of the rectum phenotypes showed cellular proportions to be largely similar, except for IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula showing increased proportions of B and T cells, and an immature epithelial subtype enriched in IF CD of the rectum without fistula (Figure 4A; Figure 3). These enrichments in adaptive cell proportions were also reflected in the inflammatory gene scoring profiles of the IF disease samples that showed higher scores (Figure 4B; Figure 5). The presence of mucosal inflammation was also the factor explaining the highest degree of variance among the cohort (Figure 4C and Figure 5). Given that the origins of perianal fistulizing CD likely reside within the microenvironments caused by an IF rectum, we were most interested in the cellular differences between the IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula. Among those individuals, an epithelial precursor subtype with an immature epithelial cell–like transcriptome had an unusually large difference in the number of differentially expressed genes (Figure 4F), likely due to the expression of multiple transitionary genes taking place within the TA compartment of the crypts. Nonetheless large changes in gene expression levels for FCGBP+ goblet, microfold epithelial, stromal, cycling B, plasma, and mast cells, along with macrophages, were observed (Figure 4F). The most remarkable differences in the pathway analysis between IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula were the upregulation of inflammatory pathways in epithelial subtypes in fistulizing disease, including those related to the vasculature wall interactions, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), T-cell receptor (TCR), adaptive immune system, interferon (IFN)-γ/cytokine signaling, interleukin (IL)13, and MHC class II (MHCII) antigen presentation (Figure 4G). Additionally, cell–cell communication pathways involving MHC class I, metabolism of lipids, noncanonical WNT signaling, and bone morphogenic protein were downregulated with elevated complement and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) pathways in IF CD of the rectum with fistula. (Figure 5).

Considering these findings, we independently tested the effect of each mediator, IFN-γ, TNF-α, or IL13, directly on rectal organoids (Figure 4H and I). We found that IFN-γ–treated organoids had larger, darker spheroids, than did untreated or IL13-treated organoids, and TNF-α–exposed organoids were dark and more necrosed (Figure 4H). In terms of signaling, we found that IFN-γ stimulated the signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT)1 pathway, and IL13 stimulated the STAT6 pathway, with no detectable response in these pathways to TNF-α (Figure 1). However, rectal organoids significantly increased their expression of IL8 in response to TNF-α under these same experimental conditions (Figure 4J). Because STAT1 is known mediator of MHCII expression, we confirmed this effect by Western blotting on additional rectal organoids derived by endoscopic biopsy from other individuals. Figure 4K shows the expression of MHCII and CD74 after IFN-γ exposure. T cells were the predominant source of IFN-γ in IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula, with higher expression in fistulizing disease. Interestingly, some IFN-γ expression was uniquely originating from epithelial M and stem cells in IF CD of the rectum with fistula, or TA and immature epithelial cells in IF CD of the rectum without fistula (Figure 6).Figure 6Cellular sources of IFNG in rectal mucosal tissue. Cellular Pathways Associated With Inflamed Crohn’s Disease of the Rectum With and Without Fistula Across Ancestral Populations

Next, we examined the differences across the ancestry groups in our cohort with active disease marked by inflammation observed at the time of endoscopy (Figure 7; Figure 8). Pathway analysis generated from scRNA-seq data of these samples showed that the most striking differences were observed in the AA individuals with IF CD of the rectum with fistula (Figure 7A and B). First, to detect ancestry-dependent variabilities, we investigated the differences between AA patients with IF CD of the rectum with fistula and EA patients with IF CD of the rectum with fistula. We observed large differences between these 2 groups involving inflammatory pathways in the epithelial compartments. Notably, pathways involving cytokine signaling, IFN signaling, and MHCII antigen presentation were significantly enriched in AA patients with IF CD of the rectum with fistula. These differences were noted in both immature and mature cells, suggesting a remodeling along the crypt axis with more prominent inflammatory signaling in the epithelial compartment of AA patients. Along with this, antimicrobial peptide–associated pathways were enriched in AA patients with IF CD of the rectum with fistula compared with EA patients. Next, to investigate the AA cohort, we examined the differences between IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula (Figure 7B). Like our findings above, patients with fistula had signaling pathways that were distinct from their nonfistulizing counterparts. Other than the inflammatory pathways described above, unique pathways such as TCR and PD-1 signaling were enriched in the epithelium of AA patients with IF CD of the rectum with fistula. Another notable difference was the upregulated immune pathways involving IL27, IL36, and IL6 signaling in the stromal compartment of AA patients with CD with fistula along with other inflammatory pathways (Figure 7B).

When comparing AA patients with EA patients, vascular wall interactions in the endothelial cell compartment were upregulated in the AA group with CD of the rectum without fistula when compared with EA patients with a similar phenotype (Figure 8A). Comparing CD of the rectum with and without fistula within the EA group showed increased MHCII antigen presentation from mature colonocytes and lower-crypt TA epithelial cells in IF CD of the rectum with fistula (Figure 8B).

A detailed examination of the cellular networks showed that individuals with CD of the rectum and fistula, regardless of their inflammation, had no appreciable inferred signals for MHC class I antigen presentation pathways across all their mucosal cell types (Figure 9C and Figure 10). In addition, there was obvious loss of signals for WNT, bone morphogenic protein, TRAIL, PVR, and PROS for IF CD of the rectum with fistula, whereas activated leukocyte cell adhesion molecule signatures were unique to IF CD of the rectum without fistula. This was supported by diminished interactions between stromal and epithelial cells during fistulizing disease (Figure 10). There were also detectable differences in WNT and CCL2 expression levels among the stromal cells from different phenotypic groups (Figure 11). Only control cells indicated expression of WNT2B and WNT5B as compared with IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula. Unique to nonfistulizing CD in the stromal compartment was the increased expression of CXCL1 and WNT5A (Figure 11). The signaling between epithelial cells was also diminished during mucosal inflammation in fistulizing disease, comparatively, as many of the epithelial–epithelial signals are intact in IF CD without fistula (Figure 10), implicating NOTCH in these processes. This is supported by a significant increase in NOTCH signaling during IF CD without fistula (Figure 5C). To further characterize the biological features of these CD4+ macrophages, we next examined their transcriptional profiles across disease phenotypes and inflammatory states. The IF status of the patients from whom biopsies were obtained accounted for the greatest degree of transcriptional variance among CD4+ macrophages (Figure 12C and Figure 13). The largest changes in transcriptional differences between macrophages with and without CD4 expression were in noninflamed (NI) CD of the rectum with fistula, where CD4+macrophages showed many upregulated genes, and IF CD of the rectum with fistulizing phenotype showed the fewest (Figure 12D). Pathway analysis revealed that CD4+macrophages in IF CD of the rectum with fistula showed increases in hyaluronan uptake and metabolism and lipid metabolism pathways as compared with macrophages that were not expressing CD4 (Figure 12E). This contrasted with CD4+ macrophages in IF CD of the rectum without fistula compared with their counterparts that were not expressing CD4, reflecting changes associated with MHCII, zeta-chain–associated protein kinase 70, PD-1, and CD28 pathways (Figure 12F and G).

To validate these transcriptional inferences, we stained mucosal biopsy sections from IF regions of CD of the rectum with fistula using antibodies for CD4 and CD68 and compared the results with similarly active CD of the rectum without fistula. This revealed clear morphologic distinctions in both the degree of colocalization of the CD4 and CD68 antibody signals and the larger aggregates uniquely in the lamina propria of the CD of the rectum with fistula (Figure 12H and I). This further establishes differences in the biology of these cells between the fistulizing and nonfistulizing phenotypes, as indicated by the single-cell data. Comparing transcriptomes of CD4+ macrophages between IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula showed changes in gene expression related to macrophage polarization and proinflammatory signaling.22 Interestingly, the CD4+ macrophage phenotype is further substantiated by their SIRPA expression (Figure 12J) that we recently found to be proinflammatory and associated with anti-TNF–refractory disease.23

We also sequenced cells harvested from perianal fistula washes from 3 samples and detected transcriptional evidence for the presence of several immune cell types, including CD4+ macrophages (Figure 12K and Figure 13D and E). Furthermore, intact tissue that was recovered in the fistula wash stained positively for CD4 and CD68, with some cells coexpressing both markers (Figure 12L, left), giving further evidence for the involvement of these cells in fistulizing disease. Interestingly, the same piece of fistula-associated mucosal tissue also stained positively for an unusually large number of cells expressing α-smooth muscle actin and vimentin (Figure 12L, right), suggesting a solid, fibrotic-like composition to the tissue associated within the fistula tract.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to define molecular and cellular features that distinguish CD of the rectum with and without fistula, while accounting for ancestry as a biological variable. We increased the power of our previous study12 by recruiting additional patients (from 13 to 50) to enable a more in-depth analysis of rectal cells from EA and AA patients with scRNA-seq (+250K cells). There are only few comparative human population studies like this, making our results an important advancement in the mapping of cellular networks specific to individuals and populations associated with CD and the impact ancestry has on chronic inflammatory diseases.

A major finding of this study is the involvement of specialized proinflammatory CD4+ macrophages27 in fistulizing CD of the rectum. These cells were associated with increased T-cell activity in the rectal mucosa and appeared to affect epithelial cell function. The emergence, activation, and retention of this distinct CD4+ macrophage population may be driven by the loss of epithelial and stromal signaling cues observed in fistulizing CD, together with alterations in CCL2, that can further alter the chronic, dysregulated state of the macrophage compartment. Our findings also raise an important question on what causes the overwhelming CD4+ macrophage-specific MHCII antigen processing response in the lamina propria in IF CD of the rectum with fistula. Mechanistically, it could be the antigen presentation from epithelial cells that elicits the increased responses from CD4+ macrophages, or alternatively, the increased proportions of CD4+CD68+ macrophages in fistulizing CD of the rectum might be related to other types of cellular activity or homing mechanisms. Interestingly, these CD4+ macrophages express SIRPA, a macrophage subpopulation marker that was found to be enriched in the intestinal mucosa in anti-TNF–refractory CD.23 Thus, their presence in CD of the rectum with fistula may indicate a severe disease course that is characteristic of this phenotype.

Further analysis of these CD4+ macrophages in single-cell transcriptomics data revealed a M2 type polarization in IF CD of the rectum with fistula (Figure 14H). Consistent with this, our spatial transcriptomic analysis in IF CD of the rectum with fistula revealed that T cells colocalize with a CD4+ macrophage population, specifically resting memory CD4+ T cells and IL10-producing regulatory T cells (Tregs) (Figure 15H and I). IL10 derived from Tregs is an established signal promoting macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype,28 suggesting a potential mechanistic explanation for macrophage differentiation observed in our data. M2-polarized macrophages have been shown to enhance fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix synthesis,29 which might explain the increased abundance of fibroblasts observed in the tissue derived from the fistula tract (Figure 12L). Emerging evidence further supports the role of M2 macrophages in the progression of the fistula tract by their increased communication with fibroblasts.21 In addition, the MMP9 and CD5L in the M2 macrophages have previously been associated with tissue matrix remodeling and chronic inflammatory responses. Although M2 macrophages are traditionally considered anti-inflammatory, other lines of evidence suggest they can also play roles in the inflammatory process.28 Another notable observation from our study was the aggregation of macrophages in the IF rectum of fistulizing CD. Using functional assays, we substantiated the possibility that myeloid cell aggregation might be related to IL8-dependent signaling by the epithelium in a TNF-α–enriched milieu23,30 when we detected a significant increase in IL8 expression in rectal organoids after stimulation with TNF-α.

Given that 92% of patients with fistulizing disease exhibit CD of the rectum involvement,31 along with evidence suggesting that AA ancestry has a higher association with severe disease manifestation,32,33 we used 2 groups of individuals having either fistulizing perianal CD or having only CD with rectal involvement but without fistula or abscess. Collectively, the cohort was roughly equal in patients with EA and AA ancestry. At the population level and without regard to ancestry, some of the largest differences we observed were between IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula, indicating a major mucosal reprogramming occurring from the stages of IF CD of the rectum to the formation of fistula and abscess in these individuals. When considering ancestry, the IF CD of the rectum within the fistula group that were AA showed upregulation of many cellular pathways across multiple cell types, a pattern not observed in patients with EA ancestry. The increased cytokine and interleukin signaling across many cell types in AA patients with IF fistulizing disease pointed to an even higher level of immune activity in the rectum than observed in EA patients. These involved pathways were also the same features distinguishing IF CD of the rectum with fistula from nonfistulizing CD in AA patients, suggesting there are exacerbating immune triggers in the rectum during fistula development that are likely promoting an increase in pathways, such as IFN cascade, in AA patients. There are supporting genetic studies showing populations with African ancestry are associated with more robust immune responses,34 with up to 10% of macrophage-expressed genes linked to an African ancestral-specific response to inflammatory stimuli.35 Although our findings are consistent with a potential genetic contribution, other factors, including environmental, microbial, and epigenetic influences, are also taking part in these mechanisms and warrant future investigation.

At the phenotypic level, the most remarkable differences between IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula are taking place in pathways within epithelial cells that span the axis of the crypt. The overwhelming upregulation involving cytokine and IL signaling of the adaptive immune response and an increase in MHCII antigen presentation by epithelial cells, with activation of PD-1 and TCR signals occurring in IF CD of the rectum with fistula, shows that the rectal epithelium continues to undergo numerous functional changes in the progression of the disease. It is not clear, however, whether some of the epithelial cell metabolic pathway differences we observed between IF CD of the rectum with and without fistula reflect insults contributing to changes in epithelial barrier function caused by inflammation or are the initial drivers that facilitate and exacerbate the environment to form a fistula. We previously detected metabolic pathway changes in rectal organoids from patients with fistulizing CD,13 suggesting these changes are inherent to those individuals’ epithelial cells because no inflammatory mediators were used to stimulate organoids in those experiments. Furthermore, the changes in fatty-acid pathways in IF CD of the rectum with fistula link metabolic pathways to microbial activity in the pathology associated with increased MHCII complexes made by rectal epithelial cells during active disease. The association of abscess formation with this pathology and enrichment of the complement pathway in CD rectum with fistula also supports a potential microbial connection as another variable in determining disease progression. However, we did not consider microbial variables in this study. The sample size, limited number of gene probes for spatial experiments, and functional assays evaluating phagocytosis and antigen presentation in macrophage–organoid cocultures were limitations of our study. Nonetheless, we attempted to overcome this with multiple orthogonal approaches to strengthen and further substantiate our findings.

We provide a valuable data set of single-cell and spatial transcriptomics from multiple ancestral populations with CD of the rectum with and without fistula to be used for ongoing studies. Our experiments with rectal organoids indicate that the signals received by the epithelium during fistulizing disease promote high levels of STAT1, STAT6, and MHCII responses that need further investigation to better understand the connection between epithelial cells, T cells, and CD4+CD68+ macrophages during or just prior to the onset of fistula development. Future investigations should focus on the factors stimulating IFN pathways during CD of the rectum and their impact on specific subtypes of epithelial cells and macrophages using ancestral populations.

Materials and Methods

Patient Recruitment and Sample Collection

Mucosal biopsy samples were obtained from the rectum of patients with CD with and without perianal fistulizing disease during endoscopy procedures under an approved Emory University Institutional Review Board 2025P010865 protocol. Control biopsies were collected from non-IBD individuals with no evidence of intestinal inflammation during routine colonoscopy. These individuals underwent colonoscopy for abdominal pain or suspected colonic polyps and later were found to have normal mucosal macroscopically and microscopically. The CD cohort eligible for inclusion in this study represented an equal distribution of AA and EA ancestry. Patient metadata are summarized in Figure 2. The presence of mucosal inflammation was determined based on macroscopic (colonoscopy) assessment. A total of 50 individuals were enrolled in the study, including 25 with CD of the rectum without perianal disease (10 IF and 15 NI rectal mucosa) and 18 with CD of the rectum with perianal fistula (7 IF and 11 NI rectal mucosa). The remaining participants were non-IBD controls. Power calculations indicated that, with 80% power at P < .05, comparison of inflamed rectal mucosa from patients with CD without perianal disease (n = 10) vs those with perianal fistula (n = 7) would be sufficient to detect a mean difference of 1.48 standard deviations between groups. Three fistula wash samples (obtained during examination under anesthesia with surgeon), 1 with tissue fragments, were additionally collected from 2 individuals with perianal CD during seton placement.

Single-Cell Isolation, Library Preparation, and Sequencing

Mucosal biopsies were protease-digested and processed for single-cell sequencing within 2 hours of endoscopy as previously described.36

Single-Cell Data Processing

After sequencing, raw BCL files were demultiplexed using 10X Genomics Cell Ranger (v7.0.1), and the resulting FASTQ files were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 to generate feature–barcode matrices. Single-cell data processing and QC were performed using Seurat (v5.0.0).37 Cells expressing fewer than 250 or more than 7000 genes, as well as those with >50% mitochondrial content, were excluded. DoubletFinder38 was used to remove potential doublets. Mitochondrial and ribosomal genes were pruned in the QC. Count matrices from 50 samples were analyzed in Seurat. Unique molecular identifier counts were log-normalized, and highly variable genes were selected. Each sample was scaled prior to principal component analysis (PCA). To correct for batch effects, reciprocal PCA was applied, followed by dimensional reduction using PCA and projection via Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with 30 dimensions. Neighborhood analysis and clustering were performed using 20 dimensions at a resolution of 0.5, identifying broad cell populations.

Cell Type Annotation and Subclustering

Clusters were annotated based on differentially expressed genes identified with the FindAllMarkers function, as well as known marker genes from published literature and cell-cycle phase information. Macrophage clusters characterized by CD68, C1QA, and C1QB expression were extracted from the integrated Seurat object. This macrophage subset was then normalized with SCTransform and integrated using the RunHarmony function.39 Subsequently, neighbor detection and clustering were repeated, and the data were visualized using UMAP at a resolution of 0.5. The resulting macrophage subclusters were annotated as in the opening section with published marker genes.40,41

Inflammation, Crypt-–Axis, and Phagocytic Activity Scoring

The aggregate expression of signaling programs within the rectal mucosal scRNA-seq data was computed using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat. Gene sets to assess inflammation, crypt axis, and phagocytic programs were performed as previously described.21,42,43

Cell–Cell Communication Analysis

CellChat44 with CellChatDB was employed to infer receptor–ligand interactions from the integrated rectal mucosal scRNA-seq dataset. Global signaling patterns were examined across cell populations within each phenotypic group, followed by pathway and ligand–receptor analysis.

Pseudobulk Principal Component Analysis

Pseudobulk single-cell PCA plots were generated by aggregating raw unique molecular identifier count matrices of each sample to assess the transcriptomic variance.

Differential Expressions and Pathway Analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function and model-based analysis of single-cell transcriptomics. It was computed on cell types with at least 10 cells for each condition.

Trajectory Analysis

To investigate phagocyte subpopulation dynamics, we performed pseudotime trajectory analysis using Palantir45 with default parameters, designating C1Q+ macrophages as the root cell population. Pseudotime estimation was used to infer directional cell-state transitions. In addition, fate probabilities were computed for each subpopulation, and the top fate-determining genes were identified and visualized using the CellRank package.

Xenium Spatial Transcriptomics Protocol and Analysis

Regions exemplifying active and inactive sites of CD of the rectum with and without fistula within formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections were identified by a board-certified pathologist. Selected slides were processed with Xenium 10X Genomics technology KIT#1000460, and human colon gene expression panel kit (1,000,642) on a Xenium Analyzer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Xenium cell segmentation was carried out using a transcript-based Baysor algorithm. Analysis of probe signals/quality was performed with Xenium Explorer. Subsequent QC analysis and annotation were performed with Sopa.25 We assessed mucosal biology using neighborhood analysis with Novae46 and Squidpy,47 along with STLearn48 for receptor–ligand interactions.

Immunofluorescence Microscopy and Quantification

Serial sections used for Xenium were also processed by standard histologic techniques. Briefly, after deparaffinization and heat-induced antigen retrieval using Diva Decloaker (BioCare Medical), slides were blocked in bovine serum albumin and stained with primary antibody (1:400 dilution; EPR6855 rabbit anti-CD4 from Abcam, mouse anti-CD68 from Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies (1:1000; Alexafluor from Thermo) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:2000) were applied for 1 hour at room temperature with multiple washes between steps. Background quenching was performed using Vector TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol and mounted with Diamond Antifade mountant. Images were acquired using a PhenoImager HT 2.0 whole-slide scanner system, and downstream analysis on CD4+CD68+ signals were performed with QuPath software.

Rectal Organoids, Western Blotting, and Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Organoids were established from rectal biopsies as previously described.49 Organoids were maintained in a modified WNT3A, R-spondin, Noggin-based media, and stimulated with IFN-γ (1 ng/mL), IL13 (10 ng/mL), and TNF-α (25 ng/mL). Western blotting was performed essentially as described in,49 and the antibodies used were against the following: STAT1 Y701, STAT1 S727, STAT6 Y641, STAT1, STAT6, MHCII, and CD74. All these antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technologies. Total RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit. Complementary DNA synthesis was performed using the Applied Biosystems TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction was carried out using Fast TaqMan Master Mix, with CXCL8-specific primers obtained from Applied Biosystems.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to assess significant differences between phenotypic groups. The Wilcoxson test was used to compare cell abundance proportions, inflammation scores, phagocytic scores, and cell abundance proportions, whereas the simple t test was used to assess macrophage aggregate surface area within IF rectum tissue from patients with CD with and without fistula. REACTOME pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using significant differentially expressed genes (adjusted P value <.05 and log2fold change ≥1). Pathways with a false discovery rate–adjusted P value < .05 were considered significant.
