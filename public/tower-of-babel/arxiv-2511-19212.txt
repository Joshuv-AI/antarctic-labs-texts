A Note on the Parameterised Complexity of Coverability in Vector Addition Systems
Michał Pilipczuk, Sylvain Schmitz, Henry Sinclair-Banks

Abstract
We investigate the parameterised complexity of the classic coverability problem for vector addition systems (VAS): given a finite set of vectors 𝑽 ⊆ ℤ d \mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d} , an initial configuration 𝒔 ∈ ℕ d \mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d} , and a target configuration 𝒕 ∈ ℕ d \mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d} , decide whether starting from 𝒔 \textstyle s , one can iteratively add vectors from 𝑽 \textstyle V to ultimately arrive at a configuration that is larger than or equal to 𝒕 \textstyle t on every coordinate, while not observing any negative value on any coordinate along the way. We consider two natural parameters for the problem: the dimension d d and the size of 𝑽 \textstyle V , defined as the total bitsize of its encoding. We present several results charting the complexity of those two parameterisations, among which the highlight is that coverability for VAS parameterised by the dimension and with all the numbers in the input encoded in unary is complete for the class 𝖷𝖭𝖫 \mathsf{XNL} under PL-reductions. We also discuss open problems in the topic, most notably the question about fixed-parameter tractability for the parameterisation by the size of 𝑽 \textstyle V .

Introduction

Vector Addition Systems.

Vector addition systems are a well-established model with a very
simple definition: a
d
-dimensional
vector addition system

(VAS)
[
31
]
is a finite set
\textstyle V
of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
which defines a step relation between configurations in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
:

\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}\to_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}
for all
\textstyle u
in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
and

\textstyle v
in
\textstyle V
, provided that
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}+\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle v$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle v$}}
is in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. One usually interprets the
d
coordinates of a configuration
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle u$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle u$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
as
d

counters
that may take non-negative integer values; then
\textstyle V
represents a set of allowed updates to the counter values, which can be applied only if none of the counters becomes negative.

In spite of this apparent simplicity, vector addition systems can
exhibit highly complex behaviours. Famously, their
reachability
problem

is
\mathsf{ACKERMANN}
-complete
[
35
,
34
,
13
]
:
given as input a
d
-dimensional VAS
\textstyle V
, an initial
configuration
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, and a target configuration
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the reachability problem asks whether
\textstyle t
is
reachable from
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
in
\textstyle V
, i.e., whether
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\to^{\ast}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}
. This combination of a simple definition
with rich behaviours makes vector addition systems—along with the
equivalent model of Petri nets—well-suited whenever one needs to
model systems managing multiple discrete resources, e.g., threads in
concurrent computations, molecules in chemical reactions
[
3
,
4
]
, organisms in
biological processes
[
5
]
, etc., but also as a theoretical tool involved in
establishing decidability and complexity statements for a variety of
decision problems in logic, formal languages, verification,
etc.
[
42
, Section 5]
.

The Coverability Problem.

In this note, we are interested in a decision problem with a less
extreme complexity: given the same input, the
coverability
problem
asks whether there exists a coordinate-wise larger or equal
configuration
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}\sqsupseteq\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}
such that
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\to^{\ast}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}
. This relaxation of the reachability
problem was first shown decidable by Karp and
Miller
[
31
]
, before being proven
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
-hard by
Lipton
[
36
]
and to belong to
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
by
Rackoff
[
40
]
.

Like reachability, coverability in vector addition systems
inter-reduces with numerous decision problems, notably in relation to
the automated verification of safety properties in concurrent or
distributed
systems
[
30
,
29
,
22
,
16
,
18
,
23
,
2
,
6
]
,
as well as reasoning on data-aware logics or
systems
[
15
,
25
,
1
]
. These
applications have motivated a thorough investigation of the
problem
[
31
,
36
,
40
,
9
,
33
,
32
,
44
]

and the development of several tools specifically targeted at solving
it
[
19
,
8
,
24
]
.

Towards Parameterised Complexity.

Rosier and
Yen
[
41
]
refined Rackoff’s analysis to identify the
contribution of several parameters to the final complexity of the
coverability problem. Among those, the main parameter driving the
complexity is the

dimension
, i.e., the number of counters of the system. Indeed,
Rackoff obtains his result by showing a bound on the length of the
shortest covering runs from the source to the target configuration,
which is in
n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d\log d)}}
when parameterised by the
dimension
d
, where
n
is the size of the input encoded in
unary. (While
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
-completeness holds with both a unary and a
binary encoding, the complexity landscape changes as soon as one
isolates the dimension as a parameter).

This upper bound on the length of shortest covering runs in
unary-encoded VAS was recently improved to
n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}}
by Künnemann, Mazowiecki, Schütze,
Sinclair-Banks, and Węgrzycki
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]
,
and this matches the
n^{2^{\Omega(d)}}
lower bound in the family of
systems constructed by Lipton
[
36
]
. In turn, this upper
bound on the length of the shortest covering runs entails that the
coverability problem with a unary encoding can be solved either in
non-deterministic space
2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\log n
or in deterministic time

n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}}

[
32
, Corollary 3.4]
; moreover, the
latter time bound is also achieved by the classical
backward
coverability algorithm

[
44
, Corollary 4.5]
. Finally, as
also shown by Künnemann et al. through a parameterised
reduction from the parameterised clique
problem
\mathsf{p\textsf{-}CLIQUE}
, this time bound is optimal if one
assumes the Exponential Time Hypothesis: under the ETH, no algorithm
running in deterministic time
n^{o(2^{d})}
for coverability may
exist
[
32
, Theorem 4.2]
.

Surprisingly, in spite of this long line of results focusing on
coverability in vector addition systems, and in particular when
isolating the dimension as a key parameter, the question of the
parameterised complexity of the problem has not been considered
(see
Section

2.4
for literature on other parameterisations on Petri nets,
which are not directly comparable).

a

-dimensional VAS
\textstyle V
, an initial configuration

, and a target

\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}

\textstyle t

\textstyle V

From the viewpoint of parameterised complexity, when using a

Fixed Systems and Parameterisation by Size.

A long-standing open question on decision problems for VAS, already
raised by Hack
[
26
, page 172]
and revived in recent
years
[
28
,
17
,
12
]
, is whether any
meaningful statements can be made about the complexity for
a
fixed
VAS. In this direction, Draghici, Haase, and
Ryzhikov
[
17
]
have recently shown that there exists a
fixed
\textstyle V
such that the coverability problem (and thus also the
reachability problem) for
\textstyle V
with a binary encoding of the
initial/target configurations is already
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard. This
complexity of coverability in a fixed VAS is also related to a
question on the length of shortest covering runs by
Czerwiński
[
12
]
. A natural way to approach these
questions within the framework of parameterised complexity is to ask
about the complexity of coverability when parameterised by
the
size
of the encoding of the system, which we denote by

\left\lVert\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}\right\rVert
.

\blacktriangleright
Problem:
{\mathsf{p\textsf{-}size\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}}

input

a
d
-dimensional VAS
\textstyle V
, an input configuration
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, and a target
configuration
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}

parameter

\left\lVert\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}\right\rVert

question

does
\textstyle s
cover
\textstyle t
in
\textstyle V
, i.e., does there
exist
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}\sqsupseteq\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}
such that
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\rightarrow_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}^{\ast}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}

This is an a priori easier problem than the one parameterised by the

).

Contributions.

Our main contribution in this note is to refine the bounds
and pinpoint the exact parameterised complexity
of
\mathsf{p\textsf{-}dim\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}
: the problem is

\mathsf{XNL}
-complete under PL reductions with a unary encoding (see
Section

3
), and

\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete under FPT reductions with a binary encoding
(see
Section

4
). These results are mainly applications of the
rich literature dedicated to the coverability problem in vector
addition systems: the upper bounds stem from the
2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\log n

non-deterministic space bounds from Rackoff’s and subsequent
works
[
40
,
41
,
32
]
,
while
\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness with a binary encoding follows from
the
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness of coverability in a fixed
VAS
[
17
, Corollary 2]
. The
\mathsf{XNL}
lower bound is a new
proof, which relies on a result of Wehar
[
46
]
on the
intersection non-emptiness problem for finite automata. Importantly
given the dearth of ‘natural’ complete problems for these two
parameterised complexity classes and in particular for
\mathsf{XNL}
, these
results significantly enrich the library of known complete problems,
as most of the inter-reducible problems we mentioned earlier also have
natural parameters that correspond to the dimension.

As a consequence of these results, we also conclude
that
\mathsf{p\textsf{-}size\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}

is
\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-complete when using a binary encoding
(see
Section

4
), and in
\mathsf{XNL}
when using a unary encoding
(see
Section

3
). Our motivation here is to restate the
questions about the complexity of coverability and reachability in
fixed VAS
[
28
,
17
,
12
]
within the
framework of parameterised complexity, which we discuss more extensively
in
Section

5
.

Parameterised Space Complexity Classes.

Although these classes are perhaps less well-known, the parameterised
paradigm can be also applied to space complexity.

Regarding nondeterministic logarithmic space complexity, a
parameterised problem
P\subseteq\Sigma\times\mathbb{N}
is in
(uniform)
\mathsf{XNL}

[
11
, Proposition 18]
if there is a
computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and a non-deterministic
algorithm that, given a pair
(x,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, decides
whether
(x,k)\in P
in space at most
f(k)\cdot\log(n)
. In
particular,
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{AW}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{AW}$}}{\mathsf{AW}}{\mathsf{AW}}}[{\mathsf{SAT}}]
is included in the closure of
\mathsf{XNL}
under FPT
reductions by
[
11
, Proposition 23]
.
When working with parameterised complexity classes
like
\mathsf{FPT}
,
\mathsf{XP}
,
\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, etc., FPT reductions suffice, but
for
\mathsf{XNL}
, one needs to work with PL reductions. When stating
completeness results, we always specify what type of reductions we
have in mind.

Regarding polynomial space complexity, a parameterised problem
P\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is in
\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
if there is a
computable function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\Sigma^{*}
and a problem
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
such that
L\in{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{PSPACE}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{PSPACE}$}}{\mathsf{PSPACE}}{\mathsf{PSPACE}}}
and for all instances
(x,k)\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
, we have
(x,k)\in P
if and only if
(x,f(k))\in L
. Thus,
\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
consists of all problems that can be
decided using at most polynomial space after some precomputation that
only involves the parameter. One can also view
\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
as the
space complexity-concerned analogue of
\mathsf{FPT}
. In fact, it is easy to
prove (see
[

, Exercise 8.40]
) that a parameterised problem

\mathsf{para}

\mathsf{PSPACE}
if and only if it can be solved in FPT space; that is, in space bounded by

for some
computable function

and

. In order to establish

\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hardness under FPT reductions, it suffices to prove that
there exists some fixed value of the parameter

for which the
problem is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard under polynomial-time reductions (see

[
21
, Corollary 2.16]
).

2.2
Vector Addition Systems and Related Models

As explained in the introduction, vector addition systems are
equivalent to a number of models; in particular,

parameterised problems.

\blacktriangleright

The problems

\mathsf{p\textsf{-}dim\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}
and

\mathsf{p\textsf{-}dim\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(PN)}
are equivalent up to
PL reductions, and so are the problems

\mathsf{p\textsf{-}size\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}
and

\mathsf{p\textsf{-}size\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(PN)}
, all this both with a unary
and with a binary encoding.

2.3
The Complexity of Coverability

Upper Bounds.

Regarding the complexity upper bounds, consider an input of the
coverability problem for a
d
-dimensional VAS
\textstyle V
with initial
configuration
\textstyle s
and target configuration
\textstyle t
, encoded in
unary with size
n=\mathit{size}(\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}})+\left\lVert\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}\right\rVert_{1}
. The approach pioneered by Rackoff
[
40
]
to
establish the
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
upper bound is the following, and will also
be pertinent for the discussion in
Section

5
. A finite
sequence of steps
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\to^{\ast}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}
for some
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}\sqsupseteq\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}
is a
covering run
or
coverability
witness
; its length is its number of steps. What Rackoff showed is
that, if there is a covering run, then there exists one of length
bounded by
n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d\log d)}}
. The best known (and actually optimal)
upper bound for this length is the following.

\blacktriangleright
Fact:
[
32
, Theorem 3.3]

Shortest covering witnesses for an instance of coverability
encoded in unary have length bounded by
n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}}
.

As a direct corollary, using algorithms that exhaustively explore the
space of bounded configurations, one can determine the complexity of
coverability in VAS.

\blacktriangleright
Corollary: cf.
[
32
, Corollary 3.4]

There exists both a deterministic
n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}}
-time algorithm
and a non-deterministic
2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\log(n)
-space algorithm
for coverability, where
n
is the size of the input encoded in
unary.

In the multi-parameter analysis of the complexity, one usually focuses
on the deterministic algorithm, which yields that coverability can be
solved in pseudo-polynomial deterministic time when the dimension is
fixed. In the parameterised setting, this also yields that

\mathsf{p\textsf{-}dim\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}
with a unary encoding is in
\mathsf{XP}
.

In this paper however, we are more interested in the other, non-deterministic algorithm,
which we repeat from
[
32
, Corollary 3.4]
for the
sake of completeness. The algorithm starts with the initial configuration and
iteratively guesses the consecutive transitions in a run while keeping track
of the current configuration and the total number of transitions
applied so far (all the numbers are encoded in binary). If during
this iteration, it encounters a configuration covering the target
configuration, then it accepts, and if the bound on the length of

n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}}
provided by
3
is exceeded without
encountering a covering configuration, it rejects. The correctness of
the algorithm follows directly from
3
. As for the
space complexity, observe that every configuration encountered during
the run will have all the counters bounded by
n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}}
, and
the same can be also said about the length counter. Hence, the space
needed to store all the relevant information is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(d\cdot\log n^{2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}})\leqslant 2^{\mathcal{O}(d)}\cdot\log n
.

Lower Bounds.

Regarding the lower bounds,
Lipton
[
36
]
was the first to show the
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
-hardness of
the reachability problem, with a proof that also applies to
coverability. The idea of the construction is to define by induction
over
d
a family of VAS
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}_{\!d}
of dimension
\mathcal{O}(d)
that builds
up counter values bounded by
2^{2^{d}}
, allowing to simulate zero
tests on those counters. This leads to a simulation of a
3
-counter machine
with counter values bounded by
2^{2^{d}}
, thereby
proving
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
-hardness.
By a slight modification of the construction, one also obtains the
following counterpart to
3
.

\blacktriangleright
Fact: c.f.
[
9
, Theorem 3]

Shortest covering witnesses for an instance of coverability
encoded in unary may have length as large as
n^{2^{\Omega(d)}}
.

Lipton’s construction was exploited as part of a parameterised
reduction from
\mathsf{p\textsf{-}CLIQUE}
to

\mathsf{p\textsf{-}dim\textsf{-}COVERABILITY(VAS)}

[
32
, Theorem 4.2]
,
showing the
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{W}$}}{\mathsf{W}}{\mathsf{W}}}[1]
-hardness of the problem.

2.4
Further Related Work

Watel, Weisser, and Barth
[
45
]
investigate an optimisation
variant of the coverability problem in weighted Petri nets: each
transition has a non-negative weight in
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, and the goal
is to find a covering witness where the sum of weights is minimal.
The parameters they consider are
\max_{p\in P,t\in T}W(p,t)
,

\max_{p\in P,t\in T}W(t,p)
the maximal input and output flow weights,

\|\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle m$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle m$}}^{\prime}\|
the size of the target marking, and the number of steps
in the sought covering witness, none of which is comparable to our
parameterisations by dimension or size of the Petri net.

Praveen
[
39
]
defines a graph associated with a Petri
net
\mathcal{N}
whose vertex set is
P
— the set of places — and edges
\{p,p^{\prime}\}

whenever there exists a transition
t
with
W(p,t)>0

and
W(t,p^{\prime})>0
. Praveen then shows that the coverability problem
parameterised by both the size of a vertex cover and by the maximal
flow weight
\max\{W(p,t),W(t,p)\mid p\in P,t\in T\}
is in

\mathsf{para}
-
\mathsf{PSPACE}

[
39
, Theorem 4.5]
. While this is in essence
a refinement of the parameterisation by the dimension
|P|
of the
Petri net (which bounds the size of a vertex cover), the added flow
weight parameter makes this result not directly comparable.

5
Discussion and Open Problems

Our results shed some light on the broader landscape of the
parameterised complexity of decision problems related to vector
addition systems. Let us consider the coverability problem and the
reachability problem; both have meaningful parameterisations either by
dimension or by size. A first set of questions is simply whether the
parameterised problem is in
\mathsf{FPT}
or not. A second set of questions
concerns bounding the length of witness runs, and is motivated by the
discussion in
Section

2.3
, where we saw that this approach
has been instrumental in the understanding of the complexity of
coverability. Given an initial configuration
\textstyle s
and a target
configuration
\textstyle t
in a VAS
\textstyle V
, a
shortest witness
is
a run
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\to^{\ast}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}
for some
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}^{\prime}\sqsupseteq\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}
in the case of coverability or a run
\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle s$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle s$}}\to^{\ast}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle V$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\displaystyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\textstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptstyle t$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\scriptscriptstyle t$}}
in the case of reachability, such that the
number of steps in the run is minimal. For a parameter
k
(the
dimension or the size of
\textstyle V
), we will say that shortest
witnesses have
fixed-parameter length
(FPT length) if there is
a computable function
f
and constant
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that shortest witnesses
have length at most
f(k)\cdot n^{c}
.

This setup leads to four questions, that can each be asked with a
parameterisation by dimension or by size, and using a unary or a
binary encoding.

(CP)

Is the parameterised coverability problem in
\mathsf{FPT}
?

(RP)

Is the parameterised reachability problem in
\mathsf{FPT}
?

(CL)

Are shortest coverability witnesses of FPT length?

(RL)

Are shortest reachability witnesses of FPT length?

Negative Cases.

Quite a few of these questions have
negative answers. In the case of parameterisation by dimension, we have the following:

•

A positive answer to

(CP)
would
entail
{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{XNL}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{XNL}$}}{\mathsf{XNL}}{\mathsf{XNL}}}\subseteq{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{FPT}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{FPT}$}}{\mathsf{FPT}}{\mathsf{FPT}}}
in the case of a unary encoding by

Theorem

3.1
, which would bring in particular a
collapse of the
\mathsf{W}
- and
\mathsf{A}
-hierarchies down to
\mathsf{FPT}
, because

{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{AW}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{AW}$}}{\mathsf{AW}}{\mathsf{AW}}}[{\mathsf{SAT}}]\subseteq[{\mathchoice{\hbox{$\mathsf{XNL}$}}{\hbox{$\mathsf{XNL}$}}{\mathsf{XNL}}{\mathsf{XNL}}}]^{\text{{$\mathsf{fpt}$}}}

by
[
11
, Proposition 23]
(recall

Figure
