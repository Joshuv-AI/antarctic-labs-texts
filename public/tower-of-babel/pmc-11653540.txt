A rare case report of severe aplastic anaemia caused by long-term use of zidovudine
Authors: Jing Zhou, Qi Wang, Yu’e Yu, Lingling Chen, Jinchuan Shi, Congxiang Huang, Rangxiao Zhuang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZidovudine is an antiviral drug used to treat acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS). Anaemia is a common adverse effect of zidovudine that usually occurs in the first 2 to 4 weeks of initial treatment. Here, we describe a patient with HIV infection who developed severe aplastic anaemia 11 years after taking zidovudine. To our knowledge, this is the first case of severe aplastic anaemia caused by the long-term use of zidovudine.Case presentationA 28-year-old male patient with AIDS developed severe aplastic anaemia after taking zidovudine for 11 years. The patient went to the emergency department due to weakness and falls. He was diagnosed with severe anaemia because his haemoglobin (Hb) level was 31 g/L and his red blood cell (RBC) count was 0.89 × 1012/L. Gastrointestinal bleeding, haematological diseases and autoimmune haemolytic anaemia were ruled out by gastroenteroscopy, stool routine examination and bone marrow puncture biopsy after admission. When the causality of adverse drug reactions was assessed, the Naranjo score of zidovudine was 8, indicating that severe aplastic anaemia was likely related to zidovudine. Given that bone marrow suppression is a side effect of zidovudine, this drug was discontinued. Lamivudine and dolutegravir sodium tablets were subsequently used as antiviral therapies. The patient’s symptoms of anaemia improved with the transfusion of suspended RBCs, oral polysaccharide iron complex capsules and folic acid tablets. After 2 weeks of treatment, the patient’s symptoms of fatigue improved, with a Hb level of 70 g/L and a RBC count of 2.13 × 1012/L, which were significantly better than before, and he was discharged. At the follow-up 6 weeks after discharge, the patient’s Hb level was 156 g/L, his RBC count was 4.67 × 1012/L, and the anaemia was resolved.ConclusionsZidovudine has bone marrow suppression toxicity, which manifests as anaemia and can occur at any stage of use. Thus, we should not only pay attention to the initial stage of medication but also regularly monitor the patient via routine blood tests in the later stage. If anaemia occurs during the use of zidovudine, physicians should consider the possibility that anaemia is related to the administration of zidovudine and prescribe medication cessation and symptomatic treatment.Keywords: Zidovudine, AIDS, Severe aplastic anaemia, Case report

Background

Zidovudine, a nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NRTI), was approved by the United States FDA in 1987 and has accurate efficacy and strong anti-HIV activity. The common adverse reactions reported with zidovudine include myelotoxicity, nausea, vomiting, headache, insomnia and other complications. Myelotoxicity is a main side effect that often manifests as aplastic anaemia [1]. Zidovudine-induced aplastic anaemia often occurs [2, 3]. However, the uniqueness of our report is that severe aplastic anaemia developed in a patient who had received zidovudine treatment for 11 years. This is rare and has never been reported in the literature. We hope that this case report alerts clinicians and pharmacists to anaemia caused by the long-term use of zidovudine.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old male was admitted to the hospital for “black stool for half a year and fatigue for half a month”. The patient had a history of AIDS for 11 years. At that time, the CD4+ T lymphocyte count was 750/µL, the Hb and RBC counts were normal, and the HIV RNA level was unknown. The patient had recently received antiviral drugs, including zidovudine 300 mg twice daily, lamivudine 300 mg, and efavirenz 600 mg once daily, with HIV-RNA < 100 IU/ml and a CD4+ T-cell count of 1108/µL. Three months prior, the patient was diagnosed with bipolar disorder and treated with agomelatine tablets, escitalopram oxalate tablets, lithium carbonate sustained-release tablets, sodium valproate sustained-release tablets, and oxazepam tablets. He had a history of erythromycin skin test positivity, and no smoking or drinking history or food allergies were reported.

The patient reported that he had passed black stool for six months at a rate of 2 to 3 times a day, which he did not pay close attention to. Approximately half a month before admission, the patient experienced fatigue and fell while walking fast. On admission, blood examination revealed a white blood cell (WBC) count of 6.17 × 109/L, a RBC count of 0.89 × 1012/L, a Hb level of 31 g/L, a reticulocyte (RET) count of 0.35%, a haematocrit (HCT) of 0.090 L/L, and a platelet (PLT) count of 288 × 109/L. His vital signs were as follows: temperature 37.8 °C, heart rate 112 beats per min, respiratory rate 20 breaths per min and blood pressure 96/69 mmHg. Physical examination revealed anaemia and pallor of the skin and mucosa. No nasal or gum bleeding or skin ecchymosis was found. The patient was weak but without dizziness, headache, abdominal distention, diarrhoea or other symptoms, and he was admitted to the hospital with a diagnosis of “gastrointestinal bleeding? AIDS”.

According to the patient’s self-report and the results of the blood examination, the possibility of gastrointestinal bleeding was considered. In addition, haematological diseases, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, and zidovudine-induced myelosuppression were not excluded. The patient received intravenous somatostatin 3 mg twice a day, haemocoagulase Bothrops atrox for injection 2 U once a day, lansoprazole 30 mg twice a day and red blood cell suspension transfusions. Considering the myelosuppressive toxicity of zidovudine, the original antiviral regimen was discontinued and switched to lamivudine and dolutegravir sodium tablets 300 mg/50 mg once a day for antiviral treatment. 3.9 High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) thin-layer plain scanning of the chest revealed a few inflammatory fibrous lesions in the lower lobes of both lungs that were scattered with small nodules. 3.10 Bone marrow puncture and biopsy were performed, and gastroscopy revealed oesophagitis and chronic nonatrophic gastritis with bile reflux without gastric ulcers or bleeding. The blood test results were as follows: RBC count of 1.78 × 109/L, 31 g/L Hb, 0.42% RET, 43.6 µmol/L serum iron, total iron binding capacity of 47.7 µmol/L, 1.69 g/L transferrin, and 1044.2 µg/L ferritin. The results of the Coombs test, stool occult blood test, parvovirus B19 antibody IgG and IgM analysis, antinuclear antibody (ANA) analysis, and other evaluations were negative. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), folate, vitamin B12, bilirubin, and transaminase levels were all within normal limits. A bone marrow smear revealed that the proportion of erythroblasts was low, and pure red cell aplasia could not be ruled out. Bone marrow biopsy (reported in Sect. 3.15, Fig. 1) revealed that the haematopoietic function of the bone marrow was low, and no tumour or haematopoietic cell hyperplasia was observed. On the 7th day of admission (2022.3.15), colonoscopy revealed no obvious evidence of bleeding. Repeated bone marrow smears and biopsies indicated that (1) the number of nucleated and megakaryocytes was low; (2) the haematopoietic function at the puncture site was poor; and (3) there was low iron in the bone marrow. Bone marrow biopsy (reported in Sect. 3.21, Fig. 2) revealed several haematopoietic cells in the bone trabecula, with both granulocyte and erythroid cells visible. The proportion of erythroid cells was relatively low, and the nuclei were shifted to the left. In addition, two megakaryocytes with roughly normal morphologies were observed. A possible diagnosis of gastrointestinal haemorrhage was ruled out by combining medical history, gastroscopy and test results. Therefore, intravenous somatostatin, haemocoagulase Bothrops atrox for injection and lansoprazole were discontinued. The anaemia gradually improved after the withdrawal of zidovudine and infusion of suspended RBCs, along with the simultaneous administration of polysaccharide iron complex capsules, folic acid tablets, mecobalamin tablets, and other supplementary haematopoietic raw materials. On day 9 after cessation of zidovudine, the laboratory data revealed that the RBC count was 2.1 × 1012/L, the Hb level was 66 g/L, and the RET count was 0.5%. At discharge, the RBC count had increased to 2.13 × 1012/L, the Hb level had increased to 70 g/L, and the RET count had increased to 8.11%, which was a significant improvement.

Discharge follow-up

During follow-up 6 weeks after discharge, the RBC count had increased to 2.1 × 1012/L, and the Hb level had increased to 66 g/L. At the 10- and 18-week follow-ups after discharge, the RBC count and Hb level had returned to normal. The patient had no symptoms of fatigue or dizziness. Discussion and conclusions

There are 39 million people living with HIV worldwide, as reported by the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) in 2023 [4]. In recent years, the mortality rate has significantly decreased, and HIV-related complications have gradually increased because of the widespread adoption of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART). Anaemia is the most common complication of the blood system among people living with HIV [5]. A systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevalence of anaemia among people living with HIV revealed that the prevalence rate of anaemia was 39.7% for children younger than 15 years old living with HIV and 46.6% for those age 15 and above living with HIV (except pregnant women), whereas the prevalence rate among pregnant women with HIV was 48.6%. Anaemia in people living with HIV is mainly mild and moderate [6], and the incidence of anaemia in patients with advanced AIDS is significantly greater than that in patients with early AIDS [7].

There are many factors that contribute to anaemia in HIV patients. (1) HIV invades bone marrow stromal cells and disrupts the bone marrow microenvironment, which may lead to an imbalance of cytokines. The upregulated cytokine IL-6 increases hepcidin, which regulates iron homeostasis and causes iron retention in macrophages and intestinal cells. Therefore, the serum iron concentration is reduced, thereby reducing the production of haemoglobin. In addition, the cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α inhibit the production of erythropoietin and interfere with the proliferation of RBC precursors [8]. (2) Patients may experience digestive symptoms such as decreased appetite, abdominal pain and diarrhoea because HIV invades the digestive tract. Thus, the reduced absorption of folic acid, vitamin B12, iron and other substances results in insufficient haematopoietic raw materials in the human body and then induces giant cell anaemia or iron deficiency anaemia [9]. (3) Late-stage HIV patients may experience opportunistic infections, such as infections caused by mycobacterium, which may inhibit bone marrow; marneffei and cryptococcus, which may inhibit the maturation of progenitor cells; and parvovirus B19, which damages mature RBCs and leads to anaemia [10–12]. (4) HIV induces B-cell activation and the response of B cells to cytomegalovirus or EB virus, leading to autoimmune haemolytic anaemia [13]. (5) The final contributing factor is HAART drug-related anaemia, such as from zidovudine [8].

It is important to identify the causes of anaemia in HIV-infected patients with anaemia. Gastrointestinal bleeding is the most common cause of anaemia. The patient in this study underwent comprehensive examination after admission, and the results of stool OB (-) and gastroscopy revealed no ulcers or bleeding, which ruled out anaemia caused by gastrointestinal bleeding. The patient’s HIV-RNA concentration was less than 100 IU/ml, and the CD4+ T lymphocyte count was 1108/µL, with folate: 10.8 nmol/L, vitamin B12: 223.64 pmol/L, serum iron: 43.6 µmol/L, transferrin: 1.69 g/L, CRP: 0.8 mg/L, and PCT: 0.049 ng/ml. An HRCT thin-layer plain scan of the chest revealed no pulmonary inflammatory lesions, and parvovirus B19 antibody IgG was negative. Furthermore, the patient had no respiratory tract, urinary tract, nervous system or digestive system symptoms. There is no evidence that anaemia is caused by the progression of AIDS and opportunistic infection. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), which is present in human RBCs, assists in glucose metabolism and protects RBCs from the threat of oxidants. Deficiency of G6PD can cause acute haemolytic anaemia. The patient’s G6PD levels were within normal limits, excluding acute haemolytic anaemia caused by G6PD deficiency [14]. Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA) is an anaemia that occurs when the body’s immune function is disrupted and the production of autoantibodies accelerates the destruction of RBCs beyond bone marrow compensation [15]. AIHA was ruled out because the patient’s Coombs test was negative. Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy are necessary diagnostic methods for haematological diseases such as aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, and myeloma. The patient’s two bone marrow biopsy results revealed low bone marrow function, but no tumour or abnormal proliferation of haematopoietic cells was observed. On the basis of the results of the comprehensive examination and medication history, the clinical pharmacists gave a score of 8 points according to the Naranjo scale, which meant that severe anaemia was probably related to zidovudine.

Zidovudine is an important component of the HAART regimen and a first-line treatment drug in China’s free regimen. However, the side effect of bone marrow suppression is a problem that must be faced during the treatment process. A retrospective cohort study of the incidence of anaemia and risk factors in HIV/AIDS patients revealed that zidovudine treatment is an important risk factor for the occurrence of moderate to severe anaemia, and the drug results in the rapid development of anaemia in patients [16]. A retrospective analysis in northeastern Nigeria compared the rates of anaemia between patients who received AZT and those who did not receive AZT-containing ART regimens. The results demonstrated that the incidence of anaemia in HIV-infected patients initiated on AZT-containing ART was 2.7 and 4.5 times greater than that in HIV-infected patients initiating a non-AZT-containing regimen at 6 and 12 months, respectively. The overall incidence of zidovudine-related anaemia was estimated to be 22.3% at 12 months [17]. In another study of 1221 HIV-infected patients treated with zidovudine, the incidence of zidovudine-induced anaemia was 14.6%, with 70.8% of cases occurring within 3 months and 93.1% occurring within 6 months [18].

The mechanism by which zidovudine-related anaemia occurs is not fully understood but may be explained by the following aspects. (1) Zidovudine and its metabolites specifically inhibit the expression of the globin gene in human RBC progenitors, which alters the development and differentiation of RBCs [19]. (2) Zidovudine is incorporated into the DNA of bone marrow cells and thus may lead to bone marrow toxicity [20]. (3) Zidovudine treatment decreases the number of erythropoietin-dependent haematopoietic progenitors derived from bone marrow [21]. (4) Zidovudine is a thymidine analogue that induces anaemia by affecting DNA synthesis. The accumulation of drugs in cells increases the level of intracellular reactive oxygen species and mitochondrial toxicity, including RBC damage [22].

According to the reported literature [7, 23], zidovudine-induced anaemia is reversible by a reduction in zidovudine dosage or withdrawal of the drug, and moderate or severe anaemia can resolve with drug withdrawal, blood transfusion and drug treatment within 2 to 3 months. In this case, the patient stopped zidovudine immediately after admission and received an intravenous infusion of 6 U of suspended RBCs, oral administration of iron polysaccharide complex capsules, folic acid tablets, and vitamin B12 tablets. His Hb level, RBC count, and RET count steadily increased after drug withdrawal and symptomatic treatment for one week. Moreover, his symptoms of anaemia completely resolved by the 10th week of follow-up after discharge. After 18 weeks of follow-up, no symptoms of anaemia were observed.

Importantly, zidovudine-associated anaemia can appear not only in the first few months of treatment but also after a long period of use. The Hb level and RBC count should be evaluated before patients start zidovudine treatment, and patients with a history of anaemia should avoid the use of zidovudine. The occurrence of anaemia is relatively undetectable; therefore, routine blood should be monitored every 2 weeks during the first three months of the initiation of zidovudine treatment. If there are no abnormalities, the monitoring frequency can be changed to once a month. Patients should seek physician assistance if their skin and mucosa are pale or if they experience fatigue, dizziness, etc. This report is helpful in enabling clinicians to be aware of anaemia caused by the long-term use of zidovudine and to understand the clinical characteristics of this drug-induced condition.
