Diffuse Interface Models for Two-Phase Flows with Phase Transition: Modeling and Existence of Weak Solutions
Helmut Abels, Harald Garcke, Julia Wittmann

Abstract
. The flow of two macroscopically immiscible, viscous, incompressible fluids with unmatched densities is studied, where a transfer of mass between the constituents by phase transition is taken into account. To this end, two quasi-incompressible diffuse interface models with singular free energies are analyzed, differing primarily in their velocity averaging. Firstly, to generalize a model by Abels, Garcke, and Grün, a thermodynamically consistent system of Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard type with source terms is derived in a framework of continuum fluid dynamics, followed by a proof of existence of weak solutions to the latter. Secondly, the quasi-stationary version of a model by Aki, Dreyer, Giesselmann, and Kraus is investigated analytically, with existence of weak solutions being established for the resulting quasi-stationary Stokes system coupled to a Cahn–Hilliard equation with a source term. Keywords: two-phase flow, Navier–Stokes equations, Cahn–Hilliard equation, diffuse interface model, weak solutions Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 35Q30, 35Q35, 35D30, 35G61 76D05, 76D03, 76T06



1.
Introduction

Modeling the behavior of two-phase flows plays a crucial role in continuum fluid dynamics, with widespread relevance across both engineering and natural sciences. The complex phenomena occurring in these processes are particularly significant due to their ability to incorporate local mass exchanges between components, often driven by phase transitions, like those arising from chemical reactions.

More precisely, this work focuses on the dynamics of a binary isothermal mixture composed of viscous, incompressible Newtonian fluids with different densities. It takes into account that in these systems, phase transitions may cause mass transfers between the constituents. Therefore, in the derivation of models describing such flows, the individual mass balance equations for each component include non-trivial source terms, representing mass production rates.

A key feature is the consideration of macroscopically immiscible fluids, for which we assume partial miscibility within a thin transition layer of positive width. This framework leads to diffuse interface models for describing the motion of the binary mixture. Typically, such models consist of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations for an averaged velocity field, which governs the fluid motion, coupled to the Cahn–Hilliard equation to bring phase separation into play.

Background.

The basic diffuse interface model for two-phase flows of incompressible fluids by Hohenberg & Halperin
[
28
]
, known as “Model H”, relies heavily on the assumption that both components have identical constant densities (matched densities). Gurtin et al.
[
26
]
derived this model rigorously and verified its thermodynamic consistency by showing a local energy dissipation inequality.
In the literature, several approaches to generalize this model to the case of unmatched densities are of significance.
Lowengrub & Truskinovsky
[
31
]
proposed a thermodynamically consistent model, characterized as quasi-incompressible in the sense that both fluids in the mixture are incompressible but their mass averaged velocity field is not divergence-free.
In contrast, Ding et al.
[
17
]
derived a model based on a solenoidal volume averaged velocity, whose thermodynamic consistency, however, is not known.
A more complex model by Boyer
[
16
]
also involves a volume averaged velocity, resulting in a divergence-free velocity field. But, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, neither global nor local energy inequalities have been established for this system either.
Finally, Abels, Garcke & Grün
[
9
]
developed a thermodynamically consistent, incompressible model using the same velocity averaging.
For further diffuse interface models for two-phase flows with unmatched densities, the reader may be referred to
[
27
,
32
]
. In addition, ten Eikelder et al.
[
34
]
presented a unified framework for various known Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard models, along with a comparison of these.

Concerning models that take phase transitions into account, we mention two works.
Aki et al.
[
12
]
proposed a thermodynamically consistent, quasi-incompressible system with mass averaged velocity allowing for mass transfers.
Based on mixture theory, ten Eikelder et al.
[
35
]
derived a multi-phase model with phase transitions which satisfies the second law of thermodynamics, and which contains a law of conservation of linear momentum for each constituent instead of one balance equation for an averaged velocity field. However, their system does not incorporate a Cahn–Hilliard or Allen–Cahn equation.

From an analytical perspective, Model H has been thoroughly investigated in the literature, see
[
15
,
25
,
3
]
to name just a few works.
Likewise, numerous results are available concerning the analysis of the model by Abels, Garcke & Grün, e.g., existence of global weak solutions
[
6
,
7
]
, well-posedness
[
36
,
10
,
21
,
22
]
as well as global regularity and asymptotic stabilization of weak solutions
[
8
]
. We further mention extensions of this model to the case with dynamic boundary conditions
[
19
,
20
,
24
]
, where a transfer of the total mass between bulk and surface is possible.

In contrast, analyzing the model by Lowengrub & Truskinovsky
[
31
]
seems more challenging due to the presence of the pressure in the equation for the chemical potential and a resulting stronger coupling between the Navier–Stokes and the Cahn–Hilliard systems. In
[
2
]
, existence of weak solutions was proven under the condition that the free energy density is regular and sufficiently “steep”, with the exponent of its gradient term exceeding the spatial dimension. Strong well-posedness of this model locally in time was established in
[
4
]
.

An alternative (but physically less justified) way to describe two-phase flows of fluids undergoing phase transitions is to govern the phase field variable by an Allen–Cahn type equation instead of the Cahn–Hilliard equation. A thermodynamically consistent, compressible Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn model was proposed by Blesgen
[
14
]
, with local well-posedness shown in
[
30
]
. For an early analytical study of a two-dimensional incompressible system with matched densities, see
[
18
]
. Moreover, Jiang at al.
[
29
]
derived a thermodynamically consistent model for incompressible flows with different densities, and proved global existence of weak solutions in three space dimensions.
We finally refer to
[
23
]
for results on global well-posedness for a mass-conserving Navier–Stokes/Allen–Cahn system.

The two models of interest.

Given a sufficiently smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\{2,3\}
, with exterior unit normal vector field
\mathbf{n}
on
\partial\Omega
, we write
Q=(0,\infty)\times\Omega
for the space-time cylinder and
S=(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega
for its lateral surface.

Model I:
To generalize the model by Abels, Garcke, and Grün
[
9
]
to the case including phase transitions, we derive the following quasi-incompressible, instationary system of inhomogeneous Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard type:

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}})\\[0.86108pt]
-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})+\nabla\lambda&=&-\varphi\nabla\mu&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}&=&-\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{v})&=&\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\mu&=&F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\mathbf{v}|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
(\mathbf{v},\varphi)|_{t=0}&=&(\mathbf{v}_{0},\varphi_{0})&\text{ in $\Omega$}.\end{array}

Here,
\mathbf{v}
is the
volume averaged
velocity of the mixture, while the phase field variable
\varphi
acts as an order parameter given as difference of the volume fractions of both fluids, such that the pure constituents correspond to
\varphi=\pm 1
. Additionally,
\lambda
denotes the pressure and
\mu
the chemical potential associated to
\varphi
. The homogeneous free energy density
F
is a double-well potential specified below, with minima close to
\pm 1
. Moreover, the viscous stress tensor
\mathbf{S}
, the mass density
\rho
, and the total mass flux
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
relative to the velocity field are determined by the aforementioned variables via the relations

(1.2)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})&=&2\nu(\varphi)\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+\eta(\varphi)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}\mathbf{I},\\[4.30554pt]
\rho(\varphi)&=&b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi,\\[4.30554pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\varphi,\nabla\mu)&=&-b_{-}m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\mu,\end{array}

where
\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}
denotes the symmetrized gradient of
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{I}
the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Eventually,
m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)>0
represents a (non-degenerate) diffusion mobility and
m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)>0
a transition mobility, whereas
\nu(\varphi)>0
as well as
\eta(\varphi)>0
are viscosity coefficients.
For constants related to the specific mass density
\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}>0
of the respective fluid, we use the abbreviations

\displaystyle b_{\pm}=\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{+}\pm\tilde{\rho}_{-}}{2},\qquad c_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\pm\tfrac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}},\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{c_{-}}{c_{+}},\qquad\text{where }\tilde{\rho}_{+}\neq\tilde{\rho}_{-}.

Concerning the boundary conditions in (
1.1
), we prescribe a no-slip boundary condition for
\mathbf{v}
. Furthermore, the homogeneous Neumann boundary condition for
\varphi
enforces a contact angle of
\pi/2
between the diffuse interface and the domain boundary, while the corresponding condition for
\mu
ensures the absence of mass flux over the boundary.

The total energy of the system is given as sum of the kinetic energy and a free energy of Ginzburg–Landau type, i.e.,

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v},\varphi)=E_{\textnormal{kin}}(\mathbf{v})+E_{\textnormal{free}}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}\rho\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)+\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x,

where
F
belongs to a class of singular free energy densities including the well-known logarithmic Flory–Huggins potential. This choice ensures that in the analysis,
\varphi
stays in the physically meaningful interval
[-1,1]
.
For any sufficiently smooth solution
(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)
of (
1.1
), the total energy dissipates over time according to the evolution law

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{v}(t),\varphi(t)\big)=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)|\nabla\mu|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Model II:
On the other hand, we study a quasi-stationary version of the diffuse interface model suggested by Aki, Dreyer, Giesselmann, and Kraus
[
12
]
. This quasi-stationary variant is relevant for scenarios involving small Reynolds numbers. From a modeling point of view, it differs from (
1.1
) in the averaging of the velocity and in the interpretation of the chemical potential, resulting in the following quasi-incompressible, inhomogeneous quasi-stationary Stokes system coupled to the Cahn–Hilliard equation:

(1.3)

\begin{array}[]{rclll}-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u})+\beta\rho\nabla\lambda&=&-\varphi\nabla\omega&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})&=&0&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{u})&=&c_{+}^{2}\big(\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\omega\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\omega\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\omega&=&F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi+\alpha\lambda&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{n})_{\bm{\tau}}&=&-\gamma\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}|_{\partial\Omega}&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\omega|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
\varphi|_{t=0}&=&\varphi_{0}&\text{ in $\Omega$}.\end{array}

In contrast to system (
1.1
),
\mathbf{u}
is defined as the
mass averaged
velocity of the mixture, and we introduce
\omega
as a modified chemical potential related to
\mu
via
\omega=\mu+\alpha\lambda
. We emphasize that the different velocity averaging leads to a stronger coupling, since the pressure enters the equation for the chemical potential. Moreover, the continuity equation is listed in (
1.3
), instead of the divergence equation, which can be reconstructed from the mass balance and the phase field equation. As previously, the relations

(1.4)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u})&=&2\nu(\varphi)\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}+\eta(\varphi)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathbf{I},\\[4.30554pt]
\rho(\varphi)&=&b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi\end{array}

are satisfied, whereas the total mass flux relative to the velocity field does not appear explicitly in this system. The occurring constants associated with the specific mass densities are denoted by

\displaystyle b_{\pm}=\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{+}\pm\tilde{\rho}_{-}}{2},\qquad c_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\pm\tfrac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}},\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{c_{-}}{c_{+}},\qquad\beta=\tfrac{2}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}+\tilde{\rho}_{-}},\qquad\text{where }\tilde{\rho}_{+}\neq\tilde{\rho}_{-}.

Instead of a no-slip boundary condition, we impose a Navier-slip boundary condition here, meaning that only the normal part of the velocity vanishes on the boundary, while the tangential part (denoted by an index
\bm{\tau}
) is related to the tangential part of the normal stresses through a friction parameter
\gamma>0
.

Since we work with a quasi-stationary model neglecting the kinetic energy, the total energy of the system only consists of the free energy

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{free}}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)+\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x,

with a singular homogeneous free energy density
F
as above. Here, the energy dissipation is governed by the law

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\textnormal{free}}\big(\varphi(t)\big)=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}|\nabla\omega|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}\omega^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma|\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s_{x}

for sufficiently smooth solutions
(\mathbf{u},\lambda,\omega,\varphi)
to (
1.3
), where
\mathrm{d}s_{x}
indicates integration with respect to the surface measure on
\partial\Omega
. Of course, acceleration terms could also be considered in (
1.3
), leading to a Navier–Stokes type momentum equation and to an additional kinetic energy term in the energy dissipation, see
[
12
]
.

Main results.

Our first central contribution is the derivation of the thermodynamically consistent diffuse interface model (
1.1
) for two-phase flows with phase transition. It describes the dynamics of an isothermal mixture of viscous, incompressible Newtonian fluids with unmatched densities that may exchange mass.

Analytically, our main results are the following two theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{0}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
and
\varphi_{0}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
with
|\varphi_{0}|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
and
\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle\in(-1,1)
. Under the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A3

) below, there exists a weak solution
(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)
of (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
4.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\varphi_{0}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
with
|\varphi_{0}|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
and
\langle\varphi_{0}\rangle\in(-1,1)
. Under the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A4

) below, there exists a weak solution
(\mathbf{u},\lambda,\omega,\varphi)
of (
1.3
) in the sense of Definition
6.1
.

Here,
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the spatial mean of a function, and we refer to Subsection
2.1
for further detailed notation.

The proof of both theorems is based on an implicit time discretization, where solutions to the time-discrete system are established by a Leray–Schauder fixed-point argument, following the strategy of
[
6
]
. Passing to the limit in the corresponding time-continuous system primarily relies on
a priori
estimates provided by an energy dissipation inequality. Regarding the non-linearities, proving an additional strong convergence of both the velocity and the phase field is required in the case of the instationary model (
1.1
), whereas in the quasi-stationary model (
1.3
), only the phase field needs to converge strongly. Handling the pressure
\lambda
, or specifically its mean-free part
\lambda_{0}
, is more intricate in (
1.3
). In order to obtain an
a priori
estimate for
\lambda_{0}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, we introduce a damping term within the time-discrete system, similar to the approach in
[
2
]
. On the time-continuous level, we employ the momentum equation to bound
\lambda_{0}
in a suitable space
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))
, where the Naver-slip boundary condition for the velocity comes into play. Finally, the limit passage in
F^{\prime}(\varphi)
differs as well: while we apply results for subgradients related to the free energy in (
1.1
), a more technical, measure theoretic argument is used in (
1.3
).

Outline.

This work is structured as follows. We start in Section
2
by introducing some notation, fixing general assumptions and collecting preliminary results of importance for our analysis. Section
3
is dedicated to the derivation of the model (
1.1
) for two-phase flows with phase transition using a volume averaged velocity. Existence of weak solutions to this system is established in Section
4
. This is followed by Section
5
, where we reformulate the model with mass averaged velocity proposed by Aki et al.
[
12
]
. Finally, in Section

, we prove existence of weak solutions to the quasi-stationary variant of the latter, which coincides with system (

).

2.2.
General Assumptions.

We make the following assumptions for the remainder of this section and for the analysis sections of this paper, which are Sections

.

(\mathbf{A1})

The set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, is a bounded domain with
\mathrm{C}^{2}

\mathbf{n}
on
\partial\Omega

(\mathbf{A2})

The potential

is of regularity

\displaystyle\lim_{s\to-1}F^{\prime}(s)=-\infty,\quad\lim_{s\to 1}F^{\prime}(s)=\infty,\quad F^{\prime\prime}(s)\geq-\kappa\text{ for some }\kappa\in\mathbb{R}.

We denote the convex part of

F_{0}(s)\coloneqq F(s)+\frac{\kappa}{2}s^{2}

(\mathbf{A3})

For the mobility and viscosity coefficients

, there exist constants

\displaystyle K_{*}\leq m_{\textnormal{j}}(s),m_{\textnormal{r}}(s),\nu(s),\eta(s)\leq K^{*}\quad\text{ for all }s\in\mathbb{R}.

(\mathbf{A4})

The friction parameter
\gamma
is positive.

Remark 2.1
.

With regard to these assumptions, we point out the following.

(i)

A homogeneous free energy density
F
satisfying (

A2

) is for instance given by the continuous extension to the interval
[-1,1]
of the logarithmic potential

\displaystyle F(s):=\frac{\theta}{2}\big((1+s)\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\ln(1-s)\big)+\frac{\theta_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}\big(1-s^{2}\big)

for all
s\in(-1,1)
with some constants
0<\theta<\theta_{\mathrm{c}}
.

(ii)

By an approximation argument it can be verified that all our results remain valid in the case
m_{\textnormal{j}}\in\mathrm{C}^{0}(\mathbb{R})
.

Neumann–Laplace equation.

Following the outline in
[
2
]
, Section 4, we provide an overview of the Neumann–Laplace operator.
To begin with, we note that
\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)
, endowed with the inner product

\displaystyle(u,v)_{\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)}\coloneqq(\nabla u,\nabla v)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

is a Hilbert space due to Poincaré’s inequality, with its dual denoted by
\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
. Next, we define the weak divergence operator
\mathrm{div}_{\mathbf{n}}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\to\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{div}_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{u},\phi\rangle_{\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega),\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)}=-(\mathbf{u},\nabla\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all }\phi\in\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega).

Accordingly, the weak Neumann–Laplace operator
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}\coloneqq\mathrm{div}_{\mathbf{n}}\nabla\colon\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
is introduced, given by

\displaystyle\langle\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}u,\phi\rangle_{\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega),\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)}=-(\nabla u,\nabla\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all }\phi\in\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega).

In view of the Lax–Milgram theorem, this operator is an isomorphism, and for
f\in\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)
and
u\in\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)
solving
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}u=f
, it follows

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\|f\|_{\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{-1}(\Omega)}.

Furthermore, if
u\in\mathrm{H}_{(0)}^{1}(\Omega)
satisfies the equation
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}u=f
for some
f\in\mathrm{L}^{p}_{(0)}(\Omega)
,
p\in(1,\infty)
, standard elliptic regularity theory implies that
u
even belongs to
\mathrm{W}^{2,p}(\Omega)
, and that it holds
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}u=f
a.e. in
\Omega
as well as
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u|_{\partial\Omega}=0
in the sense of traces. In this case, there additionally exists a constant
C_{p}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{2,p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)}.

In particular,
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}\colon\mathrm{W}^{2,p}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
is a bijective operator, where the notation from (
2.1
) is used.

Eventually, the following proposition based on a duality argument allows us to derive a concept of
very weak solutions
to the Neumann–Laplace equation.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. For every
f\in\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}
, there is a unique
u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
satisfying

\displaystyle(u,\Delta\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle f,\phi\rangle_{\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all }\phi\in\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega),

which we refer to as
very weak solution
to the Neumann–Laplace equation. Moreover, there exists a constant
C_{p}>0
such that

(2.10)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{p}\|f\|_{\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}}.

Proof.

We recall that the operator
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}\colon\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega)
is bijective, and so is its adjoint
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}^{\prime}\colon\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)\to\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}
. Consequently, for every
f\in\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}
, there exists a unique
u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
solving the equation
\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}^{\prime}u=f
. By the definition of the adjoint, it follows

\displaystyle(u,\Delta\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=(u,\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}^{\prime}u,\phi\rangle_{\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)}=\langle f,\phi\rangle_{\big(\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)}

for all
\phi\in\mathrm{W}^{2,p^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)
. Finally, the continuity of
(\Delta_{\textnormal{N}}^{\prime})^{-1}
implies estimate (
2.10
).
∎

3.
Derivation of a Model with Volume Averaged Velocity

In this section, we derive a diffuse interface model for two-phase flows with phase transitions in the framework of continuum fluid dynamics, which is formulated for incompressible, isothermal constituents with unmatched densities. Using a volume averaged velocity, we establish a generalization of the model by Abels, Garcke, and Grün
[
9
]
to a quasi-incompressible system that explicitly accounts for mass exchanges between the components. The thermodynamic consistency of the resulting model is ensured by selecting constitutive assumptions in such a way that an energy dissipation inequality is satisfied.

Balance equations.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, denote an open, bounded set representing the spatial region occupied by a binary fluid mixture whose flow is studied over the time interval
[0,\infty)
.
The indices
\pm
are used to distinguish quantities associated with each component of the mixture, where the constituents may describe either two distinct substances or two phases of a single material. For these macroscopically immiscible fluids, we assume a partial miscibility in a thin interfacial region.
It is sufficient to describe the thermodynamic state of the mixture by the following three quantities. The constituents are characterized by their respective mass density
\rho_{\pm}\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and velocity
\mathbf{v}_{\pm}\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and, considering an isothermal mixture, by a common temperature
\theta\colon[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
which is assumed to be a given constant here.

Each unmixed fluid is assigned a specific constant mass density
\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}>0
, where
\tilde{\rho}_{+}\neq\tilde{\rho}_{-}
is required. For the sum of the two volume fractions
\frac{\rho_{\pm}}{\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}}
, we assume vanishing excess volume, expressed as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{+}}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}+\frac{\rho_{-}}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}}=1,

which indicates that the fluids behave like a simple mixture, cf.
[
31
]
.

Since mass transfer between the components by phase transition is taken into account, the
partial mass balance equation
for each constituent reads

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{\pm}+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\pm}\mathbf{v}_{\pm})=r_{\pm},

with
r_{\pm}
describing the mass production rate of the respective constituent. While individual masses of the components are not conserved, we require total mass conservation for the mixture in the sense that

(3.3)

\displaystyle r_{+}+r_{-}=0,

where the total mass density
\rho\coloneqq\rho_{+}+\rho_{-}
of the mixture is given as sum of the partial mass densities
\rho_{\pm}
. Notably, we work here with a
volume averaged
velocity

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{+}}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\mathbf{v}_{+}+\frac{\rho_{-}}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}}\mathbf{v}_{-}.

Moreover, for each constituent, we consider a diffusion velocity
\mathbf{w}_{\pm}\coloneqq\mathbf{v}_{\pm}-\mathbf{v}
as well as a diffusion flux
\mathbf{J}_{\pm}\coloneqq\rho_{\pm}\mathbf{w}_{\pm}
. Summing the two partial mass balance equations (
3.2
) corresponding to the indices
\pm
and using (
3.3
) reveals the
total mass balance equation

(3.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}+\tilde{\mathbf{J}})=0,

where
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\coloneqq\mathbf{J}_{+}+\mathbf{J}_{-}
represents the total mass flux relative to the velocity field.
Next, we define the phase field variable
\varphi\coloneqq\frac{\rho_{+}}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}-\frac{\rho_{-}}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}}
as difference of the volume fractions, along with the corresponding phase field flux
\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\mathbf{J}_{+}-\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}}\mathbf{J}_{-}
. In view of (
3.1
), these definitions yield the converse relations
\rho=b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi
and
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}=b_{-}\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}
, where
b_{\pm}\coloneqq\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{+}\pm\tilde{\rho}_{-}}{2}
. Additionally, we set
c_{\pm}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\pm\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}}
for brevity.

Dividing the individual mass balance equation (
3.2
) for
\pm
by
\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}
, respectively, their difference, along with (
3.3
), gives the
phase field equation

(3.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{J}_{\varphi})=c_{+}r_{+}\eqqcolon r_{\varphi}

with the phase field production rate
r_{\varphi}
. Similarly, after dividing the partial mass balance equation (
3.2
) for
\pm
by
\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}
and summing now, we incorporate (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) to obtain the
divergence equation

(3.7)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=c_{-}r_{+}=\alpha r_{\varphi},

where we abbreviate
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{c_{-}}{c_{+}}
.

Finally, introducing a stress tensor
\tilde{\mathbf{T}}
, we assume conservation of linear momentum for the total mixture, expressed as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}-\tilde{\mathbf{T}}\big)=0.

Here, external forces are neglected.
With an objective stress tensor
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\coloneqq\tilde{\mathbf{T}}+\mathbf{v}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}}
, this can be rewritten as the
(linear) momentum balance equation

(3.8)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\big)+\nabla\cdot\big(\mathbf{v}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\big)=\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{S}}.

Energy dissipation.

In our system, we require the total energy density to be of the form
e(\mathbf{v},\varphi,\nabla\varphi)=f(\varphi,\nabla\varphi)+\rho\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}
consisting of a free energy density
f
and the kinetic energy
\rho\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}
. Along with this quantity, we consider some general energy flux
\mathbf{J}_{e}
which will be specified later. For every volume
V(t)
in
\Omega
transported with the flow with velocity
\mathbf{v}
over time
t
, the total energy is supposed to dissipate such that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{V(t)}e(\mathbf{v},\varphi,\nabla\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial V(t)}\mathbf{J}_{e}\cdot\bm{\nu}\;\mathrm{d}s_{x}\leq 0,

where
\bm{\nu}
is the outer unit normal field on
\partial V(t)
.
In view of the divergence theorem and the Reynolds transport theorem we obtain the corresponding
energy dissipation inequality
in a local form

(3.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}e+\nabla\cdot(e\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_{e}\leq 0

since
V(t)
is arbitrary. In the remainder of this section, let
\dot{\phi}\coloneqq\partial_{t}\phi+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\phi
denote the material time derivative of a function
\phi
with respect to
\mathbf{v}
, and
g_{,\phi}\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}g
the partial derivative of a function
g=g(\phi)
with respect to
\phi
. Using the formula

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{j}}\dot{\varphi}=\partial_{t}\partial_{x_{j}}\varphi+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\partial_{x_{j}}\varphi+\nabla\varphi\cdot\partial_{x_{j}}\mathbf{v}=(\partial_{x_{j}}\varphi)^{\bm{\cdot}}+\nabla\varphi\cdot\partial_{x_{j}}\mathbf{v}

we compute for the free energy density

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f+\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}f+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla f+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=f_{,\varphi}\big(\partial_{t}\varphi+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\varphi\big)+f_{,\nabla\varphi}\cdot\big(\partial_{t}\nabla\varphi+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\nabla\varphi\big)+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=f_{,\varphi}\dot{\varphi}+f_{,\nabla\varphi}\cdot(\nabla\varphi)^{\bm{\cdot}}+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=f_{,\varphi}\dot{\varphi}+f_{,\nabla\varphi}\cdot(\nabla\dot{\varphi})-\big(\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big):\nabla\mathbf{v}+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\big(f_{,\varphi}-\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big)\dot{\varphi}+\nabla\cdot\big(f_{,\nabla\varphi}\dot{\varphi}\big)-\big(\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-f\mathbf{I}\big):\nabla\mathbf{v}.

Invoking the total mass balance equation (
3.5
) and the momentum equation (
3.8
) the kinetic energy density term results in

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bigg(\rho\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\bigg)+\nabla\cdot\bigg(\rho\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\mathbf{v}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\big(\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v})\big)\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}+\rho\big(\partial_{t}\mathbf{v}+(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\big)\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}+\big(\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{S}}-(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\big)\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\bigg(-\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{v}\bigg)-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}:\nabla\mathbf{v}.

The phase field equation (
3.6
) and the divergence equation (
3.7
) provide a framework for introducing Lagrange multipliers into the dissipation inequality (
3.9
). Specifically, for any given pair of scalar functions
\mu,\lambda
, where later
\mu
will describe the chemical potential and
\lambda
a certain pressure, we have the inequality

\displaystyle-\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\partial_{t}e+\nabla\cdot(e\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_{e}-\mu\big(\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{J}_{\varphi})-r_{\varphi}\big)-\lambda\big(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}-\alpha r_{\varphi}\big)\leq 0.

In view of the above computations for the free energy density and the kinetic energy density, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle-\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\big(f_{,\varphi}-\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big)\dot{\varphi}+\nabla\cdot\big(f_{,\nabla\varphi}\dot{\varphi}\big)-\big(\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-f\mathbf{I}\big):\nabla\mathbf{v}+\nabla\cdot\bigg(-\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{v}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}:\nabla\mathbf{v}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_{e}-\mu\big(\dot{\varphi}+\varphi\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}-r_{\varphi}\big)-\lambda\big(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}-\alpha r_{\varphi}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(f_{,\varphi}-\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\mu\big)\dot{\varphi}+\nabla\cdot\bigg(f_{,\nabla\varphi}\dot{\varphi}-\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{J}_{e}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}\bigg)

(3.10)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(-\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\big(f-\mu\varphi-\lambda\big)\mathbf{I}\Big):\nabla\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\mu+r_{\varphi}\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\leq 0.

Assuming that the terms
f_{,\nabla\varphi}\dot{\varphi}-\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}+\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{J}_{e}-\mu\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}
and
-\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\big(f-\mu\varphi-\lambda\big)\mathbf{I}
as well as
\nabla\mu
and
\mu+\alpha\lambda
do not depend on
\dot{\varphi}
, and regarding that
\dot{\varphi}
may attain arbitrary values, we conclude that the term
f_{,\varphi}-\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\mu
needs to vanish. This yields

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mu=f_{,\varphi}-\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}

as equation for the chemical potential
\mu
. Furthermore, we specify the energy flux
\mathbf{J}_{e}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{e}=-f_{,\nabla\varphi}\dot{\varphi}+\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}\tilde{\mathbf{J}}-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{v}+\mu\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}

such that the divergence term in inequality (
3
) vanishes. Now, denoting the symmetric part of
\nabla\mathbf{v}
by
\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\big(\nabla\mathbf{v}+\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\big)
, the additional assumption that
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}
is symmetric implies

\displaystyle\big(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+\big(\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big):\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-f_{,\nabla\varphi}\otimes\nabla\varphi\big):\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\nabla\mathbf{v}-\nabla\mathbf{v}^{\mathrm{T}}\big).

With the skew part of
\nabla\mathbf{v}
attaining arbitrary values independent of
\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}
, it follows that the term
\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-f_{,\nabla\varphi}\otimes\nabla\varphi
vanishes. Altogether, inequality (
3
) simplifies to

(3.12)

\displaystyle-\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle=\Big(-\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\big(f-\mu\varphi-\lambda\big)\mathbf{I}\Big):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\mu+r_{\varphi}\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\leq 0.

This reveals that the mechanisms leading to energy dissipation are viscosity, diffusion and phase transition.

Constitutive assumptions.

For the dissipation inequality (
3.12
) to hold true, we propose the following
constitutive assumptions
:

(1)

The stress
\tilde{\mathbf{S}}
satisfies the identity

\displaystyle-\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\tilde{\mathbf{S}}+\big(f-\mu\varphi-\lambda\big)\mathbf{I}=-2\nu(\varphi)\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}-\eta(\varphi)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}\mathbf{I}\eqqcolon-\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})

for some viscosity coefficients
\nu(\varphi),\eta(\varphi)\geq 0
. In particular, this means that the mixture is Newtonian.

(2)

For the phase field flux
\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}
, motivated by a generalized Fick’s law, a diffusion law

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}=-m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\mu

holds, with a diffusion mobility
m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\geq 0
.

(3)

The phase field production rate
r_{\varphi}
fulfills the phase transition law

\displaystyle r_{\varphi}=-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)

for some transition mobility
m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\geq 0
.

The model.

Eventually, we insert the constitutive assumptions into our balance equations. For the momentum equation (
3.8
), we compute, invoking the equation for the chemical potential (
3.11
),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot\big(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\big)+\nabla\cdot\big(\mathbf{v}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\big)=\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{S}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\Big(\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})-\nabla\varphi\otimes f_{,\nabla\varphi}+\big(f-\mu\varphi-\lambda\big)\mathbf{I}\Big)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})-\big(\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}\big)\nabla\varphi-\mathrm{D}^{2}\varphi f_{,\nabla\varphi}+f_{,\varphi}\nabla\varphi+\mathrm{D}^{2}\varphi f_{,\nabla\varphi}-\varphi\nabla\mu-\mu\nabla\varphi-\nabla\lambda

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})-\varphi\nabla\mu-\nabla\lambda.

The divergence equation (
3.7
) takes the form

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}=\alpha r_{\varphi}=-\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big),

whereas the phase field equation (
3.6
) results in

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{v})=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\varphi}+r_{\varphi}=\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big).

Altogether, writing
Q=(0,\infty)\times\Omega
for the space-time cylinder, we derived the quasi-incompressible Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard model

(3.13)

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{v}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}})\\[0.86108pt]
-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})+\nabla\lambda&=&-\varphi\nabla\mu&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}&=&-\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{v})&=&\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\mu\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\mu&=&f_{,\varphi}-\nabla\cdot f_{,\nabla\varphi}&\text{ in $Q$},\end{array}

where the relations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v})&=&2\nu(\varphi)\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+\eta(\varphi)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}\mathbf{I},\\[4.30554pt]
\rho(\varphi)&=&b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi,\\[4.30554pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}(\varphi,\nabla\mu)&=&-b_{-}m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\mu\end{array}

are satisfied. We recall that the occurring constants are given by

\displaystyle b_{\pm}=\tfrac{\tilde{\rho}_{+}\pm\tilde{\rho}_{-}}{2},\qquad c_{\pm}=\tfrac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\pm\tfrac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}},\qquad\alpha=\tfrac{c_{-}}{c_{+}},\qquad\text{where }\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}>0,\quad\tilde{\rho}_{+}\neq\tilde{\rho}_{-},

and that the viscosity and mobility coefficients
\nu(\varphi),\eta(\varphi),m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi),m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\geq 0
are nonnegative.

With
S=(0,\infty)\times\partial\Omega
denoting the lateral surface of the space-time cylinder, we add boundary conditions to system (
3.13
), namely

(3.14)

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\mathbf{v}|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\end{array}

where
\mathbf{n}
is the outer unit normal vector field on
\partial\Omega
.
Concerning
\mathbf{v}
, this is the no-slip boundary condition for viscous fluids. Moreover, the identity
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
prescribes a contact angle of
\pi/2
between the diffuse interface and the boundary of the domain, while we ensure by
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu|_{\partial\Omega}=0
that there is no mass flux across the boundary.

Finally, before stating the formal energy balance of the system, let us recall that the total energy density is given by
e(\mathbf{v},\varphi,\nabla\varphi)=f(\varphi,\nabla\varphi)+\rho\frac{|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}
.
Multiplying the first equation of (
3.13
) by
\mathbf{v}
, the second by
\lambda
, the third by
\mu
, and the fourth by
-\partial_{t}\varphi
, then integrating over
\Omega
and summing, reveals in view of the boundary conditions (
3.14
) that every sufficiently smooth solution to the model satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}e\big(\mathbf{v}(t),\varphi(t),\nabla\varphi(t)\big)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)|\nabla\mu|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

For details, we refer to the proof of Lemma
4.7
, where the energy inequality for the corresponding time-discrete system is shown.

\displaystyle 0

we conclude

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}-\rho_{k}|\mathbf{v}_{k}|^{2}}{2h}+\rho_{k}\frac{|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{k}|^{2}}{2h}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})|\nabla\mu|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\big(F_{0}(\varphi)-F_{0}(\varphi_{k})\big)-\kappa\frac{\varphi^{2}-\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2h}+\frac{1}{h}\bigg(\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}-\frac{|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}}{2}\bigg)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Recalling the definition of
E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v},\varphi)
we have thus established the time-discrete energy inequality (
4.7
) as claimed.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
and
\varphi_{k}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
be given, and set
\rho_{k}\coloneqq b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi_{k}
. Then, there exists a solution
(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{D}(\partial E)
to the time-discrete system (
4.3a
)–(
4.3d
).

Proof.

The central concept of this proof hinges on the application of the Leray–Schauder principle. For the preliminary setup, we work with two operators
\mathcal{L}_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k}\colon X\to Y
where

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{D}(\partial E),

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

These are constructed such that a quadruple
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)\in X
solves system (
4.3a
)–(
4.3d
) if and only if the identity

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})

is satisfied for suitable operators
\mathcal{L}_{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. After first introducing an operator

\displaystyle\langle L_{k}(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu),\bm{\psi}\rangle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\bm{\psi}-\lambda\nabla\cdot\bm{\psi}+\varphi_{k}\nabla\mu\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for all }\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d},

we define, for
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)\in X
, the aforementioned operators
\mathcal{L}_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k}\colon X\to Y
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}L_{k}(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu)\\[4.30554pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}+\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\\[4.30554pt]
\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v})-\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\mu\big)+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)+\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y\\[4.30554pt]
\varphi+\partial E(\varphi)\end{pmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\rho\mathbf{v}-\rho_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}}{h}-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})-\big(\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}}-\frac{\rho-\rho_{k}}{h}-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{v})\big)\frac{\mathbf{v}}{2}-\big(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla\big)\mathbf{v}\\[4.30554pt]
0\\[4.30554pt]
-\frac{\varphi-\varphi_{k}}{h}+\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y\\[4.30554pt]
\varphi+\mu+\kappa\frac{\varphi+\varphi_{k}}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Our primary goal now is to show that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}\colon X\to Y
is invertible. To this end, we initially focus on the first three lines of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
to define the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{k}
on the space
\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\big((\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu),(\bm{\psi},\xi,\zeta)\big)\coloneqq\langle L_{k}(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu),\bm{\psi}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\xi\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}+\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\xi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\zeta\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v})+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\zeta+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\zeta+\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y\,\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x.

We observe that
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is both bounded and coercive as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\big((\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu),(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu)\big)=\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}\;\mathrm{d}x+\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\lambda\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})|\nabla\mu|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)\mu\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq C\bigg(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}x\bigg)^{2}+\|\nabla\mu\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mu+\alpha\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq C\Big(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mu+\alpha\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq C\Big(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)

with a constant
C>0
due to Korn’s and Poincaré’s inequalities and the positive lower bound on the coefficients
\nu
,
\eta
,
m_{\textnormal{j}}
and
m_{\textnormal{r}}
. Thus, the Lax–Milgram theorem ensures that for any right-hand side
(\mathbf{g}_{1},g_{2},g_{3})\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\big(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}
, there exists a unique triple
(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
fulfilling

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\big((\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu),(\bm{\psi},\xi,\zeta)\big)=\langle\mathbf{g}_{1},\bm{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}+\langle g_{2},\xi\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\langle g_{3},\zeta\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}

for all
(\bm{\psi},\xi,\zeta)\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
. In particular, for given
g_{3}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, we see that
\mu\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
is a weak solution to the elliptic equation

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\mu\big)+\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\mu

\displaystyle=g_{3}-\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v})-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\alpha\lambda

with a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition. In order to prove that
\mu
is even of regularity
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)
, we note that
\mu
additionally solves, in a weak sense, the equation

\displaystyle\Delta\mu=-\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\big)^{\!-1}\Big(\nabla\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\big)\cdot\nabla\mu+\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\mu-g_{3}-\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v})-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\alpha\lambda\Big)

with the same boundary condition as above. Since it holds
\varphi_{k}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
with
|\varphi_{k}|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
, we deduce by the chain rule that
\nabla(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k}))\in\mathrm{L}^{6}(\Omega)
, and therefore, we conclude
\nabla(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k}))\cdot\nabla\mu\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)
. With the remaining terms on the right-hand side belonging to
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, it follows that for some function
g\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)
, the equation takes the form

\displaystyle\Delta\mu=g\in\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega).

Hence, elliptic regularity theory yields
\mu\in\mathrm{W}^{2,\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)
, and in particular
\nabla\mu\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)
which embeds into
\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega)
. This implies that the product
\nabla(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k}))\cdot\nabla\mu
is even in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and thus leads to

\displaystyle\Delta\mu=g\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega).

We consequently obtain the regularity
\mu\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
along with the estimate

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|g\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}\big(\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|g_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big).

Examining the fourth line in
\mathcal{L}_{k}
in a next step,
\partial E
being a maximal monotone operator reveals that

\displaystyle I+\partial E\colon\mathcal{D}(\partial E)\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)

is invertible. The inverse operator, considered as a mapping

\displaystyle(I+\partial E)^{-1}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{2-s}(\Omega),\qquad s\in\big(0,\tfrac{1}{4}\big),

is continuous (and even compact) which can be verified using (
2.7
) and (
2.8
). For details, we refer to the proof of Lemma 4.3 in
[
6
]
.

Altogether, we have now shown that
\mathcal{L}_{k}\colon X\to Y
is invertible with an inverse operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\colon Y\to X
. Our next objective is to identify Banach spaces
\tilde{X}
,
\tilde{Y}
such that we have a continuous embedding
X\hookrightarrow\tilde{X}
and a compact embedding
\tilde{Y}\hookrightarrow Y
. This is ensured by introducing

\displaystyle\tilde{X}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2-s}(\Omega),\qquad s\in\big(0,\tfrac{1}{4}\big),

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{W}^{1,\frac{3}{2}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega).

Eventually,
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\colon\tilde{Y}\to\tilde{X}
is a compact operator.

We proceed by showing that
\mathcal{F}_{k}\colon\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
is continuous and maps bounded sets into bounded sets. To this end, we analyze each individual term in
\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})
for
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)\in\tilde{X}
similarly to
[
6
]
and achieve the following bounds:

(i)

\|\rho\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq C\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}\big(\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+1\big)
.

We have the embedding
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{6}
, and
\rho
depends affine linearly on
\varphi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}
.

(ii)

\|\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq C_{k}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}^{2}
.

Since
\rho_{k}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}
, the occurring terms of the form
\rho_{k}\partial_{l}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j}
are a product of functions in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
,
\mathrm{L}^{2}
and
\mathrm{L}^{6}
whereas the products of the type
\partial_{l}\rho_{k}\mathbf{v}_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j}
consist of three functions in
\mathrm{L}^{6}
.

(iii)

\|\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq C\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}}
.

In view of
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}=-\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{+}-\tilde{\rho}_{-}}{2}m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\mu
, the resulting expressions include terms such as
m_{\textnormal{j}}^{\prime}(\varphi_{k})\partial_{i}\varphi_{k}\partial_{j}\mu\mathbf{v}_{l}
which involve multiplied functions in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
,
\mathrm{L}^{6}
,
\mathrm{L}^{6}
and
\mathrm{L}^{6}
, as well as products
m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\mu\mathbf{v}_{l}
of functions in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
,
\mathrm{L}^{2}
and
\mathrm{L}^{6}
.

(iv)

\|\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq C_{k}\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}^{2}
.

On the one hand, arising terms of the type
\partial_{i}\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v}_{j}\mathbf{v}_{l}
are products of functions in
\mathrm{H}^{1-s}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{3}
,
\mathrm{L}^{6}
and
\mathrm{L}^{6}
. On the other hand, terms of the form
\varphi_{k}\partial_{i}\mathbf{v}_{j}\mathbf{v}_{l}
contain a product of functions in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
,
\mathrm{L}^{2}
and
\mathrm{L}^{6}
.

(v)

\|(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}}}\leq C\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}}\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}}
.

This expression consists of products
m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\partial_{i}\mu\partial_{j}\mathbf{v}_{l}
with factors in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
,
\mathrm{L}^{6}
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}
.

(vi)

The term
-\frac{\varphi-\varphi_{k}}{h}+\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y
in the third line of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
is obviously bounded in
\mathrm{W}^{1,\frac{3}{2}}
.

(vii)

It is also clear that the fourth line term
\varphi+\mu+\kappa\frac{\varphi+\varphi_{k}}{2}
is bounded in
\mathrm{H}^{1}
.

Now, we aim to apply the Leray–Schauder principle (see Theorem 6.A in
[
37
]
, for instance) on the Banach space
\tilde{Y}
. Finding a solution
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)\in X
to the time-discrete system (
4.3a
)–(
4.3d
), i.e., a solution of
\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})
, is equivalent to solving the equation

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f}),\qquad\text{where }\mathcal{K}_{k}\coloneqq\mathcal{F}_{k}\circ\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\text{ and }\mathbf{f}=\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z}).

Thus, our goal is to establish the existence of a fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{k}
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\colon\tilde{Y}\to\tilde{X}
is compact and
\mathcal{F}_{k}\colon\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
is continuous,
\mathcal{K}_{k}\colon\tilde{Y}\to\tilde{Y}
is a compact operator. In order to obtain a fixed point of
\mathcal{K}_{k}
, the Leray–Schauder principle additionally requires to show a further condition: there exists a bound
R>0
such that the following holds:

(4.7)

\displaystyle\text{If }\mathbf{f}\in\tilde{Y}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leq\vartheta\leq 1\text{ fulfills }\mathbf{f}=\vartheta\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f}),\quad\text{then }\|\mathbf{f}\|_{\tilde{Y}}\leq R.

So let
\mathbf{f}\in\tilde{Y}
and
0\leq\vartheta\leq 1
with
\mathbf{f}=\vartheta\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f})
be given. Defining
\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}(\mathbf{f})
this is equivalent to the equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\vartheta\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z}),

which corresponds to the following weak formulation:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\vartheta\frac{\rho\mathbf{v}-\rho_{k}\mathbf{v}_{k}}{h}\cdot\bm{\psi}+\vartheta\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v})\cdot\bm{\psi}+\vartheta\Big(\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}}-\frac{\rho-\rho_{k}}{h}-\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{v})\Big)\frac{\mathbf{v}}{2}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.8a)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\vartheta\big(\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\nabla\big)\mathbf{v}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x+\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\nabla\bm{\psi}-\lambda\nabla\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{k}\nabla\mu\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x

for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
, and

(4.8b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=-\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big),

(4.8c)

\displaystyle\vartheta\frac{\varphi-\varphi_{k}}{h}+\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{v})-\vartheta\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\mu\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)-\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y,

(4.8d)

\displaystyle\vartheta\varphi+\vartheta\mu+\vartheta\kappa\frac{\varphi+\varphi_{k}}{2}

\displaystyle=\varphi+F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi,

where the last three equations hold a.e. in
\Omega
. Proceeding analogously to the proof of Lemma
4.7
, we now test (
4.8a
) with
\mathbf{v}
, (
4.8b
) with
\lambda
, (
4.8c
) with
\mu
and (
4.8d
) with
\frac{\varphi-\varphi_{k}}{h}
. As in the derivation there, summing the resulting equations and using the convexity of
F_{0}
reveals

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\Omega}\vartheta\frac{1}{h}\bigg(\frac{\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}}{2}-\frac{\rho_{k}|\mathbf{v}_{k}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\rho_{k}|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{k}|^{2}}{2}\bigg)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})|\nabla\mu|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{h}\bigg(\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}-\frac{|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}}{2}\bigg)+\frac{1}{h}\big(F_{0}(\varphi)-F_{0}(\varphi_{k})\big)-\vartheta\kappa\frac{\varphi^{2}-\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2h}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}(1-\vartheta)\frac{1}{h}\bigg(\frac{\varphi^{2}}{2}-\frac{\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2}+\frac{(\varphi-\varphi_{k})^{2}}{2}\bigg)\;\mathrm{d}x+(1-\vartheta)\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{2}.

Given that
\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}(\mathbf{f})\in X
, it follows that
\varphi
belongs to the space
\mathcal{D}(\partial E)
, and in particular that
|\varphi|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
, which implies
\rho\geq 0
. Furthermore, after taking into account the nonnegativity of the terms
\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}
,
\rho_{k}|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{k}|^{2}
,
\varphi^{2}
,
(\varphi-\varphi_{k})^{2}
and
|\nabla\varphi-\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}
, we employ
\vartheta,(1-\vartheta)\leq 1
, the relation
F_{0}(\varphi)-\kappa\frac{\varphi^{2}}{2}=F(\varphi)
as well as the estimate
-\vartheta\kappa\varphi_{k}^{2}\leq|\kappa|\varphi_{k}^{2}
. Since
|\varphi|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
ensures that the term
\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}x
is bounded, we may move it to the right-hand side. In summary, this leads to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+hm_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})|\nabla\mu|^{2}+hm_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+(1-\vartheta)h\bigg(\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rho_{k}|\mathbf{v}_{k}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2}+\frac{|\nabla\varphi_{k}|^{2}}{2}+F_{0}(\varphi_{k})+|\kappa|\frac{\varphi_{k}^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\Big|\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq C_{k}.

In view of
|\varphi|\leq 1
a.e. in
\Omega
, Korn’s inequality for
\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{d}
, and the positive lower bound on
\nu,m_{\textnormal{j}},m_{\textnormal{r}}
, we arrive at

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\mu\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mu+\alpha\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+(1-\vartheta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big|\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y\Big|\leq C_{k}.

In a subsequent step, we control the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-Norm of
\mu
by considering two distinct cases. On the one hand, for
\vartheta\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
, we proceed as in the proof of Lemma
4.6
, with (
4.3d
) replaced by (
4.8d
), to get an estimate

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\Big|\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq\vartheta\Big|\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\leq C\big(\|\nabla\mu\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla\varphi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+1\big)\leq C_{k},

where the right-hand side is bounded due to (
4.9
). On the other hand, if
\vartheta\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, a direct use of (
4.9
) implies
|\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}x|\leq C_{k}
.
The control over both
|\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}x|
and
\|\nabla\mu\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
enables us to bound
\mu
in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and therefore, given that
\|\mu+\alpha\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}
, also
\lambda
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. Incorporating estimate (
4.6
), where now
g_{3}=-\vartheta\frac{\varphi-\varphi_{k}}{h}+\vartheta\int_{\Omega}\mu\;\mathrm{d}y
, refines (
4.9
) to

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\lambda\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mu\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}.

In combination with equation (
4.8d
), this also entails that
\|\partial E(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}\leq C_{k}
, from where estimate (
2.8
) further yields the bound
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}
. Altogether, we have now shown that
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)\in\tilde{X}=\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2-s}(\Omega)
, where
s\in(0,\frac{1}{4})
is arbitrary, satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\tilde{X}}\leq C_{k}.

Eventually, since
\mathbf{f}\in\tilde{Y}
fulfills
\mathbf{f}=\vartheta\mathcal{K}_{k}(\mathbf{f})=\vartheta\mathcal{F}_{k}\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}(\mathbf{f})=\vartheta\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})
and
\mathcal{F}_{k}\colon\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
maps bounded sets into bounded sets, we conclude

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{f}\|_{\tilde{Y}}=\|\vartheta\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})\|_{\tilde{Y}}\leq C_{k}\big(\|\mathbf{z}\|_{\tilde{X}}+1\big)\leq C_{k},

which verifies the Leray–Schauder condition as required.

Hence, the operator
\mathcal{K}_{k}
has a fixed point
\mathbf{f}\in\tilde{Y}\hookrightarrow Y
, and
\mathbf{z}=\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}(\mathbf{f})\in X
solves the equation
\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have thus established the existence of a solution
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi)=(\mathbf{v}_{k+1},\lambda_{k+1},\mu_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1})\in X
to the time-discrete system (
4.3a
)–(
4.3d
).
∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we present a proof of one of our main results, Theorem
1.1
. Starting from the time-discrete solutions constructed in the previous subsection, we perform a limit passage in the corresponding time-continuous system based on compactness results.

Proof.

For given
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
(\mathbf{v}_{k+1},\lambda_{k+1},\mu_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, be iteratively chosen as a solution to the time-discrete system (
4.3a
)–(
4.3d
) with time step size
h=\frac{1}{N}
and initial value
(\mathbf{v}_{0},\varphi_{0}^{N})
, where
\varphi_{0}^{N}
is specified below. The existence of such solutions, which additionally satisfy the energy inequality (
4.7
), is ensured by Lemma
4.7
and Lemma
4.8
. To apply these lemmas to the first step, we approximate the original initial value
\varphi_{0}
by functions
\varphi_{0}^{N}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
. For this construction, we use standard partial differential equation techniques, in particular the solution
u
of the heat equation

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u&=&0&\text{ in }Q,\\[1.07639pt]
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on }S,\\[1.07639pt]
u|_{t=0}&=&\varphi_{0}&\text{ in }\Omega.\end{array}

Setting
\varphi_{0}^{N}\coloneqq u|_{t=\frac{1}{N}}
ensures
\varphi_{0}^{N}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
as well as
|\varphi_{0}^{N}|\leq 1
in
\Omega
and
\varphi_{0}^{N}\to\varphi_{0}
in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
as desired.

To start, we fix the following notation for
f\in\{\mathbf{v},\lambda,\mu,\varphi\}
. On
[-h,\infty)\times\Omega
, we define the piecewise constant interpolant of the time-discrete functions by

\displaystyle f^{N}(t)\coloneqq f_{k}\qquad\text{for }t\in[(k-1)h,kh),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

and additionally, we set
\rho^{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\rho}_{+}+\tilde{\rho}_{-})+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\rho}_{+}-\tilde{\rho}_{-})\varphi^{N}
. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, it consequently holds
f^{N}((k-1)h)=f_{k}
and
f^{N}(kh)=f_{k+1}
as well as
f^{N}(t)=f_{k+1}
for
t\in[kh,(k+1)h)
. Moreover, we introduce notation for time differences, difference quotients and time shifts, namely

\displaystyle(\Delta_{h}^{+}f)(t)\coloneqq f(t+h)-f(t),\qquad(\Delta_{h}^{-}f)(t)\coloneqq f(t)-f(t-h),

\displaystyle\partial_{t,h}^{+}f(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{h}(\Delta_{h}^{+}f)(t),\qquad\partial_{t,h}^{-}f(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{h}(\Delta_{h}^{-}f)(t),

\displaystyle f_{h}(t)\coloneqq f(t-h).

From the time-discrete system (
4.3a
)–(
4.3d
), we derive in the following the corresponding time-continuous equations, where we use the discrete integration by parts formula

(4.10)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\partial_{t,h}^{-}\phi\xi\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)=-\int_{Q}\phi\partial_{t,h}^{+}\xi\;\mathrm{d}(t,x),

which holds for all
\phi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(-h,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
,
\xi\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}((0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
, provided that
h>0
is sufficiently small.
Therefore, for arbitrary
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
, testing (
4.3a
) with
\tilde{\bm{\psi}}\coloneqq\int_{kh}^{(k+1)h}\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}t
and summing over
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
yields

\displaystyle\int_{Q}-\rho^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\cdot\partial_{t,h}^{+}\bm{\psi}-\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\mathbf{v}^{N}\big):\nabla\bm{\psi}-\big(\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{N}\big):\nabla\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

(4.11a)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{Q}\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}^{N}\big):\mathrm{D}\bm{\psi}-\lambda^{N}\nabla\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)=-\int_{Q}\varphi_{h}^{N}\nabla\mu^{N}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
if
h
is small enough. We further obtain directly

(4.11b)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}^{N}=-\alpha m_{\textnormal{r}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\big(\mu^{N}+\alpha\lambda^{N}\big)\quad\text{a.e.\penalty\ in }Q,

whereas, for sufficiently small
h
, following the same testing procedure as in (
4.11a
) leads to

\displaystyle\int_{Q}-\varphi^{N}\partial_{t,h}^{+}\zeta-\varphi_{h}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

(4.11c)

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}-\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\nabla\mu^{N}\big)\cdot\nabla\zeta-m_{\textnormal{r}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\big(\mu^{N}+\alpha\lambda^{N}\big)\zeta\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

for all
\zeta\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
. Finally, it holds

(4.11d)

\displaystyle\mu^{N}+\kappa\frac{\varphi^{N}+\varphi_{h}^{N}}{2}=F_{0}^{\prime}\big(\varphi^{N}\big)-\Delta\varphi^{N}\quad\text{a.e.\penalty\ in }Q.

Next, concerning the total energy of the system, let
E_{N}
denote the piecewise linear interpolant of
E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v}_{k},\varphi_{k})
at time
t_{k}=kh
, defined as

(4.12)

\displaystyle E_{N}(t)\coloneqq\frac{(k+1)h-t}{h}E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v}_{k},\varphi_{k})+\frac{t-kh}{h}E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1})

for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
where
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. For all
t\in(t_{k},t_{k+1})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we further define the dissipation

(4.13)

\displaystyle D_{N}(t)\!\coloneqq\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}_{k+1}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}_{k+1}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})|\nabla\mu_{k+1}|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})(\mu_{k+1}+\alpha\lambda_{k+1})^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

From the discrete energy inequality (
4.7
), we obtain

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{N}(t)=\frac{E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v}_{k},\varphi_{k})-E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{v}_{k+1},\varphi_{k+1})}{h}\geq D_{N}(t)

for all
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1})
with
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. On the one hand, we multiply this inequality by a function
\sigma\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}(0,\infty)
with
\sigma\geq 0
and integrate by parts to get

(4.14)

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{v}_{0}^{N},\varphi_{0}\big)\sigma(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{N}(t)\sigma^{\prime}(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}D_{N}(t)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t.

On the other hand, directly integrating the above inequality entails the following energy inequality for the time-continuous system, which reads

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{v}^{N}(t),\varphi^{N}(t)\big)+\int_{Q_{(s,t)}}\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}^{N}\big):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}^{N}+m_{\textnormal{j}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\big|\nabla\mu^{N}\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(\tau,x)

(4.15)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{Q_{(s,t)}}m_{\textnormal{r}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\big(\mu^{N}+\alpha\lambda^{N}\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(\tau,x)\leq E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{v}^{N}(s),\varphi^{N}(s)\big)

for all
0\leq s\leq t<\infty
with
s,t\in h\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

From (
4.3
), invoking Korn’s inequality for
\mathbf{v}^{N}
and the fact that
\langle\varphi^{N}\rangle
is constant, we infer that

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{v}^{N},\varphi^{N}\big)

\displaystyle\text{ is bounded in }\mathrm{L}^{\infty}\big(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\big),

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{v}^{N},\nabla\mu^{N},\mu^{N}+\alpha\lambda^{N}\big)

\displaystyle\text{ is bounded in }\mathrm{L}^{2}\big(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\big).

Furthermore, in combination with Lemma
4.6
, we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big|\int_{\Omega}\mu^{N}\;\mathrm{d}x\Big|\;\mathrm{d}t\leq M(T)\quad\text{for all }0<T<\infty\text{ with }M\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\text{ monotone}.

Thus, we are in a position to pass to non-relabeled subsequences converging weakly (or weakly-*, respectively) as
N\to\infty
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{N}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ast)\;\,}{\rightharpoonup}\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)^{d})\cap\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}),

\displaystyle\varphi^{N}

\displaystyle\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\varphi

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\nabla\mu^{N}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\nabla\mu

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}),

\displaystyle\mu^{N}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\mu

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty,

\displaystyle\lambda^{N}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\lambda

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty.

In order to realize the limit passage in all non-linearities, we additionally show strong convergence of
\varphi^{N}
and
\mathbf{v}^{N}
in the following. We start by defining
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
as the piecewise linear interpolant of
\varphi^{N}(t_{k})
where
t_{k}=kh
and
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The time derivative of
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
is then given by a difference quotient, specifically

(4.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}^{N}=\partial_{t,h}^{-}\varphi^{N}\quad\text{for almost all }t\in(0,\infty),

and the difference of the two interpolants may be estimated by

(4.17)

\displaystyle\big\|\tilde{\varphi}^{N}-\varphi^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}\leq h\big\|\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}.

In view of the discrete integration by parts formula (
4.10
), equation (
4.11c
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\partial_{t,h}^{-}\varphi^{N}\zeta-\varphi_{h}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}-\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\nabla\mu^{N}\big)\cdot\nabla\zeta-m_{\textnormal{r}}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N}\big)\big(\mu^{N}+\alpha\lambda^{N}\big)\zeta\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

for all
\zeta\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}((0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega})
. Studying the latter, along with identity (
4.16
), reveals that
\partial_{t}\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega))
since the terms
\varphi_{h}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}
,
\nabla\mu^{N}
and
\mu+\alpha\lambda^{N}
are bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
, respectively. Moreover, observing that the boundedness of
\varphi^{N}
in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
implies boundedness of
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
in the same space, the Aubin–Lions lemma yields the strong convergence

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}^{N}\to\tilde{\varphi}\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty

for some function
\tilde{\varphi}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
. In particular it follows
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}\to\tilde{\varphi}
a.e. in
Q
along a subsequence. Furthermore, estimate (
4.17
) entails that

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}^{N}-\varphi^{N}\to 0\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega))

and thus
\tilde{\varphi}=\varphi
. Since
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}\to\varphi
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
for all
0<T<\infty
and both
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
and
\varphi^{N}
are bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
we further conclude by (
4.17
) and an interpolation argument that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\varphi^{N}\to\varphi\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty.

Moreover, we employ the embedding

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega))\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))\hookrightarrow\mathrm{BC}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)),

where this uniformly local variant can be derived immediately from Theorem 4.10.2, Chapter III in
[
13
]
. The boundedness of
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
in this space and in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
establishes
\varphi\in\mathrm{BC_{w}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
, which can be verified by applying Lemma 4.1 in
[
2

to arbitrary intervals
[0,T]
with
0<T<\infty
.

Combining the fact that
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}
is bounded in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega))
and in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))

for all

, we further deduce that
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}|_{t=0}\to\varphi|_{t=0}

. In view of
\tilde{\varphi}^{N}|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0}^{N}\to\varphi_{0}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, we ultimately arrive at the identity
\varphi|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0}
.

The affine linear dependence of
\rho^{N}
on
\varphi^{N}
enables us to derive analogous results for
\rho^{N}
.

In order to pass to the limit in (
4.11d
) now, we first point out that the right-hand side is given by
\partial E\big(\varphi^{N}\big)
which is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
for all

according to Lemma

in the same space, from where we conclude, along with (

), that

\displaystyle\big\langle\partial E\big(\varphi^{N}\big),\varphi^{N}\big\rangle\to\big\langle\mu+\kappa\varphi,\varphi\big\rangle.

With
\partial E
being a maximal monotone operator, Proposition IV.1.6 in
[
33
]
hence establishes
{\partial E(\varphi)=\mu+\kappa\varphi}
, thereby eventually proving equation (

). Since this identity additionally involves

, we are finally in a position to use estimate (
2.9
) which, due to the continuous embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
for

if

and

as well as
F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega))
.

Now, we aim to show an even better strong convergence for
\varphi^{N}
. To this end, we study Lemma
4.6
to obtain boundedness of
\varphi^{N}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))
for all
0<T<\infty
. Together with (
4.18
), an interpolation argument verifies

(4.19)

\displaystyle\varphi^{N}\to\varphi\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty.

Our next goal is to prove strong convergence of
\mathbf{v}^{N}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
for all
0<T<\infty
. With
\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}
denoting the piecewise linear interpolant of
(\rho^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N})(t_{k})
where

,

, it holds as previously that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\big(\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}\big)=\partial_{t,h}^{-}\big(\rho^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\big).

We rewrite (
4.11a
) with the help of discrete integration by parts formula (
4.10
) into

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\partial_{t,h}^{-}\big(\rho^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\big)\cdot\bm{\psi}-\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\mathbf{v}^{N}\big):\nabla\bm{\psi}-\big(\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{N}\big):\nabla\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{Q}\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}^{N}\big):\mathrm{D}\bm{\psi}-\lambda^{N}\nabla\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)=-\int_{Q}\varphi_{h}^{N}\nabla\mu^{N}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(Q)^{d}
. In the following, we study the single terms therein to show that
\partial_{t}\big(\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}\big)
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{\frac{8}{7}}(0,T;\mathrm{W}^{-1,\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega)^{d})
for arbitrary
0<T<\infty
.

(i)

\rho_{h}^{N}\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\mathbf{v}^{N}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{L}^{3})
.

Since
\mathbf{v}^{N}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\mathrm{L}^{2})
and
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{H}^{1})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{L}^{6})
and
\rho_{h}^{N}

(ii)

\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{N}

\mathrm{L}^{\frac{8}{7}}(\mathrm{L}^{\frac{4}{3}})

Given that
\tilde{\mathbf{J}}^{N}=-b_{-}m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{h}^{N})\nabla\mu^{N}
, it suffices to consider
\mathbf{v}^{N}\otimes\nabla\mu^{N}
. For the resulting terms of the form
\mathbf{v}_{i}^{N}\partial_{j}\mu^{N}
, combining

\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{L}^{1})
and
\mathrm{L}^{1}(\mathrm{L}^{\frac{3}{2}})

(iii)

\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}^{N})
,
\lambda^{N}
and
\varphi_{h}^{N}\nabla\mu^{N}
are obviously bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{L}^{2})

Thus, in particular, all the terms above are bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{\frac{8}{7}}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\frac{4}{3}}(\Omega))
in their respective dimension. As a consequence, it is also possible to test (

) with functions satisfying both

\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{L}^{8}(0,T;\mathrm{W}_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)^{d})

\partial_{t}\big(\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}\big)
is bounded in the desired space

From this fact, along with the boundedness of
\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}

we deduce in view of the Aubin–Lions lemma the strong convergence

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}\to\tilde{\mathbf{v}}\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})\text{ for all }0<T<\infty

for some

\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})

\tilde{\mathbf{v}}=\rho\mathbf{v}
. For every

, combining the strong convergence
\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}\to\rho\mathbf{v}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
and the weak convergence
\mathbf{v}^{N}\rightharpoonup\mathbf{v}
in the same space, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{Q_{(0,T)}}\rho^{N}\big|\mathbf{v}^{N}\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)\to\int_{Q_{(0,T)}}\rho|\mathbf{v}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(t,x),

and as a consequence

in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
for all
0<T<\infty
. Recalling that
\rho^{N}\to\rho
a.e. in
Q
due to (
4.18
) and that
\rho^{N}
is bounded away from zero we conclude

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{N}=\big(\rho^{N}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\big(\rho^{N}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{v}^{N}\Big)\to\mathbf{v}\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})\text{ for all }0<T<\infty

as desired. In particular, we establish the convergence
\mathbf{v}^{N}\to\mathbf{v}
a.e. in
Q
along a subsequence.

Eventually, by means of the proven convergence results, passing to the limit to verify the equations (
4.1
)–(
4.1c
) becomes possible.

Moreover, following a similar argumentation as for proving
\varphi\in\mathrm{BC_{w}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
, we obtain that the boundedness of
\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}
in

\displaystyle\mathrm{W}^{1,\frac{8}{7}}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{W}^{-1,4}(\Omega)^{d})\hookrightarrow\mathrm{BC}([0,\infty);\mathrm{W}^{-1,4}(\Omega)^{d})

and in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
, along with the strong convergence to
\rho\mathbf{v}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
for all
0<T<\infty
, implies
\rho\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{BC_{w}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
. Given that
\rho
belongs to the space
\mathrm{BC}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
and is bounded from below by a positive constant, we eventually infer
\mathbf{v}=\frac{1}{\rho}\rho\mathbf{v}\in\mathrm{BC_{w}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
.

In order to show that
\mathbf{v}
attains the initial value
\mathbf{v}_{0}
, we observe that due to the above embedding, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}|_{t=0}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\mathbf{v}|_{t=0}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x

for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
. Since
\widetilde{\rho\mathbf{v}}^{N}|_{t=0}=\rho_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}
by definition, this implies

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\mathbf{v}_{0}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\mathbf{v}|_{t=0}\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x

for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d}
and by approximation for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)^{d}
as well. Testing with the difference
\mathbf{v}_{0}-\mathbf{v}|_{t=0}
entails
\int_{\Omega}\rho_{0}\big|\mathbf{v}_{0}-\mathbf{v}|_{t=0}\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=0
and therefore
\mathbf{v}_{0}=\mathbf{v}|_{t=0}
a.e. in
\Omega
as desired.

For the remaining proof of the energy inequality (
4.1
), we refer to Subsection
4.4
which follows directly.
∎

4.4.
Energy Inequality

Eventually, we are in a position to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
by verifying the energy inequality (
4.1
). Due to the strong convergences (
4.19
) and (
4.20
) we can extract a subsequence satisfying both
\mathbf{v}^{N}(t)\to\mathbf{v}(t)
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
as well as
\varphi^{N}(t)\to\varphi(t)
in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
for almost every
t\in(0,\infty)
. For
E_{N}
defined in (
4.12
), it hence follows

\displaystyle E_{N}(t)\to E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{v}(t),\varphi(t)\big)\quad\text{for almost all }t\in(0,\infty).

Furthermore, employing lower semicontinuity of norms together with
\varphi^{N}\to\varphi
a.e. in
Q
, we obtain for
D_{N}
from (
4.13
) that

\displaystyle\liminf_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}D_{N}(t)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}D(t)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t

for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}(0,\infty)
with
\sigma\geq 0
, where the dissipation
D
is accordingly given by

\displaystyle D(t)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{v}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)|\nabla\mu|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big(\mu+\alpha\lambda\big)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Passing to the limit in the above inequality (
4.14
) thus yields

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{v}_{0},\varphi_{0}\big)\sigma(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}E_{\textnormal{tot}}(t)\sigma^{\prime}(t)\;\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}D(t)\sigma(t)\;\mathrm{d}t

for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}(0,\infty)
with
\sigma\geq 0
. According to Lemma 4.3 in
[
2
]
this is equivalent to the desired energy inequality (
4.1
).

5.
Reformulation of the Model with Mass Averaged Velocity

This section is dedicated to a slight reformulation of the thermodynamically consistent diffuse interface model by Aki et al.
[
12
]
for binary flows with phase transitions. In contrast to the model derived in Section
3
, the present quasi-incompressible system incorporates a mass averaged velocity to describe the motion of the mixture constituents with unmatched densities. The modifications performed in the following lay the groundwork for the analytical studies conducted in Section
6
.

Our starting point is the following variant of the Navier–Stokes/Cahn–Hilliard system proposed in
[
12
]
:

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\rho\big(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}\big)-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u})+\nabla\lambda&=&-\nabla p(\varphi)+\varphi\nabla\Delta\varphi&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}&=&c_{-}c_{+}\big(\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big)\big(\mu+\frac{c_{-}}{c_{+}}\lambda\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{u})&=&c_{+}^{2}\big(\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\big)\big(\mu+\frac{c_{-}}{c_{+}}\lambda\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\mu&=&F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
p(\varphi)&=&\varphi F^{\prime}(\varphi)-F(\varphi)&\text{ in $Q$},\end{array}

where the relations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u})&=&2\nu(\varphi)\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}+\eta(\varphi)\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathbf{I},\\[4.30554pt]
\rho(\varphi)&=&b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi\end{array}

are satisfied, and constants related to the specific mass densities are abbreviated by

\begin{array}[]{rcl}b_{\pm}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{+}\pm\tilde{\rho}_{-}}{2},\qquad c_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}}\pm\frac{1}{\tilde{\rho}_{-}},\qquad\text{where }\tilde{\rho}_{\pm}>0,\quad\tilde{\rho}_{+}\neq\tilde{\rho}_{-}.\end{array}

We point out that
\mathbf{u}
describes the
mass averaged
velocity of the mixture. In this context,
\mu
denotes the chemical potential,
p
represents the non-monotone pressure, and
\lambda
serves as a Lagrange multiplier ensuring the incompressibility of the constituents. For the remaining quantities, we refer to the introduction.

In order to reformulate the momentum equation, we begin by computing

\displaystyle-\nabla p(\varphi)+\varphi\nabla\Delta\varphi=-\varphi F^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\nabla\varphi+\varphi\nabla\Delta\varphi=-\varphi\nabla\big(F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi\big)=-\varphi\nabla\mu.

From this point onward, we consider
\lambda
as the pressure and work with a modified chemical potential
\omega\coloneqq\mu+\frac{c_{-}}{c_{+}}\lambda
. A simple computation reveals the identity

\displaystyle(1-\alpha\varphi)\nabla\lambda=\beta\rho\nabla\lambda,\qquad\text{where }\alpha=\tfrac{c_{-}}{c_{+}},\quad\beta=\tfrac{2}{\tilde{\rho}_{+}+\tilde{\rho}_{-}}.

Since the divergence equation together with the phase field equation are equivalent to the mass balance and the phase field equation, we choose the latter combination and finally end up with the model

(5.1)

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\rho(\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+(\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u})-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u})+\beta\rho\nabla\lambda&=&-\varphi\nabla\omega&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})&=&0&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{t}\varphi+\nabla\cdot(\varphi\mathbf{u})&=&c_{+}^{2}\big(\nabla\cdot\big(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)\nabla\omega\big)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\omega\big)&\text{ in $Q$},\\[4.30554pt]
\omega&=&F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi+\alpha\lambda&\text{ in $Q$}.\end{array}

To this system, we add the boundary conditions

(5.2)

\begin{array}[]{rclll}\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{u}|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{n})_{\bm{\tau}}&=&-\gamma\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}|_{\partial\Omega}&\text{ on $S$},\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\omega|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on $S$}.\end{array}

The Navier-slip boundary condition for
\mathbf{u}
describes that only the normal part of the velocity vanishes on the boundary, whereas the tangential part of
\mathbf{u}
is related to the tangential part of the normal stresses via some friction parameter
\gamma>0
. For
\varphi
and
\omega
, we work with a
\pi/2
-contact-angle condition and a no-flux boundary condition, respectively.

Furthermore, we note that the total energy of the system is given by

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{tot}}(\mathbf{u},\varphi)=E_{\textnormal{kin}}(\mathbf{u})+E_{\textnormal{free}}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}\rho\frac{|\mathbf{u}|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)+\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Multiplying the first equation of (
5.1
) by
\mathbf{u}
, the second by
\beta\lambda
, the third by
\omega
, and the fourth by
-\partial_{t}\varphi
, then integrating over
\Omega
and adding, reveals in view of the boundary conditions (
5.2
) that every sufficiently smooth solution to the model satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{\textnormal{tot}}\big(\mathbf{u}(t),\varphi(t)\big)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}|\nabla\omega|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}\omega^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma|\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s_{x}.

Section
6
is devoted to proving existence of weak solutions to the quasi-stationary variant of this system.

6.
Existence of Weak Solutions to the Quasi-Stationary Model with Mass Averaged Velocity

In this section, we prove existence of weak solutions to the system (
1.3
), which is the quasi-stationary version of the reformulated model from Section
5
. To this end, we impose the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A4

) for the entire section. Since the kinetic energy is neglected, the total energy of the system only consists of the free energy

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{free}}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega}F(\varphi)+\frac{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Furthermore, with
F_{0}(s)\coloneqq F(s)+\frac{\kappa}{2}s^{2}
being the convex part of

, we denote the convex part of the free energy by

\displaystyle E_{m}(\varphi)=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega}F_{0}(\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x&\text{for }\varphi\in\dom E_{m},\\
\infty&\text{else},\end{cases}

where
\dom E_{m}=\{\varphi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{(m)}(\Omega):|\varphi|\leq 1\text{ a.e.\penalty\ in }\Omega\}
. Since for solutions, the spatial mean of
\varphi
is conserved, cf. Remark
6.4
below, we can prescribe the mean
\langle\varphi\rangle=m\in(-1,1)
here without loss of generality. This enables us to use results related to the subgradient of

for functions with prescribed mean value, see Proposition

.

6.1.
Definition of Weak Solutions

In the following, we give our precise definition of weak solutions to system (

Definition 6.1
.

Let

with

a.e. in
\Omega
. Under the assumptions (

A1

)–(

A4

) a quadruple
(\mathbf{u},\lambda,\omega,\varphi)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle\in\mathrm{BC_{w}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{W}^{2,r}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}-\varphi\partial_{t}\zeta-\varphi\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\zeta\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)=\int_{Q}-m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}\nabla\omega\cdot\nabla\zeta-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}\omega\zeta\;\mathrm{d}(t,x)

(6.1d)

\displaystyle\omega=F^{\prime}(\varphi)-\Delta\varphi+\alpha\lambda\quad\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $Q$}.

Additionally, we require the boundary and initial conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $S$},

\displaystyle\varphi|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}

a.e. in
\Omega

to hold, as well as the energy inequality

\displaystyle E_{\textnormal{free}}\big(\varphi(t)\big)+\int_{Q_{(s,t)}}\mathbf{S}(\varphi,\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}):\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}+m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}|\nabla\omega|^{2}+m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)c_{+}^{2}\omega^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(\tau,x)

(6.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{S_{(s,t)}}\gamma|\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}(\tau,s_{x})\leq E_{\textnormal{free}}\big(\varphi(s)\big)

for almost all
s\in[0,\infty)
including

, and for all

.

Remark 6.2
.

Assume that the friction coefficient
\gamma
is positive, and that both the viscosity coefficient
\nu
and the mobility coefficients
m_{\textnormal{j}}
,
m_{\textnormal{r}}
are bounded from below by some positive constant. For stationary states
(\mathbf{u}^{*},\lambda^{*},\omega^{*},\varphi^{*})
of system (
1.3
), the energy inequality (
6.2
) reveals that
\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}^{*}=0
,
\omega^{*}=0
and
\mathbf{u}^{*}_{\bm{\tau}}=0
. Therefore, we derive
\mathbf{u}^{*}=0
by Korn’s inequality, cf. (3.5.11) in
[
1
]
. We conclude that

is a stationary state of the Cahn–Hilliard equation, i.e., a solution of

\begin{array}[]{rclll}F^{\prime}(\varphi^{*})-\Delta\varphi^{*}&=&-\alpha\lambda^{*}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\[2.15277pt]
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\varphi^{*}|_{\partial\Omega}&=&0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}

In contrast, if
\gamma=0
and the domain
\Omega
has a symmetry axis, Korn’s inequality is not applicable, and consequently, stationary states can also take the form of rigid motions with respect to the symmetry axis.

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\nabla\omega\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\nabla\varphi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\lambda_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\omega\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+1\big)\big(\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+1\big)

due to the embedding

since

.
Moreover, we combine (

) with the above estimate which, invoking (

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\partial E_{m}(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)}+\big|\langle F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi)\rangle\big|+\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+1\big)

\displaystyle=C\bigg(\Big\|P_{0}(\omega)+\kappa\frac{\varphi+\varphi_{k}}{2}-\kappa m-\alpha\lambda_{0}\Big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)}+\big|\langle F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi)\rangle\big|+\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+1\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq C\big(\|\lambda_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\omega\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\varphi_{k}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+1\big)\big(\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+1\big)

since

Lemma 6.6

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
\varphi_{k}\in\mathrm{H}^{2}_{(m)}(\Omega)
be given with
m\in(-1,1)
, and set
\rho_{k}\coloneqq b_{+}+b_{-}\varphi_{k}
. Then, there exists a solution
(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega,\varphi)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{D}(\partial E_{m})
to the time-discrete system (
6.3a
)–(
6.3d1
).

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
4.8
, we aim at applying the Leray–Schauder principle. To this end, setting

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{D}(\partial E_{m}),

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega),

we seek operators
\mathcal{L}_{k},\mathcal{F}_{k}\colon X\to Y
satisfying the equivalence that
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega,\varphi)\in X
solves the system (
6.3a
)–(
6.3d1
) if and only if
\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})-\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=0
. Therefore, we introduce

\displaystyle\langle L_{k}(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega),\bm{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}):\nabla\bm{\psi}-\beta\lambda_{0}\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\bm{\psi})+\varphi_{k}\nabla\omega\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\tau}}\;\mathrm{d}s_{x}

for all
\bm{\psi}\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)
, and subsequently define, for
\mathbf{z}=(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega,\varphi)\in X
, the operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\begin{pmatrix}L_{k}(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega)\\[4.30554pt]
\beta\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{u})+\beta h\lambda_{0}\\[4.30554pt]
\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{u})-c_{+}^{2}\big(\nabla\cdot(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\omega)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\omega\big)\\[4.30554pt]
\varphi+\partial E_{m}(\varphi)\end{pmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathbf{z})=\begin{pmatrix}0\\[4.30554pt]
-\beta\frac{\rho-\rho_{k}}{h}\\[4.30554pt]
-\frac{\varphi-\varphi_{k}}{h}\\[4.30554pt]
\varphi+P_{0}(\omega)+\kappa\frac{\varphi+\varphi_{k}}{2}-\kappa m-\alpha\lambda_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

In order to prove that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}\colon X\to Y
is invertible we assemble the first three lines of
\mathcal{L}_{k}
into a bilinear form
\mathcal{A}_{k}
on
\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)^{d}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\big((\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega),(\bm{\psi},\xi,\zeta)\big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\langle L_{k}(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega),\bm{\psi}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\xi\beta\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\mathbf{u})+\xi\beta h\lambda_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\zeta\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{u})+c_{+}^{2}m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\omega\cdot\nabla\zeta+c_{+}^{2}m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\omega\zeta\;\mathrm{d}x,

which is bounded and coercive as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\big((\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega),(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega)\big)

\displaystyle\geq C\Big(\|\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+h\|\lambda_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\omega\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq C\Big(\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+h\|\lambda_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|\omega\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\Big)

with a constant
C>0
in view of Korn’s inequality (cf. (3.5.11) in
[
1
]
) and the positive lower bound on the coefficients
\nu
,
\eta
,
m_{\textnormal{j}}
,
m_{\textnormal{r}}
and
\gamma
. From the Lax–Milgram theorem, we therefore derive that for any right-hand side
(\mathbf{g}_{1},g_{2},g_{3})\in\mathrm{H}^{-1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\big(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\big)^{\prime}
, there exists a unique triple
(\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{k}\big((\mathbf{u},\lambda_{0},\omega),(\bm{\psi},\xi,\zeta)\big)=\langle\mathbf{g}_{1},\bm{\psi}\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)}+\langle g_{2},\xi\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)}+\langle g_{3},\zeta\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}

for all
(\bm{\psi},\xi,\zeta)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.
In particular, for given
g_{3}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
\omega
is a weak solution to the elliptic equation

\displaystyle-c_{+}^{2}\big(\nabla\cdot(m_{\textnormal{j}}(\varphi_{k})\nabla\omega)-m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi_{k})\omega\big)=g_{3}-\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{u})

with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions whose right-hand side is in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. In light of standard elliptic regularity theory, we conclude that
\omega
belongs to
\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)
with

(6.6)

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{k}\big(\|g_{3}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\nabla\cdot(\varphi_{k}\mathbf{u})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\big).

Analogously to the proof of Lemma
4.8

\mathcal{L}_{k}
, we establish that

\displaystyle(I+\partial E_{m})^{-1}\colon\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{H}^{2-s}(\Omega),\qquad s\in\big(0,\tfrac{1}{4}\big),

is a compact operator, where we employ the results (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) for the subgradient
\partial E_{m}
.

Altogether, we have verified the existence of an inverse
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\colon Y\to X
. Next, in order to introduce Banach spaces
\tilde{X}
,
\tilde{Y}
such that we have continuous embeddings

,
\tilde{Y}\hookrightarrow Y
and such that the operator
\mathcal{L}_{k}^{-1}\colon\tilde{Y}\to\tilde{X}
is compact, we define

\displaystyle\tilde{X}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}_{(0)}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{2-s}(\Omega),\qquad s\in\big(0,\tfrac{1}{4}\big),

These definitions ensure the desired continuity of the embeddings

Furthermore, studying the operator
\mathcal{F}_{k}

Finally, to obtain a fixed point of the compact operator

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}(\varphi_{k},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}):\nabla\bm{\psi}-\beta\lambda_{0}\nabla\cdot(\rho_{k}\bm{\psi})\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}\cdot\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\tau}}\;\mathrm{d}s_{x}=\int_{\Omega}-\varphi_{k}\nabla\omega\cdot\bm{\psi}\;\mathrm{d}x

(6.7d

\displaystyle 0

r\in(\frac{2d}{d+2},\frac{d}{d-1})
. We point out that this is an admissible test function since
\mathbf{n}\cdot\nabla\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. This leads to the equation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\beta\lambda_{0}^{N}\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\;\sigma\;\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}^{N}\big):\nabla^{2}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\;\sigma\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}^{N}\big)\nabla\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}+\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}\varphi_{h}^{N}\nabla\omega^{N}\Big)\cdot\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\;\sigma\;\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\partial\Omega}\gamma\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}^{N}\cdot(\nabla\phi)_{\bm{\tau}}\;\mathrm{d}s_{x}\;\sigma\;\mathrm{d}t

for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
and
\phi\in\mathrm{W}^{2,r^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega)
. Thus,
\beta\lambda_{0}^{N}
is a very weak solution of the Neumann–Laplace equation, see Proposition
2.4
, whose right-hand side
f^{N}(t)
may be estimated by

\displaystyle\|f^{N}(t)\|_{(\mathrm{W}^{2,r^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega))^{\prime}}\leq C\Big(\big\|\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}^{N}\big)\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad+\big\|\mathbf{S}\big(\varphi_{h}^{N},\mathrm{D}\mathbf{u}^{N}\big)\nabla\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}+\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}\varphi_{h}^{N}\nabla\omega^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)}+\big\|\gamma\big(\rho_{h}^{N}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{u}_{\bm{\tau}}^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\Big)

for almost all
t\in(0,\infty)
in view of
\nabla\phi\in\mathrm{W}^{1,r^{\prime}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{C}^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. As a consequence, according to estimate (
2.10
), there exists a constant
C_{r}>0
such that

\displaystyle\|\lambda_{0}^{N}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))}\leq C_{r}\|f^{N}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}\big(0,\infty;(\mathrm{W}^{2,r^{\prime}}_{\textnormal{N}}(\Omega))^{\prime}\big)}\leq C

due to the uniform bounds derived above, from where we eventually conclude that

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}^{N}\rightharpoonup\lambda_{0}\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))

along a subsequence.

Next, in order to pass to the limit in the non-linearities, we need to show strong convergence of
\varphi^{N}
. By the same reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we first prove

(6.10)

\displaystyle\varphi^{N}\to\varphi\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,\infty;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty

as well as
\varphi\in\mathrm{BC_{w}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
and
\varphi|_{t=0}=\varphi_{0}
. Furthermore, we study the estimate from Lemma
6.5
for the piecewise constant functions to observe

\displaystyle\big\|\varphi^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{W}^{2,r}(\Omega))}+\big\|F_{0}^{\prime}\big(\varphi^{N}\big)\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\big\|\lambda_{0}^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))}+\big\|\nabla\omega^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))}+\big\|\varphi^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))}+\big\|\varphi_{h}^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))}+1\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\Big(\big\|\varphi^{N}\big\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))}+1\Big)

for all
0<T<\infty
. Since the right-hand side is bounded uniformly in
N
, we obtain for a subsequence that
\varphi^{N}\rightharpoonup\varphi
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{W}^{2,r}(\Omega))
as well as
F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi^{N})\rightharpoonup F^{*}
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))
for all
0<T<\infty
with some limit function
F^{*}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{uloc}}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))
. Finally, in view of the compact embedding
\mathrm{W}^{2,r}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, we even establish

\displaystyle\varphi^{N}\to\varphi\quad\text{in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))\text{ for all }0<T<\infty.

By means of the proven convergence results, we are now in a position to pass to the limit in (
6.8
)–(
6.8
) to establish (
6.1a
)–(
6.1c
).

Continuing with the limit passage in equation (
6.8d1
), we first note that
-\Delta\varphi^{N}\rightharpoonup-\Delta\varphi
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))
for all
0<T<\infty
since
-\Delta\colon\mathrm{W}^{2,r}(\Omega)\to\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)
is a linear bounded operator. Already knowing that all other terms in (
6.8d1
) converge weakly in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega))
for all
0<T<\infty
as well it only remains to verify
F^{*}=F^{\prime}(\varphi)
a.e. in
(0,\infty)\times\Omega
in order to pass to the correct limit in
P_{0}(F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi^{N}))
. To prove this, we follow an approach in
[
5
]
. To this end, let
0<T<\infty
and
l\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, and let
0<t<T
be arbitrary. From the strong convergence (
6.10
), it follows
\varphi^{N}(t)\to\varphi(t)
a.e. in
\Omega
along a subsequence. Additionally, Egorov’s theorem implies the existence of a set
\Omega_{l}\subset\Omega
with
|\Omega_{l}|\geq|\Omega|-\frac{1}{2l}
on which
\varphi^{N}(t)\to\varphi(t)
uniformly. Defining

\displaystyle M_{\delta,N}\coloneqq\big\{x\in\Omega:\big|\varphi^{N}(t,x)\big|>1-\delta\big\}

we see that
|M_{\delta,N}|
is decreasing in
\delta>0
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, it holds

\displaystyle c_{\delta}\coloneqq\inf_{|r|\geq 1-\delta}|F^{\prime}(r)|\to\infty\quad\text{as }\delta\to 0

due to the assumption (

A2

). This convergence and the uniform (with respect to
N
) bound on
F^{\prime}(\varphi^{N}(t))
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
entail, in light of Chebyshev’s inequality, that

\displaystyle|M_{\delta,N}|\leq\frac{1}{c_{\delta}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\big|F^{\prime}\big(\varphi^{N}(t)\big)\big|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\to 0\quad\text{as }\delta\to 0\quad\text{uniformly in }N\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, there exists
\delta=\delta(l)
independent of
N
such that it holds

\displaystyle\Big|\big\{x\in\Omega:\big|\varphi^{N}(t,x)\big|>1-\delta\big\}\Big|\leq\frac{1}{2l}\quad\text{for all }N\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, the uniform convergence above yields some
\bar{N}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|\varphi^{N}(t)-\varphi(t)|<\frac{\delta}{4}
for all
N\geq\bar{N}
on
\Omega_{l}
. With

\displaystyle\Omega_{l}^{\prime}\coloneqq\Omega_{l}\cap\big\{x\in\Omega:\big|\varphi^{\bar{N}}(t,x)\big|\leq 1-\delta\big\}

we derive that
|\Omega_{l}^{\prime}|\geq|\Omega|-\frac{1}{l}
as well as
|\varphi^{N}(t,x)|<1-\frac{\delta}{2}
for all
N\geq\bar{N}
and for all
x\in\Omega_{l}^{\prime}
. The continuity of
F^{\prime}
on
(-1,1)
shows that
F^{\prime}(\varphi^{N}(t))\to F^{\prime}(\varphi(t))
uniformly on
\Omega_{l}^{\prime}
, which results in the convergence
F^{\prime}(\varphi^{N})\to F^{\prime}(\varphi)
a.e. in
(0,\infty)\times\Omega
since
l
and
T
were arbitrary. Eventually, due to the uniqueness of both weak and pointwise limits, we conclude that
F^{*}
and
F^{\prime}(\varphi)
coincide a.e. in
(0,\infty)\times\Omega
.

Altogether, it is now possible to perform the limit passage in equation (
6.8d1
). Thus, following the same procedure as in Remark
6.4
on a time-continuous level, in particular setting
-\alpha\bar{\lambda}=\langle F_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi)\rangle-\bar{\omega}-\kappa m
a.e. in
(0,\infty)
, where
\bar{\omega}\coloneqq(\int_{\Omega}m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}x)^{-1}\int_{\Omega}m_{\textnormal{r}}(\varphi)P_{0}(\omega)\;\mathrm{d}x
, we establish the validity of equation (
6.1d
).

The remaining proof of the energy inequality (
6.2
) follows the same steps as in Subsection
4.4
.
