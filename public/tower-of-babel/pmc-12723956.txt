Multi-omics reveals FAT10 as an immunometabolic survival factor rewiring global metabolism in cancer
Authors: Jun Yan Loh, Jianhong Ching, Jean-Paul Kovalik, Shuaichen Liu, Lee Jin Lim, Samuel S Chong, Caroline G L Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFAT10 is an inflammation-induced oncogene highly overexpressed across multiple cancers. As a ubiquitin-like protein, it regulates interacting proteins by destabilizing, stabilizing or delocalizing them. Despite its extensive influence on targets from various cellular pathways, prior studies largely focused on interrogating individual protein interactions, limiting our understanding of FAT10’s broader oncogenic role.MethodsHere, we adopt a novel systems-level approach to elucidate FAT10’s function by integrating quantitative proteomics, interactomics and metabolomics analyses in a cancer cell model. Biochemical methods including western blot, Seahorse Assay and Oil-Red-O staining were used to validate FAT10’s impact on protein expression and metabolism in metabolism-related pathways. We further investigated the effect of metabolic stress on FAT10 expression by modulating metabolite levels or treatment with metabolic pathway inhibitors. FAT10-induced survival under metabolic stress and inflammation was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 assay and cleaved caspase 3 quantification.ResultsWe demonstrate that FAT10 prominently regulates metabolism-related pathways, coordinately upregulating those involved in energy uptake, production and storage (glycolysis, lipogenesis and β-oxidation), while downregulating those in energy consumption (pentose phosphate pathway, nucleotide metabolism, transcription, translation and cell cycle) and truncating the TCA cycle. These changes indicate a potential shift from a replication-oriented to a survival-oriented cellular state. Correspondingly, metabolic stress promoted inflammation-induced FAT10 transcription. FAT10 consequently enhanced cancer cell survival under concurrent metabolic stress and inflammation.ConclusionsOverall, our findings establish FAT10 as an immunometabolic survival factor that responds to metabolic stress under inflammation, propagating global metabolic changes to alleviate this stress and promote cancer survival by potentially orchestrating a fundamental shift in cellular priorities from replication to survival. Through our systems-level perspective on FAT10's regulatory activities, we contextualize its previously disparate functions and reveal a potential blueprint for cellular survival programming of metabolism-related pathways during inflammation and stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02513-4.Keywords: Cancer, FAT10, Immunometabolism, Inflammation, Multi-omics, Ubiquitin-like modifier

Background

Living systems are imbued with two fundamental goals—replication and survival. Under favorable conditions, they allocate precious resources towards growth and reproduction, for the propagation of life. However, when faced with external threats or stressors, they must adapt to acquire, conserve and store scarce resources, as well as utilize them to put up a defense for the preservation of life [1–3]. This shift in priorities from replication to survival therefore requires a rewiring of metabolic processes. Inflammation is an adaptation for survival. It correspondingly stimulates a host of immunometabolic changes—glycolysis at the expense of oxidative phosphorylation [4, 5], de novo lipid biosynthesis [6] and lipid droplet formation [1, 7]—in immune, non-immune and cancer cells.

FAT10 (HLA-F-adjacent transcript 10), also known as diubiquitin or UBD, is an inflammation-associated ubiquitin-like protein that exerts pleiotropic effects across cell types. Constitutively expressed in select immune cells [8], FAT10 supports immune functions like dendritic cell maturation [9] and T cell selection [10]. In all other cell types, basal FAT10 expression is negligible but readily inducible by inflammatory stimuli, particularly the proinflammatory cytokines TNFα and IFNγ [11]. Upon induction, FAT10 facilitates proteasomal degradation [12], antigen presentation [13], and pathogen clearance [14]. Remarkably, FAT10 also influences metabolism, promoting aging and obesity in mice [15] and lipogenesis in adipocytes [16]. Notably, FAT10 is highly overexpressed in patient tumors across multiple inflammation-driven cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, gastrointestinal, and gynecological cancers, with elevated expression associated with poorer clinicopathological characteristics [17–20]. FAT10 is not mutated or truncated in these tumors [18] and wildtype FAT10 intriguingly promotes tumorigenicity and malignancy [21], raising questions about its role in cancer pathophysiology, its relevance to other diverse functions, and potential overlaps between its cancer-related and physiological roles.

Underlying FAT10’s complexity is its ability to regulate the activity of hundreds of proteins of various cellular functions via its protein–protein interactions [22–24]. FAT10 is covalently conjugated to substrate proteins by FAT10ylation, a process mediated by its conserved C-terminal diglycine domain and an E1–E2–E3 enzyme cascade [12, 25, 26]. Additionally, FAT10 interacts non-covalently with putative interaction partners, through predominantly unmapped sites on its two ubiquitin-like domains [27]. Through these two types of interactions, FAT10 has three distinct implications on its interacting proteins. FAT10’s destabilizing effect involves the canonical FAT10ylation of substrates to directly target them for proteasomal degradation in a ubiquitin-independent manner [28]. Conversely, FAT10 can stabilize targets by antagonizing their ubiquitin-mediated degradation [29, 30]. Lastly, FAT10 can delocalize protein targets [27, 31].

To uncover the mechanisms behind FAT10’s oncogenic properties, studies have thus far focused on functional characterization of individual FAT10-interacting proteins. This approach has offered a limited view of FAT10’s ability to stabilize various known but unrelated oncogenes [19, 29, 32–34], destabilize a tumor suppressor [28] or DNA repair protein [35] and delocalize a cell cycle checkpoint protein [27] to promote cancer. While essential for establishing FAT10’s oncogenicity, this study method paints a partial and disjointed perspective of FAT10’s global function in cancer, while overlooking FAT10’s physiology. This disconnect is particularly evident in light of FAT10 interactome studies, which revealed its interaction with numerous proteins not typically linked to cancer, spanning cellular pathways like metabolism, cell cycle, transcription and translation [22–24]. Moreover, owing to the fragmented analysis of individual FAT10 targets, it remains unclear whether FAT10 can simultaneously exert the opposing effects of stabilization and destabilization on distinct categories of proteins within a single model, thereby hindering our understanding of FAT10’s function.

In this study, we integrated quantitative proteomics, interactomics and metabolomics in a cancer cell model, to forge a systems-level understanding of pathways modulated by FAT10, through its stabilizing and destabilizing effects. We demonstrate that FAT10 plays a broad role in simultaneously upregulating and downregulating distinct metabolism-related pathways, resulting in a potential shift from a replication-focused cellular state to a survival-focused one. Additionally, we uncover that metabolic stress plays a role in enhancing inflammation-induced FAT10 expression, and FAT10 in turn improves the survival of cancer cells under combined inflammatory and metabolic stress. Taken together, our findings suggest that FAT10 may be an oncogene co-opted by cancer to enhance survival in tumor microenvironments rife with metabolic stress and inflammation, by systematically remodelling central carbon metabolism and its affiliated pathways to alleviate these stresses. Our model also potentially reveals a blueprint for how molecular pathways are remodelled for cellular survival under adverse conditions.

Materials and methods

Mammalian cell culture

HCT116 cells (ATCC) were cultured in McCoy’s 5 A Medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Biological Industries). They were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Generation of constructs and stable cell lines

The FAT10 open reading frame was cloned into pcDNA3.1 (+) vector (Invitrogen) for constitutive, CMV-driven expression. The FAT10 diglycine mutant construct was generated via site-directed mutagenesis on the pcDNA3.1-FAT10 construct, to mutate the C-terminal diglycine motif to two alanine residues. This was carried out using the following primers: 5’-AATAGGATCCATGGCTCCCAATGCTTCCT-3’ (F) and 5’-AATAGCGGCCGCTCACGCTGCAATACAATAACATG-3’ (R). For stable transfection, parental HCT116 was transfected with pcDNA3.1-FAT10, pcDNA3.1-FAT10ΔGG, or pcDNA3.1 (control) using TransIT®−2020 transfection reagent (Mirus Bio) according to manufacturer’s instructions. 24 h post-transfection, cells were serially diluted and plated for colony selection under continuous treatment with 1 mg/ml Geneticin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for approximately two weeks. Following colony formation, single isolated colonies were selected and analyzed for FAT10 expression by western blot analysis.

For stable knockdown of FAT10 expression, shRNA targeting FAT10 was designed as previously described [11]. The following oligomers were annealed and cloned into the pLKO.1-TRC vector (Addgene plasmid #10,878) for synthesis of the pLKO.1-shFAT10 construct: 5’-CCGGGAGGAGCTGCCCTTGTTTCTTCTCGAGAAGAAACAAGGGCAGCTCCTCTTTTTG-3’ (F) and 5’- AATTCAAAAAGAGGAGCTGCCCTTGTTTCTTCTCGAGAAGAAACAAGGGCAGCTCCTC-3’ (R). Lentiviruses were generated via transfection of pLKO.1-shFAT10 or scramble shRNA vector (Addgene plasmid #1864), together with the 3rd generation lentiviral packaging plasmids pMD2.G (Addgene plasmid #12,259), pRSV-Rev (Addgene plasmid #12,253) and pMDLg/pRRE (Addgene plasmid #12,251), into the Lenti-X™ 293 T cell line (Takara Bio). The viruses were harvested after 72 h of incubation and used for infection of HCT116 cells in the presence of 5 μg/ml polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich). Stable clonal cells were then selected with 1.5 μg/ml puromycin (Life Technologies) and validated.

Inflammatory cytokine treatment

50 ng/ml TNFα (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 50 U/ml IFNγ (Roche) were added concurrently to cell culture medium for 12–16 h. U/ml refers to international units per milliliter.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-630, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein lysates were quantified using BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and all samples were adjusted to a final concentration of 5 μg/μl with laemmli buffer and water, prior to their boiling for SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. 50 μg of each sample were separated using a 12% resolving gel overlaid with a 4% stacking gel, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Bio-Rad) by wet transfer. Proteins were detected by standard immunoblotting procedures using the indicated antibodies and visualized with ECL Select™ Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare Life Sciences).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted using the QIAshredder and RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA concentration and purity were then measured with the NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For each sample, 1.25 μg RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Life Technologies) as per manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on the resultant cDNA using SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Life Technologies), and the 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Relative FAT10 expression of samples, to controls, were calculated and normalized to β-actin expression by the delta-delta Ct method.

iTRAQ-based quantitative proteomics

HCT116 stable cell lines were lysed and sonicated in lysis buffer (5% SDS, 50 mM TEAB, pH 7.55) supplemented with Benzonase nuclease (Merck KGaA). Protein lysates were quantitated using the RC DC protein assay (Bio-Rad) and 200 μg of protein samples were utilized for trypsin digestion using the S-Trap spin column (ProtiFi), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Peptides were eluted, vacuum dried and reconstituted in 50 mM TEAB buffer for peptide quantification using Pierce™ quantitative colorimetric peptide assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 100 μg of peptides from each sample were labelled with one of eight distinct isobaric tags from the iTRAQ Reagents 8-Plex kit (SCIEX). Labelled peptides from the eight samples were then pooled together for two-dimensional liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (2D LC–MS/MS) analysis.

Separation of pooled iTRAQ labelled peptides on the first dimension was performed via high pH reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography using the Agilent 1290 Infinity LC System (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a C18 column (Waters, 3.5 μm, 3 mm × 150 mm). Mobile phase A consisted of 20 mM ammonium formate in water, pH 10 while mobile phase B consisted of 20 mM ammonium formate in 80% acetonitrile, pH 10. Gradient elution of the peptides was performed at a flow rate of 500 μl/min, utilizing the following stepwise linear elution program: mobile phase B 0–10% for 5 min, 10–40% for 60 min, 40–70% for 15 min, 70–100% for 1 min, held at 100% for 5 min, 100–0% for 1 min and held at 0% for 10 min. 15 concatenated fractions were eluted and lyophilized. Each fraction was reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile in water for the second dimension of peptide separation by nano-liquid chromatography using the NanoLC-Ultra system coupled to the cHiPLC-nanoflex system (Eksigent) in Trap Elute configuration. Samples were injected into a ChromXP C18-CL trap column (SCIEX, 3 μm, 200 μm × 0.5 mm) and eluted on a ChromXP C18-CL analytical column (SCIEX, 3 μm, 75 μm × 15 cm) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Mobile phase A consisted of 2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B consisted of 98% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid in water. Gradient elution for peptide separation was performed as follows: mobile phase B 0–5% in 1 min, 5–12% in 15 min, 12–30% in 104 min, 30–90% in 2 min, held at 90% for 7 min, 90–5% in 3 min and held at 5% for 13 min. Tandem mass spectrometry analysis was conducted on the 15 fractions, each independently injected into the TripleTOF 5600 system (SCIEX) operating in positive ionization mode.

Protein identification and relative iTRAQ quantification were performed using ProteinPilot 5.0 software Revision 4769 (SCIEX), which employed the Paragon database search algorithm (version 5.0.0.0.4767) and the integrated false discovery rate (FDR) analysis function. The threshold for identifying proteins was set to 1% FDR.

Gene ontology and pathway analysis

Proteins were categorized into various pathways and visualized in interaction networks using the ConsensusPathDB meta-database, which integrates multiple source databases [36]. Pathway data from the following source databases were employed in this study: Reactome [37], KEGG [38], Wikipathways [39], HumanCyc [40], INOH [41], NetPath [42], SMPDB [43] and BioCarta [44]. Significantly enriched pathways met the significance threshold of p-value < 0.01.

Co-immunoprecipitation and interactomics

Anti-FAT10 monoclonal antibody was bound to Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific), in a ratio of 20 μg antibody to 1.5 mg beads, at 4 °C in a rotating mixer overnight. Bound antibodies were crosslinked to the magnetic beads using 20 mM dimethyl pimelimidate dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) in 0.2 M triethanolamine (Sigma-Aldrich), pH 8.2, for 30 min on a rotating mixer at room temperature. The crosslinking reaction was quenched by resuspending the bead-antibody complex in 1 M Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, and the crosslinked bead-antibody complex was further washed in PBS with 0.01% Tween20.

Prior to their harvesting, HCT116 cells of different conditions were treated with 10 μM MG132 for 3 h to inhibit proteasomal degradation of FAT10 targets. Cells were lysed in ice-cold lysis buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-630, 5% glycerol), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche), over 30 min on a rotating mixer at 4 °C. Cell lysates were quantified by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated with the crosslinked bead-antibody complex in a ratio of 1000 μg protein lysate to 1.5 mg beads. Immunoprecipitation was performed for 1 h on gentle rotation at 4 °C. Bead-antibody-antigen complexes were separated from cell lysates with a magnetic rack and washed with PBST. Pulldown products were eluted with 0.1 M glycine elution buffer (pH 2.5), neutralized with 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), and precipitated in 80% cold acetone (−20 °C) overnight. After centrifugation at 20,000 g and −20 °C for 20 min, acetone was aspirated and protein pellets were air-dried before being frozen at −20 °C for further processing.

The pulldown product was dissolved in TEAB buffer (5% SDS, 50 mM TEAB, pH 7.55). Trypsin digestion was performed on the protein samples using the S-Trap™ spin column as per manufacturer’s guidelines. Each sample’s peptides were then reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid in water for one-dimensional liquid chromatography (1D-LC) separation. Nano-liquid chromatography was carried out using the NanoLC-Ultra system paired with the cHiPLC-nanoflex system (Eksigent) in Trap Elute mode. The digested peptides for each sample were loaded onto a ChromXP C18-CL trap column (SCIEX, 3 μm, 200 μm × 0.5 mm) and eluted on a ChromXP C18-CL analytical column (SCIEX, 3 μm, 75 μm × 15 cm). Peptide separation was performed using a gradient elution with mobile phase A (2% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (98% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid in water) over 145 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Tandem mass spectrometry analysis was performed independently for each sample using the TripleTOF 5600 system (SCIEX) operating in positive ionization mode.

Protein identification was performed using the ProteinPilot 5.0 software Revision 4769 (SCIEX) and the protein identification threshold was set to 1% FDR.

Metabolomics and lipidomics

Stable cell lines were seeded in triplicates, allowed to attach overnight and then subjected to a change of fresh medium, with treatment of TNFα and IFNγ if necessary, for 16 h. Cells were harvested by cell scraping in ice-cold PBS and resuspended in a chilled extraction solvent comprising a 1:1 mixture of 0.6% formic acid in water and acetonitrile. The samples were subsequently stored at −80 °C before targeted metabolomics profiling of organic acids, amino acids, acylcarnitines and lipids were performed as described previously [45–47] and briefly below.

Organic acids were extracted from cell lysates using ethylacetate. The extracts were dried and derivatized with N,O-Bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide, with alpha-keto groups protected by oximation using ethoxyamine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich). The resulting trimethylsilyl derivatives of organic acids were then separated and analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). The separation was carried out on an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies) equipped with a gas chromatography column (VF-1 ms; 30 m × 0.25 mm × 1 μm), and quantification was performed by select ion monitoring with the Agilent 5975 C mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies) using stable isotope dilution. The GC oven temperature program was set to an initial temperature of 70 °C, increased at a rate of 40 °C/min to 300 °C, and held for 2 min.

Amino acids were extracted with methanol, derivatized using 3 M hydrochloric acid in butanol, dried, and finally reconstituted in methanol for analysis by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). LC–MS analysis was performed with a C18 reverse phase column (Phenomenex, 100 × 2.1 mm, 1.6 μm, Luna® Omega) installed on the Agilent 1290 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies) and coupled to a QTRAP 5500 quadrupole-ion trap mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX). Separation was accomplished using mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) with the following step gradient: mobile phase B 2% for 0.8 min, 15% in 0.1 min, 20% in 5.7 min, 50% in 0.5 min, 70% in 0.5 min, and finally re-equilibration at 2% for 0.9 min. The flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min. The compounds were ionized using electrospray ionization in positive ion mode. Chromatograms were integrated using the MultiQuant™ 3.0.3 software (AB SCIEX).

Acylcarnitines were extracted using methanol, derivatized with 3 M hydrochloric acid in methanol, dried, and further reconstituted in methanol. Acylcarnitines were then quantified by flow injection-tandem mass spectrometry on the Agilent 6430 Triple Quadrupole LC–MS system (Agilent Technologies), using a mobile phase of 80% methanol in water and a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The acquisition and processing of data were facilitated using the Agilent MassHunter Workstation software (version B.06.00).

To prepare samples for lipidomics, a ceramide/sphingoid internal standard mix (Avanti Polar Lipids) and 14:0 phosphatidylcholine (Avanti Polar Lipids) were added to samples. After equilibration, lipids were extracted by adding HPLC-grade methanol to the mixture, followed by vortexing, incubation at 50 °C for 10 min, and centrifugation to pellet the precipitated proteins. The supernatant was aspirated and dried under nitrogen gas, then reconstituted with methanol for LC–MS analysis. Lipid separation was performed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity LC system (Agilent Technologies) equipped with an Accucore™ HILIC column (2.6 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phase A consisted of 95% acetonitrile in water with 10 mM ammonium acetate, pH 8, while mobile phase B consisted of 50% acetonitrile in water with 10 mM ammonium acetate, pH 8. The elution protocol was as follows: 0–20% mobile phase B in 5 min, held for 5 min, followed by re-equilibration with 100% mobile phase A for 5 min. Mass spectrometry was conducted using an Agilent 6430 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies).

Glycolytic rate assay

The Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay (Agilent Technologies) was performed as per manufacturer’s instructions. 1.5 × 104 HCT116 cells were plated per well of the XF96 cell culture microplate. Upon their adhesion, TNFα and IFNγ were added to the necessary wells for at least 12 h before the assay. The cell culture medium was exchanged for Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay medium (10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine and 5 mM HEPES), supplemented with TNFα and IFNγ if necessary, prior to the assay. The cells were treated with 0.5 μM Rotenone, 0.5 μM Antimycin A and 50 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (Sigma-Aldrich) during the assay, with the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measured in real-time using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer. After the assay, cells from each well were lysed with RIPA buffer and total protein per well was quantitated using BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The glycolytic proton efflux rate (glycoPER) was calculated using the Seahorse Wave desktop software and glycoPER for each well was normalized against total protein from the respective wells.

Metabolic stress experiments

For serum starvation experiments, HCT116 cells were seeded in 6-well plates in complete medium (McCoy’s 5 A Medium supplemented with 10% FBS). Following their attachment overnight, cells were washed with PBS and pretreated with their respective conditions for 12 h. Serum starvation was performed by incubating cells in McCoy’s 5 A Medium with 0% FBS and 0.2% fatty acid-free Bovine Serum Albumin (FAF-BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich), or control complete medium. Cytokine pretreatment was performed by concurrent addition of 50 ng/ml TNFα (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 50 U/ml IFNγ (Roche). Treatments were then added from the 12 h time point for the indicated durations, including 10% FBS, 100 µM Palmitate:BSA (6:1 molar ratio) or control FAF-BSA. Thereafter, cells were harvested for western blotting and qPCR at the indicated endpoints.

For lipid biosynthesis inhibition, 5-(Tetradecyloxy)−2-furoic acid (TOFA) (Selleck Chemicals) was dissolved in DMSO and administered at a final concentration of 10 µM for 12 h. Beta-oxidation inhibition was achieved by dissolving (+)-Etomoxir sodium salt hydrate in water and administering it at a final concentration of 50 µM for 12 h.

Glucose deprivation experiments were performed by seeding HCT116 cells in 6-well plates in complete medium. Following their attachment overnight, cells were washed with PBS and pretreated for 12 h with Seahorse XF Base Medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco) and varying concentrations (0, 5 or 25 mM) of glucose (Sigma-Aldrich), as well as 50 ng/ml TNFα and 50 U/ml IFNγ where indicated. Cells were then treated with additional 5 mM glucose for 6 h if specified and thereafter, cells from all conditions were harvested at the 18 h endpoint for western blot and qPCR analysis.

For glycolysis inhibition, 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2DG) (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in water and administered to HCT116 cells at various doses (25, 50 or 100 mM) in complete medium for 12 h.

Survival experiments

For assessment of apoptosis, HCT116 stable cells expressing either shFAT10 or shCtrl were seeded in 6-well plates. Upon attachment, they were treated with serum starvation and inflammatory cytokines as above. To ensure continuous treatment for 42 h, fresh media, with inflammatory cytokines if necessary, were supplemented at the 24 h time point. Floating and adherent cells were harvested at the endpoint for western blot analysis.

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. 1.2 × 104 HCT116 cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of adherence, cells were washed and subject to one of the four treatment conditions with serum starvation and inflammatory cytokines. Following 24 h treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added to wells and incubated for 1 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Raw OD values were normalized against background media control wells for complete or serum starvation medium. For each stable, cell viability for the treatments was then calculated as a percentage of the control (-TNFα/IFNγ, -serum starvation) condition. Normalized cell viability for each of the four conditions was then calculated using the formula: Normalized viability = (% ViabilityshCtrl − % ViabilityshFAT10).

Results

Quantitative proteomics reveals FAT10’s association with metabolism-related pathway proteins

To investigate FAT10’s system-wide effects in cancer cells, we utilized a HCT116 colorectal carcinoma cell model to recapitulate FAT10 overexpression in colorectal tumors [17, 20]. Wildtype FAT10 was first stably overexpressed (FAT10), and its expression was then attenuated by shRNA-mediated knockdown (FAT10kd). This FAT10kd condition serves as a rigorous control to distinguish true FAT10-driven phenotypes from potential artifacts of ectopic overexpression, by comparing FAT10-overexpressing cells both to control and to FAT10kd. A C-terminal diglycine domain mutant FAT10 (FAT10ΔGG), incapable of covalent interactions but retaining non-covalent binding ability [27], was overexpressed to evaluate the role of FAT10's covalent interactions. Control (C) cells with the respective vector controls were used as reference. From Fig. 1A, FAT10-overexpressing cells (FAT10 and FAT10ΔGG) had comparable levels of ectopic FAT10, in contrast to undetectable basal FAT10 expression in C as previously reported [21]. Significant attenuation of FAT10 overexpression was achieved in FAT10kd, compared to the FAT10 clone.

FAT10 is associated with metabolism-related pathway proteins in a systematic manner. A FAT10 protein expression in stable HCT116 cell lines (C: vector control; FAT10: clone overexpressing FAT10 and scramble shRNA; FAT10kd: FAT10-overexpressing clone stably expressing shRNA targeting FAT10; FAT10ΔGG: clone overexpressing FAT10 with mutated diglycine residues G164A and G165A). GAPDH was used as the loading control. B Schematic workflow of iTRAQ-based quantitative proteomics for relative quantitation of proteins among FAT10 stable cell conditions with 2 biological replicates (R1, R2). C Venn diagram illustrating selection of confident FAT10-associated proteins. Confident FAT10-positive or -negative associated proteins were identified from the intersection of significantly upregulated (p < 0.05, average fold-change ≥ 1.5) or downregulated (p < 0.05, average fold-change ≤ − 1.5) proteins respectively in both FAT10/C and FAT10/FAT10kd comparisons. D Heatmap illustrating fold-change values for the 183 confident FAT10-associated proteins across the relevant comparisons and replicates. E Pathways for confident FAT10-associated proteins. FAT10 was both positively- (upper panel) and negatively-associated (lower panel) with metabolism-related pathways. FAT10 upregulated glycolysis, lipid and amino acid metabolism, but downregulated the pentose phosphate pathway, nucleotide metabolism as well as nucleotide-dependent processes like cell cycle, transcription and translation

FAT10 can stabilize, destabilize or delocalize its interacting proteins. To understand FAT10’s broad impact on cellular pathways, we focused our analysis on FAT10-stabilized or -destabilized targets, by identifying proteins that were positively or negatively associated with FAT10 expression respectively, in addition to having direct interaction with FAT10. Hence, we first employed an unbiased quantitative proteomics approach, using isobaric tags for relative and absolute quantitation (iTRAQ) labelling, 2-dimensional liquid chromatography (2D-LC) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), to identify FAT10-associated proteins in our cancer model. Figure 1B outlines this workflow. Pairwise comparisons across FAT10 conditions identified 70 proteins positively associated with FAT10 and 113 negatively associated, based on their significant and congruent upregulation or downregulation (p < 0.05, fold-change ≥ 1.5 or ≤ − 1.5) in FAT10 relative to C (FAT10/C) and FAT10 relative to FAT10kd (FAT10/FAT10kd) comparisons (Fig. 1C). FAT10 was among the identified proteins, confirming the methodology’s effectiveness. The heatmap in Fig. 1D illustrates the fold-change of the 183 confident FAT10-associated proteins across comparisons and replicates.

To gain insight into the functions of these proteins, we categorized them into cellular pathways using the ConsensusPathDB meta-database, which conglomerates various source databases [36, 48]. FAT10-positively associated proteins were broadly involved in metabolism, cancer, cell surface interactions and cell signaling (Fig. 1E, upper panel). Notably, among metabolic pathways, FAT10 upregulated proteins in glycolysis, lipid metabolism and amino acid metabolism. On the other hand, FAT10-negative associated proteins were enriched in metabolism, cell cycle, transcription and translation pathways (Fig. 1E, lower panel). Interestingly, FAT10 activated specific metabolic pathways while suppressing the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) and nucleotide metabolism. These metabolic pathways are functionally related as the PPP redirects carbons from glycolysis into nucleotide biosynthesis [49]. Remarkably, FAT10 not only downregulated nucleotide biosynthesis but also curtailed the more energy-efficient nucleotide salvage pathways [50], potentially limiting nucleotide supply in cancer cells. Furthermore, FAT10 decreased nucleotide demand by downregulating nucleotide-dependent cellular processes (Fig. 1E, lower panel). These include proteins involved in RNA-driven transcription and translation, as well as DNA-dependent cell cycle pathways encompassing interphase, mitosis, and associated checkpoints.

Further analysis of confident FAT10-associated proteins using FAT10ΔGG revealed that, of the 70 FAT10-upregulated proteins, 30 were associated with FAT10 through FAT10’s non-covalent interactions, while 40 required covalent interactions (Supplementary Fig S1A, upper panel). Similarly, 33 of the 113 FAT10-negative associated proteins were non-covalent interaction-dependent, and 80 were covalent-dependent (Supplementary Fig S1A, lower panel). Heatmaps illustrating the average fold-changes for the four categories of confident FAT10-associated proteins are shown in Supplementary Fig S1B. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that both types of interactions were involved in modulating metabolism-related pathways (Supplementary Fig S1C). FAT10’s covalent interactions were enriched in upregulation of amino acid and lipid metabolism, while both interaction types were involved in glycolysis induction (Supplementary Fig S1C, upper panels). For downregulated pathways, FAT10’s associations with PPP and nucleotide metabolism were covalent interaction-dependent (Supplementary Fig S1C, lower right panel), while both types of interactions were involved in cell cycle, transcription and translation pathways. Interestingly, non-covalent interactions were enriched in downregulating the M phase and cell cycle checkpoints (Supplementary Fig S1C, lower left panel), aligning with previous studies on FAT10’s disruption of MAD2 function [27].

Interactomics uncovers FAT10's direct interaction with metabolism-related pathway proteins

We next investigated FAT10-interacting proteins through interactomics. These targets were co-immunoprecipitated with FAT10 from stable cell lysates in the presence of the proteasomal inhibitor MG132 to prevent degradation. Eluted proteins were identified via mass spectrometry. This strategy is outlined in Fig. 2A. Our study identified 524 FAT10-interacting proteins across three FAT10 conditions in HCT116 (Fig. 2B). Notably, 85% (448/524) of these proteins were newly identified, while 15% (76/524) were targets reported in three previous FAT10 interactome studies performed in HEK293 [22] and HeLa [23, 24] cells (Fig. 2C). Five of these proteins (SQSTM1 [22], PCNA [35], LRRFIP2 [31], CTNNB1 [19, 28] and NUB1 [51]) previously had their interactions with FAT10 validated in vitro. The complete list of interacting proteins and their associated data is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

FAT10 interacts directly with metabolism-related pathway proteins. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the 352 proteins interacting with wildtype FAT10 were significantly enriched in metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 2D), similar to confident FAT10-associated proteins. They were involved in glycolysis, amino acid metabolism, and nucleotide-dependent processes like cell cycle, transcription, and translation. FAT10 was also found to be intricately involved in multiple steps of gene expression, binding proteins responsible for mRNA splicing, transport, RNA polymerase II subunits, translation, ribosomal components, tRNA aminoacylation, protein folding and processing in the endoplasmic reticulum. Furthermore, FAT10’s interaction with proteasome components (RPN1, RPN2 and PSMC1) supports its known role in substrate degradation [52].

Similarly, 236 proteins interacting with FAT10ΔGG were enriched in metabolism-related pathways (Supplementary Fig S2A), suggesting that FAT10’s non-covalent interactions play a broad role in cellular regulation. Beyond the pathways for wildtype FAT10, FAT10ΔGG bound novel targets like Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes and key gene expression machinery components including topoisomerase I and RNA polymerase subunits. Additionally, DNA repair proteins like PCNA [35] were notably enriched under the cell cycle category.

To validate these interactions under physiological conditions, we treated HCT116 cells with TNFα and IFNγ to induce endogenous FAT10 (iFAT10). While TNFα and IFNγ treatment induces global proteomic changes that could influence the spectrum of FAT10-interacting proteins, the 160 iFAT10-interacting proteins were similarly enriched in metabolism-related pathways (Supplementary Fig S2B), particularly nucleotide metabolism, confirming that FAT10 interacts with metabolism-related proteins even under these conditions.

Combined proteome and interactome analyses reveal FAT10-regulated targets in metabolism-related pathways

Our analyses of FAT10-associated and FAT10-interacting pathways consistently show significant enrichment of metabolism-related pathways (Fig. 3A), suggesting that FAT10 modulates these pathways posttranslationally. To pinpoint specific proteins stabilized or destabilized by FAT10, we applied the strategy outlined in Fig. 3B. Proteins were classified as stabilized or destabilized based on their positive or negative correlation with FAT10 in proteomics data, alongside evidence of direct interaction from interactomics data. Using this integrated approach, we identified 7 FAT10-stabilized and 6 FAT10-destabilized novel targets (Fig. 3C).

FAT10-stabilized proteins were enriched in glycolysis (Fig. 3D), which notably comprise the glucose transporter SLC2A1 and enzyme LDHA. Interestingly, SLC2A1 stabilization occurs through non-covalent interactions, as it was associated with FAT10 independently of the diglycine domain and interacted with both wildtype FAT10 and FAT10ΔGG (Fig. 3C). In contrast, LDHA stabilization seems dependent on covalent interactions with FAT10, although non-covalent interactions were also observed (Fig. 3C). These findings align with FAT10’s stabilizing effects, which are known to involve both its non-covalent [30] and covalent [29] interactions.

FAT10-destabilized targets were primarily involved in RNA metabolism and transcription (Fig. 3E). Consistent with FAT10's known role in destabilization via FAT10ylation [28], we identified the RNA-binding protein ZC3H4, whose negative association with FAT10 was dependent on FAT10’s diglycine domain and which was found to interact with wildtype FAT10 but not FAT10ΔGG in our experimental setup (Fig. 3C). Additionally, targets such as WDR75 and DNAJB4 were destabilized through non-covalent interactions, suggesting FAT10 may also promote destabilization independently of FAT10ylation.

Metabolomics and lipidomics reveal FAT10-associated accumulation of glycolysis and TCA cycle intermediates, essential amino acids, triacylglycerols and acylcarnitines

Thus far, our data indicates FAT10’s affiliation with proteins regulating central carbon metabolism and its subsidiary pathways, suggesting its broad role in rewiring cancer metabolism. To explore this, we conducted targeted metabolomics in our HCT116 model to assess FAT10’s impact on key metabolites in these pathways.

Given FAT10’s involvement with glycolysis and TCA cycle proteins, we investigated the organic acids in these pathways. FAT10 overexpression led to increased levels of glycolysis metabolites (pyruvate, lactate) and TCA cycle intermediates (citrate, α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), succinate, fumarate, malate), with the most pronounced accumulation in α-KG and citrate (Fig. 4A–G). Attenuating overexpressed FAT10 (FAT10kd) reduced these effects, though TCA cycle metabolites—particularly α-KG—remained elevated compared to control. This pattern reflects both the incomplete nature of shRNA-mediated knockdown and possible signal amplification effects for TCA cycle metabolites: while FAT10kd cells show significantly reduced FAT10 expression compared to FAT10 cells, they retain detectably higher FAT10 levels than control cells. These residual FAT10 levels appear to produce disproportionately large increases in metabolites like α-KG, suggesting that TCA cycle metabolites may be particularly sensitive to even modest changes in FAT10 expression. FAT10’s covalent interactions were necessary for the metabolite accumulation observed with wildtype FAT10 overexpression, as overexpressing FAT10ΔGG did not replicate these effects. Similar trends were observed in TNFα/IFNγ-treated cells (iFAT10), with FAT10 knockdown (iFAT10kd) abrogating metabolite accumulation (Supplementary Fig S3A–G), indicating that FAT10 mediates TNFα/IFNγ-induced organic acid accumulation in these pathways.

Proteomics and interactomics also pointed to FAT10's involvement in amino acid metabolism. Targeted metabolomics revealed that FAT10 overexpression primarily elevated essential amino acids (valine, isoleucine, leucine, phenylalanine, methionine, histidine, tryptophan) and selected conditionally essential amino acids (tyrosine, arginine), while negatively associating with the non-essential aspartate (Fig. 4H– X). These changes, like those in the organic acids, were contingent upon FAT10’s covalent interactions (Fig. 4H– X) and were consistent under TNFα/IFNγ treatment conditions (Supplementary Fig S3H–X).

Following FAT10’s upregulation of lipid metabolism proteins, we performed targeted lipidomics on 1146 lipid species from 20 lipid classes (Fig. 4Y). 78 lipid species were positively associated with FAT10 expression as they were significantly and congruently upregulated (p < 0.05, fold change ≥ 1.5) in both FAT10/C and FAT10/FAT10kd comparisons. The heatmap in Fig. 4Z illustrates the relative fold-changes of these 78 lipids. Most of these lipid species (76/78) were triacylglycerols, whose accumulation relied on FAT10’s covalent interactions. Consistently, FAT10 mediated TNFα/IFNγ-induced triacylglycerol accumulation (Supplementary Fig S3Y–S3Z).

Furthermore, to evaluate FAT10’s impact on beta-oxidation, targeted profiling of 69 acylcarnitines was conducted (Fig. 4AA). FAT10 was positively associated with nine acylcarnitines of varying chain lengths, each showing significant upregulation (p < 0.05, fold change ≥ 1.5) in both FAT10/C and FAT10/FAT10kd comparisons. A heatmap of relative fold-changes for these acylcarnitines is provided in Fig. 4AB, showing that FAT10’s covalent interactions were necessary for their upregulation. With one exception, FAT10 consistently mediated the TNFα/IFNγ-induced accumulation of 13 acylcarnitines (Fig S3AB).

FAT10 promotes glycolysis through the upregulation of glycolytic transporters and enzymes

Our multi-omic analyses suggest that FAT10 plays a central role in metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells. We integrated and validated these findings with biochemical assays, beginning with the glycolysis pathway (Fig. 5A).

FAT10 upregulated protein expression across the pathway, from glucose transporters (SLC2A1 and SLC2A3) to mid-pathway enzymes (ALDOC and PGK1) and the terminal LDHA (Fig. 5A, red boxes). Immunoblotting confirmed the increased expression of SLC2A1, ALDOC, and LDHA, consistent with proteomics data. Interestingly, LDHB, which catalyzes the reverse conversion of lactate to pyruvate [53], was the only protein negatively associated with FAT10, aligning with FAT10’s role in promoting glycolysis. By contrast, GAPDH expression was not altered by FAT10 in our proteomics analysis, suggesting that FAT10 regulates specific glycolytic enzymes and affirming GAPDH’s suitability as a loading control for our immunoblot experiments.

Additionally, SLC2A1, ALDOA and LDHA interacted with FAT10 (Fig. 5A, purple boxes), with SLC2A1 and LDHA correspondingly identified as FAT10-stabilized targets (Fig. 3C). Congruently, FAT10 enhanced intracellular accumulation of pyruvate and lactate (Fig. 5A, green boxes). Complementing this static snapshot of FAT10-mediated glycolysis upregulation, real-time measurements of glycolytic proton efflux rate (glycoPER) showed elevated rates of basal glycolysis in FAT10-overexpressing cells (Fig. 5B–C). This trend persisted under iFAT10 conditions, indicating that FAT10 facilitates TNFα/IFNγ-induced glycolysis (Supplementary Fig S4A–B). Notably, the pronounced FAT10-induced glycolytic rate increase contrasts with the modest intracellular lactate elevation (Figs. 4B, 5A), likely reflecting efficient lactate export to prevent intracellular acidification: the Seahorse assay detects extracellular acidification from lactate efflux, whereas metabolomics measures intracellular lactate levels.

From analysis of FAT10ΔGG, FAT10’s protein associations relied on its covalent (SLC2A3, LDHA, LDHB) or non-covalent (SLC2A1, ALDOC, PGK1) interactions. However, FAT10-mediated metabolite accumulation ultimately required its covalent interactions.

FAT10 downregulates the pentose phosphate pathway, nucleotide metabolism and nucleotide-dependent pathways

After analyzing FAT10’s role in glycolysis, we next examined its effect on the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), a glycolysis subsidiary. In contrast to its upregulation of glycolysis, FAT10 downregulated the rate-limiting enzymes linking glycolysis to the PPP—G6PD [49] and TKT [54] (Fig. 6, top left panel, red boxes). This was validated by immunoblotting, and their downregulation depended on FAT10’s covalent interactions (Fig. 6, top left panel, FAT10ΔGG).

The PPP diverts glucose-derived carbons into ribose-5-phosphate (R5P), a precursor for nucleotide biosynthesis [49]. Accordingly, FAT10 also downregulated enzymes critical for nucleotide production, including those in purine biosynthesis and pyrimidine salvage, such as PFAS, RRM2, NME2, UCK2 and TYMP (Fig. 6, top right panel, red boxes). Immunoblotting confirmed downregulation of PFAS, NME2, and UCK2, and these effects generally required FAT10’s covalent interactions. Additionally, FAT10 interacted with NME1 and NME2P1 (Fig. 6, top right panel, purple box).

Although nucleotide biosynthesis relies on amino acids for nitrogen, FAT10's downregulation of nucleotide metabolism does not fully account for the FAT10-associated alterations in amino acid levels. This indicates that other pathways likely contribute more significantly to these changes, as further discussed in the Supplementary Discussion.

Beyond reducing nucleotide supply, FAT10 directly interacted with and downregulated proteins involved in nucleotide-demanding processes, including DNA-dependent cell cycle and DNA repair, as well as RNA-dependent transcription and translation (Fig. 6, lower panel). FAT10 specifically destabilized key proteins within these pathways, including RAB1B, ZC3H4, WDR75, DNAJB4, SRRM1 and RNPS1 (Fig. 6, lower panel, red proteins). Proteins such as HUS1, XRCC3, and DDX5 were negatively associated with FAT10, with immunoblots confirming their downregulation. HUS1, a component of the 9–1-1 checkpoint complex essential for DNA replication and repair [55], and XRCC3, involved in DNA double-strand break repair [56], were suppressed. FAT10 also downregulated DDX5, a key player in transcription, splicing, and ribosome biogenesis [57]. These downregulations appeared to depend on either non-covalent or covalent FAT10 interactions. FAT10’s effects on these proteins were consistent with its role in destabilizing DNA repair factors [35] and disrupting cell cycle checkpoints [27].

FAT10 remodels the TCA Cycle for α-ketoglutarate accumulation

We showed that FAT10 enhances glycolytic flux by upregulating glucose transporters and glycolytic enzymes, while restricting its diversion into PPP, nucleotide biosynthesis and nucleotide-dependent processes. This shift increases pyruvate, which is either converted to lactate for extrusion or directed into the TCA cycle. Hence, we next examined FAT10’s influence on the TCA cycle (Fig. 7).

FAT10 remodels the TCA cycle towards α-ketoglutarate accumulation. Integrated omics analysis comprising quantitative proteomics (red boxes), interactomics (purple boxes) and metabolomics (green boxes), with validatory immunoblotting of confident FAT10-associated proteins in the TCA cycle. Metabolomics panels labeled “Fig. 4A–G, O–P” are reproduced from Fig. 4 for integrative analysis. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01 compared to C; #P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01 for other indicated comparisons; by two-tailed Student's t-test

Pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is converted to acetyl-CoA by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC), which is activated by pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase (PDP) [58]. FAT10 interacts with the PDHB subunit of PDC and the PDP1 subunit of PDP, suggesting its regulatory role in acetyl-CoA production from pyruvate (Fig. 7, top, purple boxes).

Acetyl-CoA then enters the TCA cycle, where FAT10 upregulated key metabolites (Fig. 7, centre, green boxes). Notably, FAT10 expression led to a significant accumulation of α-KG, with a progressively lower FAT10-induced increase in metabolites further from α-KG (FAT10/C average fold-change: citrate, 2.17; α-KG, 4.26; succinate, 1.55; fumarate, 1.30; malate, 1.26) (Fig. 7). iFAT10 showed a similar trend (iFAT10/C average fold-change: citrate, 1.18; α-KG, 1.96; succinate, 1.87; fumarate, 1.09; malate, 1.12), indicating that FAT10 supports TNFα/IFNγ-induced elevation of α-KG levels (Supplementary Fig S3).

This accumulation may be driven by FAT10’s suppression of the rate-limiting enzyme OGDH in the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, which converts α-KG to succinyl-CoA (Fig. 7, red box and immunoblot). Furthermore, FAT10’s interaction with TCA cycle enzymes directly upstream and downstream of OGDH, including IDH3B (isocitrate dehydrogenase) and SUCLG1 (succinate-CoA ligase), suggests multiple regulatory points contributing to α-KG accumulation (Fig. 7, lower right purple boxes). Additionally, FAT10 interacts with proteins controlling the fate of α-KG, including SLC25A11, which exports α-KG from the mitochondria, and glutamate dehydrogenases GLUD1 and GLUD2, which reversibly convert α-KG to glutamate. These suggest alternative avenues for FAT10-driven α-KG accumulation or utilization of the accumulated α-KG.

FAT10-driven TCA cycle metabolite accumulation is unlikely due to anaplerotic reactions involving amino acids (Supplementary Discussion). The exception is aspartate, the only amino acid negatively associated with FAT10 (Fig. 7, bottom left). Aspartate, via aspartate aminotransferase, replenishes oxaloacetate, which is crucial for two reasons: oxaloacetate condenses with acetyl-CoA to produce citrate, supporting the TCA cycle and lipid biosynthesis, and is converted into phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) in a rate-limiting step of gluconeogenesis [59] by phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK). Consistently, the mitochondrial isozyme PCK2 is a FAT10-positive associated protein (Fig. 7, centre left, red box and immunoblot).

Overall, our data suggest that FAT10 truncates the TCA cycle to promote α-KG accumulation and could potentially contribute to glycolytic intermediate production through PCK2 upregulation. These processes largely depend on FAT10's covalent interactions except for PCK2 upregulation, which is mediated by non-covalent interactions (Fig. 7, FAT10ΔGG).

Once in the cytosol, citrate is converted to acetyl-CoA by phosphorylated-ATP-citrate lyase (p-ACLYSer455). Triacylglycerols serve as the primary storage form of cellular energy, accumulating within intracellular lipid droplets [61]. To confirm FAT10’s role in promoting triacylglycerol accumulation, we performed oil red O staining, which revealed increased neutral lipid droplet formation in FAT10-expressing cells (Fig. 8B–C). This effect depended on FAT10’s diglycine domain (Fig. 8B –C, FAT10ΔGG) and was consistent under TNFα/IFNγ treatment (Fig S4C–D), suggesting that FAT10 mediates TNFα/IFNγ-induced lipid droplet formation.

Metabolic stress enhances FAT10 transcription under inflammation, which in turn promotes survival of simultaneously inflamed and metabolically stressed cells

We have demonstrated that FAT10 plays a central role in reshaping metabolism-related pathways, which appear to promote energy uptake, production, storage, and conservation in HCT116 cells. This metabolic adaptation may confer cancer cells with a survival advantage under metabolic stress, a common feature of the tumor microenvironment. Hence, we hypothesized that FAT10 is activated under metabolic stress to alleviate this stress and enhance cancer survival.

Serum starvation, which simulates metabolic stress in the tumor microenvironment [62], significantly increased FAT10 expression by 3- to 5-fold, independent of proinflammatory cytokine treatment (Fig. 9A). This induction was reversed with serum supplementation. Changes in FAT10 mRNA levels suggested that this response was mediated by altered FAT10 transcription and activity of its upstream transcription factors, NF-κB and STAT3 [11, 63]. Consistently, serum starvation increased both total and active NF-κB and STAT3 levels in parallel with FAT10 expression, while serum supplementation reduced them (Fig. 9A, immunoblot).

To investigate the upstream signaling pathways driving serum starvation-induced FAT10 transcription, we focused on 5' AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key regulator of energy homeostasis under metabolic stress [64]. Inhibition of AMPK with Compound C [65] diminished serum starvation-induced FAT10 expression along with STAT3 activity, suggesting AMPK acts upstream of FAT10 (Supplementary Fig S5A). However, AMPK activation with the selective agonist A769662 [66] suggested otherwise, diminishing FAT10 levels in a dose- and time- dependent manner (Supplementary Fig S5B, S5C). Given that Compound C, despite its widespread use as an AMPK antagonist, is a non-selective, broad kinase inhibitor [67], these findings potentially suggest that kinases upstream of STAT3, rather than AMPK, facilitate serum deprivation-induced FAT10 transcription. AMPK activation likely reduced metabolic stress, thereby attenuating the magnitude of serum starvation-induced FAT10 expression. This hypothesis is summarized in Supplementary Fig S5D.

To confirm that serum deprivation’s impact on FAT10 expression resulted from metabolic stress, rather than the withdrawal of non-metabolite serum components, we analysed specific serum metabolites that might influence FAT10 expression. Palmitate (C16:0), the most abundant saturated fatty acid in circulation [68], is incorporated into FAT10-upregulated triacylglycerols and undergoes increased beta-oxidation when FAT10 is induced (Fig. 4Z, S3Z, S3AB). Palmitate treatment suppressed serum starvation-induced FAT10 expression in a time-dependent manner, suggesting that its absence triggers FAT10 upregulation (Fig. 9B). Additionally, given FAT10's role in promoting lipid biosynthesis and beta-oxidation during TNFα/IFNγ treatment (Figs. 4, 8, S3), we investigated whether inhibiting these processes would induce compensatory upregulation of FAT10. As hypothesized, inhibitors of de novo fatty acid biosynthesis (TOFA [69]) and beta-oxidation (Etomoxir) induced FAT10 upregulation under proinflammatory cytokine treatment conditions and their effects were additive (Fig. 9C).

Given that serum contains glucose and FAT10 may support glucose-deprived cancer cells by enhancing glucose uptake and glycolysis (Fig. 5), we next examined the impact of glucose deprivation on FAT10 expression (Fig. 9D). Regardless of proinflammatory cytokine treatment, glucose withdrawal induced a 1.5- to 2.3-fold increase in FAT10 levels which was reversed by glucose supplementation (Fig. 9D, upper panel). Similar to serum starvation, this effect corresponded with NF-κB and STAT3 activity and expression (Fig. 9D, lower panel). To determine whether glucose deprivation-induced FAT10 expression was due to the lack of glucose availability or its utilization, we employed the glycolysis inhibitor 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2DG). By inhibiting the rate-limiting step of glycolysis, 2DG blocks glucose usage, leading to an accumulation of free glucose in the culture media over time. 2DG treatment reduced FAT10 expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 9E), indicating that FAT10 expression responds to cellular glucose availability.

Interestingly, unlike serum, palmitate and glucose deprivation, glutamine deprivation reduced FAT10 expression. Conversely, glutamine supplementation or blocking its conversion to glutamate with BPTES increased FAT10 levels, suggesting that elevated glutamine availability promotes FAT10 expression (Supplementary Fig S6A–B).

Given that metabolic stress, especially under inflammation, increases FAT10 expression, we hypothesized that FAT10 provides a survival advantage to cancer cells under these conditions. HCT116 cells expressing either shCtrl or shFAT10 were subjected to four different treatments combining proinflammatory cytokines and/or serum starvation (Fig. 9F–G). Cell survival was assessed using two methods: cleaved caspase-3 as an apoptosis marker (Fig. 9F) and the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay for cell viability (Fig. 9G). Knockdown of FAT10 had the most significant effect under concurrent TNFα/IFNγ and serum starvation treatment, with FAT10 decreasing apoptosis (Fig. 9F, lanes 7–8) and improving cell viability by 14% (Fig. 9G). In contrast, under TNFα/IFNγ treatment alone, FAT10 was associated with a smaller decrease in apoptosis (Fig. 9F, lanes 5–6, lower band) and an 8% increase in viability (Fig. 9G). As expected, FAT10 knockdown had no impact on apoptosis or viability in control and serum starvation-only conditions, as basal FAT10 expression is negligible without proinflammatory stimuli (Fig. 9F, lanes 1–4; Fig. 9G).

These results suggest that FAT10 is upregulated in response to metabolic stress along with proinflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting cancer survival under these combined conditions.

Discussion

FAT10 is significantly overexpressed in various cancers [17], serving as a key regulator of gene activity at the posttranslational level. Induced by inflammation, it interacts extensively with proteins across diverse cellular pathways, orchestrating their activities to support its central role. However, existing studies fail to uncover this broader systemic function by characterizing FAT10's individual interactions in isolation. Interactome studies attempt to bridge this knowledge gap by cataloging FAT10-interacting proteins, but they too fall short by neglecting FAT10’s effects on these targets. Cancer research on FAT10 predominantly portrays it as an “indirect oncogene”, attributing its oncogenicity to the stabilization of other influential oncogenes [29, 32, 34] or destabilization of known tumor suppressors [28]. This perspective obscures our understanding of FAT10’s physiology in relation to its pathophysiology, especially since FAT10’s interactome comprises largely non-cancer-related proteins [22–24]. In this study, we employed an integrative multi-omics approach to offer a paradigm shift in understanding FAT10’s function. By providing a systems-level analysis, we illuminate FAT10’s coordinated regulation of metabolism-related processes (Fig. 10A), which collectively drive a survival-oriented cellular phenotype in cancer, especially in the context of metabolic stress (Fig. 10B). Figure 10 presents models summarizing our findings, revealing novel insights discussed herein.

Proposed models of FAT10 induction by metabolic stress and its influence on metabolism-related pathways. A Summary of FAT10’s influence on central carbon metabolism and its affiliated pathways. FAT10 upregulates (red up arrow) glycolysis and lipid metabolism, while downregulating (blue down arrow) the pentose phosphate pathway, nucleotide metabolism and nucleotide-dependent processes. At key junctions of these interlinked pathways, FAT10 also blocked (blue cross) metabolic flux by downregulating enzyme expression or activity. These include G6PD and TKT that bridge glycolysis to the pentose phosphate pathway, OGDH at the rate-limiting step of the TCA cycle and ACLY for conversion of citrate to acetyl-CoA. B Proposed model of FAT10’s responsiveness to metabolic stress and inflammation, and its role in shifting cells from a replication- or growth-oriented state to a survival-focused state by triggering global changes in metabolism-related pathways, as supported by our multi-omics and validation data

Firstly, our study uncovers FAT10’s oncogenic role in promoting cancer survival under metabolic stress. Four key observations support this: (1) FAT10 reprograms metabolism-related pathways to cope with metabolic stress by enhancing energy and resource uptake, production, storage and conservation (Fig. 10B). Consistent with studies showing that FAT10 indirectly promotes glycolysis by stabilizing other oncogenes [33, 34], we further demonstrate that FAT10 directly supports the Warburg effect by stabilizing and/or upregulating glycolytic transporters and enzymes to enhance glucose uptake and usage (Fig. 5, S4A–B). Additionally, we show that FAT10 drives de novo triacylglycerol biosynthesis, accumulation and storage in lipid droplets (Fig. 8, S4C–D), mirroring its lipid-accumulating role under physiological conditions in mice [15] and porcine adipocytes [16]. Beyond lipid biosynthesis, FAT10 generally upregulated β-oxidation (Fig. 4AA–AB, S3AA–AB), consistent with fatty acid oxidation's established role in cancer survival under metabolic stress [70]. FAT10 also promotes the accumulation of primarily essential amino acids (Fig. 4H– X, S3H–X), likely through downregulating amino acid-consuming pathways, particularly translation (Fig. 1E). Moreover, FAT10’s destabilization of target proteins may facilitate amino acid recycling, as evidenced by its negative association with proteins bearing amino acid-rich domains such as CHORDC1 (cysteine- and histidine-rich), RNPS1 (serine-rich), and SH3BGRL2 (glutamate-rich) (Fig. 1D). FAT10’s role in energy and resource conservation is further underscored by its coordinated downregulation of anabolic pathways essential for replication and growth (Fig. 10B). (2) While FAT10 is presumed to be upregulated by inflammation in cancer [11, 18, 63], we reveal that its expression is also highly responsive to metabolic stress. We demonstrate that serum starvation induces FAT10 transcription (Fig. 9A), in line with previous findings that serum deprivation is necessary for FAT10 induction in mice hepatoma cells [71]. This effect appears to be driven by the withdrawal of serum-derived metabolites, including glucose and palmitate (Fig. 9B, 9D). Furthermore, metabolic stress-induced FAT10 transcription is likely mediated by enhanced NF-κB and STAT3 activity (Fig. 9A, 9D), consistent with prior reports of their activation under metabolic stress [72, 73]. Inhibition of lipid biosynthesis and β-oxidation pathways under TNFα/IFNγ-mediated inflammatory conditions also triggered FAT10 expression (Fig. 9C), suggesting that FAT10 may play an important role in supporting these pathways during inflammation. (3) We discovered that AMPK activation, which restores energy balance under metabolic stress [64], reduced FAT10 expression (Fig S5B–C), validating FAT10 as a metabolic stress marker under inflammation. (4) FAT10 enhanced cell survival under concurrent inflammatory and metabolic stress (Fig. 9F–G). Therefore, through our holistic assessment of FAT10’s induction and activity in a cancer model, we have elucidated FAT10’s oncogenic role as a critical nexus in regulating metabolism-related pathways, facilitating cancer survival under metabolic stress.

Interestingly, FAT10-associated proteins within each pathway were generally up- or downregulated in a manner that promoted or suppressed the respective pathway, with the notable exception of ACLY in lipid metabolism (Fig. 8, 10A). While FAT10 upregulated other lipid metabolism enzymes, triacylglycerols, and lipid droplets, both total ACLY and its active phosphorylated form (p-ACLYSer455) were downregulated. This apparent discrepancy may reflect a metabolic bottleneck strategy employed by FAT10. ACLY is the key enzyme converting cytosolic citrate to acetyl-CoA, which serves as a crucial substrate for both fatty acid synthesis [74] and protein acetylation [75]. By simultaneously constraining ACLY-mediated acetyl-CoA production while upregulating downstream lipid biosynthetic enzymes, FAT10 could preferentially channel the limited acetyl-CoA pool toward lipid synthesis, at the expense of competing acetylation processes. This coordinated regulation could have several important implications. First, restricted acetyl-CoA availability for histone acetylation could promote chromatin condensation and epigenetically suppress gene expression—consistent with our observation that FAT10 curtails transcription and translation. Supporting this, FAT10 was positively associated with the deacetylase SIRT5 [76] (Fig. 1D), and interacted with the deacetylases HDAC1 and HDAC2 (Supplementary Table S1). Previous studies have also reported interactions between FAT10 and HDAC6, as well as the acetylation of FAT10 itself [77]. Second, this metabolic bottleneck—together with FAT10-enhanced glycolytic flux and TCA cycle truncation at OGDHC—may contribute to the observed accumulation of α-ketoglutarate, a metabolite with multiple roles in cancer metabolism and oxidative stress reduction [78, 79]. Thus, the downregulation of ACLY—despite upregulation of other lipid metabolism proteins—suggests that FAT10 may employ a metabolic bottleneck strategy to tightly control acetyl-CoA supply, with potential consequences for both lipid synthesis and acetylation-dependent processes. Future studies may further investigate this hypothesis.

Secondly, our systems-level analysis potentially reveals FAT10’s role in reprogramming cancer cells from a replication- or growth-oriented state to a survival-focused one (Fig. 10B). This aligns with FAT10 being a responder to stress, triggered not only by inflammatory and metabolic stress as we have shown here, but also by viral [80, 81] and bacterial [63, 82] infections as well as genotoxic stress from chemotherapeutics [83], radiation [35] or DNA repair deficiency [84]. In addition to FAT10’s recruitment under stress, FAT10 also promotes survival under stress as shown by our data. This corroborates FAT10’s well-documented role of conferring resistance to multiple chemotherapeutic classes, including antimetabolites [85, 86], anthracyclines [87], and alkylating-like agents [83, 88]. While previously attributed to oncogene stabilization [86], our findings suggest direct mechanisms: FAT10 suppresses replication and growth pathways—the same targets of antiproliferative chemotherapeutics. This potentially induces a protective quiescent state. Additionally, FAT10's enhancement of lipid metabolism and lipid droplet formation may contribute to chemoresistance through drug sequestration [89] and oxidative or endoplasmic reticulum stress mitigation [90]. Furthermore, FAT10’s downregulation of DNA damage response and cell cycle checkpoint proteins—consistent with prior reports of it destabilizing PCNA [35], diminishing p53 transcriptional activity [63] and delocalizing MAD2 [27]—may enable continued survival despite DNA damage, exacerbate genomic instability and increase the likelihood of pro-oncogenic mutations developing, including those conferring chemoresistance [91]. Hence, our findings provide novel insights into FAT10’s role as a prosurvival oncogene and the cellular phenotype it drives, which potentially underlies FAT10-associated chemoresistance.

Beyond promoting survival, our model reveals FAT10's strategic deprioritization of replication as a tradeoff, reflecting the precedence of survival over replication under adverse conditions and limited resources (Fig. 10B). Our data indicates FAT10's coordinated downregulation of replication-related pathways, particularly those governing nucleotide supply and demand. Moreover, this tension between survival and replication is further reflected in our lipidomics analysis. FAT10 favors the diversion of fatty acids into triacylglycerol accumulation in stress-responsive lipid droplets [92] over phospholipid synthesis required for membrane formation and proliferation [93] (Fig. 4Y, 4Z, 8)—a compromise known to restrict neoplastic division [94]. Specifically, FAT10's upregulation of DGAT1 (Fig. 8A), which converts diacylglycerol to triacylglycerol, suggests reduced diversion of diacylglycerol into phospholipid synthesis. Additionally, studies examining FAT10’s impact on cancer proliferation in vitro have yielded conflicting results. While some report that FAT10 does not enhance proliferation [88, 95], others indicate otherwise [27–29]. Our data supports the notion that FAT10 does not directly promote proliferation, but may do so indirectly by enhancing genomic instability [27] or altering the stability of oncogenes [29] and tumor suppressors [28].

Thirdly, while conducted in a cancer model, our study findings may provide insights into FAT10's physiological role. This is particularly relevant since FAT10 overexpressed in patient tumors is neither mutated nor truncated [18], and the wildtype FAT10 sequence used in our study effectively rewired metabolism, suggesting this feature of FAT10 is not cancer-specific. This raises a fundamental question: why would evolution preserve a protein supporting oncogenic survival? We propose that FAT10 originated as an immunometabolic survival factor protecting host cells during infection, where inflammation and metabolic stress coincide [96]. Supporting this, metabolic stress alone enhanced FAT10 expression but this effect was most pronounced under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 9A, 9D), suggesting FAT10 evolved not to respond to general metabolic stress but specifically to metabolic stress associated with inflammation. Furthermore, the FAT10-induced metabolic remodeling we uncovered parallels immunometabolic changes in activated and inflamed immune or non-immune cells: increased lipid droplet formation with innate immune functions [1] and enhanced Warburg-like metabolism now recognized in infected cells [97]. Moreover, pathogens often hijack host gene expression machinery and cell cycle processes for replication [98], but FAT10’s coordinated downregulation of replication and growth pathways may serve as a multi-faceted defense mechanism to counter such subversion. These FAT10-induced changes we elucidated complements FAT10’s known immune defense roles in MHC class I antigen presentation [13] and pathogen degradation via xenophagy [14]. Thus, cancer may have co-opted FAT10 and its immunometabolic functions to survive similar stressors in the tumor microenvironment as infected cells, suggesting new directions for investigating FAT10's role in cellular survival during infection.

Furthermore, considering FAT10’s role in immune defense may provide insights into its activities that appear unconventional in typical cancer contexts. While nutrient deprivation typically induces FAT10 (Fig. 9), glutamine abundance increases FAT10 expression instead (Supplementary Fig S6A). Consequently, FAT10 downregulates the cancer-associated glutamine uptake receptor SLC1A5 [99] (Supplementary Fig S6C). This response may represent an antiviral defense, as viruses induce and exploit elevated glutamine and SLC1A5 for infection and replication [98, 100]. In cancers overexpressing FAT10, alternative mechanisms likely upregulate SLC1A5 or other glutamine transporters [99], counteracting FAT10's suppression and maintaining high glutamine levels to support elevated FAT10 expression.

Beyond advancing our understanding of FAT10’s global function, our study introduces a novel, high-throughput methodology for classifying FAT10’s effects and interaction types with its targets. We identified FAT10-stabilized and -destabilized targets simultaneously by integrating FAT10 association and interaction data. While we identified 13 such targets, additional targets likely exist especially among proteomics-identified FAT10-associated proteins not detected in interactomics. For example, ACLY's negative association with FAT10 was identified through proteomics but it was not detected in our interactomics, despite its recognition in other FAT10 interactome studies [22, 23]. Future studies are needed to validate such targets. Notably, while we focused on identifying stabilized and destabilized targets, these potentially represent a minority of FAT10’s influence. Among the 524 FAT10-interacting proteins uncovered through interactomics, quantitative proteomics data were available for 376 (72%), yet only 13 (3%) proteins correlated with FAT10 expression (Supplementary Table S1). This surprisingly low proportion indicates that FAT10’s primary influence on most interacting proteins may involve delocalization or yet unknown mechanisms that do not alter protein stability. Nevertheless, our findings on FAT10’s stabilizing and destabilizing effects on pathways align with its known delocalizing functions [27], suggesting that these distinct regulatory mechanisms work synergistically to modulate pathway activation or disruption.

To determine whether FAT10’s covalent and non-covalent interactions have distinct cellular roles across different functional protein categories, we employed the FAT10ΔGG mutant in our multi-omics analysis. Our investigation revealed that FAT10's effects on metabolism-related pathways involve both interaction types, but with a clear hierarchy: while both covalent and non-covalent interactions contribute to FAT10's stabilizing or destabilizing effects (Fig. 3) and its associations (Supplementary Fig S1) with metabolism-related proteins, covalent interactions were ultimately necessary for all observed metabolite changes, spanning organic acids, amino acids, triacylglycerols, and acylcarnitines (Fig. 4). This insight provides a rationale for prioritizing future mechanistic studies on covalently FAT10ylated metabolic targets, as covalent modifications are the primary drivers of FAT10’s functional metabolic outcomes. Moreover, our interactome analysis required separate mass spectrometry runs for each condition due to technical limitations, in contrast to our concurrent approach in the quantitative proteomics experiment. This limits our ability to make definitive comparisons of unique binding partners between conditions. Nevertheless, we successfully identified non-covalent interaction partners through FAT10ΔGG pulldown, along with total interacting proteins involving FAT10 and iFAT10. This analysis revealed that FAT10’s non-covalent interactions are broadly distributed across diverse functional protein pathways (Fig S2A), similar to the pattern for wildtype FAT10 (Fig. 2D), indicating that non-covalent interactions are involved in a wide range of FAT10-regulated pathways, rather than being exclusive to select ones.

Furthermore, while our analysis attributes FAT10’s influence to either covalent or non-covalent interactions based on threshold significance and fold-change values, it is notable that FAT10 can interact with certain proteins through both covalent and non-covalent interactions, sometimes with opposing effects on their stability. For example, covalent FAT10ylation destabilizes OTUB1, while non-covalent FAT10 binding promotes its stability [30]. Interestingly, our data suggest this phenomenon extends beyond isolated proteins to multiple proteins and metabolites, as evidenced by opposing effects between wildtype FAT10 and FAT10ΔGG overexpression: FAT10 elevated acylcarnitine and triacylglycerol levels while FAT10ΔGG often decreased them instead (Fig. 4Z, 4AB). At the protein level, several covalent-dependent FAT10-associated proteins showed inverse relationships with FAT10 versus FAT10ΔGG. These include CNNM3, AFDN, GLG1, CDCA7, CRABP2 and SUMO2 for FAT10-upregulated proteins (Supplementary Fig S1B, middle left panel) and ABHD10, CKB, TPX2 and TYMP for FAT10-downregulated proteins (Supplementary Fig S1B, far right panel). Immunoblotting also identified these bidirectional effects for LDHA (Fig. 5), G6PD (Fig. 6), OGDH (Fig. 7) and SCD (Fig. 8). These systemic opposing effects raise the possibility that FAT10's non-covalent interactions serve a homeostatic function, potentially modulating or reversing the effects of covalent FAT10ylation when required—an intriguing possibility that warrants further investigation. Notably, this homeostatic mechanism may be favored under low-energy conditions, since covalent modifications require ATP whereas non-covalent interactions do not.

By providing a comprehensive systems-level overview of FAT10’s function, our study paves the way for future reductive investigations that could validate and extend these findings through focused analysis of specific metabolism-related pathways, proteins, and metabolites. Such studies may explore FAT10’s covalent interactions not only by mutating the diglycine domain, but also by examining the E1–E2–E3 enzyme cascade required for FAT10ylation [25, 26] and by seeking to identify substrate binding sites. For non-covalent interactions, mapping the interaction domains on both FAT10 and its binding partners would help to address the current gap in our mechanistic understanding of FAT10's non-covalent regulatory functions. Additionally, conducting such mechanistic investigations in multiple cell models may help assess the generalizability of our findings beyond the HCT116 context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study represents the first systems-level interrogation of FAT10 using multi-omics, establishing its broad oncogenic role as an immunometabolic survival factor activated by metabolic stress under inflammation. We show that FAT10 potentially orchestrates a fundamental shift in cellular priorities—from replication to survival—enabling adaptation to stress conditions. Our analysis contextualizes wildtype FAT10's previously fragmented functions—including metabolic rewiring, cell cycle checkpoint disruption and DNA repair inhibition—within a unified survival programming framework, suggesting that while these mechanisms evolved as adaptive responses to acute inflammation, their sustained activation under chronic inflammatory conditions may promote a prolonged cellular survival state that drives cancer initiation and progression. These insights also open new therapeutic avenues: either by disrupting FAT10-driven metabolic reprogramming, or by targeting survival-promoting oncogenes like FAT10 [27, 101] alongside replication-targeting chemotherapeutics as a combination therapy addressing the twin drives underlying cancer’s persistence. Finally, by bridging FAT10’s oncogenic role with its immune origins, we provide a foundation for understanding FAT10’s immune functions and how cancer co-opts immune stress response mechanisms.
