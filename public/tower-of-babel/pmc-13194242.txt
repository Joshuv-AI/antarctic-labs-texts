Efficacy and safety of recombinant human epidermal growth factor combined with fractional carbon dioxide laser for facial atrophic acne scars: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: Shuyu Zhou, Chenqi Guo, Jingbo Zhai, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe combination of recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rhEGF) and fractional carbon dioxide (CO2) laser therapy is increasingly used in the treatment of facial atrophic acne scars.ObjectiveThis study aimed to systematically assess the efficacy and safety of rhEGF combined with fractional CO2 laser therapy for managing facial atrophic acne scars.MethodsPubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI (China National Knowledge Infrastructure), Wanfang, SinoMed, and VIP were searched from inception to July 29, 2025. Parallel-group or split-face randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing fractional CO2 laser therapy + rhEGF with fractional CO2 laser therapy for treating atrophic acne scars were considered for inclusion. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk-of-bias tool. The meta-analysis based on fixed or random effects models was carried out for Echelle d’Evaluation Clinique des Cicatrices d’acne (ECCA) scores, Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS) scores, transepidermal water loss (TEWL), etc. It was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024599311).ResultsTwelve RCTs involving 1,044 patients were included, and all trials were conducted in China. Follow-up durations across studies ranged from 1 week to 12 months. Compared with fractional CO₂ laser therapy alone, the combination therapy significantly reduced ECCA scores by a mean of 11.14 points (95% CI: -16.82 to -5.45, P = 0.0001) and VSS scores by a mean of 1.54 points (95% CI: -2.55 to -0.54, P = 0.003). The combination therapy demonstrated advantages in improving the durations of crust formation. The incidence of adverse events, particularly hyperpigmentation (RR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.20 to 0.86; P = 0.02), was significantly lower in the combination therapy group. According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate for most outcomes.ConclusionThis study suggests that rhEGF combined with fractional CO₂ laser therapy may be beneficial for treating atrophic acne scars. However, because of the low to moderate certainty of evidence according to GRADE assessment, more robust, large-scale RCTs are needed to confirm these findings and define optimal treatment parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10103-026-04881-w.

Introduction

Acne is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit, and scarring occurs in up to 95% of acne patients, often persisting into adulthood and significantly affecting self-esteem [1–4]. These scars are classified into atrophic and hypertrophic/keloidal, with atrophic scars being the most prevalent [5].

Increasingly, acne scarring is being treated with fractional CO2 laser therapy [1]. Compared with full ablative lasers, fractional lasers have fewer side effects and quicker healing [1]. The 2022 International Consensus Recommendations confirmed that fractional CO2 laser therapy effectively treated acne scars [6]. Achieving satisfactory outcomes for scarred patients requires repeated fractional laser therapy; nonetheless, this modality may induce complications, including erythema, edema, infection, and postinflammatory hyperpigmentation [7]. Consequently, clinical research has shifted to reducing the adverse effects of fractional CO2 laser therapy while enhancing their therapeutic effectiveness.

Recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rhEGF) is a growth factor produced using recombinant DNA technology. It promotes key processes in wound healing, including cell proliferation, collagen synthesis, and tissue remodeling, which can proficiently initiate and accelerate the restoration of damaged skin tissue with a remarkably low prevalence of detrimental effects [8]. Its capacity to enhance re-epithelialization and barrier repair is particularly attractive for use in individuals with darker phototypes (Fitzpatrick types III-V), as these actions may potentially lower the risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)—a frequent complication following ablative laser procedures in such populations [9, 10]. Experimental clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of topical rhEGF in improving acne scar conditions [11]. In recent years, several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have investigated fractional CO2 laser therapy combined with rhEGF versus fractional CO2 laser therapy for treating facial atrophic acne scars. The results of these trials suggest that adding rhEGF after fractional CO2 laser therapy accelerates skin regeneration and wound healing [12–23]. However, the existing RCTs on this topic are predominantly single-center studies with limited sample sizes, and their findings have not been quantitatively synthesized. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized available RCTs to evaluate the efficacy and safety of fractional CO2 laser combined with rhEGF for atrophic acne scars.

Methods

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement [24]. It was registered on PROSPERO (ID: CRD42024599311).

Search strategy

Two authors (SYZ and CQG) independently searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, SinoMed, and VIP Database from inception to July 29, 2025, to identify potentially eligible studies. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Supplementary Material 1. The search keywords included acne scars, atrophic scars, carbon dioxide (CO2) lasers, fractional laser treatment, fractional CO2 laser therapy, recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rhEGF), and randomized controlled trials (RCTs). No restrictions were placed on publication date or language. Moreover, the references of eligible studies were manually searched to identify additional eligible studies. Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched to identify potential grey literatures.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Types of studies

Parallel-group and split-face RCTs were included. Quasi-randomized RCTs were excluded.

Types of patients

Patients with atrophic acne scars were included without restrictions on age, sex, nationality, or race. The diagnosis of atrophic acne scars was based on internationally accepted diagnostic criteria, such as the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acne (2019 revised edition) [25] and the Consensus on the Treatment of Acne Scars in China (2021) [26]. Studies were included only if they addressed atrophic scars, and those on hypertrophic or mixed scar types were excluded.

Types of interventions

The experimental interventions included fractional CO2 laser therapy combined with rhEGF, while the control interventions included fractional CO2 laser therapy with or without normal saline. Concomitant topical agents (e.g., mupirocin, erythromycin, saline) were allowed only if balanced between arms, or treated as part of the intervention/control. The laser wavelength was 10,600 nm for all procedures, with no restrictions on the manufacturer, power, pulse energy, or spot diameter of the fractional laser devices. The formulation of the rhEGF was also unrestricted.

Types of outcomes

The pre-specified primary outcome was the change in acne scar severity measured by the ECCA grading scale. Secondary outcomes included the VSS score, durations of crust formation, crust removal, erythema, and pain, skin hydration, transepidermal water loss (TEWL), inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL-10), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) score, and the incidence of adverse events. The detailed information is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Study selection

Potentially eligible studies were collected from the comprehensive literature search. Two reviewers (SYZ and CQG) independently deleted ineligible studies by checking titles and abstracts according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of the remaining studies were read to identify included studies. The process of screening eligible studies was presented by the PRISMA flow diagram. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (JBZ).

Data collection and risk of bias assessment

Two authors (SYZ and CQG) independently extracted related data, such as authors, publication year, interventions. Two authors (JBZ and YZ) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool [27]. The evaluation items included random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases. Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (SYZ).

GRADE evaluation

The GRADE system was used to evaluate the quality of evidence for outcomes [28]. The degradation factors mainly include five aspects: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other considerations. In the present review, the certainty of evidence was most commonly downgraded due to concerns in two domains: (1) Risk of bias, as most included trials had unclear or high risk in allocation concealment and blinding of participants and personnel; and (2) Inconsistency, indicated by substantial statistical heterogeneity (I²>75%) in many pooled estimates. Less frequently, evidence was downgraded for imprecision when confidence intervals were wide and crossed a minimally important difference, or for indirectness due to population or intervention differences. No serious publication bias was detected, though this could not be formally assessed for most outcomes due to the limited number of studies. The quality definition is classified as extremely low quality evidence (+), low quality evidence (++), moderate quality evidence (+++), and high quality evidence (++++). Disagreements were resolved by consultation with a third reviewer (JBZ).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.4. For dichotomous outcomes, the risk ratio (RR) was calculated. For continuous outcomes, the MD with a 95% CI was applied. The χ² test and I² statistic were utilized to assess heterogeneity across the studies. If no substantial heterogeneity was detected (P > 0.10 and I²<50%), a fixed-effects model was employed for the meta-analysis. Otherwise, a random-effects model was applied. If studies assessed outcomes at multiple time points, we extracted data from the primary assessment time point as specified in each trial. Where zeros cause problems with computation of effects or standard errors, 0.5 was added to all cells for that study. A funnel plot was generated to investigate publication bias if more than ten studies were included in a meta-analysis. When using the generic inverse variance method, the imputed correlation coefficient, or any assumption for split-face trials, was conservatively treated as independent if the correlation was not available.

Results

Literature search

Based on the search criteria, 119 articles were initially retrieved, with 60 duplicates subsequently eliminated. After screening the titles and abstracts, 43 studies were selected as potentially relevant for a thorough evaluation. However, 29 of these studies were excluded due to discrepancies in study design or interventional approaches. Furthermore, two additional studies were omitted because they did not meet the criteria for RCTs. Ultimately, 12 studies were included in the meta-analysis. Quality assessment

Figure 2 presents the results of risk of bias assessment for the 12 included RCTs. Among them, two studies failed to elaborate on the methods utilized for random sequence generation, leading to an unclear risk of bias categorization. Conversely, the remaining studies clearly described their random sequence generation methods, resulting in a low risk of bias. One study mentioned subject and evaluator blindness, and two studies mentioned evaluator blinding. The remaining studies did not mention blinding, and the risk of bias was unclear. No studies reported allocation concealment, resulting in an unclear risk of bias. All articles provided thorough reports on study protocols and outcome data, thus earning a low risk of bias. However, for other potential biases, the risk was considered unclear across all studies.Fig. 2Summary of the risk of bias

Meta-analyzed results

ECCA scores

Figure 3a presents seven studies reported ECCA scores [29] with a total of 586 patients. Due to high heterogeneity (I²=97%, P < 0.00001), a random-effects model was used. The pooled results showed that rhEGF combined with fractional CO₂ laser therapy significantly reduced ECCA scores compared to laser therapy (MD=-11.14, 95% CI: -16.82 to -5.45, P = 0.0001). This improvement represents a clinically relevant reduction in scar severity.

VSS scores

Figure 3b presents three studies reported VSS scores [30] for 291 patients. A random-effects model was used due to the heterogeneity analysis results (I2 = 96%, P < 0.00001). The meta-analysis results revealed a notable reduction in the VSS score when rhEGF was combined with fractional CO₂ laser therapy compared to fractional CO2 laser therapy (MD=-1.54, 95% CI: -2.55 to -0.54, P = 0.003). This difference reflects a modest but meaningful improvement in scar appearance.Fig. 3Forest plots for ECCA scores (a) and VSS scores (b)

Forest plots for ECCA scores (a) and VSS scores (b)

The time of crust formation (days)

Six studies reported data on the time of crust formation for a total of 566 patients. Due to substantial heterogeneity (I²=98%, P < 0.00001), a random-effects model was used. The meta-analysis showed that the time of crust formation was significantly shorter in the combination therapy group (MD =-1.57, days, 95% CI: -2.29 to -0.86, P < 0.0001). This corresponds to crust formation occurring approximately 1.5 days earlier in the combination group.

The time of crust removal (days)

Seven studies reported data on the time of crust removal for a total of 697 patients. Given the substantial heterogeneity observed (I²=96%, P < 0.00001), a random-effects model was adopted for the analysis. Our meta-analysis revealed that, compared with fractional CO₂ laser therapy, combining rhEGF and fractional CO₂ laser therapy significantly reduced the time required for crust removal (MD=-2.19, days, 95% CI: -3.08 to -1.30, P < 0.00001). This corresponds to crust removal occurring approximately 2 days earlier in the combination group.

Erythema duration (days)

Five studies reported data on erythema duration for a total of 447 patients. A random effects model was selected based on the heterogeneity results (I2=100%, P < 0.00001). The combination therapy significantly shortened erythema duration compared to fractional CO₂ laser therapy (MD=-4.79, days, 95% CI: -8.29 to -1.30, P = 0.007). This represents a reduction in erythema duration of about 4.8 days.

Pain duration (days)

Three studies reported data on pain duration for a total of 296 patients. Due to the lack of heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, P = 0.94), a fixed-effects model was used. According to our meta-analysis, rhEGF combined with fractional CO₂ laser therapy significantly reduced the duration of pain (MD=-0.63, days, 95% CI: -0.75 to -0.50, P < 0.00001). This represents a reduction in pain duration of about 0.6 days, or roughly 14 h.

Skin hydration (arbitrary units, AU)

Five studies reported data on skin hydration for a total of 459 patients. A random-effects model was selected based on the heterogeneity results (I2 = 78%, P = 0.001). According to our meta-analysis, rhEGF combined with fractional CO₂ laser therapy significantly increased skin hydration compared to fractional CO₂ laser therapy (MD = 8.57, AU, 95% CI: 6.25 to 10.90, P < 0.00001). This improvement suggests enhanced skin barrier function, which is particularly important after ablative laser procedures.

TEWL [g/(h·m2)

Three studies reported data on TEWL for a total of 308 patients. A random-effects model was selected based on the heterogeneity results (I2 = 96%, P < 0.00001). According to our meta-analysis, the combination of rhEGF with fractional CO₂ laser therapy reduced percutaneous water loss compared to fractional CO₂ laser therapy, and the difference was significant statistically (MD=-5.27, g/(h·m2), 95% CI: -8.21 to -2.33, P = 0.0004).

TNF-α levels (ng/L)

Two studies reported data on levels of TNF-α for a total of 190 patients. A random-effects model was selected based on the heterogeneity assessment outcomes (I2 = 98%, P < 0.00001). There was no significant difference between the treatment group and the control group (MD=-7.67, ng/L, 95% CI: -15.65 to 0.32, P = 0.06).

IL-10 levels (ng/L)

Two studies reported data on levels of IL-10 for a total of 190 patients. A random-effects model was selected based on the heterogeneity results (I2 = 99%, P < 0.00001). There was no significant difference between the treatment group and the control group (MD=-2.09, ng/L, 95% CI: -4.69 to 0.52, P = 0.12).

The forest plots for these secondary outcomes are presented in Supplementary Material 3.

DLQI score

Figure 4 presents two studies examined daily activities, symptoms and feelings, work and study, leisure and entertainment, interpersonal relationships, and the DLQI score [31] before and after treatment in 172 patients. Overall, the DLQI score decreased (i.e., improved), with no interstudy heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Therefore, a fixed effects model was used. Compared with fractional CO₂ laser therapy, rhEGF combined with fractional CO₂ laser therapy significantly improved quality of life after treatment.Fig. 4Forest plot for the DLQI score

Adverse events

Figure 5 presents ten studies reported adverse events, including hyperpigmentation, erythema, infection, edema, telangiectasia, pain, skin sensitivity, dryness and tightness, and scar hyperplasia, totaling 1,756 adverse events reports. All reported adverse events were mild and transient, no serious adverse events reported in any of the included trials. Hyperpigmentation occurred in 3.21% (28/871) of combination-therapy patients versus 7.86% (69/878) of controls, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction (ARR) of 5% and a relative risk (RR) of 0.41. Erythema affected 2.67% (23/871) vs. 4.3% (38/878), and edema 0% (0/871) vs. 2.76% (24/878). These events resolved without medical intervention and did not necessitate treatment discontinuation or protocol modification. Subgroup analysis revealed no heterogeneity across all adverse events (I2 = 0%), and no notable disparities in adverse reaction rates were observed (P > 0.05), except for hyperpigmentation (P = 0.02). Six studies contributed data to the meta-analysis regarding hyperpigmentation, and the combined findings indicated that the incidence of hyperpigmentation was lower when rhEGF was used in conjunction with fractional CO₂ laser therapy compared to CO₂ laser therapy (RR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.20 to 0.86; P = 0.02).Fig. 5Forest plot for adverse events

Publication bias

Since fewer than ten studies per outcome measure, publication bias was not evaluated.

Sources of heterogeneity

Substantial heterogeneity (I²>75%) was observed for ECCA, VSS, and adverse event outcomes. The primary sources of this heterogeneity were variability in laser parameters (energy density: 5–25%), differences in control group interventions (use of normal saline vs. no saline), and varying follow-up durations (1 week to 12 months). We attempted to perform subgroup analyses based on laser energy density (< 15 mJ vs. ≥15 mJ) and follow-up duration (< 3 months vs. ≥3 months) for ECCA scores. However, these analyses were limited by the small number of studies in each subgroup and inconsistent reporting of laser parameters. The results of subgroup analyses were presented in Supplementary Material 4.

Level of evidence

According to our results, the evidence of ECCA scores, VSS scores, the time of crust formation, the time of crust removal, skin hydration, TEWL, TNF-α levels and IL-10 levels were identified as “low-quality”; meanwhile, the evaluation of evidence quality of pain duration and DLQI were identified as “moderate-quality”. Yet the certainty of evidence is mostly low, warranting cautious interpretation and highlighting the need for more rigorous research. Discussion

The present meta-analysis suggests that the combination of fractional CO₂ laser therapy with topical rhEGF is significantly more effective than fractional CO₂ laser monotherapy for treating facial atrophic acne scars. First, the combination therapy significantly reduced ECCA and VSS scores, indicating a likely clinically relevant improvement in scar appearance, although this is based on low-certainty evidence. Second, it yielded accelerated wound healing, as evidenced by shortened durations of crust formation, crust removal, erythema, and pain, which suggests improved patient comfort during the recovery period. Third, the combination therapy reduced the incidence of common adverse events, particularly PIH. RhEGF may mitigate PIH risk in pigmented skin by rapidly restoring the epidermal barrier and promoting keratinocyte migration, thereby reducing exposure of underlying dermal melanocytes to pro-inflammatory signals. This accelerated healing aligns with our findings of shorter crust duration and reduced hyperpigmentation incidence [9, 10]. Furthermore, increased skin hydration and reduced TEWL reflect enhanced skin barrier function—particularly important after ablative laser procedures. It is important to highlight that these promising effect sizes were observed within a body of evidence consisting mostly of small, single-center RCTs conducted in China, which often had limited reporting on blinding and allocation concealment.

Our findings on the synergistic effect of rhEGF and laser therapy are consistent with the growing body of evidence supporting combination regimens in scar management. For instance, in burn scar rehabilitation, the integration of fractional CO₂ laser with topical agents like latanoprost has demonstrated enhanced repigmentation and patient satisfaction compared to laser treatment alone [32]. Similarly, recent studies have highlighted the benefits of combining fractional CO₂ laser with other biologics and biomaterials. Roohaninasab et al. reported that the combination of fractional CO₂ laser with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and hyaluronic acid led to significant improvements in burn scar pliability, erythema, and skin hydration [33]. Likewise, Kivi et al. found that combining fractional CO₂ laser with pulsed dye laser (PDL) resulted in superior outcomes in scar vascularity and pliability compared to each modality alone [34]. The rationale behind these multimodal approaches is further supported by systematic reviews on regenerative treatments. Jafarzadeh et al. emphasized the efficacy of growth factors and cellular therapies, such as PRP and stromal vascular fraction (SVF), in managing hypertrophic scars and keloids [35], which resonates with our observations on the utility of rhEGF—a key growth factor—in enhancing laser-based treatment for acne scars.

Several limitations of the current evidence must be acknowledged. One limitation is that high heterogeneity was observed across several outcomes, likely due to variability in laser parameters, treatment protocols, and patient characteristics, as well as variations in rhEGF formulations, which precluded subgroup analyses by formulation. Despite the heterogeneity, the laser parameters most commonly employed in the included trials fell within the following ranges: pulse energy 30–50 mJ, density 5%-15%. The typical rhEGF regimen involved application 1–3 times daily for 1–4 weeks post-laser. However, substantial variation existed, and no dose-response relationship or optimal protocol could be identified from the available data. Therefore, these parameters should not be construed as recommendations, but rather as a reflection of current practice in the studied contexts. Future RCTs should systematically vary these parameters to identify optimal treatment protocols. Furthermore, the evidence is derived almost exclusively from small, single-center trials conducted entirely within China. This single-country origin, combined with the lack of standardized protocols for laser settings and rhEGF administration, limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations and healthcare settings. While Asian skin (Fitzpatrick III-IV) [36, 37] has a well-documented higher tendency for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, broader populations with similar pigmentation (e.g., Middle Eastern, South Asian) may also benefit, but differences in healthcare access and local rhEGF formulations warrant further investigation. Future studies should include diverse populations to assess the therapy’s efficacy across different ethnicities. Importantly, no patients from non‑Asian backgrounds or with lighter phototypes (Fitzpatrick I‑II) were enrolled, leaving the generalizability of our findings to these populations unclear. Another limitation is that the included studies had short-to medium-term follow-up. Critically, no trial reported on long-term outcomes such as scar recurrence rates, the need for retreatment beyond one year, or the very long-term safety profile of repeated combined treatments, which limits insights into the durability of the treatment effect. Finally, common methodological limitations include insufficient reporting of allocation concealment and blinding procedures, as well as relatively small sample sizes for some outcomes. The lack of blinding in most studies may have influenced subjective outcomes such as ECCA and VSS scores. Therefore, the pooled effect estimates, while encouraging, must be interpreted as preliminary until confirmed by larger, more rigorous, and geographically diverse trials.

Conclusion

rhEGF combined with fractional CO₂ laser appears more effective than laser alone for treating atrophic acne scars, with comparable or better safety, particularly in reducing the risk of PIH. However, given the low to moderate certainty of evidence, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and large‑scale, multicenter, well‑blinded randomized controlled trials with extended follow‑up are needed to confirm their clinical utility.
