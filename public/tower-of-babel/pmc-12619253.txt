Epidemiological stratification and sub-national tailoring of malaria interventions in Liberia
Authors: Victor S Koko, D Levi Hinneh, Odell Kumeh, Trokon Washington, Joseph O Alade, Derry Saye -Dah Duokie, C Sanford Wesseh, Luke Bawo, Thomas Davis, Jethro Zawolo, Patrick Konwloh, Dikena Jackson, Tabadeh Collins Kollah, Lent Jones, Agnes S Janafo, Moses Kerkula Jeuronlon, Beatriz Galatas, Emmanuel A Temu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria is a major cause of illness and death in Liberia. Given the high burden of disease and limited resources, Liberia implemented a subnational tailoring (SNT) approach. This approach involved stakeholder engagement, data review, and advanced analytics to update transmission risk assessment, optimize intervention targeting, and revise the national operational plan.MethodsAn SNT team was established to determine intervention targeting criteria, compile and analyse relevant data sources, and stratify malaria risk and its determinants to inform geographical targeting of interventions. The analysis was performed at the district level. Data collected and reviewed included routine malaria data from health facilities, the national survey on malaria indicators, entomological data, demographic and health surveys, and modelled malaria burden metrics.ResultsEpidemiological stratification was conducted based on modelled parasite prevalence (PfPR), incorporating results from the 2022 Malaria Indicator Survey, to inform intervention strategies. Additional indicators relevant for decision-making, such as insecticide resistance, historical malaria interventions, and access to healthcare, were also stratified. The median PfPR across the 98 health districts was 29% (SD = 4.8%), ranging from 17 to 37%. The stratification identified 84 districts as moderate transmission and 14 as high transmission, with no districts classified as low transmission. Appropriate malaria control interventions were proposed based on these strata. Findings from the SNT analysis informed the revision of the national operational plan and facilitate resource mobilization for the scale-up of dual-active nets and expanding vaccination.ConclusionThis NMCP-led subnational malaria stratification for Liberia effectively informed the targeting of eight key interventions and highlighted data gaps for future refinement. This work not only provides a framework for monitoring progress and accelerating malaria burden reduction through tailored approaches but also sets the stage for continuously data-driven decision-making, emphasizing future prioritization based on projected impact, cost, and resource availability.

Background

Malaria remains a major global public health problem, disproportionately affecting sub-Saharan Africa. It poses a significant socioeconomic concern, impacting a substantial portion of the world's population [1]. Caused by a haematozoan of the genus Plasmodium, malaria is transmitted to humans through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito during its blood meal [1]. Globally, in 2023, there were an estimated 263 million malaria cases, with an incidence of 60 cases per 1000 population at risk, and 597,000 deaths, with a mortality rate of 14 per 100,000 [1]. The WHO African Region bears the heaviest burden, accounting for approximately 94% of all malaria cases and 95% of malaria deaths worldwide in 2023 [1]. Children under five years old remain the most vulnerable group, tragically accounting for 80% of malaria deaths in Africa in 2023 [1].

Malaria is hyper-holoendemic in Liberia, with continuous transmission throughout the year. The Country’s equatorial proximity results in a nearly year-round warm climate and favourable ecological conditions for malaria vector development, sustaining year-round transmission, with Liberia no exception [1, 2]. According to the National Malaria Strategy [3], Plasmodium falciparum is the dominant malaria parasite species, accounting for up to 98% of all cases. Other species reported in Liberia include Plasmodium malariae and Plasmodium ovale, constituting approximately 2% of infected cases. The entire population of 5.2 million is at risk, with children under five years and pregnant women being the most vulnerable [3].

Liberia's climate is tropical, hot, and humid, with a rainy season from May to October and a brief dry season from December to February. The country receives abundant rainfall which exceeds 3,000 mm annually. Coastal areas receive heavy rainfall, while inland regions receive less, contributing to the country's abundant vegetation and creating ideal conditions for mosquito habitats [3].

Historically, malaria epidemiology in Liberia has been described using household-based parasite prevalence surveys. The first survey involving parasitological testing was conducted in 2009 [4]. Subsequent surveys indicate a 2.5-fold reduction in national malaria prevalence, from 45% (RDT confirmed cases) in 2016 [5] to 18% in 2022, with variations at the regional level [6]. As malaria transmission declines, its spatial distribution becomes increasingly heterogeneous, necessitating a targeted approach that accounts for variations in morbidity, vector bionomics, healthcare access, health-seeking behaviours, and other important determinants of transmission [7, 8]. To enhance program planning, the National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP) initiated sub-national stratification of malaria transmission and its determinants, providing a framework for intervention targeting and progress monitoring.

Methods

The Liberia NMCP convened an SNT task team to oversee malaria stratification, select interventions, and recommend action to address surveillance challenges. This collaborative process allowed the team to determine analysis units, select relevant data elements for decision-making, establish final stratification criteria, and define intervention selection criteria informed by the local expert knowledge and determinants of transmission. This process also allowed the team to highlight malaria surveillance challenges and formulate action recommendations. This process ensures that all stakeholders are involved, and that the best possible interventions are chosen.

The 98 health districts forming the 15 counties in Liberia were included in the SNT analysis to prioritize the malaria response in Liberia.

Data collection

Malaria case data from 2019 to 2022 were extracted from the NMCP database, comprising routine reports from public and private health facilities to the Health Information Management System (HIMS). Modelled estimates of Plasmodium parasite rate (PfPR) were provided by the Malaria Atlas Project [9]. Data on malaria intervention coverage were sourced from the malaria and demographic health surveys for the years 2016, 2019, and 2022.

Health facilities distribution

According to the 2023 MOH master health facilities list, Liberia has 740 geocoded health facilities providing malaria services, 495 (76%) public and 245 (33%) private. The highest concentration of private facilities is in Montserrado (190), followed by Margibi (28) and Nimba (13). Each county has at least one hospital (Fig. 1A).

Population census and healthcare access

Malaria prevalence estimates were derived from the 2022 General Housing and Population Census [11]. Health facility and population distribution data were used to assess geographic access to healthcare. Population estimates were based on WorldPop 1 km2 spatial resolution data, aligned with the 2022 national census. Euclidean distance analysis was applied to assess healthcare access, estimated as the percentage of the population within 5 km of a healthcare facility, for both public and private (Fig. 1B).

Annual malaria incidence

This metric represents the number of confirmed malaria cases per 1000 inhabitants in the general population annually. The incidence of malaria was estimated through a stepwise approach following an adaptation of WHO methodology used in the World Malaria Report (Method 1) [1]. For this analysis, health facility-level data on all-cause attendance, patients tested and confirmed for malaria, and presumed cases were reviewed for completeness, quality and coherence; and aggregated to the health districts per year. Data checks were presented to the NMCP and supporting partners and experts to decide whether the data was appropriate to be used for decision-making. Regional-level estimates of care seeking-behaviour within 2 weeks after a fever to different care providers were also obtained from the 2022 MIS [6]. The incidence estimation approach started by adjusting the crude number of confirmed cases for incomplete and heterogeneous testing rates. A second adjustment was made to cases adjusted for testing rates to account for the varying reported rates per health district through time. Finally, the cases adjusted for testing and reporting rates were adjusted to control the differences in care-seeking behaviour throughout the country, where all the health districts that belonged to the same region were assumed to have similar care-seeking behaviour patterns.

Results were visualized in the forms of maps and reviewed with the expert team to confirm or reject the outputs for decision-making.

Prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum parasite (PfPR) by health districts

The epidemiological stratification for Liberia was based on the 2022 modelled Plasmodium falciparum prevalence rate (PfPR) estimates generated by the Malaria Atlas Project (MAP) [9]. These specific 2022 MAP estimates rigorously incorporated the survey data from the 2022 MIS [6]. The MAP employs a sophisticated geostatistical modelling framework that integrates spatiotemporal observations of community infection prevalence, routine surveillance data, and a range of environmental and intervention-related covariates to produce fine-scale estimates of malaria burden [10]. The decision to utilize the 2022 MAP PfPR estimates was driven by their direct incorporation of the 2022 MIS data, ensuring the most accurate representation of Liberia's current malaria epidemiology for targeted interventions.

The 2022 MIS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design to ensure national and sub-national representation [6]. In total, 4,306 households were surveyed across Liberia, and 2890 children under 5 years of age underwent malaria testing. It is important to note that the 2022 MIS was not powered to provide directly representative estimates at the individual health district level; its sample sizes were designed for national and regional analyses. Therefore, the MAP modelling framework played a crucial role in leveraging these MIS data points, along with other covariates, to produce the spatially disaggregated PfPR estimates for the 98 health districts used in this stratification.

Risk classes (strata) were defined based on burden metrics, disaggregated by 98 health districts, using class breaks of the WHO Malaria Surveillance guidelines [12]:

Entomological vector profile

Vector species distribution and insecticide resistance data (2019–2022) were provided by the NMCP and partners. Insecticide sensitivity tests, against insecticides from various public health chemical families, were conducted on species of the Anopheles gambiae complex and the Anopheles funestus group, known as malaria vectors in Liberia. Resistance was confirmed when 24-h post-exposure mortality was < 90%, probable resistance with mortality between 90 and 97%, and susceptibility with mortality between 98 and 100%.

Intervention targeting and decision making

Malaria intervention targeting is crucial for accelerating malaria burden reduction. This approach aligns with Liberia’s National Malaria Strategic Plan [3] and WHO’s Global Techncal Strategy for Malaria Control (2016–2030) [13], which recommends evidence-based decision-making in intervention targeting and prioritization for optimal impact.

Environmental data

Climate data, specifically rainfall and temperature, were collected through remote sensing from NASA’s GIOVANNI site (satellite data: giovanni.gsfc.nasa.gov/). This data was accessed in November 2023. The climate data—monthly rainfall together with routine monthly malaria cases for the years 2019 to 2022 were intended to assess malaria seasonality and its potential implications for the applicability of seasonal interventions.

Statistical tools

Excel and QGIS software version 3.4 [14] were used to generate malaria incidence estimates and maps and visualize other transmission determinants. Modelled estimate for PfPR, with 2022 MIS data factored in, were generated by MAP [10].

Results

Results are presented based on limitations related to routine data, the final dataset used for decision-making, epidemiological stratification, and the spatial distribution of transmission determinants and interventions, and final intervention selection.

Routine data limitations

Preliminary data quality assessments and expert reviews of the routine HMIS data across all reporting districts identified several limitations that collectively impacted the reliability and completeness of the dataset. Quality issues included inconsistencies in reporting, missing data points, and variable data entry practices, for example, reporting only confirmed cases among health workers conducting community malaria diagnosis; missing data elements such as suspected cases (since the country does not have fever register); unreliable testing rates due to regular stock-outs and/or over-testing highly likely in private health facilities.

Preliminary stratification analysis based on routine malaria data from 2019 to 2022 revealed heterogeneity, with most districts classified as high transmission; however, the burden distribution did not align with geographical patterns or household population-based survey (MIS) results. The survey results from 2016 [5] and 2022 [6] indicated a 2.5-fold decrease in overall malaria burden, from 45% in 2016 to 18% in 2022. Additionally, ongoing activities suggest a decline in malaria cases, as evidenced by low caseloads in drug therapeutic efficacy studies (TES). The latest malaria prevalence in Monrovia for children under 5 years old was 0.7% (microscopy) and 4.1% (mRDT), the lowest compared to other regions [6]. However, routine data still showed a high malaria burden in Monrovia. This discrepancy is likely due to the high concentration of private health facilities (HFs) and primary healthcare clinics in Monrovia. The Ministry of Health's (MoH) oversight and quality assurance are primarily limited to public HFs, resulting in variable quality of care in private facilities. Previous evaluations suggest that many private clinics perform both RDT and microscopy for malaria diagnosis, despite lacking staff trained or certified for microscopy. Given the profit-driven nature of private HFs, there is a possibility of double-counting cases, as they may perform both RDT and malaria smear tests on a single patient. The large number of private health care providers makes it harder to gather accurate data. Consequently, no sensitivity analyses were performed on subsets of this routine data (e.g., restricted to public facilities), as the entire routine dataset was deemed unsuitable for informing the epidemiological stratification due to the quality concerns.

Epidemiological stratification

Due to concerns regarding data quality and case management issues, as detailed in the limitations of this study, the NMCP, based on deliberations of the SNT task team (partners and local experts), decided to exclude routine data from the final decision-making process. Similarly, while historical malaria prevalence data and older modelled estimates were reviewed during the preliminary phases of this stratification, they often presented a high and relatively uniform burden across the country. In consensus with the NMCP and local malaria experts, these historical findings were deemed not truly representative of Liberia's updated understanding of the current epidemiological situation. Therefore, the decision was made to solely rely on the 2022 MAP PfPR estimates, which incorporate the latest and most accurate 2022 MIS results [6], as the basis for the current stratification.

The modelled 2022 PfPR estimates at the health district level showed a median parasite rate of 29% (average of 40.9%) in the general population, ranging from 17 to 37% (Fig. 2A). Based on this data, parasite prevalence was categorized into three strata: lowest (< 10%), moderate (10 to < 35%), and high (≥ 35%) (Fig. 2B). The An. funestus group is more prevalent in the central and northern regions (Fig. 3A). The dominant species within the An. gambiae complex are An. gambiae sensu stricto (s.s.) and Anopheles coluzzii, with varying distributions across the country (Fig. 3B).

Spatial coverage of vector control

Long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) remain the primary malaria vector control strategy in Liberia. National mass distribution campaigns were conducted in 2016, 2021, and 2024, supplemented by routine distributions through antenatal care (ANC), the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), and school-based channels. Net coverage is evaluated using two indicators: access, defined as the proportion of the population that could sleep under a net (assuming two people per net) [16], and use, the proportion of the population who sleep under a net. The Net Use–to-Access ratio, calculated by dividing use by access, estimates the proportion of the population using nets among those with access within their household. This ratio measures the behavioural gap in net use, rather than a shortage of available nets [16].

The 2022 MIS was used to assess regional net access and utilization (Fig. 5). Net access varied by region, with Southern Central and Greater Monrovia reporting the lowest access in 2022. Net access ranged from 48% in South-Central to 66% in North-Central, while ITN use ranged from 41% in North-West to 59% in North-Central. The overall gap in ITN use was minimal, ranging from 10% in North-Central to 30% in South-Eastern B, the region with the highest burden [6]. Where nets are accessible, they are highly likely used for protection, as indicated by the net access-to-use ratio. These results show that while net access is variable across the country, ranging from 89% in North-Central and 68% in South-Eastern B, when nets are available, they are being used.

Coverage of case management interventions

In Liberia, pregnant women should take monthly doses of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine starting at 13 weeks until delivery, with a maximum of four doses as part of intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp). The program uses the third dose, IPTp3, as an indicator to measure IPTp coverage. In 2020 and 2022, most districts reported less than 50% IPTp3 coverage, with only 12 districts achieving over 80% (MOH DHIS2) (Fig. 6). Between 2016 [5] and 2022 [6], IPTp3 coverage more than doubled from 22 to 63%, though still falling short of the 80% national target. A 2021 malaria barrier study identified transport costs, distance, and lack of awareness of ANC clinic hours as major barriers to IPTp uptake [18].

To extend malaria services to remote areas, the integrated Community Case Management (iCCM) program utilizes 5296 trained community health workers (CHWs), comprising 537 Community Health Services Supervisors (CHSS) and 4759 Community Health Assistants (CHA). The National Malaria Control Programme (NMCP), in collaboration with its partners, identified eight priority interventions for Liberia's national malaria control strategy. These selections were based on a comprehensive analysis of malaria burden (utilizing 2022 modelled PfPR), prevailing vector resistance patterns, healthcare access, and the unique situation of urban settings (Fig. 8).

Malaria seasonality

A separate analysis explored rainfall seasonality, as a proxy for malaria case seasonality (acknowledging the absence of reliable routine case data for direct correlation) for their potential as direct stratifying factors. However, a strong, consistent, or predictive correlation suitable for their inclusion was not found. This is primarily due to Liberia's unique ecological context: the country experiences high, year-round rainfall, where continuous heavy rain often causes'flash flood'effects that disrupt and wash away mosquito breeding habitats. Consequently, mosquito density and malaria transmission peaks do not predictably follow rainfall patterns, unlike regions with distinct seasonal malaria. This ecological reality limits the applicability of seasonality-dependent interventions such as Seasonal Malaria Chemoprevention (SMC) in Liberia's context.

Discussion

This discussion addresses the rationale for the chosen stratification approach, and the selected intervention strategies, with an emphasis on the specific challenges faced in urban settings.

Malaria stratification and interventions

The PfPR modelled estimates, based on the 2022 malaria indicator survey, were used for malaria stratification across Liberia’s 98 health districts. Nationally, the median PfPR was 29%, with district-level estimates ranging from 17 to 37%. Our stratification classified 14 districts (14% of all districts) as high-burden, while the remaining 84 districts were categorized as moderate-burden. In terms of human population (based on the 2022 census), 6% of Liberia's population resides within these 14 high-burden districts, with the remaining 94% of the population found in the 84 moderate-burden districts. Despite routine data showing a high malaria burden in Monrovia, this was contradicted by the 2022 Malaria Indicator Survey which reported a significantly lower prevalence (~ 1%), leading to the classification of urban Montserrado (four districts in Monrovia) as a moderate burden area in our final stratification.

Given this distribution of malaria burden, where all 98 districts fall into moderate to high-risk categories, the national control strategy prioritizes universal access and coverage to prevention and treatment interventions (Table 1). Optimal access and use of effective LLINs are critical for reducing malaria transmission in the context of widely spread pyrethroid resistance in local vectors [15] and are the primary prevention method in Liberia. Nets are distributed through mass campaigns every three years and continuously through ANC, EPI, and school-based distribution in selected urban settings. Recent surveys indicate improved access to and use of LLINs. The net access-to-use ratio suggests high utilization where nets are accessible [6, 16].

MDA and IRS are reserved for emergency response, while LSM is a supplementary measure in specific urban settings and measure against An. stephensi invasion [17]. The programme will promote LSM, particularly larviciding, where it is likely to have a public health impact, in accordance with WHO recommendations.

Social and behaviour change (SBC) interventions are essential complements to structural and system-related health interventions, aiming to improve and sustain health outcomes [20]. In Liberia's malaria control strategy, SBC is integrated across all interventions to actively promote the adoption and sustained practice of key malaria-related behaviours, such as optimal ITN use [24] and appropriate healthcare-seeking behaviour [25]. To achieve this, Liberia continues to leverage interpersonal communication (IPC), targeted media campaigns (radio, television, print), and community mobilization efforts [3].

Urban Montserrado challenges and interventions

Urban Montserrado (Monrovia city) presents unique and complex challenges for malaria operations and intervention strategies. As Liberia's largest urban area, it is home to 1.92 million residents (86% residing in four urban districts) out of the country’s total 5.2 million [11]. This environment is characterized by a high density of private healthcare providers (71% in urban Montserrado; MOH 2022 data), alongside documented challenges of low intervention adoption [6] and variable quality of care from private providers [18]. Recognizing these complexities, interventions for urban Montserrado prioritize standard case management, Intermittent Preventive Treatment in Pregnancy (IPTp), and Dual AI-ITN distribution primarily through routine Antenatal Care (ANC), Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) clinics, and schools. These are complemented by tailored Social and Behaviour Change (SBC) campaigns designed to improve intervention uptake and appropriate healthcare-seeking behaviour.

The ITN distribution strategy in urban Monrovia prioritizes efficiency and appropriateness of channels rather than a redefinition of the highest-risk populations. This approach leverages the urban context’s characteristics, lower overall prevalence but higher access to static health facilities and schools. By utilizing established, consistent access points like EPI clinics for children under five and ANC for pregnant women (also protecting their newborns), the aim is to maximize coverage for key vulnerable groups without resorting to higher-cost mass campaigns in a lower-burden setting.

Beyond established interventions, LSM is specifically emphasized as a crucial supplementary vector control intervention in urban Montserrado. This strategy serves as a precautionary measure against the potential invasion of An. stephensi, a highly invasive vector species known for thriving in urban settings and a good malaria vector [1, 17]. While An. stephensi has not yet been detected in Liberia, based on initial entomological surveillance efforts conducted by the NMCP and partners at key points of entry (including the Monrovia port), its recent introduction and rapid spread in other African regions necessitate this proactive approach to prevent its invasion in Liberian urban centers.

Access to healthcare and malaria services

Liberia's strategy for malaria care access combines facility-based services with community-level care provided by Community Health Workers (CHWs) through the Integrated Community Case Management (iCCM) program. Established in 2007 and strengthened by policy revisions (2011, 2016) in response to challenges including the 2014–2015 Ebola outbreak, iCCM aims to extend effective case management for malaria and other childhood illnesses to remote and hard-to-reach areas, specifically reaching populations beyond a 5 km radius of health facilities [21,22, 23, 25].

Currently, 5296 registered CHWs deliver iCCM services, including malaria diagnosis and treatment, at the community level. The national CHW allocation target is one CHW per 250 people or 60 households.

Analysis of health care access revealed significant disparities. In 2022, geographical access to malaria care (within a 5 km health facility catchment) averaged 61% across all health districts but ranged widely from 33% (Gbarpolu) to 80% (Montserrado). Critically, 30 districts had less than 50% of their population within this 5 km range. Complementing this, assessment of iCCM coverage against the national CHW target found that 37 districts had coverage below the prescribed ratio. Seventeen districts (Table 3) were identified as particularly underserved, facing the dual challenge of both low geographical access to health facilities and inadequate CHW coverage below the national target. Expanding the iCCM programme in these areas is crucial for improving malaria care access.

Limitations and opportunities

This foundational study, Liberia's first district-level malaria stratification, encountered data-related limitations that shaped its analytical scope.

Firstly, epidemiological data considerations presented challenges. Routine malaria prevalence data were largely of insufficient quality for direct inclusion, hindering full integration of both modelled and routine malaria data as recommended by WHO guidelines [7]. This was primarily due to inconsistencies in quality of case management, diagnostic stock-outs, and routine reporting issues, as well as missing data elements such as suspected cases given lack of fever register and parasite species composition. Moreover, reliance on a single year (2022) modelled Plasmodium falciparum prevalence rate (PfPR), with results of 2022 MIS factored in, while the most accurately available, limited the capture of crucial temporal trends and dynamic nature of malaria transmission, which are crucial for understanding long-term epidemiological patterns and adapting intervention strategies.

Secondly, the methodology for assessing spatial access to healthcare had inherent limitations. The analysis used a raster data model with Euclidean distances for accessibility within a 5 km catchment [26]. This method, however, assumes straight-line travel, thereby not accounting for real-world barriers such as transport services, road networks, water bodies, and elevation [27], which significantly affect actual access to care.

Finally, the broader health information system (HIS) characteristics presented challenges for comprehensive data integration. Issues such as missing key indicators in DHIS2, scattered data sources, and sub-optimal interoperability among HIMS (e.g., DHIS2, LMIS, mSupply) influenced the depth of analysis possible.

Crucially, these limitations do not diminish the transformative importance of this analysis; rather, they underscore its pioneering role and future potential. This initial stratification offers a granular understanding of malaria burden, serving as a key baseline for Liberia's subnational intervention tailoring. Beyond guiding current control efforts and establishing a benchmark for future impact evaluation, this exercise has been instrumental in identifying specific data gaps that the Ministry of Health is committed to addressing. It has successfully galvanized local efforts to continuously improve malaria risk stratification that will profoundly shape the future of malaria control across Liberia.

Conclusion

This NMCP-led work with partner support generated the first malaria epidemiological and transmission determinant stratification to drive subnational intervention targeting in Liberia. It revealed a series of critical data gaps and contextual factor challenges that need to be addressed in order to reinforce the data-driven decision-making processes in the country. The targeting of eight interventions was discussed and a final intervention combination for implementation in the country's different health districts was selected among the NMCP and the partners. Prioritization of interventions for the following funding cycles will consider updated analysis with the inclusion of projected impact on malaria in terms of cases averted or burden reduction, as well as cost, and resource availability. This work triggered the implementation of subnational tailoring of malaria interventions in Liberia and has set the scene for future exercises to continuously inform malaria decisions in the country.
