Functional engineering of adipose-derived stem cell exosomes and its application in soft tissue regeneration
Authors: Lingyan Lu, Juzi Liu, Ye Li, Junrong Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdipose-derived stem cell exosomes are increasingly recognized as promising cell-free mediators for soft tissue repair because of their favorable biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and broad paracrine activity. By transferring regulatory RNAs, proteins, lipids, and other bioactive molecules, they participate in key regenerative processes, including angiogenesis, immune modulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and cellular proliferation. However, native adipose-derived stem cell exosomes still face important translational limitations, including limited targeting specificity, variable cargo composition, rapid clearance, and insufficient retention at injured sites.Main bodyRecent advances in exosome engineering provide new opportunities to enhance the regenerative performance of adipose-derived stem cell exosomes. Function-oriented strategies include parental-cell preconditioning, genetic engineering, direct cargo loading, surface modification, and biomaterial-assisted delivery. These approaches can enrich therapeutic cargos, improve lesion-specific targeting, prolong local retention, and enable sustained or microenvironment-responsive release. Current applications are mainly concentrated in wound healing, muscle repair, and adipose tissue regeneration or fat grafting, while tendon-to-bone healing and peripheral nerve repair represent emerging areas with growing preclinical evidence. This review summarizes the biological basis, engineering strategies, application scenarios, and translational challenges of engineered adipose-derived stem cell exosomes in defined soft tissue regenerative settings.ConclusionAlthough engineered adipose-derived stem cell exosomes show encouraging potential for soft tissue regeneration, clinical translation remains at an early stage. Future development should focus on mechanism-guided cargo design, precise targeting, responsive delivery systems, standardized characterization, and clinically scalable production. These advances may support the development of next-generation cell-free therapies for specific soft tissue repair indications.

Introduction

Functional restoration of soft tissue is essential for recovery from trauma, sports injuries, or degenerative conditions. In contrast to conventional repair, which often results in fibrotic scarring, ideal regeneration seeks to replicate the original tissue architecture, vascular network, and biomechanical integrity [1]. However, this goal remains clinically challenging, particularly in representative soft tissue repair contexts such as cutaneous wounds, skeletal muscle injury, and adipose tissue defects, where impaired vascularization, persistent inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling disorders often lead to delayed healing, fibrotic repair, or incomplete functional recovery [2]. Traditional treatments—including surgical suturing, tissue transplantation, or artificial scaffold implantation—can restore basic anatomical continuity but often fail to replicate the complex microstructure and dynamic biological microenvironment of native tissues, carrying risks such as immune rejection, mechanical mismatch, and secondary injury [3].

In recent years, the use of stem cells in regenerative medicine has shown considerable potential to circumvent these limitations. Among various stem cell types, adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) stand out as a highly suitable source for soft tissue engineering, a status attributed to their ease of access, simple isolation procedure, vigorous expansion potential, and significant paracrine activity [4]. A growing body of evidence suggests that the therapeutic benefits of ADSCs are mediated priily by their secretome, with exosomes (ADSC-Exos) being recognized as crucial vehicles for intercellular communication [5].

Nevertheless, naturally derived ADSC-Exos exhibit clear limitations in clinical applications: they lack precise targeting ability, making it difficult to accumulate sufficiently at injury sites; their therapeutic cargo content is often limited, potentially restricting efficacy; and large-scale, standardized production remains challenging [6]. These constraints have prompted increasing interest in function-oriented engineering of ADSC-Exos [7]. Established engineering strategies—such as modulating parental cell conditions to optimize cargo loading, modifying exosomal membranes to confer targeting capability, or integrating exosomes into biomimetic delivery systems for controlled release—can tailor ADSC-Exos functions according to specific regenerative demands, thereby enhancing their therapeutic specificity and potency [8]. In this review, “functional engineering” is used to organize these strategies according to the major therapeutic barriers they address, including insufficient therapeutic cargo, limited targeting specificity, rapid clearance, poor local retention, and lack of controlled release.

This review sumizes the biological basis, engineering strategies, and regenerative applications of ADSC-Exos in soft tissue repair. Particular emphasis is placed on how established engineering approaches, including cargo optimization, targeting modification, and biomaterial-assisted delivery, can be selected and combined to address the major limitations of native ADSC-Exos. Current evidence in wound healing, muscle repair, and adipose tissue regeneration/fat grafting is reviewed, followed by a critical discussion of emerging applications, clinical translation, strategy-specific limitations, and future development directions [9].

Biological basis of ADSC-exos

The therapeutic effects of ADSC-Exos are closely related to their biogenesis, cargo composition, and interactions with recipient cells. Understanding these biological features provides the basis for rational exosome engineering in soft tissue regeneration.

Biogenesis of exosomes

Exosomes are a subtype of extracellular vesicles generated through the endosomal pathway. In the canonical process, early endosomes mature into late endosomes, whose limiting membrane invaginates to form intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). These ILV-containing compartments are termed multivesicular bodies (MVBs). MVBs can either fuse with lysosomes for degradation or fuse with the plasma membrane to release ILVs into the extracellular space as exosomes [10–12]. In contrast, other EV subtypes, such as microvesicles, are mainly generated by direct outward budding of the plasma membrane [13].

Exosome biogenesis and cargo sorting are regulated by both ESCRT-dependent and ESCRT-independent mechanisms. The ESCRT machinery coordinates cargo recognition, membrane budding, and vesicle scission, while accessory molecules such as ALIX, syntenin, heparanase, Arf6, and PLD2 further participate in cargo selection and ILV formation [14–23]. ESCRT-independent mechanisms also contribute to exosome generation, particularly through tetraspanin-enriched microdomains and ceramide-mediated membrane curvature, which help organize specific membrane domains and facilitate inward budding [24–28]. Finally, Rab GTPases and SNARE proteins regulate MVB trafficking, docking, and fusion with the plasma membrane, thereby controlling exosome secretion [29–32].

These biogenesis pathways are directly relevant to the functional engineering of ADSC-Exos. Modulating endosomal sorting, membrane-domain formation, or secretion-related pathways influence exosome yield, cargo composition, and release kinetics. However, because many of these regulators also participate in broader cellular processes, manipulating them affect parental ADSC biology as well as exosome production. Therefore, biogenesis-based engineering should be evaluated not only by increased EV yield, but also by cargo consistency, vesicle identity, biological activity, and safety.

Bioactive cargos and regenerative characteristics of ADSC-Exos

The rekable ability of ADSC-Exos to mediate soft tissue regeneration essentially stems from their function as natural nanomessengers capable of delivering a regenerative repertoire of bioactive “cargo” to the injury site [33]. Unlike a general description of exosomal cargo, the cargo profile of ADSC-Exos is particularly relevant to soft tissue repair because it reflects the intrinsic biological functions of adipose tissue in vascular support, immune regulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and tissue homeostasis [34–36]. These cargos synergistically regulate multiple stages of tissue regeneration—from inflammation modulation and angiogenesis to cell proliferation and matrix remodeling—through multidimensional and multi-target actions [37].

The pro-regenerative effect of ADSC-Exos is mainly reflected in their delivery of soft tissue repair-related cargos to the damaged area [38]. These cargos include nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids, which serve critical functions in intercellular communication and regenerative regulation. Among nucleic acids, ADSC-Exos contain mRNAs and non-coding RNAs associated with angiogenesis, inflammation control, fibrosis regulation, and tissue repair. For example, mRNAs related to VEGF, TNF-α, and collagen production contribute to angiogenesis, inflammation regulation, and matrix remodeling [39]. Meanwhile, miRNAs such as miR-21, miR-126, miR-100-5p, miR-146a, and let-7c have been implicated in endothelial cell activation, macrophage polarization, anti-inflammatory regulation, and fat graft survival [40–44]. In addition, lncRNAs and circRNAs, including HOTAIR, CIRS-7, MALAT1, circ-Snhg11, and circ-IGF1R, participate in epigenetic regulation or act as competing endogenous RNAs, thereby affecting angiogenesis, oxidative stress responses, and tissue remodeling [45–47]. Additionally, DNA species such as mtDNA and ctDNA serve as biokers for oxidative stress, tumor mutations, or pathogen infections [48].

Regarding proteins, membrane proteins such as tetraspanins CD9, CD63, and CD81 mediate exosome uptake and cell targeting, regulating cell migration and immune activation [49]. Antigen-presenting molecules, including MHC-I, MHC-II, and CD1d, participate in immune regulation by presenting antigens to T cells or NKT cells [50]. Cytosolic proteins include metabolic enzymes such as GAPDH, PKM2, and LDHA, which are involved in metabolic regulation and microenvironmental remodeling [51]. More importantly, ADSC-Exos carry growth factors and signaling molecules closely associated with soft tissue regeneration, such as VEGF, TGF-β, EGF, HGF, and cytokine-related factors, which regulate angiogenesis, immune modulation, fibroblast behavior, and cell proliferation [35, 52]. These protein cargos provide an important biological basis for the application of ADSC-Exos in wound healing, ischemic injury, scar modulation, and adipose tissue regeneration.

Lipids, including cholesterol, phosphatidylserine, prostaglandin E2, and ceramide, also contribute to the biological activity and stability of ADSC-Exos [53–55]. These lipid components participate in membrane structure maintenance, exosome uptake, inflammatory regulation, and vesicle release. In the context of soft tissue regeneration, lipid-mediated signaling further influence macrophage activity, endothelial cell function, and local inflammatory responses.

Compared with BMSC- or UC-MSC-derived exosomes, ADSC-Exos appear particularly suitable for soft tissue regeneration because adipose tissue is intrinsically involved in vascular regulation, immune modulation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and tissue repair [56, 57]. In addition, ADSCs can be obtained through relatively accessible and minimally invasive procedures, show strong proliferative and secretory capacity, and are therefore advantageous for scalable exosome production and translational applications [4, 58, 59]. These biological and practical features support the selection of ADSC-Exos as a promising platform for soft tissue regenerative engineering.

The biogenesis mechanisms of ADSC-Exos reveal the engineering potential of their yield and secretion, while their ADSC-associated cargo signature forms the foundation of their functional diversity [48]. This biological understanding provides clear targets for subsequent engineering: intervention in biogenesis pathways can regulate exosome yield and release kinetics [60–62], and directional modification of cargo composition can enhance specific therapeutic functions [61]. This transition from understanding to manipulation is key to evolving ADSC-Exos from natural messengers to engineerable therapeutic platforms [63].

The biological process of soft tissue regeneration

Successful soft tissue regeneration depends on a highly coordinated sequence of inflammatory regulation, stem/progenitor cell activation, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and tissue maturation. Understanding these biological processes provides the mechanistic basis for the rational application and functional engineering of ADSC-Exos in regenerative medicine.

Soft tissue regeneration refers to a highly coordinated series of cellular and molecular events through which an organism achieves complete or near-complete restoration of tissue structure, function, and biomechanical properties following injury [56]. This process is fundamentally distinct from conventional repair, which culminates in fibrotic scarring. Its success depends on the effective operation of a sophisticated network comprising cells, the extracellular matrix (ECM), growth factors, and the immune microenvironment [64].

After inflammation is controlled, the regeneration process enters the cell proliferation stage. Tissue-resident adult stem cells/progenitor cells (e.g., epidermal stem cells) are activated, exiting quiescence and proliferating extensively [65]. Simultaneously, fibroblasts from the surrounding area are recruited to the defect site [66]. Such proliferation is precisely orchestrated by multiple signaling pathways, notably Notch and Wnt, essential for directing stem cell fate. Synchronously, angiogenesis—promoted by VEGF and bFGF—progresses [67]. The nascent capillary network provides essential oxygen and nutrients to the regenerating tissue and removes metabolic waste.

Activated stem/progenitor cells, under the precise guidance of local microenvironmental signals (including mechanical stimuli and growth factor gradients), differentiate into mature functional cells (e.g., myoblasts fuse into myotubes, tendon cells align orderly) [68]. These functional cells begin to synthesize and secrete ECM components (such as type I and III collagen, elastin) that match the native tissue, depositing and assembling them in a highly ordered manner, in contrast to the chaotic accumulation of collagen fibers in scar tissue [69]. For instance, in tendon regeneration, collagen fibers align parallel to the direction of stress, forming fiber bundle structures with good mechanical properties [36].

The newly formed tissue (often referred to as the “provisional matrix”) remains structurally and functionally immature. During the tissue remodeling phase, through the continued activity of cells (especially fibroblasts) and stimulation by mechanical load, the ECM undergoes dynamic adjustment: immature type III collagen is replaced by tougher type I collagen, and the degree of cross-linking between collagen fibers increases, gradually elevating the tissue’s tensile strength to near-normal levels [70]. Meanwhile, excess cells are cleared via apoptosis, and the initially rich vascular network undergoes physiological regression, ultimately resulting in mature regenerated tissue that closely resembles the undamaged tissue in both structure and function [71].

Functional engineering of ADSC-exos: engineering strategies

Although ADSC-Exos possess innate regenerative potential, their clinical application still faces several inherent bottlenecks [63]. Native ADSC-Exos have limited targeting specificity, variable therapeutic cargo abundance, and insufficient retention in complex injury microenvironments, which reduce their regenerative efficacy and reproducibility [72–74] .These limitations provide a rationale for function-oriented engineering strategies aimed at optimizing cargo composition, enhancing targeting capability, and improving local retention or release behavior [75].

Therefore, targeted engineering of ADSC-Exos has become important for overcoming efficacy bottlenecks and facilitating clinical translation [43]. In this review, functional engineering is used as an umbrella term to organize established ADSC-Exos engineering strategies according to the main therapeutic barriers they address, including insufficient therapeutic cargo, limited targeting specificity, rapid clearance, poor local retention, and lack of controlled release. It aims to enhance or introduce therapeutic signals associated with angiogenesis, inflammation control, and fibrosis regulation [44, 77]. Pretreatment and Culture Environment Engineering: This is the most natural and easily implementable engineering approach. Pretreating ADSCs under specific conditions can “instruct” the cells to preferentially package desired protective factors into exosomes [79].

Hypoxic Preconditioning mimics the ischemic microenvironment following soft tissue injury, reflecting the therapeutic mechanism of ADSC-Exos in hypoxic damage [80]. In hypoxically preconditioned ADSC exosomes (ADSC-HExo), the expression of circ-Snhg11 was significantly higher than in normoxic exosomes (ADSC-Exo). Subsequent research confirmed that ADSC-Exo with high circ-Snhg11 expression robustly promoted vascular endothelial cell (EC) proliferation, migration, and tube formation, thereby stimulating angiogenesis, while also polarizing macrophages toward the pro-reparative M2 phenotype [46]. In another study, characteristic exosomal proteins TSG101, CD63, and HSP60 were found at kedly increased abundance in hypoxic group exosomes (hADSCEs) compared to the normoxic group (nADSCEs). These observations, supported by functional assays, collectively indicate that hypoxic preconditioning modifies the cargo composition of exosomes [81]. Hypoxic preconditioning can also significantly upregulate the content of miR-100-5p in exosomes, resulting in stronger pro-angiogenic, anti-inflammatory effects and accelerated wound healing [42]. In diabetic wounds, hypoxic preconditioning triggers a pro-angiogenic cascade by elevating circ-IGF1R. This molecule sequesters miR-503-5p, thereby de-repressing its target genes HK2 and VEGFA and promoting angiogenesis through their combined action [47]. Another study demonstrated that miR-21-5p is preferentially packaged into exosomes under hypoxic conditions. Following transfer to human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), it represses SPRY1 expression, thereby triggering the PI3K/AKT pathway. The subsequent induction of HIF-1α and VEGFA promotes H-type vessel formation, which contributes to bone fracture healing [82].

Inflammatory Cytokine Stimulation (e.g., TNF-α, IFN-γ) can modulate ADSC-Exos to carry more immunomodulatory molecules (e.g., IL-10), enhancing their ability to direct macrophage polarization and restrain disproportionate inflammation, thereby steering the immune milieu toward a pro-regenerative state [83]. Pretreatment with IFN-γ and TNF-α enriched miR-221-3p in hADMSC-derived EVs (P-MEVs), conferring superior anti-inflammatory and repair efficacy in acute lung injury models [84]. Similarly, pretreatment of feline ADSCs with TNF-α and IFN-γ altered the cytokine and miRNA profile of their EVs [85]. Other Conditions: ADSC-Exos also show functional engineering potential under oxidative stress (e.g., H2O2), enhancing flap survival after ischemic injury [86], and in response to growth factors like PDGF and bFGF, exhibiting potent pro-angiogenic, anti-inflammatory, and vessel-stabilizing properties [87].

Genetic Engineering of Parental Cells: This method centers on directly altering gene expression in the source ADSCs. Through the application of genetic engineering tools—encompassing both viral and non-viral vectors—investigators can deliver selected nucleic acid sequences into the cells. This direct manipulation enables the generation of exosomes with precisely designed and potent biological activities [88]. For instance, the TM-nanoEP system has been used to efficiently deliver plasmid DNA into cells via a nanopore-based, non-endocytic method, yielding therapeutic small extracellular vesicles (t-sEVs) enriched with mRNA for VEGF-A and BMP-2 [89]. Similarly, exosomes obtained from ADSCs engineered to overexpress miR-126 demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in myocardial infarction models, as evidenced by diminished infarct area, stimulated angiogenesis, and attenuated cardiac injury [90]. The upregulation of miR-194 in ADSC-Exo acts to inhibit the TGF-β1 signaling pathway. This targeted suppression effectively attenuates the pathological process of hypertrophic scarring [91]. Studies also show that the pro-migratory and pro-proliferative effects of ADSC-exosomal microRNA-21 on HaCaT cells are mediated via the PI3K/AKT pathway, inducing MMP-9 upregulation [92].

Direct Exogenous Loading into Isolated Exosomes: For developing EVs as drug carriers, selecting an optimal loading strategy is critical for therapeutic success. Established approaches for exogenous loading include but are not limited to direct incubation, extrusion, electroporation, saponin-mediated permeabilization, and multiple freeze-thaw cycles [93]. Due to their differing principles, they exhibit significant variations in efficiency, applicable drug types, and impact on vesicle integrity. For instance, the incubation method, while simple, generally has low loading efficiency; Yang et al. reported a loading efficiency of only 5.06% ± 0.45% for linezolid loaded into macrophage-derived exosomes [94]. Electroporation is commonly employed as a technique for delivering nucleic acid therapeutics. However, in a study on encapsulating miR-21 inhibitor oligonucleotides, Liang et al. observed that the process can concurrently induce both drug aggregation and encapsulation. While 10 ms and 1000 V were identified as the optimal parameters, the resulting loading efficiency reached merely 0.5% [95]. To overcome these limitations, the sonication method shows higher potential; Wang et al. achieved a loading efficiency of 19.55% ± 2.48% for paclitaxel (PTX) loaded into M1 macrophage-derived exosomes via sonication, significantly enhancing its anti-tumor activity [96]. Furthermore, for hydrophobic drugs or situations requiring combination therapy, hybrid exosome technology offers an inative solution; Li et al. successfully co-encapsulated triptolide (TP) and miR-497 by fusing exosomes with RGD-modified liposomes for treating cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer, with a fusion efficiency as high as 78% ± 3% [97]. The final choice of method requires comprehensive consideration of drug properties (e.g., molecular weight, hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity), impact on EV integrity, and the feasibility of subsequent purification steps. Notably, Auquiere et al. emphasize that the effectiveness of loading methods must be accurately assessed through subsequent purification steps, as unloaded free drug can severely interfere with the determination of encapsulation efficiency and true therapeutic efficacy [98].

Overall, cargo optimization strategies differ substantially in controllability, biological complexity, and translational feasibility. Parental-cell preconditioning preserves vesicle integrity and induce coordinated endogenous cargo remodeling, but the resulting cargo profile is complex and vary with culture conditions. Genetic engineering provides stronger molecular specificity, yet it introduces additional concerns regarding vector safety, parental-cell alteration, and manufacturing consistency. Direct exogenous loading allows flexible payload customization after EV isolation, but loading efficiency, free-cargo contamination, and vesicle damage remain major technical limitations. Therefore, cargo engineering should be selected according to whether the therapeutic goal requires broad biological conditioning or precise enrichment of a defined molecule.

Targeting modification: guiding exosome homing to the soft tissue regeneration microenvironment

Despite their inherent potential as natural drug carriers, the non-specific distribution of exosomes in vivo significantly limits their therapeutic efficacy and raises concerns about off-target effects [99, 100]. Therefore, engineering exosomes to confer active “homing” capability to specific diseased tissues is key to achieving precision medicine. Current mainstream targeting modification strategies are priily divided into genetic engineering and chemical modification, aiming to precisely modulate the exosomal membrane surface to significantly enhance targeting efficiency and therapeutic index [101], (Fig. 3).

Genetic Engineering Strategies: This approach involves genetically modifying parental cells so that the exosomes they secrete naturally display specific targeting ligands on their surface. The core methodology involves fusing the gene sequence encoding a targeting peptide or protein (e.g., RVG peptide, CAP, affibody) with the gene of an exosome-specific membrane protein, such as LAMP-2B, CD63, or PDGFR [102].

The LAMP-2B fusion system is currently the most widely utilized platform. The extracellular domain of LAMP-2B provides an ideal site for inserting targeting sequences [103]. For example, engineering exosomes to display a fused rabies virus glycoprotein peptide (RVG peptide) with LAMP-2B enables specific binding to acetylcholine receptors on neuronal cells, facilitating efficient blood-brain barrier crossing and offering a el strategy for treating central nervous system diseases [104]. Likewise, surface modification of exosomes with a chondrocyte-affinity peptide (CAP) directs the targeted delivery of therapeutic miR-140 to articular cartilage, consequently attenuating disease progression in a rat osteoarthritis model [105].

Besides LAMP-2B, other membrane proteins have been used to display higher affinity targeting molecules. For instance, fusing a HER2-binding affibody (zHER) or an ankyrin repeat protein (DARPin) to the exosome surface achieved efficient targeting and drug delivery to HER2-overexpressing tumor cells [95, 106]. Another strategy involves using the PDGFR transmembrane domain to display the GE11 peptide, generating exosomes that target the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) with low proliferative activity, making them suitable for targeted tumor therapy [107].

Chemical Modification Strategies: These involve direct surface modification of purified exosomes in vitro, offering greater flexibility and modularity. This method allows for the conjugation of various non-genetically encoded targeting ligands, such as specific chemical molecules or aptamers [108].

Click chemistry, exemplified by the highly efficient copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) reaction, enables the covalent conjugation of azide-bearing targeting peptides (e.g., c(RGDyK)) to alkyne-functionalized exosome surfaces. Experiments demonstrated that such c(RGDyK)-modified exosomes effectively targeted brain ischemic areas, delivering the anti-inflammatory drug curcumin and significantly reducing inflammation and apoptosis [109]. Other studies have used PDGFR-targeted exosomes to enhance the delivery of anti-fibrotic miR-150 [110].

Lipid Anchoring is another common method. Utilizing the property of amphiphilic molecules (e.g., DSPE-PEG, cholesterol) to spontaneously insert into the lipid bilayer, lipid molecules conjugated with targeting ligands (e.g., RGD peptide, folic acid, AS1411 aptamer) are co-incubated with exosomes to achieve surface functionalization [111]. For example, anchoring RGD peptides onto the exosome membrane via DSPE-PEG guided their specific enrichment in activated endothelial cells and macrophages expressing αvβ3 integrin, thereby precisely reaching areas of neovascularization and active inflammation in soft tissue repair [111]. This method is simple to operate but requires attention to potential toxicity from lipid molecules.

Compared with cargo optimization, targeting modification mainly addresses biodistribution rather than intrinsic therapeutic potency. Genetic display strategies provide stable ligand presentation but require parental-cell manipulation and affect exosome production. Chemical modification is more modular and can be performed after EV isolation, but excessive ligand density, chemical conjugation, or lipid insertion alter surface charge, membrane integrity, immune recognition, and clearance behavior. For ADSC-Exos-based soft tissue regeneration, future targeting strategies should shift from tumor-oriented examples toward regeneration-relevant targets, such as ischemic endothelial cells, inflammatory macrophages, activated fibroblasts, and fibrotic or poorly vascularized tissue niches.

Integration with biomaterials: constructing scaffold systems for local delivery and sustained release

Integration with biomaterials represents a macro-level engineering strategy that goes beyond modifying the exosomes themselves, focusing on constructing advanced material systems to precisely control the delivery behavior of ADSC-Exos at the injury site, engineering their release kinetics and duration of action to better mimic the sustained and coordinated natural regeneration process [112] (Fig. 4).

Loading into Injectable Hydrogels: Loading engineered ADSC-Exos into thermosensitive or photo-crosslinkable injectable hydrogels (e.g., hyaluronic acid, chitosan, Pluronic F127) is one of the most promising strategies in soft tissue regeneration [113]. This system can effectively reshape the spatiotemporal distribution of ADSC-Exos: after injection, the hydrogel forms a three-dimensional network at body temperature or under light, encapsulating the exosomes as a local “drug reservoir” [114]. For instance, a Pluronic F127 hydrogel loaded with hADSC-derived exosomes enabled sustained release, which kedly promoted full-thickness wound healing in mice. This therapeutic effect was manifested by accelerated wound closure, improved re-epithelialization, increased collagen deposition, and enhanced angiogenesis, coupled with the suppression of pro-inflammatory factors including IL-6 and TNF-α [115]. Multifunctional hydrogels tightly bind exosomes through covalent bonding with surface proteins and hydrogen bonds within the network; their degradation rate is slower than the exosome release rate, which not only delays rapid clearance of exosomes by body fluids, achieving controlled sustained release, but also prevents inappropriate physical interactions of exosomes in the complex wound environment, thereby maintaining drug concentration within the optimal therapeutic window and greatly improving repair efficiency [116]. Encapsulated within a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel, hypoxia-preconditioned ADSC-derived exosomes (ADSC-HExos) serve as carriers for circ-Snhg11 delivery. This activates the miR-144-3p/NFE2L2/HIF1α signaling axis, thereby promoting vascular endothelial cell function and collectively accelerating diabetic wound healing [46]. Chitosan/silk fibroin sponges as carriers for human gingival MSC-derived exosomes also exhibit excellent wound healing capabilities [117].

Integration with 3D Printed Scaffolds: For larger tissue defects, ADSC-Exos can be combined with 3D printed bioactive scaffolds (e.g., polycolactone/gelatin scaffolds, ellularized ECM scaffolds). This method modulates the distribution of exosomes in three-dimensional space, allowing their gradual release as the scaffold material degrades. For example, 3D bioprinted GelMA hydrogels loaded with MSC exosomes can be used to repair osteochondral defects by restoring mitochondrial function and guiding cell migration to promote cartilage regeneration [118]. Loading exosomes onto ellularized ECM scaffolds (e.g., ellularized amniotic membrane, ellularized nerve scaffolds) to repair rat sciatic nerve defects can effectively promote axonal growth and functional recovery [118]. In parallel, such scaffolds offer a supportive matrix that facilitates cellular adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, thereby guiding the regenerating tissue toward a structured and functional state. Moreover, graphene-based nanomaterials (such as graphene oxide (GO) and reduced graphene oxide (rGO)) are excellent carriers for exosomes. They can be engineered into various forms—including hydrogels, films, and aerogels—for managing infected wounds. Capitalizing on their high specific surface area, strong mechanical properties, inherent conductivity, and antimicrobial potential, these materials can eradicate bacteria via mechanisms like physical disruption or photothermal conversion. This antibacterial action works in concert with the therapeutic effects of exosomes to collectively promote regenerative, scar-minimized healing [119].

Other Formulations: Microneedles, Microspheres, and Patch-Based Systems: Beyond injectable hydrogels and 3D scaffolds, other exosome–biomaterial formulations, including microneedles, microspheres, patches, sponges, and cryogels, have been developed to improve local EV delivery. Microneedle systems can bypass the skin barrier and deliver EVs into the dermis with minimal invasiveness, making them suitable for superficial wounds and skin regeneration [120, 121]. For example, microneedle-assisted delivery of ADSC-EVs has been reported to improve UV-induced photoaging, while hydrogel microneedle patches loaded with stem cell-derived mitochondria-rich EVs enabled gradual release and promoted chronic wound repair [122]. Microsphere-based systems can encapsulate EVs within biodegradable particles to improve injectability, local retention, and sustained release, although EV stability during fabrication and release remains a key concern [116]. Patch-, sponge-, cryogel-, and ellularized matrix-based systems can further combine wound coverage, mechanical support, and EV reservoir functions, thereby expanding the formulation options for ADSC-Exos-based soft tissue regeneration [123–125].

Overall, biomaterial-assisted delivery expands ADSC-Exos engineering from vesicle-level modification to tissue-level delivery control [126]. By improving local retention, spatial distribution, and release kinetics, these platforms are particularly suitable for local soft tissue defects. However, their therapeutic performance depends on the compatibility between EV stability, material degradation, mechanical properties, and the biological timing of tissue repair [116, 118].

Comparative evaluation of functional engineering strategies

Although different engineering strategies have been developed to improve the regenerative performance of ADSC-Exos, they are not functionally equivalent and should be selected according to specific therapeutic goals [78, 93, 127, 128]. Cargo loading strategies are most suitable when a defined molecular signal, such as a miRNA, circRNA, protein, or pro-angiogenic factor, needs to be enriched; however, these approaches alter vesicle integrity, loading reproducibility, and cargo stoichiometry [96–98, 129, 130]. Surface modification mainly improves tissue or cell targeting, but excessive ligand density, chemical conjugation, or membrane insertion affect membrane stability, biodistribution, and immunogenicity [127, 131]. Biomaterial-assisted delivery is particularly useful for local soft tissue repair because it improves exosome retention, protects vesicles from rapid clearance, and enables sustained or stimulus-responsive release; nevertheless, its efficacy depends strongly on material composition, degradation behavior, mechanical properties, and compatibility with the wound or tissue microenvironment [74, 112, 126, 132]. Therefore, the therapeutic value of engineered ADSC-Exos should not be judged only by whether modification improves a single outcome, but by whether the selected strategy matches the dominant biological barrier in each regenerative scenario, such as insufficient angiogenesis, persistent inflammation, poor local retention, or disordered extracellular matrix remodeling [133–136] (Table 3).

Wound healing models

Effective wound healing requires the coordinated restoration of epidermal barrier integrity, inflammatory resolution, angiogenesis, fibroblast activation, extracellular matrix deposition, and subsequent matrix remodeling [135]. As the skin functions as both a physical barrier and an immunological interface, impaired healing is often associated with persistent inflammation, oxidative stress, insufficient vascularization, delayed re-epithelialization, and disordered collagen remodeling [136]. Therefore, ADSC-Exos-based therapeutic strategies for wound repair should not be evaluated solely by wound closure rate, but also by their ability to regulate the inflammatory microenvironment, promote vascular regeneration, accelerate keratinocyte and fibroblast responses, and restore organized extracellular matrix architecture [133, 134].

In wound healing applications, functionally engineered ADSC-Exos demonstrate enhanced therapeutic efficacy through diverse engineering strategies. A prominent approach involves preconditioning parent cells under hypoxic conditions. When these hypoxia-primed ADSC-Exos are incorporated into GelMA hydrogel, they facilitate the delivery of specific cargo, such as circ-Snhg11, which modulates the miR-144-3p/NFE2L2/HIF-1α pathway to boost vascular endothelial cell activity and accelerate diabetic wound closure [46]. Beyond this, hypoxic preconditioning upregulates other circular RNAs, including circ-IGF1R and circ-Erbb2ip, which contribute to superior healing outcomes compared to normoxic EVs. The mechanism involves circ-IGF1R acting as a sponge for miR-503-5p, thereby alleviating its suppression of HK2 and VEGFA to promote angiogenesis [47]. arately, angiogenesis is enhanced alongside the reduction of oxidative stress and inflammation via the circ-Erbb2ip/miR-670-5p/Nrf1 axis [137].

Low-intensity ultrasound has also been explored as a physical stimulation approach for engineering ADSC-Exos. Application of ultrasound at 1.5 W/cm² can increase EV yield dramatically and enrich them with pro-healing miRNAs, which in turn promote keratinocyte differentiation, re-epithelialization, collagen deposition, and neovascularization [138]. Additionally, exosomes from ADSCs exhibit intrinsic anti-fibrotic effects. Their action involves the downregulation of key extracellular matrix components (e.g., collagen I/III, fibronectin, α-SMA) in keloid fibroblasts, which is mediated by the suppression of the TGF-β2/Smad3 and Notch-1 signaling pathways. This points to a role for unmodified ADSC exosomes in attenuating aberrant scar development [139].

ADSC-Exos also function as efficient nanocarriers for therapeutic agents. In diabetic wound models, administration of ADSC-Exos engineered to overexpress Nrf2 led to a ked reduction in ulcer size. This improvement was attributed to enhanced formation of granulation tissue, stimulation of angiogenesis, elevated secretion of growth factors, and concomitant suppression of inflammatory and oxidative stress responses [140]. Likewise, ADSC-Exos loaded with mmu_circ_0000250 were shown to reactivate endothelial progenitor cell function and foster angiogenesis through the induction of autophagy, contributing to improved wound closure [141]. In addition, extracellular vesicles enriched with functional mitochondria derived from ADSCs have been demonstrated to ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction and promote macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype in radiation-induced chronic skin wounds [125].

The range of therapeutic vesicles has broadened to encompass apoptotic bodies (ApoBDs) derived from ADSCs. These ApoBDs can modulate macrophage plasticity towards a reparative phenotype, attenuate wound inflammation, and support new blood vessel growth. A pivotal molecular mediator, miR-20a-5p, acts by dampening excessive JAK-STAT pathway signaling, thereby remodeling the immune milieu and presenting a el therapeutic avenue for diabetic wound healing [142].

A major challenge for EV-based therapy is their rapid clearance in the active wound environment. Consequently, research focuses on combining ADSC-Exos with biomaterials to improve local retention and enable controlled release [143]. Hyaluronic acid (HA) and its hydrogel formulations act as excellent EV carriers and ideal wound dressings [144]. Studies indicate that combining ADSC-Exos with HA effectively inhibits inflammatory cell infiltration at the wound site, promoting healing [145]. Zhou et al. constructed a Pluronic F-127 thermosensitive hydrogel loaded with ADSC-Exos, which reduced IL-6 levels and exhibited significant anti-inflammatory effects (e.g., reased TNF-α, changes in CD68 and CD206 kers) [115]. A chitosan hydrogel/ADSC-EV composite dressing also achieved sustained EV release and prolonged efficacy [146]. For infected or hypoxic wounds, advanced systems like hydrogels co-loaded with silver ions and ADSC-Exos provide simultaneous antibacterial, antioxidant, and pro-regenerative effects [147]. To directly target hypoxia, a combined delivery platform integrating oxygen and ADSC-Exos (OxOband) accelerated wound closure, enhanced collagen formation, re-epithelialization, and blood vessel growth, while reducing oxidative damage [148]. Scaffold materials, which provide structural support for cellular adhesion, can further improve EV efficacy. For instance, three-dimensional scaffolds derived from ellularized heart tissue serve as delivery vehicles for ADSC-Exos, enabling them to mitigate inflammation [149]. Human acellular amniotic membrane (hAAM) scaffolds, recognized for excellent biocompatibility, are also utilized to make carriers; research showed that hAAMs carrying ADSC-Exos improved the regulation of inflammation in diabetic wounds [150]. Together, this evidence underscores the potential of ADSC-Exos in reducing inflammation and advancing wound repair.

Muscle repair models

In muscle repair models, functionally engineered ADSC-Exos priily target ischemic, injured, or degenerated skeletal muscle tissue, leveraging their engineered paracrine signals to promote complex regenerative processes [151]. A common approach involves either preconditioning the parent ADSCs or directly engineering the EVs to increase the concentration of specific therapeutic agents. For instance, Jiang et al. obtained Netrin1-enriched ADSC-Exos (N-Exos) by overexpressing Netrin1 in ADSCs; in a diabetic limb ischemia mouse model, injection of these N-Exos significantly enhanced endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation, and induced macrophage polarization towards a reparative M2 phenotype by activating the PI3K/Akt/eNOS and MEK/ERK signaling pathways, thereby effectively promoting angiogenesis, collateral artery remodeling, and protecting muscle tissue from damage [152]. Similarly, Wang et al. developed engineered exosomes carrying a muscle-specific targeting peptide and overexpressing miR-26a, aiming for precise delivery to myocytes to limit muscle atrophy and myocardial fibrosis [153]. Beyond loading specific miRNAs or proteins, modification strategies can also extend to the exosome surface, where engineered surface molecules such as integrins, tetraspanins, or heparan sulfate proteoglycans enhance binding and internalization by target muscle cells, thereby optimizing functional output [154]. In biomaterial combination strategies, researchers have combined ADSC-Exos with hydrogels for injection into the rotator cuff injury site in rats, successfully modulating tendon-derived stem cell differentiation and promoting repair [155]. These functionally engineered vesicles play multiple roles in muscle repair: they not only improve blood perfusion and oxygen supply to the damaged area, creating a favorable microenvironment for regeneration [156], but also directly enhance both the proliferative and migratory capacities of muscle cells (e.g., C2C12 myoblasts) [157], and drive myogenic differentiation by upregulating myogenic regulatory factors like MyoD1 and myogenin, ultimately promoting the formation of new muscle fibers and the restoration of muscle structure and function [158]. Therefore, research based on muscle repair models indicates that precise functional engineering of ADSC-Exos cargo and targeting is a key direction for enhancing their therapeutic efficacy against conditions like sarcopenia and ischemic muscle injury [159].

Adipose tissue regeneration models

In soft tissue defect repair and fat grafting, improving the survival rate of transplanted fat is a central challenge, the key to which lies in addressing post-transplant ischemia and secondary absorption [160]. Functionally engineered ADSC-Exos provide an inative solution by precisely intervening in this process through multiple synergistic strategies [161]. Their mechanisms of action priily revolve around promoting vascularization, improving the ischemic microenvironment, and regulating immune inflammation, aiming to provide timely and adequate blood supply support for the grafted fat and create a favorable regenerative microenvironment [57].

Promoting Angiogenesis and Improving Blood Supply is the priy step to ensure fat survival. Research shows that hypoxic preconditioning (e.g., 5% O₂) is an efficient functional engineering strategy that significantly enhances the pro-angiogenic capacity of ADSC-Exos. Han et al. experimentally confirmed that hypoxia-preconditioned ADSC-Exos, by increasing CD31⁺ vessel density and effectively activating endothelial cell tube-forming function, improved fat graft survival rate by approximately 1.5 times [34]. This pro-angiogenic effect stems from the optimization of their cargo composition. For example, thymosin β4 (Tβ4) pretreatment induces ADSCs to secrete exosomes (Tβ4-Exos) enriched with specific miRNAs. It is indicated that Tβ4-Exos strongly promote the proliferation of endothelial cells and the growth of new blood vessels by regulating key signaling pathways such as Apelin, Hippo, and VEGF-VEGFR2, fundamentally improving the ischemic microenvironment of the grafted fat [162]. To prolong and amplify this pro-angiogenic effect, studies have attempted to combine ADSC-Exos with biomaterials to construct local sustained-release systems. For instance, combining them with platelet-rich plasma (PRP) leverages the active components provided by PRP (e.g., PDGF, VEGF) to create a synergistic effect with ADSC-Exos, jointly promoting vascular network generation and adipose tissue survival [163]. Furthermore, loading them onto ellularized adipose matrix (AAM) or hyaluronic acid scaffolds, which serve as sustained-release carriers for Exos, can continuously promote and maintain adipogenesis. Preclinical studies show that such composite systems significantly improve the long-term retention rate of fat grafts [164].

Modulation of Immune and Inflammatory Reactions represents a pivotal approach designed to transform the local environment at the transplant site from an inflammatory to a regenerative phenotype. ADSC-Exos can modulate the activity of critical immune cells through the transfer of selected miRNAs. For instance, work by Hao et al. indicated that ADSC-Exos enhance the survival of fat grafts via the delivery of let-7c miRNA, which influences macrophage polarization [40]. This points the way for direct engineering of exosomes, i.e., mass-producing “customized” exosome drugs with defined immunomodulatory functions and higher efficacy by enriching them with let-7c or other specific anti-inflammatory, pro-repair molecules (e.g., miR-21-5p, miR-146a), thereby precisely inhibiting excessive post-transplant inflammatory responses and reducing inflammatory infiltration [165].

Other emerging soft tissue regeneration models: tendon and peripheral nerve repair

Beyond wound healing, muscle repair, and adipose tissue regeneration, ADSC-Exos have also been explored in tendon-to-bone healing and peripheral nerve regeneration. In rotator cuff repair, ADSC-Exos were shown to promote tendon-derived stem cell proliferation and differentiation, and an ADSC-Exos–hydrogel complex improved the expression of osteogenic, chondrogenic, and tendon-related kers, including RUNX2, Sox-9, TNMD, TNC, and Scx, while enhancing the mechanical properties of repaired tissue [155]. In addition, circadian rhythm-regulated ADSC-derived sEVs combined with a triphasic microneedle scaffold have been reported to improve tendon-to-bone healing by regulating macrophage-mediated inflammation and fibrocartilage regeneration [166].

ADSC-Exos also show potential in peripheral nerve repair. In a mouse sciatic nerve crush injury model, locally applied ADSC-Exos improved nerve histomorphometric parameters and increased neurotrophic factors such as NGF and GDNF, with HDACs, APP, and ITGB1 suggested as potential protein mediators [167]. Moreover, exosomes derived from Schwann cell-like differentiated hADMSCs displayed enhanced nerve-oriented functions, including antioxidative protection of Schwann cells, promotion of angiogenesis, suppression of inflammatory cytokines, and stimulation of sensory neuron axonal growth [168]. These findings suggest that tendon and peripheral nerve repair represent emerging but promising extensions of ADSC-Exos-based soft tissue regeneration, although further standardized studies are required to clarify optimal dosing, delivery systems, long-term functional recovery, and translational feasibility.

Clinical translation and registered clinical studies

Although ADSC-Exos and adipose tissue-derived EVs have shown promising effects in preclinical models of soft tissue regeneration, their clinical translation remains at an early stage. Publicly registered clinical studies directly evaluating adipose tissue-derived exosomes or EVs for soft tissue repair are still scarce and are currently mainly focused on wound healing. Discussion

This review sumizes the biological basis, engineering strategies, and regenerative applications of ADSC-Exos in soft tissue repair. Importantly, this review does not aim to establish a fundamentally new engineering paradigm. Instead, it provides a structured and critical synthesis of established ADSC-Exos engineering strategies and discusses how these strategies be selected according to specific regenerative bottlenecks. Cargo optimization, surface modification, and biomaterial-assisted delivery should not be viewed as interchangeable technical modifications. Rather, they address different barriers, including insufficient therapeutic signaling, poor tissue targeting, rapid clearance, limited local retention, and inadequate spatiotemporal control. Therefore, future ADSC-Exos design should match engineering strategies with disease-specific regenerative barriers and measurable functional endpoints, rather than simply adding more modifications [127, 128, 169, 170].

A key issue in the current field is the balance between functional enhancement and biological complexity. Cargo optimization strategies, such as hypoxic preconditioning or cytokine stimulation, can enrich pro-angiogenic, immunomodulatory, or anti-fibrotic molecules in ADSC-Exos, thereby improving their therapeutic potency [41, 43, 58, 74, 171]. However, these approaches also generate highly complex and sometimes incompletely characterized cargo profiles. The therapeutic effects of engineered ADSC-Exos not be attributable to a single miRNA, circRNA, or protein, but rather to the combined action of multiple RNAs, proteins, lipids, and membrane-associated signals. Therefore, future studies should move beyond single-cargo validation and adopt multi-omics-based analyses to clarify how global cargo remodeling contributes to functional outcomes [172, 173].

Surface modification and direct exogenous loading provide more controllable methods for improving targeting specificity and therapeutic payload delivery [127, 174]. Nevertheless, these strategies compromise the intrinsic biological identity and safety advantages of native ADSC-Exos. Although native ADSC-Exos are generally considered to have low immunogenicity, surface engineering strategies weaken this advantage by introducing non-native epitopes or altering vesicle surface properties [175]. For example, sonication, electroporation, surfactant treatment, or hybrid membrane engineering alter vesicle integrity, induce aggregation, damage membrane proteins, or affect biodistribution [176–178]. Similarly, chemical conjugation, lipid anchoring, or peptide display enhance tissue targeting but could also change surface charge, protein orientation, immune recognition, and clearance behavior [174, 179, 180]. These modifications increase immune recognition, accelerate clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, or raise unexpected immunogenicity concerns after repeated administration [131]. Thus, engineered ADSC-Exos should be evaluated not only for therapeutic efficacy but also for vesicle morphology, size distribution, zeta potential, surface ker retention, cargo stability, uptake efficiency, biodistribution, immune recognition, repeated-dose tolerability, and long-term immunogenicity.

Biomaterial-assisted delivery offers another important layer of functional engineering by improving local retention, sustained release, and spatial control of ADSC-Exos at the injury site [95, 103]. Hydrogels, scaffolds, ellularized matrices, and other delivery platforms can partially overcome rapid vesicle clearance and create a regenerative niche that supports angiogenesis, immune modulation, and matrix remodeling [106, 109, 111]. However, the therapeutic performance of these systems depends on the compatibility between vesicle stability, material degradation, and release kinetics. Excessively rapid release fail to maintain an effective local concentration, whereas overly slow release delay the biological window required for inflammation control or angiogenesis. Therefore, future biomaterial–exosome systems should be designed according to the temporal requirements of specific soft tissue repair processes rather than simply serving as passive carriers.

From a translational perspective, several major barriers remain unresolved. Consistent with the limited number of registered clinical studies sumized in Sect. 4.6, the clinical translation of ADSC-Exos for soft tissue regeneration remains at an early stage, with current clinical exploration mainly focused on wound healing and limited evidence available for muscle repair, adipose tissue regeneration, tendon-to-bone healing, or peripheral nerve repair. First, scalable and standardized manufacturing remains a central challenge. Current ADSC-Exos production involves multiple variable steps, including donor selection, ADSC expansion, culture conditions, preconditioning, EV isolation, purification, storage, and potency testing [174, 181–183]. Differences in these parameters substantially affect exosome yield, cargo composition, and biological activity. Second, quality control standards for engineered ADSC-Exos remain insufficiently unified. In addition to conventional EV kers and particle concentration, clinically relevant potency assays should be established to reflect key regenerative functions such as angiogenesis, macrophage polarization, fibroblast regulation, and matrix remodeling [184]. Third, long-term safety requires more rigorous investigation, particularly for repeatedly administered, genetically engineered, chemically modified, or biomaterial-combined ADSC-Exos [129, 130, 185–187].

Future development of ADSC-Exos benefit from more mechanism-guided and data-driven design principles, including cargo selection, lesion-specific targeting, microenvironment-responsive release, and clinically scalable manufacturing. However, AI-assisted molecular design and protein language models should currently be interpreted as forward-looking conceptual tools rather than established ADSC-Exos engineering strategies. At present, there is no direct evidence that AI-assisted design, protein language models, or generative protein design frameworks have been applied to the de o design of ADSC-Exos membrane proteins, targeting peptides, or surface-display fusion constructs in soft tissue regeneration settings. In the broader EV field, AI-related studies have mainly been used for EV classification, source identification, liquid biopsy, or EV-associated protein prediction rather than therapeutic EV engineering itself [188–190]. These studies indicate that machine learning can extract informative patterns from EV-associated spectral, biophysical, or sequence-derived features, but they do not yet demonstrate AI-guided modification of EV cargo, surface ligands, biodistribution, or regenerative function.

By contrast, protein language models and generative protein design tools have shown stronger transferable potential in adjacent protein-engineering fields. Large-scale protein language models such as ProGen have demonstrated the ability to generate functional protein sequences across diverse protein families [191]. Structure-informed protein language models can improve antibody affinity and neutralizing activity, suggesting their potential value for optimizing ligand–receptor specificity [192]. RFdiffusion-based design has further enabled the de o generation of target-specific protein binders, and structure prediction tools such as AlphaFold2, ESMFold, and AlphaFold3 can support structural assessment of protein folding, interaction interfaces, and candidate fusion constructs [193–196]. These advances provide a rationale for future application of AI-assisted design to ADSC-Exos engineering, particularly in cargo–function prediction, therapeutic cargo prioritization, targeting ligand design, and pre-screening of membrane-associated peptides or fusion proteins.

Nevertheless, these approaches remain transferable tools from adjacent fields rather than validated engineering strategies for ADSC-Exos. For ADSC-Exos, AI-guided design would need to be linked to experimentally grounded datasets integrating donor-cell transcriptomes, EV proteomes, small RNA cargos, surface molecules, target-cell responses, biodistribution profiles, and regenerative outcomes. In addition, any AI-prioritized cargo combination, targeting ligand, or membrane-display construct would still require systematic validation of vesicle biogenesis, membrane topology, surface display efficiency, vesicle integrity, cargo stability, targeting specificity, biodistribution, immunogenicity, and therapeutic efficacy in relevant soft tissue regeneration models. Taken together, AI-assisted design is best viewed at present as a hypothesis-generating and pre-screening tool for future ADSC-Exos engineering, with its practical value depending on high-quality cargo–function datasets and systematic experimental validation.

Engineered exosomes also serve as potential carriers for nucleic acids, proteins, or CRISPR-Cas-related components, enabling targeted regulation of fibrosis-related, inflammatory, or angiogenic pathways [132, 197]. In addition, stimulus-responsive exosome systems enable therapeutic release or activation in response to pathological cues such as acidic pH, elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), abnormal enzyme activity, or hypoxia. For example, pH-responsive engineered exosomes orated with vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein and hyaluronan synthase type 2 have been developed to trigger membrane fusion under low-pH conditions, thereby inducing chondrocytes to produce endogenous high-molecular-weight hyaluronan and promote cartilage repair [198]. In diabetic wound models, microenvironment-responsive hydrogels have also been designed to respond to low pH, high glucose, and ROS, enabling on-demand antibacterial drug release while exosome-mediated glucose regulation improves the systemic and local repair environment [199]. More directly related to ADSC-Exos, an MMP-responsive PEG hydrogel loaded with ADSC-Exos was reported to promote diabetic wound healing by enabling enzyme-responsive exosome release, improving cellular function, and alleviating oxidative stress [169]. These examples suggest that future ADSC-Exos systems evolve from static delivery vehicles into microenvironment-adaptive therapeutic platforms. However, most stimulus-responsive designs remain at the preclinical stage, and their feasibility, safety, manufacturability, and regulatory acceptability require careful validation before clinical application.

This review also has several limitations. First, although we aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of ADSC-Exos engineering and soft tissue regeneration, this article was not designed as a systematic review. Therefore, the literature search strategy, inclusion criteria, exclusion criteria, and risk-of-bias assessment were not conducted according to PRISMA or other formal systematic review guidelines. Second, the available evidence remains largely preclinical, with substantial heterogeneity in ADSC sources, culture conditions, EV isolation methods, engineering procedures, dosing units, animal models, and outcome measurements. Third, some examples discussed in this review were derived from the broader engineered EV or MSC-EV literature rather than ADSC-Exos-specific studies. These examples were included to illustrate potentially transferable design principles, but their direct applicability to ADSC-Exos requires further experimental confirmation. Finally, clinical evidence for ADSC-Exos in soft tissue regeneration remains limited, and conclusions regarding therapeutic efficacy and long-term safety should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

ADSC-Exos represent a promising cell-free therapeutic platform for soft tissue regeneration because of their intrinsic pro-angiogenic, immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, and matrix-remodeling activities. Established engineering strategies, including cargo optimization, surface modification, and biomaterial-assisted delivery, can further enhance their regenerative specificity, potency, and durability. However, these approaches should be selected according to specific regenerative barriers, translational feasibility, and safety requirements, rather than being presented as isolated technical modifications. Future progress will depend on standardized production, rigorous biophysical and functional characterization, clinically relevant potency assays, long-term biosafety evaluation, and well-designed translational studies.
