Virtual multidisciplinary discussion across borders for interstitial lung disease: a prospective, multicentre study from India, the UK, Greece and Sri Lanka
Authors: Asmita Anilkumar Mehta, Sujeet Rajan, Subin Ahmed, Bhavin Jankharia, Athol U Wells, Mithun CB, BVM Mohan, Vimal Raj, Ritu Kulshrestha, Pralhad Prabhudesai, Aparna Irodi, Lakshmi Priya Valathara Pradeep, Amila Rathnapala, KM Antoniou, Sharada Nagoti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess diagnostic concordance and reclassification following an India-led, multinational virtual multidisciplinary discussion (V-MDD) platform for interstitial lung disease (ILD).DesignProspective, multicentre service-evaluation study.SettingTwenty-four Indian referral centres connected through a secure virtual platform, with international faculty participation from the UK, Greece and Sri Lanka.ParticipantsA total of 127 anonymised ILD cases discussed across 29 V-MDD sessions (February 2024–February 2025). Each panel included ≥4 pulmonologists, two pulmonary pathologists, one of three rotating thoracic radiologists and one of two rheumatologists, along with international experts.ResultsThe cohort (mean age 52.6±16.1 years; 53.5% female (68/127)) most frequently presented with dyspnoea (82.6%) and cough (73.2%). Pre-V-MDD diagnoses included hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and sarcoidosis as distinct disease entities, and usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) and non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) as radiological patterns, along with connective tissue disease (CTD)-ILD and other ILDs. Concordance between pre- and post-V-MDD CT diagnoses was substantial (κ=0.658; 95% CI 0.562 to 0.754; p <0.001). Histopathology concordance was similarly high (κ=0.861 for HP; κ=0.650 for other ILDs). Overall, 23% of cases were reclassified following discussion. Diagnostic stability was greatest for organising pneumonia, CTD-ILD and UIP pattern, whereas HP and sarcoidosis showed the highest reclassification rates.ConclusionsThe India-led, multinational V-MDD model demonstrated substantial diagnostic concordance and refined nearly one-quarter of ILD diagnoses. This virtual, scalable framework expands access to subspecialty expertise and offers a practical blueprint for standardising ILD care in resource-limited and cross-border settings.Keywords: Rheumatology, RESPIRATORY MEDICINE (see Thoracic Medicine), RADIOLOGY & IMAGING, HISTOPATHOLOGY

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) comprises nearly 200 conditions that result in inflammation and fibrosis of lung tissue.1 2 These entities are defined by characteristic clinical, radiological, histopathological and prognostic features.3 4 Multidisciplinary discussion (MDD) is regarded as the gold standard for ILD diagnosis and management, as it improves diagnostic confidence, inter-observer agreement and reduces misclassification.5,7 Early evaluation at specialised ILD centres has been linked to better survival and fewer hospitalisations, likely due to accurate subtype identification and timely, targeted therapy.8 9

However, access to such expertise is often limited in non-specialised settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the absence of pulmonologists, radiologists, pathologists and rheumatologists contributes to diagnostic inaccuracy and inappropriate management. 5 10Although the multidisciplinary approach is the gold standard for ILD diagnosis, it is not always feasible due to limited local expertise, geographical barriers and time constraints, but these challenges can be addressed through digital platforms.11 Virtual multidisciplinary discussions (V-MDDs) have emerged as a scalable solution to bridge this gap by enabling access to expert opinion across institutions. We report the first Pan-India virtual ILD multidisciplinary meeting (MDM) initiative and evaluate concordance between pre- and post-MDM diagnoses based on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) and histopathology findings.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a prospective, multicentre observational cohort study of patients with suspected or confirmed ILD discussed in structured V-MDDs between February 2024 and February 2025. This study evaluated a service model rather than a diagnostic test and reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

The study subjects were ILD cases, not physicians. All patient data were fully de-identified before submission, and informed consent was waived by the Institutional Ethics Committee given the anonymised, non-interventional nature of the data.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Pre-V-MDD diagnostic setting

Pre-V-MDD diagnoses were made by the primary pulmonologist at the referring centre based on available clinical, radiological and pathological data.

In some tertiary centres, these reflected the outcome of local institutional MDDs, but in most cases, they represented individual clinician assessments prior to referral. This approach reflects real-world referral patterns and diagnostic heterogeneity.

Participating centres and expert panel

A total of 24 centres across India contributed cases to the V-MDD platform. Each meeting included at least four pulmonologists, two pulmonary pathologists, one of three rotating thoracic radiologists and one of two rheumatologists, with additional participation from international experts in the UK (n=2), Greece (n=1) and Sri Lanka (n=1) (figure 1). Overview of the participating cities and collaborating centres contributing clinical cases to the Pan-India Virtual ILD Multidisciplinary Discussion platform is provided in online supplemental table S1.

India-led virtual multidisciplinary discussion network. Kochi served as the coordinating centre, linking pulmonologists, radiologists, pathologists and rheumatologists across India with international faculty from the UK, Greece and Sri Lanka.

All core panel members had ≥10 years of post-specialty clinical experience and were based at high-volume ILD referral centres (each managing >200 ILD cases annually). This ensured senior-level expertise and balanced representation across disciplines. The details of international faculty participating in the Pan-India V-MDD initiative are shown in online supplemental table S2.

Virtual platform and meeting structure

The V-MDDs were conducted using a secure, password-protected videoconferencing platform with encrypted file transfer.

DICOM HRCT images and digitised histopathology slides were uploaded in advance for centralised review.

A total of 29 meetings were held at 2–4-week intervals, each lasting up to 90 min. On average, five to six cases were discussed per session. Each case received 15–20 min, allowing structured input from all specialties.

Standardisation of case submissions

All cases were submitted using a standardised PowerPoint template containing clinical history, exposure profile, autoantibody panel, pulmonary function tests and connective tissue disease (CTD) work-up. DICOM-format HRCT scans were mandatory for radiological review, and digitised histopathology slides were shared when available.

Cases not meeting these minimum dataset criteria were deferred until complete documentation was provided.

Diagnostic categorisation

For analysis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) and sarcoidosis were classified as distinct disease entities, while usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) and non-specific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) were categorised as radiological patterns representing provisional diagnoses when no specific underlying aetiology was established.

Less frequent ILD subgroups (eg, post-infectious fibrosis, vasculitis, combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE), malignancy-associated ILD, genetic ILD, overlap syndromes) were grouped as ‘others’, with their breakdown detailed in online supplemental table S3.

Consensus development process

Each case was presented by the referring pulmonologist, followed by structured input from the radiologist, pathologists and rheumatologist. The moderator synthesised viewpoints to propose a provisional consensus diagnosis, refined through open deliberation. When disagreements persisted, further discussion continued until a unified consensus was achieved. Final diagnoses were recorded with input from the treating physician to ensure clinical applicability.

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the proportion of cases undergoing diagnostic change following V-MDD review. Secondary outcomes included:

Concordance between pre- and post-V-MDD CT (HRCT) and histopathology examination (HPE) diagnoses.

Agreement between post-V-MDD diagnoses and the final consensus diagnosis.

Category-specific agreement rates and reclassification frequencies to characterise diagnostic shifts.

Concordance was quantified using Cohen’s κ with 95% CIs. Analyses were performed separately for HRCT and HPE, comparing pre- and post-V-MDD categories as well as agreement between modalities and the final consensus diagnosis.

Correlation between HRCT and HPE reclassification was moderate (r =0.61, p <0.01). Descriptive statistics summarised demographic, clinical and exposure variables. All analyses used SPSS v21 (IBM Corp., USA), with p <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics and data privacy

This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee, Amrita Institute of Medical Sciences (ECASM-AIMS-2024–109), with additional site approvals as needed. All data were anonymised before submission, and no identifying patient information was shared. Secure videoconferencing and encrypted file transfers ensured confidentiality. De-identified data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Result

Patient characteristics

A total of 127 patients were reviewed across 29 V-MDD sessions (figure 2). The mean age was 52.6±16.1 years; 68 (53.5%) were women. Dyspnoea (82.6%) and cough (73.2%) were the dominant presenting symptoms, whereas fever (18.8%), weight loss (11%), chest pain (7.1%) and clubbing (12.6%) were less frequent (table 1). Almost one-fifth of patients reported exposure to organic antigens, and more than one-third had infection- or smoking-related exposures. These data reflect the heterogeneity of ILD presentation in real-world practice.

Baseline diagnostic spectrum

Out of 127 patients, the majority were ultimately classified as CTD-ILD (15.7%), HP (13.4%), IPF/UIP pattern (12.6%), NSIP pattern (6.3%), sarcoidosis (11.0%) and organising pneumonia (OP) (6.3%), collectively accounting for nearly two-thirds of the cohort. The remaining 34.7% of patients were categorised as ‘Others’, encompassing a diverse spectrum of diagnoses including post-infectious/post-COVID fibrosis, cystic lung diseases (lymphocytic interstitial pneumonia/lymphangioleiomyomatosis/Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome), CPFE/pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis, malignancy-associated ILD, vasculitis, genetic ILD, smoking-related ILD and unclassified or overlap patterns (table 2). In accordance with the predefined analytical framework, HP and sarcoidosis were analysed as distinct disease entities, whereas UIP and NSIP were reported as radiological pattern-based provisional diagnoses rather than final aetiological classifications. This distribution underscores the clinical heterogeneity of ILD presentations and the critical role of structured V-MDD review in refining diagnoses within complex or atypical cases. Following the V-MDD review, diagnostic refinement was most prominent in HP and sarcoidosis, whereas OP, UIP pattern, NSIP pattern and CTD-ILD remained stable. Several provisional or indeterminate cases were reassigned to the ‘Others’ category, underscoring the heterogeneity of real-world ILD and the value of structured consensus review.

Prior to V-MDD, the most frequent diagnostic categories were HP (13.4%), sarcoidosis (11.0%), NSIP pattern (6.3%), OP (6.3%) and IPF/UIP pattern (11.8%), with 38.6% classified as ‘other’ or unclassifiable. Dual pathology was identified in 2.4%. This distribution, weighted towards HP and sarcoidosis, is consistent with regional epidemiology but also underscores the substantial burden of diagnostically challenging cases referred to the national forum.

Impact of V-MDD on radiological interpretation

Concordance between pre- and post-V-MDD CT diagnoses was substantial (κ=0.658; 95% CI 0.562 to 0.754; p<0.001) (table 3). OP demonstrated the highest diagnostic stability (85.7%), followed by CTD-ILD and IPF/UIP pattern, reflecting strong agreement in radiological interpretation across the V-MDD process. In contrast, HP and sarcoidosis exhibited higher rates of diagnostic reclassification, while the greatest variability was observed in the heterogeneous ‘Others’ category. These findings highlight the limitations of stand-alone radiological interpretation, particularly in atypical presentations, and emphasise the value of structured, multidisciplinary consensus review by ILD-experienced radiologists.

Concordance between pre- and post-V-MDD HPE diagnostic categories was substantial to almost perfect (κ=0.861; 95% CI 0.783 to 0.939; p<0.001) (table 4). OP (100 %) and sarcoidosis (100 %) demonstrated the highest diagnostic stability across V-MDD review, reflecting strong agreement in these entities. Minimal reclassification occurred within the HP group, while the ‘Others’ category showed modest variability, primarily due to diagnostic refinement achieved during multidisciplinary consensus. These findings underscore the value of structured V-MDD review in accurately delineating HP and sarcoidosis from other ILD subsets, thereby enhancing diagnostic confidence.

Overall diagnostic change

Following V-MDD deliberation, the diagnosis was revised in 23% of cases. These revisions typically involved patients with discordant or borderline CT and histopathological findings, which were reconciled through integration of clinical context, exposure history and serological data. Consequently, nearly one in four patients benefitted from a more refined or entirely reclassified diagnosis, underscoring the value of structured multidisciplinary consensus in enhancing diagnostic precision

Discussion

This study represents the first India-led, multicentre experience of a V-MDD model for ILD. By connecting pulmonologists, thoracic radiologists, pulmonary pathologists and rheumatologists across multiple centres, the platform enabled systematic review of complex ILD cases within a resource-diverse setting.

A central finding was that 23% of patients underwent diagnostic revision following V-MDD review. While this proportion is lower than some international reports, it reflects both the value of multidisciplinary integration and the maturity of the referral cohort in this study. For instance, Amati et al reported a 57.3% diagnostic change, largely driven by a high proportion of pre-V-MDD ‘unclassifiable ILD’ cases, while Ageely et al noted a 37% revision rate, with nearly 40% of patients experiencing changes in management.12 13 Walsh et al demonstrated that inter-observer agreement across centres improved from κ=0.50 to κ=0.71 following multidisciplinary consensus.14

Our findings lie within this spectrum: although the proportion of reclassifications was lower, the 23% revision rate remains clinically meaningful, particularly as many submitted cases had already undergone structured preliminary evaluation prior to V-MDD. This suggests that the platform was utilised not only for establishing new diagnoses but also for refining complex or borderline cases, reflecting an evolution in referral patterns and V-MDD utilisation in India.

Table 5 provides a comparative overview of key V-MDD studies. It illustrates that while diagnostic revision rates vary (23%–57%), a consistent theme across settings is that multidisciplinary consensus significantly enhances diagnostic precision in ILD. Unlike Amati et al and Ageely et al,12 13 who focused on diagnostic trajectories and management change, respectively, our study uniquely quantified modality-specific concordance for both CT and histopathology—demonstrating κ=0.658 for CT, κ=0.861 for HP histopathology and κ=0.650 for other ILD subsets. By analysing HP and sarcoidosis as separate entities, this approach highlights where V-MDD exerts the greatest diagnostic impact—particularly in heterogeneous ILD presentations—emphasising the complementary role of radiology and pathology in improving diagnostic accuracy.

Expert composition and feasibility of the V-MDD model

Another distinctive feature of our V-MDD model was its expert composition. Each session involved at least four pulmonologists, one of the two pulmonary pathologists, one of three rotating thoracic radiologists and one of two rheumatologists, ensuring balanced representation across all core specialties. This structure prevented dominance by any single discipline and allowed discordant radiological and pathological interpretations to be reconciled within the clinical context. Notably, more than one-fifth of HP and sarcoidosis cases were reclassified following discussion, underscoring the inherent diagnostic ambiguity of these entities when interpreted in isolation and the critical value of collaborative review.

Our findings align with international experience demonstrating that V-MDD enhances diagnostic precision, reduces variability and builds diagnostic confidence, particularly in atypical or overlapping ILD patterns. Unlike institutional models in high-income countries, however, this study highlights the feasibility and scalability of a national, cross-institutional virtual model in a LMIC country context, where ILD expertise remains concentrated within a limited number of academic centres.

Limitations and future perspectives

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, broad diagnostic categories such as ‘others’ reduced granularity and may have masked more specific subtypes of ILD.15 Second, despite using a structured submission template, the quality and completeness of data varied across centres. In some cases, high-resolution CT scans or pathology slides were suboptimal, limiting the robustness of interpretation. Third, management outcomes were not formally assessed. Although anecdotal feedback suggested that V-MDD discussions often influenced further investigations, referrals or treatment adjustments, systematic collection of post-V-MDD management data was beyond the scope of this study. Finally, outcome correlation was not possible, and linking V-MDD-derived diagnoses with longitudinal follow-up, treatment response and survival will be critical to validate clinical impact.

Looking forward, integration with national ILD registries could enable prospective tracking of outcomes and provide a platform for research into disease epidemiology and therapeutic patterns.16 Standardisation of imaging and pathology submissions, alongside centralised digital repositories, would further enhance diagnostic precision. Incorporating artificial intelligence–based quantitative CT and digital pathology tools may provide additional support in equivocal cases, complementing human expertise rather than replacing it.17 Finally, expansion of this V-MDD model to other LMICs has the potential to democratise access to subspecialty expertise and foster international collaborative networks in ILD care.6

Conclusion

The Pan-India V-MDD refined nearly one in four ILD diagnoses and improved concordance across CT and histopathology. By enabling collaboration among pulmonologists, radiologists, pathologists and rheumatologists nationwide, this scalable model demonstrated feasibility in a LMIC country setting. Our findings reaffirm the central role of MDD in ILD diagnosis and highlight the potential of virtual platforms to standardise care and expand access to subspecialty expertise.
