Familial risk of pancreatic cancer and interaction with hyperglycemia, smoking, and obesity among first-degree relatives
Authors: Hyun Jung Kim, Tae Uk Kang, Heather Swan, Seon Mee Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe influence of hyperglycemia, smoking, and obesity on the familial risk of pancreatic cancer (PC) is incompletely understood. We quantified the risk of PC in first-degree relatives (FDRs) and evaluated potential interactions between family history and these modifiable factors.MethodsUsing the Korean National Health Insurance Database, we identified 6,217,057 individuals from 1.7 million families containing at least one FDR and followed them from 2002 to 2018. Hazard ratios (HRs) for incident PC were estimated for individuals with versus without an affected FDR. Additive interaction between family history and hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity was assessed with the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI).ResultsIndividuals with an affected FDR had 43 newly diagnosed cases of PC, with an adjusted HR of 2.46 (95% CI, 1.81–3.33), corresponding to an incidence of 0.51 per 10,000 person-years. Risk was greatest when the affected relative was a sibling (HR 13.57; 95% CI, 6.08–30.26), followed by an affected mother (HR 2.35; 95% CI, 1.45–3.78) and father (HR 2.05; 95% CI, 1.32–3.19). Familial risk was higher in younger participants and in women. Among those with an affected FDR, hyperglycemia (HR 3.48; 95% CI, 1.12–10.82), smoking (HR 3.24; 95% CI, 2.13–4.93), and obesity (HR 2.89; 95% CI, 1.81–4.62) further increased PC risk. RERI values were close to zero, indicating little additive interaction between family history and these factors.ConclusionFDRs of patients with PC have an approximately 2.5-fold elevated risk, which is further compounded—but not synergistically amplified—by hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity. Individuals who harbor both familial and lifestyle risk factors should be considered for genetic counseling and life style modification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15252-z.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Family history, First-degree relatives, Hyperglycemia, Smoking, Obesity

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is a leading cause of cancer-related death in developed countries [1]. Among established risk factors, genetic predisposition confers the greatest risk for developing PC. Current surveillance guidelines focus on carriers of high‑penetrance germline mutations or individuals with ≥ 2 affected first‑degree relatives (FDRs) [2]. However, individuals with only one affected FDR are generally managed similarly to those with no family history, even though an estimated 10% of patients with PC have at least one first- or second-degree relative [3].

Reported familial-risk estimates for FDRs vary widely, ranging from 1.7 to 3.5 [4–9]. Most of these estimates come from case–control studies [10–16], and even the two large registry-based investigations [4, 7] did not provide age- or sex-stratified risks. Therefore, population-based cohort is essential to accurately assess the familial risk of PC, including age- and sex-specific estimates.

Well-established environmental and lifestyle contributors to PC include diabetes, smoking, and obesity [17–19]. Given the multifactorial nature of pancreatic carcinogenesis, genetic susceptibility may modify the effects of these exposures. Prior studies have suggested gene–environment interactions involving both high- and low-penetrance PC-related genes and environmental risk factors. For example, polymorphisms in tobacco carcinogen–metabolizing enzymes and tumor suppressor genes may modulate susceptibility to cigarette smoke–induced PC [20]. CFTR variants have also been reported to interact with alcohol consumption in PC development [21]. However, these evidence remains limited by the small number of genes examined and the lack of replication across studies. Accordingly, quantifying risk among individuals who harbor both a familial predisposition and lifestyle risk factors is essential. Because lifestyle risks are modifiable, identifying such high-risk individuals and targeting them with behavioral interventions could yield meaningful public health benefits.

We constructed a population-based cohort to obtain precise estimates of the familial risk of PC among FDRs. We also examined whether hyperglycemia, smoking, and obesity modify the association between family history and PC risk, evaluating potential gene–environment interactions.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We linked three nationwide databases administered by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) of Korea: (1) the National Health Insurance (NHI) claims database, which provides International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) diagnoses and demographic characteristics; (2) the Support for Serious Illness (SSI) registry, a physician-verified cancer registry; and (3) the National Health Screening Program (NHSP), which provides biennial, standardized health-examination data for all beneficiaries and dependents [22]. The NHSP includes self-reported alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical activity, along with body mass index (BMI) and blood pressure (BP) and laboratory tests, including fasting blood glucose (FBG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and serum cholesterol.

Family-relationship ascertainment

In Korea, individuals who are employed or self-employed are eligible to enroll as NHI beneficiaries. The NHI database contains detailed family-relationship linkages, enabling identification of biological parents, full siblings, and offspring. Spouses and offspring of beneficiaries are enrolled as dependents upon marriage or birth registration. To ensure analysis of true biological FDRs, we excluded records with unconfirmed or potentially non-biological relationships—such as single-parent records with a missing linkage to the other biological parent, stepparents, half-siblings, and children not registered as dependents.

Study population

From approximately 50 million insured persons, we identified 22 million with both biological parents recorded. After excluding those younger than 20 years or those without any NHSP examination, 6,217,057 offspring from 1.7 million families constituted the analytic cohort (Fig. 1).

Outcome definition

Incident PC was defined as the first physician-verified entry in the SSI cancer registry with an ICD-10 code C25.0–C25.9, excluding C25.4, recorded between 1 January 2002 and 31 December 2018.

Exposure assessment

Participants were followed from 1 January 2002 until the earliest occurrence of PC diagnosis, death, or 31 December 2018. Individuals with a prior PC diagnosis before the NHSP examination were excluded. During the study period, starting from the date of diagnosis of the first PC case in the family, family members were considered ‘exposed’ and were identified as ‘with affected FDR’ or ‘familial group’ and accumulated person-time of ‘being affected’. If a second member was diagnosed, they were regarded as the first ‘familial case’ and remaining unaffected family members were ‘exposed’ to two ‘family cases’ from the date of diagnosis of the second case and were identified as ‘with more than one affected FDR’. In families with no PC cases, all family members were identified as ‘without affected FDR’ or the ‘non-familial group’, and they accumulated person time of ‘being unaffected’. Meanwhile, if any of them were diagnosed, that person was regarded as a ‘non-familial case’ and the rest of the family members accumulated person-time of being affected (Supplementary Figure S1).

Hyperglycemia was categorized as < 100 mg/dl (normoglycemia), 100–125 mg/dl (prediabetes), and ≥ 126 mg/dl (diabetes) [23]. Smoking was categorized as 0, < 10, 10–< 20, and ≥ 20 pack-years [22]. BMI was categorized as < 18.5 kg/m² (underweight), 18.5–22.4 kg/m² (normal), 22.5–24.9 kg/m² (overweight), and ≥ 25.0 kg/m² (obese) [24].

Covariates

Multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, alcohol intake (never, mild [< 5 drinks/week for men, < 4 drinks/week for women], moderate [5 to < 10 drinks/week for men, 4 to < 8 drinks/week for women], heavy [≥ 10 drinks/week for men, ≥ 8 drinks/week for women]), physical activity (< 2 vs. ≥ 3 sessions/week of ≥ 30 min moderate or ≥ 20 min vigorous exercise), total cholesterol (< 200, 200–239, ≥ 240 mg/dl), and blood pressure (SBP/DBP: < 120/< 80, 120–129/< 80, 130–139/80–89, 140–179/90–119, ≥ 180/≥ 120 mmHg). Covariate selection was guided by a directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Figure S2).

Statistical analysis

Incidence rates of PC with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as cases per 10,000 person-years, and incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were obtained by comparison with the relevant control group. Cox proportional-hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for overall familial risk (unadjusted and adjusted), risk by affected relative (father, mother, sibling), and sex-stratified familial risk. Age-stratified associations were estimated using IRR instead of HR because of the small number of cases. For analyses of paternal history, the control group comprised FDRs without an affected father; individuals with an affected mother or sibling were excluded from that control group. Analogous definitions were applied for maternal and sibling history analyses. All models were mutually adjusted for age, sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity, FBG, ALT, GGT, cholesterol, physical activity, and SBP/DBP, such that each variable’s HR reflected its independent association with PC risk.

Additive interaction between family history and hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity was quantified with the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and its corresponding 95% CI. RERI is calculated as: RERI = RR11–RR10–RR01 + 1, where RR11 denotes the IRR among those exposed to both family history and the lifestyle factor, RR10 the IRR for the lifestyle factor only, RR01 the IRR for family history only, and RR00 (reference: unexposed to both) is set to 1. The 95% CI for RERI was derived via the delta method, using the variance–covariance matrix of the log(HR) estimates from the Cox models and treating HRs as approximations to RRs given the rarity of PC [25]. A RERI greater than 0 indicates a synergistic interaction. Analyses used Stata 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Study population characteristics

Of the 6,217,057 participants with both biological parents identified, 47,454 (32,962 men and 14,492 women) had ≥ 1 affected FDR-including 126 with ≥ 2 affected FDRs-whereas 6,169,603 (3,922,596 men and 2,247,007 women) had no affected FDR (Table 1). Lifestyle profiles were broadly similar between groups. Participants with a family history of PC were more likely to be smokers and to have obesity, while the prevalence of hyperglycemia was comparable between groups.

Pancreatic cancer risk by affected relative

During follow-up, 43 new PC cases occurred among 47,454 affected FDRs, and 1,496 new PC cases were diagnosed among 6,169,604 non-affected FDRs. The incidence of PC was 0.51 per 10,000 person‑years (95% CI, 0.38–0.69) among individuals with an affected FDR, compared with 0.14 per 10,000 person‑years (95% CI, 0.13–0.14) among those without (Table 2). After multivariable adjustment, the HR for PC associated with any affected FDR was 2.46 (95% CI, 1.81–3.33). Risk varied by family relationship: siblings conferred the greatest risk (HR 13.57; 95% CI, 6.08–30.26), followed by mothers (HR 2.35; 95% CI, 1.45–3.78) and fathers (HR 2.05; 9% CI, 1.32–3.19).

Age- and sex-specific Familial risk

In both sex, sibling history conferred the greatest excess risk, followed by maternal and then paternal history (Table 4).

Association of hyperglycemia, smoking and obesity with PC

Hyperglycemia was positively associated with PC (prediabetes: HR 1.41; 95% CI, 1.19–1.68; diabetes: HR 1.93; 95% CI, 1.61–2.31). PC risk increased with cumulative smoking exposure in a dose–response manner (< 10 pack‑years: HR 1.08; 95% CI, 0.91–1.27; 10–< 20 pack‑years: HR 1.50; 95% CI, 1.30–1.73; ≥ 20 pack‑years: HR 1.69; 95% CI, 1.40–2.05). Obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m²) was not statistically significantly associated with PC (HR 1.18; 95% CI, 0.87–1.58) (Fig. 2).

Interaction between family history and hyperglycemia, smoking or obesity

As shown in Fig. 3, individuals with both a family history of PC and hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity had higher risks than those with a family history alone; however, the combined effects did not exceed the sum of individual risks. The joint-exposure HRs were 3.48 with hyperglycemia (RERI, 0.10; 95% CI, − 3.93 to 4.13), 3.24 with smoking (RERI, − 0.04; 95% CI, − 1.87 to 1.79), and 2.89 with obesity (RERI, 0.44; 95% CI, − 1.19 to 2.07), indicating little or no evidence of additive interaction.

Discussion

In this nationwide cohort of 6.2 million Koreans, having an affected FDR was associated with a 2.46‑fold higher risk of PC. Risks varied by relationship and were highest for siblings (HR 13.57), followed by mothers (HR 2.35) and fathers (HR 2.05). When familial predisposition coincided with hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity, the risk rose further (HRs 3.48, 3.24, and 2.89, respectively), but the joint effects were additive rather than synergistic.

The HR in FDRs with an affected family member compared with FDRs without an affected family member exceeds those of prior cohorts [5, 7, 26]. A meta‑analysis of two cohort and seven case–control studies (3951 cases and 2617 cases, respectively) reported an overall relative risk (RR) of 1.80, with higher estimates in case–control designs (RR, 2.82) than in cohorts (RR, 1.62) [26]. The Pancreatic Cancer Cohort Consortium pooled analysis (1183 cases and 1205 controls) found an odds ratio (OR) of 1.76 among FDRs [5], and the Utah Cancer Registry (4095 cases and 40,933 controls) reported an RR of 1.76 [9]. Differences may reflect ethnic variation, age at enrolment, or under‑ascertainment of family history. Ethnic differences may influence genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures; enrolment age can affect the likelihood of detecting cases during follow-up; and incomplete reporting or limited record linkage can under-ascertain family history, attenuating observed associations.

Adjustment for lifestyle factors had minimal impact on the familial HR (from 2.47 to 2.46), underscoring the crucial role of inherited susceptibility. This aligns with prior research [11], in which the RR associated with a family history of PC decreased only modestly from 3.0 to 2.8 after covariate adjustment. Nevertheless, only 10–20% of familial cases carry known pathogenic variants on multigene testing [27], suggesting additional, as-yet-undiscovered susceptibility genes.

The higher risk observed for affected siblings compared to parental transmission may reflect a combination of shared genetic predispositions, more similar early-life environmental exposures, and a greater likelihood of early-onset PC. Siblings share approximately 50% of their genome and, unlike parent–child pairs, typically experience the same household, diet, passive tobacco exposure, infectious milieu, and other childhood conditions—factors that may amplify gene–environment interactions relevant to PC. Similar sibling clustering has been documented for gastric cancer [28]. Consistent with this, a recent case–control study reported a 2.8–21.2-fold increased risk of PC among siblings of affected individuals relative to the general population [29].

Maternal transmission exceeded paternal transmission, echoing previous finding in gastric cancer. A population-based case–control study from South Korea, the ORs (95% CIs) for gastric cancer with an affected father, mother, or sibling were 1.80 (1.38–2.34), 1.95 (1.42–2.69), and 2.98 (2.31–3.83), respectively, compared with controls [28]. Potential drivers may include maternally mediated environmental influences—microbiome, early‑life diet, in‑utero exposures— as well as sex‑specific genetic effects.

Age-specific analyses showed that familial risk peaked at 40–49 years and declined thereafter, consistent with a stronger genetic contribution to early-onset disease [8, 9, 30, 31]. Women exhibited a greater relative familial risk than men (HR, 4.02 vs. 2.21), in line with prior research [9] reporting higher RRs among female than male FDRs—1.96 (95% CI, 1.45–2.65) vs. 1.55 (95% CI, 1.07–2.26). This pattern may reflect sex-modifying genetic or hormonal influences and, potentially, lower prevalence of competing environmental exposures such as smoking and alcohol use.

Hyperglycemia, smoking and-to a lesser extent-obesity independently increased PC risk in this study, consistent with prior reports [1]. A Japanese case-control study reported a 10-fold increased risk of PC among individuals with a familial history of PC, early-onset diabetes, and smoking [32]. Among FDRs, these factors further elevated risk, yet additive‑interaction metrics (RERI) were near zero, indicating parallel rather than synergistic effects. The gene–environment interaction literature in PC remains sparse; existing reports link specific variants to smoking [33], diabetes [34], and obesity [35], but sample sizes are small and genes limited.

Current expert recommendations classify PC risk into three categories: low (< 5-fold), moderate (5-10-fold), and high (≥ 10-fold) [3]. Screening is generally recommended for individuals with a ≥ 10-fold increased risk. In our study, FDRs with hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity did not reach this high-risk threshold. Accordingly, while these individuals are not screening-eligible based on these factors alone, they should be referred for genetic counseling with risk-management communication emphasizing smoking cessation and blood glucose control to reduce PC risk [17, 36].

Strengths of this study include its population‑based, prospective design, physician‑verified cancer registry, and standardized, routinely collected exposure and clinical measurements, which minimize recall bias and enhance generalizability. Limitations include the potential for misclassification of PC despite high registry validity [37], a relatively short follow-up that may have led to under-identification of PC cases, the small number of PC cases, which limited statistical power for subgroup analyses and the absence of data on germline mutations or microbiome profiles.

In conclusion, inherited factors exert a dominant influence on familial clustering of PC, with lifestyle risks acting additively rather than synergistically. Targeted prevention should priorities FDRs, particularly siblings and women, who also exhibit hyperglycemia, smoking, or obesity. Future studies should quantify gene–environment interactions to refine risk stratification and guide early-detection policy.
