RNA sequencing and histology analysis provide insights into the formation of hollow fruits in cucumber
Authors: Geng Zhou, Xiaohong Liu, Huiming Chen, Yun Tian, Chen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHollow heart is a common physiological disorder in cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) characterized by internal fruit cavities that are not visible externally, severely compromising fruit quality and marketability. Although previous studies have implicated both genetic and environmental factors, the molecular regulatory mechanisms, particularly the role of long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), remain largely uncharacterized.ResultsIn this study, we conducted an integrated histological and transcriptomic analysis to elucidate the regulatory basis of the hollow heart trait using two cucumber sister lines: H6 (hollow fruit) and H7 (non-hollow fruit). Histological observations revealed that the hollow heart trait emerges approximately two days after anthesis (DAA), marked by the progressive separation of dual cell layers in the carpel transport channels. RNA-seq analysis identified 16,176 differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) and 594 differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) between H6 and H7. From these, seven core DElncRNAs and 28 co-expressed DEmRNAs were implicated in a regulatory network enriched in plant hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways. Several key genes involved in lignin biosynthesis, auxin transport, and UDP-glycosyltransferase activity were predicted as targets of these DElncRNAs. Notably, exogenous application of plant hormones, particularly cytokinins and gibberellins, substantially reduced the formation of hollowness in H6, supporting the functional relevance of the identified regulatory components.ConclusionsThis study offers insights into the regulatory role of lncRNAs in hollow fruit development in cucumber. The elucidated lncRNA-mRNA putative regulatory network, along with its association with hormone signaling and lignin biosynthesis pathways, identifies candidate targets for future genetic and biotechnological strategies aimed at improving fruit quality. These findings provide valuable insights for future molecular breeding efforts to reduce hollow heart incidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12253-x.

Introduction

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) is an important vegetable crop with significant economic and nutritional value [1–3]. The economic output of cucumber largely depends on the development of high-yield and high-quality fruits [4, 5]. A prevalent physiological disorder, known as hollow heart, poses a major challenge in achieving these quality standards [6]. This defect is characterized by internal cracks within the central chambers of the fruit while its exterior remains unaffected, significantly undermining both the yield and quality of cucumber [7]. The development of cucumber fruits involves the growth of the carpel and ovary, with the flesh composed of the exocarp, mesocarp, and endocarp [8, 9]. The hollow heart defect predominantly occurs in the endocarp chambers and is attributed to developmental anomalies [10, 11]. Additionally, this trait exhibits high sensitivity to environmental factors. Studies have demonstrated that sharp fluctuations in temperature and humidity, the overuse of nitrogen-based fertilizers, and deficiencies in phosphorus and potassium are major contributing factors to the emergence of hollow fruits [4, 12–14].

Hollow heart, characterized by internal cavities in fruits or roots, is observed in a variety of plants such as potato, radish, watermelon, tomato, melon, and cucumber [15–19]. In cucumber, the genetic basis of this trait is not well understood. An early pivotal study by Kubicki et al. provided initial insights by developing a multi-generation population through crossing non-hollow and hollow cucumber varieties [13]. Their genetic analysis identified two genes, Es1 and Es2, as the key determinants of hollow fruits, marking the first genetic factors associated with this trait. In another genetic study involving the cucumber line WI7088D, it was found that an allele of mfh3.1 on chromosome 3 reduces the size of the hollow in mature fruits. Conversely, alleles of mfh1.1 on chromosome 1 and mfh2.1 on chromosome 2 increase the hollow size, indicating a complex genetic interplay in the development of this trait [20]. Recently, Zhou et al. made significant advances by identifying CsALMT2, an aluminum-activated malate transporter, as a candidate gene that regulates the hollow trait in cucumber fruits [21]. Additionally, in Arabidopsis thaliana, the GA receptor homolog CsGID1a has been shown to play a crucial role in fruit locule formation. Silencing CsGID1a in cucumber leads to abnormal carpel and locule development, resulting in hollow fruits and severely impaired seed formation [22]. Cheng et al. demonstrated that two bHLH transcription factors, SPATULA and ALCATRAZ, regulate fruit cavity formation by mediating carpel separation in cucumbers [8]. More recently, Li et al. performed a comprehensive comparative transcriptome analysis using both hollow and non-hollow materials, suggesting that auxin signaling and cell wall biosynthesis are involved in the formation of hollow heart in cucumber [7].

On the other hand, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have been reported to play important roles in fruit development and stress responses across various species [23, 24]. Advances in genomics and bioinformatics, particularly the extensive use of high-throughput sequencing technology, have led to the identification of a wide array of lncRNAs, which typically exceed 200 base pairs in length [25, 26]. In cucumbers, lncRNAs have been increasingly linked to fruit development [27]. For example, Hao et al. systematically identified long intergenic non-coding RNAs and revealed their involvement in reproduction and growth processes [28]. Moreover, some long intergenic non-coding RNAs were found to be targeted by miRNAs, suggesting their regulatory roles in developmental networks [29]. Additionally, Dey et al. identified 97 differentially expressed lncRNAs involved in cucumber fruit development [30]. These lncRNAs regulate genes associated with fruit firmness and ripening processes, providing insights into shelf-life improvement through lncRNA regulation. While the involvement of lncRNAs in fruit development is well documented, their roles in the specific formation of hollow fruits in cucumber remain to be elucidated.

Currently, the molecular mechanism underlying the development of hollow heart in cucumbers remains poorly understood. To date, the potential involvement of lncRNAs in the formation of hollow cucumber fruits has not been explored. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive RNA sequencing analysis to investigate the roles of mRNAs and lncRNAs in the development of this physiological disorder. We identified key genes and pathways involved in this process. Our research also revealed a complex regulatory network underlying the formation of hollow heart in cucumbers. This study not only elucidates part of the complex genetic architecture associated with this trait but also paves the way for targeted interventions that could mitigate such developmental challenges in cucumbers.

Materials and methods

Plant Materials

Two sister lines, H6 and H7, selected from the seventh generation derived from a cross between the cucumber cultivars ♀j and TS-1, were used as experimental materials for this study. Line H6 is notable for producing hollow fruits, whereas line H7 is characterized by its non-hollow fruits. The detailed process of developing these two lines is documented in Zhou et al. [21]. All samples used in this study were provided by the Hunan Vegetable Research Institute in Changsha, China. All plants were cultivated in a controlled greenhouse under day/night temperatures of 28–35 °C/18–25 °C, with an 11 h light/13 h dark photoperiod and 60–70% relative humidity. Plants were irrigated every seven days and fertilized weekly with a balanced nutrient solution (N: P:K = 17:17:17).

Characterization of Hollow heart trait

The presence of the hollow heart trait in fruits was examined across various growth stages for H6 and H7. Cross-sectional images of the fruit’s mid-sections were captured using a Deli altimeter (Model 15152, Deli Group, Ningbo, China). The total and hollow areas of each cross-section were quantified using Adobe Photoshop CS6 software. Subsequently, the proportion of the hollow area was calculated using Microsoft Excel, expressed as the ratio of the hollow area to the total cross-sectional area. To calculate the hollow rate for H6 and H7, the total number of fruits and the number of hollow fruits for both varieties were recorded. The population hollow rate was calculated using Microsoft Excel, expressed as the ratio of the number of hollow fruits to the total number of fruits. The cavity proportion was calculated in Microsoft Excel as the ratio of cavity area to total cross-sectional area.

Measurement of lignin content

The lignin content was measured for both materials two and eight days after anthesis (DAA). Fresh fruit segments were initially heated at 105 °C for 10–15 min and subsequently dried at 80 °C until reaching a constant weight. Approximately 0.01 g of the resulting powdered sample was transferred into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube, to which 1 mL of 80% ethanol was added, and the mixture was subjected to vortex mixing. The sample was then incubated in a 50 °C water bath for 20 min, with intermittent shaking every 3 min. Subsequently, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 25 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was retained for measurement. A precisely weighed amount of dried sample was incubated with 200 µL of acetyl bromide solution (25%, v/v, in glacial acetic acid) at 70 °C for 30 min. After cooling, the reaction was quenched with 200 µL of 2 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH). The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was diluted 20-fold with glacial acetic acid (50 µL supernatant plus 950 µL glacial acetic acid). An aliquot of 0.8 mL of the diluted solution was then transferred to a 1 mL quartz cuvette, and absorbance was recorded at 280 nm. The lignin content was quantified using the standard curve equation y = 0.9385x − 0.1358, with an R² value of 0.9905.

Preparation of paraffin-embedded fruit sections

Fruit segments from H6 and H7 plants were collected at various developmental stages and fixed in a 50% FAA solution for 24 h. The segments were then removed, trimmed under a fume hood, and placed in a dehydration chamber with proper labeling for identification. After dehydration, the tissues were infiltrated with paraffin using a gradient alcohol series. The embedded tissues were then sectioned at 4 μm thickness using a microtome. The sections were floated on warm water at 40 °C to flatten, then transferred to slides and dried in an oven at 60 °C. Following drying, sections underwent a staining process starting with two xylene washes, followed by a series of alcohol treatments, and concluding with an aniline blue stain. After staining, sections were mounted with neutral gum and examined under a microscope. This process was replicated three times for three samples per developmental stage.

Cryosectioning and microscopic analysis of cucumber carpel tissues

Fresh tissues from the mid-section of the carpel were collected at specific pre-flowering stages (−3 DAA, −2 DAA, −1 DAA) and were immediately cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen to ensure preservation of tissue integrity and molecular stability. The tissues were embedded in OCT compound (Sakura, Model 4583) and sectioned at 8–10 μm thickness using a microtome. The sections were placed onto anti-detachment slides and underwent a two-stage fixation process in Ethylene Glycol Ether Acetate solutions at 37 °C—first for six hours and then overnight. Subsequently, the sections were dehydrated at room temperature through a series of ethanol baths, washed with tap water, and stained with Toluidine Blue (Servicebio, Model G1032) for two minutes. After drying at 60 °C, they were cleared in xylene for five minutes, mounted in neutral balsam, and examined under a Nikon Eclipse E100 microscope with a DS-U3 imaging system. Three samples were processed for each growth stage, with each replicated three times to ensure consistency and reproducibility of the histological evaluation.

RNA sequencing

Fruit samples from the mid-section, specifically targeting the carpel transport channels, were collected at 0 DAA and 8 DAA. These samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction. The samples were organized into four groups, each with three biological replicates, totaling 12 samples, named as follows: H60D (H60D-1, H60D-2, H60D-3), H70D (H70D-1, H70D-2, H70D-3), H68D (H68D-1, H68D-2, H68D-3), and H78D (H78D-1, H78D-2, H78D-3). Total RNA was extracted using the guanidine thiocyanate method, and the quality and quantity of the RNA were assessed before proceeding to library construction and sequencing. Ribosomal RNAs were removed, and RNA libraries were constructed using the VAHTS™ Stranded mRNA-seq V2 Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NR601-01, Vazyme, China). RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 system using a paired-end (2 × 150 bp) strategy at the Beijing Novogene Institute.

Bioinformatic analysis

Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (Chinese Long v2) using TopHat (version 2.0.9). Known mRNAs were identified based on genomic sequence annotations. StringTie was used to merge multiple assemblies and generate complete transcripts for the different cucumber samples [31]. For the identification of lncRNAs, tools including PFAM, PLEK, CNCI, and CPC2 were used [32–35]. Clustering of lncRNAs was conducted via H-means using the Gene Expression Similarity Investigation Suite. Cuffdiff was employed to calculate the fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped reads (FPKM) for each transcript. The expression levels of all identified lncRNAs were quantified using StringTie-eB [31]. Differential expression of lncRNAs and mRNAs was assessed using edgeR, with a q-value threshold of < 0.01 and |log2FoldChange| >1 to define differentially expressed lncRNAs, and a q-value cutoff of < 0.01 and |log2FoldChange| >5 to define differentially expressed mRNAs. To investigate the functional roles of the genes targeted by lncRNAs, enrichment analyses were performed using the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/). Significantly enriched KEGG pathways were identified based on adjusted q-values less than 0.05.

Quantitative real-time PCR

To validate the high-throughput sequencing data and assess the expression patterns of mRNAs and lncRNAs, qRT-PCR assays were performed on a StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using a SYBR PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit (TaKaRa). The cucumber actin gene was utilized as a control gene to normalize the expression. The relative expression level of each transcript was calculated using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt). Primers for mRNAs and lncRNAs were designed using the Primer-BLAST tool on the NCBI website.

Exogenous hormone treatment experiment

The test reagents comprised five common plant hormones, including 6-benzylaminopurine (6-BA, 100 mg/L), 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D, 20 mg/L), gibberellin (GA3, 100 mg/L), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, 100 mg/L), and naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA, 500 mg/L). The five plant hormones were applied at concentrations typically used in cucumber production. On the day prior to the opening of the female cucumber flowers, these hormones were applied using a flower-dipping technique. For the control group, flowers were dipped in water. Only fruits with similar ovary sizes were selected for treatment, with 2–3 flowers per plant blooming on the same day being treated. Each treatment was replicated three times. The dipping procedure involved immersing the entire fruit, including the ovary and flower, into the solution for three seconds, taking care to prevent any accumulation of the solution post-dipping. Tags were affixed to the fruits following treatment. Fifteen days post-treatment, the fruits were harvested. Cross-sections of all fruits were scanned using a high-speed camera, and the total and hollow area percentages of each cross-section were quantified using Adobe Photoshop CS6 software.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± SD (standard deviations) of three biological replicates. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-tests (where asterisks indicate statistically significant differences: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001).

Results

The development of Hollow fruits in cucumber

H6 and H7 are sister lines derived from the South China ecotype cucumber, developed through seven generations of systematic selection. Both lines not only produce similar leaves, terminal buds, flowers, and tendrils but also display uniform external fruit characteristics during development (Fig. 1A). However, notable differences exist in the internal structure of the fruits: H6 exhibits a hollow heart, whereas H7 does not (Fig. 1B). To investigate the development of the hollow trait, this study focused on the period from three days before anthesis (−3 DAA) to 16 days after anthesis (16 DAA) for morphological observations. Initially, no sign of hollowing was observed at anthesis or earlier stages. However, starting from 2 DAA, H6 exhibited the initial formation of a vascular bundle-like hollow structure at the end of the carpel ventral line, which expanded progressively with fruit development (Fig. 1C). The vascular bundle-like hollow structure was inferred based on morphological resemblance to vascular bundles in light microscopy images. From 2 to 16 DAA, the hollowing within the inner pericarp of H6 increased, the hollow trait became more pronounced, and the locule development areas contracted outward, leading to significant alterations in the internal structure of the fruit. In contrast, H7 maintained stable internal pericarp structures throughout all stages, exhibiting normal locule development (Fig. 1C). Moreover, in the F2 population derived from the cross between H6 and H7, we observed no consistent pattern of association between the hollow trait and carpel number; both hollow (H6) and non-hollow (H7) materials included plants with 3, 4, or 5 carpels (Fig. 1D). Additionally, to investigate whether lignin content is associated with the development of hollow fruits, we measured lignin levels in both H6 and H7 at 2 DAA and 8 DAA and observed significant differences between them (Fig. 1E). However, differences in lignin content between H6 and H7 are associative rather than causal. Future studies, such as lignin biosynthesis inhibition, are required to determine whether lignin accumulation directly drives hollow formation. Our findings revealed significant differences in lignin content between the two materials at both stages, with increased lignin levels potentially contributing to the formation of hollow fruits in H6.

Cytological observations of H6 and H7

To explore the cellular structural differences related to the hollow trait, we conducted paraffin sectioning and microscopic observation of the carpel tissues at various developmental stages. The results indicated that prominent cavities were observed in the middle segment of carpel tissues in both lines before anthesis (from − 3 DAA to −1 DAA), formed by the separation of the double cell layers in the carpel transport channels (Supplementary Figures S1, S2). Quantitative analysis revealed that these cavity areas gradually decreased before anthesis, with significant differences observed between H6 and H7 until − 1 DAA (Fig. 2A). Cryosectioning of the carpel tissues further confirmed this trend, with the percentage of empty cavities in frozen sections of H6 during − 3 DAA, −2 DAA, and − 1 DAA being 63.5%, 44.8%, and 29.1%, respectively (Fig. 2B). Compared with H7, the double cell layers in H6’s carpel transport channels separated more readily and formed larger cavity areas. This indicates that the double cell layer structure in H6 is looser and more prone to separation, forming a hollow area. On the day of anthesis, the carpel transport channels of both H6 and H7 consisted of tightly packed double cell layers, with no hollow traits observed (Fig. 2A). However, starting from 2 DAA, the double cell layers in the carpel transport channel of H6 began to separate, forming an early hollowing effect. Individual cells within the double cell layers continued to enlarge and eventually disrupted the originally stable double cell layer structure, gradually weakening the cohesion between the layers until the structure completely separated, forming a hollow trait in H6. Based on these observations, we proposed a cytological model for the formation of hollow traits in cucumber fruits (Fig. 2C). In this model, an open zipper-like structure is maintained in the double cell layers of the carpel transport channel until anthesis (zipper formation); by the day of anthesis, this structure solidifies, tightly binding the double cell layers together (zipper closure); starting the second day after anthesis, unevenly enlarged cells in these layers cluster and rupture, breaking the stable structure (zipper destruction); by the fourth day, the structure fully opens, leading to the formation of the hollow trait (zipper opening). According to our model, we predict that the development of the hollow trait in cucumber fruits begins around 2 DAA and progressively intensifies as locule development exerts tension on the zipper-like double cell layers.

RNA sequencing and the identification of LncRNAs

To identify the genetic factors underlying the hollow trait in cucumber fruits, we collected four distinct fruit tissue samples for RNA sequencing, including H60D (H6 at 0 DAA, non-hollow), H68D (H6 at 8 DAA, hollow), H70D (H7 at 0 DAA, non-hollow), and H78D (H7 at 8 DAA, non-hollow). Sequencing of these samples generated a high volume of clean reads and achieved a mapping rate to the reference genome between 88.35% and 96.53%, with an average of 92.52%. This indicated that the sequencing data were of high quality, suitable for subsequent detailed analyses (Supplementary Table S1). To minimize the false positive rate in identifying lncRNAs from the assembled transcripts, we implemented an integrated approach combined with stringent filtering criteria (Fig. 3A and B). Utilizing tools such as PFAM, PLEK, CNCI, and CPC2 to analyze the intersection of the results, this approach successfully identified a total of 1,367 high-confidence lncRNAs (Fig. 3C). These lncRNAs were categorized into four groups, including 709 lincRNAs (51.87%), 360 sense-overlapping lncRNAs (26.34%), 268 antisense lncRNAs (19.60%), and 30 sense-intronic lncRNAs (2.19%) (Fig. 3D).

In conjunction with our lncRNA identification, we identified 25,598 mRNAs to facilitate a comparative study examining the distinctive features of lncRNAs. The lengths of lncRNAs ranged from 239 bp to 438,243 bp, with an average length of 4885 bp. The distribution of lncRNA lengths was predominantly between 1,000 bp and 10,000 bp, accounting for 74.47% of the total. Our comparative analysis between the lncRNAs and mRNAs highlighted significant structural differences. mRNAs typically exhibited a greater number of exons, featured longer transcripts, and possessed larger open reading frames (ORFs) compared to lncRNAs (Supplementary Figure S3A-E). Despite mRNAs generally displaying higher abundance levels across the samples, the transcript levels showed significant variability. This variation was particularly notable between the H78D group and the other three groups (H60D, H68D, and H70D), suggesting differential gene expression dynamics associated with the developmental stages and phenotypic manifestations of the hollow trait in cucumbers (Supplementary Figure S3F-H). Additionally, lncRNA functions were predicted based on co-expression (trans) and genomic proximity (cis) to mRNAs but were not experimentally validated. Their functional roles require further testing through knockdown or overexpression approaches.

Differential expression analysis of LncRNAs and mRNAs

To identify differentially expressed lncRNAs and mRNAs between hollow and non-hollow groups, we performed RNA-seq analysis. Initial correlation analysis demonstrated high correlation coefficients between the H60D and H70D groups, indicating a high degree of genetic similarity between the two cucumber lines at the day of anthesis. This similarity suggests that the initial genetic conditions of these lines are essentially consistent, providing a stable baseline for comparative studies. However, the situation changed notably at 8 DAA, where the correlation coefficients between H6 and H7 dropped significantly, reflecting divergent gene expression patterns as the hollow trait developed in H6 (Supplementary Figure S4A). Principal component analysis (PCA) further elucidated the dynamics between the groups. The analysis revealed separation by both developmental stage (PC1, 62.5% variance: 0 DAA vs. 8 DAA) and genotype (PC2, 18.2% variance: H6 vs. H7), which together accounted for 80.7% of the total variance observed (Supplementary Figure S4B).

Four comparisons were established to explore the differential expression patterns between different groups, including H68D vs. H78D (hollow vs. non-hollow), H68D vs. H60D (hollow vs. non-hollow), H78D vs. H70D (non-hollow vs. non-hollow), and H60D vs. H70D (non-hollow vs. non-hollow). These comparisons yielded a comprehensive dataset of differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs), totaling 16,176 across all groups. Of these, 4,584 mRNAs were upregulated and 11,592 were downregulated (Fig. 4A). In addition, we identified 594 differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) across the comparisons, with 127, 219, 240, and 8 lncRNAs found in each respective comparison. Among these, 224 were upregulated and 370 were downregulated, with expression levels quantified in terms of transcripts per million (TPM) differences (Fig. 4B). Further analysis was conducted through KEGG pathway enrichment of the gene set identified in the H68D vs. H78D comparison. This analysis identified several key pathways, including plant hormone signal transduction, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, oxidative phosphorylation, amino acid biosynthesis, and the metabolism of glycine, serine, and threonine (Supplementary Figure S5). Prediction of LncRNA target genes

Using a co-expression (trans-acting) based target gene prediction method, we predicted 19,385 protein-coding genes as potential targets of the 1,367 lncRNAs identified in this study. Further analysis revealed that only 37 DElncRNAs (referred to as the trans data lncRNA subset) exhibited significant correlations with genes in the core DEmRNA dataset. A subsequent Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 7A) comparing the trans data lncRNA subset with the core DElncRNA dataset identified seven overlapping DElncRNAs that were involved in 85 lncRNA-mRNA associations. For example, MSTRG.2141.1 is associated with 22 DEmRNAs, representing 25.88% of the total identified associations (Fig. 7B). The average Pearson correlation coefficient of these associations is approximately 0.98, indicating a robust co-expression relationship between these seven core DElncRNAs and 28 core DEmRNAs (Fig. 7C; Supplementary Table S4). Validation of DElncRNAs and DEmRNAs by qRT-PCR

To validate the reliability of the RNA sequencing results, six DEmRNAs and four DElncRNAs were randomly selected for qRT-PCR validation. All selected mRNAs exhibited higher expression levels in H6 compared to H7 at 8 DAA. Furthermore, the expression trends of the four selected lncRNAs were consistent with the expression trends observed for DEmRNAs in both H6 and H7 at the same developmental stage (Supplementary Figure S6). The results from the qRT-PCR validation not only corroborated the accuracy of our RNA sequencing data but also affirmed the effectiveness of the whole transcriptome data analysis. qRT-PCR validated expression trends but did not confirm functional roles. For instance, although Csa1G422480 was upregulated in H6, its role in hollow formation still requires functional validation.

Effects of different hormone treatments on fruit hollowness in cucumber

Given the significant role of plant hormones in fruit development, and our findings indicating an enrichment of plant hormone pathways, we investigated whether plant hormones influence the formation of hollow traits in cucumber fruits. We applied treatments using five common exogenous hormones to the female flowers of both H6 and H7 cucumber lines. Our results showed that all H7 plants treated with the five hormones exhibited non-hollow fruits, consistent with the control group, indicating that the application of exogenous hormones does not induce hollow formation in genetically non-hollow cucumbers. In contrast, treatment of H6 plants with four hormones resulted in a significant reduction in the percentage of hollow areas compared to the untreated controls. Specifically, the reductions were 52.17% (6-BA, p = 0.0079), 50.41% (2,4-D, p = 0.0065), 45.95% (GA₃, p = 0.012), 7.91% (IAA, p = 0.10), and 15.44% (NAA, p = 0.019), respectively, for each hormone treatment. This indicates that exogenous hormone applications can effectively mitigate the development of hollow traits in genetically hollow lines. Notably, cytokinin and gibberellin treatments were the most effective, showing greater reduction in hollow areas compared to treatments with auxin (Supplementary Figure S7).

Discussion

Hollowness is a severe internal defect in cucumbers that cannot be detected through external visual inspection. This defect is often associated with reduced yield and diminished nutritional quality in vegetable crops [36]. Accurate detection of hollowness is particularly important during post-harvest stages, such as storage, transportation, and processing, where maintaining fruit integrity is critical [6]. The occurrence of hollowness is considered an adaptive response of the plant to environmental stressors, resulting from complex interactions between genetic makeup and environmental conditions [37]. Despite a rising incidence of hollow fruits in cucumbers in recent years, the genetic basis and underlying molecular mechanisms of this defect remain poorly understood. This is primarily due to the absence of genetically stable materials that exhibit minimal environmental variation, which are essential for reliable analysis [21]. In this study, RNA sequencing was performed to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the formation of hollow fruits in cucumbers. We began our study by identifying and characterizing lncRNAs within the cucumber genome, then performed differential expression analysis to identify the key genetic factors contributing to the hollow heart trait. Moreover, we constructed a regulatory network to elucidate the intricate interactions between mRNAs and lncRNAs, revealing complex molecular interplays that govern the development of the hollow heart trait in cucumber fruits. Our findings not only deepen our understanding of the genetic and molecular basis of this trait but also highlight potential molecular targets for future breeding strategies.

A comprehensive examination of hollow fruit development in cucumbers was conducted through precision phenotyping and detailed histological assessments. Cucumber fruit development is primarily driven by changes in both cell number and cell size [38–40]. The hollow area ratio was employed as a key quantitative metric, significantly enhancing the precision of our assessments and minimizing errors associated with the late expansion of fruits, providing a more accurate measure of developmental anomalies. Our findings reveal that the hollow heart trait can slightly increase the cross-sectional area of the fruit, with the hollow heart trait beginning to form early in the fruit’s development. Notably, it appears rapidly between 2 and 4 DAA and stabilizes between 14 and 16 DAA. This suggests that, similar to external growth patterns, the development of hollow hearts as an internal structural characteristic follows a parallel developmental timeline. These observations are consistent with those made by Elkner et al., indicating that hollow hearts first appear in young cucumbers when the fruit diameter reaches approximately 1.5 cm and expand as the fruit grows [41]. In addition, the cytological model proposed in this study significantly advances our understanding of the formation of hollow heart traits in cucumber fruits. This model innovatively employs the analogy of an open or closed zipper-like structure within the double cell layers of the carpel transport channel to depict the formation of hollow hearts. When the zipper is closed, the double cell layers are tightly connected, preventing the development of hollow hearts. Conversely, when the zipper is open, these layers separate, allowing hollow hearts to form within the fruit. This model provides a vivid and intuitive representation of the cellular processes that contribute to the hollow heart trait in cucumbers.

Recent advancements in transcriptomics have profoundly enhanced our understanding of the myriad regulatory elements, such as lncRNAs, present within the whole transcriptome. These elements play critical roles not only in plant defense mechanisms against adverse conditions but also in regulating fruit development processes. This study marks the first exploration into the impact of lncRNAs on the hollow heart trait in cucumber fruits, utilizing distinct sister-line materials for a comprehensive analysis of whole transcriptome expression levels in the fruit carpel. A total of 1,367 high-confidence lncRNAs and 25,598 mRNAs were identified in this study, with lncRNAs exhibiting fewer exons, shorter lengths and ORFs, and lower expression levels compared to mRNAs, which is consistent with the features of certain lncRNAs involved in the powdery mildew response in cucumber [29]. Through in-depth analysis and prediction, seven core DElncRNAs and 28 core DEmRNAs were found to exhibit strong co-expression correlations. Co-expression of lncRNAs and mRNAs suggests potential associations but does not necessarily imply direct regulation. For example, the correlation between MSTRG.2141.1 and 22 DEmRNAs may result from indirect pathways rather than direct binding. These core differentially expressed transcripts were significantly upregulated in the hollow-fruited materials and downregulated in the non-hollow ones. Combined with qRT-PCR validation, these results suggest that they may play important roles in regulating the hollow fruit trait in cucumber. In addition, the regulatory network we constructed provides valuable insights into the molecular interactions that potentially dictate the development of this trait. Moreover, it opens avenues to identify key genes involved in the formation of hollow fruits in cucumbers through their interacting lncRNAs.

The plant hormone signal transduction pathway was found to be significantly enriched in this study, consistent with the findings reported in a recent study [7]. Hormones, as regulators of plant growth and development, are involved in the formation of hollowness [6]. Qin et al. found that regular fluctuations in gibberellin (GA) and zeatin (ZT) levels in cucumber fruits can lead to the development of hollow fruits [42]. Fan et al. observed that, compared with non-hollow fruits, the levels of GA, indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), and ZT were significantly elevated in hollow fruits [43]. Our dataset identified hormone-related candidate genes, including Csa1G288020 (auxin transport protein, log₂FC = 16.03 in H68D vs. H78D) and Csa3G872170 (gibberellin-responsive protein, log₂FC = 7.38), which were validated by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. S5). These genes align with prior reports of auxin/gibberellin involvement in fruit development [7, 22] and suggest that their lncRNA regulators (e.g., MSTRG.12167 for Csa1G288020) may mediate hormone-dependent hollow trait suppression. Moreover, evidence suggests that exogenous hormone treatments can also influence the development of hollowness [6]. In triploid watermelon, treating ovaries with CPPU at concentrations of 50–200 mg L⁻¹ has been shown to reduce the incidence of hollow fruits [17]. Similarly, applying 5 mg L⁻¹ CPPU to the ovaries of Oriental melon (Cucumis melo L. var. makuwa Makino) decreased the rate of hollowness and significantly increased the proportion of unfertilized seeds [19]. Indeed, our exogenous hormone application experiment confirmed that cytokinin and gibberellin can effectively mitigate the development of hollow heart traits in genetically hollow lines. Exogenous hormone treatments reduced hollowing at the phenotypic level but were not directly linked to the lncRNA-mRNA network. Future studies will test whether hormone application alters the expression of candidate lncRNAs and mRNAs. In conclusion, hormonal regulation clearly plays a critical role in the formation of the hollow fruit trait; however, comprehensive studies that elucidate this phenomenon in depth are still lacking.

In addition to genes associated with the plant hormone signal transduction pathway, other candidate genes potentially involved in regulating hollow fruit formation were also identified. For example, four peroxidases (Csa4G285730, Csa5G167220, Csa3G736970, and Csa4G285740) are involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway and are closely associated with lignin accumulation, which likely promotes carpel separation [44]. This is consistent with our finding that increased lignin levels potentially contribute to the formation of hollow fruits in H6. This observation also aligns with the conclusion that lignin enrichment in the carpel transport channel of the Csspt Csalc double mutant promotes carpel separation [8]. Li et al. reported that the activities of enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose are altered, resulting in incomplete carpel cell development and ultimately leading to the formation of hollowness in cucumber [7]. Additionally, several other genes that promote plant growth and development, such as those encoding UDP-glycosyltransferase, phloem protein, plant lectins, and the MYB transcription factor (Csa3G914600) were also identified [45]. Taken together, we propose a putative regulatory model that illustrates the complex interactions between lncRNAs and their targeted mRNAs and their associated pathways influencing the hollow heart trait in cucumber fruits (Fig. 9). Despite providing valuable insights, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the co-expression networks of lncRNAs and mRNAs are based solely on correlation and do not establish causation. The predicted interactions remain hypothetical, and no direct molecular or functional validation of the identified candidate lncRNAs or mRNAs was conducted. Second, although hormone signaling pathways were implicated through expression profiles, we did not experimentally link hormone treatments to changes in the transcriptome or validate their regulatory effects on candidate genes. These limitations highlight the need for future studies that incorporate functional assays, such as gene knockout, overexpression, or hormone perturbation experiments, to confirm the roles of candidate regulators and strengthen the mechanistic basis of the proposed model.

Conclusions

The comprehensive investigation of the molecular underpinnings of the hollow heart trait in cucumber fruits has significantly advanced our understanding of the genetic and regulatory complexities associated with this developmental anomaly. This study has identified a putative regulatory network involving lncRNAs and mRNAs, which appears to orchestrate the development of hollow fruits. The findings not only delineate the intricate interactions among these genetic elements but also highlight key biochemical pathways such as plant hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, which are instrumental in this process. In particular, the elucidation of specific genes and lncRNAs that are differentially expressed in hollow versus non-hollow fruits offers potential targets for genetic interventions aimed at mitigating this undesirable trait.
