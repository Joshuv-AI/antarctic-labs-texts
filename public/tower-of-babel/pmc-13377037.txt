Robust rotation-invariant fingerprint image hashing using fast wavelet transform and fractal coding for noise filtering with minimal run-time
Authors: Lakshmi Dathan, Anjaneyulu G.S.G.N
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biometric data, particularly fingerprints, provide a reliable means of personal identification; however, protecting sensitive biometric templates against unauthorized access, tampering, and reconstruction remains a significant challenge in biometric security systems. Existing fingerprint hashing approaches often face limitations in terms of computational efficiency, robustness to geometric distortions, and preservation of discriminative ridge features. To address these limitations, this article proposes a fingerprint hashing technique that integrates the Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT), Fourier–Mellin Transform (FMT), and fractal coding to improve robustness and computational efficiency. In the proposed framework, FWT is employed for noise reduction and multi-resolution feature extraction, effectively preserving essential ridge structures and improving image quality. The FMT is utilized to achieve rotation-invariant feature representation, while fractal coding performs efficient image compression and enhances robustness against distortions and dimensional variations. The integration of FWT with fractal coding further reduces computational complexity while maintaining strong discriminative capability in the generated hash codes. Comprehensive experiments conducted on the FVC2002, FVC2004, and SOCOFing fingerprint databases demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed hashing framework. The method achieves strong discriminability with an Equal Error Rate (EER) as low as 0.3864%, while maintaining low False Match Rate (FMR) and False Non-Match Rate (FNMR). In addition, the proposed approach significantly reduces computational cost, achieving an average execution time of 2.507 seconds compared to 1048.888 seconds in existing fractal-coding-based hashing approaches. Performance evaluation using PSNR, SNR, SSIM, entropy, mutual information, and edge preservation metrics confirms that the proposed framework improves noise resilience, preserves structural fingerprint features, and maintains robustness against geometric variations. These results demonstrate that the proposed approach generates compact, secure, and robust fingerprint hashes suitable for biometric authentication, forensic identification, and digital identity verification systems.

Introduction

Biometric authentication systems based on fingerprints, facial recognition, and iris patterns have become integral to modern security frameworks due to their ability to leverage unique physiological traits for reliable identity verification. Unlike conventional password-based systems, biometric data is inherently distinctive and difficult to replicate1, enabling robust and statistically consistent authentication under controlled conditions. Recent integration with deep learning techniques has further enhanced performance, particularly in complex recognition scenarios such as unconstrained facial identification, thereby expanding the applicability of biometric systems in security-critical environments.

Despite these advancements, biometric systems introduce significant security and privacy challenges. Since biometric traits are non-revocable, their compromise leads to permanent identity risks. Common threats include spoofing attacks, where fabricated biometric samples are used to deceive authentication systems2, and replay attacks involving the reuse of previously captured data. To address these vulnerabilities, modern systems incorporate liveness detection, multi-factor authentication, encryption, and biometric template protection mechanisms to safeguard stored data and prevent reconstruction or misuse3.

Among biometric modalities, fingerprints remain one of the most reliable due to their permanence and distinctiveness, as ridge patterns are formed during fetal development and remain stable throughout life4. Fingerprint Image Hashing provides a compact and fixed-length representation of fingerprint data, preserving essential structural information while enabling efficient storage and matching. Unlike traditional hashing, it is specifically designed to retain discriminative biometric characteristics. The hashing pipeline typically consists of preprocessing, feature extraction, and secure hash generation.

The development of fingerprint hashing has evolved from early feature-based methods, such as minutiae extraction and ridge analysis, to more advanced indexing techniques including locality-sensitive hashing (LSH). More recently, deep learning approaches based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs)5 and generative adversarial networks (GANs) have demonstrated strong feature learning capabilities. However, these approaches often face challenges related to robustness against noise, geometric variations (rotation and scaling), and high computational complexity in large-scale deployments.

Although transform-domain approaches such as wavelet-based methods6, Fourier–Mellin invariance7, and fractal coding techniques8 have improved robustness and representation efficiency, several limitations persist. In particular, fractal-based hashing methods suffer from high computational cost due to exhaustive domain–range matching, limited resilience to non-Gaussian noise, and a predominant focus on recognition accuracy rather than security-centric hash design. These limitations hinder their practical deployment in real-time, large-scale, and security-sensitive applications.

Recent studies have explored hybrid transform-domain representations and lightweight encoding strategies to balance robustness and computational efficiency9. While these methods demonstrate improved performance, they often lack a unified design that simultaneously addresses computational efficiency, noise robustness, and security-oriented hashing requirements.

To bridge these gaps, this work proposes a unified fingerprint hashing framework with the following key contributions: FWT-guided fractal coding for computational efficiency: the proposed method introduces a wavelet-guided constraint on fractal domain–range matching, significantly reducing search complexity while preserving self-similarity properties critical for secure hashing.Noise-adaptive multi-resolution hashing: a multi-resolution Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT) filtering strategy is employed to suppress noise across frequency bands, enhancing robustness against non-Gaussian noise (e.g., salt-and-pepper and speckle) while preserving ridge structures.Security-oriented hash design: unlike recognition-driven approaches, the framework explicitly targets biometric template protection by ensuring fixed-length representation, high entropy10, collision resistance, revocability, and robustness to content-preserving transformations.The novelty of the proposed work lies not in the individual components—fast wavelet transform (FWT), Fourier–Mellin Transform (FMT), and fractal coding—but in their structured and interdependent integration within a unified hashing pipeline. Specifically, wavelet-domain coefficients are leveraged not only for noise suppression but also to guide fractal encoding, thereby reducing computational overhead while preserving discriminative structural information. Combined with FMT-based normalization for rotation and scale invariance, this coordinated design achieves an effective balance between robustness, efficiency, and security.

From a broader perspective, transform-domain feature extraction techniques play a central role in fingerprint representation. Wavelet transforms capture localized ridge details through multi-resolution analysis, while FMT provides inherent invariance to geometric transformations. Orthogonal polynomial descriptors, such as Zernike and Legendre moments, offer compact global representations but are often sensitive to noise and segmentation errors. The proposed framework exploits the complementary strengths of wavelet analysis and FMT to achieve robust and efficient hashing, while avoiding the limitations associated with purely global descriptors. Recent advances in orthogonal polynomial-based methods11–13 further highlight the effectiveness of transform-domain representations, though they often require careful parameter tuning.

Literature review

Fingerprint authentication is a widely adopted biometric security mechanism, and fingerprint hashing has become an essential approach for secure template representation. Over the years, several methods have been proposed to generate robust, discriminative, and compact hash representations while ensuring computational efficiency and robustness to distortions.

Capelli et al.14 introduced a fingerprint identification scheme based on Locality-Sensitive Hashing (LSH) and Minutiae Cylinder-Code (MCC), where minutiae features are mapped into fixed-length transformation-invariant binary vectors for efficient large-scale search. Although the method improves indexing speed, its performance depends heavily on accurate minutiae extraction, making it sensitive to noisy or low-quality fingerprints.

Wang et al.15 proposed a shape-context-based hashing approach using SIFT-Harris feature detection to enhance robustness against content-preserving distortions. Extending this idea, Muthu et al.16 applied SIFT-Harris features with shape context descriptors for fingerprint hashing. While these approaches offer strong robustness to geometric transformations and compression, they suffer from high computational cost due to complex feature extraction.

Jin et al.17 presented a kernel-learning-based fingerprint representation that converts minutiae features into fixed-length binary codes. Despite its effectiveness, kernel selection and binarization introduce computational overhead and potential quantization errors. Similarly, Wang et al.18 proposed a cancelable fingerprint template using zoned minutiae pairs and discrete Fourier transform (DFT), which enhances template security but depends on accurate zone definition and may expose reconstruction vulnerabilities.

Kho et al.19 developed a method that encodes minutiae structures into ordered fixed-length bit strings using elliptical Gaussian modeling and Euclidean normalization, improving matching efficiency but limiting compatibility with conventional systems. Abdullahi et al.20 combined minutiae features with shape context descriptors to generate compact hash signatures; however, the method incurs increased computational overhead and lacks extensive validation across diverse datasets.

Hybrid approaches have also been explored to improve robustness and security. Shahzad et al.21 proposed an alignment-free cancelable fingerprint template using partial discrete wavelet transform (PDWT) and a window-shift-XOR model, achieving improved attack resistance at the cost of higher complexity. Wang et al.22 introduced a hybrid perceptual hashing framework combining global, local, and structural features, which enhances tampering detection but increases computational requirements.

Birouk et al.23 integrated SIFT, Harris filtering, discrete wavelet transform (DWT), and singular value decomposition (SVD) to generate robust binary hash codes, though the multi-stage pipeline reduces scalability. Gupta et al.24 combined fingerprint recognition with SHA-512 for enhanced security, but with significant computational cost. Hammad et al.25 proposed a cancelable multi-biometric framework using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) and lightweight deep learning models, which improves accuracy and privacy but depends on training data generalization.

Deep learning-based approaches have further advanced fingerprint representation. Anand et al.26 introduced PoreNet, a CNN-based model for pore-level feature learning, while Chen et al.5 proposed a hybrid Siamese CNN combined with SIFT descriptors for improved robustness under varying conditions. Vallabhadas et al.27 also developed a cancelable CNN-based template protection scheme. Despite their effectiveness, these methods require large datasets, high computational resources, and complex training procedures, limiting their applicability in lightweight systems.

Recent studies, including28, emphasize the need for fingerprint hashing frameworks that balance robustness, computational efficiency, and template security. Overall, existing methods face challenges such as sensitivity to noise and minutiae extraction errors, high computational complexity, or dependence on large training datasets.

Motivation and objectives

In 2020, Abdullahi et al. proposed “Fractal coding-based robust and alignment-free fingerprint image hashing”29, which employs the Fourier-Mellin Transform (FMT) and fractal coding to achieve rotation-invariant fingerprint hashing. The FMT effectively handles rotation variations, ensuring hash consistency regardless of fingerprint orientation. Additionally, Gaussian filtering and Haar wavelet decomposition are used in preprocessing to enhance image quality prior to hashing.

This article builds upon and improves the approach in29 by addressing several inherent limitations. Although fractal coding effectively captures self-similar structures, it is computationally expensive due to exhaustive domain–range block matching, making it less suitable for large datasets. Furthermore, Gaussian filtering, while effective for noise suppression, tends to blur ridge structures and minutiae, which are critical for fingerprint recognition. It is also primarily suited for Gaussian noise and may not handle non-Gaussian noise effectively.

To overcome these issues, the proposed method introduces the Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT) as an efficient preprocessing technique. Unlike Gaussian filtering, FWT provides multi-resolution analysis that suppresses noise while preserving important fingerprint features such as ridges and minutiae. Incorporating FWT prior to fractal encoding not only improves feature robustness but also reduces computational complexity by limiting unnecessary domain–range comparisons.

Fast wavelet transform (FWT)

Fast wavelet transform (FWT) is an efficient technique widely used in image processing for multi-resolution analysis and noise suppression. It improves computational efficiency by enabling the fast computation of Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) coefficients. In practical signal and image processing applications, the DWT is efficiently implemented using the fast wavelet transform (FWT) based on a multiresolution filter-bank structure6. The transform decomposes a signal into a set of wavelet coefficients that represent localized frequency information at different scales. This multiresolution representation makes wavelet transforms particularly suitable for analyzing image structures such as edges and textures. Fourier–Mellin transform (FMT)

The Fourier–Mellin transform (FMT) is a very powerful technique used in image processing for achieving rotation, scale, and translation invariance. The transformation confirms that fingerprint matching remains invariant under the change in orientation or position. It combines Fourier Transform and Mellin Transform to achieve the above properties. Fractal coding

Fractal coding is a method of data compression based on the property of self-similarity within the image. This technique exhibits a larger image as a collection of smaller, self-similar patterns by splitting it into smaller range and domain blocks. Each range block is contracted to a corresponding domain block. This process is repeated for all range blocks of the image.

The primary objective is to represent the entire image as a set of affine transformations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_p$$\end{document} that comprises scaling rotational and translational parameters. The technique identifies domain blocks that best match range blocks under these affine transformations. The parameters are then saved instead of pixel values.

Authentication framework

In a hash-based fingerprint authentication system, the process begins with a registration phase where the user’s fingerprint is scanned by the biometric scanner. The scanned fingerprint image undergoes a hashing procedure, finally generating a fixed-length compact Hash Digest. This hash acts as the digital template of the fingerprint.

Conventionally, both the fingerprint image and the corresponding hash could be retained in the database with the user’s data. However, for improved security, it is best to only store the hash. By excluding the raw fingerprint image from the storage, the system ensures that even in the event of a database breach, attackers cannot reconstruct the original biometric data, thereby preserving user privacy and the system’s integrity.

In the authentication step, the user provides his/her fingerprint in the authentication zone. Upon scanning the fingerprint, a hash is created using the same hashing procedure previously used and then compared to the database hashes. The comparison is performed using similarity measures such as Hamming distance32, correlation, or a threshold-based matching approach. If the generated hash is sufficiently similar to a hash in the database within the defined thresholds, the user is authenticated successfully.

It is important that the system is collision-resistant with a zero collision rate, which ensures that no two different fingerprint inputs produce the same hash output. This property safeguards against impersonation and enhances the system’s trustworthiness. Proposed hashing scheme

The proposed fingerprint hashing algorithm follows a structured multi-stage framework to ensure robustness, efficiency, and compact representation. Initially, the input fingerprint image I undergoes preprocessing to enhance quality and suppress acquisition artifacts. The Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT) is then applied to obtain multi-resolution frequency subbands, enabling noise suppression while preserving salient ridge structures. To achieve rotation invariance, the Fourier–Mellin Transform (FMT) is applied to the wavelet-domain representation, mapping the image into a rotation-invariant feature space. Unlike conventional approaches where stages operate independently, the proposed framework introduces interaction between wavelet decomposition and fractal coding, where wavelet coefficients guide the fractal domain–range matching process by restricting the candidate search space based on coefficient similarity. This reduces computational complexity while preserving discriminative information. Finally, fractal coding captures self-similar patterns to produce a compact and stable fingerprint hash. Experimental results

Various experiments are performed to analyze the effectiveness of the proposed fingerprint image hashing algorithm using multiple publicly available fingerprint databases. Specifically, experiments are conducted on the standard FVC2002, FVC2004 (DB1, DB2, DB3, and DB4)34 and SOCOFing35 fingerprint datasets. Each FVC database contains 10 distinct classes with 8 impressions per class. For consistency and to minimize intra-class noise variations, the clearest impression among the eight available samples is selected for each fingerprint, resulting in a total of 80 representative images from the FVC datasets.

The SOCOFing dataset is incorporated to evaluate the generalization capability of the proposed hashing framework on a larger and more diverse dataset. From the SOCOFing database, which contains multiple impressions per fingerprint, the clearest impression among ten samples for each fingerprint is selected, resulting in a total of 600 high-quality images, which together with the FVC datasets yielded 680 fingerprint images used for experimental analysis. Figure 3 shows two sample images from each of these databases.

While the configuration mentioned earlier is used as the default setting for all experiments, an additional parameter sensitivity analysis is conducted to evaluate the robustness of the proposed hashing framework with respect to key design parameters. This is performed by systematically varying key design parameters while keeping the remaining settings fixed. Specifically, the wavelet decomposition level, fractal block size, and number of polar angular segments are varied across practical ranges. The analysis is conducted using multiple impressions of the same fingerprint, and the resulting intra-class Hamming distances are evaluated in terms of mean and standard deviation.

Hamming distance is widely used in perceptual hashing systems because the generated hash codes are represented as binary bit strings. It measures the number of bit positions at which two hashes differ, providing a simple yet effective similarity measure between fingerprint templates. Due to its low computational complexity and direct applicability to binary hash representations, Hamming distance is particularly suitable for large-scale biometric authentication systems where rapid matching and efficient storage are essential.

The parameter sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the proposed fingerprint hashing framework exhibits strong robustness with respect to variations in key design parameters. Across all tested wavelet decomposition levels and block sizes, the intra-class Hamming distance remains nearly unchanged, indicating that the extracted hash representation is stable and not sensitive to moderate structural variations in the transform domain. In contrast, increasing the number of polar angular segments results in a gradual rise in the Hamming distance, which is expected due to finer angular resolution capturing more directional details rather than indicating instability. Importantly, no abrupt performance degradation is observed for any parameter setting. These results confirm that the proposed hashing scheme does not rely on carefully tuned parameters and maintains consistent behavior across a wide operating range, thereby reinforcing its robustness and practical applicability in real-world biometric systems.

Efficiency analysis

The robustness of the fingerprint hashing algorithm is evaluated by the Hamming distance computed between original and content-preserving manipulated fingerprint images. Discriminability is analyzed by evaluating the False Match Rate (FMR), False Non-Match Rate (FNMR), and Equal Error Rate (EER)30 across genuine and impostor fingerprints. The noise resilience of the algorithm is further tested by introducing salt-and-pepper and speckle noise. The Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR)37 values are computed to compare Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT) and Gaussian filtering. Further assessment included edge preservation and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)38 to establish that the integrity of fingerprint features is preserved. The computational efficacy of the proposed algorithm and29 is also evaluated.

Robustness analysis against content-preserving manipulations

The robustness of the proposed fingerprint hashing method is evaluated by measuring the Hamming distance between the original fingerprint hash and hashes generated from content-preserving manipulated images. Six different types of manipulations are considered, namely gamma correction, Gaussian filtering, median filtering, scaling, JPEG compression, and rotation (pixel-wise). Each manipulation type is applied using three different parameter values, as shown in Table 4. These manipulations represent common image processing operations that may occur during fingerprint acquisition, transmission, or storage.

A threshold of 800 bits is used for decision making, such that a Hamming distance below this threshold indicates that the manipulated fingerprint remains content-preserving and therefore corresponds to the same original fingerprint. Across the FVC2002, FVC2004, and SOCOFing datasets, a total of 680 original fingerprint samples are considered. Figure 11 illustrates the probability density distribution of normalized Hamming distances for perceptually similar (original–manipulated) and dissimilar (different–manipulated) fingerprint pairs. The distribution corresponding to original–manipulated pairs is concentrated at lower normalized Hamming distance values, with its mean located around 0.12. In contrast, the distribution for different–manipulated pairs is centered at significantly higher values, with a mean close to 0.6. This clear separation between the two distributions demonstrates that manipulated fingerprints remain much closer to their corresponding originals than to fingerprints from different subjects. Such a wide margin between genuine and imposter distributions is essential for robust fingerprint verification and indicates strong discriminability of the proposed hashing framework.

Noise resilience

Fingerprint images are often subjected to noises due to variations in sensor quality, environmental conditions, and acquisition imperfections. A robust fingerprint hashing algorithm should resist such distortions so slight variances in noise do not impact the generated hash. To analyze the noise resilience of the fingerprint hashing algorithm, salt-and-pepper and speckle noise are introduced into fingerprint images, and their impact is measured using Hamming distances. Salt-and-pepper noise introduces impulse noise via the random replacement of pixel values by black (0) or white (255). It can be created due to dust, scratches, or transmission errors that could arise during fingerprint acquisition. Speckle noise introduces granular distortions, mostly due to compression artifacts and sensor defects in fingerprint images. This noise is multiplicative, scaling pixel intensities rather than simply substituting one value for another. The noise density for Salt-and-pepper noise and the variance for Speckle noise are set to 2% and 0.01 respectively, assuring a suitably realistic level of noise without degradation to the image.

After the addition of noises, hashes for the 680 fingerprint images are generated, and Hamming distances are calculated with respect to the original images. If the Hamming distance is within the 800-bit threshold, the algorithm successfully preserves fingerprint identity despite noise distortions. The average Hamming distance for salt-and-pepper noise is 526.4118 and that for speckle noise is 356.8323. The results, therefore, confirm that the fingerprint hashing algorithm is robust to natural noise distortions while preserving the identity of a fingerprint. This is crucial in most real-life applications, where noise cannot be avoided due to external factors. Feature preservation and image quality metrics

It is extremely important that the system retains the key aspects of a fingerprint while reducing noise. All transformations applied for enhancement should not change the structural integrity, edge details, and minutiae features to ensure reliable recognition. The performance analysis of this approach employing FWT is compared with Gaussian filtering in terms of noise reduction, feature retention, and edge preservation by considering some key image quality metrics such as Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)37, Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM). Gaussian filtering works meticulously in the removal of Gaussian noise by averaging the pixel intensities using a Gaussian kernel. Due to blurring, it tends to smooth out the fine ridge structures, and loss of this detail in the minutiae reduces fingerprint recognition, as edges and local textures are imperative for the same. Unlike a Gaussian filter, which works in the spatial domain, FWT works in the frequency domain, and it can therefore remove noise while preserving sharp edges and fine detail. This makes FWT particularly effective for fingerprint images, where maintaining ridge structures is crucial. To point this out,the Edge Preservation Index is also calculated for both techniques.

In addition to the above metrics, entropy and mutual information (MI)39 for FWT- and Gaussian-filtered images are also computed to further evaluate the feature preservation and information content. Entropy measures the overall uncertainty or complexity in the fingerprint image: higher entropy indicates richer texture and preserved structural details, which are crucial for reliable recognition. Mutual Information quantifies the statistical dependency between the original and denoised images; higher MI indicates better retention of essential fingerprint features while removing noise.

The experimental analysis demonstrates that FWT outperforms Gaussian filtering in terms of preserving the fingerprint details and noise reduction. Figure 15 presents the original fingerprint image, its denoised versions using FWT and Gaussian filtering, and their corresponding edge maps. Comparative results (Table 9) show the consistent superior performance of FWT across all metrics, including Entropy and MI. PSNR and SNR of FWT-processed images confirm that FWT minimizes distortion while maintaining higher fidelity concerning the structure of fingerprints than Gaussian-filtered images. The high and consistent SSIM once again shows FWT’s feasibility and power in retaining structural features in fingerprint images. The higher entropy and MI values for FWT further indicate that the denoising process retains richer information content and stronger correlation with the original fingerprint image than Gaussian filtering, which is vital for subsequent recognition tasks.Fig. 15Original fingerprint, denoised (FWT and Gaussian), and edge maps.

Computational efficiency and performance analysis

To evaluate the computational efficiency of the proposed framework, a comprehensive execution time analysis was performed and compared with the existing algorithm presented in29. To ensure a fair benchmarking procedure, the existing algorithm was reimplemented in the same MATLAB environment and executed on the identical hardware platform used for the proposed method. Both implementations followed comparable preprocessing configurations and dataset inputs in order to maintain consistent experimental conditions.

From these experiments, it can be observed that the proposed algorithm requires significantly less execution time than the existing approach. The average implementation time of the proposed method is approximately 2.507 s, whereas the existing algorithm requires approximately 1048.888 s. This substantial reduction in execution time primarily results from the use of the Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT) within the fractal compression stage, which effectively reduces the computational complexity associated with feature extraction and representation.

The reduction in computational time significantly improves the practicality of the proposed fingerprint hashing framework for handling larger biometric datasets. Faster processing enables efficient fingerprint analysis and reduces computational bottlenecks that may arise in large biometric repositories. Security and privacy analysis

Building on the robustness, discriminability, and efficiency results reported in “Efficiency analysis”, this section provides a dedicated analysis of the security and privacy properties of the proposed fingerprint hashing framework under a standard biometric template protection threat model.

Threat model and security metrics

The security of a biometric hashing system depends on its ability to protect stored templates against unauthorized access and to resist active attacks during authentication. In this work, a commonly adopted threat model for biometric template protection is considered. It is assumed that an adversary may gain access to the stored biometric hash templates and may observe multiple authentication attempts, but does not possess knowledge of the internal system parameters such as wavelet decomposition configurations, threshold values, or fractal encoding parameters.

Under this threat model, several potential attack scenarios are analyzed using quantitative security metrics in order to evaluate the robustness of the proposed hashing framework.

Template leakage and inversion attacks

Template leakage and inversion attacks attempt to reconstruct the original biometric image or infer discriminative biometric information from stored hash values. Since the proposed framework applies multiple irreversible feature transformations followed by fixed-length hashing, direct reconstruction of the original fingerprint image is highly difficult in practice.

The irreversibility of the proposed representation is primarily achieved through a sequence of transformations during feature extraction. The fingerprint image is first processed using the Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT), which captures multi-resolution texture information. This is followed by the Fourier–Mellin Transform (FMT) to achieve rotation and scale normalization. Subsequently, the fractal encoding stage performs block-based domain–range mapping and quantization, which compresses structural information while discarding precise spatial relationships. These sequential transformations eliminate direct spatial correspondence between the original fingerprint image and the final hash representation, thereby making reconstruction of the original biometric sample from the stored hash computationally challenging without access to intermediate representations.

To quantitatively evaluate resistance against such attacks, two statistical measures are analyzed: hash entropy and mutual information between the original fingerprint images and the generated hash codes. High entropy indicates that the hash values are well distributed and unpredictable, while low mutual information suggests limited information leakage from the biometric data to the stored template.

The entropy of the generated hash values is evaluated using normalized Shannon entropy. For a binary hash sequence H, the entropy is computed as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E = - \sum _{i=0}^{1} p_i \log _2(p_i)$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_1$$\end{document} denote the probabilities of occurrence of bit values 0 and 1 in the generated hash sequence. Since the entropy is calculated for binary symbols, the maximum possible entropy is 1 bit per hash bit, which occurs when both bit values are equally probable.

In the proposed framework, the biometric template is represented as a fixed-length binary hash of 4096 bits. Under the assumption of an ideally uniform distribution, the theoretical maximum entropy of the hash space is therefore approximately 4096 bits, providing a large search space against brute-force guessing or template enumeration attacks.

The experimentally obtained entropy value of 0.8931 represents the average normalized per-bit Shannon entropy computed from the generated 4096-bit hashes across the 680 fingerprint samples used in the experimental evaluation. A value approaching unity indicates that the hash bits are well distributed and contain high information content, which reduces predictability and strengthens resistance against template inversion attempts.

In addition, the mean mutual information value of 0.5673 indicates a moderate statistical dependency between the fingerprint data and the generated hash representation, suggesting that only partial information from the original biometric signal is retained in the final hash codes. Together, these results suggest that the proposed hashing scheme reduces the amount of biometric information exposed through stored templates, thereby improving resistance to inversion and reconstruction attacks.

Replay attacks

Replay attacks involve reusing previously captured biometric data or hash values in an attempt to impersonate a legitimate user. In a secure biometric hashing system, variations in acquisition conditions or input perturbations should produce sufficiently different hash outputs to prevent successful replay.

To evaluate replay attack resistance, the Hamming distance between hash codes generated from replayed or perturbed inputs is computed. The experimental evaluation yielded a mean replay Hamming distance of 408.05, which is significantly higher than typical matching thresholds used in biometric systems. This indicates that replayed biometric samples are unlikely to generate matching hash codes, thereby reducing the probability of successful replay-based impersonation.

Cross-matching attacks

Cross-matching attacks attempt to link biometric templates across different databases or applications in order to track user identities. An effective template protection scheme should ensure that hash codes generated from different fingerprints are statistically independent and sufficiently separated.

To assess resistance against cross-matching, inter-class Hamming distance analysis is conducted. The results show a mean inter-class Hamming distance of 752.50, indicating a high degree of separation between hash codes corresponding to different fingerprint identities. Such large inter-class distances reduce the likelihood of accidental matches and help support unlinkability across independent databases.

The combined analysis of entropy, replay resistance, mutual information, and inter-class separability indicates that the proposed hashing framework provides a reasonable level of protection against template leakage, replay, inversion, and cross-matching attacks. Collision probability analysis

To evaluate the distinctiveness of the generated hash codes, an inter-class collision analysis is conducted by computing pairwise Hamming distances between hash codes belonging to different fingerprint identities. A collision is defined as a pair of hashes whose Hamming distance falls below the predefined matching threshold.

From a theoretical perspective, the collision probability of an ideal binary hash function depends on the length of the hash representation. For a uniformly distributed 4096-bit hash space, the probability that two randomly generated hashes collide can be approximated as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{collision}=2^{-4096}$$\end{document}. Although practical biometric hashes are not perfectly random due to inherent biometric correlations, this theoretical bound highlights the extremely large size of the available hash space and indicates that accidental collisions are highly unlikely under ideal random hash assumptions.

Based on the experimental evaluation, 5,462 collisions are observed out of 229,503 inter-class comparisons, resulting in an empirical collision rate of 0.0238 (2.38%). These comparisons were computed using the fingerprint samples selected from the SOCOFing and FVC datasets used in the experimental evaluation. This result indicates a measurable separation between intra-class similarity and inter-class dissimilarity in the generated hash space. In biometric authentication systems, template comparisons are typically performed using threshold-based similarity measures such as the False Match Rate (FMR), where limited near-collision behaviour may arise depending on the system parameters and database size40. Consequently, small inter-class overlaps can occur in practical template spaces while still maintaining reliable recognition performance. Nevertheless, further reduction of collision probability remains an important direction for future research.

It is important to note that some implementations report non-collision probability (1-collision rate). However, in this work, results are explicitly reported in terms of empirical collision rate for clarity and reproducibility. While deep learning–based template protection schemes may offer lower collision rates under large-scale training, the proposed handcrafted approach provides a favourable balance between security, interpretability, and computational efficiency, making it suitable for practical biometric template protection applications.

Revocability and unlinkability analysis

Revocability and unlinkability are essential requirements for biometric template protection. Revocability ensures that compromised templates can be replaced, while unlinkability prevents cross-matching of templates generated from the same biometric under different system parameters. These properties are essential requirements in modern biometric template protection standards and ensure that biometric identifiers can be securely managed even if stored templates are exposed.

In the proposed framework, revocability is achieved by modifying system parameters such as wavelet decomposition levels, threshold values, and fractal encoding parameters, which produce significantly different hash outputs for the same fingerprint. To quantify unlinkability, the Hamming distance between hash codes generated from the same fingerprint under different parameter settings is computed.

The experimental results show a mean unlinkability Hamming distance of 728.1043, indicating a high degree of dissimilarity between regenerated hash codes. This confirms that the proposed hashing scheme effectively prevents cross-matching and supports template revocation without compromising recognition performance.

Conclusion and future scope

This work presents a structured and computationally efficient fingerprint hashing framework that integrates established transform techniques such as Fast Wavelet Transform (FWT), Fourier–Mellin Transform (FMT), and fractal coding to enhance noise resilience while maintaining high efficiency. The FWT component plays a crucial role by providing superior noise suppression compared to conventional Gaussian filtering, effectively handling diverse noise patterns through multiresolution decomposition while preserving discriminative fingerprint features. Additionally, FWT accelerates the fractal compression stage by enabling efficient domain–range block matching in the wavelet domain, significantly reducing computational overhead without compromising hashing accuracy. Extensive experiments conducted on publicly available datasets, including FVC2002 fingerprint database, FVC2004 fingerprint database, and SOCOFing fingerprint dataset, demonstrate that the proposed method produces a compact 1049-bit hash while achieving robustness to noise and geometric transformations, making it suitable for practical fingerprint-based security applications. From a broader perspective, the framework offers a lightweight alternative to learning-based methods such as PoreNet fingerprint model, Siamese CNN fingerprint architecture, and Cancelable CNN fingerprint templates by eliminating the need for large-scale training, complex optimization, and high computational resources, while still maintaining competitive discriminability; however, such comparisons are contextual rather than empirical due to differences in datasets and protocols. Overall, the proposed approach emphasizes computational efficiency, robustness, and explainability, and future work will focus on extending resilience to more challenging noise models such as impulsive and non-Gaussian noise, as well as exploring its applicability to other biometric modalities including iris, face, and voice, thereby enabling the development of unified multimodal authentication systems that balance efficiency, security, and robustness.
