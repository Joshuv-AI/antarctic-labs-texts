Abstract
. We characterize all f f\! -algebra products on AM-spaces by constructing a canonical AM-space W X W_{X} associated to each AM-space X X , such that the f f -algebra products on X X correspond bijectively to the positive cone ( W X ) + (W_{X})_{+} . This generalizes the classical description of f f -algebra products on C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) spaces. We also identify the unique product (when it exists) that embeds X X as a closed subalgebra of C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) , and study AM-spaces for which this product exists—the so-called AM-algebras. Finally, we investigate AM-spaces that admit only the zero product, providing a characterization in the AL-space case and examples showing that no simple characterization exists in general.

1.
Introduction

An
f\!
-algebra is a vector lattice
X
endowed with a real algebra
structure in which the product of positive elements is positive and
where
a\wedge b=0
implies

(ca)\wedge b=0=(ac)\wedge b\quad\text{for all }c\in X_{+}.

G. Birkhoff and R. S. Pierce introduced
f\!
-algebras in

[
4
]
. This structure, along with the related notion
of
f\!
-rings, has been extensively studied since then (see the surveys

[
16
,
13
,
12
,
5
,
10
,
17
]
;
or, for more recent results on the topic, see

[
20
,
8
,
7
,
15
]
)
and has
found several modern applications

[
9
,
18
]
.

More generally, a Banach lattice algebra is a Banach lattice together
with a Banach algebra structure in which the product of positive
elements is positive. A. W. Wickstead asked in
[
26
, Section
4]
for a description of the most general
Banach lattice algebra structure on AM-spaces and AL-spaces. This
motivated us to describe the
f\!
-algebra products on these spaces.
It will turn out that these products have elegant characterizations; it
is not clear at all whether the general situation will also be so
tractable.

More precisely, it is shown in
2.4
that for every AM-space
X
there
exists an associated AM-space
W_{X}
such that the
f\!
-algebra products on

X
are in one-to-one correspondence with
(W_{X})_{+}
.

Section

2
is devoted to the construction of
W_{X}
and the
proof of
2.4
. This result generalizes
the well-known fact that the
f\!
-algebra products on
C(K)
, where
K
is a compact Hausdorff space, are in one-to-one correspondence
with
C(K)_{+}
(see
2.1
).

By a classical theorem of Kakutani, every AM-space
X
embeds lattice
isometrically in a space of continuous functions
C(K)
. Since every closed
subalgebra of
C(K)
is also a sublattice, a natural stronger question is
to characterize which products on
X
can be embedded lattice-algebra
isometrically in a space of continuous functions. By

3.7
, this question turns
out to be equivalent to characterizing when
X
can be isometrically embedded as a
closed (lattice and algebra) ideal of
C(K)
.

The products on an AM-space that embed in a space of continuous
functions were characterized
intrinsically in
[
22
]
. In short, these products are the ones for
which
(B_{X})_{+}
is an approximate algebraic identity. It was not
considered in
[
22
]
how many products
satisfying this property exist. It is shown in

Section

3
that such a product does not always exist but, when it does, it
is unique (
3.5
). Following
[
22
]
, the AM-spaces with this product
are called AM-algebras. In particular, the AM-algebra product on

C(K)
is the pointwise product.

In
3.8
it is shown that the AM-algebra product
enjoys nice
characterizations, similar to those of the pointwise product in a
space of continuous functions. In fact, one can take this
analogy further, and show that several of the properties relating the
lattice and algebraic structures of
C(K)
hold for general
AM-algebras. This is done in
Section

4
. To get a taste of
the kind of properties we are referring to, we present a partial version of
4.3
:
If
X
and
Y
are AM-algebras and
T\colon X\to Y
is an
operator, the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is an algebra homomorphism.

(ii)

T
is a lattice homomorphism and
T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}=T((B_{X})_{+})
.

This result is a generalization of a classical result for
operators between spaces of continuous functions: if
K
and
L
are
compact Hausdorff spaces and
T\colon C(K)\to C(L)
is an operator,
the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is an algebra homomorphism.

(ii)

T
is a lattice homomorphism and
(T\mathbb{1}_{K})^{2}=T\mathbb{1}_{K}
.

The analogy between these two results is a clear illustration of
one of the recurring themes in the study of AM-algebras, namely, that
the role played by the constant one function in
C(K)
is now played
“approximately” by
(B_{X})_{+}
.

One of the characterizations of an AM-algebra
X
is that the space

W_{X}
is, in some sense, “maximal”. In

Section

5
, we look at the other end and study those AM-spaces for which
W_{X}

is minimal. Note that every vector lattice admits an
f\!
-algebra product,
namely, the zero product. Thus we are looking at the vector lattices
that admit a unique
f\!
-algebra product. In
Section

5
, several properties of such
spaces are studied. This is motivated by the existence of a simple
characterization of AL-spaces that admit only the zero product: an AL-space admits only the zero product if and only if it
has no atoms (
5.4
). This is a simple consequence
of the characterization of the
f\!
-algebra products on AL-spaces that is
provided in
5.3
. We are able to show that there exist
AM-spaces that only admit the zero product. We are, however, unable to
provide a general and simple characterization of such spaces.

1.1.
Preliminaries

For the unexplained terminology related to vector and Banach lattices, see

[
1
,
2
,
21
,
24
]
.
A
vector lattice algebra
is a vector lattice
X
together with
a real algebra structure in which the product of positive elements is
positive. A vector lattice algebra
X
is an
f\!
-algebra
if

a\wedge b=0
implies

(ca)\wedge b=0=(ac)\wedge b\quad\text{for all }c\in X_{+}.

We shall also refer to this implication as the
f\!
-algebra property
.
Every vector lattice with the zero product is an
f\!
-algebra. Every
Archimedean
f\!
-algebra is commutative (
[
13
, Theorem 1.3]
).

A
Banach lattice algebra
is a vector lattice algebra with a
norm that makes it into a Banach lattice and a Banach algebra. If a
Banach lattice algebra satisfies the
f\!
-algebra property, then it is
called a
Banach
f\!
-algebra
.

A subspace of a vector lattice algebra that is at the same time a
sublattice and a subalgebra will be referred to as a

sublattice-algebra
. Let
X
and
Y
be vector lattice algebras.
A map
T\colon X\to Y
that is at the same time a lattice homomorphism
and an algebra homomorphism will be called a
lattice-algebra
homomorphism
. Similarly, we define lattice-algebra isomorphisms,
isometries and embeddings when
X
and
Y
are normed.

Throughout this text, and unless otherwise specified,
K
will denote a
compact Hausdorff space. Recall that the closed subalgebras of
C(K)

are also sublattices, and that closed lattice and algebraic ideals of

C(K)
coincide: they are always of the form

\{\,f\in C(K):f|_{F}=0\,\}

for a certain closed set
F\subseteq K
.

Finally, we recall the construction of the Arens products. Given
Banach spaces
A
,
B
, and
C
, and a bounded bilinear map
P\colon A\times B\to C
, its
Arens adjoint
is the bounded bilinear map

P^{*}\colon C^{*}\times A\to B^{*}
defined by
P^{*}(\phi,a)(b)=\phi(P(a,b))
for every
a\in A
,
b\in B
, and
\phi\in C^{*}
; when
A=B=C
, its
transpose
is the bounded bilinear map

P^{t}\colon A\times A\to A
defined by
P^{t}(b,a)=P(a,b)
for all

a,b\in A
. The bilinear map
P
is called
Arens regular
if

P^{t***t}=P^{***}
.

In the particular case that
A
is a Banach algebra with product

P\colon A\times A\to A
, both
P^{***}
and
P^{t***t}
define
products on
A^{**}
, called the
first
and
second Arens
product
, respectively. When
A
is a Banach lattice algebra, the
first and second Arens product make
A^{**}
into a Banach lattice
algebra with the usual lattice structure (see

[
14
]
). These products extend
P
, in the
sense that the canonical isometry
A\to A^{**}
is an algebra
homomorphism. If
P
is Arens regular, then the first and second Arens
product coincide, and the algebra
A
is said to be
Arens
regular
.

2.
f
-algebra products on AM-spaces

The following result characterizing the
f\!
-algebra products on AM-spaces
with unit has long been known.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
25
]
)
.

A product
P\colon C(K)\times C(K)\to C(K)
is an
f\!
-algebra product if and only if there exists a
weight
w\in C(K)_{+}
such that

(1)

P(f,g)(t)=w(t)f(t)g(t)\quad\text{for all }f,g\in C(K)\text{ and
}t\in K.

This product is submultiplicative if and only if
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

In view of the previous proposition, one could say that the
f\!
-algebra products on
C(K)
are parametrized by
C(K)_{+}
. The goal of
this section is to give a similar characterization for
the
f\!
-algebra products on general AM-spaces.

Let
X
be an AM-space. Denote by
\hom(X,\mathbb{R})
the set of functionals
on
X
that are lattice homomorphisms. The sets

K_{X}=\hom(X,\mathbb{R})\cap B_{X^{*}},K_{X}^{*}=K_{X}\setminus\{0\},\text{ and }K_{X}^{1}=\hom(X,\mathbb{R})\cap S_{X^{*}}

are all Hausdorff topological spaces when equipped with the
weak
∗
topology. Furthermore,
K_{X}
is compact. We say that a real
function
f
defined on a subset
S
of
B_{X^{*}}
is

positively homogeneous
if
f(\lambda x^{*})=\lambda f(x^{*})

for every
x^{*}\in S
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\lambda x^{*}\in S
. For every
x\in X
there is a continuous positively
homogeneous function

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\operatorname{ev}_{x}\colon&K_{X}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&x^{*}&\longmapsto&x^{*}(x)\\
\end{array}.

Let
C_{ph}(K_{X})
denote the set of positively homogeneous continuous
functions on
K_{X}
. With the order defined pointwise and the norm of
uniform convergence,
C_{ph}(K_{X})
is an AM-space.
According to the representation result by A. Goullet de Rugy

[
11
, Corollaire 1.31]
, the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&X&\longrightarrow&C_{ph}(K_{X})\\
&x&\longmapsto&\operatorname{ev}_{x}\\
\end{array}

defines a surjective lattice isometry. From now on, we shall identify

X
with
C_{ph}(K_{X})
.

Before characterizing all the
f\!
-algebra products on
X
we need to
compute its center

\mathcal{Z}(X)=\{\,T\in\mathcal{L}(X):\pm T\leq\lambda I\text{
for some }\lambda>0\,\}

in terms of
K_{X}
.

Denote by
C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})
the space of continuous functions
f\colon K_{X}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
that are bounded and satisfy
f(\lambda x^{*})=f(x^{*})
for all
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{1}
and
0<\lambda\leq 1
.
Note that this is a closed sublattice-algebra of
C_{b}(K_{X}^{*})
. In
particular, it is an AM-space with unit, the unit being the constant
one function in
K_{X}^{*}
.

For every
h\in C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})
, define the operator
M_{h}\colon C_{ph}(K_{X})\to C_{ph}(K_{X})
by

M_{h}(f)(x^{*})=\begin{cases}h(x^{*})f(x^{*})&\text{if }x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}\\
0&\text{if }x^{*}=0\end{cases}.

Note that
M_{h}(f)
is certainly continuous outside
0
and that, if

x_{\alpha}^{*}\to 0
in
K_{X}
with
x_{\alpha}^{*}\neq 0
for all

\alpha
big enough, then

M_{h}(f)(x_{\alpha}^{*})=h(x_{\alpha}^{*})f(x_{\alpha}^{*})\to 0

since
h(x_{\alpha}^{*})
is bounded and
f(x_{\alpha}^{*})\to 0
.
Also,
M_{h}(f)
is a positively
homogeneous function. Hence, the operator
M_{h}
is well-defined. Note
that it is also central, since it is linear and satisfies
\pm M_{h}f\leq\|h\|_{\infty}f
for all
f\geq 0
. (Side note: every positively
homogeneous function must vanish at
0
; from now on, we will
not write the value at
0
explicitly.)

Next we show that every operator in the center of
C_{ph}(K_{X})
is of the
form described above. We don’t claim any originality in this result:
it is well-known that central operators in
C_{ph}(K_{X})
are multiplication
operators (see
[
29
,
6
]
). Here, we provide a
characterization suited to our purposes using standard techniques.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
X
be an AM-space. The map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}M\colon&C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})&\longrightarrow&\mathcal{Z}(C_{ph}(K_{X}))\\
&h&\longmapsto&M_{h}\\
\end{array}

is a lattice-algebra isometry.

Proof.

It is direct to check that
M
is linear and that it is an algebra
homomorphism with
M\mathbb{1}_{K_{X}^{*}}=I
. That it is a lattice
homomorphism follows from the fact that
|C|f=|Cf|
for every
C\in\mathcal{Z}(C_{ph}(K_{X}))
and
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})_{+}
(see
[
1
, Theorem
3.30]
). The norm in

\mathcal{Z}(C_{ph}(K_{X}))
is given by
\|C\|=\inf\{\,\lambda>0:\pm C\leq\lambda I\,\}
for all
C\in\mathcal{Z}(C_{ph}(K_{X}))
(see
[
1
, Theorem
3.31]
). Therefore,
once we establish that
M
is bijective, it will follow that it is
an isometry.

Suppose
Mh=0
. For every
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
there exists
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
for which
f(x^{*})\neq 0
. Then

0=M_{h}(f)(x^{*})=h(x^{*})f(x^{*})
implies
h(x^{*})=0
. Hence

h=0
and
M
is injective.

To show that
M
is surjective we will use an adaptation of

[
27
, Lemma 2.1]
. Let
C\in\mathcal{Z}(C_{ph}(K_{X}))
be a central operator. Define a map

h\colon K_{X}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows: for every
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
,
choose an arbitrary
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
with
f(x^{*})\neq 0
and set

h(x^{*})=Cf(x^{*})/f(x^{*})
.

First of all we need to show that
h(x^{*})
is independent of the
choice of
f
. Suppose
f^{\prime}\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
is such that

f^{\prime}(x^{*})\neq 0
. Set

m=f-\frac{f(x^{*})}{f^{\prime}(x^{*})}f^{\prime}\in C_{ph}(K_{X}).

Then
m(x^{*})=0
and, since
C
is central,
\pm Cm(x^{*})\leq\|C\||m|(x^{*})=0
. Therefore

0=Cm(x^{*})=Cf(x^{*})-\frac{f(x^{*})}{f^{\prime}(x^{*})}Cf^{\prime}(x^{*})

and
Cf(x^{*})/f(x^{*})=Cf^{\prime}(x^{*})/f^{\prime}(x^{*})
. So
h
is
well-defined. Since
f
is continuous, if
f(x^{*})\neq 0

there exists a weak
∗
open neighbourhood
U
of
x^{*}
in which

f|_{U}
does not vanish. Then
h|_{U}=(Cf)|_{U}/f|_{U}
and, since both

f
and
Cf
are continuous,
h|_{U}
is continuous. Hence
h
is
locally continuous, and therefore continuous. Also note that

h
is bounded, since

|h(x^{*})|=\frac{|Cf(x^{*})|}{|f(x^{*})|}\leq\|C\|\frac{|f(x^{*})|}{|f(x^{*})|}=\|C\|.

Thus far we have constructed
h\in C_{b}(K_{X}^{*})
such that

Cf(x^{*})=h(x^{*})f(x^{*})
for all
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
and
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
with
f(x^{*})\neq 0
. Note that, if
f(x^{*})=0
, then also

Cf(x^{*})=0
(because
C
is central), so the identity

Cf(x^{*})=h(x^{*})f(x^{*})
still holds in this case. For an
element
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{1}
and
0<\lambda\leq 1
, using the
homogeneity of
f
and
Cf
:

\lambda h(x^{*})f(x^{*})=\lambda Cf(x^{*})=Cf(\lambda x^{*})=h(\lambda x^{*})f(\lambda x^{*})=\lambda h(\lambda x^{*})f(x^{*}).

Choosing an
f
for which
f(x^{*})\neq 0
shows that

h(x^{*})=h(\lambda x^{*})
. Hence
h\in C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})
and
C=M_{h}
, as
wanted.
∎

To each function
w\colon K_{X}^{1}\to\mathbb{R}
associate a map
w_{-1}\colon K_{X}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

w_{-1}(x^{*})=\frac{w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)}{\|x^{*}\|},\quad x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}.

Let
C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})
be the space of continuous bounded functions on

K_{X}^{1}
. Consider

W_{X}=\{\,w\in C_{b}(K_{X}^{1}):w_{-1}\text{ is continuous on }K_{X}^{*}\,\}.

We are going to show that
W_{X}
is a closed sublattice of

C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})
, and therefore an AM-space. Its positive cone will be
the AM-space
parametrizing the
f\!
-algebra products on
X
.
Note that we are not asking for
w_{-1}
to be bounded on
K_{X}^{*}

(in many cases it is not; see
Section

3
), we are only
asking for it to be continuous.

From
(\lambda w+w^{\prime})_{-1}=\lambda w_{-1}+(w^{\prime})_{-1}

and
|w|_{-1}=|w_{-1}|
it follows that
W_{X}
is a sublattice of

C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})
. To show that it is closed, let
(w_{n})\subseteq W_{X}
be
a sequence converging to
w
in
C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})
. For
\delta>0
, denote

K_{X}^{\delta}=\{\,x^{*}\in K_{X}:\|x^{*}\|\geq\delta\,\}.

Estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{x^{*}\in K_{X}^{\delta}}|(w_{n})_{-1}(x^{*})-w_{-1}(x^{*})|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x^{*}\in K_{X}^{\delta}}\frac{|w_{n}(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)-w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)|}{\|x^{*}\|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|w_{n}-w\|_{C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})}}{\delta}.

This shows that
(w_{n})_{-1}
converges uniformly to
w_{-1}
on

K_{X}^{\delta}
. In particular,
w_{-1}
is continuous on
K_{X}^{\delta}
. And since this is true for every
\delta>0
, it follows that

w_{-1}
is continuous on
K_{X}^{*}
. Hence
w\in W_{X}
and
W_{X}
is a
closed sublattice of
C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})
.

Every element of
(W_{X})_{+}
defines an
f\!
-algebra product on

X
in the following way:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be an AM-space and let
w\in(W_{X})_{+}
. Define

P\colon C_{ph}(K_{X})\times C_{ph}(K_{X})\to C_{ph}(K_{X})

by

P(f,g)(x^{*})=\frac{w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)}{\|x^{*}\|}f(x^{*})g(x^{*})

for all
f,g\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
and
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
(as usual,

P(f,g)(0)=0
). Then
P
is an

f\!
-algebra product on
X
. If
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, then
P
is
submultiplicative.

Proof.

Note that, in
K_{X}^{*}
,
P(f,g)=w_{-1}fg
, where juxtaposition
denotes the pointwise product. To show that
P
is an
f\!
-algebra product on
X
, the only non-trivial part is to
check that
P(f,g)
is continuous at
0
. Let
x_{\alpha}^{*}
be
a net converging to zero in
K_{X}
. For those
x_{\alpha}^{*}\neq 0
, we have

P(f,g)(x_{\alpha}^{*})=w(x_{\alpha}^{*}/\|x_{\alpha}^{*}\|)f(x_{\alpha}^{*}/\|x_{\alpha}^{*}\|)g(x_{\alpha}^{*}).

By assumption, the first two terms are bounded, while

g(x_{\alpha}^{*})\to 0
. Hence
P(f,g)
is continuous at
0
.

If
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, then for every
f,g\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
and
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
:

|P(f,g)(x^{*})|=|w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)f(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)g(x^{*})|\leq\|w\|_{\infty}\|f\|\|g\|\leq\|f\|\|g\|.

Hence
P
is submultiplicative.
∎

And every
f\!
-algebra product on
X
is of this form.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
X
be an AM-space identified with
C_{ph}(K_{X})
. A product

P\colon C_{ph}(K_{X})\times C_{ph}(K_{X})\to C_{ph}(K_{X})

is an
f\!
-algebra product if and only if there
exists a weight
w\in(W_{X})_{+}
such that

P(f,g)(x^{*})=\frac{w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)}{\|x^{*}\|}f(x^{*})g(x^{*})

for all
f,g\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
and
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
. This product is submultiplicative if and only if
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Proof.

After previous lemma, it only remains to show that
every
f\!
-algebra product is of the desired form and that, if the
product is submultiplicative, then
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.
Let
P\colon C_{ph}(K_{X})\times C_{ph}(K_{X})\to C_{ph}(K_{X})
be an
f\!
-algebra product. For
every
g\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
, the
f\!
-algebra property implies that the map

P(-,g)
is an orthomorphism. By

[
1
, Theorem 3.29]
,

P(-,g)\in\mathcal{Z}(C_{ph}(K_{X}))
, and by

2.2
there exists a unique function
h_{g}\in C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})
such that
P(-,g)=M(h_{g})
. Consider the map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}H\colon&C_{ph}(K_{X})&\longrightarrow&C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})\\
&g&\longmapsto&h_{g}\\
\end{array}.

That
H
is linear follows easily from the injectivity of
M
.
Positive central operators are lattice homomorphisms, so for every

f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})_{+}
and
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
:

\displaystyle M(h_{|g|})(f)(x^{*})

\displaystyle=P(f,|g|)(x^{*})

\displaystyle=|P(f,g)|(x^{*})

\displaystyle=|h_{g}(x^{*})f(x^{*})|

\displaystyle=M(|h_{g}|)(f)(x^{*}).

By injectivity of
M
,
h_{|g|}=|h_{g}|
, so
H
is also a lattice homomorphism.

Let
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
and let
\delta_{x^{*}}\in C_{b}^{(0)}(K_{X}^{*})^{*}
denote the usual evaluation at
x^{*}

functional. The map
H^{*}\delta_{x^{*}}=\delta_{x^{*}}\circ H\colon C_{ph}(K_{X})\to\mathbb{R}
is a lattice homomorphism. By construction, there exist

\phi(x^{*})\in K_{X}
and
w_{1}(x^{*})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
H^{*}\delta_{y^{*}}=w_{1}(y^{*})\delta_{\phi(y^{*})}
. This way we can define maps
\phi\colon K_{X}^{*}\to K_{X}

and
w_{1}\colon K_{X}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

satisfying
Hg=w_{1}g\circ\phi
in
K_{X}^{*}
. Using this and the
positive homogeneity of the functions,
P
is of the form:

(2)

P(f,g)(x^{*})=w_{1}(x^{*})f(x^{*})g(\phi(x^{*}))\quad\text{for all
}x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}.

Next we want to show that, for every
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}
for which

w_{1}(x^{*})\neq 0
,
\phi(x^{*})
is a scalar multiple of
x^{*}
. Suppose this was not true.
Then, being the elements of
K_{X}^{*}
atoms in
X^{*}
,
x^{*}
and

\phi(x^{*})
must be
disjoint. It cannot be the case that
x^{*}(x)\leq\phi(x^{*})(x)
for
all
x\in X_{+}
, so
x^{*}(x)>\phi(x^{*})(x)
for some
x\in X_{+}
.
Similarly,
x^{*}(y)<\phi(x^{*})(y)
for some
y\in X_{+}
. Then,
using that
w_{1}(x^{*})>0
,

P(\operatorname{ev}_{x},\operatorname{ev}_{y})(x^{*})=w_{1}(x^{*})x^{*}(x)\phi(x^{*})(y)>w_{1}(x^{*})x^{*}(y)\phi(x^{*})(x)=P(\operatorname{ev}_{y},\operatorname{ev}_{x})(x^{*}).

This contradicts the fact that every
f\!
-algebra product on an
Archimedean vector lattice is commutative. Hence
\phi(x^{*})

must be a positive multiple of
x^{*}
whenever
w_{1}(x^{*})\neq 0
.

Define
the map
w_{2}\colon K_{X}^{*}\to[0,1]
by
\phi(x^{*})=w_{2}(x^{*})x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|
,
whenever
w_{1}(x^{*})\neq 0
, and by
w_{2}(x^{*})=0
(or any other value)
whenever
w_{1}(x^{*})=0
. Set
w=w_{1}w_{2}
. Then (
2
)
becomes, using the homogeneity of
g
,

P(f,g)(x^{*})=\frac{w(x^{*})}{\|x^{*}\|}f(x^{*})g(x^{*}).

By the positive homogeneity of
f
and
g
,

P(f,g)(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)=\frac{w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)}{\|x^{*}\|^{2}}f(x^{*})g(x^{*}).

Also

\frac{1}{\|x^{*}\|}P(f,g)(x^{*})=\frac{w(x^{*})}{\|x^{*}\|^{2}}f(x^{*})g(x^{*})

and since
P(f,g)
is positively homogeneous, it follows by
choosing appropriate
f
and
g
that

w(x^{*})=w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)
. Hence we can restrict the
definition of
w
to
K_{X}^{1}
and write

P(f,g)(x^{*})=\frac{w(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)}{\|x^{*}\|}f(x^{*})g(x^{*})\quad\text{for
all }x^{*}\in K_{X}^{*}.

It remains to check that
w\in(W_{X})_{+}
. By construction,
w
is
positive. Since
P
is
positive on both coordinates, it must be bounded by some constant

\|P\|
, and so for every
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{1}

and
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
with
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
:

|w(x^{*})|f(x^{*})^{2}=|P(f,f)(x^{*})|\leq\|P(f,f)\|\leq\|P\|.

Fixed
x^{*}
,
f(x^{*})
gets arbitrarily close to
1
as
f

ranges through
(B_{X})_{+}
(in
fact, it reaches
1
, see
[
3
, Theorem 4.3]
), so
|w(x^{*})|\leq\|P\|
. In particular, if
P
is
submultiplicative, then
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq\|P\|\leq 1
.

To show that
w_{-1}
is continuous, let
x_{\alpha}^{*}\to x^{*}

be a convergent net in
K_{X}^{*}
. Let
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
be such that

f(x^{*})\neq 0
. Since both
f
and
P(f,f)
are continuous on

K_{X}
:

f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}\to f(x^{*})^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad w_{-1}(x_{\alpha}^{*})f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}\to w_{-1}(x^{*})f(x^{*})^{2}.

Choose
\alpha_{0}
such that
|f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}-f(x^{*})^{2}|\leq f(x^{*})^{2}/2
for all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle|w_{-1}(x_{\alpha}^{*})-w_{-1}(x^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{f(x^{*})^{2}}|f(x_{\alpha}^{*})|^{2}|w_{-1}(x_{\alpha}^{*})-w_{-1}(x^{*})|

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{f(x^{*})^{2}}|f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}w_{-1}(x_{\alpha}^{*})-f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}w_{-1}(x^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{f(x^{*})^{2}}|f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}w_{-1}(x_{\alpha}^{*})-f(x^{*})^{2}w_{-1}(x^{*})|

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\frac{2}{f(x^{*})^{2}}|f(x^{*})^{2}-f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{2}||w_{-1}(x^{*})|

for all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
. Taking limits in
\alpha
shows
that
w_{-1}(x_{\alpha}^{*})\to w_{-1}(x^{*})
.
∎

Remark
.

Let
X
be an AM-space with unit, say
X=C(K)
for a certain
K
.
Then
K_{X}^{1}
is homeomorphic to
K
(in particular, it is
compact) and therefore
W_{X}\subseteq C_{b}(K_{X}^{1})=C(K)
. Since the
norm is continuous in

(because every positive functional
attains its norm at

) it follows that

. Hence,
for AM-spaces with unit, previous theorem reduces to

.

So, for AM-spaces with unit,

, that is,

is
maximal. The AM-spaces with the property that

is maximal are

3.
AM-algebras

By Kakutani’s theorem, every AM-space embeds lattice isometrically in
a space of continuous functions. It is natural to ask: which of the
products in
2.4
embed lattice-algebra
isometrically in a space of continuous functions? (Note that every
sublattice-algebra of a space of continuous functions is an
f\!
-algebra, so it
suffices to consider
f\!
-algebra products to characterize
all

products on an AM-space that embed in a space of continuous
functions.) The products with this property were characterized
intrinsically in
[
22
]
using
the following notion.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice and let
(e_{\alpha})\subseteq X_{+}
be
a net. We say that
X
is an
AM-space with approximate unit

(e_{\alpha})
if
x^{*}(e_{\alpha})\to\|x^{*}\|
for all
x^{*}\in X_{+}
. We also say that
(e_{\alpha})
is an
approximate order
unit
of
X
.

It turns out that having an approximate order unit is equivalent to
being an AM-space (see
[
22
, Lemma 2.5]
). Providing an approximate order unit
is, morally, a quantitative way of saying that a Banach lattice is an
AM-space. This approach to AM-spaces enables us to go one step
further and establish a relation between the AM-space and algebraic
structures. Recall
that a net
(e_{\alpha})
in a Banach algebra
A
is an
approximate
identity
if both
e_{\alpha}a\to a
and
ae_{\alpha}\to a
hold for
every
a\in A
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
A
be a Banach lattice algebra and let
(e_{\alpha})\subseteq A_{+}
be a net. We say that
A
is an
AM-algebra with
approximate unit

(e_{\alpha})
if
(e_{\alpha})
is both an
approximate order unit and an approximate algebraic identity.

If
A
has an order unit
e
that is at the same time an algebraic
identity, then
A
is said to be an
AM-algebra with unit

e
.

This property characterizes the products in AM-spaces that can be
lattice-algebra isometrically embedded in spaces of continuous functions.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
22
, Theorem 2.10]
)
.

A Banach lattice algebra
A
is an AM-algebra with approximate
unit if and only if it is lattice-algebra isometric to a closed
sublattice-algebra of
C(K)
, for some compact
Hausdorff space
K
.

Moreover, we have the following analogue of Kakutani’s theorem.

Theorem 3.4

(
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

(i)

Every AM-algebra with unit is lattice-algebra
isometric to
C(K)
for some compact Hausdorff space
K
,
with the unit corresponding to the constant one function
\mathbb{1}_{K}
.

(ii)

Every AM-algebra with approximate unit is
lattice-algebra isometric to a closed sublattice-algebra
of
C(K)
for some compact Hausdorff space
K
. Moreover,
choosing
K
appropriately, there exists a closed set

F\subseteq K
such that the AM-algebra is lattice and
algebra isometric to the closed sublattice-algebra of

C(K)
:

\{\,f\in C(K):f(t)=0\text{ for all }t\in F\,\}.

In particular, it embeds as an order and
algebraic ideal in
C(K)
.

Even though it was not clear from the definition, a corollary of this
theorem is that, if
A
is an AM-algebra with an approximate unit,
then
(B_{A})_{+}
is an approximate identity.

With this language, the question of which products in an AM-space embed in
C(K)

becomes: which of the submultiplicative products in
2.4
give the
AM-space
X
a structure of AM-algebra with approximate unit? Since

X
is already an AM-space, these products are precisely the ones
making
(B_{X})_{+}
into an approximate algebraic identity.

Suppose

(f_{\alpha})=(B_{X})_{+}
is an approximate identity for the

f\!
-algebra product given by the weight
w\in(W_{X})_{+}
with norm

\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
. For all
g\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
,
P(f_{\alpha},g)\to g
, which means in particular that

0=\lim_{\alpha}\sup_{y^{*}\in K_{X}^{1}}|g(y^{*})||w(y^{*})f_{\alpha}(y^{*})-1|.

Hence
w(y^{*})f_{\alpha}(y^{*})\to 1
for all
y^{*}\in K_{X}^{1}
.
Since
0\leq f_{\alpha}(y^{*})\leq 1
for all
\alpha
,
w(y^{*})\geq 1
. On
the other hand, since the product is submultiplicative,
w(y^{*})\leq 1
. Thus
w=\mathbb{1}_{K_{X}^{1}}
, and the only product that can make
C_{ph}(K_{X})

into an AM-algebra with approximate unit is

(3)

P(f,g)(x^{*})=\frac{1}{\|x^{*}\|}f(x^{*})g(x^{*}).

The previous discussion shows, in particular:

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
X
be an AM-space. If there exists a product making
X
into an
AM-algebra with approximate unit, then it is unique.

When such a product exists, we will
simply say that
X
is an
AM-algebra
and call the associated
product the
AM-algebra product
. Of course, every AM-space
with unit is an AM-algebra, with the AM-algebra product being the
pointwise product. The following example
shows that not every AM-space is an AM-algebra.

Example 3.6
.

Consider
X=c
with the usual linear and lattice structures but
with norm

\|x\|=2\lim_{n}x_{n}\vee\|x\|_{\infty},

where
\|x\|_{\infty}=\sup_{n}|x_{n}|
. Consider the point-evaluation
functionals
\delta_{j}
at coordinates
j\in\mathbb{N}
and the limit
functional
\delta_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{n}x_{n}
. These are all
lattice homomorphisms, and it is easy to check that
\|\delta_{j}\|=1
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
, whereas
\|\delta_{\infty}\|=1/2
.
We claim that
w=\mathbb{1}_{K_{X}^{1}}\not\in W_{X}
. Indeed,

\delta_{j}\to\delta_{\infty}
in
K_{X}^{*}
, yet
w_{-1}(\delta_{j})=1
and

w_{-1}(\delta_{\infty})=\frac{w(2\delta_{\infty})}{\|\delta_{\infty}\|}=2.

Thus
w_{-1}
is not continuous on
K_{X}^{*}
.

The following theorem characterizes the AM-spaces that are AM-algebras
in several ways.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
X
be an AM-space. The following are equivalent:

(i)

X
is an AM-algebra.

(ii)

\mathbb{1}_{K_{X}^{1}}\in W_{X}
.

(iii)

The norm is weak
∗
continuous on
K_{X}^{*}
.

(iv)

W_{X}
is maximal (i.e.,

).

(v)

X
is lattice isometric to a closed subalgebra of

, for a certain

.

(vi)

X
is lattice isometric to a closed ideal of
C(K)
,
for a certain
K
.

(vii)

is lattice isometric to
C_{0}(\Omega)
, for a
certain locally compact Hausdorff space
\Omega
.

(viii)

For every

bounded above, the following

\sup\{\,\|a\|:a\in A\,\}=\inf\{\,\|b\|:b\text{ is an upper bound of }A\,\}.

Proof.

It was shown in the discussion preceding
3.5

that
(i)
implies
(ii)
. If
w=\mathbb{1}_{K_{X}^{1}}\in W_{X}
,

(ii)
implies
(iii)
. If the norm is continuous on

implies

(iv)
.

To prove that

suppose

defines an

-algebra product on

. Suppose

.

(i)

4.
Properties of AM-algebras

In AM-algebras with unit (i.e., in spaces of continuous functions)
there are several properties relating the algebraic and lattice
structures. We have already encountered two of them, namely, that
every closed subalgebra is a sublattice and that closed algebraic
ideals and closed order ideals coincide. The goal of this section is
to show that results of this kind (relating the algebraic and
lattice structures) hold for general AM-algebras.

Since, according to
3.8
, AM-algebras end up being
spaces of the form
C_{0}(\Omega)
for some locally compact Hausdorff
space
\Omega
, the lattice-algebraic properties studied in this
section are certainly known to the experts. However, we have been
unable to locate any written reference to them. For the sake of
completeness, we include them here, and prove them through an abstract
approach that also uses the spaces
C_{ph}(K_{X})
.

The following simple lemma will turn out to be very useful.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be an AM-algebra. For every
x\in X_{+}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

there exists a unique positive
n
-th root of
x
. Moreover,

\|x^{1/n}\|=\|x\|^{1/n}
.

Proof.

This is direct once we embed isometrically
X
as an ideal in a space of
continuous functions. Here we also provide an elementary
proof that exhibits the explicit expression of
x^{1/n}
.

Identify
X
with
C_{ph}(K_{X})
and let
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})_{+}
. Define the
function

\begin{array}[]{cccc}g\colon&K_{X}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&x^{*}&\longmapsto&\|x^{*}\|^{1-1/n}f(x^{*})^{1/n}\\
\end{array}.

Note that, for every
x^{*}\in K_{X}^{1}
and
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
,

g(\lambda x^{*})=\|\lambda x^{*}\|^{1-1/n}f(\lambda x^{*})^{1/n}=\lambda\|x^{*}\|^{1-1/n}f(x^{*})^{1/n}=\lambda g(x^{*}).

Thus
g
is positively homogeneous. It is also continuous on

K_{X}^{*}
, because by
3.7
the norm is continuous
on
K_{X}^{*}
. If
x_{\alpha}^{*}\to 0
in
K_{X}
, then

|g(x_{\alpha}^{*})|\leq f(x_{\alpha}^{*})^{1/n}\to 0.

This shows that
g\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
. Raising
g
to the
n
-th power for the
AM-algebra product gives:

\frac{1}{\|x^{*}\|^{n-1}}g(x^{*})^{n}=f(x^{*}).

Hence
g^{n}=f
. Since the norm is submultiplicative,
\|f\|\leq\|g\|^{n}
. But also

g(x^{*})=\|x^{*}\|^{1-1/n}f(x^{*})^{1/n}\leq f(x^{*}/\|x^{*}\|)^{1/n}\leq\|f\|^{1/n}.

Thus
\|f\|=\|g\|^{n}
. If
h\in C_{ph}(K_{X})_{+}
also satisfies

h^{n}=f
, then by writing down explicitly
h^{n}(x^{*})
it is
clear that
h(x^{*})=\|x^{*}\|^{1-1/n}f(x^{*})^{1/n}=g(x^{*})
.
∎

The following generalizes two standard facts about
C(K)
that we have
mentioned several times.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X
be an AM-algebra.

(i)

Every closed subalgebra of
X
is a closed sublattice.

(ii)

In
X
, closed algebraic ideals and closed order ideals
coincide.

Proof.

(i)

This is immediate once we see
X
as a closed subalgebra
of a certain
C(K)
.

(ii)

Represent
X
as a closed ideal in a
C(K)
. It is clear
that every order ideal of
X
is also an order ideal of

C(K)
. Therefore closed order ideals are algebraic
ideals. The converse implication is not so direct. Suppose

Y
is a closed algebraic ideal of
X
; we want to show
that
Y
is a closed algebraic ideal of
C(K)
. In particular,
Y

is a subalgebra of
X
, and by
3.10
it
is itself an AM-algebra. By
4.1
, for
every
y\in Y_{+}
there exists
y^{1/2}\in Y_{+}
such that

(y^{1/2})^{2}=y
. Let
f\in C(K)
. Since
X
is an
algebraic ideal,
fy^{1/2}\in X
. Since
Y
is an
algebraic ideal in
X
,
fy\in Y
. Finally, for arbitrary

y\in Y
, since
Y
is a sublattice
y_{+},y_{-}\in Y
, and
therefore
fy=fy_{+}-fy_{-}\in Y
. Hence
Y
is a closed
algebraic ideal of
C(K)
, and therefore a closed order
ideal of both
C(K)
and
X
.∎

Recall (for instance, from
[
1
, Section
4.3]
) that for an operator

T\colon C(K)\to C(L)
, where
K
and
L
are compact Hausdorff
spaces, the following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is an algebra homomorphism.

(ii)

T
is a lattice homomorphism and
T\mathbb{1}_{K}
is
idempotent.

(iii)

Tf=\chi_{V}f\circ\phi
for some clopen
V\subseteq L

and some continuous function
\phi\colon V\to K
.

Our next goal is to generalize this result to AM-algebras. For
AM-spaces
X
and
Y
, we say that a map
\phi\colon K_{Y}\to K_{X}
is

positively homogeneous
if
\phi(\lambda y^{*})=\lambda\phi(y^{*})
for all
y^{*}\in K_{Y}^{1}
and
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
. For a
set
B
in an algebra
A
, we denote as usual
B^{2}=\{\,b_{1}b_{2}:b_{1},b_{2}\in B\,\}
. The philosophy behind AM-algebras is that the role
played by the constant one function in spaces of continuous functions
is now played by the positive unit ball. The following result is
consistent with this philosophy.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be AM-algebras and let
T\colon X\to Y
be an
operator. The following are equivalent:

(i)

T
is an algebra homomorphism.

(ii)

T
is a lattice homomorphism and

\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}}y^{*}(y)=\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})}y^{*}(y)

for every
y^{*}\in Y^{*}
.

(iii)

Identify
X
with
C_{ph}(K_{X})
and
Y
with
C_{ph}(K_{Y})
. There
exists a continuous positively homogeneous map
\phi\colon K_{Y}\to K_{X}
with
\phi(K_{Y}^{1})\subseteq K_{X}^{1}\cup\{0\}
such that
Tf=f\circ\phi
.

Before presenting the proof, some remarks are in order.

Remark
.

The condition in
(ii)
may seem weird, but it is the
natural approximate analogue of saying that
T\mathbb{1}_{K}
is
idempotent in the case that
X=C(K)
and
Y=C(L)
. Indeed, for
spaces of continuous functions the net
(B_{X})_{+}
converges (in
norm) to
\mathbb{1}_{K}
, and therefore

T\mathbb{1}_{K}=T\big(\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}x\big)=\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})}y

while

(T\mathbb{1}_{K})^{2}=T\big(\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}x\big)^{2}=\big(\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})}y\big)^{2}=\lim_{y,z\in T((B_{X})_{+})}yz=\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}}y.

Notice that
T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}
is also an increasing net, so that the
limits that appear in the previous expression make sense. Hence,

(T\mathbb{1}_{K})^{2}=T\mathbb{1}_{K}
is equivalent to

(4)

\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})}y=\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}}y.

In the theorem, we are only asking for a weak version of this
equality to hold.

In the end, after the theorem is proved, it will turn out that
(
4
) and item
(ii)
in the theorem are
equivalent as long as
T
is a lattice homomorphism. In fact, they
are both equivalent to the stronger

T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}=T((B_{X})_{+})
. Indeed, if
T
is a lattice
homomorphism and

\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}}y^{*}(y)=\lim_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})}y^{*}(y)

holds for every
y^{*}\in Y^{*}
, then by the above theorem
T
is
an algebra homomorphism, and therefore
TxTy=T(xy)
for every
x,y\in(B_{X})_{+}
. Since
xy\in(B_{X})_{+}
, it follows that

T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}\subseteq T((B_{X})_{+})
. Conversely, if
x\in(B_{X})_{+}
, then by
4.1
there exists
y\in(B_{X})_{+}
such that
y^{2}=x
, and therefore
Tx=(Ty)^{2}\in T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}
.

Proof of
4.3
.

To show that
(i)
implies
(ii)
, suppose
T
is an
algebra homomorphism. First note that
T
is positive, for if

x\in X_{+}
, then

T(x)=T(({\sqrt{x}})^{2})=T(\sqrt{x})^{2}\geq 0,

where the square root exists by Lemma
4.1
. For a general
x\in X
:

|T(x)|^{2}=T(x^{2})=T(|x|^{2})=T(|x|)^{2}.

Since
T
is positive,
T(|x|)\geq 0
.
According to Lemma
4.1
, positive square roots are unique, so

T(|x|)=|Tx|
. By previous remark,
T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}=T((B_{X})_{+})
holds
whenever
T
is an algebra homomorphism.

Conversely, suppose that
(ii)
holds. To show that
T
is
an algebra homomorphism, it suffices to show that

T^{**}\colon X^{**}\to Y^{**}
is an algebra homomorphism,
where
X^{**}
and
Y^{**}
are equipped with the corresponding
Arens products. By
3.8
,
X^{**}
and
Y^{**}

with the Arens products are
lattice-algebra isometric to a space of continuous functions.
Also,
T^{**}
is a lattice homomorphism. Therefore it
suffices to show that
T^{**}(e)^{2}=T^{**}(e)
, where
e
is the
order unit of
X^{**}
. Recall that
e
is the weak
∗
limit
of the increasing net
(B_{X})_{+}
in
X^{**}
. Since
T^{**}

is weak
∗
to weak
∗
continuous,
T^{**}e
is the weak
∗
limit
of
T^{**}((B_{X})_{+})
.

In order to show that
T^{**}(e)^{2}=T^{**}(e)
, let us recall the steps involved in the
construction of the Arens product. AM-algebras are Arens regular, so it does not matter which one of the
Arens products we use. First, for every
x\in X
and

x^{*}\in X^{*}
, their product
x^{*}x\in X^{*}
is defined by

(x^{*}x)(y)=x^{*}(xy)
for every
y\in X
. Second, for every

x^{*}\in X^{*}
and
x^{**}\in X^{**}
, their product
x^{**}x^{*}\in X^{*}
is defined by
(x^{**}x^{*})(x)=x^{**}(x^{*}x)
for every
x\in X
. Finally, for every
x^{**},y^{**}\in X^{**}
, their Arens product is defined by
(x^{**}y^{**})(x^{*})=x^{**}(y^{**}x^{*})
for every
x^{*}\in X^{*}
.

Unfolding these definitions, the following chain of identities
holds for every
y^{*}\in(Y^{*})_{+}
:

\displaystyle T^{**}(e)^{2}(y^{*})

\displaystyle=T^{**}(e)(T^{**}(e)y^{*})

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}(T^{**}(e)y^{*})(Tx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}T^{**}(e)(y^{*}Tx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}e(T^{*}(y^{*}Tx))

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}\lim_{x^{\prime}\in(B_{X})_{+}}(T^{*}(y^{*}Tx))(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}\lim_{x^{\prime}\in(B_{X})_{+}}(y^{*}Tx)(Tx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}\lim_{x^{\prime}\in(B_{X})_{+}}y^{*}(TxTx^{\prime}).

Since
T
and
y^{*}
are positive, and the product of positive
elements is positive,
\{y^{*}(TxTx^{\prime})\}_{(x,x^{\prime})\in(B_{X})_{+}\times(B_{X})_{+}}
is an increasing net of real numbers (and the same is
true if we fix either
x
or
x^{\prime}
). Its limit is therefore its
supremum. Thus the two limits
that appear in the last displayed equation are actually suprema.
Using this fact, and the assumption, yields

\displaystyle T^{**}(e)^{2}(y^{*})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in(B_{X})_{+}}y^{*}(TxTx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})^{2}}y^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in T((B_{X})_{+})}y^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}y^{*}(Tx)

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\in(B_{X})_{+}}(T^{*}y^{*})(x)

\displaystyle=e(T^{*}y^{*})

\displaystyle=(T^{**}e)(y^{*}),

as wanted.

It remains to show the equivalence between
(i)
and

(iii)
. Identify
X
with
C_{ph}(K_{X})
and
Y
with
C_{ph}(K_{Y})
.
If
T
is an algebra homomorphism, it was shown above that
T
is
a lattice homomorphism. Also, since
T^{**}
is an algebra
homomorphism between spaces of continuous functions,
\|T\|=\|T^{**}\|=1
. For every
y^{*}\in K_{Y}
,
T^{*}\delta_{y^{*}}=\delta_{y^{*}}\circ T\colon C_{ph}(K_{X})\to\mathbb{R}
is a lattice
homomorphism. By construction, there exist
\psi(y^{*})\in K_{X}

and
w(y^{*})\in[0,1]
such that
T^{*}\delta_{y^{*}}=w(y^{*})\delta_{\psi(y^{*})}
; in other words,

Tf(y^{*})=w(y^{*})f(\psi(y^{*}))
for every
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
. Using
positive homogeneity, we can write
Tf(y^{*})=f(\phi(y^{*}))
,
where
\phi(y^{*})=w(y^{*})\psi(y^{*})\in K_{X}
.

Fix
y^{*}\in K_{Y}^{*}
with
\phi(y^{*})\neq 0
. Since
T
is an
algebra homomorphism,

T(f^{2})(y^{*})=\frac{1}{\|\phi(y^{*})\|}f(\phi(y^{*}))^{2}

coincides with

(Tf)^{2}(y^{*})=\frac{1}{\|y^{*}\|}f(\phi(y^{*}))^{2}

for every
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
. By choosing
f
with
f(\phi(y^{*}))\neq 0
(this is always possible, since
\phi(y^{*})\neq 0
) it
follows that
\|\phi(y^{*})\|=\|y^{*}\|
. Hence
\phi(K_{Y}^{1})\subseteq K_{X}^{1}\cup\{0\}
.

To complete the implication, it only remains to check that the map
\phi
is continuous and
positively homogeneous. Fixed
y^{*}\in K_{Y}^{1}
and

0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, the identity

f(\lambda\phi(y^{*}))=\lambda f(\phi(y^{*}))=\lambda Tf(y^{*})=Tf(\lambda y^{*})=f(\phi(\lambda y^{*}))

holds for every
f\in C_{ph}(K_{X})
. Hence
\phi(\lambda y^{*})=\lambda\phi(y^{*})
. Let
y_{\alpha}^{*}\to y^{*}
be a
convergent net in
K_{Y}
. For every
x\in X
, using
that
T\operatorname{ev}_{x}
is continuous,

\operatorname{ev}_{x}(\phi(y_{\alpha}^{*}))=(T\operatorname{ev}_{x})(y_{\alpha}^{*})\to(T\operatorname{ev}_{x})(y^{*})=\operatorname{ev}_{x}(\phi(y^{*})).

Hence
\phi(y_{\alpha}^{*})\to\phi(y^{*})
in the weak
∗

topology. This completes the proof that
(i)
implies

(iii)
.

To show that
(iii)
implies
(i)
, note first that

f\circ\phi\in C_{ph}(K_{Y})
because
\phi
is both continuous and
positively homogeneous. So
T
is well-defined. Let
y^{*}\in K_{Y}
. If
\phi(y^{*})=0
, then clearly

T(fg)(y^{*})=0=(TfTg)(y^{*})
. If
\phi(y^{*})\neq 0
, then by assumption

\frac{\|\phi(y^{*})\|}{\|y^{*}\|}=\|\phi(y^{*}/\|y^{*}\|)\|=1;

that is,
\|\phi(y^{*})\|=\|y^{*}\|
. It follows that

\displaystyle T(fg)(y^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|\phi(y^{*})\|}f(\phi(y^{*}))g(\phi(y^{*}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|y^{*}\|}f(\phi(y^{*}))g(\phi(y^{*}))

\displaystyle=(TfTg)(y^{*}).

Hence
T
is an algebra homomorphism.
∎

5.
AL and AM-spaces with a unique

f
-algebra product

We have first characterized all the
f\!
-algebra products on
AM-spaces, and then studied a particular product (the AM-algebra
product). The existence of this product was characterized by the fact
that the space
W_{X}
was maximal. We now turn our heads and look at
the other end. Can
W_{X}
be minimal? In other words: can an AM-space
admit a single
f\!
-algebra structure? (Namely, the one given by the zero
product.) The following result answers this question in a broader
context. Recall that a Banach lattice
X
is said to have

trivial center
if
\dim X>1
and
\mathcal{Z}(X)
consists
only of the scalar multiples of the identity.

Proposition 5.1
.

A Banach lattice with trivial center has a unique
f\!
-algebra product.

Proof.

Let
X
be a Banach lattice with trivial center.
Let
P
be an
f\!
-algebra product on
X
. For every
x\in X_{+}
,

P(x,-)
is an orthomorphism, and therefore a central operator
(see
[
1
, Theorem 3.29]
). Since the center is trivial, there
exists a scalar
\lambda(x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
P(x,-)=\lambda(x)I
. The annihilator of
P
, defined as the set

N=\{\,x\in X:P(x,y)=0\text{ for all }y\in X\,\},

is a band (see
[
13
, Section 3]
). If
P
is not the zero product,
then
N\neq X
, and therefore its disjoint complement
N^{d}
is not zero.
Let
x,y\in(N^{d})_{+}
. Since
\lambda(x),\lambda(y)\neq 0
and

\lambda(x)y=xy=yx=\lambda(y)x

it follows that
N^{d}
has dimension one. Hence

N^{d}
is a non-trivial projection band. This is a contradiction
with the fact that the center of
X
is trivial.
∎

In particular, every AM-space
X
with trivial center (see

[
11
]
and
[
27
]
for
examples of such spaces) satisfies
W_{X}=\{0\}
. The converse, however,
is false.

Example 5.2
.

Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be Banach lattices that only admit the trivial

f\!
-algebra product (for example,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}

can be Banach lattices with trivial center). Their direct
sum
X=X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}X_{2}
has non-trivial center, since both
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are non-trivial
projection bands in
X
. Let
P\colon X\times X\to X
be an
f\!
-algebra product on
X
. The
f\!
-algebra property implies that projection bands
are closed under multiplication. Hence the restriction

P|_{X_{1}\times X_{1}}\colon X_{1}\times X_{1}\to X_{1}

defines an
f\!
-algebra product on
X_{1}
. By
assumption,
P|_{X_{1}\times X_{1}}=0
.
Similarly,
P|_{X_{2}\times X_{2}}=0
. Let
(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in X

and compute

\displaystyle P((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))

\displaystyle=P((x_{1},0),(y_{1},0))+P((x_{1},0),(0,y_{2}))

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+P((0,x_{2}),(y_{1},0))+P((0,x_{2}),(0,y_{2}))

\displaystyle=P((x_{1},0),(0,y_{2}))+P((0,x_{2}),(y_{1},0))

\displaystyle=0,

where in the last equality we are using that the product of
disjoint elements is
0
. Hence
P=0
.

This prompts a natural question: is there a simple condition
characterizing the AM-spaces with a single
f\!
-algebra product? As it turns
out, for AL-spaces there is such a simple characterization. This
characterization will be a simple consequence of the next theorem that
characterizes all
f\!
-algebra products on AL-spaces.

Before presenting the result, let us recall some notions about atoms
in Banach lattices (see
[
19
, Section 26]
). An
atom
in a Banach
lattice
X
is a positive element
a\in X_{+}
for which
0\leq x\leq a
implies that

x
is a scalar multiple of
a
. The linear span of
a
is a
projection band. Denote by
P_{a}
be the associated band projection.
Thus

P_{a}x=\lambda_{a}(x)a
, for a certain
\lambda_{a}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
. The map

x\mapsto\lambda_{a}(x)
defines a positive linear functional on
X
,
called the
coordinate functional
of
a
. Let
A
be a maximal
family of norm-one atoms in
X
, and let
B=B(A)
denote the
band generated by these atoms. When
X
is order complete, the
band projection onto
B
is given by
Px=\bigvee_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)a
for every
x\in X_{+}
. When
X
is order continuous,

Px=\sum_{a\in A}\lambda_{a}(x)a=\sup_{F\in\Fin(A)}\sum_{a\in F}\lambda_{a}(x)a,

where
\Fin(A)
denotes the collection of finite subsets of
A
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
X
be an AL-space and let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a maximal
family of norm-one atoms in
X
. Let
e_{i}^{*}
be the coordinate
functional of
e_{i}
. A product
P\colon X\times X\to X
is an
f\!
-algebra product if and only if there exists a
weight
w\in\ell_{\infty}(I)_{+}
such that

P(f,g)=\sum_{i\in I}w(i)e_{i}^{*}(f)e_{i}^{*}(g)e_{i}\quad\text{for
all }f,g\in X.

This product is submultiplicative if and only if
\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1
.

Proof.

Let
P\colon L_{1}(\mu)\times L_{1}(\mu)\to L_{1}(\mu)
be an
f\!
-algebra product, for a certain measure
\mu
. First assume that
\mu
is

\sigma
-finite. Actually, we may assume that
\mu
is a
probability measure, since

L_{1}(\mu)
is lattice isometric to
L_{1}(\mu^{\prime})
for some
probability measure
measure
\mu^{\prime}
.

For every
f\in L_{1}(\mu)_{+}
the operator
P(f,-)
is in the
center of
L_{1}(\mu)_{+}
. By
[
1
, Theorem
3.34]
, there exists
w_{f}\in L_{\infty}(\mu)
such that
P(f,g)=w_{f}g
; in fact,
w_{f}=P(f,\mathbb{1})
. For a general
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
, by linearity

P(f,g)=w_{f_{+}}g-w_{f_{-}}g
, so we may define
w_{f}=w_{f_{+}}-w_{f_{-}}
.
This way we obtain a linear map
w\colon L_{1}(\mu)\to L_{\infty}(\mu)
. We
claim that
w
is a lattice homomorphism. Note that
L_{\infty}(\mu)\subseteq L_{1}(\mu)
, so the lattice operations in
L_{\infty}(\mu)

are formally the same as those in
L_{1}(\mu)
. Then

w_{|f|}=P(|f|,\mathbb{1})=|P(f,\mathbb{1})|=|w_{f}|,

because
P(-,\mathbb{1})
is a lattice homomorphism. Decompose

L_{1}(\mu)=L_{1}(\nu_{1})\oplus L_{1}(\nu_{2})
, where
\nu_{1}
is a
finite atomic measure and
\nu_{2}
is a finite
non-atomic measure. Both
L_{1}(\nu_{1})
and
L_{1}(\nu_{2})
are
projection bands in
L_{1}(\mu)
. Therefore

w|_{L_{1}(\nu_{1})}\colon L_{1}(\nu_{1})\to L_{\infty}(\mu)
is also a lattice
homomorphism. Since the dual
L_{\infty}(\nu_{1})
of
L_{1}(\nu_{1})
has no atoms (because

\nu_{1}
is non-atomic),
L_{1}(\nu_{1})
does not have non-zero functionals that
are lattice homomorphisms. But
L_{\infty}(\mu)
certainly does.
So it must be
w|_{L_{1}(\nu_{1})}=0
. If we decompose

f=f_{d}+f_{c}\in L_{1}(\nu_{1})\oplus L_{1}(\nu_{2})
, then

P(f,g)=P(f_{d},g_{d})
, because the product of disjoint elements is
zero.

Now suppose that
\mu
is a general measure. For every
f,g\in L_{1}(\mu)
, the band generated by
f
and
g

is isometric to
L_{1}(\mu^{\prime})
, where
\mu^{\prime}
is the restriction of

\mu
to the union of the supports of
f
and
g
; in particular,

\mu^{\prime}
is
\sigma
-finite. Since bands are subalgebras,
L_{1}(\mu^{\prime})
is also an
f\!
-algebra with the
inherited product. By the previous argument,
P(f,g)=P(f_{d},g_{d})

in
L_{1}(\mu^{\prime})
, where
f_{d}
and
g_{d}
denote the projections of

f
and
g
onto the band generated by the atoms in

L_{1}(\mu^{\prime})
. Since
L_{1}(\mu^{\prime})
is a band in
L_{1}(\mu)
,
f_{d}
and
g_{d}
are also the
projections of
f
and
g
onto the band generated by the atoms in

L_{1}(\mu)
.

As in the statement of the theorem, let
\{e_{i}\}_{i\in I}
be a
maximal family of norm-one atoms in
L_{1}(\mu)
and let

\{e_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in I}
be the associated coordinate functionals.
If
e_{j}^{*}(P(e_{i},e_{i}))\neq 0
were true for some
i\neq j
, then

P(e_{i},e_{i})\wedge e_{j}\geq e_{j}^{*}(P(e_{i},e_{i}))e_{j}\neq 0

contradicting the
f\!
-algebra property. So
P(e_{i},e_{i})
must be a
scalar multiple of
e_{i}
. Let
w\in\ell_{\infty}(I)_{+}
be defined by
w(i)e_{i}=P(e_{i},e_{i})
(note
that
w
is indeed bounded, because
P
is). Using that disjoint
elements have product zero:

\displaystyle P(f,g)

\displaystyle=P(f_{d},g_{d})

\displaystyle=P\bigg(\sum_{i\in I}e_{i}^{*}(f)e_{i},\sum_{j\in I}e_{j}^{*}(g)e_{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}e_{i}^{*}(f)e_{i}^{*}(g)P(e_{i},e_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in I}w(i)e_{i}^{*}(f)e_{i}^{*}(g)e_{i}.

Using this expression, it is clear that
P
is submuliplicative if
and only if
{\|w\|_{\infty}\leq 1}
.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
X
be an AL-space. The only possible
f\!
-algebra product on
X
is
the zero product if and only if
X
has no atoms.

For a general Banach lattice
X
, it is true that if
X
has an atom,
then there exists a non-trivial
f\!
-algebra product on
X
.
This is proved after the following lemma, which may be of
interest by itself.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
X
be a vector lattice. If a projection band
B
in
X

admits a non-trivial
f\!
-algebra product, so does
X
.

Proof.

Suppose
B
were a projection band in
X
with a non-trivial

f\!
-algebra product
P
. Let
Q\colon X\to B
be the band projection
associated with
B
and define
\tilde{P}\colon X\times X\to X
by

\tilde{P}(x,y)=P(Qx,Qy)
. This is certainly a product on
X
. We
will be done once we show that
\tilde{P}
satisfies the
f\!
-algebra property. If
x\wedge y=0

in
X
, then
Qx\wedge Qy=0
, because
Q
is a lattice
homomorphism. Let
z\in X_{+}
. Since
P
is an
f\!
-algebra product,

P(Qz,Qx)\wedge Qy=0
. But also
P(Qz,Qx)\wedge(y-Qy)=0
, because

P(Qz,Qx)\in B
and
y-Qy\in B^{d}
. In conclusion,

\tilde{P}(z,x)\wedge y=P(Qz,Qx)\wedge Qy+P(Qz,Qx)\wedge(y-Qy)=0,

so
\tilde{P}
does satisfy the
f\!
-algebra property.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

If a vector lattice has an atom, then it has a non-trivial
f\!
-algebra product.

Proof.

Let
X
be a vector lattice and let
a\in X_{+}
be an atom. Then

\barespn\{a\}
is a projection band that admits a non-trivial
f\!
-algebra product (namely,
P(\lambda a,\mu a)=(\lambda\mu)a
for all

\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
). By
5.5
,
X

admits a non-trivial
f\!
-algebra product.
∎

As we already know, the converse of previous result is true for
AL-spaces, but not in general (it fails already in
C[0,1]
). In the
end, we were not able to find an analogue of
5.4

for AM-spaces.
