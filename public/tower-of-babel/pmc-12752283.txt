Robust subspace structure discovery for cell type identification in scRNA-seq data
Authors: Xianyong Zhou, Xindian Wei, Cheng Liu, Wenjun Shen, Ping Xuan, Si Wu, Hau-San Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has transformed gene expression studies by enabling analysis at the individual cell level, offering unprecedented insights into cellular heterogeneity. A key challenge in scRNA-seq data analysis is cell type identification, which requires grouping cells with similar gene expression profiles using unsupervised clustering methods. However, the high dimensionality, inherent noise, and significant sparsity of scRNA-seq data present substantial obstacles to accurately determining relationships among cell samples. To address these challenges, we propose a novel deep subspace clustering approach for cell type identification that captures a more reliable subspace structure from scRNA-seq data. Our method leverages a robust self-representation learning framework to effectively characterize and learn the underlying cluster structure. This framework is optimized through an integrated strategy combining a structure-guided approach with an optimal transport algorithm, enhancing the robustness of the subspace clustering process. By mitigating the effects of noise and sparsity in scRNA-seq data, this approach enables more accurate cell clustering. Experimental results on 18 real scRNA-seq datasets demonstrate that our method outperforms several state-of-the-art clustering approaches tailored for scRNA-seq data, excelling in both accuracy and interpretability.Keywords: Deep subspace clustering, ScRNA-seq data analysis, Cell type identification

Introduction

The development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has enabled study gene expression analysis at the single-cell level. This advanced technology provides new insights into biological mechanisms, such as tumor microenvironment analysis and personalized medicine [1]. A critical task in scRNA-seq data analysis is cell type identification, which involves grouping cells based on similar gene expression patterns. This can be effectively achieved through unsupervised clustering [2–6].

Several clustering methods have been proposed to identify cell types from scRNA-seq profiles [6–8]. A common approach involves a sequential process: first, dimensionality reduction of the gene expression matrix, followed by the application of traditional clustering methods such as k-means , hierarchical clustering and based on graphs. For instance, pcaReduce [9] combines principal component analysis (PCA) with hierarchical clustering clustering, producing cell identification results. Seurat [10] constructs a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph based on the principal components of the gene expression matrix to capture the similarity between cells and performs clustering using the Louvain algorithm [11]. Similarly, CIDR [12] addresses missing data through imputation before applying dimensionality reduction and k-means clustering for cell identification. In addition, some methods further enhance clustering performance by using ensemble strategies. For example, SC3 [13] combines results from multiple clustering algorithms to achieve more stable and consistent outcomes. Meanwhile, SIMLR [14] employs multiple kernel functions to capture diverse features and relationships within the data, leading to more accurate cell type identification through clustering.

Recently, leveraging the feature representation learning capabilities of deep learning, several deep clustering methods have been successfully applied to scRNA-seq data for cell identification [15–27]. For example, scAce [20] constructs a variational autoencoder to simultaneously learn cell embeddings and cluster assignments while developing an adaptive cluster merging approach, improving clustering results without needing to estimate the number of clusters in advance. SLNMF [21] employs a non-negative matrix factorization algorithm based on network structure learning to identify cell types in scRNA-seq data, transforming the task into a constrained optimization problem. Another example, ScCAEs [22], introduces a deep embedding clustering method based on a convolutional autoencoder and soft k-means clustering, addressing high dropout rates and noise by simultaneously learning feature representations and performing clustering. In addition to traditional embedding learning, several methods incorporate the structural relationships between cells using graph neural networks (GNNs) to capture more reliable representations for cell clustering. For instance, scDSC [23] combines a ZINB-based autoencoder, a graph neural network module, and a mutual-supervision module to effectively learn and cluster scRNA-seq data, capturing both the intrinsic properties of individual cells and their relationships. Similarly, scGAC [24] constructs a cell map, refines it through network denoising, learns a cluster-friendly representation using a graph attention autoencoder, and finally clusters cells using a self-optimization method. scGNN [25] integrates a graph convolutional network (GCN) into a multi-autoencoder framework to improve cell clustering. It constructs a cell graph using a feature autoencoder, refines cell representations through a GCN-based autoencoder, and applies k-means clustering, with iterative refinement of cell representations by cluster-specific autoencoders.

Accurately estimating cell relationships and obtaining robust feature representations are central challenges in cell clustering for cell type identification. Although several studies have demonstrated effectiveness in clustering tasks, most existing methods still face critical challenges due to the high dropout rates and noise inherent in scRNA-seq data. These intrinsic properties often result in unstable clustering outcomes, making cell type identification less reliable. To overcome these challenges, we introduce a novel deep subspace clustering framework specifically designed for cell type identification. Our method first employs a structure-guided learning strategy that mitigates the tendency of deep subspace clustering models to converge to suboptimal solutions, particularly when initialized poorly or when confronted with high levels of noise in the data. Additionally, we incorporate the Wasserstein distance into the self-representation framework, where it serves as a regularization term to enhance the quality of the self-representation. This self-representation is crucial for capturing relationships among samples and for effective clustering, ultimately guiding the overall model learning process. The Wasserstein-regularized self-representation learning is efficiently optimized using a standard optimal transport algorithm. Overall, by combining a structure-guided learning strategy with optimal transport-based regularization, our method significantly strengthens the subspace clustering framework, rendering it more robust to the challenges inherent in scRNA-seq data. Wasserstein self-representation learning

These diagrams can provide a clear visualization of how cells are classified by each method in relation to their actual identities.

Sankey plots of clustering results and true cell types for ours method and comparative methods. For each subplot, the left side represents the clustering labels generated by each method, while the right side represents the true cell types

Our proposed method exhibits superior accuracy in assigning cells to their correct types compared to existing approaches. Notably, several methods struggle to distinguish major cell types in complex datasets. For example, in the Zeisel dataset, certain techniques incorrectly group interneurons, S1 pyramidal cells, and CA1 pyramidal cells into a single cluster, failing to resolve these distinct populations. In contrast, our method successfully differentiates these cell types, demonstrating its robustness in managing intricate scRNA-seq datasets. Furthermore, our approach excels in detecting rare cell types with limited sample size, a critical aspect of comprehensive scRNA-seq data analysis. For instance, our method can accurately identify natural killer (NK) cells, smooth muscle cells characterized by elevated Acta2 expression, and dendritic cells marked by high Cd74 expression, among others in mBladder data. This ability to resolve rare populations enhances the biological insights derived from scRNA-seq data.Overall, the Sankey diagrams verify the accuracy and reliability of our method in cell type assignment, highlighting its advantages over existing clustering techniques.

Differential expression analysis

Differential gene expression (DGEs) is essential to understand the biological differences between two or more sample groups. To verify the superiority of our proposed method, we selected the ‘Human ESC’ dataset and further examined the ’Time Course‘ dataset to determine whether our method benefits downstream analysis. For ‘Human ESC’, there are seven different cell subpopulations, including neuronal progenitor cells (NPCs), definitive endoderm derivative cells (DEDs), endothelial cells (ECs), trophoblast-like cells (TBs), undifferentiated H1 and H9 ESCs, and foreskin fibroblasts (HFFs). In the ‘Time Course’ dataset, the cells are collected at six different time points (0, 12, 24, 36, 72, and 96 h) during the developmental trajectory of H9 ESCs differentiating towards DEDs.

For each method, we select the top 50 DEGs of each cluster and compare their overlap to find out whether we can annotate every cluster correctly to a known cell type in the golden standard. (Top 50 DEGs in different cell types in golden standard using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test can be seen in Fig. 9). The similarity calculated in Fig. 8 equals the number of overlapped DEGs divided by 50. Each column represents a cluster acquired by a specific method, while each row represents a known cell type regarded as the golden standard. The higher heatmap value shows that it holds the highest similarity in DGEs.

Marker gene analysis in the “Human ESC” dataset. Ranking of genes in different cell types using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test

Overlap of top 50 DEGs in clusters detected by our methods, scMAE and scNAME with gold standard cell types. A Marker gene analysis in the ‘Human ESC’ dataset. B Marker gene analysis in the ‘Time Course’ dataset

As shown in Fig. 8, our method accurately maps each cell type to a distinct cluster in both datasets. Specifically, it achieves similarity scores of 0.9 for most clusters, successfully distinguishing even closely related H1 and H9 ESCs, which are assigned to clusters 0 and 4, respectively. Additionally, in the ‘Time Course’ dataset, our method clearly delineates H9 ESCs across their developmental stages, with each time point corresponding to a unique cluster. These results demonstrate that our approach reliably annotates clusters to specific cell types based on highly similar gene expression profiles.

In contrast, the second-best method, scMAE, exhibits notable limitations. For the ‘Human ESC’ dataset, scMAE assigns both H1 and H9 ESCs to cluster 3 and fails to assign any cell type to cluster 6. In the ‘Time Course’ dataset, scMAE merges time points 12 and 24 into cluster 1 and leaves another cluster without a corresponding cell type. Similarly, scNAME struggles to differentiate H1 and H9 ESCs, grouping both into cluster 0, and in the developmental case, it conflates multiple time points into fewer clusters. These shortcomings highlight that only our method consistently and accurately annotates each cluster to a known cell type across both datasets.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduce a novel deep subspace clustering approach for cell type identification in scRNA-seq data. Our method addresses the challenges posed by noise and sparsity inherent in scRNA-seq datasets by employing a robust self-representation learning framework. This framework effectively captures and learns the underlying cluster structure, enabling precise cell type delineation. To optimize this process, we integrate a structure-guided strategy with an optimal transport algorithm, which collectively enhance the robustness of the subspace clustering framework. Consequently, our approach achieves more accurate and reliable cell clustering outcomes. Experimental evaluations across a diverse set of real scRNA-seq datasets demonstrate that our method outperforms several state-of-the-art clustering techniques in both clustering accuracy and interpretability. Additionally, comprehensive model and biological analyses validate the efficacy of our approach in accurately annotating cell types and revealing biologically meaningful insights.
