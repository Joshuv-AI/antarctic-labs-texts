The Time-Periodic Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin System on the Half Space as a Mixed-Order System with General Boundary Conditions
Guillaume Neuttiens, Jonas Sauer

Abstract
. A well-posedness and maximal regularity result for the time-periodic Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system in the half space is proved. For this purpose, we introduce a novel class of complementing boundary conditions, extending the classical Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions from elliptic and parabolic theory to time-periodic mixed-order systems with general boundary conditions. Moreover, we show that the classical Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions are in general insufficient for well-posedness of mixed-order systems.

35B65, 35G45, 35M32, 82C26

1.
Introduction

This paper develops a comprehensive
L^{2}
theory for the time-periodic Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system on the half space.
We consider the system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta u_{2}&=&f_{1}&\text{in }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
\Delta u_{1}-\partial_{t}u_{1}+u_{2}&=&f_{2}&\text{in }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
\partial_{n}u_{1}|_{\{x_{n}=0\}}&=&g_{1}&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\\
\partial_{n}u_{2}|_{\{x_{n}=0\}}&=&g_{2}&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\end{array}\right.

where all functions are periodic in time with period
T>0
, and accordingly the time variable lives in the torus
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}
.
Such systems were introduced in
[
15
]
and arise naturally in the study of phase separation processes and interface dynamics when microscopic relaxation effects are taken into account.
The model augments the classical Cahn–Hilliard equation by coupling it to an additional evolution equation for the chemical potential, which leads to a symbol that is neither homogeneous nor quasi-homogeneous.
This prevents the use of classical elliptic or parabolic theory and makes the analysis considerably more delicate.
Wilke
[
25
]
has studied the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system in the initial value setting, providing a maximal
L^{p}
regularity result in the whole space, the half space and on sufficiently smooth bounded domains.

The present work provides the first maximal regularity result for system (
1
) in the time periodic setting.
Our approach reveals that the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system is a representative example of a broader class of mixed order systems with general boundary conditions.
These systems are characterised by the presence of several competing scaling behaviours in their symbols, a phenomenon that can be captured in a precise geometric way by means of Newton polygons.
Building on the general symbolic framework developed in
[
8
,
9
,
11
,
14
]
, we introduce a refined theory of potential spaces that is adapted to boundary value problems in the time-periodic context and that reflects the mixed order structure of the system.
The first main result asserts that the operator associated with system (
1
) is an isomorphism between natural Newton polygon spaces for the solution and the data.
We refer to the next section for notational conventions and the definition of the involved function spaces, but already mention that
\overline{H}^{(s,r)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
is the restriction to the half space of a purely oscillatory Sobolev space of mixed smoothness monitoring independently the
L^{2}
-norm of up to
s
derivatives in time and
r
derivatives in space, and
H^{(s,r)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
is its counterpart on the boundary
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the solution space
\mathbb{E}:=\mathbb{E}_{1}\times\mathbb{E}_{2}
, the data space
\mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{F}_{1}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and the trace space
\mathbb{G}:=\mathbb{G}_{1}\times\mathbb{G}_{2}
, given by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\mathbb{E}_{1}&:=&\overline{H}^{(1,1)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\cap\overline{H}^{(0,3)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),&\mathbb{E}_{2}&:=&\overline{H}^{(0,2)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),\\
\mathbb{F}_{1}&:=&L^{2}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),&\mathbb{F}_{2}&:=&\overline{H}^{(0,1)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),\\
\mathbb{G}_{1}&:=&H_{\perp}^{(\frac{3}{4},0)}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1})\cap H^{(0,\frac{3}{2})}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}),&\mathbb{G}_{2}&:=&H^{(0,\frac{1}{2})}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}).\end{array}

For all
f=(f_{1},f_{2})\in\mathbb{F}
and
g=(g_{1},g_{2})\in\mathbb{G}
, system (
1
) admits a unique solution
u=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{E}
, and it holds

\|u\|_{\mathbb{E}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathbb{F}}+\|g\|_{\mathbb{G}},

where the implicit constant depends only on the time period
T
and the dimension
n
.

We emphasize that Theorem
1.1
indeed constitutes a maximal regularity result in the time-periodic framework: If we introduce
S:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{F}\times\mathbb{G}
via

\displaystyle S(u_{1},u_{2})=(\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta u_{2},\Delta u_{1}-\partial_{t}u_{1}+u_{2},\partial_{n}u_{1}|_{\{x_{n}=0\}},\partial_{n}u_{2}|_{\{x_{n}=0\}}),

then Theorem
1.1
shows that
S
is an isomorphism of Banach spaces.

A central idea of our approach is a factorization of the determinant into two operators that preserve the support in the lower and upper half space, respectively.
This separates the analysis into a factor that is inverted without prescribing traces, and a factor for which solvability requires boundary traces of the appropriate order. In this way, the factorization isolates the part of the symbol that is responsible for boundary conditions.
The idea of using the Paley-Wiener theorem for such a factorization was first introduced for elliptic scalar equations in
[
4
]
and can also be found in
[
23
, Section 5.3]
.
In
[
18
,
19
]
, it was shown that this approach is also applicable for (time-periodic) parabolic scalar equations.
A core contribution of the present paper is to demonstrate that the factorization method can be adapted to treat general boundary value problems on the half space which are both of mixed-order and vector-valued.
In addition, we develop precise trace results for Newton polygon spaces.
Together, these tools allow us to identify precise algebraic obstructions that prevent certain boundary operators from yielding well-posedness on the half space.

The celebrated results by Agmon-Douglis-Nirenberg
[
1
,
2
]
show that a cornerstone of the theory of well-posedness for elliptic problems with boundaries are the
Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions
, also known as Agmon complementing conditions.
For parabolic equations, the Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions continue to be sufficient for a comprehensive well-posedness theory in the
L^{p}
framework as shown in
[
7
]
for the initial value problem and in
[
19
]
for the time-periodic case.
Let us briefly summarize the idea behind the Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions:
Consider a differential operator
\mathsf{op}[P]
whose symbol
P:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is a polynomial, and assume that for every
(\tau,\xi^{\prime})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, the complex polynomial
z\to P(\tau,\xi^{\prime},z)
is of order
2N
and has no real roots.
Then under appropriate ellipticity or parabolicity conditions one can write
P=P^{+}P^{-}
, where
P^{+}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},\cdot)
has all its
N
roots in the upper half plane and
P^{-}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},\cdot)
has all
N
roots in the lower half plane.
The Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro condition requires that the boundary operators be independent modulo the factor
P^{+}
.
In particular, Dirichlet type conditions prescribing
u|_{x_{n}=0},\partial_{n}u|_{x_{n}=0},\ldots,\partial_{n}^{N-1}u|_{x_{n}=0}
provide a basic class of boundary operators covered by this framework.
For parabolic operators, these conditions are sufficient and often necessary to guarantee the existence of a unique solution in the maximal regularity space.
For operators of mixed order however, the picture turns out to be more subtle.
Our second main theorem shows the insufficiency of the Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions for the operator

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}:=\partial_{t}-\Delta(\partial_{t}-\Delta).

This operator is closely related to the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system (
1
).
Indeed,
\mathsf{D}
can be understood as the determinant of the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system, and it will be paramount in our analysis that it
does
provide a well-posedness theory on both the whole space and the half space if endowed with appropriate boundary conditions, see Example
2.13
and Corollary
4.21
.
However, for pure Dirichlet conditions, Theorem
1.2
shows that solvability is governed not by the boundary data alone, but by a compatibility condition involving the inverse of the factor
D^{-}
corresponding to the roots of
D(\tau,\xi^{\prime},\cdot)
in the lower half plane.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
.
Consider the spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle:=\overline{H}^{(1,2)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})\cap\overline{H}^{(0,4)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}

\displaystyle:=H^{(\frac{3}{4},0)}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1})\cap H^{(0,\frac{3}{2})}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}).

The Dirichlet problem

(5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\partial_{t}u-\Delta(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u&=&f&\text{in }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\\
u|_{x_{n}=0}&=&0&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1},\\
\partial_{n}u|_{x_{n}=0}&=&0&\text{on }\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\end{array}\right.

admits a solution
u\in\mathbb{E}
if and only if
(\mathsf{op}[D^{-}]^{-1}_{+}f)|_{x_{n}=0}\in\mathbb{G}
.
In particular, there exists
f\in L^{2}_{\perp}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
such that (
5
) does not admit a solution in
\mathbb{E}
.

Here,
\mathsf{op}[m]
stands for the Fourier multiplier operator with multiplier
m
, while the notation
\mathsf{A}_{+}
is related to the half space and introduced in Definition
4.7
below.
Theorem
1.2
demonstrates that an algebraic
complementing boundary condition
adapted to Newton polygon geometry must be strictly stronger than the Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions in order to guarantee well-posedness for symbols with non-triangular Newton polygons.
We will provide precisely such a complementing boundary condition in Definition
4.19
by demanding an appropriately extended boundary matrix containing the information of
D^{+}
and the boundary operators to be a mixed order system on the boundary
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
The stronger nature of our condition compared to Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro is captured by a clear algebraic formulation, see Proposition
5.1
.
Namely, we find that the Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions are equivalent to the invertibility of the extended boundary matrix, which is strictly weaker than being a mixed order system.

To keep the presentation streamlined, we restrict ourselves to the cases required for (
1
).
In particular, we show the trace theorem (see Theorem
4.5
) only for Newton polygons with four vertices
\{(0,0),(r_{1},0),(r_{2},s_{2}),(0,s_{2})\}
.
Moreover, we only study the
\mathcal{N}
-ellipticity of the roots of the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin determinant, not of roots corresponding to general
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic symbols which are polynomial in the variable
\xi_{n}
.
Despite these restrictions, the underlying arguments apply more broadly, and we expect that the general case can be developed within the framework introduced here.
We postpone this to future work.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
Section
2
recalls the theory of Newton polygons and order functions, and introduces the Newton polygon potential spaces adapted to the time–periodic setting.
Section
3
establishes maximal regularity for mixed-order systems on the whole space, with the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system serving as the guiding example.
Section
4
develops the boundary analysis on the half space, including support preserving operators and trace theorems for Newton polygon spaces.
Finally, Section
5
applies the abstract theory to the system (
1
) and presents the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

2.1.
Newton Polygons and Order Functions

As mentioned in the introduction, the fact that the symbol

(7)

D(\tau,\xi)=\mathsf{i}\tau+|\xi|(\mathsf{i}\tau+|\xi|),\qquad(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

associated to the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin determinant is neither homogeneous nor quasi-homogeneous presents major difficulties since the usual methods related to elliptic or parabolic equations often rely on the study of homogeneities.
To tackle this problem, the notion of a Newton polygon has been introduced, see
[
8
,
9
]
.
This theory allows to treat symbols presenting themselves as a sum of products of terms with various homogeneities.
Here we recall only the results we need for our problem and refer to
[
8

for an in-depth exposition of the theory.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
E=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),...,(x_{M},y_{M})\}\subseteq[0,\infty)^{2}
be a finite set. The Newton polygon of
E
, denoted
\mathcal{N}(E)
is the convex envelope of the elements of
E
and of their projections on the coordinate axis

\mathcal{N}(E):=\conv\left(E\cup\bigcup_{m=1}^{M}\{(x_{m},0),(0,y_{m})\}\cup\{(0,0\}\right).

Throughout this section we consider a polynomial

(8)

P(\tau,\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\\
|\alpha|\leq N\end{subarray}}p_{i,\alpha}\tau^{i}\xi^{\alpha},\qquad(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The Newton polygon of
P
is a geometric representation of the degree of the monomials occuring in the multivariate polynomial
P(\tau,\xi)
.
It is constructed by considering the set of the couples
(i,\alpha)
associated to the degree of each non-zero monomial.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
P
be as in (
8
) and define
E(P):=\{(i,|\alpha|)\mid p_{i,\alpha}\neq 0\}
. Then the Newton polygon associated to
P
is
\mathcal{N}(P):=\mathcal{N}(E(P))
.

Given some finite set
E\subseteq[0,\infty)
and
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}(E)
its Newton polygon, we denote

(9)

\mathcal{N}_{V}:=\{(r_{0},s_{0}),...,(r_{J+1},s_{J+1})\}

the set of its vertices, ordered counter-clockwise starting from
(r_{0},s_{0}):=(0,0)
. Remark that by definition,
r_{1}
is the degree of the polynomial
P(\tau,\xi)
in the variable
\xi
while
s_{J+1}
is the degree of the polynomial in the variable
\tau
.
We say that
\mathcal{N}
is

r_{2}\neq r_{1}
.

As mentioned above, the concept of the Newton polygon aims at providing a geometric description of the degree of multivariate polynomials
P(\tau,\xi)
. As such, one would expect the Newton polygon of a product to be related to the ’sum’ of the Newton polygons of each factor. However, the notion of sum of polygons is not completely intuitive. Instead, it is often much easier to introduce the notion of order functions.

Definition 2.5
.

An
order function
is a function
\mu:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
that is continuous and piecewise linear. For such a function, there exists
J\in\mathbb{N}
,
0=:\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<...<\gamma_{J}\leq\gamma_{J+1}:=\infty
and
b_{1},...,b_{J+1},m_{1},...,m_{J+1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(11)

\mu(\gamma)=b_{j}+\gamma m_{j}\qquad\forall\gamma\in[\gamma_{j-1},\gamma_{j}),\quad j\in\{1,...,J+1\}.

If
\mathcal{N}
is a Newton polygon with vertices
\mathcal{N}_{V}
ordered as in (
9
), the associated order function is the order function
\mu_{\mathcal{N}}
defined by

(12)

\mu_{\mathcal{N}}(\gamma):=\max\{r_{j}+\gamma s_{j}\mid j\in\{1,\ldots,J+1\}\}.

Due to the convexity of the Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}
, one can show (see
[
8
]
Remark 2.20 ) that the function
\mu
is always convex and positive. An order function presenting itself as (
12
) with
r_{j}\geq 0,s_{j}\geq 0
for all
j=0,...,J+1
is called
strictly positive order function
. By extension, if an order function
\nu
is such that
-\nu
is strictly positive, we say that
\nu
is a
strictly negative order function
.

For
j\in\{1,...,J\}
, we define

(13)

\gamma_{j}:=\frac{r_{j}-r_{j+1}}{s_{j+1}-s_{j}},

where we understand
\gamma_{J}=\infty
if
s_{J}=s_{J+1}
. Geometrically,
\gamma_{j}
is the slope of the normal vector to the vertex
[(r_{j},s_{j})(r_{j+1},s_{j+1})]
. We also set
\gamma_{0}:=0
and
\gamma_{J+1}=\infty
. By convexity of the Newton polygon, one obtains

0=\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<...<\gamma_{J}\leq\gamma_{J+1}=\infty
. Remark that
\mathcal{N}
is regular in time if and only if
\gamma_{1}>0
.

We get the following description of the order function
\mu
. See
[
8
]

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be a Newton polygon and let
\gamma_{0},...,\gamma_{J+1}
be defined as above.
Then for
j\in\{1,...,J+1\}
and
\gamma\in[\gamma_{j-1},\gamma_{j})

\mu_{\mathcal{N}}(\gamma)=\gamma s_{j}+r_{j}.

Conversely, let
\mu
be a strictly positive order function. By definition, there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta_{0},\delta_{1},...,\delta_{K+1}
with
0=:\delta_{0}\leq\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<...<\delta_{K}\leq\delta_{K+1}:=\infty
and
b_{1},...,b_{K+1}\geq 0,m_{1},...,m_{K+1}\geq 0
such that
\mu(\gamma)=b_{k}+\gamma m_{k}
for all
\gamma\in[\delta_{k-1},\delta_{k})
.

To the strictly positive order function above, we associate the set

E(\mu):=\{(0,0)\}\cup\{(b_{k},m_{k}),(b_{k},0),(m_{k},0)\mid k=1,...K+1\}.

The Newton polygon associated to
\mu

\mathcal{N}(\mu):=\conv(E(\mu))
. One easily sees that there is a one-to-one correspondence between Newton polygons and order functions in the sense that
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathcal{N}})=\mathcal{N}
for any Newton polygon

and
\mu_{\mathcal{N}(\mu)}=\mu
for any strictly positive order function
\mu

2.5
allows us to consider also order functions that are not strictly positive, i.e. that are not associated to any Newton polygon.

For any strictly positive order function
\mu

\mathcal{N}(\mu)
with a set of vertices

\mathcal{N}(\mu)_{V}=\{(r_{0},s_{0}),...,(r_{J+1},s_{J+1})\}
, we define the
order
of
\mu
by
\ord\mu:=r_{1}
.

We now introduce the concept of weight functions. For a symbol
p(\xi)
depending only on
\xi

|p|\geq C|\xi|^{d}
with
d

P(\tau,\xi)

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\mu

2.5

(i)

If
\mu
is a strictly positive order function with associated Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}(\mu)
and its set of vertices
\mathcal{N}(\mu)_{V}:=\{(r_{0},s_{0}),(r_{1},s_{1}),...(r_{J+1},s_{J+1})\}
ordered counter-clockwise starting from
(r_{0},s_{0})=(0,0)
.
The
weight function
associated to
\mu
is
W_{\mu}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

W_{\mu}(\tau,\xi):=1+\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}|\tau|^{s_{j}}|\xi|^{r_{j}},\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R},\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(ii)

If
\mu
is a strictly negative order function, then by definition
-\mu
is strictly positive and we set

W_{\mu}(\tau,\xi):=\frac{1}{W_{-\mu}(\tau,\xi)}.

The vertices of a Newton polygon can be thought of as the points of ’maximal weight’, as explained in the following Lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{1}
be a Newton polygon and
\mu_{1}:=\mu(\mathcal{N}_{1})
the associated order function. Then
(r,s)\in\mathcal{N}_{1}
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds

|\tau|^{s}|\xi|^{r}\leq CW_{\mu_{1}}(\tau,\xi).

In particular, if
\mathcal{N}_{2}
is another Newton polygon such that
\mathcal{N}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{2}
and
\mu_{2}:=\mu(\mathcal{N}_{2})
is its order function, then there exists
C>0
such that
W_{\mu_{1}}\leq CW_{\mu_{2}}
.

Proof.

See
[
8
, Remark 2.17]
.
∎

Since the purpose of order functions is to compute easily sums and differences of Newton polygons, we are also interested in how the weight functions behave with respect to sums and differences of order functions. We have the following property.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
\mu,\nu
are both strictly positive order functions or both strictly negative order functions and
\alpha,\beta>0
, then
W_{\alpha\mu+\beta\nu}\simeq W_{\mu}^{\alpha}W_{\nu}^{\beta}
.

Proof.

See
[
8
, Lemma 2.33]
for
\alpha=\beta=1
.
The general case follows from this, since
W_{\alpha\mu}\simeq W_{\mu}^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
by Lemma
2.8
.
∎

In view of the above lemma, one can also define weight functions for the difference of order functions in the following way.
Let
\mu,\nu
be two strictly positive order functions.
We set

W_{\mu-\nu}:\simeq\frac{W_{\mu}}{W_{\nu}}.

Here
W_{\mu-\nu}
is well-defined up to the equivalence
\simeq
. Indeed, let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
be strictly positive order functions such that
\mu_{1}-\nu_{1}=\mu_{2}-\nu_{2}
. Then
\mu_{1}+\nu_{2}=\mu_{2}+\nu_{1}
are both strictly positive order functions and by the above lemma,
W_{\mu_{1}}W_{\nu_{2}}\simeq W_{\mu_{2}}W_{\nu_{1}}
.

The weight function associated to a strictly positive order function can be decomposed as a product of ’elementary’ weight functions using the slopes of the edges of the associated Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}(\mu)
. This will be particularly useful in the last sections for practical computations.
More precisely, for any
y\geq 0
, we define the elementary order functions
o_{y}(\gamma):=\max\{1,y\gamma\},\gamma>0
.
In particular, for
(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
it holds

W_{o_{y}}(\tau,\xi)=1+|\xi|+|\tau|^{y}

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\mu
be a strictly positive order function,
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}(\mu)
the associated Newton polygon and
\mathcal{N}_{V}=\{(r_{0},s_{0}),...,(r_{J+1},s_{J+1})\}
the set of its vertices. Define
\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{J+1}
as in (
13
) and assume that
\mathcal{N}
is regular in time, that is,
\gamma_{1}>0
. Then

W_{\mu}\simeq\prod_{j=1}^{J}W_{o_{1/\gamma_{j}}}^{r_{j}-r_{j+1}},

where we understand
\frac{1}{\gamma_{J}}=0
if
\gamma_{J}=\infty
.

Proof.

See Chapter I, Subsection 1.3 in
[
14
]
.
∎

Example 2.11
.

We continue the discussion for the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin determinant from Example

Its order function

We do not restrict ourselves to polynomials in the following definition, which will be of importance in Section

Definition 2.12
.

Let

be smooth and let
\mu
be the difference of strictly positive order functions.
We say that
\mu
is an

for

if there exists

such that

for all

.

We observe that if

is an upper order function for

and
\mu_{2}
is an upper order function for

.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
P:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be smooth and let
\mu
be the difference of strictly positive order functions.
We say that
P
is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function

if

(i)

\mu
is an upper order function for

.

(ii)

.

As above, the notion of

is

-elliptic for the order function

{\mathcal{N}}
-elliptic for the order function
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}
.
We immediately show that the CHG determinant is

-elliptic.

Proposition 2.14
.

The symbol

-elliptic for the associated order function
\mu_{D}

.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from

Recall that

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|D(\tau,\xi)|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|\tau|^{2}+2|\xi|^{2}|\tau|^{2}+|\xi|^{4}|\tau|^{2}+|\xi|^{8}}\geq\sqrt{|\tau|^{2}+|\xi|^{4}|\tau|^{2}+|\xi|^{8}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(|\tau|+|\xi|^{2}|\tau|+|\xi|^{4}).

Let

2.2.
Mixed-Order Systems

The notion of mixed-order systems has been introduced in
[
2
]
in order to study the regularity of solutions of systems of elliptic equations. The definition was later adapted to the Newton polygon setting in
[
8
]
.
In the following, we consider a matrix-valued symbol
L:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\mathcal{D}(\tau,\xi):=\det L(\tau,\xi)
its determinant.

Definition 2.15
.

The symbol
L
is associated to a
mixed-order system
if there exist strictly positive order functions
-s_{1},...,-s_{m},t_{1},...,t_{m}
such that the following is true.

(i)

The order function
s_{i}+t_{j}
is an upper order function of the symbol
L_{ij}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
for all
i,j\in\{1,...,m\}
.

(ii)

The determinant
\mathcal{D}
is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function
\delta:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}s_{k}+t_{k}
.

While solving systems, we will make a regular use of the adjugate symbol of
L
, denoted
\ad L
. Here

(\ad L)_{ij}:=\det L^{(ji)},\qquad i,j\in\{1,..,m\},

where
L^{(ji)}
denotes the
(m-1)\times(m-1)
matrix obtained by removing the
j
-th line and the
i
-th column of
L
.

Proposition 2.16
.

If
L:\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is a mixed-order system for the order functions
s_{1},...,s_{m}
,
t_{1},...,t_{m}
then
\ad L
is a mixed-order system for the order functions
S_{1},...,S_{m}
, and
T_{1},...,T_{m}
, with

S_{i}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{m}s_{k},\qquad T_{j}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}t_{k},\qquad i,j\in\{1,...,m\}.

Proof.

See part (I) of the proof of Theorem 2.69 in
[
8
]
.
∎

Example 2.17
.

One can give the CHG system

(14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\partial_{t}u_{1}-\Delta u_{2}&=&f_{1},\\
\Delta u_{1}-\partial_{t}u_{1}+u_{2}&=&f_{2},\end{array}\right.

a structure of mixed-order system. Indeed, the symbol matrix associated to the problem is

(15)

L(\tau,\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{i}\tau&|\xi|^{2}\\
-|\xi|^{2}-\mathsf{i}\tau&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad(\tau,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Defining the order functions

\displaystyle t_{1}(\gamma):=\max\{3,1+\gamma\},\quad t_{2}(\gamma):=2,\quad s_{1}(\gamma):=0,\quad s_{2}(\gamma):=-1,

we obtain that
s_{i}+t_{j}
is an upper order function for the symbol
L_{ij}
for all
i,j\in\{1,2\}
.
Furthermore, one has

\delta(\gamma):=(s_{1}+s_{2}+t_{1}+t_{2})(\gamma)=\max\{4,2+\gamma\}=\mu_{D}(\gamma)

with
D(\tau,\xi)=\det L(\tau,\xi)
.
Since we established in Proposition
2.14
that the determinant

is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function
\mu_{D}
, we indeed have a mixed-order system.

2.3.
Time-Periodic Setting and Purely Oscillatory Function Spaces

We start by recalling the time-periodic setting as introduced e.g. in
[
12
,
18
,
19
]
.
Any time-periodic function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
of period
T>0
can be considered as a function defined on the torus
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/T\mathbb{Z}
. Thus our aim is to solve (
14
) for some functions
u_{1},u_{2},f_{1},f_{2}
defined on
G:=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The differential structure on
G
is the one inherited from
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
through the quotient projection
\pi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to G
, that is,

C^{\infty}(G)=\{f:G\to\mathbb{C}\mid f\circ\pi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

and for all
f\in C^{\infty}(G)
,
k\in\mathbb{N},\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
,

(\partial_{t}^{k}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f)(\pi(t,x)):=\partial_{t}^{k}\partial^{\alpha}_{x}(f\circ\pi)(t,x).

For
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define
C^{\infty}_{K}(G):=\{f\in C^{\infty}(G)\mid\supp f\subseteq\mathbb{T}\times K\}
, which is turned into a Fréchet space in the usual way via the seminorms

\displaystyle\rho_{m,K}(f):=\sup\{\sum_{k+|\alpha|\leq m}|\partial_{t}^{k}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f(t,x)|\mid(t,x)\in\mathbb{T}\times K\}.

Fix an exhaustive sequence
K_{1}\subseteq K_{2}\ldots\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of compact sets.
Then we can define the test function space
C^{\infty}_{c}(G):=\bigcup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}C^{\infty}_{K_{j}}(G)
as endowed with the canonical LF topology, and introduce the set of distributions
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(G)
as the strong dual of
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
.
Since derivatives are continuous operators on
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
, the notion of distributional derivatives can be introduced by transposition in the usual way.
Integration on
G
is done with respect to the product of the Haar measure on
\mathbb{T}
and of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In particular, it holds
f\in L^{2}(G)
if and only if
f\circ\pi_{|[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\in L^{2}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Once the weak derivatives and integration are defined, one can consider the usual Sobolev spaces. In particular, we are interested in distinguishing the regularity in the variables
t
and
x
. We thus introduce for
s,r\in\mathbb{N}
the Sobolev space of dominating mixed smoothness

(16)

H^{(s,r)}(G):=\{f\in L^{2}(G)\mid\partial_{t}^{s}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f,\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f,\partial_{t}^{s}f\in L^{2}(G)\text{ for all }\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\text{ with }|\alpha|=r\}.

2.3.1.
Fourier Transform on
G

The space
G
defined above is a locally compact abelian group and thus admits a Pontryagin dual

\widehat{G}:=\frac{2\pi}{T}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

where one identifies
\widehat{G}
by associating to any
(k,\xi)\in\frac{2\pi}{T}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
the character
\chi:G\to\mathbb{C}
,
\chi(t,x):=\mathrm{e}^{\mathsf{i}x\cdot\xi+\mathsf{i}tk}
, see e.g.
[
20
, Section 1.2]
.
The measure on
\widehat{G}
will be the product of the counting measure on
\frac{2\pi}{T}\mathbb{Z}
and the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The differentiable structure on
\widehat{G}
is defined as

C^{\infty}(\widehat{G}):=\{h\in C(\widehat{G})\mid h(k,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{Z}\}

where for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\widehat{G}),(k,\xi)\in\widehat{G}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we set

\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f(k,\xi):=(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f(k,\cdot))(\xi).

In a usual fashion, we define the Schwartz-Bruhat space on
G
by

\mathcal{S}(G):=\{f\in C^{\infty}(G):\rho_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(f)<\infty\text{ for all }(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\},

where the topology is induced by the seminorms

\rho_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(f):=\sup\{|x^{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}^{\beta}f(t,x)|\mid(t,x)\in G\}.

Similarly, we introduce

\mathcal{S}(\widehat{G}):=\{h\in C^{\infty}(\widehat{G}):\hat{\rho}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(h)<\infty\text{ for all }(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}\},

with the seminorms

\hat{\rho}_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma}(h):=\sup\{|k^{\alpha}\xi^{\gamma}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}h(k,\xi)|\mid(k,\xi)\in\widehat{G}\}.

The tempered distribution spaces
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\widehat{G})
) are then introduced as the strong dual of
\mathcal{S}(G)
and
\mathcal{S}(\widehat{G})
, respectively.

Being a locally compact abelian group,
G
admits a Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{G}:L^{1}(G)\to C(\widehat{G})
defined by

\mathcal{F}_{G}f(k,\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(t,x)\exp(-\mathsf{i}x\cdot\xi-\mathsf{i}tk)dxdt,\qquad(k,\xi)\in\widehat{G}.

and similarly,
\widehat{G}
admits a Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{G}}:L^{1}(\widehat{G})\to C(G)

\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{G}}h(t,x):=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(k,\xi)\exp(-\mathsf{i}x\cdot\xi-\mathsf{i}tk)d\xi,\qquad(t,x)\in G.

It holds
\mathcal{F}_{G}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}({\mathcal{S}}(G),{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{G}))
and
\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{G}}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}({\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{G}),{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{\widehat{G}}))
, see
[
24
, Theorem 3.2]
.
By transposition, the Fourier transform defines an isomorphism
{\mathcal{F}}_{G}:={}^{t}\mathcal{F}_{\widehat{G}}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G),\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\widehat{G}))
.
We will often write
\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{F}_{G}
.

Armed with the Fourier transform on
G
, we can define Fourier multipliers in a usual fashion.
We denote by
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
the set of symbols
m\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
m
and all its derivatives are polynomially bounded.
We will also set

\mathcal{O}(\widehat{G}):=\{m_{|\widehat{G}}\mid m\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

Then for
m\in\mathcal{O}(\widehat{G})
, the multiplication
M_{m}:{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{G})\to{\mathcal{S}}(\widehat{G})
,
M_{m}(\varphi):=m\varphi
is linear and bounded, and we may write
mT:={}^{t}M_{m}(T)
for
T\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G)
.
Then for
m\in\mathcal{O}(\widehat{G})
, we define
\mathsf{op}[m]\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G),\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G))
via

(17)

\mathsf{op}[m]f:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}m\mathcal{F}f.

The definition implies in particular that
\mathsf{op}[m_{1}]\mathsf{op}[m_{2}]=\mathsf{op}[m_{1}m_{2}]=\mathsf{op}[m_{2}]\mathsf{op}[m_{1}]
for all
m_{1},m_{2}\in{\mathcal{O}}(\widehat{G})
.
A remark on the situation in
L^{2}(G)
is in order:
Since the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
restricted to
L^{2}(G)
to an isometric isomorphism
\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(L^{2}(G),L^{2}(\widehat{G}))
by
[
20
, Theorem 1.6.1]
, the definition in (
17
) stays well-defined on
L^{2}(G)
if
m\in L^{\infty}(\widehat{G})
, in which case one has
\mathsf{op}[m]\in{\mathcal{L}}(L^{2}(G),L^{2}(\widehat{G}))
.

2.3.2.
Purely Oscillatory Spaces

As explained, for example, in
[
18
]
, a way to deal with time-periodic problems is to decompose them into one time-independent problem and one time-dependent problem, called ’purely oscillatory’, where one can ignore the singularities of our multipliers at the origin
k=0
.
We introduce the time-averaging projection
\mathcal{P}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{D}(G))
and
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{D}(G))
by

\mathcal{P}f(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f(s,x)ds,\qquad\mathcal{P}_{\perp}=\id_{G}-\mathcal{P}.

Note that
\mathcal{P}
projects onto time-independent functions, while
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}
removes the temporal mean.
It is clear that
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}
are complementary continuous projections, and we continue to write
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}
for their transposes
{}^{t}\mathcal{P},{}^{t}\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G))
.
For a locally convex space
E(G)\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
, we will write
E_{\perp}(G):=\mathcal{P}_{\perp}E(G)
.
Introducing
\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}:\frac{2\pi}{T}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}(k):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if }k=0,\\
0&\text{if }k\in\frac{2\pi}{T}\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},\end{array}\right.

we remark
\mathcal{P}=\mathsf{op}[\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}]
and
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}=\mathsf{op}[1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}]
as operators on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)
.

The main point of those projections is to decompose differential equations. Given an equation of the form
\mathsf{op}[m]u=f
for some
u,f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)
and
m\in\mathcal{O}(\hat{G})
, one has

\mathsf{op}[m]f=u\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{P}\mathsf{op}[m]u&=&\mathcal{P}f,\\
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}\mathsf{op}[m]u&=&\mathcal{P}_{\perp}f,\end{array}\right.\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsf{op}[\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}m]u&=&\mathcal{P}f,\\
\mathsf{op}[(1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}})m]u&=&\mathcal{P}_{\perp}f.\end{array}\right.

By definition of
\mathcal{P}
, the line associated to
\mathcal{P}
does not depend anymore on the time and often falls into the scope of classical elliptic theory.
We will thus focus mainly on the part associated to
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}
, often called
purely oscillatory
problem. In Section 3, we will see that reducing time-periodic problem to the purely oscillatory setting allows us to consider operators
\mathsf{op}[m]
for which the symbol
m
may exhibit singularities in
k=0
.

Now consider a cut-off function
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mathbbm{1}_{[-\frac{\pi}{T},\frac{\pi}{T}]}\leq 1-\eta\leq\mathbbm{1}_{[-\frac{2\pi}{T},\frac{2\pi}{T}]}
.
In particular

(18)

\eta(\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }|\tau|\leq\frac{\pi}{T}\\
1&\text{if }|\tau|\geq\frac{2\pi}{T}.\end{array}\right.

Then
\eta_{|\frac{2\pi}{T}\mathbb{Z}}=1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We set

\mathcal{O}^{\perp}(\widehat{G}):=\{m_{|\widehat{G}}:m\in C^{\infty}((\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\text{ with }\eta m\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

For the sake of shortness, if
m\in C^{\infty}((\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
is such that
\eta m\in\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we will write
\widehat{m}:=m_{|\widehat{G}}
every time it is necessary to distinguish the symbol from its restriction to
\widehat{G}
.
For
\widehat{m}\in\mathcal{O}^{\perp}(\widehat{G})
, we set

(19)

\mathsf{op}[m]:=\mathsf{op}[(1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}})\widehat{m}]:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\perp}(G)\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\perp}(G).

Observe that this definition coincides with (
17
) if
\widehat{m}\in\mathcal{O}(\widehat{G})
.

2.3.3.
Time-Periodic Function Spaces Associated to the Newton Polygon

In order to deal efficiently with Fourier multipliers, we will introduce some potential spaces associated to order functions.
Those will be defined using the weight functions of Section 2.2 as potential.
For this purpose, it makes sense to introduce a smoothened version of the weight functions.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
\mu
be the difference of strictly positive order functions.
There exists a function
w_{\mu}\in{\mathcal{O}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
which is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function
\mu
in the sense of Definition
2.13
.

Proof.

Assume
\mu
is strictly positive, i.e.,
W_{\mu}
writes as
W_{\mu}(\tau,\xi)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}|\tau|^{s_{j}}|\xi|^{r_{j}}
. Then

w_{\mu}(\tau,\xi):=\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}\langle\tau\rangle^{s_{j}}\langle\xi\rangle^{r_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}\left(1+|\tau|^{2}\right)^{\frac{s_{j}}{2}}\left(1+|\xi|^{2}\right)^{\frac{r_{j}}{2}}

is as intended.
If
\nu
is another strictly positive order function, one sets
w_{\mu-\nu}:=\frac{w_{\mu}}{w_{\nu}}
.
∎

We can now define our Newton polygon potential spaces on
G
as follows.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
\mu
be the difference of strictly positive order functions.
The
Newton polygon potential space
associated to
\mu
is

\displaystyle H^{\mu}(G)

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{op}[w_{-\mu}]L^{2}(G).

For
s,r\in[0,\infty)
, we write
H^{(s,r)}(G):=H^{\mu_{(s,r)}}(G)
with
\mu_{(s,r)}=\mu({\mathcal{N}}(\{(r,s)\}))
.

Observe that by definition, we have
\|f\|_{\mu}=\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}
.
Moreover, since for the weight function of
\mu_{(s,r)}
we have
W_{\mu_{(s,r)}}(\tau,\xi)=1+|\xi|^{r}+|\tau|^{s}|\xi|^{r}+|\tau|^{s}
, we may use a standard cut-off method (see for example
[
5
]
Theorem 6.2.3) to deduce that the above definition of
H^{(s,r)}(G)
is consistent with (
16
) for
s,r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore, we can relate general Newton polygon potential spaces to the intersection of Sobolev spaces
H^{(s,r)}(G)
of dominating mixed smoothness at the vertices of the Newton polygon.
In particular, the geometric representation of the Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}(\mu)
encodes all the information needed to describe
H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G)
in terms of Sobolev spaces.

Proposition 2.20
.

Let
E\subseteq[0,\infty)^{2}
be a finite set,
\mathcal{N}:=\mathcal{N}(E)
the associated Newton polygon with vertices
\mathcal{N}_{V}:=\{(r_{0},s_{0}),...,(r_{J+1},s_{J+1})\}
and
\mu
the associated order function. Then

\bigcap_{(r,s)\in E}H^{(s,r)}(G)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{J+1}H^{(s_{i},r_{i})}(G)=H^{\mu}(G)

with equivalent norms.

Proof.

Set

X:=\bigcap_{(s,r)\in E}H^{(s,r)}(G),\qquad Y:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{J+1}H^{(s_{i},r_{i})}(G),\qquad Z:=H^{\mu}(G).

The inclusion
X\subseteq Y
is trivial.
To show
Y\subseteq Z
, let
f\in Y
.
Then

\|f\|_{Z}=\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu_{(s_{j},r_{j})}}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}\|f\|_{H^{(s,r)}(G)}

where we used that

m:=\frac{w_{\mu}}{\sum_{j=1}^{J+1}w_{\mu_{(s_{j},r_{j})}}}\in L^{\infty}(\widehat{G})

is a multiplier in
L^{2}_{\perp}(G)
by Plancherel’s theorem. Thus
Y\subseteq Z
.

We now show
Z\subseteq X
.
Let
f\in Z
.
Since
E\subseteq\mathcal{N}(E)
, we may use Lemma
2.8
and once again Plancherel’s theorem to obtain that

\widetilde{m}:=\frac{w_{\mu_{(s,r)}}}{w_{\mu}}

is a multiplier in
L^{2}_{\perp}(G)
for all
(r,s)\in E
.
Thus

\|f\|_{H^{(s,r)}(G)}=\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu_{(s,r)}}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}\lesssim\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)},

and the conclusion follows.
∎

Let us also record that the operators
\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]
associated to the weight functions act as lift operators in the potential spaces.

Proposition 2.21
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be differences of strictly positive order functions.
Then it holds that
\mathsf{op}[w_{\nu}]\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(H^{\mu+\nu}(G),H^{\nu}(G))
with
\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]^{-1}:=\mathsf{op}[w_{-\mu}]
.

Proof.

Let
f\in H^{\mu+\nu}(G)
.
Then

\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]f\|_{\nu}=\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\nu}w_{\mu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}=\|\mathsf{op}[\frac{w_{\nu}w_{\mu}}{w_{\mu+\nu}}]\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu+\nu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}.

Since
m:=\frac{w_{\nu}w_{\mu}}{w_{\mu+\nu}}\in L^{\infty}(\widehat{G})
, we have

\|\mathsf{op}[\frac{w_{\nu}w_{\mu}}{w_{\mu+\nu}}]\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu+\nu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}\lesssim\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu+\nu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}=C\|f\|_{\mu+\nu}.

The boundedness of the inverse follows by analogy.
∎

As a corollary, one obtains that all spaces
H^{\mu}(G)
are isomorphic to one another.
Furthermore, we remark that since
H^{\mu}(G)\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(G)
, we have defined its purely oscillatory counterpart
H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G)
in Section
2.3.2
.
Since
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}=\mathsf{op}[1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}}]
commutes with all operators of the form
\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu}]
, one can replace all instances of a Newton polygon potential space
H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G)
by its purely oscillatory counterpart
H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G)
in Propositions
2.20
and
2.21
.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
be differences of strictly positive order functions.
For
\theta\in(0,1)
define
\mu:=(1-\theta)\mu_{0}+\theta\mu_{1}
.
Then it holds

\displaystyle[H^{\mu_{0}}(G),H^{\mu_{1}}(G)]_{\theta}=H^{\mu}(G)\quad\text{and}\quad[H^{\mu_{0}}_{\perp}(G),H^{\mu_{1}}_{\perp}(G)]_{\theta}=H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G).

with equivalent norms.

Proof.

By the Plancherel’s theorem, the Fourier transform is an isometric isomorphism between
H^{\mu}(G)
and
L^{2}(\widehat{G},w_{\mu})
, where
L^{2}(\widehat{G},w_{\mu})
is the weighted
L^{2}
space defined by the norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(\widehat{G};w_{\mu})}^{2}:=\int_{\widehat{G}}|f(k,\xi)|^{2}w_{\mu}(k,\xi)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}(k,\xi).

Observe that
[L^{2}(\widehat{G};w_{\mu_{0}}),L^{2}(\widehat{G};w_{\mu_{1}})]_{\theta}=L^{2}(\widehat{G};w_{\mu_{0}}^{1-\theta}w_{\mu_{1}}^{\theta})
with equivalent norms, see e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 5.5.3]
.
Since
w_{\mu_{0}}^{1-\theta}w_{\mu_{1}}^{\theta}\simeq w_{\mu}
by Lemma
2.9
, the first identity follows in view of Lemma
2.1
.

The projection
\mathcal{P}_{\perp}:H^{\nu}(G)\to H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G)
is a retraction for all
\nu\in\{\mu_{0},\mu_{1},\mu\}
, the right-inverse being given by the inclusion
i:H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G)\hookrightarrow H^{\nu}(G)
,
i(f):=f
.
Thus, we also obtain the second identity by Lemma
2.1
.
∎

3.
The Whole Space Problem

3.1.
Maximal Regularity of the CHG Determinant

We are now able to state well-posedness results for time-periodic mixed-order scalar equations on the whole space, see Theorem
3.2
below.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be differences of strictly positive order functions and
\widehat{P}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\perp}(\widehat{G})
.
Assume that
\mu
is an upper order function for
P
.
Then
\mathsf{op}[P]\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\mu+\nu}_{\perp}(G),H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G))
.

Proof.

Since
w_{\mu+\nu}\cong w_{\mu}w_{\nu}
, we have

m:=(1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}})\frac{Pw_{\nu}}{w_{\mu+\nu}}\in L^{\infty}(\widehat{G}),

so that
m
is a Fourier multiplier in
L^{2}_{\perp}(G)
.
Therefore, we have for all
f\in H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G)

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{op}[P]f\|_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\|\mathsf{op}[(1-\delta_{\mathbb{Z}})\frac{Pw_{\nu}}{w_{\mu+\nu}}]\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu+\nu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}\leq C\|\mathsf{op}[w_{\mu+\nu}]f\|_{L^{2}(G)}=C\|f\|_{\mu+\nu}.\qed

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be differences of strictly positive order functions and
\widehat{P}\in{\mathcal{O}}^{\perp}(\widehat{G})
.
Assume furthermore that
P
is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function
\mu
.
Then
\mathsf{op}[P]\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(H^{\mu+\nu}_{\perp}(G),H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G))
with
\mathsf{op}[P]^{-1}=\mathsf{op}[\frac{1}{P}]
.
In particular, for all
f\in H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G)
, the equation
\mathsf{op}[P]u=f
has a unique solution
u\in H^{\nu+\mu}_{\perp}(G)
, and with
c:=\|\mathsf{op}[\frac{1}{P}]\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G),H^{\mu+\nu}_{\perp}(G))}
it holds

\|u\|_{\mu+\nu}\leq c\|f\|_{\nu}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
we have
\mathsf{op}[P]\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\mu+\nu}_{\perp}(G),H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G))
.
Since
P
is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic, we have
P(\tau,\xi)\neq 0
for
\tau\neq 0
. Thus
\widehat{P}\in\mathcal{O}^{\perp}(\widehat{G})
implies
\frac{1}{\widehat{P}}\in\mathcal{O}^{\perp}(\widehat{G})
.
Furthermore,
P
being
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function
\mu
implies that
-\mu
is an upper order function for
\frac{1}{P}
and the conclusion follows again from Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

A trivial consequence of the above theorem is that the operator
\partial_{t}=\mathsf{op}[\mathsf{i}\tau]
is bounded and invertible in Newton polygon potential spaces. In particular, one gets a Poincaré-type inequality in the following sense. Let
\nu
be a difference of strictly positive order functions and consider the order function
\id
.
Then for all
k\in\mathbb{N},

\partial_{t}^{k}f\in H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G)
if and only if
f\in H^{\nu+k\id}_{\perp}(G)
, and it holds

\|f\|_{\nu}\leq\|f\|_{\nu+k\id}\cong_{\nu,k}\|\partial_{t}^{k}f\|_{\nu}.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
D
be the CHG determinant in (
7
) and
\mu_{D}
the associated order function.
Let furthermore
\nu
be the difference of strictly positive order functions.
Then for each
f\in H^{\nu}_{\perp}(G)
, there is a unique
u\in H^{\mu+\nu}(G)
with
\mathsf{op}[D]u=f
, and it holds

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mu_{D}+\nu}\lesssim_{\nu}\|f\|_{\nu}.

Proof.

Follows directly from Proposition
2.14
and Theorem
3.2
.
∎

3.2.
Mixed-Order Systems and Maximal Regularity for the CHG System

In this section, we prove the counterpart of Theorem
3.2
for mixed-order systems.
Here we consider a smooth matrix-valued symbol
L:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
as well as
\mathcal{D}:=\det L:(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
, and denote as usual
\widehat{L},\widehat{D}
the restrictions to
\widehat{G}
.
We assume that the symbols
\widehat{L}_{ij}
belong to the class
\mathcal{O}^{\perp}(\widehat{G})
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\widehat{L}
be as above and assume that
L
is a mixed-order system in the sense of Definition
2.15
with strictly positive order functions
t_{j}
, and
-s_{i}
,
i,j\in\{1,...,m\}
.
Assume additionally that
\nu
is a strictly positive order functions.
We define

\mathbb{E}:=\prod_{j=1}^{m}H^{\nu+t_{j}}_{\perp}(G)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{F}:=\prod_{i=1}^{m}H^{\nu-s_{i}}_{\perp}(G).

Then
\mathsf{op}[L]\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{E},\mathbb{F})
with
\mathsf{op}[L]^{-1}=\mathsf{op}[\ad L]\mathsf{op}[D^{-1}]
, where
D^{-1}
has to be understood as the diagonal symbol matrix with all diagonal entries equal to
\frac{1}{D}
.
In particular, for all
f=(f_{1},...,f_{m})\in\mathbb{F}
, the system
\mathsf{op}[L]u=f
admits a unique solution
u=(u_{1},...,u_{m})\in\mathbb{E}
, and it holds

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|u_{j}\|_{\nu+t_{j}}\lesssim_{\nu,s_{i},t_{i},L,m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|f_{i}\|_{\nu-s_{i}}.

Proof.

By definition of a mixed-order system,
s_{i}+t_{j}
is an upper order function for
L_{ij}
.
Thus by Proposition
3.1
,
\mathsf{op}[L_{ij}]\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\nu+t_{j}}_{\perp}(G),H^{\nu-s_{i}}_{\perp}(G))
for all
i,j\in\{1,...,m\}
.

In a similar fashion, the determinant
\mathcal{D}
is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic for the order function
\delta:=\sum_{l=1}^{m}(s_{l}+t_{l})
.
Thus, by Theorem
3.2
,
\mathsf{op}[\mathcal{D}]^{-1}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\nu-s_{i}}_{\perp}(G),H^{\nu-s_{i}+\delta}_{\perp}(G))
for all
i\in\{1,...,m\}
.
Furthermore, by Proposition
2.16
and
3.1
, we have

\mathsf{op}[(\ad L)_{ij}]\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\nu-s_{i}+\delta}_{\perp}(G),H^{\nu-s_{i}+\delta-S_{i}-T_{j}}_{\perp}(G))

for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, where
S_{i}
and
T_{j}
are defined in Proposition
2.16
.
Since
\nu-s_{i}+\delta-S_{i}-T_{j}=\nu+t_{j}
, the boundedness of
\mathsf{op}[\ad L]\mathsf{op}[D^{-1}]
follows.
Since
\mathsf{op}[L]
and
\mathsf{op}[\ad L]\mathsf{op}[D^{-1}]
are mutually inverse, this achieves the proof.
∎

Since we already showed that the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system is a mixed-order system, we obtain time-periodic maximal regularity as a direct corollary of the above theorem.

Corollary 3.6
.

For all
f_{1}\in L^{2}_{\perp}(G),f_{2}\in H^{(0,1)}_{\perp}(G)
, the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system (
14
) admits a unique solution
(u_{1},u_{2})
with
u_{1}\in H^{(0,3)}_{\perp}(G)\cap H^{(1,1)}_{\perp}(G)
,
u_{2}\in H^{(0,2)}_{\perp}(G)
.

Proof.

Set
s_{1},s_{2},t_{1},t_{2}
as in Example
2.17
. By Proposition
2.20
, we get
H^{-s_{1}}_{\perp}(G)=L^{2}_{\perp}(G)
,
H^{-s_{2}}_{\perp}(G)=H^{(0,1)}_{\perp}(G)
as well as
H^{t_{1}}_{\perp}(G)=H^{(0,3)}_{\perp}(G)\cap H^{(1,1)}_{\perp}(G)
and
H^{t_{2}}_{\perp}(G)=H^{(0,2)}_{\perp}(G)
.
The conclusion follows from Theorem
3.5
.
∎

4.
The Half Space Problem with General Boundary Conditions

In this section, we develop a theory for mixed order systems with general boundary conditions which will enable us to solve the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin system in the half space.
We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}>0\}
the upper half space, and write by analogy
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{-}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}<0\}
for the lower half space.
Then we define

\displaystyle G_{\pm}:=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\pm}\quad\text{and}\quad G^{n-1}:=\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Function spaces on
G_{\pm}
will be defined as quotient spaces of those defined in Section
2
, see Section
4.1
, while function spaces of
G^{n-1}
will be defined via trace theory, see Section
4.2
.

4.1.
Quotient Spaces

Define the extensions
e_{0}^{\pm}:C^{\infty}_{c}(G_{\pm})\to C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
via

(20)

\displaystyle e_{0}^{+}\varphi(t,x^{\prime},x_{n}):=\begin{cases}\varphi(t,x^{\prime},x_{n})&\text{if }x_{n}>0,\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad e_{0}^{-}\varphi(t,x^{\prime},x_{n}):=\begin{cases}\varphi(t,x^{\prime},x_{n})&\text{if }x_{n}<0,\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Then
e_{0}^{\pm}
is continuous, and thus we may introduce the continuous restrictions
r^{\pm}:={}^{t}e_{0}^{\pm}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G_{\pm})
.
Here
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G_{\pm})
are the usual spaces of distributions, that is the strong dual of
C^{\infty}_{c}(G)
and
C^{\infty}_{c}(G_{\pm})
, respectively.
Thus,
\ker r^{+}
is closed and so is
\ker r^{+}|_{E(G)}
for any locally convex space
E(G)
which is continuously embedded into
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(G)
.
For the sake of readability, we will write
r^{+}
instead of
r^{+}|_{E(G)}
in the sequel.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
E(G)
be a locally convex space continuously embedded into
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(G)
.
Then we introduce the
restricted spaces

\overline{E}(G_{\pm})
and the
supported spaces

\dot{E}(\overline{G_{\pm}})
via

\displaystyle\overline{E}(G_{\pm}):=r^{\pm}E(G)\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{E}(\overline{G_{\pm}}):=\{f\in E(G)\mid\supp f\subseteq\overline{G_{\pm}}\}.

As we mostly work with restricted and supported spaces on the upper half space, we sometimes write
\overline{E}:=\overline{E}(G_{+})
and
\dot{E}:=\dot{E}(\overline{G_{+}})
if no confusion can arise.
In particular, given any order function
\mu
, we obtain the restricted space

\displaystyle\overline{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}(G_{+})

\displaystyle:=\{f\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(G_{+})\mid f=r^{+}F\ \text{ for some }\ F\in H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G)\},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\overline{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}}

\displaystyle:=\|f\|_{\mu,+}:=\inf\{\|F\|_{\mu}\mid F\in H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G),r^{+}F=f\},

and the supported space

\displaystyle\dot{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}(\overline{G_{+}})

\displaystyle:=\{f\in H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G)\mid\supp f\subseteq\overline{G_{+}}\},

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\dot{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}}

\displaystyle:=\|f\|_{\mu}.

It will be convenient to note that the restriction operator acts as intended on the supported spaces.

Lemma 4.2
.

It holds
r^{\pm}\dot{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{\mp}})=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\dot{{\mathcal{D}}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{\mp}})
, i.e.
\supp f\subseteq\overline{G_{\mp}}
.
Then for
\varphi\in{\mathcal{D}}(G_{\pm})
we have
\supp e_{0}^{+}\varphi\subseteq G_{\pm}
and thus

\displaystyle\langle r^{+}f,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,e_{0}^{+}\varphi\rangle=0.\qed

Remark 4.3
.

(i)

We use the traditional notation of Hörmander for the restricted and supported spaces.
We note that
[
8
, Definition 1.63]
use slightly different terminology (besides the fact that in the present work we are dealing with time periodic functions).
In particular, our space
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\prime}(G_{+})
would be called
{}_{0}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G_{+})
in
[
8
]
.
On the other hand, in their notation the space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G_{+})
would correspond to
r^{+}\dot{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{+}})
in our notation:
Indeed, using the notation of
[
8
]
, the inclusion
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G_{+})\subseteq r^{+}\dot{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{+}})
follows from

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G_{+})=r_{0}^{+}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G)=r^{+}(1-e^{-}r^{-}){\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G)

and the observation that
(1-e^{-}r^{-}){\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G)\subseteq\dot{{\mathcal{S}}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{+}})
, while the converse inclusion follows from
e_{0}^{+}r_{0}^{+}f=f
for
f\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{+}})
(see Lemma 1.65 in
[
8
]
) and
r^{+}e^{+}_{0}f=f
for
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(G_{+})
in form of
r_{0}^{+}f=r^{+}e_{0}^{+}r_{0}^{+}f=r^{+}f
for all
f\in\dot{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\overline{G_{+}})
.

(ii)

If
E(G)\subseteq C^{\infty}(G)
, then for each
f\in\overline{E}(G_{+})
and each

there is a unique extension of
\partial_{n}^{j}f
to
\overline{G_{+}}
.
Therefore, we may regard
\overline{E}(G_{+})
as a space of functions defined on
\overline{G_{+}}
instead of
G_{+}
, and suggestively write
E(\overline{G_{+}}):=\overline{E}(G_{+})
.

4.2.
Trace Theory

The first step to treat the half space problem is to identify the trace spaces associated to our Newton polygon spaces.
We will see that the appropriate spaces are again Newton polygon spaces defined on
G^{n-1}
, and that the corresponding polygons are obtained by ’translating’ the polygon of the original space to the left.
A good understanding of those spaces will be essential to establish the (non-)existence of solutions in the later sections.
We consider the usual trace operators of
i
-th order: for
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\mathsf{Tr}_{j}:C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{G_{+}})\to C^{\infty}_{c}(G^{n-1}),\qquad\mathsf{Tr}_{j}\phi(t,x^{\prime}):=\partial_{n}^{j}\phi(t,x^{\prime},0)

and

\mathsf{Tr}^{(i)}:C^{\infty}_{c}(\overline{G_{+}})\to C^{\infty}_{c}(G^{n-1})^{i},\qquad\mathsf{Tr}^{(i)}\phi:=(\mathsf{Tr}_{0}\phi,\mathsf{Tr}_{1}\phi,...,\mathsf{Tr}_{i-1}\phi),

where we keep in mind the notation introduced in Remark
4.3
.
(ii)
.

Now consider a Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}
, the associated order function
\mu=\mu(\mathcal{N})
, and its set of vertices
\mathcal{N}_{V}=\{(r_{0},s_{0}),...,(r_{J+1},s_{J+1})\}
.
We are looking for a function space
\mathbb{G}
such that the operator
\mathsf{Tr}^{(r_{1})}
defined above extends to a bounded and surjective operator

\mathsf{Tr}^{(r_{1})}:\overline{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}(G_{+})\to\mathbb{G}.

Corollary 4.4
.

For all
r,s,r_{0},s_{0}\geq 0
such that
s_{0}\geq s
,
r_{0}\geq r
and
\theta\in(0,1)
,

[H^{(s,r)}_{\perp}(G),H^{(s,r_{0})}_{\perp}(G))\cap H^{(s_{0},r)}_{\perp}(G)]_{\theta}=H^{(s,(1-\theta)r+\theta r_{0})}_{\perp}(G)\cap H^{((1-\theta)s+\theta s_{0},r)}_{\perp}(G).

Proof.

Consider the sets
E_{0}:=\{(s,r)\}
,
E_{1}:=\{(s,r_{0}),(s_{0},r)\}
and
E:=\{(s,(1-\theta)r+\theta r_{0}),((1-\theta)s+\theta s_{0},r)\}
, as well as the associated Newton polygons
{\mathcal{N}}(E_{0})
,
{\mathcal{N}}(E_{1})
, and
{\mathcal{N}}(E)
.
For the associated order functions
\mu_{0}:=\mu({\mathcal{N}}(E_{0}))
,
\mu_{1}:=\mu({\mathcal{N}}(E_{1}))
and
\mu:=\mu({\mathcal{N}}(E))
, we have by Proposition
2.20
that

\displaystyle H^{\mu_{0}}_{\perp}(G)

\displaystyle=H^{(s,r)}_{\perp}(G),

\displaystyle H^{\mu_{1}}(G)

\displaystyle=H^{(s,r_{0})}_{\perp}(G))\cap H^{(s_{0},r)}_{\perp}(G),

\displaystyle H^{\mu}(G)

\displaystyle=H^{(s,(1-\theta)r+\theta r_{0})}_{\perp}(G)\cap H^{((1-\theta)s+\theta s_{0},r)}_{\perp}(G).

Note that

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=r+s\gamma,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\max\{r_{0}+s\gamma,r+s_{0}\gamma\},

\displaystyle\mu(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\max\{(1-\theta)r+\theta r_{0}+s\gamma,r+((1-\theta)s+\theta s_{0})\gamma\}.

In particular,
(1-\theta)\mu_{0}+\theta\mu_{1}=\mu
, so that the assertion follows from Lemma
2.22
.
∎

We now describe the trace space associated to Newton polygon spaces.
As mentioned in the introduction, we will restrict ourselves to Newton polygons of a shape similar to the one of the CHG determinant symbol (
10
). For integers
r_{1},r_{2}
with
r_{1}>r_{2}\geq 0
, and for
s_{2}\geq 0
consider a Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}
with vertices

\mathcal{N}_{V}:=\{(0,0),(r_{1},0),(r_{2},s_{2}),(0,s_{2})\}.

We set
\gamma^{\perp}_{1}:=\frac{s_{2}}{r_{2}-r_{1}}\leq 0
the slope of the line segment joining
(r_{2},s_{2})
to
(r_{1},0)
.
The associated order function to
\mathcal{N}
can be written as
\mu=(r_{1}-r_{2})o_{-\gamma^{\perp}_{1}}+r_{2}
with
\ord\mu=r_{1}
, where we recall the definition of the elementary order functions
o_{y}
in the paragraph before Proposition
2.10
.
Order functions of such type will be called
CHG-shaped
.
For
j\in\{0,\ldots,r_{1}-1\}
, define the order functions

\displaystyle T_{j}(\mu):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(r_{1}-r_{2})o_{-\gamma^{\perp}_{1}}+r_{2}-j-\frac{1}{2},&\text{for }j\in\{0,...,r_{2}-1\},\\
(r_{1}-j-\frac{1}{2})o_{-\gamma^{\perp}_{1}},&\text{for }j\in\{r_{2},...,r_{1}-1\}.\end{array}\right.

Remark that the Newton polygon
T_{j}({\mathcal{N}})
associated to
T_{j}(\mu)
is the Newton polygon
\mathcal{N}
translated to the left by
j+\frac{1}{2}
, cf. Figure
2
.
In particular we have

H^{T_{j}(\mu)}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H^{(s_{2},r_{2}-j-\frac{1}{2})}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})\cap H^{(0,r_{1}-j-\frac{1}{2})}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})&\text{for }j\in\{0,...,r_{2}-1\},\\
H^{(s_{2}+\gamma_{1}^{\perp}(j-r_{2}+\frac{1}{2}),0)}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})\cap H^{(0,r_{1}-j-\frac{1}{2})}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})&\text{for }j\in\{r_{2},...,r_{1}-1\}.\end{array}\right.

We set

(21)

T^{\mu}_{\perp}(G^{n-1}):=\prod_{j=0}^{r_{1}-1}H^{T_{j}(\mu)}_{\perp}(G^{n-1}),

and prove that it is the desired trace space.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mu
be a CHG-shaped order function and
r_{1}:=\ord\mu
.
The trace operator
\mathsf{Tr}^{(r_{1})}
extends to
\mathsf{Tr}^{(r_{1})}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G),T^{\mu}_{\perp}(G^{n-1}))
.
Furthermore, it admits a right-inverse
E^{\mu}\in{\mathcal{L}}(T^{\mu}_{\perp}(G^{n-1}),H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G))
.

Proof.

For the sake of readability, we will write
\mathbb{E}:=\overline{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}(G_{+})
,
\mathbb{G}:=T^{\mu}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})
,
H(s,r):=H_{\perp}^{(s,r)}(G^{n-1})
, and
\overline{H}(s,r):=\overline{H}^{(s,r)}_{\perp}(G_{+})
.
Let us comment on the structure of the proof: In Step 1, we show the boundedness of the trace operator, while in Step 2 we construct a bounded right-inverse.
Both steps are divided into two substeps: For each
j\in\{0,\ldots,r_{1}-1\}
, the shifted Newton polygon can have three or four vertices. We treat both cases for
j=0
in the first substep. In the second substep we lift the argument from
j=0
to general
j\in\{0,\ldots,r_{1}-1\}
.

Step
1.
We want to show that
\mathsf{Tr}^{(r_{1})}
extends to a well-defined and bounded operator
\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{G}
.
Obviously, it is sufficient to prove that
\mathsf{Tr}_{j}
extends to a bounded operator
\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{G}_{j}:=H^{T_{j}(\mu)}_{\perp}(G^{n-1})
for all
j\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\}
.

Step
1.1.
We start by showing that
\mathsf{Tr}_{0}
extends to a well-defined and bounded operator
\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{G}_{0}
. Here we have two cases to distinguish depending on the value of
r_{2}
.

If
r_{2}=0
, then the Newton polygon associated to
\mathbb{E}
is simply
\mathcal{N}(\{(r_{1},0),(0,s_{2})\})
and
\gamma^{\perp}_{1}=-\frac{s_{2}}{r_{1}}
. Thus an equivalent norm on
\mathbb{E}
is given by

\|f\|_{\mathbb{E}}:=\|\partial_{n}^{r_{1}}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H(0,0))}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H(s_{2},0)\cap H(0,r_{1}))}.

By the trace method e.g. in Section 1.8 of
[
23
]
, this implies the boundedness of

\mathsf{Tr}_{0}:\mathbb{E}\to(H(0,r_{1})\cap H(s_{2},0),H(0,0))_{\frac{1}{2r_{1}},2}=[H(0,r_{1})\cap H(s_{2},0),H(0,0)]_{\frac{1}{2r_{1}}}.

Using Corollary
4.4
, we find

\displaystyle[H(0,r_{1})\cap H(s_{2},0),H(0,0)]_{\frac{1}{2r_{1}}}

\displaystyle=H(0,r_{1}-\frac{1}{2})\cap H(s_{1}-\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\perp}}{2},0)=\mathbb{G}_{0}.

If
r_{2}\geq 1
, then an equivalent norm on
\overline{H}(s_{2},r_{2})
is given by

\|f\|_{\overline{H}(s_{2},r_{2})}\simeq\|\partial_{n}^{r_{2}}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H(s_{2},0))}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H(s_{2},r_{2}))}.

Using the trace method as above, we get the boundedness of

\mathsf{Tr}_{0}:\overline{H}(s_{2},r_{2})\to H(s_{2},r_{2}-\frac{1}{2}).

Substituting
(s_{2},r_{2})
by
(0,r_{1})
in the above, we also get boundedness of

\mathsf{Tr}_{0}:\overline{H}(0,r_{1})\to H(0,r_{1}-\frac{1}{2}),

and thus

\displaystyle\mathsf{Tr}_{0}:\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle=\overline{H}(s_{2},r_{2})\cap\overline{H}(0,r_{1})\to H(s_{2},r_{2}-\frac{1}{2})\cap H(0,r_{1}-\frac{1}{2})=\mathbb{G}_{0}

is also bounded.

Step
1.2.
For all
j=0,...,r_{1}-1
, remark that
\mathsf{Tr}_{j}=\mathsf{Tr}_{0}\circ\partial_{n}^{j}
and define

\mathbb{E}_{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{H}(s_{2},r_{2}-j)\cap\overline{H}(0,r_{1}-j)&\text{if }j=0,...,r_{2}-1\\
\overline{H}(s_{2}+\gamma_{1}^{\perp}(j-r_{2}),0)\cap\overline{H}(0,r_{1}-j)&\text{if }j=r_{2},...,r_{1}-1\end{array}\right.

Remark that the Newton polygon associated to
\mathbb{E}_{j}
is the Newton polygon of the space
\mathbb{E}
translated to the left by
j
, and that
\partial_{n}^{j}:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}_{j}
is bounded. Furthermore, replacing
\mathbb{E},\mathbb{G}_{0}
by
\mathbb{E}_{j},\mathbb{G}_{j}
in STEP 1.1, one gets that
\mathsf{Tr}_{0}:\mathbb{E}_{j}\to\mathbb{G}_{j}
is bounded, and the conclusion follows.

Step
2.
We now construct a bounded right-inverse. This part of the proof is adapted from
[
10
]
. Pick
\sigma=(\sigma_{0},...,\sigma_{r_{1}-1})\in\mathbb{G}
. For
j=0,...,r_{1}-1
, we define the operator

A_{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1-\Delta^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}&\text{if }j\in\{0,...,r_{2}-1\}\\
(1-\Delta^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}+(1+\partial_{t})^{-\gamma^{\perp}_{1}}&\text{if }j\in\{r_{2},...,r_{1}-1\},\end{array}\right.

where the Laplace operator
\Delta^{\prime}
acts in the variables
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
These operators generate analytic semigroups in all considered Newton polygon spaces (in fact, they admit a bounded
H^{\infty}
-calculus by a Fourier multiplier argument as in
[
17
, Example 10.2]
), and in particular they admit maximal
L^{2}
regularity.
Define

\eta_{j}(x_{n}):=\sum_{k=0}^{r_{1}-1}c_{kj}\mathrm{e}^{-kx_{n}A_{j}}(A^{-j}_{j}\sigma_{j}),\qquad j\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\},

where we can choose the coefficients
c_{kj}
in such a way that for
j,m\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\}
it holds

(22)

\partial_{n}^{m}\eta_{j}(0)=\delta_{m,j}\sigma_{j}.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{n}^{m}\eta_{j}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{r_{1}-1}(-k)^{m}c_{kj}A^{m-j}_{j}\sigma_{j}.

Therefore, (
22
) is verified if
VC_{j}=e_{j}
for
j\in\{1,...,r_{1}\}
, where
C_{j}=(c_{1j},...,c_{r_{1}j})
and
V
is the Vandermonde matrix. Since
V
is invertible, we can set
C_{j}=V^{-1}e_{j}
to achieve this.
We now set

\eta=\sum_{j=0}^{r_{1}-1}\eta_{j}.

By (
22
), we have
\partial_{n}^{m}\eta=\sigma_{m}
for
m\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\}
and thus
\mathsf{Tr}^{(r_{1})}\eta=\sigma
.
It is left to show that
\eta_{j}\in\mathbb{E}
for all
j\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\}
.
Once again, we will proceed in two substeps.

Step
2.1.
We show that
\eta_{0}\in\mathbb{E}
and
\|\eta_{0}\|_{\mathbb{E}}\lesssim\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{0}}
, which due to

(23)

\displaystyle\|\eta_{0}\|_{\mathbb{E}}\simeq\|\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(s_{2},r_{2}))}+\|\partial_{n}^{r_{2}}\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(s_{2},0))}+\|\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(0,r_{1}))}+\|\partial_{n}^{r_{1}}\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(G_{+})}

comes down to estimating the four contributions to the right-hand side individually.
Once again, we have two cases to consider depending on the value of
r_{2}
.

We first show the case
r_{2}\geq 1
. In this case we have
A_{0}=(1-\Delta^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
For
j\in\{1,2\}
consider
A_{0}
acting in
X:=H(s_{j},r_{j}-1)
with domain
D(A_{0}):=H(s_{j},r_{j})
. We claim that

\mathbb{G}_{0}\hookrightarrow[X,D(A_{0})]_{\frac{1}{2}}.

Indeed, Corollary
4.4
directly shows that the right-hand side is equal to
H(s_{j},r_{j}-\frac{1}{2})
, which is a vertex of the Newton polygon associated to the space
\mathbb{G}_{0}
.
Therefore, Lemma
2.8
yields the claim.
The maximal regularity of
A_{0}
now shows

\eta_{0}\in\overline{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(s_{j},r_{j}-1))\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(s_{j},r_{j}))

and in particular
\|\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(s_{j},r_{j}))}\lesssim\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{0}}
.
This is the desired estimate for the first and the third contribution to the right-hand side of (
23
).
For the second and fourth contribution to the right-hand side of (
23
), remark that

\displaystyle\partial_{n}^{r_{j}-1}\eta_{0}=\sum_{k=0}^{r_{1}-1}(-k)^{r_{j}-1}c_{k0}\mathrm{e}^{-kx_{n}A_{0}}(A_{0}^{r_{j}-1}\sigma_{0}),\qquad j\in\{1,2\}.

Set
X:=H(0,0)
and
D(A_{0})=H(0,1)
. Here one has

[X,D(A_{0})]_{\frac{1}{2}}=H(0,\frac{1}{2}),

and since
A_{0}
is
\mathcal{N}
-elliptic with weight function
w(\tau,\xi)\simeq 1+|\xi|
, Theorem
3.2
shows

A_{0}^{r_{j}-1}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{G}_{0},H(0,\frac{1}{2})\cap H(-\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\perp}}{2},0))\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbb{G}_{0},H(0,\frac{1}{2})).

Now the maximal regularity of
A_{0}
yields
\partial_{n}^{r_{j}-1}\eta_{0}\in\overline{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(0,0))
and
\|\partial_{n}^{r_{j}}\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(G_{+})}\lesssim\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{0}}
.

For the case
r_{2}=0
, the first two contributions to the right-hand side of (
23
) collapse to
\|\eta_{0}\|_{\overline{H}(s_{2},0)}
. Observe that now
A_{0}=(1-\Delta^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{2}}+(1+\partial_{t})^{-\gamma_{1}^{\perp}}
.
We consider
A_{0}
acting in
X:=H(s_{2}+\gamma_{1}^{\perp},0)
with domain
D(A_{0}):=H(s_{2},0)\cap H(s_{2}+\gamma^{\perp}_{1},1)
. We claim that

\mathbb{G}_{0}\hookrightarrow[X,D(A_{0})]_{\frac{1}{2}}.

Indeed, by Corollary
4.4
, the right-hand side is equal to
H(s_{2}+\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\perp}}{2},0)\cap H(s_{2}+\gamma_{1}^{\perp},\frac{1}{2})
, and both the points
(s_{2}+\frac{\gamma_{1}^{\perp}}{2},0)
and
(s_{2}+\gamma_{1}^{\perp},\frac{1}{2})
belong to the Newton polygon associated to the space
\mathbb{G}_{0}
, so that the embedding follows by Lemma
2.8
.
The maximal regularity of
A_{0}
thus yields

\eta_{0}\in\overline{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(s_{2}+\gamma_{1}^{\perp},0))\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+};H(s_{2},0)\cap H(s_{2}+\gamma^{\perp}_{1},1))\hookrightarrow\overline{H}(s_{2},0)

with
\|\eta_{0}\|_{\overline{H}(s_{2},0)}\lesssim\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{0}}
.

For the third contribution to the right-hand side of (
23
) we run a similar argument applied to
X:=H(0,r_{1}-1)
and
D(A_{0}):=H(-\gamma^{\perp}_{1},r_{1}-1)\cap H(0,r_{1})
.
This yields
\eta_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H(0,r_{1}))
together with the estimate
\|\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},H(0,r_{1}))}\lesssim\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{0}}
.

For the fourth contribution to the right-hand side of (
23
) we use a similar argument to the one used in the case
r_{2}\geq 1
, here taking
X:=(0,0)
and
D(A_{0})=H(\gamma_{1}^{\perp},0)\cap H(0,1)
to get
\partial_{n}^{r_{1}-1}\eta_{0}\in\overline{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+};H(0,0))
with the desired estimate
\|\partial_{n}^{r_{1}}\eta_{0}\|_{L^{2}(G_{+})}\lesssim\|\sigma_{0}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{0}}
.

Step
2.2
We now show that
\eta_{j}\in\mathbb{E}
for all
j\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\}
and
\|\eta_{j}\|_{\mathbb{E}}\lesssim\|\sigma_{j}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{j}}
.
We set

\mathbb{X}_{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}H(s_{2},r_{2}-\frac{1}{2})\cap H(0,r_{1}-\frac{1}{2})&\text{if }j\in\{0,...,r_{2}-1\}\\
H(-\gamma^{\perp}_{1}(r_{1}-\frac{1}{2}),0)\cap H(0,r_{1}-\frac{1}{2})&\text{if }j\in\{r_{2},...,r_{1}-1\}.\end{array}\right.

As in the previous step we obtain by Theorem
3.2
that
A_{j}^{-j}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\mathbb{G}_{j},\mathbb{X}_{j})
.
Therefore, it makes sense to define the maximal regularity spaces
\mathbb{Y}_{j}
of
A_{j}
corresponding to ’initial data’ in
\mathbb{X}_{j}
, i.e.

\mathbb{Y}_{j}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}&\text{if }j\in\{0,...,r_{2}-1\},\\
\overline{H}(-\gamma^{\perp}_{1}r_{1},0)\cap\overline{H}(0,r_{1})&\text{if }j\in\{r_{2},...,r_{1}-1\}.\end{array}\right.

Replacing
\mathbb{E},\mathbb{G}_{0},\sigma_{0}
in STEP 2.1 by
\mathbb{Y}_{j},\mathbb{X}_{j},A_{j}^{-j}\sigma_{j}
, we infer that

\|\eta_{j}\|_{\mathbb{Y}_{j}}\lesssim\|A_{j}^{-j}\sigma_{j}\|_{\mathbb{X}_{j}}\lesssim\|\sigma_{j}\|_{\mathbb{G}_{j}}
.
To conclude, we remark that the Newton polygon associated to the space
\mathbb{E}
is included in
\mathbb{Y}_{j}
for all
j\in\{0,...,r_{1}-1\}
. Thus
\mathbb{Y}_{j}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{E}
by Lemma
2.8
, which concludes the proof.
∎

In conclusion, the trace spaces associated to
\overline{H}^{\mu}_{\perp}(G_{+})
can be described by a Newton polygon.
We particularise these results to the Cahn-Hilliard-Gurtin determinant.

4.3.
Operators Preserving Upper and Lower Support

In order to adapt the results of Section 2 to the half space problem, the main issue we are facing is that pseudodifferential operators are
a priori
nonlocal.
Thus, not every Fourier multiplier
\mathsf{op}[m]:E(G)\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(G)
induces a well-defined operator
\overline{E}(G_{+})\to\overline{{\mathcal{S}}}{}^{\prime}(G_{+})
.

Definition 4.7

(support-preserving operators)
.

Let
E(G),F(G)\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(G)
be locally convex spaces and
\mathsf{A}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E(G),F(G))
.

(i)

\mathsf{A}

preserves support in
\overline{G_{\pm}}
(or
preserves upper/lower support
) if for every
f\in\dot{E}(\overline{G_{\pm}})
, one has
\mathsf{A}f\in\dot{F}(\overline{G_{\pm}})
.

(ii)

If
\mathsf{A}
preserves support in
\overline{G_{\pm}}
, the induced operators
\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{\pm}:r^{\pm}\dot{E}(\overline{G_{\pm}})\to r^{\pm}\dot{F}(\overline{G_{\pm}})
and
\mathsf{A}_{\mp}:\overline{E}(G_{\mp})\to\overline{F}(G_{\mp})
are defined via

\displaystyle\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{\pm}u:=r^{\pm}Ae_{0}^{\pm}u\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{A}_{\mp}u:=r^{\mp}\mathsf{A}U,

where
U\in E(G)
is any extension of
u
, that is,
r^{\mp}U=u
.

Observe that
\mathsf{A}_{\mp}
is well-defined, i.e., the above definition does not depend on the choice of the extension
U
.
Indeed, let
U_{1},U_{2}\in E(G)
with
r^{\mp}U_{1}=r^{\mp}U_{2}=u
.
Then
U_{1}-U_{2}\in\dot{E}(\overline{G_{\pm}})
.
Since
\mathsf{A}
preserves support in
\overline{G_{\pm}}
, it holds
\mathsf{A}U_{1}-\mathsf{A}U_{2}\in\dot{F}(\overline{G}_{\pm})
, which in turn implies
r^{\mp}\mathsf{A}U_{1}=r^{\mp}\mathsf{A}U_{2}
by Lemma
4.2
.
Moreover,
\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{\pm}
is the restriction of
\mathsf{A}_{\pm}
to
r^{\pm}\dot{E}(\overline{G_{\pm}})
if both exist.
Finally, if
\mathsf{A}
is a local operator (for example a differential operator), then
\mathsf{A}:\overline{E}(G_{\mp})\to\overline{F}(G_{\mp})
has a canonical meaning, and it holds
\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}_{\mp}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
E(G),F(G),H(G)\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(G)
be normed spaces and let
\mathsf{A}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E(G),F(G))
,
\mathsf{B}\in{\mathcal{L}}(F(G),H(G))
preserve lower support.

(i)

It holds
\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{-}\in{\mathcal{L}}(r^{-}\dot{E}(\overline{G_{-}}),r^{-}\dot{F}(\overline{G_{-}}))
and
\mathsf{A}_{+}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\overline{E}(G_{+}),\overline{F}(G_{+}))
, and both operator norms do not exceed
\|\mathsf{A}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(E(G),F(G))}
.

(ii)

\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E(G),H(G))
preserves lower support, and
(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A})_{-}^{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}=\dot{\mathsf{B}}_{-}\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{-}
and
(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A})_{+}=\mathsf{B}_{+}\mathsf{A}_{+}
.

(iii)

If
\mathsf{A}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(E(G),F(G))
and
\mathsf{A}^{-1}
preserves lower support, then
\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{-}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(r^{-}\dot{E}(\overline{G_{-}}),r^{-}\dot{F}(\overline{G_{-}}))
and
\mathsf{A}_{+}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(\overline{E}(G_{+}),\overline{F}(G_{+}))
with
(\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{-})^{-1}=(\mathsf{A}^{-1})_{-}^{\boldsymbol{\cdot}}=:\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{-}^{-1}
and
(\mathsf{A}_{+})^{-1}=(\mathsf{A}^{-1})_{+}:=\mathsf{A}^{-1}_{+}
.

Proof.

(i)

The assertion for
\dot{\mathsf{A}}_{-}
is trivial.
For the assertion about
\mathsf{A}_{+}
, let
u\in\overline{E}(G_{+})
. For any
U\in E(G)
such that
r^{+}U=u
, it holds
\mathsf{A}_{+}u=r^{+}\mathsf{A}U
, and thus

\displaystyle\|\mathsf{A}_{+}u\|_{\overline{F}(G_{+})}

\displaystyle=\inf\{\|V\|_{F(G)}\mid V\in F(G),r^{+}V=\mathsf{A}_{+}u\}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathsf{A}U\|_{F(G)}\leq\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathcal{L}(E(G),F(G))}\|U\|_{E(G)}.

Since the estimate holds for every extension
U\in E
of
u
, we conclude

\|\mathsf{A}_{+}u\|_{\overline{F}(G_{+})}\leq\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathcal{L}(E(G),F(G))}\inf\{\|U\|_{E(G)}\mid U\in E(G),r^{+}U=u\}=\|\mathsf{A}\|_{\mathcal{L}(E,F)}\|u\|_{\overline{E}(G_{+})}.

(ii)

By definition,
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}\in{\mathcal{L}}(E(G),H(G))
preserves lower support.
By the previous point, we thus have
(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A})_{+}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\overline{E}(G_{+}),\overline{H}(G_{+}))
,
\mathsf{A}_{+}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\overline{E}(G_{+}),\overline{F}(G_{+}))
, and
\mathsf{B}_{+}\in{\mathcal{L}}(\overline{F}(G_{+}),\overline{H}(G_{+}))
.
Moreover, for
u\in\overline{E}(G_{+})
and
U\in E(G)
with
r^{+}U=u
we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{B}_{+}\mathsf{A}_{+}u=\mathsf{B}_{+}r^{+}\mathsf{A}U=r^{+}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}U=(\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A})_{+}u,

where we have used that
r^{+}V=v
for
V:=\mathsf{A}U\in F(G)
and
v:=r^{+}\mathsf{A}U\in\overline{F}(G_{+})
.

(iii)

Follows directly from the previous point with
\mathsf{B}:=\mathsf{A}^{-1}
.
∎

In view of Proposition
4.8
, if
\mathsf{A}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathrm{iso}}(H^{\mu}_{\perp}(G),L^{2}_{\perp}(G))
preserves lower support, then one can consider the equation

Proposition 4.14
.

Let

,

\mathbb{C}_{+}

as claimed.

(i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{(m)}_{ij}:=\begin{cases}b^{(i)}_{j-1}&\text{if }i\in\{1,2\},\\

where

(ii)

\chi_{2}

\displaystyle:=T_{i-1}(\mu+\nu),\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,4+m\},

\displaystyle s_{i}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}-\chi_{i},&\text{if }i\in\{1,2\},\\

Proposition 5.1
.

For
\mathsf{B}_{1}
and
\mathsf{B}_{2}
as in (
27
), consider the associated extended boundary matrix
\mathcal{B}
in Definition
4.19
.
Then
\mathsf{B}_{1}
and
\mathsf{B}_{2}
verify the Lopatinskiĭ-Shapiro conditions for
D
if and only if
\det\mathcal{B}(\tau,\xi^{\prime})\neq 0
for all
(\tau,\xi^{\prime})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
(\tau,\xi^{\prime})\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
We have
\det\mathcal{B}(\tau,\xi^{\prime})=0
if and only if the rows of
\mathcal{B}(\tau,\xi^{\prime})
are linearly dependent.
This is the case if and only if there exist
c_{1},\ldots,c_{4}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle c_{1}B_{1}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},z)+c_{2}B_{2}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},z)=(c_{3}+c_{4}\mathsf{i}z)D^{+}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},z)\quad\text{for all }z\in\mathbb{C},

that is, if and only if
B_{1}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},\cdot)
and
B_{2}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},\cdot)
are linearly dependent modulo
D^{+}(\tau,\xi^{\prime},\cdot)
.
