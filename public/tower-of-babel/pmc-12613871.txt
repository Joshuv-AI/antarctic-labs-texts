Different phytoplankton distributions and assembly processes in marine water masses with contrasting nutrient conditions
Authors: Zhenyuan Lei, Chuanlun Zhang, Lei He, Wei Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhytoplankton play a vital role in global primary production and carbon fixation in marine ecosystems. A comprehensive understanding of their distribution patterns and assembly mechanisms under different nutrient conditions is essential for phytoplankton ecology. However, research in this field remains insufficient.ResultsThis study used environmental DNA (eDNA) technology targeting the 23S rRNA gene of phytoplankton to investigate the community structure and assembly processes of phytoplankton (algae and cyanobacteria) in the East China Sea. The analysis identified 224 phytoplankton species, including 72 Bacillariophyta, 44 Cyanobacteria, and 34 Chlorophyta. Based on the relative proportion of each phytoplankton 23S rRNA gene in the sequencing data, Bacillariophyta and Dinoflagellata were found to be predominated in the nutrient-rich Changjiang Diluted Water (CDW), while cyanobacteria dominated in the low-nutrient Kuroshio Branch Current (KBC). The distribution of phytoplankton was significantly influenced by salinity and nutrient levels. Deterministic processes primarily governed community assembly in both the CDW and the KBC. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed stronger phytoplankton interactions in the KBC compared to the CDW, suggesting enhanced cooperative or competitive dynamics. The CDW network, on the other hand, exhibited more modules, likely due to greater environmental heterogeneity.ConclusionsThis study highlights the effectiveness of eDNA technology in elucidating phytoplankton community structure and assembly mechanisms across diverse nutrient environments. These findings enhance our understanding of the ecological impacts of relatively high nutrient concentrations on phytoplankton in a changing ocean.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04437-y.Keywords: 23S rRNA gene, Phytoplankton, Assembly, East china sea

Background

Phytoplankton constitutes a remarkably diverse group of single-celled bacteria and eukaryotes that serve as the foundation of the food web in the marine ecosystem. Phytoplankton are the most important primary producers, accounting for about 50% of the net primary production of the global biosphere [1, 2]. They also influence the abundance and diversity of other marine organisms and biogeochemical cycles [3–9]. The phytoplankton community in the natural environment functions like a forest, with the entire community efficiently utilizing environmental resources, demonstrating immense carbon fixation potential. During favorable conditions, such as during algal blooms in spring and summer, the dominant phytoplankton reach their highest photosynthetic carbon fixation capacity, thereby enabling the ocean to act as a carbon sink [10]. Their biodiversity contributes to the stability, resilience, and functioning of marine ecosystems [11, 12]. With increasing nutrient inputs from human activities, offshore eutrophication is becoming more severe, with significant impacts on the phytoplankton community [13]. Furthermore, it has been documented that elevated levels of salinity have the capacity to induce osmotic stress in the process of phytoplankton assembly [14]. Notwithstanding these advances, a paucity of comparative research exists examining phytoplankton biodiversity and community assembly dynamics under contrasting nutrient regimes.

Historically, morphological classification using optical microscopy has been used to identify phytoplankton biodiversity [15]. Despite its continued utilization, this technique is encumbered by several limitations. Phytoplankton population structure analysis by morphological identification is inherently time-consuming and challenging, and it often overlooks smaller phytoplankton sizes[16, 17]. In recent years, molecular methods, which include eDNA metabarcoding techniques, have been frequently used to study phytoplankton communities In recent years, molecular methods, especially the ribosomal DNA genes have been identified as the standard target genes to investigate the community structure and diversity of microbes[18–22]. Chloroplasts are comprised of a small 30S subunit (SSU) and a large 50S subunit (LSU) [23]. For phytoplankton, primers targeting plastid 23S rRNA, plastid 16S rRNA, and 18S rRNA have been used to investigate their community structure [18, 24, 25]. The most frequently utilized genes are the SSU genes, like the 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA genes. The LSU genes encode more phylogenetic information due to their greater length and higher sequence variance [26, 27], and include the 23S rRNA gene, which could provide more phylogenetic and taxonomic information of those phytoplankton[27–30].

The assembly mechanisms of phytoplankton communities represent a crucial but less understood aspect of phytoplankton ecology [31]. Currently, Two key and complementary frameworks are employed to describe and comprehend phytoplankton community assembly: deterministic processes based on niche theory and stochastic processes rooted in neutral theory[32, 33]. Niche-based theory posits that communities emerge from a combination of deterministic biotic factors, such as species interactions like predation and competition, influenced by varying habitat preferences and community fitness, as well as abiotic factors, including environmental variables like salinity, pH, and nutrients [34, 35]. Conversely, the neutral theory asserts that community structures are determined by stochastic processes, encompassing birth and death, migration, speciation, and dispersal limitations, where a stochastic balance exists between the losses and gains of taxa [36, 37]. Prior research has indicated that deterministic processes play a relatively more significant role in prokaryotic community assembly in mangroves compared to stochastic processes [38, 39]. Nevertheless, there have been few attempts to analyze the relative impact of stochastic versus deterministic processes in phytoplankton community in marine environments under different nutrient conditions.

The East China Sea (ECS) is a marginal sea that exhibits significant interaction with the surrounding continental margin and is profoundly influenced by nutrients discharged from the Yangtze River [40]. Within the eastern ECS, the shelf water intermixes with the offshore Kuroshio water[41]. In the western ECS, the input from the Yangtze River has been shown to alter the nutrient the nutrient ratios subsequent to its interaction with ambient saline water [42]. This phenomenon exemplifies the unique characteristics of the water mass in the ECS. In recent decades, majority of investigations in the ECS have been conducted using conventional optical microscopy techniques[43]. Prorocentrum donghaiense, Skeletonema costatum, Thalassionema nitzschioides, Paralia sulcata, Chaetoceros lorenzianus were found to be the most abundant species in the ECS. Shih et al. employed high-throughput sequencing combined with metabarcoding techniques to analyze the biodiversity of haptophytes in the East China Sea[44]. However, the assembly mechanisms and controlling factors of those phytoplankton communities within the diverse water masses of the ECS still largely unrevealed.

In this study, our objectives were to: (i) investigate the phytoplankton community structure in the coastal waters of the East China Sea using primers targeting plastid 23S rRNA; (ii) analyze the collected environmental factors to explore the influence of these environmental characteristics on the phytoplankton community structure; (iii) illustrate the co-occurrence network of two different water bodies and identify potential interactions in the phytoplankton community.

Methods & materials

Samples collection and the measurement of environmental factors

From 27 August to 5 September 2015, we collected water samples at three different depths at eight stations in section A6 of the ECS (Fig. 1 A), as well as the ECS 150825–1 sample at the surface at the departure point of the voyage. The surface, middle, and bottom layers are represented by B, Z, and D respectively. Time series surface samples were also collected from the surface at stations A1-2, A6-8, and A12-4. CTD was used to measure salinity, temperature, depth, and dissolved oxygen concentration of each sample. We used a continuous flow analytical system (AAII, Bran Luebbe, Germany) to measure nutrients in 45 mL of seawater filtered through 0.22 μm polyethersulfone (PES) membranes. We also filtered 800 mL of seawater with Whatman GF/F glass fiber filters (nominal pore size ~ 0.7 μm) for eDNA analysis of phytoplankton.

A Sampling stations distribution in the ECS displayed by Ocean Date View. Environmental factors were measured at all sampling sites, and the 23S rRNA gene was extracted for subsequent analysis. B Salinity distribution in section A6

DNA extraction and bioinformatics analysis

Total DNA on membranes from all samples was extracted using the FastDNA soil genome DNA extraction kit MP bio. The extracted DNA was amplified using p23SrV_f1 and p23SrV_r1 primers as described by Sherwood & Presting [24]. The obtained gene fragments were sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform at Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Trimming and selection of high-quality sequences were performed using QIIME 2. We performed DADA2 noise reduction on the obtained sequence to eliminate low quality results (mass fraction < 20), primers, etc., to minimise the impact of errors. We obtained a table of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and representative sequences. All ASVs were annotated using BLAST. We selected all ASVs annotated as phytoplankton for the following statistical analysis.

Data analysis

Community composition was visualized using the R package “heatmap” [45]. The α-diversity indices (Chao, Shannon) were calculated using QIMME 2 and the R package “vegan”, and significance was tested with the Kruskal–Wallis test[46, 47]. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distance was visualized using the R package “vegan” [46], and permutation multiple variance analysis (PERMANOVA) was performed on β diversity results using QIIME 2.

For redundancy analysis (RDA), the species matrix was constructed using the relative sequence abundances of phytoplankton ASVs. While the environmental matrix included salinity, temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, dissolved organic carbon, and nutrient concentrations. Before analysis, all environmental factors were standardized[46, 47]. Significance was assessed by permutation tests (999 runs).

To quantify the relative contribution of stochastic and deterministic processes to the phytoplankton community assembly process, we analyse the null model (999 randomisations) [48]. We also computed the beta nearest taxon index (βNTI) and the Bray–Curtis-based Raup-Crick metric (RCbray) using the “picante” package (version 1.8.2) [49]. If βNTI values are between −2 and 2, and RCbray values are less than −0.95, this indicates homogenous dispersal. If RCbray values are greater than 0.95, it means dispersal limitation. If RCbray values are greater than −0.95 and less than 0.95, it means drift. For the βNTI, if it is less than −2, it means homogeneous selection, while if it is more than 2, it means heterogeneous selection [50].

As the correlation coefficient is widely applied to represent the strength of interspecific co-occurrence, co-occurrence networks analysis were used to describe those phytoplankton ASVs’ co-occurrence patterns [51, 52]. The phytoplankton ASVs present in more than 3 samples were used to construct the co-occurrence network. Spearman correlations were calculated, and only robust (|R|> 0.6, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) correlations were retained [53]. Visualization was done using Gephi 0.9.2. The correlation coefficient is commonly used to quantify the strength of co-occurrence between two species; here, we further employ co-occurrence networks to characterize the co-occurrence patterns of phytoplankton communities. The correlation coefficient is commonly used to represent the strength between two species’ co-occurrence pattern; we thus also use co-occurrence network here to describe their co-occurrence patterns of those phytoplankton communities.

Based on within-module connectivity (Zi) and among-module connectivity (Pi), nodes are classified into four types: network hubs (Zi > 2.5 and Pi > 0.62), module hubs (Zi > 2.5 and Pi < 0.62), connectors (Zi < 2.5 and Pi > 0.62) and peripherals (Zi < 2.5 and Pi < 0.62). In general, network hubs, module hubs and connectors are classified as keystone taxa [54–56].

Results

Environmental measurement in the East China Sea

Based on the salinity data, we infer that the nearshore side of Sect. 6 is mainly affected by the Changjiang Diluted Water, while the other side is mainly affected by the Kuroshio Branch Current (Figs. 1 B, S1). Therefore, the sampling sites on Sect. 6 were divided into CDW (affected by the Changjiang Diluted Water) and KBC (affected by the Kuroshio Branch Current) groups with the 31‰ boundary[57, 58]. The remaining samples were also divided according to this standard.

The results of the environmental measurements at each sampling site are presented in Table S1. Temperatures at all sampling sites ranged from 19.4℃ (A6-8 Z) to 27.5℃ (A6-11 B). Dissolved oxygen varied from 2.2 mg/L (A6-8 Z) to 9.2 mg/L (A6-6 B). pH varied from 8.1 (A6-8 Z) to 8.6 (A6-8 7:30 PM). Salinity increased from 13.2 (A6-3 Z) to 34.4 (A6-9 Z) from the nearshore to the ocean. For nutrients, the distribution characteristics are opposite to those of salinity. For example, nitrate increased from 0.90 μm (A6-8 7:30 PM) to 37.20 μM (ECS 150825–1), and phosphate increased from almost undetectable to 0.75 μm (ECS 150825–1). Silicate also increased from 0.70 μm (A6-11B) to 39.17 μm (ECS 150825–1). We also found that except for phosphate, there were significant differences (< 0.05) in the remaining nutrients between CDW and KBC (Fig. S2).

Sequence recovery in the plastid 23S rRNA

We obtained 907,291 sequences from 38 samples, with the number of sequences per sample ranging from 11,536 (A12-4 3:00 AM) to 36,202 (A1-2 10:00 PM). Among these sequences, 794,816 sequences were identified as cyanobacteria or eukaryotic algae. The sequence counts per sample ranged from 6,373 to 34,217. This represents 87% of the entire sequence data set, which is a higher proportion than many other data set targeting 18S rRNA gene [59]. Because primers targeting the 18S rRNA gene always contain many other animals or fungi. This evidence substantiates the efficacy of the primer targeting the 23S rRNA gene, demonstrating a high recovery rate.

Differences in ASVs composition among different water masses

The composition of the phytoplankton community varied between the different water masses (Fig. 2). From A6-2 to A6-11, the proportion of cyanobacteria increased. We drew a heatmap for the top 50 ASVs, representing 77% of the total community (Fig. 3). By clustering the samples according to species composition, we can see that the samples are divided into two different branches. Comparing the grouping according to salinity, we noticed that the samples of KBC are in the different branch from the samples of CDW. This proves that there is a good agreement between the clustering results based on salinity and species composition in the heatmap.

Heat map of the relative abundance of top 50 phytoplankton ASVs

According to the results of species annotation and heatmap, we found that there are some differences in composition between the KBC and the CDW. For example, the abundance of Bacillariophyta (ASV6, ASV13, ASV47, ASV24, ASV16, ASV42, ASV49, ASV54) are all more abundant in the CDW than in the KBC. α and β diversity patterns among different water masses

We drew the rank-abundance distribution curve (Fig. S3 B), and we noticed that many samples from the KBC have more ASVs and the distribution is relatively flat. Many samples in the CDW have a slightly lower abundance of ASVs and a steeper distribution. As mentioned above, this means that the species distribution of the KBC is relatively uniform. However, there was no significant difference in the Shannon index, Chao, and other indices between the two groups (Fig. 4 A).

Chao index and Shannon index (A) of CDW and KBC; B: β diversity and PCoA visualization based on Bray–Curtis distance

The results of β diversity calculated from the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index are shown in Fig. 4 B, which shows that there is a significant difference between the KBC and the CDW (p = 0.01). The first two eigenvalues explained 29.12% and 11.03% of the total variation, respectively. In addition, according to the visualization results, the phytoplankton community structure changes less in the KBC, while it changes more in the CDW.

Relationships between phytoplankton communities and environmental factors

RDA is used to assess the relationship between phytoplankton communities and environmental factors (Fig. 5). Axis 1 and axis 2 explain 41.75% and 12.06% of the total variance of the phytoplankton community, respectively. Firstly, we can see that salinity is one of the most important environmental factors influencing changes in the phytoplankton community. Samples from the KBC group are concentrated in the area with high salinity, while samples from the CDW group are concentrated in the area with relatively low salinity. Secondly, nutrients such as nitrate, phosphate, and silicate, are also environmental factors that significantly affect changes in the phytoplankton community. Samples from the CDW group are concentrated in the area with higher nutrients, while samples from the KBC group are the opposite. However, there is no significant correlation between temperature, DO, TN and DOC with the distribution of the phytoplankton community.

RDA results based on phytoplankton community and environmental factors show the relationship between them in the ECS. (*: 0.01 < p < 0.05, **: p ≤ 0.01)

Furthermore, an analysis of the distribution patterns between phyla shows that Bacillariophyta and Chlorophyta exhibit a negative correlation with salinity and a positive correlation with nutrient availability. Conversely, cyanobacteria display a positive correlation with salinity and a negative relationship with nutrient concentrations. Additionally, Cryptophyceae are observed to have a negative relationship with salinity.

The assembly processes of the phytoplankton community

Our NCM result shows that the immigration rate of the phytoplankton community was estimated to be 17 (CDW group) and 41 (KBC group) (Fig. 6 A). We used a null model based on the βNTI and RCbray to explore the relative contributions of stochastic and deterministic processes. The result shows that deterministic processes (58.33% and 64.84% of CDW group and KBC group, respectively) contributed more to phytoplankton assembly than stochastic processes (Fig. 6 C). For stochastic processes among the two groups, the percentage of homogenizing dispersal was greater in CDW (13.04%) than in the rare community (6.59%) in KBC. The undominated processes are about the same in two groups (28.62% in CDW and 28.57% in KBC). For deterministic processes, the percentages of heterogeneous selection and homogeneous selection are both higher in KBC (heterogeneous selection: 52.75%, homogeneous selection: 12.09%) than in CDW (heterogeneous selection: 49.64%, homogeneous selection: 8.70%).

Co-occurrence patterns of the phytoplankton community

The co-occurrence network constructed for strong (r ≥|0.6|) and significant (p < 0.05) correlations between the relative abundance of ASVs in CDW consists of 162 nodes and 739 edges, and in KBC consists of 178 nodes and 1635 edges (Fig. 7). The c-occurrence networks were used to determine the co-occurrence patterns of phytoplankton in different areas. In CDW, the top five classes with high values of eigenvector centrality are Cryptophyceae (0.332), Dictyochophyceae (0.304), Coscinodiscophyceae (0.265), Dinophyceae (0.253), and Raphidophyceae (0.207). In KBC, the top five classes are Chlorodendrophyceae (0.865), Trebouxiophyceae (0.752), Chloropicophyceae (0.584), Dictyochophyceae (0.472), and Dinophyceae (0.439).

The colors of the nodes in C and D indicate different classes. Edges on behalf of the strong correlation between ASVs (|r|> 0.60) and significant (FDR correction of p < 0.05). The size of each node represents the eigenvector centrality

In the CDW, 4 nodes of Coscinodiscophyceae, one node of Haptophyta, one node of Crytophyceae and two unclassified nodes were identified as connectors. One node of Coscinodiscophyceae and one node of Crytophyceae were identified as module hubs. In the KBC, 2 nodes of Cyanobacteria, 3 nodes of Bacillariophyceae, 3 nodes of Mediophyceae, one node of Coscinodiscophyceae, 5 nodes of Haptophyta, 2 nodes of Cryptophyceae and 2 nodes of Dinophyceae were identified as connectors. One node of Cyanobacteria, one node of Coscinodiscophyceae, one node of Haptophyta and one node of Dinophyceae were identified as module hubs.

In both networks there were more positive connections than negative ones. However, there were more negative connections in the KBC than in the CDW. The number of nodes in both networks was almost the same, but the average degree values in the KBC were higher than in the CDW, about twice as high. This meant that the interactions between the phytoplankton in the KBC were stronger than those in the CDW. It was concluded that the network in the KBC is more complex and may be more stable than that in the CDW.

Discussion

The efficacy of metabarcoding method on phytoplankton community analysis

Most previous studies have employed techniques based on microscopic cell counts, flow cytometry, or phytoplankton diagnostic pigments to analyze phytoplankton communities. The majority of studies utilizing eDNA have employed the small subunit (SSU) genes, including the 16S rRNA gene and the 18S rRNA gene. With the development of sequencing technology and primers, the 23S rRNA gene has gradually been used in the analysis of phytoplankton communities.

The conventional methods have both advantages and disadvantages. The microscopic method results in the observation of a greater number of Bacillariophyta, possibly because their larger size makes them easier to identify. However, this approach tends to underestimate the abundance of Cyanobacteria and Cryptophyta, which are relatively small in size. Pigment analysis can also be used to determine taxonomic composition by binning pigments into different taxonomic groups. However, pigment content of phytoplankton varies according to physiological status, mainly as a function of light levels and nutrient status [60]. Therefore, a specific matrix of environmental parameters and a matrix of phytoplankton pigment content are needed to further characterize the phytoplankton community.

The development of molecular techniques has led to the 18S rRNA gene and the 16S rRNA gene becoming the preferred methods for analyzing the composition of phytoplankton communities [61]. Recently, LSU gene with more phylogenetic information were also reported be used to infer the phylogeny and taxonomy of phytoplankton [62, 63]. As evidenced by the present study and previous research, sequencing the plastid 23S rRNA gene in environmental samples can effectively recover the diversity description of phytoplankton communities (Fig. S2 A).

The application of 23S rRNA gene sequencing is currently constrained by the limited size of the reference database [30]. Nevertheless, future work could focus more on expanding the 23S rRNA reference database by incorporating more representative sequences from diverse phytoplankton. In addition, multi-marker approaches, such as combining 16S, 18S with 23S rRNA may provide more information, thereby improving taxonomic resolution and ecological interpretation. Such integrative strategies would help overcome the current limitations and further enhance the accuracy of phytoplankton community analysis.

A salient finding of the present study was the revelation that the plastid 23S rRNA gene provides an accurate description of phytoplankton communities in the East China Sea. The distribution of phytoplankton, as determined by the 23S rRNA gene, exhibited a high degree of similarity with the findings of other studies employing divergent methodologies. The most prevalent species identified in the 23S rRNA gene database were found to be similar to those previously identified in the East China Sea. In conjunction with environmental factors, we identified significant factors that promote the assembly of phytoplankton communities. Our findings revealed that the phytoplankton community exhibited niche partitioning among different water masses. By integrating sequence data with environmental metadata, the plastid 23S rRNA gene provides a valuable tool for analyzing phytoplankton communities and understanding the factors that regulate their assembly.

The community composition of the phytoplankton in the East China Sea

In the ECS, changes in phytoplankton community structure and abundance are linked to changes in environmental factors, particularly changes in salinity and nutrient. The characterization of this relationship using 23S rRNA is also in good agreement with previous findings. Firstly, salinity, nutrients, and suspended particulate matter were identified as the key environmental variables affecting the phytoplankton community in this region during summer, as previously reported [64–66]. In this study, we also found that the phytoplankton community was also significantly affected by salinity and some nutrients like nitrate, phosphate, and silicate (Fig. 5).

In environments characterized by high nutrient availability, larger phytoplankton communities, such as those comprising Bacillariophyta, often prevail. Bacillariophyta and Dinoflagellata were also found to be the most prevalent species in the Yangtze estuary and the East China Sea coast [43, 67]. Cloern and Dufford [68] also demonstrated that the nutrient-rich environment of San Francisco Bay was dominated by those large phytoplankton. Conversely, in more stable, nutrient-poor waters, smaller phytoplankton such as cyanobacteria tend to dominate due to their lower nutrient requirements and higher rates of nutrient exchange. Furuya et al. observed that smaller phytoplankton, including Cyanobacteria and Cryptophyta dominated more than 80% of in the nutrient-poor Kuroshio waters, while larger Bacillariophyta dominated in the estuaries [69]. Frame and Lessard also found in their summer survey at the mouth of the Columbia River that Bacillariophyta can occupy more than 70% of the total biomass [70].

The phytoplankton community composition exhibited substantial variation between the water masses examined in this study (Figs. 2, 3, and 4). Specifically, in the Changjiang Diluted Water with lower salinity and higher nutrients, the abundance of Bacillariophyta and Dinoflagellates was found to be significantly higher than in the Kuroshio Branch Current. Conversely, the Kuroshio Branch Current exhibited a preponderance of Cyanobacteria. This finding indicates that Bacillariophyta and Dinoflagellata prevail in nearshore waters, while Cyanobacteria predominate the offshore waters. The proportion of the Bacillariophyta and Dinoflagellates exhibited an increase with rising nutrients and a decrease in salinity, while the opposite trend was observed for Cyanobacteria. This finding aligns with the earlier reports in this region, which utilized microscopic analysis and exhibited similar patterns in other areas [43, 68–70]. It should be noted that in this study, we only obtained relative proportions of those phytoplankton taxa from 23S rRNA gene sequencing, and absolute abundance or biomass was not measured, which may limit a more comprehensive understanding of community structure. Future studies incorporating absolute quantification will provide deeper insights.

Deterministic processes dominate the assembly of phytoplankton communities

The null model demonstrated that the deterministic processes and stochastic processes collectively influence the composition of phytoplankton communities in the ECS, while the deterministic processes were more significant than stochastic processes. In previous studies, deterministic processes were found to play a more significant role in microbial assembly than stochastic processes at larger spatial scales (~ 12,000 km), whereas stochastic processes were more important at smaller spatial scales (~ 1,500 km) [37, 52, 71–73]. However, the present study reveals a divergent pattern, with deterministic processes exhibiting heightened importance within the CDW and KBC (at a scale of approximately 200 km). This phenomenon may be attributed to the significant environmental heterogeneities in this region. This may be because environmental factors play an important role in this area, and their changes are large in both groups, resulting in a larger contribution of deterministic processes. This observation is consistent with the findings of previous studies. Some previous studies showed similar results. Shi et al. (2018) found that a large pH gradient can have a large effect on deterministic processes in soil bacterial communities [74]. Concurrently, Lin et al. (2023) determined that substantial alterations in environmental factors within the Pearl River system gave rise to an augmented contribution of deterministic processes in archaeal communities [75]. The environmental heterogeneity in the ECS similarly led to the significant contribution of the deterministic processes on the phytoplankton assembly (Fig. 6).

The disturbances were also found to have impact on the stochastic/deterministic balance of the microbial communities. According to Dini-Andreote et al. (2015), there are 3 phases in the development of a microbial community [76]. From phase 1 to phase 3, with the disturbances decreasing, the stochastic processes become less important. In our study, there are always more disturbances in the CDW group than in the KBC group, and its environment keeps changing over time compared to the more stable environment like KBC. This causes the proportion of stochastic process was higher in CDW than in KBC. Additionally, because the community composed of widespread species is highly adaptable to salinity and nutrients, and nutrients decrease while salinity increases from the estuary to the open sea, homogeneous selection becomes more intense with the succession of water masses from the estuary to the open sea. At the same time, unique species has weak adaptability to the change in salinity and nutrients, which means they are greatly affected by environmental changes. Heterogeneous selection reflects the effects of environment on microbes. Therefore, environmental heterogeneity has a greater impact on them, which gradually develops heterogeneous selection.

Co-occurrence networks reveal the different interaction patterns of phytoplankton communities among different water masses

In our study, the network properties were interpreted within the framework of ecological network theory, which allowed us to infer interaction patterns and community stability in the two water masses of the East China Sea.

Interspecific interactions play an important role in maintaining the stability of ecological communities [77, 78]. From our results, we can see that although the CDW had a higher nutrient concentration, the results of co-occurrence network construction showed that the interactions among phytoplankton in the CDW were not as strong as those in the KBC (Table S3). This may be due to the lower nutrient concentration, which requires phytoplankton to obtain more resources and more cooperation or competition [79, 80]. It has been confirmed that networks with higher complexity tend to be more stable, implying that stronger interspecific interactions may enhance community stability [81, 82]. The modularity of the CDW network is higher than that of the KBC network. Because niche differentiation means that phytoplankton can use different strategies to exploit resources in the environment, they can avoid competition, which is beneficial for phytoplankton in eutrophic environments [83].

Keystone taxa are crucial for the maintenance of network structure. Their disappearance can lead to the collapse of modules and networks [84, 85]. In our study, there were more keystone taxa in the KBC than that in the CDW. In CDW, there are 4 out of 8 connectors belonging to Bacillariophyta. In KBC, there are 2 out of 18 connectors belonging to Cyanobacteria, and 7 connectors belonging to Bacillariophyta. Larger phytoplankton, such as Bacillariophyta, contribute significantly to the process of carbon sequestration in regions with high nutrient levels [86]. In contrast, smaller phytoplankton like Cyanobacteria are highly efficient in nutrient-poor waters[87, 88]. The dissimilarities in phytoplankton composition and interactions observed between the CDW and KBC may affect the overall carbon fixation capacity in these water masses, with the potential to impact global carbon cycling. We also found that the number of keystone taxa had a similar trend with network complexity and stability, which was shown in a previous study [89].

While co-occurrence networks provide insights into potential interactions among phytoplankton, they should be interpreted with caution because correlations can also arise from environmental drivers or stochastic processes such as mortality. Despite these limitations, the overall network structure remains valuable for understanding its ecological functioning.

Beyond above, the observed differences in network complexity and keystone taxa suggest distinct strategies of community assembly in nutrient-rich versus nutrient-poor waters. In the CDW, higher modularity but weaker overall interactions may indicate that resource availability allows phytoplankton groups to occupy relatively independent niches, reducing the need for tightly coupled interactions. In contrast, the higher connectivity and keystone diversity in the KBC highlight the importance of cooperative or competitive interactions in maintaining community function under oligotrophic conditions. These patterns imply that offshore phytoplankton networks may be more vulnerable to disruptions in key taxa. Importantly, such structural differences could modulate ecosystem responses to global change. Increasing eutrophication causing by human may further weaken interactions in nearshore waters. Therefore, these insights underscore the value of network approaches in predicting how phytoplankton communities may respond to climate change and anthropogenic nutrient inputs.

Conclusion

An eDNA method targeting on the 23S rRNA gene was successfully utilized to analyze the composition and assembly processes of the phytoplankton communities in two water mass with contrasting nutrient conditions. Bacillariophyta and Dinoflagellata were prevail in the CDW, while smaller phytoplankton, such as cyanobacteria, was found to be dominated in the KBC. Due to high environmental heterogeneities in this region, the assembly of both the CDW and KBC phytoplankton communities is mainly driven by deterministic processes. The phytoplankton in the KBC were found to be more intricate than that of the CDW, suggesting the enhanced cooperation or competition in the low nutrient concentrations. Our study exemplifies the efficacy of eDNA techniques in studying phytoplankton community structure and shed light on revealing the mechanisms of phytoplankton community assembly in diverse nutrient environments.
