Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5 : Evaluating Overlap Handling for Full - Duplex Speech Models
Guan-Ting Lin, Shih-Yun Shan Kuan, Qirui Wang, Jiachen Lian, Tingle Li Hung-yi Lee

Abstract
While full - duplex speech agents promise natural, low - latency human–machine interaction by concurrently processing input and output speech, overlap management remains under - evaluated. We introduce Full - Duplex - Bench v1.5 , a modular, fully automated benchmark that simulates four overlap scenarios—user interruption, listener backchannel, side conversation, and ambient speech. Our framework supports both open - source and commercial models, offering a comprehensive, extensible metric suite—categorical dialogue behaviors, stop and response latency, prosodic adaptation, and perceived speech quality—that can be tailored to application - specific criteria. Benchmarking five state - of - the - art agents reveals two principal strategies— repair - first rapid yielding versus continuity - first sustained flow—and highlights scenario - dependent performance trends. The open - source design enables seamless extension with new audio assets, languages, and deployment contexts, empowering practitioners to customize and accelerate the evaluation of robust full - duplex speech systems 1 1 1 Code and data will be available at https://github.com/DanielLin94144/Full-Duplex-Bench .

1
Introduction

Full
-
duplex speech models—systems that can speak and listen concurrently—are reshaping the landscape of spoken human–computer interaction. By enabling simultaneous speech production and perception, they eliminate the rigid wait–listen–respond cycle, allowing for more fluid exchanges that support backchannels, mid
-
utterance corrections, and dynamic user engagement
[
1
]
. These capabilities are particularly valuable in settings such as in
-
car navigation, real
-
time translation, clinical triage, and hands
-
free environments, where responsiveness and natural interaction are essential.

However, overlapping speech is not an edge case but a defining characteristic of natural dialogue: over 40% of turns involve some degree of overlap
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. These overlaps arise from a variety of sources
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
: (i)
user interruptions
or barge-ins, where someone speaks over others, violating their turn to speak; (ii)
listener backchannels
(e.g., “uh
-
huh”, “right”) that signal attention but do not claim the floor; (iii)
side conversations
, where the user briefly addresses someone else while the model is speaking; and (iv)
ambient or broadcast speech
that shares the acoustic channel but is not directed at the system. If poorly managed, these phenomena can result in truncated responses, repeated outputs, or awkward silences—undermining interaction quality and user experience.

Despite the prevalence and importance of such phenomena, most existing benchmarks target single
-
turn or strictly half
-
duplex paradigms and fail to probe overlapping behaviors
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Human evaluation offers contextual insight but is labor-intensive and not scalable. Corpus-level methods based on voice activity detection (VAD), inter-pausal units (IPUs), pause statistics, and floor-transfer offsets (FTO)
[
15
]
scale well but mask fine-grained, scenario-specific behaviors and overlook semantic appropriateness. Classifier-based frameworks such as Talking Turns
[
16
]
offer automation but suffer from limited generalizability due to corpus-specific training and reliance on user study annotations.

The original
Full
-
Duplex
-
Bench

[
17
]
took a first step toward systematic overlap evaluation by focusing on user interruptions. We extend this effort in
Full
-
Duplex
-
Bench v1.5
, a modular benchmarking suite that evaluates four overlap scenarios:
user interruption
,
user backchannel
,
user talking to others
, and
background speech
. Each interaction is conducted through the model’s production API, with automatic alignment and segmentation of pre-overlap event and post-overlap event regions. The framework is designed for extensibility: users can swap in different audio conditions, real human speech, or new languages by simply replacing assets and rerunning the pipeline. All code, metrics, and annotation schemas are open-sourced for reproducibility and community-driven extensions.

Our evaluation captures both content and prosodic dimensions. We label system responses with dialogue behaviors—
Respond
,
Resume
,
Uncertain
,
Off-target
, and
Silent
—and compute signal-level features including
pitch
,
intensity
,
perceived naturalness
, as well as
stop latency
and
response latency
. This multidimensional view surfaces model trade-offs between responsiveness and coherence, highlights stylistic adaptations under different overlap types, and reveals robustness patterns that static, turn-by-turn benchmarks fail to expose.

-

-

[
21

-

-

-

2.2
Evaluation Frameworks for Full
-
Duplex Spoken Dialogue Systems

Most existing speech benchmarks
[
28
,
29
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
focus on single
-
turn responses with minimal contextual information and enforce a strictly half
-
duplex, turn
-
by
-
turn interaction paradigm. Consequently, they fail to capture genuine full
-
duplex phenomena—such as user interruptions, backchannels, address shifts, and background chatter—within realistic conversational loops.

To evaluate full-duplex interaction, human evaluation remains the gold standard for contextual judgment, but it is prohibitively
time
-
consuming
and
non
-
reproducible at scale
. Alternatively, corpus
-
level studies rely on inter
-
pausal units (IPUs), pause statistics, and floor
-

[
15
]
, typically sampling 30 s of prompts, generating 90 s of continuations, detecting turn
-

-
specific behaviors and cannot capture semantic content. Talking Turns
[
16
]
trains a classifier on human–human conversation data to detect turn changes, backchannels, and interruptions, offering an automated alternative; however, its dependence on a specific training corpus limits generalizability, and its reliance on user
-
studies data further undermines reproducibility.

In response to these limitations, we extend Full
-
Duplex
-
Bench v1.0
[
17
]
by (1) adopting a unified, real
-
time streaming interaction between user and model, (2) defining four controlled overlap scenarios to probe distinct dialogue behaviors, and (3) introducing multidimensional metrics—behavior, timing, prosody, and speech quality —thereby enabling fine
-
grained, reproducible benchmarking of both open
-
weight and API
-
only SDMs.

3.1
Interaction Framework

To simulate user interaction with the speech model, we use pre-recorded speech as the input stream. The user speech is fed to the model in a streaming manner, while the model’s streaming output is simultaneously collected. The generated speech is saved for subsequent evaluation.

Importantly, this framework supports the evaluation of both open- and closed-source models, as it makes no assumptions about the model architecture. The input data can be customized to target specific scenarios, enabling focused evaluation of a model’s capabilities. The framework is flexible and extensible, allowing users to incorporate their own input data for broader or task-specific assessments.

Full
-
Duplex
-
Bench
-
v1.5
consists of multi
-
turn prompts rendered to audio, with controlled
overlap events
that test a speech model’s ability to manage overlap, disambiguate intent, and recover. Figure
1
illustrates the overview of the proposed framework.

Each scenario is parameterized by (1) context speech in the user channel as the initial query to trigger the speech model to respond, (2) overlap event timing relative to ongoing system speech, (3) content of the overlapping speech, and (4) speaker identity and acoustic channel (same vs. other speaker, near
-
vs. far
-
field). For every trial we segment the model’s speech into
pre-overlap event
and
post-overlap event
regions using the end time of the overlap event, then evaluate post
-
overlap behavior and acoustic/timing metrics.

To naturally trigger the overlap event, we begin by using only the initial context turn and measuring each model’s response latency and output speech duration to determine an appropriate timing for introducing the overlap speech. The results are presented in Table
1
. Based on the observed response latencies (1.18–2.25 seconds) and output durations (6.79–10.28 seconds), we insert a silence gap of approximately 4 seconds between the context and the onset of overlap speech. This timing ensures that the overlap reliably coincides with the model’s ongoing response, simulating realistic overlap scenarios.

3.2
Scenarios

We illustrate the following scenarios in Figure
2
.

3.2.1
User Interruption

Description.

The user overtly cuts in while the model is speaking (e.g., “wait, hold on—what was the second date?”). Interruptions overlap with the model speech with a follow-up statement to barge in. This scenario probes
turn-taking sensitivity
(stop latency), repair behavior (
Respond
vs.
Resume
), and acoustic re-entry capabilities critical in assistive and safety-critical domains where users must redirect immediately.

Rapid and accurate interruption handling is vital for hands
-
free systems (e.g., in-car assistants) and for users in emergency contexts, where any delay or misinterpretation can lead to serious consequences. This scenario thus benchmarks both the temporal responsiveness and the semantic flexibility of full
-
duplex agents.

Data.

We generate the overlapping interruption turns using the same speaker voice as the context turn, ensuring contextual relevance. There is no acoustic difference between the two turns in terms of environment or volume. The evaluation is based on 200 samples.

3.2.2
User Backchannel

Description.

Short listener backchannel feedback (e.g., “uh
-
huh”) is injected during system speech. Unlike interruptions, backchannels usually
do not request the floor
. The challenge is to avoid spurious repair: an over
-
eager model that halts or re
-
asks for confirmation will slow task progress.

Natural backchannel support enables smoother, more human
-
like interactions, particularly in long
-
running dialogues (e.g., tutoring systems). Minimizing spurious stalls preserves conversational flow and user satisfaction.

Data.

Following the acknowledgment phrases identified in
[
30
]
, we compile a list of short feedback tokens (e.g.,
yeah, uh
-
huh, mm
-
hmm, totally
). We use the same speaker voice to synthesize the context and the overlap backchannel turn. The test set includes 99 samples.

3.2.3
User Talking to Others

Description.

The user explicitly addresses a different human interlocutor while the model is active (e.g., “Sam—can you grab the file?”). Audio is mixed so the model hears both streams. Social appropriateness demands that the model recognize it is
not the addressee
and either remain silent or quietly resume its prior task without barging in. Multi
-
party environments such as meetings and shared households make this condition essential for deployment realism.

In group settings (e.g., conference calls, smart home environments), a model must distinguish between user-to-agent and user-to-other speech to avoid confusion, accidental activation, or privacy breaches.

Data.

We use the same speaker voice as the user to synthesize both the context turn and the overlap turn. For the overlap turn, the content is explicitly directed toward another person, starting with a non-system-related name, and semantically irrelevant to the context turn. To simulate the user speaking away from the model, we apply reverberation and acoustic filtering to the overlap turn: the overall volume is reduced by around 8 dB; a high-shelf filter attenuates frequencies above 4 kHz by 5 dB to reduce brightness while preserving formants; and two indoor reflections are added at 45 ms and 120 ms with respective attenuations of 6 dB and 12 dB. There are 100 samples in this scenario.

3.2.4
Background Speech

Description.

Ambient third
-
party talk (different voice, reverberant channel) overlaps the system’s output but is not directed at it.

Real-world environments (e.g., cafés, living rooms) often contain concurrent speech. Evaluating background tolerance ensures that agents maintain accuracy and user focus without being derailed by incidental noise.

Data.

We use a different speaker voice to synthesize the background speech, which overlaps the system’s output turn but is not directed at it. The content is unrelated to the context turn and is acoustically processed to simulate environmental background noise: the overall volume is reduced by 15 dB; a low-pass filter with a 3 kHz cutoff blurs high
-
frequency components; and an echo is added with a 100 ms delay and 10 dB attenuation. The scenario consists of 100 samples.

3.3
Evaluation

3.3.1
Behavior

To analyze a full
-
duplex model’s behavior immediately after an overlap event, we extract both the content and precise word
-
level timestamps from time
-
aligned transcriptions using Nvidia
parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2
4
4

4

https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2
, then prompt a large language model (GPT-4o) to classify each post
-
overlap response into one of five behavior categories:
Respond
, where the assistant meaningfully addresses the overlapping utterance;
Resume
, where it disregards the overlap and continues or completes the pre
-
overlap task;
Uncertain
, where it signals difficulty hearing or understanding (e.g., “I didn’t catch that…,” “Could you repeat?”) without providing a content
-
specific reply; and
Unknown
, where its output is semantically unrelated or low
-
information and silence. To establish a baseline, we also present the model with the corresponding non
-
overlap input and output, enabling direct comparison of its behavior with and without induced interruption.

3.3.2
Timing

For each overlap event, we compute two timing measures:
stop latency
, defined as the interval from the onset of overlapping user speech to the moment the model stops speaking; and
response latency
, defined as the interval from the end of the overlapping speech to the model’s next utterance. Voice activity is detected using the
silero_vad
model.

3.3.3
Speech Feature Comparison

Adapting speaking style appropriately to the conversational scenario is essential for natural and effective spoken communication
[
31
,
13
]
. To assess how full-duplex models adapt their speaking behavior under overlapping speech, we analyze system responses in three core conditions:
non-overlap
,
pre-overlap
, and
post-overlap
. The
non-overlap
condition refers to uninterrupted, clean responses and serves as a reference for typical speaking behavior. In overlap scenarios, each utterance is segmented at the overlap’s end time into a
pre-overlap
segment (before the interruption begins) and a
post-overlap
segment (immediately after the overlap ends). This segmentation enables both within-turn comparisons and comparisons against clean baselines.

We consider four classes of acoustic–prosodic features to capture nuanced changes in speech production:
(1)
Pitch
— mean and standard deviation over voiced frames, reflecting average pitch level and expressiveness;
(2)
Intensity
— mean and standard deviation over speech-active regions, indicating overall loudness and its variability;
(3)
Speaking Rate
— words per minute (WPM), computed from word-level timestamps while excluding silences;
(4)
MOS Score
— an estimate of speech naturalness and quality using the UTMOSv2 model
[
32
]
, also computed on non-silent regions.

To detect whether overlap events induce statistically meaningful shifts in speech behavior, we apply
paired t-tests
across conditions. Specifically, we test:
(i) whether the
pre-overlap
and
post-overlap
segments within the same utterance differ significantly, and
(ii) whether the
post-overlap
segment differs from the
non-overlap
baseline.
We report all results with corresponding
p
-values using a threshold of
p<0.05
. Our analysis targets output segments labeled as
respond
, where the model actively initiates a new turn following an overlap event. This allows us to isolate the model’s speaking behavior in direct response to overlapping input, as opposed to passive behaviors such as remaining silent or simply resuming a previous utterance.

5.1.1
Latency: Detection versus Re-Entry

Table
3
summarizes both
stop
latency—how quickly a model ceases speaking upon encountering overlap—and
response
latency—how soon it resumes after the overlap subsides.

GPT-4o demonstrates the most decisive behavior: it stops almost immediately (0.18–0.23 s) and re-enters with a minimal pause (1.16–1.50 s) across all scenarios, indicating fast detection and stable recovery.
Moshi also stops promptly (0.42–1.16 s) but often waits longer before resuming, especially in
user_backch
and
talking_other
(3.00 s and 2.38 s), suggesting hesitancy in borderline conditions.
Freeze-Omni maintains relatively low stop latency (0.66–1.42 s) and adapts its response delay based on intent: it pauses longer after backchannels (2.16 s), but not after interruptions (1.35 s), suggesting context-aware re-entry.
Gemini and Sonic exhibit slower reactions overall, with stop latencies around 1.7–2.3 s and delayed re-entries (up to 2.76 s), especially during direct interruptions and background interference—potentially risking extended double-talk.

Together, these latency patterns reveal how models navigate the trade-off between rapid floor-yielding and timely re-engagement—an essential factor in achieving fluid full-duplex interaction.

Gap to Human-Level Responsiveness.

From a human-centered perspective, however, a substantial latency gap remains. In natural conversation, response latencies typically hover around 200 ms, enabling near-seamless turn transitions
[
34
]
. In contrast, re-entry latencies observed in our benchmark range from 1.16 s (best-case GPT-4o) to over 2.7 s (e.g., Sonic). Even the most responsive system is approximately 5–6 times slower than a typical human interlocutor. Closing this latency gap—particularly in re-entry timing—remains a critical challenge for achieving human-like fluidity in full-duplex spoken dialogue systems.

Practical Note.

Deployment scenarios may favor different overlap-handling strategies depending on interaction constraints.
For instance, applications requiring immediate responsiveness—such as in-car voice assistants—may benefit from a
repair-first
approach that prioritizes rapid yielding and turn correction.
Conversely, ambient or shared environments like smart-home hubs may be better served by a
continuity-first
policy that maintains ongoing output flow despite interruptions.
To support informed system selection and tuning, we report both the mean and variability of prosodic shifts, as well as distinct stop and response latency metrics across scenarios.

6
Conclusion

Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5
introduces the first fully automated, scenario-controlled benchmark for overlap management in full-duplex speech agents. Featuring four canonical overlap scenarios, a streaming interface that works with both open-source checkpoints and closed APIs, and a rich metric suite spanning behaviour, timing, and prosody, it surfaces performance trade-offs invisible to turn-by-turn tests. Evaluating five leading systems reveals two prevailing approaches:
repair-first
models that yield within 300 ms but risk false triggers, and
continuity-first
models that maintain flow at the cost of multi-second double-talk. Re-entry latencies (1–3 s) still lag far behind the 200-ms typical of human dialogue, and distinct post-interruption prosodic shifts emerge across models. By releasing all code, data, and evaluation scripts,
Full-Duplex-Bench v1.5
provides a ready-to-use and expandable test set that helps developers work with real-world overlapping speech and analyze system behavior.
