Dynamic controls on subsurface water chemistry and habitability on icy moons
Authors: Chengtao Zhang, Yiran Yang, Yuxuan Cai, Shenglin Zhang, Chao He, Christopher Glein, Fang Huang, Barbara Sherwood Lollar, Jihua Hao
Published: 2026

Main text

Icy moons are natural satellites whose surfaces contain a substantial fraction of frozen materials, most notably water ice. They lie beyond the snow line and orbit gas or ice giant planets, such as Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Recent observations and theoretical studies have indicated that several icy moons host regional or global subsurface liquid water oceans that likely persist for 107–109 years.1 Moreover, various organic matter and bioessential nutrients have been detected on icy shells or within plumes from several icy moons,2 implying potentially habitable ocean chemistry. Consequently, ocean-bearing moons become tantalizing targets for the search for extraterrestrial habitable environments and potential biosignatures.2

Chemical properties of subsurface oceans in icy moons

Water chemistry is a critical factor in assessing planetary habitability.2 Among the possible ocean-bearing icy moons, best constrained cases are Enceladus, Europa, Ganymede, and Titan. For example, Cassini’s analyses of the ongoing plume from Enceladus and icy grains in Saturn’s E ring suggested a major composition of Na+, Cl−, and CO32−/HCO3− with minor K+ and NH3/NH4+ in the subsurface ocean.3 Moreover, the presence of silica nanoparticles and molecular hydrogen further indicates ongoing hydrothermal activity at the ocean floor. Unlike Enceladus, current constraints on the subsurface ocean chemistry of other icy moons are heavily dependent on surface remote sensing and/or computational modeling, due to the lack of direct sampling of materials from the ocean. Nevertheless, their subsurface oceans are inferred to have a major composition of inorganic ions (e.g., Na+, Mg2+, Cl−, and/or NH4+) and common gases (e.g., CO2),4 similar to Enceladus.

However, different icy moons could have vastly different chemical conditions and availabilities of bioessential elements, affecting their habitability. For example, Enceladus’s seawater is proposed to be alkaline and reducing, with high levels of bioessential nutrients (e.g., ammonium and orthophosphate)3 and organic matter with various functional groups.2 Such chemistry may favor the stability of possible biomolecules and metabolism of any chemosynthetic microbes. Unlike Enceladus, the detection of various oxidized sulfur species on the surface of Europa suggests a possibly oxidizing and acidic ocean rich in sulfate,4 although these oxidants may also be surface radiation products. On icy moons of larger radius, such as Ganymede and Titan, high-pressure ice layer(s) would inhibit seawater alteration of the silicate seafloor,1 which plays key roles in supplying nutrients and hydrothermal energy on ocean worlds. Notably, all the aforementioned observations represent only a modern snapshot in their long history, but their habitability, particularly the potential for the origin of life, would have been heavily shaped by multiple dynamic factors discussed below.

Dynamic controls of icy moon oceans and their habitability

The subsurface oceans in icy moons are generally proposed to form early in their histories, due to the dehydration metamorphism of H-bearing materials in the interior. Following the decline in radiogenic energy, the oceans become primarily sustained by strong tidal dissipation.1 Despite these general understandings, there are severely limited evidence about the history of their formation, differentiation, and orbits,1 which could have profound influences on the long-term evolution of ocean reservoirs (Figure 1). For example, in the early stage of subsurface oceans, high heating flux from radioactive decay would favor the larger extent of ice melting and even the complete melting of high-pressure ice layer(s) in some large moons.1 Apart from the gradual decline in internal heating, the orbit of these satellites might have evolved significantly,1 which determines the intensity of tidal dissipation. One vivid example is the recent detection of a short-lived (<25 Ma) subsurface ocean on Mimas, which is proposed to be induced by eccentricity variation due to three-body resonance. The evolution of the ocean reservoir inevitably alters the physicochemical properties of ocean water.

Apart from factors mentioned above, multiple dynamic processes, including impact events, hydrothermal activities, space weathering, cryovolcanism, possible icy tectonics, and ocean circulation, could also affect the chemical composition of subsurface oceans (Figure 1). For example, moderate to large cometary impacts may be capable of melting the surface icy shell locally, thus delivering space and surface materials into the subsurface ocean. Moreover, recent observations or theoretical simulations proposed active tectonic activities of Europa’s surface ice shell or internal high-pressure ice layers in some large icy moons,5 facilitating interlayer transport of materials. Additionally, intensive volcanism may drive melting water plumes from the ice-rock boundary up to the subsurface ocean in those large icy moons, breaking the barrier of the high-pressure ice mantle.5 Despite their significance, our understanding of these dynamic processes and possible feedback in the icy moon systems remains very limited and even speculative, due to a severe lack of reliable records and observations.

These dynamic processes could heavily impact key factors of habitability, including water chemistry, energy availability, environmental complexity, and evolution stability.2 For example, possible delivery of surface sulfur species via ice tectonics might result in the deductive oxidizing and acidic features of Europa’s ocean, which differs from the characteristics of Enceladus’s ocean dominated by the serpentinization process.5 Such an oxidizing ocean is not favorable for the stability of organics from either accretion or impact delivery,4 which may explain the lack of organic detection on Europa. In addition, seafloor hydrothermal activities would also generate various interfaces of distinct water activity and prominent catalytic minerals, such as magnetite and sulfides, which could facilitate some prebiotic chemistry processes. Moreover, these exchange processes can sustain redox disequilibrium, essential for the origin and sustenance of microbial metabolism in the hidden ocean.

However, origin-of-life processes, as a planetary phenomenon on the early Earth, may require open and complex environmental settings, which is hardly achievable in the enclosed oceans of icy moons. For example, it is challenging to accomplish polymerization of the building blocks of life in subsurface oceans, which could easily occur in wet-dry cycling environments, such as coastal areas, on the primitive Earth. Given the lack of solar energy, even if cellular life could have originated in the subsurface oceans of some icy moons, the overall bioproductivity is likely to be orders of magnitude lower than in terrestrial oceans. In this regard, choosing appropriate biomarkers and detection schemes, particularly given the barrier of a thick icy shell (tens to hundreds of kilometers), is another major set of challenges.5

Space exploration of icy satellites in the next decade

The habitability of icy moons has become a frontier topic in deep space exploration. Recently, the European Space Agency (ESA) and National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) launched the Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer (JUICE) (2023) and Europa Clipper (2024) missions, respectively, which will orbit and fly by several Jovian moons including Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto.5 In addition, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) launched by NASA in 2021 has already started observing icy moons. In the future, NASA plans to launch Dragonfly in 2028 to land on and fly over Titan. China also plans to implement the “Tianwen-IV” mission around 2030 to orbit several Jovian icy moons and is evaluating further exploration of other icy moons, such as Triton.

In these space missions, the exploration of habitability characteristics and search for potential biosignatures are among the top priority goals. These objectives require interdisciplinary efforts involving astrobiology, planetary science, geochemistry, oceanography, geobiology, and geophysics. Moreover, further innovation in payloads (such as high-resolution mass spectrometry) is required, and sample acquisition operations (such as landing, drilling, and even diving into the ocean) need development to resolve some of the significant challenges discussed above, particularly the characterization of ocean chemistry. It is worth noting that beyond the solar system, there are increasing reports of potentially habitable exoplanets. Like the exploration history of the solar system, the search for potentially habitable exoplanets or moons orbiting exoplanets and their ocean signals is expected to soon become another frontier of planetary sciences. The study of these small yet potentially habitable planetary bodies will substantially broaden the traditionally defined habitable zone and expand the frontiers of the search for life in the universe.
