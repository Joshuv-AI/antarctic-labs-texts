Two-Photon Resonance Fluorescence in a Three-Level Ladder-Type Atom
Jacob Ngaha 1,2, Scott Parkins 1,3, Howard J. Carmichael 1,3

Abstract
In this work, we consider a three-level ladder-type atom driven by a coherent field, inspired by the experimental work of Gasparinetti et al. [ Phys. Rev. A 100, 033802 (2019) ]. When driven on two-photon resonance, the atom is excited into its highest energy state | f ⟩ \ket{f} by absorbing two photons simultaneously. The atom then de-excites via a cascaded decay | f ⟩ → | e ⟩ → | g ⟩ \ket{f}\rightarrow\ket{e}\rightarrow\ket{g} . Here we present a theoretical study of the atomic fluorescence spectrum where, upon strong coherent driving, the spectrum exhibits seven distinct frequencies corresponding to transitions amongst the atomic dressed states. We characterize the quantum statistics of the emitted photons by investigating the second-order correlation functions of the emitted field. We do so by considering the total field emitted by the atom and focusing on each of the dressed-state components, taking in particular a secular-approximation and deriving straightforward, transparent analytic expressions for the second-order auto- and cross-correlations.

I
Introduction

It is well known for a two-level atom that, upon strong coherent driving, the fluorescence spectrum exhibits three distinct peaks. These peaks, known as the Mollow triplet
[
1
,
2
]
, are due to transitions amongst the atomic dressed states, which are a result of the atom interacting with a strongly excited, quantized electromagnetic field mode
[
3
,
4
]
. The two-level atom is a simple, yet standard, example in the quantum optics vernacular. However, as the number of allowed atomic levels increases, so too does the complexity of the system. But in what way? In this work, we extend the two-level atom by introducing one more level, and driving, strongly and coherently, a two-photon transition.

There has been extensive work on three-level atoms in the past, and they remain a system of interest to this day
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. In particular, the work of Whitley and Stroud
[
9
]
laid some of the foundations for the theory of three-level systems, including taking a cavity QED approach by deriving steady-state behavior and absorption spectra using an operator equation of motion approach. This work was then later expanded on by Narducci et al.
[
10
]
and Manka et al.
[
11
]
for three-level atoms in the
\Xi
,
\Lambda
, and V configurations. Reference
[
11
]
is especially relevant due to its explanation of the emission spectra through the use of a dressed-state analysis in the high-intensity limit.

In this work, however, we focus on the model presented by Gasparinetti et al.: a three-level, anharmonic-ladder-type atom driven at two-photon resonance, as is relevant to the superconducting circuit QED system they implemented experimentally
[
12
,
13
]
. At two-photon resonance, the atom can only be excited to its highest energy level via the simultaneous absorption of two photons. Given the ladder-type configuration, the atom then de-excites back to the ground state via a cascaded decay through the intermediate state.

The earlier experimental work of Gasparinetti et al.
[
12
]
focused on the weak driving limit, where the fluorescence spectrum exhibits only two frequency components. There they presented auto- and cross-correlations between photon emissions of these two frequency components demonstrating strong antibunching or “superbunching” depending on the order of detection. In their later work
[
13
]
, the driving strength was increased to study the emergence of multiple spectral components, similar to the Mollow triplet. Antibunching and bunching was then demonstrated in the frequency-filtered auto-correlations of the single-photon resonance and two-photon resonance, respectively.

In this paper we present a much more in-depth theoretical study of the steady-state dynamics, incoherent fluorescence spectrum, and photon correlations of the field emitted by a three-level, ladder-type atom when driven by a resonant and coherent external field, focusing on the strong driving limit. Additionally, we further extend upon previous work on the dressed states of a three-level atom
[
14
,
15
,
10
,
11
,
16
]
by examining the photon correlations of the frequency components of the fluorescence spectrum and taking a secular approximation. This allows us to derive analytic expressions for second-order correlations of the many dressed-state transitions, i.e., the different spectral components of the fluorescence spectrum.

We begin by introducing the Hamiltonian and master equation for the radiatively damped three-level ladder-type atom in Sec.
II
. We then present a numerical survey of the atomic steady states, comparing them to analytic expressions derived for a two-level approximation of the atom. In Sec.
III
we present a survey of the incoherent atomic power spectrum when driven at, or near, two-photon resonance. Finally, Sec.
IV
is dedicated to the atomic photon correlations of the emitted field under weak and strong coherent driving. Taking a secular approximation
[
17
]
, we then derive analytic expressions for the frequency-resolved photon correlations of each spectral resonance in the strong driving limit.

[
18

, and makes use of the quantum toolbox in Python (QuTiP)

.

We consider a three-level ladder-type atom with ground state
\ket{g}
, intermediate state
\ket{e}
, and excited state
\ket{f}

\hbar\omega_{g},\hbar\omega_{e}

\displaystyle H_{A}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega_{g}\ket{g}\bra{g}+\hbar\omega_{e}\ket{e}\bra{e}+\hbar\omega_{f}\ket{f}\bra{f}

\displaystyle\quad+\hbar\frac{\Omega}{2}\left(e^{i\omega_{d}t}\ket{g}\bra{e}+e^{-i\omega_{d}t}\ket{e}\bra{g}\right)

\Omega

\displaystyle H_{A}

\displaystyle=-\hbar\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+\delta\right)\ket{e}\bra{e}-2\hbar\delta\ket{f}\bra{f}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\hbar\frac{\Omega}{2}\left(\Sigma_{+}+\Sigma_{-}\right),

(2)

\alpha\equiv\omega_{fe}-\omega_{eg}

(3)

\ket{e}\rightarrow\ket{g}

\ket{f}\rightarrow\ket{e}

\omega_{ij}=\omega_{i}-\omega_{j}
,

\delta\equiv\omega_{d}-\frac{\omega_{fg}}{2}

(4)

is the drive detuning from two-photon resonance, and

\Sigma_{-}\equiv\ket{g}\bra{e}+\xi\ket{e}\bra{f},\quad\Sigma_{+}\equiv\ket{e}\bra{g}+\xi\ket{f}\bra{e}

(5)

are the atomic lowering and raising operators, respectively. Figure
1

We model energy dissipation through spontaneous emission from the atom with the Lindblad master equation

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H_{A},\rho]+\frac{\Gamma}{2}\Lambda(\Sigma_{-})\rho,

(6)

where
\rho=\sum_{i,j=g,e,f}\rho_{ij}\ket{i}\bra{j}
is the atomic density operator,
\Gamma
is the population decay rate for the lower dipole, and
\Lambda(X)\bullet=\left(2X\bullet X^{\dagger}-X^{\dagger}X\bullet-\bullet X^{\dagger}X\right)
is the Lindblad decay superoperator.

Considering the case of two-photon resonance – where the atom is excited to the
\ket{f}
state via a
simultaneous

|\alpha|
relative to the natural linewidth
\Gamma
; for
|\alpha|\sim\Gamma
, the excited state
\ket{f}
can be populated through two separate absorption events via the intermediate state
\ket{e}
. The transmon qubit in the work of Gasparinetti et al. had an anharmonicity
\alpha/2\pi=-233
MHz and a linewidth of
\Gamma/2\pi\approx 1.9
MHz
[
13
,
13
]
, therefore, in this work, we will set
\alpha/\Gamma=-120
. Changing the anharmonicity alters the overall structure of the atomic ladder structure; however, the key results remain largely unchanged.

(7)

\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{fe}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{B}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
\Gamma\xi^{2}\\
i\xi\frac{\Omega}{2}\\

0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

(8)

and

\displaystyle\bm{M}^{(\Sigma)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\frac{\Omega}{2}&i\frac{\Omega}{2}&\Gamma&0&0&0&0\\

As one might expect, the atom remains in the ground state for weak driving amplitudes far from two-photon resonance. We only see significant population in the
\ket{e}

\ket{f}

It is clear that the dipole moment ratio also plays a significant role in the evolution of the atom; it determines the ratio of the driving strengths and the respective decay rates between the two dipole moments. When
\xi=1
, both of these ratios are the same, and we see qualitatively similar steady-state populations in the
\ket{e}

\ket{f}

\xi<1

\ket{f}

\ket{e}

\xi

\ket{f}

\xi>1

\xi=1

\xi\neq 1

\xi<1

\xi>1

\ket{f}

14

14

\xi=1

II.3
Effective two-level model

Given the relatively low population in the intermediate state, we take a secular approximation and adiabatically eliminate the
\ket{e}
state from the Hamiltonian to obtain an effective “two-level model”
[
22
,
23
]
. We assume the atom has a large anharmonicity with a driving frequency close to two-photon resonance, such that
|\alpha|\gg\delta
. The Hamiltonian for this effective two-level model is then given by:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\Delta_{g}\ket{g}\bra{g}+\hbar\left(-2\delta+\Delta_{f}\right)\ket{f}\bra{f}

\displaystyle\quad+\hbar\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\Big(\ket{g}\bra{f}+\ket{f}\bra{g}\Big).

(10)

where

\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\equiv\xi\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta}

(11)

is the
effective
two-photon driving amplitude, and

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta},

(12a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\xi\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta},

(12b)

are the induced Stark shifts to the ground and excited states, respectively (see Appendix
A
for more details). These Stark shifts arise from virtual transitions to the intermediate state
\ket{e}
via simultaneous emission and absorption at the single-photon resonances,
\omega_{eg}
and
\omega_{fe}
. The Stark shifts cause a corresponding shift in the two-photon resonance frequency; therefore, we define the
effective
drive detuning from two-photon resonance as

\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\equiv\left(-2\delta+\Delta_{f}\right)-\Delta_{g}=-2\delta+\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}-1}{\alpha/2+\delta}.

(13)

By rearranging Eq. (
13
) at resonance,
\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
, we find the shifted two-photon resonance detuning is

\delta_{\mathrm{shifted}}\equiv-\frac{\alpha}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2}+2\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\left(\xi^{2}-1\right)},

(14)

which, as previously mentioned, is shown in Fig.
2
.

Assuming a large anharmonicity, we can drop the rapidly oscillating cross terms in the Lindblad decay term of the master equation, Eq. (
6
), resulting in a master equation with two separate decay terms corresponding to decays from each dipole moment
\left(\sigma_{ij}=\ket{i}\bra{j}\right)
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H_{\mathrm{eff}},\rho]+\frac{\Gamma}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{ge})\rho+\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{ef})\rho.

(15)

Equation (
15
) can then be rewritten as two sets of coupled ordinary differential equations for the components of the reduced density operator
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Gamma\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle-\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle+\Gamma,

(16a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\xi^{2}\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle,

(16b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle

\displaystyle=-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}+i\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle,

(16c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle

\displaystyle=i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}-i\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle,

(16d)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ge}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2}-i\Delta_{g}\right)\langle\sigma_{ge}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fe}\rangle,

(17a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2}+i\Delta_{g}\right)\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle,

(17b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)-i\left(2\delta-\Delta_{f}\right)\right]\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle,

(17c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{fe}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)+i\left(2\delta-\Delta_{f}\right)\right]\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ge}\rangle.

(17d)

Solving for the steady-state atomic state populations, we find:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+4\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+\xi^{4}\Gamma^{2}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\left(2+\xi^{2}\right)+4\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+\xi^{4}\Gamma^{2}},

(18a)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi^{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\left(2+\xi^{2}\right)+4\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+\xi^{4}\Gamma^{2}},

(18b)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle_{ss}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\left(2+\xi^{2}\right)+4\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}+\xi^{4}\Gamma^{2}}.

(18c)

Assuming a strong effective two-photon drive
\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}\gg\Gamma\right)
, a large anharmonicity
\left(|\alpha|\gg\Gamma\right)
, and a drive frequency close to two-photon resonance
\left(|\alpha|/2\gg|\delta|\right)
, these analytic expressions give close agreement to the full model as computed from Eq. (
7
). In particular, we see the effect of the dipole moment ratio
\xi
on the steady-state population of the excited state
\ket{f}
shown in Fig.
2
. From Eq. (
18
), the ratio of the
\ket{e}
and
\ket{f}
steady state populations is
\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle_{ss}/\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle_{ss}=\xi^{2},

hence, for
\xi>1
, there is more population in the
\ket{e}
state than in the
\ket{f}
state, and vice versa for
\xi<1
.

III
Atomic Fluorescence and the Optical Spectrum

III.1
The atomic dressed states

By diagonalizing the Hamiltonian, Eq. (
1
), we find that the dressed-state eigenfrequencies are solutions to the characteristic polynomial,

\displaystyle\omega_{i}^{3}+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+3\delta\right)\omega_{i}^{2}+

\displaystyle\left[\delta\left(\alpha+2\delta\right)-\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)\right]\omega_{i}=2\delta\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}.

(19)

We can simplify this cubic equation by considering the special case of two-photon resonance, setting
\delta=0
. The eigenfrequencies are then
[
10
]

\displaystyle\omega_{m}=0,

(20a)

\displaystyle\omega_{u}=-\frac{\alpha}{4}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{\alpha}{4}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)},

(20b)

\displaystyle\omega_{l}=-\frac{\alpha}{4}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\alpha}{4}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)},

(20c)

with corresponding eigenvectors:

\displaystyle\ket{m}=\frac{-1}{\sqrt{1+\xi^{2}}}\Big(\xi\ket{g}-\ket{f}\Big),

(21a)

\displaystyle\ket{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\omega_{u}^{2}+\Omega^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)}}\Big(\Omega\ket{g}+2\omega_{u}\ket{e}+\xi\Omega\ket{f}\Big),

(21b)

\displaystyle\ket{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\omega_{l}^{2}+\Omega^{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)}}\Big(\Omega\ket{g}+2\omega_{l}\ket{e}+\xi\Omega\ket{f}\Big).

(21c)

These three dressed states, depicted in Fig.
3
(a), along with the uncoupled atomic states, open up several possible de-excitation channels. There are, in fact, twelve possible transitions in total, with seven distinct frequencies appearing:

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(22a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\omega_{m}-\omega_{l}\right),

(22b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{\pm 2}

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\omega_{u}-\omega_{m}\right),

(22c)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{\pm 3}

\displaystyle=\pm\left(\omega_{u}-\omega_{l}\right).

(22d)

Figure
3
(a) also shows that the possible orderings of the allowed dressed-state transitions follow a cascaded decay, as in the bare state picture.

III.2
Incoherent power spectrum near two-photon resonance

We define the normalized first-order correlation function for the three-level ladder-type atom in the steady-state limit as:

\displaystyle g^{(1)}_{ss}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle\Sigma_{+}(t+\tau)\Sigma_{-}(t)\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\Sigma_{+}\Sigma_{-}(t)\rangle\langle\Sigma_{+}\Sigma_{-}(t+\tau)\rangle}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle\Sigma_{+}(\tau)\Sigma_{-}(0)\rangle}{\langle\Sigma_{+}\Sigma_{-}\rangle_{ss}}.

(23)

From the Wiener-Khinchin theorem
[
24
,
25
]
, we can decompose the power spectrum into coherent and incoherent components
[
9
]
:

S(\omega)=S_{\mathrm{coh}}(\omega)+S_{\mathrm{inc}}(\omega),

(24)

with

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{coh}}(\omega)

IV
Atomic Photon Correlations

IV.1
Total Radiated Field

A standard, resonantly driven two-level atom can only store a single quantum of energy at any time. As the atom de-excites, and a photon is emitted, the atom must first be re-excited before it can emit another. The emitted light is therefore antibunched with an initial second-order correlation of
g^{(2)}(0)=0
.

For the three-level ladder-type atom, we define the normalized second-order correlation function in the steady-state limit as:

g^{(2)}_{ss}(\tau)=\frac{\langle\Sigma_{+}(0)\Sigma_{+}\Sigma_{-}(\tau)\Sigma_{-}(0)\rangle}{\langle\Sigma_{+}\Sigma_{-}\rangle_{ss}^{2}}.

(26)

As the collective decay operator
\Sigma_{-}
, Eq. (
5
), is a combination of the decay operators for the upper and lower dipole moments, the probability for an atomic decay to occur is proportional to
\Gamma\langle\Sigma_{+}\Sigma_{-}\rangle_{ss}=\Gamma\left(\langle\sigma_{ee}(t)\rangle+\xi^{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}(t)\rangle\right)
, which, we note, is dependent on the populations of both the intermediate and excited states.

In the low driving limit – where the degeneracy of the dressed states has not yet been lifted – the cascaded decay ensures that a photon of frequency
\omega_{fe}
is always followed by a photon of frequency
\omega_{eg}
. This is characterized in the second-order correlation function as strong bunching, with high initial values, as seen in Fig.
7
. As the driving amplitude decreases, the average rate at which the atom is excited also decreases, yet the average time between successive emissions remains the same. The initial correlation value grows as the cascaded emission becomes increasingly well-defined.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented numerical and analytic results for the steady-state behavior, fluorescence spectrum, and photon correlations of a driven three-level ladder-type atom. Previous work – Refs.
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
, in particular – have calculated emission and absorption spectra for generalized three-level ladder-type atoms, yet here we have extended the theory to a specific configuration: namely, an anharmonic ladder-type atom driven at two-photon resonance. Given a large anharmonicity between the upper and lower dipoles, the atom must be excited to its upper state via the simultaneous absorption of two photons. The atom then de-excites back to the ground state via a two-photon cascaded process.

When strongly driven with a coherent, two-photon resonant field, the fluorescence spectrum exhibits seven peaks, neatly organized into three distinct groups: a central, Mollow-like triplet from the effective two-photon drive, and two side-doublets from the Stark splitting of the bare states.

The cascaded ordering of the de-excitation also gives rise to strong bunching in the photon correlations. When weakly driven, the atom will emit a photon from the upper dipole and then from the lower dipole. As the driving amplitude gets weaker and the cascaded emissions become increasingly well-defined, the initial correlation value grows. At strong driving amplitudes, the three-level atom behaves like a two-level atom; the photon correlations have an additional oscillation at the effective Rabi frequency. Depending on the dipole moment ratio, the emitted light is also slightly antibunched, with
0.5<g^{(2)}(0)<1
, though it is never perfectly antibunched.

Taking inspiration from previous work on the two-level atom – Ref.
[
17
]
in particular – we have derived idealized expressions for frequency-resolved photon correlations. In the secular approximation, the behavior of the dressed-state auto- and cross-correlations agree with the cascaded structure of the dressed-state de-excitation pathways. Of course, the secular approximation is still an approximation. In a separate work
[
32
]
, we have taken this a step further by modeling the case in which the fluorescence is directed into cavity-based frequency-filters, whereby we can correlate the outputs of the frequency-filters to compare against the secular approximation
[
33
]
.

(46b)

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{f}

\displaystyle=-i\xi\frac{\Omega}{2}c_{e}+2i\delta c_{f}.

(46c)

We set
\dot{c}_{e}=0
and find the quasi-steady state for the intermediate state amplitude:

c_{e}^{ss}=\frac{\Omega}{2}\frac{c_{g}+\xi c_{f}}{\alpha/2+\delta}.

(47)

Equations (
46
) then reduce to a pair of coupled equations for the ground and excited states:

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{g}

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{c_{g}}{\alpha/2+\delta}-i\xi\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{c_{f}}{\alpha/2+\delta},

(48a)

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{f}

\displaystyle=-i\xi\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{c_{g}}{\alpha/2+\delta}-i\left[\left(\frac{\xi\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta}-2\delta\right]c_{f}.

(48b)

From these equations, we define the effective two-photon driving amplitude and the level shifts of the ground and excited states:

\displaystyle\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\xi\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta},

(49a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta},

(49b)

\displaystyle\Delta_{f}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{\xi\Omega}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\alpha/2+\delta}.

(49c)

We therefore define the
effective two-photon drive Hamiltonian
as:

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\Delta_{g}\ket{g}\bra{g}+\hbar\left(-2\delta+\Delta_{f}\right)\ket{f}\bra{f}

\displaystyle\quad+\hbar\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\Big(\ket{g}\bra{f}+\ket{f}\bra{g}\Big).

(50)

Assuming a large atomic anharmonicity,
|\alpha|\gg\Gamma
, we can drop any rapidly oscillating cross terms in the master equation, Eq. (
6
), such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{i\hbar}[H_{\mathrm{eff}},\rho]+\frac{\Gamma}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{ge})\rho+\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{ef})\rho.

(51)

From this, we can derive a complete set of operator moment equations for each dipole raising and lowering operator
\left(\sigma_{ij}=\ket{i}\bra{j}\right)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle,

(52a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle+\xi^{2}\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle,

(52b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\xi^{2}\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle,

(52c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}+i\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle

(52d)

\displaystyle\quad+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}-i\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle,

(52e)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ge}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2}-i\Delta_{g}\right)\langle\sigma_{ge}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fe}\rangle,

(53a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2}+i\Delta_{g}\right)\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle,

(53b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)-i\left(2\delta-\Delta_{f}\right)\right]\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{eg}\rangle,

(53c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{fe}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)+i\left(2\delta-\Delta_{f}\right)\right]\langle\sigma_{ef}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ge}\rangle.

(53d)

Using the fact that
\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle+\langle\sigma_{ee}\rangle+\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle=1
, Eqs. (
52
) further reduce to a set of four coupled equations
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Gamma\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle-\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle+\Gamma,

(54a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\xi^{2}\Gamma\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle,

(54b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle

\displaystyle=-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle+i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}+i\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\langle\sigma_{gf}\rangle,

(54c)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle

\displaystyle=i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{gg}\rangle-i\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\langle\sigma_{ff}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\frac{\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2}-i\Delta_{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\langle\sigma_{fg}\rangle.

(54d)

Appendix B
Dressed-State Approximation

Writing the bare atomic states as unit vectors,

\ket{g}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ket{e}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ket{f}\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(55)

we can also represent Hamiltonian, Eq. (
1
), in matrix form:

H_{A}\rightarrow\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&\frac{\Omega}{2}&0\\
\frac{\Omega}{2}&-\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}+\delta\right)&\xi\frac{\Omega}{2}\\
0&\xi\frac{\Omega}{2}&-2\delta\end{pmatrix}.

(56)

This matrix is diagonalizable, with

\bm{D}=\bm{S}^{-1}H_{A}\bm{S},

(57)

where

\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}\left[\omega_{m},\omega_{u},\omega_{l}\right],

(58)

is a diagonal matrix consisting of the eigenvalues, with
\omega_{l}\leq\omega_{m}\leq\omega_{u}
, and

\bm{S}=\left[\ket{m},\ket{u},\ket{l}\right]

(59)

is a matrix consisting of the corresponding eigenvectors written in the bare-state basis. The inverse of this matrix,
\bm{S}^{-1}
, then gives the bare atomic states in the “dressed-state basis”.

We can transform the atomic lowering operators Eq. (
5
), into the dressed-state basis with

\displaystyle{\Sigma}_{-}^{\prime}=\bm{S}^{-1}\Sigma_{-}\bm{S}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}^{-1}\Big(\ket{g}\bra{e}+\xi\ket{e}\bra{f}\Big)\bm{S}.

(60)

Due to the cubic nature of the characteristic polynomial, generalized analytic expressions for the dressed states for non-zero detuning are exceedingly difficult to derive. As eigenvectors can be easily computed numerically, we introduce a change of variable by defining the elements of the atomic lowering operator in the dressed-state basis:

\Sigma_{-}^{\prime}\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\ket{m}\\
\ket{u}\\
\ket{l}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{4}&a_{5}&a_{6}\\
a_{7}&a_{8}&a_{9}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ket{m}&\ket{u}&\ket{l}\end{pmatrix},

(61)

with which we will derive generalized expressions for the secular approximation.

Transforming into an interaction picture with the unitary evolution operator

\mathcal{U}(t)=e^{\frac{1}{i\hbar}H_{A}t},

(62)

the atomic lowering operator then evolves as:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{-}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(t)\Sigma_{-}^{\prime}~\mathcal{U}(t)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}e^{-i\left(\omega_{u}-\omega_{m}\right)t}&a_{3}e^{i\left(\omega_{m}-\omega_{l}\right)t}\\
a_{4}e^{i\left(\omega_{u}-\omega_{m}\right)t}&a_{5}&a_{6}e^{i\left(\omega_{u}-\omega_{l}\right)t}\\
a_{7}e^{-i\left(\omega_{m}-\omega_{l}\right)t}&a_{8}e^{-i\left(\omega_{u}-\omega_{l}\right)t}&a_{9}\end{pmatrix}.

(63)

The atomic master equation, Eq. (
1
), also transforms as:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}_{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\Gamma}{2}\Lambda\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{-}^{\prime}(t)\right)\tilde{\rho}_{D}(t),

(64)

where

\tilde{\rho}_{D}(t)=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(t)\rho_{D}~\mathcal{U}(t),\quad\rho_{D}=\bm{S}^{-1}\rho~\bm{S}.

(65)

Expanding the operators in Eq. (
64
), we then make the rotating wave approximation and drop any rapidly oscillating terms. Transforming back out of the interaction picture, the master equation can be rewritten in terms of dressed-state operators
[
9
,
10
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho_{D}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-i[\omega_{m}\sigma_{mm}+\omega_{u}\sigma_{uu}+\omega_{l}\sigma_{ll},\rho_{D}]+\frac{\Gamma a_{1}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{mm})\rho_{D}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Gamma a_{5}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{uu})\rho_{D}+\frac{\Gamma a_{9}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{ll})\rho_{D}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Gamma a_{2}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{-}^{um})\rho_{D}+\frac{\Gamma a_{4}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{+}^{um})\rho_{D}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Gamma a_{7}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{-}^{ml})\rho_{D}+\frac{\Gamma a_{3}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{+}^{ml})\rho_{D}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\Gamma a_{8}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{-}^{ul})\rho_{D}+\frac{\Gamma a_{6}^{2}}{2}\Lambda(\sigma_{+}^{ul})\rho_{D}

\displaystyle\quad+\Gamma a_{1}a_{5}\left(\sigma_{mm}\rho_{D}\sigma_{uu}+\sigma_{uu}\rho_{D}\sigma_{mm}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\Gamma a_{1}a_{9}\left(\sigma_{mm}\rho_{D}\sigma_{ll}+\sigma_{ll}\rho_{D}\sigma_{mm}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\Gamma a_{5}a_{9}\left(\sigma_{uu}\rho_{D}\sigma_{ll}+\sigma_{ll}\rho_{D}\sigma_{uu}\right),

(66)

with

\displaystyle\sigma^{um}_{-}

\displaystyle=\ket{m}\bra{u},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma^{um}_{+}

\displaystyle=\ket{u}\bra{m},

(67a)

\displaystyle\sigma^{ml}_{-}

\displaystyle=\ket{l}\bra{m},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma^{ml}_{+}

\displaystyle=\ket{m}\bra{l},

(67b)

\displaystyle\sigma^{ul}_{-}

\displaystyle=\ket{l}\bra{u},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma^{ul}_{+}

\displaystyle=\ket{u}\bra{l},

(67c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}^{D}

\displaystyle=\ket{u}\bra{u}-\ket{l}\bra{l},

\displaystyle\quad\sigma_{ii}

\displaystyle=\ket{i}\bra{i}.

(67d)

We expand the reduced density operator in the dressed-state basis and find that the off-diagonal components of the reduced density operator are completely uncoupled. We then derive two sets of moment equations for the dressed-state operators
\left(\omega_{ij}=\omega_{i}-\omega_{j}\right)
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{um}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma_{um}}{2}\mp i\omega_{um}\right)\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{um}\rangle,

(68a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ul}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma_{ul}}{2}\mp i\omega_{ul}\right)\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ul}\rangle,

(68b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ml}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\Gamma_{ml}}{2}\mp i\omega_{ml}\right)\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ml}\rangle,

(68c)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{um}

\displaystyle\equiv\Gamma\left[a_{2}^{2}+a_{4}^{2}+a_{7}^{2}+a_{8}^{2}+\left(a_{1}-a_{5}\right)^{2}\right],

(69a)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ml}

\displaystyle\equiv\Gamma\left[a_{3}^{2}+a_{4}^{2}+a_{6}^{2}+a_{7}^{2}+\left(a_{1}-a_{9}\right)^{2}\right],

(69b)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ul}

\displaystyle\equiv\Gamma\left[a_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}+a_{6}^{2}+a_{8}^{2}+\left(a_{5}-a_{9}\right)^{2}\right].

(69c)

and

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\begin{pmatrix}\langle\sigma_{mm}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{uu}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{ll}\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\bm{M}\begin{pmatrix}\langle\sigma_{mm}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{uu}\rangle\\
\langle\sigma_{ll}\rangle\end{pmatrix},

(70)

with

\bm{M}=\begin{pmatrix}-\Gamma\left(a_{4}^{2}+a_{7}^{2}\right)&\Gamma a_{2}^{2}&\Gamma a_{3}^{2}\\
\Gamma a_{4}^{2}&-\Gamma\left(a_{2}^{2}+a_{8}^{2}\right)&\Gamma a_{6}^{2}\\
\Gamma a_{7}^{2}&\Gamma a_{8}^{2}&-\Gamma\left(a_{3}^{2}+a_{6}^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(71)

Equations (
68
) have simple solutions due to their uncoupled nature:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{um}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{um}(0)\rangle e^{-\left(\frac{\Gamma_{um}}{2}\mp i\omega_{um}\right)t},

(72a)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ul}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ul}(0)\rangle e^{-\left(\frac{\Gamma_{ul}}{2}\mp i\omega_{ul}\right)t},

(72b)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ml}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\sigma_{\pm}^{ml}(0)\rangle e^{-\left(\frac{\Gamma_{ml}}{2}\mp i\omega_{ml}\right)t}.

(72c)

Unfortunately, the general solution to Eq. (
70
) is complicated. Taking the limit
\Omega\rightarrow\infty
, however, allows us derive a set of relatively simple solutions:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{mm}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{1}-2C_{2}e^{\lambda_{+}t},

(73a)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{uu}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}e^{\lambda_{-}t}-C_{3}e^{\lambda_{+}t},

(73b)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma_{ll}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}e^{\lambda_{-}t}+C_{3}e^{\lambda_{+}t},

(73c)

where

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\Big(\langle\sigma_{ll}(0)\rangle+\langle\sigma_{uu}(0)\rangle+\langle\sigma_{mm}(0)\rangle\Big),

(74a)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}\Big(\langle\sigma_{ll}(0)\rangle+\langle\sigma_{uu}(0)\rangle-2\langle\sigma_{mm}(0)\rangle\Big),

(74b)

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle\sigma_{ll}(0)\rangle-\langle\sigma_{uu}(0)\rangle\Big),

(74c)

and

\displaystyle\lambda_{-}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{-3\xi^{2}\Gamma}{2\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)},

(75a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{+}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{-\Gamma\left(1-\xi^{2}+\xi^{4}\right)}{2\left(1+\xi^{2}\right)}.

(75b)
