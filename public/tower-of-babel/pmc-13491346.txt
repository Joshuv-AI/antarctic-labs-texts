The Morphological and Mechanistic Impact of an Investment Procedure on Coronary Chronic Total Occlusions
Authors: Anja Øksnes, Akiko Maehara, Britt Engan, Vibeke Juliebø, Kalaivani Mahadevan, Kais Hyasat, Damien Collison, James Spratt, Ajay J Kirtane, Milada C Hagen, Svein Rotevatn, Tone Norekvål, Øyvind Bleie, Margaret B McEntegart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring chronic total occlusion (CTO) percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), a modification procedure is often performed after failure. Although this has been reported to improve subsequent procedural success, the anatomical impact and mechanism of benefit have never been systematically studied. The Invest-CTO study investigated whether a strategy of a planned 2-stage CTO PCI with upfront “investment” and staged “completion” procedure could improve procedural success and safety in high-risk CTO anatomy. In this analysis, we provide novel insights into the morphological and mechanistic impact of an upfront “investment procedure.”MethodsThis Invest-CTO substudy included the first 80 consecutive patients returning for completion of CTO PCI. Angiographic characteristics at baseline prior to investment were compared with those at the start of the completion procedures. Intravascular ultrasound was performed and analyzed after wire crossing during completion to identify changes in morphology.ResultsAt the completion procedure, the overall median (IQR) J-CTO score decreased from 4 (3-4) to 2 (1.8-3), P < .001. The vessel was open in 28.8% (n = 23). Ambiguity of the proximal cap decreased from 77.5% to 12.5% (P < .001), and ambiguity of the vessel course decreased from 76.3% to 26.3% (P < .017). Median lesion length also decreased from 28.2 mm to 15 mm (P < .001). Three distinct modification patterns were defined by intravascular ultrasound: “intraplaque true-to-true communication” (n = 30), “extraplaque with fenestration”(n = 16), and “proximal cap modification” including hematoma resolution and healing of vessel injury (n = 34).ConclusionsPerforming a planned investment procedure reduced occlusion complexity, created fenestrations or facilitated intraplaque communication and wire crossing, or modified the antegrade track, facilitating subsequent dissection reentry in high-risk CTOs.Keywords: chronic total occlusion, high-risk anatomy, intravascular imaging, investment procedure

Introduction

The most challenging subset of lesions to treat with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is chronic total occlusions (CTOs). Despite the presence of a CTO being an independent predictor of increased mortality,1 only 10%2 of patients with a CTO are treated with PCI, most likely due to these being more technically challenging procedures, with lower success rates, more complications, and worse outcomes.3,4

Contemporary technology and techniques, in parallel to the adoption of an algorithmic procedural approach,5 have led to success rates of 84% to 90% in multiple large registries including dedicated high-volume CTO operators.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 However, success rates fall to ∼75%11, 12, 13 and complication rates increase (8%-13%)12 with lesions of higher complexity. In less experienced centers, as reflected in national PCI registries, success rates are as low as 45% to 69%.14,15

“CTO modification,” as defined by the CTO Academic Research Consortium (ARC),16 is plaque modification performed after failure to complete crossing of an occlusion. In retrospective analyses of registry data, performing CTO modification is associated with a higher success rate if the patient returns for a second procedure, but importantly, without mitigating the high complication rate associated with the initial failed attempt.11,17,18

With an “investment procedure,” by identifying anatomically higher-risk occlusions and performing a preplanned modification followed by a planned staged completion procedure, the goal is to reduce the complexity of the anatomy, improve cumulative procedural success, and at the same time mitigate the risk associated with an initial prolonged failed attempt.19

The morphological impact of CTO modification and possible mechanisms associated with increased success at a staged completion procedure have never been systematically described. In this analysis from the Effectiveness and Safety of Planned Investment in Higher-Risk Chronic Total Occlusion Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (Invest-CTO) trial (ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT04774913), we describe the impact of an investment procedure using both coronary angiography and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) analyses (Central Illustration). In addition, we aim to improve the understanding of CTO anatomy and pathophysiology and identify lesion characteristics that may help to predict when an investment procedure will confer the greatest benefit to procedural and patient outcomes.

Methods

Study design

Invest-CTO is a prospective, single-arm, international, multicenter study, evaluating the effectiveness and safety of a preplanned investment procedure, with a subsequent completion of CTO PCI at 8 to 12 weeks, in anatomically high-risk CTOs (Figure 1). The study design has previously been described in detail.19 In this substudy, we included the first 80 consecutive patients who returned for their completion procedure, across all sites in Norway, the United Kingdom, and the United States, where a wire crossed distal to the occlusion and an IVUS catheter was successfully delivered. Demographic and procedural data were collected and stored in an electronic case report form (eCRF; VIEDOC).

Invest-CTO high-risk criteria. ADR, antegrade dissection reentry; AW, antegrade wiring; CTO, coronary total occlusion; Cx, circumflex; LAD, left anterior descending artery; RCA, right coronary artery.

Study procedures

The initial investment procedure involved wiring or knuckling across the proximal cap, occlusive segment, and distal cap of the CTO. Once the wire was determined to have tracked extraplaque, the protocol strategy was to perform noncompliant balloon dilatation of the entire occluded segment. In cases in which wire crossing was successful, the procedure was completed at the index procedure. Additional proximal cap crossing techniques and calcium modification were used if required. There was no attempt to perform antegrade reentry or to work retrograde. A final contrast injection postinvestment was strongly discouraged unless there were safety concerns, with the intention to avoid uncontrolled hydraulic dissections and injury.

The completion of the CTO PCI was performed 8 to 12 weeks after the investment procedure, using the techniques and strategy deemed appropriate by the operators. Intravascular ultrasound was mandated as early as technically possible after crossing the occlusion.

Angiographic analysis

Coronary angiographic analysis of the investment and completion procedures was performed by a core laboratory (Cardiovascular Research Foundation). CTO anatomical characteristics were described on the baseline angiogram prior to the investment procedure and at the start of the completion procedure. In addition to the CTO complexity scores (J-CTO, RECHARGE, Euro-CASTLE, and PROGRESS),7,20,21 CTO length, the presence of moderate or severe calcium, calcium location, a significant side branch (>1.5 mm) within the occluded segment or at the distal cap, and the presence and type of crossable collaterals were documented.

Baseline angiograms at the investment and completion procedures were compared with determine whether there had been any significant anatomical change, specifically resolution of an ambiguous proximal cap, vessel course, distal cap, or distal landing zone, change in occlusion length, and Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow.22

IVUS analysis

IVUS was performed during the completion procedure as early as possible after wire crossing using a 60-MHz catheter (Boston Scientific) with automated pullback at 1 mm/s. All IVUS images were analyzed by a single experienced analyst (A.M.) using echo Plaque 4.0 software (INDEC Medical Systems). Wire location was categorized as intraplaque (within the plaque) or extraplaque (within the media, adventitia, or perivascular tissue). Layered tissue was defined as a hypoechoic or isoechoic tissue clearly demarcated from the underlying native atherosclerotic plaque, suggestive of organized or healing thrombus. Neointimal hyperplasia (NIH) was defined as a homogeneous hypoechoic or isoechoic layer within the extraplaque space observed at the completion of PCI.

Statistical analyses

Data are described using descriptive statistics, categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages, whereas continuous variables are reported as medians and interquartile ranges. Means and standard deviations are provided for comparison with other datasets. All continuous variables followed a skewed distribution test (with normality assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test). As all individuals were assessed before and after, data were analyzed as matched pairs. Pairs of categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test as appropriate. Pairs of binary data were compared using the McNemar test. Pairs of continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Proportions are described as point estimates with 95% CI. All tests were 2-sided, and P values < .05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp).

The median J-CTO score was 4 (IQR 3-4) (mean 3.5 ± 0.67). In 95% of cases, the J-CTO score was ≥3. The mean RECHARGE, PROGRESS, and EURO-CASTLE scores were 4.5 ± 0.99, 2.4 ± 0.82, and 4.0 ± 1.06, respectively.

There was a difference in the techniques required to cross the proximal cap crossing between the investment and completion procedures: conventional wiring (17.5% vs 85%), cap puncture (47.5% vs 10%), IVUS-guided cap puncture (6.3% vs 2.5%), scratch-and-go (5.0% vs 2.5%), balloon-assisted subintimal entry (BASE) (17.5% vs 0%), side-BASE (3.8% vs 0%), and contrast-induced hydraulic dissection (Carlino) (7.5% vs 0%). During the investment procedure, 92.5% had plaque modification with a noncompliant balloon (NCB) only, with 2.5% (n = 2) requiring rotational atherectomy, 2.5% (n = 2) cutting balloons, and intravascular lithotripsy and scoring, both only used in 1 case. Pooling together the investment and completion procedures (n = 160), a total of 17% (n = 27) of cases required calcium modification beyond NCB.

Although an ambiguous proximal cap was frequently present prior to investment, this was significantly reduced at completion (77.5%, 95% CI, [66.8-86.1] to 12.5%, 95% CI, [6.2-21.8], P < .001). Vessel course ambiguity (76.3%, 95% CI, [65.4-85.0] to 26.3%, 95% CI, [17.0-37.3] P < .017) and the distal cap being located at a bifurcation (63.8%, 95% CI, [52.2-74.2] to 37.5%, 95% CI, [26.9-49.0], P < .001) were also less prevalent following investment.

Resolution of the distal cap (defined as >5 mm elongation of the vessel proximal to the prior distal cap) was achieved in 48.8% (95% CI, [37.4-60.2]). Median lesion length was significantly reduced (28.2 mm [22.3-46.6] to 15 mm [1.5-26.1], P < .001), with a smaller proportion of long lesions (≥20 mm) (83.8%, 95% CI, [73.8-91.0] to 38.8%, 95% CI, [28.1-50.3], P < .001). In the left anterior descending, intramyocardial bridging was identified in 2.5% prior to investment and 10% at the completion of the procedure.

IVUS impact of investment procedure

Three novel and distinct CTO modification patterns were identified and defined by IVUS (Table 5): (1) “intraplaque true-to-true communication (ITTC)” is defined as a wire passage entirely within the plaque of the CTO segment (intraplaque), connecting the true lumen proximal to the occlusion with the true lumen distal to the CTO; (2) “extraplaque with fenestration (EPF)” is defined as a wire passage outside the plaque (extraplaque) within the CTO segment, with reentry (fenestration) into the distal true lumen; and (3) “proximal cap modification (PCM)” including cases that did not meet the criteria for the first 2 patterns and were characterized by layered tissue at the proximal cap, hematoma resolution, and healing of vessel injury seen on angiography.

In the 34 cases (42.5%) with proximal cap modification, hematoma resolution, or vessel healing, only 6% (n = 2) had an angiographically open vessel. The final strategies in this group were RDR in 57.6% (n = 19), ADR in 26.5% (n = 9), AW in 11.7% (n = 4), and further modification in 5.9% (n = 2).

Looking specifically at the subgroup of 24 post-CABG occlusions, 66% (n = 16) were in the proximal cap modification group, 21% (n = 5) in the ITTC group, and 12.5% (n = 3) in the EPF group.

When pooling the ITTC and EPF groups, 46.7% (n = 21/46) had an open vessel. A final strategy of AW was used in 72%, RW in 19.6%, and a dissection reentry technique in only 8.5% of cases (3 ADRs and 1 RDR).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported analysis of the morphological and mechanistic impact of an investment procedure or modification procedure in CTO PCI. The key findings are that on returning for completion PCI, by angiography (1), approximately one-third of the vessels were open with TIMI flow ≥1; (2) proximal cap, vessel course, and distal cap ambiguity were resolved in most cases; and (3) mean occlusion length was significantly shorter. In addition, 3 novel and distinct patterns of modification were identified on IVUS: (1) ITTC, (2) EPF, and (3) proximal cap modification, including hematoma resolution and healing of vessel injury.

Although the technical strategy of the investment procedure was to modify the occlusive plaque, not to attempt reentry, it was notable to find that almost one-third of the vessels were open upon return. Although vessels were often open, when these vessels were crossed with a wire, this was more often observed to be via extraplaque fenestrations rather than via true-to-true communication during the completion procedure. This supports the hypothesis that the creation of fenestrations within the occlusion is likely to result in an open vessel with a high probability of AW. However, the ability to cross with a true-to-true communication in over a third of cases at the completion procedure suggests that despite extraplaque wire tracking and modification during the investment procedure, this facilitated subsequent intraplaque wiring. This finding is consistent with previous observations that extraplaque modification of the proximal cap (external cap crush) can facilitate subsequent intraplaque crossing of the cap. These observations support an initial strategy of AW in the majority of cases on return for the completion procedures.

With the intention of the alternative modification technique of subintimal tracking and reentry (STAR) to achieve reentry and flow distal to the occlusion, it was recently reported to be associated with vessel patency on return for staged completion of 64% (early) and 44% (late). In contrast, although the intention of “investment” was to modify the occlusive segment without attempting reentry, we observed vessel patency of 29%. No detailed description of anatomical change or IVUS analysis following STAR has yet been reported.

Our study was designed specifically to include anatomically higher-risk CTOs, confirmed by the mean J-CTO score of 3.5 ± 0.7 in this subcohort compared with 2.3 ± 1.4 in the PROGRESS-CTO Registry.9 It is known that with more complex anatomy, the retrograde approach is required more often. However, despite the higher anatomical complexity of our study population, similar rates of completion with AW were observed compared with PROGRESS-CTO (46% vs 54%), with 61% of our cases completed antegrade (AW and ADR) vs 67% in PROGRESS.14 The OPEN-CTO registry, with lower lesion complexity (mean J-CTO 2.3 ± 1.3), reported crossing with AW in 41% and ADR in 24%.10 These observations may support our hypothesis that a preplanned investment procedure may facilitate more complex cases being completed with an antegrade approach.

A further interesting observation in the EPF group was the presence of NIH in the extraplaque track on IVUS in 10/16 cases. This is consistent with the vessel having been open with flow through the fenestrations since the investment procedure. This finding of NIH in the extraplaque space is novel and reinforces previous experience and data on vessel healing. This observation could support a strategy of delayed stenting (eg, deferral of stenting during the initial procedure) or staged optimization of stents in the extraplaque space to improve long-term stent outcomes and potentially reduce the risk of perforations even during single-stage CTO PCI.

In the EPF group, when the vessel is open, the location of the fenestration can sometimes be identified on angiography. Alternatively, fenestrations can be identified by exploring with a low or intermediate penetration force wire, occasionally with IVUS guidance (Figure 3B).

One of our key observations was that in almost 70% of cases, fenestrations occurred at the site of a side branch or at the distal cap. This is consistent with the theory around the technique of STAR, where when advancing a knuckle beyond the occlusion, it is thought to be most likely to reenter at the site of a bifurcation. The key difference with investment is the intention to create a controlled fenestration within the occluded segment. As use of the STAR technique was not allowed in the study protocol, our results cannot be directly extrapolated to this or other forms of CTO modification.13

As has previously been reported, operators do not always know whether they are intra- or extraplaque.23 Indeed in 2 cases of true-to-true communication, the operator thought they had performed dissection reentry when in fact the wire had tracked deep in the plaque close to the intima, or the knuckled wire was contained intraplaque by severe calcium preventing extraplaque access.

The group defined as having proximal cap modification and vessel healing (PCM) had a higher prevalence of both CABG (44% vs 17%) and severe calcium (41% vs 35%) compared with the ITTC and EPF group. In this more complex proximal cap modification cohort, the morphological effect of the investment differed from the other 2 groups. There was less reduction in CTO length, as despite successful resolution of the proximal cap, there was less impact on the distal cap, which could be one reason that just >50% required a retrograde strategy. In nongrafted occlusions, IVUS frequently showed the distal cap to be composed of organized thrombus more likely to be resolved by modification. In contrast, in post-CABG occlusions, due to the exposure to systemic pressure via the graft, the distal cap is often more resistant and less likely to be resolved. Therefore, in this group, the benefit of the investment procedure is often to resolve the ambiguity of the proximal cap and vessel course, allow a period of vessel healing and hematoma resolution, preparing the vessel for a safer and more successful subsequent dissection reentry.

An important technical observation was the reduced need to use high penetration force wires and a lesser need for advanced proximal cap crossing techniques at the completion procedure. We hypothesize that this could translate in the overall study cohort into improved procedural safety and patient outcomes.

Limitations

There is some limitation due to patient selection as we included the first 80 patients where we were able to deliver an IVUS catheter across the lesion, intraplaque or extraplaque, at the completion of the procedure. That said, IVUS was used in 90% of cases in the overall study cohort. Also, the cases were predominantly performed by experienced operators at a limited number of centers. The IVUS patterns of modification described are novel and hypothesis-generating and thus require further investigation. As the strategy of ballooning during the investment procedure was recommended to include but not go beyond the distal cap, to avoid extending the extraplaque space distally, IVUS was not technically feasible during the investment procedure. However, IVUS was performed as early as possible during the completion procedure.

Conclusions

Performing a planned “investment procedure” as part of a 2-stage approach to CTO PCI significantly reduced the anatomical complexity of the occlusions. Although approximately one-third of these high complexity cases still require being solved retrograde, the investment procedure was associated with less complex crossing techniques and the use of lower penetration force wires at the completion procedure compared with CTOs of similar complexity. In most cases, this was because of the facilitation of an intraplaque communication or creation of fenestrations from the extraplaque to the intraplaque lumen. In other, usually more complex occlusions, the benefit was preparing the antegrade track for subsequent crossing with dissection reentry.
