Abstract
We present an inverse method for transforming a given parallel light emittance to two light distributions at different parallel target planes using two freeform reflectors. The reflectors control both the spatial and directional target coordinates of light rays. To determine the shape and position of the reflectors, we derive generating functions and use Jacobian equations to find the optical mappings to the two targets. The model is solved numerically by a three-stage least-squares algorithm. A feasibility condition is derived, which ensures that the reflectors are not self-intersecting. Several examples validate this condition and demonstrate the algorithm’s capability of realizing complex distributions.

Introduction

The goal in non-imaging optics is to design optical surfaces that convert a source light distribution to a target distribution. The two most commonly used methods are ray-trace methods and inverse methods. Ray-trace methods entail an iterative procedure where light rays propagate through the optical system and based on the deviation from the desired target distribution, the shape(s) of the optical surface(s) are altered
[

[

5

[
6
]
.

Inverse methods can be classified into two categories: methods that use optimization strategies and methods that directly solve a (generalized) Monge-Ampère equation or generated Jacobian equation; for a comprehensive overview, see

2.2
Energy conservation

We will now derive relations for energy conservation between
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}_{1}
, as well as between
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, by defining optical mappings
\bm{m}_{1}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{T}_{1}

\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{y}
and
\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y})=\bm{z}

\mathcal{S}
emits light with emittance

\mathcal{T}_{1}

and

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}}f(\bm{x})\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\iint_{\bm{m}_{1}(\mathcal{A})}g_{1}(\bm{y})\;\mathrm{d}\bm{y}=\iint_{\mathcal{A}}g_{1}(\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x}))\;|\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x}))|\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

for arbitrary
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{S}
, where
\text{D}\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
denotes the Jacobian matrix of
\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
. Similarly, energy conservation between
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
gives

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{B}}g_{1}(\bm{y})\;\mathrm{d}\bm{y}=\iint_{\bm{m}_{2}(\mathcal{B})}g_{2}(\bm{z})\;\mathrm{d}\bm{z}=\iint_{\mathcal{B}}g_{2}(\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y}))\;|\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y}))|\;\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

\displaystyle\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=\frac{f(\bm{x})}{g_{1}(\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x}))}=:F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})),

(10a)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{1}(\partial\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\partial\mathcal{T}_{1},

(11a)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{2}(\partial\mathcal{T}_{1})

2.4
G-convexity

The generating functions
G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y});V(\bm{y}))
and

given by the expressions (

) and (

satisfying the corresponding Eqs. (
7a
) and (
8a
). We select a unique solution by enforcing

and

to form either a

7

\displaystyle u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y});V(\bm{y})),

\displaystyle u_{2}(\bm{y})=\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{y})),

and in the second case, the solution reads

\displaystyle u_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}}G(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{2}(\bm{y});V(\bm{y})),

\displaystyle u_{2}(\bm{y})=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S}}H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{y})).

(13)

under the condition that the mixed Hessian matrix

\displaystyle\bm{C}=\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{y}))=\text{D}_{\bm{xy}}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{\partial x_{i}\,\partial y_{j}}\right),

(14)

is invertible for all

and

In the two-target two-reflector system, the desired illuminances

We can write
\sin(\theta_{3})=\Delta u_{2}/\Delta y_{1}
. As
\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}_{2,p}\to\underline{\hat{\bm{t}}}_{1,p}

\theta_{1}\to 0

\theta_{2}\to\tfrac{\pi}{2}

\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}+\theta_{4}=\pi

\theta_{3}\to\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\theta_{4}

\theta_{1}\to 0

\frac{\partial u_{2}}{\partial y_{2}}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial y_{2}}
. From this analysis we conclude that
\mathcal{R}_{2}
self-intersects when
\nabla_{\bm{y}}u_{2}=\nabla_{\bm{y}}V
. Thus, the second reflector does not self-intersect when
\nabla_{\bm{y}}\left(u_{2}-V\right)\neq\bm{0}
. Since two distinct rays originating from the source are parallel, they cannot hit
\mathcal{R}_{1}
in the same point and the first reflector can therefore not self-intersect.

4
The least-squares algorithm

To solve the two-target two-reflector system, we apply a three-stage least-squares algorithm.

Stage 1:
The first stage of the algorithm computes the mapping
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y})
. Since this computation is a simpler version of the computation of
\bm{y}=\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
, we first elaborate the procedure to compute
\bm{y}=\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
(see Stage 3) and outline the computation of
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y})
at the end of this section.

Stage 2:
The second stage of the algorithm proceeds by computing the optical path length
V
. In the algorithm, the second relation of Eq. (
12
) is imposed in a least-squares sense by minimizing the functional

\displaystyle K[\bm{m}_{2},V]=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}|\nabla V-\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}(\bm{y},\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y}))|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{y}.

(17)

Specifically, the algorithm finds
V\in C^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{1})
from

\displaystyle V=\underset{\psi\in C^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{1})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;K[\bm{m}_{2},\psi],

(18)

by solving the corresponding Neumann problem

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}V

\displaystyle=\nabla\bm{\cdot}\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}},

\displaystyle\bm{y}\in\mathcal{T}_{1},

(19a)

\displaystyle\nabla V\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{\mathrm{t}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}},

\displaystyle\bm{y}\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{1},

(19b)

where
\hat{\bm{n}}
is the unit outward normal on
\partial\mathcal{T}_{1}
. We derived this boundary value problem by setting the first variation of
K
with respect to
V
to zero, and solved it using a cell-centered finite volume method on a Cartesian grid. Since the solution of this boundary value problem is determined up to an additive constant, we enforce a unique solution by setting the value of
V
for a specific light ray, such as the center ray, equal to some value
V_{0}
.

Stage 3:
The final stage of the algorithm iteratively computes the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
and the reflector surfaces. To compute
\bm{y}=\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
, the algorithm starts from an initial guess
\bm{m}_{1}^{0}
, which maps the smallest bounding box enclosing source domain
\mathcal{S}
to the smallest bounding box enclosing target domain
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. Subsequently, the algorithm minimizes the functionals

\displaystyle J_{I}[\bm{m}_{1},\bm{P}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{S}}||\bm{C}\text{D}\bm{m}_{1}-\bm{P}||_{F}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(20a)

\displaystyle J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m}_{1},\bm{b}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{S}}|\bm{m}_{1}-\bm{b}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s,

(20b)

\displaystyle J[\bm{m}_{1},\bm{P},\bm{b}]

\displaystyle=\alpha J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m}_{1},\bm{P}]+(1-\alpha)J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m}_{1},\bm{b}],

(20c)

\displaystyle I[\bm{m}_{1},u_{1}]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{S}}\Big|\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1},u_{1};V)}{\partial_{w}H(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1},u_{1};V)}+\nabla u_{1}\Big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(20d)

with
||\cdot||_{F}
the Frobenius norm, by computing

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J_{\mathrm{I}}[\bm{m}_{1}^{n},\bm{P}],

(20e)

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J_{\mathrm{B}}[\bm{m}_{1}^{n},\bm{b}],

(20f)

\displaystyle\bm{m}_{1}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{m}_{1}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{S})^{2}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J[\bm{m}_{1},\bm{P}^{n+1},\bm{b}^{n+1}],

(20g)

\displaystyle u_{1}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{u_{1}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{S})}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;I[\bm{m}_{1}^{n+1},u_{1}],

(20h)

where the function spaces
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{B}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{P}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{S})^{2\times 2}\;\big|\;\bm{P}\text{ is SPD or SND, }\text{det}(\bm{P})=F_{1}(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x}))\text{det}(\bm{C})\},

(20i)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{b}\in C^{1}(\partial\mathcal{S})^{2}\;\big|\;\bm{b}(\bm{x})\in\partial\mathcal{T}_{1}\}.

(20j)

In the first two steps of Stage 3, the functionals
J_{\mathrm{I}}
and
J_{\mathrm{B}}
are minimized for
\bm{P}
and
\bm{b}
to satisfy Eq. (
15
) subject to the constraint (
16
) and the transport boundary condition (
11a
). Here, both the mapping
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y})=\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x}))
and the optical path length
V(\bm{y})
required to compute the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
are calculated using bilinear interpolation. The third step then minimizes a linear combination
J
with weighting factor
\alpha\in(0,1)
of both functionals
J_{\mathrm{I}}
and
J_{\mathrm{B}}
to update the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
. The fourth step of the scheme minimizes the functional
I
to satisfy Eq. (
13
). At the end of each iteration, we update
H(\bm{x},\bm{y},u_{1}(\bm{x});V(\bm{y}))
and the matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
using Eqs. (
8b
), (
14
) and (
15
). These steps are then repeated for
n=0,1,2,...
until convergence. Finally, we use Eqs. (
3
) to compute the shapes of the reflectors.

An in-depth analysis of the minimization of the functionals
J_{\mathrm{I}}
,
J_{\mathrm{B}}
and
J
that uses finite volume discretizations can be found in Romijn
et al.

[
7
]
. By writing the functional
I
as

\displaystyle I[u_{1},\bm{m}_{1}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{S}}\left|\bm{f}+\nabla u_{1}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

\displaystyle\bm{f}=\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1},u_{1};V)=\frac{\nabla_{\bm{x}}H(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1},u_{1};V)}{\partial_{w}H(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1},u_{1};V)},

(21)

and setting its first variation with respect to
u_{1}
to zero, we obtain the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\nabla\bm{\cdot}(\bm{f}+\nabla u_{1})

\displaystyle=\partial_{w}\bm{f}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{f}+\nabla u_{1}),

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\mathcal{S},

(22a)

\displaystyle(\bm{f}+\nabla u_{1})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\partial\mathcal{S},

(22b)

where
\hat{\bm{n}}
is the unit outward normal on
\partial\mathcal{S}
. To solve this boundary value problem for
u_{1}
, we apply a cell-centered finite volume method on a Cartesian grid. We verified that the compatibility condition
\iint_{\mathcal{S}}\partial_{w}\bm{f}\bm{\cdot}(\bm{f}+\nabla u_{1})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=0
holds in each of our simulations. The solution of boundary value problem (
22
) is determined up to an additive constant. Therefore, we enforce a unique solution by choosing the value of
u_{1}
for a specific light ray, such as the center ray, equal to a value
u_{1}(\bm{x}_{c})
.

Stage 1 revisited:
We compute
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y})
in a similar way as
\bm{y}=\bm{m}_{1}(\bm{x})
. However, since there are no optical surfaces between target planes
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, we can choose the generating function

\displaystyle G_{\mathcal{T}}=G_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}+w,

(23)

and its inverse

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{T}}=H_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)=-\bm{x}\bm{\cdot}\bm{y}+w,

(24)

corresponding to a quadratic cost function, for which a derivation can be found in
[
7
, p. 71]
. To compute
\bm{z}=\bm{m}_{2}(\bm{y})
, we use the iteration scheme (
20
) once more subject to the following modifications:

•

In each of the expressions in iteration scheme (
20
),
\bm{m}_{1}
is replaced by
\bm{m}_{2}
,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is replaced by
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, both
\mathcal{S}
and
\partial\mathcal{S}
are replaced by
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\partial\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
H(\bm{x},\bm{m}_{1},u_{1};V)
is replaced by
H_{\mathcal{T}}(\bm{x},\bm{y},w)
, and
F_{1}
is replaced by
F_{2}
.

•

The function
\widetilde{H}(\bm{x},\bm{y})
, used in expressions (
14
) and (
15
) for matrices
\bm{C}
and
\bm{P}
, is defined in terms of generating function
H_{\mathcal{T}}
instead of
H
.

5
Numerical results

In this section we provide several examples of the two-target reflector system. We will first consider the two-dimensional model and provide two examples: a standard example with different light distributions and an example which verifies the feasibility condition discussed in Sec.
3
. We then consider the three-dimensional model and provide two examples that demonstrate the potential of the least-squares algorithm.

5.1
Two-dimensional results

In the two-dimensional model,
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
are line segments, and
\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. As a consequence, Eqs. (
10
) reduce to ordinary differential equations (ODEs) that can be solved for the mappings
y=m_{1}(x)
and
z=m_{2}(y)
. Eq. (
12
) also simplifies to the ODE
V^{\prime}=\tfrac{\partial V}{\partial y}=t_{1}
, which can be solved for
V=V(y)
. Finally, taking the partial derivative on both sides of Eq. (
8a
) with respect to
x
and rearranging terms gives the ODE

\displaystyle u_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{a_{1}+a_{2}u_{1}}{a_{3}},

(25a)

with auxiliary variables

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=(V-(y-x)t_{1}-L_{1}t_{2})(y-x)-\tfrac{1}{2}(V^{2}-(y-x)^{2}-L_{1}^{2})t_{1},

(25b)

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=(V-L_{1})t_{1}-(1-t_{2})(y-x),

(25c)

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=(V-L_{1})(V-(y-x,L_{1})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}})-\tfrac{1}{2}(V^{2}-(y-x)^{2}-L_{1}^{2})(1-t_{2}),

(25d)

which can be solved for
u_{1}

and
u_{1}
for a specific light ray. Finally,
u_{2}
can be obtained from Eq. (
8a
) and Eqs. (
3

ode45

As an example, we consider an exponential light distribution

on

on

and a uniform distribution

on

. We let

,

V
for the left-most light ray to

. Fig.

shows the corresponding reflector system with

light rays. The shapes of the reflectors can be verified by ray-tracing. In this example, the reflectors do not self-intersect, which can be verified using the feasibility condition discussed in Sec.

, which states that

does not self-intersect when

. Fig.

shows a plot of the corresponding functions

and

As a second example, we consider a normal light distribution
f(x)=\exp{((\mu-x)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}))}

6
Conclusions

This paper presents an inverse method for transforming a given parallel light emittance to two illuminance patterns at different target planes using two freeform reflectors. We modeled the system using the optical path length, generating functions and used energy conservation to find generated Jacobian equations. We derived a feasibility condition that indicates the occurrence of a self-intersecting reflector and verified this condition by considering a two-dimensional simplification of the system. Finally, we used a three-stage least-squares algorithm to compute the three-dimensional reflector system with two complicated target distributions. Overall, the results advance our understanding of how the optical path length can be used to control both the position and momentum of a light ray at the target using an inverse method. In future research we will extend the algorithm by also taking non-parallel sources into account.
