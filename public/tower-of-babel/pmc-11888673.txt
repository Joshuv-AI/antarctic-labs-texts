Current Status of Knowledge, Attitude, and Perspective of Dental Practitioners Toward Stem Cell Uses in Dentistry
Authors: K Anitha, Sangamitra Asoka, Acchuthakrishnan, T Dhanraj, Preethi Arunachalam, Sudharshan Arumugam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:The purpose of this study was to evaluate dentists’ attitudes and level of knowledge about dental stem cells.Material and Methods:Five hundred dentists from Tamil Nadu, India, took part in a cross-sectional descriptive questionnaire-based survey that assessed knowledge, attitudes, and practices. For statistical analysis, percentages of the total and Pearson’s Chi-square test were employed.Results:Dental stem cells were considerably more familiar to participants with over 10 years of work experience. Of them, 93.2% are aware of the several uses for dental stem cells. A statistical test revealed a substantial relationship between the dental professionals’ academic qualifications and the sources of information, dental stem cells, and the clinical applications.Conclusion:The results of this current study demonstrated that dental professionals had an acceptable level of understanding, but there is still a need to raise awareness of the latest developments in dental stem cell applications, banking, storage, and guidelines.

INTRODUCTION

Our human body is made up of more than millions of cells that are subjected to various substances. Disease results in numerous imperfections that must be corrected for the body to keep functioning properly.[1] In the human body, healing takes place in two ways: repair and regeneration. There are specialized cells in our bodies known as stem cells, and they have the ability to regenerate to diverse bodily parts and cells as needed—they have been entrusted with this responsibility by nature.[23] Numerous types of stem cells exist, including chondrogenic (which may produce cartilage), osteogenic (which can produce bone), adipogenic (which can produce connective tissue), and neurogenic (which can produce neural tissue). Stem cells are classified as totipotent, pluripotent, multipotent, oligopotent, or unipotent based on their capacity to regenerate.[4]

An adult’s stem cells can come from any postnatal organ. Adult stem cells are further divided into nonhematopoietic – mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) based on where they came from. HSCs were extracted from the peripheral blood system or bone marrow. MSCs, such as hepatic stem cells, dermal stem cells, limbal stem cells, and dental stem cells (DSCs), are cells produced in the mesoderm layer of the fetus and develop into the adult tissues.[56] Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), periodontal ligament stem cells (PLSCs), stem cells from apical pulp (SCAP), dental fibroblast progenitor cells (DFPC), and stem cells from the exfoliated deciduous teeth are among the several kinds of dental stem cells. Specifically, exfoliated deciduous teeth are the most preferred of all these varieties due to their numerous extra benefits, including the least stressful procedure, safety, and lack of side effects.[78]

Recently, it has been demonstrated that exfoliated deciduous tooth (stem cells) are more capable of differentiating than other kinds of dental stem cells. The stem cell preservation labs supply the containers and storage material that clinicians need to keep their exfoliated deciduous teeth. Cryopreservation is used to retain these cells at 4° C. These dental stem cells are kept in specialized dental stem cell banks.[910] DSCs have the extra benefit of being a bit less problematic and being simpler to obtain than other types of stem cells. Tooth banking and DSC harvesting are becoming more and more common as a result of this. Dental professionals must be knowledgeable about the benefits and drawbacks of DSCs in order to counsel their patients accordingly. Dental stem cells are still in their beginnings in India, and there does not seem to be much knowledge about them. Thus, the purpose of this current study was to assess dental practitioners’ knowledge of stem cells.

MATERIALS AND METHOD

The current cross-sectional descriptive study was carried out from March to July 2024 using a questionnaire-based survey method. The study that was conducted involved knowledge, attitude, and practice surveys. The questionnaire was created, and 20 dentists participated in pilot research to test it out. Depending on how well the ideas related to the subject in question, they were included. There were 20 questions on the final survey. The sample size was established at 500 based on the results of a pilot study including 20 dentists, 5% alpha error, 95% confidence level, and 80% power.

Dentistry professionals who either resided or were employed in Tamil Nadu, India, made up the survey population. The questionnaire had 500 consenting dentists in total, comprising academicians from dental colleges and private dental practitioners. The questionnaire was a self-administered, hand-delivered, closed-ended questionnaire.

The first five inquiries dealt with sociodemographic factors. The sociodemographic factors were years of experience, years of qualification, gender, and field of work. The next set of questions evaluated knowledge about stem cells and DSCs, knowledge sources, tooth banking practices, applications, ethical considerations, obstacles to stem cell utilization, and strategies for raising awareness of the same.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2018 was used to enter the participant’s responses. The statistical package for social sciences, version 21.0, was used to conduct the analysis (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For statistical analysis, percentages of the total and the Chi-square test were used to analyze the data. A significant P value is one that is less than 0.05.

RESULTS

Of the 500 participants in the study, 285 (57%) were men and 215 (43%) were women. Five hundred individuals took part in the study, 200 (40%) of whom were in the 25–30 age range, 150 (30%) in the 30–35 age range, and another 150 (30%) in the 35–40 age range [Table 1]. Of the 500 participants in the survey, 466 (93.2%) had heard about the use of stem cells in dentistry, whereas 34 (6.4%) had never heard of the practice.

Dental professionals with more than 10 years of work experience knew significantly more knowledge about dental stem cells (65.4%) (P value = 0.002). Professionals with fewer than 5 years of work experience were found to know significantly less about the various kinds and uses of the dental stem cells (15.2%) (P value < 0.001). Participants with 5 to 10 years of work experience had a moderate level of understanding of stem cells (19.4%) [Graph 1]. A greater proportion of experts with over 10 years of experience stated that stem cell preservation is crucial, even after stem cells of the umbilical cord have been preserved (P value < 0.001). After the preservation of stem cells of the umbilical cord, roughly 40% of people were more knowledgeable about saving dental stem cells.

Knowledge of stem cells with respect to experience of dental professionals

A little over 30.2% of people knew about the many uses of dental stem cells in relation to oral health care, while the remaining people had no idea into their instruction. There were 51.8% of dentists who are prepared to invest in dental stem cell banking and other professionals who are not yet prepared to collect dental stem cells in their dental practice.

A statistically significant 45% of participants stated that the high cost, ethical quandaries, and low patient knowledge of dental stem cells are the main barriers preventing patients from receiving dental stem cell therapy. Nearly 80.2% of participants recommended that dental practitioners be made more aware of dental stem cells and tooth banking through seminars, journals, academic curricula, conferences, continuing dentistry education, and public notice [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

The study’s present goal was to assess Tamil Nadu dentists’ knowledge, awareness, and perception of the purposes of stem cells. Dental pulp stem cells, exfoliated deciduous tooth stem cells, and apical papilla stem cells are examples of dental stem cells. Dental stem cells can be used to regenerate the periodontium, pulp, dentin, and whole tooth. The concept of utilizing stem cells, gene therapy, and other regeneration methods is the foundation of regenerative dentistry. Dental stem cells come in a variety of forms and can be used to treat conditions like bone regeneration, brain tissue, muscular dystrophy, and heart disease. Transcell Biologics Pvt. Ltd., Ree Labs, and Stemade Biotech are three stem cell banks that can preserve dental stem cells for up to 20 years.[111213] In contrast to our study, less than 2% of the 500 survey respondents knew of any relatives who had stem cell banking, and 466 (93.2%) of them had heard about the use of stem cells in dentistry.

All of the group’s participants were ignorant of the Indian Council of Medical Research’s ethical requirements for the clinical application of dental stem cells and the methods for preserving them. Chitroda PK et al.’s findings[14] were consistent with the findings of this investigation.

Evidence of SHED’s capacity for multipotential differentiation and widespread proliferation was presented by Miura et al.[8] Ideal candidates for isolating and extracting stem cells include primary incisors and canines that are free of disease and have at least one-third of their root length intact. Participants’ understanding of the teeth used for banking was moderate.

In our study, there are around 51.8% of dental professionals who are willing to invest in dental stem cell banking and other professionals who are not yet ready to gather dental stem cells for their clinical practice. DSCs have demonstrated the ability to regenerate pulp dentin-like complex, dentin, and odontoblasts. Moreover, DSCs have strong nondental uses because they can differentiate into neurons, osteocytes, and adipocytes. The wide range of uses and illness treatments available from stem cell research is the primary driver of its increasing advancement. The ability of stem cells to regenerate tissue can be very helpful for a certain patient population dealing with systemic disorders. For patients to get the most out of DSC storage, dentists need to be aware of these applications and advise them accordingly.[8]

The overwhelming majority of participants in this study expressed a desire to attend CDE programs or conferences in the near future.[15] This might be attributed to growing research and applications in the field, as well as a possible lack of in-depth understanding of dental stem cells. This calls for the organization of dental stem cells related to training and CDE programs. It is important to raise knowledge of these standards since dental residents’ perspectives may assist to reinterpret them, which could improve the safety of surgeries or treatments involving dental stem cells.

CONCLUSION

This study reveals that dental professionals were aware of dental stem cells, but they were still ignorant of the collection method, ethical issues, and guidelines surrounding dental stem cells. To enable dental stem cells to be used for regeneration, the field of dental stem cells needs to be expanded upon, professionals need to become skilled in their use, and patients need to be made aware of them. Dental curricula and educational programs should include specific information about dental stem cells, storage techniques, and time constraints. This will pave the way for new developments in dental stem cell therapy. Future in vivo research will also be necessary to increase the applicability of dental stem cells in dental treatment.
