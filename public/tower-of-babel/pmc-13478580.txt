A smaller and more efficient one-hot encoding for QUBO
Authors: Shinji Takasugi, Hideo Nakaya, Yoshihiro Nakao, Kenta Sawamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne-hot constraints are widely used for quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) in quantum and simulated annealing to solve optimization problems. They correspond to exactly-one constraints for Boolean satisfiability problems (SAT), which have many different encodings proposed mainly to reduce SAT problem size. We have examined QUBO encodings, both corresponding to previously proposed SAT encodings and newly invented, to determine the best encoding in terms of QUBO size and solution quality. Our experiments show that reduced commander, our new encoding, gives one of the best trade-offs between QUBO size and solution quality for the benchmark problem studied in this work.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Prior research on one-hot constraints in QUBO has commonly overlooked the similarity between QUBO and the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT), which checks whether a given Boolean formula can be made true by assigning appropriate truth values to the formula’s variables. Prior research on one-hot encodings has primarily focused on reducing QUBO size, while their impact on solution quality, which is important in practical applications, has been largely overlooked. Therefore, in this work, we evaluate one-hot encodings from both perspectives and aim to find the best trade-off between QUBO size and solution quality.

Methods

First, we examine five existing one-hot encodings, then we propose a new one, to evaluate both QUBO size and solution quality. Note that QUBO size is an absolute limitation due to hardware constraints such as memory capacity, whereas solution quality is a performance factor where small differences are acceptable. To increase solution quality, we also demonstrate the optimizability of the parameters of each encoding, which, to our knowledge, has been overlooked. The parameters we optimize are not constraint weights but generalization of each encoding for a wider research scope.

To compare solution quality, we use the median energy of obtained solutions of the same benchmark problem. Due to the probabilistic nature of quantum and simulated annealing, the minimum energy among the solutions may vary and it tends to become smaller (i.e. better) as the number of anneals increases, while the median energy remains approximately constant.

Commander

The basic idea of commander encoding in SAT1 is two-level selection where one subset is selected first and then one variable is selected from the subset. It divides a set of n variables into p subsets. A commander variable is assigned to each subset, and the p commander variables have an exactly-one constraint. If a commander variable is false, all the variables of its assigned subset must be false. If a commander variable is true, its assigned subset must have at most one or exactly one true variable, depending on whether it is an at-most-one constraint or an exactly-one constraint respectively. Discussion

Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 show results of each encoding with various parameters. Pairwise encoding generally gives better solutions than the other encodings. It is known that the Hamming distance, which is the number of different binary variables between two states, is related to the success probability of a QUBO problem14, and pairwise encoding has the smallest Hamming distance between two one-hot states. Suppose one of the one-hot states is the global optimum. The Hamming distance to it from another one-hot state is 2. In product encoding, for example, the Hamming distance is 4 if they share a variable in either U or V and 6 otherwise, which means that it is harder to reach the global optimum in product encoding than in pairwise encoding. That also explains why α =0.75 is better than α =1 in pairwise encoding. The former gives a lower energy to the state with all 0’s than to the states with two 1’s, while the latter gives the same energy to both. The Hamming distance to the global optimum from the state with all 0’s is 1, while that from a state with two 1’s is 1 if one of the two 1’s is also 1 in the global optimum, and 3 otherwise. Therefore, it is harder to reach the global optimum from a state with two 1’s, which should be avoided. The average Hamming distances of the encodings are shown in Table 11.

Binary and product encodings have large Hamming distances to the closest neighbor, which reduces the efficiency in simulated annealing and makes them unpractical in solving real-world problems.

Domain wall encoding has the smallest Hamming distance of 1 to the closest neighbor, which means it allows a movement from a one-hot state to another without breaking the one-hot constraint, but the Hamming distance to the global optimum is large, which makes it difficult to reach it. We have also found that in simulated annealing, it needs many more steps to reach a one-hot state from an initial random state and therefore needs a much larger weight for constraints, which makes it difficult to use for real-world problems.

Reduced commander encoding has the second smallest average Hamming distance from a one-hot state to the global optimum, after pairwise encoding. That means reduced commander encoding is the most practical encoding when pairwise encoding is not acceptable for its large QUBO size.

Pairwise, commander, and reduced commander can be improved by choosing the best α as shown in Tables 4, 8, 9. The efficiency and the QUBO size in reduced commander encoding have a trade-off relationship as in Table 9. We also found that the optimal value of α varies with the number of subsets: α <1 performs best when the number of subsets is small whereas α =1 performs best when the number of subsets is large. We find that reduced commander encoding provides one of the best trade-offs between QUBO size and solution quality, especially when simple pairwise encoding produces a prohibitively large QUBO, because it produces solutions as good as pairwise encoding while requiring a smaller QUBO. This can be explained by its small average Hamming distances to the global optimum (Table 11). It has flexibility in QUBO size for the best performance possible on a given upper limit of QUBO size. Users should first optimize the reduced commander encoding parameters for each specific problem to achieve the best performance using a small number of anneals, since, in theory, the number of anneals does not influence the median energy, and then increase the number of anneals to obtain the best solution.

The usefulness of reduced commander encoding depends on problems. For example, graph coloring problem uses rather small one-hot sets, and QUBO size reduction is not very large, whereas traveling salesman problem may use large one-hot sets where reduced commander encoding is useful.

In addition, we also checked the one-hot encodings above in quantum annealing and found a similar trend, except that domain wall performs much better than in simulated annealing, which will be a subject of further study.
