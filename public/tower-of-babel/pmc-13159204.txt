Neonatal conjunctivitis due to uncommon pathogen Streptococcus mitis/oralis: a case report
Authors: Ali Kutay Kılınç, Nezihat Desen Büyüksoy, Yağmur Seda Yeşiltaş, Aylin Üner Bozdayı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal conjunctivitis (NC), historically attributed to Neisseria gonorrhoeae, is now known to be caused by various pathogens, including Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, and Chlamydia trachomatis. Although viridans group streptococci are generally regarded as commensal organisms, they may cause opportunistic conjunctival infections. Here, we present a case of NC caused by Streptococcus mitis/oralis, a member of the Mitis group of viridans group streptococci. A 15-day-old female neonate presented with bilateral severe eyelid edema and moderate hemopurulent discharge that precluded anterior segment evaluation. Symptoms began at nine days of age with serous discharge, which gradually worsened. The severe presentation initially raised concern for orbital cellulitis. Upon hospital admission, a conjunctival swab was obtained, and treatment was initiated with topical moxifloxacin and fusidic acid, along with empirical intravenous penicillin and gentamicin. Eyelid edema and discharge improved significantly within 24–48 h. Culture results confirmed Streptococcus mitis/oralis, which was sensitive to the administered antibiotics. Complete clinical recovery was subsequently achieved without complications. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of differentiating severe NC from orbital cellulitis and underscores the potential of S. mitis/oralis to cause significant ocular morbidity in neonates. The prompt and dramatic response to antibacterial treatment prevented long-term complications, and this case supports the hypothesis of postnatal transmission from the neonate’s own nasopharyngeal flora or that of the caregiver rather than exclusive acquisition from the birth canal.Keywords: Neonatal conjunctivitis, Newborn conjunctivitis, Ophthalmia neonatorum, Streptococcus mitis/oralis

Introduction

Historically, neonatal conjunctivitis (NC) was primarily caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Today, however, a broader spectrum of causes has been recognized, including chemical, viral, fungal, and bacterial agents such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae. Rare reports also implicate α-hemolytic streptococci—organisms typically part of the commensal flora—as the underlying pathogens. Despite advances in neonatal care, disparities in healthcare access continue to result in unequal global outcomes. Newborns have highly permeable corneas and limited tear production, which reduces the natural clearance of pathogens and renders neonates particularly susceptible to ocular infections. Such infections can cause lifelong ocular complications. [1, 2]

Staphylococcus aureus is the most common bacterial pathogen causing NC in Nepal, southeastern China, and Singapore. With the widespread use of prophylactic eye drops in newborns—many of which are possibly ineffective against Chlamydia trachomatis—conjunctivitis has also emerged as a prominent cause of NC [3–5].

Viridans group streptococci (VGS) are Gram-positive cocci that form alpha-hemolytic colonies on sheep blood agar and constitute normal flora in the oral cavity, upper respiratory tract, gastrointestinal system, urogenital tract, and skin. VGS are classified into five groups based on biochemical reactions and 16 S rDNA sequencing with the Mitis group receiving particular research interest due to its inclusion of Streptococcus pneumoniae. Unlike these generally commensal VGS, S. pneumoniae is highly pathogenic. Members of the Mitis group including S. mitis, S. oralis, and S. pneumoniae, share anatomical niches and exhibit ongoing gene exchange, transformation, and lysis. Gram stain typically shows cocci (with exceptions); colonies are optochin-resistant and bile-insoluble, distinguishing them from S. pneumoniae [6, 7]. Streptococcus mitis, a viridans group streptococcus, normally colonizes the human oropharynx as a commensal. However, it can cause infections such as infective endocarditis, bacteremia, and sepsis. Its colonization factors enable competition with more pathogenic oropharyngeal microorganisms for space and nutrients. In immunocompromised patients, these factors serve as virulence mechanisms, promoting opportunistic infections [8]. Even conjunctival infections caused by typically benign pathogens such as Streptococcus mitis/oralis should not be underestimated, as they carry risks of serious complications, including meningitis [9]. Here, we report a severe case of NC from conjunctival colonization by S. mitis/oralis, highlighting its clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, and management.

Case presentatıon

A 15-day-old female neonate, born at term via normal vaginal delivery, as the second child of a 27-year-old mother, was referred from pediatrics to our ophthalmology outpatient clinic with suspected orbital cellulitis. She presented with bilateral severe eyelid edema that prevented examination of the cornea or bulbar conjunctiva, accompanied by moderate hemopurulent discharge.

According to the family, symptoms began at nine days of age as serous discharge from one eye and gradually progressed to purulent discharge, particularly in the mornings. During a routine neonatal check-up on day ten, the family mentioned these sympthoms which were considered mild and common in newborns; therefore no intervention was initiated at that time. Over the ensuing days, the discharge became more voluminous and purulent, along with progressive eyelid swelling. At the routine pediatric visit on day 15, the severe eyelid edema prompted an ophthalmology consultation.

The mother had attended regular prenatal visits and all screening tests and follow-up ultrasonography were normal. She reported no infections during pregnancy. The neonate was born at 40 weeks and 3 days of gestation, weighing 2880 g, and has been in good health with stable vital signs since birth. Neurological examination revealed normal fontanelles and positive Moro, Grasp, and Babinski reflexes; and normal muscle tone. Systemic examination was otherwise unremarkable.

At initial ophthalmic evaluation, orbital cellulitis could not be definitively excluded. Eversion of the upper eyelid revealed a protruding, markedly swollen palpebral conjunctiva resembling congenital eyelid eversion. Corneal evaluation using a lid speculum was limited due to ocular deviation; however, lateral palpation confirmed normal globe pressure, and ultrasonography verified preserved ocular integrity. No imaging was performed, as it would not alter the initial management and was reserved for later consideration if clinical deterioration occurred.

The patient was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit per protocol for suspected early-onset sepsis. A conjunctival discharge swab culture was obtained prior to initiation of treatment. Laboratory investigation showed a CRP of 6 mg/L, IL-6 of 25.76 ng/L, and WBC count of 18.62 × 109/L. Following consultation with the pediatric service, empiric therapy was initiated with intravenous penicillin (50,000 IU/kg/day) and gentamicin (4 mg/kg/day) were initiated, complemented by topical moxifloxacin (six times daily) and fusidic acid (once daily).

Bedside evaluation 16–24 h post-treatment initiation demonstrated substantial resolution of eyelid edema, permitting corneal visualization without the use of a lid speculum. The cornea was clear without opacification (no infiltration, edema, haziness or NaFl staining), and the anterior chamber was formed. Natural eye movements without restrictions were observed, and optic nerves were normal on indirect ophthalmoscopy. By treatment day two, further reductions in eyelid edema and conjunctival discharge was observed, and a subpalpebral membrane, now more discernible, was mechanically debrided.

No significant fever was observed during the patient’s stay in the intensive care unit. Blood cultures obtained during hospitalization showed no growth. Pre-treatment conjunctival swab culture identified Streptococcus mitis/oralis, susceptible to all tested antibiotics, including penicillin G; thus, the empiric antibiotic regimen remained unaltered. Topical moxifloxacin was tapered to three times daily, and intravenous antibiotics were administered for a total of 10 days until complete clinical and laboratory resolution was achieved. Neurological examinations throughout hospitalization were unremarkable, as was bedside transcranial ultrasonography.

Microbiological analysıs

Conjunctival samples were collected by gently applying a sterile cotton swab to the conjunctiva secretions, taking care to avoid contact with the eyelashes or eyelids to minimize contamination. The samples were then sent to the laboratory for culture. Cultures were performed by inoculating the swabs onto blood and chocolate agar plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a CO₂ incubator. Plates showing no growth after 48 h were deemed negative. Resulting colonies were identified via biochemical assays using the automated IVD-MALDI Biotyper® (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) system. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the VITEK 2 (bioMérieux, France).

Discussion

The timing of symptom onset following term vaginal delivery initially pointed towards Herpes viruses or Chlamydia trachomatis based on established diagnostic algorithms [1]. Although corneal perforation remained a suspicion until eyelid edema resolved, a more critical concern was the potential for posterior extension of infection. Although retrobulbar spread is rarely reported in the literature, empirical treatment targeted this risk. Notably, it should be taken into consideration that the risk of sepsis caused by NC may be higher than the risk caused by retrobulbar cellulitis spread [10, 11]. In this regard, gram staining of conjunctival swabs and blood analyses offer valuable insights into the causative pathogen of neonatal conjunctivitis.

During the patient’s initial examination, the limited ophthalmological evaluation made it difficult to rule out orbital cellulitis. The family was concerned about subjecting the newborn to an MRI under general anesthesia. From our perspective particularly it might miss the corneal perforation. Although an MRI seemed unavoidable due to the lack of response to treatment, the clinical improvement observed 16–24 h later changed our perspective.

Various regimens have been proposed for NC includes intravenous cephalosporin, oral macrolide, and topical fluoroquinolone regimen applied by Law et al. [12] In our case, despite the absence of sepsis-related clinical signs, laboratory findings indicated bacterial infection. Following the pediatric consultation and Turkish Neonatology Association guidelines, empirical intravenous penicillin and gentamicin were initiated for suspected early-onset sepsis [13]. Ultimately, earlier topical treatment at the first follow-up might have sufficed and the clinical condition may not have been serious enough to require intravenous treatment. Since the patient was expected to be discharged after full recovery, the treatment was extended in accordance with the guideline.

Systemic moxifloxacin is not FDA-approved for pediatric use due to arthropathy risk. However, topical ophthalmic formulations have been shown to be safe and effective across all ages, with no effect on weight-bearing joints—unlike certain oral quinolones, which cause arthropathy in immature animals [14, 15]. Additionally, Boadi-Kusi et al. have proposed ciprofloxacin and gentamicin as options for treating antibiotic-resistant bacterial NC [16]. Fluoroquinolones also have potential activity against Chlamydia species [17], and the dramatic clinical improvement observed in our patient reduced the necessity for adjunctive oral azithromycin. Nevertheless, it is important to note that prophylactic ocular treatments in newborns may not be effective against Chlamydia species [18].

The pathogen from the patient was identified as Streptococcus Mitis/oralis using MALDI-TOF MS (score was 1979), with the identification confirmed by resistance to an optochin disk. Although rare, some Mitis group streptococci may be misidentified as S. Pneumoniae due to shared evolutionary ancestry and horizontal gene transfer among species within the same niche. Accurate species identification is therefore crucial for assessing pathogenic potential, disease burden, and antimicrobial resistance patterns among pneumococci and other Mitis streptococci [19, 20].

Our findings, consistent with those reported by Krohn et al., suggest that pathogens implicated in NC do not invariably originate from the birth canal but may instead be acquired postnatally from the neonate’s own nasopharyngeal flora or from caregivers [21]. Although patient received routine neonatal ocular prophylaxis (netilmicin sulfate) at birth this observation implies that management of NC may require individualized clinical judgement beyond standard algorithms. However, other studies have shown that these pathogens may found in vaginal flora and cause intraamniotic infection [22].

Regardless of treatment response, prolonged eyelid edema in neonates should be closely monitored, as it may result in deprivation amblyopia [23].

The Streptococcus mitis/oralis conjunctivitis encountered in our patient was characterized by hemopurulent discharge and severe eyelid edema with the hemorrhagic component likely attributable to a subconjunctival membrane. This case is notable for its unusually severe presentation of NC which closely mimicked orbital cellulitis and posed a significant diagnostic challenge. The identification of Streptococcus mitis/oralis, an uncommon and typically commensal pathogen, as the causative organism underscores the potential for viridans group streptococci to cause significant ocular disease in neonates. The prompt initiation of appropriate systemic and topical antibacterial therapy resulted in a rapid and dramatic clinical response, preventing ocular and systemic complications. This case emphasizes the importance of early recognition, microbiological confirmation, and timely treatment in severe NC, even when caused by organisms traditionally considered to have low virulence.
