Cognitive Radio for Satellite TT & C System: A General Dataset Using Software-defined Radio
Authors: Yi Zhang, Bo Zang, Hongbing Ji, Lin Li, Shiyao Li, Leyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid advancement of satellite communication systems necessitates the integration of cognitive radio technologies to ensure system adaptability. However, existing public datasets are predominantly centered on terrestrial signal features, severely constraining their applicability in space environments. Here, we introduce RML24, the first open-source benchmark dataset specifically engineered for deep learning-based satellite Telemetry, Tracking, and Command (TT&C) signal processing. We generated over 1.3 million signal samples using Software Defined Radio (SDR) and Radio Frequency (RF) transceiver platforms, encompassing 22 modulation schemes prevalent in TT&C systems. RML24 incorporates simulated satellite channel models, authentic RF link effects, and signal propagation phenomena to align the data distribution with real-world signal characteristics. All datasets and associated model codes are publicly available. Furthermore, to facilitate broader cognitive radio research, RML24 includes comprehensive and precise annotations for tasks such as deep learning-based parameter estimation and demodulation. By providing a high-quality resource tailored for satellite communications, RML24 aims to accelerate the development of intelligent, adaptive, and deep learning-driven satellite systems.

Background & Summary

With the development of expansive low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite constellations and enhancements in satellite communication links1, satellite communications are poised to play an essential role in securing data connectivity for both fixed and mobile users in the future. In the context of increasingly scarce spectrum resources, cognitive radio technology, through efficient spectrum sensing, has introduced significant innovation and optimization to satellite communications2,3.

Deep learning (DL) is inherently data-driven4–7 making the quality and representativeness of the dataset critical for model deployment. While the cognitive radio community has benefited significantly from established benchmarks—most notably the RadioML series and related datasets8–10 which integrate Software Defined Radio (SDR) simulations with Over-the-Air (OTA) measurements—these resources are predominantly tailored to terrestrial communication scenarios. Consequently, there remains a significant lack of a unified, open-source research data benchmark specifically designed for the satellite cognitive communication domain, limiting the development of algorithms suitable for space-based environments.

The evolution of 5G and forthcoming 6G networks emphasizes the integration of cognitive radio technologies into non-terrestrial networks (NTN), particularly Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite constellations. To adapt to these dynamic communication environments, research has increasingly pivoted toward Deep Learning (DL) methodologies that extract robust features directly from raw signal data rather than relying on predefined rules. Beyond standard Automatic Modulation Recognition (AMR), data-driven approaches have demonstrated significant promise in complex physical layer tasks, including channel estimation11–13, blind parameter estimation14–17, and neural demodulation18–20. Furthermore, the emergence of end-to-end learning paradigms—such as autoencoder-based communication systems—aims to concurrently optimize transmitter and receiver components, thereby enhancing overall link reliability18. While various architectures, ranging from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to Transformers21–25, have been explored to handle signal distortions, their performance in real-world deployment is fundamentally constrained by the quality and domain-specificity of the training data.

However, as cognitive radio evolves into satellite communication scenarios, existing public benchmarks exhibit significant limitations. First, predecessor datasets are predominantly tailored for terrestrial wireless channels, failing to capture the unique propagation characteristics of space-ground links, such as large-scale Doppler shifts and specific satellite modulation schemes. Second, previous works have heavily relied on purely software-simulated environments. This approach often overlooks the stochastic non-linearities and hardware impairments introduced by real Radio Frequency (RF) transmission chains, leading to a “simulation-to-reality” gap where models fail to generalize to actual signals. Third, most existing datasets focus exclusively on classification labels (AMR). The lack of granular annotations—such as Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), carrier frequency offsets, and ground-truth bit sequences—severely restricts their applicability to broader cognitive tasks like signal quality analysis and demodulation. Therefore, creating a high-fidelity, multi-purpose dataset specifically engineered for the satellite domain has become imperative.

In operational space missions, Telemetry, Tracking, and Command (TT&C) systems rely on rigorous standards to ensure reliable communication links. State-of-the-art telecommand receivers26,27, and telemetry architectures are fundamentally governed by specific synchronization and channel coding schemes, such as those defined by the Consultative Committee for Space Data Systems (CCSDS)28,29. To address these challenges, we propose RML24, a hardware-in-the-loop satellite TT&C dataset. Rather than capturing a purely physical space-ground link, RML24 focuses on combining standard-compliant satellite waveforms and simulated channel dynamics with real RF front-end artifacts. This approach models the theoretical transmission characteristics of satellite TT&C signals while incorporating authentic hardware impairments and complex channel conditions, including significant Doppler effects, multipath fading, and clock drift. Unlike previous datasets limited to classification, RML24 provides meticulous annotations for modulation types, signal parameters, and raw bit information, supporting a wide range of data-driven challenges such as modulation classification8, channel parameter estimation30, and demodulation20. By benchmarking prevalent DL models under varied bandwidths and sensing performance conditions, this work establishes a robust baseline for the community. RML24 aims to serve as a standard resource to accelerate the development of intelligent, adaptive algorithms capable of operating in real-world satellite communication scenarios. Method

The RML24 dataset was constructed using a hybrid generation pipeline that integrates SDR simulations via GNU Radio with empirical OTA measurements using the USRP X410 platform. This approach captures both the theoretical characteristics of satellite TT&C waveforms and the stochastic physical impairments inherent in radio frequency (RF) links. The dataset generation workflow consists of four distinct stages: baseband signal synthesis, channel impairment modeling, hardware-in-the-loop acquisition, and data post-processing. Although we generally minimize the use of equations, the description of signal generation requires detailed modeling and parameters. To prevent potential misunderstanding, essential formulas are preserved in key modeling parts to facilitate comprehension.

Timing error

The crystal oscillator (XO) is a vital component in wireless systems, where it generates a stable oscillation signal critical for ensuring the precise operation of local oscillators, analog-to-digital converters (ADC), and digital-to-analog converters (DAC) sampling timing via a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) and frequency multipliers9. Nonlinear Distortion

On the satellite payload side, the nonlinear memory effects, resulting from input/output filters and high-power amplifiers, contribute to significant signal distortion36. Satellite communication systems rely on microwave vacuum amplification devices capable of continuous high-power output to facilitate ultra-long-distance communication. Representative examples include traveling wave tubes (TWTs) and multi-cavity klystrons. Compared to solid-state power amplifiers in traditional ground-based radio equipment, RF power amplifiers on satellites are more susceptible to significant nonlinear characteristics, resulting in nonlinear distortion.

The Saleh model is a mathematical model used to describe the nonlinear behavior of RF power amplifiers. It effectively characterizes the amplitude-amplitude (AM/AM) and amplitude-phase (AM/PM) conversion characteristics of amplifiers. Channel Mode

In satellite communication channels, the most significant channel characteristics are the Doppler shift caused by the rapid movement of LEO satellites, path loss, and cloud and rain attenuation. Channel fading during transmission is divided into large-scale fading and small-scale fading. Large-scale fading is primarily caused by path loss, shadow fading, and atmospheric gas absorption losses, which are the core limiting factors for satellite communication link performance, mainly causing systematic attenuation of the signal amplitude. Therefore, RML24 maps large-scale fading to the variations in SNR in the dataset. Small-scale fading is mainly caused by instantaneous changes in signal strength or phase due to multipath effects or other medium inhomogeneities during signal transmission. Small-scale fading includes multipath delay spread caused by diffraction or scattering of the signal, and Doppler spread in the frequency domain. However, in practical satellite communication scenarios, the environment is relatively open with only a few weak multipath signals, so small-scale fading may not be as apparent in the dataset.

Ref. 37,38 have analyzed universal channels within 6G frameworks, pinpointing satellite communication scenarios not adequately covered by existing models such as B5GCM39, 3GPP TR 38.90140, IMT-202041, and QuaDRiGa42. We have referenced these models to construct a satellite communication scenario, disregarding the impact of large-scale fading on signal amplitude, and focusing on the small-scale fading in satellite communication scenarios.

In satellite mobile communications, the movement of the terminal and the Doppler effect cause the arriving signal waves at the terminal to experience a Doppler shift. Different angles of incidence result in different Doppler shifts, so the superposition of all scattered components forms a continuous Doppler spectrum. The shape of the Doppler power spectrum density function determines the signal’s time-domain fading waveform, short-term correlation, and fading rate, among other channel-influencing factors. The different relative velocities between the satellite and the terminal cause each multipath component to have different Doppler shifts. Hardware-in-the-Loop Acquisition (OTA)

Ref. 31 emphasized the importance of using SDR and RF semi-physical platforms to construct OTA datasets. After the RF link and real-world propagation effects, the generated signals are more realistic, which can improve the efficiency of the transfer learning task. However, this approach requires developers to have strong SDR development skills and expertise, and the efficiency of data generation is limited by the hardware.

The configuration of OTA data transmission is shown in Fig. 2. The dataset generation flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 3. OTA transmission and reception are performed using the USRP X410 SDR platform. The software-generated baseband signals are passed through the platform’s internal DAC, up-converted via the LO, and amplified by the RF power amplifiers before being transmitted through L-band omnidirectional antennas (length: 7 cm, diameter: 0.8 cm). On the receiver side, the signal chain employs corresponding antennas, down-converters, and ADC. This physical layer signal flow ensures that the dataset inherently captures realistic, hardware-induced artifacts. Specifically, we upconvert the modulated and pulse-shaped signal to 1 GHz using the X410 RF chain and then transmit it via an omnidirectional antenna. Both the transmission and reception equipment are located in a relatively ideal and uncontaminated environment. Once the signal has propagated through the actual medium, it is captured by the X410 omnidirectional antenna and downconverted to the baseband.

To precisely align the start position of the received signal with the original bit information, we perform a matched correlation between the pulse-shaped transmitted signal and the received signal, thus obtaining the exact starting position aligned with the original bit information. Subsequently, we introduce hardware artifacts and channel impairment effects into the model, and finally, noise is added at a predetermined SNR using an Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) operation. Because the physical transmission environment is relatively ideal, the dataset does not capture a true over-the-air ’satellite propagation’ channel. Instead, the primary purpose of the physical RF transmission is to introduce genuine hardware impairments (e.g., true oscillator phase noise, hardware thermal noise) that cannot be perfectly modeled by software. Therefore, the dataset is most accurately described as a hybrid framework that integrates standard-compliant satellite waveforms, simulated space-to-ground channel impairments, and authentic RF-chain artifacts.

To reflect operational spectrum sensing conditions, the dataset uses a variable code-rate signal source matched to existing satellite communication bandwidths. For single-layer modulations, symbol rates of 100 kHz, 200 kHz, and 500 kHz are available; for composite modulations, 20 kHz, 50 kHz, and 100 kHz are used. The system utilizes a fixed baseband sampling rate of 1 MHz. To avoid ambiguity, the signal’s occupied bandwidth (Bocc) is distinct from this sampling rate and is strictly determined by the symbol rate (Rs) and the RRC roll-off factor (αr), defined as Bocc = Rs(1 + αr). Each sample sequence length is fixed at l = 2048 (2.048 ms), yielding 1.3 million records. SNR span -20 dB to 20 dB in 2 dB steps. For l = 2048, generating a complete dataset requires approximately 85 hours.

The impact of satellite hardware on the data cannot be ignored. XO frequency errors, when scaled, are provided to the ADC, DAC, and local oscillator, resulting in SRO and CFO. In this work, we set the RF frequency at 1 GHz, with the maximum frequency deviation for XO, LO, and SRO for each sample set to 5 Hz, 500 Hz, and 50 Hz9. Section II details the significant nonlinear effects of high-power satellite equipment on the signal. The Saleh model fits the AM/AM and AM/PM characteristics of the power amplifier, simulating signal non-linear distortion, as shown in (16). Considering the level of distortion of the real signals, we set the model parameters to κa = 1.6623, λa = 0.0052, κp = 4.0033, and λp = 9.1040.

To ensure the representativeness of our channel assumptions, the simulated dynamics and multipath fading have been designed with reference to the 3GPP TR 38.81143 standard for NTN. The 3GPP NTN model specifies that satellite links are typically dominated by LOS paths, exhibiting fewer multipath reflections and smaller delay spreads compared to terrestrial channels. Our adopted Rician fading model accurately reflects this with standard-compliant delay profiles and a small number of taps. Furthermore, it is important to clarify the scientific rationale behind the specific Doppler distribution. While raw Doppler shifts in LEO scenarios can be substantial, modern TT&C ground receivers perform coarse Doppler pre-compensation utilizing satellite ephemeris data prior to baseband processing. Therefore, the fD ~ U(−1kHz, 1kHz) range is engineered as a controlled research benchmark to model the residual Doppler shift (uncompensated frequency offset) that persists at the cognitive processing stage. Additionally, this range directly encompasses the raw Doppler dynamics typical of Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO) TT&C links, making the dataset versatile for benchmarking diverse operational use-cases. Phase errors from frequency offsets, Doppler shifts, sampling inaccuracy, and TX-RX synchronisation are drawn from ϕ ~ U(0, 2π).

Technical Validation

To assess the integrity of the RML24 dataset and establish its utility for AMR tasks, a two-fold validation strategy was employed: signal fidelity verification and DL baseline benchmarking. To ensure the reproducibility of the baseline AMR results, the neural network models were trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−3, a batch size of 64, and a maximum of 100 epochs utilizing a categorical cross-entropy loss function.

Signal Fidelity and Physical Validation

The correctness of generated signals was first verified through statistical analysis. Power Spectral Density (PSD) measurements of post-channel signals confirmed that bandwidth occupancy and sideband levels matched theoretical expectations for each modulation type. Constellation diagrams can be used for signal quality assessment, bit error rate analysis, and algorithm verification tasks. To confirm the realistic incorporation of hardware and channel impairments, we analyzed the signal constellations at various intermediate processing stages (Fig. 5). As anticipated, the scatter plots exhibit distinct constellation rotations attributable to local oscillator frequency error (ferrLo) and clock frequency offset (ζerr), representing the accumulated phase error throughout the transmitter-receiver (TX-RX) chain. To further validate the accuracy of the signal modeling process, we generated enhanced density-colored constellation diagrams (Fig. 6). These visualizations reveal specific physical artifacts: (1) inter-symbol interference (ISI) introduced by the Root Raised Cosine (RRC) filter prior to matched filtering; (2) non-linear amplitude (Aout) and phase φout distortions induced by amplifier saturation; and (3) amplitude dispersion caused by Rician fading. The presence of these observable impairments confirms that the dataset faithfully preserves the physical layer challenges required for robust algorithm benchmarking.

Accurate information labels are essential for DL-based demodulation datasets, but they are difficult to obtain in deployed systems. RML24 addresses this by correlating over-the-air captures from an X410 high-gain antenna with the transmitted pulse-shaped signal (Fig. 3). Threshold detection of the correlation peak locates the start of the signal, enabling precise sample extraction. The resulting sample naturally incorporates hardware, channel, and AWGN effects.

While qualitative visualizations of these samples are illustrative, we conducted two quantitative validation experiments using representative QPSK signals to rigorously verify our dataset generation pipeline. First, a Demodulation Baseline was established to quantitatively assess signal recovery. Further validation was performed with a GNU Radio demodulator replicating the RML24 signal model (Fig. 7). The impaired signals—containing fD,ϕerr,ferrLo and ζerr—underwent filtering, carrier synchronization and equalization, matched filtering, and timing recovery to restore the sequence. By evaluating the Bit Error Rate (BER) of these restored sequences across varying SNR levels (Fig. 8), we verified that the underlying information remains structurally intact and fully decodable after passing through both the physical USRP transmission and the synthetic impairment injection. Second, a Frequency Offset Distribution Estimation was performed under high SNR conditions to validate the precision of our physical parameter modeling (Fig. 9). The measurement results confirm that the extracted frequency offsets strictly follow the intended statistical distributions and are tightly bound within the configured limits.

Deep Learning Benchmark Analysis

Time Length Impact on Classification Performance

In data-centric deep learning (DL) methods, the parameters of the samples influence the model’s robustness and generalization. We explored the effect of sample time length, denoted as l, within our training set by inputting the data into ResNet-18 for training. The details of the model parameters and training strategies are provided in Table 3. Figure 10 illustrates how the model’s performance varies with different sample time lengths. From the figure, it can be seen that the time length of the samples has a significant impact on the model’s classification performance. Insufficient sample time complicates DL-based demodulation and modulation classification tasks. Specifically, under high signal-to-noise ratio conditions, doubling the sample time length improves classification accuracy by approximately 7%. However, when the sample length is l = 2048, the model’s performance does not show a significant improvement, even though the amount of information in the sample has doubled. Considering factors such as memory limitations, training time, dataset size, data collection time window, and overall classification performance, we ultimately determined l = 2048 to be the optimal sample time length for RML24.

Bandwidth Change Impact on Classification

In communication sensing systems, received signal bandwidth varies and can affect classification. RML24 incorporates realistic bandwidth variation using a random bit-rate generator that samples from actual satellite signal bandwidths (Table 2). To assess the effect of bandwidth variations, we generated a controlled baseline dataset, denoted as RML24-100 kHz. The suffix ’100 kHz’ explicitly indicates that the symbol rate for all signals in this variant is strictly fixed at 100 kHz, distinguishing it from the standard RML24 dataset, which contains diverse symbol rates. Accuracy-SNR curves for matched and cross-testing scenarios are shown in Fig. 11. Under high SNR, models trained on RML24 and RML24-100 kHz performed similarly. When a model trained on RML24 was tested on RML24-100 kHz, accuracy remained above 80%. However, at 14 dB SNR (Fig. 12), higher-order modulations (8PSK, 16QAM, 32QAM, 64QAM, 16APSK, 32APSK) were misclassified, reducing accuracy. This reflects the difficulty of recognising higher-order schemes without signal-processing compensation. In contrast, a model trained on RML24-100kHz achieved 40% accuracy when tested on RML24, indicating that training with bandwidth variation in RML24 improves robustness and generalisation.

Classification performance of RML24 and RML24-100 kHz datasets.

The confusion matrix for model classification and recognition where train RML24 and test RML24-100kHz at a high SNR of 14 dB.

Rather than a standard baseline comparison, the cross-dataset evaluation between RML24 and RML24-100 kHz is specifically designed to assess Out-of-Distribution generalization. Under the manifold assumption, radio signals lie on low-dimensional manifolds dictated by key physical parameters like signal bandwidth. If a model is trained on a single fixed bandwidth (RML24-100 kHz), it exhibits poor generalization to realistic variations, with accuracy degrading to 40%. However, training on the diverse RML24 dataset forces the network to extract bandwidth-agnostic features, preserving an accuracy of over 80% on unseen bandwidths. This demonstrates that dataset diversity across fundamental physical parameters is critical for learning true data distributions and achieving real-world robustness.

Baseline for AMR Applications on RML24

AMR aims to identify the modulation type of received satellite signals, enabling downstream signal processing tasks. DL methods have demonstrated superior feature extraction and classification capabilities compared to traditional approaches, resulting in lower false alarm rates and improved robustness under incomplete data and complex channel conditions.

To establish benchmarks for the RML24 dataset, six commonly used AMR network architectures—CNN-4, AlexNet, VGG-16, ResNet-18, CGDNN, and CLDNN—were implemented using identical training and evaluation settings (Table 3). All models were trained with Adam optimizer, a batch size of 128, and a learning rate scheduler (initial rate = 1, decay = 0.1 every 10 epochs). Input signals had dimensions of (2, 2048), with ReLU and Softmax activation functions. The dataset was split using a 20.

Benchmark results are summarized in Table 4 and Fig. 13. Under consistent conditions, ResNet-18 achieved the highest average recognition accuracy (68%), and a peak accuracy of 97% at 18 dB SNR. At high SNR, most models exceeded 80% accuracy, with ResNet-18 and CLDNN surpassing 95%. This highlights the effectiveness of residual connections and recurrent layers for capturing spatial and temporal features in AMR tasks. Figure 14 shows ResNet-18 accuracy significantly improves with SNR, excedding 90% for most modulations above 10 dB. Additionally, the average confusion matrices (Fig. 15) show that benchmark models generally struggle with higher-order modulations (overall accuracy <60%). Cross-referencing with Fig. 14 reveals this confusion predominantly occurs at low SNRs (<5 dB), highlighting the susceptibility of blind recognition to satellite channel impairments.

Example Applications

Beyond AMR, the ’cognitive radio’ framing of this dataset is justified by its capacity to serve as a comprehensive testbed for various physical layer tasks critical to modern space-air-ground networks. Researchers can exploit the unique combination of standard-compliant software synthesis and physical hardware-in-the-loop impairments to advance the following cognitive tasks:

Signal Detection and Spectrum Sensing: The extensive SNR range (down to −20 dB) allows researchers to evaluate energy detection or deep learning-based sensing algorithms for identifying weak satellite beacons or spectral white spaces under severe noise conditions.
