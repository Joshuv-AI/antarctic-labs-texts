Differential Cancer Risks and Noninvasive Predictors in MASLD, MetALD, and ALD: A Longitudinal Analysis
Authors: Hana Park, Ji Young Lee, Ja Eun Koo, Jong Soo Lee, Sungwon Park, Hye Won Park, Hye-Sook Chang, Hong-Kyu Kim, Jaewon Choe, Ju Hyun Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsThe recently proposed classification of steatotic liver disease (SLD) comprises metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), MASLD with increased alcohol intake (MetALD), and alcohol-related liver disease (ALD). Long-term cancer risk across these phenotypes remains incompletely characterized, and was investigated in this study.MethodsThis longitudinal cohort study included 43,994 adults who underwent health check-ups in 2010–2011. Liver steatosis was diagnosed by ultrasonography. The Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) and steatosis-associated fibrosis estimator (SAFE) scores were evaluated as predictors of cancer incidence.ResultsOver a median 10.5 years (398,202 person-years), 3,169 cancers occurred (795.8 per 100,000 person-years). Versus no SLD, overall cancer risk was elevated in ALD (incidence rate ratio [IRR], 1.38; 95% confidence interval, 1.08 to 1.79) but not MASLD or MetALD. Hepatocellular carcinoma risk increased across all SLD groups, most strongly in the MetALD and ALD groups, with a more modest association in the MASLD group that was attenuated in sensitivity analyses. ALD was associated with an elevated risk of esophageal cancer (IRR, 5.98) and stomach cancer (IRR, 2.41), and MetALD with an increased risk of stomach cancer (IRR, 1.61). FIB-4 and SAFE showed dose–response associations with cancer that were substantially attenuated after age adjustment, persisting mainly at the highest categories.ConclusionsCancer risk differs across MASLD, MetALD, and ALD, with alcohol-related phenotypes carrying the highest burden. Noninvasive fibrosis indices such as FIB-4 and SAFE may help stratify risk—though largely age-related—and could inform risk-based surveillance in SLD.

INTRODUCTION

The recently introduced steatotic liver disease (SLD) framework, encompassing metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), MASLD with increased alcohol intake (MetALD), and alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), has refined disease classification and carries distinct clinical and prognostic implications.1-3 Within this framework, the relationship between MASLD and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk has been increasingly characterized,4 underscoring the need to delineate cancer risk across the individual SLD subgroups.

Liver cancer has long been associated with alcohol consumption, which has been classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.5,6 Furthermore, metabolic dysfunction, including obesity, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, has been implicated in the development of several extrahepatic malignancies, such as colorectal, pancreatic, and breast cancers.7 Despite this evidence, there remains a paucity of longitudinal studies investigating cancer incidence across the newly defined SLD subgroups. In particular, the carcinogenic risk in MetALD and ALD populations, which were previously excluded from nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)-based research, is not well characterized.

Noninvasive fibrosis assessment tools, including the Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) and the steatosis-associated fibrosis estimator (SAFE) score, are utilized extensively in the stratification of patients at risk of advanced liver disease. The FIB-4 has been validated as a simple and reliable predictor of advanced fibrosis in chronic liver diseases, including NAFLD.8,9 The SAFE score, a more recent development, has also demonstrated robust performance in identifying advanced fibrosis in MASLD across diverse cohorts.10-13 While the primary focus of these indices is fibrosis prediction, it is noteworthy that they also have the capacity to detect systemic metabolic burden and liver injury. The potential for these factors to serve as predictors of cancer risk in SLD populations is not yet fully elucidated; however, the establishment of such associations could lead to an expansion in their clinical utility and the development of risk-based surveillance strategies.

In this large-scale longitudinal cohort study of asymptomatic adults undergoing health screening, the primary objectives were twofold: first, to compare cancer incidence among the MASLD, MetALD, and ALD groups, and secondly, to evaluate the predictive value of FIB-4 and SAFE scores for cancer occurrence. The study aimed to provide novel evidence supporting tailored cancer surveillance strategies for patients with SLD, with a particular focus on those with alcohol-related components.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study population

The present study analyzed data from 66,332 individuals who underwent routine health check-ups at the Health Screening and Promotion Center, Asan Medical Center, Seoul, Republic of Korea, between January 2010 and December 2011. Eligible participants were adults aged 20 to 75 years. Participants were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) history of liver cirrhosis or chronic liver disease, or ultrasonographic findings suggestive of these conditions (reported as “liver cirrhosis,” “chronic liver disease,” “early liver cirrhosis,” or “coarse hepatic echotexture”); (2) positive hepatitis B surface antigen or anti-hepatitis C virus antibody; (3) history of previous organ transplantation; (4) history of cancer diagnosis or presence of cancer at baseline; (5) follow-up duration of less than 1 year; or (6) missing key data including laboratory results, anthropometric parameters and information regarding alcohol intake or smoking status. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Asan Medical Center in Seoul (IRB number: 2024-0227). Informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective study design.

2. Definition of SLD subclassification

Individuals with hepatic steatosis present on ultrasonography were considered to have SLD. Hepatic steatosis was generally diagnosed on ultrasonography based on the presence of bright hepatic parenchyma, contrast between the liver and kidney, deep beam attenuation, and bright vessel walls.14 According to the SLD classification criteria in the multisociety Delphi consensus statement, the definitions for each group are as follows.1 In the context of SLD, individuals with low-level alcohol consumption (drinking ≤2–3 times per week), accompanied by one or more of the following cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs), were designated as MASLD: (1) obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥23 kg/m2 in Asians) or waist circumference >90 cm for men and >80 cm for women (following Asian criteria); (2) fasting serum glucose ≥5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) or 2-hour post-load glucose level ≥7.8 mmol/L (140 mg/dL) or glycated hemoglobin ≥5.7% (39 mmol/mol) or type 2 diabetes or treatment for type 2 diabetes; (3) blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or specific antihypertensive drug treatment; (4) plasma triglycerides ≥1.70 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) or lipid-lowering treatment; and (5) plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≤1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) for men and ≤1.3 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) for women or lipid-lowering treatment. Alcohol intake was assessed using a standardized self-administered questionnaire that recorded drinking frequency (none; ≤1, 2–3, 4–6 times per week; or daily). Because quantitative (g/day) intake was not available, these frequency categories were used to operationalize the consensus alcohol thresholds for SLD subclassification (reference: <30/<20, 30–60/20–50, and ≥60/≥50 g/day for men/women), applied identically to both sexes. Among individuals with SLD and one or more CMRFs, those with moderate alcohol consumption (drinking 4–6 times per week) were defined as having MetALD. In the context of SLD cases, individuals with daily alcohol consumption, irrespective of CMRF presence, were designated as having ALD. Individuals with SLD who had neither CMRF nor significant alcohol consumption were classified as cryptogenic SLD. Because this group fell outside the three index phenotypes (MASLD, MetALD, and ALD) that were the focus of this study and comprised a small number of participants, it was analyzed separately rather than included in the primary comparison.

3. Assessment of SLD severity

The severity of SLD was determined by using the FIB-4 and the SAFE score. The FIB-4 was calculated using the following formula: age×aspartate aminotransferase (IU/L)/(platelet count [103/µL]×alanine aminotransferase [IU/L]1/2) and analyzed with cutoff points of 1.3 and 2.67 to define three groups.15 The SAFE score was calculated using the following formula: SAFE=(2.97×age)+(5.99×BMI [BMI >40 set to 40])+(62.85×diabetes [0 if absent, 1 if present])+(154.85×Ln [aspartate aminotransferase, IU/L])−(58.23×Ln [alanine aminotransferase, IU/L])+(195.48×Ln [globulin, g/dL])−(141.61×Ln [platelets, 103/µL])−75. The individuals with SAFE scores <0, 0–100, and >100 were categorized into three groups, respectively.10

4. Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of cancer during the follow-up period. The identification of cancer diagnoses was accomplished through a combination of linkage with the Korean National Cancer Registry and institutional medical records, using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes. Participants were followed from the baseline period until the date of cancer diagnosis, death, or the conclusion of the study, depending on the outcome. To mitigate the likelihood of latent cancerous lesions being present at the initial evaluation, subjects who had been diagnosed with cancer within 1 year of their health checkup were excluded from the study. Additionally, to minimize outcome misclassification, participants who did not visit the hospital for more than 2 years after their last follow-up appointment and only revisited after cancer diagnosis were also excluded, but no participant met this criterion (n=0); therefore, no individuals were excluded on this basis. Following the application of these criteria, a total of 43,994 individuals were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). The median follow-up period for the total participants was 10.5 years, amounting to 398,202 person-years.

5. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarize baseline characteristics across SLD groups; continuous variables are presented as the mean±standard deviation and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Groups were compared using t-tests or analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables, or chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Incidence rates were calculated per 100,000 person-years, and Poisson regression was used to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) adjusted for age (continuous), sex, and smoking status (never, former or current). As complementary analyses, baseline imbalance was addressed using stabilized inverse-probability-of-treatment weights (IPTW) from a multinomial propensity model (age, sex, smoking, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, and physical activity), and Poisson models were additionally adjusted for the same covariates. For the fibrosis scores, Cox models were further adjusted for age and examined within age strata, and the incremental predictive value of each score beyond age was assessed by likelihood-ratio test. Statistical significance was set at two-sided p<0.05. All analyses were performed using R 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python 3.10 (with the pandas, SciPy, statsmodels, and lifelines packages) via the MediStat Analyst platform.

RESULTS

1. Baseline clinical characteristics of the study population

The final analysis included a total of 43,994 participants, with a mean age of 49.5±9.8 years (Table 1). Among the subjects, 15,015 (34.1%) exhibited hepatic steatosis. Subgroup classification revealed that 12,334 (28.0%) were classified as MASLD, 1,775 (4.0%) as MetALD, and 607 (1.4%) as ALD. An additional 299 (0.7%) were categorized as cryptogenic SLD and were analyzed separately rather than included in the primary comparison (their cancer risk did not differ significantly from the no SLD group) (Supplementary Table 1).

The MASLD, MetALD, and ALD groups exhibited distinctly different patterns in their overall clinical and biochemical profiles compared to the no SLD group. The male predominance was particularly pronounced in the MetALD (97.1%), ALD (96.2%) and MASLD (71.7%) groups, compared with the no SLD (48.0%) group (p<0.001). The MetALD group generally exhibited intermediate features between MASLD and ALD groups. For instance, BMI, waist circumference, fasting glucose, and triglyceride levels showed a gradient of MASLD<MetALD<ALD. Furthermore, lifestyle characteristics exhibited variability across distinct groups. The prevalence of current smoking was 23.9% in MASLD, 43.7% in MetALD, and 46.6% in ALD (p<0.001). Noninvasive fibrosis markers exhibited a stepwise increase across SLD categories. The prevalence of FIB-4 >2.67 exhibited an increase from 1.3% in MASLD to 2.4% in MetALD and 7.1% in ALD (p<0.001). Similarly, SAFE score >100 was observed in 8.2% of MASLD, 9.7% of MetALD, and 19.6% of ALD participants (p<0.001).

2. Overall and sex-specific cancer incidence rates by SLD subgroup

During a median follow-up period of 10.5 years, a total of 3,169 participants developed cancer, corresponding to an overall incidence rate of 795.8 cases per 100,000 person-years (Table 2). A comparison of cancer incidence rates revealed a higher incidence among individuals with MASLD (843.2), MetALD (861.9) and ALD (1,197.4) compared with individuals without SLD (762.4).

The analysis stratified by sex revealed that the highest cancer incidence rate among men was observed in the ALD group (1,219.1 per 100,000 person-years), followed by MetALD (872.3), no SLD (820.7), and MASLD (807.3). In women, a higher incidence of the condition was observed in the MASLD group (933.4) compared with individuals in the no SLD group (708.8). The cancer incidence rates among women with MetALD and ALD were documented as 477.3 and 560.2, respectively, indicating comparatively lower rates in comparison to other groups.

3. Site-specific cancer IRRs by SLD subgroup

In the subsequent stage of analysis, a comparison was conducted between cancer incidences rates by site. The number of cancer cases and the incidence rate (cases per 100,000 person-years) by site for each SLD group are presented in Supplementary Table 2. As demonstrated in Table 3, Poisson regression adjusted for age, sex, and smoking status revealed differences in cancer incidence across SLD subgroups compared with the no SLD group. The risk of developing HCC was found to be significantly elevated in all SLD subgroups, MASLD (IRR, 2.12; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.05 to 4.26; p=0.035), MetALD (IRR, 7.86; 95% CI, 3.31 to 18.66; p<0.001) and ALD (IRR, 12.02; 95% CI, 4.73 to 30.51; p<0.001), compared with the no SLD group.

Although there was no statistically significant increase in risk in any subgroup when extrahepatic (non-HCC) cancers were analyzed as a combined endpoint compared to the no SLD group (MASLD IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.91 to 1.07; MetALD IRR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.88 to 1.27; ALD IRR, 1.25; 95% CI, 0.96 to 1.63; p=0.094), the risk of each site-specific cancer differed among the SLD subgroups. In the MASLD group, the risk of developing cancers of the kidney or bladder was significantly elevated (IRR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.08 to 2.07; p=0.015). A tendency towards elevated hematologic malignancies was identified, exhibiting marginal statistical significance (IRR, 1.50; 95% CI, 0.94 to 2.40; p=0.089). In the MetALD group, the risk of developing stomach cancer increased significantly (IRR, 1.61; 95% CI, 1.07 to 2.40; p=0.021). The ALD group demonstrated a significantly elevated risk of developing overall cancer in comparison to the no SLD group (IRR, 1.38; 95% CI, 1.08 to 1.79; p=0.011). Furthermore, in this group, the markedly increased risk was observed not only in HCC but also in esophageal cancer (IRR, 5.98; 95% CI, 1.94 to 18.44; p=0.002), and stomach cancer (IRR, 2.41; 95% CI, 1.46 to 3.98; p=0.001).

4. Sex-stratified cancer IRRs by SLD subgroup

The analysis of cancer IRRs by sex is presented in Tables 4 and 5. The number of cancer cases and the incidence rate (cases per 100,000 person-years) for men and women are presented in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, respectively.

In the male cohort, compared with the no SLD group, the risk of HCC was found to be significantly increased in subjects with MetALD (IRR, 7.59; 95% CI, 3.18 to 18.12; p<0.001) and ALD (IRR, 11.58; 95% CI, 4.54 to 29.50; p<0.001), although in MASLD this increase was only marginal (IRR, 1.96; 95% CI, 0.95 to 4.08; p=0.071). The MASLD group did not demonstrate an elevated overall cancer risk in comparison with the no SLD group (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.89 to 1.08; p=0.704). However, the risk of developing cancers of the kidney or bladder was found to be significantly elevated (IRR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.07 to 2.19; p=0.019). In contrast to the prevailing assumptions, the incidence of skin cancer was found to be considerably lower in the MASLD group compared to the no SLD group (IRR, 0.35; 95% CI, 0.14 to 0.85; p=0.020). In the MetALD group, the risk of developing overall cancer increased with marginal significance (IRR, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.98 to 1.41; p=0.080), and a marginal increase in the risk of esophageal cancer was also observed (IRR, 2.77; 95% CI, 0.89 to 8.68; p=0.080). A substantial increase in the risk of stomach cancer was observed in men with MetALD (IRR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.08 to 2.43; p=0.021). The ALD group demonstrated the most extensive carcinogenic profile, exhibiting heightened risks for overall cancers (IRR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.07 to 1.77; p=0.014), esophageal cancer (IRR, 6.47; 95% CI, 2.07 to 20.20; p=0.001), and stomach cancer (IRR, 2.44; 95% CI, 1.47 to 4.05; p=0.001) as well as the risk of HCC.

In women, MASLD was associated with a significant increase in overall cancers (IRR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.04 to 1.36; p=0.012) and gynecological cancers (IRR, 1.49; 95% CI, 1.02 to 2.19; p=0.040) but did not reach statistical significance for other individual cancer types. Conversely, the MetALD and ALD groups in women showed no significant association or were unanalyzable for most cancer sites, including overall cancer, likely due to the limited number of cases available for analysis.

5. Sensitivity analyses

To address baseline differences across SLD subgroups, we performed a complementary IPTW analysis and an expanded multivariable adjustment (additionally including BMI, diabetes, hypertension, and physical activity). After IPTW, overall-cancer IRRs versus no SLD were attenuated and no longer significant in any subgroup (MASLD: IRR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.92 to 1.08; MetALD: IRR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.82 to 1.26; ALD: IRR, 1.18; 95% CI, 0.85 to 1.63). With expanded multivariable adjustment, the elevated overall-cancer risk in ALD persisted (IRR, 1.34; 95% CI, 1.04 to 1.72), consistent with the primary model. The defining site-specific signals were robust to both approaches: the HCC gradient persisted in MetALD (IPTW IRR, 7.77; 95% CI, 3.18 to 18.98; p<0.001) and ALD (IPTW IRR, 15.05; 95% CI, 5.34 to 42.40; p<0.001), as did the upper-gastrointestinal cancer excess in ALD (IPTW IRR, 3.25; 95% CI, 1.86 to 5.67; p<0.001); only the modest MASLD–HCC association was attenuated (Supplementary Table 5).

In a further sensitivity analysis addressing potential alcohol misclassification, borderline drinkers (2–3 times per week) near the MASLD–MetALD boundary were excluded and, alternatively, reclassified as MetALD. The principal findings were unchanged: ALD remained the only subgroup with a significantly elevated overall-cancer risk, and the HCC gradient across MetALD and ALD persisted; the modest MASLD–HCC association lost statistical significance (Supplementary Table 6).

To distinguish the contribution of alcohol from that of hepatic steatosis, we compared daily drinkers with and without SLD, using non-daily-drinking individuals without SLD as the reference. Relative to this reference, both daily drinkers without SLD (n=852; IRR, 1.36; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.69; p=0.006) and the ALD group (IRR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.11 to 1.83; p=0.006) showed a similarly elevated overall-cancer risk, and the two did not differ from each other (ALD vs daily drinkers without SLD: IRR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.76 to 1.45; p=0.768). By contrast, relative to equally heavy drinkers without SLD, ALD carried a higher risk of upper-gastrointestinal cancer (IRR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.02 to 3.84; p=0.043) and HCC (IRR, 5.17; 95% CI, 1.07 to 24.93; p=0.041) (Supplementary Table 7).

6. Association between noninvasive fibrosis scores and cancer risk

To assess the relationship between the noninvasive markers FIB-4 and SAFE score and cancer risk, analyses were conducted using a Cox proportional hazards model (Table 6). An escalating trend of cancer risk was observed across score categories within all SLD subgroups for FIB-4. In comparison with FIB-4 <1.3, the intermediate group (1.3–2.67) and the advanced group (>2.67) exhibited elevated hazards, with all differences proving to be statistically significant. In the MASLD group, the hazard ratios (HRs) were 1.62 (95% CI, 1.42 to 1.85; p<0.001) and 2.36 (95% CI, 1.60 to 3.48; p<0.001). In the MetALD group, the corresponding HRs were 1.52 (95% CI, 1.06 to 2.17; p=0.022) and 4.31 (95% CI, 2.27 to 8.18; p<0.001). In the ALD group, the risk of cancer was found to be significantly elevated, with HRs of 1.78 (95% CI, 1.01 to 3.15; p=0.047) and 5.28 (95% CI, 2.41 to 11.57; p<0.001).

An analysis of SAFE scores also revealed a parallel dose–response pattern in cancer risk across score categories. In comparison with SAFE <0, the SAFE score showed a parallel dose–response pattern, with significantly elevated risk particularly in the higher score categories (most consistently the >100 group). For MASLD, HRs were 1.50 (95% CI, 1.31 to 1.73; p<0.001) and 2.17 (95% CI, 1.78 to 2.63; p<0.001), respectively. In the MetALD group, the cancer risk significantly increased only in the SAFE >100 group with HRs of 2.18 (95% CI, 1.33 to 3.57; p=0.002). In the ALD group, cancer risk was elevated in the SAFE >100 group with marginal significance (HR, 1.97; 95% CI, 0.97 to 4.00; p=0.060).

However, after additional adjustment for age, the dose–response associations of FIB-4 and SAFE were substantially attenuated, with independent associations retained mainly at the highest score categories (FIB-4 >2.67: HR 1.24 [95% CI, 1.01 to 1.53] in the total cohort; SAFE >100: HR 1.25 [95% CI, 1.09 to 1.44]); most subgroup-specific and intermediate score category associations became non-significant (Supplementary Table 8). In age-stratified analyses, both scores continued to stratify risk among participants aged 50–64 years (Supplementary Table 9). A likelihood-ratio test compared a Cox model containing age, sex, and smoking with the same model after adding the fibrosis score. The SAFE score provided significant additional predictive information once age was already accounted for (χ²=10.0, p=0.007), whereas the additional contribution of FIB-4 did not reach significance (χ²=4.8, p=0.091) (Supplementary Table 10).

DISCUSSION

In this large-scale longitudinal cohort study, the incidence of cancer in newly defined SLD was higher than in the group without SLD. This excess was largely attributable to HCC; although extrahepatic cancer risk, considered as a combined endpoint, was not significantly elevated, the specific cancer types associated with significantly increased risk differed across the SLD subgroups. The MASLD group exhibited an augmented risk of HCC and urological cancer. The MetALD group exhibited an elevated risk of developing stomach cancer as well as HCC. Individuals with ALD exhibited the highest overall cancer burden, particularly regarding HCC and upper gastrointestinal cancers, such as esophageal and stomach cancer. When the analysis was stratified by sex, these results were replicated in men. For women, the limited sample size in the MetALD and ALD groups reduced the precision of incidence estimates and constrained intergroup comparison; however, an increased risk of overall cancer and gynecological cancer was confirmed in the female MASLD group.

The present findings extend the scope of previous research on the association between fatty liver disease and cancer risk. A multitude of studies have previously established a correlation between NAFLD and HCC, in addition to an augmented risk of developing colorectal, pancreatic, and breast cancers.16-19 The more recent metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease definition has demonstrated enhanced predictive value, particularly for colorectal cancer in individuals with advanced fibrosis.20 Meta-analytic evidence further substantiates elevated extrahepatic cancer risk in both NAFLD and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease.21,22 However, most studies excluded individuals with substantial alcohol intake.

Alcohol has been identified as a carcinogen, which has been demonstrated to promote DNA damage, oxidative stress, and acetaldehyde accumulation.6,23 Furthermore, metabolic dysfunction has been demonstrated to drive chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and lipotoxicity.24,25 A plethora of experimental evidence has indicated that alcohol and metabolic stress act synergistically to exacerbate hepatic and systemic injury. Combined exposure to alcohol and obesity has been shown to induce greater oxidative stress, steatosis, and fibrosis than either factor alone in preclinical models.26 In a similar manner, dual models of alcoholic and metabolic fatty liver disease demonstrate exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and proinflammatory cytokine activation.27,28 Collectively, these findings suggest that the co-occurrence of alcohol and metabolic dysfunction may be associated with a higher cancer burden across multiple organ systems.29-31 However, because this was an observational study, these findings indicate association rather than causation; reverse causation and shared metabolic or lifestyle risk factors cannot be excluded.32

The present study employed the novel SLD classification to demonstrate that alcohol-related groups (MetALD and ALD) exhibit an elevated cancer burden. Notably, the increased risk of upper gastrointestinal cancers in MetALD and ALD contrasted with the absence of such an increase in MASLD. Because alcohol itself is an established carcinogen, we sought to disentangle the effects of alcohol and hepatic steatosis by comparing daily drinkers with and without SLD. The excess overall-cancer risk in ALD appeared to be attributable largely to alcohol exposure itself, as daily drinkers without steatosis carried a comparable overall-cancer burden. For hepatocellular and upper gastrointestinal cancers, however, ALD conferred additional risk beyond that of equally heavy drinkers without SLD, indicating an independent contribution of SLD at these sites. This HCC comparison should be interpreted cautiously, as only two such cancers occurred among daily drinkers without SLD, yielding an imprecise estimate.

These site-specific findings are consistent with prior evidence. Previous studies have reported positive associations between alcohol consumption and upper gastrointestinal cancers33,34 and a further study demonstrated a synergistic carcinogenic effect between alcohol and liver injury, with a markedly elevated incidence of upper gastrointestinal malignancies in patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis.35 Building on these reports, our study applies the SLD framework to stratify this risk across MASLD, MetALD, and ALD. Collectively, these results highlight the elevated risk of upper gastrointestinal cancer in the alcohol-related SLD groups and support extending surveillance in these patients beyond HCC to include upper gastrointestinal cancers.

In contrast to the alcohol-related SLD groups, the MASLD group exhibited an increased incidence of urological cancers in men and gynecological cancers in women. Previous studies of NAFLD have suggested potential associations with urological and gynecological cancers. Specifically, an association has been observed between NAFLD and bladder cancer,36 and a large meta-analysis reported that NAFLD was associated with a 1.2- to 1.5-fold increased risk of extrahepatic malignancies, including urological and gynecological cancers.21 Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating similar associations in the alcohol-excluded SLD group. In the MASLD group, where the confounding influence of alcohol was absent, the elevated risk of these cancers may stem from systemic inflammation associated with metabolic disorders.24 An intriguing finding was the observation of a lower incidence of skin cancer among men with MASLD. The underlying causes of this phenomenon remain to be elucidated and necessitate further investigation to ascertain its biological or epidemiological implications.

The clinical implications of our study are particularly relevant for risk stratification and surveillance strategies in clinical practice. First, the results of this study suggest that cancer surveillance in patients with SLD should not be uniform; rather, it should be adapted according to subgroup and risk profile. The present study found that both MetALD and ALD patients, particularly men, constitute a cohort with a considerably elevated risk of developing not only HCC but also upper gastrointestinal cancers. This finding necessitates closer monitoring and potentially earlier initiation of endoscopic or imaging-based screening for individuals in these groups. In MASLD, men showed heightened susceptibility to urological cancers and women to gynecological cancers. These findings are significant because they provide the first evidence suggesting differences in cancer vulnerability may exist between the sexes even within the same SLD subgroup. These findings suggest that more meticulous urological or gynecological surveillance should be considered for this population. Second, FIB-4 and SAFE were associated with cancer risk and may aid risk stratification in patients with SLD. The SAFE score integrates age, BMI, diabetes, aminotransferases, globulin, and platelets to estimate the likelihood of advanced fibrosis; a score <0 identifies individuals at low risk (a high-sensitivity rule-out threshold), whereas >100 denotes high risk, with the intermediate range (0–100) reflecting indeterminate risk.10-13,37-39 Because age is a heavily weighted component of both scores, a substantial part of this crude association reflected age; after age adjustment, independent associations persisted mainly at the highest score categories and among middle-aged participants, and SAFE—which incorporates additional metabolic and hepatic parameters—retained significant predictive value beyond age whereas FIB-4 did not, although the absolute gain in discrimination was modest for both. These findings support the use of FIB-4 and SAFE as composite, age-inclusive tools for practical risk stratification rather than as age-independent biomarkers of cancer risk.37-39 Because both scores rely on routine laboratory and clinical data, their integration into health check-up programs and primary care is feasible, and patients with elevated FIB-4 (>2.67) or SAFE (>100) scores could be prioritized for enhanced surveillance, lifestyle modification, or referral to specialized care.

The present study is accompanied by several unavoidable limitations. First, alcohol intake was self-reported and recorded as drinking frequency rather than quantity, which approximates the consensus gram-based thresholds; underreporting or this frequency-based operationalization could misclassify individuals near phenotype boundaries. In sensitivity analyses that excluded or reclassified borderline drinkers, the principal findings were robust, whereas the modest MASLD–HCC association lost significance—consistent with a small contribution of under-reported alcohol among borderline MASLD drinkers. Second, steatosis was assessed by ultrasonography alone, and age-independent measures of liver fibrosis—such as vibration-controlled transient elastography (liver stiffness measurement) or other imaging modalities—were not obtained in this health check-up cohort. Consequently, the noninvasive indices we could evaluate (FIB-4 and SAFE) both incorporate age as a heavily weighted component, and we were unable to determine whether an age-independent fibrosis measure would predict cancer risk beyond age; this question warrants dedicated investigation in cohorts with elastography- or imaging-based fibrosis assessment. Third, the marked male predominance in MetALD (97.1%) and ALD (96.2%) rendered the female analyses underpowered; in particular, only two and one cancer cases occurred among women with MetALD and ALD, respectively. Female-specific estimates for the alcohol-related phenotypes should therefore be interpreted with great caution and not over-interpreted. Fourth, cancer diagnoses were confirmed through linkage with the National Cancer Registry. The National Cancer Registry of South Korea is legally mandated and has been shown to capture over 95% of new cancer diagnoses, supporting a high level of registration completeness.40,41 The leading cancer sites in our cohort (thyroid, stomach, breast, lung, prostate, and colorectum) are concordant with the most frequently diagnosed cancers reported by the Korea Central Cancer Registry,42 supporting the representativeness of the observed cancer spectrum. Nonetheless, the single-center, health check-up nature of the cohort—comprising a relatively healthy, male-predominant population—may limit generalizability. Finally, a systematic history of steatogenic medications was not available; however, drug-induced steatosis is not classified within the MASLD/MetALD/ALD definitions, and any residual misclassification would be expected to be non-differential.

Notwithstanding, the findings support a transition from organ-specific surveillance to more comprehensive preventive strategies in patients with SLD. While surveillance for liver cancer is already established in chronic liver disease, our findings suggest that targeted screening for extrahepatic cancers may also be beneficial for selected SLD patients, particularly those with high fibrosis scores. Integrating fibrosis-based risk stratification into national cancer screening programs has the potential to enhance early detection and improve treatment outcomes.

In conclusion, MASLD, MetALD, and ALD show differential cancer risks, with particularly high burdens observed in alcohol-related groups. FIB-4 and SAFE were associated with cancer risk, although this association was largely age-related; the scores may nonetheless serve as practical, composite (age-inclusive) tools for risk-based stratification, with SAFE retaining independent predictive value beyond age. The findings of this study underscore the necessity for tailored cancer surveillance strategies that consider both metabolic dysfunction and alcohol consumption, with the incorporation of readily available noninvasive scores into routine risk assessment to improve early detection and guide personalized preventive care in patients with SLD.
