Sustained moderate to high triglyceride-glucose index level is associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia in middle-aged and elderly adults: a nationally representative cohort study
Authors: Zhaoping Li, Zhiyong Zhu, Liyong Chen, Hao Bai, Qian Zhou, Li Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a novel surrogate marker for insulin resistance (IR), has shown emerging links to sarcopenia. Yet the comprehensive association between the TyG Index and sarcopenia risk remains inconsistent, especially regarding the role of longitudinal TyG exposure patterns. This study comprehensively examines the relationships of baseline TyG index, cumulative TyG index, and TyG index subgroups (defined by two time-point measurements) with incident sarcopenia in Chinese middle-aged and older adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from two China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) subcohorts: 6033 participants (aged ≥45) for baseline TyG index and sarcopenia; 5256 individuals for cumulative TyG and TyG index subgroup analyses. Sarcopenia was defined as low muscle mass along with either low muscle strength or physical performance. Hierarchical cluster analysis using TyG values measured in 2011 and 2015 was performed to identify distinct longitudinal TyG distribution patterns. Baseline TyG index was analyzed using multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models, whereas cumulative TyG index and TyG index subgroups were assessed with multivariable logistic regression. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were conducted to test for linear and nonlinear shapes of each associationResultsThe mean age of participants was 57.16 (8.43) years, with 3207 females (51.8%) in the sample. During the 4-year observational period, 566 (9.38%) participants developed sarcopenia. Following adjustments for confounders, both the baseline index and the cumulative TyG index associated with sarcopenia exhibited significant nonlinear L-shapes, with inflection points observed at approximately 9.46 and 35.49, respectively. Three distinct subgroups: persistently low, moderate, and persistently high TyG groups were identified. Compared with the persistently low TyG index group, the moderate and persistently high TyG index groups showed gradually lower odds of sarcopenia, with multivariable-adjusted ORs of 0.526 (95% CI, 0.394–0.701) and 0.273 (95% CI, 0.184–0.406), respectively. Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the associations between TyG indices and sarcopenia were more significant in males, individuals with lower BMI, and rural residents, with specific population variations across different TyG measures.ConclusionThis longitudinal cohort study demonstrates that TyG indices exhibit L-shaped associations with sarcopenia status in Chinese adults aged ≥45 years, with identifiable thresholds. These findings suggest that the TyG index may serve as a biomarker indicative of a potential threshold for muscle health. Validation in multi-ethnic cohorts and mechanistic studies are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02993-4.Keywords: Triglyceride glucose index, Sarcopenia, CHARLS, Hierarchical clustering

Introduction

Sarcopenia, a geriatric syndrome marked by progressive decline in muscle mass and strength [1, 2], has emerged as a significant global health concern, affecting 10%-16% of the elderly population worldwide [3]. This condition predisposes individuals to an approximately 3-fold higher risk of falls, fractures, functional disability, and all-cause mortality [4], substantially increasing healthcare burdens through frequent hospitalizations, longer hospital stays, and higher direct medical costs [5, 6]. Early identification of modifiable risk factors is crucial for the prevention, treatment, and delaying progression of sarcopenia.

The pathogenesis of sarcopenia remains incompletely understood but is known to involve factors such as aging, inflammation, and insulin resistance (IR) [7]. IR impairs muscle health through interconnected pathways: it suppresses the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway [8] and triggers chronic inflammation [9] that activates protein breakdown. Furthermore, ectopic lipid deposition within muscle induces lipotoxicity and oxidative stress, impairing mitochondrial function, while dysregulated adipokine secretion further disrupts the balance of the ‘fat-muscle axis’ [10, 11]. Therefore, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, as a valid surrogate marker of insulin resistance, likely influences sarcopenia through these integrated biological pathways.

The TyG index, derived from triglycerides and fasting blood glucose [12], has been linked to various conditions, including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and other metabolic disorders [13, 14]. In particular, its potential association with sarcopenia has garnered increasing research interest. Current epidemiological findings remain contradictory. Data from the American National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey indicate a positive relationship between the TyG index and low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and the incidence of sarcopenia [15], with each 1-unit TyG increase corresponding to a 59% higher sarcopenia risk (OR = 1.59) [15]. Similarly, findings from the National Health and Nutritional Examination Survey in South Korea supported this positive association [16]. However, a large cohort study in China found no significant TyG-sarcopenia association after full covariate adjustment (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.68–1.82) [17]. Notably, existing studies are limited by cross-sectional designs and lack exploration of long-term dynamic changes in the TyG index over follow-up years. This study comprehensively evaluates the relationships between baseline TyG index, cumulative TyG index exposure, TyG clustering subtypes, and incident sarcopenia in a nationally representative Chinese cohort, with the goal of contributing to more effective prevention and treatment strategies for this condition.

Methods

Study population

The study population was drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationwide cohort project (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/) of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years since its 2011 inception. The study protocol received ethical clearance from Peking University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015) and complied with Helsinki Declaration standards.

Recruitment employed a multistage stratified sampling approach with probability proportional to population size, covering 150 county-level administrative units across 28 provincial divisions. Initial enrollment during the 2011 baseline phase (Wave 1) included 17,705 subjects, with subsequent follow-ups occurring in 2013 (Wave 2) and 2015 (Wave 3). Further details are available in prior publications [18]. For baseline TyG-sarcopenia associations, we applied exclusion criteria: 1 Pre-existing sarcopenia diagnosis; 2. Missing baseline (Wave 1) TyG data. This yielded 6,033 eligible participants. To assess cumulative TyG index effects and TyG clustering subtypes, we further excluded individuals lacking Wave 3 TyG index measurements (final n = 5,256). Exposures

Fasting plasma glucose and triglyceride concentrations from Waves 1–3 were assayed using a Hitachi 7180 automated analyzer (Tokyo, Japan), demonstrating high analytical precision (inter-assay CV < 5%). The TyG index was derived from triglycerides and glucose based on previous studies [19].

Changes in the TyG index were assessed using unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis, based on TyG values from Wave 1 and Wave 3. In the cluster analysis, the Euclidean distance metric and the Ward.D2 clustering method were applied. The number of clusters was incrementally increased, and model fit indices were compared to determine the optimal model, considering prioritizing biological interpretability alongside statistical fit.

Assessment of sarcopenia status

The diagnosis of sarcopenia was based on the criteria established by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia in the 2019 Consensus [22]. Participants were classified as having sarcopenia if they exhibited low muscle mass along with either low muscle strength or low physical performance [23].

Low muscle mass was defined as appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) < 7.0 kg/m2 for males or < 5.4 kg/m2 for females. ASMI was calculated as ASM in kilograms divided by height squared (m2). ASM was calculated by a validated anthropometric equation model [24], which was widely used in research for Chinese adults: ASM = 0.193 × weight (kg) + 0.107 × height (cm) − 4.157 × gender (1 for male, 2 for female) − 0.037 × age (year) − 2.631. This validated equation has shown excellent agreement with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA)-measured ASM and has been widely applied in epidemiological research [25, 26].

Muscle strength was assessed using grip strength, measured twice for both the dominant and non-dominant hands with a handgrip dynamometer. The maximum value was used for analysis. According to the AWGS criteria, low grip strength was defined as < 28 kg for males and < 18 kg for females.

Physical performance was evaluated using gait speed and the five-time chair stand test. Low physical performance was defined as taking ≥ 12 s to complete the five-time chair stand test or having a gait speed < 1.0 m/s during a 6-meter walk [27].

Covariates

Social demographics, lifestyle characteristics, and self-reported disease assessed at baseline (wave 1, 2011) were included as covariates. Sociodemographic measures comprised chronological age (years), biological sex (male/female), anthropometric body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), educational attainment (categorized as below primary school, primary school, middle school, or above), marital status (married, others), and residence (rural, urban). Lifestyle characteristics included smoking status (yes/no) and drinking habits (yes/no). Medical conditions included physician-diagnosed hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia (all yes/no), and cancer history (yes/no). An “unknown” category was assigned for variables with missing data in lifestyle characteristics and self-reported diseases.

Statistical analyses

Baseline TyG derived from 2011 survey data was adopted to explore its prospective association with sarcopenia identified in 2015. Cumulative TyG and hierarchical clustering subtypes were defined using 2011 and 2015 TyG data, while incident sarcopenia was ascertained at the 2015 follow-up survey. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as counts with weighted percentages. Missing values for smoking, drinking, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cancer) were classified into a dedicated missing category. To compare the baseline characteristics between different quartiles of the TyG index, the weighted chi-square test and the weighted variance analysis were used for categorical and continuous variables, respectively.

We used restricted cubic spline (RCS) models fitted for Cox proportional hazards models with three knots to flexibly model and visualize the dose-response association of the baseline TyG index with sarcopenia. For the cumulative TyG index, we similarly applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions within logistic regression models. The optimal number of knots (three to five) for the association between each continuous variable and sarcopenia was selected based on the Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Distinct TyG index trajectory groups were identified using hierarchical cluster analysis, and the associations between TyG trajectories and incident sarcopenia were subsequently quantified via multivariable logistic regression. In the primary analysis, age, sex, marital status, hukou status, educational level, smoking status, drinking habits, self-reported comorbidity history (hypertension, and cancer) were adjusted. In sensitivity analyses, we additionally adjusted for diabetes, dyslipidemia, and BMI, either individually or in combination, to evaluate whether these variables (potential mediators or colliders) altered the observed association. We assessed multicollinearity among covariates using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF) with adjustment for degrees of freedom (GVIF^(1/(2×df))). We calculated the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for all included continuous independent variables. The VIF values for all variables were well below 10 (typically less than 2), indicating no severe multicollinearity issues in the models. Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW) was further performed to minimize selection bias and verify the robustness of our findings. The E-value analysis was conducted to evaluate the strength of unmeasured confounding needed to explain away the associations [28]. All computations utilized R v4.4.1 (R Foundation), with α = 0.05 defining statistical significance (two-tailed).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The mean (SD) age of participants was 57.16 (8.43) years, with 3207 females (51.8%) in the sample. During a follow-up of 4.00 [3.92-4.00] years, 566 (9.38%) participants developed sarcopenia. The average TyG index was 8.73 (0.68). Table 1 displays baseline characteristics categorized by baseline TyG index quartiles of the included participants. Significant differences were observed across baseline TyG quartiles for age, sex, smoking status, drinking habits, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and sarcopenia occurrence (all P < 0.01). Baseline characteristics of participants included or excluded from analyses due to missing information are shown in Supplementary Table 2. Baseline comparisons showed significant differences in age, marital status, residence, lifestyle factors, and chronic conditions between included and excluded participants. BMI was well-balanced across the two groups.

Association of baseline TyG index with the sarcopenia incidence

Collinearity diagnostics revealed no evidence of multicollinearity (Supplementary Table 3). Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a significant inverse association (HR = 0.598, 95% CI: 0.518–0.689, P-nonlinear = 0.0851) (Fig. 2A). A two-piece Cox proportional hazards model identified a threshold effect at TyG = 9.46 (95% CI: 9.22–9.85; P = 0.011). Below this threshold, higher TyG levels were associated with a decreased risk of sarcopenia (HR = 0.533, 95% CI: 0.453–0.627, P < 0.001), whereas above the threshold, no significant association was observed (HR = 1.125, 95% CI: 0.722–1.754, P = 0.602)（Table 2). Subgroup interaction tests demonstrated that gender, marital status, smoking, and drinking significantly modified the nonlinear association between the baseline TyG index and sarcopenia (all P < 0.05). No significant interactions were observed for age grouping, education, residential area and hypertension (all P > 0.05), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. IPW sensitivity analysis was applied to mitigate selection bias. After inverse probability weighting (IPW) adjustment, the TyG index maintained a robust negative association with sarcopenia, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A.

To assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding on the study findings, we calculated the E-value. An unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both the TyG index and sarcopenia by a risk ratio of at least 3.16 to fully explain away the observed inverse association. Furthermore, the E-value for the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval was 2.57, indicating that an unmeasured confounder with a risk ratio of 2.57 would be sufficient to move the upper confidence limit to the null value of 1.0. These findings suggest that the results are reasonably robust to unmeasured confounding.

To explore the underlying mechanisms linking the TyG index to sarcopenia, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. In the model adjusting only for sociodemographic factors, hypertension, and cancer history, as well as after further adjustment for hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, the TyG index was significantly and negatively associated with sarcopenia risk (all P < 0.001). However, when BMI was included in the adjustment, the effect size of TyG was substantially attenuated (Supplemental Fig. 3). This pattern of change suggests that BMI may lie on the causal pathway from TyG to sarcopenia, acting as a mediator. Accordingly, we formally tested the mediating effect of BMI. As shown in Supplement Fig. 4A, mediation analysis using revealed that BMI mediated 92.7% of the association between the TyG index and sarcopenia.

Association of cumulative-TyG index with sarcopenia incidence

Restricted cubic spline regression identified a significant nonlinear L-shaped association pattern (P-nonlinear = 0.040), in which the inverse link tended to level off at higher cumulative TyG levels (Fig. 2B). A threshold was identified at TyG = 35.49 (95% CI: 34.91–36.19; P = 0.010), demarcating two distinct phases. Below this threshold, each unit increase in the cumulative TyG index was correlated with lower odds (OR = 0.732, 95% CI: 0.680–0.789, P < 0.001). Above the threshold, the inverse association tended to plateau, and no significant correlation was detected (OR = 0.919, 95% CI: 0.814–1.038, P = 0.174)（Table 2）.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential factors that may alter the above nonlinear association. No obvious differences in the magnitude of the association were observed across subgroups stratified by gender, age grouping, educational level, marital status, residential area, smoking status, alcohol drinking and hypertension (all P > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 5). The IPW sensitivity analysis indicated that the linear association between the cumulative TyG index and sarcopenia status remained stable (P < 0.001), while the originally significant nonlinear trend (P = 0.040) became nonsignificant after weighting (P = 0.240) (Supplementary Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that the association between the cumulative TyG index and sarcopenia is dominated by a linear effect, and the nonlinear association is attenuated after IPW adjustment. So the observed nonlinear pattern should be interpreted with caution as it may reflect residual sampling bias.

E-value analysis showed that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both the cumulative TyG index and sarcopenia by a risk ratio of at least 2.07 (point estimate) or 1.85 (upper confidence limit) to fully explain the observed inverse association (OR = 0.732, 95% CI: 0.680–0.789), suggesting moderate robustness to unmeasured confounding.

In sensitivity models with additional adjustment for diabetes, dyslipidemia, and BMI (individually or in combination), the inverse association between the cumulative TyG index and sarcopenia status remained robust, and the linear component was statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. 3). Standardized mediation analysis showed that BMI mediated 60.43% of the total association between the cumulative TyG index and sarcopenia (Supplement Fig. 4B).

Association of TyG index subgroups derived by hierarchical clustering with sarcopenia incidence

Hierarchical cluster analysis based on TyG index values in 2011 and 2015 identified three distinct subgroups: persistently low, moderate, and persistently high TyG groups（Table 3), as visualized in Fig. 3A-B. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) confirmed that these three clusters differed significantly in the joint distribution of the TyG index across the two time points (Pillai’s trace = 0.742, F = 1548.2, P < 0.001). Univariate ANOVA revealed significant between-cluster differences in the TyG index at both 2011 (F = 2780, P < 0.001) and 2015 (F = 4535, P < 0.001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction further demonstrated that all three clusters were significantly different from one another at both time points (all P < 0.001). The average silhouette coefficient was 0.28, indicating a moderate but valid clustering structure in this population-based sample.

In multivariable-adjusted model, graded differences in sarcopenia-related indicators were observed across distinct TyG subgroups (Fig. 3C). Using the persistently low TyG group as the reference group, the moderate TyG group presented a significant lower lower odds of sarcopenia (OR = 0.526; 95% CI, 0.394–0.701), representing a 47.4% reduction in relevant odds. Meanwhile, the persistently high TyG group showed a statistically significant lower odds of sarcopenia (OR = 0.273; 95% CI, 0.184–0.406), corresponding to a 72.7% reduction in relevant odds. These findings remained robust after inverse probability weighting (IPW) adjustment, with ORs of 0.499 (95% CI: 0.331–0.752) for the moderate group and 0.234 (95% CI: 0.137–0.400) for the high group.

Subgroup analyses indicated that the association between TyG clustering subtypes and sarcopenia risk was consistent across these subgroups (Supplementary Table 4). Due to insufficient sample size, subgroup analysis for cancer patients was precluded. The inverse association remained consistent in sensitivity analyses that excluded cancer patients, supporting the robustness of the primary findings. In sensitivity analyses, among individuals without cancer, the negative association remained robust (OR change < 5%) (Supplementary Table 5).

To evaluate the potential influence of unmeasured confounding, we performed an E-value sensitivity analysis. The E-values for the observed ORs of 0.526 (95% CI: 0.394–0.701) and 0.273 (95% CI: 0.184–0.406) were 3.21 and 6.79, respectively, indicating that the inverse associations were robust to unmeasured confounding.

Discussion

This population-based cohort study systematically evaluated longitudinal associations of baseline TyG index, cumulative TyG exposure, and TyG clustering subtypes with sarcopenia incidence in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. The results showed that higher baseline TyG levels and greater cumulative TyG exposure were inversely related to sarcopenia risk, and distinct TyG clustering subtypes also presented divergent risks of sarcopenia.

The TyG index, a validated surrogate marker of insulin resistance (IR) [29, 30], has emerged as a central mediator in sarcopenia pathogenesis. Mounting evidence underscores that IR and sarcopenia can interact reciprocally. IR can hinder the translocation of glucose transporter protein-4 (GLUT-4) in myocytes [31, 32], disrupting glucose uptake and shifting protein turnover toward catabolism, thereby accelerating muscle atrophy [33]. Conversely, sarcopenia exacerbates IR via multiple pathways [15, 34]: 1) impairing the glucose storage capacity; 2) promoting abnormal distribution of lipids and infiltration; 3) inducing lipotoxicity in skeletal muscle; 4) increasing levels of branched-chain amino acids; and 5) reducing the proportion of type I muscle fibers in skeletal muscle. These reciprocal mechanisms create a vicious cycle linking metabolic dysregulation and musculoskeletal decline. However, the causality linking IR and sarcopenia remains incompletely characterized.

Although previous investigations have examined TyG index-sarcopenia associations, inconsistent findings persist. Studies based on U.S. populations generally suggest a positive association between the TyG index and sarcopenia risk, the patterns of association differ [15, 35]. Research by Yang et al. [35] indicates a nonlinear relationship with a clear threshold effect (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 2.62–4.83 when TyG > 9.14), whereas Wei et al. [15] reported an inverted U-shaped association. In contrast, studies in Asian populations have shown different trends. For instance, a Korean study found an inverse association between the TyG index and muscle mass [36], and a recent nationwide cohort study in China also suggested that a higher TyG index was associated with a reduced risk of sarcopenia, although this association weakened after adjusting for BMI [17]. These discrepancies may stem from differences in the racial characteristics of study populations, diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, sample age structures, and whether body composition-related confounding factors (such as BMI and body fat distribution) were adequately controlled. Crucially, no longitudinal studies have evaluated how temporal TyG fluctuations influence sarcopenia risk, representing a significant evidence gap.

Our nationally representative cohort analysis provides novel insights: elevated baseline TyG levels, cumulative TyG burden, and high-stable TyG clustering subtypes were associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia in China’s aging population. The risk reductions were accentuated in males, married individuals, smokers, and drinkers. Through three complementary analytical approaches, revealing consistent patterns, it deepens our understanding of this association. Both baseline and cumulative TyG measurements showed significant L-shaped associations with sarcopenia risk, marked by distinct threshold effects. This suggests the existence of a potential TyG range within the clinically normal spectrum associated with the lowest sarcopenia risk. Below this threshold, higher baseline TyG levels were associated with a significant reduction in risk, whereas above it, the association diminished. Similarly, cumulative TyG analysis identified a potential threshold of 35.49, and below this level, rising cumulative TyG values were inversely correlated with sarcopenia odds, showing a 26.8% lower statistical probability of developing sarcopenia. The cluster analysis aligns with the threshold effects observed in the continuous analyses, indicating that maintaining moderate-to-high TyG levels within the normal physiological range over time is inversely associated with sarcopenia risk when compared with persistently low TyG status. Collectively, these findings propose a metabolic optimization model, suggesting that specific ranges of TyG indices may be beneficial for muscle health, and that metabolic stability, rather than absolute values alone, might be more critical for muscle preservation.

In this study, we observed that participants with higher TyG index levels tended to have higher BMI values but a lower prevalence of sarcopenia. However, the inverse association between baseline TyG index and sarcopenia disappeared after IPW adjustment, whereas cumulative TyG index and long-term high TyG exposure remained significantly correlated with sarcopenia risk. These findings indicated that the effect of baseline TyG exposure on sarcopenia was largely mediated by obesity and insulin resistance. By contrast, long-term sustained high TyG exposure may exert protective effects independent of general insulin resistance and obesity. This phenomenon reflects a typical “obesity paradox”, which is consistent with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [37]. Obesity is significantly associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia (pooled OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.48–0.91) in older populations, supporting the existence of an obesity paradox in musculoskeletal health [37]. Individuals with higher BMI or greater adiposity often present higher muscle mass and muscle strength due to increased mechanical loading and enhanced muscle hypertrophy driven by body weight. BMI may play a crucial role in the relationship between the TyG index and sarcopenia, as a mediating factor as well as a confounder [17]. BMI likely acts as a key mediating variable: “obesity” indicated by the TyG index may induce increased total lean mass, which in turn may affect sarcopenia development via protecting muscle mass and strength [38]. Notably, ASM in this study was estimated using an anthropometric equation rather than measured directly by DXA. While this validated equation has shown good agreement with DXA-measured ASM and is widely used in large-scale epidemiological studies, it may still introduce non-differential or differential misclassification of sarcopenia status. Specifically, in individuals with higher BMI, fat mass may be misclassified as lean mass by the equation, potentially underestimating the prevalence of sarcopenia in obese participants. This measurement bias could partially contribute to the observed “obesity paradox”.

In addition, both cumulative TyG indicators and clustering subtypes were constructed using 2015 TyG measurements, while incident sarcopenia was also identified in the same follow-up wave. Such simultaneous acquisition of partial exposure data and outcome events leads to temporal overlap, making it difficult to fully distinguish the chronological sequence between exposure and disease onset. Accordingly, reverse causation cannot be ruled out; for instance, early sarcopenia-related metabolic disturbances, weight loss, and altered nutritional status may in turn affect circulating TyG levels, which may partly explain the observed inverse association and the obesity paradox phenomenon in this study.

So, this “obesity paradox” does not imply that obesity is beneficial. This finding carries important practical implications: when assessing the impact of metabolic indicators (e.g., the TyG index) on muscle health, individual “obesity” status must be taken into account. For older adults and populations at high metabolic risk, integrated management of metabolic health and body composition should therefore be adopted as a unified strategy for sarcopenia prevention.

The observed subgroup heterogeneity, where the inverse TyG-sarcopenia association was more pronounced in males and among alcohol drinkers, merits mechanistic consideration. The sex disparity may stem from a confluence of biological and sociobehavioral dimorphisms. Biologically, estrogen in females exerts protective effects on glucose homeostasis and muscle protein synthesis [39–41], potentially attenuating the association between the TyG index and sarcopenia progression. This finding does not support the view that alcohol consumption constitutes a protective behavior; it may reflect unmeasured lifestyle or metabolic resilience factors and should not be used to encourage alcohol consumption. Lifestyle factors further amplify these differences: males exhibit higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption [42], which exacerbate oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in skeletal muscle [43, 44]. Marital partnerships foster health-promoting environments through dyadic support systems—including spousal health behavior modeling, nutritional optimization, and collaborative care adherence [45]. Notably, partners with advanced education positively influence smoking cessation and dietary improvements, indirectly modulating sarcopenia risk [46]. Additionally, marital relationships often provide emotional and economic stability, factors linked to reduced depression and better overall health management, further supporting this hypothesis [47]. Although the mechanisms underlying the association between the TyG index and sarcopenia pathogenesis remain unclear, these findings highlight the potential role of longitudinal TyG index trajectories in mitigating sarcopenia risk, particularly within specific demographic subgroups.

It is required to mention that our study possesses multiple advantages: (1) a nationally representative sample of Chinese older adults from CHARLS, ensuring demographic diversity and statistical power; (2) innovative assessment of cumulative TyG exposure and longitudinal TyG clustering subtypes, surpassing conventional cross-sectional approaches; (3) rigorous adjustment for sociodemographic, behavioral, and clinical confounders. This longitudinal design provides critical insights into TyG’s temporal relationships with sarcopenic processes, informing targeted surveillance strategies.

Several methodological constraints should be acknowledged. First, substantial participant exclusion and demographic disparities between included and excluded individuals may introduce selection bias and limit population generalizability. IPW sensitivity analysis further indicated that the observed association might be affected by selection bias, and our findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Second, our findings are based on a Chinese cohort aged ≥ 45 years and may not generalize to other ethnicities. Third, the 4-year observational window (2011–2015) may insufficiently capture long-term TyG trajectory effects, though our models detected significant sarcopenia risk stratification even within this timeframe. Fourth, residual confounding from unquantified factors (e.g., medication regimens, exercise intensity) persists despite comprehensive covariate adjustment. Finally, although longitudinal analyses revealed robust TyG-sarcopenia associations, the biological pathways and causal directionality remain undetermined. Therefore, more research based on multi-ethnic cohorts with extended follow-up periods is necessary to further corroborate our findings.

Conclusions

This longitudinal cohort study demonstrates that both baseline and cumulative TyG were nonlinearly and inversely associated with sarcopenia risk in Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years. The trajectory analysis further demonstrates that sustained moderate-to-high TyG levels were independently associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia compared with persistently low TyG status. These results suggest the existence of a potential TyG range for muscle health maintenance, challenging conventional assumptions about metabolic markers in sarcopenia prevention. However, some results were attenuated after IPW, and the temporal sequence between cumulative exposure and outcome needs further validation. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require replication in studies with clearer longitudinal timing, direct muscle mass measurements, and multi-ethnic samples.
