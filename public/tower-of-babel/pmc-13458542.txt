Unexpected diversity of flower-associated Aureobasidium reveals sixteen novel species from northern Thailand
Authors: Chanokned Senwanna, Pratthana Kodchasee, Chirayut Kathongthung, Milan C. Samarakoon, Pannida Khunnamwong, Jaturong Kumla, Nakarin Suwannarach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAureobasidium species are widely distributed fungi commonly associated with diverse ecological niches, including floral environments. However, their diversity in tropical regions, particularly in flower-associated habitats, remains poorly explored. During a survey of fungi associated with flowers from diverse plant hosts in northern Thailand in 2024, a total of 54 Aureobasidium strains were isolated from 31 flower species. Based on a polyphasic approach integrating multilocus phylogenetic analyses, morphological and physiological characteristics, and growth temperature profiles, these strains were identified as 16 novel species (Aureobasidium anthicolasp. nov., A. bougainvilleaesp. nov., A. catharanthisp. nov., A. chiangmaiensesp. nov., A. floralesp. nov., A. floricolasp. nov., A. florigenumsp. nov., A. kwanphayaoensesp. nov., A. lannaensesp. nov., A. oroxylisp. nov., A. phayaoensesp. nov., A. plumeriaesp. nov., A. pollinicolasp. nov., A. saisamorniaesp. nov., A. savitreeaesp. nov., and A. siamensesp. nov.) and five previously known species (A. albui, A. castaneae, A. melanogenum, A. musti, and A. tremulum). Species within the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense groups were delimited as novel species based on the genealogical concordance phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR) concept, supported by pairwise homoplasy index tests and physiological traits. Carbon assimilation profiles represent useful phenotypic characteristics for distinguishing closely related species. Moreover, these are the first geographic records of A. albui, A. castaneae, A. musti, and A. tremulum in Thailand. Full descriptions, color photographs, illustrations, and a phylogenetic tree showing the phylogenetic positions of the Aureobasidium species obtained in this study are provided. This study highlights floral habitats as important and previously underexplored ecological niches for Aureobasidium in tropical ecosystems, providing new insights into its diversity, ecology, and distribution.

Introduction

The family Saccotheciaceae (Dothideales) was established by Bonorden (1864) to accommodate Saccothecium. To date, 11 genera are included in this family, namely Aureobasidium, Banningia, Columnosphaeria, Kabatiella, Moringomyces, Paivomyces, Pseudoselenophoma, Pseudoseptoria, Pseudosydowia, Saccothecium, and Selenophoma (Hyde et al. 2024; Nascimento et al. 2025), with members occurring as saprophytes, endophytes, and pathogens in both extreme and benign environments, on various substrates, and on human skin (Hongsanan et al. 2020; Samaddar and Sharma 2023; da Silva et al. 2024; Jumbam et al. 2025). Among them, species of Aureobasidium are ascomycetous black yeast-like fungi that occupy a wide range of environments and substrates, including air, human skin, water, mangrove ecosystems, and organic and inorganic outdoor materials (i.e., food, house dust, phylloplanes, rocks, soil, and wood), playing roles as saprophytes, endophytes, and pathogens (Gostinčar et al. 2014; van Nieuwenhuijzen 2014; Li et al. 2015; Bennamoun et al. 2016; Nasr et al. 2018; Di Francesco et al. 2023; da Silva et al. 2024; Czachura and Piątek 2025; Jumbam et al. 2025). In addition, species of the genus are recognized as polyextremotolerant fungi that can survive under various extreme conditions, such as hypersaline water, glacial ice, deserts, frozen or salt-preserved food, and radiation-polluted areas (Gostinčar et al. 2014; Černoša et al. 2021, 2025; Zajc et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2024). Aureobasidium species not only possess the ability to synthesize a variety of enzymes and natural products with potential applications in biotechnology and other industrial fields but are also used to promote plant growth and as biocontrol agents against various plant pathogens, such as Alternaria alternata, Botrytis cinerea, Colletotrichum acutatum, Fusarium culmorum, Monilinia laxa, Penicillium digitatum, P. expansum, and Pseudomonas syringae pv. actinidiae (Wachowska and Głowacka 2014; Spadaro and Droby 2016; Di Francesco et al. 2017, 2023; Gawai 2018; Prasongsuk et al. 2018; Agirman and Erten 2020; Yalage Don et al. 2020; Galli et al. 2021; Cignola et al. 2023; Iqbal et al. 2023; Cornish et al. 2025). Furthermore, A. pullulans is recognized for its ability to degrade aromatic pollutants and plastics (Webb et al. 2000; Wang et al. 2022a; Rensink et al. 2024).

Members of the genus Aureobasidium are morphologically classified as dimorphic fungi, exhibiting both yeast and mycelial forms that vary depending on the species and environmental conditions (Gaur et al. 2010; van Nieuwenhuijzen 2014). They are characterized by dark, slimy colonies, micronematous conidiogenous cells, and aseptate ellipsoidal conidia; primary conidia produce yeast-like budding secondary conidia (Prabhugaonkar and Pratibha 2018). Currently, 62 species are accepted in the genus Aureobasidium, of which 11 lack sequence data and have not been phylogenetically confirmed (Czachura and Piątek 2025; Jumbam et al. 2025; MycoBank 2026; Tan et al. 2026a, 2026b). Among these, A. melanogenum and A. pullulans are common species with the widest distributions and most diverse habitats (Onetto et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2022a; Rensink et al. 2024). Traditionally, species delimitation of Aureobasidium was based primarily on morphological characteristics, such as colony appearance, pigmentation, and conidial morphology; however, these traits often show high phenotypic variability and overlap among species, leading to taxonomic ambiguity (Dennis and Buhagiar 1973; Hermanides-Nijhof 1977; Kocková-Kratochvílová et al. 1980). Physiological and biochemical characteristics, including growth temperature profiles and carbon and nitrogen assimilation profiles, are also used to support the identification and differentiation of Aureobasidium species (de Hoog and Yurlova 1994; Yurlova and de Hoog 1997; Nasr et al. 2018; Jumbam et al. 2025). Subsequently, with the advent of molecular techniques, particularly DNA sequencing, species identification has become more reliable. Early studies primarily utilized ribosomal DNA regions, such as the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), for species-level identification (Yurlova et al. 1995, 1996; de Hoog et al. 1999; Prasongsuk et al. 2005; Peterson et al. 2013). Nevertheless, because of the limited resolution among closely related taxa, multilocus phylogenetic approaches incorporating additional gene regions, such as the 28S large subunit (LSU), RNA polymerase II second-largest subunit (rpb2), translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1), and β-tubulin (tub) loci, have been increasingly applied to resolve species boundaries and elucidate evolutionary relationships within the genus (Zalar et al. 2008; Humphries et al. 2017; Prasongsuk et al. 2018; da Silva et al. 2024; Jumbam et al. 2025). Additionally, phylogenetic analyses based on whole-genome sequences have demonstrated that the four A. pullulans varieties represent distinct species, namely A. melanogenum, A. namibiae, A. pullulans, and A. subglaciale (Gostinčar et al. 2014). Recent advances in species delimitation methods have improved taxonomic resolution within Aureobasidium. Comparative studies of genealogical concordance phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR), coalescent-based species recognition (CBSR), and distance-based species recognition (DBSR) have shown that GCPSR provides the most accurate and consistent approach for defining species boundaries within the genus (Jumbam et al. 2025). This approach can be supported by physiological characterization, particularly carbon assimilation and fermentation tests, to improve the resolution of species delineation (Jumbam et al. 2025).

Several studies have established flowers as important habitats for fungal diversity. Floral structures provide unique microenvironments with abundant nutrients, transient moisture, and frequent biotic interactions, creating favorable conditions for fungal colonization (Vannette et al. 2021; Larrouy et al. 2023; Ruraż et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2024). A wide range of fungi, including yeasts, endophytes, epiphytes, and latent pathogens, have been reported from floral tissues, with common genera including Alternaria, Aspergillus, Aureobasidium, Candida, Cladosporium, Colletotrichum, Fusarium, Metschnikowia, and Penicillium (Zhou et al. 2014; Moreira et al. 2021; Zimowska et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2022; Di Francesco et al. 2023; Kanpiengjai et al. 2023; Agarbati et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2024). Aureobasidium species, particularly A. pullulans, have been increasingly recognized as common inhabitants of floral environments across diverse geographic regions and host plants. For instance, A. pullulans has been reported from a variety of floral sources, including tiger lily (Lilium sp.), monkshood (Aconitum carmichaeli), wild daisy (Chrysanthemum boreale), and eastern daisy fleabane (Erigeron anuus) in South Korea (Anastassiadis et al. 2003; Kim et al. 2015; Kim and Kim 2015; Kim et al. 2018; Choi et al. 2019); pink node flowers (Caesulia axillaris) in India (Choudhury et al. 2011); and the nectar of kidneyvetch (Anthyllis vulneraria), snapdragons (Antirrhinum australe), willow-leaved foxglove (Digitalis obscura), and blue broom (Erinacea anthyllis) in Spain (Pozo et al. 2011). It has also been reported from hummingbird flowers (Epilobium canum) in the USA (Morris et al. 2020). In Brazil, A. pullulans and A. thailandense have been isolated from inflorescences of bromeliad plants (Félix et al. 2021). The successful colonization of floral environments by Aureobasidium can be attributed to several adaptive features, including its ability to withstand osmotic stress in high-sugar nectar, resistance to UV radiation and desiccation through melanin production, and efficient surface colonization via extracellular polysaccharides (Gostinčar et al. 2014; Zalar et al. 2008). Previous studies found that A. pullulans effectively colonizes floral tissues, particularly stigmas, and suppresses fire blight through the competitive exclusion of the pathogen (Temple et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2023). Furthermore, the dispersal capacity of Aureobasidium in floral environments, influenced by factors such as wind, rainfall, microhabitat heterogeneity, resource availability, and biotic interactions (e.g., pollinators), may contribute to shaping its distribution and diversification (Choi et al. 2019; Pozo et al. 2011; Di Francesco et al. 2023; Lignon et al. 2025).

Thailand is noted to be one of the world’s most biodiverse countries, with an estimated 15,000 plant species (approximately 8% of the global total), providing a foundation for diverse floral habitats that may support unique fungal and microbial communities (Donnelly 2024; Office of Natural Resources and Environmental Policy and Planning 2024; Saensouk et al. 2025). Nevertheless, research on flower-associated fungi in Thailand remains limited. Additionally, the diversity of Aureobasidium species within the country remains poorly investigated, with only a few reports available (Punnapayak et al. 2003; Peterson et al. 2013). To date, A. melanogenum, A. pullulans, and A. thailandense are among the few Aureobasidium species reported from various substrates in Thailand, and they have been investigated for their physiological characteristics and extracellular polysaccharide production (Sudhadham 2001; Punnapayak et al. 2003; Zalar et al. 2008; Peterson et al. 2013; Prasongsuk et al. 2018). During a survey of fungi associated with flowers in northern Thailand in 2024, a total of 54 Aureobasidium strains were isolated. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize and identify these Aureobasidium strains using morphological characteristics, growth temperature profiles, and multilocus phylogenetic analyses of the ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub loci. Moreover, following Jumbam et al. (2025), multiple species delimitation approaches, including GCPSR and CBSR analyses, together with physiological characteristics, were employed to delimit novel species within the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense species groups.

Materials and methods

Flower collection and fungal isolation

Healthy flowers from 31 plant species (Fig. 1) were randomly collected from living plants in Mueang Chiang Mai and Phrao Districts (Chiang Mai Province) and Mueang Phayao District (Phayao Province), northern Thailand, from July to September 2024. Flowers were placed in sterile plastic bags, stored at 4 °C, and processed for fungal isolation within 24 h of collection.

Some flower samples collected from northern Thailand (A–L) and their internal parts (M–X). A, MAntigonon leptopus; B, NBidens pilosa; C, OCatharanthus roseus; D, PCheilocostus speciosus; E, QEhretia microphylla; F, RExacum affine; G, SJasminum sambac; H, TOroxylum indicum; I, UPentas lanceolata; J, VRosa sp.; K, WTagetes erecta; L, XTecoma stans. Scale bars: 1 cm (A–L); 1 mm (M–X).

The excised internal parts of flowers, including the carpel, stamen, pollen, and nectary, were removed at random from each flower species using sterile forceps. The samples were immersed in 5 mL of sterile saline solution (0.85% w/v NaCl) for 15 min and then vortexed for 1 min. The solution was then spread on potato dextrose agar (PDA; Condalab, Laboratorios Conda S.A., Spain) supplemented with 50 mg/L chloramphenicol. After incubation for 72 h at 25 °C in the dark, colonies with a characteristic shiny appearance were selected and transferred to fresh PDA plates for further incubation at 25 °C. Each pure fungal strain was preserved for short-term storage on PDA slants at 4 °C and for long-term storage in 20% glycerol at –80 °C at the Sustainable Development of Biological Resources Culture Collection (SDBR-CMU), Faculty of Science, Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai, Thailand. Holotype specimens of the new species were deposited as dried specimens in the Chiang Mai University Biology Department’s Herbarium (CMUB), Chiang Mai University, Chiang Mai Province, Thailand, whereas ex-type living cultures were permanently maintained in a metabolically inactive state at SDBR-CMU, the Thailand Bioresource Research Center Culture Collection (TBRC), Pathum Thani Province, Thailand, and the Guizhou Medical University Culture Collection (GMBCC), Guiyang, China. Nomenclatural and taxonomic information was deposited in the MycoBank database (http://www.mycobank.org/).

Fungal identification

Morphological studies

Pure cultures were examined on PDA, malt extract agar (MEA; Gibco, Life Technologies Corporation, USA), and oatmeal agar (OA; Difco, Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA). Colony diameters and culture characteristics were recorded after two weeks of incubation at 25 °C in the dark. Colony colors were recorded according to the Methuen Handbook of Colour (Kornerup and Wanscher 1967). In addition, the cardinal growth temperatures of the fungal strains were determined on MEA at 4, 10, 25, 30, and 37 °C after two weeks of incubation in the dark (Jumbam et al. 2025). A Nikon DS-Ri2 camera fitted to a Nikon ECLIPSE Ni compound microscope (Tokyo, Japan) was used to examine and capture microscopic features, including hyphae, conidiophores, conidiogenous cells, conidia, and chlamydospores. The fungal structures were measured using the Tarosoft Image Framework program (v. 0.9.0.7). Adobe Photoshop version 22.4.2 (Adobe Systems, USA) was employed to create the photographic plates.

DNA extractions, polymerase chain reaction, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from two-week-old Aureobasidium cultures of each strain using the OMEGA E.Z.N.A.® Forensic DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub loci were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the ITS5/ITS4, LR0R/LR5, fRPB2-5F/fRPB2-7cR, EF1-728F/EF1-986R, and Bt2a/Bt2b primer pairs, respectively. PCR amplification and sequencing were performed according to previous studies (Senwanna et al. 2024, 2025; da Silva et al. 2024; Czachura and Piątek 2025; Kodchasee et al. 2026).

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analyses

The closest matching taxa were determined using nucleotide BLAST searches conducted in GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The alignments of each individual locus were compiled using representative sequences of the closest relatives of the strains, selected based on previous phylogenetic studies (da Silva et al. 2024; Czachura and Piątek 2025; Jumbam et al. 2025). Furthermore, the combined ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub datasets were analyzed to confirm the phylogenetic placement of the new strains. The obtained alignments were deposited in TreeBase (https://www.treebase.org/) under submission number 32509 (Suppl. material 1). Phylogenetic analysis of the combined alignment was conducted using maximum likelihood (ML) in RAxML-HPC2 on XSEDE (v. 8.2.12) via the CIPRES web portal and Bayesian inference (BI) in MrBayes v. 3.2.6 (Ronquist et al. 2012).

In the ML tree, ML bootstrap support values (MLBS) were estimated under the GTRGAMMA nucleotide substitution model with 1,000 replicates. The best-fit models used for the BI analyses, which were determined based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC) using MrModeltest v. 2.3 (Nylander 2004), were SYM+I+G for ITS, GTR+I+G for LSU, rpb2, and tub, and GTR+G for tef1. Four simultaneous Markov chains were run for 6,000,000 generations, with trees sampled every 100 generations. The analysis was terminated when the average standard deviation of split frequencies fell below 0.01. The first 15,000 trees were discarded as burn-in, and the remaining 45,000 trees were used to calculate posterior probabilities (PP) in the majority-rule consensus tree. A phylogram generated from the ML analysis was visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.0 (Rambaut 2016) and edited using Adobe Illustrator version 25.2.3 and Adobe Photoshop version 22.4.2 (Adobe Systems, USA). The new nucleotide sequence data were deposited in GenBank.

Physiological characterization

The physiological characteristics of the newly identified Aureobasidium species obtained in this study were assessed according to the standardized methods described by Kurtzman et al. (2011), and the results of the growth tests were recorded after three weeks of incubation. Carbon and nitrogen assimilation tests were conducted in liquid media, whereas nitrogen assimilation tests were performed using starved inocula. Glucose fermentation was examined in a liquid medium using Durham fermentation tubes. Three replicates were conducted for each strain.

Species delimitation analyses

Coalescent-based species recognition (CBSR)

CBSR analyses were conducted using the Poisson tree processes (PTP) (Zhang et al. 2013) and general mixed Yule-coalescent (GMYC) (Fujisawa and Barraclough 2013) models. The Bayesian implementation of the PTP model (bPTP) was conducted using the PTP web server (https://species.h-its.org/ptp/; accessed on 25 May 2026), applying both the maximum-likelihood solution (mlbPTP; Zhang et al. 2013) under a single Poisson distribution and the Bayesian solution (bPTP). The multi-rate PTP (mPTP; https://mptp.h-its.org/#/tree; accessed on 25 May 2026) was also performed (Kapli et al. 2017). This improved version of PTP does not require user-defined parameters and estimates support values for each delimited species using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). These values can be used to assess confidence in the maximum-likelihood delimitation. The maximum-likelihood tree obtained from the phylogenetic analysis was used as input for all PTP-based analyses. For GMYC analysis, the input file was generated using the BEAST v. 1.10.4 package (Suchard et al. 2018). The XML file was prepared in BEAUti v. 1.10.4 using the same alignment as that used in the phylogenetic analysis and was run in BEAST via the CIPRES Science Gateway. The resulting log file was examined in Tracer v. 1.7, and the tree file was summarized using TreeAnnotator v. 2.6.2. The resulting ultrametric tree was visualized in FigTree v. 1.4.0 and uploaded to the GMYC web server (https://species.h-its.org/gmyc/; accessed on 27 May 2026) using the single-threshold method. The resulting delimitation was then annotated on the phylogenetic tree for final presentation.

Genealogical concordance phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR)

GCPSR analyses using the pairwise homoplasy index (PHI; Φw) were conducted in SplitsTree4 v. 4.17.1 (Huson and Bryant 2006) to assess the level of recombination among phylogenetically closely related species represented by type strains generated in this study within the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense species groups. The concatenated ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub datasets of these related species were used for the PHI test and related analyses. The relationships between the newly generated strains and other closely related species were visualized by constructing split graphs using the LogDet transformation and split decomposition options. PHI results equal to or greater than 0.05 (Φw ≥ 0.05) indicated no significant recombination within the dataset, suggesting that related species within the group were genetically distinct. In contrast, PHI results below 0.05 (Φw < 0.05) indicated significant recombination within the dataset.

Results

Fungal isolation and sequence similarity

A total of 54 Aureobasidium strains were isolated from 31 flower species collected in northern Thailand (Suppl. material 2). The highest numbers of strains were recovered from Catharanthus roseus and Ocimum tenuiflorum (four strains each), followed by Jatropha integerrima, Pentas lanceolata, Plumeria obtusa, Senna spectabilis, and Tectona grandis (three strains each). DNA sequences (ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub) obtained from each strain were deposited in GenBank (Suppl. material 3). Based on morphological characteristics and molecular data, all strains were confirmed to belong to the genus Aureobasidium. Based on sequence similarity, 12 strains were identified as five previously known species, including A. albui (one strain), A. castaneae (one strain), A. melanogenum (two strains), A. musti (two strains), and A. tremulum (six strains). This represents the first report of A. albui, A. castaneae, A. musti, and A. tremulum from Thailand. The remaining 42 strains potentially represented novel species. Therefore, a multilocus phylogenetic approach was employed to confirm their phylogenetic placement in combination with multiple species-recognition criteria.

Phylogeny

Phylogenetic analyses were based on a concatenated multilocus dataset of ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub sequences comprising 155 taxa from representative strains of Aureobasidium species, including the outgroups. The total alignment comprised 3164 characters, including gaps (ITS: 1–612; LSU: 613–1474; rpb2: 1475–2398; tef1: 2399–2734; tub: 2735–3164). ML and BI analyses resulted in similar tree topologies that were consistent with those of previous studies (da Silva et al. 2024; Czachura and Piątek 2025; Jumbam et al. 2025). The best-scoring tree from the RAxML analysis, with a final ML optimization likelihood value of –23264.237773, is presented in Fig. 2. The RAxML analysis of the combined multilocus dataset had 1,455 distinct alignment patterns, with 34.98% undetermined characters or gaps. The estimated base frequencies were as follows: A = 0.247157, C = 0.245183, G = 0.268181, T = 0.239479; substitution rates AC = 1.991704, AG = 4.332866, AT = 1.683882, CG = 1.084287, CT = 10.819685, GT = 1.000000; gamma distribution shape parameter α = 0.539966. The average standard deviation of split frequencies reached 0.009496.

Multilocus phylogenetic analyses revealed that the newly generated sequences from 54 strains grouped within Aureobasidium, representing 16 novel species and five previously known species (A. albui SDBR-CMU866, A. castaneae SDBR-CMU835, A. melanogenum SDBR-CMU817 and SDBR-CMU855, A. musti SDBR-CMU825 and SDBR-CMU864, and A. tremulum SDBR-CMU814, SDBR-CMU816, SDBR-CMU820, SDBR-CMU834, SDBR-CMU838, and SDBR-CMU856) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, most Aureobasidium species could be grouped in accordance with previous studies into the A. namibiae, A. melanogenum, A. pullulans, A. subglaciale, and A. thailandense species groups (Zalar et al. 2008; Jumbam et al. 2025). The A. melanogenum group comprised A. gimbutasiae, A. melanogenum, A. motuoense, A. musti, A. zhengzhenxiangiae, and together with 18 strains representing seven new species (A. chiangmaiense SDBR-CMU818 and SDBR-CMU818, A. phayaoense SDBR-CMU811, SDBR-CMU852, and SDBR-CMU857, A. plumeriae SDBR-CMU826 and SDBR-CMU836, A. pollinicola SDBR-CMU810, SDBR-CMU813, SDBR-CMU843, and SDBR-CMU861, A. saisamorniae SDBR-CMU846, SDBR-CMU848, and SDBR-CMU854, A. savitreeae SDBR-CMU824 and SDBR-CMU841, and A. siamense SDBR-CMU836 and SDBR-CMU847) identified in this study, with 87% MLBS and 0.99 BIPP support. Additionally, the A. thailandense group comprised six previously described species (A. albui, A. castaneae, A. essambei, A. microtermitis, A. thailandense, and A. vanuatuense) and 18 strains representing six new species (A. anthicola SDBR-CMU821 and SDBR-CMU851, A. florale SDBR-CMU830, SDBR-CMU831, SDBR-CMU832, SDBR-CMU840, SDBR-CMU850, and SDBR-CMU862, A. floricola SDBR-CMU829 and SDBR-CMU853, A. florigenum SDBR-CMU828 and SDBR-CMU865, A. kwanphayaoense SDBR-CMU839, SDBR-CMU844, SDBR-CMU849, and SDBR-CMU860, and A. lannaense SDBR-CMU833 and SDBR-CMU863) identified in this study. Three additional new Aureobasidium species, namely A. bougainvilleae (SDBR-CMU858 and SDBR-CMU859), A. catharanthi (SDBR-CMU812 and SDBR-CMU842), and A. oroxyli (SDBR-CMU822 and SDBR-CMU827), and one known species, A. tremulum (SDBR-CMU814, SDBR-CMU816, SDBR-CMU820, SDBR-CMU834, SDBR-CMU838, and SDBR-CMU856), were resolved as distinct monophyletic lineages separate from other previously known species. Furthermore, GCPSR, using the pairwise homoplasy index (PHI), was applied to confirm the delimitation of new species within both the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense groups.

Species delimitation analyses

Coalescent-based species recognition (CBSR)

Among the PTP-based methods (Fig. 2), the highest number of putative species was delimited by bPTP (49 species), followed by mlbPTP (39 species) and mPTP (23 species). The mPTP method grouped members of the A. melanogenum and A. namibiae groups, together with A. aerium, A. frostiae, and A. uvarum, into a single species, whereas bPTP delimited the strains of A. aerium as a single distinct species, and mlbPTP separated each strain as an individual species. Within the A. melanogenum group, mlbPTP delimited all strains, including A. frostiae and A. uvarum, as a single species, whereas bPTP delimited eight species. Notably, A. frostiae and two strains of A. uvarum were treated as conspecific by bPTP. Within the A. namibiae group, mlbPTP delimited six species: species 1 comprised A. diazvalderramae, A. fermentans, A. intercalariosporum, A. khasianum, A. leucospermi, A. namibiae, A. orasae, A. pini, A. rubi, and A. xishuangbannaense; species 2 comprised A. insectorum strains LPL-1C and XZY249M1; species 3 comprised A. insectorum strain XZY65-10; species 4 comprised A. insectorum strain KCL139 (type strain); species 5 corresponded to A. cavalettoi; and species 6 corresponded to A. epipinicola. In contrast, the bPTP analysis recognized nine species: species 1 comprised A. diazvalderramae, A. fermentans, A. intercalariosporum, A. khasianum, A. leucospermi, A. namibiae, A. pini, A. rubi, and A. xishuangbannaense, whereas species 2–9 were delimited as distinct species represented by individual strains of A. cavalettoi, A. epipinicola, A. insectorum, and A. orasae. Within the A. subglaciale group, mlbPTP, bPTP, and mPTP grouped the three strains into a single species. In the A. pullulans group, most taxa were delimited as distinct species by all PTP-based methods, except for A. lini and A. toomeae, which were grouped as one species by mPTP but resolved as distinct species by mlbPTP and bPTP. Aureobasidium aurantiacum and Aureobasidium sp. (as A. zeae) were treated as the same species by all PTP-based methods. For A. iranianum, mlbPTP and bPTP produced consistent results, whereas mPTP grouped all strains into a single species. Within the A. thailandense group, all PTP-based methods grouped A. albui, A. anthicola, A. castaneae, A. florale, A. floricola, A. florigenum, A. kwanphayaoense, A. lannaense, and A. vanuatuense as a single species. Two strains of A. thailandense were identified as a single species by mlbPTP and bPTP, whereas mPTP separated them as distinct species. The mlbPTP and bPTP methods each separated the two strains of A. microtermitis as individual species, whereas mPTP grouped them into a single species. Aureobasidium essambei was consistently treated as a distinct species by all PTP-based methods. The mPTP method grouped A. bougainvilleae, A. caatingaense, A. catharanthi, A. peruvianum, and A. santateresinhaense into a single species. Similarly, A. faidherbiae, Aureobasidium sp. C802, and A. welwitschiae were also grouped into a single species, whereas A. harposporum, A. mangrovei, A. planticola, and Aureobasidium sp. SAM12 were delimited as distinct species. Both mlbPTP and bPTP grouped A. bougainvilleae, A. caatingaense, and A. catharanthi into a single species, whereas A. faidherbiae, A. peruvianum, A. santateresinhaense, Aureobasidium sp. C802, Aureobasidium sp. SAM12, and A. welwitschiae were each delimited as a distinct species. Finally, A. planticola in bPTP and A. harposporum and A. mangrovei in mlbPTP were split such that each strain was delimited as an individual species.

The GMYC analysis identified 96 species hypotheses within Aureobasidium (Fig. 2). The analysis recovered species boundaries largely consistent with those inferred from the multilocus phylogenetic analyses. However, A. insectorum, A. melanogenum, A. motuoense, A. musti, A. pullulans, and A. tremulum were not well delimited. In addition, strains of A. albui, A. iranianum, A. planticola, A. proteae, A. subglaciale, A. thailandense, and A. uvarum were split into distinct species by GMYC. Notably, A. albui SA57 was treated as conspecific with A. floricola, consistent with the combined phylogenetic analyses.

The results of the species delimitation analyses revealed that the PTP-based methods were generally inconsistent with the multilocus phylogenetic analyses. In several lineages, PTP approaches either over-lumped distinct taxa into a single species (e.g., in the A. melanogenum and A. namibiae groups) or over-split closely related strains. In contrast, the GMYC model revealed a higher level of congruence with the multilocus phylogenetic analyses. Most species boundaries were consistent with the phylogenetic tree, indicating that GMYC more accurately captured evolutionary divergence among lineages. GMYC tended to separate strains within A. iranianum and A. thailandense; however, it was more effective in resolving species-level distinctions overall.

Genealogical concordance phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR)

The level of recombination among newly generated strains and representative strains of phylogenetically closely related species in the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense groups was assessed using the PHI test based on a combined multilocus dataset of ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub sequences. The PHI result showed no evidence of significant recombination (Φw = 0.9965) among A. chiangmaiense, A. gimbutasiae, A. melanogenum, A. motuoense, A. musti, A. phayaoense, A. plumeriae, A. pollinicola, A. saisamorniae, A. savitreeae, A. siamense, and A. zhengzhenxiangiae in the A. melanogenum group (Fig. 3). In the A. thailandense group, no significant recombination was detected (Φw = 0.9612) among A. anthicola, A. albui, A. castaneae, A. essambei, A. floricola, A. florigenum, A. florale, A. kwanphayaoense, A. lannaense, A. microtermitis, A. thailandense, and A. vanuatuense (Fig. 4).

Physiological characterization

Physiological tests were performed for the 16 newly identified Aureobasidium species described in this study, and the results are presented in Table 1. The results revealed distinct carbon assimilation patterns that provided valuable phenotypic evidence supporting the delimitation of the newly described species from their closest phylogenetic relatives. Glucose fermentation was negative for all 16 Aureobasidium species. Ammonium sulfate, ethylamine HCl, L-lysine, cadaverine, and creatine were assimilated as nitrogen sources by all Aureobasidium species, whereas none assimilated potassium nitrate or sodium nitrite. During the carbon assimilation tests, A. anthicola produced light brown pigmentation when grown in salicin, D-glucono-1,5-lactone, and D-gluconate. Aureobasidium siamense produced light brown pigmentation along with dark brown sediment when grown in D-glucono-1,5-lactone. In contrast, A. oroxyli developed light pink pigmentation when grown in D-glucose and was also the only species unable to assimilate N-acetyl-D-glucosamine and galactitol. Aureobasidium savitreeae produced light pink pigmentation and dark brown sediment during the assimilation of D-glucose, D-xylose, L-arabinose, sucrose, raffinose, melezitose, and erythritol. Moreover, none of the Aureobasidium species assimilated soluble starch or methanol. Only A. chiangmaiense was able to assimilate inulin. Among the strains examined, A. floricola and A. phayaoense did not assimilate methyl-α-D-glucoside and succinate, respectively. The inability to assimilate DL-lactate was observed in A. chiangmaiense, A. florale, A. oroxyli, and A. phayaoense. Discussion

Species identification within the genus Aureobasidium is challenging because of extensive morphological variation and overlapping phenotypic characteristics among taxa (de Hoog and Yurlova 1994; Yurlova and de Hoog 1997). Physiological characteristics (e.g., growth temperature profiles and carbon and nitrogen assimilation profiles) and exopolysaccharide structure have also been used to support species identification (Hermanides-Nijhof 1977; Yurlova and de Hoog 1997; Urzì et al. 1999; Prasongsuk et al. 2005; Wu et al. 2023; Jumbam et al. 2025). Molecular approaches have been used for the identification of Aureobasidium species. Commonly used barcode regions, such as ITS and LSU, often provide insufficient resolution for species delimitation (da Silva et al. 2024). Genomic analyses later demonstrated that these varieties represent distinct species in the A. pullulans group (Gostinčar et al. 2014). Protein-coding genes (e.g., elo2, rpb2, tef1, and tub) have therefore been recommended for resolving phylogenetic relationships and delineating species boundaries in Aureobasidium (Zalar et al. 2008; Gostinčar et al. 2014; Parra et al. 2023; da Silva et al. 2024; Czachura and Piątek 2025). Consequently, accurate identification requires an integrative approach that combines morphological data with multilocus phylogenetic analyses (da Silva et al. 2024; Jumbam et al. 2025). Moreover, owing to the high morphological and genetic variability among species, genealogical concordance phylogenetic species recognition (GCPSR) and phylogenetic species recognition (PSR) have been applied to resolve cryptic fungal taxa, including Aureobasidium (Liu et al. 2016; Hilário et al. 2021; Jumbam et al. 2025). Comparative assessments of species recognition methods demonstrated that GCPSR provides the most effective framework for delimiting species boundaries within Aureobasidium. Furthermore, physiological characteristics, particularly carbon assimilation and fermentation profiles, represent useful complementary characteristics that can aid in the discrimination of closely related taxa and support phylogenetically inferred species boundaries (Jumbam et al. 2025).

In this study, 16 novel species and five previously known species of Aureobasidium, isolated from 31 flower species in northern Thailand, are described based on morphological and physiological characteristics, growth temperature profiles, and multilocus phylogenetic analyses of ITS, LSU, rpb2, tef1, and tub sequence data. The combined analyses supported the classification of Aureobasidium into five groups, namely, the A. melanogenum, A. namibiae, A. pullulans, A. subglaciale, and A. thailandense groups, and corroborated the placement of the representative strains, consistent with previous phylogenetic studies (Zalar et al. 2008; da Silva et al. 2024; Jumbam et al. 2025). Phylogenetic analyses based on multilocus sequence data revealed that the majority of Aureobasidium species obtained in this study clustered within the A. melanogenum species group (viz. A. chiangmaiense, A. melanogenum, A. musti, A. phayaoense, A. plumeriae, A. pollinicola, A. saisamorniae, A. savitreeae, and A. siamense) and the A. thailandense species group (viz. A. albui, A. anthicola, A. castaneae, A. florale, A. floricola, A. florigenum, A. lannaense, and A. kwanphayaoense). Aureobasidium bougainvilleae, A. catharanthi, A. oroxyli, and A. tremulum formed a distinct, well-supported clade within Aureobasidium (Fig. 2). In addition to pairwise DNA sequence comparisons among related species within the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense groups, this study demonstrates that Aureobasidium species can be effectively distinguished based on rpb2 and tef1 sequences, which provide high phylogenetic resolution, whereas ITS and tub are useful for differentiating certain species. It is therefore recommended that future taxonomic studies of Aureobasidium employ a standardized set of molecular markers, including ITS, rpb2, tef1, and additional informative loci, to ensure consistent species identification and facilitate comprehensive phylogenetic analyses across the genus. Furthermore, the GCPSR, GMYC, and PTP (bPTP, mlbPTP, and mPTP) analyses were implemented for species delimitation within an integrative taxonomic framework combining multilocus phylogenetic analyses. The GCPSR and GMYC approaches delimited species boundaries that were largely congruent with well-supported clades, thereby providing independent support for species-level differentiation, particularly within the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense groups. However, GMYC tended to oversplit lineages, consistent with previous studies suggesting that it may overestimate species numbers (Fujisawa and Barraclough 2013; Talavera et al. 2013). In contrast, discrepancies among the mlbPTP, bPTP, and mPTP delimitation methods were observed in closely related lineages within species complexes (i.e., the A. melanogenum, A. namibiae, A. pullulans, and A. thailandense groups), in agreement with Jumbam et al. (2025). These inconsistencies likely reflect evolutionary processes such as incomplete lineage sorting or ongoing gene flow, which can obscure species boundaries. Consequently, reliance on a single delimitation method may result in the over- or underestimation of species diversity. In the present study, GCPSR analyses were considered the most suitable approach for species delimitation, consistent with the findings of Jumbam et al. (2025). The phylogenetic structure observed in this study highlights the high diversity and complex evolutionary history of Aureobasidium. The presence of multiple well-supported clades, combined with the detection of numerous putative new species, suggests that species diversity in this genus remains underestimated. Although most species boundaries are well supported, some groups exhibit complex patterns that highlight the need for additional data (e.g., population-level sampling, ecological traits, or genomic analyses) to fully resolve species limits. Physiological characteristics provided valuable supporting evidence for distinguishing closely related species within the genus Aureobasidium. In particular, diagnostic differences in carbon assimilation profiles, pigmentation development, and sediment formation during substrate utilization proved useful for differentiating the newly described species from their closest phylogenetic relatives. These findings are consistent with the recommendations of Jumbam et al. (2025), who emphasized that physiological characterization, particularly carbon assimilation and fermentation tests, should complement morphological and multilocus phylogenetic analyses in a polyphasic approach to species delimitation within Aureobasidium. Therefore, based on the findings of the present study, the adoption of a polyphasic approach for species identification and delimitation in Aureobasidium is recommended by integrating multilocus phylogenetic, morphological, and physiological data. This integrative framework provides a more robust and reliable basis for recognizing species boundaries, particularly among morphologically similar and cryptic species. Future studies should incorporate broader taxon sampling and additional gene loci to improve the resolution of species boundaries within Aureobasidium, alongside the integration of genomic data, multiple species-delimitation approaches, and ecological and physiological data.

Aureobasidium is a ubiquitous and cosmopolitan genus isolated from diverse substrates worldwide (Zalar et al. 2008; Zajc et al. 2020, 2022; Lee et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2022b; da Silva et al. 2024; Jumbam et al. 2025). However, species within the genus exhibit either broad or restricted distributions, reflecting ecological differentiation rather than random variation within populations (Zalar et al. 2008; Gostinčar et al. 2014, 2019; Černoša et al. 2021; Zajc et al. 2022). This difference may reflect distinct evolutionary strategies and ecological adaptations within the genus (Jia et al. 2019; Akbari et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2024). For example, A. melanogenum and A. pullulans are highly versatile and polyextremotolerant species known for their ability to thrive in diverse and extreme environments, reflecting their remarkable ecological adaptability (Zalar et al. 2008; Gostinčar et al. 2019; Zajc et al. 2020; Černoša et al. 2021). Owing to these adaptations, genome-based studies of these species from different environments have been conducted to investigate their ecological versatility, biotechnological and metabolic potential, stress tolerance, and taxonomic features (Gostinčar et al. 2014; Černoša et al. 2021). Notably, these studies have revealed contrasting population structures. Aureobasidium pullulans exhibits high levels of recombination, which may obscure species boundaries and complicate phylogenetic inference, consistent with its flexible lifestyle and frequent exposure to diverse environmental conditions (Gostinčar et al. 2014). In contrast, A. melanogenum, which is associated with more restricted habitats, shows little to no evidence of recombination within the species, a pattern also observed in A. subglaciale (Zajc et al. 2022). As mentioned above, Aureobasidium species, particularly A. pullulans and A. thailandense, have frequently been isolated from floral habitats (Temple et al. 2020; Félix et al. 2021; Di Francesco et al. 2023; Agarbati et al. 2024). The frequent occurrence and high diversity of Aureobasidium species in floral environments can be attributed to the combined effects of ecological suitability, physiological adaptability, and dispersal dynamics (Temple et al. 2020; Morris et al. 2020; Ruraż et al. 2023). Flowers represent nutrient-rich yet selective microhabitats, driven by sugar-rich nectar, fluctuating environmental conditions, and short life spans, all of which favor fast-growing and stress-tolerant microorganisms (Ushio et al. 2015; Vannette et al. 2021; Niu et al. 2022). Therefore, microorganisms associated with flowers are adapted for survival through traits such as rapid colonization and persistence under osmotic and desiccation stress (Herrera et al. 2008; Mittelbach et al. 2015; Morris et al. 2020). Aureobasidium exhibits a wide range of stress-tolerance traits that enable its persistence on exposed plant surfaces, including osmotolerance in high-sugar environments, resistance to ultraviolet radiation via melanin production, and the secretion of extracellular polysaccharides that facilitate adhesion and biofilm formation (Gostinčar et al. 2014; Di Francesco et al. 2023; Rensink et al. 2024). Furthermore, floral structures serve as sites of microbial exchange, where pollinators facilitate the transfer of microorganisms among plant hosts, thereby increasing species mixing and promoting microbial diversity (Morris et al. 2020; Cullen et al. 2021; Lignon et al. 2025). This process promotes the continual dispersal of Aureobasidium from diverse reservoirs into floral environments, consistent with its broad ecological amplitude and generalist life strategy (Andrews and Harris 2000; Glushakova and Chernov 2007; Zalar et al. 2008), and likely contributes to its persistence and elevated diversity within these habitats.

This study highlights that flowers represent an important source for investigating the diversity of Aureobasidium species and emphasizes the need for further investigations. The adaptive and survival mechanisms of flower-associated Aureobasidium identified in this study warrant further investigation. Furthermore, future studies should employ different culture media and culture-independent methods to comprehensively characterize the diversity of Aureobasidium in floral environments, including seasonal variations, floral species, and floral distribution patterns. In addition, integrating genome-based approaches, broader geographic sampling, and ecological investigations will be essential to elucidate the diversity, distribution, and functional roles of flower-associated Aureobasidium species.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that flowers in northern Thailand harbor unexpectedly high Aureobasidium diversity (21 species from 31 plant species), highlighting floral habitats as important and previously underexplored reservoirs of fungal diversity in tropical ecosystems. The discovery of 16 novel species, together with five known species and four species recorded for the first time in Thailand, substantially expands current knowledge of the taxonomy, diversity, and geographic distribution of Aureobasidium. The application of a polyphasic approach integrating multilocus phylogenetic analyses, morphological and physiological characteristics, growth temperature profiles, and GCPSR supported by PHI tests provides robust species delimitation within this complex group, particularly within the A. melanogenum and A. thailandense groups. These findings significantly enhance current knowledge of Aureobasidium in tropical regions and underscore the ecological significance of flowers as key fungal niches, highlighting the need for further systematic surveys of flower-associated mycobiota in tropical ecosystems.
