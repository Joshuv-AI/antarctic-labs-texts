Association of new dog ownership with physical activity and social contact: the retrospective study
Authors: Yu Taniguchi, Maasa Kobayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated whether new dog ownership without past ownership leads to increased physical activity and social contact compared to non-dog ownership. We analyzed data from 1,210 participants: 81 new dog owners who have never owned a dog before and started ownership within the past year, 614 non-dog owners with a past ownership history, and 515 non-dog owners without a past ownership history. New dog owners reported significant positive changes in walking activity compared with non-dog owners without a past ownership history (coefficient=-4.83, SE = 2.44, P value = 0.048). New dog owners perceived significant positive changes in the frequency of non-face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors as compared with non-dog owners with (Coefficient=-0.31, SE = 0.14, P value = 0.022) and without a past ownership history (Coefficient=-0.33, SE = 0.14, P value = 0.018). This study of new dog owners will help elucidate the mechanisms through which increased physical activity and social contact may contribute to a positive association between dog ownership and health outcomes.

Introduction

Accumulating evidence on human–animal interactions indicates that dog ownership has protective effects against adverse health outcomes1–8. For example, dog walking was related to the maintenance of several cognitive functions in general community-residing older adults9. We previously reported that older dog owners with an exercise habit had lower risk of developing dementia whereas those without an exercise habit did not have lower risk10, and that older dog owners without social isolation had lower risk of developing dementia whereas those with social isolation did not have lower risk10. Several studies have investigated the relationship between dog ownership with exercise11–15 and social interaction such as direct contact13,16,17. However, while these studies suggest a longitudinal association between dog ownership and physical activity, it is unclear whether the dog owners in these studies had past ownership experience, and the effect of past ownership experience on physical activity remains unknown. Individuals with higher exercise habits due to past dog ownership might be more likely to own a dog again. Additionally, it has not been clarified whether dog ownership leads to changes in social contact. It is necessary to examine the changes in physical activity and social contact in new dog owners who have never owned a dog before and compare them to a control group. Accordingly, we considered that if new dog ownership without past ownership experience demonstrated a relationship between ownership and increased physical activity and social contact, this could help elucidate the mechanism underlying dog ownership and health outcomes1–6,8–21.

Here, using survey data of new dog owners without past ownership experience and non-dog owners collected by the Japan Pet Food Association, we analyzed changes in physical activity levels and the frequency of social contact. The aim of this study was to determine whether new dog ownership without past ownership experience leads to increased physical activity and social contact compared to non-dog ownership.

Results

Characteristics

This study enrolled 1,210 participants in a survey conducted by the Japan Pet Food Association in 2024. Mean (SD) age was 50.7 (16.5) years and 55.7% were women. Median income was 4 million − 6 million yen. 47.9% owned a house, 68.9% were living alone, and 59.0% were married. Mean (SD) Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) score was 2.5 (1.5) and the World Health Organization Five (WHO-5) Well-Being Index score was 50.5 (22.8). Among the 1,210 participants, 81 were new dog owners, 614 were non-dog owners with a past dog ownership history, and 515 were non-dog owners without a past ownership history (Table 1). With regard to the size of the 81 newly owned dogs, 59.3% were toy-sized, 30.9% were small, and 9.9% were middle-sized or large dogs. Average (SD) weight was 8.2 (18.4) kg and average age was 2.1 (3.0) years.

Changes in physical activity and social contact

Mean values (SD) of physical activity and social contact one year ago and the present are compared in Figs. 1 and 2. Among new dog owners, vigorous physical activity increased from 23.7 (32.8) Metabolic equivalents (METs)-hours/week to 24.3 (26.2) METs-hours/week (the median values were 16.0 and 13.3). The corresponding values decreased from 28.3 (46.4) to 26.8 (39.6) among non-dog owners with a past ownership history (the median values were 13.3 and 12.0) and from 24.6 (34.1) to 22.4 (29.5) among non-dog owners without a past ownership history (the median values were 10.7 and 8.0). Moderate physical activity among new dog owners increased from 11.5 (13.3) METs-hours/week one year ago to 12.7 (14.0) METs-hours/week at present (the median values were 5.3 and 8.0), versus respective decreases in value from 17.1 (28.6) to 15.1 (21.2) among non-dog owners with a past ownership history (the median values were 8.0 and 7.9) and from 16.2 (22.9) to 16.5 (27.2) among those without a past ownership history (the median values were 6.0 and 5.3). Walking activity increased 14.8 (15.5) METs-hours/week to 18.0 (15.4) METs-hours/week among new dog owners (the median values were 11.5 and 14.6). Respective values were 28.0 (34.8) and 27.6 (36.1) among non-dog owners with past ownership history (the median values were 16.5 and 16.5) and 28.9 (40.4) and 27.7 (38.4) among non-dog owners without past ownership history (the median values were 16.5 and 13.2) (Fig. 1). The frequency of face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors increased from 2.2 (1.7) points to 2.5 (1.8) points among new dog owners. Respective values were 1.8 (1.7) and 1.8 (1.7) among non-dog owners with past ownership history and 1.6 (1.6) and 1.7 (1.6) among those without past ownership history. The frequency of non-face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors increased from 2.2 (1.8) points to 2.6 (2.2) points among new dog owners, versus 1.9 (1.9) and 2.0 (2.0) among non-dog owners with past ownership history and 1.8 (1.6) and 1.8 (2.0) among non-dog owners without past ownership history, respectively (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The main finding of this retrospective study was that new dog owners without a past dog ownership history reported positive 1-year changes in physical activity, i.e., walking activity and in social contact, i.e., non-face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors after controlling for demographic characteristics. This retrospective study suggests a relationship between new dog ownership and improvements in both physical activity levels and social contact.

Previous studies have reported positive associations between dog ownership and physical activity7,11–13,22−24 and social contact13,16,17. Christian et al. reviewed 29 studies and reported that dog owners engage in more walking and physical activity than non-dog owners7,14,15. Gee et al. reviewed 145 studies and reported positive effects of animal-assisted intervention on social behaviors or social interactions and that dog walking may provide positive social outcomes that result from engaging in the community25. Our present study demonstrated that new dog owners reported positive changes in physical activity and social contact compared to non-owners. Life with a dog requires walking activity such as daily dog walking and longer dog ownership is associated with higher physical activity26. Based on our present results, we suggest that as new dog owners spend more time with their dogs, they acquire an exercise habit and increase their walking activity. In contrast, new dog ownership was not associated with significant change in vigorous or moderate physical activity. The finding that more than 90% of new dog owners in this study had small dogs may have affect non-significant changes in vigorous and moderate physical activity. Future research should examine the impact of new ownership of large dogs on vigorous and moderate physical activity. Of interest, new dog owners showed a positive effect regarding social contact compared to non-dog owners. Reasons for this significant effect on non-face-to-face contact might include communication through phone calls, text messages, or social media with friends and neighbors who they meet through their dogs. Although no significant changes were observed in face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors, dog owners tended to have positive changes. Mean values of social contact either remained the same or slightly increased between one year ago and the present even in non-dog owners, which likely contributed to the non-significant changes in face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors among new dog owners. Further study should examine whether non-face-to-face contact has the same positive effects on psychological and physical health as face-to-face contact. When comparing changes in physical activity and social contact between non-dog owners with and without past ownership history, similar changes were observed in both groups. We confirmed that previous experience of dog ownership alone did not lead to increased physical activity or social contact.

This study has several strengths. First, we collected data from new dog owners who had never owned a dog before. Their participation in the survey was incidental and they were not selected intentionally to start dog ownership. This enabled us to examine the physical and social effects of dog ownership in a population with no prior experience of dog ownership. Next, the study was able to compare changes in social contact, in addition to physical activity, between new dog owners and non-dog owners. Although previous studies have reported changes in physical activity14,15, this study demonstrated that dog ownership has not only physical but also social effects.

Several limitations of the study also warrant mention. First, the survey asked about physical activity and social contact one year ago, raising the possibility of recall bias. Because this bias affected both new dog owners and non-dog owners, it is unlikely to have disturbed the results. In future, longitudinal studies should examine changes in physical activity, social contact, and the initiation of dog ownership over time. We also suspect that expectation bias disproportionately affected new dog owners, and the design of this retrospective and observational study does not allow us to examine whether this bias occurred. Second, the data were collected through an internet survey by the Japan Pet Food Association, and a degree of selection bias related to participant ability to respond to an internet survey may have been present. Further, the survey targeted individuals aged 20 to 79 years, and a causal relationship of new dog ownership with physical activity and social contact in subjects aged under 20 and over 80 is unclear. Third, although we included several demographic characteristics in this study, we did not examine other variables, such as chronic disease. Previous studies have reported that dog ownership was associated with lower risk of cardiovascular events20,21. Therefore, it is necessary to confirm whether our findings are also seen in dog owners with several medical histories. Fourth, the association of new dog ownership with physical activity and social contact might differ depending on dog size. In this study, over 90% of dogs were toy-sized or small dogs, and middle-large new dog owners had significantly higher moderate physical activity (mean METs-hours/week of 8.6 for toy-sized new dog owners, 12.5 for small new dog owners, and 28.4 middle-large new dog owners; P value from analysis of variance < 0.001) and walking activity (18.7, 15.3 and 27.1 METs-hours/week, respectively; P value 0.011). Future research of these associations in populations with a higher proportion of middle-large dog owners may improve the clarity of these results. Finally, because this study was an observational study rather than a randomized controlled trial, baseline characteristics were not uniform between new dog owners and non-dog owners with and without a past ownership history. Therefore, we adjusted for heterogeneity in characteristics between groups by including several demographic variables as controls in our statistical models.

In summary, we found that new dog owners without past dog ownership reported positive changes in walking activity and non-face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors compared with non-dog owners with and without past ownership history in the preceding year, after controlling for demographic characteristics. This study suggests a relationship between new dog ownership and improvements in both physical activity levels and social contact. This study of new dog owners will help to consider the mechanisms through which increased physical activity level and social contact may link dog ownership to health outcomes.

Methods

Participants

We used data collected in a survey conducted by the Japan Pet Food Association in 2024. Briefly, the Japan Pet Food Association has conducted annual internet surveys since 2004 to determine the number of animals in each household and the conditions of their living environment. In October 2024, the Association commissioned the internet survey to a research company. This company surveyed individuals aged 20 to 79 years who were sampled from all over Japan by place of residence and had confirmed their willingness to participate. The survey collected data on each participant’s dog ownership status and dog ownership history. In this study, we identified not only new dog owners and non-dog owners without a past ownership history as current exposure but also non-dog owners with a past dog ownership history as past exposure. A total of 1,210 participants were identified: 81 new dog owners who are current dog owners, had never previously owned a dog before, and started owning a dog within the past year, 614 non-dog owners with a past dog ownership history, and 515 non-dog owners without a past ownership history. Informed consent was obtained via the website from all participants before the survey was conducted, and all protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute for Environmental Studies (No.2024-017). The study was conducted in strict adherence with the Declaration of Helsinki. The authors were unable to access information that could identify individual participants during or after data collection.

Definition of physical activity and social contact

We assessed vigorous physical activity, moderate physical activity, and walking activity using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) Short-Form27. The IPAQ was initially developed to facilitate surveillance of physical activity based on a global standard, and has since become the most widely used physical activity questionnaire. Vigorous physical activity, moderate physical activity, and walking activity are shown as METs-hours/week. Social contact was assessed by determining the frequencies of face-to-face and non–face-to-face contact with friends and neighbors13. Participants selected responses from the following options: nothing, 1 time or fewer times a month, 1 time a month, 2–3 times a month, 1 time a week, 2–3 times a week, 4–5 times a week, or 6–7 times a week. We scored this answer on a scale of 0–7, with higher scores indicating a higher frequency of social contact. This retrospective study enquired about physical activity and social contact one year ago and at present.

Other measurements

The covariates included present demographic characteristics such as sex, age, household income, region, living arrangement, number of family members living together, occupation, marital status, Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)28, and World Health Organization Five (WHO-5) Well-Being Index29. The included regions in Japan were Hokkaido, Tohoku, Kanto, Keihin, Hokuriku, Tokai, Keihanshin, Chugoku, Shikoku, and Kyushu. Living arrangement was categorized as own house, own apartment, rented house, rented apartment, company housing, and others. The number of family members was categorized as 0,1,2,3, or 4 or more. Occupation was categorized as company employee, manager, government employee, self-employed, professional, temporary, part-time worker, high school student, university student, housewife, unemployed, or other. The GDS is a screening tool for depression in community-dwelling older populations and shows clinical utility with young and middle-aged adults. The WHO-5 is designed to measure psychological health. Scores range from 0 to 100, with lower scores indicating lower positive mood and vitality.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance and the chi-square test were used for comparison of demographic and health characteristics between new dog owners, non-dog owners with a past ownership history, and non-dog owners without a past ownership history. Mean values of physical activity and social contact were compared at one year ago and the present among new dog owners, non-dog owners with a past ownership history, and non-dog owners without a past ownership history. Changes in physical activity and social contact at two time points (one year ago and the present) between new dog owners and non-dog owners with and without past ownership history were examined by a linear mixed model that uses time as a mixed effect. Potential confounders were selected by all authors based on a priori theoretical reasonings and evaluated for collinearity: sex, age, household income, region, living arrangement, number of family members living together, occupation, marital status, GDS, and WHO-5 Well-Being Index. This study collected data on dog’s age, size (toy size, small, medium to large), weight and breed, however, due to the small number of new dog owners, results of the stratified analysis on dog size were only used in discussion. Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata SE (version 18; Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA), with P values of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.
