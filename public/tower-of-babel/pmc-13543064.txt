Piezo1-mediated Mechanotransduction in the musculoskeletal system: Signaling networks and therapeutic perspectives
Authors: Hong Zhao, Junyan Huang, Yayi Tan, Shiying Shen, Zehao Ying, Zhengyan Li, Yizhe Xiong, Haiya Ge, Xiang Wang, Hongsheng Zhan, Ying Shen, Guoqing Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanical forces are integral to musculoskeletal homeostasis and pathology. Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive ion channel widely expressed in bone, cartilage, muscle, and tendon. It converts mechanical stimuli (tension, compression, shear) into Ca2+ influx and downstream signals, regulating proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, inflammation, and apoptosis. This review summarizes Piezo1's structure, gating mechanisms, tissue expression, and functions in the musculoskeletal system, focusing on cartilage degeneration, subchondral bone remodeling, skeletal remodeling, muscle maintenance, and tendon adaptation. Under physiological conditions, Piezo1 contributes to tissue homeostasis; however, under abnormal or sustained loads, excessive Piezo1 activation can disrupt Ca2+ balance, amplify inflammation, promote apoptosis, and contribute to pathological remodeling in osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, muscle injury, and tendon degeneration. Preclinical strategies, including chemical agonists/inhibitors, traditional Chinese medicine components, gene-based approaches, and stem-cell-related interventions, have been explored in cell and animal models. Piezo1 is an important mechanosensitive regulator linking mechanical cues to musculoskeletal biology, although its tissue-specific roles, interactions with other mechanosensitive channels, and clinical translational value require further study.Keywords: Piezo1, Musculoskeletal system, Mechanotransduction, Calcium signaling, Osteoarthritis, Therapeutic strategies

Background

The musculoskeletal system, comprising muscles, bones, and connective tissues, relies on mechanical forces to maintain structure and function. Mechanical forces help maintain normal musculoskeletal structure and function. They also regulate cellular signaling, metabolism, development, repair, and regeneration (Polet and Labonte, 2024). This perception and transduction of mechanical signals contributes to musculoskeletal homeostasis and disease-related responses (Balberova et al., 2021; Ti et al., 2025).

The process of mechanotransduction requires a series of specific proteins known as mechanical transducers to convert physical mechanical force signals into biochemical signals. As the primary pathways of mechanotransduction, mechanically activated ion channels can directly receive mechanical stimuli applied to the cell membrane, enabling rapid responses to membrane excitation and intracellular signaling activation (Arnadóttir and Chalfie, 2010; Martinac and Poole, 2018). By sensing mechanical stimuli (such as membrane tension or extracellular forces), they mediate the inflow or outflow of intracellular ions, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways to regulate cellular function (Liu et al., 2025a).

The family of mechanically sensitive channels includes Piezo channels, transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, and two-pore-domain potassium channels (K2P channels). Among these, Piezo channels have become a major focus of research in recent years due to their unique structural characteristics and highly efficient mechanical responsiveness (Fang et al., 2021; Cheng et al., 2025).

Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel of the Piezo family, is highly expressed in musculoskeletal tissues. It responds rapidly to mechanical stimuli such as tension, compression, and shear and influences gene expression, cell fate, and tissue remodeling through intracellular Ca2+ signaling (Savadipour et al., 2023a).

Abnormal mechanical forces contribute to musculoskeletal disorders, such as osteoarthritis (OA) and muscle atrophy, which are associated with disruptions in mechanical signal transduction. As a mechanosensor, Piezo1 detects mechanical stimuli and also regulates pathological processes, including inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and metabolic reprogramming. Piezo1 links mechanical stimuli to downstream inflammatory, apoptotic, and metabolic responses (Liu et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2025).

Accordingly, this review examines Piezo1-mediated mechanotransduction in the musculoskeletal system, with particular attention to downstream signaling, tissue-specific functions, and preclinical therapeutic strategies.

Overview of Piezo1

Structural and gating mechanisms of the Piezo1 channel

The gating mechanism of the Piezo1 mechanosensitive channel underpins its function, and this mechanical gating mechanism provides the molecular basis for the conversion of mechanical stimuli into electrochemical signals. It primarily involves conformational changes in the channel under mechanical force and the regulation of ion permeability. Piezo1 is embedded in the cell membrane as a trimer, with each subunit forming a ‘blade’-like structure that curves into the membrane, giving the channel an arched conformation at rest. When external mechanical stimuli (such as increased membrane tension or cellular compression) act upon the cell membrane, the ‘blade’ structure of Piezo1 gradually unfolds from its curved state, inducing the opening of the central ion channel. This allows rapid influx of cations such as Ca2+ and converts mechanical stimuli into electrochemical signals (Liu et al., 2025b; Ullah et al., 2024).

Piezo1 possesses multiple ion channel gates; the plug domain works in concert with mechanical stimulation to regulate the opening and closing of ion channels via a mechanically triggered ‘insertion locking’ mechanism, thereby ensuring strict gating of the channels (Geng et al., 2020).

This mechanism not only ensures the channel's mechanical sensitivity, but also enables Piezo1 to respond to mechanical stimuli of varying intensities within complex cellular mechanical environments.

In addition to mechanical activation, the rapid inactivation properties of Piezo1 play a significant role in preventing excessive channel opening and maintaining cellular Ca2+ homeostasis. The inactivation of Piezo1 is primarily regulated by specific domains within the channel, such as the extracellular cap domain, whose structural flexibility is key to both mechanical activation and rapid inactivation (Lewis and Grandl, 2020; Zhou and Martinac, 2023).

The inactivation of Piezo1 is also influenced by the intracellular membrane lipid environment and cholesterol levels; for example, neutral sphingolipase-active ceramides can prevent channel inactivation and keep the channels open in order to adapt to changes in shear stress within endothelial cells (Shi et al., 2020).

The gating mechanism of Piezo1 also involves the sensing of membrane tension and the role of the cytoskeleton. In the ‘force-from-lipid model’ of force transduction, membrane tension directly regulates the channel conformation; whereas in the ‘force-from-filament model’, force sensing and transmission are achieved through interactions between the channel and the cytoskeleton and adhesion molecules (such as the E-cadherin-β-catenin complex) (Wang et al., 2022a; Vasileva and Chubinskiy-Nadezhdin, 2023).

The ‘blade’-like structure of Piezo1 is capable of responding to deformations caused by membrane tension, while its integration with the cytoskeleton ensures effective sensing of and response to both local and distant mechanical forces (Dumitru et al., 2021).

Together, these structural features allow Piezo1 to respond to diverse mechanical inputs (Hendrickx et al., 2021; Xie and Hang, 2024). The Piezo1 channel opens in response to mechanical forces by inducing structural conformational changes. Under the coordinated regulation of an inactivation mechanism mediated by specific structural domains, the membrane lipid environment, and the cytoskeleton, it forms an efficient and precise mechanosensitive system. Owing to these properties, Piezo1 exhibits high mechanical sensitivity and participates in multiple cellular functions across different tissues. This has made Piezo1 an important focus in mechanotransduction research (Lin et al., 2022; Shan et al., 2025).

Expression and distribution of Piezo1 in the musculoskeletal system

Piezo1 expression has been reported in skeletal muscle cells, tendon fibroblasts, chondrocytes, osteoblast-lineage cells, and subchondral bone-associated tissues (Du et al., 2024; Mirzoev, 2023; Götschi et al., 2023; Dienes et al., 2023; Brylka et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026; Wu et al., 2022). Its distribution enables these cell types to respond to tissue-specific mechanical environments. Expression of Piezo1 in skeletal muscle and tendons

Skeletal muscle generates mechanical force, while tendons transmit muscle-generated forces to bone. Piezo1 has been reported in skeletal muscle cells and tendon tissue (Mirzoev, 2023; Dienes et al., 2023; Nakamichi et al., 2022).

Expression of Piezo1 in chondrocytes

Articular cartilage plays a vital role in the musculoskeletal system by cushioning and distributing mechanical loads, and Piezo1 contributes to the detection of compressive and shear forces in chondrocytes. Chondrocyte-specific inactivation of Piezo1 impairs postnatal trabecular bone formation and can cause skeletal fragility and fractures. In contrast, the same genetic model attenuated several OA-related changes after ACLT, indicating context-dependent roles in skeletal development and OA (Brylka et al., 2024).

During the progression of osteoarthritis, the expression of Piezo1 increases significantly, and it plays a role in the process of pathological cartilage degeneration. In osteoarthritic lesions, abnormal or persistent mechanical stress and inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β) can induce a significant increase in Piezo1 expression, leading to excessive channel activation. This, in turn, influences chondrocyte autophagy and apoptosis via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, promoting increased chondrocyte apoptosis and inhibiting autophagy, which further aggravates joint degeneration (Brylka et al., 2024; Han et al., 2025). Overexpression of Piezo1 leads to increased intracellular calcium ion concentrations, promoting cytoskeletal reorganization, cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, and inhibiting cell proliferation (Sun et al., 2020).

Exercise-related changes in Piezo1 expression have also been reported in articular cartilage (Jing et al., 2024). However, abnormal or excessive mechanical stress triggers cell apoptosis and matrix degradation through Piezo1 activation, thereby exacerbating cartilage damage.

Expression of Piezo1 in subchondral bone and other related tissues

Subchondral bone bears and transmits mechanical loads within the joint, and changes in its mechanical state can greatly influence the biological behavior of the overlying cartilage. Piezo1 expression in subchondral bone-associated cells has been linked to bone remodeling and mechanical adaptation (Du et al., 2024). Research has shown that in osteoarthritis models, the expression of FoxC1 and Piezo1 is elevated. FoxC1 activates Piezo1/YAP signaling in subchondral osteoblasts and increases osteogenic markers, including ALP, COL1A1, RUNX2, and osteocalcin. The same study also reported pro-inflammatory changes, subchondral sclerosis, and microvascularization (Li et al., 2024).

Recent research has also implicated osteoblast Piezo1 in pathological mechanotransduction under mechanical overload. In a knee osteoarthritis (KOA) model, mechanical overload activated Piezo1 in osteoblasts, promoted Ca2+ influx, and drove JAK2/STAT3 signaling (Li et al., 2026).

Piezo1 is also involved in TMJOA-related cartilage and subchondral bone damage. In a rat model of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, upregulation of Piezo1 was closely associated with cartilage and subchondral bone damage; the Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 clearly alleviated tissue damage and reduced pSmad3 expression, suggesting that Piezo1 plays a crucial regulatory role in the pathological remodeling of subchondral bone (Wu et al., 2022).

Activation mechanism and downstream signaling pathways of Piezo1

Within the musculoskeletal system, mechanical stimulation is a key factor in maintaining tissue homeostasis. Mechanical stimulation activates Piezo1-dependent Ca2+ entry and downstream signaling.

Mechanical stimulation-induced Ca2+ influx

Piezo1 is a mechanosensitive cation channel that is highly sensitive to changes in mechanical forces (such as shear stress, tensile stress and pressure). Mechanical stimulation activates Piezo1 and induces Ca2+ influx (Wu et al., 2017). The increase in intracellular calcium subsequently triggers several downstream pathways involved in proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and inflammation (Thien et al., 2024) (Fig. 2).

Several studies have shown that Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx exhibits distinct spatiotemporal dynamics that influence the intensity and duration of cellular responses to mechanical signals. For example, in adipose-derived stem cells, pressure stimulation activated a Piezo1/CaMKII/β-catenin pathway and promoted osteogenic differentiation (Tan et al., 2025). When mechanical stimuli remain within physiological ranges, Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ signaling can support tissue homeostasis; under abnormal or sustained overload, excessive Piezo1 activation can produce dysregulated Ca2+ signaling and initiate downstream pathological responses.

Ca2+/CaMKII signaling in musculoskeletal Mechanotransduction

Piezo1 dysfunction can alter mechanochemical signaling across skeletal muscle, cartilage, and subchondral bone, contributing to musculoskeletal dysfunction (Lei et al., 2024).

The Piezo1 protein is involved in mechanical transduction in chondrocytes. High-strain mechanical stimulation activates Piezo1 channels, leading to increased Ca2+ influx, which promotes the onset and progression of osteoarthritis; inhibiting Piezo1 activity may have a protective effect on chondrocytes (He et al., 2024).

Piezo1 also contributes to skeletal muscle mechanobiology. When the muscle cell membrane is damaged, Piezo1 becomes overactivated, leading to pathological excessive Ca2+ influx, which in turn triggers inflammatory responses and fibrosis (Lei et al., 2024).

Prolonged mechanical loading can increase intracellular Ca2+ and activate CaMKII. Ca2+ binds to calmodulin, which activates CaMKII and promotes autophosphorylation at Thr286. In adipose-derived stem cells, pressure stimulation promoted osteogenic differentiation through a Piezo1/CaMKII/β-catenin pathway (Tan et al., 2025). However, current evidence does not establish Piezo1-mediated CaMKII activation as a common mechanism across cartilage, subchondral bone, and skeletal muscle.

Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ signaling is not confined to intracellular events and can also regulate intercellular communication. In osteocytes, Piezo1 activation increases intracellular Ca2+ and promotes the opening of connexin 43 (Cx43) hemichannels through PI3K signaling, thereby supporting anabolic responses to mechanical loading (Zeng et al., 2022).

In cartilaginous endplate cells, Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx activates the CaMKII/Drp1 pathway, promotes mitochondrial fission, and contributes to inflammation-induced cartilaginous endplate degeneration (Lin et al., 2025).

Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ signaling can engage CaMKII in selected musculoskeletal models and influence downstream differentiation or stress responses (Tan et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025).

Potential convergence between CaMKII and HDAC4 signaling

HDAC4 is an important regulator of chondrocyte differentiation and skeletal development, and chondrocyte-specific HDAC4 deletion causes abnormal hypertrophy and premature ossification (Du et al., 2020). Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx can activate CaMKII in some musculoskeletal models (Tan et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025), raising the possibility that CaMKII-related signaling may converge with HDAC4-dependent transcriptional regulation. However, the complete Piezo1-Ca2+-CaMKII-HDAC4 sequence has not been directly demonstrated in a single musculoskeletal model.

Abnormal mechanical loading alters Piezo1-dependent Ca2+ signaling in chondrocytes, while HDAC4 independently regulates chondrocyte differentiation and OA-related gene expression. These observations provide a rationale for considering potential signaling convergence, but they do not establish a direct Piezo1/CaMKII/HDAC4 sequence in OA (Du et al., 2020; Savadipour et al., 2023b).

Independent evidence supports an important role for HDAC4 in OA cartilage. In human OA cartilage and an ACLT rat model, reduced HDAC4 was associated with ER stress and chondrocyte apoptosis, whereas HDAC4 overexpression suppressed ATF4/CHOP signaling and attenuated cartilage degeneration (Gu et al., 2024). In a DMM mouse model, HBP-A treatment restored HDAC4 expression in articular cartilage and quadriceps and attenuated cartilage degeneration and quadriceps atrophy (Wang et al., 2025a). These findings support HDAC4 as a relevant mechanosensitive regulator, while the direct link from Piezo1/CaMKII to HDAC4 remains to be tested (Fig. 3).

Other downstream effects and signal integration

Beyond its role as a mechanically sensitive ion channel, Piezo1 integrates multiple downstream signaling pathways involved in musculoskeletal mechanobiology. In addition to the Ca2+/CaMK axis, the pathological effects of Piezo1 in the musculoskeletal system depend on the integrated action of multiple parallel pathways. Piezo1 engages different downstream pathways across tissues and loading conditions (Savadipour et al., 2023a; Lei et al., 2024). Reported pathways include YAP/TAZ, MAPK, AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin, cGAS-STING, JAK2/STAT3, and TRPV4-related signaling.

YAP/TAZ are mechanically responsive transcriptional co-activators that participate in Piezo1-mediated mechanotransduction. In OA subchondral bone osteoblasts, FoxC1 and Piezo1 expression are elevated. FoxC1-driven Piezo1 activation, together with downstream YAP signaling, increases osteogenic markers and pro-inflammatory responses, whereas inhibition of Piezo1 reverses these effects (Li et al., 2024). Conditional knockout of FoxC1 or Piezo1 also reduces subchondral osteosclerosis and microangiogenesis in mice (Li et al., 2024).

The MAPK pathway, comprising ERK, p38 and JNK, is a group of signaling modules downstream of Piezo1 characterized by a ‘rapid response’. In mesenchymal stem cells, hydrostatic pressure and Yoda1 promoted BMP2 expression and osteogenic differentiation through Piezo1 (Sugimoto et al., 2017). ERK1/2 and p38 are also involved in chondrocyte hypertrophic responses to OA subchondral osteoblasts (Prasadam et al., 2010), but this study did not place them directly downstream of Piezo1.

In MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts, fluid shear stress increased Piezo1 and RUNX2 expression, and Piezo1 silencing impaired AKT/GSK-3β/β-catenin activation (Song et al., 2020). In ADSCs, pressure-induced osteogenic differentiation involved Piezo1/CaMKII/β-catenin signaling (Tan et al., 2025). Together, these studies link Piezo1 to β-catenin-dependent osteogenic responses under mechanical stimulation.

A recent study showed that mechanical stress increased Piezo1 expression and Ca2+ influx in chondrocytes, leading to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, mtDNA release, and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. Piezo1 conditional knockout reduced cGAS-STING activation and cartilage degradation in vivo (Sun et al., 2025). Separately, mechanical overloading induced GPX4-regulated chondrocyte ferroptosis through Piezo1-facilitated Ca2+ influx (Wang et al., 2022b).

Piezo1/JAK2/STAT3 signaling has also been identified in subchondral osteoblasts under mechanical overload. In a KOA model, abnormal loading activated Piezo1, increased Ca2+ influx, and promoted JAK2/STAT3 signaling, osteogenic differentiation, and subchondral bone sclerosis (Li et al., 2026).

Taken together, Piezo1 does not signal through a single downstream route. The pathway engaged depends on cell type and mechanical context, with reported involvement of CaMKII, YAP, MAPK, β-catenin, cGAS-STING, and JAK/STAT signaling.

Specific examples of Piezo1's functions in the musculoskeletal system

Cartilage and subchondral bone homeostasis and osteoarthritis

Within the musculoskeletal system, articular cartilage and the subchondral bone beneath it together form a key functional unit responsible for the transmission and cushioning of mechanical loads. Piezo1 acts as a mechanosensor in chondrocytes, directly regulating the balance between synthesis and degradation of the cartilage matrix to maintain the homeostasis of cartilage and subchondral bone. Numerous studies have demonstrated that Piezo1 is highly expressed in chondrocytes and subchondral bone tissue, and that its expression levels are closely associated with the onset and progression of osteoarthritis (OA). Human OA specimens showed increased Piezo1 protein abundance in articular cartilage, subchondral bone, trabecular bone, and cartilaginous regions of osteophytes (Brylka et al., 2024). In mice, chondrocyte-specific Piezo1 inactivation impaired postnatal trabecular bone formation but attenuated several OA-related changes, including cartilage degeneration and osteophyte formation (Brylka et al., 2024).

Recent transcriptomic and human genetic studies further illustrate the context dependence of PIEZO1 in cartilage. PIEZO1 activation produces a distinct chondrocyte transcriptional program, whereas reduced channel open probability has been associated with familial OA susceptibility (Nims et al., 2024; Jurynec et al., 2026).

In a knee OA model, mechanical stress upregulated Piezo1 and impaired mitochondrial autophagy. This was accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction, increased pro-apoptotic and inflammatory factors, cartilage matrix degradation, and chondrocyte apoptosis (Yu et al., 2025a). In OA models exposed to excessive mechanical strain, GsMTx4 reduced chondrocyte apoptosis and cartilage degeneration through Piezo1/CaN/NFAT1 signaling (Ren et al., 2023).

In addition to directly affecting chondrocytes, Piezo1 is also involved in the metabolic regulation of subchondral bone. Mechanical stress increased Piezo1-dependent glycolytic activity in MSCs and altered MSC-Th17 crosstalk. These changes were associated with inflammatory responses and osteochondral damage (Zhou et al., 2025).

Synovial studies extend Piezo1-related mechanobiology beyond cartilage and subchondral bone. Mechanical overload activates Piezo1-related NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling in fibroblast-like synoviocytes, while macrophage-specific Piezo1 deletion or targeted knockdown reduces inflammatory polarization and OA progression (Yu et al., 2025b; Yan et al., 2026).

Within cartilage, Piezo1 has been linked to PI3K/AKT/mTORC1, cGAS-STING, and CaN/NFAT1 signaling, which contribute to catabolic, inflammatory, and cell-death responses in OA (Han et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025; Ren et al., 2023).Chondrocyte-specific Piezo1 deletion attenuated DMM-induced OA, whereas intra-articular Yoda1 aggravated OA lesions. In primary human articular chondrocytes, Piezo1 activation increased RUNX2, MMP13, and ADAMTS5 through PI3K-AKT signaling (Gan et al., 2024).

A recent study further linked excessive mechanical activation of Piezo1 to Kdm5c-mediated epigenetic silencing in chondrocytes. Kdm5c loss or pharmacological inhibition restored cartilage-anabolic gene expression and attenuated cartilage degeneration in experimental OA models (Kan et al., 2026).

In summary, Piezo1 serves as a representative mechanically sensitive channel in chondrocytes that senses mechanical loading, maintains the balance between cartilage and subchondral bone, and, under abnormal mechanical loading, regulates Ca2+ and various downstream signaling pathways to mediate cartilage degeneration and bone destruction in OA (Fig. 4).

Regulatory role in skeletal remodeling

Bone tissue is a dynamic tissue that relies heavily on mechanical stimuli to maintain its structure and function. Piezo1 modulates the response of osteocytes to mechanical stress, thereby maintaining the dynamic balance between bone formation and resorption, and influencing bone density and mechanical properties. Studies on periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) have revealed that, under compressive forces, Piezo1 expression is significantly upregulated, activating the β-catenin signaling pathway and promoting alveolar bone remodeling. Under compressive force, Piezo1 and activated β-catenin increased in periodontal ligament cells. Piezo1 inhibition reduced β-catenin activation as well as osteogenic and osteoclastic activities. In vivo, Piezo1 inhibition decreased orthodontic tooth movement and reduced osteogenic and osteoclastic markers on the compression side (Jiang et al., 2024).

Piezo1 also plays a role in osteoporosis. Osteoblast-lineage Piezo1 deficiency caused reduced bone mass, spontaneous fractures, and increased bone resorption in mice. These mice were also resistant to additional bone loss induced by mechanical unloading (Wang et al., 2020). Further studies have shown that treatment combining the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 with mechanical loading effectively slows age-related bone loss and enhances bone structural integrity, supporting a role for Piezo1 in skeletal mechanosensing and bone remodeling (Wasi et al., 2024).

Recent bone studies further show that Piezo1 controls osteocyte maturation and dendrite development through YAP-CCN1/2-Src signaling. In BMMSCs, Piezo1 activation suppresses marrow adipogenesis through a CaMKII-KLF2-dependent anti-inflammatory mechanism (Hu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b).

Piezo1 has also been linked to skeletal repair. In fracture models, Piezo1/β-catenin/LARS2 signaling supported chondrocyte mitochondrial function and endochondral ossification, while a SIRT1-Piezo1 axis promoted bone formation and repair in mice (Zhang et al., 2025; Gan et al., 2025).

Maintenance of muscle structure and function

As the primary mechanical effector organ, skeletal muscle relies heavily on precise perception of mechanical loads for its structure and function. To respond to stimuli such as stretch and contraction, muscle cells convert mechanical forces into intracellular signals that support growth and repair.

Piezo1 is expressed in skeletal muscle, and skeletal muscle-specific overexpression of Piezo1 attenuated immobilization-induced muscle loss in mice (Inoue et al., 2025). Piezo1 also contributes to muscle stem-cell homeostasis: Piezo1 deficiency causes premature activation and p53/p21-dependent senescence of muscle stem cells (Peng et al., 2022), while genetic loss of Piezo1 impairs satellite-cell-mediated muscle regeneration (Hirano et al., 2023).

Together, these findings support a role for Piezo1 in skeletal muscle maintenance and regeneration through effects on muscle cells and muscle stem cells (Inoue et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2022; Hirano et al., 2023).

Mechanical adaptation and repair of tendons

Tendon cells express Piezo1 and respond to mechanical loading. In tendon-specific R2482H Piezo1 gain-of-function mice, tendons were more compliant and stored more elastic energy, while jumping and running performance were improved without detectable changes in muscle (Nakamichi et al., 2022). In humans, a PIEZO1 gain-of-function variant has been associated with increased tendon stiffness (Götschi et al., 2023).

Across musculoskeletal tissues, the effects of Piezo1 activation are strongly context dependent. Sustained or excessive Piezo1 activity in articular cartilage is generally associated with catabolic, inflammatory, and cell-death responses, whereas appropriate Piezo1 activity in bone, skeletal muscle, and tendon can support remodeling, tissue maintenance, or mechanical adaptation. These divergent effects are likely influenced by cell type, loading magnitude and duration, and the downstream signaling networks engaged. Accordingly, therapeutic modulation of Piezo1 requires consideration of both tissue context and mechanical environment (Brylka et al., 2024; Nakamichi et al., 2022; Gan et al., 2024; Wasi et al., 2024; Inoue et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2022; Hirano et al., 2023).

Therapeutic strategies for Western medicines based on the Piezo1 signaling pathway

Development and application of Piezo1 pathway-targeted drugs

Yoda1 is a widely used Piezo1 agonist, but its effects are tissue dependent. In osteocytic cells, Yoda1 increased intracellular Ca2+ and suppressed Sost expression through Akt signaling (Sasaki et al., 2020). In pre-osteoclasts, Piezo1 activation inhibited osteoclastogenesis through a Ca2+-independent PP2A-Akt/NFATc1 mechanism (Shindo et al., 2025). In contrast, intra-articular Yoda1 aggravated DMM-induced OA in cartilage (Gan et al., 2024). It should be noted that Piezo1 activation may have dual effects across different tissues and pathological stages; its dosage, duration of action, and tissue specificity require further clarification in future studies.

GsMTx4 is widely used experimentally to suppress mechanosensitive channel activity, including Piezo1. In OA models exposed to excessive mechanical strain, GsMTx4 reduced chondrocyte apoptosis and cartilage degeneration through Piezo1/CaN/NFAT1 signaling (Ren et al., 2023). GsMTx4 also reduced cartilage and subchondral bone damage in a rat TMJOA model (Wu et al., 2022). Evidence for Piezo1 modulators remains predominantly preclinical. In mature mice, Yoda1 alone or with mechanical loading reduced age-related and doxorubicin-associated bone deterioration (Wasi et al., 2024).

Ca2+-dependent HDAC4 regulation and related intervention evidence

Ca2+-dependent kinase signaling provides an important link between intracellular Ca2+ changes and HDAC4 regulation in musculoskeletal cells. In adult skeletal muscle fibers, repetitive stimulation increased nuclear Ca2+ and activated nuclear CaMKII, leading to HDAC4 nuclear export; this response was blocked by the CaMK inhibitor KN-62 (Liu et al., 2005). In growth plate chondrocytes, CaMKIV promoted HDAC4 phosphorylation and cytoplasmic relocation, whereas inhibition of CaMKIV blocked this process (Guan et al., 2012). These studies show that Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent kinase signaling can regulate HDAC4 localization in musculoskeletal tissues, although the dominant CaMK isoform differs among cell types.

HDAC4 is also directly involved in musculoskeletal disease progression. In human OA cartilage, HDAC4 expression was lower in severely degraded regions than in relatively preserved cartilage. In an ACLT rat model, intra-articular HDAC4 overexpression reduced ATF4/CHOP signaling, chondrocyte apoptosis, and cartilage degeneration (Gu et al., 2024). In skeletal muscle, class IIa HDAC inhibition reduced denervation-associated loss of myosin heavy chain and affected PGC-1α- and Hsc70-related pathways (Luo et al., 2019). More recently, HDAC4 inhibition and combined aerobic and resistance exercise were shown to attenuate dexamethasone-induced muscle atrophy through HDAC4/FoxO3a-related regulation (Liang et al., 2025).

Together, these findings support Ca2+-dependent HDAC4 regulation as an important component of musculoskeletal mechanotransduction and identify HDAC4 as a context-dependent intervention target. Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx may converge on this regulatory network in selected musculoskeletal settings, but the complete Piezo1/Ca2+/CaMKII/HDAC4 sequence has not yet been directly validated as a therapeutic pathway in a single musculoskeletal model.

Mechanisms of action of traditional Chinese medicine in regulating the Piezo1 and Ca2+/CaMKII-HDAC4 signaling pathways

Regulatory effects of active components in traditional Chinese medicine on the Piezo1 pathway

TCM-derived interventions have recently been investigated for their effects on Piezo1-related signaling in musculoskeletal models. Shengyu Decoction contains multiple bioactive components. Four compounds identified by UPLC-Q-TOF-MS were shown to interact with Piezo1 by molecular docking and surface plasmon resonance. In rat KOA and chondrocyte models, Shengyu Decoction reduced cartilage injury and suppressed Piezo1-related ER stress signaling (Song et al., 2025). A recent rat study also found that Gubi Zhitong Formula attenuated mechanically induced KOA and modulated Piezo1-mediated Ca2+/PI3K-AKT/NF-κB signaling (Zhou et al., 2026).

Xueshuantong inhibits shear stress-mediated platelet aggregation and Ca2+ influx through Piezo1-related calpain-2/talin-1 signaling (Liu et al., 2021). This study provides direct evidence in platelets, but not in musculoskeletal tissue. The RRP complex has also been studied in low-shear-stress vascular endothelial models, where Piezo1 was identified as a possible target (Zhang et al., 2020); this evidence should be presented as cardiovascular rather than musculoskeletal.

Although existing studies are primarily based on cellular and animal models, and the exact binding sites have not yet been fully elucidated, the aforementioned results provide important experimental evidence for the targeting of the Piezo1 pathway by traditional Chinese medicine.

Ca2+/CaMKII/HDAC4-related effects and evidence levels

Fuzi Decoction suppressed osteoclast differentiation and bone loss mainly through NF-κB-related signaling in an osteoporosis model (Li et al., 2023). More directly within a musculoskeletal model, HBP-A attenuated cartilage degeneration and quadriceps atrophy in DMM-induced KOA mice while suppressing MLK3/P38 MAPK signaling and restoring HDAC4 expression in both tissues (Wang et al., 2025a). Outside the musculoskeletal system, Panax quinquefolium saponin inhibited CaMKII/HDAC4-related signaling in a simulated-microgravity cardiac model (Sun et al., 2019).

Current TCM evidence falls into different levels. Shengyu Decoction provides direct musculoskeletal evidence linking a TCM formula to Piezo1 in KOA (Song et al., 2025), and HBP-A provides direct musculoskeletal evidence involving HDAC4-related signaling in cartilage and quadriceps (Wang et al., 2025a). Xueshuantong and the RRP complex provide supportive evidence from platelet or vascular models (Liu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020), while Panax quinquefolium saponin provides indirect cardiac evidence involving CaMKII/HDAC4 (Sun et al., 2019).

Advanced biotechnologies targeting Piezo1-related Mechanotransduction

Conditional knockout and other genetic models have been used to investigate Piezo1 function in musculoskeletal tissues. Piezo1 deletion in fibrocartilage stem cells impaired condylar development and homeostasis, while attenuating pathological changes in a TMJOA model (Wang et al., 2025c). These genetic models clarify tissue-specific Piezo1 functions but do not constitute therapeutic gene-editing studies.

Piezo1 regulates the responses of several musculoskeletal stem and progenitor cell populations to mechanical cues. In periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) increased Piezo1 expression and promoted endothelial differentiation and tube formation. GsMTx4 attenuated these effects (Hu et al., 2022). Separately, LIPUS has also been shown to promote osteogenic differentiation of human periodontal ligament cells through integrin β1-related signaling (Hu et al., 2014), although Piezo1 was not examined in that study. Piezo1 activation has also been reported to promote osteogenic and cementogenic differentiation of human periodontal ligament progenitor cells (Kono et al., 2025).

Direct evidence is also available in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). The Piezo1 agonist MCB-22-174 induced Ca2+ influx and promoted BMSC proliferation through ERK- and CaMKII-related signaling. It also enhanced osteogenic differentiation and attenuated disuse osteoporosis in vivo (Hao et al., 2024). In another study, micro/nano-topographic cues activated Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx in BMSCs and were associated with NFATc1/Wnt/β-catenin and YAP signaling during osteogenic differentiation (Wu et al., 2025). These findings indicate that Piezo1 can translate different mechanical or pharmacological inputs into osteogenic responses through distinct downstream pathways.

Recent studies extend these findings to disuse osteoporosis and lineage plasticity. Piezo1 activation improved BMSC mitochondrial biogenesis through AMPK/SIRT1/PGC-1α signaling, while Piezo1/YAP signaling regulated osteogenic-tenogenic plasticity in periosteal progenitor cells (Chen et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025d).

Piezo1 also has a direct role in muscle stem cells. Piezo1 deficiency causes premature activation and p53/p21-dependent senescence of muscle stem cells, thereby impairing their regenerative capacity (Peng et al., 2022). In a separate genetic study, Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx was required for normal satellite-cell function. MuSC-specific Piezo1 deletion delayed myofiber regeneration after injury and was accompanied by mitotic defects and impaired Piezo1-Rho signaling (Hirano et al., 2023). Together, these studies support Piezo1 as an important regulator of musculoskeletal stem-cell fate and regeneration, although most evidence remains mechanistic and preclinical.

Conclusion

Piezo1 is a major mechanosensor in bone, cartilage, skeletal muscle, and tendon and contributes to tissue-specific responses to mechanical loading. Its downstream effects involve several Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways; however, the complete Piezo1-Ca2+-CaMKII-HDAC4 sequence remains a proposed model rather than an established common axis across musculoskeletal tissues. Interactions with other mechanosensitive channels and preclinical interventions targeting Piezo1 warrant further investigation. TCM-derived interventions such as Shengyu Decoction have shown Piezo1-related effects in preclinical KOA models, whereas evidence from platelet, vascular, or cardiac models should be considered indirect for musculoskeletal application.

TCM-derived interventions add another preclinical dimension to Piezo1-related mechanobiology. Shengyu Decoction provides direct Piezo1-related evidence in KOA, while HBP-A provides direct HDAC4-related evidence in cartilage and quadriceps. Further work is needed to define tissue-specific mechanisms, safety, and translational value.
