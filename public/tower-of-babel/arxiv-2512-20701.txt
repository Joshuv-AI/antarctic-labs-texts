A new converse theorem for Borcherds products
Ingmar Metzler

Abstract
. We establish a new converse theorem for Borcherds products. Moreover, the injectivity of the Kudla–Millson theta lift is demonstrated in the O(n,2) case in greater generality than is currently available in the literature. Both results are derived under the assumption of a single hyperbolic split of the base lattice.



1.
Introduction

In 1998, Richard Borcherds was awarded the Fields Medal in recognition of his pioneering contributions
to the field of automorphic forms and mathematical physics
[
OR
]
.
He had introduced vertex operator algebras as well as a multiplicative lift of automorphic forms
and employed these tools to prove the Conway–Norton moonshine conjecture.
The constructed lift – nowadays referred to as
Borcherds lift
– maps
vector-valued elliptic modular forms to orthogonal automorphic forms with infinite product expansions.
These
Borcherds products
provide a diverse array of examples of orthogonal modular forms.

To be more explicit, let
(L,\qq)
be an even lattice of signature
({m^{+}},2)

and
\mathbb{D}
be the associated Grassmannian which carries a natural complex structure.
The natural arithmetic subgroup to consider is the
discriminant kernel

\Gamma(L)\leq\OG(L)

which is a finite index subgroup stabilising the discriminant group of
L
.
On the elliptic side, let
\mathcal{L}=L^{\prime}/L
and consider holomorphic modular functions

f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{L}]
of weight
k\in\mathbb{Z}/2
,
transforming with the dual Weil representation
\overline{\rho}_{L}
which may possess poles at the cusp.
This space is referred to as the space of

weakly holomorphic modular forms
and is denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{L^{-},k}^{!}
.

Such a form
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L^{-},k}^{!}
admits a Fourier expansion

f(\tau)=\sum_{\mu\in L^{\prime}/L}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}-\qq(\mu)}a(\mu,m)e^{2\pi i\tau m}\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}

where
\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}
denotes the standard basis vector of
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{L}]
being
1
in the
\mu
component and
0
elsewhere.
Assume
f
has integral principal part,
meaning that
a(\mu,m)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
m<0
.
Then the Borcherds lift associates to
f
a meromorphic modular form
\Psi(z,f)
on
\mathbb{D}

for
\Gamma(L)
with unitary multiplier system of finite order such that

a)

the weight of
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
is given by
a(0,0)/2
,

b)

the divisor of
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
equals

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\in L^{\prime}/L}\sum_{n>0}a(\mu,-n)Z(\mu,n),

where

Z(\mu,n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in L+\mu\\
\qq(\lambda)=n\end{subarray}}\{z\in\mathbb{D}\mid z\perp\lambda\}

is the
Heegner divisor
of discriminant
(\mu,n)
(cf. Definition
3.2
),

c)

the target form
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
has explicit infinite product expansions.

As previously stated,
this lift gives rise to a variety of new orthogonal modular forms and also to the monster denominator formula,
which is utilised to prove the moonshine conjecture.
In
[
Bor98
, Pr. 16.10]
, Borcherds himself poses the question whether his lift may be reversed.
That is, given a meromorphic modular form
F
for
\Gamma(L)
whose zeros and poles are supported on special divisors
Z(\mu,n)
,
is there a form
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L^{-},k}^{!}
such that
\Psi(z,f)
equals
F
up to a non-zero constant factor?
In this paper we provide the following answer (cf. Theorem
6.9
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
L
splits a hyperbolic plane and
{m^{+}}>3
.
Then every meromorphic modular form
F
with respect to
\Gamma(L)
whose divisor is a linear combination of special divisors
Z(\mu,n)
is (up to a non-zero constant factor) the Borcherds lift
\Psi(z,f)

of a weakly holomorphic modular form
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L^{-},1-{m^{+}}/2}^{!}
.

The case
{m^{+}}=3
is treated separately in Section
6
.
More specifically, the same result holds when the Witt rank of
L
equals
1
,
while a weaker version is established for Witt rank
2
.
The result stated above improves upon the previously strongest known result
[
Bru14
, Thm. 1.2]
,
which either required an additional scaled hyperbolic split or needed a transition to a sublattice of
L
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is intricate.
As a first step, we invoke a celebrated duality statement by Bruinier
[
Bru02
]

rendering the above converse statement equivalent to the injectivity of another theta lift with wide-ranging applications:
the
Kudla–Millson
lift.

The Kudla–Millson lift

In the 1980s, Kudla and Millson
[
KM86
]
introduced special Schwartz forms
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}
on the symmetric spaces attached
to the classical groups
\OG({m^{+}},{m^{-}})
,
\UG({m^{+}},{m^{-}})
, and
\Sp({m^{+}},{m^{-}})
,
taking values in closed differential forms.
Their principal objective was to investigate cohomology classes of special cycles by means of
a theta correspondence, generalising the celebrated work of Hirzebruch and Zagier
[
HZ76
]

on Hilbert modular surfaces.
More precisely, in the orthogonal case, Kudla and Millson symmetrised the Schwartz forms
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}

over a base lattice
L

and let the Weil representation of the symplectic group act on
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}
in order to obtain a kernel
(cf. Definition
5.3
)

{\Theta_{\textnormal{KM}}}(\tau,z),\quad\tau\in\mathbb{H},\quad z\in\mathbb{D}

in a symplectic and an orthogonal variable, which transforms automorphically in both variables.
These may then be employed as an integral kernel to shift automorphic objects from the symplectic to the orthogonal setting or vice versa.
Such an association is referred to as a
theta lift
.
The authors extended their work in
[
KM87
]
in great generality; for an introduction, see

[
KM90
]
and
[
BF04
]
.
The applications of the Kudla–Millson theta correspondence range from the study of the cohomology
of orthogonal and unitary Shimura varieties
[
KM86
]
and Arakelov theory of Shimura varieties
[
Kud04
]
over specific counting problems
[
EGS23
]
,
constructing mock modular forms and higher dimensional error functions
[
FK17
]
to proving a converse theorem for Borcherds products
[
Bru02
,
Bru14
]
.

In this paper, we consider the Kudla–Millson lift on the space of vector-valued elliptic modular forms associated to the Weil representation
{\rho}_{L}
of an even lattice
L
of signature
({m^{+}},2)
.
To be more explicit, let
k=\rk(L)/2
and
\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
denote the space of cusp forms
for the Weil representation
{\rho}_{L}
.
Then

{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{L,k}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1,1)}(Y_{L}),\quad f\mapsto\int_{\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}}\langle f,{\Theta_{\textnormal{KM}}}\rangle\Im^{k}\;\textnormal{d}\mu

defines a linear map to the space of square-integrable harmonic differential forms of Hodge type
(1{,}1)
on the variety
Y_{L}=\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
and is referred to as the
Kudla–Millson lift
.
Here
\mu
denotes the hyperbolic measure on the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
on which
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
acts via Möbius transforms.
The question of its injectivity already arose in
[
KM90
]

and may be used to compute the rational Picard number of the underlying Shimura variety
[
Ber+16
]
,
as well as to derive properties of cones generated by special cycles
[
BM19
]

[
Zuf22
]
.
However,
the primary application we have in mind is the converse Theorem
1.1
for Borcherds products.

There have been multiple results on the injectivity of the Kudla–Millson lift over the past two decades
presented in
[
Bru02
, Thm. 5.12]
,
[
BF10
, Cor. 4.11]
,
[
Bru14
, Thm. 5.3]
,
[
Zuf24
, Thm. 6.1]
, and
[
Ste23
, Cor. 7.8]
.
These advances are based on three fundamentally different methods.
While there has been a recent success in unifying the results above by Zuffetti and the author
[
MZ26
, Thm. 6.2]
by generalising Zuffetti’s method, this has not shed any new light on the converse theorem for Borcherds products.
The most potent result
for this application remained Theorem
5.8
by Bruinier
which provides injectivity under the assumption that the lattice
L
splits a hyperbolic plane
as well as a scaled hyperbolic plane.

In the context of this paper,
we present a new approach to proving the injectivity of the Kudla–Millson lift by means of
computing cycle integrals on the orthogonal variety.
This approach eliminates the need for an additional scaled hyperbolic split, leading to the following result
(cf. Theorem
6.5
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(L,\qq)
be an even lattice of signature
({m^{+}},2)
with
{m^{+}}>3
.
Assume that
L
splits a hyperbolic plane.
Then the Kudla–Millson lift
{{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{L,1+{m^{+}}/2}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1,1)}(Y_{L})
is injective.

The case
{m^{+}}=3
is also discussed in Subsection
6.2
and proven for the lattice
L
having Witt
rank
1
.
It should be stressed that in the case of no hyperbolic split, Bruinier constructed a subspace of the kernel
and demonstrated that it is in general non-trivial even in
the simple instance of lattices of prime level
[
Bru14
, Sec. 6.1]
, disproving injectivity.

Our new proof strategy is to compute special cycle integrals of Kudla–Millson liftings
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}(f)

of cusp forms
f\in\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
.
For suitably chosen cycles, these may be expressed in terms of special values of certain
L
-series
L(f,s)

associated to
f
.
If
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}(f)
is assumed to vanish, its cycle integral must vanish as well,
yielding zeroes of the aforementioned
L
-series.
As a consequence, relations between Fourier coefficients of
f
appearing in the construction of
L(f,s)
are established.
By constructing an infinite family of subseries of these special
L
-values – via the same cycle integrals –
we isolate the coefficients, ultimately showing that each of them must vanish.
Since the Kudla–Millson lift is linear, injectivity is inferred.
As a consequence, we deduce a converse theorem for Borcherds products under minimal splitting assumptions.

2.
Vector-valued modular forms

We briefly present the setting for this paper, including lattices,
discriminant forms, the Weil representation, and elliptic modular forms.

2.1.
The classical setting

Let
(L,\qq)
denote a non-degenerate even quadratic lattice of signature
({m^{+}},{m^{-}})
with associated bilinear form
\bilf
such that
\qq(x)\coloneq\bilf(x,x)/2
.

\Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(L,\mathbb{Z})
may be identified via
\bilf
with a lattice
L^{\prime}\subset L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
referred to as the
dual
and we write
\level(L)

for the level of the lattice, being the smallest positive integer such that
\bilf(L^{\prime},\level(L)\cdot L^{\prime})

is even. Recall the inclusion
L\leq L^{\prime}
and note that
\mathcal{L}\coloneq L^{\prime}/L
is a finite abelian group
whose order is the absolute value of the determinant of a representing matrix of
\bilf
.
Further, the quadratic form
\qq
descends to a quadratic form
\qd\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}
that is non-degenerate
and such a pair
(\mathcal{L},\qd)
is called a
discriminant form
.
In fact, any discriminant form arises from a non-degenerate even lattice in the above fashion.
If we set
\sig(\mathcal{L})={m^{+}}-{m^{-}}\mod 8
for some lattice
L
inducing
\mathcal{L}
,
this quantity is well defined by Milgram’s formula.
For a natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}
denote by
L(n)
the pair
(L,n\cdot\qq)
and set
\mathcal{L}(n)\coloneq L(n)^{\prime}/L(n)
.

Additionally, write
{{}_{n}\mathcal{L}}
for the
n
-torsion in
\mathcal{L}
and
{{}^{n}\!\mathcal{L}}
for the
n
-multiples.
We have the exact sequence

(2.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{}_{n}\mathcal{L}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{L}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{}^{n}\!\mathcal{L}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdot n}

and find that
{{}^{n}\!\mathcal{L}}
is exactly the orthogonal complement of
{{}_{n}\mathcal{L}}
.
We denote by
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{L}]
the group algebra of
\mathcal{L}
with standard basis
\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
.
There is a representation of
\Gamma(1)\coloneq\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{L}]
first described by Schoeneberg.
In fact, to obtain a well-defined representation when
\sig(\mathcal{L})
is odd,
it must be considered on the metaplectic extension, rather than on
\SL_{2}
directly.
Let
\MGLtwop(\mathbb{R})
denote the metaplectic double cover of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
.
A model is given by pairs
(\gamma,\phi)
, where
\gamma\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
\phi\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}

is a holomorphic square root of the factor of automorphy
j(\gamma,\tau)
, meaning
\phi^{2}(\tau)=j(\gamma,\tau)
.
Multiplication is then declared by

(\gamma,\phi(\tau))(\gamma^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}(\tau))=(\gamma\gamma^{\prime},\phi(\gamma^{\prime}\tau)\phi^{\prime}(\tau)).

Projection to the first component
\MGLtwop\ni(\gamma,\phi)\mapsto\gamma\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
defines a covering.
The action of elements
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\gamma\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
is given by Möbius transforms

\tau\mapsto(a\tau+b)/(c\tau+d)
with diagonal invariance group
\mathbb{R}^{\times}\into\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and induces an action on
\MGLtwop(\mathbb{R})
via pullback.
For the preimage of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
in
\MGLtwop(\mathbb{R})
, we write
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
This group is generated by the two elements
\overline{T}=\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),1\right)

and
\overline{S}=\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),\sqrt{\tau}\right)
, where
\sqrt{\tau}
denotes the standard holomorphic square root.
These elements project to the standard generators of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\overline{S}^{2}=\overline{Z}\coloneq\left(-\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),i\right)

generates the centre of
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

We now turn towards describing the Weil representation.
It suffices to describe the action of the Weil representation
\rho_{L}
associated to
L
on the generators
\overline{T}
and
\overline{S}
of
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.
We abbreviate
e(\,\cdot\,)\coloneq\exp(2\pi i\,\cdot\,)
and note that these operations are given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\rho}_{L}(\overline{T})\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=e(\qd(\lambda))\cdot\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda},

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\rho}_{L}(\overline{S})\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{e(-\sig(\mathcal{L})/8)}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{L}|}}\cdot\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}e(-\beta(\lambda,\mu))\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}.

It is apparent that the representation
\rho_{L}
only depends on the discriminant form
\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{L},\qd)
be a discriminant form.
Then
{\rho}_{\mathcal{L}}\coloneq{\rho}_{L}
is a unitary representation of
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{L}]
that is fully determined by
(
2.2
) and (
2.3
).

Note that the generator of the centre
\overline{Z}
operates for
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
as

(2.4)

\displaystyle{\rho}_{\mathcal{L}}(\overline{Z})\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}=e(-\sig(\mathcal{L})/4)\cdot\mathfrak{e}_{-\lambda}.

As a consequence, the Weil representation
\rho_{\mathcal{L}}
factors through
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/\langle\overline{Z}^{2}\rangle\simeq\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
if, and only if,
\sig(\mathcal{L})\equiv 0\mod 2
.
Further, writing
N\coloneq\level(\mathcal{L})
we find that
{\rho}_{\mathcal{L}}
is trivial on the congruence subgroup
\Gamma(N)
for even rank,
so that it factors through the finite group

\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})\simeq\Gamma(1)/\Gamma(N).

In case of odd rank, the oddity formula
[
Joh98
, Chap. 15 p. 383 (30)]
implies
4\mid N
, in particular,
\mathcal{L}
contains
2
-adic Jordan components.
In this case, there is a well known section

(2.5)

\displaystyle s\colon\Gamma(4)\to\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z}),\qquad\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\left(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\epsilon_{d}^{-1}\left(\frac{c}{d}\right)\sqrt{j(\gamma,\tau)}\right)

with
\epsilon_{d}=1
or
i
, depending on whether
d\equiv 1
or
3\mod 4
.
The argument at the end of
[
Bor00
, Thm. 5.4]
yields that
{\rho}_{\mathcal{L}}
is trivial on
s(\Gamma(N))
and factors through the central extension of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
by
\{\pm 1\}
given by

\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})/s(\Gamma(N)).

The natural projection
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\to\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
has the section
\gamma\mapsto\tilde{\gamma}\coloneq(\gamma,\sqrt{j(\gamma,\tau)})
,
where we selected the standard branch of the holomorphic square root. Note that this mapping is not a group homomorphism.
Before continuing we briefly recall the notion of a classical modular form.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\gamma\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
be a function. Define

f|_{k}\gamma(\tau)\coloneq\det(\gamma)^{k/2}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-k}f(\gamma\tau).

Finally, a holomorphic modular form
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
of weight
k
for some congruence subgroup
\Gamma\leq\Gamma(1)
with character
\chi
on
\Gamma

is a holomorphic function such that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have
f|_{k}\gamma=\chi(\gamma)f
and
f
is holomorphic at the cusps of
\Gamma
.
The space of such functions is denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{k}(\Gamma,\chi)
and the subspace of elements vanishing at the cusps is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{k}(\Gamma,\chi)

and called the space of cusp forms.
For the technical modifications required for forms of half-integral weight, we refer to
[
Shi73
, p. 444]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A function
f\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}[L^{\prime}/L]
is called
(vector-valued) modular function
of weight
k\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
if for all
\gamma=(M,\phi)\in\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
the following transformation law holds

f(\gamma\tau)=\phi(\tau)^{2k}\cdot\left[\rho_{L}(\gamma)f\right](\tau).

The operation of
\overline{T}
implies that such a function
f
possesses a Fourier expansion if it is holomorphic,
meaning there are coefficients
a(\lambda,n)\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(2.6)

f(\tau)=\sum_{\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L}\sum_{n\in\qd(\lambda)+\mathbb{Z}}a(\lambda,n)e(n\tau)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}.

In case the discriminant group
L^{\prime}/L
is trivial, the index
\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L
is eliminated from the notation.
We say that a holomorphic modular function
f
is a
modular form
,
if all coefficients
a(\lambda,n)
in (
2.6
) vanish for negative
n\in\mathbb{Q}
.
Further, it is called a
cusp form
, if all coefficients
a(\lambda,n)
vanish for non-positive
n\in\mathbb{Q}
.
The spaces of such modular functions are denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{L,k}
and
\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
.
Given a modular form
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L,k}
, there is a general procedure to induce a modular form to a sublattice
M\leq L
.
In that case,
M\leq L\leq L^{\prime}\leq M^{\prime}
implying the inclusion
L^{\prime}/M\leq M^{\prime}/M
and
there is a lift
\uparrow_{L}^{M}\colon\mathcal{M}_{L,k}\to\mathcal{M}_{M,k}
declared by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\left(\uparrow_{L}^{M}f\right)_{\mu}=\begin{cases}f_{\overline{\mu}},&\textnormal{if }\mu\in L^{\prime}/M,\\
0,&\textnormal{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\overline{\mu}
refers to the image of
\mu\in L^{\prime}/M
under the projection to
L^{\prime}/L
(cf.
[
Sch15
, Sec. 4]
).
By computing its adjoint, we obtain an operator
\downarrow^{M}_{L}\colon\mathcal{M}_{M,k}\to\mathcal{M}_{L,k}
with

(2.8)

\displaystyle(\downarrow^{M}_{L}g)_{\overline{\mu}}=\sum_{\nu\in L/M}g_{\mu+\nu}

where
\mu
denotes an arbitrary but fixed lift of some
\overline{\mu}\in L^{\prime}/L
to
M^{\prime}/M
.

There are also more concrete examples of modular forms, theta functions, in particular.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
L
be even positive definite,
\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L
, and
\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}
.
Define

(2.9)

\displaystyle\theta_{L,\lambda}(\tau)\coloneq\;

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in L+\lambda}e\left(\tau\qq(l)\right),\qquad\Theta_{L}\coloneq\sum_{\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L}\theta_{L,\lambda}(\tau)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}.

These theta series
\Theta_{L}
are modular forms of weight
{m^{+}}/2
(cf.
[
Bor98
, Thm. 4.1]
)
and their Fourier coefficients equal representation numbers of the lattice
L
.

Remark 2.5
.

The operator
\downarrow^{M}_{L}

translates the theta function associated with
M
to a theta function of
L
. In the notation of Definition
2.22
this means

\displaystyle\downarrow^{M}_{L}\left(\Theta_{M}(\tau,z;\varphi)\right)

\displaystyle=\;\downarrow^{M}_{L}\left(\sum_{\lambda\in M^{\prime}/M}\sum_{x\in M+\lambda}(\omega_{\infty}(g_{\tau})\varphi)(x,z)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L}\sum_{x\in L+\lambda}(\omega_{\infty}(g_{\tau})\varphi)(x,z)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\;\Theta_{L}(\tau,z;\varphi).

Proposition 2.6
.

For
M\leq L
even non-degenerate lattices and
k\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
, there are linear operators

\downarrow^{M}_{L}\colon\mathcal{M}_{M,k}\to\mathcal{M}_{L,k},\qquad\uparrow_{L}^{M}\colon\mathcal{M}_{L,k}\to\mathcal{M}_{M,k}

fulfilling the following properties.

a)

The operators map modular (cusp) forms to modular (cusp) forms.

b)

The lift
\downarrow^{M}_{L}
is compatible with theta functions, i.e.
\downarrow^{M}_{L}(\Theta_{M})=\Theta_{L}
.

c)

The operators are adjoint to each other on the space of cusp forms:

\langle\,\cdot\,,\downarrow^{M}_{L}(\,\cdot\,)\rangle_{L}=\langle\uparrow_{L}^{M}(\,\cdot\,),\,\cdot\,\rangle_{M}.

This identity also holds, if one of the cusp forms is replaced by a modular form.

d)

The image
B\coloneq\im(\uparrow_{L}^{M})
is the subspace consisting of functions that are supported on
L^{\prime}/M
.
We find

\displaystyle\downarrow^{M}_{L}\circ\uparrow_{L}^{M}

\displaystyle=|L/M|\cdot\id,

\displaystyle\uparrow_{L}^{M}\circ\downarrow^{M}_{L}\big|_{B}

\displaystyle=|L/M|\cdot\id_{B}.

Beyond the theta series presented above, Eisenstein series represent
another significant class of modular forms. We refer to
[
Kie21
, 2.5 pp. 30-36]
and
[
BK03
]
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
be isotropic and
k\in\mathbb{Z}/2
.
Define the
Eisenstein series

(2.10)

E_{L,\lambda,k}(\tau,s)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma_{\infty}}\setminus\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\left(\Im(\tau)^{s}\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}\right)|_{L,k}\gamma.

By construction this function is
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
modular of weight
k
.

Theorem 2.8
.

The Eisenstein series
E_{L,\lambda,k}
converges normally on
\mathbb{H}
for

2.2.
Eisenstein series

There is also a representation-theoretic approach to Eisenstein series, described succinctly in
[

]
which is suitable for applying the Siegel–Weil formula.
We recall here the essentials of the construction, following their notation,
and refer to the source for details.

For the group
G_{p}=\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{p})
the upper triangular matrices
P
form a maximal parabolic subgroup, its standard Borel subgroup.
We install the following notation:

(2.12)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&a^{-1}\end{pmatrix}=:M(a)\;\middle|\;a\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times}\right\},\quad N

\displaystyle\coloneq\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}=:N(b)\;\middle|\;b\in\mathbb{Q}_{p}\right\}.

Note that
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{A})
admits the so called

N(\mathbb{A})M(\mathbb{A})K

with
K=K_{\infty}K_{f}

K_{\infty}=\SO_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
K_{f}=\SL_{2}(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})

Write

k_{\vartheta}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\vartheta)&\sin(\vartheta)\\
-\sin(\vartheta)&\cos(\vartheta)\end{pmatrix}\in K_{\infty}\coloneq\SO_{2}(\mathbb{R})

(2.13)

\displaystyle g_{\tau}k_{\vartheta}i=\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}.

This decomposition lifts to

\SL_{2}(\mathbb{A})
by the torus
\mathbb{T}

where the respective preimages of groups in

[

Additionally, recall that there is a two-fold cover, denoted by
\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{A})
sitting inside
\MSL(\mathbb{A})
.
Moreover, note that for a rational quadratic space
(V,\qq)

there is a Weil representation

of
\MSL(\mathbb{Q}_{p})

Additionally, we will require the characters associated to the quadratic space induced by the Hilbert symbol

(2.14)

\mathbb{Q}_{p}^{\times}\ni a_{p}\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{p}(a_{p},\disc(V_{p})),\qquad\mathbb{A}^{\times}\ni(a_{p})\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{A}}(a,\disc(V))\coloneq\prod_{p}\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{Q}_{p}}(a_{p},\disc(V_{p})).

For an idele class character

\displaystyle\Phi(p^{\prime}g^{\prime},s)=\chi(a)\cdot|a|^{s+1}\Phi(g^{\prime},s)\begin{cases}1,&\textnormal{in the even case,}\\
z,&\textnormal{in the odd case,}\end{cases}

The simplest example of a section at a finite place is the normalised standard section.

Proposition 2.20

With the notation as above we find the following identity

For this equality, the natural identification

is used.

We follow
[
BY09
, Sec. 2]

We begin our calculation by inserting the explicit term for the section
\Phi_{\infty}^{l}
that has been calculated in Example
2.13
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\hat{L},l}(\tau,s)=\,

\displaystyle\Im(\tau)^{-l/2}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{E}(g_{\tau},s;\Phi_{\infty}^{l}\otimes\Phi_{\mu})\varphi_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash\Gamma(1)}\frac{\Im(\gamma\tau)^{(s+1-l)/2}}{j(\gamma,\tau)^{l}}\Phi_{\mu}(\gamma)\varphi_{\mu}.

\Phi_{\mu}

\lambda_{f}(\varphi_{\mu})\in I_{f}(s_{0},\chi)

the following identity is true.

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(\varphi_{\mu})(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\langle\omega_{f}(\gamma)\varphi_{\mu},\varphi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\overline{\omega_{f}(\gamma^{-1})}\varphi_{0},\varphi_{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho_{L}(\gamma^{-1})\varphi_{0},\varphi_{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho_{L}(\gamma^{-1})\mathfrak{e}_{0},\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{e}_{0},\rho_{L}(\gamma)\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}\rangle.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\hat{L},l}(\tau,s)\simeq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\in\overline{\Gamma_{\infty}}\backslash\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\frac{\Im(\gamma\tau)^{(s+1-l)/2}}{j(\gamma,\tau)^{l}}\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{L}}\langle\rho_{L}(\gamma^{-1})\mathfrak{e}_{0},\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}\rangle\mathfrak{e}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\;

2.3.
Theta series

In the classical setting for a positive definite quadratic space
(V,\qq)
of even dimension
m
,
select the standard Gaussian
\varphi_{0}\colon V_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C},\quad v\mapsto\exp(-2\pi\qq(v))

\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}

g_{\tau}=N(u)M(\sqrt{v})

\omega_{\infty}

\omega_{\infty}(g_{\tau})\varphi_{0}(x)=e^{2\pi i\qq(x)u}v^{m/4}\varphi_{0}(\sqrt{v}x)=v^{m/4}\exp(2\pi i\qq(x)\tau).

Definition 2.21
.

Let
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{R}})
and
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}=L^{\prime}/L

\theta_{L,\lambda}(\tau;\varphi)=v^{-m/4}\cdot\sum_{x\in L+\lambda}[\omega_{\infty}(g_{\tau})\varphi](x)

\varphi

\lambda+L

We note that we may let
\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})

\varphi

In that sense,
\theta_{L,\lambda}

\mathbb{H}\times\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})
.
Combining the above theta functions for different cosets of the dual lattice yields the
following theta function.

Definition 2.22
.

Let
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{R}})
.
Then

\Theta_{L}(\tau,h;\varphi)=v^{-m/4}\cdot\sum_{x\in L^{\prime}}[\omega_{\infty}(g_{\tau})\varphi](h^{-1}x)\mathfrak{e}_{x+L}

is called
theta function
associated to
\varphi
.
In case the Schwartz form
\varphi
is invariant under the action of a maximal compact

h
factors through
\mathbb{D}
and we write

\Theta_{L}(\tau,z;\varphi)
for

Moreover, for fixed

A prominent example is the classical case based on (

)

In the indefinite case, the naive Gaussian

For an element

\qq_{z}^{+}(v)=\qq(v_{z^{\perp}})-\qq(v_{z})

In fact, with

\displaystyle\Theta_{L}(\tau;\varphi_{\infty})\;=\;

\displaystyle v^{-\frac{m}{4}}\cdot\hskip-30.0pt\sum_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in L_{1}^{\prime}/L_{1}\oplus L_{2}^{\prime}/L_{2}}\sum_{l_{1}\in L_{1}+\lambda_{1},l_{2}\in L_{2}+\lambda_{2}}\left(\omega_{\infty}(g_{\tau})\varphi_{\infty}\left((l_{1},l_{2})\right)\right)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}}

so that

(3.2)

\displaystyle V=W\oplus\mathbb{Q}e_{1}\oplus\mathbb{Q}e_{2}.

Then we define

(3.3)

\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{\pm}\coloneq\{z=x+iy\in W_{\mathbb{C}}\mid\qq(y)<0\}.

We write
Z=(z,a,b)\in V_{\mathbb{C}}
for an element in the associated complex space given by
z+ae_{1}+be_{2}

with
z\in W_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then the map

\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{\pm}\ni z\mapsto\psi(z)=[(z,-\qq(z)-\qq(e_{2}),1)]\in\mathcal{K}

defines a biholomorphic association.
Set
\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{+}
and
\mathcal{K}^{+}
to be the respective connected components in bijection with
\mathbb{D}^{+}
.
The action of
\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})
on
P(V_{\mathbb{C}})
carries over to
\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{+}
and induces an action by fractional linear transformations.
This yields for
\gamma\in O(V_{\mathbb{R}})
a
factor of automorphy

j(\gamma,z)
such that
\gamma\psi(z)=j(\gamma,z)\psi(\gamma z)
.
Then for
r\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
there is a map
w_{r}
on
\OG^{+}(V_{\mathbb{R}})\times\OG^{+}(V_{\mathbb{R}})
to the roots of unity of order bounded by
\textnormal{den}(r)
such that

j(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2},Z)^{r}=w_{r}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\cdot j(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}z)^{r}j(\gamma_{2},z)^{r}.

Similar to the elliptic setting there is a notion of modular forms in the orthogonal case.
We refer to
[
Bru02
, 3.3 p. 84]
for a more detailed description.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma\leq\OG^{+}(V_{\mathbb{R}})
and
r\in\mathbb{Q}
.
A multiplier system of weight
r
for
\Gamma
is a map

\chi\colon\Gamma\to\mathbb{T},\qquad\textnormal{such that}\qquad\chi(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})=w_{r}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})^{-1}\cdot\chi(\gamma_{1})\chi(\gamma_{2}).

With the notion of a multiplier system, modular forms on the tube domain
\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{+}
may be defined.
Let
V
be isotropic.
An orthogonal
meromorphic modular form
with respect to the group

\Gamma\leq\Gamma(L)
of finite index of weight
r\in\mathbb{Q}
and multiplier system
\chi

is a meromorphic function
F
on
\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{+}
with transformation property

F(\gamma z)=\chi(\gamma)j(\gamma,z)^{r}F(z).

When defining the Petersson slash operator in analogy to the elliptic case,
Eisenstein series may be constructed in a similar fashion as an example of modular forms.
We will not require these in the following, but the curious reader may consult
[
Kie22
, 7 p. 2867]
.
Further, non-trivial examples of modular forms may be constructed via the Borcherds lift,
relating weakly holomorphic vector-valued elliptic modular forms to meromorphic orthogonal modular forms (see Section
5
).

3.1.
Special divisors

In algebraic geometry divisors are a common tool for investigating the structure of varieties.
A feature of the orthogonal setting is that the associated Shimura varieties have an abundance of divisors
being induced by sub-varieties of the same type in all codimensions.
These give rise to so called
special cycles
which
will play a key role in proving the injectivity of the Kudla–Millson lift in Section
6
.
In the following, we describe the construction of these special cycles of codimension
1
.

Recall that
(V,\qq)
has signature
({m^{+}},2)

and select some
v\in V
with
\qq(v)>0
.
Then the subspace
V_{v}\coloneq v^{\perp}

has signature
({m^{+}}-1,2)
and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{v}\coloneq\left\{[z]\in\mathcal{K}^{+}\mid(z,v)=0\right\}

defines an analytic divisor on
\mathcal{K}^{+}
being exactly the hermitian symmetric domain corresponding to
(V_{v},\qq)
.
In other words, we find

(3.5)

\mathcal{Z}_{v}\simeq\mathbb{D}_{v}\coloneq\{U\subset V_{v}(\mathbb{R})\mid\dim(U)=2\textnormal{ and }\qq|_{U}<0\}.

Here,
\mathbb{D}_{v}
can be realised as the Grassmannian attached to the stabiliser
\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})_{v}
of
v
in
\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})
.
We call
\mathcal{Z}_{v}
a
special divisor
.
Its corresponding description in the tube domain model looks as follows.
Write
v=v_{W}+ae_{2}+be_{1}
as above (cf. (
3.2
))
so that

\mathcal{Z}_{v}\simeq\left\{z\in\mathcal{H}^{+}\mid a\qq(z)-\bilf(z,v_{W})-a\qq(e_{2})-b=0\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{+}.

This description gives rise to the term
rational quadratic divisor
.
Further, the following combination of these divisors appears naturally in numerous applications (cf. Theorem
5.1
).

Definition 3.2
.

Select
\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L
as well as a rational number
n>0
.
Then

(3.6)

Z(\lambda,n)\coloneq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\lambda+L\\
\qq(v)=n\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Z}_{v}

defines an analytic divisor on
\mathcal{K}^{+}
which is called
Heegner divisor
of discriminant
(\lambda,n)
.

Recall that to an arithmetic subgroup
\Gamma\leq\OG(L)
there is
an (orthogonal) Shimura variety

Y_{\Gamma}=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{D}
.
Now, if
\Gamma
lies in the discriminant kernel,
i.e. acts trivially on the discriminant group
L^{\prime}/L
,
then the Heegner divisor
descends to an algebraic divisor on
Y_{\Gamma}
, which is also denoted by
Z(\lambda,n)
. This may be verified by Chow’s lemma.

Alternatively, the Heegner divisors might be symmetrised over
L^{\prime}/L
to yield an algebraic divisor on
Y_{\Gamma}
, namely

(3.7)

Z(m)\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L}Z(\lambda,m).

We also require the following more primitive divisor which is directly induced by (
3.4
).
Let
\Gamma_{v}\coloneq\Gamma\cap\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})_{v}
be the intersection with the stabiliser of
v
.
Then we find that

(3.8)

\mathcal{Z}(v)\coloneq\Gamma_{v}\backslash\mathbb{D}_{v}\to Y_{\Gamma}

defines a (in general relative) cycle. These cycles play a fundamental role in the proof of the injectivity of the Kudla–Millson lift
in Section
6.1
.

The existence of such a family of algebraic divisors is distinctive for orthogonal and unitary groups.

4.
Siegel-Weil formula

Theta series and Eisenstein series form two fundamental families of automorphic forms.
In many cases
(cf.
[
Mül24
, Thm. 6.7]
)
the space of cusp forms is spanned by theta series, which is complemented by Eisenstein series.
A central tool relating theta series to Eisenstein series is the
Siegel–Weil
formula.
In
4.3
we follow Kudla
[
Kud03
, Sec. 4.3]

in applying it to compute certain geometric integrals of the Kudla–Millson Schwartz form, which will be essential for the injectivity arguments in Section
6
.
Recall that
L
is an even lattice of signature
({m^{+}},2)
and the dimension of the rational quadratic space
V=L\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}

is denoted by
m
.

4.1.
Siegel–Weil formula

The subject of this subsection is the so called
Siegel–Weil
formula which establishes a relation between Eisenstein series as in (
2.19
)
evaluated at a special point
s_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
and theta integrals as described in (
4.1
).
It is an adelic extension of the classical Siegel formula and has first been proven by André Weil
[
Wei65
]
.

For a Schwartz–Bruhat function
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{A}})
define the adelic theta distribution

\theta(g^{\prime},h;\varphi)=\sum_{x\in V_{\mathbb{Q}}}(\omega(g^{\prime})\varphi)(h^{-1}x)

as a function in
g^{\prime}\in\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{A})
and
h\in\OG(V_{\mathbb{A}})
.
Averaging over the argument
h
delivers the following standard theta integral.

Definition 4.1
.

For a Schwartz–Bruhat function
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{A}})
define the following theta integral.

(4.1)

I(g^{\prime};\varphi)\coloneq\int_{\OG(V_{\mathbb{Q}})\setminus\OG(V_{\mathbb{A}})}\theta(g^{\prime},h;\varphi)\;\textnormal{d}h,

where
\textrm{d}h
denotes the standard invariant normalised measure on the quotient such that
\vol(\OG(V_{\mathbb{Q}})\setminus\OG(V_{\mathbb{A}}))=1
.

By Weil’s convergence criterion, the integral
I(g;\varphi)
above is absolutely convergent in case the rational space
V
is anisotropic or
\dim(V)=m>2+r
,
where
r
denotes the Witt rank of
V
.
In case this criterion is not matched, the integral may still be regularised
as in
[
KR94
, p. 41]
and numerous results may be transferred to this setting.
With these preparations we require only one additional piece of notation to state the Siegel–Weil formula.
Let
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{A}})
be a Schwartz–Bruhat form and
\Phi\in I(s,\chi)
its associated
standard section through the principal series representation arising from the intertwining operator
as described in (
2.18
) (also compare Lemma
2.14
).

Definition 4.2
.

With the notation as above, write

\mathcal{E}(g^{\prime},s;\varphi)\coloneq\mathcal{E}(g^{\prime},s;\Phi),\qquad\mathcal{E}(\tau,s;\varphi)\coloneq\mathcal{E}(\tau,s;\Phi).

Theorem 4.3

(Siegel–Weil formula,
[
Kud03
, Thm. 4.1]
)
.

Assume
m>2+r
or that
V
is anisotropic.
Then the theta integral
I(g^{\prime};\varphi)
for
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(V_{\mathbb{A}})
is absolutely convergent.
Further,
\mathcal{E}(g^{\prime},s;\varphi)
is holomorphic at
s_{0}=\frac{m}{2}-1
and the following identity holds:

\mathcal{E}(g^{\prime},s_{0};\varphi)=\kappa\cdot I(g^{\prime};\varphi),

where
\kappa=2
, if
m\leq 2
, and
\kappa=1
otherwise.

In particular, the Eisenstein series associated to a Schwartz–Bruhat form bears information
about representation numbers of a quadratic lattice in its Fourier expansion at the critical point
s_{0}=\frac{m}{2}-1
.
This is remarkable since the Eisenstein series is essentially built from local data,
while the theta integral involves global arithmetic.
Hence, theta integrals associated to different quadratic spaces
may be related to each other, via the Eisenstein series, once the local data agrees.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
V_{p},V_{p}^{\prime}
be two quadratic
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
spaces of dimension
m
.
Suppose these spaces induce the same quadratic character
\chi_{V_{p}}=\chi_{V_{p}^{\prime}}
via the Hilbert symbol.
Two functions
\varphi_{p}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{p})
and
\varphi_{p}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{p}^{\prime})
are called
matching
, if they give rise to the same section via the intertwining operator
\lambda_{p}(\varphi_{p})=\lambda_{p}^{\prime}(\varphi_{p}^{\prime})
.

In fact, in case of
m>4
and
p
non-archimedean, every
\varphi_{p}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{p})
has a
matching function
\varphi_{p}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{p}^{\prime})
, since the
local principal series
I_{p}(s_{0},\chi_{p})
is irreducible.

In order to compare theta integrals as suggested above, it is hence sufficient to find a non-trivial match
at the archimedean place. One solution to that problem is given by the Kudla–Millson Schwartz function
which is introduced in the following section.

4.2.
Kudla–Millson Schwartz form

Let
(V,\qq)
have signature
({m^{+}},2)
and dimension
m
.
Recall that for a complex manifold
D
, the space of smooth forms of Hodge type
(1,1)

is denoted by
\mathscr{A}^{(1,1)}(D)
.
Kudla and Millson have constructed a form
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{R}})\otimes\mathscr{A}^{(1,1)}(\mathbb{D})
,
where
\mathbb{D}
denotes the oriented Grassmannian associated to the base lattice
L
, with the following properties.

i)

For all
h\in\OG(V_{\mathbb{R}})

(4.2)

h^{\ast}{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}(h^{-1}x)={\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}(x).

ii)

The form
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}
has weight
m/2
for
K_{\infty}^{\prime}
for the Weil representation
\omega_{\infty}
,
meaning

(4.3)

\omega_{\infty}(k^{\prime}){\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}=\nu_{m/2}(k^{\prime}){\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}},

where the character
\nu_{l}
is presented in Remark
2.12
.

iii)

The form
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}
is closed, i.e.

(4.4)

\;\textnormal{d}{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}=0

for the exterior differential
d
on
\mathbb{D}
.

This form
matches
the Gaussian on a positive definite space of the same dimension in the sense that it gives rise to the same section through the principal series representation.
To specify the meaning of this statement, we define the following form.

Definition 4.5
.

Define the following closed
\OG(V_{\infty})
invariant
(1,1)
form on
\mathbb{D}
:

(4.5)

\Omega\coloneqq{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}(0).

An explicit description in local coordinates
on the tube domain model of that form is presented in
[
Kud03
, Prop. 4.11]
.
It is given by

(4.6)

\Omega=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left[-\bilf(y,y)^{-2}\bilf(y,\;\textnormal{d}z)\wedge\bilf(y,\;\textnormal{d}\overline{z})+\bilf(y,y)^{-1}\tfrac{1}{2}\bilf(\;\textnormal{d}z,\;\textnormal{d}\overline{z})\right]

in coordinates on the tube domain model
\mathcal{H}_{{m^{+}}}^{\pm}
(cf. (
3.3
)).
Its negative
-\Omega
is an invariant
Kähler form
on
\mathbb{D}
and descends to a Kähler form on the variety
X_{K}
given in (
4.9
).

Note that we may write

{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}(x)\wedge\Omega^{m-1}={\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}(x)\Omega^{m}

for a function
{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{R}})\otimes A^{(0,0)}(\mathbb{D})
.
The
\OG(V_{\infty})
invariance of
\Omega
then implies that

{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}(hx,hz)={\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}(x,z)

for any
h\in\OG(V_{\infty})
.
Further, setting
k\coloneq\tfrac{m}{2}
we find

(4.7)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\infty}({\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}(\,\cdot\,,z))=\Phi^{k}(s_{0})\Omega,\quad\lambda_{\infty}({\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}(\,\cdot\,,z))=\Phi^{k}(s_{0})

for any
z\in\mathbb{D}
, where
\lambda_{\infty}
denotes the intertwining operator described in
Lemma
2.14
and
\Phi_{\infty}^{k}
denotes the standard section
in
I_{\infty}(s,\chi_{V})
at the infinite place presented
in Example
2.13
.
This means, the function
{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}
will be associated by
\lambda_{\infty}
to the same section as
the standard Gaussian
\varphi_{0}
on a space
V^{\prime}
of signature
(m,0)
if the local characters agree, i.e. they are
matching
.
If further, we have two matching functions at the finite places
\varphi_{f}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{f})
and
\varphi_{f}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{f}^{\prime})
, the Siegel–Weil formula (Theorem
4.3
) yields

(4.8)

I(g^{\prime};{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f})=E(g^{\prime},s_{0};{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f})=E(g^{\prime},s_{0};\varphi_{0}\otimes\varphi_{f}^{\prime})=I(g^{\prime};\varphi_{0}\otimes\varphi_{f}^{\prime}).

Before turning towards an essential application of the Siegel–Weil formula within the scope of this paper,
we remark that a more explicit representation of the Kudla–Millson Schwartz form
is found in
[
MZ26
]
.

4.3.
A geometric integral

Denote the GSpin group
associated to the lattice
(L,\qq)
of signature
({m^{+}},2)
by
H
.
Moreover, denote the oriented Grassmannian of
L
by
\mathbb{D}
and
choose a compact open subgroup
K_{f}\subset H(\mathbb{A}_{f})
.
Then we consider the variety

(4.9)

X_{K}\coloneq H(\mathbb{Q})\backslash(\mathbb{D}\times H(\mathbb{A}_{f}))/K_{f}

and have that

X_{K}=\bigsqcup_{j=1}\Gamma_{j}\backslash\mathbb{D}^{+},

where
\mathbb{D}^{+}
is a connected component of
\mathbb{D}
.
We write
Y_{j}\coloneq\Gamma_{j}\backslash\mathbb{D}^{+}
and note that there are

\lvert\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}:\Cliffnorm(K_{f})\rvert
of these components.
Here,
\Cliffnorm
denotes the Clifford norm.
With this preparation we may state the following modified version of
[
Kud03
, Prop. 4.17]
.

Proposition 4.6
.

We find for a compact subgroup
K_{f}\leq H(\mathbb{A}_{f})
with
K_{f}\cap Z(\mathbb{A})\simeq\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
under the isomorphism
Z(\mathbb{A})\simeq\mathbb{A}^{\times}
that

(-1)^{{m^{+}}}\tfrac{1}{4}\vol(K_{f})\cdot\int_{X_{K}}\theta(g^{\prime};{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f})\wedge\Omega^{{m^{+}}-1}=I(g^{\prime};{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f}).

Further, the integral on the right hand side over each component
Y_{j}
of
X_{K}
is identical.
Hence, for any component
Y_{j}
the following identity holds

(-1)^{{m^{+}}}\tfrac{1}{4}\vol(K_{f})\cdot\lvert\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}:\Cliffnorm(K_{f})\rvert\cdot\int_{Y_{j}}\theta(g^{\prime};{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f})\wedge\Omega^{{m^{+}}-1}=I(g^{\prime};{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f}).

We would like to apply this result in the more classical setting of varieties defined at the infinite place over
the orthogonal group
\OG
as in
[
Bor98
]
and recall that

1\to\mathbb{Q}^{\times}\to H(\mathbb{Q})\to\SO(V)\to 1,

as well as

H(\hat{\mathbb{Z}})\to\Gamma^{\prime}(\hat{L})\to 1,

where
\Gamma^{\prime}(\hat{L})
denotes the discriminant kernel in
\SO(\hat{L})
.
For
\Gamma\leq\SO(L)
, we may consider the closure
\overline{\Gamma}<\SO(V_{\mathbb{A}_{f}})

and further select a cover
K_{f}<H(\mathbb{A}_{f})
, containing
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
.
In that case, we have that the Shimura variety
X_{K}
of the pair
(H,K_{f})
is
the same as the variety associated to
(\SO,\overline{\Gamma})
(cf.
[
BY21
, p. 1669]
):

\SO(V)\backslash\mathbb{D}\times\SO(V_{\mathbb{A}_{f}})/\overline{\Gamma}\simeq H(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathbb{D}\times H(\mathbb{A}_{f})/K_{f}.

In the special case of
K_{f}=\GSpin_{\hat{L}}
,
we find that for the choice of
h_{j}
being equal to the neutral element that

Y_{j}\simeq Y^{\prime}(L)=\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}^{+}
, where
\Gamma^{\prime}(L)
denotes the discriminant kernel in
\SO
.
In fact,
if we specialise further, to the case of
L
splitting a hyperbolic plane,
we find that the index
\lvert\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}:\Cliffnorm(K_{f})\rvert
equals
1
,
so that there is only one connected component identifying with
Y^{\prime}(L)=\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
.
All in all, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
(L,\qq)
be an even quadratic lattice of signature
({m^{+}},2)

and select
K_{f}=\GSpin_{\hat{L}}
.
Further, write
Y_{L}=\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}

for the variety associated to the discriminant kernel
\Gamma(L)
in
\OG(L)

and select an element
\varphi_{f}\in{\mathcal{S}}(V_{\mathbb{A}_{f}})
that is invariant under
\Gamma(L)
.
Then

(4.10)

\int_{Y_{L}}\theta(g^{\prime};{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f})\wedge\Omega^{{m^{+}}-1}=(-1)^{{m^{+}}}\frac{4c_{K}}{\lvert\hat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}:\Cliffnorm(K_{f})\rvert}\cdot I(g^{\prime},{\tilde{\varphi}_{\textnormal{KM}}}\otimes\varphi_{f}),

where
c_{K}=1
or
1/2
, depending on whether
\Gamma(L)\cap\SO(V)\backslash\mathbb{D}\to\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
is bijective or two-to-one.

If considered in the special orthogonal setting, meaning integrating over

\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
instead of
\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
on the left hand side
of (
4.10
), the statement follows immediately from
Proposition
4.6
.
The only task remaining is hence the translation to the orthogonal setting.
By assumption, the integrand is invariant under pullback by elements from
\Gamma(L)
.
In fact, the form
\Omega
is invariant under
\OG(V_{\infty})
and
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}
fulfils (
4.2
).
Further,
\varphi_{f}
was chosen to be invariant under
\Gamma(L)
as well,
so that we may translate the domain of integration by

\gamma\in\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\Gamma(L)
and combine a fundamental domain for

\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
from translates of a domain for

\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
.
Consequently, the integral in question in (
4.10
) equals the integral over

\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
divided by the multiplicity of the cover
\Gamma^{\prime}(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}\to\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
.

Finally, we note that

[\Gamma(L):\Gamma^{\prime}(L)]\leq[\OG(V):\SO(V)]=2,

meaning that the considered cover represents at most a double-cover.
∎

5.
Theta lifts

The two theta lifts central to our considerations are the Borcherds lift and the Kudla–Millson lift.
The former provides a multiplicative correspondence from vector-valued weakly holomorphic modular forms to orthogonal modular forms with explicit product expansions.
The latter constitutes a linear lift from cusp forms to harmonic differential forms on orthogonal Shimura varieties.
These two lifts are closely related: in particular, injectivity of the Kudla–Millson lift is, under suitable hypotheses, equivalent to a converse theorem for Borcherds products (see Theorem
5.10
).
In this section we recall their definitions and review known injectivity results.
Let
L
denote an even lattice of signature
({m^{+}},{m^{-}})
.

5.1.
The Borcherds lift

In his 1995 paper
[
Bor95
]
, Borcherds introduced a multiplicative lift from weakly holomorphic modular forms of weight
1/2

to meromorphic modular forms, producing infinite product expansions.
The weights and divisors of the resulting forms are determined explicitly in terms of the Fourier coefficients of the input.
Notable applications include infinite product expansions for the discriminant function, the
j
-invariant, and the Monster denominator formula
used to prove the moonshine conjecture.

In his renowned 1998 paper
[
Bor98
]
Borcherds significantly expanded on his initial approach,
presenting a multiplicative lift that maps weakly holomorphic
vector-valued modular forms of weight
1-{m^{+}}/2

to meromorphic automorphic forms for certain arithmetic subgroups of
\OG_{{m^{+}},2}(\mathbb{R})
,
i.e. to the orthogonal setting.
In the case of
{m^{+}}=1
, the association may be interpreted as a purely symplectic lift,
due to the accidental isomorphism of
\SO_{1,2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\Sp_{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
For a concise source on the construction
see
[
Bru14
]
.

Theorem 5.1

(Borcherds)
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L^{-},1-{m^{+}}/2}^{!}
be a weakly holomorphic modular form with Fourier coefficients
a(\lambda,n)
as in (
2.6
). Assume that
a(\lambda,n)
is integral for
n\leq 0
.
Then there exists a meromorphic modular form
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
for the discriminant kernel
\Gamma(L)

with unitary multiplier system of finite order, such that the following conditions are satisfied.

a)

The weight of
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
is given by
a(0,0)/2
.

b)

The divisor of the target form
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
is determined by the principal part of
f
:

(5.1)

\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\mu\in L^{\prime}/L}\sum_{n>0}a(\mu,-n)Z(\mu,n),

where

Z(\mu,n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mu+L\\
\qq(v)=n\end{subarray}}\{z\in\mathbb{D}\mid z\perp v\}

is the
Heegner divisor
of discriminant
(\mu,n)
(cf. Definition
3.2
plus the subsequent comment).

c)

The target form
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
has explicit infinite product expansions at cusps.

For further details regarding the product expansions that are constructed for different cusps,
or background material on the construction itself,
we refer the reader to
[
Bor98
, Thm. 13.3]
and conclude with the following definition.

Definition 5.2
.

For a form
f\in\mathcal{M}_{L^{-},1-{m^{+}}/2}^{!}
as above, we refer to
\Psi(\,\cdot\,,f)
as the
Borcherds lift
of the form
f

5.2.
The Kudla–Millson lift

Recall that the Kudla–Millson Schwartz form
{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}
has been introduced in
4.2
.
It is noteworthy that
\Omega\coloneq{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}(0,\,\cdot\,)
is the Euler form
of the symmetric space
\mathbb{D}

and in the hermitian case of
{m^{-}}=2
, its negative defines a Kähler form.
In the latter case, a description in coordinates on the associated tube domain model has been provided in (
4.6
).

With the Kudla–Millson Schwartz form available, the associated theta kernel may be constructed as presented
in Definition
2.22
.

Definition 5.3
.

For
\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}
and
z\in\mathbb{D}
the
Kudla–Millson theta function
of the lattice
L
is given by

(5.2)

{\Theta_{\textnormal{KM}}}(\tau,z)\coloneq\Theta_{L}(\tau,z;{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}})=v^{-m/4}\sum_{\lambda\in L^{\prime}/L}\sum_{l\in\lambda+L}\left({\omega}_{\infty}(g_{\tau}){\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}}\right)(l,z)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}.

It should be noted that there is a more explicit construction provided in
[
BF04
, Sec. 2,4]
based on which the
form is explicitly expressed in terms of Siegel theta functions in
[
MZ26
]
.
As with the Borcherds lift, the theta kernel associated to the Kudla–Millson Schwartz form may be
utilised as an integral kernel to construct a theta lift.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
L
be an even lattice of signature
({m^{+}},{m^{-}})
with rank
m
and
set
k\coloneq m/2
.
Then for a cusp form
f\in\mathcal{S}_{L,k}(\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
and
\tau=u+iv\in\mathbb{H}
,
the association

(5.3)

f\mapsto{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}(f)\coloneq\int_{\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}}v^{k}\langle f(\tau),\Theta_{L}(\tau,z;{\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}}})\rangle\,\frac{\;\textnormal{d}u\;\textnormal{d}v}{v^{2}}

defines a map to the
{m^{-}}
-forms
\mathscr{A}^{{m^{-}}}(\mathbb{D})

and is referred to as the
Kudla–Millson lift
.

The target form
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}(f)
is in general closed and inherits the invariance under
the discriminant kernel subgroup
\Gamma(L)
of
L
on the orthogonal side from the theta form
{\Theta_{\textnormal{KM}}}
.
Consequently, it descends to a form on the quotient space
Y_{L}=\Gamma(L)\backslash\mathbb{D}
.
In the hermitian case of
{m^{-}}=2
, we obtain a map

(5.4)

{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}:\mathcal{S}_{L,1+{m^{+}}/2}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1,1)}(Y_{L}),

to the space of square-integrable harmonic differential forms of Hodge type
(1,1)

on the orthogonal modular variety associated to the discriminant kernel
\Gamma(L)
.

The applications of the Kudla–Millson theta correspondence range from the study of the cohomology
of orthogonal and unitary Shimura varieties
[
KM86
]
and Arakelov theory of Shimura varieties
[
Kud04
]
over specific counting problems
[
EGS23
]
,
constructing mock modular forms and higher dimensional error functions
[
FK17
]
, to proving a converse theorem for Borcherds products
[
Bru02
]

[
Bru14
]
.
It is the latter application that we have in mind which is related to the injectivity of the Kudla–Millson lift.
The question of its injectivity already arose in
[
KM90
]

and may be used to compute the rational Picard number of the underlying Shimura variety
[
Ber+16
]
,
as well as to derive properties of cones generated by special cycles
[
BM19
]

[
Zuf22
]
.
Since the Kudla–Millson lift has been constructed, there have been a number of advances in proving its injectivity.
To the best of the author’s knowledge, the first such result in the literature is the following.

Theorem 5.5

(
[
Bru02
, Thm. 5.12]
)
.

Let
(L,\qq)
be an even lattice of signature
({m^{+}},2)
such that it splits two hyperbolic planes
L\simeq K\oplus H\oplus H
.
Then the Kudla–Millson lift
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}:\mathcal{S}_{L,1+{m^{+}}/2}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1,1)}(Y_{L})
is injective.

The proof is based upon the computation of the Fourier expansion of the target form,
which reveals information about the Fourier coefficients of the initial form
f
and leads to the conclusion
that it must vanish if its image does.
The second hyperbolic split is required in order to guarantee that indices of Fourier coefficients are
representable by the lattice in a certain fashion similar to Lemma
6.6
.

There has been a second advance by the same author in collaboration with Jens Funke in
[
BF10
]
.
In this instance, the authors develop a new strategy of proof,
by utilising the doubling method to compute the
L^{2}
–norm of
the Kudla–Millson lift by means of the Rallis inner product formula
in order to conclude injectivity in the unimodular case for general signature.
In addition, the authors consider a twist of the Kudla–Millson lift,
denoted by
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KMF},l}}
, which was introduced by
Funke and Millson
[
FM12
]
with a parameter

that allows for inputting forms of higher weight.
The twisted lift

maps to the space of

-valued closed differential forms on
Y_{L}
.
Here,
\widetilde{{\operatorname{Sym}}}^{l}(V)
denotes the local system on
\mathbb{D}

associated to the
l
-th symmetric power of
V
.
Details are provided in the primary source or in a compact fashion in
[
BF10
]

and we refer to the lift
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KMF},l}}
as the
Kudla–Millson–Funke

lift of degree
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The latter source also contains the following injectivity statement.

Theorem 5.6

(
[
BF10
, Cor. 4.11]
)
.

Assume that
m>\max\{4,3+r\},{m^{+}}>1,{m^{-}}+l

and that

This approach has recently been generalised by Stein to include the case of maximal lattices
by solving intricate local integrals for bad primes in the same setting.

Theorem 5.7

(
[
Ste23
, Cor. 7.8]
)
.

Let

{m^{-}}+l

Nevertheless, the case with the most compelling application within this paper remains the

since it relates to the Borcherds lift – a connection that is highlighted below.
In this case, the strongest result available in the literature is attributed to Bruinier
and proven by refining his approach in

through the development of a newform theory for

Theorem 5.8

Let

It should also be noted that quite recently, Zuffetti and the author have extended this result to the case of general signature, also including the Funke–Millson twist.

Theorem 5.9

Let

Then the Kudla–Millson lift
{{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}}\colon\mathcal{S}_{L,1+{m^{+}}/2}\to\mathcal{H}^{(1,1)}(Y_{L})
is injective.

Recall that the Kudla–Millson lift associates to
an elliptic cusp form
f\in\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
a form

{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}(f)=\int_{\Mp_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}}v^{k}\langle f(\tau),\Theta(\tau,z;\varphi_{\textnormal{KM}})\rangle\frac{\;\textnormal{d}u\;\textnormal{d}v}{v^{2}}\in\mathcal{H}^{(1,1)}(Y_{L}).

We now outline the strategy of the proof.
By Corollary
6.4
, the vanishing of
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}(f)
for some

f\in\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
implies the vanishing of special
L
-values associated
to primitive
{\ell_{0}}\in L^{\prime}
of positive norm.
These
L
-values are constructed by summing over Fourier coefficients supported along rational lines in
V
.
The key observation is that if
L
splits a hyperbolic plane, then the primitivity assumption may be dropped.
With that advantage, the series
L_{{\ell_{0}}}(f,{m^{+}})
may be decomposed into vanishing subseries,
supported on arbitrary multiples of
{\ell_{0}}
, which suffices to force the vanishing of the Fourier coefficients themselves.

In order to deduce the vanishing of
f
from the vanishing of the
L
-values in (
6.10
) two issues must be addressed.
First, the
L
-functions involve only coefficients of the form
a(\overline{\ell},\qq(\ell))
for vectors
\ell\in L^{\prime}
of positive norm,
leaving open the behaviour of coefficients with other indices.
Second, the resulting linear relations span an infinite dimensional system and are not obviously sufficient to force
f=0
.
While the second issue can, in principle, be resolved using Hecke theory, the first is solely a property of the lattice
L
.
The key insight is that under a hyperbolic split, all indices can be realised as
(\overline{\ell},q(\ell))
for suitable
\ell\in L^{\prime}
.

Lemma 6.6
.

Suppose that
L\simeq K\oplus H
splits a hyperbolic plane. Then every pair
(\lambda,n)
, where
\lambda\in\mathcal{L}
and
n\in\qd(\lambda)+\mathbb{Z}

arises as
(\overline{{\ell_{0}}},\qq({\ell_{0}}))

for some primitive element
{\ell_{0}}\in L^{\prime}
.

Let
(\lambda,n)
be an admissible index for a Fourier coefficient.
Choose a representative
\ell\in L^{\prime}
with
\overline{\ell}=\lambda
,
meaning
\qq(\ell)\equiv n\mod\mathbb{Z}

and note that the class
\overline{\ell}\in\mathcal{L}
is invariant under translating
\ell
by elements in
H
.
Since
L\simeq K\oplus H
splits a hyperbolic plane
H=\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
,
where
e_{1},e_{2}
denote the standard generators with
\qq(e_{1})=\qq(e_{2})=0
and
\bilf(e_{1},e_{2})=1
, we find that
\qq(\ell)=\qq(\ell|_{K})+\qq(\ell|_{H})

and for all
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
that
\qq(m_{1}e_{1}+m_{2}e_{2})=m_{1}m_{2}

so that the primitive vector

\ell_{\lambda}\coloneq\ell|_{K}\oplus\left[(n-\qq(\ell|_{K}))e_{1}+e_{2}\right]

fulfils the desired property
(\overline{\ell_{\lambda}},\qq(\ell_{\lambda}))=(\lambda,n)
.
∎

We note that the
L
-series
L_{\ell}(f,s)
depends solely on the pair
(\overline{\ell},\qq(\ell))
,
so that in conjunction with Corollary
6.4
we immediately conclude the following assertion.

Corollary 6.7
.

Let
(L,\qq)
be an even
\mathbb{Z}
lattice of signature
({m^{+}},2)
and assume
it splits a hyperbolic plane.
Set
k\coloneq 1+{m^{+}}/2
and suppose that
f\in\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
is annihilated by
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}
.
If
{m^{+}}>3
, then for any
\ell\in L^{\prime}
of positive norm the following special
L
-value vanishes:

L_{\ell}(f,{m^{+}})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{a(n\overline{\ell},\qq(n\ell))}{n^{{m^{+}}}}=0.

As we will see, this also eliminates the second problem in proving the main theorem without resorting to Hecke theory by utilising an inclusion–exclusion trick.

Lemma 6.8
.

Assume
L
splits a hyperbolic plane and
f\in\mathcal{S}_{L,k}
for
{m^{+}}>3
is annihilated by
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}
.
Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be a natural number and
\ell\in L^{\prime}
be of positive norm.
Then

L_{\ell}^{N}(f,{m^{+}})\overset{\textnormal{def}}{=}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
\gcd(n,N)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{a(n\overline{\ell},\qq(n\ell))}{n^{{m^{+}}}}=0.

Note that it suffices to consider square free
N
.
We will prove the assertion by induction on the number of distinct prime divisors of
N
.
The case
N=1
is identical to Corollary
6.7
.
Suppose the statement is true for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
.
Select a prime number
p\nmid N
and note that by the assumption
{m^{+}}>3

absolute convergence guarantees

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
\gcd(n,pN)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{a(n\overline{\ell},\qq(n\ell))}{n^{{m^{+}}}}\;

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
\gcd(n,N)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{a(n\overline{\ell},\qq(n\ell))}{n^{{m^{+}}}}-\frac{1}{p^{{m^{+}}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
\gcd(n,N)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{a(np\overline{\ell},\qq(np\ell))}{n^{{m^{+}}}}

\displaystyle=L_{\ell}^{N}(f,{m^{+}})-\frac{1}{p^{{m^{+}}}}\,L_{p\ell}^{N}(f,{m^{+}}).

Now the right hand side vanishes by the induction hypothesis, completing the proof.
∎

The lemma above is the last ingredient for the proof of the main theorem.

Suppose there was a non-trivial form
f\in\ker({\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}})
with Fourier expansion

f(\tau)=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{n\in\qd(\lambda)+\mathbb{Z}}a(\lambda,n)\cdot e(n\tau)\mathfrak{e}_{\lambda}.

Select a pair
(\lambda,n)
for which
a(\lambda,n)\neq 0
.
By Lemma
6.6
, the hyperbolic split guarantees the existence of some
\ell\in L^{\prime}
of positive norm with
(\overline{\ell},\qq(\ell))=(\lambda,n)
.
Further, the assumption of
{m^{+}}>3
implies that
L_{\ell}(f,{m^{+}})=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{N}}a(d\overline{\ell},d^{2}\qq(\ell))/d^{{m^{+}}}
converges absolutely.
Hence, there must be some number
M\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathbb{N}\\
d>M\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\lvert a(d\overline{\ell},d^{2}\qq(\ell))\rvert}{d^{m^{+}}}<\lvert a(\overline{\ell},\qq(\ell))\rvert.

Consequently, selecting
N\coloneq M!
results in

(6.11)

L_{\ell}^{N}(f,{m^{+}})=a(\overline{\ell},\qq(\ell))+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1<d\in\mathbb{N}\\
\gcd(d,N)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{a(d\overline{\ell},d^{2}\qq(\ell))}{d^{{m^{+}}}}\neq 0.

However,
f
was assumed to be annihilated by
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}
, implying by Lemma
6.8
that the left hand side of (
6.11
) vanishes
– a contradiction!
As a consequence, the assumption of a non-trivial element in the kernel of
{\Lambda_{\textnormal{KM}}}
must be incorrect i.e. the Kudla–Millson lift is injective.
∎
