RNA base-modification and A-to-I editing landscapes of oxidatively stressed human retinal pigment epithelium under Quantum Molecular Resonance electrotherapy and blood-derived secretome
Authors: Domenico Mordà, Simona Alibrandi, Concetta Scimone, Angela D’ascola, Federica Aliquò, Giorgia Abate, Gianantonio Pozzato, Sergio Zaccaria Scalinci, Rosalia D’angelo, Antonina Sidoti, Luigi Donato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChemical modifications of RNA bases and adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing are regulatory layers essentially unexplored in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), whose oxidative dysfunction underlies age-related macular degeneration. We mapped both layers in ARPE-19 cells exposed to tert-butyl hydroperoxide under two non-pharmacological interventions, Quantum Molecular Resonance (QMR) electrotherapy and a patient blood-derived secretome, across 56 libraries spanning four arms, three time points and matched stressed and stress-free conditions (GEO: GSE328981). Base modifications were called with HARMLNC and A-to-I editing screened with a transition-signal caller, then re-analysed with REDItools3, JACUSA2 and LoDEI. The high-confidence set comprised 241 calls at 15 sites, all of classes canonical to tRNA and rRNA. The most prevalent was an adenosine modification at chrM:2617 in mitochondrial 16S rRNA (MT-RNR2), detected in 51 of 56 libraries and in every arm. Two positions 1 bp apart lie in an EZR/SYTL3 overlap region, whereas a third chromosome 6 site maps to HLA-C, 127.5 Mb away. No position of either layer differed between treatment arms: 7 testable modification sites (minimum p = 0.19) and, at single-nucleotide resolution under dedicated callers agreeing to r = 0.975–0.992, 15 curated editing positions (minimum q = 0.169). Two one-sided tests bound any change in substitution composition within 1.4% and in mean editing frequency within 10% for secretome and combination and 20% for QMR. Against a stress-free baseline internal to the cohort, only chrM:13710 (MT-ND5) changed detectably (q = 0.03). These layers are therefore small, largely constitutive and quantifiably undisturbed by either intervention at the resolution available.

Introduction

The RPE under oxidative siege: a convergence of pathogenic axes in AMD

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of irreversible central vision loss in individuals over 60 in industrialized countries, affecting more than 196 million people worldwide and projected to reach 288 million by 2040 [1,2]. No disease-modifying therapy is approved for the prevalent atrophic form, and current anti-VEGF biologics address only the neovascular endpoint [3,4]. The pathobiology of AMD converges on dysfunction of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), a polarized monolayer of post-mitotic cells that sustains photoreceptor survival through phagocytosis of shed outer segments, directional transport, maintenance of the outer blood-retinal barrier and secretion of PEDF, VEGF-A and CNTF [5,6]. The oxidative burden imposed by this metabolic workload renders the RPE vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostatic failure, extracellular-matrix deterioration and chronic para-inflammation [7–9]. In vitro, exposure of ARPE-19 cells to tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) reproduces this milieu, eliciting mitochondrial damage, NF-kB and p38/MAPK activation and glutathione depletion, and is widely used for mechanistic studies of RPE cytoprotection [10,11]. Against this backdrop, Quantum Molecular Resonance (QMR) electrotherapy and patient blood-derived (PBD) secretome have emerged as complementary non-pharmacological interventions engaging multiple cytoprotective axes [12–16].

The epitranscriptome: a chemical modification layer invisible to standard transcriptomics

Each transcript carries a context-specific pattern drawn from over 170 chemically distinct base modifications, which collectively constitute the epitranscriptome [17,18]. These marks, including N1-methyladenosine (m1A), N2,N2-dimethylguanosine (m2G/m22G), 3-methylcytidine (m3C), N6-isopentenyladenosine (i6A), N6-threonylcarbamoyladenosine (t6A), dihydrouridine (D) and pseudouridine (Y), are installed by writer enzymes, removed by erasers and recognized by readers, regulating transcript stability, translation efficiency, RNA structure and RNA–protein interaction [19,20]. In the mitochondrial compartment they are particularly critical: m1A at conserved positions of the 12S and 16S rRNA, catalysed by TFB1M and its functional homologs, is required for ribosomal-subunit assembly and translational fidelity, and its loss causes respiratory-chain dysfunction [21–23]. RNA base modifications in the RPE have been characterized only in limited contexts, primarily ADAR-mediated A-to-I editing in retinal degeneration [24–26]; transcriptomic studies of stressed RPE, including our own analyses of A2E- and blue-light-stressed cells, have addressed abundance and non-coding architecture but not the chemical state of the transcript [27,28]. Standard bulk RNA-seq, including the analyses previously performed on the dataset examined here, is blind to this chemical layer: it captures abundance and splicing but not the modification state of individual nucleotides [29].

Detecting base modifications and A-to-I editing from short-read RNA-seq, and the limits of that approach

Detecting RNA base modifications from standard Illumina short-read RNA-seq exploits the mis-incorporation signatures left by modified bases during reverse transcription. A modified nucleotide may cause misreading, truncation or non-templated insertion at a frequency exceeding the background error rate, and the resulting mismatch pattern can be distinguished from single-nucleotide variants and sequencing noise using probabilistic models [30,31]. HARMLNC, an adaptation of the HAMR framework, implements this principle: candidate sites are identified from non-reference nucleotide frequencies exceeding a background model under a hypergeometric enrichment test (h1) and a four-way substitution-pattern test (h4), and the most likely modification identity is assigned from a curated MODOMICS signature library [18,32,33]. Two limitations of this strategy must be stated at the outset, because they bound every result reported here. First, inference from miscalling is indirect and probabilistic: it detects only those modifications that perturb reverse transcription, it is blind to marks that do not (most notably N6-methyladenosine, m6A, the most abundant internal mRNA modification), and because several modifications share an indistinguishable substitution signature the h4 classifier returns candidate sets rather than single identities. Standard RNA-seq is therefore not an optimal substrate for epitranscriptomic mapping. Modification-specific chemical or antibody-based protocols (MeRIP/m6A-seq and GLORI for m6A, bisulphite sequencing for m5C, RiboMeth-seq for 2’-O-methylation) and native-RNA sequencing on the Oxford Nanopore platform, which reads modified bases directly from the ionic-current signal, are the appropriate methods for definitive detection and stoichiometry [20,29,34–36]. Second, the assignment is a prediction against a signature library, not a chemical measurement, and requires orthogonal confirmation. We therefore treat the HARMLNC output as a screen, report the candidate class rather than a single modification wherever the signature is degenerate, and cross-check the resulting positions against the published literature and variant databases (Section 3.2). As a second, mechanistically distinct layer we ran Tsniffer, a transition-signal detector that flags clustered base-transition regions; in this dataset its output is dominated by A-to-G and T-to-C transitions, the canonical signature of ADAR-mediated A-to-I editing [24–26]. Because A-to-I editing is a deamination rather than a methyl or isopentenyl modification, Tsniffer is an independent screen of a different chemistry, not a confirmatory second caller for the HARMLNC sites. Two conventions are fixed here and used throughout. Site denotes a single-nucleotide genomic coordinate and is reserved for the base-modification layer; window denotes the multi-nucleotide interval reported by the transition-signal caller for the editing layer, for the reasons set out in Section 2.7. All events reported here are post-transcriptional marks on RNA, not modifications of DNA.

The multi-omics series: rationale and objectives

The ARPE-19 RNA-seq dataset analysed here (GEO: GSE328981) spans four treatment conditions (CTRL, QMR/RESONO, SECRETOME, QMR+SECRETOME) across 8 H, 24 H and 72 H, in matched oxidatively stressed and stress-free conditions, and has been the subject of five parallel multi-omics analyses. Published and submitted work from this series has established that: (i) QMR restores a fraction of stress-deregulated microRNAs, engaging NF-kB/NLRP3-regulating (miR-590-3p) and anti-inflammatory (miR-146a-5p) programs, while PBD secretome preferentially induces regenerative miRNA signatures [37]; (ii) bulk RNA-seq revealed QMR-driven transcriptional reprogramming of antioxidant defenses (GCLC, GCLM, GSR, HMOX1), mitochondrial fitness and proteostasis [38]; (iii) the lncRNA/circRNA landscape identified QMR as the dominant driver of transcriptomic variance, with circASPH upregulated by QMR and circCRIM1 hyperactivated by the combination [39]; (iv) oxidative stress induces a chimeric-transcript burden that QMR and secretome attenuate at discrete nodes (CTDNEP1, COL7A1, HSD17B7) [40]; and (v) expressed-variant analysis identified QMR as a mitochondrial-surveillance activator (PINK1 induction, DNM1 suppression) while the secretome drove SERPINF1/PEDF upregulation [41]. None of these has addressed the chemical state of the expressed transcriptome. The present study adds two further layers: a HARMLNC-derived map of RNA base modifications and a genome-wide A-to-I editing screen. They are treated as distinct layers because they differ in chemistry, are detected by different tools and are installed by different enzyme families. The aims are: (i) to define the base-modification set detectable in oxidatively stressed ARPE-19 cells and identify positions present across all conditions; (ii) to test whether QMR or secretome alter that set; (iii) to describe its temporal distribution across 8 H-72 H; (iv) to characterize the A-to-I editing landscape and test, with effect sizes and formal equivalence bounds, whether the interventions alter it; and (v) to relate both layers to the five parallel analyses of the same experiment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, oxidative stress induction and experimental design

Human ARPE-19 cells (ATCC CRL-2302, Manassas, VA, USA) were maintained in DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin at 37 C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cell-line identity was validated by short-tandem-repeat profiling (PowerPlex 10, Promega; STR concordance > 80%), and cultures were confirmed mycoplasma-free every 2 months (MycoAlert PLUS, Lonza). The design comprised four treatment conditions, no-intervention control (CTRL), QMR electrotherapy (RESONO), patient blood-derived secretome (SECRETOME) and their combination (QMR+SECRETOME), across three time points (8 H, 24 H, 72 H), each sampled in two oxidative-stress conditions. In the stressed condition, sub-confluent monolayers were exposed to tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP; Sigma-Aldrich) at a final concentration of 75 uM maintained continuously; the stress-free condition was handled identically without tBHP. Biological replication was n = 1 per stress condition at 8 H and n = 3 at 24 H and 72 H. Full details of seeding density, treatment scheduling, QMR parameters and secretome preparation have been described previously [37,38]. The base-modification analysis reported here draws on 56 sequencing libraries that yielded at least one high-confidence call, comprising 14 libraries per treatment arm (7 oxidatively stressed and 7 stress-free) and, per arm, 2 libraries at 8 H, 6 at 24 H and 6 at 72 H. The A-to-I editing screen was run on 57 libraries; the single additional library has no counterpart in the modification layer and is retained only in the editing analysis, so editing-layer and modification-layer denominators differ by one throughout. A per-library manifest is provided as Supplementary Table S1.

Quantum molecular resonance (QMR) treatment

QMR stimulation was delivered using a benchtop Quantum Molecular Resonance generator (Telea Electronic Engineering srl, Sandrigo, Italy; 230 V, 50/60 Hz, max 250 VA) emitting multifrequency signals with a fundamental frequency of 4 MHz and harmonics from 8 to 64 MHz. Two flat stainless-steel electrodes were inserted at the edge of the 90 mm culture plate until contacting the medium surface. The culture-medium volume was maintained at 12 mL, yielding an estimated electric-field strength of 1.1 ± 0.2 V/cm at 2 mm above the monolayer (validated via Ag/AgCl microelectrodes, n = 3) and a current density of ≈12 mA/cm2. Each QMR session lasted 10 minutes at a nominal power setting of 40 (≈10 W output). Cells assigned to the 8 H and 24 H time points received a single session at t = 0 H; cells at 72 H received three sessions at t = 0 H, ≈24 H and ≈48 H. Sham-treated controls underwent identical plate handling and electrode positioning with the device powered off. Full protocol details are reported in [37,38].

Patient blood-derived secretome preparation and application

The PBD secretome was prepared from peripheral venous blood collected from adult donors under written informed consent and institutional ethics approval (Ethics Committee of A.O.U. G. Martino, Approval No. 858, 23 June 2017). Blood was collected into sodium-citrate tubes, activated with CaCl2 (final 20–25 mM) at 37°C for 1 H followed by overnight incubation at 4°C, and the resulting fibrin clot was removed by centrifugation (2,000–3,000 × g, 10 min). The clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane, quantified by BCA assay, aliquoted and stored at −80°C. For application, thawed aliquots were diluted 1:1 with fresh culture medium (final 50% v/v). Full preparation and quality-control details are reported in [37,38].

RNA extraction, library preparation and RNA-seq data

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen) or the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen) with on-column DNase I digestion. RNA integrity was assessed by automated capillary electrophoresis (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer; RIN ≥ 7.0). A single library chemistry was used throughout: libraries were mRNA-enriched or ribosomal-RNA-depleted (poly(A) selection or TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero), followed by fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation and PCR. Paired-end libraries (2 × 150 bp) were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 to a target depth of ≥ 30 million read pairs per sample. Raw data are deposited under GEO accession GSE328981; per-sample read quality was assessed with FastQC. In the deposited sample identifiers the token OX denotes the oxidatively stressed condition and its absence the stress-free condition; it does not denote a library preparation, since all libraries were prepared with the same chemistry.

Read alignment and pre-processing for modification calling

Raw reads were quality-trimmed (Trimmomatic v0.39; ILLUMINACLIP TruSeq3-PE.fa:2:30:10, LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15, MINLEN:36) and aligned to GRCh38/hg38 with STAR v2.7.10a in two-pass mode, with parameters critical for modification calling (–outSAMattributes NH HI AS NM MD; –outFilterMismatchNmax 999; –outFilterMismatchNoverReadLmax 0.04; –alignIntronMin 20; –alignIntronMax 1,000,000). Soft-clipping was disabled (–alignEndsType EndToEnd) to prevent soft-clipped mismatches from inflating false modification signals. Duplicates were marked with Picard MarkDuplicates and retained for modification calling, since PCR duplicates at genuine modification sites carry the same mismatch pattern and their removal could reduce sensitivity for low-abundance modifications. A minimum mapping quality of MAPQ ≥ 20 was applied throughout to exclude multi-mapping reads.

HARMLNC modification calling pipeline

HARMLNC was applied to all BAM files following the HAMR framework [32,33] with lncRNA-inclusive adaptations. For each position meeting coverage criteria (depth ≥ 10; non-reference fraction ≥ 0.05), two sequential tests were performed. Test h1 (hypergeometric enrichment) evaluated whether the observed non-reference frequency exceeded the expected background mis-incorporation rate estimated from positions with no predicted modification; positions with h1 p < 0.01 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction proceeded to the second test. Test h4 (substitution-pattern classification) assessed which curated MODOMICS signature best explained the observed substitution profile, assigning modifications to one of m1A, m1I, ms2i6A, m2G, m22G, m3C, i6A, t6A, D or Y. Per-sample outputs (chromosome, position, strand, reference nucleotide, modification identity, h1 and h4 p-values, depth, non-reference count) and feature-annotated BED files were merged into a consolidated modification matrix retaining site coordinates, sample identity, group, time point and statistical metrics. We excluded positions overlapping common dbSNP b151 polymorphisms (MAF > 1%) to minimize germline-variant confounding. This filter removes only common variants; the re-annotation reported in Supplementary Table S4 queries the full current dbSNP catalogue, so a position can pass the calling filter and still carry a variant flag if the overlapping variant is rare or has no recorded population frequency, as is the case for the flags noted at chrM:13710 and chr6:31270453.

A-to-I editing analysis (Tsniffer transition-signal caller)

As a distinct molecular layer, RNA A-to-I editing was profiled with the Tsniffer transition-signal caller, run on the same aligned samples (57 libraries). Tsniffer flags clustered base-transition regions per chromosome and reports, for each region, the transition type, the average target transition frequency (editing frequency), the number of constituent transition sites, the region length and a dbSNP-overlap warning. Two properties of this output constrain its interpretation and are stated here rather than in the limitations alone. First, the caller reports windows, not single nucleotides: across the regions analysed the median width is 372 bp (range 106–2509 bp; none of width 1), so each reported unit pools many editable positions and a per-window frequency is an average over them, not a site stoichiometry. We therefore use the term window rather than site or locus for Tsniffer output throughout, and reserve site for single-nucleotide positions. Second, the caller is strand-unaware: an A-to-I event on a minus-strand transcript is recorded as a T-to-C transition in genome coordinates, so the near-equal A-to-G and T-to-C counts represent one chemistry recorded in two orientations rather than two signals, and we report their sum as the A-to-I total and their ratio only as a quality metric. Across the 57 runs the output comprised approximately 499,000 transition regions, of which about 89% were A-to-G or T-to-C. Regions flagged with a dbSNP/SNP warning were excluded to limit germline-variant contamination (108,449 removed; 334,236 retained). To avoid the coverage dependence of raw region counts, the primary metric was a frequency-based per-sample editing index, defined as the mean editing frequency across that sample’s retained A-to-I regions, compared across treatments (Kruskal–Wallis). For window-level differential analysis, overlapping regions were merged into consensus windows; windows recurrently detected (≥20 libraries, all four treatment arms represented) were tested for a treatment effect on per-sample editing frequency by Kruskal–Wallis, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all tested windows. Because a rolling-window caller of this kind is designed for landscape-level survey and is not the appropriate instrument for resolving individual recoding events, we additionally measured editing directly at a panel of canonical recoding positions at single-nucleotide resolution (Section 2.15).

Definition of the high-confidence base-modification set

The high-confidence base-modification set was defined from HARMLNC calls passing the h1 and h4 statistical thresholds and the replicate and SNP filters described above. The resulting set comprises 241 calls across 56 sequencing libraries, resolving to 15 unique genomic sites and 17 site-modification-class combinations. Positional comparison with the Tsniffer transition screen showed that only the mitochondrial position chrM:2617 was shared between the two readouts, consistent with their detection of distinct modification classes. Per-call read support (non-reference and reference counts, depth and per-test p-values) for every one of the 241 calls is provided in Supplementary Table S2, and the annotated site-level summary in Supplementary Table S3.

Genomic annotation and AMD/RPE gene prioritization

High-confidence sites were annotated with BEDTools intersect against Ensembl/GENCODE comprehensive gene annotation for feature-level classification (CDS, UTR, exon, intron, intergenic) and gene assignment, against MODOMICS for modification-class functional annotation and against curated AMD/RPE gene resources consistent with the prioritization framework of the parallel expressed-variant analysis [41]. Mitochondrial sites were mapped to the human mitochondrial genome (NC_012920.1) and annotated against MitoCarta. Sites within 50 bp of known ADAR A-to-I editing positions (RADAR, REDIportal) were flagged to prevent misclassification of editing events as base modifications.

Cluster analysis, visualization and statistics

Sample relationships were examined from a binary presence/absence matrix of high-confidence sites (R package pheatmap; Euclidean distance, Ward’s linkage); group-level frequency maps were generated with ggplot2 and genomic tracks with karyoploteR/circlize. Per-site detection frequency, per-treatment detection counts (out of 14 libraries per treatment arm), per-time-point counts and mean non-reference frequency were computed directly from the consolidated matrix. For sites detected in at least six libraries (7 of 15 sites), a treatment effect on non-reference frequency was tested by one-way ANOVA across arms; sites below that threshold were not tested. Six of the seven were detected in all four arms; the seventh, chr21:8401440, was absent from CTRL and was tested across the three arms in which it was detected. Analyses were performed in R v4.3.1 and Python v3.9 (pandas, scipy). Several descriptive terms recur below; we fix their meaning here and use them only as defined. Constitutive denotes a position detected in at least 75% of libraries and in all four treatment arms; it is a statement of detection prevalence, not of biological necessity. Most prevalent position denotes the single site with the highest detection count and is used descriptively. Non-reference frequency denotes the fraction of reads carrying a non-reference base at a called position; we do not describe any site as high-stoichiometry, because reverse-transcriptase miscalling rates are a function of both modification occupancy and the efficiency with which a given mark perturbs the enzyme, and therefore do not measure stoichiometry. Treatment-leaning denotes a position detected in more libraries of one arm than of the others in the absence of a statistically significant difference in frequency, and is a descriptive statement about detection counts only. We make no claim of synergy, since demonstrating it would require an effect exceeding both monotherapies with statistical support, which these data do not provide. Given the limited number of unique sites and the low per-site library counts, between-arm comparisons of detection counts are reported descriptively and require validation in larger cohorts.

Software versions and computational environment

Analyses were performed on a high-performance computing cluster (CentOS 7.9) with the following principal software packages: Trimmomatic v0.39, STAR v2.7.10a, Picard v2.27.5, BEDTools v2.30.0, SAMtools v1.15.1; R v4.3.1 with pheatmap v1.0.12, ggplot2 v3.4.2, rstatix v0.7.2, karyoploteR v1.20.0, circlize v0.4.15 and ComplexHeatmap v2.12.1; and Python v3.9.7 with pandas v1.5.3 and scipy v1.10.1 for data integration and cross-caller concordance. The HARMLNC pipeline was executed using the published HAMR framework code with custom adaptations for lncRNA inclusion; Tsniffer Canonical was run with the human reference modification panel (MODOMICS 2022 + RMBase v2.0). Custom scripts are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Oxidative-stress baseline: the stress-free contrast internal to the cohort

Because the corrected library metadata (Section 2.4) establishes that each treatment arm was sampled both under continuous tBHP and in the stress-free condition, the cohort contains an internal unstressed baseline. We used it to ask whether oxidative stress itself alters the positions reported here. For every high-confidence site and for every position of the canonical recoding panel (Section 2.15), non-reference frequency was extracted from all libraries with read coverage at that coordinate, irrespective of whether the position had been called in that library, so that the contrast is a comparison of frequencies rather than of detection events. Stress-free and tBHP-exposed libraries were compared by Mann-Whitney U test across the full set and, in parallel, by Wilcoxon signed-rank test on replicate-matched pairs (same arm, time point and replicate), with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all positions tested. Results are reported in Section 3.10, with per-position depth and testability in Supplementary Table S6 and test results in Supplementary Table S7. An independent stress-free/tBHP library set external to this cohort, used only as a consistency check, is reported in Supplementary Table S8.

Effect sizes, confidence intervals and equivalence testing

A non-significant null-hypothesis test does not establish equivalence. For every treatment comparison in the editing layer, we therefore report the estimated difference from control, its relative size and a 90% confidence interval, and we test equivalence formally by two one-sided tests (TOST) against pre-specified margins of 10%, 15% and 20% of the control mean, fitted in a linear model with library as the unit of analysis (57 libraries, 50 residual degrees of freedom). The metrics tested were the mean and median per-sample editing frequency, the number of retained editing regions and the A-to-G fraction of transitions, the last as a measure of substitution composition. We report the smallest margin at which equivalence is supported for each metric and comparison, and we state explicitly where equivalence is not supported at any tested margin. A power calculation for the modification layer is given in Supplementary Table S12. Full equivalence results are in Supplementary Table S11.

Canonical recoding panel and orthogonal checks on the editing layer

To test editing at positions established in the literature rather than only at de novo caller output, we assembled a panel of 13 canonical A-to-I recoding sites in coding sequence (GRIA2 Q607R and R764G, GRIK2 I567V and Y571C, CYFIP2 K320E, BLCAP Y2C and Q5R, IGFBP7 K95R, FLNA Q2341R, COPA I164V, GABRA3 I342M, AZIN1 S367G, NEIL1 K242R). Each position was verified independently before use by two checks: retrieval of the Ensembl transcript codon at the annotated CDS offset, and a strand-aware query of the reference genome base, requiring A on plus-strand and T on minus-strand transcripts. All 13 passed both checks (Supplementary Table S10). Editing at these coordinates was then quantified directly from the alignments at single-nucleotide resolution, independently of the Tsniffer windows, and compared across treatment arms and between stress-free and tBHP conditions. We also asked how many of the 13 fell within any reported Tsniffer window, as a bound on the sensitivity of the window-based screen. Recurrent editing windows were additionally evaluated for two candidate artefact classes, coincidence with catalogued germline variants (dbSNP) and a reference base incompatible with A-to-I chemistry, each assessed across the full extent of the window rather than at its start coordinate. Neither discriminates among windows at this resolution (Section 3.7), and neither was applied as a filter. Window extents, gene overlap, editable-position counts and variant density are reported in Supplementary Table S9.

Orthogonal editing callers and de novo editome

Because a window-based transition detector is not the appropriate instrument for site-resolved editing analysis, the editing layer was re-examined with three dedicated tools. REDItools3 [42] was run in known-sites mode across 44 libraries at the 28 curated positions of the recoding panel and the reported modification-layer coordinates, and its per-site frequencies were compared against an independent pysam pileup of the same alignments. JACUSA2 [43] was run in call-2 mode on the same coordinates and, separately, on the de novo candidate set. A de novo transcriptome-wide scan across 16 libraries provided the discovery set, from which recurrent A-to-G candidates were retained (detected in at least 8 of 16 libraries at frequency below 0.35) and cross-checked with the second caller; candidates were annotated for repeat context against RepeatMasker, with germline homozygous positions and the curated recoding panel serving as negative controls for Alu enrichment. Differential editing was tested with LoDEI [44] (v1.1.3, ISR mode, window 25, m = 10, –rm_snps, q < 0.05), which scans a sliding window and reports a windowed editing index. Because that scan carries no built-in specificity control, we repeated it identically on each of the 11 non-A-to-G mismatch types as an internal null, and we ran the oxidative-stress contrast three ways: across all libraries, within the single sequencing batch balanced for stress state, and as a batch contrast matched for stress state, so that the technical and biological axes could be separated. Treatment-arm effects at the curated positions were tested by Kruskal–Wallis across the four arms on libraries with coverage of at least 20 reads, and oxidative-stress effects at the same positions by Mann-Whitney U, each with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across sites. Results are given in Supplementary Tables S14 to S17.

Results

Scope and composition of the detected base-modification set

Across the analysed cohort, the high-confidence modification-call set comprised 241 calls distributed over 56 sequencing libraries (14 per treatment arm), resolving to 15 unique genomic sites and 17 site-modification-class combinations, two sites carrying two class assignments each Figure 1(A,B). Six modification classes were represented: i6A/t6A (71 calls), m1A/m1I/ms2i6A (63), dihydrouridine D (52), m2G/m22G (41), m3C (13) and pseudouridine Y (1). Combined labels such as i6A/t6A or m1A/m1I/ms2i6A denote modifications that share an indistinguishable substitution signature under the h4 classifier and cannot be resolved individually from short-read data; the slash marks a candidate set, not the co-occurrence of all listed modifications. It is worth stating plainly what this composition implies: every class recovered here is one canonically associated with transfer RNA [45,46] or ribosomal RNA [47], and the genomic contexts are mitochondrial rRNA, rDNA-bearing contigs and the chromosome 21 rDNA cluster. No modification class specific to messenger RNA was detected, and m6A, the most abundant internal mRNA mark, is not detectable by this method (Section 1.3). The set is therefore best described as a small, largely tRNA/rRNA-derived modification set rather than a transcriptome-wide landscape. Sites mapped to eight chromosomes and contigs (chr2, chr6, chr7, chr11, chr21, chrM, GL000220.1, KI270733.1), with chromosome 6 and the mitochondrial genome contributing the most prevalent and most deeply covered positions. The arm-level call burden was highest in SECRETOME (71 calls), followed by QMR+SECRETOME (62), QMR (56) and CTRL (52) Figure 1(C). Six sites were detected in all four treatment arms, and four sites were each detected in a single library, one in each arm Figure 1(D); Section 3.4. These are counts of detection events, not frequency measurements; the corresponding test of treatment effect on non-reference frequency is reported in Section 4.4. Per-chromosome site counts, the genomic position and modification class of each site, and the two mitochondrial positions are shown in Figure S2.

The most prevalent and most deeply covered position: chrM:2617 in the mitochondrial 16S rRNA (MT-RNR2)

The single most prevalent and most deeply covered event was a modification at chrM:2617, mapping to the mitochondrial 16S rRNA (MT-RNR2). It was detected in 51 of 56 libraries (91%), in all four treatment arms (CTRL 12/14, QMR 12/14, SECRETOME 14/14, QMR+SECRETOME 13/14) and at all three time points, with the highest read depth of any site (maximum 71,462) and a mean non-reference frequency of 0.38 (Table 1). By the definition fixed in Section 2.10 it is one of three constitutive positions in the set, together with chr6:158766314 (49/56, 88%) and KI270733.1:134740 (42/56, 75%). The h4 classifier assigns it to the degenerate m1A/m1I/ms2i6A signature class and cannot discriminate among those candidates from short-read data. We therefore checked the position against the published record rather than asserting an identity: N1-methyladenosine has been mapped in human mitochondrial 16S rRNA in this region by dedicated analyses of mitochondrial m1A/G modification [48], and the position is consistent with the TFB1M-dependent adenosine methylation required for mitoribosomal assembly and translational fidelity [21–23]. The most probable identity is therefore m1A, and we use that assignment only with this qualification. A one-way ANOVA across treatment arms gave no evidence of a treatment effect on non-reference frequency at this position (F = 1.10, p = 0.36); the largest deviation of any arm mean from control was 0.067 frequency units. The site is detected in every arm, at comparable frequency, under all conditions tested.

A second mitochondrial position at chrM:13710, mapping to NADH dehydrogenase subunit 5 (MT-ND5), carried the same degenerate signature class but was of markedly lower prevalence (4/56) and lower frequency (mean 0.09), restricted to SECRETOME (2/14) and QMR+SECRETOME (2/14) samples, with no calls in CTRL or QMR alone. This position is likewise reported as m1A in the mitochondrial modification literature [48]. Its caller-assigned reference nucleotide disagrees with the Ensembl reference base, and it coincides with a catalogued germline variant (rs200295632); both flags are recorded in Supplementary Table S4. With four supporting libraries and these annotation caveats, the secretome-restricted detection pattern is reported as an observation requiring confirmation, not as a treatment effect. The one contrast in which this position does change detectably is the oxidative-stress comparison of Section 3.10.

Two adjacent chromosome 6 positions in an EZR/SYTL3 overlap region, and a third position that maps to HLA-C

Three high-confidence sites lie on chromosome 6, and they do not constitute a single locus. Two are adjacent, separated by a single base pair, and fall in a region where the GENCODE annotations of EZR and SYTL3 overlap. The third lies 127.5 Mb away, on the opposite arm of the chromosome, and maps to HLA-C at 6p21.33. The three are therefore described separately below.

chr6:158,766,314 (i6A/t6A), EZR/SYTL3 overlap region. This was the most prevalent non-mitochondrial position in the dataset, detected in 49 of 56 libraries (88%) across all four arms (CTRL 12, QMR 13, SECRETOME 13, QMR+SECRETOME 11 of 14 each), with the highest mean non-reference frequency of any site (0.85; maximum depth 261). It meets the operational definition of a constitutive position. There was no treatment effect on frequency (F = 0.97, p = 0.42; largest arm deviation from control 0.020).

chr6:158,766,313 (m3C), EZR/SYTL3 overlap region. Immediately adjacent to the position above, this 3-methylcytidine call was detected in 13 of 56 libraries (23%) at high frequency where present (mean 0.79; maximum depth 282), with detection counts of CTRL 3, QMR 1, SECRETOME 4 and QMR+SECRETOME 5 of 14. The detection pattern leans towards the secretome-containing arms, but the frequency where the site is called does not differ across arms (F = 0.71, p = 0.57; largest arm deviation 0.045), and with 13 positive libraries the detection-count pattern is descriptive only. Both this position and its neighbour fall within a window carrying catalogued germline variation (three dbSNP records, 17 variant alleles; Supplementary Table S4), which is an alternative source of non-reference reads that the present data cannot exclude.

chr6:31,270,453 (m2G/m22G), HLA-C. This position maps to HLA-C in the MHC class I region at 6p21.33. It was detected in 40 of 56 libraries (71%) at uniformly low non-reference frequency (mean 0.13) but at the second-highest read depth in the dataset (maximum 2,310; mean depth at called positions approximately 1,100), with balanced detection across arms (CTRL 11, QMR 9, SECRETOME 9, QMR+SECRETOME 11 of 14) and no treatment effect (F = 0.80, p = 0.50; largest arm deviation 0.007). We flag a specific and serious alternative explanation for this signal. HLA-C is among the most polymorphic loci in the human genome, the position coincides with catalogued variation (rs1050716, rs796887986), and a low, stable non-reference fraction at high depth in a hyperpolymorphic gene is the expected signature of allelic mismatch between the sample and the reference haplotype rather than of a chemical modification. Because the present data cannot distinguish these possibilities, this position is reported as a candidate requiring allele-aware re-analysis, and no biological interpretation is placed on it.

Taken together, the chromosome 6 signal comprises one adjacent pair of positions in an EZR/SYTL3 overlap region, of which one is constitutive and one is low-prevalence, and one unrelated position in HLA-C for which a germline explanation is at least as plausible as a modification. Because these data resolve neither which transcript carries the adjacent pair nor whether the marks have any functional consequence, we draw no mechanistic inference from them; Section 4.2 states what the observation supports and Section 5.7 the experiments that would test it.

Detection-count differences between arms are not accompanied by frequency differences

chr7:102568040 (i6A/t6A). A position at chr7:102568040, in a transcript without a gene symbol at this coordinate, carrying an i6A/t6A signature with a minor m1A/m1I/ms2i6A call, was detected in 18 of 56 libraries (32%; mean frequency 0.56; maximum depth 64), with detection counts of SECRETOME 8, QMR+SECRETOME 5, QMR 3 and CTRL 2 of 14. This is the largest detection-count skew in the dataset and is best described as secretome-leaning in the sense defined in Section 2.10. It is not accompanied by a difference in non-reference frequency between arms (F = 1.59, p = 0.24), which is the largest arm deviation observed at any testable site (0.106 frequency units) and still not significant Figure 3(B).

chr21 cluster and contig positions. A cluster of low-prevalence positions on chromosome 21 (8218402 D; 8,401,440 D; 8,442,467 m1A/m1I/ms2i6A; 8,445,672 i6A/t6A) was detected in 2 to 9 libraries each and distributed across QMR, SECRETOME and combination samples rather than being exclusive to any arm; the most prevalent, chr21:8401440, was detected in 9 libraries (CTRL 0, QMR 2, SECRETOME 5, QMR+SECRETOME 2) with no treatment effect on frequency (F = 0.95, p = 0.44). These coordinates lie in the chromosome 21 rDNA cluster. A dihydrouridine position at KI270733.1:134740, also an rDNA-bearing contig, was broadly detected (42/56; CTRL 10, QMR 12, SECRETOME 11, QMR+SECRETOME 9) with no treatment effect (F = 1.66, p = 0.19), the smallest p-value among the seven testable sites. Four sites were each detected in exactly one library, one in each treatment arm: chr2:113708616 (CTRL), GL000220.1:117877 (QMR), chr11:65506159 (SECRETOME) and GL000220.1:114669 (QMR+SECRETOME, at 72 H). These are singleton observations and support no inference, and no arm is distinguished by them Figure 3(A,C). Taken together, of the 15 sites, 7 met the prespecified threshold for testing (detection in at least six libraries; six of the seven in all four arms, chr21:8401440 in three) and none showed a significant treatment effect on non-reference frequency; the smallest p-value across the seven was 0.19, and no p-value approached significance after any correction (Supplementary Table S13). The arm-level differences visible in this dataset are therefore differences in how often a position is called, not in how modified it is where it is called, and the former is sensitive to depth and to detection thresholds.

Apparent temporal accrual is accounted for by library number

Modification calls were more numerous at the later time points (72 H: 111 calls; 24 H: 97; 8 H: 33). This pattern does not survive normalization by library number. The cohort contains 8 libraries at 8 H and 24 at each of 24 H and 72 H, because biological replication was n = 1 per stress condition at 8 H and n = 3 at 24 H and 72 H (Section 2.1). Expressed per library, the call rate is essentially flat: 4.12 calls per library at 8 H, 4.04 at 24 H and 4.62 at 72 H, and the mean number of unique sites per library follows the same profile (4.12, 4.04 and 4.62, with standard deviations of 1.76, 1.62 and 1.68). The modest excess at 72 H is within the scatter between libraries at a single time point. At 8 H the calls are dominated by the constitutive positions (chrM:2617 and chr6:158766314); the low-prevalence positions appear at 24 H and 72 H, which is the expected consequence of sampling three times as many libraries. The data are therefore consistent with a modification set that is stable across the 8 H to 72 H window, observed with unequal sampling effort, and do not support a staged temporal programme (Figure S3).

The A-to-I editing layer: no detectable treatment effect, with equivalence bounds

As a distinct molecular layer, we profiled RNA A-to-I editing with the Tsniffer transition-signal caller on the same samples (57 libraries; Section 2.1). A-to-I editing is chemically distinct from the base modifications of Sections 3.1 to 3.6: it is the deamination of adenosine to inosine by ADAR enzymes, not a methyl or isopentenyl modification, so the two layers report different chemistry, and the two readouts share only the mitochondrial position chrM:2617. After exclusion of SNP-flagged regions (108,449 removed), 334,236 A-to-I editing regions were retained. The signal carried the expected hallmarks of ADAR-mediated editing: the A-to-G and T-to-C counts were near-equal (169,592 and 164,644; A-to-G fraction 0.507, standard deviation across libraries 0.007), which is the signature of one chemistry recorded in two orientations by a strand-unaware caller rather than of two distinct signals (Section 2.7); the regions were enriched on the most Alu-dense chromosomes (chr1, chr19 and chr17 contributing the largest counts; Figure 4(B); and they spanned a broad frequency range (median 0.022, interquartile range 0.007 to 0.085; Figure 4(C). Consensus merging defined 61,278 editing windows, of which 4,170 were recurrent in at least 10 libraries and 151 in at least 28. These units are windows of median width 372 bp, not single nucleotides, so a per-window frequency averages over many editable positions, and we use the term window rather than locus or site for them throughout.

Against this landscape we tested whether QMR or secretome alter editing, reporting effect sizes and equivalence bounds rather than a null-hypothesis test alone. The frequency-based global editing index did not differ across arms (stress-free libraries: CTRL 0.061, QMR 0.068, SECRETOME 0.061, QMR+SECRETOME 0.059; Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.68; tBHP-exposed libraries: p = 0.61; Figure 4(A); per-library values in Supplementary Table S5). Because failure to reject a null hypothesis does not establish equivalence, we bounded the effects by two one-sided tests (Section 2.14; Supplementary Table S11). For the A-to-G fraction of transitions, a measure of substitution composition, all three comparisons against control were equivalent at the tightest margin tested: estimated differences of −0.05% (QMR), +0.003% (SECRETOME) and −0.52% (QMR+SECRETOME), with 90% confidence intervals contained within 1.4% in every case. For the mean per-sample editing frequency the bounds are looser and differ by arm: SECRETOME (+1.7%, 90% CI −5.8% to +9.2%) and QMR+SECRETOME (+0.3%, 90% CI −7.2% to +7.8%) are equivalent to control within a 10% margin, whereas QMR (+7.6%, 90% CI + 0.2% to +15.1%) is equivalent only within a 20% margin and is not equivalent at 10% or 15%. We state this asymmetry explicitly: for QMR, changes in mean editing frequency of up to roughly 15% are not excluded by these data. At the window level, none of the 940 recurrent editing windows testable across all four arms showed a treatment effect surviving Benjamini–Hochberg correction (0/940 at FDR < 0.05; minimum adjusted p approximately 0.99; Figure 4(D). Twenty-three windows reached nominal p < 0.05; at 940 tests approximately 47 are expected by chance, so the nominal hits are fewer than chance expectation and provide no evidence of a treatment effect. They are not reported as candidates.

Gene annotation of the recurrent editing windows against Ensembl (GRCh38) is given in Table 3. Because the reported units are windows spanning 116 to 2,509 bp rather than single nucleotides, we examined two candidate quality filters and report that neither is valid at this resolution. The first was the identity of the reference base at the coordinate reported for each window. That coordinate is the window start, not an edited position: retrieving the reference sequence of each window shows that all contain many editable positions (58, 439 and 176 adenosines plus thymidines in the three windows whose start base is C or G), so the base at the boundary carries no information about whether A-to-I events occur within. In particular the NDUFS1 window (chr2:206,118,291–206,119,146) overlaps a minus-strand transcript, on which A-to-I editing is recorded as T-to-C in genome coordinates, and contains 200 thymidines. The second was coincidence with catalogued germline variants, which proves equally uninformative: variant density is uniformly high across every window (0.30 to 0.51 per base pair, Ensembl variation), reflecting the density of dbSNP rather than any property of these particular regions. We therefore apply neither filter and retain the windows as called. One caveat does discriminate. Two windows carry a near-saturation mean frequency, chr2:63,622,533–63,622,727 at 0.99 and chr12:12,851,102–12,851,217 at 0.97, against a genome-wide median of 0.022, and both overlap processed pseudogenes (PRELID1P6 and an RPS6-derived pseudogene). A mean approaching completion across a window containing dozens of editable positions is not a plausible ADAR phenotype, whereas systematic mismatch to the reference through paralogous alignment produces exactly this signature. These two are therefore excluded from the constitutive set rather than interpreted as hyper-edited positions. Extended quantitative and quality-control analyses

To move beyond presence and absence summaries, we examined the quantitative structure and robustness of the data directly. Hierarchical clustering of non-reference frequencies across the 15 sites and 56 libraries recovered the three constitutive positions (chrM:2617, chr6:158766314 and KI270733.1:134740) as a block detected in most libraries, with the low-prevalence positions forming sparser clusters; a per-site analysis with 95% confidence intervals showed broadly overlapping arm means at every testable site, consistent with the absence of significant treatment effects reported in Sections 3.3 and 3.4 (Figure 5). Principal-component analysis of the modification profiles did not separate the treatment arms along the first two principal components, and the number of sites detected per library was independent of read depth, indicating that the detection pattern is not a simple coverage artefact (Figure S5). A repeat-context analysis of sampled recurrent editing windows showed that editing within Alu and SINE elements was uniformly low-level (median approximately 0.03), whereas the rare near-fully-edited windows lay outside annotated repeats; as noted in Section 3.7, those high-frequency windows are the ones most likely to be germline in origin, and this panel should be read with that caveat (Figure 6). Complementary per-sample quality metrics, read-depth distributions and modification-class frequency profiles are shown in Figure S1.

Editing at canonical recoding positions measured at single-nucleotide resolution

Because a rolling-window caller is not the appropriate instrument for resolving individual recoding events, we measured editing directly at 13 canonical A-to-I recoding positions in coding sequence, each independently verified by codon retrieval and a strand-aware reference-base check before use (Section 2.15; Supplementary Table S10). Three results follow. First, none of the 13 positions falls inside any of the editing windows reported by the window-based screen. This bounds the sensitivity of that screen directly: it surveys the repeat-associated editing landscape but does not recover the canonical recoding sites, and no claim about recoding should be drawn from its output. Second, coverage rather than editing limits what can be measured in this cell type. Of the 13 positions, 7 reached testable depth across the cohort (median depth 128 to 3,606): BLCAP Y2C (median editing frequency 0.093), BLCAP Q5R (0.055), COPA I164V (0.039), IGFBP7 K95R (0.028), AZIN1 S367G (0.007), FLNA Q2341R (0.0003) and CYFIP2 K320E (0.000). The remaining 6 were not measurable: GRIA2 Q607R and R764G, GRIK2 I567V and Y571C and GABRA3 I342M are essentially unexpressed in ARPE-19 (median depth 0 to 1), and NEIL1 K242R fell below the depth threshold (median 12). The measurable values are consistent with the expected profile of a non-neuronal epithelial cell, in which the well-known neuronal recoding substrates are absent and editing is confined to the broadly expressed targets. Third, none of the 7 measurable positions differed significantly between treatment arms, nor between oxidatively stressed and stress-free libraries after correction (Section 3.10; all q > 0.4). We note one negative control worth reporting: in an independent, smaller library set NEIL1 K242R appeared strongly stress-associated when reads were pooled (Fisher p = 3.7e-06), but the apparent effect does not survive correction for between-library overdispersion (quasi-binomial p = 0.13; Mann-Whitney across libraries p = 0.057), and we therefore do not report it as a finding (Supplementary Table S8).

The stress-free baseline internal to the cohort

Every treatment arm was sampled both under continuous tBHP and in the stress-free condition, so the effect of oxidative stress itself on these positions can be measured. Using all libraries with read coverage at each coordinate irrespective of whether a call was made there (44 libraries: 23 stress-free, 21 tBHP-exposed), we compared non-reference frequency between conditions at every high-confidence site and at every position of the recoding panel, by Mann-Whitney U test and by Wilcoxon signed-rank test on 19 replicate-matched pairs, with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all positions (Section 2.13). One position changed detectably. At chrM:13710 (MT-ND5) the non-reference frequency was lower under oxidative stress than in the stress-free condition (0.013 versus 0.025; difference −0.012; unpaired q = 0.029; paired Wilcoxon q = 0.0037). No other position survived correction. In particular chrM:2617, the most prevalent position in the dataset, did not differ significantly between stressed and stress-free libraries (0.310 versus 0.361; difference −0.051; unpaired q = 0.44), and neither did the two EZR/SYTL3 positions, the HLA-C position, chr7:102568040 or any measurable recoding position. Two conclusions follow, and both bear directly on the interpretation of the treatment comparisons. First, the modification set described here is not a stress-induced landscape: with the exception of MT-ND5 it is present at comparable frequency with and without tBHP, which supports its description as constitutive but removes any basis for calling it an oxidative-stress signature. Second, the persistence of chrM:2617 at comparable frequency in both conditions indicates that the oxidative paradigm employed here does not suppress mitoribosomal methylation at the level detectable by this assay. Per-position depth and testability are given in Supplementary Table S6 and the full test results in Supplementary Table S7; an independent, smaller stress-free/tBHP library set external to this cohort is reported separately in Supplementary Table S8.

Three orthogonal callers reproduce the editing quantification and detect no treatment effect

The editing layer was re-analysed with three dedicated callers (Section 2.16). Three results follow, bearing respectively on the reliability of the quantification, on the treatment question and on what limits this cohort. First, the quantification is reproducible. Across the curated positions REDItools3 and JACUSA2 agree with each other and with an independent pileup of the same alignments to Pearson r = 0.975 to 0.992 at coverage of at least 20 reads, and the two callers agree on the per-library Alu editing index to r = 0.95 (Supplementary Table S14). The de novo scan recovers the expected biology: of 12,376 candidate editing positions, 88.3% fall within Alu elements, an 8.4-fold enrichment over the genomic Alu fraction, whereas germline homozygous positions called from the same libraries show 5.3% Alu occupancy and the curated recoding panel 3.6%, as expected for non-repeat coding positions (Supplementary Table S17). The scan also recovers known recoding targets directly, including NEIL1 K242R, BLCAP Y2C and Q5R, and COPA I164V. Second, and directly addressing the possibility that individual sites might be dysregulated even where bulk activity is not, no site shows a treatment effect. Of the curated positions, 15 reached testable coverage in all four arms; none differed between arms after correction (minimum p = 0.169, minimum q = 0.169; Supplementary Table S15), and the Alu editing index likewise did not differ between arms under either caller (p = 0.82 and p = 0.97; p = 0.97 within the batch-balanced subset). The site-level result therefore agrees with the window-level result of Section 3.7 and with the equivalence bounds reported there. Third, the oxidative-stress contrast reproduces the one positive finding of Section 3.10 independently: at chrM:13710 (MT-ND5) the REDItools3 quantification confirms a lower frequency under stress (q = 0.00055), as does the HLA-C position (q = 0.0035), the latter carrying the allelic caveat of Section 3.3. NEIL1 K242R reaches q = 0.048 on 18 libraries, consistent with the marginal status assigned to it in Section 3.9.

The differential scan also identifies what dominates variation in this cohort, and it is technical. LoDEI returned 745 significant A-to-G windows of 52,239 tested in the oxidative-stress contrast, against exactly zero significant windows in each of the 11 control mismatch types scanned identically, which establishes that the signal is specific to the A-to-I substitution rather than a generic artefact (Supplementary Table S16); 87.5% of the significant windows lie in Alu elements against 8.0% of randomly sampled tested windows. That contrast is, however, confounded, because the three libraries of the second sequencing batch all fall in one stress group. Breaking the confound in both directions locates the variation: a batch contrast matched for stress state returns 2.65% of windows significant, whereas the stress contrast restricted to the single balanced batch returns 0.66% and is only 81% one-directional, against 100% for batch. The dominant axis of differential editing in this dataset is therefore the sequencing batch; a smaller stress-associated component survives batch control but is not separable from residual technical variation at eight libraries per group, and no component is attributable to treatment. We report this as a property of the cohort rather than as a biological finding, and state it among the limitations.

Discussion

The mitochondrial 16S rRNA position and what its persistence does, and does not, show

The most robust observation of this study is the detection of a modification at chrM:2617 of MT-RNR2 in 51 of 56 libraries, at the highest read depth in the dataset, in every treatment arm and, as Section 3.10 shows directly, at comparable frequency with and without oxidative stress. The position corresponds to a conserved adenosine of the mitochondrial large ribosomal subunit, where TFB1M-catalysed N1-methylation is required for mt-LSU assembly and mitoribosomal translational fidelity [21–23] and where m1A has been mapped directly in human mitochondrial rRNA [48]. Loss of this methylation collapses mitochondrial translation, a phenotype that echoes the respiratory decline documented in AMD donor RPE [7,52]. Our interpretation is deliberately narrow. The mark is present under tBHP, QMR, secretome and the combination at frequencies that differ neither between arms (p = 0.36) nor between stress conditions (q = 0.44); none of the interventions abolishes it. That licences a statement about one modification, at one position, in one cell line, over 72 hours: no disruption of this mark was detected. It licences no statement about mitochondrial safety, since this experiment assays neither mitochondrial translation, respiratory-chain activity, membrane potential, mtDNA integrity, chronic exposure nor any tissue- or organism-level endpoint, and a single preserved rRNA mark is not a surrogate for any of them. The lower-prevalence MT-ND5 position (chrM:13710) is the one position showing a detectable change, and that change is associated with oxidative stress rather than treatment (Section 3.10); with four supporting calls, a reference-base flag and a catalogued variant, it warrants targeted confirmation.

The chromosome 6 positions and the limits of their interpretation

The three chromosome 6 positions are not one locus, and the distance between them bears directly on how they may be read. Two lie 1 bp apart in a region where the EZR and SYTL3 gene models overlap; the third lies 127.5 Mb away in HLA-C at 6p21.33 (Section 3.3). Two consequences follow for interpretation. First, the adjacent pair cannot be assigned to a transcript: the coordinates fall within overlapping annotations, and short-read data of this kind do not resolve which message carries the marks. SYTL3 encodes a synaptotagmin-like calcium sensor implicated in apical-membrane organization and in the regulated exocytosis of extracellular vesicles from epithelial cells, including the RPE [53,54], and EZR encodes a membrane-cytoskeleton linker; either would be of interest in this cell type, which is precisely why the ambiguity matters. No functional assay of translation efficiency or protein abundance was performed here, so any consequence of these marks is untested, and we advance no mechanistic model for them. Second, the HLA-C position carries an explicit caveat: a low, stable non-reference fraction at high depth in one of the most polymorphic genes in the genome is at least as consistent with allelic mismatch against the reference as with a chemical mark, and we place no biological interpretation on it. We note without building on it that the editing layer independently returns a recurrent window in MICA about 145 kb away in the same MHC class I region.

Specificity of action and bounded non-interference with the RNA-recoding layer

The parallel analyses of this dataset described two layers at which QMR and secretome demonstrably act: a regulatory layer of miRNA-network reprogramming [37] and lncRNA/circRNA remodelling [39], and a structural layer attenuating the oxidative-stress-induced chimeric-transcript burden [40]. It is natural to ask whether the chemical state of RNA constitutes a third such layer. These data indicate that it does not, and the shape of that negative matters more than its mere presence. In the base-modification layer, none of the seven positions supporting a frequency comparison differs between arms, the smallest p-value being 0.19. In the editing layer the same holds at three independent levels of resolution: no window of 940 survives multiple-testing correction, none of the 15 curated positions reaching testable coverage differs between arms when quantified by a dedicated caller (minimum q = 0.169), and the Alu editing index is unchanged between arms under either of two callers that agree with each other to r = 0.95 (Sections 3.7 and 3.11). What this yields is a bounded absence of effect rather than an unbounded null: two one-sided tests place any treatment-associated change in substitution composition within 1.4%, and in mean per-sample editing frequency within 10% for secretome and for the combination and within 20% for QMR. The bound specifies what the data exclude, not only what they failed to detect. It is not, and should not be read as, an assessment of safety, for the reasons set out in Section 4.1.

Read together with the parallel layers, this bounded null speaks to how these interventions act. An agent that reprograms microRNA networks, remodels the non-coding transcriptome and reduces a structural-fidelity burden, while leaving the epitranscriptome and the canonical recoding set quantifiably undisturbed, is more consistent with action through defined routes than with generalized perturbation of RNA metabolism. For an intervention delivered as an electromagnetic field that distinction is not incidental, since diffuse off-pathway action is the obvious a priori concern, and the present layer is a sensitive place to look for one: miscalling and A-to-I deamination report on nucleotide-level chemistry rather than on transcript abundance. A converging observation supports the same reading: in the parallel bulk transcriptome of this dataset [38] none of the relevant writer or editor enzymes, including ADAR, ADARB1, TFB1M, NSUN2 [55], METTL2A/2B and METTL8, was transcriptionally induced by either intervention (all false-discovery rates > 0.5). Two caveats bound the interpretation. Absence of a detectable effect is not evidence that none exists: a target-selective change smaller than the margins above, or confined to positions below the coverage threshold applied here, would have escaped detection, and the equivalence bounds indicate the replication a properly powered test would require. And the layer remains informative as a description of state rather than of response: the constitutive positions, chrM:2617 in particular, are candidate epitranscriptomic descriptors of the oxidatively stressed RPE, each requiring confirmation by the orthogonal chemistry of Section 5.6.

The combination condition

Across the five previously reported layers, the QMR+SECRETOME combination generated the broadest response: the largest differentially expressed gene set [38], unique miRNA species [37], hyperactivated circCRIM1 [39], the greatest chimeric-burden reduction [40] and the highest EFEMP1 with progressive MYO7A reactivation [41]. The present layer does not reproduce that pattern. The combination produced the second-highest call burden (62 of 241) and one singleton call at GL000220.1:114669; each of the other three arms also contributed exactly one singleton (Section 4.4), so no arm is distinguished by them, and no weight is placed on a single call in a single library. The chromosome 21 cluster comprises low-prevalence positions of heterogeneous class distributed across all treated arms within the rDNA cluster. In accordance with Section 3.1 we make no claim of synergy at the epitranscriptomic level: that would require an effect exceeding both monotherapies with statistical support, and no effect of any kind was detected.

The A-to-I editing layer: bounded equivalence rather than preservation

Running Tsniffer alongside HARMLNC placed the modification set in the wider context of RNA recoding. Its output is dominated by A-to-G and T-to-C transitions, the ADAR signature, a class distinct from the methyl and isopentenyl marks scored by HARMLNC and previously implicated in the RPE oxidative-stress response by our group [24]. This is why the two readouts share only chrM:2617: they detect different chemistry, so Tsniffer is not a confirmatory caller for the HARMLNC positions. On inference, a non-significant Kruskal–Wallis test and an absence of FDR-surviving windows would not by themselves establish equivalence, which is why we report bounded estimates instead; the margins are given in Section 4.7 and their interpretation in Section 5.3. What we claim is therefore weaker than preservation: no treatment effect on bulk editing was detected, within the stated margins, at the resolution and sample size available, and the nominal window hits are fewer than chance expectation.

The limits of the window screen bound what it can say, and they are why the layer was re-analysed with dedicated tools. The screen reports windows of median width 372 bp rather than single nucleotides and recovers none of the 13 verified canonical recoding positions (Section 4.9); it surveys the repeat-associated editing landscape and is not an instrument for recoding analysis. Site-resolved callers are the appropriate instruments, and Section 4.11 reports the result of applying three of them: the quantification proves reproducible across callers, the de novo editome behaves as ADAR product should, and the treatment null holds at single-nucleotide resolution, so the window-level and site-level analyses agree. What the site-resolved work adds beyond confirmation is a diagnosis of this cohort: the dominant axis of differential editing is the sequencing batch rather than any biological variable, and the stress-associated component that survives batch control is small and not separable from residual technical variation at the available replication. That is a limitation of the design, not a property of the interventions, and we state it as such (Section 5.6). Applying variant-aware review to the recurrent windows is equally instructive: neither the reference base at the reported coordinate, which is a window boundary rather than an edited position, nor coincidence with catalogued variants, which is near-universal at current dbSNP density, discriminates among them (Section 4.7). The one criterion that does is frequency itself: the two windows with near-saturation means overlap processed pseudogenes, where paralogous mismatch is more parsimonious than complete editing, and these are excluded. What remains is a set of recurrent editing regions, including one overlapping MICA, an MHC class I stress ligand relevant to the para-inflammatory axis of AMD [56,57], and one overlapping NDUFS1, whose editing is stable across arms. We report that stability at window resolution and infer neither an immune set-point nor a Complex I recoding programme from it.

Limitations

The limitations of this study are substantial and we state them without hedging. First, and governing everything else, modification calls derive computationally from short-read mis-incorporation signals. This is an indirect inference, it detects only marks that perturb reverse transcription, and the assignment is a prediction against a signature library rather than a chemical measurement. Several classes share indistinguishable signatures, so 12 of the 17 site-class combinations reported here carry degenerate multi-modification labels and no single identity can be claimed for them. Definitive assignment requires orthogonal chemistry: mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), modification-specific enrichment sequencing or native-RNA nanopore sequencing. Second, the detected set is small (15 positions, of which 7 support any statistical test) and consists entirely of modification classes canonical to tRNA and rRNA, in mitochondrial, rDNA-contig and rDNA-cluster contexts. It is not a transcriptome-wide landscape, and no mRNA-specific modification was recovered. m6A, the most abundant internal mRNA mark, is undetectable by this approach; MeRIP-seq or GLORI would be required. Third, several positions carry annotation flags that the present data cannot resolve: three sites have a caller reference nucleotide disagreeing with the Ensembl reference base, several coincide with catalogued germline variants, and the HLA-C position lies in a hyperpolymorphic locus where allelic mismatch is a plausible alternative to a chemical mark. Fourth, no functional assay was performed. Translation efficiency, ribosome occupancy and protein abundance were not measured, so any consequence of these marks remains untested and no mechanistic model is advanced for them. Fifth, the window-based editing screen is strand-unaware, samples low-abundance non-coding RNAs more shallowly than abundant transcripts, and recovers none of the canonical recoding positions; the site-resolved re-analysis of Section 4.11 addresses that gap at the curated positions but does not extend a site-level treatment test transcriptome-wide, which would require deeper replication than this cohort provides. Sixth, the differential editing analysis is confounded by sequencing batch: the three libraries of the second batch all fall in one oxidative-stress group, the batch contrast returns four times as many significant windows as the stress contrast restricted to the balanced batch, and at eight libraries per group the residual stress-associated component cannot be separated from technical variation (Section 4.11). No component of it is attributable to treatment, and none of the conclusions of this study rests on it. Seventh, statistical power is limited throughout: with 14 libraries per arm and 7 per stress condition, only large effects are detectable, the equivalence margins we can support are correspondingly wide for the mean editing frequency (Section 4.7), and the 8 H time point, with one replicate per stress condition, cannot support inference at all. Eighth, ARPE-19 cells do not reproduce the barrier function, apical-basal polarity, pigmentation or metabolic profile of native RPE; primary human RPE or iPSC-derived RPE monolayers would be required before extending any of these observations to the tissue. Finally, the cohort covers a single sub-lethal oxidative paradigm over 72 hours in one cell line, and the stress contrast reported in Section 4.10 is itself based on 44 libraries and 19 matched pairs.

Future directions

The priorities that follow from this work are shaped by what it could not establish. Orthogonal chemical validation comes first: LC-MS/MS quantification of the positions reported here, and native-RNA nanopore sequencing, which would resolve inosine and methyl marks directly, discriminate the degenerate signature classes that short reads cannot, and provide stoichiometry rather than miscalling rates. For the chromosome 6 positions specifically, allele-aware reanalysis with donor genotypes is needed to determine whether the HLA-C signal is chemical or germline, and transcript-resolved analysis is needed to establish whether the adjacent pair lies in EZR or SYTL3 message; if a modification is confirmed there, ribosome profiling and targeted protein quantification would test its functional consequence. For the editing layer, the site-resolved analysis reported in Section 4.11 should be extended transcriptome-wide in a batch-balanced cohort: the present design confounds sequencing batch with oxidative-stress state, and separating them is the precondition for any site-level claim about stress or treatment beyond the curated positions tested here. Enlarging the cohort is necessary for any of the treatment questions: the present design supports only the detection of large effects, and the equivalence bounds reported here indicate the sample size a properly powered study would need. Finally, validation in primary human RPE and in AMD patient-derived iPSC-RPE, and extension to RPE-derived extracellular vesicles, would establish whether any of this transfers beyond an immortalized monolayer; RNA carried by vitreous-derived extracellular vesicles would be the natural place to look for a non-invasive correlate, should any of these marks prove treatment-responsive in a properly powered design. The DNA-methylation layer is outside the scope of this RNA-based analysis and is being characterized separately.

Conclusions

This study characterizes the chemical state of the RPE transcriptome, comprising both internal base modifications and A-to-I editing, in ARPE-19 cells under oxidative stress and under two non-pharmacological interventions, QMR electrotherapy and patient blood-derived secretome. The base-modification map comprises 241 high-confidence calls across 56 libraries, resolving to 15 unique positions. Its composition is narrow: every class recovered is canonical to transfer or ribosomal RNA, and the contexts are mitochondrial rRNA, rDNA-bearing contigs and the chromosome 21 rDNA cluster. Three positions are constitutive by the definition used here: a modification at chrM:2617 of the mitochondrial 16S rRNA (MT-RNR2), most probably m1A, detected in 51 of 56 libraries at the highest depth in the dataset; an i6A/t6A position at chr6:158,766,314 in an EZR/SYTL3 overlap region carrying the highest non-reference frequency of any site; and a dihydrouridine position on the rDNA-bearing contig KI270733.1. None varies with treatment. Of the 15 positions, 7 support a statistical test and none shows a treatment effect on non-reference frequency (minimum p = 0.19); arm-level differences in this dataset are differences in detection counts, not in modification level. The A-to-I editing layer likewise shows no detectable treatment effect: no window of 940 survives multiple-testing correction, and formal equivalence testing bounds any effect on substitution composition within 1.4% for all arms, and on mean editing frequency within 10% for secretome and combination and within 20% for QMR. A stress-free baseline internal to the cohort, made available by a library-metadata correction described here, shows that with the single exception of MT-ND5 these positions are present at comparable frequency with and without oxidative stress. Re-analysis with three dedicated editing callers reproduces the quantification, recovers a de novo editome with the Alu enrichment expected of ADAR products and confirms at single-nucleotide resolution that no curated position differs between treatment arms; the same analysis identifies the sequencing batch, rather than any biological variable, as the dominant axis of differential editing in this cohort, which we report as a limitation of the design. What the study contributes is a first, bounded description of the RPE modification and editing layers under oxidative stress, together with the finding that neither layer is measurably altered by QMR or secretome at the resolution and sample size available, and an explicit statement of the margins within which that holds. Placed beside the layers at which these interventions do act, that bounded non-interference is itself informative: it is more consistent with action through defined routes than with generalized perturbation of RNA metabolism, and it quantifies, rather than merely asserts, how little of the RNA-recoding layer is disturbed.
