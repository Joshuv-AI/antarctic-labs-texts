Intermittent Hypoxia-Induced Inflammation and the Formation of Exhaled Volatile Organic Compound Profiles in Obstructive Sleep Apnea-Hypopnea Syndrome: A Narrative Review
Authors: Yang Wang, Lei Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is characterized by recurrent upper airway obstruction during sleep, intermittent hypoxia (IH), and sleep fragmentation, and is associated with oxidative stress, chronic low-grade inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and upper airway remodeling. These pathophysiological disturbances may alter the generation and release of volatile metabolites, thereby providing a rationale for investigating exhaled volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles as noninvasive indicators of OSAHS-related redox imbalance, inflammatory activity, and metabolic abnormalities. Focusing on IH-induced inflammatory responses as a central mechanistic link, this review examines the potential mechanisms through which inflammation contributes to changes in exhaled VOC profiles in OSAHS. Repeated hypoxia-reoxygenation and upper airway mechanical stress may activate inflammatory signaling pathways, including nuclear factor κB, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α, and NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3, and may influence the generation and release of volatile metabolites such as aldehydes, alkanes, acetone, alcohols, and isoprene through amplified oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, metabolic reprogramming, and alterations in the local airway microenvironment. Available clinical studies suggest that exhaled VOC profiles in patients with OSAHS may be associated with disease severity, nocturnal hypoxic burden, metabolic abnormalities, and treatment response. However, the specificity and reproducibility of candidate VOCs remain limited by individual metabolic profiles, exogenous exposures, and methodological heterogeneity. Future studies should integrate standardized breath sampling, high-confidence compound identification, longitudinal validation, and concurrent assessment of inflammatory, oxidative stress, and metabolic markers. Inflammation-related VOC profiling may offer mechanistic insight into the pathophysiological heterogeneity of OSAHS and support the development of noninvasive assessment tools and more personalized management strategies.Keywords: obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome, volatile organic compounds, intermittent hypoxia, inflammation, breathomics

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is a common sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by recurrent narrowing or collapse of the upper airway during sleep, resulting in intermittent hypoxia (IH) and sleep fragmentation.1 Epidemiological studies indicate that OSAHS poses a substantial global health burden, with nearly one billion individuals affected worldwide.2 In addition to excessive daytime sleepiness, OSAHS is closely associated with cognitive impairment and cardiometabolic comorbidities, including hypertension, coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and stroke.3–5 Although polysomnography (PSG) remains the diagnostic gold standard,6 its use in large-scale screening and routine clinical practice is limited by high cost, long waiting times, and the first-night effect.7,8 Therefore, the development of noninvasive biomarkers that reflect key pathophysiological processes in OSAHS has become an important research direction in this field. In recent years, analysis of exhaled volatile organic compounds (VOCs) has shown promise in diseases such as lung cancer and asthma, supporting investigation of this approach in OSAHS.9,10 Because exhaled VOCs may reflect IH-related inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and metabolic remodeling,11–13 VOC-based breathomics may offer a novel noninvasive strategy for pathophysiological characterization, risk stratification, and adjunctive assessment of OSAHS.14 Drawing on recent studies, this review focuses on IH-induced inflammatory responses as a central mechanistic link in OSAHS, summarizes the mechanistic evidence connecting inflammatory signaling activation to alterations in exhaled VOC profiles, and further discusses the potential value of inflammation-related VOCs in noninvasive assessment, pathophysiological stratification, and monitoring of treatment response. Biological Basis for VOCs as Biomarkers in OSAHS

The biological basis for using VOCs as biomarkers in OSAHS does not depend on any single compound representing the disease; rather, exhaled VOC profiles may capture the dynamic metabolic state shaped by the combined effects of IH, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and metabolic remodeling. Exhaled VOCs may originate from endogenous metabolic processes, but they can also be influenced by exogenous factors such as diet, medications, smoking, and environmental exposure. Therefore, when interpreting OSAHS-related changes in VOCs, it is essential to distinguish disease-related endogenous signals from exogenous confounding sources.15 Among endogenous sources, some VOCs are associated with lipid metabolism, lipid peroxidation, carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and enzymatic reactions, providing a biochemical basis for their ability to reflect redox status and metabolic activity.16

In OSAHS, the repeated hypoxia-reoxygenation pattern of IH may impose an oxidative stress burden resembling ischemia-reperfusion injury. Basic research has shown that chronic intermittent hypoxia can induce mitochondrial dysfunction and contribute to tissue injury through ROS generation and inflammation-related pathways such as thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP)/NLRP3/IL-1β signaling.17 Excessive ROS can attack polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes and trigger lipid peroxidation. In vitro studies of lipid peroxidation have shown that oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids can release volatile aldehydes such as pentanal, hexanal, heptanal, and nonanal. These small volatile products may enter exhaled breath through circulatory transport and alveolar gas exchange, making lipid peroxidation a plausible source of altered exhaled VOCs in OSAHS.18

In addition to oxidative stress, OSAHS-related IH may also affect the generation of endogenous VOCs through enhanced sympathetic activity, inflammatory cytokine release, and remodeling of energy metabolism.19 For example, exhaled acetone is associated with fatty acid oxidation and ketone body metabolism.20,21 Recent studies have found that nocturnal dynamics of exhaled acetone in patients with OSAHS are associated with clinical indices such as the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) and arousal index, suggesting that OSAHS-related sleep-disordered breathing events may influence systemic ketone body metabolism.22 In addition, proton transfer reaction mass spectrometry (PTR-MS) studies have found alterations in some aldehyde-related signals in the exhaled breath of patients with OSAHS. Among them, the m045 signal differs between patients with OSAHS and controls and is associated with AHI. This signal is presumed to be related to acetaldehyde, suggesting that exhaled aldehyde profiles may reflect OSAHS-related oxidative stress and inflammatory burden. However, its chemical identity and metabolic origin still require further confirmation using methods such as gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS).23 Overall, exhaled VOC profiles do not merely reflect local airway pathology; rather, they represent a chemical phenotype shaped by the combined effects of systemic oxidative stress, metabolic remodeling, and local airway inflammation, providing a biological rationale for their use as noninvasive biomarkers in OSAHS.

Major Pathways Linking Inflammation to VOC Generation and Release

Activation and Amplification of OSAHS-Related Inflammation

In mechanistic explanations of OSAHS-related alterations in VOC profiles, inflammation serves as a key mechanistic link. It is triggered by the combined effects of IH, reoxygenation injury, and snoring-related mechanical stimulation, and, in turn, influences VOC generation and release through amplified oxidative stress, metabolic reprogramming, and alterations in the local airway microenvironment. Accordingly, inflammation provides the organizing framework for this review. OSAHS-related inflammation does not reflect an isolated increase in a single mediator; rather, it is a sustained process driven by recurrent sleep-disordered breathing events. During sleep, repeated narrowing or collapse of the upper airway can lead to hypoxia-reoxygenation cycles and induce ischemia-reperfusion-like oxidative injury. At the same time, snoring and repeated stretching of the airway wall can directly affect the upper airway mucosa and local immune microenvironment. Together, these stimuli may extend inflammation from the local airway to a persistent, low-grade systemic state, contributing to tissue injury and systemic pathological changes.24 Immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages play important roles in this process by releasing cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory mediators, thereby contributing to local inflammatory amplification and maintenance of systemic inflammation.25

At the molecular level, IH-induced oxidative injury is an important trigger of the inflammatory cascade. NF-κB is one of the key transcriptional regulatory nodes and cooperates with HIF-1α in inflammatory amplification.26,27 Sustained activation of NF-κB is also influenced by multiple upstream regulatory mechanisms. For example, IH can induce dysregulated expression of specific microRNAs (miRNAs) and enhance NF-κB-related inflammatory signaling by suppressing negative regulatory molecules such as suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3).26 Sentrin-specific protease 1 (SENP1)-mediated de-SUMOylation of NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO) may also affect NF-κB activity and downstream inflammatory responses.28 Meanwhile, NF-κB and HIF-1α form a positive feedback loop: NF-κB can enhance HIF1A transcription, whereas stabilized HIF-1α can act as a cooperative transcriptional regulator involved in inflammatory amplification.29 Recent studies in patients with OSAHS and in vitro models further indicate that NLRP3 inflammasome activation in monocytes from patients with severe OSAHS is associated with AHI and hypoxemia-related indices, and that IH can promote NLRP3-related inflammatory signaling through an HIF-1α-dependent mechanism, thereby linking hypoxic stimulation to systemic inflammatory responses.30 In addition, snoring-related mechanical vibratory stress may also participate in the initiation of airway oxidative stress and inflammatory transcriptional programs, suggesting that inflammatory amplification in OSAHS is not driven by hypoxia alone but by the combined effects of hypoxia, reoxygenation, and mechanical stimulation.31 Figure 2 summarizes the IH-related NF-κB/HIF-1α inflammatory amplification network and its representative regulatory nodes.

In terms of spatial progression, OSAHS-related inflammation generally appears to be initiated locally in the airway and then gradually extends systemically. Because the upper airway is the site most directly affected by collapse, vibration, and airflow turbulence, local oxidative stress and inflammatory changes may occur relatively early. Recent studies of the upper airway mucosa have shown increased oxidative stress and reduced antioxidant capacity in patients with OSAHS, suggesting that local oxidative injury may contribute to OSAHS-related airway inflammation.32 Studies of circulating biomarkers have also found that lipid peroxidation markers, such as 8-isoprostane, are associated with disease severity in patients with OSAHS, indicating an increased systemic oxidative stress burden.33 As IH and mechanical stimulation recur, locally generated inflammatory mediators may spill over into the systemic circulation, leading to a chronic low-grade systemic inflammatory state. IL-6, a representative proinflammatory cytokine, may link local injury to systemic inflammation and is also associated with hypoxic burden, dyslipidemia, and associated organ damage.34,35 C-reactive protein (CRP), as an acute-phase response marker, shows a dose-response relationship with OSAHS severity.36,37 In addition, FSTL1 and the IL-33/ST2 signaling pathway have also been implicated in OSAHS-related systemic inflammatory amplification and may serve as potential markers reflecting inflammatory burden.38,39

Therefore, in OSAHS, inflammatory responses are often jointly initiated by local upper airway injury and IH-related oxidative stress, and are continuously amplified through signaling pathways such as NF-κB/HIF-1α and the involvement of immune cells. This results in the coexistence of local airway inflammation and systemic low-grade inflammation. This process not only helps explain the association between OSAHS and multisystem injury, including endothelial dysfunction and cardiometabolic complications,40 but also provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how inflammation contributes to the formation of exhaled VOC profiles through lipid peroxidation, metabolic reprogramming, and alterations in the airway microenvironment.

Biochemical Pathways Linking Inflammatory Signaling to VOC Generation

Building on the inflammatory activation and amplification described above, this section further discusses how inflammatory signaling regulates the generation and release of VOCs. In OSAHS, inflammatory responses do not serve as a direct source of VOCs; rather, they indirectly influence the generation, release, and composition of volatile metabolites by altering systemic redox status, patterns of energy metabolism, and the local airway microenvironment. Three principal mechanisms may contribute to OSAHS-related changes in VOC profiles. First, inflammatory responses amplify oxidative stress and promote peroxidative cleavage of membrane lipids and fatty acids, generating lipid peroxidation-related VOCs such as aldehydes and alkanes. Second, inflammatory cytokines and IH jointly affect glucose and lipid metabolism as well as related enzymatic activities, leading to changes in metabolic VOCs such as acetone and isoprene. Third, local airway inflammation may alter the epithelial barrier, mucus secretion, microbiota, and gas-exchange environment, thereby affecting the release and detection of VOCs in exhaled breath. Current evidence should be interpreted at two levels. Direct evidence from patients with OSAHS demonstrates altered airway and systemic inflammatory or oxidative markers and associations between exhaled VOC features and clinical indices. However, the causal pathways linking specific inflammatory signals to individual VOCs are largely inferred from experimental or non-OSAHS studies and should be considered biologically plausible but not yet established in humans.

Oxidative Stress-Mediated Lipid Peroxidation and VOC Generation

Inflammation-amplified oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation are key biochemical mechanisms underlying VOC generation. Oxidative stress refers to an imbalanced state that occurs when ROS production exceeds antioxidant capacity.41 ROS are highly reactive oxygen-containing molecules, including superoxide anion (O2·−), hydroxyl radical (·OH), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Under physiological conditions, ROS play important roles in cellular signaling and redox regulation; however, excessive accumulation of ROS can cause oxidative damage to cellular components such as proteins, lipids, and DNA. In OSAHS, recurrent hypoxia-reoxygenation produces recurrent redox cycling, which can promote mitochondrial electron transport chain dysfunction, activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases, and ROS release from inflammatory cells, thereby creating a mutually reinforcing amplification loop between oxidative stress and inflammation.42

Evidence from several biological compartments supports enhanced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in patients with OSAHS. Studies of the peripheral circulation have shown elevated lipid peroxidation-related markers, such as malondialdehyde, in patients with OSAHS, suggesting that membrane lipids and lipoprotein components may be subject to greater oxidative damage.43 Studies of exhaled breath condensate have also found increased levels of oxidative stress markers, such as 8-isoprostane, after nocturnal sleep in patients with OSAHS, with some markers decreasing after CPAP therapy. These findings indicate that IH-related oxidative injury is not confined to the circulatory system, but can also be detected in airway or breath-related samples.44 In this pathological context, lipid peroxidation may serve as an important biochemical source of VOC generation. Cell membranes and lipoproteins are rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are susceptible to ROS attack and free radical chain reactions, sequentially forming lipid radicals, lipid peroxyl radicals, and lipid hydroperoxides. The latter are unstable and can further decompose into various secondary oxidation products, including aldehydes, ketones, alkanes, and other oxygenated organic compounds.18,45 Compared with nonvolatile markers of oxidative damage, these small-molecule products are sufficiently volatile. Once generated, they can be released locally from the airway or transported through the bloodstream to the lungs and exhaled, thereby contribute to altered exhaled VOC profiles.46

With respect to compound classes, aldehydes and alkanes are characteristic volatile products of lipid peroxidation. Aldehydes such as acetaldehyde, hexanal, heptanal, and nonanal can arise from chain-cleavage reactions following fatty acid peroxidation, whereas low-molecular-weight alkanes such as ethane, pentane, and nonane can arise from the oxidative cleavage of different fatty acids.18,45,46 These compounds are not necessarily specific to OSAHS, but their changes may reflect oxidative stress burden, lipid oxidative injury, and inflammatory status. Some studies of exhaled VOCs in OSAHS have also observed differences in volatile metabolic profiles between patients and healthy controls, and certain alkanes or oxygenated VOCs have been associated with AHI, nocturnal hypoxic burden, or changes before and after CPAP therapy. These findings provide clinical observational support for the mechanistic chain of “IH-oxidative stress-lipid peroxidation-VOC alteration”.23,47,48

Therefore, IH-induced chronic low-grade inflammation may sustain oxidative burden and lipid peroxidation, creating conditions favorable to the generation of related VOCs. However, these compounds are not specific to OSAHS, and relevant confounding factors and methodological limitations must be addressed in clinical translation studies.47,49

Inflammation-Induced Metabolic Reprogramming and Metabolic VOC Generation

Beyond lipid peroxidation, inflammatory responses may also indirectly affect the generation and release of certain metabolic VOCs by altering systemic metabolic status. Chronic recurrent IH in patients with OSAHS may be accompanied by sympathetic activation, disrupted sleep architecture, and abnormalities in glucose and lipid metabolism, thereby affecting energy substrate utilization and metabolic homeostasis.50,51 Unlike lipid peroxidation-related VOCs such as aldehydes and alkanes, metabolic VOCs, including acetone, alcohols, and isoprene, are more closely associated with fatty acid oxidation, ketone body production, redox state, and cholesterol-related metabolic processes. Therefore, when interpreting OSAHS-related alterations in VOC profiles, it is also necessary to consider the effects of hypoxia and inflammation on systemic metabolic networks.

Mechanistically, IH can affect cellular energy metabolism through signaling pathways such as HIF-1α and NF-κB. Activation of HIF-1α can enhance glucose uptake and glycolytic pathways while affecting mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation; NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses may alter the expression and activity of metabolic enzymes through proinflammatory cytokine release, immune cell activation, and redox signaling disturbances.51,52 Meanwhile, inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β can interfere with insulin signaling and promote insulin resistance.52–55 Reactive molecules such as ROS and reactive nitrogen species may also affect the function of enzymes involved in glucose and lipid metabolism through oxidative modification, nitration, or covalent adduct formation, with representative redox-sensitive targets including glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), aconitase, and diacylglycerol acyltransferase 2 (DGAT2).51,52,56,57 These changes do not necessarily directly generate a specific VOC, but they may influence the generation, transport, and exhalation of volatile metabolites by altering substrate availability, metabolic flux, and redox status.

Among metabolic VOCs, acetone is a representative example. Acetone is mainly associated with fatty acid β-oxidation and ketone body metabolism. It can be formed from acetoacetate and subsequently enter the lungs via the bloodstream before being eliminated in exhaled breath.58,59 Recurrent nocturnal hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, and sympathetic activation in patients with OSAHS may promote lipolysis and increase the release of free fatty acids. When glucose utilization is limited or insulin resistance worsens, energy metabolism may shift toward greater reliance on fatty acid oxidation, thereby affecting ketone body production and exhaled acetone levels. Recent studies suggest that nocturnal dynamics of exhaled acetone may be associated with sleep-disordered breathing parameters such as AHI and arousal index.22 In addition to acetone, alcohol VOCs may be related to alcohol metabolism, gut microbiota metabolism, and changes in the local reducing environment. Isoprene is one of the more common endogenous VOCs in human exhaled breath and has been linked to the mevalonate pathway and cholesterol synthesis.60,61

Existing studies of exhaled VOCs in OSAHS have detected various metabolism-related volatile components in patient samples and have observed associations between certain VOCs and AHI, nocturnal hypoxic burden, or changes before and after treatment. Aoki et al found that some VOCs in patients with OSAHS were associated with AHI, arousal index, and the duration of oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry (SpO2) ≤90%, and that isoprene and acetone decreased after CPAP therapy.48 Nowak et al validated a set of OSAHS-related breath biomarkers using breath metabolomics, suggesting that exhaled metabolic profiles may reflect systemic metabolic disturbances and cardiovascular risk in OSAHS.14 Schmidt et al also reported alterations in exhaled metabolites related to glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism in patients with severe OSAHS.62 In addition, Gui et al analyzed exhaled breath samples from patients with OSAHS using PTR-MS and observed differences in VOC profiles between patients and healthy controls, with some signals potentially associated with AHI.23

Overall, inflammation-induced metabolic reprogramming represents another mechanism underlying OSAHS-related alterations in VOC profiles. This mechanism reflects the combined effects of hypoxia, inflammation, and oxidative stress on glucose and lipid metabolism, ketone body metabolism, alcohol metabolism, and cholesterol-related metabolic processes, resulting in changes in the generation, transport, and exhalation of certain metabolic volatile products. Compared with lipid peroxidation-related VOCs, metabolic VOCs are more sensitive to energy substrate utilization and sampling time. Therefore, their interpretation should account for body weight, glucose and lipid metabolism, and sampling time.49,63

Airway Inflammatory Microenvironment Remodeling and VOC Release

In addition to lipid peroxidation and metabolic reprogramming, OSAHS-related alterations in the local airway microenvironment may also affect the release of VOCs into exhaled breath. During sleep, patients with OSAHS repeatedly experience upper airway collapse and re-expansion. Local tissues are chronically exposed to the combined effects of IH, mechanical stretching, mucosal friction, and inflammatory responses, which may lead to airway epithelial injury, mucosal inflammation, altered redox status, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling.27,64 Compared with the two mechanisms described above, remodeling of the airway inflammatory microenvironment mainly regulates the entry of VOCs into exhaled breath by altering the local substrate environment, epithelial barrier permeability, the mucus/gas-exchange interface, and the diffusion, retention, and clearance of volatile molecules. It is therefore best viewed as a microenvironmental regulatory mechanism linking local inflammatory injury to VOC release.

ECM is a dynamic network composed of collagen, elastin, proteoglycans, and other components. It provides mechanical support for airway tissues and also regulates cell adhesion, migration, inflammatory responses, and tissue repair.64 Under chronic inflammatory conditions, the balance between ECM synthesis and degradation may be disrupted, and the activity of ECM-degrading enzymes such as MMPs may increase, promoting structural remodeling of local tissues. Recent studies have shown that serum MMP-9 levels in patients with OSAHS are associated with disease severity indicators, including AHI and the proportion of sleep time spent with nocturnal oxygen saturation below 90%, suggesting that OSAHS-related inflammation and hypoxic burden may be linked to ECM degradation and tissue remodeling.65 Studies of upper airway tissues have also found increased expression of remodeling-related molecules, such as MMP-9 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), in the soft palate or uvular tissues of patients with OSAHS, further supporting the presence of local inflammation and structural remodeling in the upper airway.66

These local microenvironmental changes may affect VOC profiles at multiple levels. First, airway mucosal inflammation and oxidative stress can alter the local redox state, making substrates such as lipids, proteins, and amino acids more susceptible to oxidation or degradation, thereby providing conditions for the formation of aldehydes, ketones, and other oxygenated VOCs. Second, epithelial barrier injury and altered mucus secretion may influence the diffusion, retention, and clearance of local metabolites toward the airway lumen, making certain volatile molecules more readily detectable in exhaled breath. Third, matrix fragments generated by ECM degradation may act as biologically active signaling molecules that further regulate inflammatory cell recruitment, oxidative stress, and tissue repair, thereby sustaining a local chronic inflammatory microenvironment and indirectly affecting the persistent release of VOCs.67

Recent imaging and breathomics studies also provide indirect support for this view. One study using positron emission tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI) observed increased inflammation-related metabolic activity in the pharyngeal mucosa of patients with OSAHS, which decreased after CPAP treatment, suggesting that upper airway inflammation may be at least partially reversible.68 Breathomics studies further indicate that exhaled VOCs originate not only from systemic metabolism and circulating metabolites, but may also be influenced by the local airway environment, exogenous exposures, sampling methods, and gas-exchange processes.15,63 Therefore, changes in VOC profiles observed in OSAHS may reflect the combined contributions of systemic inflammatory-metabolic signals and local upper airway microenvironmental alterations.

Overall, remodeling of the airway inflammatory microenvironment contributes to OSAHS-related alterations in VOC profiles primarily at the level of local release and regulation. Current evidence suggests that upper airway inflammation, ECM remodeling, and epithelial barrier changes may jointly affect the local substrate environment and the conditions under which volatile molecules enter exhaled breath. However, direct causal links between these changes and specific VOCs remain poorly established. Future studies should integrate upper airway inflammatory markers, tissue remodeling molecules, fractionated breath sampling, and VOC profile analysis to clarify the contribution of the local airway microenvironment to breath testing in OSAHS.

Clinical Translation of Inflammation-Related VOCs in OSAHS: Applications and Challenges

The studies discussed above have described the biological basis for the generation of inflammation-related VOCs in terms of oxidative stress, metabolic remodeling, and the airway inflammatory microenvironment. Building on this mechanistic framework, research on inflammation-related VOCs in OSAHS is gradually moving from mechanistic exploration toward clinical translation. Key steps in this process include breath sample collection, detection and profile recognition, biological interpretation, analytical and clinical validation, and potential applications in adjunctive diagnosis, severity assessment, and personalized management. As shown in Table 2, recent research on exhaled VOCs in OSAHS has gradually shifted from simply describing profile differences to validating candidate breath biomarkers, screening feature ions, analyzing metabolic mechanisms, and observing the dynamics of specific metabolites. Existing studies suggest that exhaled VOC profiles are altered in patients with OSAHS, and that some volatile components or feature signals are associated with AHI, nocturnal hypoxic burden, treatment status, and metabolic abnormalities. These findings indicate that exhaled VOCs may reflect the pathophysiological characteristics of OSAHS in terms of oxidative stress, metabolic remodeling, and the airway inflammatory microenvironment. However, the differentially abundant VOCs identified across studies are not yet fully consistent, and the specific chemical identities of some feature peaks or ion signals still require further confirmation. This suggests that studies of exhaled VOCs in OSAHS require further standardization in terms of detection platforms, compound identification, and external validation. The following sections analyze their potential roles in adjunctive diagnosis, disease phenotyping, treatment monitoring, and the challenges of clinical translation.

Potential Value of VOC Profiles in Adjunctive Diagnosis and Disease Phenotyping

Current evidence suggests that exhaled breath composition does not simply reflect airway ventilation status, but may also encode pathophysiological information related to inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbances.14,49,63 The establishment of clinical breathomics datasets indicates that molecular features in exhaled breath are amenable to data-driven analysis and serial measurement.71 Compared with blood inflammatory markers, breath testing is noninvasive, easy to perform, and suitable for serial monitoring, giving it potential translational value in the adjunctive assessment of OSAHS.

However, candidate VOCs identified across different studies remain inconsistent, making it difficult to regard any single VOC as a stable and reliable diagnostic biomarker. In contrast, multivariable profiles comprising multiple VOCs may better reflect the complex pathophysiology and multifactorial nature of OSAHS. Recent studies of exhaled VOCs in chronic respiratory diseases have shown that combinations of multiple VOCs integrated with machine learning models can distinguish chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm), and healthy controls, suggesting that VOC profiles may be more suitable than single compounds for disease classification.72 Based on this understanding, exhaled VOCs are currently best viewed as adjuncts to PSG that may help identify patients with marked inflammatory burden, enhanced oxidative stress, or metabolic abnormalities.

Beyond adjunctive diagnosis, VOC profiles may also provide useful information for assessing OSAHS severity. Existing breath studies in OSAHS suggest that some exhaled metabolites or feature ions are associated with AHI, nocturnal hypoxic burden, arousal index, or changes before and after treatment.14,22,23,62,69,70 Mechanistically, as IH worsens, oxidative stress and inflammatory responses may also intensify, subsequently affecting lipid peroxidation, ketone body metabolism, and the local airway microenvironment, leading to changes in the composition and abundance of exhaled VOCs. Nevertheless, VOC changes are not necessarily a direct reflection of a higher AHI. AHI mainly describes the frequency of respiratory events, whereas VOC profiles are more likely to reflect the overall pathophysiological and metabolic burden.

Therefore, the value of VOC profiles may extend beyond adjunctive diagnosis of OSAHS; they may also be used to explore phenotypes with distinct pathological characteristics. For example, integrating VOC profiles with AHI, lowest oxygen saturation, oxygen desaturation index, inflammatory cytokines, and metabolic markers may help distinguish patient subgroups characterized primarily by hypoxic-inflammatory burden from those characterized primarily by metabolic abnormalities.

Sex Differences and Their Potential Influence on VOC Profiles

Sex may represent an important source of heterogeneity in OSAHS-related VOC profiles. Compared with men, women with OSAHS generally exhibit lower upper airway collapsibility and loop gain during non-rapid eye movement sleep, whereas rapid eye movement sleep-related respiratory events may be relatively more prominent.73 Sex-related differences in body fat distribution, sex hormone status, inflammatory responses, insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolism may alter the magnitude and temporal pattern of IH-induced oxidative stress and metabolic remodeling.74,75 These differences could, in turn, influence lipid peroxidation-derived VOCs, such as aldehydes and alkanes, as well as metabolic VOCs, such as acetone and isoprene.76 However, direct evidence for sex-specific VOC signatures in OSAHS remains limited. Future breathomics studies should include adequate numbers of women, report sex-stratified results, consider menopausal status and hormone-related factors, and examine whether sex modifies the associations between VOC profiles, hypoxic burden, and treatment response.

Effects of Therapeutic Interventions on Inflammation-Related VOCs and Their Clinical Implications

Therapeutic interventions provide a useful framework for determining whether VOC profiles track changes in disease activity. CPAP therapy can reduce nocturnal IH and may improve markers of inflammation and oxidative stress.77,78 Parallel changes in hypoxic burden, inflammatory markers, and specific VOCs after CPAP would support the responsiveness of VOC profiles to improvements in OSAHS pathophysiology. Previous studies have also observed changes in several VOCs after CPAP therapy.48 These findings suggest that exhaled VOCs may be useful not only for assessing disease status, but also for monitoring post-treatment pathophysiological changes.

However, based on current evidence, post-treatment changes in VOCs are influenced by multiple factors, including improved ventilation, restoration of sleep architecture, changes in body weight, diet, comorbid metabolic diseases, and medication use, and therefore cannot be simply attributed to improvement in inflammation. A recent study of tirzepatide for obesity-related OSAHS showed simultaneous improvements in AHI, body weight, hypoxic burden, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), further indicating that treatment responses often involve respiratory, inflammatory, and metabolic dimensions.79 In addition, some studies suggest that the effects of CPAP on inflammation-related markers are not entirely consistent, and unfavorable changes in endothelial inflammation-related markers may even occur in specific populations with cardiovascular risk.80 Future studies may adopt paired pre- and post-treatment designs and concurrently measure inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and target VOCs to determine whether VOC changes are consistent with reductions in inflammatory burden in OSAHS. Convergent evidence from longitudinal follow-up, paired pre- and post-intervention analyses, and mechanistic experiments will be needed to establish the causal role of inflammation in OSAHS-related VOC profile alterations.81

Confounding Factors and Standardization Issues in Clinical Translation

Although exhaled VOCs show promise in the assessment of OSAHS, their clinical translation remains limited by confounding factors, methodological heterogeneity, small study populations, and limited independent external validation. These limitations may increase the risk of model overfitting and restrict the generalizability of reported findings. Clinical interpretation of exhaled VOCs requires adequate control of confounding factors. In addition to OSAHS-related nocturnal hypoxia and inflammatory burden, diet, smoking, environmental exposure, medication use, oral microbiota, baseline metabolic status, and breath sampling methods may all alter VOC profiles.82–84 Because several of these factors affect the same oxidative and metabolic pathways implicated in OSAHS, they may reduce the disease specificity of VOC profiles and obscure whether the observed changes originate from OSAHS itself. Therefore, future studies should record and control these variables during study design to distinguish disease-related endogenous signals from exogenous or background metabolic signals. Recent breathomics studies emphasize that background-air sampling, rigorous control of exogenous contamination, and high-confidence compound identification are prerequisites for translating exploratory breath signals into reliable candidate biomarkers.71,82,84

Obesity is one of the most important confounders. Obesity is not only a major risk factor for the development and progression of OSAHS, but also a major determinant of the metabolic background that shapes VOC profiles.85,86 On the one hand, obesity can increase the tendency toward upper airway collapse through upper airway fat deposition, pharyngeal narrowing, and altered respiratory mechanics, thereby worsening nocturnal hypoxic burden.87–89 On the other hand, obesity itself is often accompanied by chronic low-grade inflammation, insulin resistance, altered fatty acid oxidation, and lipid metabolic abnormalities.90 These changes may affect exhaled components such as acetone, alkanes, and aldehydes. Therefore, VOC profile alterations in patients with OSAHS cannot be simply attributed to IH or inflammatory responses, but may also reflect obesity-related metabolic disturbances and baseline inflammatory status.

Methodological differences are another important factor limiting the generalizability of findings. Existing studies remain inconsistent in sampling time, breathing maneuvers, sample storage, detection platforms, data preprocessing, and statistical modeling methods, resulting in limited agreement in the differentially abundant VOCs identified across studies.82–84 Therefore, clinical translational studies should not focus solely on discriminative performance, but should also report confidence in compound identification, stability of variable selection, external validation results, and model performance after stratification by different confounding factors.72,83 Future studies should further standardize breath collection and detection procedures while controlling for obesity, diabetes, smoking, diet, and environmental exposure, and should evaluate model stability using multicenter samples and external validation. Adequate control of confounding factors and standardized analytical procedures are essential before OSAHS-related VOCs can progress from exploratory biomarkers to clinically useful adjunctive assessment tools. At present, VOC profiling should therefore be regarded as an exploratory adjunct rather than a replacement for PSG.

Conclusions

In summary, inflammatory responses represent a critical mechanistic link between sleep-disordered breathing events in OSAHS and the chemical phenotype of exhaled VOCs. IH, reoxygenation injury, and upper airway mechanical stimulation may jointly promote local and systemic inflammatory responses and induce continuous changes in oxidative injury, metabolic regulation, and the airway microenvironment governing VOC release, thereby providing a pathophysiological basis for alterations in exhaled VOC profiles. Current evidence suggests that exhaled VOC profiles in patients with OSAHS may be associated with disease severity, nocturnal hypoxic burden, metabolic abnormalities, and treatment status. These associations suggest that breathomics may serve as a supplementary noninvasive assessment approach beyond PSG for adjunctive diagnosis, pathophysiological phenotyping, and treatment response monitoring. However, current research remains at the exploratory and validation stages. Candidate VOCs identified across studies are not fully consistent, and the chemical identities of some feature peaks or ion signals require further confirmation. Their clinical interpretation also remains affected by individual metabolic profiles, exogenous exposures, and differences in analytical procedures.

Future research on OSAHS-related VOCs should therefore shift from single-biomarker screening toward an integrated approach combining multicomponent profile analysis with mechanistic validation. Standardized procedures for breath collection, sample storage, detection and analysis, and data modeling should be established under strict control of potential confounding factors. Multicenter studies with large sample sizes and longitudinal follow-up are also required. Furthermore, integrating VOC profiles with AHI, hypoxic burden, inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, and metabolic indicators may help clarify their biological origins and clinical significance. Overall, inflammation-related VOCs provide a novel window into the pathophysiological heterogeneity of OSAHS and may support noninvasive assessment and more personalized disease management.

Literature Search Strategy

A narrative literature search was conducted using PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar from database inception to August 2026. Search terms included combinations of “obstructive sleep apnea”, “OSAHS”, “intermittent hypoxia”, “inflammation”, “oxidative stress”, “volatile organic compounds”, “VOCs”, “breathomics”, “metabolic reprogramming”, “airway remodeling”, “CPAP”, and “sex differences”. The reference lists of relevant articles were also screened to identify additional studies. Peer-reviewed original studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, consensus statements, and relevant narrative reviews were considered when they addressed the mechanistic or clinical relationships among OSAHS, intermittent hypoxia, inflammatory and oxidative responses, and exhaled VOC profiles. Duplicate records, non-peer-reviewed publications, and studies without direct relevance to the scope of this review were excluded. Because this was a narrative review, articles were selected according to their relevance, methodological quality, and contribution to the proposed conceptual framework rather than through a formal systematic-review protocol.
