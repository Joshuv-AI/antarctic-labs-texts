Effect of Size-Controlled Nanofluid on Mechanical Properties, Microstructure, and Rheological Behavior of Cement Slurry for Oil Well Cementing
Authors: Ramaswamy Gautam, Abhinav Hazra, Prashant Faujdar, Suvendu Sen, Bibhash Chandra Mishra, Tushar Sharma, Shailesh Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe optimal design of cement slurry by balancing various cement additives and cement is critical for effective oil well cementation job. However, given adverse circumstances of application, existing additives may not be sufficient to perform suitably in challenging conditions, leading to premature cement hydration, formation of microcracks, and gas channeling pathways. Thus, this study explores the use of a single-step silica nanofluid (NP size: 5–10, 90–100, and 250–300 nm and concentration: 1, 3, and 5 wt %) as an additive and explores its effect on thickening time, fluid loss, and rheological behavior of class G cement slurry at high-pressure and high-temperature (HPHT) conditions (135 °C and 3625 psi). The improvement in thickening time, fluid loss, and rheology of conventional slurry was greater for low NP size than the nanofluid of high NP size: the nanofluid size, e.g., 5–10 nm, and concentration (1 wt %) were found to accelerate the thickening time by 30–40% while reducing fluid loss from 38 mL (no silica, slurry CS) to 30 mL (with silica, slurry C1). The rheological behavior was studied via shear (viscosity) and dynamic (elastic moduli, G′) modes to evaluate the viscosity, hysteresis, and elastic response of slurry with and without nanofluid. The inclusion of nanofluid slightly reduced the slurry viscosity; however, all slurries exhibited shear thinning with superior fitting with the power law model. As compared to slurry CS, hysteresis of slurry C1 was least dependent on shear deformation, and thus, it showed that it almost matched viscosity profiles during loading and unloading cycles. The addition of silica was found to maintain the original properties of cement slurry, establishing that cement had not agglomerated, and no sedimentation was observed even at shear rates of 1000 s–1. The results of this study greatly promote the use of silica nanofluid as an important additive in class G cement for cementation operations, which is unlikely with a two-step nanofluid where nanoparticles are expensive, and upon mixing, they tend to agglomerate and make large size clusters.

Introduction

Conventionally, cement slurry is prepared by combining one of the types of cement (such as class A, B, C, G, and H) with water and other additives followed by its pumping down the casing and into the annulus as part of well completion operation. The primary goal of a well completion operation is to support and hold the casing while preventing formation fluid from corroding it.1,2 Another critical cementing goal is to produce zonal isolation of formations penetrated by the wellbore. Sometimes, cementation is also performed to close a part of an abandoned well.3 It prevents formation fluid from migrating between the casing and hole. Oil well cementation is performed at varying temperature and pressure conditions; thus, the cement beneath the casing must be of high quality for both primary cementing and workover and stimulation activities.3−5 High-quality cement extends the life of borehole by ensuring that the casing is appropriately supported and mitigates the risk of microcrack formation.6,7 Microcracks are tiny cracks that form in set cement because of drying shrinkage or other stress-inducing factors.8 These cracks can be caused by a variety of factors, including changes in temperature, pressure, or humidity, and can weaken the overall sheath of the set cement. The presence of microcracks in cement can lead to failure of cementing process due to fracture propagation as shown by the proposed schematic in Figure 1 for set conventional cement. If a crack is propagated far enough, it can create a pathway for fluid to flow through the cement (Figure 1), which can compromise the integrity of zonal isolation provided by cement. Additionally, the propagation of microcracks can weaken the mechanical strength of set cement, making it more susceptible to failure under load. This is the hallmark of low cement quality, and its presence behind the casing causes fluid channeling, which may demand remedial cementing, prolonging, and increasing the cost of cement project as such a well may require intervention.4

Thus, to improve the properties of a cement, certain additives can be added for various operating conditions.1,4 Other additives, e.g., thinners, extenders, lightweight agents, gas migration agents, corrosion inhibitors, and hydrogen sulfide and oxygen scavengers, may also be used; however, the efficacy of these additives has been found to reduce at adverse conditions of pressure and temperature.9−12 Cement slurry is susceptible to degradation at high temperature, especially during thickening period, as high temperature reduces the thickening time and affects the rheology, fluid loss, stability, and compressive strength, compromising their performance over a long term.1,10,13 In previous works,14,15 the use of polymeric additives as stabilizing agents in oil well cementing was least recommended as they were found to lose their effectiveness at 90 °C and completely lost it at 150 °C. Similarly, the use of sodium chloride caused salt-induced aggregation at high temperature (>110 °C) due to excessive water loss.4 Currently, antisettling agents are available only for moderate temperatures (>60 °C), and very few additives identified for the use of a high-temperature regime.16 This is important because most of the newer wells, being drilled, exhibit high temperature.17 The failure of conventional additives under these conditions has prompted the oil and gas industry to search for a new class of additives, which resist such adverse effects while maintaining their performance.18

The role of novel nanomaterials, as cementing additives, has received significant attention.4,19 For example, metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) of different metallic compositions and different forms (nanosilica, titanium dioxide, iron oxide, aluminum oxide, copper oxide, and zinc peroxide) have been used as cement additives to address a wide range of typical field concerns.20−22 In addition, the application of nanomaterials (either in dry or suspension form) such as TiO2, ZnO2, fullerenes, carbon nanotubes, silica, alumina, and clay has been investigated for their capacity to boost strength, stiffness, and ductility of cement slurries.20 However, most commonly, nanosilica or SiO2 has been used in several oilfield applications, including that of well completion due to their ease of fabrication, compatibility with oilfield environment, and low cost.4 The addition of silica NPs to cement slurry has been found favorable, resulting in improved cement functionality. Silica NPs tend to fill the spaces between cementitious grains in both hydrated and nonhydrated materials, creating a filling effect and improving the mechanical stability of cement by up to 25% as demonstrated in previous studies.20−23 The addition of silica NPs in cement has also produced nucleation sites for cement hydration, which provided effective setting of cement with significant control on the growth of microcracks as depicted in Figure 1. Silica reacts with calcium hydroxide and forms an additional phase of CSH (calcium–silicate–hydrate) bonds which help to increase cement strength.24−26 In a previous work,27 adding silica NPs (0.5 wt %) to class H cement was favorable in increasing cement strength by 16% while separately adding 0.15 wt % silica NPs improved the cement strength of class G cement by 7%.28 In a study,29 mixing 0.0132 wt % of mesoporous silica NPs in commercial cement yielded a compressive strength value of 6.67 MPa with a porosity and permeability of 8.94% and 0.025 mD, respectively. This exhibited up to 10% improvement over conventional cement without silica NPs. The benefits (improved strength, fluid loss, and stability) of using silica NPs in oil well cements have also been observed in other research works.20,30 However, in several lab and field investigations,20,31 silica NPs have been found to agglomerate during addition to cement slurry. For effective performance, silica NPs should show an independent particle size distribution; otherwise, they usually tend to show an increase in particle size (under agglomeration). As a result, the performance of silica NPs was found to degrade the mechanical strength of class H cement by 12%.21,27 Also, in some class G cements, mixing silica NPs has been found to be problematic as high surface energy contributed to agglomeration of NPs.32

Thus, the role of single-step silica nanofluids becomes very important as they exhibit independent silica NPs of controllable size in suspension. Also, they provide higher resistance to agglomeration, and consequently, a greater duration of stability is ensured than two-step silica nanofluids.24,33,34 Furthermore, since the nanofluid is prepared in a water-based medium, it is comparatively much easier to disperse in a cement slurry.35,36 This has the potential to improve the performance efficacy of cement slurry for varying adverse environments. However, these stable colloidal silica suspensions, prepared using a single-step route in water or viscous solutions, are yet to be explored for oilfield cementation. Thus, in this study, a cement slurry of class G cement was prepared (as per oilfield recommended composition) with and without single-step silica nanofluids of different sizes and concentrations, followed by the investigation of their comparative effectiveness on compressive strength, fluid loss, and rheological properties at pressure (3625 psi) and temperature (135 °C) conditions. The rheological analysis of cement sample (with and without silica nanofluid) was performed to evaluate the efficacy of cement slurry under flow in varying shear conditions. The role of silica nanofluids on cement setting time and amplitude sweep moduli of cement slurry was also explored as a function of varying silica content. The observations of the study can be a useful indicator for nanofluid use in improving cement performance at high-temperature and high-pressure conditions.

Preparation and Analysis of Nanofluid

Single-step silica nanofluids were prepared using a well-established Stober sol–gel process.37 For the purpose of simplicity, a quick overview of the procedure for making single-step silica nanofluids and characterizing them has been presented.33 The Stober sol–gel method is a simple and efficient method for producing silica NPs.38 Tetraethylorthosilicate, often known as TEOS (a kind of sodium alkoxide), is utilized as a precursor. Following the hydrolysis of TEOS in solution, polycondensation starts. This results in the formation of Si–O–Si bonds and a sol consisting of scattered particles. The addition of ammonia is crucial because the presence of ammonium ions affects the average particle size distribution in the reaction media. DLS (dynamic light scattering) experiments have shown that an increase in ammonia concentration leads to an increase in particle size. Similar parallels have been discovered in prior examinations.39,40 Initially, a PAM base fluid with a 1000 ppm concentration was created by dissolving 0.1 g of PAM in 100 mL of water. To achieve appropriate dispersion and homogeneity, the mixture was stirred on the stirrer at a continuous 600 rpm for 8 h. To make single-step nanofluids, 100 mL of PAM solution and 50 mL of ethanol were combined in a covered beaker and swirled at 300 rpm for 30 min. After 15 min of agitation, the beaker was placed in a sonicator to homogenize the dispersion. Sonication is a mechanical process involving the use of sound energy to create random agitation in particles at a high frequency (>20 kHz), and it is required for the preparation of a homogeneous suspension of colloidal particles. However, it has been demonstrated that polymer (PAM) qualities are temperature sensitive,36,41 and degradation was defined as a change in PAM properties generated by an increase in temperature during sonication.

TEOS (0.045–0.45 mol) was added using a pipet drop by drop, while the solution was sonicated for 30 min. Then, 0.28 mol of ammonium hydroxide was added to the solution in a dropwise manner to facilitate the condensation reaction. After 15 min of inactivity in the sonicator, the solution adopted the appearance of a milky white suspension, indicating the presence of silica NPs (NPs). A small drop (1–1.5 μL) of the nanofluid was put on a glass slide, dried in an oven at 150 °C, and then examined using a SEM to assess the form and size of the NPs in the fluid (FE-SEM, JEOL, JSM-7900F). The pH value of the solutions was measured using a Hanna Digital pH meter (Model-HI98129, range: 0–14), calibrated with known buffer solutions.

Nanofluids of three different concentrations (1, 3, and 5 wt %) with varying nanoparticle size (viz., 5–10, 90–100, and 250–300 nm) were prepared in this study. The size of the NPs in the produced silica nanofluid suspensions was determined utilizing a dynamic light scattering (DLS) equipment (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK) with a scattering angle of 173°. DLS is used to evaluate particle size distribution by monitoring time-dependent fluctuations.42 To ensure minimal sample contamination, all measurements were performed in well-cleaned and dried cuvettes.

Design of Cement Slurry

The cement slurry was mixed as per API RP 10B2 recommendations but, for proper mixing of cement, a Hamilton beach mixer was used for time around 1 min. A cement slurry of class G was prepared using a high-shear Hamilton Beach mixer at 20,000 rpm for 1 min. First, all additives (shown in Table 2) were carefully weighed and premixed with cement. DI water was poured in a mixing cup, and cement with additives was gradually added into the cup to prepare a slurry; slow addition of cement is advised to inhibit the formation of any lump. In this study, two types of nanofluid-treated cement slurries were prepared where the amount of DI water varied with two different wt % (i.e., 45 and 42.5% BWOC: by weight of cement). In the first slurry (type A), the amount of DI water was kept as 45% BWOC (by weight of cement), and the nanofluid (2.5%) was additionally added over the prepared slurry. In the second slurry (type B), 2.5% of nanofluid was premixed with DI water (42.5% BWOC) to keep the water/cement ratio as 45% in the slurry. Table 3 provides the details of both the slurries (with nomenclature). In addition, the schematic of slurry preparation with and without the nanofluid is also shown in Figure 2.

Static Fluid Loss Test of Slurry

Static fluid loss tests were conducted according to the API RP 10B2 procedure using the HPHT filter press.43 The differential pressure of 1000 psi was maintained at 90 °C. Since the operating temperature was lower than 93 °C, no backpressure was required. To measure fluid loss, the setup shown in Figure 3 was used. Cement slurries with and without the nanofluid were investigated for fluid loss and suitably compared to understand the role of nanofluid on fluid loss. Static fluid loss is a measure of fluid lost from cement slurry during the setting process.

Filter press, equipped with HPHT assembly, to measure fluid loss in cement slurry. A syringe pump was used to pressurize the filter press with N2 at 1000 psi.

Mechanistically, it is defined as the volume of fluid (usually water) that has leaked out of slurry and is not contained within the set cement. The fluid loss instrument works by simulating down-hole conditions where fluid loss can occur, when cement is injected to plug annulus. As shown in Table 3, cement slurries, e.g., CS and C1–C10, were tested for static fluid loss at 1000 psi and 90 °C. The slurry was filled in cup up to the marked level from bottom. The bottom lid was closed with filter paper of mesh size 325 (45 μm) and 60 mesh (250 μm) backing screen. Pressure was applied through a syringe pump exhibiting nitrogen gas in its long cylinder on top. When the system reached desired temperature, the bottom valve was gradually opened for the fluid to escape through filter paper and mesh. The amount of fluid collected in the measuring cylinder after 30 min was reported as fluid loss, as demonstrated by API guidelines in the literature.44

Thickening Time and Compressive Strength Tests of Cement

The cement thickening studies were performed for all slurries (CS and C1–C10) using a cement consistometer (see Figure 4). The maximum operating temperature of the consistometer was 204 °C. The thickening time studies were performed to estimate the maximum time available for a slurry to remain in flowing condition before setting.

Atmospheric consistometer to measure the thickening time of cement slurry. In the left side of the figure, there is a cylinder in which the slurry is poured and the lid is closed along with pedals attached to it.

The thickening of cement slurry was measured in Bearden unit of consistency (Bc), a dimensionless quantity from 0 to 100 units. Thickening time is the time required for the cement to reach a consistency of 100 Bc before setting. Bc number also corresponds to torque required to deflect an electrical potentiometer when the slurry is placed in the cup. The thickening tests on cement slurry were conducted at 90 °C. The cement slurry was poured in the cup up to 2–3 mm below the thread and then the bottom lid was closed. The potentiometer was attached to the cup, and the whole assembly was kept in a cylinder for test. The reading on dial exhibits an initial consistency of slurry. With time and temperature, the consistency of the slurry tends to increase. In this study, time to reach 30 Bc consistency was reported since 30 Bc corresponds to the time of initial setting in cement.45 The test was stopped after the reading reached 100 Bc, referred to as thickening time of cement slurry.

The compressive strength of hardened cement was tested on an automatic compressive strength testing machine (ACTM) of HLA 592 series of HEICO Corporation. The image of ACTM is shown in Figure 5. First, a cement mold was prepared for the cement curing autoclave, operated at 135 °C and 3625 psi for 48 h. The experimental setup of the HPHT cement curing autoclave is also shown in Figure 5. It helps to simulate the downhole environment for cement slurry so that their performance upon hydration can be investigated to obtain the desired performance. The mold was properly greased with silicon grease from inside before pouring the slurry, so that the cured samples do not stick while retrieving. Then, the mold containing slurry was placed in a curing autoclave filled with silicon oil. The top lid was tightened, and the pressure gauge was mounted to monitor the pressure. The pressure gradually increased to 3625 psi keeping the temperature constant at 135 °C. After 48 h, the slurry samples hardened due to the hydration reaction. Cement blocks were withdrawn from the autoclave chamber and submerged in DI water for cooling.

Experimental setup for determining the compressive strength of cured cement blocks. Scanning Electron Microscopy Analysis of Cement

SEM and EDAX (energy-dispersive X-ray) mapping of cement blocks were performed to ascertain the packing/distribution of silica in cement. SEM along with EDAX mapping was performed on aJEOL, JSM-7900F (JSM-7900F Schottky, Japan). SEM was performed on hydrated cement samples, which were thoroughly cleaned with a brush and dried in a vacuum oven to remove any remaining moisture and dust particles. The samples were removed after 6 h from a vacuum oven and immediately wrapped in aluminum foil to prevent any contamination from dust particles. Cement samples with and without nanofluids were examined in SEM analysis.

Rheological Analysis of Cement Slurries

A detailed rheological investigation of the cement slurries was carried out using a compact rheometer (MCR-52, Anton Paar, Physica, Austria), whose layout has been reported in Figure 6. These rheological investigations were conducted in a concentric cylinder arrangement of the rheometer. The tests were conducted at 3625 psi and 135 °C, and the shear rate was varied from 1 to 1000 s–1. The rheological investigation was performed in both shear (viscosity and yield stress) and dynamic modes (storage modulus: G′) for cement slurries at HPHT conditions, while the pressure was applied using a N2 cylinder and a hydraulic hand pump, and temperature was maintained using a temperature control bath, utilizing a silicone oil.35 A wide range of shear rates from 1 to 1000 s–1 was varied to perform shear measurements on cement slurry. Dynamic tests were conducted for strain sweep (amplitude sweep) measurements. The strain sweep analysis was performed to obtain G′ with respect to strain amplitude (0.1 to 100%), whereas frequency was fixed at 10 rad s–1. All observations were repeated thrice to ensure reproducibility in the results, and the uncertainty in the results was found to be in the order of 1.5–9%. Various parts of the rheometer (bob, cup, and spindle) were cleaned after each sample study with DI water and subsequently dried with the air blower to reduce cross contamination and improve the quality of results.

Experimental setup for rheological characterization at a temperature of 135 °C and 3625 psi. N2 was used to pressurize the pressure cell of a rheometer with the help of a syringe pump.

Results and Discussion

First, fluid loss and thickening time results of silica-treated cement slurries at HPHT conditions are reported, followed by the discussion on compressive strength results of cement. Subsequently, morphological and rheological analysis (both shear and dynamic) results are discussed to evaluate the role of silica treatment on cement properties. Finally, the rheological hysteresis of nanofluid-treated slurry was discussed and compared with the one of conventional cement slurry.

The C2 slurry exhibited a fluid loss of 32 mL, while that of C3 was found to be 35 mL. Thus, it indicates that fluid loss slightly increased with increasing NP size in the slurry. The reason might be associated with the lower size of NP in the slurry as the NP of lower size exhibits higher specific area and energy to interact with embedded ingredients in the system.

It is expected that NPs of 5–10 nm in C1 slurry interacted and superiorly filled the openings between cement grains probably, resulting in probably the reduced rate of outflow of water from cement.46−48 On the other hand, NPs of higher size, e.g., 250–300 nm, could not develop an intense cementitious structure, and therefore, the rate of out-flow was slightly higher for C2 and C3 slurries. The impact of higher NP concentration on fluid loss is investigated for NP size as 250–300 nm, and the results are shown in Table 4. C4 and C5 slurries exhibit NP concentration as 3 and 5 wt %, respectively. It was observed that the fluid loss value of C3 (35 mL for 250–300 nm/1 wt %) reduced to 33 mL when the concentration increased to 3 wt % (C4), keeping the NP size constant. With a further increase in NP concentration (5 wt %), the fluid loss reduced to 31 mL for slurry C5. It is to be noted here that the NP size in C3, C4, and C5 slurries was constant, viz., 250–300 nm; however, the NP concentration varied to 1, 3, and 5 wt %, respectively. Thus, it can be understood that increasing NP concentration also reduced the amount of fluid loss from the slurry, which might be credited to higher population of NPs in the system.49 Since the cement slurry of higher NP size (250–300 nm) exhibited higher fluid loss than the one of lower NP size (5–10 nm), the increase in NP concentration can be a plausible solution to control fluid loss in cement slurry. It is possible that higher population of NPs led to more adsorption between cement grains, and as a result, it developed an impermeable layer against which water could not percolate enough. Therefore, it can be concluded that either small NP size or higher NP concentration is favorable for the reduction in fluid loss from conventional cement slurries.

Table 4 also shows fluid loss results for type B slurries, where 2.5% of nanofluid was premixed with DI water (42.5% BWOC) followed by the mixing of cement and additives. The fluid loss value of 27 mL was measured for slurry C6, which was lower than the value (30 mL) of counterpart C1 (where the nanofluid was added over the prepared slurry). Thus, it is to be noted here that premixing of nanofluid (5–10 nm) in DI water was favorable, and it reduced the amount of fluid loss by 10%. A similar amount was observed for C7 and C8 slurries, where nanofluids of higher NP size (90–100 and 250–300 nm) were premixed with DI water (Figure 7b). For C7 and C8 slurries, the fluid loss value was measured as 29 and 32 mL, respectively, ∼9% and 8.5% lower than the values associated with C2 and C3, respectively. The credit might be attributed to premixing of nanofluid in DI water, and thus, nanofluid received enough time to alter the properties of DI water so that, when cement was mixed, it could have affected the cement properties during its preparation in a mixer. However, in another case (where the nanofluid was added after the preparation of slurry), NPs could not affect the slurry much as the sites were already occupied/absorbed by water molecules. With an increase in NP concentration (3 and 5 wt %, NP size: 250–300 nm), the fluid loss value was determined as 31 mL (C9) and 29.5 mL (C10), respectively (Figure 7c). These values are also lower than the values determined for C4 (33 mL) and C5 (31 mL) slurries, which evidently proves that nanofluid inclusion before cement was advantageous to control fluid loss in slurry. The nanofluid was synthesized by the sol–gel method and that too using a single-step method. So, the cost of nanofluid only involves the cost of chemicals involved in the synthesis of nanofluids. Specifically, to prepare 1000 mL of 1 wt % nanofluid, it will take not more than INR 140/- and for 5 wt %, the cost is around INR 470/-. However, commercially available silica NPs cost around INR 7020/- for 100 g NPs. Thus, the cost of a synthesized single-step silica nanofluid of similar NP quantity is quite cheaper than commercially available silica NPs.

Thickening Time of Nanofluid-Treated Cement Slurry

Cement slurries are colloidal suspensions consisting of distinct solid and liquid phases. The thickening time is the time required for a cement to show inability of being pumped or begin to thick. The sooner the cement slurry hydrates, the shorter the cement’s setting time. The tests of thickening time of cement slurries were conducted at 90 °C, and the results are also provided in Table 4 and Figure 7, where time to reach 30 Bc (t30) often corresponds to initial setting of cement.50 Conventionally, cement sets by hydration of water entrapped in slurry as established by a consistometer. Hydration is a chemical reaction wherein chemicals in cement create chemical bonds with molecules of water.51 Silica NPs can act as hydration sites throughout the voids in cement; thus, uniform setting in cement is expected as proposed for the role of silica NP on cement hydration and microstructure in Figure 8.

Proposed schematic to illustrate the impact of silica NPs on cement hydration and microstructure refinement in a conventional class G cement.

Initially, classical slurry (CS) was tested for consistency, which was found to be 21 Bc. Further, CS took 124 min to reach 30 Bc (Table 4) and additional around 20 min to attain 100 Bc. When slurries C1–C5 were tested for thickening time, initial consistency of slurry C1 was determined as 20 Bc. Since the nanofluid was included in the C1 slurry, its flow ability improved, and as a result, initial thickening time (t30) reached 165 min (Table 4). Further, it took an additional 30 min to reach a consistency of 100 Bc, which implies that a total of 195 min were taken by C1 before showing the inability of being pumped. It indicates that C1 remained in the pumping condition for spare 51 min than CS (∼35% greater flowing time than CS), which is advantageous for pumping slurry at deeper depths where conventional slurry usually displays challenges or premature thickening. In other studies,21,46 the credit of delay in thickening time has also been given to PAM. C2 and C3 slurries took 160 and 156 min, respectively, to reach a consistency level of 30 Bc (Table 4).52 For C2 and C3 slurries, the size of NP was higher (90–100 nm/C2 and 250–300 nm/C3), and these results are in line with fluid loss results where higher NP size resulted into greater fluid loss than lower NP size in the slurry. The effect of higher NP concentration (3 and 5 wt %) on the thickening time of slurry is also investigated, and these results are reported for C4 and C5 slurries in Figure 7c. These slurries took 154 min (C4) and 145 min (C5) to reach a consistency level of 30 Bc, still higher than 124 min of CS. Thus, both concentration and size of NP are critical properties to affect the properties of cement slurry. In this study, lower concentration and size (1 wt % and 5–10 nm) of NPs showed promising results than higher concentration and size.

Similarly, for another set of slurries, where the nanofluid was mixed before the preparation of cement, C6 took 174 min to reach 30 Bc, whereas C7 and C8 slurries needed 168 and 164 min, respectively (Figure 7b). The thickening time of C6 slurry was higher (∼5.5%) than the time taken by C1 slurry, which again supports premixing of nanofluid (5–10 nm) in DI water as a favorable route of slurry design. Further, with increasing NP concentration (3 and 5 wt %), slurry C8 exhibited a slight increase in thickening time, indicating that a consistency time of 30 Bc reached the level of 169 and 161 min for C9 and C10, respectively (Figure 7c). Furthermore, an additional 25–20 min was required by slurry samples to reach a consistency of 100 Bc. It is also to be noted here that the two sets of slurries (C1–C5) and (C6–C10) had different thickening, which may be attributed to the sequence of nanofluid inclusion (before/after) in slurry. However, in both the cases, nanofluid inclusion in cement slurry was favorable as compared to the slurry prepared without the nanofluid (CS). It is also possible that increased concentration of NPs provided increased number of nucleation sites for hydration that resulted into a rapid hydration reaction.4,53 Finally, it can be concluded that the slurry, prepared with a nanofluid of size 5–10 nm, exhibited a least amount of fluid loss (slurries C1 and C6), indicating that its thickening time increased by 35%. For slurries C2, C3, C7, and C8 (Figure 7a,b), a considerable decrease in thickening time was observed when the particle size of the nanofluid increased to 90–100 and 250–300 nm. On the other hand, with increasing NP concentration (from 1 to 3 and 5 wt %), the thickening time increased by 22% for slurries C4, C5, C9, and C10 (Figure 7c). Thus, the presence of colloidal silica in cement slurry inhibits excess water from being expulsed into formation, allowing cement slurry to stay wet for longer periods of time.

Effect of Nanofluids on the Compressive Strength of Cement

The compressive strength of cured cement blocks was measured by a destructive method where peak load was determined for each cement block (with and without nanofluid) after 48 h followed by determination of compressive strength from eq 1. Table 4 and Figure 9 show the results of compressive strength along with actual images of cement blocks before and after the test at 135 °C and 3625 psi. For cement block of slurry CS, compressive strength was measured as 2214 psi, while the C1 block exhibited a higher compressive strength of 2610 psi. It is to be noted here that the compressive strength value of conventional cement increased by 18% after the addition of 1 wt % silica nanofluid (5–10 nm). Further, it was observed that the compressive strength value slightly reduced to 2491 and 2465 psi, respectively, for C2 and C3 blocks (Table 4). This is consistent with fluid loss and thickening results of these slurries where lower NP size (5–10 nm)-based cement slurry displayed superior performance than the ones of higher NP size. For C4 and C5 blocks, the concentration of NP was higher (3 and 5 wt %), and thus, the compressive strength value of the C3 block was further found to be 2403 and 2391 psi, respectively (Table 4). A decrease of 2.5% and 3% in compressive strength of the C3 block is observed after the inclusion of higher concentration (3 and 5 wt %) of a nanofluid of 250–300 nm. The results of compressive strength for other set of slurries are also presented in Table 4. The compressive strength of the C6 block was lower (2561 psi) than the value associated with the C1 block (2610 psi).

Compressive strength results of conventional (CS) and nanofluid-treated cement slurries as a function of varying NP size (C1/C2/C3/C6/C7/C8) and concentration (C4/C5/C9/C10) after aging at 135 °C and 3625 psi for 48 h in a HPHT cement curing autoclave.

For C6, the compressive strength was ∼2% lower than that of C1, and the reason for lower compressive strength in the C6 block can be attributed to a less amount of water available for hydration reaction and low compaction that reduces the compressive strength. The compressive strength further reduces with increasing NP size, and it reached to the level of 2476 and 2448 psi for C7 and C8 blocks, respectively (Table 4). With increasing NP concentration, the compressive strength was calculated as 2402 and 2385 psi for C9 and C10 blocks, respectively (Figure 9). Thus, it is to be noted here that the compressive strength of both the nanofluid-treated cement blocks was higher than the one of a conventional CS block. However, the increment was slightly higher for the set of cement blocks (C1–C5) in which the nanofluid was added after the preparation of slurry. This marginal increase in the compressive strength may be attributed to the more amount of water available for hydration reaction or more compaction during curing test in an autoclave. The research findings suggested that increasing the concentration of nanosilica in cement slurry leads to reduction in the density of slurry,54 which was mechanistically counted for the reduction in compressive strength of cement. In this study, similar results were observed when increasing nanofluid concentration leads to significant reduction in compressive strength of cement slurry (C4/C5/C9/C10) than C3/C8 slurries. Mechanistically, cement suffers from strength retrogression at temperature above 110 °C.55,56 In the oilfield industry, the requirement of chemicals is usually high, and procuring costly NPs in bulk size will increase the overall cost of the project. Thus, it is economically good if industries cut down the use of costly NPs without compromising the performance of the cementing process. In this study, though cement prepared with higher NP concentration showed fair control on fluid loss and thickening time but these properties were better when the NP concentration was 1 wt %. Moreover, for 1 wt %, the combination of least NP size (5–10 nm) is more advisable to use as lower size of NPs create better filtering cake against the formation and shows least retention during flow through tortuous and heterogeneous pore throats.57−59 In this study, different concentrations (1–5 wt %) and amounts (1–20%) of nanofluids were used in cement slurry, and it was found that the nanofluid impact on cement slurry followed an optimal concentration beyond which no significant impact was observed on the properties of cement slurry. As per the results, the upper limit of the nanofluid in cement slurry was around 10%, and the optimum concentration of the nanofluid can be regarded as 1–3 wt % or an optimum amount of 5–6%.

Shear Rheological Analysis of Cement Slurries

Next, the shear rheological analysis of cement slurries was performed. Rheological analysis of cement slurry is an important tool to predict its end use performance and physical properties in set cement. The rheological analysis of slurries was performed at 135 °C and 3625 psi. By studying the rheological behavior of the slurry under various flow conditions, researchers can gain an insight into the fluid behavior during pumping, placement, and setting and can identify conditions that are likely to lead the formation of microcracks.5,61 These results have been reported in Figure 12. The cement slurries displayed a shear thinning nature wherein upon increasing shear rate applied, the viscosity decreased. It was found that the viscosity of CS was 57,110 mPa s at 1 s–1, which dropped to 193 mPa s at 1000 s–1 (Figure 12a). It clearly confirms classical shear thinning behavior of CS slurry. For C1 and C6 slurries, a similar behavior was observed where an initial viscosity of 44,012 and 45,823 mPa s (at 1 s–1) decreased to 301 and 280 mPa s at 1000 s–1, respectively. As compared to C6, the viscosity value of both C1 was marginally lower, which might be attributed to more amount of water in that slurry. For slurries C2 and C7, a nanofluid of higher NP size (90–100 nm) was used, and therefore, their viscosity values were lower (31,051 and 26,051 mPa s, respectively) that the ones of C1 and C6 where a nanofluid of lower NP size (5–10 nm) was used. This indicates that viscosity is indirectly proportional to NP size: the higher the size of NP in slurry, the lower is the value of viscosity. It is expected as a NP of lower size has the tendency to occupy more sites in cement sheath where no NP of higher size will go readily. Moreover, a NP of lower size possesses high surface area, and as a result, it creates grater interaction with embedded additives in the cement and other viscous suspensions, which is also supported by the research findings in reported literature based on Pickering emulsion, drilling fluid, and polymer solution.62 Further, the effect of NP size on the viscosity of cement slurry was evident from viscosity results of C3 and C8 (Figure 12b,c) as these slurries not only showed shear thinning behavior but also exhibited lower viscosity values of 17,275 and 18,910 mPa s (1 s–1), respectively, consistent with the effect of higher NP size on the viscosity of slurry. With higher NP concentration (3 wt %), the viscosity of slurry increased to 18,261 (C4) and 31,215 mPa s (C9), higher than the viscosity value of C3 and C8 which consisted of the same nanofluid (250–300 nm) of lower NP concentration (1 wt %) (Figure 12b,c).

Further elevation in NP concentration (5 wt %) led to an increase in slurry viscosity, resulting in C5 and C10 exhibiting viscosity values of 24,162 and 35,428 mPa s (at 1 s–1), respectively (Figure 12d). Thus, in this study, the results of the effect of NP size and concentration on the viscosity of cement slurry confirmed that slurries containing a nanofluid of lower NP size (5–10 nm) showed marginal change in viscosity as compared to slurries containing higher NP size (90–100 and 250–300 nm). Similarly, slurries with high NP concentration (3 and 5 wt %) exhibited slight change in viscosity. In addition, it can be stated that all cement slurries showed flow ability, as evident from shear thinning behavior at 135 °C (Figure 12). It was found that the power law model comparatively showed better fit for the flow behavior of these cement slurries with coefficient (R2) value in between 0.96 and 0.99 (96 to 99%) as given in Table 5. where η is the apparent viscosity of the fluid, K is the consistency index, γ is the shear rate, and n is the power law exponent. The power law exponent is a measure of the fluid’s rheological behavior and can range from 0 to 1, with different values of n corresponding to different types of non-Newtonian fluid behavior.63,64 The power law is an important factor to consider during cement slurry injection and flow because it provides a way to quantify the rheological behavior of the slurry and to predict how it will behave under different flow conditions. The power law equation can be used to predict the apparent viscosity of the slurry as a function of shear rate and consistency index, which can be used to design pumping and placement operations, and to estimate the maximum pumping rate and pressure that can be used without damaging the wellbore. The value of the power law exponent can have a significant impact on the fluid flow and behavior of a cement slurry in the field. Conventionally, a lower value of the power law exponent (closer to 0) indicates a more shear-thinning fluid. In general, shear-thinning fluids are more easily pumped and placed, while shear-thickening fluids are more challenging to pump and place. Additionally, the value of the power law exponent can impact the fluid loss from the slurry during pumping and placement and can affect the slurry’s ability to maintain stability and provide adequate zonal isolation. Elastic Response of Cement Slurries

The dynamic oscillatory mode (strain sweep analysis: G′ vs shear strain) of rheological analysis is a type of test that involves viscoelastic characterization of a material such as a cement slurry. This type of test involves a range of dynamic strain and measures the resulting response of material.66 Strain sweep analysis was performed at 135 °C and 3625 psi to envisage the elastic response of cement slurries (conventional: CS and nanofluid treated: type A/B). The resulting elastic response of cement slurries is useful to understand their flow behavior in different flow regimes for flow through annulus, casing, and channel b/w casing and rock, where a different rate of deformation is expected. For cement slurry, G′ vs shear strain can provide valuable information about flow behavior of slurry during the setting and curing process. For CS, the initial value of G′ (at 0.1%) was found to be 1029.5 Pa, which reduced to 912.24 Pa at 100% shear strain as shown in Figure 13a.

For C7 and C8, the G′ value at 100% was found to be 940 and 927 Pa, respectively, as shown in Figure 13b. From G′ data, it can be established that the nanofluid improved the elastic response of cement slurry and made it more favorable for the industrial application as compared to conventional slurry. Thus, the results of the investigation can be used to optimize the composition and processing parameters of the slurry to ensure that it meets the required performance standards for a given application in drilling or cementing job. Moreover, the results of dynamic amplitude sweep can be particularly valuable because they can help to predict the behavior of slurry during drilling, pumping, and placement operations. This information can be used to make decisions about pumping rate, pumping pressure, and other parameters that can impact the performance of slurry in wellbore. Additionally, the results of rheological investigation can be used to assess the suitability of slurry for different types of wellbore environments and to identify potential issues that may arise during the cementing process.61

Hysteresis Analysis of Cement Slurries

Conventionally, the cement slurry experiences varying shear rate as it travels from the wellhead to wellbore in the reservoir through porous media. Consequently, the effect of applied shear on cement injectivity is drastically altered even if the rate of cement injection or its velocity remains constant, a behavior also observed in polymers during injection.67,68 Thus, to understand this effect, the cement slurries (nanofluid treated: C1 and conventional: CS) were subjected to varying shear from zero-shear conditions to 1000 s–1, and it was reduced again to zero shear. With the help of viscosity vs shear rate profiles, the hysteresis loops were drawn for CS and C1 in Figure 14. If no degradation of cement slurry takes place, the viscosity values during loading (increasing shear) and unloading (decreasing shear) would remain almost constant, and no change in viscosity profile would be observed. As per Figure 14, both the slurries did not exactly match the viscosity profiles during loading (increasing shear) and unloading (decreasing shear); however, C1 showed relatively better response as its viscosity profiles seem to be closer than the ones of CS (Figure 14b). In slurry CS, some change in values of viscosity were observed during the regime of 10–100 s–1 (Figure 14a).

This was hardly observed for slurry C1, establishing the role of silica NP as a stabilizing agent for cement rheology. A cement with degradation profile is bound to display cracks during setting and may compromise well completion. Thus, when silica NPs were introduced to the cement slurry, the gap in loading and unloading profiles reduced significantly, establishing the superior role of silica-enriched cement slurry for injection.

Conclusions

Cementation is one of the most important steps of well completion, and operators have learned it the hard way after cement sheaths have failed to provide adequate zonal isolation due to the formation of microcracks and vugs, along with adverse HPHT conditions. In this study, single-step silica nanofluids of varying NP size (5–10, 90–100, and 250–300 nm) and concentration (1, 3, and 5 wt %) were used as an additive to examine its role on thickening time, fluid loss, compressive strength, and rheological behavior of a conventional class G cement. Two types of cement slurries (type A: C1/C2/C3/C4/C5 and type B: C6/C7/C8/C9/C10) were prepared using nanofluids, and both demonstrated that the inclusion of single-step silica nanofluids significantly improved different properties of the class G cement; however, the rate of improvement varied as per NP size, concentration, and method of slurry preparation. The inclusion of low NP size (5–10 nm) was found favorable as it provided greater improvement in compressive strength (16–18% increase), fluid loss (20% reduction), and thickening time of slurry than high NP size (90–100 and 250–300 nm). The trait of single step, wherein the NP exhibits an independent and nonagglomerated form, was evident by SEM images which confirmed uniform distribution of silica throughout the cement. The rheological results of HPHT conditions (135 °C and 3625 psi) showed that all slurries, including conventional CS, followed classical shear thinning behavior with viscosity values closer to the viscosity value of conventional slurry. Nanofluid-treated slurries showed better elastic response as they exhibited minimal impact of strain deformation on G′ values and trend, which significantly varied and reduced in the case of conventional slurry CS. Even, hysteresis analysis of slurry C1 was more rheologically stable than slurry CS as its viscosity and profiles (obtained during loading/unloading cycles) almost matched which promotes industrial application (such as cementing, injection, and drilling) of this slurry. These results have important implications for the performance of class G cement in well completion operations, especially when the slurry is used at HPHT conditions.
