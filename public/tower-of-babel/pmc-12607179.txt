Platelet-rich plasma enhanced therapy for aseptic atrophic tibial diaphyseal nonunion: a single-center retrospective controlled study of 90 cases
Authors: Guoqing Niu, Shiheng Wang, Jianrong Chen, Zhiliang Ma, Ke Jie, Jiuxu Yao, Zhiling Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough successful outcomes of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in treating nonunion fractures have been reported, there are widespread confounding factors and biases in the studies. This study aims to assess the biological benefits of PRP-enhanced therapy while controlling for the two core factors of nonunion fractures—biomechanics and biology.MethodsRetrospective analysis of medical records of patients with aseptic atrophic tibial diaphyseal fracture nonunion treated with a single locking plate in a tertiary trauma center. Patients were divided into the PRP group and the non-PRP group based on the use of PRP. The surgical procedure for the non-PRP group was a single locked plate fixation combined with autologous iliac bone grafting. For the PRP group, PRP-enhanced autologous iliac bone grafting and the creation of a bioactive chamber were added. The outcome measures included the union rate, average healing time, visual analog scale pain score, lower limb function score, and complications.ResultsA total of 90 patients were included, with 39 patients in the PRP group and 51 patients in the non-PRP group. The average follow-up was 16.15 ± 3.14 months in the PRP group and 15.86 ± 3.26 months in the non-PRP group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of demographic characteristics and disease parameters (P > 0.05). At 9 months post-revision surgery, the healing rate in the PRP group was higher than that in the non-PRP group (58.97% vs 33.33%, p = 0.015,χ2 = 5.885), but the final union rate did not differ significantly (92.31% vs 78.43%, p = 0.072,χ2 = 3.240). After excluding cases of persistent nonunion, the PRP group had a significantly shorter average healing time compared to the non-PRP group (8.88 ± 2.03 months vs 10.54 ± 2.59 months, p = 0.002, t = −3.103,95%CI: [ −2.74, −0.60]) and a higher lower limb function score (67.72 ± 4.18 vs 64.55 ± 5.98, p = 0.003, t = 2.700, 95%CI: [0.83, 5.52]). The PRP group had 4 (10.26%) complications, while the non-PRP group had 8 (15.69%) complications.ConclusionIn the treatment of atrophic fracture nonunion, PRP enhanced autologous iliac bone grafting can shorten bone healing time and accelerate the healing process, thereby improving limb function more rapidly. This finding provides a superior treatment option clinically for patients in need of accelerated nonunion healing and early recovery of lower limb function.Keywords: Platelet-rich plasma, Autologous iliac bone grafting, Nonunion fracture, Tibial diaphyseal nonunion, Atrophic nonunion, Aseptic nonunion, Diamond concept

Introduction

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the United States defined nonunion of fractures in 1986 as a fracture that has not healed within nine months or shows no significant progress on radiographic examination for three consecutive months [1]. However, this definition does not apply to all fractures, as the expected healing time depends on the fracture site, fracture type, and overall health condition [1, 2]. Due to the triangular shape of the tibial diaphysis, thick anterior cortical bone, and minimal soft tissue coverage, particularly in fractures of the lower third of the tibia, which rely on a single blood supply, this area is one of the most prone to nonunion following fracture [1, 3]. According to the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) [3], the annual incidence rate of tibial nonunion is 12–19%, and this rate is even higher in developing countries.

Currently, autologous iliac bone grafting (AICBG) is considered the “gold standard” for treating atrophic nonunion fractures. However, not all AICBG treatments yield optimal results [3, 5, 42]. To address this complex challenge, the AAOS recommends a mixed approach, using biological agents to enhance autologous bone grafting [3]. For example, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), demineralized bone matrix (DBM), and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) are used as biological growth factors or bone defect fillers [4]. This approach shows great potential for innovation and has been the subject of pioneering research in recent years [35]. However, it lacks unbiased, robust clinical evidence, and reports on its efficacy show significant discrepancies [4, 6]. Specifically, PRP has attracted significant attention in various subfields, but there is a lack of critical evidence supporting its role in treating nonunion fractures [4, 7, 8]. As reported by Elliott et al. [2], these studies require improved design to control for biomechanical factors and other confounding variables to validate their biological effects. Our previous research [9] provided updated evidence in support of this type of study, demonstrating that PRP-enhanced AICBG therapy significantly promotes femoral healing compared to AICBG alone. However, some degree of bias and confounding factors remain. Therefore, the present study aims to evaluate the efficacy of PRP-enhanced therapy in treating aseptic atrophic tibial diaphyseal nonunion and, through a relatively robust study design (simultaneously control the two core factors of atrophic nonunion, namely biomechanical factors and biological factors), to further clarify the biological effects of PRP in nonunion fractures.

Patients and methods

This is a single-center retrospective controlled study, which has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Foshan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No.: KY[2024]215), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local laws. All participants provided written informed consent. A retrospective analysis was conducted on the medical records of patients treated for aseptic atrophic tibial diaphyseal nonunion with revision surgery at a tertiary trauma hospital between January 2019 and July 2024. The diagnosis of nonunion followed the FDA’s definition [1]. Nonunion classification was based on the Weber and Cech classification, dividing it into hypertrophic, atrophic, and oligotrophic types [32]. Patients were divided into the PRP group and the non-PRP group based on whether PRP was used. The non-PRP group received single locked plate fixation with structural autologous iliac bone grafting, while the PRP group received PRP-enhanced therapy in addition to the non-PRP procedure.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years with closed epiphyses; (2) aseptic nonunion diagnosed by intraoperative deep tissue culture and pathology; (3) atrophic nonunion fracture; (4) fractures located in the 42 regions according to the Orthopaedic Trauma Association (OTA) classification; (5) single locked plate fixation; (6) autologous iliac bone grafting treatment; (7) bone defect ≤ 5 cm; (8) PRP-enhanced therapy (for PRP group only).

The exclusion criteria were: (1) pathological fractures; (2) infectious nonunion fractures; (3) use of non-single locked plate fixation; (4) missing medical records or loss to follow-up; (5) diagnosed osteoporosis by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; (6) hematological diseases; (7) use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications; (8) abnormal platelet count; (9) multiple nonunion fractures; (10) use of synthetic bone or allografts.

The study’s imaging outcomes included the healing rates at the 9-month revision and the final follow-up [9]; clinical outcomes [9, 11, 20] included the average time to healing, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain scores [33], Lower Extremity Function Scale (LEFS) scores [34], and complications (donor site morbidity, calf surgical site infection, hematoma, heterotopic ossification, thrombosis, and chronic pain were considered complications).

Surgical procedure

All included patients were treated by the same surgical team, with bone grafting harvested from the anterior iliac crest for both groups. The revision surgeries strictly followed the methods reported in the literature [9–12]. After general anesthesia or epidural anesthesia, the patient was placed in a supine position. The original surgical incision scar was followed to expose the nonunion site (Fig. 1 A), with careful protection of the medial calf soft tissue. After exposing the nonunion site, the failed internal fixation device was removed. Thorough debridement of the nonunion site was performed to remove all non-viable tissue (for pathological testing), leaving fresh, hemorrhaging bone at the fracture site. The medullary cavity was re-communicated, and the fracture site exhibited the “red pepper” sign (Fig. 1 BCD). The surgical field was irrigated, and the length and alignment of the calf were corrected. A locked compression plate was placed on the lateral or medial side of the tibia (The plate insertion site is determined by disease requirements and surgeon preference.), with 1–2 screws placed at both ends for temporary fixation (Fig. 1 H).

A: Exposure of the nonunion site, showing discontinuity at the fracture ends and an obliterated medullary cavity; B: Debridement of all non-viable tissues; C: Exposure of rough, freshly bleeding bone surfaces and recanalization of the medullary cavity; DEF: Harvesting of autologous iliac bone of equal volume according to the shape and extent of the defect area; G: Immersion of the bone to be grafted in platelet-rich plasma (PRP) to ensure adequate adsorption of PRP; H: After correction of tibial alignment, placement of a plate on the lateral side to temporarily maintain reduction; I: After tightly impacting the PRP-impregnated bone grafts into the defect area, insertion of appropriate screws with compression to achieve close contact between the grafted bone and the host tibia; JK: With the graft area as the center, suturing of the surrounding remaining viable tissues (e.g., periosteum) into a chamber that encloses the graft area, followed by injection of the remaining PRP into this chamber

PRP Group: Fifty milliliters of blood were drawn from the patient’s elbow or neck vein and added to a PRP tube pre-filled with 5 mL of sodium citrate anticoagulant. The sample was sent to the laboratory and placed in a centrifuge, where it was spun at 2000 r/min for 10 min. Approximately 10 mL of light yellow supernatant was extracted, and the PRP concentration was tested, which was found to be 4–5 times the original platelet count (range: 700 × 10^9/L to 900 × 10^9/L). Simultaneously, bone graft was harvested from the ipsilateral anterior iliac crest, with an equal volume of full-thickness iliac bone grafts being obtained (Fig. 1 EF). The iliac bone grafts were trimmed according to the shape of the defect. The trimmed full-thickness iliac bone grafts and cancellous bone were immersed in 10 mL of PRP solution for 2 min (Fig. 1 G). The PRP-enhanced full-thickness iliac bone grafts were then implanted into the defect area, and the remaining cancellous bone was tightly packed into the gaps (Fig. 1I). The remaining PRP solution was stored in a sterile incubator at 4 °C. Subsequently, appropriate screws were placed on both sides of the plate for compression fixation (Fig. 1I). After ensuring hemostasis, residual active tissues such as the periosteum, deep fascia, and soft tissue around the nonunion site were continuously and tightly sutured, creating a sealed cavity adjacent to the bone surface. The remaining PRP solution was injected into the sealed cavity using a syringe (Fig. 1 JK). Finally, the incision was closed with tension-relieving sutures layer by layer, followed by compression dressing.

Non-PRP Group: After harvesting bone graft from the ipsilateral anterior iliac crest, the bone was implanted into the defect site, followed by plate fixation. Then, suture the remaining periosteum, deep fascia, and skin layers, with layer-by-layer tension-relieving sutures ensured, and finally apply routine compression dressing to the leg incision.

Postoperative management and rehabilitation

All patients received systematic smoking cessation guidance and health education on fracture nonunion prior to surgery. A second-generation cephalosporin was administered prophylactically during surgery and continued for 48 h postoperatively. Bedside rehabilitation training started on the first postoperative day, including ankle pump exercises and quadriceps isometric contraction exercises. Light weight-bearing walking training with a walker was initiated within 2–4 weeks postoperatively, gradually increasing weight-bearing based on clinical and radiographic improvements. Patients were followed up in the outpatient clinic at 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively. An X-ray was taken during each visit to assess union status, and if the X-ray was insufficient, a CT scan was performed. The healing criteria were a modified Radiographic Union Score for Tibia Fractures (mRUST) ≥ 12 points [30], and the ability to walk independently for at least 300 m (evaluated independently by a senior trauma specialist and two radiologists not involved in the study). mRUST: This score assesses the cortical bone callus on anteroposterior and lateral X-ray images based on four cortices. Each cortex is rated from 1 to 4, with a total score of 16. The final follow-up assessment included the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) pain score and Lower Extremity Function Scale (LEFS) score (assessed by a senior orthopedic surgery postgraduate student). Complications were recorded from outpatient records and confirmed by a follow-up phone call.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, age), baseline features (e.g., smoking status, length of hospitalization, injury mechanism), disease parameters (e.g., disease duration, preoperative scores), efficacy indicators, and complications were collected and analyzed for all patients.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage. For continuous variables, normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared between the two groups using an independent sample t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Data analysis was performed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA), with all tests being two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 278 medical records were reviewed, with 188 excluded based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the PRP group, 83 cases were excluded, including 26 cases of hypertrophic nonunion, 23 cases of non-tibial diaphyseal nonunion, 19 cases of non-single plate fixation (5 intramedullary nails, 9 intramedullary nails combined with auxiliary plates, 4 double plates, 1 Taylor frame), 7 cases of large bone defects, 4 cases of multiple nonunions, 2 cases of aspirin use, and 2 cases of infectious nonunion. In the non-PRP group, 105 cases were excluded, including 28 cases of hypertrophic nonunion, 27 cases of non-tibial diaphyseal nonunion, 31 cases of non-single plate fixation (1 intramedullary nail, 21 intramedullary nails with auxiliary plates, 7 double plates, 2 external fixators), 12 cases of large bone defects, 3 cases of multiple nonunions, 3 cases of infectious nonunion, and 1 case with missing medical records.

Finally, 90 patients were included in the study. In the PRP group, 39 patients (29 males [74.36%], 10 females [25.64%]) had a mean age of 43.10 ± 13.66 years (range: 20–77 years); in the non-PRP group, 51 patients (39 males [76.47%], 12 females [23.53%]) had a mean age of 44.04 ± 14.26 years (range: 18–74 years). The mean follow-up was 16.15 ± 3.14 months (range: 12–24 months) for the PRP group and 15.86 ± 3.26 months (range: 12–24 months) for the non-PRP group, with no significant difference in follow-up duration (p = 0.533). There were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between the two groups (Table 1).

Discussion

In recent years, the use of biological agents in orthopedics has gained increasing attention [4, 6] [38]. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), as an autologous blood product, contains concentrated platelets and various growth factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor, and epidermal growth factor. These growth factors promote the migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells, thereby aiding in the repair and regeneration of bone tissue [7, 8, 18] [38]. In our study, PRP involvement significantly improved the success rate of autologous iliac bone grafting combined with plate fixation for treating atrophic tibial diaphyseal nonunion, shortening the time required for bone healing. This reduction in healing time allows for earlier initiation of advanced rehabilitation, accelerating functional recovery of the limb. Furthermore, due to the timely initiation of the rehabilitation process, the incidence of complications such as thrombosis and chronic pain is reduced. With the innovation of preparation techniques, methods, and concepts, PRP has become highly favored in the treatment of bone and joint injuries, including wound repair, anti-inflammatory pain relief, tendon and soft tissue repair, and bone tissue regeneration [9, 13–16] [36] [38] [39]. Previous studies [7, 8] have reported the success of PRP in the treatment of nonunion fractures, including but not limited to PRP gel loaded with multiple drugs, ultrasound-guided percutaneous PRP injections, PRP combined with autologous bone grafting, and PRP combined with demineralized bone matrix (DBM) or mixed biological agents. Despite the reported success of PRP in the treatment of nonunion fractures, the results of using PRP alone have been unsatisfactory [7, 8, 17, 18]. One major reason is that PRP, as a growth factor, does not directly promote the differentiation and proliferation of osteoblasts; rather, it indirectly induces mesenchymal stem cells to differentiate into osteoblasts, facilitating new bone formation [18, 19] [41]. Therefore, recent research has focused on combined therapies [4, 7–9] [42] [43], such as PRP combined with autologous bone grafting, or PRP combined with bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP), mesenchymal stem cells (MSC), hydroxyapatite (HA), or demineralized bone matrix (DBM). In this combined therapy approach, PRP combined with autologous bone grafting has been reported by most researchers [8]. The advantage lies in the fact that autologous bone grafts provide a bone-conductive scaffold and have bone-inductive properties due to their rich content of stem cells. PRP, rich in multiple growth factors, enhances the biological activity of the “bone healing unit” when combined with autologous bone grafting. This combined therapy follows the "Diamond Theory" proposed by Calori, GM et al. [10]. This approach also aligns with the AAOS-recommended method, which supports the use of combined therapies in the treatment of tibial nonunion fractures, with a success rate of up to 95% [3]. In our study, the PRP group showed advantages in both healing time and early healing rate compared to the non-PRP group, suggesting that PRP-enhanced autologous iliac bone grafting (AICBG) offers superior biological potential compared to AICBG alone.

Although most studies report success with PRP combined with autologous bone grafting in the treatment of nonunion fractures, several confounding factors and limitations remain [8, 9, 17, 19–27] [37] [40]. These include: (1) the inclusion of various types of nonunion, making it difficult to determine the true efficacy of PRP in atrophic nonunion; (2) the inclusion of different fixation types, which complicates the assessment of PRP’s biological effects due to biomechanical interference; (3) flaws in the administration of liquid PRP, leading to leakage and insufficient effective dose and concentration [8, 9, 20]; (4) the lack of consensus on preparation techniques, dosage, and concentration [8, 18]. The centrifugation protocols vary from 1–3 spins, with centrifugation rates ranging from 1500 r/min to 3000 r/min, and PRP volumes ranging from 5 to 50 mL, with concentrations varying from 2 × to 10 × the original platelet count. These variations limit the widespread use and clinical translation of PRP-enhanced techniques in the treatment of nonunion fractures [18]. Furthermore, the use of multiple biological agents complicates the attribution of therapeutic effects to PRP alone; and retrospective controlled studies without balanced baseline characteristics or studies without control groups have low evidence quality. As David Gagnon et al. [4] lamented, “We may never see a high-quality, robust Phase I study to clarify the efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness of such products.” This is due to the fact that the treatment of atrophic nonunion fractures requires simultaneous optimization of the two core elements: biomechanics and biology [1, 3, 4, 6]. However, previous studies rarely strictly control for both factors simultaneously. In our study, both groups uniformly used a single locked plate fixation to control for biomechanical differences between the two groups. The cases included in both groups were all aseptic atrophic tibial diaphyseal nonunions, and we also calculated and analyzed the differences in average bone defects between the two groups to control for baseline biological differences. By strictly controlling for mechanical and biological factors, we minimized baseline differences between selected cases and reduced the impact of confounding factors, allowing us to better observe the biological benefits of PRP.

In a retrospective controlled study involving 43 patients, Nie et al. [20] mixed liquid PRP with DBM powder (with a small amount of autologous iliac cancellous bone added in some cases) to form a “cement-like” composite, which was then implanted into the bone defect site, achieving a healing rate of 93.75%. This method not only enhanced the biological activity of the composite but also prevented leakage of the liquid PRP. However, their study involved various skeletal sites (clavicle, humerus, radius, femur, and tibia) and multiple types of nonunion, and did not control for mechanical factors. In another study of 38 cases of long bone nonunion, Cevolani et al. [19] controlled for mechanical factors but used a mixture of several biological agents (DBM + MSCs + PRP + PRF). Although the results indicated that the combined formulation was effective, the specific biological benefit of PRP could not be delineated. Similarly, Calori et al. [17] conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial involving 120 cases, in which PRP was mixed with various biological materials (hydroxyapatite, allograft, autograft). The resulting heterogeneity limited the interpretability of the findings. In the study by Memeo et al. [22], intramedullary nailing combined with PRP was used to treat nonunion of the ulna and radius. Although both mechanical and biological factors were controlled, the small sample size (n = 7) undermines the generalizability of their findings. A recent controlled study involving 66 cases of aseptic femoral shaft nonunion [9] showed that the group treated with PRP combined with AICBG had superior healing rates and shorter mean healing times than the AICBG-alone group. Subgroup analysis also suggested a favorable effect of PRP + AICBG in the treatment of atrophic femoral shaft nonunion. However, the study involved heterogeneous fixation methods and nonunion types, which limited the applicability of its results. In contrast to the above studies [17, 19, 20, 22], our study was designed with both groups receiving the same treatment—single locked plate fixation combined with autologous iliac bone grafting—for aseptic atrophic tibial shaft nonunion. We also compared the average bone defect size between the two groups. By rigorously controlling the two core factors—biomechanics and biology—we were able to isolate and validate the therapeutic benefit of PRP. The results demonstrated that PRP-enhanced autologous iliac bone grafting effectively accelerated early healing (mean healing time: 8.88 ± 2.03 months vs. 10.54 ± 2.59 months, p = 0.002) in atrophic tibial shaft nonunion. However, it appeared to have no direct benefit on the final union rate (92.31% vs. 78.43%, p = 0.072). We hypothesize that the reason for this phenomenon may be that PRP at the fracture site has already exerted its effects or been exhausted in the early stage, and there is insufficient PRP in the middle and late stages of subsequent fracture healing—resulting in no statistically significant difference in the healing rates between the two groups in the middle and late stages of this study. A histomorphological study by Thord et al. [27] suggested that PRP may exert its effects early in bone healing but shows diminished regenerative capacity beyond six months post-administration. A recent review [7] also emphasized the importance of sustained and controlled release of growth factors to match the regenerative timeline. Based on these insights, we propose that future studies should consider staged PRP administration at different time points to test and validate this hypothesis. Moreover, the operative time was significantly longer in the PRP group compared to the non-PRP group (142.64 ± 40.35 min vs. 132.73 ± 48.03 min, p = 0.027), largely due to additional steps including blood collection, PRP preparation, bone graft immersion, and bioactive chamber construction. Nevertheless, our findings indicate that the added surgical time is worthwhile.

Choi et al. [28] combined BMP with hydroxyapatite granules and autologous cancellous bone to fill bone defects. The microporous structure of hydroxyapatite and autologous bone effectively absorbed and retained BMP, and surrounding soft tissues formed a scaffold that guided bone regeneration, reducing BMP leakage and promoting anatomically shaped bone formation. Similarly, Chamseddine et al. [29] used a periosteal decortication technique combined with autologous bone grafting to treat femoral nonunion. They tightly sutured the soft tissues at the fracture site, ensuring close contact between the decorticated and host bone. Nie et al. [20] also improved PRP retention and biological activity by mixing it with DBM into a cement-like form and covering the grafts with soft tissue. In our protocol, the harvested iliac bone was soaked in liquid PRP, allowing it to become thoroughly infused. The cancellous bone, rich in micropores and porous structures, provided an ideal scaffold for PRP adsorption and retention. Additionally, we created a “bioactive chamber” by suturing the surrounding periosteum and fascia into a sealed compartment around the fracture site after achieving hemostasis. The PRP solution was injected into this chamber. We believe this approach provides a slow, sustained release of growth factors in the early phase of bone healing, enhancing the biological potential of the “bone healing unit” and reversing the course of atrophic nonunion.

It should be noted that mechanical factors are indispensable during fracture healing, as bone union relies on anatomical reduction and stable fixation [31]. In this study, all patients were treated with plate fixation. Compared to intramedullary nailing, plating allows sufficient bone grafting, which is crucial for atrophic nonunion that requires absolute stability for grafting and osteogenesis induction [1] [3] [5] [6]. This fixation strategy is consistent with previous studies [5] [11] [12]. However, there is currently a lack of conclusive evidence regarding whether plate fixation and intramedullary nailing differ in efficacy when treating atrophic tibial shaft nonunion [3] [6]. Future studies are recommended to compare the outcomes of these two fixation methods under controlled biological conditions.

As a retrospective case–control study, we employed measures to minimize bias, including radiographic assessments by one orthopedic trauma specialist and two independent radiologists, and functional scoring by an orthopedic postgraduate not involved in the study. However, selection and assessment bias may still exist. Additionally, variability in PRP preparation techniques may have influenced the consistency of outcomes. It should be noted that while plate internal fixation was employed for all cases in this study, the placement of plates lacks standardization; meanwhile, there are differences in soft tissue suturing techniques between the two groups, and this difference may potentially affect the incidence of incision-related complications. Furthermore, this study has limitations such as restricted patient characteristics, a small sample size, and a limited follow-up period, which may constrain the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Future studies should assess early bone callus formation under standardized mechanical conditions to further elucidate the early-stage benefits of PRP. Based on the findings of Thord et al. [27], Bacevich et al. [7], and our own study, we also recommend time-staged PRP administration in future trials. Moreover, multicenter, prospective, randomized controlled trials are essential to establish standardized protocols for PRP-enhanced therapies.

Conclusion

By simultaneously controlling the two core factors of nonunion—biomechanics and biology—this study demonstrated that PRP-enhanced autologous iliac bone grafting can accelerate early healing and improve limb function recovery in patients with atrophic nonunion. However, its benefit in the mid-to-late stages of fracture healing appears limited. This finding provides a superior treatment option for patients who require accelerated healing of nonunion and early restoration of lower limb function, and also offers evidence-based support for the role of PRP in nonunion treatment—specifically to “accelerate the healing process” rather than to “increase the ultimate healing rate.” Additionally, our study offers an effective and technically straightforward PRP delivery method applicable during revision surgery for nonunion.
