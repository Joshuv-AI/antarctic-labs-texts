Quiescent Big Bang formation in 2 + 1 2+1 dimensions
Liam Urban

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the past asymptotics of ( 2 + 1 ) (2+1) -dimensional solutions to the Einstein scalar-field Vlasov system which are close to Friedman-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker spacetimes on an initial hypersurface diffeomorphic to a closed orientable surface M M of arbitrary genus. We prove that such solutions are past causally geodesically incomplete, form a curvature singularity and exhibit stable Kretschmann scalar blow-up in the contracting direction. In particular, the spacetime is C 2 C^{2} -inextendible towards the past where causal geodesics become incomplete. Moreover, we show that geometry and matter are asymptotically velocity term dominated toward the past, remaining close to their background counterparts. When viewed on the co-mass shell, the Vlasov distribution in particular converges to a limiting distribution on the Big Bang hypersurface, while asymptotics of the spatial metric lead to slight degeneracies when trying to control the components of the Vlasov energy-momentum tensor. These also manifest when viewing the distribution function on the mass shell, where velocities generally approach a smooth one-dimensional subbundle of the tangent bundle, leading the distribution to become highly anisotropic, when viewed in terms of the geometry induced by the constant curvature spatial reference metric. Compared to previous results in higher dimensions, in particular [ 31 ] , inhomogeneous terms in the wave and Vlasov equations factor in more strongly in our setting, which a priori creates additional hurdles at high orders while largely keeping the quiescent system intact. As a corollary, our main result shows that the Strong Cosmic Censorship conjecture holds for certain polarized U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) -symmetric solutions to the Einstein vacuum equations that emanate from a spatial hypersurface diffeomorphic to M × 𝕊 1 M\times\mathbb{S}^{1} .

In this section, I outline the structure of this paper and comment on the main features of the proof of Theorem

This work relies on a
(2+1)

(2+1)

for the conformal

In Section

In order to control Vlasov matter at order zero, one needs to control derivatives of the reference distribution, which introduces terms containing

Since we only have to control the scalar curvature, one can use the momentum constraint to obtain this estimate directly without requiring additional high order estimates for the shear. , which would otherwise prevent us from closing the bootstrap argument. The additional weighted shear and curvature energies are then needed to obtain a closed estimate from the total energy, since Vlasov matter appears linearly in the evolution equations of all other variables. Since the Vlasov terms that enter the geometric evolution do so at a rate that is strictly weaker than
t^{-1}
, this is still sufficient to obtain an appropriate total energy estimate. In turn, the resulting total energy bound controls Vlasov matter sufficiently well to yield a strong bound on the quiescent system, see (
5.25
).

To control Vlasov matter, one can use a hierarchy between horizontal and vertical derivatives in the rescaled Vlasov equation (
2.14j
) analogous to that in
[
31
]
. This allows us to introduce scaled Vlasov energies into the total energy in a way that admits a closed total estimate and to obtain the non-scaled Vlasov energy estimates (
5.20
), improving the remaining Vlasov bootstrap assumptions.

We note that one might expect three potential roadblocks from differences in decay rates compared to
[
31
]
:

(1)

In the background solution, the Vlasov density
\rho_{FLRW}^{Vl}
behaves as
t^{-\frac{3}{2}}
instead of
t^{-\frac{4}{3}}
toward the Big Bang, thus potentially having a larger impact on the spacetime asymptotics compared to the scalar field whose matter density behaves like
t^{-2}
in both cases.

(2)

In the rescaled wave equation, spatial derivative terms are of order
1
instead of order
t^{\frac{1}{3}}
; see (
2.14i
) and
[
31
, (2.20p)]
.

(3)

In the rescaled Vlasov equation, the horizontal term diverges at order
t^{-\frac{1}{2}}
instead of
t^{-\frac{1}{3}}
; see (
2.14j
) and
[
31
, (2.19a)]
.

The first point does lead to weaker convergence rates for the lapse and consequently all other variables in Theorem
6.1
due to its effect on the lapse equations; see (
2.14g
). However, the Vlasov density and pressure are still strictly asymptotically dominated by scalar field matter, which suffices for our purposes. The other two points could only become problematic at top order, since at low orders, it similarly is sufficient that either of these rates are integrable in time to obtain the bounds in Section
4
. At top order, to deal with issues that do arise from the third point, the scaled energy estimate for the scalar curvature remains sufficient, see Lemma
5.5
and (
5.27
).

Once the energy bounds in Corollary
5.8
are obtained, the main theorem in Section
6
follows by standard arguments as in, for example,
[
59
,
64
,
31
]
. The asymptotic behaviour of the dual Vlasov distribution function
f^{\sharp}
on the mass shell is related to that of the spatial metric since the renormalisation of velocities is similar to that used for the renormalised spatial metric
\mathcal{G}
in Corollary
6.2
, which converges toward the Big Bang hypersurface. The metric
\mathcal{G}
in Corollary
6.2
also corresponds to the spatial metrics used in the framework of
[

.

Finally, translating these results to the polarized
U(1)
symmetric vacuum setting, Corollary
6.5
follows by taking the asymptotic control on the solution variables established in Theorem

and, with these in hand, computing the norm of the second fundamental form as well as the Kretschmann scalar with respect to the induced foliation. The necessary curvature formulas are collected in Section

.

1.3.
Further applications

To conclude the introduction, I briefly discuss two potential extensions of this work:

Remark 1.3

(Polarized

-symmetric Einstein-Vlasov solutions)
.

Given Theorem

and Corollary

[

to polarized

2.1.
Notation

We will denote the Levi-Civita connection of a Semi-Riemannian metric
\mathfrak{g}
by
\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]
and its Christoffel symbols by
\Gamma[\mathfrak{g}]
. The associated volume form is denoted by
{\text{{dvol}}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
, the associated trace operator by
\mathrm{tr}_{\mathfrak{g}}
and the divergence with respect to
\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]
by
\text{{div}}_{\mathfrak{g}}
. In the case where
\mathfrak{g}
is Riemannian, we denote the induced inner product and pointwise norm by
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert_{\mathfrak{g}}
respectively and write
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}=\sqrt{1+\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert_{\mathfrak{g}}^{2}}
. For the Riemann curvature tensor
\text{{Riem}}[\mathfrak{g}]
of
\mathfrak{g}
, we use the sign convention

\text{{Riem}}[\mathfrak{g}](X,Y)Z=\left(\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]_{X}\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]_{Y}-\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]_{Y}\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]_{X}-\nabla[\mathfrak{g}]_{[X,Y]}\right)Z\,.

for vector fields
X,Y
and
Z
. The Ricci and scalar curvature are denoted by
\text{{Ric}}[\mathfrak{g}]
and
R[\mathfrak{g}]
respectively. Recall that, when
\mathfrak{g}
is a two-dimensional Riemannian metric, one has

\text{{Riem}}[\mathfrak{g}]_{ijkl}=\frac{R[\mathfrak{g}]}{2}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{ik}\,\mathfrak{g}_{jl}-\mathfrak{g}_{il}\,\mathfrak{g}_{jk}\right)\ \text{and}\ \text{{Ric}}[\mathfrak{g}]_{ij}=\frac{R[\mathfrak{g}]}{2}\mathfrak{g}_{ij}\,.

For most of this paper, we work with a
(2+1)
-dimensional spacetime
(\overline{M},\overline{g})
that admits a time function
t\in C^{\infty}(\overline{M})
whose level sets
M_{s}=t^{-1}(\{s\}),\ s\in t(\overline{M})
are diffeomorphic to a closed orientable surface
M
and have constant mean curvature for all
s\in t(\overline{M})
. In particular,
\overline{M}
is diffeomorphic to
I\times M
where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an open interval.

In constant mean curvature transported coordinate (CMCTC) gauge,
\overline{g}
takes the form

(2.1)

\overline{g}=-n^{2}\,dt^{2}+g

where
g
is a time-dependent Riemannian metric on
M
. We will abbreviate
\nabla\equiv\nabla[g]
and
\overline{\nabla}\equiv\nabla[\overline{g}]
. In our convention, the lapse
n
is positive, i.e..,

n=(-\overline{g}(\overline{\nabla}t,\overline{\nabla}t))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,.

The future directed timelike unit normal
e_{0}
to
M_{t}
is given by
e_{0}=n^{-1}\partial_{t}
.

We will often work with the time-dependent rescaled Riemannian metric
G=a(t)^{-2}\,g
on
M_{t}
where the scale factor
a\in C^{\infty}(I)
is introduced in Lemma
2.1
. Note that, for a time-dependent vector field
X
and a time-dependent tensor field
\mathfrak{T}
on
M
, one has
\nabla[G]_{X}\mathfrak{T}=\nabla_{X}\mathfrak{T}
. We use
\Delta_{G}
to denote the Laplace-Beltrami operator with respect to
G
.

The second fundamental form is a symmetric
(0,2)
-tensor on
M_{t}
given by

k(X,Y)=-\overline{g}(\overline{\nabla}_{X}e_{0},Y)\,.

We will assume that the mean curvature
\tau:=\mathrm{tr}_{g}k
of
M_{t}
is given by

(2.2)

\tau=-2\,\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\,.

In the following, Latin indices
i,j,\dots
refer to indices with respect to a given local coordinate system
(x^{i})_{i=1,2}
on
M
or to the momentum coordinates
(p_{i})_{i=1,2}
on the co-mass shell induced by such coordinates; see Section
2.3
.

Lowercase Greek letters are spacetime indices with respect to
\overline{M}
, running from
0
to
2
. The index
0
always denotes tensor components relative to
e_{0}
. We use Einstein summation convention if not stated otherwise.

We raise and lower indices with respect to
\overline{g}
for tensors on
\overline{M}
and
g
for those on
M
without comment. When we raise indices of a tensor
\mathfrak{T}
on
M
with respect to
G
, the result is denoted by
\mathfrak{T}^{\sharp}
. To simplify notation, we will occasionally write
\mathfrak{T}_{1}\ast\mathfrak{T}_{2}
for an arbitrary contraction of time-dependent tensor fields on
M
with respect to
G
up to multiplicative constant and
\nabla^{I}\mathfrak{T}
for
I\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
when
I
covariant derivatives of a time-dependent tensor
\mathfrak{T}
are taken.

We will also work with polarized
U(1)
-symmetric
(3+1)
-dimensional spacetimes
(\check{M}\cong\overline{M}\times\mathbb{S}^{1},\check{g})
. In such settings,
\partial_{x^{3}}
refers to the extended coordinate derivative on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and the index
3
to components with respect to
\partial_{x^{3}}
.

Finally, for real functions
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}
with
\zeta_{2}\geq 0
, we write
\zeta_{1}\lesssim\zeta_{2}
if there exists a constant
K>0
such that
\max\{\zeta_{1},0\}\leq K\zeta_{2}
. If
\zeta_{1}
is also nonnegative, we write
\zeta_{1}\simeq\zeta_{2}
if
\zeta_{1}\lesssim\zeta_{2}
and
\zeta_{2}\lesssim\zeta_{1}
holds.

2.2.
The reference solutions

Our main result deals with perturbations of FLRW backgrounds with isotropic scalar field and Vlasov matter. Using standard formulas for warped product spacetimes as in, for example,
[

Lemma 2.1

(FLRW solutions to the ESFV system)
.

Let
(\overline{M},\overline{g}_{FLRW})
be an FLRW spacetime of the form

\overline{M}=(0,T)\times M,\quad\overline{g}_{FLRW}=-dt^{2}+a(t)^{2}\,\gamma

where
T\in(0,\infty]
,
a\in C^{\infty}(0,T)
is a positive function and
(M,\gamma)
is a closed orientable surface with constant curvature
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
. Further, let
{\phi}_{FLRW}
and

be spatially homogeneous and isotropic solutions to (

) and (

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\phi}_{FLRW}=C\,a(t)^{-2},\quad\nabla{\phi}_{FLRW}=0,

(2.3b)

\displaystyle{f}_{FLRW}(t,x,p_{i})={\mathcal{F}}\left((\gamma^{-1})^{ij}\,p_{i}\,p_{j}\right)

for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\mathcal{F}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,\infty),[0,\infty))
.
We define

(2.3c)

\displaystyle{\rho}_{FLRW}^{Vl}(t):=

\displaystyle\ 2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}R\sqrt{m^{2}\,a(t)^{2}+R^{2}}\,\mathcal{F}(R^{2})\,dR\,,

(2.3d)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}_{FLRW}^{Vl}(t):=

\displaystyle\,\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{R^{3}}{\sqrt{m^{2}\,a(t)^{2}+R^{2}}}\,\mathcal{F}(R^{2})\,dR\,,

where
m=0
in the massless case and

in the massive case.

Then,

(2.3e)

\dot{a}^{2}=4\pi\,C^{2}\,a^{-2}+4\pi\,a^{-1}\left(\rho^{Vl}_{FLRW}+2\,\mathfrak{p}^{Vl}_{FLRW}\right)-{\kappa}\,.

Note that
\rho^{Vl}_{FLRW}
and

\rho^{Vl}_{FLRW}=a^{3}\,T_{00}^{Vl}[\overline{g}_{FLRW},f_{FLRW}]\,\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{p}^{Vl}_{FLRW}=\frac{1}{2}\,a^{3}\,\mathrm{tr}_{a^{2}\gamma}\left(T^{Vl}[\overline{g}_{FLRW},f_{FLRW}]\right)\,.

Moreover, observe that
(\overline{M},\overline{g})
is of the form (
2.1

of the constant time surfaces

satisfies (
2.2

) implies the following identities for the mean curvature

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\tau^{2}=

\displaystyle\ 16\pi\,C^{2}\,a^{-4}+16\pi\,a^{-3}\left(\rho^{Vl}_{FLRW}+2\,\mathfrak{p}^{Vl}_{FLRW}\right)-4\,\kappa\,a^{-2}\,,

(2.4b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tau=

\displaystyle\ 16\pi\,C^{2}\,a^{-4}+8\pi\,a^{-3}\left(\rho^{Vl}_{FLRW}+4\,\mathfrak{p}^{Vl}_{FLRW}\right)-2\,\kappa\,a^{-2}\,.

Throughout, we assume
a(0)=0
, that
a
is positive somewhere and that the scalar field is non-trivial, i.e., that

. Under these assumptions, one obtains the following result as in
[

, Lemma 2.2]
and
[

Lemma 2.2

(Properties of the scale factor)
.

The singular initial value problem (
2.3e
) with
a(0)=0
and
C\neq 0
has a unique nonnegative solution on the maximal interval of existence
(0,T)
for some
T\in(0,\infty]

\left(-\frac{T}{2},\frac{T}{2}\right)\ni t\mapsto a\left(t+\frac{T}{2}\right)

.

As

. Further, given

and

independent of

and

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\sqrt{4\pi}\,\lvert C\rvert\,a(t)^{-1}\leq

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t_{0}}a(s)^{-2-q}\,ds\leq

(Local well-posedness and continuation criteria for the

Remark 3.6

(On local well-posedness for polarized

-symmetric vacuum solutions)

For the sake of this work, we refrain from separately establishing a local well-posedness statement for the vacuum solutions arising from polarized

Remark 6.3

(Asymptotics of the constraint equations)
.

Recall that (
6.5
) is the limit of the renormalised Hamiltonian constraint. Unfortunately, one cannot obtain a similar limit for the momentum constraint since the Levi-Civita connection associated to the spatial metric degenerates along with the metric itself, preventing a limit on the divergence of the shear, along with causing degeneracies in
\jmath^{Vl}
. While it might be possible to recast both of these terms with the help of
\mathcal{G}
, this would require tracking eigendirections of the shear along the evolution. Even works that use such frames explicitly, including
[
51
]
, lose control of this frame towards the past, preventing an asymptotic momentum constraint. In light of
[
39
]
, where one can ensure that a Gaussian approximate VTD solution satisfies the momentum constraint given Einstein nonlinear scalar-field data on the Big Bang hypersurface unlike for the VTD solution in CMCTC gauge, this might be an issue with the foliation itself. We leave this question open to future work.

Remark 6.4

(Concentration of Vlasov particle velocities)
.

Note that (
6.7
) implies that the Vlasov distribution is asymptotically velocity term dominated along with the other solution variables if viewed on the co-mass shell. However, as in
[
31
, Remark 8.5]
, the picture changes when one tries to analyse the velocity of Vlasov particles rather than their (conjugate) momenta and views the distribution function on the mass shell. In short,
“lifting”
to the mass shell means one must renormalize the spatial metric as indicated by Corollary
6.2
. The resulting velocity characteristics are, consequently, dominated by their VTD component

Q_{t,x}(q)=\exp\left[-2\int_{t}^{t_{0}}a(s)^{-2}\,ds\cdot\left(\hat{K}_{Bang}\right)_{x}\right]\odot a(t)^{-2}q\,.

Thus and in line with Corollary
6.2
, it is useful to consider the FLRW renormalised particle velocities
w^{i}=a(t)^{-2}q^{i}
and the a posteriori expansion-renormalised velocities

W_{t,x}(w)=\exp\left[2\int_{t}^{t_{0}}a(s)^{-2}\,ds\cdot\left(\hat{K}_{Bang}\right)_{x}\right]\odot w.

Indeed, one can prove by similar means as in Theorem
6.1
that, for

f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{asymp}}(t,x,w):=f^{\sharp}(t,x,W^{-1}_{t,x}(w))\,,

the support of
f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{asymp}}(t,\cdot,\cdot)
also remains compact and that this renormalised function converges to an asymptotic footprint
f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{Bang}}
. In particular, one has

\mathrm{supp}f^{\sharp}(t,x,\cdot)=\left\{W_{t,x}(w)\,|\,w\in\mathrm{supp}f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{asymp}}(t,x,\cdot)\right\}

by definition of
f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{asymp}}
. When approaching the Big Bang, particle velocities thus concentrate in directions
w
where
\lim_{t\to 0}W_{t,x}(q)
remains bounded.

To identify these directions, first consider the massive case (
m=1
) where particles can be initially at rest, i.e., consider
x\in M
such that
\mathring{f}(x,0)\neq 0
. In the case where
(\hat{K}_{\textrm{Bang}})_{x}=0
,
f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{asymp}}
simply agrees with the (FLRW renormalised) initial distribution and
f^{\sharp}
converges to an asymptotic profile
f_{\textrm{Bang}}
that is close to the FLRW distribution. Otherwise, the tracefree and self-adjoint operator
(\hat{K}_{\textrm{Bang}})_{x}
has precisely one positive and one negative eigendirection. Denoting these eigenspaces by
E_{x,\pm}
, it follows that
\lim_{t\to 0}W_{t,x}(q)=0
is satisfied if one takes
w\in E_{x,-}
. Otherwise,
\lim_{t\to 0}W_{t,x}(q)=\infty
holds and
w
lies outside of
\mathrm{supp}f^{\sharp}(t,x,\cdot)
for small enough
t>0
. Thus, (
6.7
) implies the following pointwise limit for any
w\in T^{\ast}_{x}M
.

\lim_{t\to 0}f^{\sharp}(t,x,q)=f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{Bang}}(x,0)\,\chi_{E_{x,-}}(w(q))\,.

If
f^{\sharp}_{\textrm{Bang}}(\cdot,0)\neq 0
, which holds for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
due to (
6.6
), the velocity support thus concentrates toward
E_{x,-}
and any element of
E_{x,-}
lies in the support.

For
m=0
, or more generally when the initial particle distribution vanishes in the neighbourhood of the zero section for some
x\in M
,

may also vanish at the origin for some
x\in M
. In that case, for any
q\in T^{\ast}_{x}M
, the Vlasov distribution vanishes both if
W_{t,x}(q)
becomes too large or too small. Denoting the orthogonal projectors onto

by
\mathbb{P}_{\pm}
, one has

\sup_{q\in\mathrm{supp}f^{\sharp}(t,x,\cdot)}\lvert\mathbb{P}_{+}w(q)\rvert_{\gamma}\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad t\downarrow 0

as well as

\inf_{q\in\mathrm{supp}f^{\sharp}(t,x,\cdot)}\lvert\mathbb{P}_{-}w(q)\rvert_{\gamma}\to\infty\,\quad\text{as}\quad t\downarrow 0

while the momentum support still remains bounded for any

; see (

). In other words, particle velocities concentrate in the direction of

toward the Big Bang, but become arbitrarily large.

Finally, one expects that
\hat{K}_{Bang}=0
is a non-generic case, and thus that velocity concentration is generic. However, this is not immediately apparent from Theorem
6.1
, as we do not obtain a scattering result. However, nearby Kasner solutions must be contained in the solutions covered by Theorem
6.1
for which
\hat{K}_{Bang}
only vanishes if it coincides with the FLRW solution. Since the construction above also ensures that the footprint states depend on initial data continuously, it follows that the set of solutions for which velocities concentrate is, at least, of positive measure. In light of the scattering result
[
43
]
for the linearized Einstein scalar-field system, it further is reasonable to conjecture that
\hat{K}_{Bang}
vanishing entirely is truly non-generic, but this still remains open even in the full Einstein scalar-field setting.

\displaystyle\,\frac{2}{3}\,K_{t_{1}}\,D^{\frac{1}{2}}\,t^{\frac{3}{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{\frac{5}{2}}\right)\quad\text{as}\ t\downarrow 0\,.

In both cases, this implies

t(S)=\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\,K_{t_{1}}^{-\frac{2}{3}}\,D^{-\frac{1}{3}}\,S^{\frac{2}{3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(S\right)\,.

Plugging this back into (
7.2
), along with using (
7.3
) and (
7.4
), implies the following expansions for
b(S)^{2}
and
b_{3}(S)^{2}
as
S
approaches
0
.

\displaystyle b(S)^{2}=

\displaystyle\,a(t(S))^{2}\exp\left(4\sqrt{\pi}\,C\,\int_{t(S)}^{t_{1}}a(s)^{-2}\,ds\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K^{2}_{t_{1}}\,\left(4{\sqrt{\pi}\,\lvert C\rvert}\,t(S)-\kappa\,t(S)^{2}\right)\frac{t(S)}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,\lvert C\rvert-\kappa\,t(S)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K^{2}_{t_{1}}\,t(S)^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(t(S)^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{\frac{4}{3}}\,K_{t_{1}}^{\frac{2}{3}}\,D^{-\frac{2}{3}}\,S^{\frac{4}{3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(S^{2}\right)

\displaystyle b_{3}(S)^{2}=

\displaystyle\,\exp\left(-2\sqrt{4\pi}\,C\,\int_{t(S)}^{t_{1}}a(s)^{-2}\,ds\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}^{-2}\,D\,t(S)^{-1}+\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{-\frac{2}{3}}\,K_{t_{1}}^{-\frac{1}{3}}\,D^{\frac{4}{3}}\,S^{-\frac{2}{3}}+\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)\,.

Turning now to the case where
C>0
, we first compute for
\kappa=-1
that

\displaystyle S(t)=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}\,\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\frac{D^{-1}+s}{s}}\,ds=K_{t_{1}}\,D^{-1}\,\int_{0}^{D\,t}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{u}}\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2K_{t_{1}}\,D^{-1}\,\int_{0}^{\mathrm{arsinh}(\sqrt{D\,t})}\cosh(x)^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}\,D^{-1}\left(\sqrt{D\,t}\sqrt{1+D\,t}+\mathrm{arsinh}\left(\sqrt{D\,t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2K_{t_{1}}\,\sqrt{\frac{t}{D}}+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\quad\text{as}\ t\downarrow 0\,.

For
\kappa=1
, one has

\displaystyle S(t)=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}\,\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\frac{D^{-1}-s}{s}}\,ds=D^{-1}\,\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\frac{1}{u}-1}\,du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2K_{t_{1}}\,D^{-1}\,\int_{0}^{\mathrm{arcsin}(\sqrt{D\,t})}\sin(x)^{2}\,ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}\,D^{-1}\left[\sqrt{D\,t}\sqrt{1-D\,t}+\mathrm{arcsin}(\sqrt{D\,t})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2K_{t_{1}}\,\sqrt{\frac{t}{D}}+\mathcal{O}\left(t^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\quad\text{as}\ t\downarrow 0\,.

Thus, as
S
approaches
0
, one now has

\displaystyle b(S)^{2}=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}^{2}\,\left(4\sqrt{\pi}\,C\,t(S)-\kappa\,t(S)^{2}\right)\,\frac{4\sqrt{\pi}\,C-\kappa\,t(S)}{t(S)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,16\pi\,C^{2}\,K_{t_{1}}+\mathcal{O}\left(t(S)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,16\pi\,C^{2}\,K_{t_{1}}+\mathcal{O}\left(S^{2}\right)\ \text{and}

\displaystyle b_{3}(S)^{2}=

\displaystyle\,K_{t_{1}}^{-2}\,\frac{t(S)}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,C+t(S)}=K_{t_{1}}^{-2}\,\frac{t(S)}{4\sqrt{\pi}\,C}+\mathcal{O}\left(t(S)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,4\pi\,C^{2}\,K_{t_{1}}^{-4}\,S^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(S^{3}\right)\,.

7.2.
Christoffel symbols and curvature components

In this section, we collect formulas for the Christoffel symbols and Riemann curvature tensor for spacetime metrics under consideration in Corollary
6.5
. Therein, the spacetime metric on
I\times M\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
takes the form

\check{g}=e^{-2\sqrt{4\pi}\,\phi}\left(-n^{2}\,dt^{2}+g\right)+e^{2\sqrt{4\pi}\,\phi}\,(dx^{3})^{2}\,,

where
g
is a Riemannian metric on
M
. Additionally, if
k
denotes the second fundamental form with respect to the metric
g
along
(M_{t})_{t\in(0,t_{0}]}
,
\text{tr}_{g}k=\tau
only depends on time. Below, the index
t
corresponds to the vector field
\partial_{t}
and we write
\hat{k}_{ij}=k_{ij}-\frac{\tau}{2}\,g_{ij}
as well as
\psi=n^{-1}\,\partial_{t}\phi
.

The Christoffel symbols
\check{\Gamma}
with respect to
\check{g}
take the following form:

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{t}_{tt}=

\displaystyle\,\partial_{t}n-\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\psi,

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{t}_{ti}=

\displaystyle\,\partial_{i}\log(n)-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\partial_{i}\phi\,,

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{l}_{tt}=

\displaystyle\,n\,g^{lm}\,\partial_{m}n+\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{2}\,g^{lm}\,\partial_{m}\phi

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{l}_{tj}=

\displaystyle\,-n\left(k^{l}_{\ j}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi\,\mathbb{I}^{l}_{j}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{t}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\,-n^{-1}\left(k_{ij}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi\,g_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{l}_{ij}=

\displaystyle\,\Gamma^{l}_{ij}-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\partial_{i}\phi\,\mathbb{I}^{l}_{j}-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\partial_{j}\phi\,\mathbb{I}^{l}_{i}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\partial_{m}\phi\,g^{lm}g_{ij}\,,

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{t3}=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\psi

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{3i}=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,\partial_{i}\phi\,,

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{t}_{33}=

\displaystyle\,\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{-1}\,e^{4\sqrt{4\pi}\phi}\,\psi

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{i}_{33}=

\displaystyle\,-\sqrt{4\pi}\,e^{4\sqrt{4\pi}\phi}\,g^{ij}\,\partial_{j}\phi\,,

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle\,\check{\Gamma}^{\rho}_{3\nu}=\check{\Gamma}^{3}_{33}=0\,.

With these, one can compute the curvature components listed below. For components in which the index
3
does not occur, it can simplify computation to view
\check{g}|_{I\times M}=e^{-2\sqrt{4\pi}\,\phi}\,\overline{g}
as a conformal transformation and use standard formulas that then relate
\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]
with
\text{{Riem}}[\overline{g}]
. Additionally, we use
\square_{\overline{g}}\phi=0
, i.e.,

e_{0}\psi=n^{-1}\,\partial_{t}\psi=\Delta_{G}\phi+\langle\nabla\log(n),\nabla\phi\rangle_{g}+{\tau}\,\psi\,,

as well as
\text{{Ric}}[\overline{g}]_{\mu\nu}=8\pi\,\overline{\nabla}_{\mu}\phi\,\overline{\nabla}_{\nu}\phi
and that the Weyl tensor
W[\overline{g}]
vanishes.

The Riemann curvature tensor is fully described by the following components:

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{t}}_{itj}=

\displaystyle\,{\text{{Riem}}[\overline{g}]^{t}}_{itj}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\phi+\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,k_{ij}\,\psi+4\pi\,\nabla_{i}\phi\,\nabla_{j}\phi

\displaystyle\,+\left(-\sqrt{4\pi}\,e_{0}\psi+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\langle\nabla\log(n),\nabla\phi\rangle_{g}+4\pi\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert_{g}^{2}\right)g_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,-\sqrt{4\pi}\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi+\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\psi\,g_{ij}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\hat{k}_{ij}\psi+\left(-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\Delta_{G}\phi-4\pi\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert_{g}^{2}\right)g_{ij}

\displaystyle\,+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\phi\,,

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{t}}_{ijl}=

\displaystyle\,{\text{{Riem}}[\overline{g}]^{t}}_{ijl}+\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{-1}\,\nabla_{j}\psi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,k^{r}_{\ l}\,\nabla_{r}\phi-8\pi\,n\,\psi\,\nabla_{l}\phi\right)g_{ij}

\displaystyle\,-\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{-1}\,\nabla_{j}\psi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,k^{r}_{\ j}\,\nabla_{r}\phi-8\pi\,n\,\psi\,\nabla_{j}\phi\right)g_{il}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{-1}\,\nabla_{l}\psi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,k^{r}_{\ l}\,\nabla_{r}\phi\right)g_{ij}-\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{-1}\,\nabla_{j}\psi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,k^{r}_{\ j}\,\nabla_{r}\phi\right)g_{il}\,,

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{12}}_{12}=

\displaystyle\,{\text{{Riem}}[\overline{g}]^{12}}_{12}+\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,\nabla^{1}\nabla_{1}\phi-4\pi\,\nabla^{1}\phi\,\nabla_{1}\phi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,{k}^{1}_{\ 1}\,\psi\right)

\displaystyle\,+\left(\sqrt{4\pi}\,\nabla^{2}\nabla_{2}\phi+4\pi\,\nabla^{2}\phi\,\nabla_{2}\phi+\sqrt{4\pi}\,{k}^{2}_{\ 2}\,\psi\right)-4\pi\left(-\psi^{2}+\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi\left(\frac{\tau}{2}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi\right)+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\Delta_{G}\phi\,,

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{3}}_{t3t}=

\displaystyle\,-\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{2}\,e_{0}\psi-8\pi\,(n\,\psi)^{2}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla n\rangle_{g}+4\pi\,n^{2}\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert_{g}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{2}\,\psi\,\left(\frac{\tau}{2}+\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi\right)-\sqrt{4\pi}\,n^{2}\,\Delta_{G}\phi+\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla n\rangle_{g}+4\pi\,n^{2}\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert_{g}^{2}\,,

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{3}}_{i3j}=

\displaystyle\,-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\phi-12\pi\,n\,\psi\,\nabla_{i}\phi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\psi\,\left(\hat{k}_{ij}+\left(\frac{\tau}{2}+\sqrt{4\pi}\psi\right)\,g_{ij}\right)+4\pi\,\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert_{g}^{2}\,g_{ij}\,,

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{3}}_{i3t}=

\displaystyle\,-\sqrt{4\pi}\,\nabla_{i}(n\,\psi)+\sqrt{4\pi}\psi\,\nabla_{i}n-12\pi\,n\,\psi\,\nabla_{i}\phi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\hat{k}^{r}_{\ i}\,\nabla_{r}\phi-\sqrt{4\pi}\,n\,\tau\,\nabla_{i}\phi\,,

\displaystyle{\text{{Riem}}[\check{g}]^{3}}_{\mu\nu\rho}=

\displaystyle\,0\,.
