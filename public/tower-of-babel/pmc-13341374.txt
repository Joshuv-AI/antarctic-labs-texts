Risk of Age‐Related Ocular Diseases in Non‐Diabetic Adults With Obesity Using Glucagon‐Like Peptide 1 Receptor Agonists
Authors: Kuo‐En Chen, Po‐Chun Wang, Hui‐An Lin, Sheng‐Feng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo assess whether glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) are associated with a lower incidence of age‐related ocular diseases in non‐diabetic older adults with overweight or obesity.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, propensity score–matched cohort study using the TriNetX global research network. Non‐diabetic, overweight or obese adults aged ≥ 60 years with an ophthalmic disease–naïve baseline were enrolled. Patients receiving liraglutide or semaglutide were matched 1:1 with users of alternative weight‐loss medications (bupropion–naltrexone, phentermine–topiramate, setmelanotide or orlistat). Outcomes included up to 5‐year risks of incident cataract, age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), ocular hypertension, primary open‐angle glaucoma (POAG) and dry eye syndrome (DES).ResultsAfter matching, 68 536 patients were included (34 268 per group). GLP‐1RAs use was significantly associated with a lower 5‐year risk of cataract (RR: 0.45; 95% CI, 0.40–0.50), AMD (RR: 0.33; 95% CI, 0.23–0.48), ocular hypertension (RR: 0.56; 95% CI, 0.34–0.91), POAG (RR: 0.50; 95% CI, 0.32–0.78) and DES (RR: 0.36; 95% CI, 0.31–0.42). Sensitivity analyses consistently demonstrated a lower risk across cataract subtypes, including nuclear (RR: 0.42; 95% CI, 0.37–0.48), cortical (RR: 0.37; 95% CI, 0.36–0.55) and posterior subcapsular cataracts (RR: 0.48; 95% CI, 0.26–0.91). Similar reductions were observed in retinal haemorrhage or edema (RR: 0.44; 95% CI, 0.30–0.63) and the use of dry eye‐related medication (RR: 0.44; 95% CI, 0.39–0.50).ConclusionsGLP‐1RAs were associated with lower risks of multiple age‐related ocular conditions in non‐diabetic older adults.Keywords: age‐related macular degeneration, cataract, dry eye syndrome, glaucoma, GLP‐1 receptor agonists, non‐diabetic, obesity

Introduction

Age‐related ocular diseases—including cataract, age‐related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma and dry eye syndrome (DES)—are leading causes of visual disability among older adults, thereby causing a loss of independence and imposing a substantial global healthcare burden [1]. Cataract remains the most common cause of blindness worldwide, with both its prevalence and associated disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) continuing to rise as populations age [2]. AMD and glaucoma are major contributors to irreversible visual impairment, and their global prevalence is projected to reach approximately 288 million and 112 million individuals by 2040, respectively [3, 4]. Although DES does not lead to blindness, it remains one of the most prevalent ocular disorders in the elderly, substantially reducing comfort and interfering with daily activities and quality of life [5]. Collectively, these conditions underscore the growing impact of ageing on ocular health and represent a significant challenge for both public health and clinical care.

Beyond chronological ageing, obesity exacerbates susceptibility to ocular disease by promoting chronic inflammation, oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction, which accelerate ocular tissue ageing and damage [6, 7]. The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) has identified obesity as an established risk factor for cataract formation [8]. Epidemiologic studies further indicate that obesity confers a 32% higher risk of late‐stage AMD and is associated with elevated intraocular pressure linked to primary open‐angle glaucoma (POAG) [9, 10, 11]. Moreover, low‐grade systemic inflammation characteristic of metabolic syndrome raises the risk of DES, further contributing to ocular morbidity in the elderly [6]. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of weight management not only for systemic health but also for maintaining ocular integrity and function.

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) were initially developed as glucose‐lowering agents for type 2 diabetes and have since been approved for obesity management, where they promote substantial weight loss primarily through appetite suppression and delayed gastric emptying [12, 13, 14]. Compared with conventional weight‐loss medications, GLP‐1RAs provide broader therapeutic benefits. Beyond their metabolic effects, they exhibit anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant properties, with emerging evidence indicating protective roles against cardiovascular events, neurodegenerative conditions and immune‐mediated disorders [15, 16, 17]. These pleiotropic properties raise the possibility that GLP‐1RAs may confer benefits in age‐related ocular diseases.

In ophthalmology, emerging evidence suggests that GLP‐1RAs may be associated with a lower risk of diabetic retinopathy, AMD and glaucoma among patients with diabetes [18, 19, 20]. However, some studies have raised concerns regarding a potential association between GLP‐1RA use and nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION); recent real‐world evidence has not shown a consistent increase in NAION risk [21, 22, 23]. Despite their expanding clinical applications, the association between GLP‐1RAs and the risk of incident age‐related ocular diseases among non‐diabetic, obese older adults remains uncertain. To address this gap, we conducted a large‐scale electronic health record‐based analysis to evaluate the association of GLP‐1RAs, compared with conventional weight‐loss therapies, with the risk of cataract, AMD, glaucoma and DES.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from the TriNetX Research Network, a global federated health research platform that provides access to deidentified electronic medical records from the healthcare organizations (HCOs) worldwide. Analyses were conducted within the Global Collaborative Network of the TriNetX platform, covering data from over 150 HCOs worldwide. Data were accessed on September 29, 2025. All patient information in TriNetX is deidentified and aggregated, preventing any reidentification. Accordingly, the study was deemed exempt from ethical review by the Joint Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University (Approval No. N202511019).

Study Population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study spanning a 20‐year period, from September 29, 2005, to September 29, 2025. The primary analysis included patients aged ≥ 60 years with a documented diagnosis of overweight or obesity. Individuals with pre‐existing diabetes mellitus were excluded. To ensure the identification of incident outcomes, patients with any pre‐existing ocular diseases were excluded from the main outcome cohort, thereby defining an ophthalmology disease‐naïve baseline population. Excluded conditions included age‐related cataract, nonexudative and exudative AMD, ocular hypertension, POAG and DES.

Patients were grouped based on their prescribed pharmacologic treatment. The GLP‐1RAs group included patients who had been prescribed liraglutide or semaglutide, with medication use occurring within 6 months before or on the initiation date. Patients treated with tirzepatide were excluded to avoid potential confounding from dual GIP/GLP‐1 activity. The alternative weight‐loss (AWL) group comprised patients who received at least one non–GLP‐1RAs weight control medication, such as bupropion–naltrexone, phentermine–topiramate, setmelanotide or orlistat. The corresponding RxNorm and ATC codes used for drug identification in TriNetX are listed in Table S1. All data were extracted from medical history and prescription records prior to the index date.

Main Outcomes

The primary outcomes were age‐related ocular diseases including age‐related cataract, AMD, ocular hypertension, POAG and DES. For each outcome, incident cases were defined as the first recorded diagnosis documented after the index date. Risk estimates were evaluated at 1‐, 3‐ and 5‐year follow‐up intervals following medication initiation. Following a target trial emulation framework, patients were classified and analysed according to their treatment status at cohort entry, analogous to an intention‐to‐treat approach. Post‐index discontinuation, interruption or switching was not used to reclassify exposure groups during follow‐up. The corresponding ICD‐10 codes for ophthalmologic diagnoses are summarized in Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using the TriNetX integrated analytics platform. To minimize baseline confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between the GLP‐1RAs and AWL groups (Table S3). Propensity scores, representing the probability of assignment to either treatment group, were estimated using logistic regression. A 1:1 matching ratio was implemented with a nearest‐neighbour greedy algorithm and a calliper width of 0.1 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values > 0.1 indicating potential imbalance. Comparative risk estimates between groups were calculated for each ocular outcome and expressed as risk ratios (RRs), risk differences (RDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values. Cumulative incidence over time was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between‐group differences were assessed using the log‐rank test. Statistical significance was determined using two‐sided p values < 0.05.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed within disease‐specific naïve populations to test the robustness of the findings. In these analyses, only patients with a prior diagnosis of the respective outcome (cataract, AMD, OHTN, POAG or DES) were excluded, whereas other ocular conditions were not. To further assess the consistency of associations, additional analyses examined cataract subtypes—including nuclear, cortical and posterior subcapsular cataracts—as well as cataract surgery. Comparable evaluations were also performed for secondary ocular outcomes, including retinal haemorrhage or edema (RH/RE) and dry eye‐related medication use. Each outcome‐specific population was analysed independently to estimate the incidence and relative risk of new‐onset ocular events between the GLP‐1RAs and AWL groups at 1‐, 3‐ and 5‐year follow‐up intervals, yielding RRs, RDs and 95% CIs. Codes used for case identification are listed in Table S2. Furthermore, to account for the historical shift in prescribing practices (specifically the indication of GLP‐1 RAs for weight management since 2014) [12], an additional time‐restricted (2015–2025) sensitivity analysis was conducted to re‐evaluate the ocular outcomes.

Results

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Before PSM, 170 831 patients were identified—34 325 in the GLP‐1RAs group and 136 506 in the AWL group. Patients receiving GLP‐1RAs were slightly older (67.8 ± 6.3 vs. 68.5 ± 6.8 years) and less often female (64.4% vs. 71.8%). The GLP‐1RAs group had a higher mean body mass index (BMI) (37.3 ± 6.9 vs. 35.7 ± 6.9 kg/m2). Comorbidities were generally more prevalent among GLP‐1RAs users, including hypertension (64.7% vs. 54.2%), ischemic heart disease (20.1% vs. 13.4%) and hyperlipidemia (44.7% vs. 37.1%). Glucocorticoids were also higher in the GLP‐1RAs group (67.6% vs. 56.2%), reflecting a greater baseline medication burden. After 1:1 PSM, a total of 68 536 patients were successfully matched, with 34 268 patients in each group. Post‐matching, no significant differences were observed between groups in demographics, race/ethnicity, comorbidities or medication use, with all SMDs reduced to < 0.1. Main Outcomes

Cataract

GLP‐1RAs use was associated with a consistently lower incidence of new‐onset cataract compared with the AWL user across all follow‐up intervals. The RRs at 1, 3, 5 years were 0.791 (95% CI, 0.683–0.916; p = 0.0016), 0.552 (95% CI, 0.495–0.617; p < 0.0001) and 0.447 (95% CI, 0.403–0.496; p < 0.0001), respectively—demonstrating a sustained and progressive reduction in cataract risk associated with GLP‐1RAs therapy.

Age‐Related Macular Degeneration

For both nonexudative and exudative AMD, GLP‐1RAs users consistently demonstrated a lower risk than the AWL group, with the inverse association strengthening over time. The RRs were 0.64 (95% CI, 0.35–1.06; p = 0.074) at 1 year, 0.47 (95% CI, 0.31–0.71; p = 0.0002) at 3 years and 0.33 (95% CI, 0.23–0.48; p < 0.0001), highlighting a progressive and durable reduction in AMD incidence associated with GLP‐1RAs therapy.

Ocular Hypertension

No significant differences in ocular hypertension incidence were observed between groups at 1 or 3 years. By 5 years, however, GLP‐1RAs users showed a modest but significant risk reduction (0.07% vs. 0.13%; RR, 0.556; 95% CI, 0.341–0.906; p = 0.0168).

Primary Open‐Angle Glaucoma

The risk of new‐onset POAG was comparable at 1 year (0.06% vs. 0.06%; RR, 0.955; 95% CI, 0.525–1.735; p = 0.8788), but progressively diverged thereafter, favouring GLP‐1RA group. Significant reductions were observed at 3 years (RR, 0.592; 95% CI, 0.374–0.937; p = 0.0235) and 5 years (RR, 0.500; 95% CI, 0.323–0.77SU5; p = 0.0016).

Dry Eye Syndrome

GLP‐1RAs therapy was associated with a consistently lower incidence of DES across the follow‐up period. The RRs at 1, 3 and 5 years were 0.561 (95% CI, 0.452–0.695; p < 0.0001), 0.461 (95% CI, 0.392–0.541; p < 0.0001) and 0.359 (95% CI, 0.308–0.417; p < 0.0001), respectively, indicating a robust and sustained inverse association with GLP‐1RAs use. Sensitivity Analysis

Across these disease‐specific naïve cohorts, GLP‐1RAs therapy consistently demonstrated lower risks of new‐onset ocular diseases compared with the AWL group. The lower risk was evident across all major conditions, including cataract (5‐year RR, 0.464; 95% CI, 0.420–0.514; p < 0.0001), AMD (5‐year RR, 0.351; 95% CI, 0.251–0.491; p < 0.0001), ocular hypertension (5‐year RR, 0.481; 95% CI, 0.325–0.711; p = 0.0002), POAG (5‐year RR, 0.425; 95% CI, 0.290–0.625; p < 0.0001) and DES (5‐year RR, 0.364; 95% CI, 0.319–0.415; p < 0.0001).

Similar reductions were observed across cataract subtypes over 5‐year follow‐up periods, including nuclear cataract (RR, 0.421; 95% CI, 0.371–0.478; p < 0.0001), cortical cataract (RR, 0.374; 95% CI, 0.356–0.545; p < 0.0001) and posterior subcapsular cataract (RR, 0.483; 95% CI, 0.255–0.913; p = 0.0221) and extended to cataract surgery (RR, 0.330; 95% CI, 0.246–0.442; p < 0.0001). Comparable reduction was also noted in RH/RE (RR, 0.435; 95% CI, 0.300–0.630; p < 0.0001) and dry eye‐related medication use (RR, 0.441; 95% CI, 0.388–0.500; p < 0.0001). Complete sensitivity analyses estimates and forest plots are provided in Tables S4–S7 and Figures [Link], [Link], respectively.

Finally, in the time‐restricted sensitivity analysis (2015–2025), the significant long‐term protective associations of GLP‐1 RAs were reaffirmed at the 5‐year follow‐up across most ocular outcomes (Table S8).

Discussion

In this large multicenter study of non‐diabetic, obese older adults, treatment with GLP‐1RAs significantly lowered the risk of several age‐related eye diseases—including cataract, AMD, glaucoma and DES—over 5 years of follow‐up. These protective associations strengthened with time and remained robust in multiple sensitivity analyses, suggesting that GLP‐1RAs may be associated with better ocular and metabolic outcomes.

Cataract

GLP‐1RA users developed roughly half as many cataracts as those receiving conventional weight‐loss agents, contrasting with an earlier study in diabetic populations that reported minimal benefit [19]. Diabetic patients experience substantial oxidative stress, protein glycation and photochemical damage to lens proteins, all of which can overshadow or counteract the antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory actions of GLP‐1RAs [24]. In contrast, our non‐diabetic cohort, free from these diabetes‐related metabolic and oxidative disturbances, provides a clearer setting in which potential favourable associations with the lens can be observed.

A recent retrospective study of non‐diabetic adults likewise reported reduced cataract risk with GLP‐1RA therapy [25], but our study extends these findings in several important ways. First, we constructed a fully ophthalmology disease–naïve baseline, excluding all major eye diseases rather than cataract alone, avoiding detection bias and confounding from pre‐existing eye conditions that influence cataract diagnosis. Second, our comparator group consisted of contemporary weight‐loss medications, improving real‐world relevance compared with prior analyses that relied heavily on legacy agents no longer in clinical use. Third, by evaluating cataract surgery as a hard endpoint, we demonstrated that GLP‐1RAs may slow not only diagnostic onset but also clinically meaningful progression of lens opacity.

Age‐Related Macular Degeneration

GLP‐1RAs therapy also reduced the risk of both exudative and non‐exudative AMD, as well as retinal haemorrhage or edema, with the inverse association intensifying over time. Although a recent study reported that GLP‐1RAs users had a higher risk of developing neovascular AMD compared with non‐users, that analysis was limited to a diabetic population [26]. In such patients, hyperglycemia and microangiopathy may have confounded the neovascular pathophysiology. In contrast, several large‐scale cohort studies have demonstrated that GLP‐1RAs were associated with a lower risk of the development and progression of AMD [19, 27]. Collectively, these findings suggest that GLP‐1RAs confer retinal protection beyond glycemic control through anti‐inflammatory and antioxidative mechanisms.

Preclinical evidence further supports these observations. In a mouse model, liraglutide inhibited choroidal neovascularization by suppressing intraocular inflammation and NOD‐like receptor protein 3 inflammasome signalling [28]. In vitro, the same agent reduced lipid dysregulation and oxidative stress through activation of AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and enhanced lipophagy [29]. Together, these results suggest that GLP‐1RAs may counteract multiple pathogenic pathways involved in retinal ageing.

Glaucoma

The protective association between GLP‐1RAs and glaucoma, especially POAG, has been reported in several large retrospective and meta‐analytic studies in diabetic populations [20, 30, 31, 32]. Our findings were consistent with prior studies in non‐diabetic individuals [33], showing approximately a 50%–60% lower risk of ocular hypertension and POAG compared with AWL therapies. Our ophthalmic disease–naïve population provided a clearer view of incident disease risk rather than treatment outcomes. The consistent reduction in new‐onset POAG supports that GLP‐1RAs likely influence intraocular pressure regulation or confer neuroprotection through anti‐inflammatory pathways. These results strengthen the evidence for systemic metabolic modulation as a viable preventive strategy for glaucoma. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to the 2015–2025 period, GLP‐1RA use was associated with lower risks of POAG, whereas the association with OHTN was borderline (p = 0.0522) at 5 years. It suggests that GLP‐1RA may influence glaucoma through mechanisms beyond intraocular pressure alone, involving neuroinflammation, metabolic stress or retinal ganglion cell injury [34, 35].

Dry Eye Syndrome

GLP‐1RAs were also linked to a markedly lower incidence of DES. A large‐scale cohort study found that GLP‐1RAs were related to a lower incidence of external eye diseases, including DES, in patients with type 2 diabetes [36]. Our study extends this evidence to non‐diabetic adults, confirming a consistent lower risk.

Inflammation plays a central role in DES pathogenesis, and GLP‐1RAs are known to dampen inflammatory signalling and oxidative stress. Preclinical studies show that GLP‐1 receptors are expressed in the lacrimal gland, and that topical GLP‐1RAs administration reduces inflammation and enhances tear secretion in diabetic mouse models [37]. These findings provide biological plausibility and suggest potential for GLP‐1RAs as future topical therapies for ocular surface disease.

Clinical Translation

These results highlight a new opportunity for ophthalmologists to engage in preventive care for age‐related eye diseases among obese, non‐diabetic older adults—a group often seen in practice but rarely targeted for metabolic intervention. Although GLP‐1RAs are typically prescribed for diabetes or weight management, their ocular benefits suggest that timely referral to primary care or metabolic specialists could benefit high‐risk individuals. We consider ophthalmology clinics can provide a unique window for early identification and intervention. Incorporating metabolic therapies such as GLP‐1RAs into preventive eye‐care strategies could shift the focus from managing advanced vision loss to maintaining ocular health across ageing.

Strengths and Limitations

The study's strengths include its large, diverse multicenter population in the TriNetX platform and its focus on ophthalmic disease–naïve individuals, which enabled assessment of true incident disease risk. Multiple sensitivity analyses across subgroups confirmed the robustness of the findings. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. As with all retrospective analyses, potential misclassification bias exists due to reliance on ICD‐10 and CPT coding, assuming consistent documentation across sites. In addition, general EHR databases such as TriNetX may underestimate the true incidence of ophthalmic diseases, as many patients receive eye care at specialized outpatient clinics outside the network [38]. Unrecorded over‐the‐counter medication use, out‐of‐network prescribing, variations in real‐world treatment duration, and the inability to confirm long‐term medication adherence may also have introduced little exposure misclassification.

Continuous BMI values were available for approximately 70% of patients, although cohort entry was primarily defined using ICD‐10 obesity diagnoses. Despite incomplete continuous BMI data, PSM achieved good overall covariate balance, with all post‐matching SMDs < 0.1. Finally, the TriNetX database is not ophthalmology‐specific and lacks key data such as visual acuity, imaging results and disease staging. Future prospective studies using eye‐specific registries are needed to verify causality and elucidate the mechanisms by which GLP‐1RAs protect ocular tissues.

Conclusion

GLP‐1RA therapy lowered the risk of cataract, AMD, glaucoma and DES in non‐diabetic, obese older adults over 5 years of observation. Beyond effective weight management, GLP‐1RAs use was associated with a lower risk of age‐related ocular diseases. Clarifying the biological pathways that link metabolic regulation to ocular health will be essential for translating these findings into clinical practice.
