A Case for an Inhomogeneous Einstein-de Sitter Universe
Peter Raffai, Dominika E. R. Kis, Dávid A. Ködmön, Adrienn Pataki, Rebeka L. Böttger, Gergely Dálya

Abstract
In our local-to-global cosmological framework, cosmic acceleration arises from local dynamics in an inhomogeneous Einstein–de Sitter (iEdS) universe without invoking dark energy. An iEdS universe follows a quasilinear coasting evolution from an Einstein–de Sitter to a Milne state, as an effective negative curvature emerges from growing inhomogeneities without breaking spatial flatness. Acceleration can arise from structure formation amplifying this effect. We test two realizations, iEdS(1) and iEdS(2), with H 0 = { 70.24 , 74.00 } ​ km ​ s − 1 ​ Mpc − 1 H_{0}=\{70.24,74.00\}\ \mathrm{km\ s^{-1}\ Mpc^{-1}} and Ω m , 0 = { 0.290 , 0.261 } \Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}=\{0.290,0.261\} , against CMB, BAO, and SN Ia data. iEdS(1) fits better than Λ \Lambda CDM and alleviates the H 0 H_{0} tension, whereas iEdS(2) fully resolves it while remaining broadly consistent with the data. Both models yield t 0 ≃ 13.64 ​ Gyr {t_{0}\simeq 13.64\ \mathrm{Gyr}} , consistent with globular-cluster estimates.



I
Introduction

The flat Lambda Cold Dark Matter (
\Lambda
CDM) model is the prevailing cosmological model, describing a spatially flat universe with dark energy in the form of a cosmological constant (
\Lambda
)
30
. It shows remarkable concordance with diverse observations across cosmic epochs
44
, including three standard precision probes: cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies
25
;
24
;
4
, baryon acoustic oscillations (BAOs) identified in galaxy surveys
20
;
5
;
2
, and the type Ia supernova (SN Ia) distance modulus–redshift relation
37
;
32
;
9
. Despite its successes, tensions between locally measured parameters and those derived from CMB and BAO observations —most notably in the Hubble constant (
H_{0}
)
16
and the structure growth parameter (
S_{8}
)
17
— as well as other anomalies
31
, may indicate the need for further refinement of this cosmological framework.

The flat
\Lambda
CDM model is based on a global-to-local approach. It assumes large-scale homogeneity and isotropy (the cosmological principle
29
), which imply a global scale factor
a(t)
evolving with a universal (cosmic) time
t
according to the Friedmann equations
22
;
18
. Local inhomogeneities (large-scale structure and gravitational gradients
8
) and motions (peculiar velocities
42
) are typically treated as perturbations evolving in a uniformly expanding background, or as potential contributors to the global Friedmann dynamics through the nonlinear nature of Einstein’s field equations, an effect known as cosmological backreaction
21
;
12
. It has been proposed that backreaction could mimic a cosmological-constant–like term in the global expansion
15
, though the prevailing view, despite numerous counterclaims (e.g.
11
), is that its magnitude is too small to account for the observed acceleration of the universe.

In this paper, we present a local-to-global framework showing that the observed cosmic acceleration, well described by an apparent
\Lambda>0
term, arises naturally from local dynamics in an inhomogeneous Einstein–de Sitter (hereafter
iEdS
) universe without invoking dark energy. In Sec.
II
, we show that such an iEdS universe follows a quasilinear coasting evolution, starting from the Einstein–de Sitter state
19
with negligible inhomogeneities at recombination, then transitioning through an era of accelerated expansion, and finally asymptoting to a Milne universe
29
with
a(t)\propto t
coasting evolution. The acceleration arises as a purely general-relativistic effect of structure formation, which amplifies an effective negative spatial curvature that emerges naturally without breaking spatial flatness. Fitting two realizations, iEdS(1) and iEdS(2) (Sec.
III
), to the Planck 2018 CMB temperature power spectrum
4
, BAO data from DESI DR2
1
, and SNe Ia in the Pantheon+ sample
39
, we find that the iEdS framework can provide better fits than
\Lambda
CDM, while alleviating or even resolving the Hubble tension. Conclusions are summarized in Sec.
IV
.

II
Theoretical foundations

In place of the cosmological principle, we base our framework on two principles:

(i)

The global evolution of the universe follows the Friedmann equations, with scale factor
a(t)
and cosmological parameters
\{\theta\}
.

(ii)

This global evolution can be represented by an ensemble of finite-volume regions evolving according to the Friedmann equations, each with its own scale factor
a_{i}(t)
and cosmological parameters
\{\theta_{i}\}
. The global scale factor is then

a(t)^{3}=\frac{\sum_{i}V_{i}}{\sum_{i}V_{0,i}}\equiv\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}(t)^{3}V_{0,i}}{\sum_{i}V_{0,i}},

(1)

where
V_{i}
is the volume of the
i
-th region at cosmic time
t
, and
V_{0,i}
denotes
V_{i}
at
t_{0}
, with
a_{i}(t_{0})\equiv 1
for all
i
, so that
a(t_{0})=1
.

Note that, in principle, each region can have its own cosmic-time analog
t_{i}
, related to the global time by a lapse function
L_{i}(t)
via
\mathrm{d}t_{i}=L_{i}(t)\mathrm{d}t
. In most practical cases these functions are indistinguishable from
L_{i}(t)=1
and can always be absorbed into the local Hubble parameters
H_{i}(t)
. Also,
a_{i}(t)=0
occurs at the same
t
for all
i
if and only if
\{\theta_{i}\}=\{\theta\}
for all
i
; otherwise, the time of the Big Bang, defined by
a(t)=0
, corresponds to the moment when all
a_{i}(t)
vanish.

The validity of principle (i) is also ensured in standard cosmology, as a consequence of the global symmetries imposed on Einstein’s field equations by the cosmological principle. While principle (ii) is not guaranteed to hold in general, it is always implicitly assumed when testing models or inferring parameters from observations covering single or multiple finite volumes. In doing so, however, cosmological probes have relied on a third principle, which we reject:

(iii)

For the finite volumes probed,
\{\theta_{i}\}=\{\theta\}
for all
i
(and thus
a_{i}(t)=a(t)
for all
i
) is always satisfied.

As we show in this work, the assumption that accelerated expansion is sourced by an extra component (dark energy), distinct from matter, radiation, and curvature, is a consequence of principle (iii), and can be avoided if only principles (i) and (ii) are applied.

From principles (i) and (ii), we obtain the global Hubble and deceleration parameters by differentiating Eq. (
1
) with respect to
t
:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{a}}{a}=\frac{\sum_{i}H_{i}V_{i}}{\sum_{i}V_{i}}\equiv\left\langle H_{i}\right\rangle,

(2)

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-\frac{\ddot{a}}{a\left\langle H_{i}^{2}\right\rangle}=\left\langle q_{i}\right\rangle-2\!\left(1-\frac{\left\langle H_{i}\right\rangle^{2}}{\left\langle H_{i}^{2}\right\rangle}\right),

(3)

with

q_{i}\equiv-\frac{\ddot{a}_{i}}{a_{i}\left\langle H_{i}^{2}\right\rangle},

(4)

where the Hubble constant is
H_{0}=H(t_{0})
(hereafter we denote
\langle H_{i}\rangle
and
\langle H_{i}(t_{0})\rangle
by
H
and
H_{0}
, respectively). Without dark energy (implying
\langle q_{i}\rangle\geq 0
), a necessary condition for global acceleration (
q<0
) is
{H^{2}<\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle}
. When this holds,
q<0
may occur even if
\langle q_{i}\rangle>0
, and the
{H^{2}<\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle}
condition is always satisfied if at least one region is contracting (
H_{i}<0
).

Neglecting radiation, we consider an iEdS universe represented by regions with matter density
\rho_{\mathrm{m},i}
and curvature density
\rho_{\mathrm{k},i}
. From principle (ii) it follows that

H_{i}^{2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\left(\rho_{\mathrm{m},i}+\rho_{\mathrm{k},i}\right),

(5)

with
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}=1
at all times, where

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{8\pi G}{3}\frac{\langle\rho_{\mathrm{m},i}\rangle}{\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\frac{\rho_{\mathrm{m},0}a^{-3}}{\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle},

(6)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{m},0}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}\rho_{\mathrm{m},i}(t_{0})V_{0,i}}{\sum_{i}V_{0,i}},

(7)

and

\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}\equiv\frac{8\pi G}{3}\frac{\langle\rho_{\mathrm{k},i}\rangle}{\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{\left\langle\Omega_{\mathrm{k},i}(t_{0})H_{i}(t_{0})^{2}a_{i}^{-2}\right\rangle}{\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle},

(8)

so that
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}
can become nonzero even if the universe is globally flat at
t_{0}
(
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}(t_{0})=0
) or at any other time. In fact, regions with
\Omega_{\mathrm{k},i}>0
expand faster with
t
and gain more weight in volume averaging than those with
\Omega_{\mathrm{k},i}\leq 0
. Consequently, even a universe that starts with
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}\leq 0
(
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\geq 1
) evolves toward
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}>0
and
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}<1
, asymptoting to a Milne universe (
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}=1
) as its final state.

Note that in the iEdS case

\left\langle q_{i}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

(9)

so that from Eq. (
3
)

q=\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}-2\left(1-\frac{H^{2}}{\left\langle H_{i}^{2}\right\rangle}\right).

(10)

Let us ascribe the second term in Eq. (
10
) to a fictitious component ’x’ with
\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}>0
as

q=\frac{1}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1+3w_{\mathrm{x}}\right)\Omega_{\mathrm{x}},

(11)

which implies

w_{\mathrm{x}}=-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{4}{3}\left(1-\frac{H^{2}}{\left\langle H_{i}^{2}\right\rangle}\right)\frac{1}{\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}},

(12)

with

\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}=\frac{\langle H_{i}(t_{0})^{2}\rangle}{\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle}\Omega_{\mathrm{x},0}\exp\left[3\int_{a}^{1}\frac{1+w_{\mathrm{x}}(a^{\prime})}{a^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}a^{\prime}\right].

(13)

In general, the ratio
H^{2}/\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle
—and hence
q
,
\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}
, and
w_{\mathrm{x}}
in Eqs. (
11
)–(
13
)—can only be modeled numerically and constrained observationally. In papers
36
;
26
;
35
we showed that at late times the
a(t)\propto t
coasting evolution, a feature of curvature-dominated expansion, fits various cosmological datasets. Motivated by these results, we adopt two analytical approximations linking
\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}
to the effective global curvature:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\langle H_{i}(t_{0})^{2}\rangle}{\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle}\left(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\right)a^{-2},

(14)

\displaystyle H^{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\langle H_{i}(t_{0})^{2}\rangle\left(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\right)a^{-2}.

(15)

Since, from Eqs. (
5
) and (
14
),

\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle=\langle H_{i}(t_{0})^{2}\rangle\left[\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}a^{-3}+(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0})a^{-2}\right],

(16)

Eq. (
15
) corresponds to a universe in which all matter contributions to
H^{2}\equiv\langle H_{i}\rangle^{2}
are canceled by contracting (
H_{i}<0
) matter-dominated regions. Note that this assumption, and Eqs. (
14
)–(
15
), need not be valid throughout the entire cosmic evolution, only at late times when matter domination ceases and
\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}
begins to influence the global expansion.

From Eq. (
12
) and Eqs (
14
)-(
16
), we obtain

w_{\mathrm{x}}(a)\approx-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{4}{3}\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}}{\left(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\right)}a^{-1},

(17)

which, when substituted into Eq. (
13
), gives

\rho_{\mathrm{x}}(z)=\rho_{\mathrm{x,0}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{4\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}}{1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}}\,z\right]\left(1+z\right)^{2},

(18)

with
a=(1+z)^{-1}
. These relations describe an effective spatial curvature that grows from
\Omega_{\mathrm{x}}=0
at early times to
\Omega_{\mathrm{x},0}=1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}
today. From Eq. (
17
),
w_{\mathrm{x}}(+\infty)=-1/3
, while
w_{\mathrm{x}}(1)\simeq-1
if
\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\simeq 1/3
, explaining why the present accelerated expansion of our universe with
\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}\simeq 1/3
is usually attributed to a cosmological constant with
w_{\Lambda}=-1
.

We test the iEdS framework by replacing Eq. (
15
) with the more general form

H^{2}\approx r^{2}\langle H_{i}(t_{0})^{2}\rangle(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0})a^{-2},

(19)

where
r
is a dimensionless free parameter (
r=1
recovers Eq. (
15
)). Equations (
17
)–(
18
) then generalize to

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{x}}(a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{4}{3}\left[\frac{a_{\mathrm{x}}}{a}+\left(1-r^{2}\right)\right]

(20)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{x}}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{x,0}}\exp\!\left[-4a_{\mathrm{x}}z\right]\left(1+z\right)^{2(2r^{2}-1)},

(21)

with
a_{\mathrm{x}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0}/(1-\Omega_{\mathrm{m},0})
. In data fits, we use these
w_{\mathrm{x}}(a)
and
\rho_{\mathrm{x}}(z)
(with
\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}=0
) directly, without the approximations in Eqs. (
14
)-(
15
) and (
19
), which were only applied in their derivations. The
\Lambda
CDM case with
w_{\Lambda}=-1
and
\rho_{\Lambda}(z)=\rho_{\Lambda,0}
is recovered by setting
a_{\mathrm{x}}=0
and
r^{2}=0.5
. The
w(a)=w_{0}+w_{a}(1-a)
Chevallier–Polarski–Linder parameterization used in
w_{0}w_{a}
CDM cosmology
14
;
28
;
1
is similarly recovered by expanding
w_{\mathrm{x}}
around
a=1
as
w_{\mathrm{x}}=w(a)+\mathcal{O}((1-a)^{2})
, where

\displaystyle w_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{4}{3}\left[a_{\mathrm{x}}+\left(1-r^{2}\right)\right],

(22)

\displaystyle w_{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4}{3}a_{\mathrm{x}}.

(23)

Spatial curvature affects angular diameter and luminosity distances not only through the expansion function but also via a geometric factor arising as light from distant sources propagates toward the observer, crossing thin shells at various redshifts. Whereas local curvatures enter the expansion through volume averages (Eq. (
8
)), this factor requires surface averages over the projected cross-sections of the regions:

\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{geom}}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\frac{\{\rho_{\mathrm{k},i}\}}{\{H_{i}^{2}\}},

(24)

where
\{X\}=\sum_{i}XA_{i}/\sum_{i}A_{i}
denotes an average over projected areas
A_{i}=a_{i}^{2}A_{0,i}
. Since
\rho_{\mathrm{k},i}\propto a_{i}^{-2}
,
\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{geom}}
remains zero if initially zero. As a projected surface average, however, it is subject to ensemble properties and cosmic variance. Consequently, CMB and Pantheon+ SNe Ia (full-sky) should yield consistent values, whereas DESI DR2 (covering roughly one-third of the sky) could, in principle, differ. Nevertheless, in all cases
\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{geom}}
is expected to be negligible, and we therefore fix it to zero in data fits. Our choice is further supported by the negligible impact
\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{geom}}
has on BAO and SN Ia fits within the low-redshift ranges of DESI DR2 and Pantheon+ (
z\lesssim 2.3
). The only non-negligible impact on data fits occurs for the CMB at the high redshift of recombination (
z_{*}\simeq 1090
), where a nonzero
\Omega_{k}^{\mathrm{geom}}
should indeed be taken into account.

IV
Conclusion

iEdS(1) and iEdS(2) are specific realizations of the broader iEdS framework, which is based on the local-to-global approach defined by principles (i)–(ii) (see Sec.
II
). In this framework, the accelerated expansion emerges as a purely gravitational effect within general relativity, without invoking alternative gravity theories or scalar fields as dark-energy components. Despite their analytic approximations in Eqs. (
14
)–(
15
) and (
19
), iEdS(1) and iEdS(2) demonstrate (Sec.
III
) that iEdS cosmologies can match or even outperform
\Lambda
CDM in fitting CMB, BAO, and SNe Ia data, while alleviating or resolving the Hubble tension (see Tables
1
–
2
). The way forward is to develop a realistic model of structure formation—specifically for
H^{2}\langle H_{i}^{2}\rangle^{-1}
in Eq. (
10
)—through simulations and observations, to construct the global iEdS evolution and test it against precision cosmological probes. As we demonstrated here, such a model could eliminate the need for dark energy, since the effective
w_{\mathrm{x}}
in Eq. (
12
), unlike
w_{\mathrm{DE}}
, is fully determined by matter and curvature.

The Planck 2018 baseline
\Lambda
CDM model gives an age of the universe of
{t_{0}=13.797\pm 0.023\ \mathrm{Gyr}}
. For the flat
\Lambda
CDM model fitted to Pantheon+ SNe
9
, the derived age is
{t_{0}=12.4\pm 0.3\ \mathrm{Gyr}}
, in
{\sim 3\sigma}
tension with the globular-cluster estimate
{t_{0}=13.6\pm 0.3\ \mathrm{Gyr}}

43
. Our
camb
runs yield
{t_{0}=13.663\ \mathrm{Gyr}}
and
{t_{0}=13.620\ \mathrm{Gyr}}
for iEdS(1) and iEdS(2), respectively, both consistent with globular-cluster ages. The corresponding
{S_{8}=0.831}
and
{S_{8}=0.819}
values are slightly lower but consistent with the Planck
\Lambda
CDM result
{S_{8}=0.834\pm 0.016}

4
. However, without a detailed theory of structure formation and dedicated
S_{8}
fits to late-time structure data within the iEdS framework, no conclusions can yet be drawn about how iEdS cosmology affects the
S_{8}
tension.

Similarly to a
\Lambda
CDM universe, an iEdS one avoids both recollapse (Big Crunch) and divergent expansion (Big Rip), asymptotically approaching a dark, thermodynamically frozen equilibrium state known as the Big Freeze. In contrast, in a universe undergoing eternal accelerated expansion, such as that described by the
\Lambda
CDM model, the maximum comoving distances that can be reached or observed are finite. This is not the case in the iEdS model, whose dynamics asymptotically approach linear expansion,
a(t)\propto t
. If our universe indeed follows an iEdS evolution, both comoving horizons are infinite, revealing a cosmos without unreachable or unobservable realms, where every part of the universe is, in principle, open to discovery, regardless of whether the universe is finite or infinite in size.
