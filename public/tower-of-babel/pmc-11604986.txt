Type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer: genetic causality explored via Mendelian randomization
Authors: Qiuhong Ke, Yongbing Huang, Libin Cheng, Chaolin Lin, Linhua Zhao, Wulong Huang, Zhisheng Chen, Yimin Xu, Yipiao Huang, Lanlan Cai, Bin Lin, Rui Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global burden of type 2 diabetes (T2D) and colorectal cancer (CRC) continues to rise. Observational studies have suggested a link between T2D and an elevated risk of CRC. However, these studies are often susceptible to confounding factors and reverse causation, leading to inconsistent findings. There is a specific gap in knowledge regarding the causal nature of the relationship between T2D and CRC, which this study aims to address using a more robust approach. To fill this gap, we employed Mendelian randomization (MR), a method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causality, providing clearer insight into the genetic links between T2D and CRC.MethodsA bidirectional MR study was conducted to investigate the causal links between T2D and CRC. Genetic instruments for T2D were derived from two large genome-wide association studies (GWAS) with a total sample size of X individuals, and CRC genetic instruments were obtained from a GWAS with Y individuals. The MR analysis utilized the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method as the primary analysis, alongside sensitivity analyses such as heterogeneity and pleiotropy analysis to account for potential pleiotropy and bias. Additionally, to enhance the robustness of the findings and minimize the influence of data from different GWAS sources, we performed a meta-analysis of the IVW results.ResultsThe MR analysis and meta-analysis revealed that genetically predicted T2D is associated with an increased risk of CRC (Pooled ORIVW = 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12, P = 0.003). However, the reverse analysis did not indicate a causal effect of genetically predicted CRC on T2D risk (Pooled ORIVW = 1.02, 95% CI 0.96–1.09, P = 0.469). Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of these findings, indicating no evidence of heterogeneity or pleiotropic effects that could bias the results (all P > 0.05).ConclusionOur bidirectional MR study and meta-analysis provide evidence that T2D increases the risk of colorectal cancer. However, there is no evidence to support a reverse causal relationship. These findings highlight the importance of monitoring and managing T2D as part of CRC prevention strategies.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Type 2, Colorectal neoplasms, Mendelian randomization analysis, Genome-wide association study, Causality

Introduction

The increasing global prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) has become a major public health concern [1–3]. Characterized by chronic hyperglycemia due to insulin resistance or deficiency [4–7], T2D is associated with numerous complications, including cardiovascular diseases, neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy [8–11]. Additionally, the association between T2D and cancer is receiving increasing attention in clinical practice [12, 13].

Recent epidemiological studies have suggested a potential association between T2D and an increased risk of developing colorectal cancer (CRC) [14, 15]. Individuals with T2D appear to have a higher incidence of CRC compared to non-diabetics, possibly due to shared risk factors such as obesity, poor diet, sedentary lifestyle, and chronic inflammation. Hyperinsulinemia and increased levels of insulin-like growth factors (IGFs) in T2D patients have also been proposed as biological mechanisms promoting carcinogenesis [16, 17]. However, these studies often suffer from significant limitations, including confounding variables and reverse causation, which hinder the ability to draw robust conclusions about the causal nature of the relationship between T2D and CRC.

To address these limitations, Mendelian randomization (MR) presents a powerful epidemiological approach [18]. MR utilizes genetic variants associated with an exposure (e.g., T2D) as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate the causal effect of the exposure on an outcome (e.g., CRC) [19]. This method leverages the random allocation of alleles at conception, which helps to minimize confounding and reverse causation, providing more reliable causal inferences [18, 20]. In this study, we adopted a bidirectional MR approach to explore the potential causal relationships between T2D and CRC, investigating not only whether T2D influences CRC risk but also whether CRC has an effect on T2D development. Additionally, to further mitigate the impact of data source variability, we conducted a meta-analysis of the IVW results, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the genetic evidence.

Our study aims to fill the gap in understanding the genetic causal links between T2D and CRC. By elucidating these relationships, we provide valuable insights into the shared pathophysiological mechanisms, which could inform both clinical practice and future research. Effective management of T2D may play a role in reducing the risk of CRC, highlighting the need for integrated prevention and management strategies. Furthermore, these findings suggest that future research should explore the underlying biological pathways and investigate potential interventions targeting both conditions. From a policy perspective, incorporating T2D as a factor in CRC risk assessments could enhance public health strategies and influence guidelines aimed at reducing the burden of both diseases.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Summary statistics for T2D and CRC were obtained from the GWAS Catalog [21], a comprehensive repository of GWAS results, with the ID of “CST007515” (discovery cohort of T2D, sample size = 298,957), “GCST006867” (replication cohort of T2D, sample size = 655,666) and “GCST012879” (sample size = 32,072) respectively. These datasets provide robust data, ensuring the selection of reliable IVs for our analysis. The GWAS summary data used in this study are summarized in Table 1. The data were accessed from publicly available databases, ensuring transparency and reproducibility of our study. Given that individual-level data were not utilized, no additional ethical approval was necessary.

Genetic instruments

The selection of genetic instruments is based on the fundamental assumptions [22] of MR (Fig. 1). Genetic variants associated with T2D and CRC at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10–8) were selected as IVs. To ensure the validity of these instruments, we performed clumping to retain independent single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.001 within a 10 Mb window). Furthermore, to avoid the potential impact of weak IVs, SNPs with F-values < 10 were excluded (The formula for calculating the F-value is as follows [23]: F=R21-R2×N-K-1K). Finally, the MR Steiger test was employed to exclude SNPs with reverse causality, and Mendelian randomization pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) was applied to remove outliers [24].

Statistical analysis

The MR analysis primarily employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, as it provides the most efficient estimate when all genetic variants are valid instrumental variables and no pleiotropy is present. IVW is widely regarded as the main method for MR analysis due to its high statistical power. In addition to IVW, supplementary methods, including MR-Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode, were used to account for potential pleiotropy and provide robustness against violations of MR assumptions. MR-Egger, in particular, allows for the detection and correction of directional pleiotropy, while the weighted median method can provide consistent causal estimates even if up to 50% of the genetic instruments are invalid. To assess the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed. Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity among SNP estimates. Leave-one-out analysis was conducted to identify whether any single SNP disproportionately influenced the results. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO global test, which identify whether pleiotropic effects could bias the results. Additionally, a meta-analysis of the IVW findings was performed to ensure the robustness of the genetic evidence and provide a comprehensive evaluation (Fig. 2). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1) with the “TwoSampleMR” and “MRPRESSO” packages for MR analyses, and meta-analyses were conducted using the “meta” package. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (Table 2).

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis

In the discovery cohort, the IVW results suggest that T2D could increase the risk of CRC by 9% (odds ratio [OR] = 1.09, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01–1.17, P = 0.026), and this finding was further validated in the replication cohort (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 1.00–1.12, P = 0.036) (Fig. 3). Finally, we combined the effect sizes of the two cohorts through a meta-analysis, and the result indicates that T2D may be a potential risk factor for CRC (Pooled ORIVW = 1.07, 95% CI 1.02–1.12, P = 0.003) (Fig. 4).

Reverse Mendelian randomization analysis

In the discovery cohort, the IVW results suggest that from a genetic causal perspective, CRC is unlikely to cause T2D (OR = 1.03, 95% CI 0.93–1.13, P = 0.608), and this finding was further validated in the replication cohort (OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.93–1.05, P = 0.747) (Fig. 5). Finally, we combined the effect sizes of the two cohorts through a meta-analysis, and the results indicate that CRC is likely not a potential risk factor for T2D (Pooled ORIVW = 1.02, 95% CI 0.96–1.09, P = 0.469) (Fig. 6).

The Cochran's Q test indicate that heterogeneity is not significant in our bidirectional MR study (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 7A). Leave-one-out analysis suggests minimal influence from any single SNP (Fig. 7B). Discussion

This study applied MR to explore the genetic causal relationship between T2D and colorectal cancer CRC. The results demonstrated that T2D significantly increases the risk of CRC, whereas CRC does not have a reverse causal effect on T2D. This finding provides crucial genetic evidence for the association between T2D and CRC, with significant implications for clinical practice and the understanding of underlying mechanisms.

Clinically, numerous epidemiological studies have established that T2D patients are at a higher risk of developing CRC. For instance, Lawler et al. found that T2D patients had a 47% increased risk of CRC compared to non-diabetic individuals [25]. Furthermore, Lu et al. highlighted that hyperinsulinemia could enhance the proliferation and metastasis of human colorectal cancer [26]. These clinical and experimental observations are consistent with our findings, further confirming T2D as an independent risk factor for CRC.

Mechanistically, several biological pathways may explain the link between T2D and CRC. IGFs are known to promote tumor cell growth and proliferation through the insulin receptor and IGF-1 receptor signaling pathways [27, 28]. Obesity-related diseases such as T2D induce oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, resulting in DNA damage and increased mutation rates, thereby elevating cancer risk [29]. Chronic inflammation and metabolic syndrome, common in T2D patients, have also been implicated in CRC pathogenesis [30, 31].

The primary strength of our study lies in the use of MR, which leverages genetic variants as IVs to mitigate confounding and reverse causation issues inherent in observational studies. This method provides a robust framework for inferring causality between T2D and CRC. For instance, Smith and Ebrahim emphasized the power of MR in addressing causality in epidemiology. Additionally, to mitigate the potential impact of GWAS data from different sources on our conclusions, two separate GWAS datasets related to T2D were used to cross-validate the MR analysis. Meta-analysis was employed to combine the effect sizes from both the discovery and replication cohorts, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings. Finally, we conducted a variety of sensitivity analyses to further strengthen the robustness of our MR results.

However, this study has inevitable limitations. MR assumes that IVs are associated only with the exposure and not with confounders. While rigorous screening was employed, residual pleiotropy could not be entirely excluded. Additionally, the study sample predominantly comprised individuals of European descent, which may restrict the applicability of the findings to diverse ethnic populations. Additionally, in the reverse MR analysis, the lack of sufficient SNPs resulted in a lower explanatory power of the IVs for CRC, and the inability to implement various MR analysis methods. This may ultimately lead to potential bias in the reverse MR results. Furthermore, we did not explore different subtypes of CRC or investigate potential mediating pathways. Finally, we did not further validate our conclusions using clinical data.

The implications of this study for clinical practice and public health are significant. Given the increased risk of CRC in T2D patients, it is critical to prioritize CRC screening and prevention strategies for this population. Effective management of T2D through glycemic control, weight management, and lifestyle modifications may reduce CRC risk.

Our study underscores the importance of genetic approaches in understanding disease causality. Future research should integrate multi-omics data to elucidate the molecular mechanisms linking T2D and CRC. Moreover, exploring the relationships between other metabolic disorders and cancer across diverse populations will enhance our understanding of the complex interplay between metabolic dysregulation and cancer.

In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence that T2D increases the risk of CRC, emphasizing the need for targeted screening and prevention efforts. The findings also highlight the potential of MR in unraveling complex disease relationships and guiding future research directions.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence from Mendelian randomization and meta-analysis that genetically predicted T2D is associated with an increased risk of CRC. These findings underscore the importance of integrating CRC risk assessment into the prevention and management strategies for T2D. Further multicenter clinical studies and mechanistic research are needed to confirm these results and explore the underlying biological pathways.
