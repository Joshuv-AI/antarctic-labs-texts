SynthTools: A Framework for Scaling Synthetic Tools for Agent Development


Abstract
AI agents increasingly rely on external tools to solve complex, long-horizon tasks. Advancing such agents requires reproducible evaluation and large-scale training in controllable, diverse, and realistic tool-use environments. However, real-world APIs are limited in availability, domain coverage, and stability, often requiring access keys and imposing rate limits, which render them impractical for stable evaluation or scalable training. To address these challenges, we introduce SynthTools , a flexible and scalable framework for generating synthetic tool ecosystems. Our framework consists of three core components: Tool Generation for automatic and scalable creation of diverse tools, Tool Simulation to emulate realistic tool behaviors, and Tool Audit to ensure correctness and consistency of tool simulation. To illustrate its scalability, we show that SynthTools can readily produce toolsets that span twice as many domains and twice as many tools per domain as prior work. Furthermore, the tool simulation and tool audit components demonstrate strong reliability, achieving 94 % 94\% and 99 % 99\% accuracy respectively. Finally, we construct downstream tasks from the generated tools that even state-of-the-art models struggle to complete. By enabling scalable, diverse, and reliable tool ecosystems, SynthTools provides a practical path toward large-scale training and stable evaluation of tool-use agents. Our code is available at .

Introduction

Large language model (LLM) agents have drawn considerable attention for their potential to tackle complex, real-world tasks.
To complete such tasks, modern agents are increasingly envisioned to make coordinated and precise use of multiple tools
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
)
.
This positions tool-use proficiency as a central capability of a performant agent.
However, current models continue to face challenges in using tools reliably
(
Yao et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
;
Patil et al. 2024a
)
.
As evident throughout major advances in machine learning
(
Deng et al. 2009
;
Hoffmann et al. 2022
)
, the scale and diversity of training sets are among the most critical factors influencing the quality of a model.
Therefore, to realize the vision of autonomous tool-use agents, it is essential to obtain a comprehensive toolset for training and evaluation.

A straightforward approach is to directly use real-world APIs as tools, since they offer authenticity and reflect practical constraints.
However, they pose practical problems such as requirements for API keys, usage quotas, rate limits, and frequent interface changes or deprecations, all of which complicates scalable environment creation and compromise reproducibility
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
.
To work around these limitations, existing efforts focus on manually curating real APIs or building high-fidelity replicas
(
Xu et al. 2023
;
Tang et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
.
Yet, these hand-crafted collections are naturally limited in both the number of available tools and the diversity of covered domains (see Figure
1
).
For instance, ACEbench
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
includes only eight broad domains, and
\tau
-bench
(
Yao et al. 2024
)
covers just two.
This leads us to the central question for this paper:

How can we build a scalable, diverse tool ecosystem for agent training and evaluation?

In this work, we propose
SynthTools
, a scalable framework for generating, simulating, and auditing synthetic tools to create a controllable and diverse foundation for building tool-use agents. This framework allows researchers to systematically generate tasks that vary in difficulty and domain.
For example, to study an agent’s ability to identify relevant tools for a given task, one can include additional unnecessary but seemingly relevant tools as distractors (e.g.,
Fei et al. 2025
).
Alternatively, one can design tasks that require long sequences of tool calls to assess agents’ planning and compositional reasoning capabilities.
By providing flexible and precise control over task construction, synthetic tools enable targeted capability evaluation and rich post-training environments previously infeasible with real-world APIs.

The framework consists of three components:
(1)
Tool Generation
, which generates a wide variety of synthetic tools with diverse interfaces and functionalities (see Figure
3
);
(2)
Tool Simulation
, which emulates tool behaviors to closely mirror realistic interactions
; and (3)
Tool Audit
, which provides a scalable assessment of the reliability and consistency of generated tool responses to ensure tool simulation’s veracity.

Our tool generation module is
scalable
.
To demonstrate this capability, we show that the module can readily produce toolsets that exceed all prior works by more than
2
\times
in both the number of fields and the number of tools per field (see Figure
1
).
Importantly, these results represent only a fraction of the system’s capacity;
we conservatively estimate that it can generate at least an order of magnitude more in both dimensions.
Beyond scale, SynthTools covers richer domains and more complex tools.
For example, the framework allows us to construct toolsets in domains such as

or

, in stark contrast with narrower domains like

Related Work

Agent Tool Use Evaluation.

Recent benchmarks such as APIBench, API-Bank, ToolBench, Tool-Alpaca, and ToolQA evaluate LLM agents on their tool-use capabilities. These benchmarks involve both real-world APIs
(
Li et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
)
and carefully curated synthetic API simulators tailored to specific domains
(
Patil et al. 2024b
;
Tang et al. 2023
;
Zhuang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2023
;
Yao et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
;
Guo et al. 2025
)
. With the emergence of MCP servers, some recent studies have shifted focus toward evaluating agents using tools hosted on MCP servers
(
Mo et al. 2025
;
Yin et al. 2025
)
.
While these efforts provide valuable benchmarks for agent performance, they often face scalability challenges due to difficulty in manually curating accessible APIs, resulting in limited number and diversity of tools.
Moreover, both real APIs and MCP servers present practical obstacles such as access restrictions, rate limitations, and unstable interfaces.
In contrast, SynthTools offers scalable, diverse, and controllable testbed.

Post-training Agents for Tool Use.

Fine-tuning LLMs on curated tool-use datasets has shown promising results
(
Qin et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2025
;
Prabhakar et al. 2025
)
.
For instance, ToolLLaMA constructs a large-scale dataset from real tools available on RapidAPI Hub and fine-tunes models to perform tool-augmented tasks
(
Qin et al. 2023
)
.
Other key studies
(
Tang et al. 2023
;
Kimi 2025
;
Sullivan et al. 2025
)
demonstrate the potential of synthetic tool environments for fine-tuning. However, the dataset introduced by
Kimi 2025
is proprietary; the focus of
Sullivan et al. 2025
is on toolchains limited to predetermined types limited in generalizability; and
Tang et al. 2023
explore mainly the simulation of real APIs, but does not offer clear path to scaling number of tools available.
Building on these insights, we introduce an open-source framework for generating and simulating synthetic tools that are both diverse and easily composable.
Meanwhile, there has been a surge of frameworks that offer thoughtful abstractions for environments — such as ARE, OpenEnv, Verl, and SkyRL
(
Sheng et al. 2024
;
Andrews et al. 2025
;
Spisak et al. 2025
;
Griggs et al. 2025
)
— for training and evaluating LLM agents on tool-use settings.
However, these frameworks generally lack large-scale environments or tool ecosystems.
The scale and variety of tools and tasks provided by SynthTools nicely complement these efforts.

3
SynthTools Framework and Desiderata

Agentic systems rely heavily on the breadth and scale of the tools they can access.
However, curating diverse tools at scale remains a major challenge.
In this work, we explore a promising alternative that harnesses the generative capabilities of LLMs themselves to synthesize and simulate tools:
the SynthTools framework.
SynthTools integrates three core modules designed with specific desiderata to ensure scalability, realism, and reliability across the entire tool-generation process:

1.

The
Tool Generation
module requires
scalability
,
diversity
, and
realism
.
It must produce a wide variety of tool specifications that mirror the complexity of real-world interfaces to provide a rich foundation for developing tool-use agents (see Section
4
).
Furthermore, the tools must enable generation of tasks, environments where interacting with set of tools available is necessary for successes (see Section
7
).
Note that this addresses one of the key bottlenecks — the lack of scalable rich environments — in many concurrent frameworks for agent development
(
Sheng et al. 2024
;
Andrews et al. 2025
;
Griggs et al. 2025
;
Spisak et al. 2025
)
.

2.

The
Tool Simulation
module takes in the tool specifications generated prior, and simulates realistic tool responses when receiving certain calls.
The module must be
reliable
and
flexible
, ensuring that generated tools respond consistently while accommodating varied input–output behaviors specified by the framework user.
The latter is particularly important, since it supports broad experimental setups and thereby allows researchers great flexibility to generate various tasks (see Section
5
).
Note that prior works often lack this flexibility, whether due to the limited breath of real APIs considered, or to overly specific simulation design
(
Tang et al. 2023
;
Guo et al. 2025
;
Sullivan et al. 2025
)
.

3.

The
Tool Audit
module further ensures the veracity of the tool responses provided by the Tool Simulation module.
The module serves three purposes:
(1) it enables large-scale evaluation of simulation fidelity of the simulation module,
(2) filters out tools that exhibit incorrect or unstable responses due to complicate or ill-specified tool specifications, and
(3) provides a plug-and-play mechanism for retrying tool responses to further improve correctness of simulated tool responses.
Naturally, the Tool Audit module prioritizes
accuracy
and a low
false positive rate
(see Section
6
).

In the following sections, we describe in detail how each module is designed to achieve all of the desiderata listed above.

For tool generation, we wish to exploit the generative capabilities of LLMs to synthesize tools.
Yet, naively generating tools via LLMs often results in redundant, aimless, overly simplistic toolsets.
For example, directly prompting ChatGPT 5 Pro
(
OpenAI 2025
)
to generate tools typically yields trivial tools such as
robotics.create_task
or
docs.delete
, lacking the granularity illustrated in Figure
2
.
Evidently, such shallow and weakly related tools are ill-suited for constructing meaningful tasks with which to study agents.

To address the lack of tool
diversity
and
complexity
, we propose a scalable framework for synthesizing large and non-redundant toolsets grounded in meaningful tasks and workflows.
Our approach employs a hierarchical domain evolution procedure (Figure
3
) that systematically refines a broad domain into a concrete and coherent toolset. This process is anchored in practitioner workflows as simulated by LLMs. Starting from a general domain, we progressively decompose it into subdomains, then into task families, and finally into specific tools whose interfaces reflect domain-relevant constraints and interactions.
Concretely, the module proceeds as follows:

1.

Field
\rightarrow
Sub-domain.

Given a seed set of fields (e.g.,
healthcare
,
finance
,
materials science
), we prompt a large language model (LLM) to propose coherent subdomains that: (a) partition typical workflows; (b) surface stakeholders and entities operating in the field; and (c) admit meaningful, tool-addressable operations.

2.

Sub-domain
\rightarrow
Task.

For each subdomain, the model proposes a family of tasks.
Each task contains natural task statement suitable for documentation and test-case generation.

3.

Task
\rightarrow
Tool.

The model then instantiates each task with a set of concrete tools.
Tools are encouraged to be
composable
: each tool specifies upstream dependencies (what it consumes) and downstream affordances (what it produces), enabling creation of tasks that require consecutive use of tools.

We use targeted prompting at each stage to control diversity, complexity, and input/output (I/O) characteristics of the tools.
The module finally produce a set of
tools
, each characterized by: (i) a name and natural-language description; (ii) a parameter schema; and (iii) an I/O contract (preconditions, postconditions, and error modes). Formally, a tool is a tuple
(
name
,
description
,
parameters
,
usage
,
failure_modes
,
output_schema
).

Naturally, owing to the generative capabilities of LLMs, the Tool Generation module also demonstrates strong
scalability
.
As shown in Figure

, the module can generate a set of tools that spans
100+
distinct fields, where each field comprising at least
1,000
tools.
As alluded to earlier, a key concern is the
diversity
of such generated tools.
We demonstrate the desired richness of the generated tools alongside two dimensions:

•

Across fields:
To demonstrate the richness of our generated tools across fields, we create 50 tools for each of 100 different fields.

While the selected domains are not exhaustive, this experiment shows that our pipeline can produce a numerous and non-overlapping set of tools from various application areas.

A key question is whether increasing the number of tools results in genuinely novel tools or merely duplicates. To explore this, we focus on the e-commerce and retail domain and scale the tool count up to 1,000. As shown in Figure

We find approximately 9% of near-duplicate tools are filtered as a result, providing supporting evidence for the diversity of tools generated.

5
Tool Simulation

Once the tools are generated, the next step is to systematically emulate the API call behavior for each tool configuration (e.g. Figure
2
).
The key requirement for the tool simulator is
reliability
.
A reliable simulator must return appropriate error messages for incorrect or incomplete calls; and generate valid responses for correct ones conditioned on the
metadata
that encodes the current task state. For instance, a reliable flight booking tool should either return
‘‘no seat available’’
or issue a ticket ID depending on whether any seats matching the query (economy, business) remain available (the metadata). Achieving this behavior consistently in practice can be challenging.

To achieve reliability, we decompose the simulation procedure into two distinct stages:
parameter validation
and
response generation
. In both stages, we prompt a LLM with access to the tool configuration to emulate tool behavior.
The two stages are as follows:

1.

Parameter validation.

In this stage, the simulator emulates an API gateway by ensuring that all schema and structural constraints are satisfied. It begins by verifying the tool name, then checks for the presence of all required parameters, correct data types, mutual consistency, and any cross-field constraints. If any condition fails, the simulator returns a specific error message identifying the first issue encountered, along with the corresponding HTTP status code mirroring conventional API gateway behavior.

2.

Response generation for valid calls.
If parameter validation is successful, the simulator advances to the response generation stage.
A key design choice is to incorporate
metadata
associated with the tool call during response generation.
Depending on the relation between the metadata and tool call, the stage proceeds via either
data generation
or
information deduction
.
For tool calls that are valid but does not pertain to information specified in the metadata, the simulator produces realistic outputs that adhere to predefined schemas and domain-specific patterns.
In contrast, when the tool call demands reasoning over metadata, the simulator systematically cross-references the information to infer the current system state and logically derive the precise response the API would produce.

To ensure reliable simulator behavior, we refined its prompts through extensive manual testing, iteratively updating them based on observed failure cases until achieving desired performance (see Section
D.2
for the final version).
We then evaluated the finalized prompts using two complementary experiments: one through
manual inspection
of a moderate number of tool responses, and another via the
Tool Audit
module (Section
6
), which employs a language model as a judge to assess thousands of simulated tool responses.

For both experiments, we systematically examine each generated tool with a comprehensive suite of test calls spanning four distinct modes:

1.

Schema failures (basic parameter validation).
The tool call cannot be parsed or validated at the programmatic level due to missing required parameters, incorrect parameter types, or malformed inputs that prevent basic function invocation.

2.

Constraint failures (tool-specific validation errors).

The parameter schema is satisfied, but certain constraints are violated.
For example, mismatched array lengths, invalid value ranges, logical inconsistencies between parameters, or contradictions with the provided metadata, such as referencing non-existent user records or attempting operations on unavailable resources.

3.

Successful executions with new input.
The tool call is valid at all levels and the simulator should return responses.
However, the call does not match with any information provided in the metadata, and hence the tool must generate a new reasonable response.

4.

Successful executions with known input.
The tool call is valid and the simulator should return responses that are consistent with the tool specification and provided metadata, correctly performing operations or returning requested data.
Note that in this case, since we know the desired output exactly, whether the tool responses match the information given in the metadata can be
automatically
verified.

Manual Evaluation

This experiment involves two separate tool config sources.
(a) First, we tested the simualtor prompt on simulating the tools we generated via SynthTools.
(b) Then, to further highlight flexibility and reliability of the tool simulation module, we evaluate the simulator pipeline on ACEBench
(

Our tool simulator demonstrates high accuracy (
\geq

94%

For source (a), we found the Tool Simulation module achieves an accuracy of
\bm{97\%}

Among the responses, 200 originated from the first three modes, achieving a manually verified accuracy of 94%.
The remaining 498 responses belonged to the fourth mode, which was automatically evaluated and reached an accuracy of 98%.

For source (b), we aligned our simulator’s metadata to ACEBench specifications, ensuring consistent initial states.

94%

Tool Audit

As mentioned earlier, the
Tool Audit
module serves the purposes of (1) scaling the tool accuracy experiments (detailed in Section
5
),
(2) pruning ill-specified or difficult-to-simulate tools (see Figure
7
),
and (3) offering an independent module that can verify correctness of tool response on the fly and enable retries in case of failures.
For these purposes, it is critical that the Tool Audit module is
reliable
, identifying incorrect responses with
high accuracy
and
low false positive rate
.

To power the Tool Audit module, we employ a carefully engineered
LLM judge
.
The judge receives the tool specification, test call, and simulator response as input, then returns a structured judgment comprising correct/incorrect status, confidence score, and detailed rationale for correctness and compliance assessment.
Naturally, we expect this judge to have a easier job than generator due to the generator-verifier gap
(
Song et al. 2025
)
.
We iteratively refined the judge through testing with hand-crafted edge cases (e.g., subtle type violations, cross-field incompatibilities) until it reliably identified each error type with appropriate rationale.

Next, to rigorously assess the ability of the final LLM judge, we constructed a suite of stress tests focused on mismatch detection between tool calls and simulator responses. Each test case consists of a deliberately crafted (tool call, response) pair designed to probe whether the judge correctly flags inconsistencies between the simulator’s behavior and the tool call, or appropriately accepts valid simulator responses.
This setup allows us to systematically examine the (1) accuracy — the judge’s overall ability to make correct judgments, and (2) false positive rate — whether the judge refrains from incorrectly accepting invalid simulator responses.

Figure 7:

Our Tool Audit component uses the an LLM judge to filter the tools specifications that are prone to errors, after deduplication.

We group the test cases into six categories:

•

Failure mode 1: Incorrect call due to schema violations; simulator mishandles.

•

Failure mode 2: Tool call schema correct but violates documented constraints; simulator mishandles.

•

Failure mode 3: Fully correct call; simulator output wrong (e.g., Status code incorrect, response/output inconsistent with the specific tool call made).

•

Success mode 1: Incorrect call; simulator handles correctly.

•

Success mode 2: Correct call that triggers documented constraints / errors; simulator handles correctly.

•

Success mode 3: Fully correct call; simulator responds correctly.

We generated 2-3 (tool call, response) pairs for each failure mode and 1 pair for each success mode.
For these tests, we manually verified the results.
In addition, we leveraged the 498 mode-4 tool responses (see Section
5
) to further evaluate judge reliability, as the correctness of these responses is known automatically.

Across the 200 manual-inspected and 498 automatically-graded tests, the LLM-judge is correct for 680 cases, achieving an accuracy of
99%
.
Notably, we observed a false positive rate of
0%
— the judge successfully identified all incorrect simulator behaviors.
In the manually verified 200 stress test cases, the judge made only three errors, and in the latter set, six errors.
These strong results establish the robustness of our judge in diagnosing tool simulator performance and reinforce confidence in its role within our tool audit pipeline.

Generated Tools.

These steps result in a carefully curated collection of high-quality tools, suitable for downstream training and evaluation tasks.
We provide these tools in our public codebase.

Task Generation.

Our tool-generation module naturally produces inter-related tools within shared domains, making them particularly well-suited for downstream task construction.
To illustrate this, we create a few dozen multi-turn tasks from the tools by prompting an LLM.
A key component in this process is the creation of
metadata

For instance, a task that requires the agent to purchase an 8x H100 80GB SXM5 rack would be infeasible if the metadata indicates a bank balance of only
7

To ensure such consistency, we prompt the LLM to generate the metadata jointly with the task description.

8
Conclusion

We introduced SynthTools, a scalable framework that generates, simulates, and audits synthetic tools.
Our approach unifies hierarchical tool generation, realistic behavior simulation, and rigorous auditing.
Experiments show that the tool generation module can produce diverse and reliable toolsets at scale, while both the tool simulation and audit modules maintain high accuracy across domains.
We further demonstrated that complex tasks, challenging even for SOTA models, can be constructed from the tools generated by our pipeline.
By decoupling agent development from the constraints of real-world APIs, SynthTools provides a scalable path to curate a stable and extensible repository of tools, providing a crucial foundation development of tool-use agents.

Looking ahead, we envision extending the pipeline to allow automatic construction of increasingly difficult tasks.
Moreover, while SOTA models serve as accurate tool simulator and judge, they can be costly.
Collecting a large collection of demonstrations could enable smaller models to perform these roles efficiently, which would significantly reduce the cost of tool simulation and audit and further enhance the scalability of SynthTools.

Appendix B
Task Demonstrations

Here, we showcase the verisimilitude of the generated tasks = as well as their potential complexity for current agents in solving them.
We illustrate the behavior of current LLM agents with a simplified multi-turn verifiable reward environment, testing their ability to reach a carefully human crafted solution.

There are three parties in the interactions: the
agent
that tries to solve the task at hand, the
tool simulator
that simulates the exploratory tools needed by the agent using the information in the metadata and potentially altering the environment states, and the
verifier
that checks whether the solution corresponds to the ground truth.

The timeline is as follows:

1.

The agent is given a task description and a list of tools strictly needed for the task, with their MCP schemas.

2.

The agent runs exploratory tool calls and interacts with the tool simulator.

3.

The tool simulator simulates the tool calls using only the information in the metadata and alters the state of the environment during the process as needed.

4.

When confident enough, the agent outputs the complete sequence of tool calls needed to solve the task, with the respective parameters.

5.

The verifier checks correctness against human crafted solution to ensure validity.

6.

The interaction stops if the check is true, and if false, the model can keep exploring and try new attempts until the maximum number of turns is reached.

An attempt is considered correct based on the following principles. Two tool calls are considered equivalent if the tool name, after normalization, is the same and if the set of normalized arguments is identical. The use of optional parameters, when not explicitly stated in the task, is considered a mistake, as it introduces unnecessary deviations from the intended solution. Furthermore, all the tool calls necessary for solving the task must be present in the attempt, meaning that the human crafted solution must be fully contained within the agent’s sequence of tool calls.

Current AI agents naturally behave differently and achieve different results based on the task difficulty, simple tasks (up to 4 unique tools needed), medium tasks (up to 8 tools), and complex tasks (up to 12 tools). Performance is evaluated not only by whether the task is solved, but also by the number of attempts required. The ideal number of attempts equals the number of tool calls needed, plus one additional attempt to submit the solution. First, we observe that agents correctly respect tool schemas; it is extremely rare that they make a syntactically incorrect tool call. Errors can arise from supplying arguments that are not strictly required by the task, as well as from omitting necessary tool calls. Moreover, wrong exploratory tool call can easily cause the trajectory to diverge toward an incorrect intermediate state due to changes in the environment, which then must be corrected with additional tool calls increasing the interactions needed.
In
Easy tasks
the agent solves these in the ideal number of turns (Task Simple in Appendix
B
).
Regarding
Medium tasks
the agent reaches the correct solution after one extra solution attempt but with two extra interactions (Task Medium in Appendix
B
).
In
Complex tasks
we fully demonstrate the potential of task generation.
Task Complex is an example with 11 unique tools and 19 tool calls to solve the task. The agent was not able to solve such tasks within 30 turns; the provided solution was missing one tool call that was explicitly required in the task description. We expect to observe different results in the presence of semantically similar distractor tools. We also expect that an LLM judge can substitute for human labeling in the current setup.

Appendix E
Deduplication details

To eliminate near-duplicate tools generated through hierarchical evolution, we employ a multi-stage de-duplication pipeline. The process begins with exact de-duplication applied independently within each domain/field. We first normalize and compare the
tool_name
attributes, followed by the
tool_body
, which encompasses the description, parameters, usage, and output schema. This step reduces redundancy and prevents unnecessary computation in subsequent stages.

We then implement a semantic de-duplication pipeline, adapted from SemDepDup—a state-of-the-art method proposed by
Abbas et al. 2023
—tailored to the structural characteristics of our dataset. The semantic phase proceeds as follows:

We construct an embedding-based similarity graph over the normalized
tool_name
and
tool_body
fields. Specifically, let
e(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the embedding of tool
t
, and define the coine similarity between tool
t_{i}
and
t_{j}
as
S_{ij}=\cos\!\big(e(t_{i}),e(t_{j})\big)
. An adjancency matrix is then defined as

A_{ij}={\mathbf{1}}\{S_{ij}\geq\tau\},

\tau\in(0,1),

and the corresponding undirected similarity graph is

\mathcal{G}=\big\{(i,j):A_{ij}=1\big\}
. Connected components in
\mathcal{G}

represent candidate duplicate sets: singleton nodes are considered unique tools, whereas multi-node components are treated as clusters of near-duplicates. We then apply a selection algorithm (Algorithm
1
) to retain representative tools from each component.
