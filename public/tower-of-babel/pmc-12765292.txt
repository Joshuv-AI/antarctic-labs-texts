The role of TRIM proteins in the pathogenesis of mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Martina Di Rienzo, Candida Zuchegna, Valentina Perri, Mauro Piacentini, Laura Falasca, Alessandra Romagnoli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) is an intracellular pathogen responsible for tuberculosis (TB), a disease that remains the leading infectious cause of death worldwide. Several host cell proteins have been reported to play a significant role in regulating Mtb infection and replication. Among these, TRIM proteins are a large family of E3 ubiquitin ligases that have been described as being involved in host defence against Mtb, by controlling two important cellular processes: autophagy and the inflammatory response. Autophagy is a highly conserved cellular process deputed to the degradation of damaged or unnecessary components, such as organelles and protein aggregates. Autophagy is induced by a variety of stress stimuli, including nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, infection, oxidative stress and DNA damage, to maintain cellular homeostasis and survival. Autophagy plays a crucial role in host defense against infections by selectively targeting and delivering intracellular pathogens, like bacteria, viruses, and parasites to the lysosomes for degradation. Inflammation is a biological coordinated cellular process involving the secretion of cytokines and chemokines by innate immune cells, that can be triggered by various factors, including damaged cells, toxins and pathogens. Pathogen-Associated Molecular Patterns (PAMPs), found on microbes, are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on immune cells, which trigger signaling pathways leading to pro-inflammatory responses and the elimination of the pathogen. Danger-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs) are host-derived molecules released from damaged cells that activate the same PRRs to initiate or perpetuate inflammation. In this review, we discuss the emerging role played by TRIM proteins in regulating autophagy and pro-inflammatory response during Mtb infection, pointing out the crucial role of TRIM in the complex crosstalk between these processes.Clinical trial number Not applicable.

Introduction

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) is an intracellular pathogen responsible for tuberculosis (TB) and in 2023 remains the leading infectious cause of human death (10.8 million new cases of TB and 1.25 million deaths globally) [1]. Through inhalation of droplets containing Mtb, this pathogen infects the alveolar macrophages and translocates across the epithelium into the lung’s interstitial tissue, initiating the infection [2].

The outcome of the infection is determined by the interaction between Mtb and the innate immune system of the pulmonary alveoli, where macrophages, epithelial and dendritic cells, are essential in detecting pathogens and activating the immune response. The host response is crucial in determining whether the Mtb is eliminated, contained as latent infection, or causes active disease [2]. Lung resident alveolar macrophages (AMs) are the first cells to be infected with Mtb and play a cell-protective response, by ingesting the bacteria through phagocytosis. After engulfing the pathogen, AMs initiate an antimicrobial response by fusing the phagosomes, the membrane-bound vesicles containing the bacteria, with lysosomes. This fusion forms a phagolysosome, creating an acidic environment and delivering enzymes to degrade and destroy the bacteria. In parallel, dendritic cells enable the connection between the innate and adaptive immune responses to mycobacteria by processing and presenting antigens to T lymphocytes cells [3]. This process is crucial for controlling Mtb infection and initiating an effective adaptive immune response.

These immune cells express a variety of receptors, that play a central role in recognizing Mtb and facilitating its clearance through the stimulation of different cellular functions involved in the host-defence against pathogens, such as inflammation, phagocytosis, apoptosis and autophagy [4].

The TRIM family proteins

The TRIM (tripartite motif) family proteins are one of the largest classes of the Really Interesting New Gene (RING)-type E3 ubiquitin ligases consisting of more than 80 distinct members in the human genome, characterized by a common tripartite motif in their N-terminal region which is constituted by a RING finger domain, one or two B-Box-(B1 and B2) domains and Coiled-Coil region. TRIM proteins are divided into 11 sub-groups (from C-I to C-XI), based on their variable C-terminal domains, which may mediate substrate recognition and protein–protein interactions, allowing for high specificity when targeting substrates. The C-IV subgroup, containing the immunoglobulin-like PRY-SPRY domain, is the largest one with over 30 members [6].

TRIMs can catalyse the formation of different types of ubiquitin chains that may have either degradative or regulatory roles [7]. For instance, K48-linked chains signal for proteasomal degradation [8, 9], while K63-linked chains are involved in non-degradative pathways like signalling or DNA repair [10, 11]. Specific TRIMs have also been identified as E3 ligases for SUMO, another ubiquitin-like protein, suggesting a wider role in protein modification beyond ubiquitination [12]. Moreover, eight human TRIMs are RING-less, suggesting they function as scaffold proteins or carry out their functions in a ubiquitin independent manner [7].

TRIM proteins are involved in a wide range of cellular processes, including signal transduction pathways, transcriptional regulation, protein modification, cellular differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, autophagy, oncogenesis, and immunity [13, 14]. Many members of the TRIM family have emerged as important regulators of both viral and bacterial replication by regulating two important cellular processes, closely interconnected, that control immune responses such as autophagy and inflammatory response [15]. Notably, several TRIM proteins have been described to be involved in host defence against Mtb. This review explores the role of TRIM proteins in the regulation of Mtb infection and replication, mainly focusing on the involvement of inflammation and autophagy in the pathogenesis of TB.

Autophagy pathway

Autophagy is a lysosomal degradative intracellular process that allows the turnover of damaged organelles and aggregated proteins in both basal and stressful conditions. The cargos to be degraded are engulfed in double-membrane vesicles called autophagosomes, which then fuse with lysosomes for degradation [16, 17].

Autophagy process is negatively regulated by the serine/threonine kinase mTOR. Following autophagy initiation, mTOR is inhibited and the ULK complex (ULK1/2, ATG13, ATG101, and FIP200) is activated [18]. ULK1 stimulates the activity of the BECLIN-1 complex (BECLIN 1, AMBRA1, VPS34, VPS15, and ATG14L) phosphorylating BECLIN 1 and AMBRA1 proteins [19]. This protein complex is responsible for phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) production by the class III PI 3-kinase, VPS34. PI3P enables the formation of an isolation membrane, called the omegasome, which evolves into the phagophore [20]. ATG9 vesicles are considered to be the initial membrane platform for autophagosome formation. In particular, ATG9 binds to the ULK1 and BECN1 complexes and ATG2A protein. ATG2A transfers lipids from the ER to the growing phagophore where ATG9 scrambles them to redistribute lipids between the phagophore leaflets. WIPI-2 protein interacts with PI3P on the phagophore and ATG16L, enabling the recruitment of the ATG12–ATG5–ATG16L complex and LC3, which are required for phagophore elongation and the recruitment of material for degradation. LC3 interacts with several autophagy cargo receptors, (e.g. NBR1, NDP52, SQSTM1/p62 and Optineurin), which materially link the cargos to be degraded to the expanding autophagosome, therefore conferring specificity to autophagic degradation [21].

When the phagophore closes, it forms a double-membrane vesicle named autophagosome. Autophagosome maturation requires a second BECLIN-1 complex composed of BECLIN 1, UVRAG, VPS34, VPS15 and BIF-1. The proteins RUBICON and BCL-2 negatively regulate this complex. Lastly, the autophagosome moves along the cytoskeleton and fuses with the lysosome to form a large vesicle called autolysosome, in which the sequestered material will be degraded [19].

In addition to mediating unnecessary or damaged cell components degradation, autophagy has an important role in immune defense by selectively targeting and delivering intracellular pathogens, like bacteria, viruses, and parasites, to the lysosomes for degradation. Host selective autophagy of intracellular pathogens is known as xenophagy [22]. Several studies using human and mouse models have confirmed that autophagy plays a crucial role in restricting Mtb replication in macrophages [23, 24]. Ubiquitination of Mtb or its containing cellular compartments serves as a crucial signal for degradation of intracellular bacteria by autophagy, that are subsequently recognized by autophagy receptor proteins such as SQSTM1/p62, NDP52, NBR1 and Optineurin, thereby delivering the bacteria to the autophagosome for lysosomal degradation [25, 26]. So far, two E3 ubiquitin ligase enzymes, PARKIN and SMURF1, have been described to mediate Mtb ubiquitination for autophagy activation. PARKIN attaches K63-linked ubiquitin chains to Mtb-associated substrates, while SMURF1 uses K48-linked [25, 26]. Moreover, Mtb double strand DNA (dsDNA) generated from lysosomal degradation could further promote autophagy through activation of STING pathway [27]. However, Mtb has developed several strategies to evade the autophagic response in host cells. Indeed, Mtb can produce several effector molecules, which directly or indirectly target autophagy machinery. For example, the Mtb Eis N-acetyltransferase protein increases the acetylation levels of histone H3, stimulating IL-10 production and activating the AKT/mTOR pathway that inhibits autophagy and can also acetylate Lys-55 of the mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-16 (DUSP16/MKP-7), initiating JNK-dependent autophagy inhibition [28]. Moreover, Mtb-secreted acid phosphatase SapM interferes with the autophagosome-lysosome fusion process by targeting RAB7 protein. In addition, interfering with host microRNAs by modulating their expression is an alternative successful strategy adopted by Mtb to evade the autophagic pathway. Indeed, Mtb can modulate the expression of several cellular miRNAs, like miR-17, miR-125a, miR-33, miR-27a, miR-155, and mir144, resulting in autophagy inhibition by the direct repression of a broad range of crucial autophagy regulators [27].

Role of TRIM proteins in regulation of autophagy and in Mtb infections

Several members of TRIM family have been shown either stimulate or inhibit autophagy at different stages by acting as scaffold proteins, ubiquitinating targets, serving as cargo receptors for selective autophagy and modulating the activity of autophagy-related genes and signalling pathways [29].

Some TRIM proteins, like TRIM5α, 6, 16, 20, 21, 24, 32, 49, and 50, interacts with ULK1 and/or BECLIN 1 protein complexes, thereby promoting their activation in a ubiquitin dependent or independent manner [30–33]. TRIM proteins can act as autophagic receptors to recognize and deliver specific substrates, like pathogens or protein aggregates, to autophagosomes for degradation, contributing to selective autophagy (e.g. TRIM5α, TRIM14) [32, 34]. Moreover, several TRIMs influence the autophagy process by regulating AMPK and mTOR signalling pathways (TRIM28, TRIM37, and TRIM19) [29] and controlling the expression of genes involved in autophagy by modulating transcription factors, which can activate or suppress the overall autophagic process (e.g. TRIM22) [35]. Finally, a subgroup of TRIM proteins, such as TRIM17 or TRIM59, inhibits autophagy initiation by interacting with BECLIN 1 protein complex [15].

Specifically, TRIM16 associates with GALECTIN-3 and coordinates the recruitment of the autophagy regulators ULK1, BECLIN 1 and ATG16L1 upon damaged endomembranes. TRIM14 has been reported to localize with ubiquitin, the selective autophagy adapter proteins SQSTM1/p62 and TAX1BP1, and LC3 within the Mtb-containing phagosome to modulate bacterial survival [34].

Another TRIM implicated in the regulation of Mtb replication is TRIM22. TRIM22 has been reported to be induced and promote autophagy response during Mtb infection in THP-1 derived macrophage cells. In particular, TRIM22 activates autophagy by regulating NF-κB expression and its downstream gene BECLIN 1, in infected macrophages, although the molecular mechanism through TRIM22 modulates NF-κB is still unknown. Moreover, increased bacterial survival was observed in cells with reduced expression of TRIM22. Interestingly, this effect is abrogated by rapamycin, suggesting that TRIM22 restricts bacterial survival by enhancing autophagy process [35].

TRIM27 has been identified as a cellular protective factor against Mtb by promoting autophagy process in an E3 ligase independent manner. Specifically, nuclear TRIM27 acts as a transcriptional activator of TFEB gene expression during Mtb infection. Mechanistically, TRIM27 interacts with both TFEB transcription factor CREB1 and TFEB promoter, enhancing the binding of CREB1 to TFEB promoter, thereby promoting TFEB-mediated autophagy gene transcription [37].

Finally, TRIM32, a well-known regulator of autophagy process [31], positively regulates autophagy in THP-1 macrophages infected with Mtb promoting the recruitment of BECLIN 1 to the phagophore and the ubiquitination and sequestration of Mtb into the autophagosome [38].

Inflammatory pathways

Inflammation is a biological coordinated response of the innate immune cells involving the secretion of cytokines and chemokines to fight microbial infections [42]. However, prolonged activation of the inflammatory response can cause harmful effects by causing host tissue destruction and leading to severe and potentially fatal illnesses [42].

Immune cells like dendritic cells and macrophages, together with other innate immune cells like monocytes and neutrophils, express a variety of Pathogen Recognition Receptors (PRR) [43]. These receptors are crucial for the innate immune system’s ability to detect threats, and their signaling pathways are essential for establishing early inflammatory responses, which help contain infections.

PRR are protein sensors on and inside cells that recognize specific molecular patterns associated with pathogens (PAMPs) or signals from damaged host cells (DAMPs) that trigger a cascade of events, primarily involving transcription factor activation and consequently Interferons (IFNs) and pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines [43].

Mammalian cells express five PRRs families, including toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), RIG-I-like receptors, C-type lectin-like receptors and cytosolic DNA sensors. Different families of PRR have distinct structural features and recognize different PAMPs and DAMPs, but they often share common downstream signalling pathways [43].

PAMPs recognized by TLRs include proteins, lipids, lipoproteins and nucleic acids derived from a large range of microbes like viruses, bacteria, fungi and parasites. The recognition of PAMPs by TLRs initiates a signalling cascade involving adaptor proteins like TRIF and MyD88, which in turn lead to the phosphorylation and the activation of transcription factors such as AP-1, NF-κB, IRF3/IRF7 which then induce inflammatory cytokines and interferons production to fight infections [44].

Cytoplasmic PAMPs are recognized by PRRs from the RLR and NLR receptor families, as well as dsDNA sensors. RLR receptors (MDA5, LGP2 and RIG-I) are RNA helicases that sense viral RNA and activate the signalling pathway through the adaptor protein MAVS [45, 46]. NLR receptors are a large family of intracellular PRRs that detect a wide range of stimuli [47]. Among the NLRs, Nod1 and Nod2 receptors detect bacterial cell wall degradation products, while NLRP3 receptor (NALP3) responds to a variety of stimuli to initiate the formation of the inflammasome complex [44]. The NLRP3 inflammasome-activated response results in caspase-1 activation, which in turn leads to the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 pro-inflammatory cytokines [48]. Moreover, host cells respond to the presence of pathogen dsDNA and not completely digested cellular dsDNA and activate signalling pathways regulated by the proteins STING and TBK1 [49, 50]. Recognition of cytoplasmic PAMPs by different receptors can ultimately lead to activation of IRF3/IRF7 and NF-κB transcription factors, through the involvement of multiple molecular adaptors [51].

The cytosolic RNA- and DNA-sensing pathways are crucial parts of the immune response to Mtb, with growing evidence showing an intricate crosstalk between different sensors and regulators [52]. Mtb employs diverse strategies to evade the host’s cytosolic nucleic acid-sensing pathways by manipulating its molecular regulatory machinery, to facilitate its survival and growth. Emerging evidence from animal models suggests that Mtb infection induces type I interferon production through the activation of cGAS and RLR pathways, which in turn impair host resistance and promote Mtb bacterial growth [52]. In contrast, activation of other sensors, like NLRP3, AIM2 and NOD2, can stimulate the production of host protective inflammatory cytokines [27].

Role of TRIM proteins in inflammatory pathways and in Mtb infections

Many studies have described a crucial role of TRIM proteins in the regulation of different components of inflammatory process and PRR signalling pathway [6, 53, 54]. In particular, TRIM56 and TRIM38 regulate TLR4 and TLR3 mediated type I interferon production. TRIM19, 21, 26 and 28 are required for the regulation of IRF3/IRF7 transcription factors. A large set of TRIM proteins (TRIM6, 13, 14, 19, 20, 21, 23, 26, 27 and 29) participates in the regulation of the IKK protein complex, while TRAF6/TAK1 protein complex is regulated by TRIM5, 8, 12c, 30a and 38. Furthermore, the RNA protein sensors RIG-I and MD5A have been reported to be regulated by several TRIM including TRIM4, 13, 14, 25 and 65, while MAVS is regulated by TRIM25 and 44. Finally, TRIM14, 21, 32, 38, 56 and 30 are involved in the regulation of cGAS/STING DNA protein sensors. TRIM11 and TRIM68 have been described to negatively regulate TBK1 and the activator of NF-ĸB TRK fused gene (TFG) [53].

TRIM14 was reported to inhibit type I IFN response during macrophage infection with Mtb. In particular, TRIM14 interacts with the cytosolic DNA protein sensors cGAS and TBK1, acting as a scaffold protein to promote STAT3 phosphorylation at Ser727 and inhibit the expression of IFN stimulated gene (ISG) [34]. Moreover, a remarkable inhibition of Mtb replication in macrophages knocked out for TRIM14 has been observed by overexpressing inducible NO synthase (iNOS) [34].

TRIM25 is an RNA-binding E3 ubiquitin ligase with a PRY-SPRY N-terminal domain which plays a crucial role in the regulation of innate immunity [15]. TRIM25 has been reported to be significantly overexpressed in PBMCs of patients with tuberculosis compared with healthy controls [39]. Moreover, it has been reported that TRIM25 expression is enhanced in RAW264.7 macrophage cells following Mtb infection, promoting Mtb survival by stimulating P-p38 MAPK and inhibiting P-p65 NF-ĸB signalling pathway [39].

TRIM27 suppresses Mtb survival in macrophages by promoting host inflammatory responses through the activation of JNK/p38 MAPK signalling as well as cell apoptosis. In addition, PtpA, a secreted protein tyrosine phosphatase, that is essential for intracellular Mtb survival, can counteract the ability of TRIM27 to regulate JNK/p38 MAPK signalling and apoptosis interfering with the binding to the TRIM27 RING domain, suggesting that TRIM27 acts in an E3 ubiquitin ligase activity-dependent manner [40].

Several TRIM genes, that are involved in the regulation of innate immune signalling (TRIM4, 16, 27, 32, 35, 46, 47, 65 and 68) have been found to be significantly decreased in patients with active TB when compared to individuals with latent TB infection (LTBI) and healthy controls. Furthermore, in vitro experiments suggest that the expression of these TRIMs inversely correlates with bacterial load and cytokine production in macrophages infected with Mycobacterium smegmatis (a less virulent mycobacterial organism) [55].

In addition, TRIM68, a negative regulator of type I Interferon production, was identified as a biomarker correlated with the association between TB and several diseases, including COVID-19 and lung adenocarcinoma, in a bioinformatics analysis of gene expression [56].

Conclusion

It is now well known that the autophagy process is deeply connected with inflammatory and immune responses. Autophagy controls inflammation by targeting and degrading intracellular pathogens, removing inflammatory molecules and inflammasome components and regulating pro-inflammatory cytokine production and secretion [57]. Conversely, inflammatory cytokines and immune cells can influence autophagy activity [58]. This crosstalk is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and ensuring effective immune surveillance and defence against pathogens. Therefore, understanding this complex interplay between autophagy and inflammatory response is of fundamental importance to develop potential therapeutic strategies, such as using autophagy modulators (inducers or inhibitors) to fight infections.

Both autophagy and inflammatory cytokines are involved in the host defence against Mtb, but the molecular mechanisms involved in their regulation of this process during Mtb infection are still largely unknown. In this scenario, TRIM family proteins have come out as crucial regulators of both autophagy and inflammation process [58]. Therefore, elucidating how TRIM proteins specifically target inflammatory regulators for degradation via the autophagy pathway during Mtb infection or how TRIM proteins regulate autophagy in modulating cytokine production is essential for developing successful therapeutic strategies and vaccines.

Recently, TRIM proteins have gained increasing consideration as targets for drug discovery [59, 60]. However, since proteins of this family can modulate the stability and function of a variety of biological regulators, blocking or promoting just one function or domain may not be helpful in obtaining the requested therapeutic results.

Therefore, manipulating TRIMs activity requires a comprehensive understanding of their mechanism of action in different cellular contexts, by the analysis of their multidomain nature and a complete characterization of their structural features, including TRIM self-interactions and oligomerization, and active conformations of the catalytic domain, to identify possible druggable structural motifs [61].

Indeed, potential therapeutic options targeting TRIMs activity should not be restricted to interfering with the RING catalytic domain and their E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, but also include other strategies, such as, disrupting b-box/coiled-coil-mediated dimerization or targeting the variable C-terminal domains.

For example, the NHL repeat domains that characterize the class VII of TRIM proteins, can be exploited for drug design due to their structural features, specifically their conserved β-propeller tertiary conformation, since effective inhibitors for this specific structural fold have been discovered in other proteins such as for the Keap1 protein [83].

In this context, in silico modeling studies and compound library screening represent an essential tool to identify potential drug candidates able to modulate TRIM activity, as in the case of the compounds found targeting the NHL domains of TRIM71 [84, 85] or the RNA aptamers that specifically prevent the binding between Influenza NS1 and TRIM25 [86].

Remarkably, TRIM proteins could be valid targets for the development of novel therapeutic approaches based on targeted protein degradation (TPD) [87]. TPD is a promising strategy that engages the cell’s own ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) machinery, which has gained great attention for treating infectious diseases with the aim to degrade host proteins that are essential for the pathogen’s life cycle or replication [59, 60].

In conclusion, further research on TRIMs mechanism of action during Mtb infection is critically needed to elucidate the complex balance between autophagic processes and inflammatory response as well as the underlying molecular machinery providing insights into the use of TRIMs as therapeutic targets for disease treatment.
