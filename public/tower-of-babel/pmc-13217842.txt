The KDM-family inhibitor JIB-04 sensitizes AML cells to venetoclax by inducing a ferroptosis-like phenotype
Authors: Katharina Wohlan, Dandan Fan, Anna G Guzman, Alexis Quezada, Hyunji Park, Elmira Khabusheva, Farzane Sivandzade, Matthew Wither, Amber Alcorn, Tamara Thevarajah, Steven M Kornblau, Courtney D DiNardo, Jianzhong Su, Margaret A Goodell, Rachel E Rau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Inhibition of histone lysine–specific demethylases by JIB-04 induces ferroptosis-like phenotype in AML due to increased oxidative stress.•JIB-04 synergizes with venetoclax to eliminate AML cells.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a heterogeneous hematological malignancy that increases in frequency with aging. Approximately 60% of newly diagnosed patients in the United States are aged ≥65 years, and the 5-year survival rate for this age group is ∼20%.1 Standard induction chemotherapy usually includes high-dose cytarabine combined with an anthracycline (7+3), but older patients are often unfit for this intense therapy due to comorbidities, poor performance status, and unfavorable disease characteristics.2, 3, 4 For patients unfit for standard therapy treatment, options are limited.

The combination of the B-cell leukemia/lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) inhibitor venetoclax in combination with hypomethylating agents (HMAs) azacitidine or decitabine5 or low-dose cytarabine (LDAC)6 have shown promising results in clinical trials compared to HMA and LDAC alone and have been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of patients with AML unfit for standard therapy. However, a significant patient fraction does not respond to venetoclax-containing regimens, and nearly all responding patient will ultimately relapse.7 Upregulation of the antiapoptotic BCL-2 family members myeloid cell leukemia-1 (MCL-1) and B-cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-XL) as well as metabolic shifts have been reported as possible resistance mechanisms to venetoclax.8, 9, 10 Thus, other drug combinations with venetoclax need to be investigated to increase sensitivity and circumvent resistance.

In recent years, histone lysine–specific demethylases (KDMs) have been of interest as new options for targeted therapy in AML because they are generally overexpressed in different subtypes.11,12 In particular, the histone lysine–specific demethylase 1 (LSD1; also known as KDM1A) has been well studied in AML, and different LSD1 inhibitors have been explored as single agents or in combination with other drugs in clinical trials.13 Cai et al recently reported a beneficial effect of LSD1 inhibition in combination with venetoclax in AML treatment,14 and the first clinical trials are ongoing or recently terminated (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT05597306 and NCT04748848). In this study, we sought to explore therapeutic inhibition of other KDM family members alone and in combination with venetoclax in AML. The small molecule JIB-04 is a potent inhibitor of KDM4, KDM5, and KDM6 family members.15 Pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of these KDM family members individually have been shown to reduce proliferation and induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in AML.12,16,17 By targeting multiple KDMs and thus different histone methylation marks, we hypothesized that JIB-04 would efficiently target AML cells and synergize with venetoclax in eliminating AML cells. Here, we show that JIB-04 inhibits proliferation and induces cell death in AML cell lines and that the combination with venetoclax results in a superior drug response compared to single-agent treatments in AML cell lines and primary AML cells. Transcriptome and histone methylation data suggest JIB-04 treatment induces a ferroptosis-like phenotype, the likely mechanism causing cell death. Induction of this ferroptosis-like state induced by increased oxidative stress by JIB-04 seems to be crucial for sensitizing AML cells to venetoclax. These preclinical data suggest the treatment with JIB-04 and venetoclax is an effective antileukemic drug combination that has the potential to overcome venetoclax resistance in AML cells.

Methods

Cell culture and patient samples

The human AML cell line OCI-AML3 was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Kasumi-1, HL-60, and MOLM-13 cell lines were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and passaged every 3 to 4 days.

Peripheral blood or bone marrow (BM) mononuclear cells (MNC) were obtained from 9 patients with AML and 8 healthy controls. Samples from patients with AML were provided by the Leukemia Sample Bank at the MD Anderson Cancer Center (Houston, TX). Healthy BM samples were received from the Cell and Tissue Processing Laboratory at BCM and the BM transplant unit at Texas Children’s Hospital (Houston, TX). Cord blood cells were provided by the MD Anderson Cancer Center Cord Blood bank. AML MNC and CD34+ MNC were cultured in StemSpan SFEM II (Stemcell Technologies) supplemented with 10 ng/mL human Fms-related tyrosine kinase 3 (hFLT3), 10 ng/mL human thyroperoxidase (hTPO), and 10 ng/mL human stem cell factor (hSCF) for up to 72 hours after isolation.

Isolation of CD34+ stem and progenitor cells

MNC from healthy BM or cord blood were isolated using Ficoll according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were selected for human CD34 using CD34-conjugated microbeads and QuadroMacs columns (both Miltenyi) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Drug sensitivity assay

AML cell lines were treated with either dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or increasing concentrations of JIB-04 (Sigma) for 72 hours. Drug response was measured using the MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide) cell proliferation kit I (Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The combination index (CI) was calculated by median effect analysis using the method of Chou-Talalay,18 using the CompuSyn software (ComboSyn Inc, Paramus, NJ). The CI reflects synergism (CI < 1), additive effects (C = 1), or antagonism (CI > 1).

Cell differentiation

AML cell lines were treated with DMSO or JIB-04 for 72 hours. To monitor the differentiation status, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stained with CD11b (BD) for 30 minutes at 4°C. Cells were washed with PBS and recorded using flow cytometry (LSRII; Becton Dickinson) and analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Apoptosis assay

Flow cytometric detection of annexin V binding and propidium iodide (PI) was used to identify early and late apoptotic cells. AML cell lines were plated in a 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and treated with either DMSO, JIB-04, venetoclax, or the combination of JIB-04 and venetoclax at increasing concentrations for 72 hours. The antioxidant N-acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC; Fisher Scientific) was used to block JIB-04–induced cell death. Cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and stained with 5μL Annexin V-Allophycocyanin (APC) (BD Bioscience) and 5 μg/mL PI (Sigma) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Cells per sample were recorded using flow cytometry (LSRII, Becton Dickinson) and analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Patient samples were treated with DMSO, 100nM JIB-04, 100nM venetoclax, or the combination of both drugs. After 72 hours, cells were washed with PBS, stained with Annexin V-APC and PI, and analyzed using the LSRII flow cytometer.

Ferroptosis assays

AML cells were treated with JIB-04, the antioxidant NAC, the pan caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (MedChemExpress), or indicated combinations for up to 48 hours. Cells were stained with 2.5μM MitoSOX Red, 1μM BODYPI 581/591 C11 (both Thermo Fisher), 2μM MitoPerOx (MedChemExpress), or 2.5μM BioTracker FerroOrange (Sigma) in HBSS for 30 minutes at 37°C. Cells were washed twice with Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) before measuring the mean fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry (LSRII, Becton Dickinson) and analyzed using the FlowJo software. DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, dihydrochloride; Invitrogen) was used to exclude dead cells.

Animal studies

For our orthotopic tumor model, we injected OCI-AML3 cells into immunodeficient NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG; The Jackson Laboratory) pretreated with the indicated inhibitors. Briefly, OCI-AML3 cells were treated with DMSO, 40nM JIB-04, 1μM venetoclax, or the combination of both drugs for 24 hours. Eight-week-old female NSG mice were sublethally irradiated (2.5 Gy) and injected IV with 2 × 106 viable cells from each treatment group. Mice were constantly monitored for signs of disease, and humane euthanasia was performed when animals had signs of severe disease. All mice were housed in a pathogen-free animal facility. All animal procedures and studies were done in accordance with the institutional animal care and use committee.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The number of experimental replicates (n) are specified in the figure legends. Two-tailed independent Student t test and analysis of variance tests were used to determine significance between 2 groups. Survival analyses were assessed by Kaplan-Meier plots and log-rank tests. Results were considered statistically significant at the following values: ∗P < .05; ∗∗P < .01; ∗∗∗P < .001; ∗∗∗∗P < .0001.

Detailed methods are provided in the supplemental Methods.

All human patient samples were obtained through institutional review board–approved protocols after informed written consent and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, before any study procedure.

Results

JIB-04 inhibits cell proliferation and induces cell death in AML cell lines

To evaluate the response of AML cells to different pan-KDM inhibitors, we treated the AML cell lines OCI-AML3 and MOLM-13 with increasing concentrations of CPI-455, GSK-J4, and JIB-04 and evaluated the drug response by annexin V/PI staining after 72 hours (Figure 1A). These 3 commercially available small molecule inhibitors have been reported to inhibit multiple KDMs.15,19,20 Both OCI-AML3 and MOLM-13 cells showed an increase in annexin V–positive/PI-positive cells at low nanomolar concentrations of JIB-04, whereas high concentrations of GSK-J4 were needed to achieve a similar response. No response to CPI-455 treatment was observed in either cell line. Because JIB-04 generated the most striking response in both AML cell lines, we decided to study the effects of this KDM inhibitor in more depth in multiple AML cell lines. To determine the 50% inhibitory (or inhibition or infective) concentration (IC50) in OCI-AML3, HL-60, Kasumi-1, and MOLM-13 cells, we treated them with increasing concentrations of JIB-04 and measured the number of viable cells after 72 hours using the MTT assay (Figure 1B). JIB-04 reduced the number of viable cells for all AML cell lines at low nanomolar concentrations, with IC50 of 20nM, 8.5nM, 14nM, and 30nM, respectively. To determine what caused the decreased cell number, we stained the cells with annexin V/PI after 72 hours of JIB-04 treatment and observed a dose-dependent induction of cell death across all cell lines examined (Figure 1C). However, higher concentrations of JIB-04 were required to induce cell death than to inhibit cell proliferation. Thus, we examined the cell surface expression of CD11b by flow cytometry to measure a possible differentiation induction by JIB-04 (Figure 1D). A modest increase in CD11b expression was noted after JIB-04 treatment, suggesting terminal cell differentiation is only minimally induced in JIB-04–treated AML cells. To assess the effect of JIB-04 on the self-renewal capacity of healthy human CD34+ MNC, we isolated CD34+ cells from cord blood and plated them up to 3 times in a colony-forming/replating assay (supplemental Figure 1A-B). The CD34+ cells were treated with DMSO or 100nM JIB-04 in the secondary plating. For tertiary plating, the harvested JIB-04–treated CD34+ cells were either treated with JIB-04 (continuous) or DMSO (JIB-04 washout). Although continuous JIB-04 reduced tertiary replating by ∼50%, washout of JIB-04 resulted similar replating capacity as the continuous DMSO control group, indicating immediate recovery of healthy CD34+ cells after removal of JIB-04. Taken together, we demonstrate that AML cell lines show a high sensitivity to JIB-04 and that JIB-04 suppresses cell proliferation and induces cell death. In contrast, JIB-04 inhibits the replating capacity of healthy CD34+ MNC with prolonged exposure; however, with intermittent treatment, cells rebound once JIB-04 is removed, indicating that short courses of JIB-04 are well tolerated.

The combination of JIB-04 and venetoclax is highly synergistic in AML cells

To determine the efficacy of the JIB-04 and venetoclax combination treatment in AML, we treated a panel of AML cell lines with increasing doses of JIB-04 and venetoclax alone and in combination (Figure 2A-B). Venetoclax sensitivity was determined by MTT assay (supplemental Figure 2A-B). AML cell lines with an IC50 < 1μM were labeled as venetoclax sensitive, and AML cell lines with an IC50 ≥ 1μM were considered venetoclax resistant. The single-drug treatments resulted in a moderate response in all AML cell lines. HL-60 and MOLM-13 cells, which are sensitive to low concentrations of venetoclax, showed strong synergism with the drug combination treatment. Strikingly, the combination of JIB-04 and venetoclax resulted in synergistic killing of both OCI-AML3 and Kasumi-1 cells, which have been shown to be highly resistant to venetoclax treatment. To further evaluate possible clinical implications of this drug combination, we treated healthy CD34+ MNC and primary patient AML cells with JIB-04, venetoclax, or the combination of both drugs (Figure 2C). We noted a trend toward improved response with the drug combination compared to either JIB-04 or venetoclax alone in primary AML cells, although the analysis was limited by small sample size. Importantly, with the same doses and duration of therapy, we observed no induction of cell death in the healthy CD34+ MNC, suggesting a therapeutic window for this combinatorial approach. Next, we hypothesized that the antileukemic effect of the JIB-04 and venetoclax combination will reduce AML cell engraftment in vivo. To evaluate the efficacy of our drug combination in vivo, we treated OCI-AML3 cells with either DMSO, JIB-04, venetoclax, or the combination of both drugs for 24 hours before transplanting them via tail vein injection into NSG mice (Figure 2D). Mice inoculated with a normalized number of viable OCI-AML3 cells treated with JIB-04 and venetoclax had a significant longer mean survival time than the other treatment groups. Collectively, we demonstrated that JIB-04 sensitizes AML cells to venetoclax and that the drug combination treatment is highly synergistic in AML cells.

The KDM inhibitor JIB-04 leads to increased H3K36me3 and upregulation of ferroptosis inducers

Because JIB-04 inhibits multiple members of the KDM4, KDM5, and KDM6 families, we investigated changes in histone methylation marks after JIB-04 treatment and attendant changes in the transcriptome. OCI-AML3 cells were treated with JIB-04 for 24 hours and 48 hours and analyzed for changes in H3 methylation and gene expression by chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing and RNA sequencing, respectively. Overall, we did not observe changes in global methylation in any of the methylation marks that could be affected by JIB-04 treatment (supplemental Figure 3A). At the annotated gene level, a reliable signal change was detected only for H3K36me3 (supplemental Figure 3B), and we observed a significant increase in H3K36me3 after 24 to 48 hours of JIB-04 treatment at gene loci of known ferroptosis inducers such as HMOX1, SAT1, and PTGS2 (Figure 3A). In alignment, increased gene expression could be observed for those 3 genes (Figure 3B). Interestingly, the expression of HMOX1 mRNA increased already after 24 hours even when no changes in H3K36me3 could be observed at this point (Figure 3C), indicating additional mechanisms affecting HMOX1 gene expression after JIB-04 treatment. Generally, increased HMOX1 and PTGS2 gene expression occurred in all AML cell lines investigated, whereas SAT1 expression differed (Figure 3C-E; supplemental Figure 3C-E). However, upregulation of protein expression was consistently observed for HMOX1 but not SAT1 and PTGS2 (Figure 3F). Upregulated HMOX1 expression has been reported under oxidative stress through the E2F2/E2F8-CDCA2-BRCA1-NRF2 signaling cascade.21 We observed upregulation of proteins in that pathway in JIB-04–treated AML cells (Figure 3G), indicating an additional regulatory mechanism for increased HMOX1 expression besides H3K36me3. Gene ontology analysis revealed upregulation of the stress response mechanism, response to reactive oxygen species (ROS), and positive regulation of programmed cell death (Figure 3H; supplemental Figure 3F). In contrast, we identified genes involved in amino acid metabolism, amino acid transport, and ferroptosis suppressors to be downregulated upon JIB-04 treatment (Figure 3I; supplemental Figure 3G-H). Among these genes, we found the known ferroptosis suppressor SLC7A11 and confirmed that SLC7A11 protein expression was indeed downregulated after 48 hours in AML cell lines (supplemental Figure 3I). Overall, our results indicate that exposure to JIB-04 results in ferroptotic cell death in AML cells.

JIB-04 treatment causes cell death by ferroptosis-like mechanism in AML cells

To confirm ferroptosis as the cause of cell death induced by JIB-04 treatment in AML, we orthogonally assessed for JIB-04–induced ferroptosis in multiple AML cell lines using flow cytometry assays. Common indicators of ferroptosis are accumulation of ROS, lipid peroxidation, and increase of ferrous iron. Indeed, we detected an increase in mitochondrial ROS and peroxidation of mitochondrial lipids in multiple AML cells as soon as 24 hours after JIB-04 treatment (Figure 4A-B; supplemental Figure 4A-B). Accumulation of ferrous iron was observed after 48 hours of treatment, accompanied by signs of lipid peroxidation in all AML cell lines (Figure 4C-D; supplemental Figure 4C-D). To investigate the role of ferroptosis in JIB-04–induced cell death in primary AML cells, we treated the cells for 48 hours with JIB-04 and assessed gene expression levels for the identified ferroptosis inducers. In all investigated primary AML samples (AML1-4), the significant upregulation of HMOX1, SAT1, and PTGS2 can be observed after JIB-04 treatment (Figure 4E-G). In accordance with the increased gene expression, features of ferroptosis, including accumulation of ROS and increased lipid peroxidation, were triggered by JIB-04 exposure (Figure 4H-J). Interestingly, the strong response of the AML1 patient sample observed at 48 hours, especially for ROS accumulation, correlated with a 8× to 14× increase in gene expression of all 3 genes. Taken together, the presence of increased ROS, lipid peroxidation, and an increased labile iron pool, as well as an increased expression of ferroptosis-inducing genes, revealed ferroptosis to likely be the cause of JIB-04–induced cell death in AML cells.

Induction of ferroptosis-like phenotype by JIB-04 drives synergism with venetoclax

The accumulation of mitochondrial ROS is an important driver of ferroptosis22 and can be observed within 24 hours of JIB-04 treatment in AML cells. To further support ferroptosis as the likely main mechanism induced by JIB-04, we assessed the impact of the antioxidant NAC to protect AML cells from ferroptosis induction by JIB-04. Indeed, cotreatment with NAC prevents the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS and lipid peroxidation in AML cell lines, indicating the early increase in mitochondrial ROS to be essential for JIB-04–promoted ferroptosis (Figure 5A-C). In contrast, the pan caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK failed to suppress the cytotoxic activity of JIB-04, because ferroptosis is not driven by caspase activity.22 Accordingly, the suppression of ROS accumulation by the antioxidant NAC blocks the upregulation of ferroptosis inducer HMOX1 (Figure 5D). To confirm that ferroptosis induction drives the synergy between JIB-04 and venetoclax, we treated OCI-AML3 and MOLM-13 cells with both inhibitors and simultaneously added NAC to block ferroptosis (Figure 5E-F). Strikingly, NAC intervention eliminated the synergistic effect of the JIB-04 and venetoclax combination treatment. Collectively, these data strongly suggest that the antileukemic effect of JIB-04 is driven by ferroptosis and ferroptosis onset is responsible for the synergism between JIB-04 and venetoclax.

Discussion

The identification of new vulnerabilities in AML has been a major focus in the field for years due to limited treatment options, especially for older adult patients, and the occurrence of resistance mechanisms. The BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of older adult patients with AML unfit for conventional chemotherapy, in combination with HMAs or LDAC. Additional trials have been conducted with other therapeutic agents, showing combination therapies with venetoclax to be superior for the overall response rate in patients with AML.23, 24, 25 Unfortunately, nearly 30% of patients with AML fail to respond or relapse eventually,26 suggesting the emergence of resistance mechanisms. Our study showed that inhibition of KDMs by JIB-04 potently synergizes with venetoclax. Thus, the combination of these 2 drugs represents a reasonable new strategy for AML treatment.

KDMs are generally upregulated in AML cells compared to normal hematopoietic cells, indicating potential for therapeutic intervention. We explored the efficacy of different KDM inhibitors and found the highest sensitivity to the pan-selective KDM inhibitor JIB-04. The cytotoxic effect of JIB-04 resulted in proliferation inhibition and cell death in multiple subtypes of AML, indicating its clinical potential. More importantly, our data show strong synergism between JIB-04 and venetoclax in AML cell lines and the combination is also superior to single treatments in primary AML cells. In addition, cytotoxicity in healthy hematopoietic cells is a huge concern in treating patients, and our data suggest a higher toxicity threshold for hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells than for AML blasts.

We investigated the underlying mechanism driving JIB-04 cytotoxicity and found indications for ROS-related stress responses and ferroptosis. Although Wang et al reported the potential of JIB-04 to inhibit multiple KDMs,15 we only found increase in H3K36me3, indicating inhibition of KDM4A or KDM4C are likely to be the main targets of JIB-04 in AML cells. We found increase of H3K36me3 at loci of the ferroptosis inducers HMOX1, SAT1, and PTGS2.27, 28, 29 However, only HMOX1 gene and protein expression was consistently up in all AML cell lines and primary cells tested. HMOX1 upregulation might be sufficient for ferroptosis induction, as has been reported.27,30 However, other data suggest HMOX1 could be cytoprotective,31 thus more studies are needed to investigate the role of HMOX1 in ferroptosis in AML. Our data also indicate that ferroptosis-protective genes such as SLC7A11, ASNS, or ASS132, 33, 34 fail to be upregulated or are even downregulated upon JIB-04 treatment, weakening the defense mechanism of the cells. Overall, we observed a different mechanism of action in AML from what has been reported for some solid cancers, in which JIB-04 treatment resulted in cell cycle arrest, regulation of tumor suppressors and oncogenes, and apoptosis.35, 36, 37

In recent years, various substances have been found to induce ferroptosis in AML cells,38, 39, 40, 41, 42 indicating that AML cells are susceptible to ferroptotic cell death. AML cells rely mainly on mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation to drive leukemogenesis,43,44 a feature that is also implicated in chemoresistance in AML.45,46 Studies show that ROS level are elevated in AML cells47 and mutations in Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) receptor, isocitrate dehydrogenase genes (IDH1 and IDH2), or Ras GTPase have been implicated in driving increased ROS production.48, 49, 50, 51 Additionally, AML cells have an abnormal lipid metabolism, resulting in an increase in polyunsaturated fatty acid.52 Polyunsaturated fatty acids are important components of cell membranes, and their oxygenation has been directly linked to ferroptosis.53,54 Lastly, due to their highly proliferative nature, AML cells rely on an increased iron supply, indicated by upregulation of transferrin receptor 1.55 Taken together, all these features drive leukemogenesis in AML but also makes AML cells more susceptible to ferroptosis induction. In line with previous reports, increased level of mitochondrial ROS, lipid peroxidation, and an increased labile iron pool were detectable upon treatment with JIB-04, supporting ferroptosis as a potential targetable vulnerability of AML cells.

Our findings indicate that early upregulation of ROS after JIB-04 treatment seems to be essential to drive the ferroptosis-like phenotype. Treatment with the antioxidant NAC was sufficient to block the JIB-04–induced phenotype in AML cells, whereas the caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK failed to protect the cells. Interestingly, upregulation of the known ferroptosis driver HMOX1 was blocked upon NAC exposure, indicating ROS accumulation to be essential for HMOX1 expression, likely through NRF2.56 NAC treatment also inhibited the synergism between JIB-04 and venetoclax, leading to the conclusion that the ferroptosis-like phenotype induced by JIB-04 is the driver for sensitizing AML cells to venetoclax. Yu et al recently reported improved response to venetoclax due to ferroptosis in AML, supporting our observations.57 Further studies need to be conducted to identify AML subtypes that can benefit from this approach and whether ferroptosis can prevent or overcome venetoclax resistance. RAS pathway mutations have been shown to drive venetoclax resistance in leukemic stem cells.58 These patients might benefit from dual treatment with a ferroptosis-inducing agent, because Ras-mutant leukemia cells have high ROS levels and might be more susceptible to ferroptosis.

In summary, we revealed that targeting KDM family members by the KDM inhibitor JIB-04 likely leads to ferroptosis in AML cells. The activation of a ferroptosis-like phenotype by JIB-04 is significant in sensitizing AML cells to venetoclax and moreover overcomes venetoclax resistance. JIB-04 could be a useful tool compound to study the vulnerability of AML cells to oxidative stress–driven cell death, such as ferroptosis, and to explore the potential benefit in combination with other drugs for AML treatment. This study emphasizes the broader relevance of ROS-driven cell death and the development of ferroptosis-inducing compounds with good bioavailability for combination strategies in AML because their reliance on mitochondrial metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation makes leukemia cells more susceptible to oxidative stress–related cell death, overall offering a new therapeutic strategy.
