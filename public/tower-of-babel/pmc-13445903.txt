An exploratory data-driven stratification of depression based on functional network connectivity and symptom profiles suggests heterogeneous responses to iTBS
Authors: Jiang Wang, Shiying Wang, Zeyang Zhao, Nan Li, Bin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe marked clinical and biological heterogeneity of major depressive disorder (MDD) suggests that it may be more appropriately conceptualized as a syndrome with multiple biologically relevant patterns. However, traditional stratification approaches based on either clinical symptomatology or isolated biological markers, including neuroimaging data, often fail to capture the complex brain-behavior relationships underlying MDD.MethodsIn this study, we applied regularized canonical correlation analysis to capture multivariate covariation between functional network connectivity (FNC) and symptom profiles. Based on the resulting low-dimensional representation, we performed clustering to explore potential stratification patterns in a cohort of 51 patients with MDD and examined whether these patterns were associated with variation in response to intermittent theta burst stimulation. Transcriptional signatures related to pattern-specific FNC abnormalities were further explored using partial least squares regression.ResultsOur study found two stratification patterns in MDD. After false discovery rate correction, only increased connectivity between the ventral default mode network (vDMN) and cerebellar network in MDD2 relative to healthy controls remained significant. In exploratory uncorrected analyses, MDD1 was characterized by increased connectivity between the vDMN and salience network, as well as between the dorsal DMN (dDMN) and orbitofrontal cortex, together with greater depressive severity and more prominent appetite disturbance, whereas MDD2 showed broader connectivity increases and more prominent sleep disturbance. Imaging transcriptomic analysis provided complementary molecular contextualization. Treatment-related connectivity changes were mainly observed in MDD2, while patients within both stratification patterns showed symptom improvement, with appetite disturbance improving in MDD1 and sleep disturbance improving in MDD2. Finally, prediction analyses suggested potential prognostic relevance of baseline FNC features, particularly the connectivity between the dDMN and right executive control network.ConclusionsBy integrating neuroimaging, clinical symptomatology, and transcriptomic data, this study offers a preliminary, data-driven framework for exploring heterogeneity in MDD and its potential relevance to treatment sensitivity. These results may inform future efforts to refine biologically grounded stratification strategies for neuromodulation-based treatment prediction.Trial registrationThe clinical trial number is ChiCTR2100054793, and the registration date is December 27, 2021.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08230-7.Keywords: Major depressive disorder, Magnetic resonance imaging, Functional network connectivity, Intermittent theta burst stimulation, Transcriptomic profiles

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a serious psychiatric disease that significantly impairs patients’ quality of life and social functioning. Despite the availability of various treatment options, including novel antidepressant medications, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal for many individuals, with approximately 30% of patients showing resistance to sequential antidepressant interventions [1]. One of the major challenges in improving treatment efficacy lies in the considerable heterogeneity of MDD, both in symptom presentation and underlying neurobiological mechanisms [2, 3], which has hindered progress in developing personalized, precision-based interventions. This heterogeneity in treatment response across individuals indicates that MDD likely comprises multiple neurobiological relevant stratification patterns beyond conventional diagnostic boundaries [4], each probably involving distinct pathophysiological mechanisms and symptom dimensions.

Numerous previous studies have made significant efforts to identify MDD subtypes using data-driven approaches [4–6]. However, traditional subtyping methods have primarily relied on clinical symptomatology or focused on isolated biological markers including neuroimaging [7], electroencephalography [8], or molecular biomarkers [9]. While informative, these single-domain approaches may fail to capture the complex brain-behavior relationships underlying MDD and may limit the clinical utility of subtype classifications. Currently, emerging studies have attempted to integrate both neurobiological and clinical features to subtype MDD [4]. However, most of these efforts have been limited by the high dimensionality of neurobiological data, which often leads to weak and unstable clustering outcomes [10]. Moreover, many of these studies are cross-sectional and often fail to investigate whether different MDD subtypes exhibit shared or distinct responses to therapeutic interventions. These limitations hinder our understanding of the underlying mechanisms specific to each subtype and impede the development of tailored treatment strategies for MDD.

Neuromodulation approaches for treating MDD are particularly well suited for stratification-informed evaluation, as they directly engage distributed brain circuits and allow treatment effects to be tracked with objective neurophysiological markers [11]. Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is a time-efficient and cost-effective form of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) [12]. It has attracted growing clinical interest given evidence of antidepressant efficacy comparable to standard high-frequency rTMS with markedly shorter session duration and favorable tolerability, thereby improving scalability in routine practice [13, 14]. However, treatment response to iTBS also varies substantially across individuals [13, 15], highlighting the need to identify biologically and clinically grounded stratification patterns that capture differential treatment sensitivity.

Therefore, this study aims to explore data-driven stratification patterns in MDD by integrating resting-state functional network connectivity (FNC) derived from the functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) with symptom profiles. FNC, which reflects the temporal coherence between brain networks during rest, was selected as the primary neuroimaging measure for its ability to reveal how different brain networks coordinate and communicate in the context of MDD [16]. To capture the multivariate associations between these domains, we applied regularized canonical correlation analysis (RCCA) [17]. Based on the derived low-dimensional representation, we performed clustering to examine potential stratification patterns and subsequently explored their neurobiological and clinical characteristics. In addition, we examined pattern-specific transcription–neuroimaging associations by linking gene expression data to FNC alterations within each clustering pattern. Finally, we investigated whether these data-driven stratification patterns were associated with heterogeneous responses to iTBS, with the aim of generating hypotheses for future studies on treatment prediction in MDD.

Methods

Participants

This study included 51 patients with MDD recruited from Tianjin Anding Hospital and 51 healthy controls (HCs), matched for age and sex from the local area by poster advertisements, between November 2022 and July 2025 (clinical trial number: ChiCTR2100054793). Demographics for the total sample see Supplementary Table S1. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Brain Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University and Tianjin Anding Hospital. The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved this study on July 15, 2021 (IRB Nos. 2021-040 and 2022-43). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) age 19 to 60 years; (2) a diagnosis of MDD according to the DSM-5, confirmed by the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.) [18]; (3) stable medication regimen for at least 2 weeks.

The exclusion criteria for all participants were as follows: (1) severe physical illnesses (e.g., malignant tumors, multi-organ failure) or serious organic brain disorders (e.g., epilepsy, stroke); (2) presence of psychotic symptoms during the current depressive episode; (3) history of electroconvulsive therapy within the past six months; (4) metal implants above the chest; (5) pregnancy; (6) history of alcohol or substance abuse within the past year; (7) inability to communicate or cooperate effectively with the study protocol; (8) participation in other interventional clinical trials.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

Neuroimaging data were acquired with a whole-body 3.0 T MRI scanner (Siemens Prisma, Erlangen, Germany), using a 64-channel head coil, including T1-weighted anatomic imaging and resting-state fMRI. During fMRI acquisition, participants were instructed to relax with their eyes closed and to remain still without falling asleep. The fMRI scan lasted 6 min and comprised 450 volumes. Detailed sequence parameters and acquisition protocols are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Preprocessing of the fMRI data was conducted using the Data Processing Assistant for Resting-State fMRI toolbox (DPARSF; http://rfmri.org/DPARSF) implemented in MATLAB R2020a. The preprocessing pipeline included removal of the first 10 volumes, followed by realignment for head motion correction. Participants exhibiting translational motion exceeding 3 mm or rotational motion greater than 3° were excluded. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were co-registered to the resting-state functional images, and segmentation was performed using the DARTEL method. The images were then normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, resampled to 2 × 2 × 2 mm voxel size, and spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm. Global signal regression was not applied [19].

Group independent component analysis

Group independent component analysis (GICA) was performed using the Group ICA of fMRI toolbox (GIFT) (version 4.0.6.0; https://trendscenter.org/software/gift) in MATLAB. Firstly, dimensionality reduction of the fMRI data was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) in a two-stage procedure. At the individual-subject level, each participant’s fMRI data were reduced to 26 principal components, and these reduced datasets were then concatenated across subjects to form an aggregate dataset. A second PCA was subsequently conducted at the group level to further reduce the aggregate data to 17 components for ICA decomposition. The optimal model order was estimated using the minimum description length (MDL) criterion based on FWHM [20]. ICA decomposition was then performed using the information maximization (Infomax) algorithm, and this process was repeated 100 times with both randinit and bootstrap modes in the ICASSO package to ensure the stability of the results [21]. Finally, spatial maps and time courses for each independent component were back-reconstructed based on the aggregate components and the results of dimensionality reduction.

Next, components with the highest correlation coefficients to reference templates from a functional atlas [22] were selected through the automated process in the GIFT toolbox, and further verified visually by matching them to known canonical resting-state networks. Components primarily localized in cerebrospinal fluid or white matter, or exhibiting low spatial correlation with gray matter, were regarded as noise components and excluded. The retained components were then categorized into distinct functional networks, including the ventral default mode network (vDMN), dorsal default mode network (dDMN), salience network (SN), sensorimotor network (SMN), right executive control network (RECN), left executive control network (LECN), language network (LN), primary visual network (PVN), higher visual network (HVN), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and cerebellar network (CB).

Finally, prior to FNC estimation, the retained component time series were further post-processed in the GIFT toolbox with detrending (order = 3), despiking, and temporal low-pass filtering using a high-frequency cutoff of 0.15 Hz. FNC was then assessed by extracting the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) time series of each independent component and computing Pearson correlation coefficients for all pairwise component combinations. To facilitate subsequent statistical analyses, the resulting correlation coefficients were converted to z-scores using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation.

RCCA and subtyping with K-means clustering

As more robust and interpretable stratification patterns may emerge from a low-dimensional representation that captures the strongest associations between neuroimaging measures and clinical symptoms [10], we first computed Spearman’s correlations between all connectivity features and each item of the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [23] and Hamilton Rating Scale for Anxiety (HAMA) [24]. We used MADRS rather than the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), as prior evidence suggests that MADRS has advantages over HAMD-17 in outpatient clinical trial samples [25]. We then retained only those associations that were statistically significant (p < 0.05).

These selected significant connectivity features were then entered into a RCCA model, which was used to derive a low-dimensional projection for subsequent clustering. Details regarding RCCA regularization parameter optimization is provided in the Supplementary Methods. To identify potential MDD stratification patterns, we applied K-means clustering to the pair of canonical variates (one summarizing connectivity features and the other summarizing symptom profiles) from the leading RCCA components, which reflect the maximal shared variance between the two multivariate domains. The statistical significance of the RCCA model was assessed using permutation testing with 1000 iterations. The NbClust package in R (version 4.2.3; https://cran.r-project.org/mirrors.html) was used to estimate the optimal number of clusters [26]. Cluster stability was further evaluated using the Jaccard coefficient and a bootstrap procedure (n = 1000) implemented in the ‘fpc’ package in R [27].

Transcriptomic correlates of pattern-specific FNC alterations

Transcriptomic profiles were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA, http://human.brain-map.org), comprising microarray-based expression profiles for over 20,000 genes measured across 3702 spatially annotated tissue samples from six postmortem human brains (ages 24–57 years). Gene expression matrices were preprocessed using the Abagen toolbox (https://github.com/rmarkello/abagen) implemented in Python [28]. Detailed preprocessing procedures are described in the Supplementary Methods. Following preprocessing, gene expression values were aggregated within each network by averaging across all samples assigned to the same network, resulting in a network-level gene expression matrix (11 networks × 15,633 genes) for further analyses.

To identify transcriptional signatures associated with FNC alterations, we employed partial least squares regression (PLSR) to model the relationship between the FNC and the gene expression patterns, with 15,633 gene expression values as predictor variables (X), and net t-values as the response variable (Y). The net t-value was calculated by summing all positive t-values within each network and subtracting the sum of the absolute values of negative t-values [29]. These t values were derived from between-group t tests assessing FNC differences between MDD stratification patterns and HCs. The fitted PLSR model generated latent components, which are linear combinations of predictor variables explaining variance in the response variables. To assess the statistical significance of the PLS components, permutation testing was performed, repeating the process 1000 times. Furthermore, we used bootstrap resampling to estimate gene weights, calculating z-scores by dividing the original weights by their bootstrap standard deviations. Finally, the first component from partial least squares regression (PLS1) was selected, as it typically explains the largest proportion of variance, for subsequent analysis.

For functional characterization of PLS1 associated genes, comprehensive enrichment analysis was performed using the FUMA platform (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/). Gene selection was performed by ranking genes based on their normalized weight (z-score), which was calculated as the ratio of the weight to its standard deviation, with stringent significance thresholds applied (z > 4 for PLS1 + genes; z < − 4 for PLS1 − genes) [30]. Enrichment analysis was then conducted to examine Gene Ontology (GO) terms across molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and cellular component (CC) categories, as well as Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were considered statistically significant at a Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate-corrected p value (pBH−FDR) < 0.05.

iTBS treatment related analysis

To evaluate treatment effects, we also examined changes in clinical symptom severity and FNC following iTBS. Analyses were conducted in the overall patient cohort and within each stratification pattern to explore both overall treatment-related effects and pattern-specific changes in clinical and neurofunctional measures. Detailed iTBS treatment parameters are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

In addition, baseline abnormal FNC features showing group differences between patients with MDD and HCs were used as inputs to a Naïve Bayes classifier to explore their potential value for predicting iTBS treatment response in the overall MDD sample and within each stratification pattern. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation as an internal validation procedure. Treatment response was defined according to the percentage reduction in MADRS total scores, with a reduction of 50% or more classified as response and a reduction of less than 50% classified as non-response.

Statistical analysis

Chi-squared tests were used for categorical variables. For continuous variables, two-sample t-tests (or Mann-Whitney U tests) were applied to independent groups, and Welch’s ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Paired t-tests (or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) were used for paired data. Statistical significance was set as two-tailed p < 0.05.

Abnormal FNC was analyzed using the Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) toolbox (http://rfmri.org/DPABI). FNC differences between MDD patients and HCs, as well as between stratification patterns, were assessed using two-sample t-tests, with age, sex, and head motion included as covariates. Additionally, paired t-tests were performed to compare connectivity between pre- and post-treatment within each stratification pattern, controlling for head motion as a covariate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, FDR correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (q < 0.05) to all relevant FNC comparisons, including group comparisons, pre-post treatment analyses, and stratification-related contrasts, as well as to the item-level symptom comparisons. To further evaluate the potential influence of residual head motion on significant FNC findings, we performed post hoc motion-sensitivity analyses. Specifically, residual motion–connectivity correlations were examined for significant FNC after scrubbing and adjustment for age, sex, and group. Results

GICA, RCCA and K-means clustering

Using the MDL criterion in GIFT, the optimal number of independent components (ICs) was determined to be 17, which was subsequently used to extract ICs from both the baseline and post-iTBS datasets for MDD patients and HCs. Among the 17 independent components, six were identified as noise components, leaving 11 for subsequent connectivity analyses. The selected 11 independent components were separately assigned to the following 11 networks, PVN, HVN, OFC, SMN, RECN, CB, LN, LECN, vDMN, SN, and dDMN.

The RCCA analysis identified several components, with the first few components representing canonical correlations that describe the maximum covariation between the two multidimensional datasets (FNC and clinical symptoms). We selected one of these components, which showed the highest canonical correlation and statistical significance (r = 0.914, p_perm = 0.014), for subsequent cluster analysis.

K-means clustering yielded two stratification patterns among the 51 patients with MDD, comprising 24 patients in MDD1 and 27 patients in MDD2. Validation and stability analyses supported the two-cluster solution, with a silhouette index of 0.57 and a Jaccard similarity of 0.95 (Supplementary Figure S1), suggesting reasonable internal separation and stability.

Clinical and demographic characteristics across stratification patterns in MDD

Demographic and clinical characteristics for each stratification pattern of MDD patients are shown in Table 1. No significant differences were observed among the MDD1, MDD2 and HCs groups in the distributions of age, sex, HAMA score, or Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [31] score. MDD1 had higher scores on MADRS (p = 0.007), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 items (PHQ-9) [32] (p = 0.034), the first five items from the Scale for Suicide Ideation (SSI-5) [33] (p = 0.004) and Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS) [34] (p = 0.02) compared with the MDD2. Item-level analyses were further conducted to examine potential differences in individual MADRS and HAMA items between MDD1 and MDD2 using Mann-Whitney U tests. Eight symptom items showed nominal differences (p < 0.05, uncorrected), suggesting more prominent appetite disturbance in MDD1 and more prominent sleep reduction in MDD2 (Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. 2e, f).

Group comparisons of FNC

After FDR correction, only the comparison between MDD2 and HCs remained significant, with increased connectivity between the vDMN and CB in MDD2 (Fig. 2c). No significant differences were retained after correction for the comparisons of all MDD vs. HC, MDD1 vs. HC, or MDD1 vs. MDD2.

In exploratory uncorrected analyses, relative to HCs, patients with MDD exhibited increased connectivity between the vDMN and SN, vDMN and CB, dDMN and RECN, and between the dDMN and OFC (Fig. 2a). Stratification-related analyses further suggested different connectivity patterns between MDD1 and MDD2. MDD1 showed increased connectivity between the vDMN and SN and between the dDMN and OFC (Fig. 2b). In contrast, MDD2 showed broader increases in connectivity, including between the vDMN and CB, SMN and CB, dDMN and LECN, dDMN and RECN, and between the dDMN and vDMN (Fig. 2c). Direct comparisons between MDD1 and MDD2 in the uncorrected analyses also suggested differences across several connections, including vDMN-CB, vDMN-LECN, HVN-LECN, PVN-LECN, PVN-LN, SMN-CB, and SMN-dDMN (Fig. 2d). The corresponding Cohen’s d effect sizes for these comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table S4. To account for potential heterogeneity in medication exposure among patients, we additionally repeated the patient-only FNC comparisons with medication exposure included as a covariate (Supplementary Table S5, S6, S7). We also conducted post hoc motion-sensitivity analyses to evaluate potential residual motion effects, with detailed results provided in Supplementary Table S8.

Transcriptomic profiles related to FNC alterations in each stratification pattern

PLSR analysis revealed significant spatial covariation between network-level gene expression profiles and FNC alterations across MDD stratification patterns. Based on the results of the PLSR analysis, PLS1 explained 73.33% of the variance for MDD1 (p_perm = 0.026) and 75.16% of the variance for MDD2 (p_perm = 0.029). The spatial distribution of PLS1 scores was correlated with FNC alteration patterns in both MDD1 (r = 0.84, p = 0.003) and MDD2 (r = 0.75, p = 0.01) (Fig. 3).

iTBS treatment related analysis

After FDR correction, no significant pre-post changes were retained in the overall MDD group or in MDD1. The corresponding corrected results for all comparisons are presented in Supplementary Table S9. In exploratory uncorrected analyses, treatment-related connectivity changes were observed mainly in MDD2, including reduced connectivity between the dDMN and RECN, as well as between the vDMN and CB, relative to baseline (Fig. 5c). In the overall MDD group, reduced connectivity between the vDMN and CB was also observed after iTBS treatment (Fig. 5a), whereas no significant post-treatment changes were detected in MDD1 (Fig. 5b).

Regarding clinical symptom changes, patients in the overall MDD group and in both stratification patterns showed reductions in depression and anxiety total scores after iTBS treatment (Supplementary Figure S3). More importantly, each stratification pattern exhibited specific improvements in individual symptom items (Supplementary Tables S10, S11). MDD1 showed improvement in reduced appetite, whereas this change was not observed in MDD2. Conversely, MDD2 showed improvement in sleep disturbance, which was not evident in MDD1. When comparing treatment-related changes between MDD1 and MDD2, no significant differences were observed in overall depression or anxiety total scores (Supplementary Table S12). However, some symptom-item changes differed between the two stratification patterns, most notably for reduced appetite (Supplementary Table S13).

We further explored whether baseline abnormal FNC features had potential prognostic value for iTBS treatment response using a Naive Bayes classifier. In the overall MDD sample, the classifier based on dDMN-RECN connectivity differentiated responders from non-responders with an accuracy (ACC) of 73.0% and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.70, whereas the model incorporating all abnormal connectivities yielded lower performance (ACC = 64.9%, AUC = 0.60) (Fig. 5d). Stratification-related prediction analyses suggested heterogeneity in predictive performance. In MDD1, prediction performance was limited across both single-connectivity and all-connectivity models (Fig. 5e). In contrast, for MDD2, the classifier showed higher cross-validated performance when using dDMN-RECN (ACC = 75.0%, AUC = 0.87), dDMN-LECN (ACC = 75.0%, AUC = 0.76), or all abnormal connectivities (ACC = 75.0%, AUC = 0.87) as predictors (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

The present study used an integrative, data-driven stratification framework to explore heterogeneity in MDD by combining resting-state fMRI-derived functional network connectivity with clinical symptom profiles. This approach may provide a useful framework for characterizing variation in network organization and symptom expression beyond analyses based on a single modality alone. Imaging transcriptomic analyses were further used to provide complementary molecular contextualization by linking spatial patterns of FNC abnormalities with gene-expression profiles. In addition, we examined whether these stratification patterns were associated with differences in clinical and neurofunctional changes following iTBS treatment, as well as their potential relevance for treatment-response prediction. Overall, these findings offer hypothesis-generating evidence that neurobiologically informed stratification may help refine the characterization of MDD heterogeneity and inform future studies of neuromodulation response.

Among the two stratification patterns, overall depressive severity was higher in MDD1 than in MDD2, whereas anxiety severity did not differ between the two patterns. Beyond overall severity, the two patterns showed partially different symptom profiles. MDD1 was characterized by more pronounced appetite disturbance, suicidal thoughts, and inner tension, whereas MDD2 showed more prominent sleep disturbance. This pattern suggests that the observed stratification may not simply reflect a general depression severity gradient, but may also capture variation in specific symptom dimensions. Symptom heterogeneity in sleep problems, appetite changes, and suicidal ideation has been widely reported in depression [2]. By linking these symptom dimensions to differences in large-scale FNC patterns, our findings provide a hypothesis-generating neurobiological perspective on clinical heterogeneity in MDD.

Large‑scale network dysfunction in depression has frequently been associated with altered interactions among the DMN, SN, cerebellar circuits and executive control systems, which have been linked to core features including rumination, attention dysregulation and affective bias [35]. In this study, MDD1 showed increased connectivity between the vDMN and SN, as well as between the dDMN and OFC. The DMN is involved in self-referential processing and rumination [36], while the SN contributes to the processing of emotionally salient stimuli [37]. Therefore, increased vDMN-SN connectivity may reflect enhanced coupling between internally oriented self-referential processes and salience processing, which could be relevant to negative affective rumination in depression. Increased dDMN-OFC connectivity may also be related to altered affective valuation and regulatory processes, which have been implicated in depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation [38]. In contrast, MDD2 showed broader increases in connectivity involving the cerebellum and multiple intrinsic networks. Although the cerebellum has traditionally been associated with motor control, increasing evidence suggests that it also participates in emotional processing and cognitive–affective integration [39]. Previous studies have also reported altered cerebellar coupling with intrinsic networks including the DMN [40]. Moreover, cerebellar involvement has been linked to somatic and sleep-related symptom dimensions [41]. The increased cerebellar-cortical connectivity observed in MDD2 may represent a network-level feature associated with more prominent sleep disturbance.

Additionally, the PLSR results provided molecular contextualization for the observed FNC alteration patterns by linking them to gene-expression profiles. The shared enrichment of immune response and vascular development terms among PLS1 + genes in both stratification patterns is consistent with prior evidence implicating immune and vascular-related processes in MDD [42, 43]. These enrichment patterns may provide a plausible biological context linking peripheral or central inflammatory signaling with neurovascular regulation and blood-brain barrier function, processes that have been suggested to influence large-scale functional coupling among intrinsic brain networks [44, 45]. Similarly, the enrichment of synapse- and plasticity-related terms among PLS1 − genes may indicate that FNC alteration patterns are spatially related to gene-expression profiles involved in synaptic organization and neural plasticity [46, 47]. This observation is also consistent with prior work suggesting a potential role for neuroimmune modulation of synaptic function and plasticity in shaping the broader transcriptional context of FNC abnormalities [48–50]. Beyond these shared annotations, pattern-specific enrichments were also observed. In MDD1, the unique highlight of alcohol and xenobiotic metabolism, in conjunction with immune and vascular terms, may index altered detoxification and redox-handling capacity that is often carried by glial and metabolic support systems [51]. In MDD2, enrichment related to wound healing, tissue repair, organelle membrane organization, and calcium-regulated exocytosis may point to molecular annotations associated with cellular stress regulation and vesicle-release processes. These annotations may provide a candidate molecular context for interpreting the broader baseline connectivity alterations and treatment-related FNC changes observed in MDD2.

The treatment-related FNC changes observed after iTBS may provide preliminary evidence for stratification-related differences in large-scale network organization. Previous studies have suggested that MDD is associated with increased connectivity within the DMN and altered interactions between the DMN and ECN [35], a systems-level profile that has been linked to maladaptive internally oriented processing and reduced cognitive control over negative mentation [36, 52]. Therefore, the post-treatment reduction in dDMN-RECN connectivity observed in MDD2 may be consistent with a partial normalization of DMN-ECN interactions, which could be relevant to improved regulation of internally generated negative thoughts. This interpretation is in line with prior evidence that prefrontal neuromodulation can influence DMN-related connectivity patterns in depression [53]. In addition, the reduction in vDMN-CB connectivity, mainly observed in MDD2, suggests that cerebellar-cortical coupling may be a treatment-sensitive feature of this stratification pattern. Given prior reports of altered cerebellar interactions with intrinsic networks in depression, vDMN-CB connectivity may provide a candidate network-level context for understanding treatment-related changes in a subset of patients.

More broadly, our findings support the view that response to TMS in MDD is heterogeneous rather than uniform across patients. Recent real-world evidence from Asian MDD cohorts suggests that treatment outcomes are influenced by demographic, clinical, and protocol-related factors, underscoring the importance of more refined stratification strategies [54]. From this perspective, the absence of detectable post-treatment FNC changes in MDD1, despite symptomatic improvement, may indicate that treatment-related neural adaptations in this pattern were either less pronounced at the level of ICA-derived FNC measures or occurred through mechanisms not captured by the current network metrics. This observation further highlights the importance to align biomarkers with pattern-specific circuit mechanisms [7]. Furthermore, the predictive analyses provide preliminary evidence that stratification may help refine treatment-response prediction. In the overall sample, a single DMN-related feature, dDMN–RECN connectivity, showed better performance than the model including all abnormal FNC features. However, this predictive signal was mainly observed in MDD2, whereas prediction performance in MDD1 was limited. This pattern is broadly consistent with prior work suggesting that connectivity-based markers may help stratify neuromodulation outcomes. It also suggests that aggregating heterogeneous network abnormalities across patients may dilute prognostic information, whereas stratification-aware models may better capture outcome-relevant variation [55].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the stratification analyses were exploratory and based on a data-driven framework. The initial feature screening before RCCA used an uncorrected threshold, which may have increased the risk of false-positive feature inclusion, and some group differences did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, likely reflecting limited statistical power. Second, the transcriptomic results should be interpreted as molecular contextualization rather than direct molecular evidence from our clinical MDD sample. The AHBA is based on only six adult postmortem donors with uneven spatial sampling, and none of the donors had MDD. Therefore, the observed gene-expression associations cannot be regarded as disease-specific molecular signatures of the data-driven stratification patterns. In addition, because the transcriptomic PLSR analysis operated on only 11 network-level observations, individual gene weights should be interpreted cautiously, and the enrichment findings should be viewed as exploratory aggregate-level signals rather than direct mechanistic evidence. Third, the allocation of iTBS targeting methods remained blinded at the current stage. Consequently, we were unable to examine the distribution of 5-cm versus FC-guided targeting across the stratification patterns or to formally assess their potential influence on treatment response. Therefore, the observed stratification-related treatment-response signals may have been partly influenced by an unequal distribution of targeting protocols and should be interpreted with caution. Once the planned sample size is reached and unblinding is performed, follow-up analyses will be conducted to evaluate the distribution and potential effects of targeting method on treatment-related outcomes.

Conclusions

This study used an integrative, data-driven stratification approach based on functional network connectivity and symptom profiles and observed two exploratory clustering patterns in adult MDD. The stratification analysis suggested variation in network-level abnormalities and iTBS-related connectivity reconfiguration, pointing to potentially meaningful heterogeneity in circuit organization and treatment-related response across patients with MDD. Imaging transcriptomic analyses provided complementary molecular contextualization, indicating that the spatial patterns of network dysconnectivity were related to network-specific gene expression patterns, including shared immune/vascular themes and pattern-specific enrichments. Moreover, predictive analyses suggested that this stratification framework may have exploratory prognostic relevance, with a parsimonious DMN-related feature outperforming the full feature-set model in the overall sample and discrimination being largely driven by MDD2. These findings support the potential value of an integrative framework linking brain-circuit variation with molecular context for future treatment stratification. Further longitudinal validation in larger independent cohorts is needed to determine the robustness and potential clinical relevance of this approach.
