Angina, Anomalous Coronaries, Surgical Correction, and Return to Olympic Cycling
Authors: William S Walker, Max Ray, Andre La Gerche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA) is an uncommon congenital abnormality associated with sudden cardiac death (SCD), particularly in young athletes.Case SummaryWe present a case of a 4-time Olympic medalist track cyclist who experienced sporadic anginal symptoms during maximal efforts and was found to have an anomalous aortic origin of the right coronary artery (AAORCA). The athlete had a successful surgical reimplantation and returned to elite sport.DiscussionManagement decisions are complex: The investigations used to confirm ischemia and to stratify SCD risk are imperfect, and the surgical treatment carries potential risk. Furthermore, the impact of a sternotomy and coronary reimplantation on competitive sports performance has not been detailed.Take-Home MessagesIn the absence of definitive ischemic evidence from history or diagnostic tests, diagnosing high-risk AAOCA can be challenging. The decision to pursue surgical intervention should carefully consider the low rate of SCD and the risk/impact of surgery.Key words: anomalous coronary, cardiac imaging, congenital, sports cardiology, sudden cardiac death

History of Presentation

A 34-year-old man, a 4-time Olympian and Olympic medalist, presented to our institution for guidance regarding returning to competition after reimplantation of anomalous aortic origin of the right coronary artery (AAORCA). Before surgery, the patient had experienced intermittent exertional dyspnea and angina after intense efforts during training and racing over a span of 2 years. The symptoms occurred during peak exertion or early recovery.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant past medical history. There was no family history of cardiac disease or sudden death.

Investigations

A 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) was performed during the athlete's first presentation to an emergency department for angina. The ECG showed electrical criteria for high QRS voltages, early repolarization changes and T-wave inversion in leads V1 and V2, consistent with changes common to endurance athletes, (Figure 1). The patient's high-sensitivity cardiac troponin level was 12 ng/L initially and was unchanged 2 hours later (normal: <21 ng/L).

Nonspecific ST-segment abnormality was seen in the precordial leads.

He was referred to the cardiology team for outpatient follow-up, and computed tomography coronary angiography (CTCA) was performed. The CTCA showed a right coronary artery (RCA) with an anomalous origin from the left coronary cusp and a course between the aortic root and the pulmonary outflow tract at the level of the pulmonary valve (Figure 2). There was mild smooth tapering of the vessel as it coursed between these 2 structures. The remainder of the RCA was of normal appearance, with no plaque or stenosis (Figure 3).

A stress echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular size and wall thickness, normal left ventricular ejection fraction (60%), and normal regional wall motion at rest. The athlete completed a submaximal 8 min Bruce protocol, reaching a maximum heart rate of 174 beats/min. There was no chest pain induced, ST-segment changes on ECG, and there were no inducible wall motion abnormalities on echocardiography immediately after exercise.

Management

The patient was referred to a cardiac surgeon for an opinion. Given the symptomatic presentation, the “malignant” course between the right ventricular outflow tract and aortic root, along with 50% to 70% luminal stenosis of the ostial and proximal RCA, reimplantation of the anomalous RCA was recommended. Because of the “high-risk” anatomical features and presence of exertional symptoms, the patient was offered and elected to undergo surgery for reimplantation of the anomalous RCA.

A partial medium sternotomy was performed. After initiating cardiopulmonary bypass via an aortic and 2-stage venous cannula, the RCA was mobilized and implanted into the ascending aorta. The orifice was checked by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography and was found to be widely patent.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperative recovery was uncomplicated. The patient was reviewed by the sports cardiology team 5 weeks after surgery. As rehabilitation progressed, comprehensive evaluation—including stress echocardiography and cardiopulmonary exercise testing—was performed to exclude ischemia and arrhythmias prior to resuming higher intensity training.

Resting echocardiography demonstrated mild dilatation of all 4 cardiac chambers, consistent with athletic remodeling. Using a continuous ramp protocol on cycle ergometer (0.5 W/s, starting at 50 W), the patient achieved a high exercise workload with a Vo2 max of 55 mL/kg/min at 320 W and 201 beats/min. There was no ECG or echocardiographic evidence of ischemia, and no chest pain or breathlessness. Given these results, he was cleared to return to intense training and competition in a closely supervised, graded fashion.

Less than 1 year after surgery, the athlete began experiencing intermittent episodes of angina and breathlessness during intense exertion. These symptoms were described as transient and nonradiating, occasionally associated with fatigue after prolonged exercise sessions. There was no associated syncope, palpitations, or orthopnea. The athlete described the symptoms as being similar to those experienced preoperatively. Investigations performed during an emergency department presentation for chest pain—including ECG and troponin—were normal. While competing internationally, his team physicians arranged a CTCA and stress magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The CTCA showed a patent RCA. Stress MRI showed no inducible myocardial ischemia, and there was no late gadolinium enhancement. A presumptive diagnosis of pericarditis was made by his team doctors. Aspirin and colchicine were commenced, without clear resolution of symptoms.

After returning from competition, the patient returned to the sports cardiology clinic for assessment and a stress echocardiogram. A tailored sprint protocol was performed to mirror the demands of track cycling. He was able to complete multiple 600-W efforts with a peak heart rate of 195 beats/min without symptoms. There was no ECG or echocardiographic evidence of inducible ischemia. The patient was reassured, though no clear diagnosis was made for his intermittent episodes of angina and breathlessness. Colchicine and aspirin were ceased. The patient returned to unrestricted training and competition. He qualified for and competed at the Paris Olympics in 2024.

Discussion

Anomalous aortic origin of the left coronary artery (AAOLCA) is recognized as a potentially high-risk condition, with surgery recommended in the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines.1 On the other hand, AAORCA is frequently detected as an incidental finding and is increasingly recognized as having a benign outcome for many patients, particularly those who survive to adulthood.2 The population prevalence of AAORCA is 6 to 7 times that of AAOLCA,3,4 which contrasts with the prevalence of AAOLCA being 4 times higher than AAORCA in young athletic sudden cardiac death (SCD) cohorts.4 There is substantial heterogeneity in the literature regarding the risk of SCD associated with an anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA). Whereas Petek et al5 identified AAOCA as the cause of SCD in 58 of 454 (13%) young competitive athletes in the United States, Finocchiaro et al6 observed that only 30 of 5,100 consecutive SCD cases (0.6%) were attributable to AAOCA. Notably, this proportion approaches the estimated community prevalence. Similarly, Paratz et al7 reported 3 individuals with AAOCA among 490 cases of SCD, but in none of these individuals was AAOCA thought to be the cause of the SCD. The reason for these widely disparate experiences is not clear.

Recent studies using coronary imaging provide a complementary experience that also enables an assessment of the relationship between AAOCA, symptoms, and treatment. A recent large Italian cohort of consecutive patients undergoing CTCA revealed AAOCA in 1% of individuals, of whom only 5% underwent surgical correction.8 Symptoms, mortality, and adverse cardiac events were similar in a comparison between patients with and without AAOCA. Interestingly, Gentile et al8 found that those patients with “high-risk” features such as an interarterial course, acute takeoff angle, slit-like ostium, and intramural course did not have higher rates of symptoms or events than patients without these features. High-volume centers no longer consider these features an automatic indication for surgery.2

The ongoing challenge is finding those few patients at high risk while attempting to reassure and avoid overtreating the majority who are at low risk. The current approach involves integration of anatomy, ischemia testing, and symptoms. However, the experience of Gentile et al8 highlights the limitations of our current knowledge base.

The scenario of reproducible symptoms with objective evidence of ischemia in RCA territory is relatively clear, but unfortunately rare. Current ischemia testing lacks high positive and negative predictive value owing to the complex nature of ischemia in AAOCA, which involves both a fixed and dynamic component that is not simulated well by any current testing modality.

In our case, the rationale for recommending surgery was based on symptoms and on prognostic grounds. Despite no evidence of myocardial ischemia on functional testing, it was felt that the patient's symptoms likely represented intermittent ischemia and that constriction of his proximal RCA represented “high-risk” anatomy. However, with over a decade of elite racing and a recent Olympic silver medal, one may have wondered why his symptoms had only recently developed. The recurrence of his chest pain after surgery suggests that his initial pain may not have been ischemic, especially given that he was comprehensively assessed afterward and no anatomical or functional evidence for coronary obstruction was found. The second reason for recommending surgery, to prevent SCD, may have been sound.

We can only speculate on what our management would have been because this would have undoubtedly involved shared decision-making based on the results of investigations. However, the approach in our clinic reflects the fact that AAOCA is not uncommon while SCD due to AAOCA is rare, and thus additional features that may suggest a worse prognosis would generally be required to recommend surgery. Evidence of myocardial ischemia (symptoms, ECG changes, arrhythmias, wall motion abnormalities, or perfusion defects) on maximal exercise testing would be a good indication for surgery. We would also tend to perform a cardiac MRI scan to assess for endocardial scar in the territory supplied by the anomalous artery as evidence of previous ischemic injury and would consider this a relative indication for surgery. Finally, the anatomy cannot be completely overlooked. There is sound rationale that AAORCA, as in this case, is of lesser concern than AAOLCA. Additional anatomical features such as “slit-like orifice,” acute angle, and intramural course provide an anatomical rationale as to how they could cause ischemia, but we remain skeptical about the reproducibility and significance of these findings without more substantive evidence that they are more commonly associated with symptoms, arrhythmias, syncope, or arrest. Putting everything together in this case, we may speculate that shared decision-making with this patient would have started with an expression of uncertainty about the relationship between the CTCA results and his symptoms and even greater uncertainty regarding any prognostic significance. However, it should be noted that the patient has done very well, and we can only speculate that he would have remained incident free had he been treated conservatively.

Decisions regarding return to play are also difficult. The athlete with uncorrected AAOCA is at an elevated risk of SCD, although this increase in risk is likely trivial to modest depending on clinical features. On the other hand, it may be incorrect to assume that this risk is entirely eliminated postsurgery. Surgery is complex. Coronary reimplantation or coronary unroofing has an acute surgical risk, but it is also noteworthy that the medium- and long-term outcomes are also not well documented. It is difficult to compare the risks of surgery and a conservative approach when the adverse outcomes associated with either option are likely rare. We would argue that decisions regarding return to sport should be similar to those used for prognostication, and in both uncorrected and corrected anomalies the absence of symptoms and inducible ischemia should be adequate to enable an athlete to return to play.

This is a remarkable case of difficult hurdles: The athlete's decision, with the guidance of his clinicians, to undergo surgery, followed by the physical and psychological challenges of recovery, rehabilitation, and return to the highest level of elite sport. As far as we are aware, he is unique in returning from coronary surgery to endurance sport at an Olympic level.

Conclusions

AAOCAs are an uncommon cause of SCD. They are often diagnosed as an incidental finding, and identifying an association with symptoms or objective ischemia can be challenging. This case highlights the challenges when dealing with a young athlete with the highest physical aspirations and the challenges of shared decision-making when certainty about symptom resolution and risk of events is far from assured. It also highlights the need for further large cohort studies that can determine the incidence of cardiac events in association with higher risk anatomical features. Perhaps in the short term there is sufficient evidence to state that the terms “malignant” and “high risk” are misguided, serve to overstate the relative risk, and make a balanced discussion more challenging.
