Dynamics of stochastic microorganism flocculation models
Alexandru Hening, Nguyen T. Hieu, Dang H. Nguyen, Nhu Nguyen

Abstract
. In this paper we study the dynamics of stochastic microorganism flocculation models. Given the strong influence of environmental and seasonal fluctuations that are present in these models, we propose a stochastic model that includes multiple layers of stochasticity, from small Brownian fluctuations, to possibly large changes due to environmental ‘shifts’. We are able to give a full classification of the asymptotic behavior of these models. New techniques had to be developed to prove the persistence and extinction of the process as the system is not in Kolmogorov form and, as a result, the analysis is significantly more involved. Keywords. flocculation model, switching diffusion, ergodicity, invariant measures, persistence, extinction



1.
Introduction

Flocculation describes a process where particles coagulate, coalesce or agglomerate due to weak interactions. This leads to phase separation due to the formation of large clusters. A flocculant is an agent that can lead to flocculation and therefore to a coagulation of various dispersed particles into an aggregate - see
[
33
,
32
,
34
,
24
]
. There are three main types of flocculants: organic, inorganic and biological. There are good reasons
[
24
]
to prefer bioflocculants as organic and inorganic flocculants can be unsafe due to toxicity. Among bioflocculants an interesting class is the one composed of microbial flocculants. These are not toxic and do not provide primary or seconday sources of pollution
[
26
]
. Microbial flocculants have already been used extensively in various industrial fields, from wastewater treatment
[
1
,
31
]
and activated sludge dewatering
[
25
]
to the removal of pathogens from water
[
28
,
41
]
and in the harvesting of marine microalgae
[
23
]
.

Our goal was to explore some important mathematical models which describe the flocculation of microorganisms and to describe the long-term behavior of these systems. It turns out that the analysis is quite involved as these systems are not in the standard Kolmogorov form

\frac{dX_{i}}{dt}(t)=X_{i}f_{i}(X(t))

which usually appears in the population dynamics literature.

The role of the environment in shaping species interactions cannot be overlooked. Since environmental conditions often vary in random and unpredictable ways, it is essential to develop mathematical models that can account for both stochastic environmental fluctuations and the nonlinear dynamics of intra- and interspecies interactions.

There are cases where biotic interactions alone may drive a species to extinction, yet the introduction of random environmental variation can reverse this outcome and promote coexistence. Conversely, deterministic systems in which all species persist may lead to extinction once environmental randomness is introduced. Recent advances in this field rely on modeling population dynamics through discrete- or continuous-time Markov processes and studying their long-term behavior (
[
10
,
13
,
12
,
22
,
36
,
35
,
6
,
3
,
2
,
8
,
7
,
14
,
4
]
).

One useful approach to investigating species coexistence is to calculate the average per-capita growth rate of a population when it is rare. A positive value suggests that the population tends to increase from low density, while a negative value implies decline and eventual extinction. In two-species systems, coexistence occurs when each species is capable of invading while rare, provided the other species is at equilibrium (
[
37
,
9
,
12
]
).

A well-established theoretical framework exists for coexistence in deterministic models (
[
20
,
21
,
19
]
). Beginning with the foundational work of
[
20
]
, it was demonstrated that persistence is guaranteed if there exists a set of fixed weights assigned to the populations such that the weighted sum of their invasion rates remains positive for any invariant measure supported on the boundary.

More recently, considerable attention has been given to to the study of stochastic differential equations (SDE) models models. For general stochastic differential equations with arbitrary noise intensity, sufficient conditions for persistence and extinction have been established in
[
35
,
17
,
15
,
16
,
4
,
14
]
.

While SDE models are well studied, Brownian noise is not always the most suitable type of noise. Even though, one could argue, small Brownian fluctuations should always be included, sometimes there are more abrupt and significant environmental changes. These can be modeled by a discrete component
\xi(t)
which switches randomly between finitely many environmental states. This leads to stochastic differential equations with switching. In a fixed environmental state the system is modeled by stochastic differential equations. This way we can capture the more realistic behavior of two types of environmental fluctuations:

•

major changes of the environment (daily or seasonal changes),

•

small Brownian fluctuations within each environment.

General properties for these processes have been studied thoroughly
[
38
,
42
,
30
]
. However, there are few general results regarding the persistence of ecological systems modeled by stochastic differential equations with switching
[
18
]
.

Another novelty in the model studied in this paper is that boundedness and dissipativity conditions fail. Loosely speaking, dissipativity requires that if a species has a high density, then the species will strongly drift towards zero. This fails in our model and therefore we cannot immediately use the methods from
[
17
,
15
,
16
]
.

1.1.
Mathematical Setup and Results

Consider first the model without any environmental fluctuations.

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=[D(S_{0}-S(t))-\mu_{1}S(t)X(t)]

\displaystyle\frac{dX}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}S(t)X(t)-DX(t)-h_{2}X(t)P(t)

\displaystyle\frac{dP}{dt}(t)

\displaystyle=D(P_{0}-P(t))-h_{3}X(t)P(t)

where
S(t),X(t),P(t)
are the concentrations of the nutrient (or the medium), microorganisms and flocculants at time
t\geq 0
. The parameters have the following biological interpretations:
D,S_{0}>0
are the velocity and the input concentration of the medium,
\mu_{1}>0
is the consumption or death rate of the medium,
\mu_{2}>0
is the yield of the microorganisms,
h_{2}>0
is the flocculation rate,
P_{0}>0
is the input concentration of flocculant, and
h_{3}>0
is the consumption rate of the flocculant.

The long-term behavior of (
1.1
) is known. According to
[
39
,
40
]
, if
S_{0}<\frac{h_{2}P_{0}+D}{\mu_{2}}
the microorganisms go extinct and the dynamics converges to
(S_{0},0,P_{0})
while if
S_{0}>\frac{h_{2}P_{0}+D}{\mu_{2}}
there exists a unique asymptotically stable fixed point
(S^{*},X^{*},P^{*})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3,\circ}:=(0,\infty)^{3}.

We consider a stochastic model of the form

(1.2)

\displaystyle dS(t)

\displaystyle=[D(\xi(t))(S_{0}-S(t))-\mu_{1}(\xi(t))S(t)X(t)]dt+\sigma_{1}S(t)dW_{1}(t)

\displaystyle dX(t)

\displaystyle=[\mu_{2}(\xi(t))S(t)X(t)-D(\xi(t))X(t)-h_{2}(\xi(t))X(t)P(t)]dt+\sigma_{2}X(t)dW_{2}(t)

\displaystyle dP(t)

\displaystyle=[D(\xi(t))(P_{0}-P(t))-h_{3}(\xi(t))X(t)P(t)]dt+\sigma_{3}P(t)dW_{3}(t)

where
(W_{1}(t),W_{2}(t),W_{3}(t))
is a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Define
\mathbf{Y}(t):=(S(t),X(t),P(t))
and let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3,\circ}
denote the initial conditions, that is
\mathbf{Y}(0):=(S(0),X(0),P(0))=\mathbf{y}=(s,x,p)=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
.

The switching process
\xi(t)
lives on a finite state space
\mathcal{M}:=\{1,\dots,m_{0}\}
and is defined by

(1.3)

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\xi(t+\Delta)=j~|~r(t)=i,\mathbf{Y}(s),r(s),s\leq t\}=q_{ij}\Delta+o(\Delta)\text{ if }i\neq j\ \hbox{ and }\\
&\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\xi(t+\Delta)=i~|~r(t)=i,\mathbf{Y}(s),r(s),s\leq t\}=1+q_{ii}\Delta+o(\Delta).\end{array}

The above equation says that the probability that the process
\xi(t)
jumps from
\xi(t)=i
to the state
j\neq i
in the small time
\Delta
is approximately equal to
q_{ij}\Delta
.

Here
q_{ii}:=-\sum_{j\neq i}q_{ij}
quantifies the probability of staying in the same state
i
. We will assume that
Q=(q_{ij})_{m_{0}\times m_{0}}
is irreducible. This implies that the process
\xi(t)
has a unique stationary distribution
(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{m_{0}})
which is the unique solution of

\sum_{j}(\pi_{j}q_{ji}-\pi_{i}q_{ij})=0.

It is well-known
[
38
]
that a process
(\mathbf{Y}(t),\xi(t))
satisfying (
1.2
) and (
1.3
)
is a Markov process with generator acting on functions
G:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\mathcal{N}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
that are continuously differentiable in
\mathbf{y}
for each
k\in\mathcal{N}
as

(1.4)

\mathcal{L}G(\mathbf{y},k)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}(\mathbf{y},k)\frac{\partial G}{\partial y_{i}}(\mathbf{y},k)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{3}\sigma_{l}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial y_{l}^{2}}(\mathbf{y},k)+\sum_{l\in\mathcal{M}}q_{kl}G(\mathbf{y},l).

where
F_{i}(\mathbf{y},k)
are the drift terms from (
1.2
).

The first theorem tells us that we can bound the moments of the process, and that the process stays in compact sets with a large probability.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any initial value
(\mathbf{y},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}
, there exists uniquely a global solution

\mathbf{Y}(t)
to (
1.2
)
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\{\mathbf{Y}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+},\ \forall t\geq 0\}=1.

Moreover,
S(t)>0
and
P(t)\geq 0
for all
t>0
with probability 1 and if
X(0)=0
then
X(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
with probability 1. If
X(0)>0
then
X(t)>0
for all
t\geq 0
with probability 1.
We also have that
(\mathbf{Y}(t),\xi(t))
is a Markov-Feller process on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{M}
.
Furthermore,
there exists
q_{0}>1
such that for any
q\in[1,q_{0}]
,

(1.5)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y},k}(1+|\mathbf{Y}(t)|)^{q}\leq(1+|\mathbf{y}|)^{q}e^{-c_{1,q}t}+c_{2,q}\text{ where }|\mathbf{y}|=s+x+p.

for some positive constants
c_{1,q}
and
c_{2,q}
.
There exists
\overline{K}>0
such that

(1.6)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y},k}(1+|\mathbf{Y}(t)|)^{2}\leq e^{\overline{K}t}(1+|\mathbf{y}|)^{2}.

For any
\varepsilon>0,H>0,T>0

\widetilde{K}(\varepsilon,H,T)>0
such that

(1.7)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\left\{|\mathbf{Y}|\leq\widetilde{K}(\varepsilon,H,T),0\leq t\leq T\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon\text{ given }|\mathbf{y}|\leq H.

Let
(\widehat{S},\widehat{P})
satisfy

(1.8)

\displaystyle d\widehat{S}(t)

\displaystyle=[D(\xi(t))(S_{0}-\widehat{S}(t))]dt+\sigma_{1}\widehat{S}(t)dW_{1}(t)

\displaystyle d\widehat{P}(t)

\displaystyle=[D(\xi(t))(P_{0}-\widehat{P}(t))]dt+\sigma_{3}\widehat{P}(t)dW_{3}(t).

By an easy application of Theorem
1.1
we get the following.

Corollary 1.2
.

The solution process
(\widehat{S}(t),\widehat{P}(t),\xi(t))
of (
1.8
) is a Markov Feller process on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}
and for any initial value
(s,p,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}

(\widehat{S}(t),\widehat{P}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+},t\geq 0

Moreover, we have

(1.9)

\mathbb{E}_{s,p,k}(1+\widehat{S}(t)+\widehat{P}(t))^{q}\leq(1+s+p)e^{-c_{1,q}t}+c_{2,q}.

There is a unique invariant probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}
and the following two properties hold

(1)

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M})=1
.

(2)

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}(1+s+p)^{q}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}(dsdp,k)\leq c_{2,q},q\leq q_{0}.

Proof.

The moment boundedness from (
1.9
) together with the nondegeneracy of the diffusion implies by
[
38
]
the existence of a unique invariant probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}
. We get that
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M})=1
because any initial value
(s,p,k)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}

(\widehat{S}(t),\widehat{P}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{2,\circ}_{+},t\geq 0

∎

Using Corollary

we can define the invasion rate of the microorganisms by

(1.10)

\Lambda=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(\mu_{2}(k)s-h_{2}(k)p-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}(k)}{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}_{b}(dsdp,k).

Remark 1.1
.

Loosely speaking, the invasion rate (also called the Lyapunov exponent) tells us what happens when one introduces the microorganisms at an infinitesimally small density in the ecosystem where the nutrient and flocculants are at stationarity. To see the intuition behind invasion rates and population dynamics we refer the reader to
[
35
,
17
,
15
]

Define

(1.11)

\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{Q}-{\rm diag}(D(1),D(2),\cdots,D(m_{0})).

Then
\mathbf{A}
is nonsingular.
Let

\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta(1),\cdots,\eta(m_{0}))^{\top}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{2}

\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta(1),\cdots,\zeta(m_{0}))^{\top}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{h}_{1}

\mathbf{m}_{2}=(-\mu_{2}(1),\cdots,-\mu_{2}(m_{0}))^{\top}
and
\mathbf{h}_{1}=(h_{1}(1),\cdots,h_{1}(m_{0}))^{\top}

The next lemma shows that we can explicitly compute

Lemma 1.1
.

We have

\Lambda=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\left[D(k)(\xi(k)+\zeta(k))-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}(k)}{2}\right]\pi_{k}.

Usually invasion-rates cannot be computed explicitly for higher dimensional systems so it is very interesting that it can be done in this setting.

The next result tells us that if
\lambda
is negative then the microorganisms go extinct with probability

. Furthermore, it also gives the exact exponential rate of extinction.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
\Lambda<0
then for any
\mathbf{Y}(0)=\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3,\circ}
we have with probability 1 that

(1.12)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\ln X(t)}{t}=\Lambda

which means that
X

\Lambda
.

If
\Lambda>0
we get stochastic persistence as shown in the next result.

Theorem 1.4
.

If
\Lambda>0
then there exist an invariant measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\circ}
on
\mathbb{R}^{3,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}
,
q>1
such that for any
(\mathbf{y},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{3,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}

(1.13)

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{q-1}\|P_{t}(\mathbf{y},k,\cdot)-\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\circ}(\cdot)\|_{TV}=0,

where

P_{t}(\mathbf{y},k,\cdot)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y}}((\mathbf{Y}(t),\xi(t))\in\cdot)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\leq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}\left[V^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(\zeta))+C^{\backprime}\right]\right]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\leq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}q\frac{\beta\lambda}{2}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}-\zeta)V^{q-1}(\mathbf{Y}(\zeta))\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\leq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}(V(\mathbf{y})+\widetilde{M}(\zeta))^{q}+C^{\backprime}\right]

\displaystyle-q\frac{\beta\lambda}{2}T^{\backprime}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\leq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}(V(\mathbf{y})+\widetilde{M}(\zeta))^{q-1}\right]

\displaystyle\text{ (due to \eqref{e9-lm3.3})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\leq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}\left(V^{q}(\mathbf{y})-\frac{q\frac{\beta\lambda}{2}T^{\backprime}}{2}V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+q\widetilde{M}(\zeta)V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+C^{\backprime}(|\widetilde{M}(\zeta)|^{q}+1)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\text{ ( thanks to \eqref{lm3.0-e3} ).}

If
T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\leq\zeta\leq T^{\backprime}n^{\backprime}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}V^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}))\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}V^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(\zeta))+C^{\backprime}\right]

\displaystyle+qC_{1}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}-\zeta)V^{q-1}(\mathbf{Y}(\zeta))\right]

(by applying (
3.9
) and the strong Markov property)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}[(V(\mathbf{y})+\widetilde{M}(\zeta)-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{4}\zeta)^{q}+C^{\backprime}]\right]

\displaystyle+qC_{1}T^{\backprime}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}(V(\mathbf{y})+\widetilde{M}(\zeta)-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{4}\zeta)^{q-1}\right]

(in view of (
3.13
))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}\left(V^{q}(\mathbf{y})-q\frac{\alpha_{0}}{4}\zeta V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+q\widetilde{M}(\zeta)V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+C^{\backprime}\left(|\widetilde{M}(\zeta)|+1\right)^{q}\right)\right]

\displaystyle+2qC_{1}T^{\backprime}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}\left(V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+|\widetilde{M}(\zeta)|^{q-1}\right)\right]

(using (
3.1
) and the inequality
|x+y|^{q-1}\leq 2(|x|^{q-1}+|y|^{q-1})
)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{\zeta\geq T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1)\}}\left(V^{q}(\mathbf{y})-\frac{q\beta\lambda T^{\backprime}}{4}V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+q\widetilde{M}(\zeta)V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+C^{\backprime}\left(|\widetilde{M}(\zeta)|+1\right)^{q}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\text{ (because }\frac{\alpha_{0}}{4}\zeta\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{4}T^{\backprime}(n^{\backprime}-1))\geq(2C_{1}+\frac{\beta\lambda}{4})T^{\backprime}).

(3.15)

As a result, by adding (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) and noting that
\mathbb{E}_{x}\widetilde{M}(\zeta)=0
, we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}V^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}))\leq

\displaystyle V^{q}(\mathbf{y})-q\frac{\beta\lambda}{4}T^{\backprime}V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+C^{\backprime}\mathbb{E}_{x}(|\widetilde{M}(\zeta)|+1)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle V^{q}(\mathbf{y})-q\frac{\beta\lambda}{4}T^{\backprime}V^{q-1}(\mathbf{y})+C^{\backprime}U^{q}(\mathbf{y})

where the inequality
\mathbb{E}_{x}(|\widetilde{M}(\zeta)|+1)^{q}\leq C^{\backprime}U^{q}(\mathbf{y})
comes from an application of
the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy Inequality, Hölder’s inequality and (
3.8
) and (
1.6
).

From (
1.5
), we have

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}U^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}))\leq

\displaystyle U^{q}(\mathbf{y})-\left(1-e^{-k_{2q}n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}}\right)U^{q}(\mathbf{y})+\frac{k_{1q}}{k_{2q}}.

Combining (
3.16
) and (
3.17
), we can easily get that

(3.18)

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[V^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}))+CU^{q}(\mathbf{Y}(n^{\backprime}T^{\backprime}))\right]\leq V^{q}(\mathbf{y})+CU^{q}(\mathbf{y})-\kappa^{\backprime}[V^{q}(\mathbf{y})+CU^{q}(\mathbf{y})]^{(q-1)/q}+C^{\backprime},

for some
\kappa^{\backprime}>0,C^{\backprime}>0
and sufficiently large

.
∎

4.
Nonlinear Perturbed models

As a novelty, we also wanted to explore what happens when the white noise perturbations which influence (
1.1
) are nonlinear.
More specifically, assume that the dynamics is given by

(4.1)

\displaystyle dS(t)

\displaystyle=[D(S_{0}-S(t))-\mu_{1}(\xi(t))S(t)X(t)]dt+(\sigma_{1}(\xi(t))+\sigma_{4}(\xi(t))S(t))S(t)dW_{1}(t)

\displaystyle dX(t)

\displaystyle=[\mu_{2}(\xi(t))S(t)X(t)-D(\xi(t))X(t)-h_{2}(\xi(t))X(t)P(t)]dt+(\sigma_{2}(\xi(t))+\sigma_{5}(\xi(t)X(t))X(t)dW_{2}(t)

\displaystyle dP(t)

\displaystyle=[D(\xi(t))(P_{0}-P(t))-h_{3}(\xi(t))X(t)P(t)]dt+(\sigma_{3}(\xi(t))+\sigma_{6}(\xi(t))P(t))P(t)dW_{3}(t)

where
(W_{1}(t),W_{2}(t),W_{3}(t))
is a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
\mathbf{Y}(t):=(S(t),X(t),P(t))
and let
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3,\circ}
denote the initial conditions, that is
\mathbf{Y}(0):=(S(0),X(0),P(0))=\mathbf{y}
. We will use the framework developed in
[
15
]
which allows to treat processes and auxiliary variables. This way, the process can be in Kolmogorov form while the auxiliary variables can be in non-Kolmogorov form.

Specifically, we assume that
(S(t),P(t),\alpha(t))
is the auxiliary variable and we focus on the extinction and persistence of the process
X(t)
.
Letting
\widetilde{U}(\mathbf{y})=(s+x+p)^{\frac{1}{2}}

we have

(4.2)

[{\mathcal{L}}_{2}\widetilde{U}](\mathbf{y},k)\leq\widetilde{c}_{1}-\widetilde{c}_{2}\widetilde{U}^{3}(\mathbf{y}).

We can therefore see that
[
15
, Assumption 3.1]
holds for the model
4.1
.

When
X(t)=0\,t\geq 0
, (
4.1
) reduces to the system:

(4.3)

\displaystyle d\overline{S}(t)

\displaystyle=[D(S_{0}-\overline{S}(t))]dt+(\sigma_{1}(\xi(t))+\sigma_{4}(\xi(t))\overline{S}(t))\overline{S}(t)dW_{1}(t)

\displaystyle d\overline{P}(t)

\displaystyle=D(\xi(t))(P_{0}-\overline{P}(t))dt+(\sigma_{3}(\xi(t))+\sigma_{6}(\xi(t))\overline{P}(t))\overline{P}(t)dW_{3}(t)

In view of (
4.2
), by applying Dynkin’s formula in the same manner as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y},k}\widetilde{U}(s,0,p)\leq e^{-\widetilde{c}_{2}t}\tilde{U}(s,0,p)+\frac{\widetilde{c}_{1}}{\widetilde{c}_{2}},t\geq 0,

and

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y},k}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\widetilde{U}^{5}(\overline{S}(u),0,P(u))du\leq\widetilde{c}_{1}+\frac{1}{t}\widetilde{U}(s,0,p).

As a result, the set of invariant probability measures of
(\overline{S}(t),\overline{P}(t),\xi(t))
is non empty and for any invariant probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}U^{5}(s,0,p)\boldsymbol{\nu}(s,0,p,k)\leq\widetilde{c}_{1}.

Since
U^{5}(s,0,p)=(s+p)^{\frac{5}{2}}
is
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-integrable, we can define

\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}\left(\mu_{2}(k)s-h_{2}(k)p-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}(k)}{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\nu}(dsdp,k).

While the probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is probably not unique, the value
\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})
is unique due to the following claim.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any invariant probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}
of
(\overline{S}(t),\overline{P}(t),\xi(t))
, we have

\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\left[D(k)(\xi(k)+\zeta(k))-\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}(k)}{2}\right]\pi_{k}.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to Lemma
1.1
and is therefore omitted.
∎

Because
[
15
, Assumption 3.1]
holds for model
4.1
and
\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})
is unique, we can apply Theorem 3.2, Theorem 3.4 and Theorem 3.5 of
[
15
]
to obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})>0
then
X(t)
is stochastically persistent in probability, that is
for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\Delta>0
such that

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\{X(t)\geq\Delta\}\geq 1-\varepsilon,(\mathbf{y},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{3,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}.

We also have that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\left\{\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}X(s)ds>0\right\}=1.

If
\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})<0
then for any
\varepsilon>0
,
(s,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, there exists
\delta_{s,p}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},k}\left\{\lim{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln X(t)=\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})<0\right\}\geq 1-\varepsilon

whenever
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}=(\widetilde{s},\widetilde{x},\widetilde{p})
satisfies
|\widetilde{s}-s|+|\widetilde{p}-p|+\widetilde{x}\leq\delta_{s,p}
.
Moreover, if the boundary
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\{0\}
is accessible from the interior, that is, for any
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{+},k\in\mathcal{M}
, we can find
K>0
such that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\{|S(t)|+|P(t)|\leq K\text{ and }X(t)<\varepsilon\}>0\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0,

then

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\left\{\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln X(t)=\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})<0\right\}=1,\text{ for any }(\mathbf{y},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{3,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}.

The next result gives us an easy condition which ensures almost sure extinction.

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that
\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})<0
. If there is
k_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\sigma_{2}(k_{0})\sigma_{5}(k_{0})>0
then we have

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\left\{\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln X(t)=\lambda(\boldsymbol{\nu})<0\right\}=1,\text{ for any }(\mathbf{y},k)\in\mathbb{R}^{3,\circ}_{+}\times\mathcal{M}.

Proof.

We only need to show that if
\sigma_{2}(k_{0})\sigma_{5}(k_{0})>0
then the boundary
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\{0\}
is accessible from the interior. It turns out that the accessibility of a certain region of space can be checked using deterministic control theory.
Consider the following control system associated with (
4.1
):

(4.4)

\displaystyle dS_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=[D(S_{0}-S_{u}(t))-\mu_{1}(\psi(t))S_{u}(t)X_{u}(t)+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{1}(\psi(t))+2\sigma_{4}(\psi(t))S_{u}(t))]dt

\displaystyle+(\sigma_{1}(\psi(t))+\sigma_{4}(\psi(t))S_{u}(t))S_{u}(t)\phi_{1}(t)dt

\displaystyle dX_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=[\mu_{2}(\psi(t))S_{u}(t)X_{u}(t)-D(\psi(t))X_{u}(t)-h_{2}(\psi(t))X_{u}(t)P_{u}(t)+\dfrac{1}{2}(\sigma_{2}(\psi(t))+2\sigma_{5}(\psi(t)X_{u}(t))]dt

\displaystyle+(\sigma_{2}(\psi(t))+\sigma_{5}(\psi(t)X_{u}(t))X_{u}(t)\phi_{2}(t)dt

\displaystyle dP_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=[D(\psi(t))(P_{0}-P_{u}(t))-h_{3}(\psi(t))X_{u}(t)P_{u}(t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{3}(\psi(t))+2\sigma_{6}(\psi(t))P_{u}(t)]dt+(\sigma_{3}(\xi(t))+\sigma_{6}(\xi(t))P_{u}(t))P_{u}(t)\phi_{1}(t)dt

where
\phi_{i}(t),i=1,2,3
are real-valued càdlàg functions and
\psi(t)
a
\mathcal{M}
-valued càdlàg function.
In view of various support theorems, see Sections 4.3, 5.1 and 6 from and
[
4
]
, if we can find controls
u(t)=(\psi(t),\phi_{1}(t),\phi_{2}(t),\phi_{3}(t))
satisfying (
4.4
) such that

\psi(0)=k,(S_{u}(0),X_{u}(0),P_{u}(0)=\mathbf{y}\quad\text{ and }(S_{u}(T),X_{u}(T),P_{u}(T))=\mathbf{y}^{\diamond}=(s^{\diamond},x^{\diamond},p^{\diamond})

then

(4.5)

\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\left\{|\mathbf{Y}(T)-\mathbf{y}^{\diamond}|\leq\varepsilon\right\}>0\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0.

Now, we define a control as follows:
\psi(t)\equiv k_{0},t\geq 1
,

\phi_{1}(t)\equiv-\sgn(\sigma_{4}(k_{0})),\phi_{3}(t)\equiv-\sgn(\sigma_{6}(k_{0}))

and
\phi_{2}(t)
is a feedback control such that
dX_{u}(t)\leq-X_{u}(t)dt,t\geq 1
.
To be more specific, since
\sigma_{2}(k_{0})\sigma_{5}(k_{0})>0
, which leads to
\sgn(\sigma_{2}(k_{0}))\left[\sigma_{2}(k_{0})+\sigma_{5}(k_{0})X_{u}(t)\right]>|\sigma_{2}(k_{0})|
, we can select

\phi_{2}(t)=\begin{cases}-1-\mu_{2}(k_{0})S_{u}(t)-\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{2}(k_{0})+2\sigma_{5}(k_{0})X_{u}(t))&\text{ if }\sigma_{2}(k_{0})>0\\
1+\mu_{2}(k_{0})S_{u}(t)&\text{ if }\sigma_{2}(k_{0})<0.\end{cases}

Since
dX_{u}(t)\leq-X_{u}(t)dt,t\geq 1
, we have

(4.6)

\lim_{t\to\infty}X_{u}(t)=0

On the other hand, with
\psi(t)\equiv k_{0},t\geq 1
,

\phi_{1}(t)=\phi_{2}(t)=0,t\in[0,1)
and
\phi_{1}(t)=-\sgn(\sigma_{4}(k_{0})),\phi_{3}(t)=-\sgn(\sigma_{6}(k_{0})),t\geq 1
, one can see that

d[S_{u}(t)+P_{u}(t)]\leq[k_{1}-k_{2}(S_{u}(t)+P_{u}(t))^{2}]dt,t\geq 1.

This inequality implies

(4.7)

\limsup_{t\to\infty}(S_{u}(t)+P_{u}(t))\leq k_{3}:=\left(\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) and applying (
4.5
), we deduce that

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{y},k}\{|S(t)|+|P(t)|\leq k_{3}+1\text{ and }X(t)<\varepsilon\}>0\text{ for any }\varepsilon>0,

which means the boundary
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\{0\}
is accessible.
The corollary is therefore proved.
∎
