Evaluating anticancer potentials of potentized preparations in an in-vivo xenograft model
Authors: Rajesh Shah, Gitanjali Talele, Nirmal Kumar Kasinathan, Madan Barkume, Jyoti Kode
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundXenografts in immunodeficient mice play a pivotal role in testing novel anti-cancer treatments. Xenograft models expedite the drug discovery process, offering a cost-effective alternative to conventional animal models and providing essential data for clinical trials. We have followed the approach described by the Developmental Therapeutics Program of the National Cancer Institute (NCI), USA to investigate the therapeutic responses.ObjectivesIn this research, potentized preparations derived from biomaterial, referred to as nosodes, have exhibited promising effectiveness against cancer in laboratory experiments. This study seeks to further substantiate these findings by employing animal models.MethodPotentized preparations from category nosodes sourced from biomaterials of HIV, Cancer tissue, Hepatitis C and a combination underwent testing within the NCI's preclinical evaluation protocols using Xenograft models (HOP62). All the experimental mice were randomly assigned to one of six groups (n = 6), including vehicle and positive controls. These preparations were administered orally at a dosage of 0.1 ml, five days a week, over a four-week period. The mice were closely monitored at regular intervals for 32 days, with observations regarding changes in body weight, tumor volume, morbidity, and mortality. Relative tumor volume (RTV) was calculated as the tumor volume on the day of measurement divided by the tumor volume on day 1.ResultsThe groups treated with Hepatitis C 30c and HIV 100c nosodes have not shown effect with respect to Relative Tumor Volume (RTV). Evidence of significant tumor regression was observed for RTV on day 30 in groups treated with HIV nosode 30c (P = 0.002), and Cancer nosode 30c (P = 0.005). Percentage Survival was noted better in HIV nosode 30c treated group from day 25, however, in other groups survival percentage remained constant. Varied animal body weight in all groups was noted. Significant differences in tumor volume with respect to time in all treated groups were observed.ConclusionResults are suggestive of tumor regression which is encouraging to undertake further clinical trials to explore the anticancer potential of HIV nosode and Cancer nosode.

Introduction

In the xenograft model, immune-deficient or immunocompromised mice are used where human cancer cells are transplanted into organs or under the skin. In this model, the mice easily accept the xenograft that comes from human cancer cells [1].

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has used a xenograft model to examine the response to cancer therapy [2,3]. This model allows the use of actual human tissue and the therapeutic activity may be studied in a few weeks. We used this model for our study.

Potentized preparations derived from diseased tissues or microbes have shown efficacy against the diseases like HIV, Hepatitis C and cancer[[4], [5], [6]]. Gaurisankar Sa et al. [7] have shown regression of cancer tumors using potentized medicines. Our previous work using nosodes has shown anti-cancer activity in in vitro assays [[8], [9], [10]]. In our prior in vitro research, we observed significant growth inhibition at 50% (GI50) and complete growth suppression (TGI) in sulforhodamine B (SRB) cytotoxicity assays when using HIV, Hepatitis C, and Cancer nosodes at concentrations below 10 μg/ml [10].

This work is a further step in the animal model to validate our previous results. HOP-62 (lung adenocarcinoma) xenograft model which represents lung cancer was used in this study [11,12].

Materials and methods

HIV nosode was developed from the biomaterial containing HIV type I and type II viruses [13], and Hepatitis C nosode from HCV type I and type III viruses [14]. The Cancer nosode was developed from cancer tissues [15]. All preparations were made as per the HPI (Homoeopathy Pharmacopeia of India) guidelines and other relevant protocols. Cancer nosode was prepared using various cancer tissues, including Squamous cell carcinoma (skin) and Adenocarcinoma (rectum), among others. Potentization is a method of exerting ten serial succussions in a bottle containing one part of the source material (organic or inorganic) with ninety-nine parts (1:99) of non-medicated vehicle (alcohol or water) vigorously succussing the solution at each step, making it 1c (one centesimal, at 10-2) potency. One part of 1c is mixed again with ninety-nine parts, undergoing the same agitation process, making it 2c potency (10−4). Likewise, 3c (10−6), 4c (10−8), up to 30c (10−60) potency, and more are prepared. Adriamycin was used as a positive control and water for injection was used as vehicle control.

Xenograft in-vivo method

After positive leads from the in vitro analysis, in-vivo anti-cancer activity was evaluated against the lung cancer mice model. We evaluated the effectiveness of the in-vivo xenograft model HOP-62. The study was divided into four groups (Two slots of 4 groups; study 490 and 493). The body weight and tumor volume were measured every alternate day till the experimental duration. The animals were maintained in cages with filter paper covers under atmospheric conditions. Cages, covers, bedding, food and water were changed, and sterilized weekly. Animals were handled in a sterile manner.

All procedures involving mice were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee, ACTREC, Tata Memorial Centre, Navi Mumbai (Proposal # 1/2015) and were adhered to CPCSEA guidelines (Registration Number: 65/GO/ReBiBt/S/99/CPCSEA). Six to eight-week-old in-house bred immunodeficient NOD-SCID mice were included. Animals that were aged above and below six to eight weeks, and did not satisfy the body weight, tumor size criteria were excluded from the study. Animals received human care and all efforts were undertaken to minimize animal suffering before and during the experiments.

In-vivo efficacy experiment design

The anticipated propagation of the HOP-62 tumor was achieved at The Advanced Centre for Treatment, Research and Education in Cancer (ACTREC). The study was done in two slots, one was numbered 490 and another 493. In study 490, tumor transplants were done on May 7, 2021, the experiment started on June 1, 2021 and ended on June 30, 2021. In study 493, tumor transplants were done on June 21, 2021, the experiment started on July 19, 2021 and ended on August 19, 2021.

For evaluation of in-vivo antitumor efficacy, experiment was conducted according to the method described previously [11,12,16]. 1 million Human HOP-62 cells were injected subcutaneously per mouse and observed for growth of tumors. When average tumor volume reached approx. 50–60 mm3, all the mice were randomized into desired groups for the study (n = 6/group) before the start of the treatment (Table 1). A sample size of six animals per group was considered as per the NCI criteria, the minimum required to allow statistical significance. A total of 48 animals were used in this study. In study 490, the desired groups comprised tumor-bearing control (Group A), Positive control (Adriamycin: Group B), HIV nosode 30c treated (Group C), and Cancer nosode 30c treated (Group D). The dosing protocol for Groups C and D was 0.1 ml 5 days a week for 4 weeks orally, Group B positive control group dose and frequency was 2.5 mg/kg once a week for 4 weeks. In study 493, the vehicle control group (Group A), Hepatitis C nosode 30c treated (Group B), HIV nosode and Cancer nosode combination treated (Group C), and HIV nosode 100c treated (Group D). All the study groups were administered the preparations at the dose of 0.1 ml, 5 days a week for 4 weeks. The mice were observed at regular intervals for 32 days for body weight, tumor volume, morbidity, and mortality. The data were represented as Relative Tumor Volume (RTV) in cc, T/C (ratio of test versus control), and survival. Tumor volume was calculated as per NCI protocol described elsewhere [11,12,16] using the formula [(w1)2 × w2) × (π/6)], where w1 and w2 were the smallest and the largest tumor diameter (cm), respectively. RTV was measured as tumor volume on the day of measurement/tumor volume on day 1. The T/C ratio indicates antitumor effectiveness. Study design.

T = mean tumor volume of the drug-treated group; RTV = mean tumor volume of the drug-treated group on the study day of interest – mean tumor volume of the drug-treated group on the initial day of dosing; C = mean tumor volume of the control group, as per the NCI, USA guidelines.

Randomization and blinding

A simple randomization method of randomly grouping the animals was adopted. Medicines were assigned codes as HIV nosode 30c (IMAD101), Cancer nosode 30c (CANA210, Ca-Nosode), Hepatitis C nosode 30c (HCNA110), HIV nosode 100c (ADMI100), and a combination of HIV nosode 30c and Cancer nosode 30c (COMB330). The blind was maintained throughout the study and decoding was done after data analysis.

Endpoint measurement

Tumor volume, survival, and body weight were recorded. As per the NCI guidelines [1,2], biological activity was considered highly significant if T/C ≤ 0.2 and significant activity in case of T/C ≤ 0.42. (10,11)

Mortality and weight loss ≥4 g/mouse was considered to indicate toxicity. Relative Tumor Volume (RTV) was measured as Tumor Volume on the day of measurement/Tumor Volume on day 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance P value was calculated between the four groups tested, i.e. tumor-bearing control, ADR group, HIV nosode 30c, and Cancer nosode 30c group by One-way ANOVA test and Box-Whisker plot for evaluation of in-vivo animal experiment data.

Safety

The nosodes dosage regime was well tolerated by all the mice studied. No toxicity and mortality in any nosode-treated groups were noted, however, two mice reported mortality in the Adriamycin group on day 25.

Discussion

Khuda-Bukhsh et al. [17], Gauri Sa et al. [7], and Banerji et al. [18] have documented anticancer activities of potentized medicines. Our previous study [8] has documented that HIV 30c nosode has anti-cancer therapeutic potential. The study had shown cytotoxic effects on A549 cells as revealed by morphological changes like shrinkage of cell and blebbing of the cell membrane. Other molecular mechanisms observed were DNA fragmentation, ROS generation, MMP, and its inhibitory action on cell proliferation. In A549 cells treated with HIV nosode have shown significant apoptotic cell death, reduced A-549 cell growth, and changes in levels of Top II in the cellular nucleus.

In this study, we studied tumor regression and significant differences relative to tumor volume on day 30 in groups treated with HIV nosode 30c (IMAD, P = 0.002) and Cancer nosode 30c (CANA, P = 0.005, suggesting a control of tumor progression. Hepatitis C nosode did not exhibit any changes. In our earlier study, HIV 30c, Cancer nosode, and Hepatitis C nosode have shown the anticancer activity on the A549 cell line measured as percentage growth inhibition in the SRB assays [10].

HIV proteins can act alone or in collaboration with other known oncoproteins, specifically originating from the oncogenic human viruses such as human hepatitis B and C viruses, and human papilloma viruses of high carcinogenic risk, which cause the bulk of malignancies in people living with HIV-1 on ART [19]. Compared with the general population, people infected with HIV are currently about 500 times more likely to be diagnosed with Kaposi sarcoma, 12 times more likely to be diagnosed with non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and, among women, 3 times more likely to be diagnosed with cervical cancer. HIV proteins, namely envelope protein gp120, accessory protein negative factor Nef, matrix protein p17, transactivator of transcription Tat and reverse transcriptase RT, are known to be oncogenic per se, to induce oxidative stress and to be released from the infected or expressing cells [19].

Oncogenic substances need to be explored for their anti-cancer properties in nano-dose as per the law of Similars used in homeopathy. Our previous studies with potentized oncogenic materials HIV virus, Hepatitis C virus, Cancer Nosode and Alcohol [10] make an encouraging basis for scientifically supporting the hypothesis, which will need future support by more experiments. Our ongoing studies (under publication) have shown leads in this direction.

On the new drug development path, it is commonly observed that many drugs showing in-vitro activity fail to produce similar efficacy in animal models, which may be due to a lack of knowledge about the dose-schedule and sensitivity of the model. We may have to work to optimize the dose, potency, frequency, and selection of the target model in future studies.

It is interesting to note that HIV nosode and Cancer nosode showed efficacy individually, however, did not show efficacy when combined. This may suggest that the body may have different response ability to single drug versus complex drugs, requiring further exploration. It may be noted that there were no deaths in the nosode-treated (and untreated vehicle control) groups, while two mice did not survive in the Adriamycin-treated group beyond day 25, indicating better survival in the nosode-treated groups.

The need for alternative and safer anti-cancer therapeutics cannot be overemphasized. This study's findings, reassuring our initial anti-cancer potentials demonstrated with the same drugs in in-vitro studies, encourage further research.

Conclusion

Our results suggest control of tumor progression which is encouraging enough to undertake further clinical trials to explore the anticancer potential of HIV nosode and Cancer nosode.
