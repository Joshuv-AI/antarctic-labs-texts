Applicability of Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Experiment in Analyzing Pore and Fluid Distribution Characteristics of Tight Sandstone: A Case Study in the Julu Area, Bohai Bay Basin, China
Authors: Zhenfei Jiang, Yanming Zhu, Peng Li, Yang Wang, Jie Xiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Characterizing the pore and fluid distribution is critical for evaluating the reservoir potential of new areas. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is considered as an experimental method capable of full-scale characterization of pore characteristics. However, the T2 spectrum of a saturated sample is affected by a combination of sample and experimental parameters, and it is important to confirm whether the T2 spectrum fully reflects the sample pore information. Eight tight sandstone samples from the Julu area were selected for thin section identification, mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP), NMR, NMR cryoporometry (NMRC), and centrifugation experiments to critically analyze the applicability of the NMR results. Two methods, the similarity method and Kozeny’s equation method, were used to calculate surface relaxivity, a critical parameter for converting NMR T2 signals into pore information. The discussion focuses on the applicability of the calculated surface relaxivity and the phenomenon of T2 signal changes in a short relaxation range after centrifugation. The main results are as follows: The surface relaxivity values calculated using the different methods differed significantly. The surface relaxivity calculated using the same method reflected the relative magnitude of the true surface relaxivity of the samples. For the samples with large surface relaxivity, there may be partial misses of the short relaxation signal, the NMR porosity was smaller than the gas-measured porosity, there was a variation in the T2 spectrum in the short relaxation range after centrifugation, and the calculated surface relaxivity was small. The surface relaxivity calculated using Kozeny’s equation was nearly accurate, but perhaps smaller than the true value. The T2 spectra mainly reflected macropore information. This study suggests that PSDs converted from T2 spectra of saturated samples should be judged with relative caution rather than solely based on the peak or range correspondence between the two curves, and the minimum centrifugal radius can be used as a constraint.

Introduction

Tight sandstone gas is a significant unconventional gas that has been well explored and developed in several areas.1,2 The coal measure sandstones in the Julu area of the Linqing Depression are well developed and have considerable exploration potential. Analyzing the pore and fluid distribution characteristics is critical for evaluating the storage and seepage capacity of the sandstones in the new area.3−6 Tight sandstones typically contain both nanoscale and micron-scale pores.7 There are many methods for pore characterization, and each method has a unique principle and scope of application. For example, thin section identification, computerized tomography (CT) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) visually obtain information on sample compositions and pores.8,9 However, these methods are often limited to qualitative or semiquantitative judgments because of the choice of the field of view and the problem of representativeness.10,11 Low-temperature nitrogen adsorption (LTNA) experiments are commonly used to test samples for nanoscale pores in the pore size range of 2–200 nm; however, there are often artificial peaks in the small pore range.11,12 Nuclear magnetic resonance cryoporometry (NMRC) experiments can detect a much larger range of nanopores (2–1000 or 2–500 nm) than LTNA can, although the test range is constrained by the probe fluid and instrumentation.13−16 Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) experiments can reflect both nanoscale and micron-scale pores. However, the measurement of small pores was affected by the mercury injection volume and saturation, and the measurement of large pores can be affected by the narrow pore throat (pore shielding effect), both with some errors.10,17 Therefore, different methods have different advantages and disadvantages, and it is appropriate to adopt a joint perspective to analyze the multiscale pores of the sample in detail.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is considered as a nondestructive experiment that can also probe both nanopores and micrometer pores.18 However, translating the NMR experimental results into pore information requires a key parameter: surface relaxivity. In the literature, the surface relaxivity is usually replaced directly with an empirical value or calculated using a similarity method. For example, because the inverse cumulative curve of the mercury injection saturation is similar to the inverse cumulative curve of the T2 spectrum, their correlation is used to calculate the surface relaxivity.19,20 The peak of the PSD for the small pore range obtained from LTNA fits that of the T2 spectrum to calculate the surface relaxivity.21,22 The surface relaxivity is calculated by directly comparing the semiquantitative peak information obtained from CT or SEM scans with the peak of the T2 spectrum.23,24 The advantage of the similarity method is the ability to visually and easily compare the T2 spectrum of NMR with the PSD curves obtained by other test methods. In the centrifugation method, surface relaxivity is obtained from the correlation between the T2 cutoff value and the pore radius corresponding to the centrifugal force.25,26 The centrifugation method is more suitable for samples with a higher seepage capacity. In addition, surface relaxivity can be calculated using the Kozeny equation method, which calculates the surface relaxivity based on the relationship among porosity, permeability, and pore radius.27 The advantage of this method is that the relative magnitude of the surface relaxivity of the samples can be conveniently obtained by using basic physical parameters. Besides, the digital petrophysics method, experimental parameter method, and diffusion method have also been used to determine the surface relaxivity.28−30

However, as mentioned above, each pore detection method has a range of applicability and has such an effect when calculating the surface relaxivity. For example, accurately measuring large pores using MIP experiments is difficult, owing to the pore shielding effect. When comparing the inverse cumulative mercury injection curve with the T2 spectrum, the calculated surface relaxivity may be smaller than the true value. Furthermore, the original results of MIP are often affected by matrix compression effects, which can also affect the calculation of surface relaxivity.31 The surface relaxivity of the same sample calculated by different methods varies, and the transformation of T2 spectra to PSDs is nonunique.22,27,32 The reliability of the calculated results is worth analyzing. More importantly, the T2 spectra of saturated samples were often assumed to reflect the complete pore information in past studies, but in fact, the accuracy of the T2 spectra is affected by the matching of sample and experimental parameters. For example, when the sample contains many paramagnetic minerals, it may lead to an inhomogeneous internal magnetic field, which in turn triggers an off-resonance frequency and a reduction in echo amplitude and relaxation time.33 The effects of the diffusive pore coupling cannot be neglected for samples with complex pore structures and fast relaxation components.34−36 Furthermore, too large an echo time (TE) setting can lead to a partial miss of the short relaxation signal corresponding to the small pore,37,38 which can affect the calculation of surface relaxivity. The true surface relaxivities of the different samples vary, indicating that the appropriate experimental parameters for the sample are also different. Therefore, it is important to identify whether the T2 spectrum of a saturated sample is complete and accurate. The peak and range correspondence of T2 spectra from NMR experiments and PSDs obtained by other methods has often been used in past studies to illustrate the reliability of T2 spectral conversion results. However, this good correspondence can come from artificial overfitting. More constraints should be attached to verify the applicability of the T2 spectrum.

NMR experiments can also analyze the movable fluid content and distribution characteristics of a sample in conjunction with centrifugation experiments. In the literature, the T2 spectrum of a saturated sample was divided into a bound fluid part and a movable fluid part based on the T2 cutoff value.39−41 However, there remained some movable fluids distributed in the T2 spectrum below the T2 cutoff value.42 Two T2 cutoff values have been used to divide the T2 spectrum into fully movable, partially movable, and fully immovable parts.43,44 The movable fluid is mainly distributed in large pores and pores controlled by large pore throats, and the bound fluid is present in small pores and large pores controlled by small pore throats.45 Centrifugal force controls the theoretical minimum pore radius corresponding to the moving fluid.25,46 However, the T2 signal changes completely in the short relaxation range after the centrifugation of some samples.47−50 This phenomenon suggests that the short relaxation signal of the T2 spectra of these samples may reflect macropore information to some extent. It might be possible to analyze the applicability of T2 in terms of the lower limit of the centrifugal radius.

In this study, eight tight sandstone samples from the Julu area were selected, and thin section identification, MIP, NMRC, and NMR experiments were conducted. We critically analyzed the applicability of the NMR results in terms of surface relaxivity, the difference between NMR porosity and gas-measured porosity, and the lower limits of movable fluids. First, the comparative results of PSDs obtained from NMRC and MIP were used as the basis for the analysis of the T2 spectra from NMR. Subsequently, two methods, the similarity method and Kozeny’s equation method, were used to calculate surface relaxivity. The applicability of the surface relaxivity calculated by different methods was discussed. The pore and fluid distribution ranges measured by the NMR experiments were discussed based on a fixed TE (0.13 ms) and samples with different surface relaxivities. Finally, the influencing factors and practical applications of the movable fluid content were briefly analyzed. The reservoir potential of the samples was evaluated based on the results of this discussion. This study objectively reflects the applicability of various experiments and the preliminary evaluation of the reservoir potential of the new area.

Geological Background

The study area is located southwest of the Bohai Bay Basin and west of the Linqing Depression (Figure 1a,b). The Linqing Depression is a fault sub-basin located in southwestern Bohai Bay Basin and formed in Mesozoic-Cenozoic,51 and several source rock reservoirs are developed in this area.19,52 The study area consists of two secondary tectonic units: the Julu syncline and the Guangzong anticline (Figure 1c). Several NE-trending normal faults are present. The Shanxi and Taiyuan formations in the area are coal-measure strata with a gas potential. The strata depth is small in the east and large in the west.

The sampling site is located in the eastern part of the study area, and the burial depth is shallow. A vertical series of samples was collected, eight of which were selected for this study (Figure 1d). The analysis of the shallow samples provides guidance for the exploration and development of deep strata in the study area.

Sample and Experiment

The eight samples selected for this study were all core samples. Samples S1, S2, S3, and S4 were collected from the Shanxi Formation, and samples S5, S6, S7, and S8 were collected from the Taiyuan Formation. Cylindrical samples were drilled from each core sample for porosity, permeability, NMR, and centrifugation experiments (Figure 2). The cylindrical samples were then dried and crushed into small blocks and powder samples for the MIP and NMRC experiments, respectively, and parallel samples were selected for casting thin sections.

Porosity and Permeability

The samples used for the porosity and permeability tests were cylindrical (5 cm in length and 2.5 cm in diameter). The instrument used for the porosity tests was the QKX-II helium porosity tester, according to the Chinese Oil and Gas Industry standard (GB/T 29172–2012). The instrument used for the permeability tests was a PDP-200 pressure pulse permeameter, an unsteady method with a test range of 0.00001–0.1 mD. The measurement process was fully automated, and the data acquisition system automatically collected data.

MIP

The instrument used for the MIP experiments was the AUTOPORE 9500, according to China’s standard (GB/T 21650.1–2008). The samples used for the MIP experiments were block samples with 1 cm3 in volume. The experimental pressure range in this study was 0.0086–413.44 MPa, and the corresponding pore throat radius was 85.94–0.0018 μm. NMRC

NMRC12–010 V, manufactured by Niumag Corporation Ltd., was used for the NMRC experiments. The samples used in the experiments were 40–60 mesh powder samples saturated with water for 24 h in a vacuum saturation device. The experimental temperature gradually increased from −35.0 to −0.1 °C, and the accuracy of the instrument, NMRC12–010 V, was 0.1 °C. The Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) sequence was used for the tests, and the key parameters of the sequence were waiting time (TW) = 2000 ms, echo number (NECH) = 3000, and number of scans (NS) = 64. NMR

The instrument used for the NMR experiments was a MesoMR23–060H–I instrument manufactured by Niumag Corporation Ltd., China. It had a permanent magnet with a resonance frequency of 23.4 MHz. The samples used for the NMR experiments were cylindrical (5 cm in length and 2.5 cm in diameter). The transverse relaxation time (T2) of fluid in pores was measured using the CPMG sequence, and the key parameters of the sequence were TW (3000 ms), NECH (8000), NS (64), and TE (0.13 ms). The experimental procedure was as follows: (1) the samples were placed in an oven to dry at 100 °C for 24 h, removed from the oven, and weighed. Each dry sample was placed in the NMR instrument to detect the T2 signal. (2) All of the dry samples were then placed in a vacuum pressure saturation device and vacuumed for 4 h. Next, the samples were saturated with distilled water for 24 h (water pressure of 15 MPa) and weighed after being removed from the device. Each saturated sample was put into an NMR instrument to detect the T2 signal. The cumulative NMR signal of the saturated samples was converted into porosity by the standard sample scale. (3) Next, each saturated sample was placed in the centrifuge, and the centrifuge rotation speed was set at 4700 rpm. After 2 h of centrifugation, the sample was removed and weighed. The centrifuged samples were placed in the NMR instrument one by one to detect the T2 signal.

Results

Lithology and Physical Properties

The lithological and physical characteristics of the samples are given in Tables 1 and 2. The thin section identification can reflect information on mineral composition and micrometer-scale pores (Figure 3). The lithology of the samples was mainly feldspar debris sandstone followed by debris sandstone. The main rock components were quartz, feldspar, and debris, with an average content of 41.4, 9.4, and 29.6%, respectively. There were several types of rock debris, including schist, phyllite, mudstone, and quartzite. Some rock debris was replaced by calcite, and some rock debris was distributed between the particles in the matrix state. The clay content of the samples was low and the clay minerals were predominantly kaolinite and Illite present as matrix and cement. Among them, samples S1 and S8 had relatively high clay contents of 5 and 12%. The rock cement was mainly calcite, siderite, and clay minerals, with an average content of 15%. The cement of samples S1 and S6 was mainly siderite, and the cement of samples S2 and S3 was mainly calcite, with 38 and 27% calcite content, respectively. The particle roundness was angular and subangular, and the sorting ranged from poor to medium. The areal porosities of the samples were low, approximately 1%. The intergranular pores of the samples were fewer, and the intragranular dissolution pores were developed. Sample S7 exhibited microcracks (Figure 3g). The gas-measured permeability and porosity of the samples were 0.00781 × 10–3 μm2 and 5.17%, respectively, indicating that the samples were tight sandstone. In addition, the porosity and permeability were not significantly correlated, indicating that the pore throat system of the sample was complex.

Discussion

PSD Characteristics

Comparison of PSDs Obtained from the MIP and NMRC Experiments

Analysis of the PSD of samples is useful for assessing the dominant pore size range of different stratigraphic reservoirs and, thus, accurately evaluates the resource potential. The PSD of the sample can be obtained directly from the NMRC and MIP experiments, and PSDs between 2 and 500 nm can be compared. The results show that the pore volumes obtained from NMRC were typically larger than those obtained from MIP, which may be related to the sample specifications and experimental principles. The samples used for MIP were in block form, and the samples used for NMRC were in powder form, which may have resulted in previously closed pores being detected by NMRC.60,61 In addition, mercury is a nonwetting probe that requires extreme pressure to enter the sample, and the mercury saturation in the experiment was generally less than 100%. For example, the saturation of sample S1 was only 49.4%, indicating that there are numerous undetected pores.

Although the pore volumes of the samples measured using NMRC and MIP differed, the measured PSDs were comparable. Figure 7 shows the PSDs of the different samples measured by NMRC and MIP. The results indicate that the PSDs obtained from NMRC can be mainly classified as unimodal and multipeaked types, and the PSDs obtained from MIP can be mainly classified as narrow unimodal, bimodal, and broad unimodal types. Additionally, the larger the values of the mercury injection volume (V1) and saturation (Sa), the more comparable the PSDs measured by MIP and NMRC. The PSDs of sample S1 measured by MIP and NMRC were approximately unimodal, with a distinct primary peak, and the primary peak of the PSD measured by MIP corresponds to the secondary peak of the PSD measured by NMRC (Figure 7a). The PSD of sample S2 measured by MIP was approximately narrow unimodal with a distinct primary peak, and that measured by NMRC was multipeaked, with the primary peak of the former corresponding to the secondary peak of the latter (Figure 7b). The results for samples S1 and S2 show that the PSDs measured by MIP misjudged the position of the main peak at low values of Sa and V1. The PSDs of samples S3, S5, and S6 measured by MIP were bimodal, and those measured by NMRC were approximately unimodal with a distinct primary peak; the left peak of the PSDs measured by MIP corresponded to the primary peak of the PSDs measured by NMRC (Figure 7c,e,f). The results for samples S3, S5, and S6 indicate that the characterization of the small pores by MIP increases in accuracy as the values of V1 and Sa increase. Moreover, the PSDs obtained from MIP are bimodal, but the proportion of the right peaks remains likely to be overestimated. The PSDs of samples S4, S7, and S8 measured by MIP were the broad unimodal type, and those measured by NMRC were multipeaked type, with good agreement in the range of 10–500 nm (Figure 7d,g,h).

Comparison of PSD curves obtained from NMRC and MIP.

Overall, although the pore volumes of the samples measured using NMRC and MIP differed, the responses of the two methods to the relative contents were comparable over the same measurement interval. The comparability of the PSD peaks obtained from the two experiments also suggests the possibility of analyzing the surface relaxivity.

Surface Relaxivity Calculated by Two Methods

The multiscale pore information on the sample can be reflected in the T2 spectra obtained from the NMR experiments. However, the conversion of the T2 spectra into PSD curves requires surface relaxivity. The comparison of the PSDs obtained from the MIP and NMRC experiments demonstrated that the responses of the two experiments to the relative pore content within 2–500 nm were similar. In addition, the first peak on the left side of the T2 spectra of the saturated samples, the main peak of the PSDs obtained from NMRC, and the peaks on the left side of the PSDs obtained from MIP show great similarities in the morphology and amplitude. Therefore, in this study, the geometric means (rg) of the pore radius in the range of the main peaks of the PSDs obtained from NMRC were calculated, considering the information on the peaks on the left side of the PSDs obtained from MIP. The geometric means (T2g-peak) of the first peak on the left side of the T2 spectra were calculated. Surface relaxivity is affected by various factors, including the paramagnetic mineral content, magnetization rate, and magnetic mineral distribution characteristics.12,32 The surface relaxivity of a sample is usually regarded as a constant, and values calculated by different methods should be similar. However, the large difference in surface relaxivity calculated by the two methods, in this case, may be related to the choice of the calculation range and the settings of the experimental parameters. In calculating the surface relaxivity, we assumed that the T2 spectra are complete and accurate. However, in fact, the T2 spectra can be influenced by both the sample and experimental parameters. For example, TE is a key parameter in NMR experiments, and its improper setting results in the T2 spectrum not reflecting the complete information on the pores of the sample.37 Too small a TE can lead to overcharacterization of the basal signal (kerogen and structural water) and this can result in an overestimate of the porosity, while too large a TE can lead to signal deficiency and a small T2 spectrum range.10,38 At the same time, when the sample contains more paramagnetic minerals and the TE is excessive, there is a non-negligible lack of T2 signal due to the internal magnetic field.33,62

As shown in the results of the NMR experiments, some samples showed large changes in signal in the short relaxation range before and after centrifugation (Figure 6), and the ϕNMR was generally smaller than the ϕhelium (Tables 1 and 4). Those results suggest that there may be incomplete testing of the short relaxation signal of the small pores in the experiments, which would lead to a large geometric mean value of T2 and a small calculated surface relaxivity. Because the values of ρnmrc were calculated from the shorter relaxation signal, the error in the value of ρnmrc will be larger than that of ρk compared to the true surface relaxivity. The values of Δρ (Δρ= ρk – ρnmrc) were not explicitly related to the permeability, and the Δρ increased with an increase in the relative surface relaxivity (Figure 9b). This finding suggests that the greater the true surface relaxivity of the sample, the higher the degree of incompleteness of the T2 short relaxation signal under the current experimental conditions. In addition, the calculated surface relaxivity is small relative to the true surface relaxivity, the error of ρnmrc is larger than that of ρk, and the difference between ρk and ρnmrc increases. The values Δρ, ρk, and ρnmrc have a positive correlation with the difference between ϕhelium and ϕNMR, and the correlation coefficients increase gradually (Figure 9c,d). This evidence further indicates that the incomplete characterization of the short relaxation signal is responsible for the errors in the calculated surface relaxivity and the smaller ϕNMR than ϕhelium.

Based on those interpretations, the following predictions were made: (1) the surface relaxivity calculated by both methods is subject to errors, the error of ρk is relatively small, and it is relatively reasonable to convert the T2 spectra into PSDs curves by ρk; and (2) the greater the true surface relaxivity of the samples, the more significant the signal change in the short relaxation range (T2 < 1 ms) before and after centrifugation, and the short relaxation signal mainly responds to large pores information (>100 nm). The two aforementioned predictions are verified next.

Comparison of PSDs Obtained from the MIP, NMRC, and NMR Experiments

The surface relaxivity calculated by using the two methods was used to convert the T2 spectra into PSD curves, as shown in Figure 10. The value of ρk was generally larger than that of ρnmrc, and using a large surface relaxivity resulted in a rightward shift of the PSD curves. The geometric means of T2 of the samples ranged from 0.46 to 3.20 ms, with an average of 1.76 ms (Table 5). The geometric means of the pore diameters calculated using ρnmrc ranged from 7.14 to 22.03 nm, with an average of 14.30 nm. The geometric means of the pore diameters calculated using ρk ranged from 94.47 to 149.33 nm, with an average of 113.59 nm. The range of PSDs calculated by using ρnmrc basically covers the range of PSDs obtained from the NMRC and MIP experiments. The first peak on the left side of the PSDs calculated using ρnmrc is consistent with the main peak of PSDs obtained from NMRC, except for sample S1 (Figure 10b–h). In addition, the first peaks on the left side of the PSDs calculated using ρk generally correspond to the secondary peaks of the PSDs obtained from NMRC. The PSD curves calculated by NMR reflect more information on the macropores than those obtained by MIP. This result may be related to the pore shielding effect observed in the MIP experiments.

Comparison of PSD curves from NMRC, MIP, and NMR (NMR-ρnmrc and NMR-ρk: PSDs were calculated by ρnmrc and ρk, respectively).

Although the PSDs calculated by ρnmrc match well with those obtained from MIP and NMRC in range and peak, this correspondence is related to the artificially selected computational data. Unlike the NMR and MIP experiments, the NMRC experiments used powder samples, which may result in additional information about small pores being reflected by NMRC.60,61 Additionally, the effective minimum pore diameter of the PSDs obtained by the NMR experiments was 0.4 nm, which is influenced by the diameters of the water molecules. However, the minimum T2 signal of sample S7 was converted into pore diameters 0.067 and 0.28 nm through ρnmrc and ρk, respectively. This result may be related to the skeleton signal but is more likely to have occurred because the calculated surface relaxivity is smaller than the true surface relaxivity. More importantly, the short relaxation signal of the T2 spectra of the samples changed significantly before and after centrifugation (Figure 6), suggesting that the radius of pores in this range may be larger than the minimum centrifugal radius (108 nm) rather than mesopores or micropores. Moreover, the PSDs calculated by ρk, although not consistent with those obtained from NMRC in the small pore range (<10 nm), characterize additional detailed information about the large pores (>10 μm). The micrographs show the presence of pores >10 μm in most of the samples (Figure 3). Therefore, for the samples in this study, the PSDs were more reasonably calculated by ρk and broadly reflected the relative dominance range of the larger pores.

The combined comparison of the PSDs curves obtained from the NMRC, MIP, and NMR (ρk) experiments, respectively, showed that the amplitude of the PSDs curves obtained from NMR (ρk) was not as good as that from NMRC in the small pore range. The NMRC results showed that the pores were equally developed in a range of less than 10 nm, which has a certain potential for gas storage. The primary peak of the PSDs obtained from MIP and NMR (ρk) was distributed in the range of 10–100 nm. The PSDs obtained from NMR (ρk) are more reliable in characterizing large pores (>100 nm) than those obtained from MIP. Notably, even the PSDs calculated by ρk are subject to errors, shown by the difference between the PSD curves of the saturated samples and those of the centrifuged samples within a range smaller than the minimum centrifugal radius (108 nm). This difference indicates that the true surface relaxivity should be greater than ρk and that the PSD curves transformed by the T2 spectra should still be shifted to the right. Because the true surface relaxivity of samples S2 and S7 may be relatively small, the degree of off-resonance frequency of the T2 spectra is small, or TE is relatively appropriate. In addition, the true surface relaxivity of other samples may be large, the short relaxation signal is poorly tested, resulting in a small calculated surface relaxivity. Therefore, although the peak and range of the PSDs obtained from the MIP, NMRC, and NMR experiments can be well corresponded by artificial data fitting, the T2 spectra of the present NMR experiments more accurately reflected the information on macropores and poorly characterized micropores and mesopores. This finding is also confirmed by the distribution characteristics of the movable fluid.

Distribution Characteristics and Influencing Factors of Movable Fluid

Distribution Characteristics of Movable Fluid

The distribution characteristics of the movable fluid can be reflected by the difference between the T2 spectra of the saturated samples and the T2 spectra of the centrifuged samples. The distribution curves for the movable fluid can be divided into fully movable, partially movable, and approximately immovable parts (Figure 11). The approximately immovable maximum boundary value refers to the maximum T2 value in the short relaxation range (<1 ms), corresponding to a proportional difference of less than 0.001 between the T2 spectra of the saturated and centrifuged samples. It was set up to exclude some of the inversion errors and highlight the differences in the T2 spectra of the movable fluid in the short relaxation range (<1 ms) from the different samples (Figure 11).

Distribution characteristics of the movable fluid (R-ρnmrc and R-ρk: the ratios of the content of movable fluid below the values of T2l-ρnmrc and T2l-ρk to the porosity of the movable fluid, respectively).

The T2 spectra of the movable fluids can be broadly divided into two categories. The first type includes samples S1, S3, S4, S5, S6, and S8, whose T2 spectra include completely movable, partially movable, and approximately immovable parts, with a main peak in the short relaxation range (<1 ms). The second type includes samples S2 and S7, whose T2 spectra include partially movable and approximately immovable parts, with a main peak in the relatively long relaxation range (>1 ms). The T2 geometric means of the movable fluid in the samples ranged from 0.28 to 48 ms, with an average value of 10 ms (Table 5). The T2 geometric means of the movable fluid are smaller in the first type of sample than in the second type. There were differences in the continuity of the primary and secondary peaks in the T2 spectra of the movable fluid of the first type. For example, the T2 spectrum of the movable fluid of sample S4 has a significantly poorer continuity of the primary and secondary peaks than does sample S5 (Figure 11d,e). In addition, the presence of negative peaks in the T2 spectra of the movable fluid of samples S7 and S8 indicates an increase rather than a decrease in the fluid signal after centrifugation (Figure 11g,h), which may be related to the ink-type bottle pores, bound water in the walls of large pores, or the fluid redistributed in the complex pores during centrifugation.25,63

The T2 cutoff values (the red lines in Figure 11) varied between the samples. There remained a significant amount of movable fluid below the T2 cutoff values. The centrifugal force for the experiments was 1.33 MPa, which corresponded to a centrifugal radius of 108 nm. The centrifugal radius can be converted from ρnmrc and ρk for the samples to the T2 values (T2l-ρnmrc and T2l-ρk, the black and blue lines in Figure 11, respectively) as the theoretical lower limit of the movable fluid. The values of T2l-ρnmrc are generally smaller than those of T2l-ρk. However, there was still some movable fluid below the T2l-ρk values. The ratios (R-ρnmrc and R-ρk) of the content of the movable fluid below the values of T2l-ρnmrc and T2l-ρk to the ϕnmrm of the samples were calculated (Figure 11a and Table 5). With the exception of samples S2 and S7, the values of R-ρnmrc and R-ρk were greater than 50%. The phenomenon of the existence of a completely movable fluid in the short relaxation time range has also been reported but not explained in detail.39,48,64

The results of this study suggest that this phenomenon may be related to the match between the experimental parameters and the true surface relaxivity of the samples. The TE value for the NMR experiments was 0.13 ms, between 0.06 and 0.3 ms, which is a commonly used range in the literature.10,27,38,64 As mentioned above, the surface relaxivity calculated using the same method can reflect the relative magnitude of the true surface relaxivity of the different samples. And the settings of experimental parameters largely affect the variation of T2 spectrum distribution. Predictably, when the NMR porosity is smaller than the gas-measured porosity, decreasing the TE can obtain a wider T2 spectrum, more short relaxation signals, and a reduction in the difference between the NMR porosity and the gas-measured porosity.37,38 For samples S2 and S7, whose true surface relaxivity may be relatively small, the experimental parameter TE is relatively suitable; thus, there is little movable fluid in the short relaxation time ranges and the error in T2l-ρk is relatively small (Figure 11b,g). In general, there is a large amount of movable fluid in the wide pore throats and large pore spaces corresponding to relatively long T2 relaxation times, and there is almost no movable fluid in the small pore throats and rough pore walls corresponding to relatively short T2 relaxation times. However, the movable fluid distribution curves of samples S1, S3, S4, S5, S6, and S8 indicate that a large amount of movable fluid was present in the short T2 relaxation time range (Figure 11). This phenomenon may suggest that for samples S1, S3, S4, S5, S6, and S8, whose true surface relaxivity is relatively large, the TE (0.13 ms) is too large to detect the shorter T2 signals corresponding to smaller pores. As a result, the T2 spectra obtained experimentally reflect information only for pores with radii greater than 108 nm, and the existing methods cannot translate the T2 spectra into complete, accurate PSDs for the samples. Moreover, there is a positive correlation between ρnmrc and R-ρnmrc as well as between ρk and R-ρk, respectively (Figure 12). This finding shows that for samples with a relatively large true surface relaxivity, the errors obtained from the calculations are large.

Relationship between ρnmrc and R-ρnmrc as well as between ρk and R-ρk.

Therefore, for new samples, the typical experimental parameters should be set to determine the relative size of the surface relaxivity of the samples, and then, the parameters should be adjusted according to the experimental purpose to obtain accurate information within a certain range of pores. If the long relaxation signal is well detected, the right cumulative similarity method is suitable for converting T2 spectra to PSDs; the short relaxation is well characterized, and the left cumulative similarity method is suitable for converting T2 spectra to PSDs. In addition, the literature has suggested that treating the surface relaxivity of a sample as a constant may lead to errors.29 The phenomenon of short relaxation changes after centrifugation may also represent a case of short relaxation in large pores, which is related to the inhomogeneity of the surface relaxivity or the occurrence of pore coupling. In summary, PSDs converted from T2 spectra of saturated samples should be judged with relative caution rather than solely based on the peak or range correspondence between the two curves, and the minimum centrifugal radius or other conditions can be used as constraints.

Influencing Factors and Applications of Movable Fluid Content

Despite some uncertainty in the PSDs corresponding to the T2 spectra of the samples, the NMR results were statistically significant and the content of movable fluid was relatively reliable. Except for samples S1 and S6, there was a weak negative correlation between the movable fluid content and quartz content and a positive correlation between the movable fluid content and rock debris content (Figure 13a,c). In addition, there was a weak positive correlation between the movable fluid and the feldspar content (Figure 13b). Those results may be because the quartz primarily affects the intergranular pores, and the intragranular dissolved pores predominate in the samples. Moreover, the results of the casting thin section identification showed that the samples had more dissolution pores in the rock debris and feldspar and fewer intergranular pores (Figure 3).

Conclusions

The applicability of the MIP, NMR, and NMRC experiments was discussed based on eight tight sandstone samples from the new area. The reservoir potential of the samples was evaluated based on the results of this discussion.(1)The comparative results of the NMRC and MIP experiments show that the pore volumes of the samples obtained from NMRC are much larger than those obtained from MIP. The PSDs obtained from the NMRC and MIP have similar responses to the relative content in the same pore range. At low mercury injection volumes and saturations, the PSDs obtained from MIP may overestimate the proportion of mesopores or macropores in the total pore space.
