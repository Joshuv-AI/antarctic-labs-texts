Community health worker support to adolescents and young adults with sickle cell disease: A multi-method analysis on transition-related support
Authors: Jayanne Forrest, Claudio Galea, Caren Steinway, Olivia Teng, Jack Chen, Sophia Jan, Banu Aygun, Abena Appiah-Kubi, Nataly Apollonsky, Donna Boruchov, Omar Niss, Biree Andemariam, Katherine Wu, Emmanuel Nortey, Kim Smith-Whitley, Symme Trachtenberg, Tanisha Belton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransitioning to adult care is challenging for adolescent and young adults (AYA) with sickle cell disease (SCD), often leading to increased morbidity and mortality. Community health workers (CHWs) may offer patient-centered support during this vulnerable transition.MethodsWe conducted a multi-method descriptive analysis of CHW encounter documentation from the CHW arm of the COMETS randomized clinical trial, which compared CHW support, mobile health, and enhanced usual care in improving health-related quality of life among transitioning AYA with SCD. Quantitative analyses characterized participant engagement and progression of participant-identified, transition-related goals during a 6-month intervention period. Qualitative analyses examined CHW documentation to contextualize how CHWs supported development of self-management and self-advocacy skills among AYA with SCD.ResultsAmong the 120 participants (median age 18.7 years; 52% female; 92% Black or African American), 95% were successfully contacted within a median of 5 days. Participants set an average of 2.5 goals during the six-month intervention period, with 70% achieved by the end of the program. Qualitative analysis of CHW documentation revealed primary themes of transition readiness (48%), lifestyle improvements (21%), and hospitalizations or medical emergencies (19%).ConclusionThis multi-method descriptive analysis demonstrates that CHWs can provide effective, scalable support for AYA with SCD during the transition to adult care. Future studies should explore the value and applicability of this model to other chronic pediatric conditions.

Introduction

Adolescents and young adults (AYA) with sickle cell disease (SCD) experience alarming rates of mortality and morbidity, particularly during the transition from pediatric to adult care. Although advances in screening and treatment have enabled most children living with SCD to survive into adulthood, the transition remains a period of significant vulnerability. AYA with SCD aged 16–25 face a seven-fold increase in mortality during this transition period,1 and they have the highest rates of hospitalizations, emergency room visits, and hospital readmissions compared to other SCD age groups.1 The transition is especially complex for individuals with SCD due to disease-specific challenges and increasing disease progression during adolescence. Unique barriers to transition care for AYA with SCD include difficulties transferring to an accessible adult hematology provider, negative experiences involving their personal identity, and the impact of systemic inequities when seeking adult-oriented healthcare. Our analysis includes adolescents and young adults who are anticipated to transition to adult-oriented care within 12 months, based on clinical assessment and institutional transfer practices, rather than age alone.2

In this study, transition is conceptualized as a developmental process that prepares adolescents and young adults for adult-oriented care through the acquisition of self-management, self-advocacy, and healthcare navigation skills. While transfer to an adult provider is an important milestone, it is not the sole focus of transition support in this analysis.

Compared to other chronic diseases, historically SCD has received significantly less research funding and public attention.3 For example, although SCD has three times the prevalence of cystic fibrosis, federal and foundation funding is significantly greater per patient for cystic fibrosis.4 This disparity contributes to limited access to comprehensive and specialized care, particularly in adult settings.

In addition, most individuals with SCD in the U.S. are insured through Medicaid insurance,5 which can limit access to specialized care. Studies show that only 60% of pediatric sickle cell centers transfer patients to an adult hematologist specializing in SCD.6 In states that have not implemented Medicaid expansions,7 AYA may also be at risk of losing insurance at age 19 raising concerns of financial burden. Delays in transitioning to adult providers and reduced access to specialized care are associated with poorer health outcomes in this population.8, 9

SCD primarily affects African Americans, who account for about 90% of all diagnoses in the U.S. Racial disparities in healthcare are well documented and may further complicate the transition to adult care for individuals with SCD.10, 11 Qualitative studies have identified negative healthcare experiences as key transition barriers, including being mislabeled as drug-seeking when seeking pain management and encountering poor communication from physicians.12, 13 These experiences contribute to mistrust of adult care providers and decrease healthcare utilization within this population.14, 15

Community Health Workers (CHW) are non-clinician professionals who work to bridge gaps between patients and healthcare systems.16 CHWs may improve engagement in care, self-management, adherence to treatment plans, and overall health outcomes. Studies have shown that CHW interventions are effective in improving outcomes in chronic conditions such as diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease, especially in diverse, underserved patient populations.17 CHW programs have gained broader attention within health systems, and the World Health Organization has called for integration of CHWs into care teams and prioritization of related research.18 One structured and scalable CHW model, Individualized Management for Patient-Centered Targets (IMPaCT), has shown promise across various hospital settings in the northeastern U.S. and has been adapted specifically for supporting AYA with SCD during transition. The model includes CHW recruiting guidelines, standardized training, and a goal-setting patient-centered approach.19, 20, 21, 22

To better understand the engagement of AYA with SCD within this adapted IMPaCT model, we conducted a multi-method descriptive analysis of the CHW intervention arm of the COMETS study (Community Health Workers and Mobile Health for Emerging Adults Transitioning Sickle Cell Disease Care). Specifically, we aim to (1) quantify participant engagement and transition-related goal achievement with CHWs and (2) explore themes in CHW documentation to illustrate how CHWs facilitate self-management and self-advocacy skill development among AYA with SCD during the transition to adult care.

Methods

Design

The COMETS study, a randomized clinical trial, compares the effectiveness of two self-management support interventions, community health workers (CHWs)22 and mobile health (mHealth),23 compared to enhanced usual care in improving health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for AYA with SCD transitioning to adulthood. Institutional review board (IRB) approval was received in June 2018 (IB 18–015106) at the prime institution, the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia, and submitted to each participating site for site level approach. COMETS trial sites include five children’s hospitals with pediatric SCD centers in CT, NY, OH, and PA. Each site had at least one CHW, with a maximum of two at any site. Participants were randomized to one of the three arms: enhanced usual care plus CHW support, enhanced usual care plus access to an mHealth app, or enhanced usual care alone for a 6-month period. This manuscript presents a multi-method descriptive analysis of engagement and transition-related skill development among participants randomized to the CHW intervention arm of the COMETS trial only.

Sample

Eligible participants were young adult patients aged 17 years or older with a SCD diagnosis (all genotypes) who were receiving care at a pediatric SCD center at enrollment and were eligible for transition to adult care within 12 months. Eligibility for anticipated transition within 12 months was determined by the clinical care team based on institutional transfer practices and transition readiness discussions. Because transition timing varies across health systems and was disrupted during the COVID-19 pandemic, age alone was not used to define transition readiness. Exclusion criteria included lack of access to a mobile device (tablet or computer) with the ability to interact with a mobile- or web-based program, severe intellectual disability, and non-English speakers. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Participants received detailed information regarding study procedures, risks, and their rights, including the ability to withdraw at any time. Consent was obtained either in person or electronically via REDCap. Participants enrolled at age 17 were re-consented upon turning 18. A waiver of parental permission and waiver of documentation of consent were granted by the IRB due to minimal risk.

CHW intervention – adapted IMPaCT framework

The CHWs in the study ranged from 22 to 70 years old, with a median age of 29 years old. A total of 15 individuals completed the initial onboarding and training. Out of the 15, 7 (47%) had previous research experience, 8 (53%) had previous clinical experience, and 11 (73%) reported SCD experience. Previous research experience was defined as prior job experience working on research studies. Clinical experience was defined as previous educational training or work experience in a hospital or clinic setting and SCD experience was defined as personal or work experience with the SCD population. Personal experience included having family members with SCD.

The CHW intervention provided weekly synchronous support to study participants during a 6-month period. Roadmap goal setting, a model for individualized action plans for achieving patient-centered goals, was carried out after enrollment. Young adults identified specific transition-related goals and areas in which they wanted support. Some goals identified by participants included employment challenges, specifically how to communicate health needs, request time off, or explain unplanned absences related to SCD complications. These issues were revisted and supported longitudinally over the 6-month intervention period.

CHWs established relationships with participants by scheduling an initial in-person meeting or phone call to discuss foundational questions like who the patient is, what is important to them, what their hopes and fears are, and what their perspective is on the root cause of their health problems. From these initial interactions, CHWs documented a patient snapshot to summarize the participant in 5 words and elucidate both long- and short-term goals to work on during the 6-month intervention.

CHWs had personal and/or professional experience with SCD, including some who had recently transitioned from pediatric to adult SCD care. Our CHWs were racially diverse and able to use their background to build a better rapport with participants. Our CHW training program is unique in that it was adapted by a group of multidisciplinary clinicians, researchers, and individuals with SCD, to specifically address SCD and transition care. In the development phase, there were weekly one-hour virtual design sessions focused on refining CHW role and training components over a 1-year period. Representatives ranging from adult and pediatric hematologists and clinical psychologists to SCD program directors and social workers participated to further refine the responsibilities and necessary elements of the initial CHW training.

CHWs underwent a comprehensive training program adapted from the IMPaCT framework, detailed in Belton et al., to specifically support AYA with SCD throughout the transition period.22 The COMETS adaptation included defined phases to build a relationship between the CHW and patient, address critical knowledge gaps, set self-directed goals, and support goal achievement through weekly check-ins. Once complete, CHWs received weekly supervision, where they reviewed cases and debriefed. Caseloads varied by site, but on average each CHW held a caseload of around four patients (range 0–13). The CHWs were able to address urgent issues on an as-needed basis with the site supervisor and trial manager. Monthly CHW huddles were also conducted to allow interaction between CHWs from different sites. These huddles facilitated discussion of tough patient cases, reviewed CHW workflows, and periodically invited experts to talk about specific clinical or social issues. Further details of the training program and intervention are available in Belton et al.22

Data collection

CHWs documented all patient encounters over the 6-month intervention period, including the method and length of each interaction. CHWs also tracked participant-developed goals and progression through goal completion throughout the program. At the end of the intervention period, participants were asked to rate their experience with the CHW. The CHW documentation was analyzed using both quantitative and qualitative methods. Source documents for both quantitative and qualitative data were pulled from de-identified REDCap24, 25 data collection tools and compiled in Excel. Quantitative data included participant-reported demographic information collected at enrollment, as well as CHW- reported engagement metrics and goal status. CHWs tracked goal progression through successive REDCap surveys, and the de-identified data on goal completion was extracted for our analysis. The qualitative data consisted of CHW-generated encounter documentation throughout the 6-month intervention period. CHWs were able to track the goals (e.g. new, completed, abandoned) through REDCap forms, and revisit encounter documentation to reassess progress and navigate future meaningful interactions with participants. CHWs were expected to document the modality (e.g. text, email, call), duration (in minutes), and summary of the content of the interactions for each encounter. Qualitative data was de-identified and analyzed in excel spreadsheets to assess for themes in content of CHW interactions with participants.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative analysis

The quantitative analysis examined participant engagement with CHWs and progress in self-management and self-advocacy by examining goal setting and goal completion during the 6-month intervention period. Engagement methods included the number of participants contacted, interaction modality and duration, and the number of meaningful interactions. Meaningful interactions were defined by the study team as encounters in which CHWs and participants connected and discussed progress toward participant identified goals. Measures of progress included the number of goals set and completed by participants, as well as the participants’ final rating of their CHWs. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the level of engagement and goal-related outcomes.

Qualitative analysis

The qualitative analysis explored themes within the CHW documentation to better understand how CHWs supported self-management and self-advocacy skills among participants. A predefined codebook was manually created deductively based on the COMETS study’s primary and secondary outcome measures (health-related quality of life, transition readiness, social support) and the COMETS CHW’s defined scope of work.22 Additional codes were identified and closely defined based on emerging themes in the responses collected for Patient-Reported Outcomes. The codebook was further refined and reviewed by team members with qualitative research expertise. Utilizing the predefined codebook, free text encounter documentation was independently coded by two coders (Table 1). Encounters were either coded with these themes or assigned “no code” designations, with a max of two codes per encounter. Coding was iterative, with periodic meetings to update the codebook for enhanced clarity. Following the coding process, analyses included frequency of codes and inter-coder agreement. Frequencies of each code were calculated for each coder and compared through a 2 × 2 matrix to assess agreement. Overall inter-coder agreement was calculated as the proportion of identical code assignments across all coded entries. “No code” designations were considered as agreements in this analysis.

Participant engagement and transition-related goal achievement

Quantitative analyses assessed how engagement between AYA with SCD and CHWs utilizing the IMPaCT model26 facilitates quantifiable goal achievement and self-advocacy skills.

CHWs successfully contacted 95% (n = 111) of participants within a median of 5 days (range: 0–132 days) of assignment to a participant. Participants set an average of 2.5 goals over the course of the 6-month intervention (total= 297) categorized as: Improving Skills to Manage Health (e.g., tracking health issues) (32%; n = 96); Increasing Knowledge of Planning for School and Work (e.g., workplace skills and expectations) (31%; 94); Increasing Knowledge of SCD and Healthcare (e.g., healthy behaviors) (21%; 63); and Improving Skills for Navigating Health Systems and Self-Advocacy (e.g. making appointments) (15%; 44) (Fig. 1). By program end, 70% of goals (n = 208) were achieved, and 13% (n = 16) were abandoned after 1 month. CHWs made 3114 contacts over the 6-month period (average 25 per participant) primarily via phone (54%; n = 1643) and text message (38%; n = 1160). Other modalities included email (2.9%; n = 88), in-person at a clinic visit (1.5%; n = 46), video conferencing (0.8%; n = 23), and in-person in community settings (0.6%; n = 17). Of these, 63% (n = 1900) contacts were meaningful interactions, defined by the study team as an interaction where CHWs and participants connected and discussed goal progress, averaging 21 min (median: 10 min; range 0–240 min). Participants rated their interaction with CHWs an average rating of 9.6 out of 10, 73% said they would highly recommend the program to others, suggesting program benefit and desire for scalability.

Transition readiness

CHWs facilitated concrete steps towards transition readiness, empowering participants to independently manage appointment scheduling, prescription refills, medication adherence, and transportation. This support is evident in CHW documentation. For instance, addressing transportation barriers, a CHW recorded, “We discussed patients schedule today to get an idea of why he finds it difficult to keep up with his maintenance appointments. Realized it was a transportation issue so I will begin the process to set him up with a medical cab. Will check in next week.” (20, male).

Beyond direct healthcare management, CHWs assisted participants in documenting their SCD to secure accommodations at school or work and access helpful resources (see Table 3). This increased autonomy in healthcare facilitated a smoother transition from high school to post-secondary education, equipping participants to effectively communicate their needs to healthcare providers, disability services, friends, faculty, and others. While improving health management skills was the most common goal, individual approaches varied, demonstrating the personalized nature of CHW support.

Many of the AYA with SCD in this study were approaching high school graduation (median age of 18.7). While some participants started the intervention with established medication adherence, SCD knowledge, and self-reliance, most looked to improve these skills through their work with a CHW. Adolescents in high school often depend on parents or guardians for healthcare management tasks such as scheduling appointments, obtaining medication, and navigating health insurance. The CHW intervention helped these young people overcome this significant hurdle in the transition to adult care. Learning about their health insurance, finding providers local to their new colleges/jobs, and understanding how different medications helped prevent pain crises empowered participants to envision themselves managing their healthcare independently. This increased self-efficacy is reflected in CHW documentation. For example, a CHW documented a participant’s text message expressing newfound confidence: “…actually just talking to you these past weeks have helped me realize that I can do anything I put my mind too and you have helped me build my confidence up I just wanted to say thanks for all your positive energy it really helps.” (23, male). The positive impact of the CHW relationship is further illustrated by another participant’s feedback:We reviewed our time together and she expressed gratitude for my support. We spoke about her triumphs and challenges, and I asked how she felt about the goals she set at the beginning. She feels that she was able to complete them to her liking and felt comfortable moving forward on her own… She expressed sadness at no longer speaking to me every week. (21, female).

In addition to supporting AYA with SCD in their healthcare management and self-advocacy skills, CHWs also played a role in supporting participants in instances of discrimination and subsequent emotional distress when seeking appropriate medical care. These results demonstrate that CHWs can be effective at engaging AYA with SCD for general healthcare maintenance and emergent issues like hospitalizations for acute pain crises. Our results also illustrate the role of the CHW in directly supporting AYA with SCD who sought more independence and autonomy in their healthcare while preparing to transition.

Discussion

This multi-method descriptive analysis examined engagement and transition-related skill development among AYA with SCD who participated in a six-month CHW intervention, Quantitative findings demonstrated high levels of participant engagement, frequent meaningful interactions, and substantial progression of participant-identified transition-related goals. Qualitative analyses contextualized these findings by illustrating how CHWs supported self-management, self-advocacy, and navigation of healthcare and social systems during a critical developmental period.

Engagement and communication in transition support

Consistent and flexible communication emerged as a key feature of CHW engagement. Most CHW-participant interactions occurred via phone and text message, enabling regular contact despite competing school, work, and health demands common among AYAs. Rather than reflecting measured communication preferences, these patterns demonstrate the feasibility or virtual and asynchronous communication modalities for maintaining engagement during transition. Prior studies among adolescents and young adults with chronic conditions, including cancer, similarly highlight the importance of accessible and developmentally appropriate communication approaches that promote autonomy and sustained connection with care teams.27, 28

Transition readiness and skill development

This study assessed engagement between CHWs and AYA with SCD through quantifiable goal setting and achievement, as well as qualitative themes derived from meaningful participant interactions. Findings indicate that CHWs were able to consistently engage participants and support progression toward individualized transition-related goals across domains including health management, education, work, and healthcare navigation.

Although this analysis did not assess clinical outcomes such a hospitalization frequency or healthcare utilization, qualitative documentation suggests that CHWs supported participants in reflecting on factors contributing to acute care use and, in some cases, encouraged timely engagement with medical care. These findings align with prior literature demonstrating potential of CHW interventions to support care engagement among individuals with chronic conditions.12, 21, 29

A small 2023 study examining communication preferences between healthcare providers and AYA with cancer found that patients prefer to use text and email as their primary communication with their medical providers.22 Another 2025 study conducted to understand communication experiences of AYA with cancer in the UK found that effective communication reassured young patients, increased their autonomy, and improved perceived understanding.27 Aligning with the communication preferences of AYA, our CHWs were able to initiate contact with participants and build rapport through consistent weekly contact.28

Role of CHWs in supporting transition readiness

Findings from this analysis suggest that CHW-supported goal setting and consistent engagement may facilitate development of transition readiness skills, including self-management and self-advocacy. These competencies are foundational to successful transition to adult-oriented care, even when transfer itself does not occur within a defined timeframe. By supporting participants in navigating appointments, medications, education, work, and healthcare communication, CHWs may help prepare AYA with SCD for future adult care engagement.

Our 6-month intervention fostered engagement between CHWs and AYA with SCD that potentially leads to increased health comprehension and self-efficacy. These findings align with prior literature demonstrating potential of CHW interventions to support care engagement among individuals with chronic conditions.22 Though our study did not evaluate the number of hospitalizations or reductions thereof, our qualitative analyses suggest that CHWs helped facilitate reflection on the events leading to a pain crisis (e.g. dehydration, medication nonadherence) and in some cases encouraged participants to seek medical care before their pain crises got more severe (Table 3).

First, our results align with those of other CHW interventions for children with chronic health conditions by demonstrating that CHWs promote medication compliance and medical appointment attendance.15 Several outside factors such as pharmacy access, cost, health literacy, and side effects influence medication adherence. CHWs play an instrumental role in helping AYA with SCD navigate these barriers, improving communication, and fostering stronger connections with their medical team. Second, as our CHWs worked with participants to increase medication adherence, advocate for health accommodations, and seek comprehensive medical care, they help shape better healthcare decision-making that may improve the quality of life for AYA with SCD. The CHW role is key because it is akin to having a patient navigator, helping participants overcome the barriers of navigating specialty care alone through assisting the process of finding specialists and scheduling appointments for transitioning care (Table 3). Our study’s adapted CHW training and 6-month intervention might serve as an effective model for refining and creating CHW programs, which may also subsequently inform the expansion of existing models to reimburse CHW services. Current literature highlights the need for optimized CHW training and intervention that is culturally aligned with the mental health, social, and medical needs of SCD patients. Existing practices are incomplete in addressing access to culturally sensitive healthcare and control of symptoms (e.g. pain), which decreases quality of life and increases medical expenditure. Our comprehensive CHW training, adapted from the IMPaCT model, likely played a crucial role in the observed improvements in transition readiness and lifestyle modifications amongst AYA with SCD.22 The training’s emphasis on foundational CHW skills like clear communication and ongoing education needs like goal-setting and deciding next steps were key to assessing CHW job readiness, i.e. engaging patients and facilitating successful setting and reaching of goals. Given that goal setting was a primary outcome in our quantitative analysis (Fig. 1), we believe the effectiveness of the engagement between CHWs and AYA with SCD is underscored by encouraging participants to make tangible changes that address the root cause of their problems. This may have equipped CHWs to effectively address the multifaceted challenges faced by AYA with SCD during transition.

Self-advocacy, racism, and structural barriers

Our adapted CHW training and racially-diverse cohort of CHWs (many of whom have SCD) address existing inadequacies and promote culturally-sensitive care. Our results demonstrate that CHW support can facilitate participants’ identification and reflection on adverse experiences with the healthcare system. CHW documentation captured participant experiences with stigma, discrimination, and mistrust in the medical system (Table 3). They also capture participant frustration with inadequate pain management during hospitalization, highlighting the impacts of racial discrimination as barriers to proper patient care during pain crises. Consistent with previously published literature, many patients with SCD are Black and experience the burden of medical racism and stigmatization during their hospitalizations.23 When providers distrust a patient’s knowledge of their own disease or misinterpret requests for pain medications as drug-seeking, they fail to provide the equitable and necessary care. CHWs offered patients a safe place to share these experiences and provided strategies for self-advocacy as participants navigate new adult care environments.

The IMPaCT model trains CHWs to build rapport, identify individualized life and health goals, and provide consistent follow-up. This patient-centered approach has been successfully implemented and evaluated.26 Our results demonstrate how CHWs can offer insight to participants and encourage them to seek various forms of healthcare including treatment of acute vaso-occlusive events, mental health services, and nutrition counseling (Table 3). In our study, CHWs demonstrated effective engagement through weekly contact and goal-oriented discussions, frequently leading to improved health behaviors and self-advocacy amid adversity.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the CHW intervention occurred within a clinical trial, and its effectiveness may differ in broader implementation. While we attempted to model real-world CHW workflow, the research context may have influenced participant engagement and outcomes. Second, the CHW caseloads varied across hospital sites based on the number of participants in that specific CHW intervention location, but data suggests that between 10 and 30 patients is optimal.29 Although some full-time CHWs assisted patients outside the clinical trial, we believe a lower caseload is generally better. The variation in caseloads may affect the model for implementation across hospital systems with different resources. Based on New York State’s CHW reimbursement rate,5 for example, a CHW may need an average of 10 meaningful interactions a day to be cost-neutral. This also relates to challenges around compensation for CHWs, specifically securing sustainable funding for these programs outside of resources that studies may provide. Although the New York State CHW reimbursement plan is a great example for states without payment programs, there is still a lack of adequate funding for CHW programs to be integrated into health systems.

Our qualitative analysis also has limitations. CHW encounter documentation, while valuable, may not fully capture all interaction details, potentially leading to classification issues. Brief documentation of follow-up discussions, without specific indicators of progress, may have resulted in under-coding of certain themes. Furthermore, variability in CHW documentation style (level of detail, use of direct quotes versus summaries) and training across health systems may have introduced information bias. While documenting encounters during or immediately after their contact with the participant minimized recall bias, variations in documentation practices may have influenced theme identification. Despite these limitations, the high inter-rater reliability (94% agreement) strengthens the validity of the qualitative findings.

Our multi-method analysis provides complementary insights: quantitative data describing engagement and goal progression within participant-identified transition domains, with qualitative findings contextualizing how CHW support facilitated development of transition readiness skills towards self-management and self-advocacy. These results are particularly important in the transition from pediatric to adult SCD care, as the structured support and follow-up for pediatric patients changes once they switch to an adult provider. There are other barriers to transition care that our study does not directly address, such as establishing rapport with a new provider to increase patient-follow up. However, a CHW model may help address this by ensuring the patient understands the resources they need (i.e. phone numbers, reminders for follow-up appointments, insurance coverage) to start a relationship with a new provider.

Conclusion

In conclusion, AYA with SCD demonstrated high levels of engagement with CHWs through consistent, weekly contact, and goal-oriented discussions. Most goals set during the 6-month intervention were achieved by the end of the program, with many participants expressing interest in independently scheduling their medical appointments and managing their insurance. Furthermore, encounter documentation summarizing these discussions revealed several themes, including transition readiness for health management, education, and work as well as lifestyle improvements such as hydration, exercise and mental health counseling. The successful engagement of participants by CHWs indicates that this program may increase patient satisfaction and real-time goal setting and achievement. These characteristics may position the CHW program to be both beneficial and potentially scalable to other hospital systems in the context of mediating transition care. This study’s results may promote the effectiveness of tailored interventions, like CHW programs, for marginalized populations such as AYA with SCD. Future studies should explore the applicability of CHW interventions for pediatric patients with other chronic diseases. Implementing and evaluating this model in other hospital settings could further elucidate its impact on health outcomes and mortality risk for AYA with SCD.
