Expert patients improve health literacy, self-care attitudes, and practices in people with diabetes: a quasi-experimental study in Tanzania
Authors: Stephen M. Kibusi, Grace J. Mollel, Angelina A. Joho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
People living with diabetes require a long-term commitment to disease management to minimize and control the risk of developing different life-threatening health complications, high medical expenses, reduced quality of life, and mortality. This study evaluated the effectiveness of utilizing expert patients – selected patients who fulfilled set criteria and received brief training to act as peer educators, through a peer-to-peer learning strategy in enhancing health literacy, self-care attitudes, and practices among adult patients with diabetes. Quasi-experimental non-randomized, two-arm study involving 144 randomly selected participants, but non-randomly assigned to intervention (DDRH) and control (BMH) arms. A convenience method was applied to assign participants to the two groups, with 72 in the intervention group and 72 in the control group. The effectiveness of the Patient-Mediated Health Education (PMHE) Intervention using Expert Patients was evaluated by comparing outcomes in terms of medication adherence, health literacy, attitude, and self-care behaviour between the control group, which continued to receive conventional health education, and the intervention group, which received the PMHE intervention. Six expert patients were trained and conducted two one-hour face-to-face PMHE peer-to-peer learning sessions. A two-sample independent t-test was used to compare the mean difference between the two groups. p < 0.05 was considered to be significant The independent t-test revealed a significant difference between groups after the intervention, with mean scores in the post-test for health literacy showing: Intervention group post-test M = 63.18 (95% CI: 62.65–63.72); Control group post-test M = 54.73 (95% CI: 53.99–55.47); mean difference = 8.45, t = 9.327, p < 0.001. The self-care attitude at post-test was M = 30.31 (95% CI: 29.93–30.69) for the intervention group and M = 22.38 (95% CI: 18.59–26.15) for the control group (p-value < 0.001). Self-care practice after the intervention showed a M = 8.67 (95% CI: 8.54–8.79) for the intervention group and 5.85 (95% CI: 3.40–8.30) for the control group (p-value < 0.001). The findings demonstrate that a low-budget peer-to-peer learning approach utilizing expert patients is effective in enhancing health literacy and self-care behaviours among adults living with type 2 diabetes in a low-resource setting. We recommend the incorporation of this model into national diabetes care guidelines, emphasizing the utilization of expert patients within the peer learning framework. This approach has the potential to lower long-term healthcare costs while simultaneously improving patient outcomes.Trial registration This trial was registered retrospectively with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry, with registration number PACTR202509776757091. The study was conducted in 2020, and trial registration was completed in 2025. We acknowledge that retrospective registration is a limitation that may affect the transparency and interpretability of findings. We strongly recommend prospective registration in all future trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51359-4.

Introduction

Attainment of the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly goal number 3, which offers a blueprint to achieving healthy lives and promoting the well-being of all at all ages1, is threatened by the increasing number of morbidity and mortality due to non-communicable diseases, including Diabetes Mellitus (DM)2. Diabetes Mellitus is a long-lasting, metabolic disease characterized by raised blood glucose levels, which, if not controlled, leads over time to severe damage to the eyes, kidneys, blood vessels, heart, and nerves3. Globally, the number of people with DM has risen from 151 million in 2000 to 589 million in 2024 and is estimated to be 853 million in 20504. The global adult deaths associated with DM in 2024 were 3.4 million4.

In Africa, the prevalence of DM was 24.6 million in 20244 and is expected to rise to 59.5 million in 20504. This prevalence increase of 142% higher than the global increase of 45% the same year, 2000–20244. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the number of adults living with DM was estimated to be 15.5 (9.8–27.8) million in 2017, with a regional prevalence of ~ 6%, and associated healthcare costs of USD 3.3 billion5. By 2045, this number was projected to increase by 162.5%, with an estimated 40.7 million adults suffering from type 2 diabetes and costs rising to USD 6 billion6.

In Tanzania, the number of people (20–79 years) with type 2 DM was 134.8 thousand in 2000, with a dramatic increase to 2.9 million in 20247. In Dodoma, the capital city of Tanzania and one of the fastest-urbanizing regions, the overall prevalence of type 2 DM was found to be 63.9%. While 44.6% of respondents were diagnosed during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 pandemic, and 19.3% diagnosed before the pandemic8. Correspondingly, there is an increase in the prevalence of DM-related complications. The prevalence of any microvascular complications of diabetes among newly diagnosed diabetes patients was high; neuropathy was 72.2%9, nephropathy was 14.5%, and retinopathy was 15.4%10. Overall, diabetes contributes to a high burden of disease in terms of all inpatient days and physician office visits due to its chronic complications, thus resulting in higher medical expenditures in an already overburdened healthcare system11. Pooled data from 54 countries reveal that more than 80% of cases of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) are caused by diabetes, hypertension, or a combination of both12.

In Tanzania, people living with diabetes have been reported to have low knowledge of diabetes13–15, and non-adherence to anti-diabetic medications15; resulting in poor glycemic control and poor self-care practices15–17 as well as poor blood pressure control18. This resulted in Tanzania failing to meet its goal of reducing the burden (morbidity, disability, and premature mortality) related to non-communicable diseases by 20% by 202019. Tanzania also experiences a critical shortage of healthcare workers, which limits the implementation of well-intentioned preventive and disease management interventions, including the Diabetes Self-Management Education program provided in diabetic clinics across the country20,21.

Diabetes Self-Management Education (DSME) is the cornerstone of care for individuals with diabetes22. It addresses the patient’s consistent participation in self-care behaviours such as attention to diet, exercise, preventive care measures, drug adherence, and self-monitoring of blood glucose. Normally, DSMEs are healthcare provider-led and form the cornerstone of self-management of DM, which aims to empower patients about their disease, treatment modalities, and prevention of complications for better outcomes23. Despite the good intentions of this approach, it has not been very effective in empowering patients and, in most instances, leaves the family of the patients less knowledgeable and less informed about the self-management of diabetes24.

Various community-based educational interventions, such as family-based home health education, community health workers, and peer support learning strategies, have been used to facilitate patient-centred DSME. The role of patient engagement and empowerment in DSME has not been well studied despite its potential impact on effective learning, shaping self-care practices among patients, and improving healthcare-patient communication25–28. Peer support learning strategy is a system of giving and receiving help originating from key principles of respect, shared responsibility, and mutual agreement on what is helpful, or is a supportive relationship between people who have a common lived experience29. Globally, this method has been used and shown effectiveness in improving health literacy, self-care behaviour in diet, physical activity, blood sugar testing, foot care, and medication, as intervention was conducted in Iran30, Shiraz (Iran)31, Zanjan, Iran32, Kenya33, and New York34. However, little is known about the effectiveness of peer support learning strategies using expert patients in facilitating DSME to diabetes patients in Tanzania and other low-resource settings. Ineffective teaching methods, a lack of support from diabetic peers, inadequate family support, a shortage of healthcare providers, and a lack of self-management skills are some of the prominent factors that contributed to the failure of DSME implementation in low-resource settings, including Tanzania35. Despite the efforts made by the Tanzanian government, poor DSME implementations have been linked with a high prevalence of low levels of knowledge on diabetes36, low adherence37, and poor glycemic control37 among DM patients.

DM demands the client’s active involvement in daily self-care practice and lifestyle changes to prevent significant complications. A pilot study on the peer support learning strategy for diabetes self-management found that the use of peer leaders was feasible for ongoing and sustained education in the community38. Furthermore, little is known about the effectiveness of using peer-to-peer among adults living with type 2 diabetes in learning to improve self-care management and enhance self-management behaviours.

The theory of Constructivism posits that teaching and learning are processes grounded in the understanding that learning stems from the mental construction of knowledge. In essence, learner learn by integrating new information with their existing knowledge39. Constructivists emphasize that the context of instruction, along with learners’ beliefs and attitudes, significantly influences learning. This theory maintains that individuals construct knowledge and meaning through their experiences40. Furthermore, from the constructivist perspective, social communities play a vital role in the construction of meaning. In other words, learners cannot truly develop understanding without social interactions and interdependent relationships. This theory advocates for teaching approaches that prioritize peer support learning strategies, emphasizing social interaction to engage learners in tasks and enhance learning outcomes.

This study, therefore, explored the effectiveness of Patient-Mediated Health Education (PMHE) using Expert patients in a peer-to-peer learning strategy in empowering people living with diabetes with the necessary self-care knowledge and long-term disease self-management among adults living with diabetes in the Dodoma region, Tanzania.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was a quasi-experimental, non-randomized, two-arm study aimed at comparing the effects of a Patient-Mediated Health Education Intervention with conventional patient education methods, on self-care practices among people living with diabetes receiving care at two selected diabetic clinics: Dodoma Referral Regional Hospital (DRRH) and Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH). These are key facilities that receive the majority of referred patients with various medical conditions, including diabetes. These hospitals are situated in the Dodoma region, which serves as both the capital city and an administrative centre of Tanzania. This rapidly growing region is characterized by its cultural diversity, as many government officials are relocating to Dodoma from different parts of the country, drawn by the concentration of ministry offices. This influx of people has led to lifestyle changes, such as increased consumption of fast food and a more sedentary way of living, which heightens the risk of hypertension and diabetes among the population. Consequently, conducting this study in the Dodoma region is expected to yield insights that reflect the broader scope of diabetes patients in Tanzania.

The trial took place from May to August 2020. In these two hospitals, people living with diabetes are managed by the consultants’ doctors and nurses, who have a busy schedule providing care in the wards and in the clinics. The design also involved assessing the effect of the intervention on health literacy, attitude, and Self-Care Behaviour by comparing these outcomes between the control and intervention participants.

Study population

The study comprised adult patients aged 18 to 80 years with type 2 diabetes who had been receiving care for more than six months at the diabetic clinics of the two hospitals in Dodoma. Participants with known multi-morbidity were excluded from the study.

Sampling method

A purposive sampling procedure was used to select two hospitals (DRRH and BMH), the regional referral and consultant hospitals in the region, respectively. Expert patients were selected purposively after fulfilling the set criteria, including a minimum of two years of treatment experience, consistent adherence to routine clinic visits and health education, as well as recommendations from the clinic. Simple random sampling using the rotary method was employed to select participants simultaneously for both the intervention and control groups. The sampling frame was obtained from the clinic register. From the register, all patients were assigned random numbers. The random numbers were drawn from a pool of all eligible participants until the required number was reached. The chosen numbers were returned to the box after each draw, allowing for the possibility of multiple selections while maintaining equal chances for selection in every draw. This procedure was used to select study participants through a lottery with replacement. Participants were recruited using random sampling from the eligible population. Allocation into intervention (DDRH) and control (BMH) arms was non-randomized; a convenience method was applied to assign participants to the study. These two hospitals operate diabetic clinics and serve the same population of patients residing in Dodoma city and its surrounding districts.

Description of the pre-post intervention

Pre-intervention

Selection of expert patients

Expert patients were drawn from a list of patients provided in the diabetic clinics with well-controlled glucose levels and consistent attendance. Patients with fasting blood plasma glucose (FPG) of ≤ 126 mg/dL were considered to have good glucose level control, as reviewed from patients’ files, as was done in the previous studies42,43. All patients underwent a blood plasma glucose test at 9:00 AM, before eating. Active attendance was assessed using patient files. From the files, active attendance was defined as regular attendance at diabetes clinics. The patients were fluent in Swahili, the national language of Tanzania, and possessed good communication skills; they could pronounce words correctly, were friendly, and refrained from using harsh language. Additionally, they had an ordinary level of education (Secondary school).

From the list of controlled glucose levels and consistent attendance, the best 6 out of 10 were randomly selected, after being recommended by the Nurse in charge of the Clinic, who knew the patients well, and the nurse involved in the patient section. The recruited expert patients attended three days of training sessions on how to facilitate and structure the different sessions, as was done in a previous study44. The training was conducted by the researchers and covered topics such as understanding basic information about diabetes, including its types, causes, symptoms, and signs, as well as basic self-care details and the complications and their early warning signs. The training also covered basic interpersonal communication and teaching styles recommended for a peer-to-peer learning strategy, as was done in previous studies42,45. The researchers evaluated the session by performing pre- and post-training assessments, as was done in previous studies46. Finally, the facilitation skills of the expert patients, under the supervision of the research team, were evaluated. The selected expert patients were given the teaching materials and guidelines for facilitating their sessions, as was done in previous studies47.

During the Intervention

Two face-to-face Diabetes self-management education sessions (DSMES) were held at the hospital’s diabetic clinic, utilizing the PMHE model with expert patients as facilitators. The DSMES covered essential topics such as healthy eating, being active, taking medication, monitoring glucose, reducing risk (treating acute and chronic complications), health coping, and reducing stress. The intervention was conducted at DRRH, while the BMH served as the control group. The intervention and control groups ranged in size from 5 to 15 people48. The sessions lasted one hour and were held in a series of two meetings. The sessions were delivered through case scenarios, demonstrations, question-and-answer sessions, and discussions. Between the first meeting and the next, two health education messages were sent to the intervention group through mobile messages each week, as was done in previous studies49. During the first week, messages sent contained information related to health literacy and diabetes; during the second week, messages included information about self-care attitude, practices and diabetes complications. The messages were sent by the trained Expert Patients (also referred to as peer supporters) after being reviewed for correctness, accuracy, and clearance by the diabetic nurse. The interval between the two meetings was three weeks. During face-to-face discussions, a research assistant was assigned to monitor and record the conduct of the educational session, ensuring that the intervention ran as intended. After the session, the researchers held a debriefing meeting with the Expert Patients to discuss any challenges and ways to improve the subsequent sessions. The control group continued with routine health education from the clinic42. After one month, the outcomes in terms of drug adherence, self-care health literacy, self-care attitude, and self-care practice were evaluated, as was done in a previous study39, in both the intervention and control groups (Fig. 1).

Data collection and research tools

A structured interviewer-administered questionnaire was used for data collection. The questionnaire was adapted from previous studies50,51 and modified to make it easy to understand, including translating it into the local language of Swahili. To ensure the validity of the questionnaire, we analyzed its internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient52. We pretested the questionnaire before and after translation among 20 people living with diabetes from DRRH, using the same inclusion criteria as in the main study. However, they were excluded from the final trial to prevent bias and duplication.

We modified some of the questions that were not clear. We validated the questionnaire based on a Cronbach’s alpha value of ≥ 0.752. The Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.764, 0.714, and 0.823 for knowledge, attitude, and self-care practice of diabetic mellitus, respectively, indicate that the questionnaire is reliable. The questionnaire consisted of five sections, covering socio-demographic information, diabetes knowledge, attitudes towards diabetes, self-care practices, and adherence. The socio-demographic section consisted of 27 questions, the diabetes knowledge section had 18 questions, the attitude towards diabetes section contained 9 questions, the self-care practice (behaviour) section included 14 questions, and the adherence section had 4 questions. Data collection took place in the outpatient diabetic clinic at both DRRH and BMH, conducted by the researcher and four research assistants (registered nurses) from the respective health facilities. The research assistants were trained for two days on how to collect data, including completing the questionnaire, maintaining confidentiality, managing conflicts of interest, and other important topics.

Definition of variables

Expert patients

In this study, Expert patients were selected from a group of patients who fulfilled set criteria and then empowered through brief training to act as peer educators, empowering their fellow patients through a peer-to-peer learning strategy. We had several reasons for utilizing expert patients. These patients have valuable experience living with diabetes mellitus (DM) and possess firsthand knowledge of managing chronic conditions; they can share their experience in all behaviours such as exercise, diet, and medication adherence. They offer insights that textbooks or simulations simply cannot provide, making learning more patient-centred, such as social and emotional support and peers helping their colleagues to cope with social or emotional barriers. Expert patients can connect individuals to clinical care and community resources, bridging the gap between patients and healthcare professionals while encouraging them to seek appropriate clinical and community resources.

Patient-centred medical and health education

Patient-Centred Medical and Health Education (PMHE) is an approach that incorporates patients’ voices and experiences into the teaching process. In this model, expert patients serve as facilitators, guiding learners to gain insight into health conditions through lived experiences. This method prioritizes person-centred care, empathy, and practical problem-solving over traditional theoretical instruction.

Knowledge of diabetes mellitus

Data was collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted from a previous study conducted in Tanzania15. The questionnaire was initially in English, then translated into Kiswahili (the national language of Tanzania) and back-translated to English for consistency. The questionnaire consisted of 18 items used to measure knowledge. The mean score was used as a cut-off point of 11.25. Participants who scored low on the mean were considered to have inadequate knowledge, and those who scored at or above the mean were regarded as having adequate knowledge53. The assessment of knowledge was done before and after the implementation of diabetic patient-mediated health education.

Attitude toward diabetes

The Diabetes Attitudinal Scale (DAS) consists of 9 scale questions, which were measured using a 5-point Likert scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neither agree nor disagree, 4 = agree, and 5 = strongly agree. The items were administered and graded on a 1–5 scale. The principal component analysis (PCA) was performed twice. Upon initial analysis, all questions were included in the study, resulting in four components. However, 2 questions with a score less than 0.45 were excluded from the component matrix, resulting in 7 questions. The second PCA was performed using 7 questions, and after analysis, 3 components were obtained; no question was found to weigh less than 0.45. Factor one was chosen as the score for attitude, as its variance increased from 22.730 to 27.047. The cut-off point for determining positive and negative attitudes was a mean score of 5.497 ± 1.986. Participants with scores equal to or greater than the mean were considered to have a positive attitude, while those with scores less than the mean were considered to have a negative attitude, and vice versa.

Self-care practice

Self-care practice was assessed using 13 questions, with responses of “Yes” or “No”; each correct response was scored 1 point. Principal component analysis was performed to eliminate weak items. This was done twice: initially, all questions were included in the study, resulting in 6 components. Subsequently, 2 questions with a score less than 0.45 were excluded from the component matrix, leaving 11 questions. The second analysis was performed which involved 11 questions after analysis 5 components were obtained and 1 question which was found to weigh less than 0.45 was excluded again remained with 10 questions which were analyzed again for the third round and all 10 questions were found to weigh 0.5 and above, factor one was chosen as the score for skill as it is variance was raised from 19.954 to 23.241. The mean score of 9% was used as a cut-off point to distinguish between appropriate and inappropriate practice.

Adherence to medication

Morisky Green Levine Medication Adherence Scale (MGLS) was used to assess adherence to medication based on patients’ self-reported recall of using diabetic medications over the previous two weeks. In MGLS, the score ranged from 0 to 4, and each of the four items was in (Yes/No) format. One point was given for the ‘’Yes’’ answer and 0 for the ‘’No’’ answer. PCA was performed once, and all questions remained. The uppermost exact answer weight was 1 grade, and the lowest response weight was 0 grade, for a total score of 4. The scores were reformatted to percentages, whereby ≤ 25% indicated low adherence, 50–75% indicated intermediate adherence, and 76–100% indicated high adherence. Adherence was categorized as good adherence when the score is 3, intermediate adherence 2, and 0–1 low adherence50. The category was done based on the percentile score50.

Data analysis

Data entry and statistical analysis were completed using the SPSS program version 26.0. Principal factor analysis was used to explore the relationship among the item variables measuring health literacy, self-care attitude, and those in the self-care practice composite variables. The study identified the dimensions within the composite variables that can be easily managed for further analysis. A two-sample independent t-test was used to compare the means between the intervention and the control group. We used Cohen’s d for mean differences to explain the magnitude of the difference between the two groups as small, medium, or large. The formula used was: Cohen’s d = (M1 − M2) / SDpooled, where SDpooled = √[(SD1² + SD2²) / 2]. Effect size interpretation followed Cohen (1988): d = 0.2 (small effect), d = 0.5 (medium effect), and d ≥ 0.8 (large effect)54 A confidence interval of 95% with a margin of error of 5% (0.05) was used to measure statistical significance. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Social demographic characteristics

Almost all social demographic characteristics of study participants between control and intervention groups had no significant difference except for source of income (chi-square 16.139, p-value < 0.05). Other results indicated that most of the study participants 44 (61.1%) in the control and 43 (59.7%) in the intervention groups, were attending the diabetic clinics at BMH and DRRH, respectively. The mean age of study participants was 54.86 ± 16.78 years in the control group and 53.33 ± 18.56 years in the intervention group. More than half were females, 43 (59.7%) in the control and 48 (66.7%) in the intervention group. The majority of study participants were married; 54 (75%) in the control group, and 52 (72.2%) in the intervention group. Regarding the level of education, most of the study participants had primary education, 38 (52.8%) in the control group and 46 (63.9%) in the intervention group. Regarding the source of income of study participants, the majority are small-scale businesses; in the control group, 20 (27.8%), and 41 (56.9%) in the intervention group. A large proportion of study participants, 47 (65.3%) in the control group and 38 (52.8%) in the intervention group, had never drunk. The majority of participants, 63 (87.5%) in the control group and 65 (90.3%) in the intervention study, had never smoked (Table 1).

Before the intervention, the control and intervention groups were relatively comparable in terms of diabetes self-care practice, suggesting that the two groups were reasonably equivalent at baseline, and there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (mean difference = 2.82, t = 1.41 and p = 0.161). However, following the intervention, the intervention group scored higher compared to the control group, and the difference was statistically significant (mean difference = 0.66, t = 4.67, and p = 0.000), with an effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.85. demonstrating that the intervention was effective in enhancing self-care practice for diabetes (Table 4).

Discussions

This quasi-experimental study demonstrated the effectiveness of a patient-mediated health education intervention in improving drug adherence, self-care health literacy (knowledge), self-care attitude and self-care practice. Diabetes management involves not only routine medical treatment but also health education aimed at maintaining psychological well-being and improving health outcomes for people with diabetes55. Currently, diabetes educational interventions for people living with diabetes in Tanzania primarily focus on treatment and health education provided by healthcare providers56,57. These interventions help to improve patient condition through passive transformation of theoretical knowledge, which does not actively involve the patient; Consequently, this approach reduces the people living with diabetes’s responsibility for their own care58.

Regarding self-care practices, the study’s findings showed significant improvements when peer support training for diabetics was used. Similar results have been reported elsewhere, including in the Netherlands59, Kenya60, and Indonesia29,61. Reported that patients’ learning use of peer-to-peer support plays a significant role in self-management and improves patient clinical outcomes. This is because PMHE offers structured communication sequences designed to improve participants’ confidence in executing specific self-care tasks62. The observed changes might also be due to the method of assessment of self-reporting used in this study, as employed in other research. Additionally, the improvement could stem from the teaching methods used by peer groups, such as scenarios, demonstrations, questions, and answers. Another probable reason might be that peer interactions provide crucial emotional support, helping to reduce feelings of isolation as patients can openly discuss their concerns, fears, and practical advice63–65. Peers communicate in a straightforward, relatable, and accessible manner, using simple language, making discussions more actionable and meaningful for those navigating the challenges of diabetes. The peer support learning strategy is a patient-centred approach that offers people living with diabetes an opportunity to participate in care strongly.

The intervention group notably improves self-care literacy significantly. This illustrates that patients sharing knowledge and experiences with others living with diabetes is an essential factor in improving and maintaining behaviour change and health conditions. The knowledge of diabetes is a crucial aspect of self-care management for people with the condition66. It is supported by other quasi-experimental studies and randomized clinical trials, conducted in Bangladesh, Denmark and China, where the results showed that peer education helped improve knowledge of diabetes compared to education provided by healthcare providers, respectively60,67,68.

PMHE can foster relationships with people with diabetes and enable them to share their understanding of the disease; however, many health professionals lack this kind of genuine, and demonstrative support for managing the disease. This aligns with findings from other studies, which found that healthcare professionals often do not offer the same emotional and practical support as peers living with diabetes60,67,68. In many clinics, healthcare providers often rely on established guidelines when educating patients, including those in diabetic clinics. Unfortunately, these practices rarely involve patients in sharing their experiences, concerns, and insights related to living with diabetes. Furthermore, clinics and hospitals frequently face staff shortages, leading to high patient volumes that hinder providers’ ability to deliver comprehensive education. Consequently, healthcare workers often lack sufficient time to listen attentively to each patient, limiting opportunities for questions. When questions are raised, the responses may be brief, which can lead to non-adherence to diabetes management instructions and potential complications. The current study’s findings contrast with a quasi-experimental study conducted in Iran on adult living with diabetes, which showed that both intervention and control groups experienced improvements in their knowledge level47. This discrepancy may be due to differences in geographical location and healthcare facilities between the participants of the current study and those in other research.

To address this issue, it is essential to involve expert patients in the educational process for their peers living with diabetes. This approach fosters open communication, creating an environment where patients feel comfortable asking questions and sharing personal experiences. Such interactions build a unique bond based on trust and understanding. Peer educators act as role models, demonstrating effective self-management techniques. When patients see their peers managing their health successfully, it boosts their confidence and motivation59. Findings from the current study confirmed that a significant improvement in attitude was associated with PMHE itself. consistent with another longitudinal cohort study conducted in India on people living with diabetes. This may be due to healthcare providers lacking sufficient time to deliver training and ongoing support, owing to the increasing number of patients with diabetes compared to the available staff in diabetic clinics69.

Regarding medication adherence, the results indicated that the peer support learning strategy had a significant effect on adherence to diabetic medication over the last two weeks, compared to the control group. This aligns with a systematic review conducted in Iran assessing the impact of peer education on self-care behaviours in people living with diabetes which found that peer education increased medication adherence and demonstrated the acceptability and suitability of the intervention51,62. Additionally, these findings contrast with a cluster randomised controlled trial conducted in England, which found no impact of a peer support learning strategy on self-reported adherence to diabetic medication70. This discrepancy may be due to challenges in accurately recalling medication adherence among adults or differences in the methodological approaches of the current and previous studies. between the current study and the previous study.

Strengths

The study addressed a critical healthcare challenge in Tanzania on a topic that desperately needs innovative and sustainable solutions. The expert patient model has been demonstrated to be culturally appropriate and implementable. This study utilized readily available expert patients as peer support and showed that, with minimal facilitation and guidance, patients can effectively transfer knowledge and change behaviours of their colleagues; this was a key success factor for this intervention.

The study covered a comprehensive set of outcomes and their measurements across three domains, providing robust evidence of the intervention’s effectiveness. Most importantly, the peer-to-peer approach leveraged the powerful influence of lived experience and community support systems that are integral to Tanzanian culture.

Limitations and weaknesses

Several limitations were identified in this study, including a limited number of participants, which impacted the representativeness of the sample. To overcome this, we have used a rigor methodology and mitigated the limitation. Another limitation was the reported self-care practice, including adherence to anti-diabetic therapies, which might have contributed to information and recall biases. However, we overcome this by asking questions for a short period within a week, and we used a standardized, structured questions. The limited time affected the study’s ability to assess the long-term benefits for the participants, including adherence to anti-diabetic medications, health literacy, attitude, and self-care behaviour. Another limitation is professional bias when listening to the participants. We overcome this by training the interviewers to avoid leading questions or evaluative language, and to avoid interaction during sessions. Also, we used standardized questionnaire to minimize subjective influence during data collection.

The use of non-random allocation of hospitals could have influenced the study site’s potential bias. We overcome this by critically selecting hospitals which use the same document contextual factors (resources, patient demographics, policies and standard care protocols).

Another limitation is the effect of hospital-level cluster because allocation occurred at the hospital level, not the individual level. Individual observations may therefore not be fully independent. Hospital-level differences in staffing, clinical protocols, patient demographics, and standard care may have independently influenced outcomes beyond the intervention itself even though both of the hospital are referral hospitals with comparable service levels. Our analyses were conducted at the individual level without clustering adjustment, which may result in inflated precision. A properly designed cluster-randomized trial with mixed-effects analysis would be needed to fully address this.

Socioeconomic confounding was evident in this study, at baseline a statistically significant difference in source of income was found between groups (chi-square = 16.139, p = 0.003). The intervention group had substantially more small-scale business participants (56.9% vs. 27.8%). This difference was not controlled for in the analysis and may have independently influenced the observed outcomes. Future studies should include income as a covariate in ANCOVA models.

Another limitation is in our analysis where post-test comparisons were done without baseline adjustment despite of significant differences to some of important variables between intervention and control. Our analyses compared post-test group means without ANCOVA adjustment for baseline scores. While groups were comparable at baseline for most outcomes, this approach does not fully account for regression to the mean. ANCOVA or change-score analyses would provide more robust estimates.

Conclusion

The findings from this study provide preliminary evidence suggesting that the Peer Support Learning Strategy may effectively improve knowledge, attitudes, and self-care practices among adult patients with diabetes. These results highlight the significance of involving patients as peer supporters or experts in the long-term management of diabetes in adults. The peer support learning model can be a valuable supplementary approach for delivering Diabetes Self-Care Management Education to adult living with diabetes in resource-constrained communities, such as Tanzania. Additionally, this model should be expanded to other regions within Tanzania and to similar resource-limited settings beyond the country. Policymakers and hospital management should acknowledge the importance of peer support and integrate it into care models. Further research should conduct large-scale studies of the model with extended follow-up and fully assess its impact. Given the non-randomized, two-site design, short follow-up period, and the inability to fully control for hospital-level clustering and socioeconomic confounding, the results of this study should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of promise rather than definitive proof of effectiveness. These findings provide evidence for designing larger cluster-randomized controlled trials with robust analyses and longer follow-up periods.
