Human mandibular densitometric values in Nigeria's major ethnic groups

Background

Bone mineral density (BMD) measures the inorganic mineral content in bone, thus providing qualitative details of bone which determine its biomechanical properties [1, 2]. BMD varies across the skeletal system and multiple factors such as gender, age, diseases, genetics and lifestyle [2–5]. Quantification methods of BMD are diagnostic radiography, single-energy and dual-energy photon absorptiometry, neutron activation, single-energy and double energy x-ray absorptiometry, quantitative computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, quantitative ultrasound and Compton scattering [3]. Absorption in X-ray is proportional to the amount of calcium present in bone. Although reduction in bone mass becomes obvious after a decrease of about 20–40% in BMD, it was reported that grayscale values from radiographic images are directly proportional to BMD. Therefore, densitometric grayscale values of human bone speaks to its strength and health [6–8]. They vary from bone to bone in human skeleton and among various populations [8].

Densitometric grayscale values can be derived from radiographic images using a variety of techniques [1–5, 9, 10] . One of such is the computer assisted densitometric image analysis (CADIA) system. This involves the use of an image analysis software such as ImageJ, IDRISI Kilimanjaro, Digora and Orthoread to analyze a digital radiograph [1, 11, 12]. CADIA has been used to quantify variation in bone mineralization in a variety of pathological condition[13–16]. Although quantification of BMD by radiographic method may be dependent on the observer’s experience, the use of CADIA system has been found to be a reliable and sensitive tool [5, 17] .

Nigeria is a multi-ethnic nation with a diverse population and cultural practices. Some of these practices include dietary preference for consumption of tough fibrous diet that requires active engagement of muscles of mastication [18–20]. This may affect mandibular BMD of average Nigerians compared to other population with a less fibrous diet. To the best of our knowledge, there are no baseline densitometric values of human mandible in the Nigerian population. This makes it difficult to refer to normal values when treatments that involve bone healing and regeneration are carried out. The mean values of the bone density before implant surgery or orthodontic treatment will help clinicians in treatment planning and provide guidance in decision regarding the type of implant, implant position and healing period [21]. We hypothesized that bone mineral density may vary among and within each ethnic group across Nigeria. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the normative densitometric values of human mandible among three major ethnic groups in Nigeria.

Method

After obtaining ethics approval (protocol number ERC/2021/12/11) from the Ethics and Research Committee of Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals Complex, Ile-Ife, digital orthopantomographs (OPG) and clinical notes of 282 eligible Nigerians were retrieved. Inclusion criteria were 18 to 40 years old Nigerians with no clinical and radiographic jaw pathologies and absence of systemic bone diseases. Others were patients with no previous history of radiotherapy, chemotherapy and orthodontic treatment. Pregnant or lactating mothers, patients on antiresorptive medications (bisphosphonates) and those with metastatic bone malignancies were excluded. Using the formular for descriptive study whose main outcome will be expressed as mean [22] with a standard deviation of 0.03 g/cm2 for mandibular body bone mineral density in Kelly et al. [23] margin of error of 0.02 and 95% confidence interval, an estimated sample size of 25 images per group was calculated.

All OPG images were taken by a PM 2002 CC Proline machine (Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) with head positions standardized as much as possible and metallic objects that could constitute artefacts were removed from the head and neck region of each patient. All digital OPG images were acquired with an operational voltage of 80 kVp, maximum current of 12 mA and exposure time of 18 s. Digital OPGs of eligible participants were uploaded to the freely available ImageJ software (Wayne Rasband, National Institute of Health, USA) to determine the bone densities of specific locations (anterior mandible, body and angle) on the mandible [13, 24]. By clicking on the analyze command icon, the mean optical density of the selected region of the mandible was generated (Fig. 1a). Using the software, the density of bone in each anatomical location of the mandible was determined by three trained and validated observers independently (Fig. 1b and c) and a mean of the three values was computed. To ensure standardization, only digital OPG images that were taken using the aforementioned exposure conditions were included in the study. The generated values were entered into the study proforma.

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0. The normality of data was assessed by Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test with the level of significance set at p < 0.05. Variables that deviated from normality (p < 0.05) were transformed using logarithmic (log 10) or square root transformations. Intra-observer reliability was assessed using intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). Continuous variables were described with range, mean, and standard deviation. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and proportions. Pearson’s Chi-square statistics was used to determine relationship between BMD values and categorical variables (age group, gender and ethnicity). Student’s t test was used to compare the mean BMD values at different mandibular sites (e.g., anterior mandible vs. mandibular body, mandibular body vs. mandibular angle) within each ethnic group. One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the mean BMD values across the three ethnic groups for each mandibular site, followed by post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. To simultaneously analyze the BMD differences across multiple mandibular sites while comparing the three ethnic groups, Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was used. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Data were normally distributed except for the mean BMD grayscale values of anterior mandible, mandibular body and mandibular angle in the three ethnic groups. These variables were transformed using logarithmic (log10) and square root transformations (Supplementary Table 1).

Study population

Orthopantomograms of 280 participants aged 18 to 40 years with a mean (SD) at 30.47 (5.97) years were reviewed in this study. Figures 2 and 3 show the participants’ distribution across age groups by their gender and ethnic groups. They were 138 males and 142 females from the three major ethnic groups in Nigeria (Hausa = 91, Igbo = 93, and Yoruba = 96). Participants’ distribution across age groups did not differ significantly by gender (p = 0.71) and ethnic groups (p = 0.072). Intra-observer reliability was assessed using intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), which revealed excellent reliability (0.991–0.999) among the three observers (Supplementary Table 2).

Bone Mineral Density (BMD) across age groups and ethnic groups

Participants aged 18 to 20 years had the highest mean BMD at the anterior mandible (138.68 ± 16.8 grayscale value), while the lowest mean was seen in the 31–35 years age group (121.56 ± 22.77 grayscale value). The highest mandibular body BMD was also seen in the 18-20-year age group (98.30 ± 31.8 grayscale value) and the lowest in the 26–30 years age group (92.22 ± 28.09 grayscale value). The highest BMD for mandibular angle was observed in the 26–30-year age group (123.69 ± 21.17 grayscale value) while the lowest was in the 31–35-year age group with 117.47 ± 20.99 grayscale value. Overall, mean mandibular BMD for adult Nigerians were 127.81 ± 20.02, 94.15 ± 23.62 and 119.29 ± 20.89 grayscale values for anterior mandible, mandibular body and mandibular angle respectively (Table 1).

Hausa subjects’ mean BMD did not differ significantly from Igbos at the anterior mandible, mandibular body and angle (p = 0.035, 0.084 and 0.507 respectively) but was significantly different from the Yorubas at all sites (p < 0.001, p = 0.009 and p = 0.025 respectively). Bone mineral density across gender

There was no statistically significant difference (p = 0.937, F = 0.006) in the mean BMD values between male and female participants across the studied mandibular sites (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

The mean mandibular BMD for adult Nigerians were 127.81 ± 26.02, 94.15 ± 23.62 and 119.29 ± 20.89 grayscale values for anterior mandible, mandibular body and mandibular angle respectively. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report mandibular BMD values in healthy 18–40 years-old Nigerians across the three major ethnic groups, thereby providing baseline data for the studied population. The choice of the studied age group was informed by the desire to avoid active bone mineral changes associated with ongoing development in the very young and ageing processes including hormonal dysfunctions (peri and postmenopausal skeletal changes) in the elderly population. The use of digital OPG images is predicated on its ready accessibility and affordability to participants across the three major ethnic groups in Nigeria compared with computed tomography, which are only ordered for radiological assessment of clinically obvious maxillofacial pathologies. Furthermore, ImageJ was used as a CADIA tool because its freely available (open source) and user friendly [13].

This study shows that participants aged 18–20 years exhibited the highest mean BMD values in the anterior mandible and mandibular body, suggesting that peak bone density may be achieved in late adolescence or early adulthood. This is consistent with reports from other studies showing that about 60% of bone growth takes place during adolescence and reports that the earliest age to reach peak bone density is between 17 and 18 years [25, 26]. We however found no significant difference in anterior mandibular BMD across age groups, which may indicate that mandibular BMD remains relatively stable during young adulthood. This finding contrasts with mandibular body and angle gray scale values in our study, where BMD typically increases until around 30 years of age. The stability in anterior mandibular BMD might be due to the specific mechanical demands placed on it, which could help maintain its density through continuous use.

In the present study, mean BMD values of male and female participants did not differ significantly around age group. While one does not expect differences between males and females in the young adult population, it could be anticipated in the older subjects knowing that pre-menopausal changes may be setting in. This is similar to the findings of Ahmed et al. [21] who reported the jaw BMD of Pakistani population aged between 18 and 40 years.

Nigeria is a multiethnic nation with three major tribes, namely Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba. In this study, there were significant differences in BMD across the ethnic groups at all the studied mandibular sites (anterior mandible, mandibular body and mandibular angle). With the anterior mandible showing the largest difference, followed by the mandibular body and mandibular angle. Based on these findings, the study hypothesis was accepted.

Yoruba ethnic group demonstrated notably denser bone structure in the anterior regions compared to the Hausa and Igbo. Variations in dietary pattern in Nigeria’s multiethnic population is a well-established fact which could go on to affect the jawbone density by reason of nutritional components, dietary texture and masticatory load exerted on the jaws [18–20]. This finding is consistent with other studies that show substantial racial differences in BMD even within individuals of African or Asian origin [27]. Additionally, the variations can be attributed to genetic variations in the different ethnic groups [28].

Clinical implications

The anterior mandible offers excellent bone quality for dental implant placement. This is similar to the findings of Hao et al. [29] in China and Ahmed and colleagues [21] in Pakistan who reported anterior mandible as the site with the highest BMD in human mandible. This site and other teeth bearing regions of the mandible are suitable for dental implant placement except in cases of bone loss where bone grafting may be considered. A few studies suggest that higher BMD is directly related to good osseointegration which always increases implant stability and helps decrease the chances of peri-implantitis [30–33]. On the other hand, low BMD may jeopardize primary stability and negatively affect the healing phase at the implant interface, with potential development of implantitis and implant failure [31].

Bone mineral density significantly impacts grafting outcomes, particularly when considering the mandible as both a potential donor and recipient site. This study shows that the anterior mandible has the highest bone mineral density among the mandibular sites, suggesting its potential as an optimal donor site due to its capacity to serve a source of dense bone that enhance graft stability and integration. High mineral density in bone grafts increases the likelihood of successful osseointegration at the recipient site, as dense bone has greater resistance to resorption and provides a more stable foundation for new bone formation [34]. While as a recipient site, its high mineral density may facilitate higher graft survival and integration rates.

Differences in BMD at the various sites in the mandible may influence orthodontic treatment planning [35]. Areas with a higher mean BMD grayscale value such as the anterior mandible may need application of greater force to attain tooth movement compared to areas with lower BMD grayscale values such as the body of the mandible. This shows that space closure in the anterior mandibular area may take a longer time to occur. Also, impacted tooth traction will require a longer time for alignment within the arch since high forces are not generally indicated for tooth movement in orthodontics (due to attendant hyalinization) thus prolonged tooth movement is undertaken [36]. Accelerated tooth movement procedures or surgery will be faster in the body of mandible area due to lower BMD compared to the anterior mandible area [37]. Furthermore, the anterior mandible area will provide better support for the use of temporary anchorage devices (TAD) for intraoral full anchorage for teeth movement [38].

Findings from this study suggest that regions of the mandible with higher BMD grayscale value (anterior mandibular and angle) possess better strength to mechanical pressure thus lower chances of fracture while areas like the mandibular body may be vulnerable to fracture. Although vulnerability to fracture depends on the severity of injury, mechanism of injury and presence of teeth sockets in the teeth bearing region of the mandible especially if the teeth are impacted and unerupted [39, 40]. However, various clinical and epidemiological studies of mandibular fractures have reported anterior mandibular region to be least susceptible while angle (single fracture) and condyle (more than one fracture) and mandibular body are more at risk of being fractured [40, 41].

Strengths and limitations

One limitation of this study is that human mandibular BMD was determined using digital OPG images and not a more contemporary cone beam and conventional computerized tomography (CT) images which were not readily accessible to us. None of our centers use cone beam CT while conventional CT images do not meet our inclusion criteria (taken almost always for pathologies). However, inclusion of a representative Nigerian adults with statistically equal proportion of the three major tribes and sexes is one of the strengths of this study.

Future studies

Determining the BMD values in paediatric mandible (Nigerian subjects aged 10–17 years) using advanced imaging modalities and software is a desirable next line of research.

Conclusion

We reported mandibular BMD data in healthy 18–40 years-old Nigerians across the three major ethnic groups using OPGs and imageJ. This study provides insights into site-specific BMD variations across the anterior, body and mandibular angle regions in the three major Nigerian ethnic groups. The results substantiate the importance of age/ethnic considerations in individualized treatment planning based on localized bone density of different sites on the mandible. This could be particularly important when planning for surgical, orthodontic or prosthetic procedures in the lower jaw.
