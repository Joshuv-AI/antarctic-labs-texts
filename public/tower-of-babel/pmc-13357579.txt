A non-enzymatic function of neuraminidase 1 restrains hepatic glucagon response in mice
Authors: Xiao-Meng Sun, Li-Zeng Zhu, Guo-Dong Wu, Ting Wang, Xin-Yue Zhou, Xi-Hua Liu, Qi Wang, Wei Jiang, Bao-Lin Liu, An Pan, Lian-Wen Qi, Qun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDysregulated hepatic gluconeogenesis driven by glucagon is a key contributor to hyperglycemia in diabetes, yet the molecular mechanisms that restrain this pathway remain incompletely understood. This study reveals neuraminidase 1 (NEU1) as a suppressor of hepatic glucagon-driven gluconeogenesis in diabetes. We found that glucagon challenge downregulates hepatic NEU1 expression, inversely correlating with fasting blood glucose levels in individuals with diabetes. Functionally, liver-specific NEU1 overexpression antagonizes hepatic gluconeogenesis in glucagon-challenged mice and high fat diet (HFD)-fed mice, while NEU1 knockout augments glucagon response. Additionally, NEU1 inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis in a non-enzymatic manner. Mechanistically, NEU1 interacts with SAM68 to activate GCN5 promoter, leading to increase of PGC-1α acetylation. The inhibitory effect of NEU1 overexpression on gluconeogenesis is abrogated by GCN5 knockdown in HFD-fed mice. A NEU1-targeted screening strategy identified α-hederin and oleanolic acid as synergistic inhibitors of hepatic glucagon response through NEU1 stabilization and SAM68 binding enhancement. Here we show an inhibitory role of NEU1 in the hepatic glucagon response and suggest a potential avenue for targeting NEU1 to treat diabetes.Subject terms: Type 2 diabetes, Metabolic disorders, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Insulin and glucagon play pivotal roles in maintaining whole-body glucose homeostasis. Insulin mitigates postprandial hyperglycemia by facilitating glucose uptake into target tissues, while glucagon stimulates hepatic gluconeogenesis to sustain blood glucose levels during fasting or starvation1. Accumulating evidence from human and animal studies shows that plasma glucagon concentrations are abnormally elevated in individuals with obesity or diabetes2,3. Dysregulation of the hepatic glucagon response induces excessive hepatic glucose production, contributing to fasting hyperglycemia in diabetes4,5. Hepatic glucose production involves two key processes: glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, both of which are intricately regulated by nutrient and hormonal signaling pathways. Mechanisms to reduce circulating glucagon level and antagonize the hepatic glucagon response in target tissues are well-recognized means to reducing hyperglycemia in diabetes6–9.

PGC-1α is a potent stimulator of hepatic gluconeogenesis, partly by upregulating the expression of key gluconeogenic enzymes such as glucose-6-phosphatase (G6Pase) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK1). It also plays a crucial role in the activation of the fasting gluconeogenic program by co-activating transcription factors forkhead box protein O1 (FoxO1) and hepatocyte nuclear factor 410,11. The activity of PGC-1α is intricately regulated through multiple mechanisms, including its transcriptional regulation12, protein stability13, and post-translational modifications14,15. Of these, the acetylation of PGC-1α has been extensively studied across various nutrient and energetic states15. PGC-1α contains at least 13 acetylation sites15, these acetylation modifications are negatively correlated with protein activity. General control non-repressed protein 5 (GCN5) is responsible for catalyzing the acetylation of PGC-1α. This process is accompanied by a shift in PGC-1α’s localization from the promoter regions of its target genes to unidentified nuclear foci16. Moreover, GCN5 has been shown to effectively repress the transcription of PGC-1α’s target genes in both hepatocytes and myotubes16,17, highlighting its role in the intricate regulatory network that modulates gluconeogenesis and glucose homeostasis.

Neuraminidases (sialidases) are abundant lysosomal/plasma membrane enzymes that catabolize sialylated glycoconjugates and trim cell surface sialoglycoproteins18. The most well-studied is influenza virus surface neuraminidase, which removes sialic acid from host receptors to facilitate viral release19. Mammalian neuraminidases include four members (NEU1-NEU4), all mediating the initial step of glycoconjugate degradation via sialic acid removal20. Neuraminidases participate in glucose and lipid metabolism regulation, with NEU1 being critical for metabolic homeostasis21,22. Increased NEU1 enzymatic activity in insulin-sensitive tissues ameliorates insulin resistance and glucose intolerance, supporting it as a potential therapeutic target for type 2 diabetes21–23. Specifically, NEU1 exerts its regulatory function through desialylation of key metabolic receptors, most prominently the insulin receptor (IR). By cleaving terminal sialic acids from the IR’s N-linked glycans, NEU1 induces a conformational change that promotes the formation of the active IR dimer, thereby enhancing downstream insulin signaling cascades21. Recent studies have highlighted non-enzymatic activity of NEU1 in modulating a variety of signaling pathways24,25. Our previous research demonstrated that NEU1 binds to the transcription factor GATA4, thereby driving cardiac hypertrophy26. However, the role of NEU1 in hepatic gluconeogenesis, specifically its non-enzymatic activity role in glucagon response, remains largely unexplored.

In this work, we comprehensively investigated the function of NEU1 in hepatic glucagon response. NEU1 was identified as a key inhibitor of excessive hepatic glucose production in diabetes. Mechanistically, NEU1 exerts its effect in a non-enzymatic manner by interacting with SAM68 to activate GCN5 transcription. This leads to increased acetylation of PGC-1α, thereby suppressing the hepatic glucagon response. Furthermore, we employed a targeted screening strategy and discovered that the combination of α-hederin and oleanolic acid synergistically inhibits the hepatic gluconeogenesis by stabilizing NEU1 and enhancing its interaction with SAM68. These findings characterize NEU1’s potential as a therapeutic target for diabetes.

Results

Glucagon impaired the protein expression of NEU1 in the liver

In glucagon-challenged mice, we observed that NEU1 protein was impaired in the liver (Fig. 1a, e), while the expression of other isoforms including NEU2, NEU3 and NEU4 were not affected (Fig. 1a). Notably, the mRNA levels of Neu family genes were not changed (Fig. 1b). These observations were recapitulated in primary hepatocytes with glucagon stimulation (Fig. 1c, d). In close agreement, hepatic NEU1 expression was also reduced in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed, ob/ob, and db/db mice (Fig. 1e, f), which are characterized by elevated glucagon levels (Supplementary Fig. 1a–c). Moreover, hepatic NEU1 expression decreased in a dose-dependent manner upon glucagon injection (Fig. 1g), corresponded to a negative correlation (R2 = 0.6095) between NEU1 protein and serum glucagon levels (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, NEU1 expression was reduced in a concentration and time-dependent manner upon glucagon stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b).

In line with the findings in animal models, the NEU1 protein levels in human liver samples from individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) were also significantly downregulated compared to those from non-diabetic (ND) individuals (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Table 1), further validating a negative linear correlation (R2 = 0.7092) between the NEU1 expression and fasting blood glucose (Fig. 1j).

Since Neu1 mRNA level was not affected by glucagon, we further investigated whether the reduction of NEU1 is linked to post-transcriptional regulation. Lysosomal inhibitor chloroquine, but not proteasome inhibitor MG132, prevented the loss of NEU1 protein (Supplementary Fig. 4a–c), suggesting that glucagon impaired NEU1 through lysosomal degradation pathway.

NEU1 knockout augmented hepatic gluconeogenesis in mice

Hepatic NEU1 deficiency alone is sufficient to increase fasting glucose in mice (Fig. 2b). In glucagon tolerance test (GTT), hepatic Neu1 deficiency augmented hyperglycemic response (Fig. 2c). Expressions of the hepatic gluconeogenic genes (Pck1 and G6pc) and proteins were enhanced by Neu1 knockout in the presence of glucagon challenge (Fig. 2d, e).

NEU1 restrained hepatic glucagon response in a non-enzymatic function

To the mechanism, it has been reported that the enzymatic activity of NEU1 influences insulin sensitivity by desialylating glycan chains on the IR22 (Fig. 5a). Consistent with this, we observed that Neu1 HepKO mice exhibited reduced insulin sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 11). Furthermore, under glucagon stimulation, knockdown/overexpression of Neu1 inhibited/enhanced insulin signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 12, 13). We further substituted alanine residues at key catalytic sites of NEU1 (Asp97, Glu258, Tyr364, Glu388) using site-directed mutagenesis. This mutation led to increased α2,6-linked sialylation levels (detected by SNA lectin) without affecting NEU1 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. 14). The mutated NEU1 failed to effectively activate the insulin signaling or desialylate the IR (Fig. 5b, c), demonstrating that NEU1 regulates insulin sensitivity in an enzymatic activity-dependent manner.

Interestingly, under glucagon stimulation, the mutated NEU1 still suppressed the expression of gluconeogenic genes (Fig. 5d) and reduced hepatic glucose production (Fig. 5e), indicating that NEU1 regulates gluconeogenesis independently of its enzymatic activity. Subsequently, to validate the ability of the mutant NEU1 to improve glucose metabolism in vivo, we established a liver-specific NEU1 enzymatic activity mutant model by tail vein injection of AAV8 mutant NEU1 (AAV-Neu1Mut) in Neu1 HepKO mice (Fig. 5f). Compared to wild-type NEU1, mutation of the NEU1 catalytic sites increased sialylation levels in both liver and serum (Supplementary Fig. 15). However, no significant differences were observed in fasting blood glucose, GTT, and PTT between AAV-Neu1Mut and AAV-Neu1 mice, except for OGTT (Fig. 5g–j). Furthermore, under glucagon challenge, the enzymatic mutation did not alter the expression levels of key gluconeogenic genes and proteins (Fig. 5k, l). Notably, this suppression of gluconeogenesis persisted even when insulin sensitivity was significantly impaired (Fig. 5m). These in vivo results further demonstrate that NEU1 regulates gluconeogenesis via an enzyme activity-independent mechanism, and this effect is not reliant on changes in insulin sensitivity.

NEU1 increased acetylation of PGC−1α via activating GCN5

The hepatic glucagon response is primarily mediated by the canonical cyclic AMP (cAMP)/protein kinase A (PKA)/cAMP-response element-binding protein (CREB) pathway27 (Fig. 6a). But, neither knockdown nor overexpression of NEU1 affected this cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling axis (Supplementary Fig. 16). Activation of PKA by cAMP leads to the phosphorylation of CREB, which subsequently enhances the activity of the transcriptional coactivator PGC-1α28. The transcriptional efficacy of PGC-1α, a prerequisite for activating gluconeogenic genes, is further governed by its acetylation status29. Notably, overexpression of Neu1 did not alter the mRNA level of Ppargc1a in hepatocytes (Fig. 6b), but significantly suppressed PGC-1α protein expression and enhanced the acetylation and ubiquitination of PGC-1α (Fig. 6c, d).

PGC-1α acetylation and ubiquitination is dynamically regulated by deacetylases SIRTs and the acetyltransferase GCN529. Intriguingly, Neu1 overexpression in hepatocytes significantly increases mRNA level of Gcn5, but showed non-significant effects on Sirts (Fig. 6e). Consistently, Neu1 overexpression elevated GCN5 protein level (Supplementary Fig. 17). To the contrast, Neu1 knockdown decreased Gcn5 mRNA level in hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 18). Co-immunoprecipitation assay further revealed that glucagon stimulation impaired the interaction between PGC-1α and GCN5, and Neu1 overexpression diminished this effect (Fig. 6f, g and Supplementary Fig. 19). Crucially, in Gcn5 deficient hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 20), the inhibitory effect of NEU1 on gluconeogenesis was abolished (Fig. 6h, i).

To confirm if NEU1 regulates hepatic gluconeogenesis depending on GCN5 in vivo, hepatic Gcn5 was knockdown and Neu1 was overexpressed by tail vein injection of AAV8 in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Fig. 21a, b). As expected, 12 weeks of HFD feeding elevated circulating glucagon (Fig. 6k). Neu1 overexpression effectively lowered fasting hyperglycemia, Pck1 and G6pc genes induction, and improved PTT and OGTT in a GCN5-dependent manner without altering glucagon levels (Fig. 6l–o). No significant influences on metabolic parameters were observed in AAV-injected mice during HFD feeding (Supplementary Fig. 21c–e).

NEU1 interacted with SAM68 to activate Gcn5 promoter

To identify potential NEU1-interacting proteins, we performed a co-immunoprecipitation-liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry assay using an anti-NEU1 antibody on hepatocyte (Fig. 7a). Among the proteins identified, SAM68 is reported to regulate the activity of transcription factors30 (Fig. 7b). In situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) was performed to validate the interaction of SAM68 and NEU1. The strong fluorescent signals suggest a direct interaction between SAM68 and NEU1 in hepatocytes (Fig. 7c). In addition, the high sensitivity structured Illumination microscope (HIS-SIM) result showed that the co-localization between SAM68 and NEU1 was reduced under glucagon stimulation (Fig. 7d). Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) was further used to detect the direct interaction between SAM68 and NEU1 in hepatocytes. If binding happens, the VC155 fragment fused to NEU1 and the VN173 fused to SAM68 are brought into proximity, thus producing a fluorescent signal. Fluorescent signals detected in hepatocytes indicate a strong binding of SAM68 and NEU1 (Fig. 7e). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) showed that SAM68 displayed a favorable binding affinity to NEU1 (Fig. 7f). Moreover, we confirmed that NEU1 bound to SAM68 in primary hepatocytes by co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 7g).

To characterize the binding domains mediating the SAM68-NEU1 interaction, we first employed immunoprecipitation assays using truncated constructs and identified the specific regions responsible for binding. Our results showed that the N-terminal region (amino acids 1-111) of NEU1 constitutes the major domain responsible for its interaction with SAM68 (Fig. 7h). Moreover, it was observed that the amino acid regions 100-260 and 197-327 of SAM68 are key domains required for binding to NEU1 (Fig. 7i). Furthermore, we predicted the binding sites between NEU1 and SAM68 using AlphaFold3 (Fig. 7j). Besides the catalytic sites in NEU1, residues E89, N132, L133, E218, Q247, and Y279 were predicted to be key binding sites for SAM68. To further test the binding of SAM68 to these sites, alanine residues were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis to replace each site. Immunoblotting assay showed that mutagenesis with alanine residue of NEU1 (E89A, N132A, L133A, E218A, Q247A, Y279A) blocked the binding with SAM68 (Fig. 7k), and diminished its inhibitory effects on gluconeogenic genes (Fig. 7l).

To determine whether the inhibitory effect of NEU1 on gluconeogenesis is dependent on SAM68, gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed. The inhibitory effect of Neu1 overexpression on glucagon-induced gluconeogenesis was augmented in Sam68 deficient hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 22a, b), and was abolished by Sam68 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 22c, d).

It is known that SAM68 translocates to the nucleus in response to glucagon signaling to promote gene transcription31. Neu1 overexpression impeded this process by suppressing the nuclear exclusion of SAM68, whereas Neu1 knockdown promoted the nuclear translocation of SAM68 following glucagon stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 23). This suggests that NEU1 retains SAM68 in the cytoplasm, thereby limiting its transcriptional activity within the nucleus. We then investigated the functional consequence of this cytoplasmic retention on Gcn5 expression. Luciferase reporter assays revealed that SAM68 overexpression significantly inhibited NEU1-induced Gcn5 transcription (Fig. 7m). Consistent with this, Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR confirmed the binding of SAM68 to the promoter regions of Gcn5 in hepatocytes overexpressing Neu1 (Fig. 7n, o and Supplementary Fig. 24).

Herb compound combination synergistically inhibited hepatic glucagon response

In order to identify compounds that sustain NEU1 expression or enhance the NEU1-SAM68 interaction, we conducted a two-step screen of 109 natural products using Simple Western and SPR assays (Fig. 8a). Simple Western analysis revealed that α-hederin most effectively stabilized NEU1 protein (Fig. 8b), a result confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 25a). Parallel SPR screening for compounds that promote NEU1-SAM68 binding identified oleanolic acid as the top candidate (Fig. 8c, d), which was further validated by immunoprecipitation (Supplementary Fig. 25b). Functionally, α-hederin (1 μM) and oleanolic acid (1 μM) showed stronger inhibitory effects on the expression of Pck1 and G6pc in primary hepatocytes compared with other compounds, with no cytotoxicity observed at this concentration (Fig. 8e and Supplementary Fig. 26a, b). Their combination not only significantly suppressed glucagon-induced gluconeogenesis in vitro but also exhibited a synergistic effect (Fig. 8f). The effects of herb compound combination on gluconeogenesis were blocked by Neu1 knockdown in primary hepatocytes, indicating the inhibitory effect depending on NEU1 (Fig. 8g).

Consistent with these in vitro findings, co-administration of α-hederin and oleanolic acid (1 mg/kg each) effectively inhibited hepatic gluconeogenesis in glucagon-challenged wild-type mice (Fig. 8h–j) but was diminished in Neu1 HepKO mice (Fig. 8k–m). Additionally, this combined treatment specifically promoted insulin signaling pathways in the liver, with no significant effects observed in muscle or adipose tissue (Supplementary Fig. 27).

Discussion

This study delineates a pivotal role of NEU1 in hepatic gluconeogenesis, as evidenced by genetic, in vivo, and in vitro experiments. The major findings of this work include: (1) Hepatic NEU1 levels were significantly decreased in clinical liver samples from diabetic patients, glucagon-disordered mice or hepatocytes. (2) We characterized NEU1 as an inhibitor of hepatic gluconeogenesis through genetically-engineered mice and primary hepatocytes models. (3) Mechanistically, NEU1 interacts with SAM68 to activate Gcn5 promoter, leading to increase of PGC-1α acetylation. (4) We screened the herb compound combination and identified a synergistic hypoglycemic pair, α‑hederin and oleanolic acid, which acts by stabilizing NEU1 expression and promoting its binding to SAM68. The proposed mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 9. Collectively, our data reveal a non-enzymatic mechanism of NEU1 and point to it as a potential new strategy for diabetes treatment.

Scheme of the role for NEU1 in restraining hepatic gluconeogenesis.

Under basal condition, NEU1 binds to SAM68 and prevents its nuclear translocation. The GCN5 catalyzes PGC-1α acetylation, promoting its ubiquitination and degradation, thereby suppressing gluconeogenic gene transcription. Upon glucagon stimulation, hepatic NEU1 is degraded via the lysosomal pathway, reducing its binding to SAM68. SAM68 then translocates into the nucleus, inhibits the GCN5 promoter, and downregulates GCN5 expression. Consequently, decreased acetylation stabilizes PGC-1α, enabling it to activate downstream gluconeogenic gene expression. Created in BioRender. Li-Zeng, Z. (https://BioRender.com/huh36wx).

Insulin and glucagon work in concert to maintain systemic glucose homeostasis. Although prior studies have linked NEU1 to glucose regulation via the insulin receptor pathway21,22, our clamp data provide direct in vivo evidence that hepatic knockout of NEU1 impairs both systemic and hepatic insulin sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 11). The insulin receptor is a known target of NEU1, which promotes receptor activation by removing terminal sialic acid residues from its glycan chains, supporting the view that NEU1’s enzymatic activity is essential for modulating insulin signaling22,23. Further studies using an enzymatically inactive NEU1 mutant, both in vitro and in vivo, confirm that NEU1 primarily depends on this catalytic function to activate insulin signaling through desialylation of the IR (Fig. 5). By contrast, NEU1 inhibition of gluconeogenesis persisted even in the absence of enzymatic activity (Fig. 5), indicating that this regulatory action is independent of both insulin signaling and NEU1 catalytic function. Together, these findings reveal two distinct mechanisms through which NEU1 regulates glucose metabolism: one enzyme-dependent and linked to insulin sensitization, and another enzyme-independent and directed at gluconeogenic control. These dual modes of action suggest that future therapeutic strategies targeting NEU1 for diabetes should not only seek to modulate its enzymatic activity but also consider approaches to stabilize or enhance NEU1 expression, thereby harnessing both arms of its glucoregulatory function.

Pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockdown of NEU1 has been shown to ameliorate extrahepatic pathologies such as cardiac hypertrophy26 and renal fibrosis25, as well as hepatic lipid accumulation in non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease32. This stands in direct contrast to its protective role in hepatic glucose metabolism, where NEU1 activation or sustained expression counteracts insulin resistance and suppresses gluconeogenesis. Such stark, context‑dependent duality underscores the therapeutic challenge of targeting NEU1, while its suppression may be beneficial in certain disease settings, it risks impairing essential glucoregulatory functions. Consequently, the development of future NEU1‑targeted therapies might prioritize tissue selectivity and mechanism‑based specificity to balance these opposing roles and avoid adverse systemic metabolic outcomes resulting from broad NEU1 inhibition. Notably, in our studies, hepatocyte‑specific knockdown or overexpression of Neu1 did not alter body weight gain, or hepatic lipid deposition in HFD‑fed mice, which contrasted with reports that systemic neuraminidase inhibitors reduce body weight in obese mice32,33. We speculate that this discrepancy may arise from the non‑tissue‑selective nature of pharmacological inhibitors, which likely affect NEU1 activity across multiple tissues, including adipose tissue where NEU1 influence lipolysis34.

NEU1 was initially characterized as a lysosomal enzyme that can translocate to the cell surface, where it removes terminal sialic acids from membrane receptors35 In this study, we demonstrate that NEU1 interacts with SAM68 in the cytoplasm and inhibits SAM68 nuclear translocation. This cytoplasmic interaction was confirmed by HIS-SIM, BiFC and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assays (Fig. 7d, e and Supplementary Fig. 23). These observations align with our group’s previous finding that NEU1 can dynamically shuttle between cellular compartments to exert distinct pathological functions: in the nucleus, it interacts with transcriptional factors such as GATA4 to promote cardiac remodeling26, while in the cytoplasm, it binds to receptors including ALK5 to drive renal fibrosis25. SAM68 is a ubiquitously expressed RNA‑binding protein containing a KH domain36,37. It carries a nuclear localization signal in its C‑terminus, enabling nucleocytoplasmic shuttling and allowing it to fulfill transcriptional and post‑transcriptional regulatory roles38,39. Previous studies have shown that SAM68 interacts with and stabilizes CRTC2, a key coactivator that promotes hepatic gluconeogenesis by activating transcription of the rate‑limiting enzymes Pck1 and G6pc40. Interestingly, our results reveal that SAM68 also binds directly to the promoter of Gcn5. The downregulation of GCN5 leads to reduced acetylation of PGC‑1α, which in turn promote hepatic gluconeogenesis, uncovering a new mechanism by which SAM68 regulates glucose metabolism.

The herb compound combination of α-hederin and oleanolic acid, which we screened for its ability to stabilize NEU1 and promote NEU1-SAM68 binding, demonstrated a synergistic glucose-lowering effect. Notably, both compounds coexist naturally in the herb Lonicera japonica41 and Hedera helix42, which has been reported to inhibit α‑glucosidase—an established target for modulating postprandial glucose43,44. Oleanolic acid is a natural triterpenoid found in various foods and medicinal plants. Preclinical studies have shown that it exhibits hypoglycemic properties, along with protective effects against diabetes-associated oxidative stress45. A clinical trial in Spain further reported that consumption of oleanolic acid-enriched olive oil reduced the risk of developing type 2 diabetes by 55.2% in prediabetic individuals46, supporting its promise for diabetes prevention. Research on α‑hederin remains largely preclinical47–50, and its clinical translation is limited by poor solubility, low oral bioavailability, and cytotoxicity. In an acute toxicity study, our combination at a dose 200 times the therapeutic level induced a mild increase in serum aminotransferases in mice, with no adverse effects observed in other tissues (Supplementary Fig. 26). Future strategies should therefore employ lower, safer doses to ensure pharmacological safety.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations that should be addressed in future research: (1) The relative contribution of enzymatic versus non-enzymatic functions of NEU1 to systemic glucose regulation requires further investigation; (2) α-hederin treatment exhibited mild hepatotoxicity and a narrow therapeutic window, necessitating further chemical modifications to enhance its therapeutic potential; (3) The potential involvement of NEU1 in gluconeogenesis triggered by various nutrition or hormonal signals remains to be determined.

In conclusion, this study identifies an inhibitory role for NEU1 in glucagon-induced gluconeogenesis and suggests a potential therapeutic approach by targeting NEU1 to treat diabetes.

Methods

Liver specimens from human

All liver samples were obtained from adult patients undergoing liver biopsy, with specimens sourced from Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital in China. The study participants, including both patients and controls, comprised males and females ranging in age from 48 to 82 years, all of whom provided written informed consent for sample collection. Participants with fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L were classified as having T2D. According to health records, T2D patients had been diagnosed with the disease for at least one year prior to undergoing liver biopsy. Control samples were obtained from distant non-cancerous tissues of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma, which were confirmed to have no pathological abnormalities. Detailed information of all participants is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Animals and treatment

8-week-old male C57BL/6 J, ob/ob and db/db mice were purchased from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Biotechnology (Nanjing, China). Mice were fed either a high fat diet (60% kcal from fat; #D12492; Research diet, America) or a normal chow diet (10% kcal from fat; # XTI01WC-011; Xietong Organism, China) for 12 weeks. Db/db mice were fed a normal chow diet. Animals were housed under controlled humidity and temperature in standard facilities (24–26 °C) under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h dark/light cycle (lights on between 8 AM and 8 PM).

Liver-specific Neu1 knockout mice

Hepatocyte-specific Neu1 deletion mice (Neu1 HepKO mice) were created by mating Neu1 Flox (C57BL/6JGpt-H11em1Cflox/Gpt, GemPharmatech, China) mice with Alb-Cre mice (C57BL/6JGpt-H11em1Cin(Alb-iCre)/Gpt, GemPharmatech, China). The filial 1 progeny (Neu1fl/+, Alb-Cre+) mice litters with heterozygous deletion of Neu1 gene that harbored the Alb-Cre transgene were obtained. The Neu1fl/+, Alb-Cre+ mice further crossed with Neu1 Flox mice to obtain the mice expressing complete deletion of NEU1 in the filial 2 progeny (Neu1fl/fl, Alb-Cre+), named as Neu1 HepKO mice. Neu1 Flox mice littermates were used in the study as controls for the obtained Neu1 HepKO mice.

Cell line

HEK-293 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China; STR-authenticated). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Passaging was performed the day after seeding, and subsequent subcultures were carried out every 2 days upon reaching 80–90% confluence. All experiments were conducted using cells at passages 3–10.

Primary hepatocytes isolation and culture

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from livers of male C57BL/6J mice (6-8 weeks). Briefly, mice were anesthetized and cannulated at their portal veins by 0.5% collagenase IV (Gibco, America) solution. Digested liver was carefully excised and filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (biosharp, China). DMEM containing 50% percoll (Sigma, America) was used to remove cell debris and separate dead cells by gradient centrifugation (4 °C, 350 g, 5 min). The isolated hepatocytes were cultured in DMEM containing 10%FBS in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. All treatments in hepatocytes are indicated in the figure legends.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The total RNA was extracted using TRIzolTM Reagent (Vazyme, China). Generally, cDNA was synthesized using a cDNA Synthesis kit (Yeasen, China). All qRT-PCR reactions were performed on the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR 96 System (ThermoFisher, America) using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China). All the primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blot

Samples were electrophoresed on 8–12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked by 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 2 h and then incubated with primary antibody (Supplementary Table 3) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies. Proteins were detected by electrochemiluminescence (ECL) (Tanon, China). Automated Simple Western system (Bio-Techne, America) was also used to quantified the sample protein.

Immunofluorescence

Primary hepatocytes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C for overnight. After incubation with secondary antibodies and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), the cells were analyzed using a confocal scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan) or superresolution imaging with HIS-SIM (High Sensitivity Structured Illumination, CSR Biotech, China).

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

Duolink in situ kit (Sigma-Aldrich, America) were used following the manufacturer’s protocol. After fixation and permeation, hepatocytes were incubated with NEU1 and SAM68 antibodies for 40 min and a pair of PLA probes (anti-mouse Minus and anti-Rabbit Plus) for 60 min; ligase was added for 30 min, followed by amplification of signal for 100 min using red detection reagents. Sealed with clear nail polish. Fluorescence signal amplification was observed using the confocal scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC)

Transfections were carried out using the Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (ThermoFisher, America), with pBiFC-NEU1-VC155 and pBiFC-SAM68-VN173 plasmids. After 24 h transfection, cells were incubated with Hoechst 33342 staining solution (Beyotime, China). Fluorescence signal amplification was observed using the confocal scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamps

The mouse hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp was performed according to established protocols51. Following anesthesia, a catheter (PE-10) was implanted into the right jugular vein, and mice were allowed to recover for 5 days. After a 5 h fast, mice were weighed and placed in a restrainer. The basal period commenced with a bolus infusion of stable isotope-labeled glucose ([6,6-2H] glucose, 500 μg/kg), followed by a continuous tracer infusion at a constant rate (50 μg/kg/min) to label the endogenous glucose pool. Blood samples were collected at t = -10 and 0 min to determine basal metabolic parameters. The clamp period was then initiated with a continuous infusion of insulin at a fixed rate (4 mU kg/min). Concurrently, a variable-rate infusion of exogenous glucose was administered. Blood glucose levels were monitored every 10 min, and the glucose infusion rate was adjusted dynamically to rapidly achieve and maintain euglycemia at a target level of 100 ± 5 mg/dL. Additional blood samples were collected at t = 110 and 120 min. All plasma samples were deproteinized by mixing with cold methanol, centrifuged, and the resulting supernatant was lyophilized for subsequent analysis. Finally, plasma isotopic enrichment was determined using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry, enabling the calculation of relevant parameters under both basal and insulin-stimulated conditions.

Statistics

All results were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by using a two-tailed Student’s t test, and those among three or more groups by using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical significance analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, America).
