Returned but Not Restored: Experiences of Health and Access to Care Among Return Migrants in Sub‐Saharan Africa—A Scoping Review
Authors: Sif Sofie Vange, Gershim Asiki, Ama de‐Graft Aikins, Charles Agyemang, Eshetu Girma, Ernestina Korleki Dankyi, Marie Norredam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionReturn migrants form a diverse group, ranging from highly skilled to low‐skilled workers, who travel by regular or irregular migration routes and experience differing levels of physical and mental harm during transit and at destination. Further, their conditions of return may pose significant hardships and risks for their health. Despite the significant number of return migrants in Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA), limited research exists from the region addressing the health status of return migrants and their access to healthcare services.ObjectiveTo examine experiences of health and access to care among return migrants in SSA.MethodsA scoping review was conducted including studies available at PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, The Cochrane Library, PsychINFO, and Global Index Medicus from the database inception to January 2025. The primary outcomes included were: (re)migration‐related risk factors for ill health, health outcomes upon return and re‐integration and access to healthcare services.ResultsThis scoping review identified 15 studies from four SSA countries, with the majority originating from Ethiopia. Return migrants' health and wellbeing are affected by experiences of violence during their migration journey, which impacts their mental and physical health. Return migrants report experiencing stigma, mental health and physical health symptoms, as well as challenges to accessing health services both during the migration journey and upon return. After return, they report experiencing social isolation and stigma, which complicate their reintegration.ConclusionOur findings underscore the scarcity of research on the health and wellbeing of return migrants in SSA, emphasizing the need for further studies to better understand the challenges they face and develop targeted interventions to address the health and wellbeing of return migrants in SSA.

Introduction

According to the United Nations, there were an estimated 304 million international migrants globally in 2024 [1]. While precise figures are difficult to obtain, the International Organization of Migration (IOM) has identified 40.4 million return migrants in the last two decades [2]. Despite uncertainties in these numbers, policy attention has increased around return migrants, particularly in their countries of origin, countries of destination [3] and especially in countries enforcing their return [4]. Regulations are becoming more stringent in efforts to return irregular migrants [5]. However, the return experiences of this specific group have received less attention.

Return migration is defined as the act or process of going back or being taken back to the point of departure whether within a country or between countries [6]. Millions of Africans live outside of their country of origin either within the region or outside [7], with the major drivers for African migration being economic and social opportunities, conflict and climate change [8, 9, 10]. For many, migration represents a hopeful yet uncertain path to a better future, prompting individuals across Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) to leave their homes in search of better opportunities for themselves and their families. However, some migrants are forced to return through deportation from host countries while others return voluntarily, through spontaneous or assisted return, such as the 41,430 assisted returns to SSA reported by IOM in 2023 [11]. Return migrants form a diverse group, ranging from highly skilled to low‐skilled workers, who travel by regular or irregular migration routes and experience differing levels of physical and mental harm during the journey and at the destination. The two most dominant subgroups of returnees are the migrants travelling irregularly across Sahara towards Libya and Europe and the domestic work‐migrants to Arab states [12]. The perils they encounter during the journey to and at the destination and the conditions of their return pose significant hardships and risks for them [13]. Some may experience gender‐based violence and other forms of violence, rape, road accidents, dehydration, exhaustion, illness, as well as hazardous living conditions such as staying in overcrowded detention and deportation centers [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. These experiences expose migrants to both physical and mental health challenges, which linger even after they have returned to countries of origin. While at the destination, an interplay of social determinants, health risk factors and structural barriers—such as immigration and labour policies—shapes migrants' access to healthcare, which further compounds their health conditions upon return [19]. Back in their countries of origin, the health challenges of returnees can be exacerbated by the complex dynamics between returnees, family and their communities [20, 21]. Existing studies from SSA and globally on return migrants have predominantly focused on economic and social reintegration [22, 23, 24, 25]. Cultural reintegration has received comparatively less attention [25, 26, 27], and the healthcare needs and access to services for returnees remain underexplored. Here, reintegration is defined as successful and sustainable when returnees achieve economic self‐sufficiency, social stability and psychosocial well‐being [28].

Studies on the health of return migrants have cast particular attention to return migrants in Latin America [29, 30], especially return migrants from the United States to Mexico [22, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37]. These studies report health vulnerabilities such as stress, anxiety and mental disorders as common among deported migrants, highlighting the need for mental health access [29, 31, 37]. Interestingly, a study from Nepal reported a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms among male return migrants compared to non‐migrants [38]. Additional studies on return Nepalese migrants highlight limited healthcare access abroad, poor sanitation and occupational exposures linked to risk of kidney disease [39, 40]. A systematic review of studies from Asia, SSA, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean investigated the effects of international migration on the health of households with migrants and on the health of returned migrants [41]. They found that return migrants generally experience poorer mental health and exhibit more risk factors, such as smoking and alcohol consumption. The review concluded that it is difficult to generalize across regions and returnee populations, as the health effects of return migration appear to be influenced by factors such as gender, ethnicity, age and the specific migratory experience and regional political context [41]. Despite the significant number of return migrants in SSA, there remains a notable paucity of research from the region addressing the health status of return migrants and their access to healthcare services. This gap, along with the expected future increase in return migration to SSA and the inconsistencies in findings across different regions, highlights the need for research focused specifically on SSA. Therefore, the aim of this scoping review was to examine experiences of health and access to care among return migrants in SSA.

Methods

Search Strategy and Selection Criteria

For this scoping review we searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, The Cochrane Library, PsychINFO and Global Index Medicus from the database inception to the 15th of October 2024. We searched for both qualitative and quantitative original studies in SSA and included both forced and voluntary return migration. Additional publications were identified through references and the authors own sources until the 31st of January 2025. The full list of our key search terms is provided in Table 1. Two reviewers independently screened title and abstract of 406 articles and excluded those that were not relevant due to geographical scope, study population, publication type and language. The full text of the remaining 42 studies was independently screened to assess eligibility. Consensus for inclusion were reached between the two reviewers and discrepancy on study inclusion were resolved through discussion. The review was done in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews Checklist [42]. For the purposes of this scoping review, we define return migrants as individuals or groups who have returned from international migration, that is, beyond their country's borders, without a scheduled plan for further outmigration. We excluded: (i) non‐empirical research, (ii) non‐peer‐reviewed studies, (iii) internal and circular migration, (iv) Internally Displaced Persons, (v) and short‐term medical returns. We also excluded COVID‐19 studies, as they were conducted under unique circumstances that significantly impacted global migrants. We defined health and well‐being based on WHOs definition of health as ‘a state of complete physical, mental and social well‐being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity’ [43]. We tailored our search strategies to each database and used controlled vocabulary where available. Language was limited to English.

Data Analysis

We extracted data on study design, setting and population as well as study focus of relevance for this review. To facilitate comparison across diverse study types, we extracted the specific stage of migration addressed in each study. This decision was necessary to highlight that the circumstances surrounding the migration process can create differential health risks for migrants at the different stages of migration [44] including at pre‐departure, travel, in destination country, return transit and upon return [45].

Outcomes of interest were health status of return migration, their access to health care services and psycho‐social wellbeing upon return. The included studies were coded thematically, and patterns among them were identified, creating three major categories for outcomes to be reported by: (Re)migration‐related risk factors for ill health, health outcomes and re‐integration and access to healthcare service at destination and upon return. In some cases, findings overlapped between two categories, which reflect the complexity of return migrants' experiences and their health outcomes and needs.

Results

We identified 681 articles, of which 275 were duplicates, leaving 406 studies to be screened. Forty‐two full text articles were reviewed for eligibility, of which 15 met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). Most of the included studies (10/15) were conducted in Ethiopia, with the remaining studies conducted in two countries, namely Ghana (3/15) and Nigeria (2/15). Seven of the included studies applied qualitative methods, six applied quantitative methods, and the remaining two used a mixed method. The studies examined various migrant groups, with distinct migration routes, objectives and levels of voluntariness in their return. The groups included: deportees [46], evacuated migrants from Libya [23, 47], survivors of human trafficking [48, 49, 50, 51, 52], return migrants from Middle Eastern countries [53, 54, 55, 56, 57] and return migrants from diverse locations [58, 59], together representing more than five thousand return migrants across all studies (Table 2). The duration of migration was reported in eight studies, spanning from 3 months to 6.5 years [46, 48, 49, 50, 52, 53, 57, 59]. Eight studies reported on duration of return, ranging from the time of re‐entry only [48, 49, 50, 52] to stays of less than 6 months [58], and up to 2.5 years [55, 57, 59].

While all studies collected data at the stage of reintegration, participants' experiences spanned the various stages of migration including health risks in destination countries [53], exposure to sexual violence and health hazards during travel, at destination and upon return [50, 59], drug use during migration and after return [55] or health and well‐being upon return [23, 47, 51, 58] (Table 2). Only one study explicitly reported on positive health outcomes after return [51]. Additionally, two studies reported indirect positive outcomes related to health and access to health services for return migrants [56, 57].

(Return)migration‐ Related Risk Factors for Ill Health

Six studies reported exposure to human trafficking, sexual violence and physical violence during migration, with some studies linking these experiences with health after return. In a study from Ethiopia, a high risk of human trafficking or exploitation among returned/deported migrants was associated with young age, being female, a rural resident, or feeling hopelessness at success in home country [49]. According to a study on sexual violence at each stage of human trafficking, being of young age raised the odds of experiencing sexual violence [50]. The study found that over two‐thirds of female victims of human trafficking were sexually violated during the trafficking period, with the highest prevalence at the destination (58.1%) and during the travelling period (35%) [50]. These findings showed a higher magnitude of sexual violence among female victims of human trafficking compared with the general Ethiopian female population of reproductive age, where lifetime sexual violence was reported as 10% [50]. In another study on human trafficking returnees in Ethiopia, it was reported how experiences of violence (both physical and sexual) during trafficking had a direct positive effect on symptoms of anxiety and how a history of detention had a direct positive effect on symptoms of PTSD [52]. Likewise, a study on returning labour migrants from Middle Eastern countries found that 32% of participants had experienced physical abuse during the travel or at destination and further found a significant association with experiences of physical abuse and common mental disorders (CMD) [56]. In another study from Ethiopia on returning migrants focusing on female domestic workers, health risks during migration included injuries from strong cleaning solvents, abuse and social isolation [53].

Health Outcomes Upon Return

Both quantitative and qualitative studies investigated the mental health of return migrants. Symptoms of mental distress, depression, anxiety, stress, or PTSD among return migrants were reported in five studies [52, 54, 56, 57, 58]. In two studies from Ethiopia, over half of the study participants reported symptoms of depression, with 58.3% of human trafficking returnees in one study [52] and 57.3% of migrant returnees from the Middle East and South Africa in another study [57], respectively. Two studies from Ethiopia—one quantitative and one mixed‐method—reported on the prevalence of CMD among return migrants of 27.6% [58] and 29.2% [56], respectively. The most frequent symptoms in both studies included frequent headaches, feeling unhappy, feeling nervous, difficulty sleeping and poor appetite [56, 58]. A quantitative study on psychological distress of return migrants in Ethiopia showed that returnees from South Africa have a lower risk of developing psychological distress compared to those returning from Middle Eastern countries [57]. According to a study on deportees in Ghana, particularly returnees who had been detained in camps and prisons prior to their return, suffered from physical and mental health complications after return [46]. In a study on drug use among returnees during migration and after return, 38.5% of the respondents reported using drugs after return to Nigeria due to reasons such as continued dependence on drugs, frustration, processing of pre‐ and post‐migration stress and trauma and because of no employment [55].

Few studies investigated the physical health of return migrants [48, 59]. One study on victims of human trafficking found that the magnitude of physical health symptoms was greater during the trafficking period than prior to migration, with returnees reporting symptoms such as weight loss, forgetfulness and reproductive health problems [48]. Another study examined the health hazards faced by migrants at various stages of migration—while travelling, at their destination and upon return. The highest health risks were reported during travel (39%) and at the destination (32%), with the lowest after return (20.5%). Health hazards also varied by stage of migration, with acute illness, physical injury and psychological trauma being the most reported during travel and at the destination, while only acute illnesses like malaria and diarrhoea were frequently reported after return [59].

Re‐Integration and Access to Healthcare Services

Challenges with reintegrating after return were investigated in different studies from different perspectives. The challenge of having to comprehend the disappointment of unfulfilled migration ambitions was reported in the studies from Ghana and Niger [23, 47], resulting for some in social isolation, dependence on family and declining food security for themselves and their dependent family [23, 47]. In a qualitative study on Ghanaian deportees, returnees likewise faced difficulties accessing food, resulting in health complications [46]. In addition, returnees had difficulties accessing jobs and paying their medical bills, which challenged their health and well‐being [46].

The studies from Ghana addressed the effect of community reception of the return migrant. One study found that many deported migrants were likely to be perceived negatively as failures and a disappointment by their families [46]. Deported or involuntarily returned migrants experienced social isolation, stigma and being referred to as stupid or lazy for returning empty‐handed from their migration [23, 46, 47]. In addition, involuntary return may affect dependent family members and deteriorate their own and their families' social and economic positions, as migrants often support multiple family members [23, 47]. Furthermore, experiences of stigma and discrimination during reintegration were found to be positively associated with symptoms of anxiety, depression and stress among return migrants [57]. However, most participants in a study of survivors of human trafficking in Nigeria reported having good relations with their families and strong ties to the community [51].

In a study on Ethiopian return labour migrants from Middle Eastern countries, an association was established between experiences during migration and health after return [56]. Denied access to health care during their stay in the host country as well as experiences of detention and physical violence impacted their health during reintegration as these experiences were significantly associated with symptoms of CMD upon return [56]. Despite a high prevalence of CMD among the participating returnees and some availability of mental health services, only a small proportion were accessing mental health services [56].

In a study from Nigeria examining survivors of human trafficking's physical and mental wellbeing after access to reintegration assistance, respondents reported good health and sense of wellbeing immediately after completing the program, but later their economic insecurity left them with minimal ability to access health care services and maintain their health [51].

Discussion

This scoping review identified only 15 studies from three SSA countries, with the majority originating from Ethiopia. Our study highlights a major research gap regarding return migration and health in SSA countries. Research on access to health care services for return migrants is scarce, with only 3 studies addressing this topic, none of which are interventional [46, 51, 56]. In addition, many of the studies had a cross‐sectional study design, highlighting the need for more longitudinal studies to explore the development over an extended period. Consequently, health during reintegration was only addressed in few studies, calling for more extensive studies on health experiences in the post‐return phase. Notably, only one study explicitly reported positive health outcomes after return.

Our review suggests that return migrants' health and wellbeing are affected by experiences of violence during their migration journey, especially physical and sexual violence, which are closely linked to symptoms of CMD and physical ill health. In addition, some studies indicate that return migrants experience stigma, mental health and physical health symptoms, as well as challenges to accessing health services both during the migration journey and upon return.

Studies on the health of migrants during transit in North Africa and the Mediterranean support the findings of our review that migrants experience numerous risks during the journey such as violence, rape and dehydration, all of which significantly impact their health [16, 17, 18]. Further, studies from other regions indicate that return migrants may experience deteriorated physical health compared to non‐migrants [40, 60, 61] suggesting a need to investigate whether the same is evident in SSA.

After return, migrants often encounter significant challenges, including the emotional toll of unfulfilled migration ambitions, unemployment, food insecurity, stigma and difficulty accessing healthcare and supportive structures, which complicate their reintegration. Furthermore, our study found that the health of return migrants may be negatively affected by poor community reception and stigma after return, as it can lead to social isolation, anxiety, stress and depression. The circumstances of return may also impact the health of migrants upon return. Studies comparing migrants from Mexico who return voluntarily versus those who are forcibly returned show that migrants who experience forced return generally have poorer health than those who return voluntarily [34, 62, 63]. Similar studies from SSA are needed to determine whether the same is evident in SSA and if targeted health initiatives are necessary for different groups of return migrants.

Return migrants represent a diverse group including victims of human trafficking, labour migrants and involuntary returnees. While aspects of this diversity are reflected in the included studies, the evidence remains uneven and may disproportionately focus on more vulnerable sub‐groups like irregular migrants. Thus, further research is needed to fully understand the health needs of different groups within the return migrant population, such as higher‐skilled return migrants. Current knowledge on the diversity of these groups from SSA countries is still limited and patchy; however, the available evidence underscores the importance of addressing the challenges these migrants face to improve their chances for successful and sustainable reintegration. In this context, the role of the community emerges as an essential factor. A report on return migrants from OECD countries found that family ties are the primary driver for return, while local communities play a significant role in shaping both the decision to return and the reintegration experience [4]. However, knowledge on how communities can support and impact return migrants in SSA is essential to impactfully address health and the reintegration of the large number of return migrants in the region. Existing support systems for re‐integration in SSA are primarily facilitated by the IOM offering assisted voluntary return and reintegration programs. Although important for those it affects, the beneficiaries of IOM support represent only a fraction of return migrants [4]. The diversity of groups and adverse routes of both migration and return complicates the issue but makes it no less relevant to address return migration both in research and practice to enable us to understand and tackle return migrants' health and reintegration. Policies are needed to foster successful reintegration of return migrants that not only address economic self‐sufficiency, social stability and psychosocial well‐being, as defined by IOM [28], but incorporate both mental and physical health as critical components. While reintegration is increasingly recognized as a policy priority [11], policies remain limited, as do centralized data collection and management systems essential for evidence‐based policymaking. This highlights the urgent need for more attention on return migrants' reintegration.

This scoping review provides a comprehensive overview of the available research on the health of return migrants in SSA countries, identifying health risks for return migrants and identifying gaps in research, but it has its limitations. First, due to the scope and methodology of the review, no critical appraisal or bias assessment is available. Second, although we conducted a broad search, some articles might have been missed if they were published in another language than English, used other keywords, or were published in a journal not indexed in the included databases. Third, our review may disproportionally reflect experiences of victims of human trafficking and involuntary returns. As a result, the findings may not fully capture the broader range of return migrants' experiences in SSA and thus limit generalizability.

Conclusions

Our findings show that return migrants experience health risks at various stages of migration such as exposure to human trafficking, physical and sexual violence and mental health issues such as anxiety and depression during the migration journey and at the destination. Returnees also face significant difficulties upon return, including unfulfilled migration aspirations, unemployment, food insecurity, social isolation, stigma and limited access to healthcare, which impact their health status and needs upon return. These challenges vary across different groups, but more research is required to inform policy makers and to identify needs and develop targeted interventions to address access to care and the health of return migrants in SSA.
