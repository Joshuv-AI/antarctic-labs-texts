Influencing factors of oil-bearing capacity in tight sandstones based on pore structure characterization: a case study of the Fuyu reservoir in the Xinmiao oilfield, Southern Songliao Basin
Authors: Hailong Wang, Zhongcheng Li, Sijie He, Feifei Fang, Hongxue Wang, Hairui Wu, Qi Liu, Qing Guo, Tingting Wang, Yuanhao Li, You Yu, Chenxu Bian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe tight sandstone reservoir exhibits a complex pore structure and strong heterogeneity. Accurate characterization of its pore structure is crucial for improving the efficiency of tight oil development. This study investigates tight sandstone samples exhibiting different oil-bearing levels from the Fuyu oil reservoir of the fourth member of the Quantou Formation in the Xinmiao area, located in the southern Songliao Basin. The influence of pore structure on oil-bearing capacity was investigated through a series of systematic experiments, including low-pressure nitrogen adsorption (LPN2A), high-pressure mercury intrusion (HPMI), constant-rate mercury intrusion (CRMI), nano-computed tomography (nano-CT), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR). The results show that: (1) The microscopic pore structure influences the macroscopic oil-bearing capacity. As the oil-bearing level of the sample increases, the corresponding pore structure gradually improves, and the oil saturation increases to 32.93%, 40.36%, 42.61%, and 50.80%, respectively. (2) The oil saturation of fluorescent samples was primarily contributed by small pores (T2 < 10 ms), with a contribution rate of 78.17%. The oil saturation of oil trace and oil flecked samples was mainly contributed by small pores and medium pores (10 < T2 < 100 ms), with combined contribution rates of 93.75% and 84.35%, respectively. The oil saturation of oil immersion samples was primarily contributed by medium pores and large pores (T2 > 100 ms), with a contribution rate of 54.45%. (3) Pore-throat connectivity and its heterogeneity are the key factors influencing the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area. The pore-throat radius, connected pore throat volume percentage and NMR fractal dimension are the best parameters to characterize oil-bearing capacity. (4) The lower limit for oil-bearing capacity in the tight sandstone reservoir of the study area is oil trace, and the corresponding threshold values of a pore–throat radius of 1.19 μm, a connected pore–throat volume percentage of 47.5%, and an NMR fractal dimension of 2.8169. This research establishes a quantitative linkage between pore-throat structure and oil-bearing capacity, providing diagnostic parameters for tight oil evaluation.Keywords: Tight sandstone, Oil-bearing capacity, Pore structure characterization, Fuyu oil reservoir, Xinmiao oilfield, Southern songliao basinSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

With the gradual depletion of conventional oil and gas resources worldwide, the exploration and development of unconventional oil and gas resources have become an important direction in the energy field1–3. Tight sandstone oil and gas, as typical unconventional resources, are characterized by enormous reserves and wide distribution. However, their complex pore structures and strong heterogeneity result in poor reservoir fluid mobility and low recovery efficiency4–6. In China, many large tight sandstone oil and gas fields have been discovered in the Ordos, Songliao, and Junggar Basins. Among these, the Fuyu reservoir in the Xinmiao Oilfield, located in the southern Songliao Basin, is a representative example of a tight sandstone reservoir, typically exhibiting porosity below 10% and permeability less than 0.1 mD7–11. Despite the significant resource potential in this area, the complex pore-throat structure of the reservoir and the unclear understanding of the remaining oil distribution have resulted in persistently low waterflooding efficiency, severely constraining economic exploitation12.

Exploration and development practices have demonstrated significant complexity and heterogeneity in the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone reservoirs, both at the reservoir scale and the core scale. The difference in oil saturation within different parts of tight sandstone reservoirs reflects the complexity of this oil-bearing capacity13. These phenomena have become an important factor restricting the exploration and development of tight oil and gas. Therefore, it is of positive significance to strengthen the research on the influencing factors of tight sandstone oil-bearing capacity and clarify the coupling relationship between microscopic pore structure and oil-bearing capacity to guide the efficient exploration and development of tight oil and gas14.

On the microscopic scale, the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone is influenced by the reservoir pore structure and its heterogeneity, while on the macroscopic scale, it is jointly controlled by sedimentary environment, diagenesis, and tectonic movements15. Constructive diagenesis (such as dissolution) can increase secondary porosity and improve reservoir performance, while destructive diagenesis (such as compaction and cementation) leads to a significant reduction in pores16. Therefore, accurately characterizing the pore structure becomes essential. Traditional single experimental methods (such as NMR and MI methods) can only reveal part of the pore size distribution characteristics and are insufficient to fully characterize the complex pore throat network17–19. Integrating multiple experimental techniques and systematically analyzing the mechanisms by which pore structure influences crude oil mobility is of great significance for optimizing development strategies and improving recovery20–25. Conventional Euclidean geometry is insufficient to accurately characterize the complex pore throat system of tight sandstone reservoirs. As an effective tool, fractal theory has been widely used to characterize the complex heterogeneity of unconventional reservoirs in recent years26–29. The fractal dimension calculated based on fractal theory is an ideal parameter to quantitatively describe the complexity of the microscopic pore structure of unconventional oil and gas reservoirs30,31. This method reflects the micro-heterogeneity of reservoir pore throat structure and provides an effective means to quantitatively characterize the size distribution, heterogeneity and complexity of pores32.

To investigate the reasons for the difference in oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone, four tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels were selected from the Fuyu oil reservoir of the fourth member of the Quantou Formation in Xinmiao Oilfield, southern Songliao Basin. The LPN2A, HPMI, CRMI, nano-CT, and NMR experiments were conducted to construct the full-scale (nano-to-micron) pore throat distribution characteristics. In addition, the fractal dimension is calculated based on the fractal theory. By systematically comparing the pore throat structure parameters and fractal dimension characteristics of four different oil-bearing samples, the internal relationship between the microscopic pore structure of tight sandstone and its heterogeneity and oil-bearing capacity in the study area was discussed. Although previous studies have investigated pore structure–fluid relationships, few have quantitatively coupled multi-technique pore data (LPN2A, HPMI, CRMI, nano-CT, and NMR) to oil-bearing classification in the Fuyu reservoir, so the research results can provide important reference for tight oil and gas exploration and development.

Regional geological setting

Songliao Basin is located in the northeast of China, which is a large continental rift-depression composite basin in the Mesozoic and Cenozoic (Fig. 1)33,34. Its tectonic evolution experienced three stages: rifting (J-K1), depression (K2), and atrophy (Cz). The Xinmiao Oilfield in the study area is located in the south of the basin (Fig. 2). Controlled by regional faults, the area is characterized by alternating weak extension and compression activity, forming a wide and gentle anticline and fault block structure. The area entered a stable depression stage in the sedimentary period of the late Cretaceous Qingshankou Formation, and developed thick lacustrine mudstone and sandstone interbeds35. Among them, the Fuyu oil reservoir is dominated by large-scale shallow-water delta-lacustrine sediment. Affected by multiple sources, underwater distributary channels, mouth bars, and sheet sand microfacies are widely developed in the delta front36. The spatial differentiation of these microfacies directly controls the macroscopic distribution of reservoir quality: the underwater distributary channel sand body is the best reservoir unit in the area because of its strong hydrodynamic force, coarse grain size of sediment and good sorting, which constitutes a high porosity and permeability zone; the mouth-bar sands, modified by wave reworking, display moderate sorting and moderate-to-good reservoir quality; whereas the sheet sands are thin, contain higher clay content, and possess low primary porosity, commonly forming low porosity and permeability barrier layer. The sand body exhibits thin-bedded and lenticular, and frequently interbedded with dark mudstone, forming a typical “sand-mud thin interbedded” structure, resulting in strong heterogeneity of the reservoir. The rock type is dominated by feldspar lithic sandstone, with a low composition and textural maturity. The final quality of tight sandstone reservoirs in the study area is the result of the joint control of sedimentary microfacies and diagenesis37. Strong mechanical compaction is the main reason for the significant reduction of primary pores (commonly decreasing to below 10%), especially in sandstones rich in plastic debris. The observation of thin slices confirms that the unstable components, such as feldspar and volcanic debris, are generally dissolved, and the secondary dissolved pores produced constitute the most important effective reservoir space at present. It is inferred that these dissolutions are related to the organic acids released during the mature hydrocarbon expulsion process of the underlying source rocks, and are preferentially developed in the distributary channel sand bodies with good permeability. In addition, carbonate (calcite, iron calcite), siliceous (quartz overgrowth) and clay minerals (such as kaolinite, illite) cementation are generally developed. Among them, patchy distributions of carbonate cement are strongly inferred to exert significant destructive and plugging effects on local pore systems. The tight oil accumulation in the fourth member of the Qinquan Formation has the characteristics of “source-reservoir adjacent and near-source accumulation”. The oil source comes from the underlying high-quality deep lacustrine source rock of the first member of Qingshankou Formation (K2qn1). Oil and gas migrate through micro-fractures and pore networks over short distances, forming continuous reservoirs in tight sandstones.

Experimental samples and methods

Experimental samples

The coring well is located in Xinmiao Oilfield, Fuxin Uplift Belt, southern Songliao Basin (Fig. 2d). The oilfield development began in 2003. After more than 20 years of waterflood development, the current recovery is 15.5%. To deepen the understanding of the remaining oil in the study area, an evaluation well (M20-27–35 well) was deployed in the middle of Xinmiao Oilfield in 2023. The target layer is the fourth member of the Lower Cretaceous Quantou Formation. The core was harvested 105 m by sealed coring. The samples were taken from the Fuyu oil reservoir. The core observation shows that there are apparent differences in the oil-bearing capacity of the target layer in the longitudinal direction, which are divided into four display levels, namely fluorescence, oil trace, oil flecked and oil immersion, corresponding to oil-bearing areas of 0%, 0–5%, 5–40%, and greater than 40% of the total rock area, respectively (Fig. 3). In this study, four tight sandstone samples representing different oil-bearing levels were selected for research, and corresponding test experiments were carried out according to the research needs.

Experimental method

The technical route of this study is shown in Fig. 4, and the specific experimental methods are described below.

Technical route.

LPN2A experiment

The instrument used in the LPN2A experiment is the ASAP2460 four-station automatic rapid specific surface area and pore analyzer. The measurement range of pore size is 0.35 ~ 100 nm, and the minimum relative pressure is 4 × 10− 5. The shale sample was made into a 40-mesh powder and dried for 6 h. 1 ~ 2 g powder was vacuum degassed at 100 °C for 8 h and placed in liquid nitrogen. The pore morphology can be determined according to the type of adsorption-desorption curve, and the distribution of pore volume and specific surface area with pore size is obtained by the BJH model and the BET model, respectively. The relevant content can be found in detail in the reference38,39.

HPMI and CRMI

The HPMI experimental instrument is an AutoPore IV 9520 automatic mercury intrusion apparatus. The pore size range is 3 nm ~ 1000 μm, the pressure range is 0.2 ~ 60,000 psia, and the mercury volume accuracy is less than 0.1 µL. The sample was made into a core column with a diameter of 2.5 cm, dried and dehydrated for 24 h, and then vacuum treated before being placed into a mercury intrusion apparatus. The pressure was applied from small to large within a certain range, and the cumulative distribution curves of pore size corresponding to different pressures were obtained. The relevant content can be found in detail in the reference40.

The CRMI experimental instrument adopts the ASPE-730 constant-rate mercury intrusion experimental device. The injection rate can be as low as 0.00005 mL/min and as high as 1 mL/min. The core takes a cylinder with a cross-sectional area of 1 cm2, the pressure range is 0 ~ 1000 psi, and the spatial resolution of the rock pore structure is 0.00001 mL. When mercury is injected into rock pores as a non-wetting phase, external pressure is required. The contact angle, interfacial tension and pore throat radius determine the mercury injection pressure. The constant-rate mercury intrusion technique involves injecting mercury into rock pores at a very low quasi-static mercury injection rate (usually 5 × 10− 5 mL/min). During the analysis, the contact angle and interfacial tension remain constant. The experiment is completed when the displacement pressure reaches 6.2 MPa. Pores and throats in the rock are distinguished according to the rise and fall of the mercury injection pressure, and the capillary pressure curves of pores and throats are obtained. Subsequently, the distribution of pore and throat radius and pore throat radius ratio is calculated. The relevant content can be found in detail in the reference17,41.

NMR

The NMR experiment was performed using a MicroMR20-025 V instrument developed by Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Corporation, with the main frequency of 23 MHz, the core diameter of 25 mm, the core length of 2 ~ 4 cm, the echo interval of 0.2 ms, the waiting time of 6 s, the echo number of 8000, and the scan times of 128. The experiment procedure includes the following steps: oil washing, drying, weighing, and physical property testing of the core samples, among other procedures. The washing oil solvent is a combination of xylene, acetone, and chloroform, with a volume ratio of 70:15:15. The samples were saturated with formation water after oil washing and drying, and the NMR T₂ spectra were subsequently measured under water-saturated conditions. The crude oil displacement experiment was conducted on core samples saturated with formation water, with a displacement volume of 10 pore volumes (PV). After displacement, the NMR T₂ spectrum of the sample in the oil-saturated, irreducible water state was acquired. The relevant content can be found in detail in the reference42.

Nano-CT scanning

This experiment was conducted using the Nano-Voxel 3000 CT digital core analysis system, which operates within a working voltage range of 40 ~ 180 kV and a power range of 1 ~ 15 W. A 2 mm diameter core column was drilled in the typical area of the core sample for nano-CT scanning and three-dimensional model reconstruction. The system offers a scanning resolution of up to 0.2 μm. The Phoenix Datosx 2 Acq X software was used to reconstruct the three-dimensional structure model of a series of CT scan images. Using professional data processing software VOLUME GRAPHICS STUDIO MAX and AVIZO 9.0 to analyze and process the reconstructed three-dimensional digital model, display the three-dimensional internal view, and extract pore geometric parameters, such as pore and throat diameter, pore and throat number, pore volume and specific surface area, connected pore volume percentage, and coordination number. The relevant content can be found in detail in the reference43.

Results analysis

Physical properties

There is no obvious correlation between oil-bearing capacity and porosity of tight sandstone samples in the Fuyu oil reservoir in the study area (Fig. 5a). For example, the oil-bearing levels of samples M2, M3, and M4 are oil trace, oil flecked, and oil immersion, respectively. However, their porosity is very similar, at approximately 15% (15.06% to 15.19%). Sample M1 has the lowest oil-bearing level (fluorescence), and its porosity is only 8.85%, which is significantly lower than that of the other three samples, indicating that high porosity may not necessarily correspond to good oil-bearing capacity. However, to achieve oil trace level and above, it may be necessary to have high porosity.

Porosity and permeability curves of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

The significant positive correlation between oil-bearing capacity and permeability of tight sandstone samples in the Fuyu oil reservoir in the study area (Fig. 5b). As oil-bearing capacity increases, permeability increases significantly, indicating that pore throat connectivity is the key factor controlling the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area.

Pore structure characterization

LPN2A experiment

The low-pressure nitrogen isothermal adsorption-desorption curve and the corresponding pore size distribution curve of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels in the study area are shown in Fig. 6. According to the IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) classification, the low-pressure nitrogen adsorption curves of all samples belong to type IV (Figs. 6a ~ 6 d)33,44. When the relative pressure P/P0 is low, a certain amount of adsorption occurs, corresponding to the adsorption of a single molecular layer and multiple molecular layers. With the increase of P/P0, there is an obvious hysteresis loop between the adsorption and desorption curves, which corresponds to the capillary condensation of mesopores. When P/P0 increases to close to 1, the adsorption curve still does not reach the straight section, which corresponds to the filling of macropores, indicating that the pores of tight sandstone are composed of micropores (< 2 nm), mesopores (2 ~ 50 nm), and macropores (> 50 nm). The pore connectivity has a significant control effect on the shape of the adsorption curve. As the permeability and oil-bearing capacity gradually become better from sample M1 to M4, the initial adsorption capacity and the maximum adsorption capacity are significantly reduced, and the hysteresis loop amplitude is also significantly weakened. All samples showed H3-type hysteresis loops, indicating that the pore structure is dominated by ink bottle-shaped pores with large pores and fine throats. This structural feature is mainly due to strong compaction and cementation, resulting in the contact relationship between residual intergranular pores gradually changing from point contact to line contact, which in turn affects pore connectivity and fluid occurrence behavior.

Low-pressure nitrogen adsorption-desorption curves (a ~ d) and pore size distribution curves (e ~ h) of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

As the oil-bearing capacity of the tight sandstone samples in the study area improves, from sample M1 to sample M4, the peak value of the low-pressure nitrogen adsorption pore size distribution curve gradually shifts to the right, indicating that the reservoir quality improves (Figs. 6e ~ 6f). The pore size of sample M1 is mainly distributed in the range of 5 ~ 90 nm, and the peak value is 30 ~ 40 nm. The pore size of sample M2 is mainly distributed in the range of 10 ~ 100 nm, and the peak value is 30 ~ 50 nm. The pore size of sample M3 is mainly distributed in the range of 20 ~ 100 nm, and the peak value is 40 ~ 50 nm. The pore size of sample M4 is mainly distributed in the range of 30 ~ 130 nm, and the peak value is 50 ~ 60 nm.

HPMI experiment

The capillary pressure curves and pore size distribution curves of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels show significant differences (Fig. 7).

High-pressure mercury intrusion capillary pressure curves (a ~ d), pore-throat radius distribution curves (e ~ h), and contributions of pore-throat radius to permeability (i ~ l) for tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

The sample M1 (fluorescence) exhibits the worst pore throat connectivity (Fig. 7a). The threshold pressure is 1.73 MPa, with a maximum pore throat radius of 0.43 μm and an average pore throat radius of 0.05 μm. The separation coefficient is 0.05. When the pressure is less than 1 MPa, the mercury injection is not obvious (< 2%), and the maximum mercury injection saturation is 80.37%. The pore throat radius distribution curve of sample M1 shows a bimodal structure (Fig. 7e), both of which are nanoscale (peak values are 10 nm and 100 nm, respectively). The permeability is mainly contributed by the pore throat with a pore size less than 0.025 μm (Fig. 7i).

The pore-throat connectivity of sample M2 (oil trace) is general (Fig. 7b). The threshold pressure is 0.98 MPa, with a maximum pore throat radius of 0.76 μm and an average pore throat radius of 0.14 μm. The separation coefficient is 0.09. When the pressure is less than 1 MPa, the mercury injection is not obvious (< 5%), and the maximum mercury injection saturation is 95.03%. The pore throat radius distribution curve of sample M2 shows a single peak structure (Fig. 7f), which is nanoscale (peak 250 nm), and the micron pore throat system is not developed. The permeability is mainly contributed by the pore throat with a pore size less than 0.1 μm. (Fig. 7g).

The sample M3 (oil flecked) exhibits good pore throat connectivity (Fig. 7c). The threshold pressure is 0.42 MPa, with a maximum pore throat radius of 1.79 μm and an average pore throat radius of 0.28 μm. The separation coefficient is 0.30. When the pressure is less than 1 MPa, there is an obvious increase in mercury saturation (> 20%), and the maximum mercury saturation reaches 91.90%. The M3 pore throat radius distribution curve of the sample also shows a bimodal structure (Fig. 7g). The left peak is nano-scale, accounting for a small proportion, and the right peak is nano-micron (peak at about 1 μm), accounting for a large proportion. The permeability is mainly contributed by the pore throat with a pore size less than 0.25 μm. (Fig. 7k).

The sample M4 (oil immersion) exhibits the best pore throat connectivity (Fig. 7d). It has a minimum threshold pressure of 0.16 MPa, with a maximum pore throat radius of 4.67 μm and an average pore throat radius of 1.07 μm. The separation coefficient is 1.03. In the range of pressure less than 1 MPa, there is an obvious increase in mercury saturation (> 40%), and the maximum mercury saturation reaches 85.89%. The pore throat radius distribution curve of sample M4 shows a bimodal structure (Fig. 7h). The left peak is nano-scale, accounting for a small proportion, and the right peak is micron-scale (peak exceeds 2 μm), accounting for a large proportion. The permeability is mainly contributed by the pore throat with a pore size less than 1 μm. (Fig. 7l).

CRMI experiment

Compared to the HPMI experiment, the parameters related to the throat in the CRMI experiment results can be more intuitively reflected. The CRMI records three curves: throat mercury injection, pore mercury injection, and total mercury injection (Fig. 8). According to the characteristics of mercury injection, the samples were divided into two categories. Sample M1 represents a distinct category, characterized by a threshold pressure of 0.55 MPa and a throat-dominated structure. The shape of its total mercury intrusion curve closely matches that of the throat intrusion curve (Fig. 8a), indicating that the sample mainly develops throats, with a relatively low proportion of pore volume. Samples M2, M3, and M4 belong to the same category, characterized by a combined pore and throat structure. Specifically, the initial portion of the total mercury intrusion curve is governed by pore filling, while at higher pressures, the curve becomes increasingly dominated by throat intrusion (Figs. 8b ~ d). This category of samples exhibits relatively low threshold pressures, measured at 0.12 MPa, 0.21 MPa, and 0.37 MPa for samples M2, M3, and M4, respectively. As the pressure increases, mercury intrusion into the throats continues to rise, while the pore volume increment occurs within a narrow pressure range beginning at the threshold pressure. This behavior indicates that a small number of large throats predominantly control the pore body reflected by CRMI.

CRMI curves of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

Figure 9 shows the distribution characteristics of pores, throats, and pore-throat ratio obtained by CRMI, where the “throat” only refers to the part connecting the pore body. The pore distribution of samples with different oil-bearing levels is relatively uniform (Fig. 9a). The pore radius is mostly 100 ~ 300 μm, and the main peak is 120 ~ 180 μm. However, there are obvious differences in the distribution characteristics of throat radius and pore-throat ratio between different samples (Fig. 9b and c). As the oil-bearing levels of the sample increase, the corresponding throat radius gradually increases, and the pore-throat ratio gradually increases. The huge difference between pore and throat radius leads to the abnormally large pore-throat ratio of tight sandstone, which is distributed in the range of 30 ~ 450. The large pore throat ratio is the most significant difference between tight sandstone and conventional sandstone reservoirs, which is the main reason for the poor oil and gas abundance and low output of tight sandstone17.

Distribution curves of pore radius, throat radius and pore throat ratio of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

NMR experiment

According to the characteristics of the NMR T2 spectrum curve, the samples were divided into two categories. The sample M1 is a separate category (Fig. 10a), and the NMR T2 spectrum exhibits a single peak shape, indicating that small pores dominate the sample and has poor pore connectivity. Samples M2, M3, and M4 belong to the same category, and the NMR T2 spectrum shows a bimodal shape (Figs. 10b ~ 10 d), revealing that small pores, medium pores, and large pores are relatively well developed, with good pore connectivity. The right peak amplitude of the NMR T2 spectrum of the sample M3 and M4, with the highest oil-bearing levels in these samples, is higher than the left peak, while the right peak amplitude of the sample M2 is slightly lower than the left peak, indicating that larger pores dominate the samples M3 and M4, while the sample M2 has a higher proportion of medium and small pores.

NMR T2 spectrum curves of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

Fractal dimension calculation based on NMR

Since the 1980 s, fractal theory has been applied to the quantitative study of pore structure of the sandstone reservoirs27. Based on different experimental methods and theoretical models, scholars have proposed many methods for calculating the fractal dimension of unconventional oil and gas reservoirs. Among them, HPMI and LPN2A experiments are the most common methods28,45–47. In recent years, NMR experiment has attracted increasing attention because they can characterize pores as low as nanometers and as high as microns, and are more comprehensive than HPMI and LPN2A experiments48,49.

The fractal dimension model calculated based on NMR data is generally established under the assumption that the pore system is spherical. In sandstone reservoirs, the relaxation rate of hydrogen-containing fluids in the pore–throat system (the length of the relaxation time) is primarily determined by the strength of the interaction between fluid molecules and the solid surface of the pore-throat walls. Nano-CT scanning experiment

X-ray tomography (X-CT) reconstructs the three-dimensional structure of the microscopic pore throat of tight sandstone through cone X-ray penetration and 360-degree rotation imaging. The generated three-dimensional digital core can qualitatively and quantitatively analyze the pore throat size, connectivity, and morphology. Compared with mercury injection, electron microscopy, and other methods, CT scanning offers several advantages, including being all-round, large-scale, fast, and non-destructive, allowing for more intuitive study of the microscopic pore characteristics of reservoirs51,52.

The two-dimensional slice images of core samples obtained based on nano-CT scanning are shown in Figs. 12a ~ d (grayscale images). The dark black area represents pores, and the gray and white areas represent rock skeletons, where white indicates higher-density minerals. The three-dimensional reconstruction of the sample was performed using Avizo Fire 9 software (Figs. 12e ~ h), and the threshold segmentation was based on previous methods. On this basis, the ball-stick network model of the sample was established (Figs. 12i ~ l). In this model, a single pore in the unconventional reservoir pore-throat three-dimensional network space is regarded as a node, and the number of throats connected to its edge is defined as the coordination number of the node. The ball-stick model can effectively simplify the complex heterogeneity, internal tortuosity and irregular pore-throat connection of the sample, abstracting the pores as small balls and the connected throats as long sticks. As shown in Fig. 12, the tight sandstone samples in the study area at the micro-nano scale have the characteristics of different pore throat sizes and irregular pore shapes. The distribution state of the pore throat mainly includes contiguous pores and isolated pores. The connectivity of contiguous pores is better than that of isolated pores. The latter is mostly “dead pores”, which are mostly isolated in three-dimensional space and do not have connectivity. The total pores and connected pores of the three-dimensional model of the high oil-bearing level samples are relatively high, indicating that the connectivity of the pore throat network controls the oil-bearing capacity.

Discussion

Oil-bearing enrichment pore size range

The T2 value in the NMR T2 spectrum has a one-to-one correspondence with the pore throat radius53. According to the size of the T2 value, the pores of tight sandstone samples in the study area are divided into three categories: large pores (T2 > 100 ms), medium pores (10 ms < T2 < 100 ms), and small pores (T2 < 10 ms). By comparing the T2 spectra of saturated water and saturated oil, the oil saturation of the sample can be calculated, and the oil saturation in different sizes of pores can be further obtained. With the increase in oil-bearing levels, the oil saturation of samples M1, M2, M3, and M4 increased in turn, reaching 32.93%, 40.36%, 42.61%, and 50.80% (Fig. 13a), respectively. For different oil-bearing samples, the contribution of different types of pores to oil saturation is significantly different. As the oil-bearing level of the sample increases, the contribution of small pores to the total oil saturation gradually decreases. The contribution of medium pores initially increases, then decreases, and the contribution of large pores gradually increases (Figs. 13b ~ d). This indicates that the content of both large pores and small pores is a crucial factor in determining the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area. The NMR T2 spectrum of this type of sample (M2, M3, and M4) shows a bimodal structure. The high content of large pores means that the pore throat connectivity is good, which is conducive to the enrichment and migration of crude oil.

Oil saturation (a), percentage of oil saturation in small, medium and large pores (b ~ d) of tight sandstone samples with different oil-bearing levels.

Influencing factors of oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone

Influence of reservoir pore specific surface area on oil-bearing capacity

As shown in Fig. 14, the pore-specific surface area significantly affects the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area. The pore specific surface area (average 0.1924 m2/g) of high oil-bearing samples (M3 and M4) was significantly lower than that of low oil-bearing samples (M1 and M2, average 0.9349 m2/g), and the former was only about 1/5 (20.58%) of the latter. This suggests that a lower pore-specific surface area typically indicates better pore connectivity, which in turn corresponds to higher oil-bearing capacity. The main mechanism is that the pore-specific surface area is mainly contributed by small-pore-size nanopores, which provide adsorption retention points for the bound fluid (immobile fluid) and seriously restrict the effectiveness of the connected pore network8. As the pore-specific surface area increases, the pore surface roughness and heterogeneity also increase, leading to a higher saturation of the bound fluid on the pore surface. This elevated bound fluid content hinders crude oil filling and migration, thereby reducing overall oil-bearing capacity.

Experimental parameters of low-pressure nitrogen adsorption.

Influence of pore throat connectivity

As shown in Fig. 15, the effect of pore structure parameters obtained based on high-pressure mercury intrusion experiments on oil-bearing capacity is shown. As the oil-bearing level of the sample increases, the threshold pressure decreases accordingly (Fig. 15a), and the two are strongly correlated, confirming that the microscopic pore-throat connectivity is the key factor controlling the oil-bearing capacity of the tight sandstone in the study area. The maximum mercury saturation of high oil-bearing samples (M2, M3 and M4, with an average of 90.94%) is significantly higher than that of low oil-bearing samples (M1, SHg=80.37%), indicating that the maximum mercury saturation can characterize oil-bearing capacity as a whole (Fig. 15b). However, the maximum mercury saturation of samples M2, M3, and M4 is 85.89%, 91.90% and 95.03%, respectively, which is inconsistent with the order of oil-bearing level. This is mainly because, on the one hand, the maximum mercury saturation can fully characterize pore connectivity, including nano-scale pores and micron-scale macropores. The previous conclusions have confirmed that nano-scale pores have a limited effect on oil-bearing capacity, which leads to the maximum mercury volume not accurately reflecting the oil-bearing capacity of the sample. On the other hand, the high-pressure mercury injection experiment cannot distinguish between pores and throats. The maximum mercury saturation is a parameter that reflects the overall connectivity of pores and throats. It has been confirmed that the throat radius is more effective than pores to characterize the connectivity of tight sandstone reservoirs54. The median radius and the maximum pore throat radius have a significant control effect on the oil-bearing capacity of the tight sandstone in the study area (Fig. 15c and d). The median radius refers to the pore throat radius corresponding to 50% mercury injection, and the maximum pore throat radius refers to the radius corresponding to the threshold pressure. The correlation between the maximum pore throat radius and the oil-bearing capacity is significantly stronger than that with the median radius, indicating that the larger pores primarily influence the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area, and the control effect of small pores on the oil-bearing capacity is less pronounced.

Connectivity parameters of high-pressure mercury intrusion experiment.

The study of Gong et al.55 showed that the average values of maximum pore-throat radius and median radius of oil-bearing samples in the Qian’an area of southern Songliao Basin were 6.12 μm and 0.59 μm, respectively, which were comparable to those of oil-immersed samples in the study area (4.67 μm and 0.43 μm, respectively). The average values of the maximum pore-throat radius and the median radius of the non-oil samples are 0.40 μm and 0.05 μm, respectively, which belong to the same level as the fluorescent samples (0.47 μm and 0.02 μm, respectively) in the study area.

As shown in Fig. 16, the relationship between pore structure parameters and oil-bearing capacity obtained based on CRMI experiments is shown. With the increase in the oil-bearing level of the sample, the pore radius increases as a whole, but it is not very obvious. The throat radius increases in turn, and the positive correlation with the oil-bearing capacity of the sample is obvious, while the pore throat ratio decreases as a whole. Previous studies have confirmed that throat radius and pore-throat ratio are the key factors affecting the seepage capacity of tight sandstone reservoirs, rather than pore radius54. The pore-throat network of tight sandstone reservoirs is usually characterized by ink-bottle-shaped pores with large pores and fine throats. As a channel to connect the pore network, although its volume accounts for a small percentage of the total volume (usually less than 50%), its contribution to permeability is very large (usually greater than 80%)56. Therefore, when evaluating tight sandstone oil and gas resources, the size of the reservoir throat radius should be considered an important factor.

Connectivity parameters of constant-rate mercury injection experiment.

As shown in Fig. 17, the relationship between pore structure parameters and oil-bearing capacity obtained based on CT experiments is shown. The volume percentage of connected volume refers to the percentage of the calculated connected pore throat volume in the total volume of the sample, and the coordination number refers to the number of throats between adjacent pores directly connected to a single pore, which is an important parameter to judge the connectivity of the pore throat network57. As the oil-bearing level of the sample increases, the volume percentage of the connected volume and the coordination number also gradually increase, indicating that these two parameters can effectively reflect the microscopic pore-throat connectivity of the tight sandstone samples in the study area. Furthermore, the connectivity controls the oil-bearing capacity of the samples.

Influence of reservoir heterogeneity

The microscopic heterogeneity of pore structure refers to the degree of pore throat distribution. Scholars usually use fractal dimension and sorting coefficient to describe the microscopic heterogeneity of tight sandstone reservoirs, and have achieved good application results6,49,58. The microscopic heterogeneity parameters (DNMR and Sorting coefficient) of different oil-bearing tight sandstone samples are shown in Fig. 18. From sample M1 to sample M4, the fractal dimension decreases with the increase in oil-bearing capacity. Previous studies have shown that the larger the fractal dimension and sorting coefficient, the more complex the pore structure and the stronger the heterogeneity6,58. Liu et al.59 selected the Paleogene low-permeability sandstone reservoir of the G oilfield in the Bohai Bay Basin as the research object. Systematically analyzed the influence of pore connectivity and heterogeneity on fluid occurrence characteristics by means of casting thin sections, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), HPMI and NMR. Based on NMR experiment and fractal theory, the fractal dimensions of pores with different scales were studied and calculated. The results show that the bound fluid mainly occurs in the small pore-throat system characterized by the small pore-throat radius, poor connectivity, and relatively large fractal dimension. The medium and large pore systems have good microscopic connectivity and lower fractal dimensions, and are dominated by movable fluids. The complex pore structure will greatly limit the enrichment and migration of crude oil in tight sandstone reservoirs. In contrast, a smaller DNMR corresponds to a relatively simple pore network and a smoother pore-throat surface. This structural feature effectively reduces the flow resistance of crude oil, thereby enhancing its storage capacity in the reservoir and ultimately leading to higher oil saturation. Therefore, the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area is negatively correlated with the two parameters of DNMR and sorting coefficient.

Conclusion

(1) Microscopic pore structure is a key factor affecting the macroscopic oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone samples in the study area. As the oil-bearing level of the sample increases from fluorescence to oil immersion, the corresponding pore structure gradually improves, and the oil saturation also increases accordingly, which are 32.93%, 40.36%, 42.61% and 50.80%, respectively.

(2) In different oil-bearing samples, the contribution of dominant pore throat size to oil saturation is significantly different. The oil saturation of the fluorescent sample is mainly contributed by small pores (T2 < 10 ms), and the contribution rate is as high as 78.17%. The oil saturation of oil trace and oil flecked samples was mainly contributed by small pores (T2 < 10 ms) and medium pores (10 ms < T2 < 100 ms), and the total contribution rates were 93.75% and 84.35%, respectively. The oil saturation of oil-immersed samples is mainly contributed by medium pores (10 ms < T2 < 100 ms) and large pores (T2 > 100 ms), with a contribution rate of 54.45%.

(3) Pore-throat connectivity and its heterogeneity are the key factors influencing the oil-bearing capacity of tight sandstone in the study area. The pore-throat radius, connected pore throat volume percentage and NMR fractal dimension are the best parameters to characterize oil-bearing capacity. The lower limit for oil-bearing capacity in the tight sandstone reservoir of the study area is oil trace, and the corresponding threshold values of a pore–throat radius of 1.19 μm, a connected pore–throat volume percentage of 47.5%, and an NMR fractal dimension of 2.8169.

Future work should couple micro-CT-derived pore networks with dynamic flow simulations to predict effective oil saturation.
