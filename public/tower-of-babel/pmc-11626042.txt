Dissecting cellulitis mimicking kerion in a 9-year-old boy
Authors: Amanda Loesch, Katalin Ferenczi, Mary Wu Chang
Published: 2024

Introduction

Dissecting cellulitis of the scalp (DCS) is an uncommon disease characterized by painful boggy and suppurative nodules and sinus tracts of the scalp. DCS is a progressive disorder that can cause extensive scarring alopecia. In DCS, an intense inflammatory response is believed to be caused by occlusion of follicles by keratin, and sometimes potentiated by overlying bacterial infection. DCS is part of the follicular occlusion tetrad, along with hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), acne conglobata, and pilonidal cysts. Typically, DCS is diagnosed in men aged between 20 and 40 years with skin type IV and above.1 On histology, early stages are characterized by dense lymphocytic, neutrophilic, and histiocytic infiltrate. Later stages are defined by granulomas of lymphocytes, plasma cells, and foreign-body giant cells. Fibrotic changes are typical of end-stage disease.2 Clinically, DCS can be mistaken for bacterial or fungal infections (eg, abscesses or kerion), or other inflammatory scalp dermatoses (eg, alopecic and aseptic nodules of the scalp [AANS]). We present a 9-year-old boy with DCS originally thought to have tinea capitis and kerion. To our knowledge, this is the youngest reported case of DCS.

Case Presentation

A 9-year-old healthy boy with skin type V presented to the pediatric dermatology clinic with a 1-year history of scalp nodules and alopecia. He had been treated with ketoconazole shampoo. On examination, he had scattered alopecic scaly round thin plaques, cervical lymphadenopathy, tender papules and small nodules, some with drainage (Fig 1). There was no family history of fungal infection or scalp lesions, however, his mother had HS managed with adalimumab. The patient was prescribed oral griseofulvin 20 mg/kg/d for 6 weeks and topical ketoconazole shampoo for presumptive tinea capitis and kerion. Because of discomfort, a 7-day course of oral prednisolone was also given. Fungal culture and potassium hydroxide examination of the scalp scrapings obtained before treatment, were negative. After completion of this regimen, the boggy, painful lesions resolved, leaving round plaques of scarring alopecia. Two weeks later, the scalp nodules recurred. Repeat fungal and bacterial cultures were negative. He then received subsequent treatments with oral terbinafine, prednisolone, and oral cephalexin, all with temporary reduction in the size and drainage of the nodules but the scalp never fully cleared. Eventually, the nodules recurred and were worse than initial presentation (Fig 2).

Initial presentation to pediatric dermatology clinic. Scalp with multiple circular thin plaques with alopecia and scale, as well as some boggy thin nodules, mimicking tinea capitis with kerion.

After initial improvement, swollen, boggy weeping nodules developed despite treatment with oral terbinafine, griseofulvin, antibiotics, and short courses of prednisone. Punch biopsy was performed during this visit.

A punch biopsy was performed from an inflamed scalp nodule and a trial of intralesional kenalog (ILK) was injected to the largest nodule. On histopathology, a predominantly reticular dermal, periadnexal and perivascular, mixed inflammatory infiltrate composed of lymphocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, and occasional eosinophils were seen. Periodic acid–Schiff stain was negative. Dermal fibrosis with focal scarring was present (Fig 3). These findings, coupled with the clinical picture, were consistent with DCS.

Histologic examination showed a (A) predominantly reticular dermal perifollicular and perivascular inflammatory infiltrate and dermal fibrosis. B, The infiltrate was composed of lymphocytes, plasma cells, occasional eosinophils, and (C) neutrophils. (Original magnifications: A, ×4; B and C, ×40.)

Treatment options were discussed including anti-inflammatory antibiotics (eg, doxycycline), ILK, isotretinoin, systemic corticosteroids, and adalimumab. The initial ILK treatment was successful and the parents preferred avoiding systemic agents, thus a mutual decision was made to proceed with ILK to remaining nodules. Triamcinolone triamcinolone acetonide 10 mg/mL (ranging from 0.3 mL total volume to 3 lesions, to 0.9 mL to 5 lesions) was injected into inflamed scalp nodules monthly, for 3 months. The nodules resolved leaving focal alopecic scarred plaques (Fig 4). Three months later, he experienced recurrent nodules. As the parents continued to decline systemic medications, he was again treated with ILK with good response. He is currently in remission; however, long-term follow-up is indicated as recrudescence is possible.

After serial intralesional triamcinolone injections, some plaques and nodules resolved fully with complete hair regrowth. Other nodules resolved leaving scarred plaques.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the youngest reported case of DCS. The diagnosis was challenging because of the patient’s young age and presentation consistent with kerion. Fungal culture is usually negative in inflamed kerion,3 and extended courses of oral antifungal therapy are often required for complete clearance. Conversely, there have been reports of children with tinea capitis4 or kerion5 initially diagnosed and treated as DCS, which only resolved when appropriate oral antifungal agents were administered. In contrast, DCS is a progressive inflammatory disease that typically begins no sooner than the second decade of life.1

AANS is a disorder that also presents with inflammatory scalp nodules (Table I).1,2,6, 7, 8, 9 Unlike DCS, AANS is typical in prepubertal children. In contrast to AANS, DCS is more prominent in patients with tightly coiled or Afro-textured hair. On dermatoscopy, both disorders share similar findings. Both entities demonstrate inflammatory dermal infiltrates and can be difficult to distinguish histologically.6

Doxycycline or minocycline are often given as first-line treatment for mild-to-moderate DCS; the benefits are likely secondary to anti-inflammatory effects. However, in young children, tetracyclines are relatively contraindicated because of tooth staining. Isotretinoin can be used for moderate-to-severe DCS, however, relapse is common upon discontinuation.8 Oral or intralesional corticosteroids are used as adjuvant therapy in fulminant presentations. In our patient, the improvements seen after treatment for kerion were likely because of the anti-inflammatory effects of prednisolone and antimicrobials. For refractory DCS, treatment options include biologics, epilation, and surgical excision.9 There have been many reports of DCS treated successfully with tumor necrosis factor-alfa inhibitors, such as adalimumab.

Adalimumab is a favorable treatment for both HS and DCS, suggesting a common activation of tumor necrosis factor-α inflammatory pathways in both processes.10 Interestingly, our patient’s mother has HS controlled with adalimumab, suggesting a familial predisposition to inflammatory “follicular occlusion” disorders. Although currently classified as different disease entities, HS and DCS have similarities, including age of onset, predilection for darker skin tones, and significant inflammation. Additionally, both disorders have proven similar genetic and environmental triggers.10

DCS is a chronic disease that usually requires ongoing management. Although ILK has induced temporary remissions in our patient, he will need to be followed for disease recurrence. If worsening, a systemic agent such as oral isotretinoin, adalimumab, or other biologic therapy could be considered.8 At this time, his parents are declining all systemic agents.

This case illustrates that DCS can occur in prepubertal children, and can mimic kerion, making diagnosis challenging.
