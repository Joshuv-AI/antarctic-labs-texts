Remnant Cholesterol in Young Adulthood Is Associated With Left Ventricular Remodeling and Dysfunction in Middle Age: The CARDIA Study
Authors: Xinghao Xu, Zhaoyan Wang, Rihua Huang, Yue Guo, Zhenyu Xiong, Xiaodong Zhuang, Xinxue Liao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Recent studies have shown that remnant cholesterol (RC) is associated with incident heart failure; however, its association with left ventricular (LV) structure and function is unclear. We aimed to evaluate the association between RC levels in young adulthood and LV structure and function in middle age.METHODS:We included 3321 participants from the CARDIA study (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) at baseline. RC was calculated as total cholesterol minus high-density lipoprotein cholesterol minus calculated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and the RC trajectories that followed a similar pattern of change over time were identified using the latent class growth mixture model. LV structure and function were assessed using echocardiography at CARDIA study year 25. Multivariable linear regression models were performed to assess the associations of both baseline and trajectories of RC levels with LV structure and function.RESULTS:Among 3321 participants, the mean age was 24.99±3.62 years: 1450 (43.90%) were male, and 1561 (47.00%) were Black. After multivariate adjustment, higher baseline RC (per SD in log-transformed) was associated with higher LV mass index (β=1.29; P=0.004), worse global longitudinal strain (β=0.19; P<0.001), worse global circumferential strain (β=0.16; P=0.014), lower septal e′ (β=−0.26; P<0.001), lower lateral e′ (β=−0.18; P=0.003), and higher E/e′ (β=0.15; P=0.003). Three RC trajectories were identified during follow-up: low increasing (42.4%), moderate increasing (45.5%), and high increasing (12.1%). Similarly, compared with the low-increasing group, the high-increasing RC trajectory group was related to higher LV mass index, worse global longitudinal strain, lower septal e′, lower lateral e′, and higher E/e′.CONCLUSIONS:Elevated RC levels in young adulthood were related to adverse LV structural and functional alterations in midlife. Long-term trajectories of RC levels during young adulthood help identify individuals at a higher risk for adverse LV remodeling and dysfunction.Registration:URL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique identifier: NCT00005130.

CLINICAL PERSPECTIVE

Remnant cholesterol (RC) is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Moreover, recent studies have shown that RC is associated with incident heart failure; however, its association with left ventricle structure and function is unclear. This study sought to evaluate the longitudinal associations of RC during young adulthood with ventricle structure and function in midlife. Our study demonstrated that elevated RC levels were related to adverse alterations in ventricle structure and function, even in individuals with low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels below the target level. Moreover, compared with the low-increasing RC trajectory group, the high-increasing RC trajectory group was associated with echocardiographic markers of adverse ventricle remodeling and dysfunction. These findings suggest that early and frequent screening for RC levels may assist in the early identification of high-risk individuals and emphasize the importance of maintaining low RC levels beginning in young adulthood.

Heart failure (HF) is one of the most common, costly, disabling, and life-threatening diseases.1 Subclinical structural and functional changes in the left ventricle (LV) frequently occur before symptomatic HF and are considered the pre-HF stage.2 Due to the poor prognosis and limited effective therapies for HF, early identification of risk factors leading to unfavorable LV changes and timely modification of them are crucial.

Dyslipidemia is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease, including coronary heart disease, stroke, and peripheral artery disease.3 Moreover, a recent Mendelian randomization study revealed that low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was causally related to adverse LV alterations.4 Recently, the detrimental impact of remnant cholesterol (RC) has been identified beyond LDL-C.5 RC, which is the cholesterol content of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, consisting of low-density lipoproteins, intermediate-density lipoproteins, and chylomicron remnants, has emerged as a risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.5–8 Recent studies have found that elevated RC levels are linked to a higher risk of HF.9,10 Considering the key role of adverse LV structural and functional alterations in the occurrence of HF, data on the association of RC with LV structure and function remain limited. A previous study reported that RC was related to adverse LV functional changes.11 However, the previous study only included patients with type 2 diabetes and was inherently limited by a small sample size and a reliance on RC levels assessed at a single point in time. It is still unclear how RC and its trajectories derived from several measurements over time relate to LV structure and function in young general populations.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the associations between baseline RC levels and different trajectories of RC levels during young adulthood with LV structure and function at midlife using longitudinal data from the CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) study.

METHODS

Data documentation for CARDIA is publicly available at cardia.dopm.uab.edu.

Study Design and Population

The CARDIA study (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults) is a prospective and biracial (Black and White) cohort investigation that was conducted across 4 US communities (Birmingham, AL; Chicago, IL; Minneapolis, MN; and Oakland, CA). Initially, at the baseline examinations from 1985 to 1986 (year 0), 5115 healthy young individuals (18–30 years) were registered. Eight follow-up examinations were performed after the baseline examination at years 2, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30. In that order, response rates were 91%, 86%, 81%, 79%, 74%, 72%, 72%, and 71%. Details about the CARDIA study have been reported previously.12 The institutional review boards at each of the participating sites gave their approval to the CARDIA study protocols. Each participant provided informed consent.

For our study, participants not completing an echocardiographic examination at year 25 (n=1641), missing baseline RC values (n=25), and missing other covariates (n=128) were excluded. The final sample of 3321 participants was left to explore the relationship between baseline RC level and LV structure and function. Participants who had <3 valid RC values during follow-up visits (n=249) were further excluded. This left a sample of 3072 people to study the relationship between the RC trajectory and LV structure and function (Figure S1).

Lipid Measurements

Lipid measurements were performed using fasting blood samples (at years 0, 5, 7, 10, 15, 20, and 25). Total cholesterol and triglyceride were quantified enzymatically. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol was measured following precipitation with magnesium/dextran.

Levels of LDL-C were estimated via the Martin/Hopkins equation because Friedewald-calculated LDL-C values are unreliable when triglyceride levels are >150 mg/d.13 Levels of RC were calculated by subtracting high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and calculated LDL-C from total cholesterol.5

Other Covariates

At the baseline examination, data on demographic characteristics and cardiovascular risk factors were collected using standardized protocols (eg, age, sex, race, educational level, total physical activity, smoking status, drinking status, and antihypertensive medication use).12

Body mass index was calculated by dividing height (kg) by weight in square (m2). The fast plasma glucose was determined by the hexokinase UV method. Three measurements of blood pressure were obtained after participants rested for 5 minutes. In our study, we used the average value of the last 2 blood pressure readings for analysis. Diabetes at year 0 was defined as fast plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL or taking antidiabetic medications, at year 25 was defined as fast plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥6.5%, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test ≥200 mg/dL by oral glucose tolerance test, or taking antidiabetic medications. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or taking antihypertension medications.

Echocardiography Measurements

At year 25, echocardiography measurements were conducted by well-trained sonographers following standardized protocols at each participating site.14,15 Then, the digitalized images acquired using the Artida cardiac ultrasound scanner were transmitted to the Johns Hopkins University Echocardiography Reading Center for quality control and analysis. Details about the echocardiographic examination at year 25 have been reported previously.14,15 Excellent reproducibility of echocardiographic examinations at year 25 was reported.14

For LV structure assessment, LV mass index (LVMI) and relative wall thickness were measured. LVMI was calculated as LV mass estimated from the Devereux formulation indexed to body surface area that was estimated from Du Bois formula. Relative wall thickness was estimated by dividing posterior wall thickness by LV internal dimension 2×. For LV systolic function assessment, LV ejection fraction, global longitudinal strain (GLS), and global circumferential strain (GCS) were measured. GLS and GCS were obtained from speckle-tracking echocardiography. Less negative values of GLS or GCS represent worse systolic function. For LV diastolic function assessment, early peak diastolic mitral inflow velocity (E), early peak diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′) at the septal and lateral wall, and left atrial volume index were evaluated. E/e′, a measure of LV filling pressure, was estimated by dividing E by the mean of lateral and septal e′. Left atrial volume index was derived from left atrial volume indexed to body surface area.

LV remodeling and dysfunction were defined based on current guidelines.16,17 LV remodeling included LV hypertrophy (LVMI >115 g/m2 for men and >95 g/m2 for women) and concentric remodeling (relative wall thickness >0.42). LV diastolic dysfunction included impaired relaxation (septal e′ <7 cm/s or lateral e′ <10 cm/s) and increased filling pressure (E/e′ ≥14). The definition of LV systolic dysfunction was LV ejection fraction <50%.

Statistical Analysis

Given the distribution of RC levels was markedly skewed, RC was log-transformed to approximate normality in the present study. Continuous and categorical data were described as mean±SD and frequency (percentage), respectively. Participants were grouped by baseline RC concentration tertiles. ANOVA, the Kruskal-Wallis test, and the Pearson χ2 test were used, as needed, to investigate the group differences.

Multivariable linear regression models were used to investigate the relationships between baseline RC levels and the echocardiographic indices of LV structure and function. The covariates collected at the baseline examination were adjusted in these models: age, sex, race, drinking status, smoking status, educational level, total physical activity, body mass index, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, fast plasma glucose, antihypertensive medication use, and LDL-C. The echocardiographic outcomes were transformed into binary variables for categorical analyses based on the guidelines. Multivariable Poisson regression models were conducted to explore the relationship between RC levels at year 0 and LV remodeling and dysfunction at year 25, reporting risk ratios (RRs) and 95% CIs. The same covariates were adjusted in line with the multivariable linear regression models.

Group-based trajectory modeling was developed to identify clusters of individuals who followed a similar pattern of change over time.18 We utilized group-based trajectory modeling to ascertain disparate longitudinal RC level trajectories across the whole follow-up period. Participants exhibiting similar patterns of change in the RC from baseline to the 25 years were identified and assigned to the corresponding groups. We examined models starting from 2 to 5 groups to evaluate the optimal number of trajectories based on the following criteria: (1) higher Bayesian information criterion; (2) a minimum of 5% of participants assigned to each trajectory group; and (3) mean posterior P>0.7. Ultimately, a 3-group trajectory model was determined to be the optimal fitting solution (Table S1). Multivariable linear regression models were performed to assess associations between RC trajectories and echocardiographic indices. Multivariable Poisson regression models were conducted to explore the relationship between RC trajectories and LV remodeling and dysfunction. Covariates mentioned above were adjusted in these models.

Sensitivity analyses were performed to investigate associations between RC and echocardiographic indices among participants with LDL-C levels below the target level. Based on the current guideline, statins may be indicated for young adults (20–39 years) when LDL-C levels are >160 mg/dL (4.144 mmol/L).19 Moreover, evidence from a recent study suggests statins are recommended when LDL-C levels are >130 mg/dL (3.367 mmol/L).20 Thus, 2 sensitivity analyses were performed to assess associations between RC levels at baseline and echocardiographic indices in the subset of participants with LDL-C <4.144 and <3.367 mmol/L. Sensitivity analysis was also performed only in individuals with high LDL-C. Given the noticeable impact of hypertension and diabetes on our analysis, further sensitivity analyses incorporated additional adjustments for hypertension and diabetes status at year 25. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses without adjusting for body mass index and fasting plasma glucose to confirm the robustness of the results.

The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to adjust P values for multiple comparisons by controlling false discovery rate.

R version 4.2.1 for Windows (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Stata 17.0 (Stata Corp LLC, TX) were used for all analyses. A 2-tailed P<0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Table displays the baseline characteristics of the 3321 individuals included in this study. The average age of all participants at baseline was 24.99±3.62 years, 1561 (47.00%) were Black, and 1450 (43.90%) were male. The mean level of RC at baseline was 0.41±0.16 mmol/L. Participants were divided into 3 groups based on the tertiles of log-RC levels at baseline. Individuals with higher levels of log-RC were older, more likely to be male, White, and smokers, had greater body mass index, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, the fast plasma glucose, triglyceride, total cholesterol, and LDL-C, and had a lower level of HDL-C. However, no difference was observed across log-RC tertiles in the use of antihypertensive medication, the prevalence of diabetes, drinking, education level, or total physical activity. Relative to participants who attended echocardiographic examination, those who did not complete the examination were more likely to be male, Black, smokers, and had higher level of systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and fasting plasma glucose (Table S2). Baseline RC and LV Structure and Function

Figure 1 displays the relationship between baseline RC levels and LV structural and functional indices. For LV structure, after covariates were fully adjusted, higher baseline RC levels (per SD in log-transformed) were significantly correlated with higher LVMI (β=1.29; P=0.004). For LV systolic function, despite there being no association between RC levels and LV ejection fraction, higher RC levels (per SD in log-transformed) were positively correlated with GLS (β=0.19; P<0.001) and GCS (β=0.16; P=0.014), indicating reduced contractile performance. For LV diastolic function, greater RC levels (per SD in log-transformed) were significantly correlated with lower septal e (β=−0.26; P<0.001), lateral e′ (β=−0.18; P=0.003), and higher E/e′′ (β=0.15; P=0.003) in fully adjusted models. No correlation between RC levels and left atrial volume index was observed.

Baseline RC and LV Remodeling and Dysfunction

Figure S2 shows the correlation between baseline RC levels and the presence of LV remodeling and dysfunction. In the multivariable models, there was a borderline significant association between the baseline RC level and LV hypertrophy (RR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.03–1.26]; P=0.059) but not related to concentric remodeling, LV systolic dysfunction, or LV diastolic dysfunction.

RC Trajectory and LV Structure and Function

Among the 3072 participants included in the trajectory analysis, 3 discrete RC trajectory groups were generated (Figure 2), namely, low increasing, moderate increasing, and high increasing (distribution of participants among RC trajectories [n=1303; 42.4%, n=1397; 45.5%, and n=372; 12.1%]). The median (interquartile range) changes in RC levels between baseline examination and final examination were 0.08 (0.01–0.15) for low-increasing RC, 0.14 (0.04–0.27) for moderate-increasing RC, and 0.21 (−0.01 to 0.45) for high-increasing RC (Table S4). Compared with the low-increasing group, the high-increasing RC trajectory group was related to higher LVMI (β=3.93; P=0.012), higher relative wall thickness (β=0.014; P=0.012), worse GLS (β=0.96; P<0.001), lower septal e′ (β=−1.11; P<0.001), lower lateral e′ (β=−1.23; P<0.001), and higher E/e′ (β=0.78; P<0.001) but not with LV ejection fraction, GCS, or LAVI (Figure 3A through C).

Sensitivity Analysis

In the subset of participants with LDL-C <4.144 mmol/L at baseline, greater RC levels (per SD in log-transformed) were significantly associated with higher LVMI (β=1.19; P=0.011), worse GLS (β=0.17; P=0.004), worse GCS (β=0.17; P=0.013), lower septal e′ (β=−0.26; P<0.001), lower lateral e′ (β=−0.16; P=0.009), and higher E/e′ ratio (β=0.14; P=0.010; Table S5). In the subset of participants with LDL-C <3.367 mmol/L, although the association between baseline RC levels and GCS, lateral e′, and E/e′ was attenuated, elevated baseline RC levels were associated with worse LVMI, GLS, and septal e′ (Table S6). Among participants with LDL-C ≥3.367 mmol/L, possibly due to the smaller sample size, the association of elevated RC at baseline was only significant for E/e′ and borderline significant for GLS and lateral e′ (Table S7). Additionally, the association between the RC level and LV structure and function remains robust after further adjustments for hypertension and diabetes status at year 25 (Table S8) and without adjustment for body mass index and fasting glucose (Table S9).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that higher baseline RC levels in young adulthood were related to unfavorable LV structural and functional alterations in middle age. Moreover, we identified 3 RC trajectories in young adults and discovered that the high RC trajectory group was related to unfavorable LV structural and functional changes. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to comprehensively assess the association between RC levels and long-term RC trajectories with LV structure and function in a young general population.

Previous epidemiological studies have shown that traditional lipids are associated with LV structure and function.21,22 Furthermore, a genetic study revealed that higher LDL-C and triglyceride were causally related to higher LV mass.4 These findings indicate that lipids may lead to an adverse outcome by directly causing unfavorable LV structural and functional alterations. Recently, the detrimental impact of RC has been identified beyond traditional lipids.5 RC is the cholesterol carried by TRLs. It has been well established that RC is a strong predictor of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.6–8 Moreover, recent studies have shown that RC is related to incident HF.9,10 However, the data on the associations of RC levels with LV structure and function are relatively limited. A previous study found that higher RC was related to myocardial dysfunction in type 2 diabetes populations.11 This study extended the previous work by showing the relationships between RC and LV structure and function among a general population. Similar to the previous observations, we found that higher RC in young adulthood was related to worse LV systolic and diastolic function in midlife. In addition, we also found that high RC was associated with increased LVMI, which was different from the prior study. Population differences may partly explain the inconsistent results. Additionally, risk factors are dynamic and fluctuate over time. Persistent dyslipidemia can lead to greater cardiovascular disease risk.23 Previous studies have found that different lipid trajectory patterns are associated with risks of cardiovascular diseases, underscoring the importance of maintaining optimal lipid levels over the long term.24–26 However, to date, most studies of RC have relied on single lipid measurements, and no prior studies have described lipid trajectory patterns of RC and assessed their relationships with cardiac structure and function. Herein, taking RC levels varying over time into account, we identified 3 distinct RC trajectories based on serial measurements across young adulthood to detect the long-term impact of RC on LV. We found that long-lasting high levels of RC during young adulthood were associated with unfavorable LV structural and functional alterations. Notably, adverse LV structural and functional alterations may increase the risk of HF and other cardiovascular events.27–29 Taken together, our observations demonstrated that high RC levels during young adulthood were related to unfavorable LV structural and functional changes in midlife, and early management of RC may help prevent the development of HF and other adverse outcomes.

Several mechanisms may partly explain the relationships between RC and LV structure and function. First, RC can enter the coronary arterial intima and be engulfed by macrophages to form atherosclerotic plaque, leading to cardiac ischemia.6,7 Global longitudinal strain, an early marker of cardiac ischemia,30 is worse in those with high RC levels, indicating that atherosclerosis may be a potential mechanistic pathway between RC and LV remodeling. Moreover, RC is associated with arterial stiffness.31,32 Arterial stiffness can increase LV afterload, leading to compensatory LV remodeling.33 Additionally, RC may cause aortic valve stenosis and low-grade systemic inflammation,34,35 both of which are implicated in LV remodeling.

Our findings have several important clinical implications. First, our investigation revealed that higher RC levels in adolescence were linked to LV remodeling and dysfunction, underscoring the potential value of early RC level screening for the identification of patients at high risk for developing HF and other adverse outcomes at midlife. Second, we discovered that the high-increasing trajectory group was related to LV hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction, despite baseline RC not being associated with subclinical LV remodeling and dysfunction, indicating that longitudinal monitoring of RC levels could further aid in identifying high-risk populations. Meanwhile, the long-term strategy of maintaining low RC levels would be more beneficial for cardiovascular disease management by monitoring the trajectory of individual cholesterol levels over time and intervening accordingly. Third, greater RC was also related to adverse LV changes among participants with serum LDL-C concentrations below goal levels, indicating that RC is a potential target for the prevention of HF and other cardiovascular events. Several agents have been shown to lower RC levels, such as fibrates, high-dose n-3 fatty acids, and inhibitors of apolipoprotein C3 and angiopoietin-related 3 genes.8 Future randomized control trials are required to test whether these agents can provide clinical benefits in the prevention of HF and other cardiovascular diseases, especially among individuals with optimal LDL-C levels.

The advantages of our study include a prospective cohort, a long-term follow-up, and a large sample size. However, there are also some limitations to our research. First, the observational nature of our study limits the ability to establish the causal relationships between RC levels and LV adverse remodeling and dysfunction. Second, despite numerous variables having been accounted for, there may still be residual confounding factors that have not been adjusted. Third, RC levels were calculated rather than measured directly, which might differ from the true level. However, there is a strong correlation between the calculated and measured RC levels.36 Fourth, the participants who did not attend echocardiographic examination at year 25 have a higher comorbidity burden. Excluding these unhealthier participants may introduce selection bias toward lower-risk participants, which would tend to bias our results to the null. Finally, our study only included Black and White populations; the results need to be validated in other ethnicities.

CONCLUSIONS

This study shows that elevated RC levels in young adulthood are related to adverse alterations in LV structure and function in midlife, even in individuals with optimal LDL-C levels. Moreover, long-term exposure to high RC levels during young adulthood is related to adverse LV remodeling and dysfunction. These findings suggest that early and frequent screening for RC levels may assist in the early identification of high-risk individuals and emphasize the importance of maintaining low RC levels beginning in young adulthood.
