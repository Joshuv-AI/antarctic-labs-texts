Linear maps preserving the Cullis’ determinant. II
Alexander Guterman, Andrey Yurkov

Abstract
This paper is the second in the series of papers devoted to the explicit description of linear maps preserving the Cullis’ determinant of rectangular matrices with entries belonging to an arbitrary ground field which is large enough. In this part we solve the linear preserver problem for the Cullis’ determinant defined on the spaces of matrices of size n × k n\times k with k ≥ 4 , n ≥ k + 2 k\geq 4,\;n\geq k+2 and n + k n+k is odd. In comparison with the case when n + k n+k is even, in this case linear maps preserving the Cullis’ determinant could be singular and are represented as a sum of two linear maps: first is two-sided matrix multiplication and second is any linear map whose image consists of matrices, all rows of which are equal.

The theory of linear maps preserving matrix invariants is well-known direction of research in Linear Algebra connected with other fields of mathematics. It has a long history which dates back to the beginning of 20th century in the works of Frobenius
[

The Cullis’ determinant is an invariant of rectangular matrix generalizing the notion of the ordinary determinant of square matrix which was introduced by Cullis in
[
2
]
and studied later by Radić in
[
13
,
15
,
14
,
16
]
, Makarewicz, Mozgawa, Pikuta and Szałkowski in
[
7
,
6
,
8
]
, Amiri, Fathy and Bayat in
[

]
, and Nakagami and Yanay in
[

]
. Thus, the question regarding the description of linear maps preserving the Cullis’ determinant arises naturally. The detailed introduction to the subject could be found in
[
5
]
.

In particular,
[
5
]
contains the description of linear maps preserving the Cullis determinant
\det_{n\,k}
for the case when
k\geq 4
,
n\geq k+2
and
n+k
is even as it stated in the following theorem (all necessary definitions are given in Section
2
).

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
, Theorem 5.14]
)
.

Assume that
k\geq 4,n\geq k+2,n+k
is even and
|{\mathbb{F}}|>k
. Let
T\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
be a linear map. Then
\det_{n\,k}(T(X))=\det_{n\,k}(X)
for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
if and only if there exist
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
such that

\det_{n\,k}\Bigl(A(|i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]\Bigr)\det_{k}\Bigl(B\Bigr)=(-1)^{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{k}-1-\ldots-k}

for all increasing sequences
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
and

T(X)=AXB

for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}}).

The parity of
n+k
is significant because if
n+k
is odd, then there exist linear maps preserving the Cullis’ determinant which do not have such form (Corollary
2.18
).

However, in this paper we show that the case
(n,k)
with
n\geq k
and
n+k
odd reduces to the case
(n-1,k)
(Lemma
4.10
(c)
). Using this reduction we prove the main theorem of this paper providing the description of linear maps preserving
\det_{n\,k}
if
n+k

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem
4.12
)
.

Assume that
|{\mathbb{F}}|>k\geq 4
,
n\geq k+2
and
n+k
is odd. Let
T\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
be a linear map. Then
\det_{n\,k}(T(X))=\det_{n\,k}(X)
for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
if and only if there exist
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
such that

\det_{n\,k}\Bigl(A(|i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]\Bigr)\det_{k}\Bigl(B\Bigr)=(-1)^{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{k}-1-\ldots-k}

for all increasing sequences
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
and a linear map
\phi\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to W_{n\,k}
such that

\det_{n\,k}\Bigl(A(|i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]\Bigr)\det_{k}\Bigl(B\Bigr)=(-1)^{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{k}-1-\ldots-k}

for all increasing sequences
1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{k}\leq n
and

T(X)=AXB+\phi(X)

for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}}).
Here
W_{n\,k}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
denotes the space of matrices, all rows of which are equal, being a radical of
\det_{n\,k}
.

Our proof of the main theorem relies on the notion of the radical of a function which was introduced and studied by Waterhouse
[
18
]
(Definition
2.23

with
k\geq 4,n\geq k+2
and

(n-1,k)
with

and
(n-1)+k
even is done by finding the radical of
\det_{n\,k}
explicitly and using its properties.

Thus, the results of the current paper and its predecessor
[
5
]
provide the solution to linear preserver problem for the Cullis’ determinant if

and the ground field is large enough. The cases

and

are considered in
[

]
as well. The case

is considered in

need a special approach because the proof for the case

relies on the following lemma and will be discussed separately. We remark that in this paper we apply the results obtained in
[

]
and we do not use any results from
[

]
.

Lemma 1.3

(
[
5
, Lemma 5.13]
)

Assume that

(

-th column, which is equal to one. By

we denote the element of a matrix

-th row and

For

For

We use the notation for submatrices following

(

Theorem 2.9

(
[

)

1.

For

2.

5.

Adding a linear combination of columns of

Corollary 2.11

Definition 2.22

(Cf.
[

Let

(Cf.
[

)

If

Lemma 2.24

(Cf.
[

\rad(f)

Lemma 2.25
.

If

is a linear map preserving

\Ker T\subseteq\rad(f)
.

Proof.

Indeed, assume that

Then

=f(T(\mathbf{v})+\lambda T(\mathbf{w}))=f(T(\mathbf{v})+\mathbf{0})=f(\mathbf{v})

for all
\mathbf{v}\in V
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{F}}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{w}\in\rad(f)
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose that

is odd,

and

X=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{2}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{3}&1&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{4}&0&1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

Proof.

Observe that

\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{2}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{3}&1&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{4}&0&1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

x_{2}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{3}&1&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{4}&0&1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{k}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n}&0&0&\cdots&1\end{vmatrix}_{n\,k}-\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{2}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{3}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{4}&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{k}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{n}&0&0&\cdots&0\end{vmatrix}_{n\,k}.

(3.1)

Consider the first term in the difference (
3.1
). Lemma
2.16
implies that it is zero since
n-k-1
is even.

Now consider the second term in the difference (
3.1
). Using the Laplace expansion along the second column for
k-2
times we obtain that

\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{2}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
x_{3}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
x_{4}&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{k}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

x_{2}&1\\
x_{k+1}&0\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n}&0\end{vmatrix}_{n-k+2\,2},

where

Hence,

(3.3)

The Laplace expansion along the second column yields that

\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}&1\\
x_{2}&1\\
x_{k+1}&0\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n}&0\end{vmatrix}_{n-k+1\,2}=(-1)^{2+1}\begin{vmatrix}x_{2}\\
x_{k+1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{vmatrix}_{n-k+1\,1}+(-1)^{2+2}\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{k+1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{vmatrix}_{n-k+1\,1}\\
=-\left(x_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{i}x_{k+i}\right)+\left(x_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{i}x_{k+i}\right)=x_{1}-x_{2}.

(3.4)

Thus, after making the substitution of (
3.4

3.2

3.3

\det_{n\,k}(X)=0-(-1)^{k-2}(x_{1}-x_{2})=(-1)^{k-1}(x_{1}-x_{2}).

∎

1

3

4
The proof of the main theorem

In this section we describe a relationship between linear
\det_{n\,k}
-preservers and
\det_{(n-1)\,k}
-preservers. This relationship allows us to reduce the linear preserver problem for
\det_{n\,k}
to the linear preserver problem for
\det_{(n-1)\,k}
as it is done in Lemma
4.10
. In conclusion we obtain a proof of the main theorem of this paper.

The relationship is established through the space
{\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
defined below which is possible to identify with
{\mathcal{M}}_{(n-1)\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
by the linear maps converting
\det_{n\,k}
to
\det_{n-1\,k}
and vice versa. These linear maps are defined in Definition
4.2
and Definition
4.5
, respectively.

Definition 4.1
.

By
{\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
we denote a linear space of all matrices such that their last row equal to zero, i.e.

{\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})=\{X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\mid x_{n\,j}=0,1\leq j\leq k\}.

Definition 4.2
.

For every matrix
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
by
L^{-}(X)\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1\,k}(\mathbb{F})
we denote the matrix defined by

L^{-}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\end{pmatrix}.

Lemma 4.3
.

L^{-}(X)=M^{-}X
for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
, where

M^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&-1\\
0&1&\ldots&0&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&-1\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1\,n}({\mathbb{F}}).

Proof.

It could be shown by direct computation. Indeed, if
X=(x_{i\,j})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}}),
then

M^{-}X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&-1\\
0&1&\ldots&0&-1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1\,1}&\cdots&x_{1\,k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{(n-1)\,1}&\cdots&x_{(n-1)\,k}\\
x_{n\,1}&\cdots&x_{n\,k}\\
\end{pmatrix}\\
=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1\,1}-x_{n\,1}&\cdots&x_{1\,k}-x_{n\,k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{(n-1)\,1}-x_{n\,1}&\cdots&x_{(n-1)\,k}-x_{n\,k}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\end{pmatrix}\\
=L^{-}(X).

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
n\geq k\geq 1
and
n+k
is odd. Then
\det_{n\,k}(X)=\det_{(n-1)\,k}(L^{-}(X))
for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
.

Proof.

Lemma
2.17
implies that
\det_{n\,k}(X)=\det_{n\,k}(X^{\prime})
, where

X^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\det_{n\,k}(X^{\prime})=\det_{n-1\,k}(L^{-}(X))
by Lemma
2.15
because the last row of
X^{\prime}
is zero and
X^{\prime}[1,\ldots,n-1|)=L^{-}(X)
.
∎

Definition 4.5
.

For every matrix
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n-1\,k}(\mathbb{F})
by
L^{+}(X)\in{\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}(\mathbb{F})
we denote the matrix obtained from

by adjoining to it the zero row as the last row; that is

L^{+}(X)=\begin{pmatrix}X\\

.

L^{+}(X)=M^{+}X
for all

, where

M^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\\

The proof could be done by the direct computation and similar to the proof of Lemma

.
∎

\det_{(n-1)\,k}(Y)=\det_{n\,k}(L^{+}(Y))

.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of the previous lemma. Let
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
. Then

\det_{(n-1)\,k}(L^{-}(X))=\det_{n\,k}(X)

(4.4)

by Lemma
4.4
. Also

\det_{(n-1)\,k}(S(L^{-}(X)))=\det_{(n-1)\,k}(L^{-}(X))

(4.5)

because
S
preserves
\det_{(n-1)\,k}
.

Lemma
4.7
implies that

\det_{(n-1)\,k}(S(L^{-}(X)))=\det_{n\,k}(L^{+}(S(L^{-}(X))).

(4.6)

The equalities (
4.4
), (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) aligned together imply that

\det_{n\,k}(X)=\det_{n\,k}(L^{+}(S(L^{-}(X)))\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})

Since
L^{+}\circ S\circ L^{+}=T
by the definition of
T
, we conclude that
T
preserves
\det_{n\,k}
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume that
|{\mathbb{F}}|>k
,
n>k
and
n+k
is odd. Let
T\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
be a linear map such that
\det_{n\,k}(T(X))=\det_{n\,k}(X)
and
\Img(T)={\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
. Then the following statements hold.

(a)

\Ker(T)=\rad(\det_{n\,k})
.

(b)

L^{+}\circ L^{-}\circ T=T
.

(c)

T\circ L^{+}\circ L^{-}=T
.

(d)

If a linear map
S
is defined by

S=L^{-}\circ T\circ L^{+},

(4.7)

then
T=L^{+}\circ S\circ L^{-}
.

Proof.

(a)

The obvious equality
{\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\cap\rad(\det_{n\,k})=\{0\}
implies that
T(\rad(\det_{n\,k}))=\{0\}
because

T(\rad(\det_{n\,k}))\subseteq\Img(T)={\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})

and
T(\rad(\det_{n\,k}))\subseteq\rad(\det_{n\,k})
by Lemma
2.27
(a)
.

Therefore,
\Ker(T)\supseteq\rad(\det_{n\,k})
. Since
\Ker(T)\subseteq\rad(\det_{n\,k})
by Lemma
2.25
, then we conclude that
\Ker(T)=\rad(\det_{n\,k})
.

(b)

Indeed, if
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
, then

T(X)=\begin{pmatrix}(T(X))[1|)\\
\vdots\\
(T(X))[n-1|)\\
0\end{pmatrix}

because
\Img(T)={\mathcal{M}}^{0}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
. Hence,

L^{+}\circ L^{-}\circ T(X)=L^{+}(L^{-}(T(X)))=L^{+}(L^{-}(\begin{pmatrix}(T(X))[1|)\\
\vdots\\
(T(X))[n-1|)\\
0\end{pmatrix}))\\
=L^{+}(\begin{pmatrix}(T(X))[1|)\\
\vdots\\
(T(X))[n-1|)\end{pmatrix})=\begin{pmatrix}(T(X))[1|)\\
\vdots\\
(T(X))[n-1|)\\
0\end{pmatrix}=T(X).

for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
.

(c)

On the one hand,

T\circ L^{+}\circ L^{-}(X)=T(L^{+}(L^{-}(X)))\\
=T(L^{+}(\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\end{pmatrix}))=T(\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\\
0\end{pmatrix})

for all
X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
. On the other hand,

T(X)=T(\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\\
0\end{pmatrix})+T(\begin{pmatrix}X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n|)\\
X[n|)\end{pmatrix})\\
=T(\begin{pmatrix}X[1|)-X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n-1|)-X[n|)\\
0\end{pmatrix})

since
\begin{pmatrix}X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n|)\\
X[n|)\end{pmatrix}\in W_{n\,k}
and consequently
T(\begin{pmatrix}X[n|)\\
\vdots\\
X[n|)\\
X[n|)\end{pmatrix})=0
by
(a)
.

(d)

This part is a direct consequence from
(b)
and
(c)
. Indeed,

L^{+}\circ S\circ L^{-}\overset{\eqref{lem:FromNKOddToNKEvenIfKerW:eq}}{=\joinrel=}L^{+}\circ\left(L^{-}\circ T\circ L^{+}\right)\circ L^{-}\\
=\left(L^{+}\circ L^{-}\circ T\right)\circ L^{+}\circ L^{-}\overset{\ref{lem:FromNKOddToNKEvenIfKerW:part5}}{=}T\circ L^{+}\circ L^{-}\overset{\ref{lem:FromNKOddToNKEvenIfKerW:part6}}{=}T.

∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Assume that
|{\mathbb{F}}|>k
,
n>k
and
n+k
is odd. Let
S
be a linear map on
{\mathcal{M}}_{(n-1)\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
and
L
be a linear map on
{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
defined by

T=L^{-}\circ S\circ L^{+}.

If there exist
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{(n-1)\,(n-1)}({\mathbb{F}})
and
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfying the condition (
2.3
) such that

S(Y)=A^{\prime}YB^{\prime}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;Y\in{\mathcal{M}}_{(n-1)\,k}({\mathbb{F}}),

then there exist
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,n}({\mathbb{F}})
and
B\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
satisfying the condition (
2.3
) such that

T(X)=AXB\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}}).

Proof.

Since
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
satisfy the condition (
2.3
), then
S
is a linear map preserving
\det_{(n-1)\,k}
by Lemma
2.19
. Hence
T
is a linear map preserving
\det_{n\,k}
by Lemma
4.9
.

The equality
T=L^{+}\circ S\circ L^{-}
implies that

T(X)=M^{+}(S(M^{-}X))

. Recall there that

and
M^{-}
are defined in Lemma
4.3
and Lemma

. Therefore,

T(X)=M^{+}(A^{\prime}(M^{-}X)B^{\prime})=(M^{+}A^{\prime}M^{-})XB^{\prime}=AXB

for all

Since
T
is a linear map preserving
\det_{n\,k}
as we have shown above, then
A
and
B
satisfy the condition (
2.3
) by Lemma
2.19
.
∎

Theorem 4.12
.

Assume that
|{\mathbb{F}}|>k\geq 4
,
n\geq k+2
and
n+k
is odd. Let
T\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})

and

\det_{n\,k}\Bigl(A(|i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}]\Bigr)\det_{k}\Bigl(B\Bigr)=(-1)^{i_{1}+\ldots+i_{k}-1-\ldots-k}

Here

.

) for certain

2.19
implies that a linear map

2.18

\det_{n\,k}

4.10
(d)

4.8
.Lemma
2.20

Remark 4.13

Remark 4.14
.

There exist linear maps of the form (
4.9
) which do not admit a representation
X\mapsto A^{\prime}XB^{\prime}
for any
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,n}
,
B^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{k\,k}
. For example a linear map
T^{\prime}\colon{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})
defined by

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-x_{1\,1}&0&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}\;\;\mbox{for all}\;\;X\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\,k}({\mathbb{F}})

has the form (
