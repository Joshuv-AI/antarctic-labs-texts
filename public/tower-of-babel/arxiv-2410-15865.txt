Principles of semantic and functional efficiency in grammatical patterning
Emily Cheng, Francesca Franzon

Abstract
Grammatical features such as number and gender serve two central functions in human languages. While they encode salient semantic attributes like numerosity and animacy, they also offload sentence processing cost by predictably linking words together via grammatical agreement. Grammars exhibit consistent organizational patterns across diverse languages, invariably rooted in a semantic foundation — a widely confirmed but still theoretically unexplained phenomenon. To explain the basis of universal grammatical patterns, we unify two fundamental properties of grammar, semantic encoding and agreement-based predictability, into a single information-theoretic objective under cognitive constraints. Our analyses reveal that grammatical organization provably inherits from perceptual attributes, but that grammars empirically prioritize functional goals, promoting efficient language processing over semantic encoding. An information-theoretic model of grammatical organization

We explain the empirical distribution of grammatical values as a function of communicative goals and constraints. To do this, we model the organization of grammatical systems as a multi-level optimization problem, from which we later derive grammatical universals.

Grammatical features need to satisfy semantic and functional goals. Semantically, they need to encode attributes of a referent, in a consistent way across different referents. For example, the grammatical feminine [+Fem] for any referent, like a cat (‘gata’), conveys that the cat is female; ideally, the same [+Fem] consistently means
+female
for words denoting other referents, like a friend (‘amiga’).
Now considering grammar’s functional role, the distribution of grammatical values across different words has implications for processing ease. For instance, if all nouns in Spanish were grammatically masculine, then seeing a masculine article ‘el’ (‘the’) would give no predictive benefit for the upcoming noun. In contrast, if the grammatical masculine and feminine are distributed 50-50 across nouns, then on average, seeing ‘el’ halves the set of potential upcoming nouns, making prediction easier.

We see, then, that grammatical systems are organized on two levels: grammatical values need to distribute probability mass over
instances
of a given word, and across different
words
in the lexical system. As such, we propose an objective, Eq. (
1
), at
two scales
, the word level and the system level.
In this section, we derive Eq. (
1

1

Semantic encoding and compression at the word level

We first consider the word level objective. To communicate about an instance of a referent,

Memory-surprisal tradeoff underlies semantic specification

We just showed that grammatical values inherit from semantic features. Now, we consider the manner of this inheritance through the lens of underspecification. In particular, we show that semantic underspecification in grammatical systems mathematically results from Eq. (

More precisely, semantic specification can be divided into three categories: (1) full specification, in which grammar perfectly encodes semantics, (2) underspecification, in which grammar lossily encodes semantics, and (3) neutralization, in which grammar does not encode semantics. The three categories correspond to three sets of encoding strategies

Overall, results suggest that semantic goals shape grammatical value distributions within semantically-interpreted subsystems; at the same time, functional goals disperse values across the entire lexicon.

Discussion

We proposed an information-theoretic explanation of how semantic and functional goals shape grammatical systems, which we validated on cross-linguistically diverse, large-scale corpora. We showed that universally shared grammatical structures and related word distributions emerge when jointly optimizing for surprisal, memory and on-line processing terms.

Our grammatical organization objective, posed as a multi-level optimization, extends previous findings in the functionalist paradigm of linguistics. The functionalist perspective attempts to explain linguistic phenomena via cognitive constraints and communicative goals
[
80
,
11
,
29
,
23
]
, often using the information theoretic formalism of Shannon’s communication channel
[
70
]
. Language, viewed as the mapping from message to signal, is typically modeled to minimize the receiver’s surprisal while minimizing the sender’s effort
[
82
,
55
]
.

Along these lines, Futrell and Hahn
[
34
]
put forth a general framework in which common formalisms in linguistic theory can be derived from the following information-theoretic objective:

\displaystyle\min_{f(s|m)}

\displaystyle\qquad\mathcal{H}(M|S)\qquad\text{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}surprisal} }

(4a)

subj. to

\displaystyle\qquad\langle C(s)\rangle=k.\qquad\text{{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}cost} }

(4b)

In various contexts, linguistic organization reflects instatiations of (
4
). Among them, the
Information Bottleneck
(IB) objective, explaining, e.g., organization of semantic systems
[
52
,
80
]
and some grammatical markers
[
58
]
; the memory-surprisal tradeoff that explains, e.g., dependency locality
[
32
]
and morpheme order
[
42
]
; and others that posit Zipf’s law to result from surprisal minimization pressures in language processing
[
63
,
82
,
27
,
70
]
.

Objective functions like Eq. (
4
) can describe meaning encoding in the lexicon, although they average out word-level effects to model the entire system. They also apply to grammar, though in a limited fashion. Instead, our formulation at two scales captures behavior in meaningful subsystems such as animate nouns, and more granularly, for each referent. In the edge case where
\alpha_{x}=1
for all
x
and an average is taken over
x
, our Eq. (
1
) takes a similar form to Eq. (
4
).

We have expanded previous formalizations centered on meaning encoding
[
80
,
58
,
52
]
, by integrating goals of single communicative instances at the word level and processing-oriented objectives at the system level
[
23
,
29
]
. To this purpose, our approach considers the frequency of use of grammatical values, revealing the foundations of three core phenomena of grammatical organization: underspecification, semantic inheritance, and agreement-based discriminability, then verified in real languages.

Underspecification

Previous research demonstrated that grammatical systems optimally encode semantic attributes into values
[
58
]
. In their work, Mollica and colleagues
[
58
]
use IB to estimate optimality of number, tense, and evidentiality markers in real languages. However, they only consider semantic aspects of grammar, and, moreover, restrict analysis to the distribution of
types
, or unique values in a system. Instead, we take the view that the functionalist formalism should consider language use, which we quantify on
tokens
, namely instances of words, corresponding to instances of referents.

We showed empirically that grammars do not prioritize the encoding of semantic attributes in language use. In particular, underspecification consistently appears in the gender distribution for animate nouns, where the more frequent use of masculine corresponds to an ambiguous semantic coding, as depicted in
fig.

1
b. Our model does not explain why the underspecified value is consistently masculine across languages, but it predicts that a more frequent value is likely to be interpreted as underspecified. A higher number of male referents denoting human occupations, perhaps due to historical and cultural drivers, can bias the frequency towards the masculine value
[
45
,
43
]
. By satisfying the pressure towards simplicity, the grammatical masculine may then be interpreted and reused as underspecified in the whole system.
Our perspective of underspecification suggests that, beyond the true distribution of semantic attributes, the use of a value can be influenced by its own frequency. This view bridges vocabulary-based theories of meaning encoding with theories causally relating word frequency to processing ease
[
81
,
50
,
63
]
. As our data reflect a single moment in linguistic evolution, we cannot describe how underspecification arises over time, and leave diachronic modeling to future work.

Semantic inheritance

Although grammatical values do not maximize meaning transmission, they inherit from semantic attributes as a foundation. Other models of language efficiency have overlooked this aspect: in Eq. (
4
),
f
minimizes surprisal subject to a cost constraint. From formulation
4
, it is not guaranteed that the number of messages bounds the number of signals. This is because instantiations of Eq. (
4
) admit optimal solutions that produce many synonyms for one meaning
[
7
,
76
]
. Instead, our Eq. (
1
) inverts the objective Eq. (
4a
) and constraint Eq. (
4b
) so that cost is minimized subject to bounded surprisal. That semantic attributes constrain the set of grammatical values follows from this key difference, suggesting that Zipf’s PLE can explain a universally occurring property for known languages
[
14
,
15
]
.

Our model takes for granted that some attributes, and not others, are grammaticalized cross-linguistically. This may stem from the perceptual and cultural salience of encoded attributes, which may be desirable to communicate to conspecifics
[
38
,
75
,
30
]
. We take the view that the chosen encoded attributes would enter in Objective
1
as another inner optimization that constrains
\mathbf{A}
based on extra-linguistic environmental factors.

Agreement-based discriminability

Finally, we showed for 12 languages that number and gender values distribute to optimize discriminability through agreement, enabling word prediction.
Our analysis can be applied to other grammatical features like tense, aspect, mood, nominal classifiers, and to the organization of lexical words, especially when they have a role in prediction
[
41
,
24
]
. In these cases, the different balance between semantic and functional goals may result in different distributions.

Conclusion

We have presented evidence suggesting languages prioritize functional processing via grammatical agreement. Together with our theoretical finding that grammatical values inherit from semantic categories, results suggest that a language’s grammatical values may arise from communicating about semantics in a part of the lexicon; then, these values are co-opted by the overall system to reduce the cost of language processing. Overall, our results suggest that grammar inherits its values from semantic attributes, but does not maximize their transmission, instead distributing them for optimal processing.

Methods

Our study relies on the information-theoretic framework in Shannon’s theory of communication
[
70
]
. Here, we define and motivate the information-theoretic terms in Eq. (
1
), then describe our preprocessing and analysis of grammatical distributions in 12 languages.

Information-theoretic formalism

Entropy and other information-theoretic measures provide the mathematical formalism for Shannon’s theory of communication
[
70
]
. As such, it is natural to describe the communicative pressures in Eq. (
1
) such as coding complexity, referent informativity, and semantic consistency in information-theoretic terms
[
34
,
37
]
.

In Eq. (
1
), we use the Shannon entropy
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
to describe feature complexity at the word level and discriminability at the system level. The Shannon entropy
\mathcal{H}(X)
of a discrete random variable
X
supported on
\mathcal{X}
is defined as

\mathcal{H}(X)\triangleq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p(x)\log p(x),

(5)

and describes the amount of
information
in bits contained in
X
. Entropy is moreover an intuitive measure of
cost
, where cost is equivalent to
coding length
of
X
. As such, in Eq. (
1
) we describe feature complexity given a referent
\mathcal{H}(W_{A}|X=x)
as a cost given by the feature’s coding length. Entropy describes the information content of
X
, but, equivalently, its uncertainty or dispersion. At the system level, discriminability is optimal when grammatical values are maximally disperse in the lexicon
[
29
]
, which motivates the max-entropy term
\mathcal{H}(W_{A})
in Eq. (
1
).

One can also measure the
conditional entropy
, or surprisal, of a random variable
Y
conditioned on random variable
X
, that is, the coding length of
Y
given
X
. This is given by the conditional entropy

\mathcal{H}(Y|X)\triangleq-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(x,y)\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(y)},

(6)

where
Y
is supported on
\mathcal{Y}
. The higher the conditional entropy
\mathcal{H}(Y|X)
, the harder it is to predict
Y
given
X
. As such, conditional entropy formalizes referent informativity at the word level
-\mathcal{H}(A|W_{A},X=x)
, where
W_{A}
is more informative about
A
if the coding length of
A|W_{A}
decreases. On the system level, conditional entropy
\mathcal{H}(W_{A}|A)
formalizes
consistency
of grammatical value given semantic value:
W_{A}
should be predictable given
A
.

Language data

We analyzed the distribution of nouns across large-scale corpora in 12 languages, representing different grammatical systems: German, English, Dutch, Swedish (Germanic); French, Italian, Spanish, Catalan (Romance); Polish, Slovene (Slavic): Hebrew and Arabic (Semitic). We obtained grammatical gender and number information for noun occurrences using Stanza, a pre-trained, neural part-of-speech and grammatical features tagger
[
66
]
. We refer to the above data as the set of
all nouns
.

Except for English, Dutch, and Swedish, all languages tested have a set of animate nouns where grammatical gender is semantically interpreted, often referring to animals, nationality, or profession (e.g.,
una amiga
,
un amigo
in Spanish). For those 9 languages, we created the subset of animate nouns by filtering the set of all nouns for animacy, then choosing those inflected or derived in both the masculine and feminine. Due to noise in the automatic tagger, the animate subset was checked by hand for each language. Detailed preprocessing steps may be found in the SI.

In both all nouns and the animate subset, we estimate the entropy of grammatical values
W_{A}
. We also report their KL-divergence to the uniform distribution, where KL-divergence quantifies the disparity between the observed distribution
p
and the optimal uniform distribution
q
. The KL-divergence is defined as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{KL}(q||p)\triangleq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}q(x)\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}.

(7)

KL-divergence describes the excess coding length when using the uniform distribution
q
to describe the observed
p
. Lower KL-divergence means
p
is closer to uniform, in our case, more optimal for agreement-based discriminability.

\mathcal{W}_{A}
. Finally, the max-entropy term in Eq. (
8a

We first consider the simplicity constraint in Eq. (

\displaystyle\qquad\underset{\text{size}}{|W_{A}|}

(11a)

\displaystyle\qquad\underset{\text{consistency}}{\mathcal{H}(W_{A}|A)\leq c}

(11b)

\displaystyle\qquad f:A\mapsto W_{A}

(11c)

\displaystyle\qquad\underset{\text{size}}{|W_{A}|}-\beta\underset{\text{consistency}}{\mathcal{H}(W_{A}|A)}

The pressure towards simplicity at the system level further constrains the set of
f
, by imposing global correlations between the attribute-value mappings for referent. This effectively constrains the overall vocabulary space
\mathcal{W}_{A}
; where without it, the plural for ‘cat’ may differ from the plural for “friend”, the consistency term forces them to be the same. Likewise, the size reduction term eliminates synonyms within-and-between referents. After these simplicity conditions are satisfied, entropy maximization is applied (Eq.
8a
) to assign nouns to grammatical values in a maximally dispersed way. This completes the system-level objective.

(Total features in a system)
.

For

Data Collection and Preprocessing

Here, we outline the preprocessing steps to obtain the set of all nouns and the animate subset for all languages. All analyses are based on the language-specific Wikipedias. Although we considered other data sources such as Universal Dependencies
[
59
]
and Wacky Wide Web corpora
[
25
,
2
]
, we chose Wikipedia due to a combination of its large size and typological spread. For a single language, the steps in our pipeline are summarized as follows:

1.

Tag entire Wikipedia corpus for part-of-speech, lemma, grammatical gender, and grammatical number

2.

To form the set of all nouns, take the intersection of words tagged as
NOUN
by Stanza and by WordNet.

3.

To form the set of animate nouns, take the lemmas that are in the set of words labeled
PERSON
or
ANIMAL
in WordNet.

All preprocessing steps were done in Python, using publicly available resources.

Wikipedia data preprocessing

The Wikipedia data are sourced from Huggingface, found at
https://huggingface.co/datasets/wikimedia/wikipedia
. Data consists of entire articles, cleaned to strip markdown and references, with a time cutoff of January 20, 2023 for all languages.

Using Stanza
[
66
]
, an automatic tagger, we convert each article to CONLL-U format
[
8
]
, which lists each word tagged with its part-of-speech (POS), lemma, and morphological features (grammatical features like gender and number). The available POS and morphological features for each language may be found on the language-specific reference treebanks listed on the Stanza webpage:
https://stanfordnlp.github.io/stanza/performance.html
.

Selecting the set of all nouns

The results of the automatic tagger were often noisy, such that words tagged
NOUN
may have been, for instance, proper nouns or non-words. To remedy this, we culled the set of nouns by restricting the set of words tagged
NOUN
by Stanza to those also tagged
NOUN
by WordNet, a lexicographic resource available for many languages in the
nltk
Python package
[
5
,
57
]
. This yields the set of all nouns for all the languages.

Selecting the animate subset

We then selected a subset of animate nouns for which grammatical gender is semantically interpreted for Romance, Slavic, Semitic languages, and German (group A). For completeness, we did the same for Dutch, Swedish, and English (group B), although the gender and number distributions on the animate subset were not relevant for analysis.

First, for both groups A and B, we selected a subset of nouns from the all-nouns set that were labelled
ANIMAL
or
PERSON
in WordNet, that is, nouns that have a semantic gender or biological sex. The German WordNet
[
71
]
was sparsely annotated for these domains, so we translated the lemmas from English WordNet
[
57
]
to German, allowing for better coverage. We stopped here for group A. For group B, we chose the animate subset as follows. We kept all lemmas that exhibit all combinations of
\{
Fem, Masc
\}\times\{
Sing, Plur, (Dual if applicable)
\}
. This was considering both inflection and derivation, where inflection would be, for example,
gato
[+Masc] /
gata
[+Fem] (Spanish, cat), and derivation modifies the ending of the word as in
der Student
[+Masc] /
die Studentin
[+Fem] (German, student). This creates a subset of nouns where types are distributed uniformly across all possible combinations, while tokens are not. The token distribution, which captures language
use
, is then our distribution of interest.
