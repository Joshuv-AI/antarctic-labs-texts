Anatomical relationship between the terminal thoracic duct and brachial plexus: A cadaveric study
Authors: Adilson J.M. de Oliveira, Mario Gilberto Siqueira, João Paulo Mota Telles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo study the anatomical relationship between the terminal portion of the thoracic duct (TD) and the brachial plexus (BP).MethodsAnatomical study performed on 72 fresh cadavers, dissected only on the left side. Anatomical structures were preserved, and measurements were performed using an digital caliper. Statistical analysis was performed with a 95% confidence interval and a precision level set at 3 mm.ResultsThe TD terminated on the left side in 100% of specimens. Three termination sites of the thoracic duct (TD) were identified: the internal jugular vein (50.0%), the junction between the internal jugular and subclavian veins (45.8%), and directly into the subclavian vein (4.2%). The TD ran parallel to the brachial plexus (BP) in 62.5% of cases and perpendicular in 37.5%. The structures closest to the TD were the phrenic nerve and the C5 root, at an average distance of 17 mm.DiscussionThis anatomical proximity has important surgical implications. Understanding these relationships may reduce the risk of TD injury during BP surgeries.ConclusionThere is a consistent anatomical relationship between the TD and BP, typically within 2 cm, highlighting the need for careful dissection in this region.

Introduction

Traumatic brachial plexus injuries are generally disabling and represent a significant burden, particularly in developing countries where motorcycle-related traffic accidents are common (Faglioni et al., 2014).

The surgical techniques for brachial plexus reconstruction have progressively evolved over recent decades, resulting in improved outcomes and reduced complication rates (Martins et al., 2013; Siqueira and Martins, 2011; Millesi, 2003; Delaney et al., 2017; Jortay and Bisschop, 2001).

Thoracic duct (TD) injuries are rare but serious complications during surgical procedures involving the left cervical region (Terzis and Kokkalis, 2010; Marcus and Clarke, 2003; Stuart, 1907; Nussenbaum et al., 2000; Allen and Briggs, 1969; Nair et al., 2007; Itkin et al., 2010; Wemyss-Holden et al., 2001; Novelli et al., 2019; Ammar et al., 2003; Skandalakis et al., 2007; Derakhshan et al., 2017; Louzada et al., 2016), with an incidence of up to 8% in cervical explorations (Allen and Briggs, 1969; Nair et al., 2007; Itkin et al., 2010; Wemyss-Holden et al., 2001; Novelli et al., 2019; Ammar et al., 2003; Skandalakis et al., 2007; Derakhshan et al., 2017; Louzada et al., 2016). In brachial plexus surgeries specifically, the incidence is lower, ranging from 1% to 5%. (Terzis and Kokkalis, 2010), (Stuart, 1907) Limited anatomical knowledge of the TD and its relationship with the brachial plexus (BP) among surgeons can be a contributing factor to increased surgical technical difficulty. To the best of our knowledge, previous studies have addressed the supraclavicular anatomy of the TD or BP separately; however, to date, no studies have evaluated the anatomical relationship between these two structures.

Although uncommon, TD injury is associated with considerable morbidity, requiring prolonged hospitalization in some cases (up to 5 to 10 days), implementation of a fat-free diet, and, in up to 25% of cases, open or endovascular surgical intervention. In more severe scenarios, outcomes may be life-threatening, with reported mortality rates of up to 7.5%. (Stuart, 1907), (Nussenbaum et al., 2000) Surgical anatomical knowledge is an important tool for improving surgical outcomes.

Thus, a precise understanding of the anatomical relationship between the TD and BP may help reduce the risk of iatrogenic injury, leading to better patient outcomes and decreased institutional costs.

The aim of this study is to investigate the anatomical relationship between the TD and the BP, identify the anatomical location of its terminal portion, and determine its relationship with the closest structures of the brachial plexus.

Methods

Sample size calculation

For the sample size calculation, aimed at addressing the primary objective of estimating the average distance between the upper edge of the clavicle and the most cranial point of the thoracic duct (TD), the minimum and maximum values described in the literature were considered, with estimates of 0.0 cm and 8.0 cm, respectively, and a population standard deviation of 1.3 cm. Based on t-distribution assumptions and a 95% confidence interval, a precision of 0.3 cm (3 mm) was obtained, resulting in a required sample size of 72 specimens.

Given the absence of prior data on TD–BP relationships, this parameter was used as a surrogate for sample size estimation.

Specimen selection

The specimens were consecutively selected from the Death Verification Service. According to national law, traumatic deaths or deaths suspected to be related to any crime are not eligible for anatomical research in this service; therefore, these specimens were excluded. Only the left side was dissected, based on previous literature reporting the presence of the TD on the left side in 95–100% of cases. In this study, fresh, non-frozen specimens were dissected within a postmortem interval of up to 12 h.

Adult specimens.

Postmortem interval of up to 12 h.

Previous neck surgery.

Dissection description

The cadaver was placed in a horizontal supine position.

A flap of skin and subcutaneous tissue was dissected together and reflected cranially, exposing the posterior triangle of the neck, limited posteriorly by the trapezius muscle, anteriorly by the sternocleidomastoid muscle, and inferiorly by the middle third of the clavicle in the supraclavicular area.

The supraclavicular fat was dissected in the inferior part of the posterior triangle of the neck.

The external jugular vein and the omohyoid muscle were identified, ligated, and resected.

After partial removal of the supraclavicular fat, the C5 root of the BP could be identified emerging from the interscalene space, between the anterior and middle scalene muscles.

Partial resection of the posterior fibers of the sternocleidomastoid muscle allowed exposure of the left carotid artery, left vagus nerve, and left internal jugular vein.

The junction between the internal jugular vein and the subclavian vein was identified.

Circumferential dissection of these veins, namely the internal jugular and subclavian veins, usually allowed identification of the junction of the TD with the venous system.

When the TD was not identified at this point, the clavicle was disarticulated from the sternum and retracted laterally, allowing more proximal dissection. Fig. 1 shows an overview of the supraclavicular region anatomy.

At the end of the dissection, several measurements were taken. The use of silicone tapes to retract anatomical structures was avoided, as they could displace them from their original position and compromise the measurements. After dissection and measurements, the structure identified as the TD was sectioned and inspected. The TD was characterized by the absence of blood within its lumen and by the presence of a viscous, fatty, whitish secretion consistent with chyle.

Measurements

All measurements were performed by the same surgeon using a 150-mm Universal Digital Caliper from ZAAS, model MTX-316119. The following measurements were performed:

Distance between the most cranial point of the TD and the upper edge of the clavicle, measured along a perpendicular line, as shown in Fig. 2.

Diameter of the TD at its termination.

Distance between the TD and the closest anatomical structure of the BP.

Distance between the TD and the phrenic nerve (PN) at the point of greatest proximity between both anatomical structures.

In addition to these measurements, the position of the TD in relation to the brachial plexus was recorded as parallel or perpendicular, according to whether the TD crossed the BP.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using graphical methods, as well as kurtosis and skewness values, and were described as means and standard deviations or medians and quartiles, as appropriate. All analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, 2021).

Ethical aspects

The research protocol was submitted to and approved by the University Research Ethics Committee, approval number 6.054.051.

No interventions other than those described were performed on the specimens. Anonymity was guaranteed.

This research project involving cadaveric specimens falls within the category of studies that do not require an informed consent form, in accordance with national regulations governing the use of human bodies for research and educational purposes.

Results

Seventy-two specimens were included in the analysis. The mean age was 67.4 years (SD ± 14.5 years), with the majority of specimens being female (62.5%). Anthropometric variables are presented in Table 1.

The thoracic duct (TD) had a supraclavicular termination in 68 specimens (94.4%). Four specimens (5.6%) had a retroclavicular position. An ampulla at the termination was observed in 10 specimens (13.9%). Variables related to TD measurements are presented in Table 2, and the correlation analysis of TD measurements is shown in Figs. 3 and 4.

Three termination sites of the thoracic duct (TD) were identified in the dissected specimens: the internal jugular vein (50.0%), the junction between the internal jugular and subclavian veins (45.8%), and direct termination into the subclavian vein (4.2%). The two main variations in TD termination are illustrated inFig. 5.

Regarding the number of thoracic duct (TD) terminations, only two variants were observed in the present study: single termination (83.3%) and double termination within the same venous vessel (16.7%).

The phrenic nerve was the anatomical structure closest to the TD in 48.9% of specimens, followed by the C5 root in 40.3%, and the middle trunk in 10.8%.

The TD ran parallel to the brachial plexus (BP) in 62.5% of cases and perpendicular in 37.5%. When parallel, both structures were located within the same anatomical plane; when perpendicular, the TD was superficial to the BP in 100% of cases as in Fig. 6.

The closest neural structure to the TD was the phrenic nerve in 48.6% of cases, followed by the C5 root in 40.3%, the upper trunk in 9.7%, and other neural structures in 1.4%. The mean distance between the TD and the closest neural structure was 17 ± 13.03 mm.

Discussion

Although uncommon, intraoperative injury to the thoracic duct (TD) is a complication associated with significant postoperative morbidity and even mortality (Stuart, 1907; Nussenbaum et al., 2000).

To the best of our knowledge, our series represents the largest anatomical dissection study of the TD in fresh, non-frozen cadavers reported in the literature, and the first to examine the anatomical relationship between the TD and the brachial plexus (BP).

Dissection in fresh, non-embalmed specimens yields higher-quality anatomical information, as formaldehyde dehydrates tissues, compromising measurement accuracy. Furthermore, formaldehyde fixation causes all structures to assume similar color and consistency, making anatomical dissection more difficult and limiting the surgical applicability of anatomical findings. We identified only one prior study involving 25 fresh specimens (Louzada et al., 2016), another with 40 specimens combining fresh and embalmed cadavers, and a third involving 20 surgical patients (Bellier et al., 2020).

The main findings of the present study were clear and in agreement with previously published data: the TD was consistently located on the left side, its diameter was in line with previous descriptions ranging from 3 to 5 mm (3.07 mm in our study) (Louzada et al., 2016; Bellier et al., 2020; Parsons and Sargent, 1909; Hematti and Mehran, 2011; Gottlieb and Greenfield, 1956; O'Hagan et al., 2021; Van Pernis, 1949), and it was almost always found between 3 and 4 cm above the clavicle (3.9 cm in our study).

When applying our data to left-sided BP surgery, it is possible to infer that the TD lies at approximately the same level (in relation to the clavicle) as commonly used surgical approaches to the brachial plexus, which are typically performed via an incision approximately 2.5 cm above the clavicle, with dissection proceeding in both cranial and caudal directions (Martins et al., 2013; Siqueira and Martins, 2011; Millesi, 2003).

Based on our results, we suggest an anatomical triangle located in the most inferior and anterior portion of the posterior triangle of the neck, within which the TD could be found in approximately 94% of specimens. The base of this triangle is defined by a straight line along the superior edge of the clavicle, extending 5 cm laterally from the midline to the left. The medial border follows the posterior edge of the sternocleidomastoid muscle cranially for 7 cm, and the lateral border is a straight line connecting the endpoints of the inferior and medial borders, as illustrated in Fig. 7. This may be used by surgeons as an additional anatomical landmark for left-sided brachial plexus surgery.

When evaluating the relationship between the thoracic duct (TD) and the brachial plexus (BP), we found that the anatomical structures most frequently adjacent to the TD were, in order of frequency, the phrenic nerve (PN) and the C5 root. These structures are commonly dissected by surgeons, as they represent two of the main axon donors in brachial plexus reconstruction procedures (Martins et al., 2013; Siqueira and Martins, 2011; Millesi, 2003). The TD typically lies parallel and medial to the PN; however, in more than one-third of cases, it was found to be perpendicular, crossing the surgical field, where it may be misidentified as fibrotic tissue or fat and is therefore prone to inadvertent injury.

When the left PN is selected as an axon donor, we suggest, based on the findings of this study, that surgeons begin by dissecting and identifying the TD as a safety measure. This includes identifying the internal jugular vein (IJV) and actively inspecting for any structure that drains into it.

Two statistically significant associations emerged from our anatomical findings, both with relevant implications for surgeries in the supraclavicular region.(1)The greater the supraclavicular extension of the TD, the more likely it was to run parallel to the PN;(2)The greater the supraclavicular extension of the TD, the more frequently it terminated in the IJV.

These findings may serve as a suggestion for surgeons to actively dissect and identify the IJV and to explore the position of the TD before proceeding. Conversely, a lower-positioned TD was more frequently perpendicular to the PN and BP; thus, in more distal dissections involving C5 or the upper trunk, special attention should be paid to structures crossing the field from medial to lateral.

The present findings have practical applications across various neurosurgical procedures, including high-flow bypass, carotid endarterectomy, vagus nerve stimulator implantation, and cervical discectomy, as well as in surgeries from other specialties involving the region where the TD is located.

In this large anatomical series, we identified several notable variations of the TD that are rarely described in the literature. The most frequent site of TD termination in our study was the IJV (50%), which is consistent with previous reports, with no significant variation. Published data show an average frequency of 54% across various studies (Louzada et al., 2016; Bellier et al., 2020), although with a wide reported range from 4% to 84% (Louzada et al., 2016; Bellier et al., 2020). The second most common site in our series was the junction of the IJV and subclavian vein (45%), closely matching values reported in the literature (45.8%), although some authors have described this site as the most frequent (Louzada et al., 2016; Bellier et al., 2020; Parsons and Sargent, 1909; Hematti and Mehran, 2011; Gottlieb and Greenfield, 1956; O'Hagan et al., 2021; Van Pernis, 1949).

The TD was observed as a single structure in the majority of specimens, as reported in prior anatomical studies (Louzada et al., 2016; Bellier et al., 2020; Parsons and Sargent, 1909; Hematti and Mehran, 2011; Gottlieb and Greenfield, 1956; O'Hagan et al., 2021; Van Pernis, 1949) and corroborated by our findings. The formation of an ampulla at the terminal portion of the TD was infrequent.

Based on the anatomical data presented in this study, we infer that the most likely location to encounter the TD is medial to the BP, near the PN, and external to the interscalene space.

Our study presents two major strengths: (1) to our knowledge, it includes the largest sample size in anatomical studies of the TD (N = 72 specimens), which increases the statistical robustness of our findings; and (2) it was conducted on fresh cadavers, allowing for superior anatomical fidelity and data accuracy.

Limitations

We acknowledge that the sociodemographic characteristics of our study population differ from those of patients who typically present with brachial plexus (BP) injuries and undergo surgical treatment. Our sample consisted predominantly of female specimens, with ages ranging from 23 to 94 years (mean 67 years), with only one specimen younger than 40 years. In contrast, the surgical BP population is generally composed of younger individuals (20–40 years) and is predominantly male.

Measurements were performed only once, the sample size calculation was based on a single parameter, and interobserver agreement was not assessed.

However, there is no evidence in the literature to suggest that vascular position or caliber undergoes clinically relevant changes with advancing age. Age-related variations are largely limited to alterations in vessel wall properties, such as increased fragility or stiffness under specific physiological or pathological conditions, without affecting their anatomical course.

Therefore, we consider that the demographic differences observed in our sample do not represent a significant limitation in terms of anatomical validity and do not compromise the generalizability of our findings or their applicability to surgical practice.

Conclusion

The thoracic duct (TD) in the present study was almost always positioned in the supraclavicular region on the left side. The brachial plexus structures closest to the TD were the phrenic nerve (PN) and the C5 root, with a distance of less than 2 cm in most cases. The TD was consistently located outside the interscalene space and, in the majority of cases, did not intersect with the brachial plexus structures.

The present study contributes to the understanding of the anatomical relationship between the TD and the BP, which may be useful for surgeons and for future clinical research.
