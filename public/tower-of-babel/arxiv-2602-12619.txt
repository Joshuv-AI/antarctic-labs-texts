Asymptotically self-similar graph-like solutions to a multi-dimensional surface diffusion flow equation under contact angle and no-flux boundary conditions In the memory of Professor Louis Nirenberg – a founding father of modern PDE theory
Yoshikazu Giga, Sho Katayama

Abstract
This paper studies Mullins’ model of thermal grooving which consists of a surface diffusion flow equation with contact angle and no-flux boundary conditions. We consider this problem in a multi-dimensional half space and prove that if the slope of the initial data is close to that consistent with the contact angle, then there exists a unique global-in-time solution. In particular, we show the existence of a self-similar solution for a given behavior at the space infinity. We also show that our global solution converges to a self-similar solution as the time tends to infinity if the initial data is asymptotically homogeneous at the space infinity. No assumption on the size of the contact angle is imposed.



1
Introduction

We consider a surface diffusion flow equation of an evolving family
\{\Gamma(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
of hypersurfaces in a half space
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N+1}=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}
, where

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}=\left\{x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\bigm|x_{N}>0\right\},

for
N=1,2,\ldots
i.e.,
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
with a contact angle boundary condition together with the no-flux boundary condition.
It is of the form

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}H

\displaystyle\text{on}\quad\Gamma(t)\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N+1},

(1.1)

\displaystyle\nu\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta\right)

\displaystyle\text{on}\quad\bar{\Gamma}(t)\cap\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N+1},

(1.2)

\displaystyle\nu\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}H

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\quad\bar{\Gamma}(t)\cap\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N+1},

(1.3)

for
t>0
.
Here
H
denotes the (
N
times) mean curvature of
\Gamma(t)
in the direction of the unit normal
\mathbf{n}
of
\Gamma(t)
, and
\Delta_{\Gamma}=\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}
denotes the Laplace Beltrami operator, where
\nabla_{\Gamma}
denotes the surface gradient while
\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}
denotes the surface divergence;
see e.g.
[
G06
]
and
Appendix A
;
V
denotes the normal velocity of
\Gamma(t)
in the direction of
\mathbf{n}
.
The equation (
1.1
) is called the surface diffusion flow equation.
The boundary condition (
1.2
) is called a contact angle condition with angle
\frac{\pi}{2}-\theta
.
Here
\theta
is given constant with
|\theta|<\pi/2
and
\nu=(0,\ldots,0,-1,0)
is the outward unit normal of
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N+1}
and
\nu\cdot\mathbf{n}
denotes the inner product of
\nu
and
\mathbf{n}
.
The condition (
1.3
) is often called the no-flux condition.

The goal of this paper is to study the large time behavior of a solution when
\Gamma(0)
is given as the graph of a function
u_{0}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
(a graph-like surface) as well as the existence of a unique global-in-time classical solution.
Our results are summarized as follows.

(i)

(Global existence)
If
\nabla u_{0}=(D_{1}u_{0},\ldots,D_{N}u_{0})
is close to
(\tan\theta)e_{N}
with
e_{N}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
(at least in
L^{\infty}
sense), there exists a unique global-in-time (still graph-like) solution

\Gamma(t)=\left\{\left(x,u(x,t)\right)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\right\}

to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) such that
\nabla u
is still close to
(\tan\theta)e_{N}
.
(We do not assume that
\tan\theta
is small, unlike
[
AG14
]
and
[
AK25
]
.)

(ii)

(Self-similar solution)
Assume that
u_{0}
is homogeneous in the sense that
u_{0}(\sigma x)=\sigma u_{0}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
and
\sigma>0
and close to
(\tan\theta)e_{N}
.
Then, problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
) possesses a unique self-similar solution

\Gamma(t)=\left\{\left(x,u(x,t)\right)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\right\},

i.e., a solution satisfying
u^{\sigma}=u
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N+1}\times(0,\infty)
for all
\sigma>0
, where
u^{\sigma}(x,t)=\sigma^{-1/4}u(\sigma^{1/4}x,\sigma t)
, such that
\nabla u
is close to
(\tan\theta)e_{N}
with
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}
.
Note that
u=(\tan\theta)e_{N}
is always a trivial self-similar solution.
Depending on the behavior at space infinity (characterized by
u_{0}
), there exist many nontrivial self-similar solutions.
For example, if
\tan\theta
is not zero but small, we may take
u_{0}\equiv 0
to get a non-trivial (spatially bounded) self-similar solution.

(iii)

(Stability)
If initial data is close to a homogeneous function at the space infinity, the solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) converges to a self-similar solution as
t\to\infty
.

The global existence result (i) is known when
\Gamma(t)
is a curve, i.e.,
N=1
, by T. Asai and Y. Kohsaka
[
AK25
]
, where
\tan\theta
is assumed to be small and
u_{0}
is bounded.
This implies (ii) for small
\tan\theta
by taking
u_{0}\equiv 0
.
If the no-flux condition (
1.3
) is linearized around
u_{xx}=0
for
N=1
, results similar to
(i)–(iii) were proved by T. Asai and the first author
[
AG14
]
, when
\tan\theta
is small and
u_{0}
is at least bounded.
A general strategy to construct a self-similar solution used in these papers and also in the present paper is to solve the problem globally in time for homogeneous initial data
u_{0}
i.e.,
u_{0}
satisfies
u_{0}^{\sigma}(x)=u_{0}(x)
for all
\sigma>0
.
This idea was first introduced by the first author and T. Miyakawa
[
GM89
]
for the Navier–Stokes equations and developed for many other equations by T. Cazenave and F. B. Weissler
[
CW98
]
.
For the surface diffusion flow equations, a graph-like self-similar solution on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
was first constructed by H. Koch and T. Lamm
[
KL12
]
.
The stability (iii) of a self-similar solution was already noted in
[
AG14
]
.
However, in order to prove the uniform convergence in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
, i.e., convergence in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N})
, one needs to control behavior near space infinity of
u^{\sigma}
uniformly for
\sigma>1
, for example, by showing the equi-decay property of
u^{\sigma}
, as studied in a book
[
GGS10
]
by the first author with M.-H. Giga and J. Saal for the vorticity equations.
For a graph-like self-similar solution on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
to the surface diffusion flow equation, the stability in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
sense was established by H. Du and N. K. Yip
[
DY23
]
by controlling behavior at the space infinity.
In our present paper, we do not touch stability in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N})
.
We also note that
it is not trivial to say that if
\Gamma(0)
is graph-like, so is
\Gamma(t)
.
In fact, for the surface diffusion flow equation in the whole
\mathbb{R}^{1}
, a graph-like solution may lose the graph-like property in a finite time as shown by C. M. Elliott and S. Maier–Paape
[
EM01
]
.

To explain our strategy, let us write down the equations (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) when
\Gamma(t)
is given as the graph of a function
u=u(x,t)
, i.e.,

\Gamma(t)=\left\{\left(x,u(x,t)\right)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\right\}.

The details are given in
Appendix A
for the reader’s convenience.
Let
\mathbf{n}
be the upward unit normal vector field of
\Gamma(t)
.
Its explicit form is

\mathbf{n}=\frac{(-\nabla u,1)}{\omega},\quad\omega=\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}.

Then the contact angle condition (
1.2
) can be written as

D_{N}u-\omega\sin\theta=0.

If we use the tangential gradient
\nabla^{\prime}=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{N-1})
, then this can be rewritten as

D_{N}u-\gamma\sqrt{1+|\nabla^{\prime}u|^{2}}=0,\quad\gamma=\tan\theta.

(1.4)

We next observe that

\nabla_{\Gamma}H=\left(P(\nabla u)\nabla H,\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla H}{\omega^{2}}\right),\quad P(\nabla u)=I_{N}-\frac{\nabla u\otimes\nabla u}{1+|\nabla u|^{2}},

where
I_{N}
denotes the
N\times N
identity matrix; see Proposition
B.2
.
Then the no-flux condition (
1.3
) can be written as

\left(P(\nabla u)\nabla H\right)_{N}=0\quad\text{or}\quad\left(\omega P(\nabla u)\nabla H\right)_{N}=0,

(1.5)

where
(w)_{N}
denotes the
N
th component of a vector
w
.
Since the upward normal velocity
V
equals

V=\frac{u_{t}}{\omega},\quad u_{t}=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t},

the equation (
1.1
) can be written as

\frac{u_{t}}{\omega}+\frac{1}{\omega}\operatorname{div}\left(\omega P(\nabla u)\nabla H\right)=0

or

u_{t}+\operatorname{div}\left(\omega P(\nabla u)\nabla H\right)=0

(1.6)

if we notice that
\Delta_{\Gamma}f=\omega^{-1}\operatorname{div}\left(\omega P(\nabla u)\nabla f\right)
; see Proposition
B.3
.
The mean curvature
H
can be written as

H=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{P(\nabla u)\nabla^{2}u}{\omega}\right)=\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\omega}\right),

where
\operatorname{tr}
denotes the trace; see Proposition
B.4
.
Gathering (
1.4
)–(
1.6
), we now obtain equations for
u
equivalent to (
1.1
)–(
1.3
).
Its explicit form is

\displaystyle u_{t}+\operatorname{div}\left(\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}P(\nabla u)\nabla\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{P(\nabla u)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\nabla^{2}u\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,T),

(1.7)

\displaystyle D_{N}u-\gamma\left(1+|\nabla^{\prime}u|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,T),

(1.8)

\displaystyle\left(\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}P(\nabla u)\nabla\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{P(\nabla u)}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}}\nabla^{2}u\right)\right)_{N}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in}\quad\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,T).

(1.9)

The corresponding stationary problem is a typical elliptic boundary value problem as remarked in
Appendix A
.
Since its resolvent problem satisfies necessary ellipticity conditions (see
[
Ag
]
,
[
Ta
]
,
[
DHP03
]
,
[
PS
]
), it is possible to solve this problem at least locally in time if one starts from some
L^{p}
-type (
1<p<\infty
) Besov initial data.
However, since a homogeneous function may not belong to such a space, one cannot apply this theory to constructing self-similar solutions.
We would rather work on
L^{\infty}
type spaces so that they accommodate homogeneous functions.
The choice of function spaces is similar to that of
[
AK25
]
,
[
AG14
]
.
However, our way to handle the no-flux boundary condition is quite different from
[
AK25
]
, even for the case
N=1
.

To see our idea, let us consider the one-dimensional case.
The problem (
1.7
)–(
1.9
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle u_{t}+\left(\frac{1}{(1+u_{x}^{2})^{1/2}}\kappa_{x}\right)_{x}

\displaystyle=0,\quad x>0,\quad t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle u_{x}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\tan\theta,\quad t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+u_{x}^{2})^{1/2}}\kappa_{x}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=0,\quad t\in(0,T),

where
\kappa=H=u_{xx}/(1+u_{x}^{2})^{3/2}
and
f_{x}=\partial f/\partial x
.
For a given
\gamma_{0}=\tan\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
we consider
v=u-\gamma_{0}x
.
Then
v
must satisfy

\displaystyle v_{t}+(\beta v_{xxx}-G)_{x}

\displaystyle=0,\quad x>0,\quad t\in(0,T),

(1.10)

\displaystyle v_{x}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\tan\theta-\tan\theta_{0},\quad t\in(0,T),

(1.11)

\displaystyle(\beta v_{xxx}-G)(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=0,\quad t\in(0,T),

(1.12)

where
\beta=1/(1+\gamma_{0}^{2})^{2}
and
G
is a remainder term which is small if
v
is small in
C^{3}
sense.
Note that the remainder terms in the first and the third equations are the same.
We take this advantage to handle the nonlinear term
G
while the paper
[
AK25
]
did not use this special structure so the proof is rather long.
If
\beta>0
is a given constant, we actually have a simple solution formula for a given
G
for (
1.10
)–(
1.12
).
It is of the form

v(t)=ax+e^{-\beta t(\partial_{x}^{4})_{n}}(v_{0}-ax)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{x}e^{-\beta(t-s)(\partial_{x}^{4})_{d}}G(s)\,ds,

where
v|_{t=0}=v_{0}
and
a=\tan\theta-\tan\theta_{0}
;
(\partial_{x}^{4})_{n}
denotes the biharmonic operator with the “Neumann” boundary condition
v_{x}=v_{xxx}=0
while
(\partial_{x}^{4})_{d}
denotes the biharmonic operator with the “Dirichlet” condition
v=v_{xx}=0
.
This is easy to observe.
Indeed, we may assume
a=0
and
v_{0}=0
by subtracting

ax+e^{-\beta t(\partial_{x}^{4})_{n}}(v_{0}-ax)

from
v
.
We notice that

w=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\beta(t-s)(\partial_{x}^{4})_{d}}G(s)\,ds

solves

\displaystyle w_{t}+\beta w_{xxxx}-G

\displaystyle=0,\quad x>0,\quad t>0,

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=0,\quad x=0,\quad t>0,

\displaystyle w_{xx}

\displaystyle=0,\quad x=0,\quad t>0,

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=0,\quad x>0,\quad t=0.

It is easy to see that
v=w_{x}
solves (
1.10
), (
1.11
) with
a=\tan\theta-\tan\theta_{0}=0
and
v|_{t=0}=0
.
To check (
1.12
), we observe that
w=0
at
x=0
implies
w_{t}=0
.
Thus

\beta v_{xxx}-G=\beta w_{xxxx}-G=-w_{t}=0\quad\text{at}\quad x=0,

which is (
1.12
).
Using this observation, we linearize (
1.10
)–(
1.12
) around zero and construct a solution in Hölder space.
The basic idea is the same as in our paper
[
GGK25
]
.
We use classical estimates in
[
So65
]
rather than Sobolev-type estimates used in
[
KL12
]
.
We further give a time-decay rate of
v
by using scaled Hölder spaces introduced in
[
GGK25
]
.

Since we are working in a multi-dimensional setting, there needs to be a control of tangential derivations
\nabla^{\prime}u
.
Although technically involved, the procedure itself is standard.

Compared with our results and results in
[
AK25
]
for
N=1
, there are several differences.
First of all their function spaces are different although both
use Hölder spaces with weights in time.
However, the solution in
[
AK25
]
is constructed in some weighted Hölder spaces where differentiability in time is not guaranteed.
Their solution is a mild solution which is a weak solution.
Since our solution is smooth, the uniqueness in
[
AK25
]
implies that their solution is also smooth.

The boundary problem of the surface diffusion flow equation has been mainly studied in one-dimensional setting, i.e.,
N=1
, except the work of M. Gößwein
[
Go19
]
.
Under the right angle boundary conditions, stability of part of straight lines or circles (which are equilibria) is studied in H. Garcke, K. Ito and Y. Kohsaka
[
GIK05
]
,
[
GIK08
]
, M. Gazwani and J. McCoy
[
GM
]
, and G. Wheeler and V.-M. Wheeler
[
WW
]
.
Other than self-similar solutions constructed in
[
AK25
]
, several special solutions, such as traveling wave solutions are constructed.
For example, T. Kagaya and Y. Kohsaka
[
KK
]
constructed non-convex traveling waves with two end points on
\mathbb{R}^{1}\times\{0\}
with given contact angle and no-flux condition.
They showed that there is a chance that the curve may not be in the half plane
\mathbb{R}^{1}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{+}
even if the curve is in the half plane near end points.
The existence of a non-compact, complete translating solution (soliton) was proved by W. J. Ogden and M. Warren
[
OW
]
.
In both studies, the problem is reduced to analysis of ordinary differential equations of the function associated with the Gauss map.

2
Function spaces and main results

As in
[
GGK25
]
, we introduce scaled Hölder norms.
Let
f
be a (possibly vector or tensor valued) continuous function on
\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)
.
For
\lambda,\mu\in(0,1)
we define Hölder seminorms
of
f
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)
by

\displaystyle\lfloor f]_{C_{x}^{\lambda}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}:=\sup\left\{\frac{\left|f(x,t)-f(y,t)\right|}{|x-y|^{\lambda}}\biggm|x,y\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}},\ x\neq y,\ t\in(a,b)\right\},

\displaystyle[f]_{C_{t}^{\mu}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}:=\sup\left\{\frac{\left|f(x,s)-f(x,t)\right|}{|t-s|^{\mu}}\biggm|s,t\in(a,b),\ s\neq t,\ x\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\right\}.

We set

\displaystyle[f]_{C_{x}^{0}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}

\displaystyle=[f]_{C_{t}^{0}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}:=\|f\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(a,b)\right)}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left|f(x,t)\right|\bigm|x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},\ t\in(a,b)\right\}.

For
\lambda\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we next define space-time Hölder seminorms by

[f]_{C^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\ell+Mm=\lfloor\lambda\rfloor\end{subarray}}[\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}f]_{C_{x}^{\lambda-\lfloor\lambda\rfloor}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}\\
+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\lambda-M<\ell+Mm\leq\lambda\end{subarray}}[\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}f]_{C_{t}^{(\lambda-\ell-Mm)/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}.

Here
\nabla^{\ell}h
denotes an
\ell
-tensor (
\mathbb{R}^{N^{\ell}}
-valued function) consisting of all
\ell
th spatial partial derivatives of
h
and
\lfloor\lambda\rfloor
denotes the largest integer less than
\lambda
.
For
\lambda\geq 0
and
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we define Hölder norms by

\|f\|_{BC^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\ell+Mm\leq\lambda\end{subarray}}\|\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}f\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(a,b)\right)}+[f]_{C^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}.

For a bounded interval
(a,b)
, it is convenient to introduce a scaled Hölder norm
\|f\|_{BC^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}
by

\displaystyle\|f\|^{\prime}_{BC^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\\
\ell+Mm\leq\lambda\end{subarray}}(b-a)^{\ell/M+m}\|\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}f\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(a,b)\right)}

\displaystyle+(b-a)^{\lambda/M}[f]_{C^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)}.

Scaled Hölder norms have the scaling invariant property

\|\bar{f}\|^{\prime}_{BC^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,1)\right)}=\|f\|_{BC^{\lambda,\lambda/M}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(a,b)\right)},

(2.1)

where

\bar{f}(y,s):=f\left((b-a)^{1/M}y,\ a+(b-a)s\right),\quad(y,s)\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,1).

We now introduce a few useful function spaces to handle equation (
1.7
) by taking
M=4
.
For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\mu\in[0,1)
, we set

Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}:=\left\{v\in C\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,\infty)\right)\Bigm|\|v\|_{Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}}<\infty\right\}

with

\|v\|_{Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}}:=\sup_{t>0}t^{-\alpha/4}\|v\|^{\prime}_{BC^{k+\mu,(k+\mu)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(t/2,t)\right)}.

Since we mainly study gradient of a solution
v
to (
1.7
), we introduce

\hat{Z}_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}:=\left\{v\in C\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,\infty)\right)\Bigm|\nabla v\in Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}\right\}.

We shall write unweighted spaces simply by

Z^{k+\mu}:=Z_{0}^{k+\mu},\quad\hat{Z}^{k+\mu}:=\hat{Z}_{0}^{k+\mu}.

Similar spaces for
\mathbb{R}^{N^{k}}
-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
and functions on
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
are defined and denoted by the same notations unless there arises confusion.

For a closed set
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we denote by
\operatorname{Lip}(K)
the space of all Lipschitz continuous functions in
K
.

We are now in a position to state our main results.
We begin with global solvability of (
1.7
)–(
1.9
) with given initial data.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\mu\in(0,1)
and
\gamma_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then there exist constants
\varepsilon^{*}>0
and
\delta^{*}>0
such that if
\gamma=\tan\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and
u_{0}\in\operatorname{Lip}(\overline{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}})
satisfies

|\gamma-\gamma_{0}|<\varepsilon^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\nabla u_{0}-\gamma_{0}e_{N}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})}<\varepsilon^{*},

then the problem (
1.7
)–(
1.9
) with
T=\infty
admits a unique solution
u
with initial data
u_{0}
in the set

\hat{B}_{\delta^{*}}:=\left\{v\in\hat{Z}^{4+\mu}\Bigm|\|\nabla v-\gamma_{0}e_{N}\|_{Z^{4+\mu}}\leq\delta^{*}\right\},

(2.2)

where
e_{N}=(0,\ldots,0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Moreover,

).
∎

4
Linear estimates

We shall derive a few estimates for volume and layer potentials in our rescaled Hölder norms.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\alpha>-4
,
\mu\in(0,1)
.
For
f\in Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}
, set

(V_{n}f)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{n}^{2}}f(s)\,ds

Then
V_{n}
is a well-defined bounded linear operator from
Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}
to
Z_{\alpha+4}^{k+4+\mu}
.
In other words, the estimate

\|V_{n}f\|_{Z_{\alpha+4}^{k+4+\mu}}\leq C\|f\|_{Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}}

(4.1)

holds with some constant
C
depending only on
k
,
\alpha
,
\mu
.
Similarly,
V_{d}
,
V_{v}
are bounded from
Z_{\alpha}^{k+\mu}
to
Z_{\alpha+4}^{k+4+\mu}
, where

V_{d}f=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{d}^{2}}f(s)\,ds,\quad V_{v}G=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{v}^{2}}G(s)\,ds.

Proof.

We fix
\widetilde{t}>0
and let
t\in(4\widetilde{t},8\widetilde{t})
.
Set

I:=\int_{0}^{\widetilde{t}}e^{-(t-s)L_{n}^{2}}f(s)\,ds,\quad I\!I:=\int_{\widetilde{t}}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{n}^{2}}f(s)\,ds.

We first estimate
I
. Since
\tau-s>t/4
for
s\in(0,\widetilde{t})
and
\tau\in(t/2,t)
, it follows from a semigroup estimate for the biharmonic heat semigroup that

\left\|\nabla^{\ell}e^{-(\tau-s)L_{n}^{2}}f(s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N})}\leq C(\tau-s)^{-\ell/4}\left\|f(s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N})}\leq Ct^{-\ell/4}s^{\alpha/4}\|f\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}

for
s\in(0,\widetilde{t})
,
\tau\in(t/2,t)
, and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
(Since
e^{-tL_{n}^{2}}g=b*g_{\mathrm{even}}
, such estimates are reduced to the case of the whole space which are well known; see e.g.
[
KL12
]
,
[
DY23
]
.)
This implies that
I
is well-defined, and the estimate

\|\nabla^{\ell}I\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(t/2,t))}\leq Ct^{(\alpha+4-\ell)/4}\|f\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}

holds for
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Furthermore, since
\partial_{t}I=-L^{2}I
, we see that

\|\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}I\|_{L^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(t/2,t)\right)}\leq Ct^{(\alpha+4-\ell-4m)/4}\|f\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}

for
\ell,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
By interpolation, we obtain

\|I\|_{BC^{k+4+\mu,(k+4+\mu)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(t/2,t)\right)}^{\prime}\leq Ct^{(\alpha+4)/4}\|f\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}.

(4.2)

We next estimate
I\!I
.
We see that
I\!I
solves

\displaystyle\partial_{t}I\!I=-L^{2}I\!I+f\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(\widetilde{t},t),

\displaystyle D_{N}I\!I=0,\quad D_{N}LI\!I=0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(\widetilde{t},t),

\displaystyle I\!I(\cdot,\widetilde{t})=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}.

We use the parabolic Schauder estimate.
However, instead of estimating
I\!I
itself, we estimate its scaled function defined by

\widetilde{I\!I}(y,s):=I\!I\left((t-\widetilde{t})^{1/4}y,\ \widetilde{t}+(t-\widetilde{t})s\right),\ (y,s)\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,1).

This function
\widetilde{I\!I}
satisfies

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\widetilde{I\!I}=-L^{2}\widetilde{I\!I}+\widetilde{f}\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,1)

\displaystyle D_{N}\widetilde{I\!I}=0,\quad D_{N}\widetilde{LI\!I}=0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{I\!I}(\cdot,0)=0\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N},

where

\widetilde{f}(y,s):=(t-\widetilde{t})f\left((t-\widetilde{t})^{1/4}y,\ \widetilde{t}+(t-\widetilde{t})s\right),\quad(y,s)\in\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,1).

(See also
[
GGK25
, Section 2.3]
.)
Using the parabolic Schauder estimate up to the boundary
[
So65
]
(see also Proposition
A.5
), we obtain

\|\widetilde{I\!I}\|^{\prime}_{BC^{k+4+\mu,(k+4+\mu)/4}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,1)\right)}\leq C\|f\|^{\prime}_{BC^{k+\mu,(k+\mu)/4}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,1)\right)}.

This together with the scale invariant property (
2.1
) and
\widetilde{t}\in(t/8,t/4)
implies that

\|I\!I\|_{BC^{k+4+\mu,(k+4+\mu)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(\widetilde{t},t)\right)}^{\prime}\leq Ct\|f\|_{BC^{k+\mu,(k+\mu)/4}\left(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(\widetilde{t},t)\right)}^{\prime}\leq Ct^{(\alpha+4)/4}\|f\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}.

(4.3)

Combining (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we obtain (
4.1
).
This completes the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Lemma 4.2

(Estimate for a layer potential)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\alpha>-4
,
\mu\in(0,1)
.
For
h\in Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}
defined on
\partial\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
, define a function
Sh
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
by

(Sh)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}E(t-s)h(s)\,ds,\quad t>0

where

\left(E(t)h\right)(x):=\int_{\partial\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}L_{x}b_{n}(x,y,t)h(y)\,d\sigma(y),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}.

Then
S
is a well-defined bounded linear operator from
Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}
to
Z^{k+1+\mu}_{\alpha+1}
.
In other words,

\|Sh\|_{Z^{k+1+\mu}_{\alpha+1}}\leq C\|h\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}

(4.4)

with some constant
C
depending only on
k
,
\alpha
,
\mu
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{h}(x,t):=h\left((x^{\prime},0),t\right)
.
It clearly holds that
\|\overline{h}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}\leq\|h\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}
.
It follows from the definition of
E(t)
that

E(t-s)h(s)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}D_{y_{N}}L_{x}b_{n}(x,y,t-s)\overline{h}(y,s)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}}D_{x_{N}}L_{x}b_{d}(x,y,t-s)\overline{h}(y,s)\,dy.

This together with the semigroup estimate that

\left\|E(t-s)h(s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})}\leq C(t-s)^{-3/4}\left\|\overline{h}(s)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+})}\leq C(t-s)^{-3/4}s^{\alpha}\|h\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha}}.

Thus
Sh
is well-defined and

Sh(t)=-D_{N}L\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{d}^{2}}\overline{h}(s)\,ds.

(4.5)

Furthermore, the estimate (
4.1
) implies (
4.4
).
∎

5
Estimates of nonlinear terms

In Section
3
, we reduced the problem (
1.7
)–(
1.9
) with initial data
v_{0}
to an integral equation

\displaystyle v(t)=e^{-tL_{n}^{2}}v_{0}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}E(t-s)C\left[\nabla v(s)\right]\,ds

(5.1)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{v}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{A}[\nabla v]\nabla^{3}v-F[\nabla v]\right)(s)\,ds.

We do not solve this equation directly.
Instead we shall solve the equation for its gradient
w=\nabla v
which is obtained by differentiating (
5.1
).
Its explicit form is

\displaystyle w(t)=e^{-tL_{v}^{2}}\nabla v_{0}

\displaystyle+\nabla\int_{0}^{t}E(t-s)C\left[w(s)\right]\,ds

\displaystyle-\nabla\operatorname{div}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)L_{v}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{A}[w]\nabla^{2}w-F[w]\right)(s)\,ds

(5.2)

If we set

then equation (

In this section we estimate

which is a contribution from nonlinear terms.
Our goal in this section
is to prove that

is strict contraction in a small ball of
Z^{4+\mu}
.
In other words the term is regarded as a perturbation term.

Theorem 5.1
.

Assume that
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Then the operator
\Lambda
in (
5.3
) is a well-defined nonlinear operator from

to
Z^{4+\mu}

Moreover, there exists a constant

for all

B_{\delta}^{k+\mu}:=\left\{w\in Z^{k+\mu}\Bigm|\|w\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\leq\delta\right\}.

We recall a couple of basic estimates for products and composite functions.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

,

, and

\displaystyle\|g_{1}\ldots g_{m}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{m}}}\leq C\|g_{1}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{1}}}\ldots\|g_{m}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{m}}},

&\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|g_{1}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{1}}}\ldots\|g_{i-1}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{i-1}}}\|g_{i}-h_{i}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{i}}}\|h_{i+1}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{i+1}}}\ldots\|h_{m}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}_{\alpha_{m}}}\end{aligned}

(

,

.

\begin{gathered}\|A(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\leq C,\\
\|A(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})-A(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\leq C\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}\|g_{i}-h_{i}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\end{gathered}

(5.5)

for all
g_{i},h_{i}\in B^{k+\mu}_{R}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

These two propositions can be proved as in
[
GGK25
, Proposition 2.1]
so we omit the proofs.

As applications of Propositions
5.2
and
5.3

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\mu\in(0,1)
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Define
B^{k+\mu}_{\delta}:=\{w\in Z^{k+\mu}:\|w\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\leq\delta\}

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{A}[w]\right\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\leq C\delta^{2},\quad\left\|\mathbb{A}[w_{1}]-\mathbb{A}[w_{2}]\right\|_{Z^{k+\mu}}\leq C\delta\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}},

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left\|F[w]\right\|_{Z^{k-1+\mu}_{-2}}\leq C\delta^{2},\quad\left\|F[w_{1}]-F[w_{2}]\right\|_{Z^{k-1+\mu}_{-2}}\leq C\delta\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{Z^{k+\mu}},

(5.7)

for all

,

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|\mathbb{A}[w_{1}]\right\|_{Z^{2+\mu}}\left\|\nabla^{2}(w_{1}-w_{2})\right\|_{Z^{2+\mu}_{-2}}+C\left\|\mathbb{A}[w_{1}]-\mathbb{A}[w_{2}]\right\|_{Z^{2+\mu}}\|\nabla^{2}w_{2}\|_{Z^{2+\mu}_{-2}}

Combining (

Proof of Corollary

and

.
Moreover, for

we observe that

\displaystyle\left|u^{\sigma}(x,s)-u^{\sigma}(y,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla u^{\sigma}\|_{L^{\infty}}|x-y|+\int_{s}^{t}\left|u_{t}^{\sigma}(x,\tau)\right|\,d\tau

\displaystyle\leq C\left(|x-y|+(t-s)^{1/4}\right),

\displaystyle\left|u^{\sigma}(x,t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|u^{\sigma}(0,0)\right|+C\left(|x|+t^{1/4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+|x|+t^{1/4}\right),

for

,

,
\sigma>1
with

independent of

,
y
,

s
,
\sigma
.

By the Ascoli–Arzelá theorem with a diagonal argument, for any sequence

, there exists a subsequence

such that

converges to some

) in the topology of

;
in other words

uniformly in any compact set of
\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times[0,\infty)
, and
\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}u^{\sigma_{n_{j}}}\to\nabla^{\ell}\partial_{t}^{m}U
for any conpact set of
\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times|0,\infty)
for all

Thus

must solve (

)–(
1.9
) and

By the assumption (

.
Since the limit is independent of a choice of a subsequence, the convergence

In other words,

u^{\sigma}\to u^{\gamma,\psi}\quad\text{as}\quad\sigma\to 0

in the topology of

C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times(0,\infty)\right)\cap C_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(\overline{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}}\times[0,\infty)\right).

The proof is now complete.

x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Although these formulas are well-known, we give their proofs for the reader’s convenience.

We first recall a definition of
\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}
and
\nabla_{\Gamma}
.
Let
\bar{\nabla}
denote the gradient in

\mathbb{R}^{N+1}=\left\{(x,y)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},y\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

i.e.,

\bar{\nabla}=\left(\nabla,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{N},D_{N+1}).

Let
\bar{X}
be a
C^{1}
vector field on
\Gamma
of the form
\bar{X}=(X,Y)
with
X\in C^{1}(\Gamma)^{N}
,
Y\in C^{1}(\Gamma)
.
Let
f\in C^{1}(\Gamma)
.
Let
\mathbf{n}
be a unit normal vector field of
\Gamma
.
Then, we define

\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\bar{X}:=\operatorname{tr}\left((I_{N+1}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})\bar{\nabla}\bar{X}\right),\quad\nabla_{\Gamma}f:=(I_{N+1}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n})\bar{\nabla}f.

Here
\bar{X}
and
f
are extended in a tubular neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\Gamma
so that it is
C^{1}
in
\mathcal{N}
.
As well known (see e.g.
[
G06
]
), the values
\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\bar{X}
and
\nabla_{\Gamma}f
are independent of the choice of extensions.
In the case when
\Gamma
is the graph of a function, we may assume that
\bar{X}
and
f
are independent of
y
, i.e.,
\partial_{y}\bar{X}=0
,
\partial_{y}f=0
.
The operator
I_{N+1}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
is the orthogonal projection to the tangent plane of
\Gamma
at
\left(x,u(x)\right)
.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
If
\Gamma
is given as the graph of a function
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, then

(\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\bar{X})\left(x,u(x)\right)=\omega^{-1}(x)\left(\operatorname{div}(\omega X)\right)(x)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega,\quad\omega=\left(1+|\nabla u|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

provided that
\bar{X}
is tangential to
\Gamma
, i.e.,
\bar{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
, where
\bar{X}=(X,Y)
is a
C^{1}
vector field on
\Gamma
parametrized by
x\in\Omega
.
Here
\operatorname{div}
denotes the divergence in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{n}
denote the upward unit normal vector field, i.e.,

\mathbf{n}=\frac{\mathbf{m}}{\omega},\quad\mathbf{m}=(-\nabla u,1).

Since
\bar{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}X^{i}n_{i}
with
X^{N+1}=Y
is zero, we observe
that
D_{j}(\bar{X}\cdot\mathbf{m})=0
so that

\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}m_{i}D_{j}X^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}(-D_{j}m_{i})X^{i}\quad\text{(}1\leq j\leq N+1\text{)},

where
D_{N+1}=\partial/\partial y
and
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{N+1})
.
Since
m_{N+1}=1
is a constant, we see that

\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}m_{i}D_{j}X^{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(-D_{j}m_{i})X^{i}.

Since
\bar{X}
is independent of
x_{N+1}=y
, we now conclude
that

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\bar{\nabla}\bar{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N+1}\omega^{-2}m_{i}m_{j}D_{j}X^{i}=\omega^{-2}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}m_{j}(-D_{j}m_{i})X^{i}

\displaystyle=\omega^{-2}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}m_{j}(-D_{i}m_{j})X^{i}.

Here we note that
m_{j}=-D_{j}u
so that
D_{i}m_{j}=D_{j}m_{i}
.
Since
\nabla\omega=\omega^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}m_{j}\nabla m_{j}
, we now observe that

\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\bar{\nabla}\bar{X})=-\omega^{-1}\nabla\omega\cdot X.

Thus

\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\bar{X}=\operatorname{div}X+\omega^{-1}\nabla\omega\cdot X=\omega^{-1}\operatorname{div}(\omega X).

∎

Let us now calculate
\nabla_{\Gamma}f
when
\Gamma
is given as the graph of a
C^{1}
function
u
.
We recall the
N\times N
matrix

P(\nabla u)=I_{N}-\frac{\nabla u\otimes\nabla u}{\omega^{2}}.

Then the operator
I_{N+1}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}
is of the form

I_{N+1}-\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}=\omega^{-2}\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{2}P(\nabla u)&\nabla u\\
\nabla u^{T}&|\nabla u|^{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
w^{T}
denotes the transpose of a vector
w
.
We thus obtain

Proposition B.2
.

If
\Gamma
is given as the graph of
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
, then

(\nabla_{\Gamma}f)\left(x,u(x)\right)=\left(P(\nabla u)\nabla f,\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla f}{\omega^{2}}\right)(x)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega,

where
f
is a
C^{1}
function on
\Gamma
parametrized by
x\in\Omega
.

Combining Propositions
B.1
and
B.2
, we obtain a well-known formula for the Laplace-Beltrami operator.

Proposition B.3
.

Assume that
\Gamma
is given as the graph of
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Let
f
be a
C^{2}
function on
\Gamma
parametrized by
x\in\Omega
.
Then

\Delta_{\Gamma}f:=(\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}f)\left(x,u(x)\right)=\left(\omega^{-1}\operatorname{div}\left(\omega P(\nabla u)\nabla f\right)\right)(x)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega.

If
\bar{X}
is not tangential to
\Gamma
, then
\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\bar{X}
is no longer equal to
\omega^{-1}\operatorname{div}(\omega X)
.
A typical example is the mean curvature in the direction of
\mathbf{n}
, i.e.,

H=-\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{n}.

Proposition B.4
.

If
\Gamma
is given as the graph of
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, then

H\left(x,u(x)\right)=\left(\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\omega}\right)\right)(x)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{P(\nabla u)\nabla^{2}u}{\omega}\right)(x)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\Omega.

Proof.

If
\bar{X}=\mathbf{n}
and
\mathbf{n}
is extended to
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
so that
D_{N+1}\mathbf{n}=0
, then

\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{n}\otimes\mathbf{n}\bar{X})=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N+1}n_{i}n_{j}D_{j}n_{i}=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N+1}n_{j}\frac{1}{2}D_{j}|n_{i}|^{2}=0

since
|\mathbf{n}|=1
, where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{N+1})
.
Thus

H=-\operatorname{div}_{\Gamma}\mathbf{n}=-\operatorname{div}\mathbf{n}=-\operatorname{div}\left(-\frac{\nabla u}{\omega}\right)=\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\omega}\right).

A direct calcuration shows that

\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\omega}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{P(\nabla u)\nabla^{2}u}{\omega}\right)

so the proof is now complete.
∎
