Particle physics models of interacting bosonic dark energy and fermionic dark matter in Einstein scalar Gauss-Bonnet gravity
Simran Arora, Saddam Hussain, Benjamin Rose, Anzhong Wang

Abstract
We explore a cosmological framework in which a Gauss-Bonnet (GB) coupled scalar field, acting as dark energy, interacts with a fermionic dark matter field through a coupling obtained from the point of view of particle physics. This setup is inspired by string/M-theory, and two representative scalar field potentials are investigated: exponential and power-law. A distinctive feature of the GB-coupled models is their potential to alter the propagation speed of gravitational waves (GWs), a property with significant implications in light of recent multi-messenger astrophysical observations. To account for this, we analyze models under two scenarios: one where the GW speed differs from that of light and the other where they are equal, but all consistent with current observational constraints. The dynamical evolution of the system is investigated by reformulating the field equations into an autonomous dynamical system, enabling a detailed analysis of the Universe’s long-term behavior, including the radiation-, matter- and dark energy-dominated epochs. We constrain the model parameters using a broad set of recent observational data, including mock high-redshift measurements from the Roman Space Telescope. Our findings indicate that both potentials yield cosmologies that are in excellent agreement with current data, closely tracking the expansion history predicted by the standard Λ \Lambda CDM model, while still allowing room for subtle deviations that could be tested by future observations. Keywords: Dark Matter, Dark Energy, Interaction, Cosmological Observations



I
Introduction

The discovery of the accelerated expansion of the Universe at late times
Riess et al. 1998
;
Perlmutter et al. 1999
has significantly challenged our understanding of the fundamental physical laws and fields that govern cosmic evolution. Within the framework of a statistically homogeneous and isotropic universe, this acceleration is most commonly attributed to a hypothetical fluid with negative pressure, referred to as dark energy, typically modeled by a positive cosmological constant
\Lambda
, characterized by an equation of state
w_{\Lambda}=-1
. However, theoretical challenges associated with the cosmological constant, including its striking discrepancy with predictions from quantum field theory
Weinberg 1989
and the requirement of finely tuned initial conditions
Zlatev et al. 1999
, have motivated the search for more fundamental mechanisms underlying the observed late-time acceleration
Joyce et al. 2015
. Despite these efforts, no alternative model has gained widespread acceptance than the
\Lambda
CDM framework, which still remains the prevailing cosmological paradigm, assuming the validity of the cosmological constant hypothesis.

Over the past decade, increasingly precise cosmological observations have revealed significant tensions within the
\Lambda
CDM framework. Most notably, there exists a greater than
5\sigma
discrepancy between the Hubble constant inferred from cosmic microwave background (CMB) measurements by the
Planck
satellite
Aghanim et al. 2020
and the local distance-ladder estimations reported by the SH0ES collaboration
Riess et al. 2022
. There also exist tensions between the galactic scale matter clustering inferred from Planck and the one derived from galaxy surveys and weak gravitational lensing observations
Di Valentino et al. 2021a
;
Amon et al. 2022
;
Abbott et al. 2022
. These persistent discrepancies strongly suggest that the foundational assumptions of the standard cosmological model may require revision or replacement. In response, a wide range of alternative models have been proposed to explain late-time acceleration.
Recently, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration reported a
3.9\sigma
indication of dynamical dark energy in its first data release (DR1), which was further strengthened to
4.2\sigma
in the second data release (DR2)
Adame et al. 2025
;
Abdul Karim et al. 2025
. This result has generated significant interest and sparked active discussions regarding potential signatures of new physics beyond the standard
\Lambda
CDM paradigm.

A wide range of alternative models have been proposed to account for the late-time acceleration of the Universe. These include potential-driven scalar field models, commonly referred to as quintessence
Peebles and Ratra 2003
;
Berghaus et al. 2024
;
Kritpetch et al. 2025
, and kinetically driven frameworks such as
k
-essence
Hussain et al. 2025
;
Armendariz-Picon et al. 2001
;
Scherrer 2004
;
Hussain et al. 2023a
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2023
. Other notable candidates involve non-canonical fields, including tachyonic and ghost fields
Bagla et al. 2003
;
Khoeini-Moghaddam et al. 2023
;
Cai et al. 2011
;
Hussain et al. 2023b
. In addition, modified gravity theories
Sotiriou and Faraoni 2010
;
Nojiri and Odintsov 2006
;
Sokoliuk et al. 2023
;
Gorji et al. 2021
;
De Felice et al. 2020
;
Arora et al. 2022
;
Gadbail et al. 2024
and scalar–tensor formulations
Kobayashi 2019
;
Elizalde et al. 2024
;
Odintsov et al. 2025
;
Hussain et al. 2023c
offer alternatives, extending the geometric foundations of General Relativity (GR) to explain the observed cosmic acceleration without invoking exotic matter components. Among the wide landscape of modified gravity theories, the Einstein–scalar-Gauss–Bonnet (EsGB) gravity occupies a particularly privileged position. Initially proposed by Lanczos
Lanczos 1938
and subsequently generalized by Lovelock
Lovelock 1971
, the theory is uniquely distinguished by its ability to modify GR without introducing additional fundamental fields.
More attractively, EsGB gravity can be obtained as an effective low-energy theory from string/M-theory
Gross and Sloan 1987
;
Bento and Bertolami 1996
;
Ferrara et al. 1996
;
Antoniadis et al. 1997
, but still
maintains the field equations as second-order, a necessary condition for avoiding the Ostrogradsky instability
Ostrogradsky 1850
that typically plagues higher-derivative theories. For more details of the latter, see, for example,
Wang 2017
and references therein.

The EsGB theory extends the Einstein–Hilbert action by introducing a scalar field coupled to the Gauss–Bonnet term, a particular combination of quadratic curvature invariants. This term emerges naturally in the low-energy effective action of string theory and captures higher-curvature corrections without introducing ghost instabilities
Nojiri et al. 2005
;
Tsujikawa and Sami 2007
;
Cognola et al. 2007
. Crucially, the Gauss-Bonnet combination is the unique curvature-squared correction that preserves the second-order nature of the field equations, thereby maintaining the predictive power and consistency of the theory
Odintsov and Oikonomou 2019
;
Antoniou et al. 2018
;
Julié and Berti 2019
;
East and Pretorius 2022
;
Millano et al. 2023
;
Granda and Jimenez 2014
;
Zhang et al. 2020
. From both theoretical and phenomenological perspectives, EsGB gravity offers a compelling framework to explain cosmic acceleration through dynamical scalar-curvature couplings, rather than a static cosmological constant. Henceforth, these models remain under active investigations, with ongoing efforts aimed at constraining them via astrophysical observations and assessing their consistency with cosmological data.

In this work, we explore a framework in which both dark matter and dark energy are described as fields from the point of view of particle physics rather than as fluids. This framework allows us to write down naturally the interactions between the two dark components. Interacting models between the two dark components have garnered great interest for their potential to address the Hubble tension and the coincidence problem
Wang et al. 2024
;
Benisty et al. 2024
;
Di Valentino et al. 2020
;
Di Valentino et al. 2021b
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Wang and Wang 2014
. In particular, in this paper we consider a framework,
where dark energy is modeled by the GB scalar field, while dark matter by a fermionic field. The latter provides a particle-based description of dark matter and has been shown to drive cosmic expansion, with several studies demonstrating their viability in both early- and late-time cosmological evolution
Chakraborty et al. 2025
;
Samojeden et al. 2010
;
Ribas et al. 2016
;
Benisty 2023
;
Grams et al. 2014
;
Farrar and Peebles 2004
;
Bean et al. 2008
;
Feng 2010
.

In our setup, a non-gravitational interaction between the scalar and fermionic fields is introduced at the action level via the term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}(\phi,\psi)
, where
\phi
and
\psi
denote respectively the GB couple scalar field and the fermionic scalar field. This term facilitates a direct exchange of energy and momentum between the dark components, leading to modified equations of motion that depart from the standard non-interacting scenario significantly.
Crucially, this formulation naturally constrains the form of the interaction, avoiding the arbitrariness often found in purely phenomenological models. In particular, this stands in contrast to fluid-based interacting dark sector approaches, where interactions are typically imposed through an ad hoc source term in the continuity equations
Wang and Wang 2014
;
Benisty et al. 2024
;
Bolotin et al. 2014
;
Chatterjee et al. 2021
;
Hussain 2025
.

Furthermore, due to the coupling between the scalar field and the Gauss-Bonnet term, the theory naturally introduces higher-curvature corrections that modify the gravitational dynamics. A key prediction of such models is a deviation in the propagation speed of gravitational waves (GWs), which has been tightly constrained by recent multi-messenger observations
Abbott et al. 2017
;
Terente Díaz et al. 2023
;
Ezquiaga and Zumalacárregui 2017
. In particular, the joint detection of GWs and gamma-ray bursts from the binary neutron star merger GW170817 imposes a stringent bound on the GW speed

\left|c_{T}^{2}-1\right|<5\times 10^{-16},

where
c_{T}
denotes the GW speed in natural units. In light of this, we consider two scenarios in our analysis: one with
c_{T}^{2}\neq 1
and the other with
c_{T}^{2}=1
, but in both scenarios we assume that the above observational constraint is satisfied.

The dynamics of the coupled system are investigated using dynamical systems techniques, focusing on models with exponential and power-law scalar potentials. Within the spatially flat FLRW background, we derive the equations of motion and relevant cosmological quantities.
We analyze the dynamical behavior of the interacting models and perform a numerical stability analysis. To constrain the model parameters, we employ a comprehensive set of observational data, including model-independent Hubble parameter measurements, Type Ia Supernovae (Pantheon+ and DES5YR), DESI Baryon Acoustic Oscillation data (DR2), and mock data from the Roman Space Telescope.

outlines the dynamics of the interacting models. Sec.
IV
describes the observational data sets used for parameter constraints. Sec.
V
discusses the results, and finally Sec.
VI

II
Coupled Einstein-scalar-Gauss-Bonnet gravity

In this section, let us consider a framework in which dark energy and dark matter are described respectively by a bosonic and a fermionic scalar field, interacting through an effective Lagrangian.

II.1
General Framework

We formulate this scenario within the EsGB gravity framework
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Cicoli et al. 2024
;
Fernandes et al. 2022
, described by the action

S_{\text{EsGB}}=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\left[\frac{R}{2\kappa^{2}}+f(\phi)\mathcal{G}+\mathcal{L}_{\phi}+\mathcal{L}_{\psi}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}+\mathcal{L}_{m}\right],

(1)

where
\kappa\equiv 8\pi G/c^{4}
,
c
is the speed of light in vacuum, and
g\equiv\det(g_{\mu\nu})
. The Ricci scalar is denoted by
R
, and the Gauss-Bonnet invariant is defined as:

\mathcal{G}=R^{2}-4R_{\mu\nu}R^{\mu\nu}+R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}.

(2)

Throughout this work, we adopt natural units by setting
\kappa=1
. The scalar field
\phi
, associated with dark energy, couples non-minimally to the GB term via a general coupling function
f(\phi)
. All other components of the Universe are assumed to interact minimally with the metric.

Building on this framework, we incorporate the dark matter sector as a non-relativistic Dirac fermion, represented by the field
\psi
. In addition to its gravitational interaction with other components of matter fields in the Universe, the fermion couples directly to the scalar field
\phi
through a Yukawa-type interaction, introduced via the interaction Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}(\phi,\psi)

Micheletti 2012
;
Micheletti 2012
;
Ribas et al. 2016
;
McDonough et al. 2022
. The complete dynamics are governed by the following individual Lagrangian components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla^{\mu}\phi-V(\phi),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\left[\overline{\psi}\Gamma^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\psi-(\nabla_{\mu}\overline{\psi})\Gamma^{\mu}\psi\right]-W(\overline{\psi},\psi),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=-M(\phi)\overline{\psi}\psi,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\text{m}}(g_{\mu\nu},\psi_{m}),

(3)

where
V(\phi)
is the scalar potential,
M(\phi)
is a field-dependent mass function mediating the coupling between
\phi
and
\psi
, and
W(\overline{\psi},\psi)
accounts for possible fermionic self-interactions. Here,
\Gamma^{\mu}=e^{\mu}{}_{a}\gamma^{a}
are the curved-space gamma matrices, constructed from the vierbeins
e^{\mu}{}_{a}
and the flat-space Dirac matrices
\gamma^{a}
, satisfying
\{\Gamma^{\mu},\Gamma^{\nu}\}=2g^{\mu\nu}
, and
\overline{\psi}\equiv\psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}
denotes the Dirac adjoint spinor. The covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mu}\psi
includes the spin connection to ensure general covariance. The remaining matter fields, collectively denoted by
\psi_{m}
, are assumed to couple minimally to the metric only.

The field equations governing the dynamics of the system are derived by varying the total action with respect to the metric, the fermionic (spinor) field and the scalar field, respectively. This yields the following set of coupled equations
Benisty 2023
;
Lepe et al. 2011
:

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R=T^{GB}_{\mu\nu}+T^{\phi}_{\mu\nu}+T^{\psi}_{\mu\nu}+T_{\mu\nu}^{(\phi,\psi)}+T_{\mu\nu},

(4)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi=\frac{dV}{d\phi}-\alpha\frac{df}{d\phi}{\cal G}+\frac{dM(\phi)}{d\phi}\overline{\psi}\psi-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{m}}}{\partial\phi},

(5)

\displaystyle i\,\Gamma^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\psi-M(\phi)\psi-\frac{\partial W}{\partial\overline{\psi}}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{m}}}{\partial\overline{\psi}}=0,

(6)

\displaystyle i(\nabla_{\mu}\overline{\psi})\Gamma^{\mu}+M(\phi)\overline{\psi}+\frac{\partial W}{\partial\psi}-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\text{m}}}{\partial\psi}=0,

(7)

where
\nabla^{2}\equiv g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}
, and the corresponding energy momentum tensors are

\displaystyle T^{GB}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}f\right)R-2g_{\mu\nu}\left(\nabla_{\rho}\nabla^{\rho}f\right)R-4\left(\nabla^{\rho}\nabla_{\nu}f\right)R_{\mu\rho}

\displaystyle-4\left(\nabla^{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}f\right)R_{\nu\rho}+4\left(\nabla^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}f\right)R_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle+4g_{\mu\nu}\left(\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\sigma}f\right)R_{\rho\sigma}-4\left(\nabla^{\rho}\nabla^{\sigma}f\right)R_{\mu\rho\nu\sigma}\ ,

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\phi\nabla_{\nu}\phi-g_{\mu\nu}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\alpha}\phi\nabla_{\alpha}\phi+V(\phi)\bigg],

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{i}{4}\left[(\nabla_{\nu}\overline{\psi})\Gamma_{\mu}\psi+(\nabla_{\mu}\overline{\psi})\Gamma_{\nu}\psi-\overline{\psi}\Gamma_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\psi-\overline{\psi}\Gamma_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\psi\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\Big[(\nabla_{\lambda}\overline{\psi})\Gamma^{\lambda}\,\psi-\overline{\psi}\,\Gamma^{\lambda}\,\nabla_{\lambda}\psi\Big]-g_{\mu\nu}W(\overline{\psi},\psi),

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}^{(\phi,\psi)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-g_{\mu\nu}M(\phi)\overline{\psi}\psi,

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_{\text{m}}}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}}.

(8)

II.2
Homogeneous and Isotropic Background

In the following analysis, we adopt a spatially flat Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) universe, described by the line element

ds^{2}=dt^{2}-a(t)^{2}\left(dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}\right),

(9)

where
a(t)
is the cosmic scale factor, and the Gauss–Bonnet invariant simplifies to:

\mathcal{G}=24H^{2}(\dot{H}+H^{2}),

(10)

where
H=\dot{a}/a
denotes the Hubble parameter. The other matter fields are assumed to be described by

\displaystyle T_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{m}\,u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+p_{m}\,(g_{\mu\nu}+u_{\mu}u_{\nu}),

(11)

where
u^{\mu}=(1,\,\vec{0})
denotes the four-velocity of the fluid in the comoving frame, satisfying the condition
u^{\mu}u_{\mu}=-1
.
\rho_{m}
and
p_{m}
represent the energy density and pressure of the background fluid, respectively.

Then, the field equations reduce to the following set of coupled differential equations:

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+\frac{dV(\phi)}{d\phi}-\frac{df(\phi)}{d\phi}G+\frac{dM(\phi)}{d\phi}\overline{\psi}\psi=0,

(12)

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}+\frac{3}{2}H\psi+i\gamma^{0}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\overline{\psi}}+iM(\phi)\gamma^{0}\psi=0,

(13)

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{\psi}}+\frac{3}{2}H\overline{\psi}-i\frac{\partial W}{\partial\psi}-iM(\phi)\overline{\psi}\gamma^{0}=0.

(14)

The corresponding energy densities of the scalar and fermionic fields are defined by:

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+V(\phi),

(15)

\displaystyle p_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}-V(\phi),

(16)

\displaystyle\rho_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(\psi,\overline{\psi})+M(\phi)\psi\overline{\psi},

(17)

\displaystyle p_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial\psi}\frac{\psi}{2}+\frac{\partial W}{\partial\overline{\psi}}\frac{\overline{\psi}}{2}-W(\psi,\overline{\psi}).

(18)

The first Friedmann equation takes the form:

3H^{2}=\rho_{\phi}+\rho_{\psi}-24\dot{f}(\phi)H^{3}\,+\rho_{m},

(19)

where
\dot{f}(\phi)
represents the derivative of
f
with respect to cosmic time
t
and
\rho_{m}
is the energy density of pressureless baryonic matter. The evolution equations for the energy densities of the scalar and fermionic fields, the
baryonic matter and radiation are

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\phi}+3H(\rho_{\phi}+p_{\phi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\phi)\dot{\phi}G-Q\,,

(20)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\psi}+3H(\rho_{\psi}+p_{\psi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Q\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{m}+3H\rho_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(22)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{r}+4H\rho_{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(23)

where
f^{\prime}(\phi)\equiv df(\phi)/d\phi
and
Q\equiv M^{\prime}(\phi)\,\dot{\phi}\,\overline{\psi}\psi
denotes the interaction term between the two dark components.

In this framework, the interaction results in a dynamical exchange of energy within the dark sector,
which modifies the standard conservation equations. The sign of
Q
determines the direction of energy transfer:
Q>0
corresponds to energy flowing from dark energy to dark matter, while
Q<0
implies the reverse
Bolotin et al. 2014
. Such couplings naturally happen in string/M-theory and impose constraints on possible forms of interactions, including those chosen at the phenomenological level.

Equation (
20
) can be reformulated to include an effective pressure term, yielding:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho_{\phi}}+3H(\rho_{\phi}+p^{\phi}_{\text{eff}})=-Q\,,

(24)

where the effective pressure is defined as:

\displaystyle p^{\phi}_{\text{eff}}\equiv p_{\phi}-8H\dot{\phi}(\dot{H}+H^{2})f^{\prime}(\phi)\,.

(25)

Based on these definitions, the equations of state (EoS) parameters for the scalar and fermionic fields are given by:

\displaystyle w_{\phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p^{\phi}_{\text{eff}}}{\rho_{\phi}}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}-V(\phi)-8H\dot{\phi}(\dot{H}+H^{2})f^{\prime}(\phi)}{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+V(\phi)},~~~~~~

(26)

\displaystyle w_{\psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\psi}}{\rho_{\psi}}=\frac{\frac{\psi}{2}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\psi}+\frac{\overline{\psi}}{2}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\overline{\psi}}-W(\psi,\overline{\psi})}{W(\psi,\overline{\psi})+M(\phi)\psi\overline{\psi}},

(27)

\displaystyle w_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-1-\frac{2\dot{H}}{3H^{2}}.

(28)

The equation of state (EoS) parameter quantifies the ratio of pressure to energy density for a cosmological component and plays a central role in determining the Universe’s expansion dynamics. The total EoS parameter,
w_{\text{tot}}
, reflects the combined effect of all fields on cosmic evolution. An accelerating Universe requires
-1\leq w<-\tfrac{1}{3}
, while
w\simeq 0
characterizes a matter-dominated era. Values
w<-1
correspond to the phantom regime.

II.3
Fermionic Field as Dark Matter

In the context of dark matter, fermionic fields provide important and viable models. In particular, when coupled appropriately to a bosonic scalar field, a fermionic field can dynamically behave as a pressureless matter component
Stiele et al. 2010
;
Micheletti 2010
;
Lepe et al. 2011
. The dynamical equations for the fermionic field
\psi
and its adjoint
\overline{\psi}
, in the presence of a coupling to a bosonic scalar field, are given by (as discussed in the previous section):

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}+\frac{3}{2}H\psi+i\gamma^{0}\frac{\partial W}{\partial\overline{\psi}}+iM(\phi)\gamma^{0}\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(29)

\displaystyle\dot{\overline{\psi}}+\frac{3}{2}H\overline{\psi}-i\frac{\partial W}{\partial\psi}\gamma^{0}-iM(\phi)\overline{\psi}\gamma^{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(30)

where we assume that there is no direct interaction between
\psi
and baryonic matter and radiation, except the gravitational ones.

To investigate the energy evolution of the fermionic field, we analyze the time evolution of the bilinear quantity
\psi\overline{\psi}
. Taking its time derivative and combining the fermionic field equations yields:

\frac{d}{dt}(\psi\overline{\psi})+3H\psi\overline{\psi}=i\gamma^{0}\left(\frac{\partial W}{\partial\psi}\psi-\frac{\partial W}{\partial\overline{\psi}}\overline{\psi}\right).

(31)

When the self-interaction potential takes the form
W(\psi,\overline{\psi})\sim(\psi\overline{\psi})^{n}
, the right-hand side vanishes due to the symmetry of the derivatives, resulting in:

\frac{d}{dt}(\psi\overline{\psi})+3H\psi\overline{\psi}=0\,.

(32)

This immediately yields
\psi\overline{\psi}\propto a^{-3}
. Then, the energy density and equation of state (EoS) parameter of the fermionic field scale as
\rho_{\psi}\propto a^{-3n}
and
w_{\psi}=n-1
, respectively. Thus, different choices of
n
interpolate between various cosmological behaviors. In particular,
n=1
produces the scaling behavior of non-relativistic pressureless matter and reinforces the interpretation of the fermionic bilinear as an effective dark matter component. On the other hand,
n>1
yields stiffer fluids (e.g., when
n=2
we have
w_{\psi}=1
), while
n<2/3
leads to dark energy-like dynamics with
w_{\psi}<-1/3
.

This framework not only provides a rich structure for modeling the dark sector but also offers a natural interpretation of fermionic fields as constituents of dark matter. By coupling a fermionic field
\psi
to a scalar sector—typically through a self-interaction potential
W(\psi,\overline{\psi})
and a mass interaction term
M(\phi)
, one introduces a mechanism by which dark matter can evolve dynamically over cosmic time. Notably, the interaction term renders the dark matter mass dependent on the quintessence field
\phi
. As a result, the non-relativistic dark matter energy density does not redshift strictly as
a^{-3}
, as in the case of ordinary matter, but instead evolves proportionally to
M(\phi)/a^{3}
.

III
Formulation of dynamical system

To investigate the dynamics of the non-minimally coupled system, we begin by introducing the following set of dimensionless dynamical variables:

\displaystyle x=\dfrac{\dot{\phi}}{\sqrt{6}H},\quad y=\dfrac{\sqrt{V}}{\sqrt{3}H},\quad z=\frac{\sqrt{W}}{\sqrt{3}H},

\displaystyle u=\frac{\sqrt{M\psi\overline{\psi}}}{\sqrt{3}H},\quad v=f_{,\phi}H^{2},\quad\varrho=\frac{\sqrt{\rho_{r}}}{\sqrt{3}H}.

(33)

In addition, we define the auxiliary variables:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\frac{V_{,\phi}}{V},\quad\Gamma_{2}=\frac{M_{,\phi}}{M},\quad\Sigma_{1}=\frac{\psi\ W_{,\psi}}{W},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}=\frac{\overline{\psi}\ W_{,\overline{\psi}}}{W},\quad A=\frac{f_{,\phi\phi}}{f_{,\phi}},\quad B=\frac{i\gamma^{0}M}{H}.

(34)

With these definitions, the Friedmann constraint equation takes the form:

1=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}+u^{2}+\varrho^{2}-8\sqrt{6}\left(xv\right).

(35)

The system can then be reformulated as an autonomous set of first-order differential equations:

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-3x-\frac{3\Gamma_{1}y^{2}}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{24v}{\sqrt{6}}-\frac{3\Gamma_{2}u^{2}}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\left(\frac{24}{\sqrt{6}}v-x\right),~~~~~~

(36)

\displaystyle y^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{6}\Gamma_{1}xy}{2}-y\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{3z}{4}\left(\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}\right)+\frac{z\ B}{2}\left(\Sigma_{2}-\Sigma_{1}\right)-z\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}},

(38)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\Gamma_{2}xu-\frac{3}{2}u+\frac{1}{2}z^{2}\frac{B}{u}\left(\Sigma_{1}-\Sigma_{2}\right)-u\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\,,

(39)

\displaystyle v^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{6}Axv+2v\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\ ,

(40)

\displaystyle B^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{6}B\Gamma_{2}x-B\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}},

(41)

\displaystyle\varrho^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varrho\left(2+\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\right).

(42)

We denote a derivative with respect to
N=\ln a
as
()^{\prime}=\frac{d()}{dN}=\frac{1}{H}\frac{d()}{dt}
, and note that
dN=H\,dt=d\ln a
.

Additionally, the tensor propagation speed
c_{T}^{2}
, which depends on the Gauss–Bonnet coupling function
f(\phi)
, is given by
Tsujikawa and Sami 2007
;
Odintsov and Oikonomou 2019
;
Fier et al. 2025

c_{T}^{2}=\frac{1+8\kappa^{2}\ddot{f}}{1+8\kappa^{2}H\dot{f}}\,.

(43)

Recent gravitational wave observations have confirmed that the propagation speed of gravitational waves closely matches the speed of light

•

CC Data:
This data set comprises 32 model-independent measurements of the Hubble parameter, commonly known as Cosmic Chronometers (CC). It probes the expansion history by using massive, passively evolving galaxies with old stellar populations and minimal star formation, offering reliable estimates of

VI
Conclusions

The interaction between dark matter and dark energy has been widely explored and is attracting more and more attention. In this work, we study a non-gravitational interaction within the framework of Einstein–scalar–Gauss–Bonnet (EsGB) gravity, a low-energy limit of the string/M-theory
Gross and Sloan 1987
;
Bento and Bertolami 1996
;
Ferrara et al. 1996
;
Antoniadis et al. 1997
, where dark energy is modeled by the coupled GB scalar field and dark matter by a fermionic scalar field. The interaction arises naturally from the action, rendering the framework theoretically well-grounded.

We consider two forms of the GB scalar field potentials: quadratic and exponential, and introduce a coupling between the scalar field and the Gauss–Bonnet term via a general function
f(\phi)
. The form of this coupling is constrained by recent gravitational wave observations, which place stringent bounds on the propagation speed of tensor modes. This constraint allows the analysis to remain broadly model-independent while remaining consistent with current observational limits.

As a first step, we apply dynamical systems techniques to study the global behavior of the cosmological evolution. This method offers insights that are largely independent of specific initial conditions. Our results show that both models successfully reproduce the standard sequence of cosmological epochs: radiation-, matter-, and dark energy-dominated epochs, closely tracing the expansion history of the standard
\Lambda
CDM model.

To complement the theoretical analysis, we perform a comprehensive observational study using the most recent data sets, including cosmic chronometers (CC), Type Ia supernovae from Pantheon+, DESY5, and mock Roman survey data, along with BAO measurements from DESI DR2. The results indicate that, for certain combinations of data, the interacting models considered here are mildly favored over the standard
\Lambda
CDM model. This finding is particularly interesting in light of the recent DESI DR2 results, which also suggest the possibility of a dynamical dark energy component and a potential departure from the canonical
\Lambda
CDM cosmology.

Additionally, we conduct a comparative analysis together with the standard
\Lambda
CDM model using the AIC and BIC as model selection tools. When combined with the Pantheon+ dataset, Models I and II are nearly indistinguishable from
\Lambda
CDM, with both AIC and BIC showing a slight preference from the standard model. However, other dataset combinations reveal a more nuanced picture. In particular, for the
DESI+CC+DESY
and
DESI+CC+Roman
configurations, the interacting models demonstrate comparable or marginally better performance relative to
\Lambda
CDM. These results suggest that the proposed interacting scenarios are competitive with the standard model in accounting for current cosmological observations.

It is noteworthy that the models presented here naturally avoid phantom behavior, owing to the evolution of the Gauss–Bonnet coupling function. When constrained by observational data, this coupling diminishes at low redshifts, effectively suppressing the influence of the GB term in the late Universe. At higher redshifts, although both the GB coupling and interaction terms grow, the parameter space is tightly constrained by stability conditions, ensuring that the equation-of-state remains within the non-phantom regime. This balance is crucial, as previous GB or interacting scalar field models have often faced stability issues, even without crossing into the phantom domain. In this context, the absence of phantom behavior is not a shortcoming, but rather a hallmark of the model’s theoretical robustness and consistency with current observational constraints.

Naturally, a complete evaluation of the proposed models requires extending the analysis beyond the background level to include cosmological perturbations. In particular, comparison with the full Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) dataset would yield tighter and more informative constraints. While we anticipate that the inclusion of forthcoming high-precision data will sharpen parameter estimates and further test the robustness of the theory, a detailed perturbative analysis lies beyond the scope of the present work and is left for future investigations.
