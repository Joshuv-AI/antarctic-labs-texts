Research note: A longitudinal evaluation of feather corticosterone in two broiler genotypes in a pasture-based system☆
Authors: AS Aderibigbe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite increasing attention to broiler welfare, there is still limited information comparing physiological stress responses between fast-growing (FG) and slow-growing (SG) genotypes under evolving production conditions. Investigating this is important, as it can inform the development of more sustainable approaches in poultry management. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the stress response of FG Cornish broilers and SG Freedom Ranger broilers over a five-week period in a pasture-based system. Feather corticosterone levels were measured as a noninvasive biomarker for stress. Our results revealed no significant differences in stress levels between the FG and SG broilers. However, significant changes in stress level were observed over time (P < 0.05) regardless of genotype. These findings indicate that under the given experimental conditions, temporal changes in feather CORT were detectable whereas no genotype-related differences were observed.Keywords: Broiler, Corticosterone, Pasture, Poultry, Stress

Introduction

The welfare of broiler chickens in commercial production is increasingly scrutinized because housing and management conditions directly influence stress levels, health, and overall well-being. Stress in poultry can lead to a range of negative outcomes, including reduced growth rates, poor feed conversion, and increased susceptibility to disease (Liu et al., 2020). These challenges have prompted a growing interest in alternative poultry production systems, such as pasture-based or free-range systems, which are often considered more humane and sustainable compared to conventional indoor systems. Pasture-based systems allow birds access to outdoors, opportunities for natural behaviors, reduced stocking densities, and potentially lower stress levels (Phillips and Heins, 2021). However, these systems also present unique challenges, such as exposure to predators, variable weather conditions and inconsistent forage availability that may introduce additional stressors. Understanding how different broiler breeds respond to these conditions is therefore essential for optimizing breed selection and improving welfare outcomes in a pasture-based system.

Fast-growing broiler breeds (FG), such as the Cornish Cross are often presumed to be more susceptible to stress due to rapid growth rates and associated metabolic demands (Pontalti et al., 2025). In contrast, slower-growing breeds (SG) such as Freedom Ranger tend to exhibit greater locomotor activity, improved adaptability to outdoor environments and consumer-perceived advantages in meat quality (Stadig et al., 2016; Pontalti et al., 2025). Recent comparative work shows that physiological, skeletal, and immune-related differences such as circulating IgG levels between FG and SG broilers can emerge early and persist throughout rearing (Çapar Akyüz et al., 2022, 2024). Despite the growing adoption of pasture-based systems, there is limited research comparing the stress responses of FG and SG broilers breed over time. This gap is particularly relevant as more producers consider adopting FG breeds in outdoor systems, where their stress resilience remains insufficiently characterized.

Feather corticosterone (CORT) provides an integrated measure of chronic stress accumulated during feather growth and has been widely used to assess long-term HPA-axis activity in broilers. Previous studies have demonstrated that feather CORT reliably correlates with behavioral and physiological indicators of stress in commercial broilers (Bortolotti et al., 2008; Weimer et al., 2018). We hypothesized that SG broilers would exhibit lower feather CORT concentrations than FG broilers when reared under similar pasture conditions, due to presumed differences in metabolic load and behavioral adaptability. Therefore, the present study was designed to test this hypothesis by evaluating the stress responses of FG versus SG broiler breeds raised on pasture over a five-week rearing period. The results from this study are intended to improve our understanding of genotype-specific stress dynamics in pasture-raised broilers and support the development of more sustainable, welfare-oriented production strategies.

Materials and methods

Birds, housing and sampling

The study was carried out on two broiler flocks raised on two pasture poultry farms that are located within a 10-mile radius of each other in Northwest Florida. In both settings, chicks were brooded indoors from d 0 to 21 under standard conditions and subsequently transferred to mobile pens on pasture that were moved daily to fresh pasture. One flock consisted of 120 Cornish Cross broilers (FG) reared on pasture from week 3 to week 8 post hatch, while the second flock comprised 200 Freedom Ranger broilers (SG) reared from week 3 to week 11 post-hatch. Stocking density at each site followed standard guidelines (approximately 1.92 ft² per bird). Environmental conditions were monitored using a Kestrel Drop-3 logger, with average temperatures for both sites ranging from 63 to 77°F and relative humidity from 39 to 65%. Both locations provided ad libitum access to a commercially formulated, nutritionally adequate diet (22% CP Meat Bird Start-to-Finish; Kalmbach Feeds Inc, Upper Sandusky, OH) and fresh water supplied through a nipple drinker system. Since each genotype was raised on a different farm, genotype and farm effects were inherently confounded and thus even with similar management practices, environmental conditions, same day sample collection, any site-specific differences could not be statistically separated from genotype effects. This limitation is acknowledged in the interpretation of our results.

Feather sampling was conducted weekly over a five-week period (weeks 4–8 for FG and weeks 6–10 in SG), to align with each genotype’s growth trajectory and ensure sampling close to processing age. Each week, on same day, 12 different birds per flock were randomly selected, and no bird was sampled more than once to prevent potential regrowth-related effects on feather CORT. Fully grown body feathers from the intrascapular region were collected to ensure consistency in feather type, maturity, and anatomical location.

Corticosterone analysis

Feather CORT was extracted using a methanol-based protocol adapted from Bortolotti et al. (2008). Briefly, the calamus was removed and feather vanes were cut into fragments <4 mm². Samples were submerged in 10 mL of methanol (VWR International, Batavia, IL) and sonicated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by overnight incubation at 50°C with continuous agitation. Following incubation, the methanol solvent was separated from feather material. The residual feather material and filtration apparatus were rinsed with approximately 2.5 mL methanol, and the washes were pooled with the initial methanol extract. The extract was evaporated at 50°C under a fume hood until dry. Dried residues were reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline. When necessary, samples were centrifuged after reconstitution to remove any remaining particulates and then stored at −20°C until CORT analysis. CORT concentrations in reconstituted extracts were quantified by a DetectX Corticosterone EIA kit (Arbor Assays, Ann Arbor, MI). The inter- and intra-assay coefficients of variations were less than 10%. Samples were run in duplicates. Feather CORT values were standardized to feather mass (pg CORT/ mg of feathers).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using PROC MIXED (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) to evaluate the effects of genotype (FG vs. SG) and sampling week on feather CORT concentrations. The model included genotype, sampling week, and their interaction as fixed effects. Bird age at the time of sampling was included as a continuous covariate to account for age-related variation in stress physiology. Because different birds were sampled each week, no repeated statement was used. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD, and significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Feather CORT is a recognized biomarker of chronic stress in poultry, reflecting long-term activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and differentiate stressed from non-stressed birds (Bortolotti et al., 2008; Weimer et al., 2018).

In the present study, no detectable difference in feather CORT was observed between the two broiler genotypes under the specific conditions of this study (Table 1; P = 0.913). Although FG and SG strains differ in growth rate, metabolic load, and behavioral tendencies, these differences did not translate into detectable variation in chronic stress physiology during the pasture-based production period. However, under the conditions of the present study, it is pertinent to state that genotype and farm were confounded, as each genotype was raised on a different farm. Thus, the absence of a detectable genotype effect cannot be interpreted as evidence that the strains respond similarly in biological terms. Specific farm-level factors such as management differences or environmental micro-conditions may have influenced CORT levels (Nawab et al., 2018; Bilal et al., 2021). Therefore, genotype comparisons must be interpreted cautiously and strictly within the context of this study design. Nonetheless, under the relatively mild and stable environmental conditions recorded during this study, it is likely that both genotypes experienced similar external pressures, resulting in comparable CORT profiles.

In contrast, sampling period had a significant effect on feather CORT (Table 1; P = 0.026). Feather CORT concentrations varied over the five sampling periods, with the highest values observed in period 3 and suggests temporal fluctuations rather than a monotonic increase. Such variation may reflect age-related physiological changes, social dynamics, or cumulative exposure to routine production stressors as birds approached market age (Weimer et al., 2018). While this study did not find significant differences in CORT between the two broiler chicken breeds, elevated CORT is known to redirect nutrients away from growth toward maintenance and survival, promoting protein catabolism, altering energy metabolism, and reducing feed efficiency (Liu et al., 2020). Therefore, even in the absence of genotype differences, the variability in CORT over time underscores the importance of minimizing the impact of environmental and management stress during production.

In conclusion, there were no detectable differences in stress levels between the fast or slow broiler genotypes under the specific conditions of this study. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to potential differences in farm management practices and resultant effect on specific bird genotypes. Also, temporal variation in CORT suggests that environmental or developmental factors may influence stress physiology in pasture-raised broilers, regardless of breed. Further controlled studies are needed to disentangle these effects and correlate with tissue level responses between different bird strains.
