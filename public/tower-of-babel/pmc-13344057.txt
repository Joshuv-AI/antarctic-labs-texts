Supporting Survivor-Centered Care Through Digital Health Integration
Authors: Jordan G. Marchak, Rebecca Williamson-Lewis, Michael O’Connor, Michael Palgon, Yu-Ra Kim, Michael Schuckenbrock, Paula Edwards, Abraham Mendoza, Anthony Adams, Brian Arriaga-Solis, Justin Parker, Harry Tinoco-Giraldo, Jason King, C. Michael Fordis, Karen E. Effinger, Maria Monica Gramatges
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Survivors of childhood cancer face barriers to receiving guideline-based, long-term follow-up care. Two digital tools, Passport for Care (PFC) and Cancer SurvivorLink (SurvivorLink), address complementary gaps by enabling tailored survivorship care plan (SCP) generation, updating, storage, and sharing. As part of a joint initiative to integrate the tools, 18 survivorship clinics using PFC and/or SurvivorLink were surveyed to assess awareness of features and preferred information dissemination strategies. Overall, awareness of core features of each tool was high, but knowledge of educational and clinician-focused resources was limited. Respondents prioritized distinct dissemination strategies for clinics, clinicians, survivors, and broader community engagement.

Introduction

Despite the need for long-term follow-up, only one-third of survivors of childhood cancer report receiving care addressing risk for late effects, and even fewer receive risk-reduction counseling and screening [1]. Along with individual, systematic, and policy-level barriers (e.g., insurance limitations, competing responsibilities, distance to survivorship, and availability of such services), barriers specific to survivors of childhood cancer further complicate the delivery of survivor-focused care [2]. Suboptimal transitions of survivors, from pediatric to adult-based care and from specialty-focused to primary-focused care, lead to deficits in transferring diagnosis and treatment data and disseminating guideline-informed risk assessment and screening recommendations in long-term follow-up [1, 3–9].

Universal distribution of survivorship care plans (SCPs)—which include individually tailored treatment summaries, guideline-informed late effect risks, and recommended screening—is one strategy to address these gaps. SCPs improve health professionals’ knowledge of survivorship care and late effects [10]. Survivors with an SCP are more likely to receive appropriate late-effects screening [11] and have superior event-free and overall survival [12]. In the Childhood Cancer Survivor Study, SCPs were associated with increased adherence to recommended screening, especially when both the survivor and their PCP had access; however, only 27% of survivors had access [13]. Thus, there is a critical need to augment survivors’ access to SCPs and facilitate sharing with providers.

This need has motivated the development of innovative tools designed for survivors of childhood cancer to support SCP generation, storage, and sharing:

Passport for Care (PFC), a web-based clinical decision support tool developed in collaboration with the Children’s Oncology Group (COG) Long-Term Follow-Up Guidelines Committee and Nursing Discipline [14], facilitates clinicians’ use of the guidelines by translating treatment exposures into tailored risk estimates and surveillance recommendations [15]. Since 2008, PFC has served as the primary HIPAA-compliant survivor database and guidelines decision support for most COG clinics [16]. PFC generates and stores dynamic SCPs that are automatically updated as the COG Guidelines are revised. Survivors with access to their PFC-generated SCP can access up-to-date evidence-based risk assessments and screening recommendations in English or Spanish via the web portal (cancersurvivor.passportforcare.org) or PFC mobile health application [17].

Cancer SurvivorLink (https://CancerSurvivorLink.org/, SurvivorLink) is a web-based platform created [18] and scaled [19] to improve adherence to risk-based survivor care. In contrast to PFC, SurvivorLink is a patient-controlled electronic personal health record designed to allow survivors (≥ 18 years) or caregivers (survivor < 18 years) to upload, store, and share SCPs and other health documents (e.g., surveillance results, fertility assessments, and immunizations). SurvivorLink links survivors with their medical teams, enabling survivors to transmit pertinent medical documents directly to their healthcare providers to facilitate long-term follow-up care and transferring to adult care [20].

Both PFC and SurvivorLink were developed with user-centered, participatory design to enhance usability and utility [16, 18, 21]. Both are free to use and include survivorship-specific educational resources for patients, families, and providers. The services offered by PFC and SurvivorLink are distinct yet complementary, addressing different barriers related to survivor care delivery, but the use of one tool versus the other largely depends on institutional preference and existing partnerships.

As part of an initiative to integrate PFC and SurvivorLink resources to improve survivor-centered care, we conducted a survey of partner clinics to assess awareness of each resource and preferences for resource integration. Our approach of leveraging clinic input to guide integration offers generalizable insights for disseminating and implementing other survivorship clinical decision-support and digital health tools.

Methods/Results

We administered a 32-item survey in REDCap [22, 23] of PFC and SurvivorLink partner clinics from August to October 2025, inviting all 12 SurvivorLink partner clinics (including seven who also use PFC) and used purposive sampling to target 15 more of the 165 PFC partner clinics to ensure representation across clinic size, geographic location, and SCP delivery models (Table 1). Both services allow multiple clinicians and staff users at each site, but require a clinician contact lead to serve as the site administrator. The designated contact lead for each site was invited to complete the survey, but was allowed to delegate this responsibility if desired. Each site invited to participate had been using either PFC or SurvivorLink, or both, for at least 1 year. Respondents included nine SurvivorLink-affiliated clinics (75%) and 14 PFC-affiliated clinics (64%), including five affiliated with both, for an overall response rate of 67% (18/27 invited clinics). Specifically, 88% of the respondents were female versus 50% of non-respondents, and the proportion of advanced practice providers (vs. MD/DO providers) was similar between respondent and non-respondent groups (17% vs. 13%). No incentive was provided. This study was determined by the Baylor College of Medicine Institutional Review Board as constituting nonhuman subjects research. Therefore, no written consent was obtained.

Awareness of PFC and SurvivorLink features (Table 1).

All respondents were aware that PFC provides survivor access to individualized SCPs and downloads, with most recognizing automatic guideline updates, embedded COG Health Links, and availability of SCPs in English and Spanish (83%–89%). Awareness of PFC informational resources was lower (39%). Most respondents were aware of SurvivorLink’s features for storing and sharing SCPs and other health documents, educational materials, and curated web resources on the SurvivorLink website (55%–61%), while awareness of provider upload capabilities and clinician resources was limited (39%).

Preferred outreach methods and materials for digital care delivery tools (Table 2).

Presentations at national meetings (89%) and email notifications to clinicians (78%) were considered the most effective methods for informing survivorship clinics about digital care delivery tools. To promote digital tools to survivorship clinicians, respondents rated informational slide decks for local sharing (78%), video orientation (72%), and short promotional videos distributed via social media and presented at national meetings (72%) as most helpful. Among resources to promote digital tools to survivors during clinic visits, short lay-language promotional videos (72%) and brief clinician talking points (67%) were rated most helpful. To promote digital tools within the broader survivorship community, the most helpful strategies identified by respondents were social media outreach (83%) and promotion by childhood cancer foundations and nonprofit organizations (67%).

Discussion

Our findings highlight awareness gaps and opportunities to improve promotion of two widely used pediatric cancer survivorship care delivery tools, PFC and SurvivorLink, with implications for other digital health tools across survivorship clinical contexts. While overall awareness of core care delivery features to support survivors was high, awareness of PFC informational resources and SurvivorLink clinician tools and education was notably lower. These results indicate a need for targeted education about features to enhance patient engagement with PFC and increase clinician utility of SurvivorLink.

Results also suggest a need for distinct dissemination strategies for digital health tools across multiple levels, including the clinic, clinician, patient, and broader community. To engage clinics, implementers should consider leveraging established professional communication channels, such as conference-based engagement and direct email campaigns. Practical, ready-to-use materials (e.g., slide decks, orientation videos) are valued for clinician education to promote the use of digital care delivery tools. For patient engagement, respondents prioritized simple, accessible content (e.g., short videos, clinician talking points) that can be integrated into clinic workflows. These clinician- and survivor-level resources are essential components of comprehensive implementation toolkits for digital health tools. Finally, social media outreach and collaboration with childhood cancer organizations were valued for broader community engagement, underscoring the need for partnership with trusted advocacy organizations and strategic use of social media to amplify reach.

Strengths of this study include participation (69% response rate) from 18 survivorship programs representing diverse clinic sizes, geographic locations, and care delivery models, and the incorporation of clinic perspectives into dissemination strategies, which is critical for promoting adoption and reach of digital health tools. Limitations include a small sample size, which restricted the analyses to descriptive statistics and may have introduced response bias. Future research should evaluate the impact of integrated digital health solutions, such as PFC and SurvivorLink, on survivor outcomes, identify strategies to sustain engagement across diverse settings and survivor populations, and assess factors influencing broader adoption and implementation.
