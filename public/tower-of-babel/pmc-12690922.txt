A TAp63alpha truncating variant associated with primary ovarian insufficiency lowers the cellular apoptotic rate
Authors: Silvia Moleri, Sara Casafina, Maria Orietta Borghi, Luca Persani, Raffaella Rossetti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary ovarian insufficiency (POI) is a complex endocrine disorder with impairment of ovarian function before the age of 40 that can associate with mutations in TAp63alpha (TAp63α), a transcription factor essential for maintaining genome integrity in the female germline. Variants located in the C-terminal can constitutively self-activate TAp63α and induce oocyte apoptosis, resulting in POI. This research examined the involvement of the R643* truncating variant of TAp63α in the development of POI.MethodsExperiments were performed in the human osteosarcoma-derived SAOS-2 cell line, a non-germline in vitro model selected due to its lack of endogenous TAp63α expression. This cell line cannot fully recapitulate the ovarian follicle microenvironment; however, the R643* variant was investigated alongside the previously validated R594* POI-related variant to confirm the robustness of the methodology. DNA damage was induced using Doxorubicin treatment, and subsequent analyses were performed using Western immunoblotting, luciferase assays, quantitative PCR, and flow cytometry.ResultsThe R643* variant of the TAp63α protein was expressed at minimal RNA and protein levels in the transfected SAOS-2 cells (p < 0.001). The overexpression of the TAp63α mutant R643* in SAOS-2 cells resulted in a significant alteration in the expression of target genes. Specifically, BAX expression was decreased (adjusted p = 0.0008), whereas the expression of IRF6, K14, WNT4, and MASPIN was significantly elevated (adjusted p < 0.0001). MDM2 expression also increased (adjusted p < 0.05). Notably, stable R643* variant expression increased cell survival following Doxorubicin treatment (adjusted p < 0.05).ConclusionsWe propose a new molecular mechanism for the induction of POI. Our in vitro characterization indicated that the R643* variant of TAp63α may increase the susceptibility of germ cells to DNA damage accumulation during the extended period of meiotic arrest, likely due to a decrease in apoptosis. Concurrently, it appears to stimulate pathways and genes driving uncontrolled follicular development and proliferation. The consequence of these combined effects would be the persistence of poorly competent oocytes, which ultimately leads to premature depletion of the follicular reserve.Clinical trial numbernot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-025-01881-2.

Background

One major cause of infertility in women is Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI). POI is defined as a complex endocrine disorder caused by an impairment in ovarian follicle function before the age of 40. Its global prevalence has been estimated at 3.5% worldwide [1], with the annual incidence increasing exponentially with increasing age [2]. Various pathogenic mechanisms can impair ovarian function, such as a diminished number of primordial follicles at birth, accelerated follicular atresia or altered/blocked follicle maturation [3]. POI is heterogeneous and can manifest as primary amenorrhea, with or without pubertal development, gonadal dysgenesis, or secondary amenorrhea caused by other congenital or acquired abnormalities [4]. There are multiple causes of POI, including autoimmunity, iatrogenic causes and genetic mutations. The pathogenesis remains unknown in most cases, but strong evidence supports the genetic basis for the idiopathic forms. The variable expressivity of POI, often within the same family, suggests a possible multifactorial/oligogenic architecture [5, 6]. Among the candidates whose alterations have been associated with the POI phenotype, an increasing number of reports have revealed a role for the TP63 (Tumor protein p63) gene [7–15].

The human TP63 gene (NM_003722.5) is located on chromosome 3q27 and encodes the protein p63, a member of the same family of p53 and p73 transcription factors. It is considered a master regulator of epidermal commitment, proliferation, differentiation, stemness, aging, and apoptosis [16–18]. The structure of its promoter permits the expression of multiple isoforms. In particular, the TAp63alpha isoform (TAp63α) is expressed predominantly within the nucleus of oocytes of primordial and primary follicles [19]. Here, TAp63α is normally maintained in an inactive closed dimeric state with poor affinity for DNA [20]. However, the exposure of oocytes to chemotherapeutics or prolonged meiotic arrest leads to the accumulation of DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) which triggers TAp63α activation and increased binding affinity for DNA [21]. Once activated, tetrameric TAp63α can directly bind to promoters of proapoptotic genes, such as BAX, inducing the activation of apoptotic pathways and leading to the death of damaged oocytes [22].

The role of TAp63α in oocytes is maintaining the genome integrity during the prolonged meiotic arrest to faithfully transmit genetic information and preserve the species throughout generations [23]. Mutations or inactivation of TAp63α can lead to a diminished quality or depletion of the ovarian reserve, thus causing infertility. In this context, the C-terminal domain of activated TAp63α has proven to be essential for the quality control of primary oocytes with DNA damage by triggering cell death. A genetic mouse model lacking the C-term domains shows the exhaustion of primary oocytes during the first week after birth, similar to POI in women [24].

So far, various mutations affecting the C-terminal domains and leading to truncated TAp63α proteins have been implicated in POI, frequently in association with syndromic ectodermal dysplasia and limb malformations [17, 25] (Table 1). Functional studies have demonstrated that some of these variations would disrupt TAp63α dimerization, leading to constitutive protein activation and increased transcription of target genes, ultimately contributing to accelerated oocyte loss [26]. This expanding spectrum of TAp63α-related POI underscores the importance of considering TP63 as a candidate gene in the genetic evaluation of women with POI, even in the absence of other classic TP63-associated features.

We recently identified the nonsense variant p.R643* (hereinafter referred to as R643*) in two Italian sisters presenting with primary amenorrhea and infertility as the only symptoms [5]. The variant was inherited from the apparently unaffected father. This study aims to estimate the contribution of the R643* variant to POI, by analysing its in vitro effects on apoptosis, cellular maturation and differentiation (refer to cited literature for detailed pathway descriptions) [27–37]. Specifically, we investigated the variant’s impact on apoptosis induction in the presence of DNA DSBs.

Methods

Patients

The probands (Fig. 1a, II-1 and II-2) are two Italian sisters affected by hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. These patients presented with hypostaturality and were diagnosed with primary amenorrhea at 16 and 13 years of age, respectively. The younger patient II-2 also had celiac disease. No family history consistent with pubertal delay was reported, and the other female members of their family (Fig. 1a, I-2 and II-3) achieved menarche normally. Baseline biochemical assessments revealed high levels of serum gonadotropins with undetectable estradiol for both probands (Fig. 1b). Somatotropin (GH) levels were within the reference range, whereas Insulin-like Growth Factor 1 (IGF-1) levels were remarkably low in both probands for their chronological age (155 ng/ml and 61 ng/ml, respectively) (Fig. 1b). Pelvic ultrasound, performed to explore the internal genitalia of II-1, revealed a reduced size of the uterus (30 mm hysterometry) and ovaries (15 mm diameter each) for her chronological age. Chromosomal abnormalities were excluded, and karyotype resulted 46,XX in both patients. Reagents

Primers for site-directed mutagenesis, Taqman probes, and primers for Sybr green assays are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Constructs for transfections and luciferase assays and antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Cell culture

Experiments were performed in the human sarcoma-derived SAOS-2 cell line (HTB-85, ATCC, Washington, DC, NW, USA). A limitation of this study is the use of a non-germline cellular model to characterise POI-related variants. However, SAOS-2 cell line was specifically chosen for the lack of expression of endogenous TAp63α [38], enabling us to study the variants of TAp63α in a controlled environment without background interference. This cell line was maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in McCoy’s 5 A Modified Medium, supplemented with 15% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (all from Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 0.2% Plasmocin (InvivoGen, Toulouse France), from now on referred as “complete medium”. For stable transfection, the complete medium was further supplemented with neomycin antibiotic (G-418, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) at a previously established concentration of 400 µg/ml for transfectant selection and then at 200 µg/ml for transfectant expansion and maintenance. The SAOS-2 cells were plated as follows: 12 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates for the MTT (3-(4,5-dimetylthiazole-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide)-based assay (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and the Caspase Glo 3/7 assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA); 2 × 105 cells/well in 12-well plates for Dual Luciferase reporter assay; 7 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates for immunoblotting, real-time PCR and cytofluorimetry; and 4 × 106 cells in 100 mm Petri dishes for stable transfections.

Transfections

Transient transfections were performed 24 h after seeding with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturers’ instructions, in optiMEM medium (Gibco). The cells were transiently transfected with pcDNA3-HA-TAp63α expression vectors containing the TAp63α wild-type (WT), R643* or R594* sequence. The R594* is a gain-of-function mutation, previously identified, which leads to the constitutive activation of TAp63α and has been linked to POI [13, 26]. The pcDNA3-HA vector backbone was used as a negative control (mock). Depending on the pathway under study, WT or mutant TAp63α vectors were co-transfected with different Firefly luciferase gene reporters. Specifically, reporter vectors with the BAX, K14 [39], or IRF6 [28] promoters were used. The Renilla reporter (pRL-CMV) was co-transfected for normalization. Five hours post transfection, optiMEM medium was replaced with complete medium. For stable transfections, the TAp63α WT, R643* or R594* vectors were transfected 24 h after seeding. In this case, 48 h post transfection, each dish was split into ten 100 mm Petri dishes. Twenty-four hours later, complete medium was replaced with medium supplemented with neomycin for selection. When single colonies appeared after 2 weeks of culture, picking was performed by cloning rings (SP Bel Art, NJ, USA) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Each single colony was transferred to 24-well plates and expanded in maintenance medium, and one clone per construct was selected among those displaying the highest transcript expression. We acknowledge that the use of only one clone per construct may not account for the variability between clones, which could influence the reproducibility of our findings.

Treatments

Doxorubicin (DX, Selleckchem, Huston, TX, USA), an anthracycline antibiotic, is a chemotherapeutic and intercalating agent that generates DSBs in DNA, leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. We treated both transiently transfected and stable SAOS-2 cells with 1 µM DX in complete medium 24 h post transfection to induce DSBs in all experiments. Supplementary Fig. 1 illustrates the experimental determination of the DX dose used for inducing DNA DSBs in SAOS-2 cells.

MG-132 (Merck-Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) is a proteasome inhibitor that blocks the degradation of ubiquitinated proteins and induces the accumulation of unfolded proteins. Since proteasome can degrade proteins involved in apoptosis, including caspase precursors, by using MG-132 we could increase the stability and concentration of these proteins, thus simplifying the study of caspases and their roles in apoptotic processes. Briefly, we incubated complete medium supplemented with 10 µM MG-132 for 30 min at 37 °C before immunoblotting and the Caspase-Glo 3/7 assay were performed. Next, 10 µM MG-132 was added to RIPA buffer (Radio-Immune Precipitation Assay, Merck-Millipore) for protein recovery.

Immunoblotting

Both transiently and stably transfected SAOS-2 cells were lysed 24 h after DX treatment in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Roche) and sonicated. The purified total protein was first quantified via a BCA assay (Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific), denatured, separated on NuPage 4–12% BIS-Tris acrylamide gels, and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific). The signals were detected with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) Westar Supernova (Cyanagen, Bologna, Italy) after primary and secondary antibody incubation. Densitometric analysis was performed via NIH ImageJ software. Protein expression data, derived from two independent experiments, and γH2AX quantification data, from three independent experiments, were plotted after normalization against the TAp63α WT construct.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

Forty-eight hours post transfection, cells were assayed via the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), as previously described [40]. Luminometric signals in Relative Light Units (RLU) were detected by a Fluoroskan Ascent FL (ThermoLab System, Thermo Fisher Scientific) through Ascent Software version 2.6 (ThermoLab System). Firefly luciferase luminescence was normalized to Renilla luciferase luminescence. For BAX promoter, mean fold induction relative to the WT construct was determined from triplicate samples in at least four independent experiments. For IRF6 and K14 promoters, at least three independent experiments were performed.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription

Total RNA was extracted from DX-treated and untreated (NT) SAOS-2 cells with RNeasy Kit and digested on columns with DNase I using the RNase-free DNase set (all from Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using oligodT and Superscript II reverse transcriptase (all from Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Quantitative PCR: TaqMan assay

The differential expression of 4 genes with a specific role in ovarian follicle functions and known to be targets of TAp63α was assessed via Taqman real-time PCR. In detail, we examined the expression of: MDM2, which has a putative role in several ovarian granulosa cells to improve oocyte quality [31, 32]; WNT4, which is expressed in fetal and adult human ovaries [33] and is required for ovarian follicle development [34]; MASPIN, which is highly expressed in human ovarian cancer [35, 36]; and EGFR, which has a role in oocyte growth and maturation [37]. PCR reactions were performed using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix and the TaqMan Gene Expression Assays listed in Supplementary Table 1 (all from Applied Biosystem, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the detection of target genes, FAM probes were employed, whereas the ACTB-VIC probe was utilized for the detection of the ACTIN gene, which served as an endogenous control. All experiments were performed in two independent biological replicates, with each sample run in technical triplicates.

Quantitative PCR: SYBR green assay

The differential expression of TAp63α was conducted using Sybr Green-based Real Time PCR. TAp63α-specific primers have been used for the target gene detection (Supplementary Table 1). Expression levels were normalized against the constitutively expressed housekeeping gene, HPRT, which served as an endogenous control. All experiments were performed at least in two independent biological replicates, with each sample run in technical triplicates.

Thermal cycling and data analysis

Both Taqman and Sybr Green PCR reactions were performed using the QuantStudio 12 K Flex instrument (all from Applied Biosystems). Relative quantification was determined using the comparative Ct​ (2−ΔΔCt​) method, normalizing to housekeeping genes. The results are presented as the mean fold change relative to the WT control samples, with associated standard error of the mean (s.e.m.).

Caspase Glo 3/7 assay

We measured the caspase-3 and − 7 activities in stable cell lines through the Caspase Glo 3/7 assay (Promega). Caspases-3 and − 7 are the effector caspases responsible for the controlled degradation process in apoptotic cells. Briefly, following the manufacturer’s instructions, 24 h after seeding, the cells were treated with DX for 2 h. The assay reagent was incubated for an additional hour, and then the luminometric signal of 4 replicates for each condition was detected by a Fluoroskan Ascent FL luminometer through Ascent Software version 2.6 (ThermoLab System). The mean values of 4 independent experiments are shown as the fold induction relative to the WT construct.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was used to measure cell death, distinguishing between apoptosis, necrosis, and cell cycle perturbation, 24 h after DX treatment. Four independent experiments were performed. First, the cells were harvested via gentle trypsinization and washed with PBS. Briefly, cells were collected after weak centrifugation, and the resulting pellets were resuspended in Annexin binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC was then added according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each sample was incubated for 15 min at room temperature before adding Annexin binding buffer and Propidium Iodide (PI) (all reagents from Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then incubated for 10 min on ice. Samples stained PI only, Annexin V only, and not stained were used as controls. The gate used to separate the cell population (i.e., early apoptotic cells with green fluorescence, apoptotic cells marked with both PI and Annexin V, live cells with little or no fluorescence, and necrotic cells marked with Annexin V) was established on the basis of the physical parameters of SAOS-2 cell line and maintained constant for all samples.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are shown as the mean ± s.e.m. (standard error of the mean). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad PRISM 8.4.2 software (La Jolla, CA, USA). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for repeated measurements was conducted to identify differences between groups on the basis of two independent factors (treatment and genetic variant) and to account for replicates within the experiments. A P value < 0.05 was considered of statistical significance: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001. Following the most appropriate ANOVA test, different post hoc testing methods were used depending on the aim of the analysis. Tukey’s test was used to compare all possible pairs of groups, whereas Dunnett’s test was used to compare the NT control group with all other groups.

Results

The R643* variant of TAp63α is associated with primary amenorrhea and primary ovarian insufficiency in an Italian pedigree

Through NGS analysis, we identified the C > T heterozygous substitution at residue c.1927 within exon 14 of the TP63 gene (NM_003722.5) in two Italian sisters (Fig. 1a, II-1 and II-2) affected by primary amenorrhea. This variation generates a premature stop codon at residue 643 of the protein TAp63α (R643*) in the C-terminal of the protein. We classified this variant as pathogenic following the ACMG (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics) guidelines through in silico analysis using the Franklin database v.86 by Genoox (https://franklin.genoox.com/clinical-db/variant/snp/chr3-189612175-C-T?app=acmg-classification). To further assess the variant pathogenic role, we extended the mutation analysis to patients’ relatives (Fig. 1a, I-1, I-2 and II-3). Pedigree genetic analysis revealed the presence of the R643* variant in the heterozygous father (I-1), but not in the healthy mother (I-2) or in the healthy sister (II-3). The sequence of the TAp63α gene was confirmed for all family members via Sanger sequencing (Fig. 1b).

By extending genetic analysis, we were able to identify in both probands: the c.5894G >T (p.R1965L) and c.5885_5886insA (p.R1963Afs*65) variants in the PLEC gene (NM_201380), and the c.710G >A (p.G237D) variant in the FOXL2 gene (NM_023067). These variants were found also in their healthy female relatives (Figs. 1a and I and 2 and II-3), as recently reported [5]. Further, the c.1340 C >T (p.S447L) variant in the FANCA gene (NM_000135) was found only in one of the affected sisters (II-1) and in her father (I-1). All reported variants were described as rare and of unknown significance (VUS) according to ACMG guidelines, except for the insertion in the PLEC gene which resulted likely pathogenic. Based on family genetic analysis, we concluded that only the TAp63α variant segregates with the clinical phenotype of ovarian dysgenesis in the family.

C-terminal variants impair TAp63α expression and can differentially affect apoptosis in SAOS-2 cells

To evaluate the RNA and protein expression of TAp63α variants, we used both transiently and stably transfected SAOS-2 cells. These cells were either NT or treated with a previously established dose of 1 µM DX to induce DNA DSBs (Supplementary Fig. 1a-d). To investigate the expression profiles of the TAp63α variants, we performed quantitative analyses at both the transcript and protein levels. Our results revealed a distinct pattern of transcript expression between the two examined variants. The R643* transcript exhibited significantly reduced expression compared with both the R594* variant (adjusted p = 0.0437 in NT cells; adjusted p = 0.0095 in DX-treated cells) and the WT transcript. However, the difference in expression between R643* and WT did not reach statistical significance (adjusted p = 0.3268 in NT cells; adjusted p = 0.2090 in DX-treated cells) (see Supplementary Fig. 2a). Notably, at the protein level, analysis of both transiently (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c) and stably transfected cells (Supplementary Fig. 3) indicated that both R643* and R594* variants were minimally expressed. This low protein abundance was consistent across the different experimental conditions tested (transient and stable transfections).

The apoptotic response in presence of TAp63α variants was first assessed through luciferase assay for the BAX promoter. The expression of the R643* protein significantly decreased the luciferase activity and thus the activation of BAX (adjusted P value = 0.0008) in comparison with WT. Contrarywise, the R594* variant highly increased BAX expression (adjusted P value < 0.0001) (Fig. 2a, left). R643* activates pathways related to development and cell differentiation in SAOS-2 cells

We characterized the role of the R643* mutant in other relevant pathways for follicular development and differentiation by analysing the IRF6 and K14 promoters by reporter assays. The effect of the R643* variant was compared with those of the WT and R594* variant (Fig. 4a, b). The results indicated significant augmented activation of both promoters (adjusted P value < 0.0001) in presence of the R643* variant (Fig. 4a, b). To further elucidate the role of the R643* variant in processes of development and differentiation, we performed a gene expression profiling analysis via quantitative Real Time-PCR on a priori selected genes, such as MDM2 (OMIM *164785), WNT4 (OMIM *603490), EGFR (OMIM *131550), and MASPIN (OMIM *154790). These genes have been validated as targets of TAp63α action and representatives of pathways relevant for ovarian follicles, such as cell proliferation, development, growth, maturation, ovulation, and steroidogenesis [41]. We observed that the gene expression of WNT4 resulted switched on by the presence of WT TAp63α (adjusted P values = 0.0001), but it was unchanged for MDM2 and MASPIN or apparently decreased for EGFR (Fig. 4c, d, e, f). When activated by the R643* variant, MDM2 and MASPIN genes resulted upregulated (for MDM2: adjusted P value = 0.0227, for MASPIN: adjusted P value < 0.0001) while in the WT and R594* cells, their levels substantially overlapped (Fig. 4c, e). A significant increase in WNT4 mRNA levels was observed relative to WT in the presence of the R594* variant (adjusted P value = 0.0070). Notably, a more substantial increase was found with the R643* variant (adjusted P value < 0.0001) (Fig. 4d). Finally, the expression of EGFR resulted substantially unaffected by the presence of WT or mutants TAp63α (Fig. 4f). Only two independent replicates could be performed in the case of WNT4, MASPIN, and EGFR due to limited sample availability, thus making related findings preliminary. Discussion

We described the molecular and functional effects of the POI associated TAp63α R643* variant on processes such as apoptosis, cell differentiation and development. Collectively, our results suggest that the R643* variation may cause a diminished pro-apoptotic rate in cells and the promotion of uncontrolled differentiation and proliferation of DNA damaged follicles. Conversely, previously reported C-terminal variants of TAp63α associated with POI were gain-of-function variations, such as the R594* variant, which lead to constitutive activation of TAp63α, and ultimately cause uncontrolled oocyte death [13, 26]. Our findings highlight the critical role of tightly regulated TAp63α activity in maintaining oocyte health and the ovarian reserve.

We identified in two Italian sisters presenting with primary amenorrhea the R643* variant of TAp63α. The same variant was recently identified in association with POI, undetectable ovaries and infertility in an unrelated Danish family. So far, no functional characterization was provided [10]. Therefore, the functional characterization of this variant is crucial for understanding the role of this gene in POI development and its potential pleiotropic effects. For our functional investigations, the SAOS-2 cell line was selected as cellular model because it does not express endogenous TAp63α. We acknowledge that the use of an osteosarcoma cell line cannot fully recapitulate the complex environment of an ovarian follicle, which represent a limitation of the current work. The osteosarcoma environment of the SAOS-2 cells may indeed introduce context-specific artifacts, making it challenging to directly extrapolate and reproduce the observed mechanism to the physiological setting of the ovarian follicle. Despite the mechanisms observed in SAOS-2 cells provide a valuable starting point for investigating the role of TAp63α, further experiments using ovarian follicles cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) may be necessary to validate our findings. Nevertheless, we established the bona fide of our experimental methods by testing our variant in parallel with a gain-of-function mutation associated with POI, which was previously described and validated in a knockout mouse model (i.e. R594* variant) [13].

We first evaluated the TAp63α RNA and protein expression. The barely detectable protein expression of the R594* mutant protein, similar to the results obtained by Huang and colleagues [13], would confirm that this variant exhibits self-activation. Indeed, it is known that active forms of TAp63α are more difficult to detect because of their high turnover rate [42, 43]. In contrast, the observed low transcript and protein expression of R643* suggests a potential mechanism of haploinsufficiency. Future investigations are necessary to verify our hypothesis and to elucidate its significance in POI development. A possible explanation could be attributed either to nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), that degrades TAp63α transcripts containing premature R643* stop codon before they can be translated into dysfunctional proteins, or to rapid degradation of the truncated protein. Thus, the detection of mutant TAp63α proteins has proven challenging likely due to distinct underlying causes. Despite both variants lead to the absence of a functional C-terminal domain and the exposure of sites prone to self-aggregation, we hypothesized that they might contribute to the premature depletion of ovarian follicles through distinct mechanisms. Specifically, the R594* variant was observed to trigger the BAX promoter and significantly increase caspase activity. This, in turn, would lead to a marked increase in both early and late apoptotic cells, ultimately resulting in constitutively induced cell death and the development of POI. In contrast, the R643* variant was observed to reduce apoptosis by significantly decreasing BAX promoter activation and could also lower the caspase cascade. R643* expression also appeared to reduce the number of early and late apoptotic cells, but concurrently, it showed a slight but significant increment of live cells, especially after DNA damage induction. Collectively, the concordance between the diminished expression of BAX, the apparent reduction of caspases and the increment of live cells, would suggest that the R643* variant might reduce the capacity of cells to respond to apoptotic signals. A lower apoptotic rate in oocytes could imply an accumulation of DNA damage during the prolonged meiotic arrest. As recently hypothesized, DNA-damaged follicles may exhibit lower germline competency, ultimately leading to ovarian failure [44].

To further assess the potential impact of R643* variant on follicular maturation and development, we investigated its impact on established targets. We first evaluated pathways activated by IRF6 and K14 promoters. IRF6 is considered representative of the developmental pathway in oocytes because of its expression in bovine oocytes [29]. It is known to interact with TAp63 in the development of the palate in mice [28] and plays a conserved role in the development of the epithelium in X. laevis and D. rerio embryos [45]. Our luciferase data revealed that the presence of both variants highly increased IRF6 promoter activity and, by inference, they might increase the development pathway in ovarian follicles. The K14 promoter is transcriptionally active in murine oocytes [30, 46] and in the basal layer of the epidermis, where it modulates the expression of a physiologically regulated protein during keratinocyte differentiation [47]. As a model of differentiation pathways, the presence of R643* variant showed augmented K14 promoter activation and thus this variant might increase oocyte differentiation, unlike the R594* variant. We further observed that the presence of the R643* variant could also affect the differential expression of other targets of TAp63α. We observed a significant increment in expression of MDM2, which is a critical regulator of the TAp63 protein and plays a pivotal role in cellular stress responses, such as DNA damage. Increased MDM2 expression could influence follicular maturation, inhibit apoptosis, and potentially contribute to abnormal follicular development. WNT4 typically promotes normal antral follicle development and stabilizes β-catenin, both crucial for follicular maturation. Our findings suggest the R643* variant might affect these processes. The R643* variant notably increased WNT4 expression, and this elevated expression could lead to the dysregulated and premature proliferation of nonfunctional ovarian follicular cells. Finally, we observed that MASPIN transcription was upregulated by the WT TAp63α, and the presence of R643* variant further increased its expression. Our results suggests that MASPIN might be involved in modulating processes like cell survival, growth, and differentiation, that potentially could be influenced by R643* variant. In the case of WNT4, MASPIN, and EGFR, findings should be considered preliminary and further validation is required to confirm our observations. Taken together, these findings propose that the R643* variant could elicit divergent effects, causing a loss of function in apoptosis and a gain of function in proliferation.

Based on the effects of the two variants observed in our in vitro model, we propose that the two different nonsense variants of TAp63α could lead to POI and the premature decline of ovarian reserve through distinct molecular mechanisms. Our results suggest that the R594* variant induces POI by promoting oocyte depletion, mainly through direct induction of apoptosis. On the contrary, we hypothesize that R643* variant might slower apoptosis and foster pathways and genes that would lead to premature uncontrolled follicular development and proliferation and, eventually, to premature loss of the ovarian reserve. Our model suggests that in this dysregulated environment, the expression of the R643* variant in germ cells might favour the accumulation of DNA damage during the extended meiotic arrest period, as a result of a diminished apoptosis rate. The persistence of poorly competent oocytes could ultimately have led to the onset of POI (Fig. 5). The failure to remove DNA-damaged cells and their uncontrolled proliferation has already been described in other pathological conditions beyond ovarian failure, such as Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS). Indeed, while most studies indicate generally increased apoptosis in ovarian granulosa cells in PCOS, there is evidence of reduced apoptosis in specific cellular contexts through the modulation of protein expression or pathways [48, 49]. The association of decreased apoptosis with other ovarian diseases further supports the potential role of the R643* variant in the development of ovarian failure in our patients. Conclusions

In conclusion, this study suggests a potential novel molecular mechanism by which TAp63α gene variants may contribute to the premature follicular depletion in women affected with POI. These findings may contribute to our understanding of this disease, facilitate genetic counselling for women and their families, and suggest potential avenues for targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at preserving the ovarian reserve. Future research should be focused on the specific pathways through which these variants exert their effects.
