Role of podoplanin in tongue squamous cell carcinoma and the regulation of malignant cellular behaviors via the ERK/MMP-2 signaling axis
Authors: Xiaowei Sheng, Liping Ding, Qiang Zhang, Yong Lu, Jun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic significance and functional role of podoplanin (PDPN) in tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) remain incompletely defined. This study investigated PDPN expression, prognostic relevance, effects on malignant cellular behaviors, and involvement of the ERK/MMP-2 signaling axis.MethodsPDPN levels in TSCC and matched surrounding noncancerous tissues were detected via immunohistochemistry. The link between PDPN levels and clinicopathological parameters was analyzed, and the prognostic value of PDPN was evaluated. Functional assays were conducted via PDPN knockdown and overexpression models in SCC-9 and CAL-27 cells, respectively. Extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion, wound healing, cell proliferation, and Transwell migration and invasion analyses were conducted. Bioinformatics prediction, Western blot analysis, and treatment with a specific ERK inhibitor were integrated to clarify the regulatory effects of PDPN on MMP-2 via the MAPK-ERK axis. Furthermore, a xenograft model was established in nude mice to verify the tumor-promoting effects of PDPN in vivo.ResultsPDPN was significantly upregulated in TSCC tissues and correlated with poor differentiation, advanced tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage, and lymphatic invasion. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified PDPN as an independent unfavorable prognostic factor for patients with TSCC. Functional assays revealed that PDPN promoted cell migration, proliferation, and invasion, which were mediated through the ERK/MMP-2 pathway. In vivo, PDPN knockdown reduced tumor growth and decreased MMP-2 expression.ConclusionsPDPN is overexpressed in TSCC and possesses independent prognostic value. PDPN promotes TSCC progression via the ERK/MMP-2 signaling axis, which suggests its utility as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target for patients with TSCC.Keywords: Podoplanin (PDPN), tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC), prognosis, ERK/MMP-2 signaling axis, invasion

Key findings

• Podoplanin (PDPN) was upregulated in tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) and associated with adverse clinicopathological features and overall survival (OS).

• PDPN promoted adhesion, proliferation, migration, and invasion through ERK/MMP-2 signaling.

• PDPN knockdown suppressed xenograft growth and reduced Ki-67, p-ERK, and MMP-2 expression.

What is known and what is new?

• PDPN is linked to invasion, metastasis, and poor prognosis in several cancers.

• PDPN independently predicted adverse OS in TSCC and promoted malignant behavior through ERK/MMP-2 signaling.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• PDPN and ERK/MMP-2 signaling merit further validation as prognostic and therapeutic targets in TSCC.

Introduction

Tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is an aggressive malignancy arising in the oral tongue. Oral cavity cancer accounted for approximately 42% of the estimated 758,000 new lip, oral cavity, and pharyngeal cancer cases worldwide in 2022, highlighting a substantial global disease burden (1). TSCC is an aggressive malignancy arising in the oral tongue. It is clinically characterized by slow onset, rapid local growth, and a pronounced tendency for early regional lymph node metastasis (2-4). Although multimodal therapeutic strategies, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, have been progressively refined in recent years, no substantial improvement in overall prognosis has been achieved, particularly in individuals with advanced-stage disease, with the 5-year survival rates remaining at approximately 50% (5,6). Extensive tongue resection can impair speech and swallowing and adversely affect quality of life (7). Consequently, the identification of novel molecular determinants that drive the malignant behavior of TSCC is urgently needed to enhance prognostic stratification and facilitate the development of more effective therapeutic interventions.

Podoplanin (PDPN) is a mucin-type transmembrane glycoprotein. Several studies have indicated the presence of aberrant PDPN overexpression (OE) in various human malignancies, including gastric, ovarian, and lung cancers (8-10). Raised levels of PDPN have been linked to aggressive tumor phenotypes—such as those with enhanced migratory and invasive capacities—increased incidence of lymph node metastasis, resistance to chemotherapy, and unfavorable clinical outcomes (11,12). Although a potential link between PDPN and oral carcinogenesis has been suggested, the biological role of PDPN in this process remains unclear. Indeed, comprehensive clinical validation combined with functional characterization is necessary to clarify its involvement in cell proliferation, extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion, migration, and invasion. Such research is essential to establishing PDPN as a putative oncogenic driver and prognostic biomarker in TSCC.

Tumor invasion and metastasis are not singular events but rather multistep biological processes involving sustained cellular proliferation, cytoskeletal remodeling, enhanced adhesion to the ECM, degradation of the basement membrane, and the acquisition of migratory and invasive capacities. Among these processes, the interaction between tumor cells and ECM components is considered a fundamental prerequisite for the initiation of invasion, whereas the degradation of the basement membrane and interstitial matrix mediated by matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) represents a critical step that enables tumor cells to breach tissue barriers (13). MMP-2, a prototypical gelatinase, has been found to modulate the degradation of type IV collagen and facilitate tumor cell infiltration (14). Meanwhile, the MAPK/ERK pathway, which is aberrantly activated in a number of malignancies, is widely recognized to be a canonical signaling axis that contributes to the formation of multiple malignant phenotypes by regulating cellular proliferation, migration, and MMP expression (15). Therefore, given the sequential cascade consisting of cell-matrix adhesion, pathway activation, and protease-mediated matrix degradation, we sought to determine whether PDPN promotes invasion and metastasis in TSCC through the regulation of MMP-2 and to clarify the related pathways. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-2027/rc).

Methods

Data sources

Three independent transcriptomic microarray datasets of TSCC, namely GSE9844, GSE13601, and GSE34105, were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. R software version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was employed for the normalization and preprocessing of raw expression data. The differential expression between tumor and corresponding normal control tissues in each cohort was assessed via the limma R package, with an absolute log2 fold change (|log2 FC|) >1 and an adjusted P<0.05 serving as the selection criteria. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Immunohistochemistry

A tissue microarray was purchased from Zhongke Guanghua Intelligent Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi’an, China), and comprised 74 paired TSCC specimens and adjacent nontumorous tissues with follow-up information.

TSCC tissue microarrays and paraffin-embedded sections derived from nude mouse xenografts were subjected to deparaffinization and rehydration, followed by antigen retrieval under high-pressure conditions in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidases were inactivated with 3% H2O2, which was followed by blocking with goat serum (cat. No. ZLI-9022; Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against human PDPN (monoclonal; 1:200; cat. No. ab128994; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Ki-67 (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), phosphorylated p44/42 MAPK (p-ERK1/2; Thr202/Tyr204; rabbit monoclonal, clone D13.14.4E; 1:400; cat. No. 4370; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), or MMP-2 (mouse monoclonal, clone 101; 1:100; cat. No. 436000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). On the following day, samples were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibodies and then visualized with DAB substrate (cat. No. D1505853; Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining (cat. No. ZLI-9610, Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Immunohistochemical staining in xenograft tumors was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53t; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Ki-67 expression was quantified as the percentage of positively stained tumor-cell nuclei, with at least 1,000 tumor cells counted across five non-overlapping viable tumor fields per specimen. PDPN, p-ERK, and MMP-2 staining was quantified using ImageJ after hematoxylin-DAB color deconvolution, and mean optical density was calculated as integrated optical density divided by the DAB-positive area.

PDPN expression was evaluated using a semiquantitative score integrating staining intensity and the proportion of positive tumor cells (16). Staining intensities were classified as follows: 3= brown, 2= yellow-brown, 1= light yellow, and 0= negative. The positive cell percentage was graded as follows: 4, >75%; 3, 51–75%; 2, 26–50%; 1, 5–25%; and 0, <5%. The two scores were added together to obtain an overall score between 0 and 7. A score of 0–3 indicated low expression, while a score of 4–7 indicated a high expression.

Cell lines and culture

Human TSCC cell lines (SCC-9, SCC-15, CAL-27, and SCC-4) were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) (17). Primary human oral keratinocytes (HOKs; cat. No. JY-T247) were purchased from Shanghai Jinyuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The HOKs were non-immortalized primary cells isolated from healthy human oral mucosal epithelium.

SCC-9, SCC-15, and SCC-4 cells were cultured in a 1:1 mixture of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium and Ham’s F-12 medium (DMEM/F-12) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 400 ng/mL hydrocortisone, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. CAL-27 cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. HOKs were maintained in the manufacturer-recommended primary oral keratinocyte medium, consisting of 500 mL basal medium, 5 mL keratinocyte growth supplement, and 5 mL penicillin-streptomycin. No fetal bovine serum was additionally added to the HOK culture medium. The proprietary growth supplement was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The culture medium was replaced every 2–3 days.

SCC-9, SCC-15, CAL-27, and SCC-4 cells were used between passages 5 and 20 after recovery, whereas primary HOKs were used at passages 2–5 (18). All cells were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before experimentation.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total cellular RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as directed by the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (cat. No. K1622; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR amplification was performed with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix. GAPDH was the internal reference, and relative gene expression was assessed via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

After harvest, radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) with 1% phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to lyse cells on ice for 30 min, which were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C for the collection of the supernatants containing proteins. Proteins were quantified with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology). The standard curve is provided as Figure S1. The proteins were mixed with loading buffer (5×) and heated at 100 °C for denaturation for 5 min. Subsequently, 30 μg of protein in each lane was resolved via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis before transfer to methanol-wetted polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), which were blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature (RT) for 1 h and probed overnight with primary antibodies diluted in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) at 4 °C. On the following day, blots were rinsed with TBST and treated with HRP-linked secondary antibodies at RT for 1 h. After additional rinsing, an enhanced chemiluminescence kit was employed to detect protein bands, which were imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Lentiviral infection and establishment of stable cell lines

For establishing stable PDPN-knockdown (KD) cells, a negative control sequence (sh-NC) and a short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting PDPN (shPDPN#1: AGGCAUUCGCAUCGAGGAUCU/AGAUCCUCGAUGCGAAUGCCU; shPDPN#2: UGACCCUGGUUGGAAUCAUAG/CUAUGAUUCCAACCAGGGUCA) were designed and cloned into a lentiviral vector. For OE analyses, a lentiviral expression vector was transfected with the full-length PDPN cDNA, and an empty vector (Vector-NC) was employed as the control. Lentiviral particles were obtained from Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). SCC-9 and CAL-27 cells (for KD and OE, respectively) were cultured at appropriate densities and transfected with lentiviral particles in the presence of Polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h. Subsequently, the culture media were refreshed. After 48 h, puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) selection was applied until stable transfectants were established. qRT-PCR was conducted to verify transfection efficiency.

Cell adhesion assay

For the cell adhesion assay, 48-well plates precoated with collagen I, collagen IV, fibronectin, or laminin-1 were employed. For the negative control, plates were coated with 1% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich). After equilibration to ambient temperature, logarithmic-phase cells were resuspended in serum-free medium (1.0×105 cells/mL). Cell suspensions (150 μL) were seeded into each well at 37 °C for 60 min before addition of the cell staining solution (200 μL) in each well at RT for 10 min. After aspiration of the solution, the wells were rinsed gently four times with deionized water before air-drying and subsequent addition of the extraction solution (200 μL) for 10 min at RT. Absorbances at 560 nm were read with a microplate reader to quantify the number of adherent cells. Three technical replicate wells were used for each condition in each independent experiment.

Trypan blue exclusion assay

Cells in the logarithmic-growth phase were harvested, prepared as single-cell suspensions, and counted. Subsequently, the cells (5×103/well) were grown in 96-well plates, with three replicate wells/group. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after seeding, cells were collected and treated with 0.4% trypan blue (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:1 ratio. A hemocytometer was used to count viable (unstained) cells under a microscope, with cell viability (%) calculated as follows: cell viability = viable cells/(viable cells + dead cells) ×100%.

Wound-healing assay

Logarithmically growing cells were collected, prepared as single-cell suspensions, and propagated (5×105 cells/well) in six-well plates. When 100% confluence was reached, the monolayer was uniformly scratched with a sterile 200 μL pipette tip (Axygen; Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA). Floating cells and debris were discarded after a wash with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco), which was followed by the addition of serum-free medium. The cells were subsequently maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Images of the same predefined wound fields were acquired immediately after scratching (0 h) and after 24 h using an inverted microscope at the same magnification and under identical imaging conditions. The cell-free wound area was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53t; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). After spatial calibration, the wound boundaries were manually delineated using the polygon-selection tool, and the wound area was measured using the Measure function.

The wound-closure percentage for each matched field was initially calculated as follows: wound closure (%) = [(A0 − A24)/A0] ×100, where A0 and A24 represent the cell-free wound areas at 0 and 24 h, respectively. Measurements from three predefined non-overlapping fields within each well were averaged to obtain one value per well. To facilitate comparisons across independently performed experiments, the mean wound-closure value of the corresponding control group was subsequently normalized to 100%. The relative migration rate was calculated as follows: relative migration rate (%) = wound closure of the experimental sample/mean wound closure of the corresponding control group ×100. Accordingly, values greater than 100% indicate greater wound closure relative to the corresponding control group rather than an absolute wound closure exceeding 100%.

Transwell invasion and migration assays

Logarithmic growth phase cells were resuspended in serum-negative medium at 2×105 and 5×105 cells/mL concentrations for migration and invasion assays, respectively. For the invasion test, diluted Matrigel (Corning Inc.) at a ratio of 1:8 (50 μL per well) was used to precoat the top Transwell chambers (Corning Inc.) for 2 h at 37 °C to enable the reconstitution of the basement membrane layer. No coating was used for migration experiments. Cell suspension (200 μL) was then introduced into the top chamber, while complete medium (600 μL) with 20% FBS was placed in the bottom chamber. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, nonmigrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were carefully cleared with a cotton swab, while those below the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 mins and subsequently dyed with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 30 min. Five random microscopic fields were observed under an inverted microscope, and the quantity of migrating or invading cells was enumerated.

Subcutaneous nude mouse xenograft model

Six-week-old male BALB/c nude mice weighing 18–22 g were obtained from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions (22±2 °C; 50–60% humidity; 12-h light-dark cycle) with free access to food and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, mice were randomly assigned to the sh-NC, shPDPN#1, or shPDPN#2 group (n=5/group; total n=15). No animals were lost or excluded, and all 15 animals were analyzed.

Stable SCC-9 cell lines were harvested and resuspended in PBS. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 2×106 cells suspended in 100 μL of PBS into the right dorsal flank. The experimental unit was an individual mouse. Tumor length and width were measured every 3 days with digital calipers by an investigator blinded to group allocation. Tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: volume =0.5 × length × width2. The primary outcome measure was tumor volume, while tumor weight and immunohistochemical expression of PDPN, Ki-67, phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK), and MMP-2 were the predefined secondary outcome measures. No animals were excluded from the study, and all animals completed the experimental protocol. No unexpected adverse events occurred during the experiment. On day 29 after inoculation, mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation, which was followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Tumors were excised, weighed, photographed, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical analyses.

Animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing University (approval No. IACUC-D2402074) and were conducted in accordance with the Chinese national guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Statistical analysis

All statistical measurements were conducted with GraphPad Prism 10.0 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA) and SPSS 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous normally distributed variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and were compared with independent-samples t-tests. Nonnormally distributed data are denoted as the median and interquartile range and were analyzed with Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers with percentages and were compared with the Fisher exact test. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance was conducted. Univariable Cox proportional-hazards regression analysis was first performed, followed by multivariable Cox regression for variables with P<0.10 in the univariable analysis. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All in vitro experiments were independently repeated at least three times using separately prepared cell cultures within the specified passage ranges. Each experimental condition included at least two technical replicates in each independent experiment, unless otherwise stated. Measurements from technical replicates were averaged to generate one value for each independent biological replicate, and statistical analyses were based on the independent biological replicates.

Results

Integrated analysis of GEO TSCC transcriptomic data

The GSE9844, GSE13601, and GSE34105 datasets were subjected to normalization and differential expression analysis using bioinformatics tools, resulting in the identification of a total of 26 candidate genes that were consistently and significantly upregulated across all three datasets (Figure 1). These genes were subsequently ranked according to FC in expression, with MMP12, PDPN, and IL24 identified as the top three most highly expressed genes. Based on the integrated bioinformatics findings and supporting evidence from the literature, PDPN was found to be stably overexpressed in TSCC and was suggested to be involved in tumor malignant progression; however, its precise biological activity and related molecular pathways in TSCC remain insufficiently defined. Therefore, PDPN was selected as the primary target for further in-depth investigation in the present study.

PDPN was overexpressed in TSCC tissues

Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that PDPN protein levels were notably enhanced in TSCC tissues relative to adjacent nontumorous tissues (Figure 2). With respect to subcellular localization, positive PDPN staining was predominantly observed along the cell membrane of carcinoma cells, with partial distribution detected in the cytoplasm.

According to immunohistochemical scoring results, patients exhibiting low PDPN staining were placed into the low-expression cohort (n=32), whereas those with moderate or strong staining were placed into the high-expression cohort (n=42). Univariable and multivariate Cox regression analysis of overall survival (OS)

Univariable Cox regression analysis showed that advanced TNM stage [hazard ratio (HR) =5.096, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.923–13.503; P=0.001], poor histological differentiation (HR =15.060, 95% CI: 5.481–41.374; P<0.001), lymphatic invasion (HR =2.709, 95% CI: 1.207–6.082; P=0.02), and high PDPN expression (HR =2.301, 95% CI: 1.021–5.186; P=0.04) were associated with poorer OS. Variables with P<0.10 in the univariable analyses were subsequently included in the multivariable Cox regression model. Multivariable analysis showed that high PDPN expression remained independently associated with poorer OS [HR =2.698, 95% CI: 1.023–7.116; P=0.045]. Advanced TNM stage, poor histopathological differentiation, and lymphatic invasion also remained independently associated with poorer OS (all P<0.05; Table 2).

Compared to normal HOKs, cells from the TSCC lineage, including SCC-9, SCC-4, and SCC-15, exhibited significantly elevated PDPN protein and messenger RNA (mRNA) levels (P<0.05; Figure 3A,3B). Based on these findings, the SCC-9 cell line, which exhibited relatively high PDPN expression, was selected for KD experiments, whereas the CAL-27 cell line, characterized by relatively low PDPN expression, was used in OE experiments. Successful PDPN knockdown in SCC-9 cells and PDPN OE in CAL-27 cells were confirmed by qRT-PCR and Western blotting (Figure S2).

Effect of PDPN-KD and PDPN-OE on TSCC cell adhesion and proliferation

Among SCC-9 cells, the adhesion capacity of cells to collagen I, collagen IV, and fibronectin was significantly reduced following PDPN-KD (shPDPN#1 and shPDPN#2) as compared to treatment with sh-NC (P<0.05) (Figure 4A). Among CAL-27 cells, as compared to treatment with Vector-NC, PDPN-OE markedly enhanced cellular adhesion to collagen I and collagen IV (P<0.01) (Figure 4B).

Regarding cell proliferation, in SCC-9 cells, the proliferative capacity was significantly suppressed 72 h after transfection with PDPN-targeting lentiviral constructs (shPDPN#1 and shPDPN#2) as compared to transfection with sh-NC (P<0.05; Figure 4C). Conversely, in CAL-27 cells, proliferation was significantly promoted 72 h after transfection with the PDPN-OE lentiviral vector as compared to transfection with Vector-NC (P<0.05; Figure 4D).

Effect of PDPN-KD and PDPN-OE on TSCC cell migration and invasion ability

Wound-healing assays demonstrated that the migratory capacity of SCC-9 cells was significantly suppressed following transfection with PDPN-targeting lentiviral constructs (shPDPN#1 and shPDPN#2) as compared to the control condition (P<0.01) (Figure 5A). In contrast, the migration capacity of CAL-27 cells was markedly enhanced after transfection with the PDPN-OE lentiviral vector as compared to Vector-NC (P<0.05; Figure 5B).

Prediction of potential downstream targets regulated by PDPN

Co-expression analysis based on the COEXPEDIA database revealed that genes highly correlated with PDPN included THY1, DCN, ANGPTL2, CTSK, and MMP-2 (Figure 6A). Further validation via the Harmonizome database indicated that GJA1, MMP-2, PCDH18, FIBIN, and DPYSL3 also exhibited strong correlations with PDPN (Figure 6B). In SCC-9 cells, PDPN-KD was shown to significantly downregulate MMP-2 expression, while no evident effect on MMP-9 expression was observed (Figure 6C). Conversely, in CAL-27 cells, PDPN-OE markedly upregulated MMP-2 expression, whereas MMP-9 levels remained largely unchanged (Figure 6D).

PDPN modulated MAPK signaling

Western blotting indicated that PDPN-KD markedly reduced the level of p-ERK in SCC-9 cells, whereas PDPN-OE significantly increased p-ERK levels in CAL-27 cells. In contrast, total ERK (t-ERK) expression remained largely unchanged under both conditions. Meanwhile, no significant changes were observed in the levels of phosphorylated JNK (p-JNK) or p38 (p-p38) in either cell line (Figure 7).

PDPN regulated MMP-2 expression via the MAPK/ERK pathway and influenced cell migration and invasion

Discussion

In this study, PDPN levels were significantly elevated in TSCC tissues as compared to in adjacent nontumorous tissues, with high PDPN levels being associated with advanced TNM stage, poor differentiation, and lymphatic vessel invasion, suggesting a close association with enhanced tumor aggressiveness and malignant progression (19-23). These data were largely consistent with earlier reports on squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (24), esophageal cancer (25), and lung cancer (26), indicating that aberrant upregulation of PDPN may represent a common molecular feature underlying malignant transformation in squamous epithelial-derived tumors (27,28). PDPN is a transmembrane mucin-type glycoprotein, and due to its particular membrane localization, may be involved in tumor cell-microenvironment interactions, regulation of cell adhesion, and transmembrane signal transduction processes (9,29,30).

Functional assays in our study further revealed that PDPN promotes the malignant phenotypes of TSCC. PDPN-KD substantially suppressed the ability of cells to migrate, proliferate, and invade, whereas PDPN-OE produced the opposite effects. In line with this, in vivo xenograft experiments demonstrated that silencing PDPN resulted in reduced tumor volume, weight, and growth rate, indicating that PDPN not only enhances malignant cellular behaviors in vitro but also promotes tumorigenicity in vivo. These findings suggest that the oncogenic role of PDPN is not restricted to the metastatic stage but extends to multiple aspects of tumor growth and progression (31,32).

Notably, it was observed that PDPN selectively enhanced the adhesion of TSCC cells to collagen I and collagen IV. Type IV collagen is a major component of the basement membrane and acts as a physiological barrier that epithelial-derived tumor cells must overcome to invade into the underlying stroma (33). In contrast, type I collagen is predominantly associated with interstitial connective tissue and serves as a principal mechanical scaffold within the tumor microenvironment (34,35). The enhanced adhesion of tumor cells to these key ECM components suggests that PDPN may facilitate more stable anchorage to both the basement membrane and stromal matrix, thereby providing a physical basis for subsequent migration, invasion, and tissue barrier disruption. This finding indicates that PDPN may promote the transition of TSCC from in situ growth to invasive behavior through remodeling of cell-ECM interactions (36,37).

In terms of mechanism, combined bioinformatics analyses and experimental validation identified MMP-2 as a potential downstream effector of PDPN. MMP-2 degrades type IV collagen and other basement membrane proteins, contributing markedly to tumor invasion and metastasis (38). In our study, PDPN-KD was shown to significantly reduce MMP-2 protein expression, whereas PDPN-OE significantly increased its expression. In contrast, MMP-9 expression remained largely unchanged, suggesting a degree of specificity in the PDPN-mediated regulation of MMP-2. These findings, when considered in conjunction with the observed enhancement of collagen adhesion, suggest that PDPN may simultaneously strengthen tumor cell-ECM interactions and promote matrix degradation via the upregulation of MMP-2, thereby enhancing the invasive and migratory capacities of TSCC cells.

Further investigation demonstrated that the regulation of MMP-2 by PDPN is primarily mediated by the MAPK/ERK signaling pathway. It was found that both PDPN-KD and PDPN-OE predominantly affected ERK phosphorylation, whereas no substantial alterations were observed in the JNK or p38 pathways, indicating a branch-specific modulation of MAPK signaling by PDPN. The association between ERK signaling and tumor cell migration, survival, proliferation, and protease levels is well-documented (39,40). To verify its functional relevance, we used the ERK-specific inhibitor SCH772984 and found that it both lowers MMP-2 expression and also effectively abrogates the enhanced migration and invasion induced by PDPN-OE. These findings indicate that ERK is not only correlated with PDPN and MMP-2 alterations but also contributes substantially to the pro-invasive effects mediated by PDPN.

The in vivo data further corroborate the findings regarding this mechanism. In xenograft tumor tissues, PDPN-KD was accompanied by concurrent downregulation of Ki-67, p-ERK, and MMP-2, suggesting that PDPN promotes tumor proliferation in vivo and may drive tumor growth and malignant progression via the ERK/MMP-2 signaling. Combining the in vivo and in vitro findings, we propose a mechanism in which PDPN promotes ERK signaling, which leads to MMP-2 upregulation and ultimately drives proliferation, migration, and invasion of TSCC cells

In terms of translation, the PDPN-ERK-MMP-2 axis may hold clinical relevance. As a membrane-associated protein, PDPN is relatively accessible for detection and may serve as a valuable biomarker for risk stratification and prognostic evaluation among patients with TSCC. Moreover, therapeutic strategies targeting PDPN itself or its downstream ERK/MMP-2 pathway may constitute novel approaches for inhibiting tumor invasion and metastasis. In summary, our findings confirm the presence of PDPN-OE in TSCC, support its association with adverse clinicopathological features, and offer insights into the mechanism by which PDPN promotes malignant progression via ERK/MMP-2 signaling. Overall, PDPN may serve as a valuable biomarker and target for the management of TSCC.

This study has several limitations. First, the clinical analysis was based on a relatively small tissue-microarray cohort from a single source, and only OS could be reliably assessed because detailed information on disease-specific death and recurrence was incomplete. Second, cell proliferation was assessed only by trypan-blue counting without confirmation using an additional proliferation assay. Finally, the xenograft sample size was small, and the subcutaneous model does not fully reproduce the orthotopic tongue tumor microenvironment.

Future studies should validate these findings in larger independent cohorts with complete survival information. Additional proliferation assays, standardized pathological assessment, and orthotopic or patient-derived tumor models would strengthen the current findings. Further studies directly targeting MMP-2 are also warranted to confirm its causal role in the PDPN/ERK/MMP-2 signaling axis and to explore the therapeutic potential of this pathway.

Conclusions

PDPN is overexpressed in TSCC and is associated with adverse clinicopathological features and poorer OS. Functional experiments indicate that PDPN promotes TSCC cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, and invasion through ERK/MMP-2 signaling, while PDPN knockdown suppresses xenograft growth. These findings support further validation of PDPN as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target in TSCC.
