Stochastic Burgers Equation from Non-Product Stationary Measures via a Generalised Second-Order Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle
P. Gonçalves 1 , M. C. Ricciuti 2 , G. M. Schütz 1,3

Abstract
We prove a generalised second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle for conservative interacting particle systems on a lattice whose stationary measures are not of product type and not invariant under particle jumps. The result, which requires neither a spectral gap bound nor an equivalence of ensembles, extends the classical framework to settings with correlated invariant measures and is based on quantitative bounds for the correlation decay. As an application, we show that the equilibrium density fluctuations of the Katz-Lebowitz-Spohn model with a given choice of parameters converge, under diffusive scaling, to the stationary energy solution of the stochastic Burgers equation.



1  Introduction

In
[
BR84
]
, the authors introduced the celebrated
Boltzmann-Gibbs principle
, a fundamental result in the field of statistical physics. Roughly speaking, it states that the space-time fluctuations of any field associated with a conservative model can be projected onto the fluctuation field of the conserved quantity. This result formalises the idea that non-conserved quantities fluctuate in a faster time scale than conserved quantities do, and thus, in the characteristic time scale of the conserved quantity, they average out and only their projection onto the conserved field survives in the limit. In
[
GJ14
]
, the authors extended that result as a
second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle
, which states that the first-order correction of the limit in the classical principle can be written as a quadratic functional of the fluctuation field of the conserved quantity. This refinement allows to show convergence of the equilibrium fluctuations of a large class of weakly-asymmetric diffusive models to the
energy solutions
of the stochastic Burgers or the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation, also introduced in
[
GJ14
]
and whose uniqueness in law was later proved in
[
GP18
]
.

One of the key ingredients of the proof in
[
GJ14
]
consists in the knowledge of a
spectral gap inequality
and an
equivalence of ensembles
for the dynamics. These assumptions are dropped in the subsequent paper
[
GJS17
]
, which provides a proof for fields whose additive functionals can be written as polynomials. However, that work still assumed that the stationary measures are of product form and invariant under permutations of nearest-neighbour coordinates. Under these hypotheses, the stochastic Burgers or KPZ equation could be derived as the scaling limit of the fluctuation field for a large class of particle systems.

Here, we generalise the result and establish what we call the
generalised second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle
for particle systems evolving on a finite lattice with
N
sites, or on the whole
\mathbb{Z}
. As in
[
GJS17
]
, our bound requires neither a spectral gap inequality nor an equivalence of ensembles. Moreover, the stationary measures need not be of product form and invariant under nearest-neighbour swaps, as long as this invariance holds up to a correction of order at most
N^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
, and the
m
-point correlation functions decay at least as fast as
N^{-\beta}
for some
\beta>0
.

Our arguments are based on a multi-scale analysis, first introduced in
[
Gon08
]
and then refined in
[
GJ14
,
GJS17
]
. The method proceeds by induction, progressively replacing averages of occupation variables over boxes of increasing size so that, in the final step, the box reaches a microscopic length proportional to the system size. At each step of the procedure, occupation variables redistribute mass across the corresponding box in a uniform way. However, in the present setting, the lack of variable-swap invariance introduces new error terms that must be controlled using bounds on the correlation functions.

As an application, we consider the Katz-Lebowitz-Spohn model
[
KLS84
]
with a specific choice of parameters. This is an asymmetric exclusion process on the discrete torus with a weak hopping bias of order
N^{-\gamma}
and with next-nearest-neighbour interaction such that the stationary measure is neither product (namely does not factorise over lattice sites) nor invariant under nearest-neighbour swaps. Using the generalised second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle, we show that its equilibrium fluctuations converge to the stationary energy solution of the stochastic Burgers equation. To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes the first derivation of the stochastic Burgers or KPZ equation at stationarity as a scaling limit in such a setting, namely from a discrete model whose stationary measures lack both factorisation and swap invariance.

In a degenerate limit of our parameter choice, the KLS model reduces to the so-called facilitated exclusion process (FEP) for which convergence of the equilibrium fluctuations to the stochastic Burgers equation was proved in
[
EZ24
]
. However, the apparent absence of factorisation of the invariant measure of the FEP merely reflects the fact that, at fixed particle number, it is a uniform distribution of dimers
[
SS04
]
– the natural analogue of the uniform distribution of monomers arising from restricting a product measure to configurations with a fixed number of particles. This uniformity property, a direct consequence of factorisation at fixed particle number, allows one to derive the convergence to the stochastic Burgers equation by standard techniques via a mapping to the zero-range process (whose invariant measure is product)
[
SS04
,
EZ24
]
. This stands in contrast to the parameter regime studied here, where the stationary measure possesses no analogous uniformity property. Moreover, our result provides the first rigorous derivation of predictions from mode-coupling theory for particle systems with non-product invariant measures. Mode-coupling theory, based on the phenomenological framework of non-linear fluctuating hydrodynamics
[
BKS85
]
, predicts for weakly driven systems a crossover from the superdiffusive KPZ universality class to the diffusive Edwards–Wilkinson (EW) class, with a critical exponent
\gamma=\tfrac{1}{2}
at which convergence to the stochastic Burgers equation is conjectured
[
Sch24
]
, as proved below.

The derivation of the stochastic Burgers equation follows the strategy outlined in
[
GJ14
]
: we first prove tightness of the sequence of fluctuation fields and then identify all limit points as energy solutions. In our setting, since the invariant measure is not of product form, additional work is required to control the fluctuation field at the initial time. Under the invariant measure, occupation variables are identically distributed but not independent, preventing a direct application of the classical central limit theorem. Nevertheless, our control of correlation decay yields quantitative bounds on the
\alpha
-mixing coefficients, allowing us to apply a generalised central limit theorem for
\alpha
-mixing sequences and thereby establish the desired Gaussian convergence.

A further technical difficulty stems from the degeneracy of the KLS dynamics considered here, in the sense that the system admits blocked configurations. This phenomenon also appeared in the porous media model studied in
[
GLT09
,
BGS16
]
, and the trick therein was to split the configuration space into “good” and “bad” sets. The good configurations are those that contain at least a mobile cluster – a pair of particles at distance at most two – which allows to construct paths that transport the mass across the whole system. The correlation bounds available here enable us to control contributions on the set of bad configurations. Hence, in order to apply the generalised second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle in this degenerate setting, we successfully combine our argument with the techniques developed in
[
BGS16
]
.

We conclude with a few directions for future work. It would be interesting to analyse the extension of our generalised second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle to the case when the invariant measures are no longer translation invariant. Another challenging problem concerns the extension of our results to non-gradient systems, where the proof is expected to be technically more involved. For the non-gradient KLS model, we expect analogous macroscopic behaviour. A related non-gradient setting that may allow one to address fluctuations near phase transitions is the locally constrained exclusion process
[
GSZ25
]
, where the invariant measure undergoes a transition from a metastable clustered phase – reminiscent of phase separation in systems with long-range interactions
[
KS17
]
– to a poorly understood homogeneous regime approaching the stationary measure of the facilitated exclusion process in a suitable limit.

2  Statement of Results

2.1  The Generalised Second-Order Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle

Our framework is the following. We consider a conservative Markov process
\{\eta_{tN^{a}}^{N},t\geq 0\}
in the accelerated time scale
tN^{a}
with generator
N^{a}\mathcal{L}^{N}
, where
N
is a scaling parameter and
a>0
; to ease the notation, we will frequently drop the superscript
N
and simply denote the process by
\{\eta_{tN^{a}},t\geq 0\}
. This system evolves the discrete lattice
\Lambda_{N}
given by the discrete torus
\mathbb{T}_{N}
with
N
points, which we read as a discretisation of the continuum space
\Lambda
(given by the one-dimensional torus) scaled by a factor
N
.
To simplify the exposition, we assume that the process is of exclusion type, so that its state space is given by
\Omega_{N}:=\{0,1\}^{\Lambda_{N}}
; however, our results can readily be adapted to more general processes evolving on
\mathcal{X}^{\Lambda_{N}}
where
\mathcal{X}
is a
bounded
space. For
x,y\in\Lambda_{N}
, let
\eta^{x,y}
denote the configuration obtained from
\eta\in\Omega_{N}
by swapping the states
\eta(x)
and
\eta(y)
, namely

\eta^{x,y}(z):=\begin{cases}\eta(y)&z=x,\\
\eta(x)&z=y,\\
\eta(z)&z\neq x,y.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Observe that, above, we read
\eta^{N,N+1}
as swapping the variables
\eta(N)
and
\eta(1)
, as we identify the sites
N+1
and
1
. Throughout, we will mainly use the notation
\eta_{x}
to denote the occupation variable
\eta(x)
.

2.1.1
Assumptions

Assumption 1
(Stationary Measures).
The model admits a family of stationary measures
\{\nu_{\rho}^{N}\}_{\rho\in I}
for some
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, and for each
\rho\in I
we assume that:

(a)

\nu_{\rho}^{N}
is invariant by translation, so that, denoting by
E_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}[\,\cdot\,]
the expectation with respect to
\nu_{\rho}^{N}
, we have that
E_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}[\eta_{x}]=:\rho_{N}
for each
x\in\Lambda_{N}
;

(b)

the measure
\nu_{\rho}^{N}
is invariant under permutations of nearest-neighbouring coordinates up to a correction of order at most
N^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
, and this correction depends on the centred configuration only
locally
and
polynomially
up to an error of order at most
N^{-2\alpha}
: namely, calling
\bar{\eta}_{x}
the centred random variable
\eta_{x}-\rho_{N}
, we can write

\frac{\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta)}=1+N^{-\alpha}\sum_{\Theta\subset\{x-m_{1},\ldots,x+m_{2}\}}c_{\Theta}\prod_{j\in\Theta}\bar{\eta}_{j}+O(N^{-2\alpha}),

(2.2)

where
m_{1}\geq 0,m_{2}\geq 1
and
\{c_{\Theta}\}_{\Theta}
are constants independent of
N
.

This assumption may seem restrictive, but is actually satisfied by a large class of models; for example, any Gibbs measures of a Hamiltonian with short-range polynomial interactions and with temperature scaling as
N^{\alpha}
satisfies (
2.2
). As we shall see, we can get a better bound for models for which this polynomial has an additional property:

Assumption 1a
(Stationary Measures).
The model satisfies
Assumption 1
, and the expression in (
2.2
) is such that, defining the coefficients
\{c_{\Theta}^{\prime}\}_{\Theta}
via

\begin{split}\left[\frac{\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta)}-1\right](\bar{\eta}_{x}-\bar{\eta}_{x+1})&=:N^{-\alpha}\sum_{\Theta\subset\{x-m_{1},\ldots,x+m_{2}\}}c_{\Theta}^{\prime}\prod_{j\in\Theta}\bar{\eta}_{j}\\
&\phantom{:=}+O(N^{-2\alpha}),\end{split}

(2.3)

these satisfy
\{\Theta:\Theta\neq\varnothing\wedge c_{\Theta}^{\prime}\neq 0\}\neq\varnothing
.

The term on the left-hand side of (
2.3
) will naturally appear in the proof of our main results, as a consequence of the fact that the underlying measure is not invariant for the swap of variables
\eta\mapsto\eta^{x,x+1}
, which means that every time we make that change of variables, we have a price to pay which is measured in the last assumption. Therefore, this term will appear repeatedly in the proof of our Boltzmann-Gibbs principle, namely Theorem
2.1
. In essence,
Assumption 1a
asserts not only that this expression decays as
N^{-\alpha}
, while being local and polynomial in the centred occupation variables, but also that its constant term vanishes. Consequently, when multiplying two such expressions, provided the sites involved are sufficiently far apart, no constant term survives and only products of occupation variables remain. Together with suitable decay estimates for correlations, this feature allows us to sharpen certain upper bounds. Our next assumption then concerns the asymptotic behaviour of the
m
-point correlation functions under the stationary measures
\{\nu_{\rho}^{N}\}_{\rho\in I}
.

Assumption 2
(Correlation Functions).
For each
\rho\in I
and for each integer
m\geq 2
, the
m
-point correlation functions of
\nu_{\rho}^{N}
decay at least as fast as
N^{-\beta}
, namely, for each
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
in
\Lambda_{N}
pairwise distinct,

\left|E_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}\bar{\eta}_{x_{j}}\right]\right|\lesssim N^{-\beta}

for some
\beta>0
.

We note that, above, the decay of the
m
-point correlation function is given in terms of a parameter
\beta
which in fact usually depends on the value of
m
, as the higher the value of
m
, the faster is the decay to zero of the correlation function; however, here we do not need to precise such dependence.

Now let
\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\nu)
denote the
Dirichlet form
of the process
\{\eta_{t}^{N},t\geq 0\}
with respect to a measure
\nu
on
\Omega_{N}
, defined by

\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\nu):=-\int_{\Omega_{N}}f\mathcal{L}^{N}f\de\nu(\eta),

(2.4)

and, for each
x\in\Lambda_{N}
, let
\tau_{x}
denote the translation operator by
x
, so that
\tau_{x}\eta_{\cdot}=\eta_{\cdot+x}
.

Assumption 3
(Dirichlet Form).
There exists a function
q_{0,1}^{N}:\Omega_{N}\to[\delta,\frac{1}{\delta}]
with
\delta>0
such that, for each
\rho\in I
, the Dirichlet form
\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\nu_{\rho}^{N})
can be written as

\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\nu_{\rho}^{N})=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}q_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta)\left[f(\eta^{x,x+1})-f(\eta)\right]^{2}\de\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta),

where
q_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta):=q_{0,1}^{N}(\tau_{x}\eta)
.

2.1.2
Main Result

Throughout, we will denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}
the probability measure on the space of càdlàg trajectories from
[0,T]
to
\Omega_{N}

While our results are stated for

2.2  Application to the KLS Model

Our second contribution is the application of Theorem
2.1
to a specific model which does not satisfy the assumptions of the classical second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs principle
[
GJ14
,
GJS17
]
, as its stationary measures are neither product nor invariant for particle jumps. In particular, we show that the equilibrium fluctuations of the Katz-Lebowitz-Spohn (KLS) model, for a given choice of parameters, converge to an appropriately defined solution of the stochastic Burgers equation.

2.2.1
Dynamics and Invariant Measure

The
KLS model
, introduced in
[
KLS84
]
, is a Markov process of exclusion type evolving on
\mathbb{T}_{N}
– so that its state space is
\Omega_{N}:=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{T}_{N}}
– whose generator acts on functions
f:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
via

\mathcal{L}^{N}f(\eta)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta)[f(\eta^{x,x+1})-f(\eta)],

where
\eta^{x,x+1}
is defined in (
2.1
) and where

\begin{split}&c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta):=\\
&p_{N}\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1})\{(1+\kappa_{N})(1-\eta_{x-1}+\eta_{x+2})+(1+\varepsilon_{N})\eta_{x-1}+(1-\varepsilon_{N})\eta_{x+2}\}\\
&+q_{N}\eta_{x+1}(1-\eta_{x})\{(1+\kappa_{N})(1-\eta_{x-1}+\eta_{x+2})+(1-\varepsilon_{N})\eta_{x-1}+(1+\varepsilon_{N})\eta_{x+2}\}.\end{split}

(2.8)

Here,
p_{N},q_{N}\geq 0
and
\varepsilon_{N},\kappa_{N}\in[-1,1]
. The dynamics can be described as follows: a particle at
x
waits, independently of the others, a random exponential time of parameter 1, after which it jumps to a neighbouring site
y=x\pm 1
with rate
c^{N}_{x,y}(\eta)
; in particular, if the site is unoccupied, then the jump is suppressed.

For any choice of the parameters
p_{N},q_{N},\varepsilon_{N}
and
\kappa_{N}
, the Gibbs measure

\nu_{N}(\eta):\propto\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon_{N}}{1-\varepsilon_{N}}\right)^{-H(\eta)}

(2.9)

with Hamiltonian

H(\eta):=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{y}\eta_{y+1}

is invariant for the dynamics; see
[
NS25
, Section 4.1]
.

Note that, for
\kappa_{N}=-1
and
\varepsilon_{N}=0
, this corresponds to the
porous media model
, namely the model with degenerate rates and whose hydrodynamic limit in the symmetric case and evolving on
\mathbb{T}_{N}
was studied in
[
GLT09
]
, and equilibrium fluctuations for the system evolving on
\mathbb{Z}
was studied in
[
BGS16
]
. For this model, the authors of
[
BGS16
]
– after showing that one can restrict to an appropriate set of configurations – applied the second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle of
[
GJS17
]
and showed that, under diffusive scaling (
a=2
), the equilibrium density fluctuations converge to the stationary energy solution of the stochastic Burgers equation. Indeed, if
\varepsilon_{N}=0
, then the measure (
2.9
) becomes the uniform measure, and indeed one can show that, both on
\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{T}_{N}
, the Bernoulli product measures of any parameter
\rho\in(0,1)
are invariant for the dynamics, so that the model falls into the assumptions of
[
GJS17
]
. However, when
\varepsilon_{N}\neq 0
, (
2.9
) is neither product nor invariant for particle jumps, so that those assumptions fail. On the other hand, when

\kappa_{N}=-1
and
\varepsilon_{N}=1
, the KLS model reduces to the
facilitated exclusion process and, as previously discussed, the measure is then uniform for any fixed particle number (interpreted as dimers), so that – up to the technicality of equivalence of ensembles – this model also effectively falls into the assumptions of
[
GJS17
]
.

Remark 2.5
.

The specific choice of
\varepsilon_{N}
is only out of convenience; our method actually works for any choice of
\varepsilon_{N}
such that
|\varepsilon_{N}|\lesssim N^{-\alpha}
.

Roughly speaking, the dynamics are those of a weakly asymmetric exclusion process on the discrete torus but with jumps from
x
that can only be performed if at least one site “close” to
x
is occupied, and in particular

1.

for right jumps, the rate
p_{N}
gets enhanced if at least the site to the left of
x
is occupied, and reduced if only that at distance
2
to the right of
x
is occupied;

2.

for left jumps, the rate
q_{N}
gets enhanced if at least the site at distance
2
to the right of
x
is occupied, and reduced if only that to the left of
x
is occupied;

if both are unoccupied, the jump is suppressed. In fact, the choice
\kappa_{N}=-1
makes the dynamics particularly interesting because in this case, as just highlighted, there are “blocked” configurations which need to be studied, just as in the porous media model. On the other hand, if
\kappa_{N}\neq 1
, one can check that the symmetric part of the current
cannot
be expressed as a gradient. Thus, the model would lie outside the standard framework and would require techniques different from those presented here.

2.2.2
Stationary Energy Solutions of the Stochastic Burgers Equation

In order to state our convergence result, we first recall the notion of stationary energy solutions of the stochastic Burgers equation, introduced in
[
GJ14
]
. The definitions and results that follow are taken from
[
GJ14
,
GJS17
,
GP18
]
.

Fix a finite time horizon
[0,T]
and let
\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
denote the set of real-valued smooth functions on the torus
\mathbb{T}
, and
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
the space of continuous paths taking values in the dual
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
.

Definition 2.6

(Stationarity)
.

We say that a process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with trajectories in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
is
stationary
if, for each
t\in[0,T]
, the
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
-valued random variable
\mathscr{Y}_{t}
is a white noise of fixed variance.

Let
\{\iota_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
denote an approximation of the identity on
\mathbb{T}
: for each
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, we define the process
\{\mathscr{B}_{t}^{\varepsilon},t\in[0,T]\}
via

\mathscr{B}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\mathscr{Y}_{s}\ast\iota_{\varepsilon}(u)\right)^{2}\nabla\phi(u)\de u\de s,

where
\ast
denotes the convolution operator. Moreover, for
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, define
\mathscr{B}_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi):=\mathscr{B}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi)-\mathscr{B}_{s}^{\varepsilon}(\phi)
. We will also denote by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}
the norm defined by

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\phi(u)^{2}\de u

for
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
.

Definition 2.7

(Energy Estimate)
.

We say that a stochastic process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with trajectories in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
satisfies an
energy estimate
if there exists a finite constant
\kappa>0
such that, for any
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, any
0<\delta\leq\varepsilon<1
and any
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathscr{B}_{s,t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi)-\mathscr{B}_{s,t}^{\delta}(\phi)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\kappa\varepsilon(t-s)\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}.

The following holds.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
be a stochastic process with trajectories in the space
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
. Assume that
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
is stationary and satisfies an energy estimate. Then the process
\{\mathscr{B}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
taking values in
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
and given by

\mathscr{B}_{t}(\phi):=\mylim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathscr{B}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi)

(2.13)

is well defined and, for any
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and any
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, it satisfies the estimate

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathscr{B}_{t}(\phi)-\mathscr{B}_{s}(\phi)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\tilde{\kappa}\varepsilon(t-s)^{\frac{3}{2}}\|\nabla\phi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}

for some constant
\tilde{\kappa}>0
.

The proposition above gives meaning to the square of a distribution-valued process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
, provided it satisfies Definition
2.7
, as highlighted in the following.

Definition 2.9

(Stationary Energy Solution of the Stochastic Burgers Equation)
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\nu,\sigma>0
, and let
\{\mathscr{W}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
be a
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
-valued Brownian motion. We say that a stochastic processes
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
with trajectories in
\mathcal{C}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
is a
stationary energy solution
of the stochastic Burgers equation

\partial_{t}\mathscr{Y}_{t}=\nu\Delta\mathscr{Y}_{t}+\lambda\nabla(\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{2})+\sqrt{2\nu\sigma^{2}}\nabla\dot{\mathscr{W}}_{t}

(2.14)

if:

1.

for each
t\in[0,T]
, the
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
-valued random variable
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
is a white noise of variance
\sigma^{2}
,

2.

the process
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
satisfies an energy estimate,

3.

for any
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, the process
\{\mathscr{M}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
defined by

\mathscr{M}_{t}(\phi)=\mathscr{Y}_{t}(\phi)-\mathscr{Y}_{0}(\phi)-\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}(\nu\Delta\phi)\de s+\lambda\mathscr{B}_{t}(\phi)

is a one-dimensional Brownian motion of variance
2\nu\sigma^{2}\|\nabla\phi\|_{|L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}
, with
\{\mathscr{B}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
defined as in (
2.13
),

4.

the reversed processes
\{\mathscr{Y}_{T-t},\mathscr{B}_{T-t}-\mathscr{B}_{T});\,t\in[0,T]\}
also satisfy item iii), but with
\lambda
replaced by
-\lambda
.

It is a known fact that the notion of an energy solution from Definition
2.9
, which is slightly different from the one originated in
[
GJ14
]
due to item iv), gives in fact uniqueness in law:

Proposition 2.10
.

If
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
and
\{\mathscr{Y}^{\prime}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
are both stationary energy solutions of (
2.14
), they have the same law.

The proof of the proposition above can be found in
[
GP18
]
.

2.2.3
Convergence of Equilibrium Fluctuations

Our process of interest is the diffusively-scaled
density fluctuation field

\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N},t\geq 0\}
, taking values in
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
and defined, for each
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, by

\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N}(\phi):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\phi\left(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}t}{N}\right)\bar{\eta}_{tN^{2}}^{N}(x).

Here,
\bar{\eta}_{tN^{2}}^{N}(x)
is the centred random variable
\eta_{tN^{2}}^{N}(x)-\bar{\rho}_{N}
, where
\bar{\rho}_{N}:=E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}[\eta_{x}^{N}]
, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{N}
is the
transport velocity
of the fluctuations. In particular, let

\boldsymbol{v}_{N}:=2bN^{2-\gamma}\bar{\rho}_{N}\left(2-3\bar{\rho}_{N}\right).

(2.15)

This choice of velocity will become clear in Section
4.2
, but we anticipate that its role is to remove the degree-one terms of the microscopic current, so that the relevant ones are the quadratic terms. Fix a finite time horizon
[0,T]
, and let
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
denote the space of càdlàg paths taking values in
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Assume that
\gamma\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\alpha\geq\frac{4}{3}
. The sequence of processes
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
converges in distribution with respect to the Skorohod topology of
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
, and:

1.

if
\gamma>\frac{1}{2}
, its limit is the stationary solution of the infinite-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation

\partial_{t}\mathscr{Y}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mathscr{Y}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\dot{\mathscr{W}}_{t};

(2.16)

2.

if
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}
, its limit is the stationary energy solution of the stochastic Burgers equation

\partial_{t}\mathscr{Y}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta\mathscr{Y}_{t}-b\nabla(\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{2})+\frac{1}{2}\nabla\dot{\mathscr{W}}_{t},

(2.17)

where
\{\mathscr{W}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
is an
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
-valued Brownian motion.

The restriction
\alpha\geq\frac{4}{3}
is due to the fact that, from Lemma
4.3
,
Assumption 2
is verified for
\beta\geq\alpha
; plugging this in (
2.6
) with
L=\varepsilon N
then yields the lower bound for
\alpha
.

Our proof strategy follows the standard program: we write the martingale equation for the fluctuation field and establish tightness in
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
for each of its terms. The main difficulty, as expected, lies in handling the degree-2 term, for which we invoke Theorem
2.1
. However, as emphasised in Section
2.2.1
,
Assumption 3
is not satisfied for this model, since the rates may be degenerate. Most of the work in Section
4
is therefore devoted to showing that one can restrict to a suitable set of configurations on which the assumption does hold.

3  Proof of the Generalised Second-Order Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle

In this section we work under
Assumptions 1, 2
and
3
and we revisit the steps used in
[
GJS17
]
to prove the second-order Boltzmann-Gibbs Principle, based on the one-block-two-block scheme introduced in
[
GPV88
]
. Compared to the strategy therein, our method consists in adding a correction – right before using the Kipnis-Varadhan inequality – that takes into account the non-invariance of the stationary measure for particle jumps. The error term coming from this correction will then yield an additional term in all of our bounds depending on the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
defined in
Assumptions 1
and
2
.

3.1  One-Block Estimate

Lemma 3.1

(One-Block Estimate)
.

Let
1\leq\ell_{0}\leq N
and let
\varphi:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function of the form
\varphi(\eta)=\cev{\eta}^{\ell}_{0}
for some
1\leq\ell\leq N
.

1.

There exists a constant
C
independent of
N
such that, for any
t>0
and any
G:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}<\infty
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{a}})\left[\bar{\eta}_{sN^{a}}(x+1)-\vec{\eta}^{\ell_{0}}_{sN^{a}}(x)\right]\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Ct\ell_{0}^{2}\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{N^{a-1}\ell}+\frac{1}{N^{a+\beta-1}}+\frac{t(\ell+\ell_{0})}{N^{2\alpha-1}\ell}+\frac{t}{N^{2\alpha+\beta-2}}+\frac{t}{N^{3\alpha-2}}\right\};

2.

if, additionally,
Assumption 1a
holds, then the bound can be improved to

Ct\ell_{0}^{2}\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}\left\{\frac{1}{N^{a-1}\ell}+\frac{1}{N^{a+\beta-1}}+\frac{t(\ell+\ell_{0})}{N^{2\alpha-1}\ell_{0}\ell}+\frac{t}{N^{2\alpha+\beta-2}}+\frac{t}{N^{3\alpha-2}}\right\}.

The same result holds by substituting
\left[\bar{\eta}_{x+1}-\vec{\eta}^{\ell_{0}}_{x}\right]
with
\left[\bar{\eta}_{x}-\cev{\eta}^{\ell_{0}}_{x}\right]
, as long as
\varphi
is of the form
\varphi(\eta)=\vec{\eta}^{\ell}_{0}
for some
1\leq\ell\leq N
.

Remark 3.2
.

Before proving this lemma, let us emphasise that, unlike the analogous result
[
GJS17
, Proposition 5]
, here (and later on) we impose a specific structure on the function
\varphi
, rather than merely assuming that its support does not intersect the set
\{0,\ldots,\ell_{0}\}
. This additional requirement stems from the fact that our stationary measures are not of product type, so some information on
\varphi
is needed in order to estimate the “crossed” terms involving it. In fact,
\varphi
need not have the exact form we impose: any function satisfying the support condition together with an explicit polynomial representation in terms of local, centred occupation variables would suffice. Nevertheless, our chosen structure as a local average is convenient and sufficient to establish Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Throughout,
C
will denote a generic positive constant independent of
N
but changing from time to time or even from line to line. For each
z\in\Lambda_{N}
, let

\sigma_{z}=\sigma_{z}(\eta):=\frac{\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta^{z,z+1})}{\nu_{\rho}^{N}(\eta)}.

(3.1)

The key observation is that we can write

\displaystyle\bar{\eta}_{x+1}-\vec{\eta}^{\ell_{0}}_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}{\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]

(3.2)

\displaystyle=\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})+\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0}),

(3.3)

where

\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0}):=\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]\left[\frac{1+\sigma_{z}}{2}\right]

and

\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0}):=\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]\left[\frac{1-\sigma_{z}}{2}\right].

By the inequality
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
, the expectation in the statement is bounded from above by

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{a}})\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta_{sN^{a}},\ell_{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

(3.4)

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{a}})\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta_{sN^{a}},\ell_{0})\de s\right)^{2}\right].

(3.5)

We start with (
3.4
). By the Kipnis-Varadhan inequality
[
KLO12
, Lemma 2.4]
, this term satisfies

(
3.4
)

\displaystyle\leq Ct\left\|\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})\right\|_{-1}^{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=Ct\mysup_{f\in L^{2}(\nu_{\rho}^{N})}\bigg\{2\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})f(\eta)\de\nu_{\rho}^{N}-N^{a}\mathscr{D}_{N}\left(f,\nu_{\rho}^{N}\right)\bigg\}.\end{split}

(3.6)

Now note that, by the equality
\eqref{eq:factor_one}=\eqref{eq:factor_sigmas}
, we can write
\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})
as

\vec{\Sigma}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})=\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]-\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0}).

(3.7)

Then, the integral term in (
3.6
) corresponding to the sum on the right-hand side of (

) can be written as

\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]\right)f(\eta)\de\nu_{\rho}^{N}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]\right)\left[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{z,z+1})\right]\de\nu_{\rho}^{N}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]\right)\left[f(\eta)+f(\eta^{z,z+1})\right]\de\nu_{\rho}^{N}.

By Young’s inequality, for any set of positive real numbers
\{A_{x}\}_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}
we have that (

) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\frac{A_{x}}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}q^{N}_{z,z+1}(\eta)\left[f(\eta)-f(\eta^{z,z+1})\right]^{2}\de\nu_{\rho}^{N}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\ell_{0}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}\sum_{y=x+2}^{x+\ell_{0}}\sum_{z=x+1}^{y-1}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\frac{1}{2A_{x}}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)^{2}\frac{\left[\bar{\eta}_{z}-\bar{\eta}_{z+1}\right]^{2}}{q_{z,z+1}^{N}(\eta)}\de\nu_{\rho}^{N},

where

is defined in

. Now, using

for each

\displaystyle\leq Ct^{2}E_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\left(\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})\right)^{2}\right]

(3.12)

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+Ct^{2}\sum_{|x-y|>\ell+\ell_{0}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)G\left(\frac{y}{N}\right)E_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\varphi(\tau_{y}\eta)\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})\vec{\Lambda}_{y}(\eta,\ell_{0})\right].

1.

, we see that

\displaystyle E_{\nu_{\rho}^{N}}\left[\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)^{2}\vec{\Lambda}_{x}(\eta,\ell_{0})^{2}\right]

.

The last preliminary result that we need in order to show Theorem

consists in the following lemma, which is known in the literature as the

as it involves a multi-scale analysis, which consists in replacing averages of particles in increasingly larger boxes. This lemma gives an upper bound on the price for one of these replacements, namely for going from a box of size
\ell_{0}
to a larger one of size
L

.

4  Equilibrium Fluctuations of the KLS Model

In this section we show Theorem
2.11
. Our approach follows the classical scheme: one first writes the discrete martingale equation for the density fluctuation field
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}
and shows that the sequence of measures
\{\mathcal{Q}_{N}\}_{N}
is tight in
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
with respect to the Skorohod topology, and then proceeds to characterise its limit point
\mathcal{Q}
as the law of an appropriate equation. As the tightness argument is standard, we only focus on the characterisation of the limit point and especially on the quadratic term in the martingale equation, which is the one that calls for Theorem
2.1
. Note, however, that as discussed in Section
2.2.1
, the model does
not
satisfy
Assumption 3
of the generalised second-order Boltzmann–Gibbs principle, since the rates may be degenerate. We will therefore show that the analysis can be restricted to a suitable set of configurations on which the assumption does hold.

Throughout, we will denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}
the probability measure on the space of càdlàg trajectories from
[0,T]
to
\Omega_{N}
corresponding to the process
\{\eta_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}
started at its stationary measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
, and by
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}[\,\cdot\,]
expectations with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}
.

4.1  Properties of the Invariant Measure

We state here some results about the stationary measure

, introduced in (

), whose proofs are postponed to Appendix

.

The first one regards the asymptotic behaviour of the partition function (

):

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\alpha>1
, then the partition function
Z_{N}^{\alpha}
satisfies

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\frac{Z_{N}^{\alpha}}{2^{N}}=1.

We will also be using the following facts about the asymptotic average of occupation variables and about the correlation functions. Note that, unlike Lemma
4.1
, these do not require the restriction
\alpha>1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
,

\left|E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}[\eta_{x}]-\frac{1}{2}\right|\lesssim N^{-\alpha}.

In particular,
\mylim_{N\to\infty}\bar{\rho}_{N}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
m\geq 2
and, given
x_{1}<\ldots<x_{m}\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, let
d_{j}:=x_{j+1}-x_{j}
for each
j=1,\ldots,m
, where
x_{m+1}:=x_{1}+N
(so that, in particular,
d_{1}+\ldots+d_{m}=N
). Then,

\left|E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}\bar{\eta}_{x_{j}}\right]\right|\lesssim N^{-\alpha d},

(4.1)

where
d:=\mymin\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}\}
.

We highlight that, in the result above, the number of sites
m
does not appear on the right-hand side of (
4.1
), as the bound only depends on the minimum distance between any pair of considered points.

Finally, the result that follows guarantees that any limit point
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
of the sequence of fields
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
satisfies item i) of Definition
2.9
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
g:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous map: for any
\alpha>0
, under the stationary measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
, the random variable

\mathscr{X}^{N}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}g\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)\bar{\eta}_{x}

converges in distribution to a mean-zero Gaussian of variance
\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{T}}g(u)^{2}\de u
.

4.2  Dynkin’s Martingales

By Dynkin’s formula (see for example
[
KL99
, Appendix 1]
), for any
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, the processes
\{\mathscr{M}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
and
\{\mathscr{N}^{N}_{t}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
defined via

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi):=\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N}(\phi)-\mathscr{Y}_{0}^{N}(\phi)-\int_{0}^{t}(N^{2}\mathcal{L}^{N}+\partial_{s})\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\de s,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}_{t}^{N}(\phi):=\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi)^{2}-\int_{0}^{t}\left\{N^{2}\mathcal{L}^{N}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\phi)^{2}-2N^{2}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\mathcal{L}^{N}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\right\}\de s

(4.3)

are both martingales with respect to the natural filtration of
\{\mathscr{Y}_{t}^{N}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
.

A simple computation shows that, for each
\phi\in\mathfrak{D}(\mathbb{T})
, the predictable quadratic variation of
\{\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N}(\phi),t\in[0,T]\}
in (
4.3
) can be written as

\left\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(\phi)\right\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta_{sN^{2}})[\eta_{sN^{2}}(x)-\eta_{sN^{2}}(x+1)]^{2}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}\de s.

Recalling the choice of rates

\begin{split}c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta)&=\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{b}{2N^{\gamma}}\Bigr)\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1})\{(\eta_{x-1}+\eta_{x+2})+\varepsilon_{N}(\eta_{x-1}-\eta_{x+2})\}\\
&\phantom{=}+\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{b}{2N^{\gamma}}\Bigr)\eta_{x+1}(1-\eta_{x})\{(\eta_{x-1}+\eta_{x+2})-\varepsilon_{N}(\eta_{x-1}-\eta_{x+2})\}\end{split}

(4.4)

and using Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
, it is immediate to see that

\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\left\langle\mathscr{M}^{N}(\phi)\right\rangle_{t}\right]=\mylim_{N\to\infty}2t\bar{\rho}_{N}^{2}(1-\bar{\rho}_{N})\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}=\frac{t}{4}\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.

By a standard argument (see for example
[
JS03
, Theorem VIII.3.11]
), this implies that
\{\mathscr{M}_{t}^{N},t\in[0,T]\}_{N}
converges in distribution in
\mathcal{D}([0,T],\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T}))
to
\frac{1}{2}\nabla\dot{\mathscr{W}}_{t}
, with
\{\mathscr{W}_{t},t\in[0,T]\}
a
\mathfrak{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{T})
-valued Brownian motion. Moreover one can check that, defining

h(\eta):=\eta_{0}\eta_{1}+\eta_{0}\eta_{-1}-\eta_{-1}\eta_{1}

and

g(\eta):=\eta_{0}\eta_{-1}+\eta_{0}\eta_{1}+\eta_{-1}\eta_{1}-2\eta_{-1}\eta_{0}\eta_{1},

the integral term in (
4.2
) can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle(N^{2}\mathcal{L}^{N}+\partial_{s})\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\phi)\de s=-\frac{\boldsymbol{v}_{N}}{N\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\phi^{\prime}\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x)\de s

(4.5)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)h(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s

(4.6)

\displaystyle+(4b\bar{\rho}_{N}-6b\bar{\rho}_{N}^{2})N^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x)\de s

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&+(b-3b\bar{\rho}_{N})N^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)\times\\
&\phantom{+(b-3b\bar{\rho}_{N})N^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}}\times[\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x+1)+\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x+2)]\de s\end{split}

(4.8)

\displaystyle-2bN^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)[\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x+1)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x+2)]\de s

(4.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&+\frac{\varepsilon_{N}}{2\sqrt{N}}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\Delta_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)g(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s\\
&+b\varepsilon_{N}N^{\frac{1}{2}-\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nabla_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\boldsymbol{v}_{N}s}{N}\Bigr)\eta_{sN^{2}}(x)[\eta_{sN^{2}}(x+1)-\eta_{sN^{2}}(x+2)]\de s\end{split}

plus a term which vanishes in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}})
as
N\to\infty
.

Since
|\varepsilon_{N}|\lesssim N^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha>1
, it is immediate to see that the two bottom lines of the equation above vanish in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}})
as
N\to\infty
. Now, by the choice of the transport velocity
\boldsymbol{v_{N}}
in (
2.15
) and a Taylor expansion of
\phi
, we see that
\eqref{eq:velocity_term}+\eqref{eq:linear_term}
vanishes in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}})
as
N\to\infty
. In fact, the transport velocity was chosen exactly so as to “kill” these two degree-one terms, which would otherwise diverge. The linear term (
4.6
) can be treated as in
[
BGS16
, Section 4a]
, and in particular it can be rewritten as

-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}2\bar{\rho}_{N}\Delta_{N}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)\bar{\eta}_{sN^{2}}(x)\de s=-\bar{\rho}_{N}\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{Y}_{s}^{N}(\Delta_{N}\phi)\de s

plus a term which vanishes in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}})
. Using Lemma
4.2
, it is standard to conclude that this yields the diffusive contribution in (
2.16
) or (
2.17
). Finally, once the quadratic term has been handled, the cubic term (
4.9
) can be treated in the same way as in
[
BGS16
, Lemma 6.2]
, and in particular, one shows that it vanishes in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}})
as
N\to\infty
. Indeed, in light of Remark
3.2
, all the lemmas in Section
3
extend to more general functions
\varphi
, provided they are of local polynomial form and satisfy the support condition. For a detailed treatment we refer the reader to
[
BGS16
]
, and omit the proof here.

The most challenging term is indeed the quadratic one, namely (
4.8
), which we will treat in the two sections that follow.

4.3  Restriction of Configurations

As previously noted and similarly to the porous media model,
Assumption 3
of Theorem
2.1
does not hold because of the potential degeneracy of the rates, so we first need to show that we can restrict (
4.8
) to an appropriate set of configurations. The idea is to split terms taking into account sets that have at least two particles at a distance at most two – what we call a
mobile cluster
– and their complement. We will call the former
good configurations
, namely configurations that have at least one mobile cluster, and the latter
bad configurations
.

We start by noting that, from Lemma
4.1
, we can deduce the following straightforward corollary. Following
[
BGS16
, Section 2.3]
, fix
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
and an integer
1\leq\ell\leq N
: we define the set of
good configurations
inside the box
\Lambda_{x}^{\ell}:=\{x+1,\ldots,x+\ell\}
of size
\ell
to the right of
x
as

\mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x)=\left\{\eta\in\Omega_{N}:\sum_{y=x}^{x+\ell-2}\eta_{y}(\eta_{y+1}+\eta_{y+2})+\eta_{x+\ell-1}\eta_{x+\ell}>0\right\},

(4.10)

as well as its complement of
bad configurations
as

\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x):=\mathscr{G}_{\ell}(x)^{c}=\left\{\eta:\sum_{y=x}^{x+\ell-2}\eta_{y}(\eta_{y+1}+\eta_{y+2})+\eta_{x+\ell-1}\eta_{x+\ell}=0\right\}.

(4.11)

Corollary 4.5
.

If
\alpha>1
, for each
x\in\mathbb{T_{N}}
, the
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
-measure of the bad configurations
\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x)
decays exponentially in
\ell
, and in particular

\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x))\lesssim 2^{-\lfloor\frac{\ell}{3}\rfloor}.

(4.12)

Proof.

Let
\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}
denote the Bernoulli product measure on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{T}_{N}}
of parameter
\frac{1}{2}
, namely
\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}\{\eta_{x}=1\}=\frac{1}{2}
. Note that, if
\eta\in\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x)
, then any two particles in the box
\Lambda_{x}^{\ell}
are at least at distance
3
, so that, if we divide
\Lambda_{x}^{\ell}
into
\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{3}\right\rfloor
consecutive segments of size
3
, each of these has at most one particle, and thus

\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x))\leq\left(4\cdot\frac{1}{2^{3}}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{\ell}{3}\rfloor}.

But now, by Lemma
4.1
, we have that

\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x))=\frac{1}{Z_{N}^{\alpha}}\sum_{\eta\in\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x)}e^{-N^{-\alpha}H(\eta)}\lesssim\frac{|\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x)|}{2^{N}}=\mu_{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathscr{B}_{\ell}(x)),

which completes the proof.
∎

Corollary
4.5
easily implies that we can discard a set of configurations
\eta
where the rates
c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta)
are potentially degenerate, which is the content of the next result.

Lemma 4.6

(Restriction of Configurations)
.

Let
\varphi:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. There exists a constant
C
such that, for any
t>0
, any positive integer
N
and any positive integers
\ell_{0},\ell\leq N
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}&\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s\right)^{2}\right]\leq Ct^{2}N^{2}2^{-\lfloor\frac{\ell_{0}}{3}\rfloor}\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}\mysup_{\eta\in\Omega_{N}}\varphi(\eta)^{2}\\
&+2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{G}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s\right)^{2}\right].\end{split}

(4.13)

Proof.

Recall the definition of good and bad configurations given in (
4.10
) and (
4.11
): by the inequality
(x+y)^{2}\leq 2x^{2}+2y^{2}
, for any integers
1\leq\ell_{0},\ell\leq N
we can bound the expectation in the statement from above by

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{G}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s\right)^{2}\right].

(4.14)

Consider (
4.14
): since the measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
is invariant for the dynamics, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and a standard convexity inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta_{sN^{2}})\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta_{sN^{2}})\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta)\de s\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}N\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)^{2}E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)^{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta)\right]

\displaystyle\leq t^{2}N^{2}\|G\|^{2}_{2,N}\mysup_{\eta\in\Omega_{N}}\varphi(\eta)^{2}\mysup_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\mathscr{B}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)).

By Corollary
4.5
, we are done.
∎

Finally, the next result is an adaptation of
[
BGS16
, Lemma 5.7]
and demonstrates how the presence of a “good box” lets us in fact get around the potential degeneracy of the rates, and more specifically, allows us to repeat all the arguments in Section
3
as long as we restrict to the presence of such a box.

Lemma 4.7

(Path Lemma)
.

Given
y,z\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, define

\sigma_{y,z}=\sigma_{y,z}(\eta):=\frac{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\eta^{y,z})}{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\eta)}.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\nu_{N}^{\alpha})
, any positive integers
N
and
\ell_{0},\ell\leq\frac{N}{2}
, any
y,z\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
and any mean-zero local function
\varphi:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\varphi(\eta^{y,z})=\varphi(\eta)
for each
\eta\in\Omega_{N}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|2\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}G\left(\frac{x}{N}\right)\varphi(\tau_{x}\eta)[\eta_{x+y}-\eta_{x+z}]\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{G}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}(\eta)f(\eta)\left[\frac{1+\sigma_{x+y,x+z}}{2}\right]\de\nu_{N}^{\alpha}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{(\ell+\ell_{0})^{2}}{N}\Var_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}(\varphi)\|G\|_{2,N}^{2}+N^{2}\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\nu_{N}^{\alpha}).

The same estimate holds by substituting
\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{G}_{\ell_{0}}(x+\ell)}
with
\boldsymbol{1}_{\mathscr{G}_{\ell_{0}}(x-\ell)}
, as long as
y
and
z
are instead in the set
\{-\ell,\ldots,-1\}
.

Remark 4.8
.

The Dirichlet form
\mathscr{D}_{N}

, it is not hard to see that

\mathscr{D}_{N}(f,\nu_{N}^{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{N}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta)[f(\eta^{x,x+1})-f(\eta)]\de\nu_{N}^{\alpha}.

(4.15)

.

4.4  Characterisation of the Limit Point

We are finally ready to study the quadratic term (
4.8
) and thus conclude the proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

By Lemma
4.2
, for each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
we have that

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\eta)}-1\right](\bar{\eta}_{x}-\bar{\eta}_{x+1})

\displaystyle=\left[e^{-N^{-\alpha}(\bar{\eta}_{x}-\bar{\eta}_{x+1})(\bar{\eta}_{x+2}-\bar{\eta}_{x-1})}-1\right](\bar{\eta}_{x}-\bar{\eta}_{x+1})

\displaystyle=-N^{-\alpha}(\bar{\eta}_{x}-\bar{\eta}_{x+1})^{2}(\bar{\eta}_{x+2}-\bar{\eta}_{x-1})+O(N^{-2\alpha})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}N^{-\alpha}(\bar{\eta}_{x-1}-\bar{\eta}_{x+2})+2N^{-\alpha}(\bar{\eta}_{x}\bar{\eta}_{x+1}\bar{\eta}_{x+2}-\bar{\eta}_{x-1}\bar{\eta}_{x}\bar{\eta}_{x+1})+O(N^{-2\alpha}),

and thus the stationary measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
satisfies
Assumption 1a
. Moreover, by Lemma

Appendix A
On the Invariant Measure of the KLS Model

This appendix is dedicated to the study of the stationary measure of the KLS model. For the sake of completeness, we first give a simple proof of the invariance of the measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
for the dynamics of the KLS model for our choice of parameters. We then show how to derive its desired properties (Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
) via the
transfer matrix
approach, and we conclude by showing Proposition
4.4
via a CLT for
\alpha
-mixing random variables.

A.1  Stationarity of the Measure

Proposition A.1
.

The measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
defined in (
2.11
) is invariant for the dynamics of the KLS model with parameters (
2.10
).

Proof.

We will actually show that the measure (
2.9
) satisfies the
global balance
condition, namely

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\left[\nu_{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})-\nu_{N}(\eta)c_{x,x+1}^{N}(\eta)\right]=0

(A.1)

for each
\eta\in\Omega_{N}
, where the rates
c_{x,x+1}^{N}
are given by (
2.8
) with
\kappa_{N}=-1
. In particular, this implies that
\int_{\Omega_{N}}\mathcal{L}^{N}f\de\nu_{N}^{\alpha}=0
for each cylinder function
f:\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, and thus the measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
is invariant for the dynamics with parameters (
2.10
).

Calling
1+\varepsilon_{N}=:a
and
1-\varepsilon_{N}=:b
, in order to show (
A.1
), it suffices to prove that, for each
\eta\in\Omega_{N}
,

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{x+1}(1-\eta_{x})[a\eta_{x-1}+b\eta_{x+2}]\frac{\nu_{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{N}(\eta)}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1})[a\eta_{x-1}+b\eta_{x+2}]

and

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1})[b\eta_{x-1}+a\eta_{x+2}]\frac{\nu_{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{N}(\eta)}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{x+1}(1-\eta_{x})[b\eta_{x-1}+a\eta_{x+2}].

We will only show the first identity, as the proof of the second one is completely analogous.

Note that

\frac{\nu_{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{N}(\eta)}=\Bigl(\frac{b}{a}\Bigr)^{(\eta_{x}-\eta_{x+1})(\eta_{x+2}-\eta_{x-1})}.

Now, on the event
\{\eta_{x+1}=1,\eta_{x}=0\}
, the expression above reads

\Bigl(\frac{b}{a}\Bigr)^{\eta_{x-1}-\eta_{x+2}}=\frac{b}{a}\eta_{x-1}(1-\eta_{x+2})+\frac{a}{b}\eta_{x+2}(1-\eta_{x-1})+1-\eta_{x-1}-\eta_{x+2}+2\eta_{x-1}\eta_{x+2},

and hence, on that event, we have that

\displaystyle[a\eta_{x-1}+b\eta_{x+2}]\frac{\nu_{N}(\eta^{x,x+1})}{\nu_{N}(\eta)}

\displaystyle=b\eta_{x-1}(1-\eta_{x+2})+(a+b)\eta_{x-1}\eta_{x+2}+a\eta_{x+2}(1-\eta_{x-1})

\displaystyle=a\eta_{x+2}+b\eta_{x-1}.

But then, it suffices to prove that

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{x+1}(1-\eta_{x})[b\eta_{x-1}+a\eta_{x+2}]=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}\eta_{x}(1-\eta_{x+1})[a\eta_{x-1}+b\eta_{x+2}],

which follows immediately by performing a shift in the summation index.
∎

A.2  Expectations via the Transfer Matrix

Define the
transfer matrix
of the measure
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
as

T:=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&e^{-N^{-\alpha}}\end{bmatrix}.

(A.2)

This matrix encodes the particle interactions by evaluating the contribution of the term
\eta_{x}\eta_{x+1}
in
e^{-N^{-\alpha}H(\eta)}
in the four distinct cases
(\eta_{x},\eta_{x+1})=\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}
, and the idea behind the transfer matrix approach is to use the matrix
T
to derive simple expressions of expected values with respect to
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
; see
[
Bax82
]
for a more general, detailed exposition. Since
T
is symmetric, it can be diagonalised, and in particular we can write
T=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}
with

Q=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
\lambda_{+}-1&\lambda_{-}-1\par\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \ \ \ \Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{+}&0\\
0&\lambda_{-}\end{bmatrix},

(A.3)

where

\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{(1+e^{-N^{-\alpha}})\pm\sqrt{(1-e^{-N^{-\alpha}})^{2}+4}}{2}.

(A.4)

We will now make use of the matrix
T
to show Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Note that the partition function
Z_{N}^{\alpha}
of
\nu_{N}^{\alpha}
can be written as

Z_{N}^{\alpha}=\text{Tr}(T^{N})=\lambda_{+}^{N}+\lambda_{-}^{N},

(A.5)

where
\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}
are the eigenvalues of
T
, given in (
A.4
). Since
|\lambda_{-}|<1
, we have that

\displaystyle\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mylog\frac{Z_{N}^{\alpha}}{2^{N}}

\displaystyle=\mylim_{N\to\infty}\mylog\frac{\left((1+e^{-N^{-\alpha}})+\sqrt{(1-e^{-N^{-\alpha}})^{2}+4}\right)^{N}}{4^{N}}

\displaystyle=\mylim_{N\to\infty}N\mylog\left(1+\frac{(1+e^{-N^{-\alpha}})+\sqrt{(1-e^{-N^{-\alpha}})^{2}+4}-4}{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\mylim_{N\to\infty}Nf_{\alpha}(N),

where
f_{\alpha}(N)\lesssim N^{-\alpha}
. Since
\alpha>1
, this completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Note that, for each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, we can write

E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}[\eta_{x}]=\frac{(T^{N})_{1,1}}{Z_{N}^{\alpha}}.

(A.6)

Using the matrix
Q
in (
A.3
) and the expression (
A.5
) for the partition function, since
\lambda_{+}
is strictly greater than
\lambda_{-}
in absolute value, this implies that

\displaystyle\left|E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}[\eta_{x}]-\frac{1}{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\lambda_{+}^{N}(\lambda_{+}-1)-\lambda_{-}^{N}(\lambda_{-}-1)}{(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})(\lambda_{+}^{N}+\lambda_{-}^{N})}-\frac{1}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\frac{\lambda_{+}-1}{\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}}-\frac{1}{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{e^{-N^{-\alpha}}-1+\sqrt{(1-e^{-N^{-\alpha}})^{2}+4}}{2\sqrt{(1-e^{-N^{-\alpha}})^{2}+4}}-\frac{1}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-\alpha},

as claimed.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Let
x_{1}<\ldots<x_{m}\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
and
d_{1},\ldots,d_{m}
be as in the statement: the key observation is that we can write

E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\eta_{x_{m}}\right]=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}(T^{d_{i}})_{1,1}}{Z_{N}^{\alpha}}.

We only show the result for
m=2
, as the argument runs the same for any value of
m
(though the computations are more tedious). By the expression above, using the matrix
Q
in (
A.3
) and the expression (
A.5
), for each
x_{1}<x_{2}
in
\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
we have that

\displaystyle E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}[\eta_{x_{1}}\eta_{x_{2}}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(T^{d_{1}})_{1,1}(T^{d_{2}})_{1,1}}{\text{Tr}(T^{N})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[\lambda_{+}^{d_{1}}(\lambda_{+}-1)-\lambda_{-}^{d_{1}}(\lambda_{-}-1)\right]\left[\lambda_{+}^{d_{2}}(\lambda_{+}-1)-\lambda_{-}^{d_{2}}(\lambda_{-}-1)\right]}{(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-})^{2}(\lambda_{+}^{N}+\lambda_{-}^{N})}.

Calling
\lambda_{+}-1=:a
and
\lambda_{-}-1=:b
, using (
A.6
) and recalling that
d_{1}+d_{2}=N
, an easy computation shows that

\displaystyle E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}

\displaystyle[\eta_{x_{1}}\eta_{x_{2}}]-E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}[\eta_{x_{1}}]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{-ab(\lambda_{+}^{N+d_{1}}\lambda_{-}^{d_{2}}+\lambda_{+}^{N+d_{2}}\lambda_{-}^{d_{1}}+\lambda_{+}^{d_{1}}\lambda_{-}^{N+d_{2}}+\lambda_{+}^{d_{2}}\lambda_{-}^{N+d_{1}}+2\lambda_{+}^{N}\lambda_{-}^{N})}{(a-b)^{2}(\lambda_{+}^{N}+\lambda_{-}^{N})^{2}}.

But now, since that
\lambda_{+}>1
and
\lambda_{-}=-\frac{1}{2}N^{-\alpha}+O(N^{-2\alpha})
, this yields

\displaystyle\left|E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}[\eta_{x_{1}}\eta_{x_{2}}]-E_{\nu_{\alpha}^{N}}[\eta_{x_{1}}]^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda_{+}^{d_{1}-N}\lambda_{-}^{d_{2}}+\lambda_{+}^{d_{2}-N}\lambda_{-}^{d_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}}\right)^{d_{1}}+\left(\frac{\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}}\right)^{d_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-\alpha\mymin\{d_{1},d_{2}\}},

as claimed. ∎

A.3  An Application of a CLT for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-Mixing Sequences

We conclude the section by showing Proposition
4.4
. Note that the random variables
\{\bar{\eta}_{x}\}_{x}
are identically distributed but
not
independent, so one cannot apply the classical CLT to show convergence of the density fluctuation field at a fixed time to a Gaussian random variable. However, as highlighted by Lemma
4.3
, this sequence is very weakly dependent, in the sense that its correlation functions quickly decay in space: as we will show, this allows us to apply a generalisation of the CLT to
\alpha
-mixing
sequences of random variables; see
[
Bra07
]
for an exhaustive exposition of CLT’s under mixing conditions.

Given a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, we define the
\alpha
-mixing coefficient
of two sub-sigma algebras
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{F}
as

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}):=\mysup_{A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}}\left|\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)-\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)\right|.

(A.7)

On the torus
\mathbb{T}_{n}:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let the distance between to subsets
I,J\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}
be

d(I,J):=\mymin_{i\in I,j\in J}\{|i-j|\wedge(n-|i-j|)\}.

Given a triangular array
\{X_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N},k\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
of random variables,
we define its
\alpha
-mixing coefficients
as

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}(r):=\mysup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I,J\subset\mathbb{T}_{n}\\
d(I,J)\geq r\end{subarray}}\alpha\big(\sigma\{\eta_{i}:i\in I\},\sigma\{\eta_{j}:j\in J\}\big)

for each integer
1\leq r\leq\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
, with the convention
\alpha_{n}(0):=\frac{1}{4}
. The result that follows can be found in
[
Rio97
, Corollary 1]
.

Theorem A.2
.

Let
\{X_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N},k\in\mathbb{T}_{n}}
be a triangular array of centred random variables with finite variance, and call
Q_{n,k}
the càdlàg inverse of
t\mapsto\mathbb{P}\{|X_{n,k}|>t\}
, namely
Q_{n,k}(u):=\mysup\{t\in[0,\infty):\mathbb{P}\{|X_{n,k}|>t\}>u\}
. Let
\{\alpha_{n}(r)\}_{r}
denote the sequence of
\alpha
-mixing coefficients of
\{X_{n,k}\}_{k}
, and let
\alpha_{n}^{-1}
be the càdlàg inverse of
t\mapsto\alpha_{n}(\lfloor t\rfloor)
. Moreover, calling
\sigma_{n}^{2}:=\Var\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{n,k}\right)
, assume that

\mylimsup_{n\to\infty}\mymax_{k,m=1,\ldots,n}\frac{\Var\big(\sum_{j=k}^{m}X_{n,j}\big)}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}<\infty

(A.8)

and

\mylim_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{n}^{-3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{n}^{-1}\Bigl(\frac{u}{2}\Bigr)Q_{n,k}^{2}(u)\mymin\left\{\alpha_{n}^{-1}\Bigl(\frac{u}{2}\Bigr)Q_{n,k}(u),\sigma_{n}\right\}\de u=0.

(A.9)

Then

\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{n,k}}{\sigma_{n}}\to\mathcal{N}(0,1)

in distribution as
n\to\infty
.

Remark A.3
.

Condition (
A.9
) plays the role of a Lindeberg-type control adapted to the mixing framework. In the independent case, the Berry-Esseen theorem requires that the normalised third moments vanish, namely

\sigma_{n}^{-3}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|X_{n,k}|^{3}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
, to ensure that no single summand, or small group of summands, dominates the fluctuations; see, for example,
[
BBG96
, Theorem 1.1]
. Under dependence, raw moments are no longer sufficient because large values can be reinforced by correlations. The integral involving the quantile functions
Q_{n,k}
and the mixing penalty
\alpha_{n}^{-1}
is a somewhat natural generalisation: it measures the contribution of the tails of each
X_{n,k}
and amplifies them according to the strength of dependence. The presence of
\mymin\{\alpha_{n}^{-1}(\frac{u}{2})Q_{n,k}(u),\sigma_{n}\}
compares this aggregated size with the global variance
\sigma_{n}
, ensuring that only “genuinely large” deviations (relative to the Gaussian fluctuation scale) are penalised. Intuitively, then, (
A.9
) rules out the possibility that a few heavy-tailed or strongly-dependent terms could upset the Gaussian limit, and guarantees that the asymptotic fluctuations arise from the collective effect of many small, weakly correlated contributions.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

We will show that, for each continuous
g:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
, the sequence
\{Y_{N,x}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N},x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}
defined via

Y_{N,x}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}g\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)\bar{\eta}_{x}^{N}

satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
A.2
. First note that, by Lemmas
4.2
and
4.3
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\Var_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}Y_{N,x}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}g\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)^{2}E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}[\bar{\eta}_{x}^{2}]+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\neq y}g\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)g\Bigl(\frac{y}{N}\Bigr)E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}[\bar{\eta}_{x}\bar{\eta}_{y}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}g\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}+O(N^{-\alpha})\right)+O\left(\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}N^{-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{4}\|g\|_{2}^{2}.

Similarly, for each
k,m\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
, we see that

\Var_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{x=k}^{m}Y_{N,x}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=k}^{m}g\Bigl(\frac{x}{N}\Bigr)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}+O(N^{-\alpha})\right)+O\left(\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}N^{-\alpha}\right)\lesssim 1,

and thus (
A.8
) is automatically satisfied.

We now claim that the
\alpha
-mixing coefficients of
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}
satisfy

\alpha_{N}(r)\lesssim N^{-\alpha r},\ \ \ \ 0\leq r\leq\lfloor\tfrac{N}{2}\rfloor.

(A.10)

Consider the transfer matrix
T
defined in (
A.2
), and let
v:=[1,\lambda_{+}-1]
be an eigenvector – corresponding to the first column of
Q
in (
A.3
) – of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{+}
. We define the
2\times 2
matrix
P
by
P_{i,j}:=\frac{v_{j}}{\lambda_{+}v_{i}}T_{i,j}
: it is then immediate to check that
P
is a transition matrix. Note that we can view
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}}
as a two-state Markov chain with transition matrix
P
started at the uniform distribution
\mu=[\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]
and conditioned to return to its starting point after
N
steps: indeed, denoting by
\{\xi_{x}\}_{x}
a stationary Markov chain with transition probabilities
P
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\{({\xi_{0}},\ldots,\xi_{N})=\eta\}}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\{\xi_{0}=\xi_{N}\}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu(\eta_{0})\prod_{x=0}^{N-1}P_{\eta_{x},\eta_{x+1}}}{\sum_{j\in\{0,1\}}\mu(j)(P^{N})_{j,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{x=0}^{N-1}e^{-N^{-\alpha}\eta_{x}\eta_{x+1}}}{\lambda_{+}^{N}(1+\rho_{N}^{N})},

(A.11)

where
\rho_{N}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
P
. It is immediate to check that
\rho_{N}=\frac{\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}}
, so that, using (
A.5
), we conclude that
\eqref{eq:markov_prob}=\nu_{N}^{\alpha}(\eta)
. Now, since
\{\eta_{x}\}_{x}
is an ergodic two-state Markov chain with smallest eigenvalue
\rho_{N}
, we have that, for each integer
0\leq r\leq\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
,

\alpha_{N}(r)\lesssim|\rho_{N}|^{r}\lesssim N^{-\alpha r}
,
where we used
\lambda_{-}=-\frac{1}{2}N^{-\alpha}+O(N^{-2\alpha})
.

We are now ready to study the expression in (
A.9
). By Markov’s inequality, it is easy to see that, for all
x\in\mathbb{T}_{N}
and any
\ell>0
,

Q_{N,x}(u)\leq\frac{E_{\nu_{N}^{\alpha}}[|Y_{N,k}|^{\ell}]^{\frac{1}{\ell}}}{u^{\frac{1}{\ell}}}\lesssim\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{N}}u^{-\frac{1}{\ell}}.

Moreover, for each integer
1\leq r\leq\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\rfloor
, we have that
\alpha_{N}^{-1}(\frac{u}{2})=r
if and only if
\frac{u}{2}\in[\alpha_{N}(r),\alpha_{N}(r-1))
, and hence, using that
\sigma_{N}\lesssim 1
,
