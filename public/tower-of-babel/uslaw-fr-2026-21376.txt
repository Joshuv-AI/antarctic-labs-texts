SUMMARY: The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (``Commission'' or 
``CFTC'') is adopting an interim final rule (``Interim Final Rule'') to 
codify its longstanding position that casino-style gambling products, 
including wagers placed on sportsbooks and casino games, are excluded 
from the ``swap'' definition.

I. Background

    Title VII of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer 
Protection Act (``Dodd-Frank Act'') established a comprehensive 
regulatory framework for swaps.\1\ Section 712(d)(1) of the Dodd-Frank 
Act provides that the Commission and the Securities and Exchange 
Commission (``SEC''), in consultation with the Board of Governors of 
the Federal Reserve System (the ``Federal Reserve Board''), shall 
jointly further define, among other terms, the term ``swap.'' \2\ 
Pursuant to this requirement, in 2012, the Commission and the SEC, in 
consultation with the Federal Reserve Board, issued a final rule 
further defining the term ``swap'' and other terms.\3\ That rule 
addressed, among other topics, the treatment of certain traditional, 
non-derivatives products, agreements, contracts, and transactions that 
they concluded Congress did not intend to be regulated as swaps or 
security-based swaps, including insurance contracts and certain 
commercial and consumer transactions.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \1\ See Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection 
Act, Public Law 111-203, 124 Stat. 1376, 1641 (2010). The text of 
the Dodd-Frank Act is available at http://www.cftc.gov/LawRegulation/OTCDERIVATIVES/index.htm.
    \2\ Dodd-Frank Act sec. 712(d)(1), codified at 15 U.S.C. 
8302(d)(1) (directing the CFTC and SEC to undertake joint rulemaking 
on covered topics). The CFTC shares jurisdiction over mixed swaps 
and security futures with the SEC, and the SEC has sole jurisdiction 
over security-based swaps. See CEA sec. 1a(44), 7 U.S.C. 1a(44), and 
secs. 3(a)(55) and 3(a)(68) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 
(``Exchange Act''), 15 U.S.C. 78c(a)(55) and 78c(a)(68). The 
Commission has consulted with the Federal Reserve Board by sharing

    This Interim Final Rule arises in the context of the ongoing debate 
regarding the scope of the Commission's jurisdiction under the 
Commodity Exchange Act (``CEA''), particularly as it relates to event 
contracts. The CFTC generally has found that many event contracts, 
including those based on sports-related events, are covered by the 
CEA's swap definition.\4\ The CEA contains six definitions of ``swap,'' 
at least four of which are applicable to event contracts.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \4\ Event contracts might be structured as other instruments 
such as futures contracts, which are excluded from the statutory 
definition of ``swap.'' CEA sec. 1a(47)(B), 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(B) 
(providing ``exclusions'' from the definition of ``swap'' under the 
CEA, including futures, options on futures, securities such as 
options on securities and indexes of securities, security-based 
swaps, and debt securities). Certain event contracts may be 
security-based swaps or other instruments subject to the SEC's 
jurisdiction. See 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(B) (providing ``exclusions'' from 
the definition of ``swap'' under the CEA, including for securities 
such as security-based swaps, certain options, and debt securities); 
see also, e.g., 15 U.S.C. 78c(a)(68)(A) (defining ``security-based 
swap'' under the Exchange Act). This Interim Final Rule does not 
further define the term ``security-based swap.''
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    First, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(i) includes binary options and other 
commodity options in the ``swap'' definition by including ``any 
agreement, contract, or transaction . . . that is a put, call, cap, 
floor, collar, or similar option of any kind that is for the purchase 
or sale, or based on the value, of 1 or more interest or other rates, 
currencies, commodities, securities, instruments of indebtedness, 
indices, quantitative measures, or other financial or economic 
interests or property of any kind.'' \5\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \5\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(i). A binary option is thus a type of 
swap which is an ``option whose payoff is either a fixed amount or

---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Second, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(ii) defines ``swap'' to include ``any 
agreement, contract, or transaction . . . that provides for any 
purchase, sale, payment, or delivery (other than a dividend on an 
equity security) that is dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or 
the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with 
a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence.'' \6\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \6\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(ii). The broad nature of clause (ii) of 
the swap definition is in keeping with the broad definition of the 
term ``commodity'' in the CEA. For example, the 1974 amendments to 
the CEA expanded the definition of the term ``commodity'' to 
encompass ``all services, rights, and interests in which contracts 
for future delivery are presently or in the future dealt in.'' See 
CEA sec. 1a(9), 7 U.S.C. 1a(9) (defining ``commodity''). In 2000, 
Congress reaffirmed the broad scope of the commodity definition in 
adding the term ``excluded commodity'' to the CEA.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Third, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(iv) includes in the ``swap''

the Dodd-Frank Act included several provisions that give the Commission 
authority to further define ``swap.'' First, Dodd-Frank Act section 
712(d)(2), 15 U.S.C. 8302(d)(1), directs the CFTC and SEC, in

amendment to the CEA in Subtitle A of the Wall Street Transparency and 
Accountability Act of 2010, which includes the term ``swap.'' Third, 
Dodd-Frank Act section 721(b), 15 U.S.C. 8321(b), directs the CFTC to

[[Page 65971]]

further define ``swap'' to include transactions and entities that have 
been structured to evade Subtitle A.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \9\ The CFTC and SEC, in consultation with the Federal Reserve 
Board, first further defined ``swap'' in a 2012 joint rulemaking. 77 
FR 48208. In that rulemaking, the CFTC and SEC noted that, while 
``[t]he statutory definitions of the terms `swap' and `security-
based swap' are detailed and comprehensive, . . . the definitions 
could be read to include certain types of agreements, contracts, and 
transactions that previously have not been considered swaps or

Dodd-Frank Act appears to suggest that Congress intended such

security-based swaps.'' The CFTC and SEC thus used their authority 
``to further clarify the treatment under the definitions of certain 
types of agreements, contracts, and transactions.'' Id. at 48211.

    In recent months, many have expressed concern that a natural, 
plain-text interpretation of the ``swap'' definition would necessarily 
encompass casino-style gambling products and, finding that consequence 
untenable, have diverged from the plain text of the statute. For 
example, the Sixth Circuit in KalshiEX LLC v. Schuler viewed sports 
event contracts as legally indistinguishable from casino-style sports 
wagers; and thus it expressed concern that, if sports event contracts 
are swaps, any sports wager--including ``every sports wager placed in a 
casino, on an online sports book, or between two friends at a bar''--
would need to ``take place on federal markets.'' \10\ Indeed, the Sixth 
Circuit explained, a person would commit a felony by engaging in 
gambling off a designated contract market (``DCM'').\11\ Thus, the 
Sixth Circuit believed that its atextual interpretation of the ``swap'' 
definition would avoid ``attach[ing] criminal penalties to a 
breathtaking amount of commonplace gambling activity.'' \12\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \10\ KalshiEX LLC v. Schuler, No. 26-3196, 2026 WL 2884087, at 
*14-15 (6th Cir. Sept. 25, 2026) (citation modified).
    \11\ Id. at *14.
    \12\ Id. (citation modified).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    The Ninth Circuit similarly reasoned in KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad that 
sports event contracts and casino-style sports wagering are 
indistinguishable, finding that the ``substance'' of the sports event 
contracts at issue was ``sports gambling, regardless of whether [the 
DCM] calls them swaps'' (and, by inference, regardless of whether the 
CFTC regulates them as swaps).\13\ And because ``there is not a 
difference under the CEA's swap definition between sports betting on 
Kalshi and sports betting in Caesar's Sportsbook, then every person 
placing a sports bet at Caesar's Sportsbook (or anywhere else for that 
matter) is violating the CEA.'' \14\ The court also expressed concern 
that ``bingo games and pingpong matches'' would fall under CFTC 
jurisdiction.\15\ For these and other reasons involving the court's 
understanding of the CEA's ``context'' and ``history,'' \16\ the court 
ultimately ``conclude[d] that these sports event contracts are likely 
not swaps under the CEA.'' \17\

    Exploiting this lack of clarity, certain States have asserted that 
event contracts traded or executed on a DCM should be State regulated 
(rather than federally regulated under the CEA). These States argue 
that if the Commission has jurisdiction over event contracts, that 
would necessarily subject casino-style sports wagers--traditionally 
regulated by the States--to the Commission's jurisdiction as well. For 
example, in response to a Commission proposed rulemaking, 40 States and 
the District of Columbia submitted a comment, arguing that sports event 
contracts are ``indistinguishable from traditional sports betting.'' 
\18\ The State of New Jersey recently argued to the United States 
Supreme Court that ``[n]othing in the [CEA] gives the CFTC 
unprecedented authority to become the sole regulator of sports gambling 
in this country.'' \19\ And the Commonwealth of Kentucky has claimed 
that determining that event contracts are swaps under the CEA amounts 
to ``[t]ransferring every ounce of authority over sports wagering from 
the States to the CFTC.'' \20\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    There also appears to be confusion within the gaming industry. The 
American Gaming Association has argued that if ``sports wagers qualify 
as `swaps,' the CEA would require all sports bets--including those 
placed on the Las Vegas Strip--to be traded on DCMs.'' \21\ In its 
view, there is ``no plausible statutory basis to distinguish retail 
sports bets from identical wagers placed on [a DCM]--if the latter are 
swaps, the former must be as well.'' \22\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \21\ Brief of Amicus Curiae American Gaming Association in 
Support of Defendants-Appellees and Affirmance, Schuler, Dkt. No. 54 
at 23 (6th Cir. June 11, 2026).
    \22\ Id. at 23-24.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    As the above discussion demonstrates, there is widespread confusion 
regarding whether the definition of a swap includes casino-style 
gambling products, including wagers placed on sportsbooks and casino 
games, traditionally regulated at the State level. In this regard, many 
courts, States, and private businesses are currently operating under 
the erroneous premise that if event contracts are swaps, then casino-
style gambling products must also be swaps subject to CFTC 
jurisdiction. That premise has led courts and States to deviate from 
the plain text of the ``swap'' definition and impose atextual 
limitations on the concept of an ``event'' that is ``associated with a 
potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence.'' \23\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \23\ Schuler, 2026 WL 2884087, at *11 (holding that ``the event 
[must] be one inherently associated with a `financial, economic, or 
commercial consequence' '' (emphasis added)).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    To address this misunderstanding, this Interim Final Rule affirms 
and codifies the Commission's longstanding position that casino-style 
gambling products, including sports-wagering contracts entered into on 
a sportsbook and casino games, are not swaps and do not fall within the 
CFTC's jurisdiction. This action is intended to remove regulatory 
ambiguity and clarify the boundaries between the Commission's 
jurisdiction over the derivatives markets and State and Tribal 
jurisdiction over casino-style gambling.
    The Commission notes that this approach is supported by a decision 
of the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit in 
KalshiEX, LLC v. Flaherty, which held that ``sports-related event 
contracts are traded on a CFTC-licensed DCM and depend on event 
outcomes associated with economic consequences'' and therefore ``fit 
within the Act's definition of `swaps' subject to the CFTC's 
jurisdiction.'' \24\ In response to the State's concern that ``anything 
from bingo games to pingpong matches will fall under the CFTC's 
jurisdiction,'' the court observed that ''[i]n such far-fetched 
scenarios, Congress's express delegation to the CTFC and the Securities 
and Exchange Commission to `further define' swaps would prove useful,'' 
and it cited the Commission's 2012 rulemaking excluding insurance 
contracts and consumer transactions.\25\ Other courts have similarly 
recognized that the CEA applies to sports event contracts that ``take[ 
] the form of swaps,'' even while the CEA ``does not federalize all 
gambling, or even all sports gambling.'' \26\ This Interim Final Rule 
implements the Third Circuit's suggested approach.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \24\ KalshiEX, LLC v. Flaherty, 172 F.4th 220, 228 (3d Cir. 
2026).
    \25\ Id.
    \26\ Coinbase Fin. Mkts., Inc. v. Raoul, Nos. 1:25-cv-15406, 
1:26-cv-03659, 1:26-cv-07363, 2026 WL 2969682, at *10 (N.D. Ill. 
Oct. 2, 2026).

II. Interim Final Rule

    The Commission is issuing this Interim Final Rule to further define 
the term ``swap'' to explicitly exclude casino-style gambling products, 
including sports-wagering contracts entered into on a sportsbook, from 
the swap definition. By enacting this Interim Final Rule, the 
Commission seeks to reiterate its longstanding position that its 
oversight of federally regulated derivatives markets does not intrude 
upon the traditional State and Tribal regulation of casino-style 
gambling products.
    Though Congress deliberately drafted a broad definition of a 
swap,\29\ the CFTC's jurisdiction is not boundless. The Commission has 
always acknowledged that certain instruments may, on their face, 
plausibly fit within the definition of ``swap'' in CEA section 1a(47), 
but that the swap definition is best read to exclude that instrument in 
light of statutory context and a longstanding tradition of regulation 
by another regulator. Gambling products offered by State or Tribe-
licensed casino operators and sportsbooks fit this mold.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \29\ CEA sec. 1a(47), 7 U.S.C. 1a(47).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Insurance serves as a prime precedent.\30\ On its face, prong (ii) 
of the ``swap'' definition could plausibly be read to encompass 
insurance contracts, because they make ``payment'' ``dependent on the 
occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event 
or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or 
commercial consequence.'' \31\ Given that potential ambiguity, when the 
Commission and the SEC jointly further defined the term ``swap'' in 
2012, the agencies explicitly noted that they did not interpret prong 
(ii) of the swap definition \32\ ``to mean that products historically 
regulated as insurance products should be included.'' \33\ The 
Commission and the SEC further emphasized that nothing in the Dodd-
Frank Act ``suggest[ed] that Congress intended for traditional 
insurance products to be regulated as swaps.'' \34\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \30\ See Further Definition Release, 77 FR at 48210, 48212-25.
    \31\ 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(ii).
    \32\ As described above, CEA section 1a(47)(A)(ii) defines 
``swap'' to include ``any agreement, contract, or transaction . . . 
that provides for any purchase, sale, payment, or delivery (other 
than a dividend on an equity security) that is dependent on the 
occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an 
event or contingency associated with a potential financial, 
economic, or commercial consequence.'' 7 U.S.C. 1a(47)(A)(ii).
    \33\ 77 FR at 48212.
    \34\ Id.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Consequently, the Commission and the SEC specifically carved out 
traditional insurance products from the swap definition by relying on 
core factual and structural differences to distinguish them from 
derivatives. A foundational distinction was whether the instrument is 
traded: the Commission and the SEC noted that ``insurance products 
traditionally have not been entered into on or subject to the rules of 
an organized exchange nor traded in secondary market transactions 
(i.e., they are not traded on an organized market or over the 
counter),'' whereas swaps are routinely traded on such venues.\35\ 
Furthermore, the Commission and SEC emphasized that ``all or part of 
swaps and security-based swaps are novated or assigned to third 
parties, usually pursuant to industry standard terms and documents.'' 
In other words, swaps can be centrally cleared, unlike insurance 
contracts.\36\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \35\ Id. at 48214; see also 17 CFR 1.3 (Swap)(4).
    \36\ 77 FR at 48208, 48214.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    The Commission and the SEC also relied on the fact that insurance 
products are ``provided by a person that is subject to supervision by 
the insurance commissioner (or similar official or agency) of any state 
or by the United States'' and are ``regulated as insurance under 
applicable state law or the laws of the United States.'' \37\ The 
agencies clarified, however, that other instruments providing 
substantially similar economic exposure to an insurance contract--such 
as certain credit default swaps--remain within the swap definition and 
subject to the CFTC's jurisdiction.\38\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \37\ 77 FR at 48220.
    \38\ Id. (comparing insurance products and credit default 
swaps). In this regard, the Commission and the SEC noted their 
``concern[ ] that certain agreements, contracts, or transactions 
that are swaps or security-based swaps might be characterized as 
insurance products to evade the regulatory regime under Title VII of 
the Dodd-Frank Act.'' Id.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    The Commission and the SEC took a similar approach to ``consumer 
and commercial agreements that historically have not been considered 
swaps or security-based swaps'' but nonetheless ``may have attributes 
that could be viewed as falling within the swap or security-based swap 
definition.'' \39\ Examples included ``acquisitions or sales of 
property (tangible and intangible), provisions of services, employment 
of individuals, and other matters that could be viewed as falling 
within the definitions.'' \40\ The Commission and the SEC excluded 
these contracts on the ground that they did ``not believe Congress 
intended to include these types of customary consumer and commercial 
agreements, contracts, or transactions in the swap or security-based 
swap definition,'' again highlighting the ``key'' point that such 
transactions are ``not traded on an organized market or over-the-
counter.'' \41\ The Commissions adopted this interpretation to address, 
among other concerns, the possibility that customary consumer and 
commercial agreements, contracts, or transactions could be subject to 
the restriction in section 2(e) of the CEA on entering into swaps with 
persons who are not eligible contract participants (``ECPs'') other 
than on, or subject to the rules of, a DCM.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \39\ Id. at 48246.
    \40\ Id.
    \41\ Id. at 48246-48.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    As explained in Part I, similar concerns have recently arisen in 
connection with traditional gambling products offered by State-
regulated gambling companies and tribes. To address this concern, the 
Commission seeks to further clarify through this Interim Final Rule 
that casino-style gambling products are not within Commission's 
jurisdiction. As explained below, the same concerns that led the 
Commission to exclude insurance contracts and consumer and commercial 
agreements from the ``swap'' definition are equally applicable to 
gambling products.
    First, and most fundamentally, gambling products do not bear the 
structural characteristics of a CEA-regulated derivative. Gambling 
products are typically conducted as bilateral transactions between a 
casino or bookkeeper (a ``house'') and its customer rather than on an 
organized market.\42\ Prices are not set by market forces and are not 
subject to negotiation; rather, customers must accept the price

[[Page 65973]]

set by the house. Because of these dynamics, gambling products are not 
inherently tradable, which is a strong indicator that these products 
are not swaps or any other kind of derivative within the Commission's 
jurisdiction.\43\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \42\ Steven D. Levitt, Why Are Gambling Markets Organised So 
Differently from Financial Markets?, 114 Econ. J. 223, 223 (2004).
    \43\ While this Interim Final Rule addresses only casino-style 
gambling products offered pursuant to State or Tribal law, the 
exclusion provided herin is non-exclusive. There may be other 
gambling-style transactions that are not swaps given how those 
transactions are structured and whether they are tradable. Examples 
could include a casual bet between friends or a casino-style 
sportsbook illegally operating in a State that prohibits gambling.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Tradability is a material consideration in determining whether a 
financial instrument has the legal status of a derivative, as the 
Commission and SEC emphasized with respect to insurance contracts and 
consumer transactions. Although the CEA's ``swap'' definition does not 
itself contain a tradability requirement, interpreters of statutory 
text nonetheless must be guided by ``[t]he broader context provided by 
other sections of the statute.'' \44\ The CEA's broader context 
repeatedly reinforces tradability as an important consideration in 
whether a particular contract qualifies as a swap.\45\ For example, CEA 
section 2(e) makes it unlawful for any person, other than an ECP, to 
enter into a swap unless executed on, or subject to the rules of, a 
DCM.\46\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \44\ Robinson v. Shell Oil Co., 519 U.S. 337, 345 (1997).
    \45\ Notably, courts have looked to the tradability of an 
instrument to determine whether it is a future or a forward 
contract. See, e.g., CFTC v. Zelener, 373 F.3d 861, 865-66 (7th Cir. 
2004).
    \46\ 7 U.S.C. 2(e).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Likewise, section 2(a)(1) of the CEA confers exclusive jurisdiction 
on the CFTC with respect to ``accounts, agreements . . . , and 
transactions involving swaps or contracts of sale of a commodity for 
future delivery,'' to the extent they are ``traded or executed on a 
[DCM] or a swap execution facility.'' \47\ Furthermore, the CEA 
provides that the transactions subject to the Commission's exclusive 
jurisdiction serve a ``national public interest by providing a means 
for managing and assuming price risks, discovering prices, or 
disseminating pricing information through trading in liquid, fair and 
financially secure trading facilities.'' \48\ Only tradable 
instruments--i.e., those that are executed multilaterally or at least 
subject to negotiation--can serve those purposes.

    The CEA's Core Principles also presuppose that derivatives are 
tradable instruments. Core Principle 9 for DCMs requires exchanges to 
provide ``a competitive, open, and efficient market and mechanism for 
executing transactions that protects the price discovery process of 
trading in the centralized market.'' \49\ Gambling products are not 
offered through ``trading'' on ``centralized market[s],'' and prices 
for gambling products are not set by ``a competitive, open, and 
efficient market''--they are unilaterally dictated by the casino or 
sportsbook. Other Core Principles for DCMs and swap execution 
facilities (``SEFs'') likewise presuppose the competitive trading of 
derivatives, which cannot be said of gambling products.\50\ For these 
reasons, gambling products are not derivatives subject to its 
jurisdiction.

``cleared,'' in that the wager obligation is not routinely transferred 
to a third party to eliminate counterparty credit risk and ensure 
contract fulfillment.

products and derivatives contracts.\53\ Furthermore, when Congress 
adopted the ``swap'' definition, it did so against a background of 
other federal statutory provisions regulating sports gambling, and the 
Commission does not presume Congress to have impliedly repealed those 
provisions.\54\

subject to a gambling-specific regulatory regime. Absent a more

contract, or transaction that is, or in the future becomes, commonly 
known to the trade as a swap.'' \56\ The Commission is unaware of any 
trade usage or market belief that gambling products are considered 
swaps in the derivatives industry. To the contrary, DCMs and industry 
groups have repeatedly emphasized to federal courts that gambling 
contracts are not swaps.\57\

    Event contracts differ from gambling products in all five respects. 
First, unlike gambling products, event contracts have the essential 
structural characteristics of a derivatives contract. By design, they 
are traded on CFTC-registered national exchanges at market-set prices 
through the interaction of bids or offers placed on a central 
orderbook, not at prices unilaterally dictated by a ``house.'' The 
exchanges are neutral;

[[Page 65974]]

they do not profit when the event contract settles one way versus 
another. Rather, they earn profit by charging transaction fees when a 
trade occurs, similar to any other derivatives trade.\58\ Such 
contracts are subject to the comprehensive DCM and derivatives clearing 
organization (``DCO'') Core Principles that serve to, among other 
things, protect customers who are trading and clearing such contracts. 
The structure of gambling wagers and event contracts is materially 
different, and therefore subject to different regulatory regimes.\59\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \58\ The CFTC recently issued a letter to Commission-regulated 
entities to remind those entities of their obligation to display 
prices clearly and not to mislead customers into mistaking market-
priced products with casino-style products. CFTC, Misleading or 
Deceptive Practices (Aug. 7, 2026), available at https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9278-26.
    \59\ Additionally, while a sportsbook or other gambling venue 
may regularly exclude winners, a DCM is subject to an impartial 
access requirement that does not allow it to bar traders who have 
profited. See 17 CFR 38.151.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Second, event contracts traded on a DCM are centrally cleared by 
CFTC-registered clearinghouses to eliminate counterparty credit risk, 
just like other derivatives transactions. Thus, when a bid and offer 
match on a DCM's central orderbook, the parties enter into a contract 
with one another, but that contract is then novated such that each 
party has a contractual relationship with a neutral, creditworthy 
clearinghouse.
    Third, there is strong textual evidence that Congress intended for 
event contracts to be considered ``swaps'' under the CEA and to give 
the Commission authority to regulate them. The second prong of the 
``swap'' definition is the most obvious illustration, as it 
specifically covers ``contract[s]'' based on ``event[s].'' \60\ 
Additionally, when Congress created the ``swap'' definition, it also 
enacted a ``Special rule for review and approval of event contracts and 
swaps contracts.'' \61\ The Special Rule recognizes that ``swaps in 
excluded commodities'' can be ``based upon the occurrence, extent of an 
occurrence, or contingency'' that ``involve[s],'' among other things, 
``gaming.'' \62\ The word `` `gaming,' as used in the special rule, 
refers to playing games,'' so ``event contracts related to . . . 
sporting events . . . could implicate the gaming category.'' \63\ 
Congress expressly gave the Commission jurisdiction over those 
contracts, confirming their status as derivatives contracts.\64\ 
Notably, Congress did not give the Commission authority over ``gaming'' 
products themselves, further confirming that gaming products like 
wagers fall outside the Commission's jurisdiction but event contracts 
that involve games are within the Commission's jurisdiction.\65\

    Fifth, as set forth in the separate Notice of Proposed Rulemaking 
published on the same day as this Interim Final Rule proposing to 
further define ``swap'' to include event contracts, the Commission 
preliminarily believes that event contracts are known to the trade as 
``swaps.'' Therefore, if certain event contracts do not qualify as 
swaps under the first or second prongs of the swap definition, they do 
qualify under the fourth prong. The same cannot be said of gambling 
products, for the reasons noted above.
    In sum, the ``swap'' definition generally should not apply where a 
product has a longstanding history of being subject to a distinct 
regulatory framework and structurally does not lend itself to the 
requirements of the CEA, including its exchange-trading requirement and 
the Core Principles. As the Commission and SEC recognized in 2012, 
insurance contracts and consumer and commercial agreements are such 
examples. Today, the Commission clarifies that casino-style gambling

[[Page 65975]]

products offered by casinos or sportsbooks likewise fit the exclusion.
    The Commission acknowledges that a person can achieve similar 
economic exposure by either entering into a gambling wager or 
purchasing an event contract. However, that does not make the two 
products one and the same, nor does it require that the two be subject 
to identical regulatory frameworks. Indeed, there are many instances in 
which a person can achieve the same (or similar) economic exposure via 
structurally different products subject to distinct regulatory regimes. 
For example, as noted, insurance and credit default swaps can provide 
similar risk mitigation, but that does not mean the two are or must be 
regulated the same way. Likewise, a person can gain exposure to a 
crypto asset by purchasing the crypto asset itself, purchasing shares 
in a firm that holds the crypto asset, or by purchasing crypto asset 
futures, each of which has distinct regulatory consequences. The same 
is true in the context of event contracts and casino-style gambling 
products.
    Based on this interpretation and to address the concerns referenced 
in Part I above, the Commission is adopting this Interim Final Rule to 
establish an exclusion confirming that traditional gambling products do 
not qualify as swaps under the CEA. Nothing herein implies or presumes 
that an agreement, contract, or transaction that does not meet any of 
the requirements of this exclusion is a swap. Such an agreement, 
contract, or transaction will require further analysis of the 
applicable facts and circumstances, including the form and substance of 
such agreement, contract, or transaction, to determine whether it is a 
gambling product, and thus not a swap.
    Similar to the exclusions for insurance contracts and consumer 
transactions, the exclusion has two components: a test relating to the 
status of the party offering the agreement, contract, or transaction 
(the ``Provider Test'') and a test relating to the terms of the 
agreement, contract, or transaction (the ``Product Test'').
    To satisfy the Provider Test, the relevant agreement, contract, or 
transaction must be provided by a person that is subject to supervision 
by the gaming commissioner (or similar official or agency) of any State 
or by a Tribe, and such agreement, contract, or transaction is 
regulated as gaming or gambling under applicable State or Tribal 
law.\74\ This requirement ensures that the Commission does not have 
jurisdiction over persons or products that have traditionally been the 
subject of State or Tribal regulation, particularly given the absence 
of an indication in Dodd-Frank that Congress intended the Commission to 
regulate these persons and transactions.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \74\ The insurance exclusion is similarly limited (outside of 
the contexts of reinsurance and non-admitted insurance) to products 
offered by a person that is subject to supervision by the insurance 
commissioner (or similar official or agency) of any State or by the 
United States and requires that the agreement, contract, or 
transaction also must be regulated as insurance under applicable 
State law or the laws of the United States. See 77 FR at 48220. The 
consumer-transaction exclusion likewise contains limitations to 
agreements, contracts, and transactions entered into by consumers or 
commercial or non-profit entities for specified purposes. See id. at 
48247.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    To satisfy the Product Test, the agreement, contract, or 
transaction must not be traded on a board of trade, an organized 
exchange, a swap execution facility, or any other market in which 
trades are executed multilaterally or subject to individual 
negotiation. For example, bets or wagers offered by a sports book or 
casino as principal to its customers, where the material terms of those 
bets or wagers are governed by standard ``house rules'' or similar 
standard terms, would satisfy the Product Test. In contrast, an 
agreement, contract, or transaction entered into on a trading 
facility,\75\ including a DCM, or a SEF,\76\ would not satisfy the 
Product Test because its price and other economic terms would be 
determined through the competitive interaction of multiple buyers and 
sellers on a multilateral basis, even though the other material terms 
of the agreement, contract, or transaction would be governed by 
standardized rules adopted by the facility.\77\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    Although there is not currently a practical overlap between the 
casino-style gambling products offered by State and Tribe-regulated 
entities (i.e., bilateral and non-tradable) and swaps offered by CFTC-
registered entities (i.e., multilateral and tradable), the Product Test 
is designed to nonetheless acknowledge the functional distinction 
between the types of products and relationships that traditionally fall 
within the scope of derivatives regulation under the CEA from those 
traditionally addressed by State or Tribal gaming regulation.\78\ By 
distinguishing agreements, contracts, or transactions entered into on a 
bilateral basis from those entered into on a multilateral basis, the 
Product Test recognizes that a principal purpose of the CEA is to 
establish a regulatory framework for price discovery on ``liquid, fair 
and financially secure trading facilities'' \79\--that is, trading on 
multilateral execution venues such as DCMs and SEFs--whereas State and 
Tribal gaming regulation typically concerns bets or wagers in which the 
odds or ``line'' are set by a licensed gaming operator acting as 
principal with its customers on a bilateral basis. The Product Test 
thereby ensures that State and Tribal-regulated entities do not, in the 
future, begin offering products properly within the Commission's 
jurisdiction, unless they do so consistent with the CEA and the 
Commission's regulations.

III. Request for Comment

    The Commission welcomes public comments from interested persons 
regarding any aspect of the Interim Final Rule. The Commission also 
seeks comment on the following specific questions. The Commission will 
take into consideration comments received and may modify the Interim 
Final Rule if warranted.
    (1) This Interim Final Rule limits the exclusion to agreements, 
contracts, and transactions offered by a person that satisfies the 
Provider Test. Are there additional categories of providers that the 
Provider Test should cover beyond State-regulated gaming companies and 
Tribes, such as those discussed in note 43? If so, please explain the 
basis for including each additional category of qualifying provider.
    (2) The Provider Test requires that the agreement, contract, or 
transaction be regulated as gaming or gambling under applicable State 
or Tribal law. Is it clear which agreements, contracts, or transactions 
are subject to these regulations? Are there better ways to define how 
State or Tribal gaming regulation should apply to agreements, 
contracts, or transactions covered by the exclusion? If so, please 
explain.
    (3) The Product Test turns on whether the agreement, contract, or 
transaction is traded on a board of trade, an organized exchange, a 
SEF, or any other market in which trades are either executed 
multilaterally or subject to individual negotiation. Are there better 
ways to draw an objective, functional distinction between swaps, on the 
one hand, and traditional gambling products, on the other hand? If so, 
please explain, including how any alternative or additional basis for a 
distinction would be consistent with the CEA, including the definitions 
of ``swap,'' ``commodity,'' and ``excluded commodity'' and the Special 
Rule.

IV. Related Matters

A. Administrative Procedure Act

1. Legal Standard
    Under section 533(b)(B) of the Administrative Procedure Act 
(``APA''), agencies need not engage in notice-and-comment rulemaking 
``when the agency for good cause finds'' that such ``notice and public 
procedure . . . are impracticable, unnecessary, or contrary to the 
public interest.'' \82\ In interpreting this ``good cause'' standard, 
courts have looked to APA Senate Report, which explains that `` 
`[i]mpracticable' means a situation in which the due and required 
execution of the agency functions would be unavoidably prevented by its 
undertaking public rulemaking proceedings . . . . `Public interest' 
supplements the term[ ] `impracticable' . . .; it requires that public 
rule-making procedures shall not prevent an agency from operating.'' 
\83\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \82\ 5 U.S.C. 553(b)(B).
    \83\ See, e.g., National Nutritional Foods Ass'n v. Kennedy, 572 
F.2d 377, 385 (2d Cir. 1978) (quoting S. Rep. No. 79-752, at 200 
(1945)).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    ``[T]here is a general consensus among the circuit courts that an 
agency's invocation of the good cause exception [to notice-and-comment 
rulemaking] should . . . be upheld . . . where following the regular 
rulemaking procedure would result in serious or real harm.'' \84\ 
Absent traditional emergencies, courts have found good cause where 
notice-and-comment rulemaking would create ``possible fiscal harm,'' 
where ``the agency's prior regulations or guidelines had been 
invalidated by a court order, and thus, immediate promulgation of a new 
regulation was needed to prevent harm to the public in the interim,'' 
\85\ and/or where such rulemakings are not intended to be 
permanent.\86\

2. Rationale for Interim Final Rule
    Recent developments require the Commission to exercise its 
statutory authority to further define the term ``swap'' to explicitly 
exclude casino-style gambling products, including sports-wagering 
contracts entered into on a sportsbook, from the swap definition. As a 
group of 39 States and the District of Columbia recently observed, 
``[f]ederal and state courts are hopelessly confused and divided over 
an issue of immense importance''--whether, among other things, the swap 
definition is broad enough to encompass casino-style gambling products, 
which would threaten, among other things, State regulatory authority 
and Tribal revenue.\87\ For example, and as described above in Part I, 
the Commonwealth of Kentucky has claimed that determining that event 
contracts are swaps under the CEA amounts to ``[t]ransferring every 
ounce of authority over sports wagering from the States to the CFTC.'' 
\88\ And the State of New Jersey recently argued to the United States 
Supreme Court that ``[n]othing in the [CEA] gives the CFTC 
unprecedented authority to become the sole regulator of sports gambling 
in this country.'' \89\

of a federal agency, the CFTC.\93\

cause.'' Accordingly, the RFA's requirements relating to an initial and 
final regulatory flexibility analysis do not apply to this rule.\108\

C. Paperwork Reduction Act

    The Paperwork Reduction Act of 1995 (``PRA'') \109\ imposes certain 
requirements on federal agencies, including the Commission, in 
connection with conducting or sponsoring any ``collection of 
information,'' as defined by the PRA. Under the PRA, an agency may not 
conduct or sponsor, and a person is not required to respond to, a 
collection of information unless it displays a currently valid control 
number from the Office of Management and Budget (``OMB'').\110\ The PRA 
is intended, in part, to minimize the paperwork burden created for 
individuals, businesses, and other persons as a result of the 
collection of information by federal agencies, and to ensure the 
greatest possible benefit and utility of information created, 
collected, maintained, used, shared, and disseminated by or for the 
federal

[[Page 65978]]

government.\111\ The PRA applies to all information, regardless of form 
or format, whenever the federal government is obtaining, causing to be 
obtained, or soliciting information, and includes required disclosure 
to third parties or the public, of facts or opinions, when the 
information collection calls for answers to identical questions posed 
to, or identical reporting or recordkeeping requirements imposed on, 
ten or more persons.\112\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \109\ 44 U.S.C. 3501 et seq.
    \110\ See 44 U.S.C. 3507(a)(3); 5 CFR 1320.5(a)(3).
    \111\ See 44 U.S.C. 3501.
    \112\ See 44 U.S.C. 3502(3).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    This Interim Final Rule would not impose any new collection of 
information requirements that require approval of OMB under 44 U.S.C.

jurisdiction, this Interim Final Rule clarifies the definition of 
``swap'' excludes casino-style gambling products.
    The Interim Final Rule does not establish any new reporting

existing, previously approved information collections to a new class of

D. Consideration of Benefits and Costs

1. Introduction
    The Commission is mindful of the economic effects, including the 
benefits and costs, of the Interim Final Rule. Section 15(a) of the CEA 
requires the Commission to consider the benefits and costs of its 
actions before promulgating a regulation under the CEA or issuing 
certain orders.\113\ As required by section 15(a), the Commission 
evaluates these benefits and costs evaluated in light of five broad 
areas of market and public concern: (1) protection of market 
participants and the public; (2) efficiency, competitiveness, and 
financial integrity of futures markets; (3) price discovery; (4) sound 
risk management practices; and (5) other public interest 
considerations.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \113\ 7 U.S.C. 19(a).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    The Interim Final Rule excludes casino-style gambling products from 
the definition of swaps. In promulgating the Interim Final Rule, the 
Commission would not change the legal rights or obligations of market 
participants under the CEA because the Commission already treats 
casino-style gambling products as outside its jurisdiction. Instead, 
consistent with the CEA, the Interim Final Rule will reduce 
interpretive uncertainty by codifying the Commission's understanding 
that regulation of casino-style gambling products is outside of the 
Commission's jurisdiction, affirming the long-standing regulatory 
authority of States and Tribes.
    The Commission qualitatively discusses the benefits and costs of 
the Interim Final Rule below because it is not practicable to quantify 
the effect of greater regulatory certainty on market participant 
behavior.
2. Baseline
    The baseline against which the benefits and costs of the Interim 
Final Rule are measured consists of the current regulatory framework 
and the current state of Commission-regulated markets.
    The Commission has exclusive jurisdiction to regulate transactions 
involving swaps (other than security-based swaps).\114\ Under the plain 
language of the CEA, casino-style gambling products are not swaps or 
any other instrument that the Commission regulates and therefore, 
outside the scope of the Commission's jurisdiction. The Commission is 
not aware of any casino-style gambling products that are treated as 
Commission-regulated instruments or otherwise traded on Commission-
licensed DCMs or SEFs.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \114\ See 7 U.S.C. 2(a)(1)(A).
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    However, as discussed above, recent court decisions regarding 
whether sports-related event contracts are swaps and the breadth of the 
swaps definition under the CEA, including casino-style gambling 
products, has created legal uncertainty.\115\
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

    \115\ Compare Flaherty, 172 F.4th at 227, with Assad, 2026 WL 
2543846.
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

3. Benefits
    Because the Interim Final Rule does not change the legal rights or 
obligations of market participants under the CEA, the primary benefit 
of the Interim Final Rule is the immediate mitigation of regulatory and 
interpretive uncertainty triggered by recent, conflicting federal 
appellate court interpretations of the CEA's swap definition.
    By expressly stating that traditional casino-style gambling 
products and sportsbook wagers do not constitute swaps, the Interim 
Final Rule could benefit gambling industry participants, State gaming 
commissions, and Tribal authorities. Insofar as industry participants 
and others are less likely to misapprehend the scope of the 
Commission's regulatory authority, they may benefit by allocating less 
capital to challenge or defend against Federal derivatives oversight, 
or to unnecessarily re-engineer their localized wagering 
infrastructures to meet incompatible Federal frameworks.
4. Costs
    Because the Interim Final Rule codifies the Commission's long-
standing operational baseline--that traditional casino-style gambling 
products reside outside its jurisdiction--it does not directly impose 
any new or incremental compliance costs under the CEA or the 
Commissions' regulations on derivatives market participants or retail 
gaming operators.
    Codification of the exclusion of casino-style gambling products 
might slightly increase the incentive for certain entities to attempt 
to structure certain derivatives contracts as locally regulated 
gambling wagers to evade Commission jurisdiction. However, because the 
alternative to Federal regulation is compliance with a patchwork of 
State and Tribal regulatory frameworks (including outright prohibition 
in some jurisdictions), the risk of increased regulatory arbitrage is 
likely minimal. If entities nevertheless seek to evade Commission 
jurisdiction, the Commission understands that State and Tribal 
regulatory frameworks--although different than the Federal regulatory 
framework--would also offer some protection to industry participants 
and the public.
5. Section 15(a) Factors
    Because the Interim Final Rule is consistent with existing law and 
market

[[Page 65979]]

practice, it will have limited impact on protecting market participants 
and the public; efficiency, competitiveness, and financial integrity of 
the futures markets; price discovery; sound risk management practices; 
and other public interest considerations.
6. Regulatory Alternatives
    Given the Interim Final Rule seeks only to clarify that casino-
style gambling products are outside of the Commission's jurisdiction, 
the Commission has not identified any viable regulatory alternatives.

F. Executive Orders 12866, 13563, and 14192

    Executive Orders 12866 and 13563 direct agencies to assess all 
costs and benefits of available regulatory alternatives and, if 
regulation is necessary, to select those regulatory approaches that 
maximize net benefits (including potential economic, environmental, 
public health and safety, and other advantages; and distributive 
impacts). Section 3(f) of Executive Order 12866 defines a ``significant 
regulatory action'' as any regulatory action that is likely to result 
in a rule that may: (1) have an annual effect on the economy of $100 
million or more or adversely affect in a material way the economy, a 
sector of the economy, productivity, competition, jobs, the 
environment, public health or safety, or State, local, or Tribal 
governments or communities; (2) create a serious inconsistency or 
otherwise interfere with an action taken or planned by another agency; 
(3) materially alter the budgetary impact of entitlements, grants, user 
fees, or loan programs or the rights and obligations of recipients 
thereof; or (4) raise novel legal or policy issues arising out of legal 
mandates, or the President's priorities.
    OMB has determined that this action is a significant regulatory 
action as defined under section 3(f) of Executive Order 12866 and 
therefore this action has been reviewed by OMB, consistent with 
Executive Order 14215.
    This Interim Final Rule is not an Executive Order 14192 regulatory 
action, because it imposes no more than de minimis net costs.

G. Congressional Review Act

    Subtitle E of the Small Business Regulatory Enforcement Fairness 
Act of 1996, also known as the Congressional Review Act (``CRA''), 
generally provides for congressional review of agency rules. CFTC must 
submit a report to Congress and the Comptroller General when it issues 
a final rule, as defined in the CRA. An agency rule, in addition to 
being subject to congressional oversight, may also be subject to a 
delayed effective date if the rule is a ``major rule.'' OIRA has 
determined that this rule is not a ``major rule'' within the meaning of 
the relevant sections of the CRA. CFTC will also file appropriate 
reports with Congress and the Comptroller General so this rule may be 
reviewed.
