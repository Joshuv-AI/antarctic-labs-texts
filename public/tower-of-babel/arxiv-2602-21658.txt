Dirichlet, Neumann, Mixed and self-dual holography:


Abstract
Motivated by applications of self-dual theories to the AdS/CFT correspondence, we study self-dual Yang–Mills theory (SDYM) and its relation to Yang–Mills theory and to Chalmers–Siegel theory with Dirichlet, Neumann, and mixed boundary conditions. A Fefferman–Graham analysis of SDYM is performed to identify its boundary CFT data. We make a proposal for self-dual holography that defines 3 ​ d 3d “self-dual CFTs”. The bulk-to-bulk and boundary-to-bulk propagators for SDYM and for Yang–Mills/Chalmers–Siegel theory with mixed boundary conditions are derived in Feynman and axial gauges. Three- and four-point functions are computed in the spinor-helicity formalism, and the relations among the results in the various theories are clarified. The flat limit and the gauge-(in)dependence of the results are analyzed.

For (A)dS/CFT applications, the most important properties are that self-dual theories admit a negative or positive cosmological constant and are UV-finite. It is the UV-divergences of the pure gravity that require a complete model of quantum gravity. This either complicates the bulk dynamics, e.g. calling for a stringy completion, or encourages one to look for simpler models in lower dimensions where gravity does not have propagating degrees of freedom. Self-dual theories, e.g. self-dual gravity can provide us with a useful compromise where a theory is simple enough, yet nontrivial and free of UV-divergences.

Two and half theories

Since all theories we consider are conformally invariant, it is advantageous to transfer them from the usual Poincare coordinates on
\text{AdS}_{4}
to the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{4}
with
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
z>0
, with the boundary still being at
z=0
. Therefore, except for Section
3.1
, we will always work in flat Euclidean space.

For AdS/CFT applications that we consider, Yang--Mills theory should be supplemented with a theta-term. To facilitate the transition to the Chalmers–Siegel formulation and to SDYM, it is convenient to express the action in the two-component spinor language:
3
3

3

Indices
A,B,...=1,2
,
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},...=1,2
are the indices of the two two-dimensional representations of the Lorentz algebra, which is
sl(2,\mathbb{C})
or
su(2)\oplus su(2)
, depending on the signature. The indices are raised by
\epsilon_{AB}=-\epsilon_{BA}
and
\epsilon_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
, e.g.
\xi^{A}=\epsilon^{AB}\xi_{B}
,
\xi^{A}\epsilon_{AB}=\xi_{B}
. For example, for the field strength we have
F_{\mu\nu}\equiv F_{AA^{\prime},BB^{\prime}}=F_{AB}\epsilon_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}+\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\epsilon_{AB}
and
F_{AB}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\partial_{AB^{\prime}}A{}_{B,}{}^{B^{\prime}}\,+\partial_{BB^{\prime}}A{}_{A,}{}^{B^{\prime}}\,+A_{A,B^{\prime}}A{}_{B,}{}^{B^{\prime}}\,+A_{B,B^{\prime}}A{}_{A,}{}^{B^{\prime}}\,)
.

\displaystyle S_{\text{YM},\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\int\left(F_{AB}F^{AB}+\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\bar{F}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right)+\frac{b}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\int\left(F_{AB}F^{AB}-\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\bar{F}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right)\,.

(2.1)

Here,
a
and
b
are the factors in front of the Yang–Mills Lagrangian
\mathrm{Tr}\,F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
and the theta-term
\mathrm{Tr}\,F\wedge F
, respectively. We find it very useful to rearrange the terms as

\displaystyle S_{\text{cYM},\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{2}\,\mathrm{Tr}\int F_{AB}F^{AB}+\frac{(b-a)}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\int\left(F_{AB}F^{AB}-\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\bar{F}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right)\,.

(2.2)

The bulk interactions are now confined to
(F_{AB})^{2}
, which is much simpler to deal with. The rest is in the topological term that disappears in flat space and leads to simple contributions in
\text{AdS}_{4}
. We call this intermediate step chiral Yang–Mills theory, cYM. Following Chalmers and Siegel
[
32
]
, by introducing an auxiliary field
\Psi_{AB}
, one can rewrite cYM as

\displaystyle S_{\text{Ch.Si.},\theta}

\displaystyle=a\epsilon\,\mathrm{Tr}\int\left(\Psi_{AB}F^{AB}-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Psi_{AB}\Psi^{AB}\right)+\tfrac{(b-a)}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\int\left(F_{AB}F^{AB}-\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\bar{F}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right)\,.

(2.3)

SDYM emerges when the
\Psi^{2}
-term, which is gauge invariant on its own, is dropped. Formally, one has to take the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and ensure that
a\epsilon
stays finite, say
\tilde{a}=1
, and
b-a
goes to
\varkappa
:

\displaystyle S_{\text{SDYM},\theta}

\displaystyle=\tilde{a}\mathrm{Tr}\int\Psi_{AB}F^{AB}+\frac{\varkappa}{4}\,\mathrm{Tr}\int\left(F_{AB}F^{AB}-\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\bar{F}^{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}\right)\,.

(2.4)

We would like to stress that
\epsilon
is not a genuine coupling constant as long as
\epsilon\neq 0
. In addition, SDYM is not a simple smooth limit of YM. It might be better to think of SDYM as of a closed subsector of YM: all solutions and amplitudes of SDYM are solutions and (a subset of) amplitudes of YM. Also, the connection between YM and SDYM requires Chalmers–Siegel theory. If one just imposes
F_{AB}=0
in the original YM action, or better in its cYM form (
2.2
), the bulk interactions vanish.
4
4

4

In Minkowski signature,
F_{AB}
and
\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}
isolate negative and positive helicity components of
A_{\mu}
. If we set
F_{AB}=0
, we are left with a single helicity and there cannot be any Lorentz-invariant action for this one.

3
AdS/CFT (self)-duality

The key steps to embed SDYM into AdS/CFT duality are: (a) to work out the Fefferman-Graham expansion to reveal the boundary data; (b) to investigate possible boundary conditions and the relation of those to YM; (c) as a result, there is a natural extrapolation of the AdS/CFT dictionary from YM to SDYM. We follow these steps below.

3.1
Fefferman-Graham expansion

YM.

The Fefferman-Graham expansion of free YM, i.e. Maxwell theory, is well-known
[

We are temporarily back to Poincare coordinates to have the standard

\displaystyle\nabla^{BB^{\prime}}\nabla_{BB^{\prime}}A_{A,A^{\prime}}-\nabla^{BB^{\prime}}\nabla_{AA^{\prime}}A_{B,B^{\prime}}=0\,,

(3.1)

can be solved asymptotically near the boundary
z=0
by plugging in the expansion
6
6

6

One notational improvement is to denote a group of symmetric indices or the indices to be symmetrized by the same letter, e.g.

\displaystyle A_{AA}=z^{\Delta}\Phi_{AA}=z^{\Delta}(\phi_{AA}^{(0)}+z\phi^{(1)}_{AA}+z^{2}\phi^{(2)}_{AA}+\dots)\,.

(3.2)

Here, we imposed the axial gauge

\epsilon_{AA^{\prime}}
. Therefore, it means that
\Phi_{A,B}
is symmetric, hence, the comma can be dropped. To make a shortcut, we in addition impose the Coulomb condition

on the boundary. Since the equation is second order, there are two independent fall-offs:
\Delta=1
and
\Delta=2
. The complete solution reads

\displaystyle\Phi^{AA}=\cosh(kz)\Phi_{0}^{AA}+\tfrac{1}{k}\sinh(kz)\Phi_{1}^{AA}=\Phi_{0}^{AA}+z\Phi_{1}^{AA}+...\,,

(3.3)

where
\Phi_{0}^{i}\equiv\Phi^{i}|_{z=0}

A_{i}=z(a_{i}+zE_{i})+...

is the electric field and

\displaystyle\Phi^{AA}=\exp[-kz]\Phi_{0}^{AA}=\Phi_{0}^{AA}-zk\Phi_{0}^{AA}+...

(3.4)

In other words, the regularity implies
E_{i}=-k\Pi_{ij}a^{j}
, where
\Pi_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-k_{i}k_{j}/k^{2}
(if we drop the transversality condition imposed on
\Phi_{i}|_{z=0}\equiv a_{i}

\epsilon_{AA^{\prime}}
-traceless) gauge, the Fefferman-Graham expansion of Maxwell theory is that of a conformally-coupled scalar field
\phi=0
that carries an additional index

SDYM: negative helicity.

The
\Psi

\Psi_{AB}

\displaystyle\nabla{}_{B}{}^{A^{\prime}}\,(z^{2}\Psi^{AB})=0\,.

(3.5)

They imply that the boundary data
\psi_{0}^{AB}=\Psi^{AB}|_{z=0}
is transverse
k_{AA}\psi_{0}^{AA}=0
and the complete expansion reads

\displaystyle\Psi^{AA}

\displaystyle=\cosh(kz)\psi_{0}^{AA}+\tfrac{1}{k}\sinh(kz)k{}^{A}{}_{B}\,\psi_{0}^{AB}\,.

(3.6)

Since the equation is first order, the boundary data is only Dirichlet. In order to find regular solutions, we need to split
\psi_{0}^{AB}

SDYM: positive helicity.

In this case there are fewer equations than fields, but there is also a gauge symmetry, (recall that
A_{A,A^{\prime}}=z\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}
)

\displaystyle\nabla{}_{A}{}^{B^{\prime}}\,A_{A,B^{\prime}}=0\,,

\displaystyle\delta A_{AA^{\prime}}=\nabla_{AA^{\prime}}\xi\,.

(3.8)

With inspiration from the
\Psi

\displaystyle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{0}_{-}e^{+kz}q_{A}k_{A^{\prime}}\,,\hskip 20.00003pt

\displaystyle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{0}_{+}e^{-kz}q_{A}\bar{k}_{A^{\prime}}\,,

(3.9)

where
q_{A}
is an arbitrary reference spinor. In the axial gauge
\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}=\Phi_{AA^{\prime}}
is symmetric, i.e.
q_{A}
is either
k_{A}
or
{{\bar{k}}}_{A}
, and similar solutions read

\displaystyle\Phi_{AA}

\displaystyle=\phi^{0}_{-}e^{+kz}k_{A}k_{A}\,,

\displaystyle\Phi_{AA}

\displaystyle=\phi^{0}_{+}e^{-kz}\bar{k}_{A}\bar{k}_{A}\,.

(3.10)

In the physical gauge,
k_{AA}\phi_{0}^{AA}=0
, the Fefferman-Graham expansion can be summarized as

\displaystyle\Phi^{AA}

\displaystyle=\cosh(kz)\phi_{0}^{AA}-\tfrac{1}{k}\sinh(kz)\not{k}\phi_{0}^{AA}\,.

(3.11)

Helicity decomposition.

To make the above more self-evident, let us adopt the physical gauge and decompose
\Psi
and
\Phi
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Phi^{AA}(\vec{k},z)&=\epsilon_{+}^{AA}(\vec{k})\Phi_{+}(\vec{k},z)+\epsilon_{-}^{AA}(\vec{k})\Phi_{-}(\vec{k},z)\,,\\
\Psi^{AA}(\vec{k},z)&=\epsilon_{+}^{AA}(\vec{k})\Psi_{+}(\vec{k},z)+\epsilon_{-}^{AA}(\vec{k})\Psi_{-}(\vec{k},z)\,,\end{aligned}

(3.12)

where the polarization vectors are defined as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{+}^{A}(\vec{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{{{\bar{k}}}^{A}}{\sqrt{2k}}\,,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{-}^{A}(\vec{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{k^{A}}{\sqrt{2k}}\,,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\pm}^{AA}(\vec{k})

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\pm}^{A}\epsilon_{\pm}^{A}\,.

(3.13)

With
D_{\pm}=\partial_{z}\pm k
the equations of motion are simply (note that
D_{+}D_{-}=\partial^{2}_{z}-k^{2}
)

\displaystyle D_{+}\Phi_{+}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle D_{-}\Psi_{+}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle D_{-}\Phi_{-}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle D_{+}\Psi_{-}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(3.14)

The Fefferman-Graham expansions for these two types of equations are

\displaystyle D_{+}f

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle f=\exp(-kz)f_{0}\,,

\displaystyle D_{-}g

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle g=\exp(+kz)g_{0}\,.

(3.15)

The helicity clearly separates solutions into (ir)regular ones. Imposing regularity in SDYM leaves us with
\Phi_{+}
and
\Psi_{-}
as the fields that can have free boundary data. In the Chalmers–Siegel theory
\Psi_{\pm}
obeys the same equations as in SDYM, but one also has
\epsilon\Psi_{\pm}=D_{\pm}\Phi_{\pm}
.

3.2
Boundary conditions

The Fefferman-Graham decomposition allows us to identify the boundary data. In YM we have
\Phi^{0}_{\pm}\leftrightarrow\Phi_{i}^{0}\equiv\Phi_{i}|_{z=0}
,
\Phi^{1}_{\pm}\leftrightarrow\Phi_{i}^{1}\equiv\partial_{z}\Phi_{i}|_{z=0}
, which, in the usual terms, are a gauge field
a_{i}
and the electric field
E_{i}
,
\partial_{i}E^{i}=0
, respectively. More invariantly,
a_{i}
modulo gauge symmetry is characterized by its magnetic field
B_{i}\sim\epsilon_{ijk}F^{jk}
. Therefore, the standard boundary conditions are: Dirichlet, where
a_{i}
or
B_{i}
are fixed on the boundary; Neumann, where
E_{i}
is fixed on the boundary. They are also called magnetic and electric.

More generally, boundary conditions select a Lagrangian submanifold of the Fefferman-Graham boundary data that is consistent with having regular solutions in the bulk. The symplectic structure of Maxwell/Yang–Mills theory at the free level,

\displaystyle\Omega=\delta^{ij}\,\delta A^{0}_{i}\wedge\delta A^{1}_{j}=\delta\Phi_{+}^{0}\wedge\delta\Phi_{+}^{1}+\delta\Phi_{-}^{0}\wedge\delta\Phi_{-}^{1}\,,

(3.16)

tolerates so-called mixed (conformally-invariant) boundary conditions
[
24
,
42
]

\displaystyle F^{AA}e^{i\gamma}+\bar{F}^{AA}e^{-i\gamma}

\displaystyle=\text{fixed}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle B_{i}\cos\gamma+iE_{i}\sin\gamma=\text{fixed}\,.

(3.17)

Here,
\gamma=0
and
\gamma=\pi/2
correspond to Dirichlet and Neumann conditions, respectively. The Yang–Mills action is already stationary with Dirichlet boundary conditions
\delta S_{\text{YM}}=\int_{\partial M}E_{i}\delta A^{i}
. The mixed boundary conditions require a boundary Chern–Simons term since
\delta S_{\text{CS}}=i\kappa\int_{\partial M}B_{i}\delta A^{i}
, where
\kappa
is the non-normalized level. With the sources turned off the action
S_{\text{YM}}+S_{\text{CS}}
is stationary provided that

\displaystyle i\kappa B_{i}+E_{i}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\kappa=-\cot(\gamma)\,.

(3.18)

In terms of the helicity components the mixed boundary conditions with sources read

\displaystyle k\Phi^{0}_{+}\cos\gamma+\Phi^{1}_{+}i\sin\gamma

\displaystyle=\text{fixed}_{+}\,,

\displaystyle-k\Phi^{0}_{-}\cos\gamma+\Phi^{1}_{-}i\sin\gamma

\displaystyle=\text{fixed}_{-}\,.

(3.19)

It is obvious that SDYM should be approached as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in the Chalmers–Siegel action, but on a manifold with boundaries it has to be done simultaneously with imposing the self-dual (SD) boundary condition (otherwise, the system is over-constrained). The SD boundary condition is
F_{AA}|_{z=0}=0
. But it is more than a boundary condition since
F_{AA}|_{z=0}=0
implies
F_{AA}=0
in the bulk as well.
9
9

9

Indeed, in terms of the boundary data a general solution for
\Phi_{\pm}
gives for
F_{AA}
:
F_{+}=e^{+kz}(\Phi^{1}_{+}+k\Phi^{0}_{+})
,
F_{-}=e^{-kz}(\Phi^{1}_{-}-k\Phi^{0}_{-})
. We see that
F_{\pm}=0
everywhere if
F_{\pm}|_{z=0}=0
. Even simpler: the first order operator
\partial_{z}\pm k
gives vanishing solution if we start from
0
on the boundary.
The SD condition can be reached at
\gamma=-i\infty
in (
3.17
), or
\kappa=-i
in (
3.18
). Another feature of the SD limit is that the initial source gets suppressed.
10
10

10

In a more standard situation of the mixed boundary conditions
a\phi+b\partial_{z}\phi|_{z=0}=Q
for a scalar field
\phi
, one can reach Dirichlet either as
b=0
or as
a\rightarrow\infty
. The latter forces one to amplify the source
Q\rightarrow aQ
, otherwise we lose degrees of freedom. This is impossible in the SD case since
F_{AA}=0
does not allow for a source.

One should not impose the SD boundary condition directly in YM. Indeed, the regular branch of
\Phi_{-}
gets eliminated. Therefore, we are left with a single degree of freedom
\Phi_{+}
and there cannot be any Lorentz-invariant action for this one, i.e. all bulk interactions are gone as well. In the Chalmers–Siegel theory the limit
\epsilon=0
preserves the number of degrees of freedom: one still has two regular solutions,
\Phi_{+}
and
\Psi_{-}
. Therefore, it is
\Phi_{+}
and
\Psi_{-}
that should have sources at the boundary:

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}^{0}

\displaystyle=\text{fixed}\,,

\displaystyle\Psi_{-}^{0}=\text{fixed}\,,

(3.20)

which is compatible with the SDYM symplectic form

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\delta\Psi_{AA}\wedge\delta\Phi^{AA}=\delta\Psi_{+}\wedge\delta\Phi_{+}+\delta\Psi_{-}\wedge\delta\Phi_{-}\,.

(3.21)

It seems that the limit is discontinuous in that the boundary data, the sources, jump from (
3.17
) to
\Phi_{+}
and
\Psi_{-}
. This can be made look “less discontinuous” by noticing that fixing
\Phi_{+}^{0}
in SDYM means the Dirichlet condition on
\Phi_{+}
in YM, i.e. the “same”
\Phi^{0}_{+}
gets fixed, while fixing
\Psi_{-}
in SDYM can be understood as the Neumann condition on
\Phi_{-}
. Indeed, with
\Phi_{-}^{1}=\text{fixed}
, the regularity implies
\Phi_{-}=-k^{-1}e^{-kz}\Phi_{-}^{1}
. Therefore,
\epsilon\Psi_{-}=D_{-}\Phi_{-}=2e^{-kz}\Phi_{-}^{1}
and we can identify
\epsilon\Psi_{-}^{0}=2\Phi_{-}^{1}
.

3.3
AdS/CFT dictionary

Let us recall the standard lore about Dirichlet, Neumann and mixed holography. The path integral in AdS with Dirichlet boundary conditions for the gauge field computes the generating functional
W_{\text{D}}[A]
of correlators of a conserved current in the boundary CFT:

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\int d^{3}x\,J^{m}(x)A_{m}(x)\right\rangle_{\text{CFT}}

\displaystyle=\int D\Phi\Big|_{\Phi^{0}_{i}\rightarrow A_{i}}\exp{S[\Phi]}\,.

(3.22)

With the Neumann boundary conditions imposed, the path integral computes the generating functional
W_{\text{N}}[E]
of correlation functions of a gauge field
A_{m}
on the boundary

\displaystyle\int DA\,\left\langle\exp\int d^{3}x\,J^{m}(x)A_{m}(x)+E^{m}A_{m}(x)+...\right\rangle_{\text{CFT}}

5
Discussion and Conclusions

In the paper we made a proposal for the AdS/CFT duality that has self-dual theories on the bulk side of the duality. Self-dual theories are chiral, i.e. they discriminate fields by helicity. Therefore, it is not surprising that the AdS/CFT dictionary in the self-dual case also has rules that depend on helicity:
\Phi
of SDYM couples to a positive helicity half
J_{+}
of the current on the boundary and
\Psi
couples to a negative helicity half
a_{-}
of a gauge field on the boundary. The latter can be dualized to
J_{-}
to build the complete current (the components that are independent for a conserved current).

Concretely, we started from Yang–Mills theory with a theta-term to achieve the most general mixed boundary conditions. We computed two-, three- and four-point correlators in YM and SDYM with the goal to reveal the relation to SDYM. As a by-product, we have also computed three and four-point functions for Dirichlet, Neumann and mixed boundary conditions in Yang–Mills theory. The Dirichlet results in one or another form have already been available, see e.g.
[

,
47

[

The relation between YM and SDYM is best seen via the Chalmers–Siegel “parent action” that reduces to both of them. However, the SD limit is subtle for many reasons, e.g. (a) the second order theory becomes a first order one, which changes the nature of the boundary problem/conditions/propagators; (b) Chalmers–Siegel’s
\epsilon
is not a genuine coupling constant; (b) YM with
F_{AB}=0
imposed is essentially an empty theory in the bulk. What seems to make sense (as in flat space) is to compute the “amplitudes” that are shared by YM and SDYM. In the AdS/CFT setting this involves dialing the mixed boundary conditions to the self-dual point. Nicely, most of the diagrams that exist in YM can be either massaged to those of Chalmers–Siegel theory (where the vertex does not have any derivatives and coincides with the one of SDYM) or can be shown to vanish in the SD limit. It is quite obvious that Chalmers–Siegel theory has a smooth limit to SDYM. One important observation is that the “right” YM theory is the one with a theta-term fine-tuned to collapse the YM Lagrangian to cYM, i.e. to
(F_{AB})^{2}
, which can be resolved into the Chalmers–Siegel action. As in flat space, SDYM in (A)dS
4
is a subsector of YM in (A)dS
4
. However, identifying the relation involves boundary conditions and a subtle self-dual limit.

All computations were done in Feynman/Lorenz and axial gauge. We showed that the leading energy pole gives a flat space amplitude. We could show directly that the Dirichlet results are gauge independent. However, Neumann/mixed and, hence, SDYM correlators are gauge dependent, which is to be expected since the dual CFT has a gauge field. In addition, it is a nonabelian gauge field and, hence, the dual currents are only covariantly conserved. Interestingly, the gauge dependence of SDYM’s results is entirely explained by that of Neumann’s, the latter is a part of the standard AdS/CFT duality.

CFT has two conformally-invariant structures:

\displaystyle\langle J_{m}(k)J_{n}(-k)\rangle

\displaystyle=a\,k\,\Pi^{\text{e}}_{mn}+b\,k\,\Pi^{\text{o}}_{mn}\,,

(A.1)

Here,
\Pi^{\text{e}}
is parity-even and
\Pi^{\text{o}}
is parity-odd:

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{e}}_{mn}

\displaystyle=\left(\delta_{mn}-\frac{k_{m}k_{n}}{k^{2}}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{o}}_{mn}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{mn\lambda}k^{\lambda}/k\,.

(A.2)

In the two-component spinor language the same structures read

\displaystyle\Pi_{AA,BB}^{\text{e}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{AB}\epsilon^{AB}+\frac{k^{AA}k^{BB}}{2k^{2}}\,,

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{o}}_{AA,BB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\epsilon_{AB}k_{AB}\,.

(A.3)

The two-point function can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle J_{AA}(k)J_{BB}(-k)\rangle

\displaystyle=a\,k\,\Pi_{AA,BB}^{\text{e}}+b\,k\,\Pi^{\text{o}}_{AA,BB}\,.

(A.4)

The same spinor-helicity language as discussed in Section
3.1
can be employed on the CFT side. With the help of the polarization vectors
\epsilon_{\pm}^{AB}
one can project two- and higher-point correlators onto components with specific helicities by contracting the currents as
J^{\alpha}=J(k)\cdot\epsilon^{\alpha}(k)
, where
\alpha=\pm
. The two-point functions in the helicity basis are

\displaystyle\langle J^{+}(k)J^{+}(-k)\rangle

\displaystyle=k(a-b)\,,

\displaystyle\langle J^{-}(k)J^{-}(-k)\rangle

\displaystyle=k(a+b)\,.

(A.5)

One observes that adding the parity odd contribution, e.g. via a Chern–Simons term, can cancel one of the two helicity structures. The parity-even/odd projectors can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{e}}_{AA,BB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4k^{2}}(k_{A}k_{A}{{\bar{k}}}_{B}{{\bar{k}}}_{B}+{{\bar{k}}}_{A}{{\bar{k}}}_{A}k_{B}k_{B})\,,

\displaystyle\Pi^{\text{o}}_{AA,BB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4k^{2}}(k_{A}k_{A}{{\bar{k}}}_{B}{{\bar{k}}}_{B}-{{\bar{k}}}_{A}{{\bar{k}}}_{A}k_{B}k_{B})\,.

(A.6)

To bring the holographic correlation functions closer to amplitudes, it is convenient to define
1\rangle=k^{A}
etc. and contractions
\langle 12\rangle=k^{A}_{1}(k)k_{A}^{2}(-k)
and express correlators in terms of such contractions. Note that
\epsilon_{\pm}
do not scale with the momentum, but
k\rangle
do. This way the two-point functions can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\langle J^{+}(k)J^{+}(-k)\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\langle 12\rangle^{2}}{k^{2}}(a-b)k\,,

\displaystyle\langle J^{-}(k)J^{-}(-k)\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{\alpha,\beta}\frac{\langle\bar{1}\bar{2}\rangle^{2}}{k^{2}}(a+b)k\,.

(A.7)

Note that the two-point kinematics is somewhat degenerate since the operators have the same momentum up to a sign and, hence,
\langle 12\rangle=k^{A}(k)k_{A}(-k)=-k^{A}(k){{\bar{k}}}_{A}(k)=+2k
. In particular, we see that in the helicity basis there is not much difference between the parity-odd contact contribution and the parity-even one.

Appendix B
Propagators

Propagators on constant curvature spacetimes have a long history, see e.g.
[
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
]
. Nevertheless, Charmers-Siegel theory and SDYM have not been discussed much in this context, as well as YM with mixed boundary conditions save for
[
43
]
. We use two gauges: Feynman gauge and axial gauge. The former is somewhat simpler and more covariant in the bulk, the latter is more handy in establishing the AdS/CFT dictionary. Below, we just list the results, see
[
44
]
for details, but let us make a few general comments. A bulk-to-bulk propagator consists of two parts: an inhomogeneous one, which can be obtained by a Fourier transform of the flat space propagator, and a homogeneous one, which is needed to impose boundary conditions and regularity in the interior of
\text{AdS}_{4}
:

\displaystyle G(-k,z;k,z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=G^{\text{hom}}(-k,z;k,z^{\prime})+G^{\text{inh}}(-k,z;k,z^{\prime})\,.

(B.1)

Bulk-to-bulk propagators in two different gauges are related by a “gauge transformation” and we prefer to relate everything to the Feynman gauge

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} G^{\text{some gauge}}_{AA^{\prime},BB^{\prime}}&(-k,z;k,z^{\prime})=G^{\text{F}}_{AA^{\prime},BB^{\prime}}(-k,z;k,z^{\prime})+\\
&+\nabla_{k,z^{\prime}}^{BB^{\prime}}\xi_{\text{L}}^{AA^{\prime}}(k,z,z^{\prime})+\nabla_{-k,z}^{AA^{\prime}}\xi_{\text{R}}^{BB^{\prime}}(k,z,z^{\prime})+\nabla_{-k,z}^{AA^{\prime}}\nabla_{k,z^{\prime}}^{BB^{\prime}}\xi_{\text{c}}(k,z,z^{\prime})\,,\end{aligned}

(B.2)

There is some redundancy in the last term, but it can be useful. Boundary conditions can always be imposed in terms of gauge-invariant quantities
\Psi_{AA}\equiv F_{AA}
,
\bar{\Psi}_{A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}\equiv\bar{F}_{A^{\prime}A^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} e^{i\gamma}\langle\Psi^{AA}(-k,z\rightarrow 0)\Psi^{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle&=-e^{-i\gamma}\langle\bar{\Psi}^{AA}(-k,z\rightarrow 0)\Psi^{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle\,,\\
e^{i\gamma}\langle\Psi^{AA}(-k,z\rightarrow 0)\bar{\Psi}^{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle&=-e^{-i\gamma}\langle\bar{\Psi}^{AA}(-k,z\rightarrow 0)\bar{\Psi}^{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle\,.\end{aligned}

(B.3)

A stronger version is sometimes possible where the bulk leg is kept in its original form of
\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle e^{i\gamma}\langle\Psi^{AA}(-k,z\rightarrow 0)\Phi^{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-e^{-i\gamma}\langle\bar{\Psi}^{AA}(-k,z\rightarrow 0)\Phi^{B,B}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle\,.

(B.4)

By Feynman gauge we mean the one that leaves
\square
as the kinetic operator in YM. In Chalmers–Siegel theory the
\langle\Psi\Phi\rangle
propagator is obtained via the
\Psi
equations of motion, i.e. as derivative of
\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
-propagator. The latter has a smooth limit to SDYM.

B.1
YM: Feynman

The YM propagator in Feynman gauge is obtained using the method of images. Here it is important that the reflection operation also acts on the indices of the gauge field. The Dirichlet/Neumann propagator is

\displaystyle\langle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{D/N}}=-\frac{\epsilon_{AB}\epsilon_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}}{2k}e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}\pm e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\frac{1}{2k}\left(\epsilon_{AB}\epsilon_{A^{\prime}B^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{AA^{\prime}}\epsilon_{BB^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(B.5)

where the
+
is for Dirichlet. Note that this propagator satisfies Dirichlet/Neumann boundary conditions in the boundary direction and Neumann/Dirichlet in the radial direction.

The propagator for mixed boundary conditions is decomposed into the Neumann component and a
\gamma
-dependent component,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\langle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle=\langle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{N}}+\langle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\gamma}\,,\end{aligned}

(B.6)

where the latter reads

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\gamma}&=\frac{\cos\gamma}{k}\Big(\cos{\gamma}\,\Pi^{\text{e}}_{AA^{\prime},BB^{\prime}}+i\sin{\gamma}\,\Pi^{\text{o}}_{AA^{\prime},BB^{\prime}}\Big)e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,.\end{aligned}

(B.7)

Note that in Feynman gauge, the Dirichlet propagator is not part of the continuous family of mixed boundary conditions, as setting
\gamma=0
does not yield the Dirichlet propagator. Instead, one has to pick the Dirichlet boundary condition by hand from (
B.5
). Meanwhile, the Neumann propagator is smoothly obtained by setting
\gamma=\pi/2
.

B.2
YM: Axial

The propagator in axial gauge can be related to the Feynman gauge propagator through (
B.2
). We denote the difference between the Feynman gauge and axial gauge propagator as
\Delta\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
. We note that the
\gamma
-dependent component of the propagator is the same for Feynman and axial gauge, so it suffices to look at the gauge variation of the Dirichlet and Neumann propagators. This is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta\langle\Phi_{A,A^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{D/N}}&=\nabla_{AA^{\prime}}^{-k,z}\xi_{BB^{\prime}}^{\text{R}}+\nabla_{BB^{\prime}}^{k,z^{\prime}}\xi^{\text{L}}_{AA^{\prime}}+\nabla_{AA^{\prime}}^{-k,z}\nabla_{BB^{\prime}}^{k,z^{\prime}}\xi^{\text{c}}\,,\\
\xi^{\text{L}}_{AA^{\prime}}&=\frac{\epsilon_{AA^{\prime}}}{4k^{2}}\Big(\text{sign}(z-z^{\prime})\big(1-e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}\big)+\big(1\pm e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\big)\Big)\,,\\
\xi^{\text{R}}_{BB^{\prime}}&=-\frac{\epsilon_{BB^{\prime}}}{4k^{2}}\Big(\text{sign}(z-z^{\prime})\big(1-e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}\big)+\big(1\pm e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\big)\Big)\,,\\
\xi^{\text{c}}&=\frac{1}{4k^{3}}\Big(e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}\pm e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}+k\big(|z-z^{\prime}|-(z+z^{\prime})\big)\Big)\,,\end{aligned}

(B.8)

where the
-
is for Dirichlet. The Dirichlet gauge parameters obey
\xi^{\text{L}}_{AA^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}=0)=\xi^{\text{R}}_{AA^{\prime}}(z=0)=\xi^{\text{c}}(z=0)=\xi^{\text{c}}(z^{\prime}=0)=0
, which proves crucial for the gauge independence of Dirichlet correlators. For Neumann boundary conditions, the gauge parameters do not vanish on the boundary. The axial gauge propagator with Dirichlet boundary conditions is known since
[
70
]
.

B.3
Chalmers–Siegel: Feynman

Chalmers–Siegel is equivalent to cYM when evaluated on-shell. It is therefore not surprising that the
\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
propagator in the two formulations agree. In addition to
\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
, Chalmers Siegel contains a
\langle\Psi\Phi\rangle
and
\langle\Psi\Psi\rangle
propagator. The
\langle\Psi\Phi\rangle
propagator is related to the
\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
one through

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle=\nabla^{-k,z}_{AA^{\prime}}\langle\Phi{}_{A,}{}^{A^{\prime}}\,(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle.

(B.9)

For the various (components of) propagators we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{D}}&=-\frac{\epsilon_{AB}}{2k}\big(k_{AB^{\prime}}-\text{sign}(z-z^{\prime})k\epsilon_{AB^{\prime}}\big)e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}+\frac{\epsilon_{AB^{\prime}}}{2k}\bar{k}_{A}k_{B}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,,\\
\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{N}}&=-\frac{\epsilon_{AB}}{2k}\big(k_{AB^{\prime}}-\text{sign}(z-z^{\prime})k\epsilon_{AB^{\prime}}\big)e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}-\frac{\epsilon_{AB^{\prime}}}{2k}\bar{k}_{A}k_{B}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,,\\
\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\gamma}&=-\frac{1+e^{-2i\gamma}}{4k^{2}}\bar{k}_{A}\bar{k}_{A}k_{B}k_{B^{\prime}}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,.\end{aligned}

(B.10)

The complete propagator for mixed boundary conditions is the sum of the Neumann “base” and of the
\gamma
-dependent piece above. The Dirichlet propagator is given as is.
One crucial subtlety exists for the
\langle\Psi\Psi\rangle
propagator. Inverting the kinetic term in the Chalmers–Siegel actions demands

\displaystyle\nabla{}_{A}{}^{A^{\prime}}\,\langle\Psi^{AA}(-k,z)\Psi^{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=0\,,

(B.11)

while taking derivatives on both legs of the
\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
propagator gives the two point function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle F^{AA}(-k,z)F^{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle&=(\nabla_{-k,z}){}^{A}{}_{A^{\prime}}\,(\nabla_{k,z^{\prime}}){}^{B}{}_{B^{\prime}}\,\langle\Phi^{AA^{\prime}}(-k,z)\Phi^{BB^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle\,.\end{aligned}

(B.12)

It consists of a term that satisfies (
B.11
) and another term containing a delta-function, which does not satisfy the equation of motion. The actual
\langle\Psi\Psi\rangle
propagators are

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Psi_{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{D}}&=\frac{\bar{k}_{A}\bar{k}_{A}k_{B}k_{B}}{2k}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,,\\
\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Psi_{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{N}}&=-\frac{\bar{k}_{A}\bar{k}_{A}k_{B}k_{B}}{2k}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,,\\
\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Psi_{BB}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\gamma}&=\frac{1+e^{-2i\gamma}}{2k}\bar{k}_{A}\bar{k}_{A}k_{B}k_{B}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,.\end{aligned}

(B.13)

B.4
Chalmers–Siegel: Axial

The gauge variations of the
\langle\Psi\Phi\rangle
and
\langle\Psi\Psi\rangle
propagators read

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Delta&\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{D/N}}=\nabla_{BB^{\prime}}^{k,z^{\prime}}\eta^{\text{L}}_{AA}\,,\\
\Delta&\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Psi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle_{\text{D/N}}=0\,,\end{aligned}

(B.14)

where
\eta^{\text{L}}_{AA}\equiv\nabla^{-k,z}_{AA^{\prime}}\xi^{\text{L}}{}_{A}{}^{A^{\prime}}\,
and
\xi^{\text{L}}_{AA^{\prime}}
is defined in (
B.8
).

B.5
SDYM: Feynman

The SDYM propagators can be obtained by either inverting the kinetic term in the SDYM action or by taking the self-dual limit
\gamma\rightarrow-i\infty
of the
\langle\Psi\Phi\rangle
propagator in Chalmers–Siegel. Note that both the
\langle\Phi\Phi\rangle
and
\langle\Psi\Psi\rangle
propagators are absent in SDYM. This leaves us with

\displaystyle\langle\Psi_{AA}(-k,z)\Phi_{B,B^{\prime}}(k,z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon_{AB}}{2k}(k_{AB^{\prime}}-\operatorname{sign}(z-z^{\prime})k\epsilon_{AB^{\prime}})e^{-k|z-z^{\prime}|}-\frac{k_{AA}}{4k^{2}}\nabla_{BB^{\prime}}^{k,z^{\prime}}e^{-k(z+z^{\prime})}\,.

(B.15)

The SDYM
\langle\Psi\Phi\rangle
propagator in axial gauge is obtained by adding the Neumann gauge variation from (
B.14
) to the Feynman propagator.
