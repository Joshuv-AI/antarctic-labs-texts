ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy DesignRetrospective cohort study.ObjectivesTo characterize longitudinal durability of cervical deformity (CD) correction, identify predictors of alignment failure, and define failure mechanism timing across the first postoperative year.Methodsof 155 operative CD patients in a prospectively maintained multicenter database, 89 had complete four-timepoint radiographic follow-up. Well-alignment was defined as cSVA <4 cm. Patients were grouped as A (maintained), B (corrected then deteriorated), or C (never aligned). DJK was most strongly associated with alignment loss (HR 5.21, 95% CI 1.94-13.98; OR 0.09), with alignment maintained 4.1 versus 9.8 months (RMST difference 5.7, p=0.001). Lower postoperative cSVA paradoxically increased failure hazard (HR 0.58/10 mm, p=0.002). Older patients deteriorated faster (Time×Age OR 0.88, p=0.022). No surgical parameters differed between groups.ConclusionsFewer than one-third of patients maintain alignment at 1 year. DJK, advanced age, and lower postoperative cSVA were most strongly associated with failure (17 events; limited precision). Mechanisms appeared to shift from junctional failure early to global decompensation late. Individualized risk stratification and surveillance are supported.Keywords: cervical deformity, sagittal alignment, distal junctional kyphosis, cervical, sagittal vertical axis, longitudinal outcomes

Introduction

Cervical deformity (CD) is a complex spinal pathology causing functional impairment, neurologic compromise, and reduced quality of life.1,2 Surgical correction improves cervical sagittal balance and patient-reported outcomes, with significant improvements in Neck Disability Index (NDI), neck pain, and EQ-5D at one year.3,4 Despite these gains, maintaining postoperative alignment remains challenging: 46% of patients fail to restore cervical sagittal vertical axis (cSVA) and 65% fail to correct T1 slope minus cervical lordosis (TS-CL) mismatch at 6 months. 5 Postoperative cervical malalignment correlates with inferior health-related quality of life (HRQL), underscoring the importance of durable correction. 6

Junctional failures, including proximal and distal junctional kyphosis (PJK/DJK), threaten correction durability in 15-35% of patients.3,5,6 Risk factors for DJK include osteoporosis, 7 preoperative cSVA greater than 30 mm, T1 slope change greater than 6 degrees, fusions of more than 5 levels, and thoracolumbar lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) selection.6,8 Other contributing factors include revision surgery, high body mass index (BMI), paraspinal muscle degeneration, and failure to address concurrent thoracolumbar deformity.5,9,10 Despite this growing risk-factor literature, the mechanisms, timing, and relative importance of different failure pathways remain poorly characterized. Prior analyses have also relied on repeated cross-sectional comparisons that treat each timepoint as statistically independent, violating the repeated-measures structure of longitudinal data and potentially inflating Type I error. This study applies Cox proportional hazards regression, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with restricted mean survival time, and mixed-effects logistic regression to characterize alignment durability, identify factors associated with correction failure, and define the temporal pattern of failure mechanisms across the first postoperative year. 11

Methods

This retrospective analysis used a prospectively maintained multicenter CD database from the International Spine Study Group (ISSG), with institutional review board (IRB) approval obtained at all participating centers. One hundred fifty-five patients underwent operative correction of CD and met inclusion criteria. All 155 contributed to the descriptive analysis of alignment at each individual visit, in which the denominator varies by visit according to imaging availability. The comparative, survival, and mixed-effects analyses require each patient to contribute alignment status at every visit, and were therefore restricted to the 89 patients (57.4%) with interpretable standing radiographs at all four timepoints (immediate postoperative, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year). The 66 patients not carried forward were excluded solely for incomplete radiographic follow-up rather than for any clinical reason; because missingness of surveillance imaging is unlikely to be independent of alignment status, the resulting potential for selection bias is examined in detail in the Discussion. Well-aligned status was defined as cSVA less than 4 cm, the most widely adopted threshold in the cervical deformity literature.3,5 Three alignment trajectory subgroups were defined: Group A (achieved and maintained correction), Group B (initial correction with subsequent deterioration), and Group C (never achieved cSVA less than 4 cm). Patients with concurrent thoracolumbar deformity were excluded to isolate cervical alignment trajectories from global spinopelvic confounders. The cohort encompassed three CD morphologies: focal cervical kyphosis, cervical flatback, and cervicothoracic deformity, classified per the ISSG cervical deformity framework. 12

DJK was defined as 10 degrees or more of kyphosis at the first two levels below the LIV; PJK as a proximal junctional angle 10 degrees or more greater than the preoperative measurement. Junctional complications were assessed only in eligible patients with a free unfused motion segment at the relevant junction. Distal and proximal junctional failure (DJF/PJF) were defined as DJK or PJK requiring surgical revision.

For patients in Group B, the mechanism responsible for loss of alignment was adjudicated at the visit at which cSVA first exceeded 40 mm. Radiographs from that visit were compared with the immediate postoperative study and the mechanism assigned according to the anatomic level at which angular change had occurred: DJK when the distal junctional angle had increased by 10 degrees or more, PJK when the proximal junctional angle had increased by 10 degrees or more, and loss of global sagittal compensation when neither junctional threshold was met but cSVA had increased in the setting of increasing T1 slope, reduced thoracic kyphosis, or altered spinopelvic parameters on full-length imaging. Where more than one criterion was satisfied, the mechanism with the larger angular change was designated primary. Mechanism assignment was performed independently by four reviewers, each blinded to trajectory group assignment. Discrepancies occurred in a small number of cases and were resolved by joint re-review of the index and immediate postoperative radiographs until consensus was reached. It should be emphasized that this adjudication is radiographic and, for patients who did not undergo revision, inferential: the mechanism is deduced from the pattern of angular change across serial films rather than confirmed by a discrete clinical or intraoperative event. Global compensation loss in particular is a diagnosis of exclusion under these criteria, assigned when no junctional threshold is crossed. These categories should therefore be read as radiographically defined patterns of deterioration rather than as verified biomechanical diagnoses.

To address repeated-measures structure, two complementary longitudinal analyses were performed. First, Cox proportional hazards regression modeled time to first alignment loss among initially aligned patients (Groups A+B, n=47, 17 events). The parsimonious primary model included baseline cSVA and DJK; an exploratory expanded model added age, cervical-thoracic pelvic angle (CTPA), thoracic kyphosis, BMI, smoking, deformity subtype, and surgical approach. With 17 events, even the parsimonious model approaches conventional events-per-variable (EPV) limits and the expanded model exceeds them; the expanded model was accordingly specified a priori as hypothesis-generating, and all hazard ratios are reported with 95% confidence intervals and interpreted as adjusted associations rather than as independent predictors. The magnitude of surgical correction and deformity-specific or age-adjusted alignment targets were not entered as covariates and were not directly assessed. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and restricted mean survival time (RMST) were calculated stratified by baseline cSVA (less than 45 mm vs 45 mm or greater) and DJK status. Second, mixed-effects logistic regression modeled well-aligned status across all four timepoints (n=356 observations; intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC]=0.36), with primary fixed effects of time, DJK, age, baseline cSVA, and a Time x Age interaction. Deformity subtype and surgical approach were entered as exploratory secondary covariates. Patient-reported outcomes (NDI, EQ-5D, and modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association [mJOA] score) were compared across trajectory groups as change from baseline. Statistical significance was set at alpha=0.05 with 95% confidence intervals throughout.

Results

Primary Analysis: Cox Proportional Hazards and Kaplan-Meier

Among 47 initially aligned patients, 17 lost correction (event rate 36.2%). The parsimonious Cox model (Figure 1) was statistically significant (concordance C=0.74, 95% CI 0.61-0.87; likelihood ratio test [LRT] p=0.003). Lower postoperative cSVA was associated with a higher hazard of correction loss after adjustment for DJK (HR 0.58 per 10 mm increase, 95% CI 0.41-0.82, p=0.002), indicating paradoxically that patients with less forward head translation postoperatively carried greater risk of subsequent deterioration. DJK showed the strongest association overall (HR 5.21, 95% CI 1.94-13.98, p=0.001), though the width of this interval - spanning an approximately two-fold to fourteen-fold hazard - reflects the limited number of events and should temper inference about effect magnitude. Proportional hazards assumptions were satisfied (Schoenfeld residuals: cSVA p=0.44, DJK p=0.31). In the expanded model (Figure 1), cervicothoracic deformity (HR 2.14, p=0.140) and combined approach compared with posterior-only (HR 2.31, p=0.118) showed directional trends that did not reach significance. Confidence intervals for these covariates are wide under EPV constraints imposed by 17 events, and they are presented strictly as hypothesis-generating trends rather than as predictors of alignment loss.

Cox Proportional Hazards - Hazard of Alignment Loss

Kaplan-Meier analysis confirmed significantly lower alignment maintenance in patients with low baseline cSVA (less than 45 mm) vs high cSVA (RMST 6.8 vs 10.1 months, difference 3.3 months, p=0.018, Table 1, Figure 2A). DJK stratification showed a larger separation: 4.1 vs 9.8 months maintained (difference 5.7 months, p=0.001, Figure 2B). Patients with DJK who initially achieved correction maintained it for an average of only 4.1 months - less than half the follow-up period.

Secondary Analysis: Mixed-Effects Logistic Regression

The mixed-effects model (Figure 3) included 356 observations across 89 patients (ICC=0.36, confirming substantial within-patient correlation and validating the mixed-model approach). Odds of being well-aligned declined 29% per postoperative visit (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.58-0.87, p=0.001). DJK showed the strongest association (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.04-0.22, p less than 0.001). Age was significant in the adjusted model (OR 0.68 per decade, p=0.012), with a larger effective sample across all timepoints than was available to the Cox model. A significant Time x Age interaction was identified (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.79-0.98, p=0.022), indicating that the negative association of age strengthens with each successive visit. Baseline cSVA remained significant (OR 0.74 per 10 mm, p=0.014). CTPA and thoracic kyphosis trended toward protection (p=0.06 and p=0.10 respectively). In the expanded model, cervicothoracic deformity (OR 0.61, p=0.107) and combined approach vs posterior-only (OR 0.54, p=0.078) trended toward lower alignment odds. These estimates are directionally consistent with the Cox model but carry wide confidence intervals and are reported strictly as hypothesis-generating trends, not as established predictors.

Delta radiographic analysis demonstrated that Group A improved significantly from baseline to 1 year (DeltacSVA -17.8 mm, DeltaC2 slope -15.7 degrees, DeltaT1 slope -9.8 degrees, DeltaCTPA -1.13; all p less than 0.01), while Group B worsened across all cervical parameters (+12.1 mm cSVA, +10.9 degrees C2 slope, +8.8 degrees T1 slope, +2.06 CTPA; all p less than 0.01). The magnitude of radiographic deterioration in Group B was substantial - a mean cSVA increase of 12.1 mm over a single postoperative year, with individual per-patient trajectories illustrated in Figure 4C. Group C showed minimal mean change (+1.7 mm cSVA), consistent with a stable but persistently malaligned state in most patients, though this aggregate stability likely conceals a subset with progressive worsening (Table 3).

Failure mechanism analysis in Group B revealed an apparent temporal shift (Table 4): DJK predominated at 3 months (2/4, 50%), PJK at 6 months (3/4, 75%), and loss of global sagittal compensation at 1 year (5/7, 71%). Overall, global compensation loss was most frequent (7/15, 47%), followed by PJK (5/15, 33%) and DJK (3/15, 20%). Focal kyphosis patients contributed disproportionately to late global compensation failures (4/7) and cervicothoracic patients to DJK events (1/3 DJK failures from 6 Group B patients). These proportions rest on 15 adjudicated mechanisms distributed across three visits (4 at 3 months, 4 at 6 months, 7 at 1 year), so that each percentage derives from a denominator of four to seven patients and a single reassignment would alter it substantially. No formal statistical test of the temporal shift was performed, and these distributions are presented as descriptive.

Discussion

This study employed Cox regression, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, and mixed-effects logistic regression to characterize alignment durability in cervical deformity, replacing cross-sectional comparisons that violate repeated-measures independence. Across all 155 patients, alignment appeared favorable: 52.4% were well-aligned immediately postoperatively, peaking at 67.2% at 3 months and declining to 64.5% at 1 year (Figure 4A). The 89 patients with imaging at every timepoint showed a more sobering pattern, deteriorating from 48.3% well-aligned at 3 months to 33.7% at 1 year (Figure 4B).

This divergence is most plausibly explained by differential attrition rather than by a true difference in surgical result. Only 89 of 155 patients (57.4%) had imaging at all four visits, and the mechanism of loss is unlikely to be random: patients who are asymptomatic, satisfied, and well-aligned have the least incentive to return for surveillance imaging, whereas patients with recurrent neck pain, deteriorating function, or a suspected junctional complication are precisely those who are re-imaged. Under this scenario the complete-follow-up cohort is enriched for failure and the 33.7% figure represents a conservative lower bound, with the true population rate lying somewhere between it and the 64.5% observed in the full cohort - itself an optimistic upper bound, since each visit retains only those patients imaged at that visit. Attrition is not uniformly benign, however: patients revised at an outside institution, too debilitated to travel, or lost after an early complication are removed from the analytic cohort and bias in the opposite direction, and much registry attrition is geographic or administrative and unrelated to alignment. The net direction is uncertain, though the balance of mechanisms favors overestimation of failure. Both figures are therefore best read as bounds rather than as point estimates, and the wider lesson is that cohort composition substantially influences reported success rates in the cervical deformity literature.

DJK was the variable most consistently associated with alignment failure across all three frameworks: HR 5.21 in the Cox model (p=0.001), an RMST difference of 5.7 months (p=0.001), and OR 0.09 in the mixed-effects model (p less than 0.001). Yet when mechanisms were adjudicated in Group B, DJK accounted for only 20% of cases, with PJK (33%) and loss of global sagittal compensation (47%) more common. This distinction is clinically useful: DJK identifies who is likely to lose alignment,13,14 while the anatomic site of failure in patients who initially achieve correction is more often proximal or global. One interpretation of the observed sequence is that distal junctional stress is greatest before fusion consolidation, that the proximal junction becomes the vulnerable zone as distal fixation matures, and that exhaustion of spinopelvic and thoracolumbar compensatory reserve drives later failures;15,16 this remains a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated progression, given the small number of events at each visit. Pragmatically, surveillance should encompass all three failure modes rather than the distal junction alone, with full-length sagittal imaging at the later visits.

The counterintuitive association between lower postoperative cSVA and higher failure hazard (HR 0.58, p=0.002) is one of the more provocative findings in this cohort, and its interpretation requires restraint. The estimate derives from a model containing 17 events; more importantly, neither the magnitude of correction achieved in each patient nor deformity-specific or age-adjusted alignment targets were directly assessed, and these are the variables that would be required to demonstrate undercorrection. What the data show is an association between a lower postoperative cSVA value and subsequent alignment loss. Undercorrection is one explanation among several, and it is offered here as a hypothesis rather than as a conclusion. Because age-adjusted alignment targets and absolute correction magnitude were not entered as model covariates, undercorrection remains an interpretive mechanism rather than a confirmed statistical determinant.

Several considerations make it a plausible one. Group B had the smallest baseline cSVA (40.5 mm), consistent with smaller absolute corrections in this group. A patient corrected only modestly finishes surgery with a cSVA close to the 40 mm threshold, so that a few degrees of junctional settling suffices to cross it, whereas a patient corrected from a severely translated starting point may absorb the same anatomic change without becoming malaligned. Less severe deformities are also typically treated with shorter constructs and fewer osteotomies, leaving the underlying kyphotic driver partly unaddressed adjacent to a newly rigid segment. Under either account it is the margin between achieved alignment and the failure threshold, rather than the absolute alignment value, that governs maintenance - a testable proposition that a future analysis incorporating correction magnitude and morphotype-specific targets could address directly. 17 Countervailing explanations exist: in cervicothoracic deformity, thoracic anatomy constrains achievable correction, so a low postoperative cSVA may reflect anatomical limitation rather than technical inadequacy. Figure 5 (panels 5A-D) illustrates a representative case; a patient with cervicothoracic dropped-head deformity achieved cSVA 28 mm after C2-T10 fusion, yet lost correction by 3 months (cSVA 41 mm), worsening to 58 mm at 1 year despite one of the most complete immediate corrections in the series.

The Time x Age interaction (OR 0.88, p=0.022) is identifiable only through longitudinal modeling and indicates that older patients deteriorate faster at each successive visit; Group C being significantly older than Groups A and B is consistent with this. The per-patient Group B trajectories in Figure 4C show that alignment loss is not uniform: some patients cross the malalignment threshold by 3 months while others maintain correction through 6 months before a later loss, a pattern compatible with discrete failure events but not diagnostic of them. Age-stratified surveillance, with more frequent monitoring of older patients during the first postoperative year, appears reasonable. 18 Group C may also contain two phenotypes - stably undercorrected patients and progressively worsening ones - which per-patient trajectory slope could distinguish in future analyses.

The relationship between these radiographic findings and clinical benefit deserves separate consideration. Change in NDI and EQ-5D from baseline to 1 year did not differ significantly between trajectory groups, and only mJOA at 6 months separated them. Several readings are possible and the data do not adjudicate between them. Radiographic deterioration may genuinely precede symptomatic decline, in which case a 1-year horizon is simply too short for the HRQL signal to emerge. Alternatively, a mean cSVA increase of 12.1 mm in Group B, while statistically robust, may fall below the threshold at which patients perceive a difference; established minimum clinically important differences exist for NDI and EQ-5D but not for cSVA, so the clinical weight of a given number of millimetres cannot be read off the radiograph. The study was also not powered for HRQL comparisons across three groups, so the absence of a significant difference is not evidence of equivalence. What can be said is that the higher revision rate in Group B (29.4% vs 13.3% in Group A and 16.7% in Group C, p=0.377) points in the direction of clinical consequence without establishing it. Radiographic alignment loss should accordingly be treated as a marker warranting closer surveillance rather than as a demonstrated determinant of patient-reported outcome at 1 year, and the durability findings reported here should not be equated with clinical failure.

These findings sit within a broader literature on normative cervical sagittal alignment. Systematic review of asymptomatic populations places normative cSVA well below the 40 mm threshold applied here, documents that deviation from normative values correlates with HRQL more consistently for cSVA and TS-CL than for cervical lordosis in isolation, and links increasing anterior head translation to rising extensor demand and junctional moment in biomechanical modeling. 19 Two implications follow. First, a single fixed threshold applied across all ages disregards the observation that normative cSVA increases with age, so that an older patient at 38 mm may sit further from their age-specific norm than a younger patient at 42 mm; this offers a plausible partial account of the Time x Age interaction, in which older patients deteriorate faster against a fixed criterion. Second, because it is alignment within the normative range that correlates with HRQL, the absence of a between-group difference in NDI and EQ-5D at 1 year may in part reflect that patients across all three trajectory groups remained outside normative range throughout follow-up, leaving little room for a differential clinical signal to emerge. Prospective evaluation against age- and sex-specific normative targets, rather than a single dichotomous threshold, is a logical next step.

Deformity subtype and surgical approach showed consistent directional trends in both models: cervicothoracic deformity (Cox HR 2.14, ME OR 0.61) and combined approach (Cox HR 2.31, ME OR 0.54) were associated with worse alignment outcomes versus focal kyphosis and posterior-only respectively, though neither reached significance and both carry wide confidence intervals under EPV constraints. These trends are mechanistically coherent - cervicothoracic deformity spans the highest-stress spinal transition zone, and combined approaches are typically reserved for the most severe deformities20,21 - and represent hypothesis-generating findings for future adequately powered subtype-stratified analyses. 22

Osteoporosis rates were similar across groups (20.0%, 23.0%, 19.5%; p=0.136) despite divergent DJK rates (3.3%, 47.1%, 42.9%). This dissociation suggests that poor bone quality contributes to junctional vulnerability without singularly determining alignment trajectory; baseline cSVA and DJK incidence were the stronger differentiating variables. Preoperative bone health optimization remains important but is unlikely to be sufficient in isolation. 23 Radiographic targets proposed in prior work - postoperative cSVA less than 32 mm, T1 slope change less than 6 degrees, correction of TS-CL mismatch, and LIV placement at least 2 levels above the first lordotic vertebra - remain reasonable planning considerations regardless of bone density status.8,24,25,26

The convergent significance of DJK across all models reinforces its value as a prevention target. Preoperative DJK risk stratification using validated scoring tools may inform patient counseling and surgical planning, particularly around LIV selection and distal fixation strategy.8,9 The Time x Age interaction argues for individualized monitoring intervals, and the mechanism distribution supports interval-specific imaging: junctional assessment early, full-spine sagittal radiographs at 6 months and 1 year. The near-doubling of revision rate in Group B relative to Group A supports a low threshold for reassessment once correction loss is documented, though the difference did not reach significance.

Several limitations apply. Complete four-point follow-up was available in 89 of 155 patients (57.4%), introducing the attrition-related selection bias detailed above; the reported 1-year rates are best understood as bounding estimates, and comparison of baseline and immediate postoperative characteristics between retained and non-retained patients, together with inverse-probability-of-attrition weighting or multiple imputation, should be undertaken in future work. The Cox model contained 17 events, which limits the confirmatory analysis to two covariates, widens all confidence intervals, and renders expanded-model covariates exploratory; hazard ratios should be read as adjusted associations rather than independent predictors. Correction magnitude and deformity-specific alignment targets were not assessed, so the undercorrection interpretation remains hypothetical. Failure mechanisms were adjudicated radiographically and, for non-revised patients, inferred from serial angular change rather than confirmed clinically; the apparent temporal progression rests on 15 events across three visits and is descriptive. RMST estimates were derived from group-level distributions and require validation with per-patient survival analysis. Patient-reported outcomes did not differ between groups at 1 year, and the study was not powered to detect such differences. Finally, follow-up was limited to one year. The analysis establishes durability across the first postoperative year but cannot speak to durability at two to five years, the interval conventionally used in adult deformity; because late failures were driven by progressive loss of global compensation still accruing at the final timepoint, the maintenance rates reported here are unlikely to improve with longer observation.

Conclusions

Durable cervical deformity correction remains elusive, with fewer than one-third of patients maintaining acceptable alignment at 1 year among those with complete radiographic follow-up. DJK was the factor most consistently associated with alignment loss, and older patients deteriorated faster over the postoperative year. Lower postoperative cSVA was also associated with higher failure risk, a finding compatible with relative undercorrection but requiring direct assessment of correction magnitude and deformity-specific targets before it can be accepted as such. Failure mechanisms appeared to shift from junctional failure early to global sagittal decompensation late, with no surgical parameters differentiating trajectory groups. Radiographic deterioration was not accompanied by significant differences in patient-reported outcome at 1 year, and should be treated as a surveillance signal rather than as established clinical failure. Individualized preoperative risk stratification and extended longitudinal surveillance are warranted; whether these trajectories persist, stabilize, or worsen beyond 12 months requires dedicated long-term study.
