Assessment of joint health in females with haemophilia: The carriers ultrasound project (CUP) study
Authors: Fernando F Corrales‐Medina, Kelli Fraga, Maria D'Almeida Bastos, Amina Rafique, Christine L Kempton, Pooja Vijayvargia, Joanna A Davis, Rachel S Kronenfeld
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe needs of haemophilia carriers (HC) have been historically overlooked. It is now recognised that HC manifests bleeding symptoms, including haemarthrosis. The natural history of joint health in HC is not yet defined.AimA multi‐institutional cross‐sectional study aimed to evaluate the characteristics of joint disease in HC, aged 18–40 years, compared to age‐matched controls.MethodsThe carrier cohort included females with confirmed HC status. Controls had no personal or family history of bleeding disorders. All females with a history of joint trauma or surgery within 12 months or any history of joint replacement were excluded. Joint health was assessed by clinical history, Haemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS) and point‐of‐care musculoskeletal ultrasonography (POC‐MSKUS).ResultsThirty HC and 30 controls were enrolled. For HC, the median factor activity level was 52% (range 17%–100%). Carriers, regardless of baseline factor activity levels, reported higher prevalence of chronic joint pain (p < .001) and swelling (p = .002) than controls. Heavy menstrual bleeding, epistaxis, gingival bleeding and easy bruising were also more prevalent in HC (p < .001). Despite HC having a higher median HJHS score (5 vs. 0, p < .001), no differences were observed when using POC‐MSKUS. HC with a body mass index ≥25 mg/m2 reported more haemarthrosis (p = .037).ConclusionsHC are at increased risk of joint‐related symptoms and poorer joint health than age‐matched controls. Dedicated follow‐up to prevent and treat joint disease in HC is imperative. This study is also a call for additional investigation to clarify the association, or lack thereof, between factor activity and joint disease.Keywords: arthropathy, carriers, haemarthrosis, haemophilia, joint health, women

INTRODUCTION

Haemophilia is an X‐linked recessive bleeding disorder associated with deficient production of coagulation factors VIII (FVIII) or IX (FIX). 1 Males with severe haemophilia (<1% factor level) are at high risk for recurrent and spontaneous bleeding events, primarily affecting joints and muscles. 2 , 3

Females with haemophilia, historically known as haemophilia carriers (HC), were believed to have asymptomatic clinical phenotypes. This misconception results in non‐optimal care for HC, who often experience lengthy delays in diagnosis, insurance denials for services and inadequate therapeutic strategies. 4 , 5

Recent studies demonstrate that HC develops bleeding events. 6 , 7 , 8 To better define the clinical burden faced by HC, the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) published a new nomenclature for HC in 2021, distinguishing five clinical categories for HC: asymptomatic HC (FVIII/IX ≥40% and no bleeding phenotype), symptomatic HC (FVIII/IX ≥40% and bleeding phenotype) and mild, moderate or severe haemophilia. 9 However, the natural history, clinical characteristics and consequences of joint bleeds in HC remain poorly understood. 4 Understanding the pathogenesis of joint disease specific to HC is crucial for preventing morbidity and improving quality of life (QoL). 4

In males with severe haemophilia, initial acute haemarthroses involving ankles, knees and/or elbows usually occur within the first 2 years. Recurrent bleeding within the same joint can progress to haemophilic arthropathy, characterised by synovial hypertrophy, cartilage damage, reduced joint space and irreversible bone deformities. 1 , 3 , 10 Males with haemophilic arthropathy report significant joint pain and disability resulting in disease‐related morbidity and poor QoL. 3

Early, continuous and multidisciplinary training to recognise, prevent and treat acute haemarthrosis is well‐established for males with haemophilia. Similar counselling is often delayed for HC, especially if they are not referred to a Hemophilia Treatment Center (HTC). This delay contributes to poor understanding of potential long‐term consequences of untreated or undiagnosed joint bleeds.

Similar to males with mild or moderate haemophilia, joint bleeds in HC may be subclinical, remaining undetected by both HC and their medical providers. 11 , 12 Recurrent subclinical joint bleeds may explain previously reported reduced joint range of motion (ROM) observed in HC when compared to non‐carrier counterparts. 13 , 14 , 15 This reduction in ROM occurs as early as pre‐adolescence. 13 , 14 , 15 Subclinical bleeds may also contribute to radiographic joint changes in HC, including subchondral cysts, haemosiderin deposits and joint effusions. 11 , 12

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for detecting and monitoring joint changes in haemophilia. Limitations precluding the widespread use of MRI include high cost, insurance constraints, length of examination and difficult accessibility in remote areas. 16 , 17 Individuals with history of multiple haemarthroses may manifest low‐signal intensity artefacts on MRI, corresponding to areas of haemosiderin deposition within the joint, which may obscure adequate visualisation of the synovium. 10

Recent advances in musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSKUS) have positioned it as a more accessible and affordable point‐of‐care (POC) screening tool. 10 , 18 POC‐MSKUS has proven to be valuable in assessing joint health in people with haemophilia, regardless of severity. 11 , 12 , 19 , 20 Ultrasonographic images can help confirm clinical or subclinical acute haemarthroses and monitor its evolution and resolution. 21 Studies have also shown good concordance between POC‐MSKUS and MRI images in the standardised Denver score. 10 , 17 In 2013, the “Hemophilia Early Arthropathy Detection with Ultrasound” (HEAD‐US) scoring system was developed and validated to evaluate joints in males with haemophilia, providing a standardised system for assessing haemophilic joint disease. 22

Studies investigating joint health in haemophilia, including those using POC‐MSKUS, have been exclusively focused on males. To our knowledge, there are no prior studies investigating the prevalence of joint changes assessed by POC‐MSKUS in HC. Our study aims to investigate the prevalence of joint‐related symptoms and subclinical joint changes by using POC‐MSKUS in HC in comparison to age‐matched non‐carrier females. We also aim to determine if the severity of joint changes associates with baseline FVIII or FIX activity levels.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

After Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval, this multi‐institutional, cross‐sectional study enrolled participants between December 2017 and August 2023 from three Comprehensive HTCs in the United States.

Participants were allocated to two cohorts. The “carrier” arm included females 18–40 years‐of‐age with a confirmed HC diagnosis (either A or B) regardless of baseline factor activity level. Carrier diagnosis was established by genetic analysis, historical FVIII or FIX activity levels and/or family history. The “control” arm included age‐matched females recruited at the University of Miami with no known personal or family history of any congenital bleeding disorder. Females in both cohorts were excluded for history of a joint injury or surgery in elbows, knees or ankles requiring medical attention in the 12 months prior to enrolment. Joint injury was defined as fracture, dislocation, cartilage damage or ligament damage. Females with history of joint replacement were also excluded.

Demographics and bleeding history

A survey was administered to obtain demographics and bleeding history. Baseline demographics included age, race, ethnicity, height, weight and body mass index (BMI). Lifetime bleeding history included the presence or history of bleeding symptoms (heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB), recurrent epistaxis, gingival bleeding and easy bruising). Individuals were also asked about the identification of other concomitant bleeding disorders, if known. Joint symptoms (recurrent joint pain or swelling, defined as the presence of any of these symptoms at least twice in a 6‐month period), sites of any prior joint bleeds and current mobility level or restriction were recorded. Mobility level was scaled based on the presence or absence of restriction in school/work and/or recreational activities.

Females in the “carrier” arm were asked about family history of haemophilia and personal prior use of any haemostatic medications. FVIII or FIX activity levels were obtained to sub‐classify HC as having normal factor activity (FVIII or FIX activity levels ≥40%) or haemophilia (FVIII or FIX activity levels <40%).

Joint health evaluation

Females in both cohorts underwent joint assessment using the standardised Haemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS) version 2.1 performed and scored by a trained physical therapist or another qualified study team member. 23 For study purposes, HJHS scores were dichotomised based on the presence (HJHS score of ≥1) or absence (HJHS score = 0) of findings. Scores are generated based on the severity and duration of swelling, severity of muscle atrophy, crepitus on motion, range of motion, joint pain, strength and global gait. The HJHS score ranges from 0 to 48, with higher scores indicating greater impairment.

All participants underwent POC‐MSKUS of bilateral elbows, knees and ankles. Images were obtained locally and centrally scored by a blinded radiologist using the standardised HEAD‐US protocol. 22 The HEAD‐US score is based on the presence and severity of synovial hypertrophy (0–2 points), cartilage damage (0–4 points) and bone changes (0–2 points) for each of the six examined joints. An abnormal HEAD‐US score was defined as ≥1.

Data analysis

Descriptive and interferential statistics were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, copyright 2023). Clinical information was compared between the two cohorts using the Pearson's Chi‐squared test, 2‐sample t‐test, or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test as indicated. Secondary analyses were performed by stratifying the carrier cohort by factor activity level and BMI.

RESULTS

Sixty females, 30 in the carrier arm and 30 in the control arm, were enrolled. Demographic data are shown in Table 1. Among the HC, 26 (86.7%) were haemophilia A and 4 (13.3%) haemophilia B carriers. Most participants were White (n = 48, 80%). Hispanics represented 53.3% (n = 33) of the population. The mean age at enrolment was 30.6 years (standard deviation (SD) 5.4 years, range 18–39 years). The overall mean BMI was 25.2 kg/m2 (SD 4.7 kg/m2). There were no differences in age or BMI between HC and controls.

Among HC, the median factor activity level was 52% (range 17%–100%) (Table 1 ). Six (20%) carriers met the criteria for mild haemophilia (FVIII or FIX levels 5%–<40%). There were no HC with moderate or severe haemophilia. Of the 24 HC with normal factor activity levels (≥40%), 8 (33.3%) had factor activity levels between 40% and 50%. One HC reported concurrent history of type 1 von Willebrand disease with a baseline von Willebrand factor activity level of 39%.

HC reported a significantly higher prevalence of bleeding symptoms compared to controls (83.3% vs. 13.3%, p < .001), including HMB (p < .001), recurrent epistaxis (p = .011), gingival bleeding (p = .011) and easy bruising (p < .001) (Table 1 ). HC often required haemostatic medications for control of their bleeding symptoms, including prior use of episodic factor replacement therapy in 8 (26.7%) (Table 1 ). No HC was prescribed a prophylactic regimen. Antifibrinolytics and desmopressin (DDAVP) use were also reported (n = 9, 30% and n = 6, 20%, respectively). Acute peri‐operative management and specific site‐injury bleeding were the most common indications for haemostatic medications (n = 7, 23.3% and n = 4, 13.3%; respectively).

Carriers had a significantly higher prevalence of reported joint symptoms, including recurrent and chronic joint pain (50% vs. 3.3%, p < .001) and joint swelling (26 .7% vs. 0%, p = .002) (Table 1 ). Three HCs (10%) reported five overt haemarthrosis events in the past. All of them were haemophilia A carriers with FVIII activity levels ranging from 43% to 98%. The most commonly affected joints were the knees and ankles (n = 2, 40% and n = 1, 20%; respectively). There were no events affecting the elbows. We were not able to determine if these events were spontaneous or trauma‐related. Similar hours of exercise per week were reported by both cohorts (5 h HC vs. 3 h controls, p = .22) and most females reported no restrictions in their mobility (96.7% HC and 100% controls). Only one HC (3.3%), with a FVIII activity level of 43%, reported limited mobility affecting work‐related and recreational activities.

When investigating joint health status, HC had significantly higher median HJHS scores than controls (5 vs. 0, p < .001) (Table 1 ). The majority of carriers had positive findings on HJHS (83.3% vs. 43.4%, p = .001), defined as a score ≥1. The ankle was most commonly affected (Supplemental Table). Despite these findings, median HEAD‐US scores were similar among the two cohorts.

Those HC with history of overt haemarthrosis (n = 3) tended to have higher HJHS scores than HC without history of haemarthrosis (6.0 vs. 4.9), though this difference was not significant (p = .675). None of these three HC had evidence of joint damage when assessed by POC‐MSKUS. Although 4 HC (13.3%), with factor activity levels ranging from 40% to 81%, were asymptomatic with no joint bleeding symptoms reported, 2 (50%) of them had positive findings on HJHS (scores 6 and 3, respectively) and one (25%) had abnormal HEAD‐US findings, indicating that even asymptomatic HC are at risk for joint health abnormalities.

Females with mild haemophilia reported a similar prevalence of bleeding symptoms as HC with normal factor activity levels (100% vs. 79.2%, p = .22), suggesting that factor activity levels do not correlate with the prevalence of bleeding symptoms (Table 2 ). However, females with mild haemophilia were more likely to require haemostatic therapies to control symptoms, including the use of factor replacement therapy (p = .013), DDAVP (p = .04) and antifibrinolytics (p = .001).

When assessing joint health, females with mild haemophilia also reported a similar prevalence of joint pain (66 .7% vs. 45.8%, p = .36) and joint swelling (50% vs. 20.8%, p = .15) than those with normal factor activity (Table 2 ). Females with mild haemophilia had significantly higher median HJHS scores than those with normal factor activity levels (7 vs. 4.5, p ≤ .001). DISCUSSION

Haemophilia carriers have been an underserved population within the bleeding disorders community, in part due to the reality of having normal or near‐normal FVIII or FIX activity levels. 7 , 9 , 24 Our findings, primarily including the higher prevalence of joint symptoms and higher HJHS scores among HC when compared to age‐matched controls, demonstrate the importance of joint health surveillance in this population, regardless of factor activity level.

Interestingly in our study, we did not observe an association between the prevalence of bleeding symptoms and residual factor activity levels, suggesting that even HC with “normal” factor activity levels may present a significant bleeding phenotype. These results are supported by prior studies suggesting that any HC could present abnormal haemostasis, and not just those with factor activity levels <40%. 6 , 7 , 8

Although only 3 of 30 (10%) carriers reported prior haemarthroses, recurrent joint pain and swelling were common in HC compared to the control cohort (50% vs. 3.3% and 26% vs. 0%, respectively). Prior studies have also reported the presence of joint symptoms in HC without history of overt joint bleeds. 13 It remains unknown whether subclinical haemarthroses might contribute to the development of joint symptoms in HC over time, as was suggested by the Joint Outcome Study for men with severe haemophilia, but these findings certainly warrant further investigations in the future. 25 Our results also corroborate prior studies reporting reduced joint ROM and the presence of structural joint changes among HC. 13 , 14 We advocate for the implementation of HJHS as a routine screening tool to monitor joint health for HC, especially among those with joint symptoms.

Despite the significant prevalence of joint symptoms in HC, we found it interesting that there was no evidence of joint structural damage when assessed by POC‐MSKUS. We intentionally restricted the age of participants to a maximum of 40 years to reduce any bias from natural age‐related joint changes described in older women. 26 , 27 We hypothesise that in our relatively young cohort (<40 years of age), structural joint changes may not be sufficiently advanced to be identifiable by POC‐MSKUS. Our results are similar to a recently published study in young males with mild haemophilia A (age range 12–42.5 years) that found that although 36.5% of the total cohort had clinical evidence of arthropathy, median HEAD‐US scores were zero, suggesting that arthropathy in individuals with non‐severe haemophilia might not always be accurately captured by POC‐MSKUS evaluation. 20 Gilbert et al. demonstrated abnormal joint findings on MRI of HC with reduced range of motion, suggesting that in this population, MRI may be a more useful tool for detecting subtle joint changes, though this requires further investigation. 13 Our study demonstrates that POC‐MSKUS should not be the only screening tool utilised to assess joint health in HC, especially those younger than 40 years‐of‐age. Aside from POC‐MSKUS, joint health in this population should be comprehensively assessed in conjunction with bleeding history, clinical evaluation including the HJHS and, when needed, MRI technology.

Another interesting finding was the association between history of overt haemarthrosis history and elevated BMI at enrolment. There are longstanding reports describing the impact of poor weight control and joint health, though not among HC. 28 , 29 All HTCs should consider providing counselling to HC on the importance of a healthy diet and exercise regimen to mitigate the risk that an increased weight might have in worsening joint health.

Overall, our study highlights the increased burden faced by HC due to the presence of excessive bleeding symptoms and poorer joint health, even in the setting of a preserved factor activity level. The need for early and proper counselling, prevention strategies and specific therapeutic protocols designed to address these needs is imperative. Providers and patient communities should be aware that careful attention must be paid to symptomatic HC, as occurs for those with haemophilia, to identify joint symptoms in an early and timely manner to prevent, or at least mitigate, the development of chronic joint disease. This process may be especially important to start before adolescence when bleeding symptoms, especially HMB, and structural joint changes related to physical activity, are more likely to manifest. 13 , 14 , 30 , 31 , 32

The Joint Outcome Study showed that prophylactic factor replacement therapy in males with severe haemophilia prevents longstanding joint damage. 25 In males with haemophilia and a severe bleeding phenotype, prophylaxis is certainly considered after the first joint bleed. In our cohort, three women reported a total of five lifetime episodes of haemarthrosis. Future studies are needed to determine the right approach and timing to implement a prophylaxis strategy in HC with a severe bleeding phenotype in order to prevent the development and further progression of chronic joint disease. We believe that the decision for prophylaxis in HC should be raised on an individual basis and should be considered in the setting of a significant bleeding phenotype, regardless of gender.

Limitations to our study include the sample size. Only a small number of women with mild haemophilia were enrolled, limiting our comparative analysis between HC with factor levels above and below 40%. Similarly, few haemophilia B carriers (n = 4) were enrolled which prevented a comparative analysis by haemophilia subtype. Participating HC were recruited directly from HTCs making it more likely for them to be symptomatic, limiting our ability to generalise our findings. Recall bias may have also impacted true symptom prevalence on self‐reported surveys. Further, detailed information about reported haemarthrosis events was limited as well. Finally, although the HEAD‐US were scored by a blinded radiologist, HJHS scores were obtained by trained providers who were aware of patient diagnosis, which could have introduced potential bias.

CONCLUSIONS

Haemophilia carriers are at increased risk of bleeding, even in the setting of normal factor activity levels. They also seem to have poorer joint health than do age‐matched controls based on the increased presence of joint‐related symptoms and worse HJHS scores. Despite these findings, our study did not detect significant POC‐MSKUS joint changes among HC. We hypothesise that these findings may become more evident later in life or when assessed with MRI. Symptomatic HC may benefit from routine joint health surveillance and screening with HJHS. Studies to further characterise the natural history of joint health and its related complications in HC are necessary and should be prioritised in the future.
