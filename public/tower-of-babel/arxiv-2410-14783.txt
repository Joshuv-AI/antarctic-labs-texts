High-Dimensional Tensor Discriminant Analysis with Incomplete Tensors
Elynn Chen ⋄, Yuefeng Han, Jiayu Li

Abstract
Tensor classification is gaining importance across fields, yet handling partially observed data remains challenging. In this paper, we introduce a novel approach to tensor classification with incomplete data, framed within high-dimensional tensor linear discriminant analysis. Specifically, we consider a high-dimensional tensor predictor with missing observations under the Missing Completely at Random (MCR) assumption and employ the Tensor Gaussian Mixture Model (TGMM) to capture the relationship between the tensor predictor and class label. We propose a Tensor Linear Discriminant Analysis with Missing Data (Tensor LDA-MD) algorithm, which manages high-dimensional tensor predictors with missing entries by leveraging the decomposable low-rank structure of the discriminant tensor. Our work establishes convergence rates for the estimation error of the discriminant tensor with incomplete data and minimax optimal bounds for the misclassification rate, addressing key gaps in the literature. Additionally, we derive large deviation bounds for the generalized mode-wise sample covariance matrix and its inverse, which are crucial tools in our analysis and hold independent interest. Our method demonstrates excellent performance in simulations and real data analysis, even with significant proportions of missing data.



Keywords:
tensor classification; high-dimensional statistics; linear discriminant analysis; missing data; Tucker low-rank; optimal rate of convergence

In the data-driven era, tensor structures have become indispensable for capturing multidimensional information across diverse fields, including neuroimaging

(

Wen et al.,, 2024
;
Jahromi and Orús,, 2024

and enhanced theoretical understanding of the prediction process

As tensor classification advances, a critical challenge emerges: tensor data are often partially observed in real-world applications. This issue stems from factors such as sensor malfunctions, limited data access permissions, and natural barriers in data collection
(
Ge et al.,, 2016
;
Osipov and Chow,, 2020
;
Zhou et al.,, 2021
)
. Despite its significance, the problem of missing data in tensor classification remains understudied. While theoretical progress has been made in related areas like tensor regression
(
Zhang et al.,, 2020
;
Liang and Tian,, 2023
)
and tensor completion
(
Zhang,, 2019
;
Xia et al.,, 2021
;
Ibriga and Sun,, 2022
)
, these frameworks do not directly translate to the classification domain.

In this paper, we address the challenge of high-dimensional tensor classification with partially observed data, using the framework of Tensor Linear Discriminant Analysis with Missing Data (Tensor LDA-MD). We consider a high-dimensional tensor predictor
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
with Missing Complete at Random (MCR) pattern, where entries are indicated by an independent binary tensor
\mathcal{S}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
. Specifically,
\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}...i_{M}}
is observed if
\mathcal{S}_{i_{1}...i_{M}}=1
, and missing otherwise. Our approach applies the Tensor LDA framework under the assumption of a Tensor Gaussian Mixture Model (TGMM) for the pair
(\mathcal{X},Y)
, where
Y\in[K]
represents the class label for
K\geq 2
classes. To simultaneously address high-dimensionality and missing data, we propose a novel two-step method. First, we construct a new sample estimator for the discriminant tensor in the presence of missing data. We then refine this estimator by exploiting a Tucker low-rank structure, which achieves consistent estimation in the high-dimensional setting. This approach diverges from traditional high-dimensional LDA by exclusively utilizing the low-rank structure of tensors, without relying on any sparsity assumptions.

Building upon this foundation, our work addresses significant gaps in the existing literature on tensor classification. While previous research has established theoretical foundations for Tensor LDA frameworks with overall sparsity discriminant structures
(
Pan et al.,, 2019
)
, and tensor envelope extensions
(
Wang et al.,, 2024
)
, these studies have not tackled the problem of missing data or provided rate-optimal guarantees regarding classification accuracy. We bridge these gaps by developing, for the first time, an optimal theory for misclassification rate under the MCR model. Tensor logistic regression is considered a special case of generalized tensor estimation in
Han et al., 2022b ()
. Our work relaxes their assumption of independent tensor elements. Additionally, our theoretical analysis of tensor estimation under the tensor-based MCR model is non-trivial due to the complexity of high-dimensional tensor structures and is of independent interest. Our extensive simulations and real data analyses demonstrate excellent classification performance, even under significant proportions of missing data.

1.1
Our Contribution

The contributions of this paper are twofold:

Methodological Innovations:
We introduce Tensor LDA-MD, a novel tensor classification method within the TGMM framework, designed to handle high-dimensionality and missing data. By leveraging the Tucker low-rank structure, our method ensures consistent estimation of the discriminant tensor and provides an optimal classification rule. Besides, we propose a novel estimation method for generalized mode-wise covariance matrices under the MCR model. These innovations broaden the applicability of our approach to a range of tensor learning scenarios in the presence of missing data.

Theoretical Advancements:

We establish the first tight minimax optimality bounds for the misclassification rate in tensor classification with missing data. These bounds provide valuable insights into classification accuracy under varying levels of missingness. The technical analysis for the missing data case is significantly more challenging than for complete data, even though the classification procedure and resulting convergence rates appear similar. To facilitate the theoretical analysis, we establish a key technical tool, which is the development of large deviation bounds for the generalized mode-wise sample covariance matrices and their inverse, under the MCR model. This technical tool could have broader applications, as it may prove valuable for addressing other related challenges in high-dimensional tensor inference with missing data. Additionally, we derive the convergence rate for discriminant tensor estimation using iterative projection, shedding light on the algorithmic behavior of our method.
Our theoretical and methodological contributions fill a significant gap in the literature, setting the stage for future research in tensor-based machine learning with incomplete data.

Tensor classification has shown great potential across various fields
(

;
Wen et al.,, 2024
)
, with methods ranging from support tensor machines
(
Hao et al.,, 2013
;
Guo et al.,, 2016

. However, these approaches often overlook the dependence structure of tensor predictors and lack theoretical guarantees. In tensor Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), key advancements include work on sparse discriminant tensor assumptions

)
, and tensor envelopes
(
Wang et al.,, 2024
)
. Yet, these methods do not indicate whether they achieve optimal classification rates and are not equipped to handle missing data.
Our work addresses these gaps by leveraging the tensor MCR model, effectively managing high dimensionality and missing data through a Tucker low-rank assumption on the discriminant tensor. This approach enables us to derive minimax optimal misclassification rates, explicitly accounting for incomplete data.

High-dimensional discriminant analysis has been extensively studied in the vector domain, rooted in Fisher’s discriminant analysis
(
Cai and Liu,, 2011
;
Witten and Tibshirani,, 2011
;
Fan et al.,, 2012
;
Mai et al.,, 2012

(

However, applying these vector-based methods to tensor data can be limiting, as their reliance on sparsity fails to capture the richer structures inherent in multi-dimensional discriminant tensors. Our approach, which focuses on the Tucker low-rank structure, captures complex interactions and dependencies in tensor data, leveraging its intrinsic low-rank nature for a more comprehensive analysis than traditional vector-based methods.

Our work extends the estimation of mode-wise covariance and precision matrices to the MCR model in the tensor setting, building on vector-based studies such as
Cai and Zhang, (2016)
and
Cai and Zhang, (2019)
. This extension tackles the unique challenges of high-dimensional tensor structures, particularly in capturing complex relationships across multiple tensor modes. For vector observations with one tensor mode fixed, their methods directly result in i.i.d. random variables, eliminating the need to account for dependencies in the other tensor modes. However, for general tensor observations, when estimating the covariance and precision matrices of a particular tensor mode, we adopt a mild assumption that the tensor variables are correlated across all the other modes, leading to a complex dependency structure.

Tensor Classification

2.1

Preliminary on Tensor Statistics

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, its spectral norm and Frobenius norm are denoted as
\|A\|_{2}
and
\|A\|_{\rm F}
, respectively. Denote its singular values
\sigma_{\max}(A)=

\sigma_{1}(A)\geq\sigma_{2}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\min\{m,n\}}(A)\geq 0
in descending order. An
M
-th order tensor
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
is characterized by its multi-index
{\cal I}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{M})
. The inner product between two tensors
{\cal X}
and
{\cal Y}
is defined as
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\rangle=\sumop\displaylimits_{{\cal I}}{\cal X}_{{\cal I}}{\cal Y}_{{\cal I}}
, where the sum is taken over all indices
{\cal I}
. Using this inner product, the Frobenius norm of
{\cal X}
is given by
\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\rm F}=\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}\rangle^{1/2}
. For a matrix
\mathnormal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d_{m}}\times d_{m}}
, the mode-
m
product
\mathcal{X}\times_{m}\mathnormal{A}
yields an
M
-th order tensor of size
d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m-1}\times\widetilde{d_{m}}\times d_{m+1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}
. Elementwise,
(\mathcal{X}\times_{m}A)_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-1},j,i_{m+1},\ldots,i_{M}}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i_{m}=1}^{d_{m}}\mathcal{X}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{M}}\cdot A_{j,i_{m}}
. The mode-
m
matricization, denoted as
\text{mat}_{m}(\mathcal{X})
, rearranges the tensor into a matrix of size
d_{m}\times\prodop\displaylimits_{k\neq m}d_{k}
. In this representation, the tensor element
\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}\dots i_{M}}
is mapped to the matrix element
(i_{m},j)
, where
j=1+\sumop\displaylimits\nolimits_{k\neq m}(i_{k}-1)\prodop\displaylimits_{l<k,l\neq m}d_{l}
. For a subset
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}
, the multi-mode matricization
{\rm mat}_{S}({\cal X})
is a
\prodop\displaylimits_{m\in S}d_{m}
-by-
\prodop\displaylimits_{m\notin S}d_{m}
matrix with
(i,j)
-th element mapped as:
i=1+\sumop\displaylimits_{m\in S}(i_{m}-1)\prodop\displaylimits_{\ell\in S,\ell<m}d_{\ell},\;j=1+\sumop\displaylimits_{m\notin S}(i_{m}-1)\prodop\displaylimits_{\ell\notin S,\ell<m}d_{\ell}.

The vectorization is denoted as
\text{vec}(\mathcal{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d=\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}
. We also denote
d_{-m}=d/d_{m}
. Lastly, let
\odot
be the Hadamard (element-wise) product of matrices and tensors. For a comprehensive review of tensor algebra and operations, refer to
Kolda and Bader, (2009)
.

The Tensor Normal (TN) distribution is an extension of the matrix normal distribution
(
Hoff,, 2011
)
. To construct a tensor with a TN distribution, consider a random tensor
{\cal Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
whose elements are independently drawn from
N(0,1)
. Given a mean tensor
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
and mode-wise covariance matrices
\Sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{m}}
for
m\in[M]
, define
{\cal X}={\cal M}+{\cal Z}\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{1/2}
. The resulting tensor
{\cal X}
follows a TN distribution, denoted as
{\cal X}\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
and
\Sigma_{m}=\mathbb{E}[{\rm mat}_{m}({\cal X}-{\cal M}){\rm mat}_{m}^{\top}({\cal X}-{\cal M})]
. The probability density function of
{\cal X}
is

f({\cal X}|{\cal M},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\left(\prodop\displaylimits\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}|{\Sigma_{m}}|^{-\frac{d}{2d_{m}}}\right)\exp\left(-\left\lVert({\cal X}-{\cal M})\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}/2\right).

A notable property of the TN distribution is its equivalence to a multivariate normal distribution under vectorization
{\cal X}\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\Leftrightarrow\vect({\cal X})\sim{\cal N}(\vect({\cal M});\Sigma_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{1})
, where
\otimes
represents the Kronecker product.

2.2

Classification with Randomly Missing Data

We consider a classification problem involving tensor-valued predictors, leveraging the Tensor Gaussian Mixture Model (TGMM) framework. Let
\mathcal{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
denote a high-dimensional tensor predictor and
Y\in\{1,2,...,K\}
represent the class label, where
K\geq 2
. The joint distribution of
(\mathcal{X},Y)
under the TGMM is characterized as follows:

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X},Y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\cdot\mathcal{T}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{M}_{k},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}),\quad\text{where }\pi_{k}=\mathbb{P}(Y=k),\quad\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1.

(1)

Here,
\mathcal{M}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
represents the mean tensor for class
k
,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(k)}:=[\Sigma_{m}^{(k)}]_{m=1}^{M}
denotes the within-class covariance matrices, and
0<\pi_{k}<1
is the prior probability for class
k
. To account for missing data, we introduce a binary tensor
\mathcal{S}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
, independent of
\mathcal{X}
, which indicates the observed entries of
\mathcal{X}
:

\mathcal{S}_{i_{1}...i_{M}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}...i_{M}}\text{ is observed},\\
0,&\text{if }\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}...i_{M}}\text{ is missing}.\end{cases}

(2)

The missing data mechanism is assumed to follow the Missing Completely at Random (MCR) model, which can be formally stated as:

Assumption 2.1

(Missing Completely at Random (MCR))
.

Define
\mathscr{S}=\{{\cal S}_{i}^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
,
i=1,...,n_{k},\;k=[K]\}
, where
{\cal S}_{i}^{(k)}
can be either deterministic or random, but independent of
({\cal X},Y)
.

Remark 1
.

Our MCR assumption generalizes various missing data mechanisms explored in the literature. It encompasses uniformly random missing entries
(
Koltchinskii et al.,, 2011
;
Negahban and Wainwright,, 2012
;
Chen et al.,, 2019
;
Cai et al.,, 2022
)
, deterministic missing patterns
(
Pimentel-Alarcón et al.,, 2016
)
, and observations following general distributions
(
Klopp,, 2014
;
Sun and Chen,, 2024
)
.

For ease of presentation, we consider the special case of
K=2
, and observe independently from each class:

\mathcal{X}_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),\quad\mathcal{X}_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{n_{2}}^{(2)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Sigma}).

Here, we assume a common covariance structure across classes (i.e.
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(1)}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{(2)}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
). Furthermore, each observation
\mathcal{X}_{i}^{(k)}
is subject to missing data, characterized by its corresponding indicator tensor
\mathcal{S}_{i}^{(k)}
. The classification task involves assigning a future data point
\mathcal{Z}
, drawn from the mixture distribution
\mathcal{Z}\sim\pi_{1}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\pi_{2}\mathcal{T}\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{M}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, to one of the two classes.

In the scenario where all parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},{\cal M}_{1},{\cal M}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
are known, a classification rule generalizing Fisher’s linear discriminant
(
Anderson,, 2003
)
for tensor data is

\delta({\cal Z})=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\langle{\cal Z}-{\cal M},\;{\cal B}\rangle+\log\Big(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1}\Big)\geq 0\right\},

(3)

where
{\cal M}=({\cal M}_{1}+{\cal M}_{2})/2
,
{\cal D}=({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})
, and
{\cal B}={\cal D}\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1}
is the
discriminant tensor
. The indicator function
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
assigns
{\cal Z}
to class 1 (value 0) or 2 (value 1). As noted by
Bishop, (2006)
, under Gaussian assumption with shared covariance matrices, this rule is equivalent to Bayes’ rule, which is known to be optimal. And the optimal misclassification rate is given by

R_{\text{opt}}=\pi_{1}\phi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})-\Delta/2)+\pi_{2}(1-\phi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})+\Delta/2)),

where
\phi
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function (CDF) and
\Delta=\sqrt{\langle{\cal B},\;{\cal D}\rangle}
represents the signal-to-noise ratio.

In practice, estimating the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
is challenging. This involves estimating both
{\cal D}={\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1}
and the inverse covariance matrices
[\Sigma_{m}^{-1}]_{m=1}^{M}
, which together contain
{\cal O}(d)
free parameters without further assumptions. The difficulty is exacerbated when dealing with incomplete data, as the effective sample size
n_{0}
becomes smaller than
n_{1}+n_{2}
, and may be significantly smaller than
d
. Additionally, estimation errors in
{\cal D}
and
[\Sigma_{m}^{-1}]_{m=1}^{M}
propagate to
\mathcal{B}
, impacting the accuracy of the discriminant rule. It is crucial to recognize that the optimal misclassification rate depends on the signal-to-noise ratio
\Delta=\sqrt{\langle\mathcal{B},\;\mathcal{D}\rangle}
, where inconsistent estimation of
{\cal B}
precludes a consistent classification rule.

To address these challenges, we propose a novel two-step approach. First, we develop a method for estimating the sample mean tensor and mode-wise covariance matrices with missing data. This method takes advantage of the fact that estimating mode-wise covariance matrices leverages
{\cal O}(n_{0}d_{-m})
column vectors, instead of only using effective sample size
n_{0}
. And a generalized sample discriminant tensor
\widehat{\cal B}
can be obtained thereafter. Secondly, we enhance the estimation by exploiting a Tucker low-rank structure on
\mathcal{B}
. The motivation for this structure will be discussed in detail in the next section. This approach offers a robust solution for accurate tensor-based classification, as will be demonstrated in the subsequent theoretical analysis.

Remark 2
.

High-dimensional LDA with randomly missing data has been studied in the vector case by
Cai and Zhang, (2019)
. One could vectorize the tensor-variate
{\cal X}
and its indicator tensor
{\cal S}
to apply their adaptive estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, yielding the discriminant rule:

\delta(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{z}-\frac{\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}}{2}\right)+\log\left(\widehat{\pi}_{2}/\widehat{\pi}_{1}\right)\geq 0\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{z}=\vect({\cal Z})
and
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}=\vect(\widehat{\cal M}_{k})
. However, this approach assumes overall sparsity and may overlook key tensor structures. Additionally, vectorizing high-dimensional tensors leads to challenges in ultrahigh dimensions. For instance, their method for estimating
\boldsymbol{\beta}
involves solving linear programming (LP) problems, which, for a tensor
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{30\times 30\times 30}
, requires handling 27,000 inequalities, making it computationally intensive.

3

Tensor LDA-MD with Tucker Low-Rank Structure

In this paper, we propose imposing a Tucker low-rank structure on the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
. To introduce Tucker low-rankness, we decompose
{\cal D}
as
{\cal D}=\widetilde{{\cal D}}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{M}
, where
\widetilde{{\cal D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{M}}
is a core tensor and
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times r_{m}}
(
r_{m}\ll d_{m}
) are loading matrices with
\text{rank}(\boldsymbol{A}_{m})=r_{m}
. This yields a low-rank representation of
{\cal B}
as:

{\cal B}=\widetilde{{\cal D}}\times_{1}(\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{1})\times_{2}\cdots\times_{M}(\Sigma_{M}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}_{M}).

Due to the identification issue
(
Han et al., 2022a,
;
Chen et al., 2024b,
)
, we follow the standard approach and represent the Tucker low-rank discriminant tensor as

{\cal B}={\cal F}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{M}\boldsymbol{U}_{M},

(4)

where
{\cal F}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{M}}
is the core tensor,
\boldsymbol{U}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times r_{m}}
are orthogonal factor loadings, and
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{M})
denotes the Tucker rank. Incorporating a low-rank structure not only reduces the degrees of freedom and addresses high dimensionality, but is also essential for handling missing data, as shown in the tensor completion literature
(
Osipov and Chow,, 2020
;
Liang and Tian,, 2023
;
Xia et al.,, 2021
)
.

Remark 3
.

The degrees of freedom (DF) for tensors with a Tucker low-rank structure provides key insights. For a rank-
(r_{1},\dots,r_{M})
tensor
{\cal T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
,
Zhang, (2019)
defines the Tucker DF as:
\text{Tucker DF}({\cal T})=r+\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}r_{m}(d_{m}-r_{m}),

where
r=\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}r_{m}
. This serves as a benchmark for the sample size required to ensure convergence in low-rank tensor estimation. Our algorithm achieves a convergence rate proportional to
\sqrt{r+\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}}
, which matches this benchmark given that
r_{m}\ll d_{m}
. For consistency and simplicity, we roughly define the degrees of freedom for the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
as:

\text{DF}({\cal B})=r+\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}.

The estimation strategy, as will be detailed in the following subsections, addresses two key aspects. Firstly, we introduce a novel method for constructing a generalized sample estimator
\widehat{\cal B}
under the MCR model, which provids an approximation of the true discriminant tensor with controlled errors. Secondly, algorithmically leveraging the low-rank assumption, we develop an efficient procedure to refine this sample estimator by iteratively projecting it onto its low-dimensional space, and filter out noise, with provable convergence guarantees.

3.1

Generalized Sample Discriminant Tensor

We propose a method to estimate the mean tensor and mode-wise covariance matrices from incomplete data. Recall that
{\cal S}_{i}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
denotes the indicator tensor corresponding to
{\cal X}_{i}^{(k)}
for
i=1,\dots,n_{k}
and
k=1,2
. We define the generalized sample mean tensor
\overline{{\cal X}}^{(k)}
element-wise for
k=1,2
,

\displaystyle\overline{{\cal X}}_{{\cal I}}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{n_{*,{\cal I}}^{(k)}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n_{k}}{\cal X}_{i,{\cal I}}^{(k)}{\cal S}_{i,{\cal I}}^{(k)}\quad\text{for all}\ {\cal I}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{M}),\ \text{where}\ n_{*,{\cal I}}^{(k)}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n_{k}}{\cal S}_{i,{\cal I}}^{(k)}.

Define

n_{*,jt,\ell t}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\matk({\cal S}_{i}^{(k)})_{jt}\matk({\cal S}_{i}^{(k)})_{\ell t},\quad n_{*}=\min_{j,\ell,t}n_{*,jt,\ell t},

(5)

for
j,\ell\in[d_{m}],\;t\in[d_{-m}]\;\;\text{and}\;\;m\in[M]
.
The generalized mode-wise sample covariance matrices for each mode
m
are then defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,j\ell}

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{d_{-m}}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\frac{\left(\matk({\cal X}_{i}^{(k)}-\overline{{\cal X}}^{(k)})_{jt}\;\odot\;\matk({\cal S}_{i}^{(k)})_{jt}\right)\left({\rm mat_{m}}^{\top}({\cal X}_{i}^{(k)}-\overline{{\cal X}}^{(k)})_{\ell t}\;\odot\;{\rm mat_{m}}^{\top}({\cal S}_{i}^{(k)})_{\ell t}\right)}{d_{-m}\cdot n_{*,jt,\ell t}},

where
j,l\in[d_{m}]
,
m\in[M]
,
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product,
d=\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}
, and
d_{-m}=d/d_{m}
. For identifibility issue, update
\widehat{\Sigma}_{M}
by

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{M}=\widehat{C}_{\sigma}^{-1}\cdot\widehat{\Sigma}_{M},

where

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,11}/\widehat{\Var}({\cal X}_{1\cdots 1}),\quad\widehat{\Var}({\cal X}_{1\cdots 1})=(n_{*,1\cdots 1}^{(1)}+n_{*,1\cdots 1}^{(2)})^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{t=1}^{n_{k}}({\cal X}_{t,1\cdots 1}^{(k)}-\overline{{\cal X}}_{1\cdots 1}^{(k)})^{2}\cdot{\cal S}_{t,1\cdots 1}^{(k)}.

The generalized sample discriminant tensor is defined as

\widehat{\cal B}=(\overline{{\cal X}}^{(2)}-\overline{{\cal X}}^{(1)})\times_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}.

(6)

Notably, our generalized covariance matrix estimation can be extended to address other incomplete tensor learning problems, making it potentially useful for further research.

We estimate the class probabilities
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
using their empirical counterparts:
\widehat{\pi}_{1}=\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}},\;\widehat{\pi}_{2}=\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}.

For the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
to be well-defined, we require positive definiteness of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}
. Our computations, inspired by
Drton et al., (2021)
, suggest that this condition is met with probability 1 if
n_{0}>d_{m}/d_{-m}
, where
n_{0}
is the effective sample size. This condition is particularly mild when the dimensions of each mode are comparable (i.e.,
d_{1}\asymp\cdots\asymp d_{M}
). When this condition is not satisfied, alternative approaches can be employed. One option is to use a perturbed estimator:
\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}^{{}^{\prime}}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}+\gamma I_{d_{m}}

(
Ledoit and Wolf,, 2004
)
. The alternatives can be used as plug-in estimators to form
\widehat{\cal B}
.

Imposing a low-rank structure on
{\cal B}
is crucial for high-dimensional LDA. This approach is not merely about improving the estimation accuracy of
{\cal B}
in the context of missing data, but is fundamental for developing a consistent LDA rule in high-dimensional settings. As
Cai and Zhang, (2021)
demonstrate for complete data scenarios, when dimensions exceed the sample size (i.e.
d\gtrsim(n_{1}+n_{2})
) and class means (
{\cal M}_{1},{\cal M}_{2}
) are unknown, no data-driven method can achieve optimal classification performance, even with known identity covariance matrices.

To recover true

.

The error bounds for the complete data case can be derived as a special case of our results. In this scenario, we have

Remark 5
.

When
\Sigma_{m}=c_{m}\boldsymbol{I}_{d_{m}}
for all
1\leq m\leq M
, Theorem 3 and Lemma 4 in
Cai and Zhang, (2018)
allow for an improved signal-to-noise ratio condition, given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{m}\geq C_{\rm gap}\left(\left(\frac{d}{n_{0}}\right)^{1/4}+\sqrt{\frac{d_{m}}{n_{0}}}+\frac{\|\matk({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})\|_{2}\max_{k\leq M}d_{k}}{\sqrt{n_{0}d}}\right),m=1,...,M.

The improvement arises from the i.i.d. assumption
\Sigma_{m}=c_{m}\boldsymbol{I}_{d_{m}}
for the mode-wise correlation structure. For a tensor-variate
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
distributed as
{\cal T}{\cal N}(0;\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, this assumption leads to an i.i.d. column-wise distribution in
\matk({\cal X})
. Consequently, these columns can be treated as realizations of i.i.d. Gaussian random vectors
\matk({\cal X})_{\cdot 1}
, effectively increasing the “sample size” and thereby improving estimation accuracy. In contrast, our theoretical analysis assumes a general mode-wise covariance structure, where these columns are neither independent nor identically distributed, which leads to more conservative error bounds.

Remark 6
.

The statistical convergence rates in (
10

11
), and (
12

\Sigma_{m}^{-1}
. Unlike

is bounded by a constant, we impose no such constraint on this difference. This lack of constraint makes the normalized estimation error bound in (

By normalizing the error relative to
\|{\cal B}\|_{\rm F}
, the bound in (
12

{\cal B}=({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1}
. Larger differences between the mean tensors result in a larger
\|{\cal B}\|_{\rm F}
, which reduces the normalized estimation error, aligning with the expectation that classification becomes easier as the differences between class means diverge. This normalization idea is also employed in deriving upper bounds for the excess misclassification rate, as discussed later.

The analysis in Theorem
4.1
leverages newly developed large deviation inequalities for the generalized mode-wise sample covariance matrices and their inverse, as well as the convergence rate of the normalization constant under the MCR model, as shown in Lemma

(

Cai and Zhang,, 2018

Han et al.,, 2020

to analyze the projected discriminant tensor estimator after each iteration. These theorems are used under conditions of sufficiently strong signal strength, which helps to quantify the impact of tensor perturbations on estimation accuracy.

Next, we develop an optimality theory for high-dimensional Tensor LDA-MD method. Specifically, consider two random samples
{\cal X}_{1}^{(1)},\cdots,{\cal X}_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
{\cal X}_{1}^{(2)},\cdots,{\cal X}_{n_{2}}^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}

{\cal Z}
into one of two classes by tensor LDA rule defined in (
8

{\cal Z}
is drawn from these distributions with prior probabilities
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}

R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(label({\cal Z})\neq\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}({\cal Z})\big),

where

denotes the probability with respect to
{\cal Z}\sim\pi_{1}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\pi_{2}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
label({\cal Z})
is the true class of
{\cal Z}
. We are interested in the excess misclassification risk
R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, to evaluate the classifier’s performance relative to the oracle Fisher’s rule, whose misclassification rate is given by
R_{\text{opt}}=\pi_{1}\phi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})-\Delta/2)+\pi_{2}(1-\phi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})+\Delta/2))

(Upper bound of misclassification rate)

The low-rankness assumption inherent in our approach leads to substantially sharper rates, characterized by

To understand the challenges of high-dimensional tensor linear discriminant analysis and establish our classifier’s optimality, we derive minimax lower bounds for the excess misclassification risk. Consider the parameter space of Tucker low-rank discriminant tensors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\Big\{\;

\displaystyle\theta=(\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}):\mathcal{M}_{1},\mathcal{M}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}},\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M},\;\Sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{m}},\;\text{for some}\;C_{0}>0,

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\otimes_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\otimes_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m})\leq C_{0},\;\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{F}\times_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{U}_{m}\;\text{with}\;\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{M}}

\mathcal{H}

L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\delta)=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\delta(\mathcal{Z})\neq\delta_{\theta}(\mathcal{Z}))

\delta_{\theta}(\mathcal{Z})
is oracle Fisher’s rule. This transformation, established in Lemma

Extensions

We extend the Tensor LDA-MD framework by generalizing the tensor predictor
{\cal X}
to non-Gaussian tensor variables. Following
Shao et al., (2011)
, we relax the normality assumption of the tensor predictor such that for any
d
-dimensional deterministic vector
\boldsymbol{h}
with
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{h}\right\rVert_{2}=1
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we assume

\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{h}^{\top}(\Sigma_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{1})^{-1/2}\text{vec}(\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{M})\leq t\right)=:\Psi(t),

(17)

where
{\cal M}
is the mean tensor,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=:[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
are the mode-wise covariance matrices, and
\Psi(t)
is an unknown distribution function symmetric about 0 and independent of
\boldsymbol{h}
. One notable class of distributions satisfying (
17
) is the tensor elliptical distribution, with a density of the form:

f(\mathcal{X})=c\cdot g\left(\text{vec}(\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{M})^{\top}\left(\Sigma_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{1}\right)^{-1}\text{vec}(\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{M})\right)

(18)

where
g(\cdot)
is a monotone function on
[0,\infty)
, and
c
is a normalization constant. As demonstrated by
Fang and Anderson, (1990)
, for Tensor LDA with known
{\cal M}_{k}
and common covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, Fisher’s rule remains optimal when the tensor variable follows a tensor elliptical distribution. Furthermore, for binary classification, the LDA achieves the optimal misclassification rate given by

R_{\text{opt}}=\pi_{1}\cdot\Psi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})-\Delta/2)+\pi_{2}\cdot\overline{\Psi}(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})+\Delta/2),

where
\overline{\Psi}(\cdot)=1-\Psi(\cdot)
and
\Delta=\sqrt{\langle{\cal B},{\cal D}\rangle}
.

In scenarios involving incomplete data, we can derive the conditional classification error of the Tensor LDA rule (
3
). Given
{\cal X}^{(k)}
drawn from a tensor elliptical distribution and the indicator tensor
{\cal S}^{(k)}
,
k=1,2
, this error extends to:

\displaystyle R(\widehat{\delta})=

\displaystyle\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\Psi\left(\widehat{\Delta}^{-1}\log(n_{2}/n_{1})-\frac{\langle\widehat{\cal M}-{\cal M}_{1},\;\widehat{\cal B}\rangle}{\widehat{\Delta}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{n_{2}}{n_{1}+n_{2}}\overline{\Psi}\left(\widehat{\Delta}^{-1}\log(n_{2}/n_{1})-\frac{\langle\widehat{\cal M}-{\cal M}_{2},\;\widehat{\cal B}\rangle}{\widehat{\Delta}}\right).

Here,
\widehat{\delta}
denotes the Tensor LDA rule, while
\widehat{\Delta}
,
\widehat{\cal M}
, and
\widehat{\cal B}
represent estimates derived from the incomplete data. In Section
6
, we conduct numerical experiments applying our proposed classifier
\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}
on two concrete examples of tensor-valued elliptical distributions:

i.

The tensor
t
-distribution, denoted as
\mathcal{T}t(\mathcal{M},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\nu)
(defined in Definition 1 in
Wang et al., (2023)
). The degrees of freedom parameter
\nu
controls the tail behavior, with smaller values leading to heavier tails.

ii.

The tensor-variate (asymmetric) Laplace distribution, denoted as
\mathcal{T}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{M},\boldsymbol{\Sigma},\lambda)
(Definition 2.3 in
Yurchenko, (2021)
). The scale parameter
\lambda
serves as the rate parameter for an exponential distribution.

The empirical results show that our method remains efficient when tensor predictors follow elliptical distributions and performs well even with a significant proportion of missing data.

Simulation

In this section, we conduct extensive simulations to validate the theoretical properties of Tensor LDA-MD, focusing on scenarios with missing data. Diverse experimental setups and evaluation metrics corroborate our findings, and comparisons with advanced classifiers like CATCH
(
Pan et al.,, 2019
)
demonstrate our method’s effectiveness.

6.1

Data Generation

Our simulations utilize tensor-variate training data
{\cal X}^{(k)}
from
{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{k};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
for
k\in\{1,2\}
, with
n_{k}
samples per class and an additional 500 samples per class for testing. We simplify the common covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
to diagonal form, noting that more general forms would only affect the theoretical upper bound by a multiplicative constant. Specifically, each
\Sigma_{m}
is an identity matrix scaled by
c^{1/M}
, while
{\cal M}_{1}=0
and
{\cal M}_{2}=c\cdot{\cal B}
, as defined in (
3
). The discriminant tensor
\mathcal{B}
is generated in two steps. First, we generate the core tensor
\mathcal{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{M}}
, ensuring similar magnitudes of singular values across matricizations, with controlled minimum singular values
\sigma_{m}
. Second, we generate Tucker loading matrices
\boldsymbol{U}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times r_{m}}
via QR decomposition of random matrices, aligning
\mathcal{B}
’s signal strength with the core tensor. The missing mechanism independently observes each tensor entry with probability
p=1-\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\in(0,1)
is the missing rate. We aim to explore the joint impact of
n_{0}
, signal strength
\sigma_{m}
, and dimensions
d_{m}
and
r_{m}
on the upper bounds of estimation errors.

Our simulations explore various scenarios. We first fix dimensions at

8

Conclusion

This paper introduces the Tensor LDA-MD method for high-dimensional tensor classification with incomplete data. Our key contributions are as follows: Methodologically, we extend mode-wise covariance estimation for tensor-variate data to the tensor-based MCR model and incorporate Tucker low-rank structure in the discriminant tensor estimation, using an iterative projection algorithm to refine the sample estimator. Theoretically, we establish the convergence rate of the discriminant tensor estimation error and minimax optimality bounds for the misclassification rate with incomplete data. We also derive large deviation bounds for generalized mode-wise covariance matrices and their inverses under the tensor-based MCR model. Our simulation studies and real data analysis demonstrate the excellent performance of our method, even with significant proportions of missing data, consistently outperforming existing approaches. This showcases its robustness and practical utility in real-world applications where incomplete data is common.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{\langle{\cal D},\;\widehat{\cal B}^{\rm tucker}\rangle/2+\langle\widehat{\cal M}-{\cal M}_{2},\;\widehat{\cal B}^{\rm tucker}\rangle}{\widehat{\Delta}}\phi^{\prime}(\frac{\langle{\cal D},\;\widehat{\cal B}^{\rm tucker}\rangle}{2\widehat{\Delta}})+O\left(\Delta^{3}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left(\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}\right)\right)\right|

Since
{\cal D}/2-(\widehat{\cal M}-{\cal M}_{1})+{\cal D}/2+(\widehat{\cal M}-{\cal M}_{2})={\cal D}-({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})=0
, then it follows that

\displaystyle\left|R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R^{*}\right|\lesssim\Delta^{3}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left(\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}\right).

Finally, combining the two pieces, we obtain

\displaystyle R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq

\displaystyle R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R^{*}+R^{*}-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left\|\widehat{\cal B}^{\rm tucker}-{\cal B}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left\|\widehat{\cal B}^{\rm tucker}-{\cal B}\right\|_{\rm F}^{4}+\Delta^{3}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left(\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}\right),

in the event
{}_{1}\cap{}_{2}
with probability at least
1-n_{0}^{-c}-\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}e^{-cd_{m}}
.

Now consider the two case. On the one hand, when
\Delta=O(1)
, by (
39
), with probability at least
1-n_{0}^{-c}-\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}e^{-cd_{m}}
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}+C_{0}\frac{\max_{1\leq m\leq M}d_{m}^{2}}{n_{0}d}\leq C\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}.

On the other hand, when
\Delta\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, by(
39
), with probability at least
1-n_{0}^{-c}-\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}e^{-cd_{m}}
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta^{3}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left(\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}\right)+C\Delta e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\frac{\max_{1\leq m\leq M}d_{m}^{2}}{n_{0}d}+C\Delta^{3}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left(\frac{\max_{1\leq m\leq M}d_{m}^{2}}{n_{0}d}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta^{3}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\left(\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}+\frac{\max_{1\leq m\leq M}d_{m}^{2}}{n_{0}d}\right)

\displaystyle=C\exp\left(-\left(\frac{1}{8}-\frac{3\log(\Delta)}{\Delta^{2}}\right)\Delta^{2}\right)\left(\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n_{0}}+\frac{\max_{1\leq m\leq M}d_{m}^{2}}{n_{0}d}\right),

where
3\log(\Delta)/\Delta^{2}
is an
o(1)
term as

.

A.3

Proof of Theorem
4.3

Note that the proof is not straightforward, partly because the excess risk
R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
does not satisfy the triangle inequality required by standard lower bound techniques. A crucial approach in this context is establishing a connection to an alternative risk function.
For a general classification rule
\delta
, we define
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\delta)=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\delta({\cal Z})\neq\delta_{\theta}({\cal Z}))
, where
\delta_{\theta}({\cal Z})
is the Fisher’s linear discriminant rule introduced in (
3
). Lemma
B.8

allows us to transform the excess risk
R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
into the risk function
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})
, as shown below:

R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\Delta}{8}e^{\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot L_{\theta}^{2}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}).

(44)

We then apply Lemma
B.10
to derive the minimax lower bound for the risk function
L_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})
.

We carefully construct a finite collection of subsets of the parameter space
{\cal H}
that characterizes the hardness of the problem.
Any
M
-th order tensor
{\cal M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
with Tucker rank
(r_{1},...,r_{M})
can be expressed as
{\cal M}={\cal F}\times_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{m}
. Here, the latent core tensor
{\cal F}
has dimensions
r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{M}
, and the loading matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times r_{m}}
for each mode
m=1,\ldots,M
. Note that due to the rotational ambiguity, the matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}
are not necessarily orthogonal.

(a). Consider the complete data case.

First, let
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}
be a fixed matrix where the
(i,i)
-th elements,
i=1,...,r_{m}
, are set to one and all other elements are zero. Denote

\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{M}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{M-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
. According to basic tensor algebra, this setup implies that

\vect({\cal M})=\boldsymbol{A}\vect({\cal F})
and
\|{\cal M}\|_{\rm F}=\|\vect({\cal F})\|_{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
be the basis vector in the standard Euclidean space whose first entry is 1 and 0 elsewhere, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{d}=[\boldsymbol{I}_{d_{m}}]_{m=1}^{M}
.
Define the following parameter space

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{0}=

\displaystyle\big\{\theta=({\cal M}_{1},\;{\cal M}_{2},\;\boldsymbol{I}_{d}):\;{\cal M}_{1}={\cal F}\times_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{m},\;{\cal M}_{2}=-{\cal M}_{1};\ \vect({\cal F})=\epsilon\boldsymbol{f}+\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{f}\in\{0,1\}^{r},\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0\big\},

where
r=\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}r_{m}
,
\epsilon=c/\sqrt{n}
,
c=O(1)
and
\lambda
is chosen to ensure that
\theta\in{\cal H}
such that

\displaystyle\Delta=({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})=4\|\epsilon\boldsymbol{f}+\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=4\epsilon^{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{2}^{2}+4\lambda^{2}.

In addition to
{\cal H}_{0}
, we also define
\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}
, for
\ell\neq m
, as fixed matrices where the
(i,i)
-th elements,
i=1,...,r_{\ell}

{\cal F}
be a tensor such that in

, the
(i,i)
-th elements,
i=1,...,(r_{m}\wedge r_{-m})
, are set to one, and all other elements are zero. As
r_{m}^{2}\leq r
, it implies that
\|{\cal M}\|_{\rm F}=\|\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\matk({\cal F})\|_{\rm F}=\|\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\|_{\rm F}
. For
m=1,...,M
, define the following parameter spaces

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{m}=

\displaystyle\big\{\theta=({\cal M}_{1},\;{\cal M}_{2},\;\boldsymbol{I}_{d}):\;{\cal M}_{1}={\cal F}\times_{k=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{k},\;{\cal M}_{2}=-{\cal M}_{1};\ \vect(\boldsymbol{A}_{m})=\epsilon\boldsymbol{g}_{m}+\lambda_{m}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{g}_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{m}r_{m}},\boldsymbol{g}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0\big\},

where
\epsilon=c/\sqrt{n}
,
c=O(1)
and
\lambda_{m}
is chosen to ensure that
\theta\in{\cal H}
such that

\displaystyle\Delta=({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})=4\|\epsilon\boldsymbol{g}_{m}+\lambda_{m}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=4\epsilon^{2}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{m}\|_{2}^{2}+4\lambda_{m}^{2}.

It is clear that
\cap_{\ell=0}^{M}{\cal H}_{\ell}\subset{\cal H}
. We shall show below separately for the minimax risks over each parameter space
{\cal H}_{\ell}
.

First, consider
{\cal H}_{0}
. By Lemma
B.11
, we can construct a sequence of
r
-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{N}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
, such that
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0
,
\rho_{H}(\boldsymbol{f}_{i},\boldsymbol{f}_{j})\geq r/8,\;\forall 0\leq i<j\leq N
, and
r\leq(8/\log 2)\log N
, where
\rho_{H}
denotes the Hamming distance.
To apply Lemma
B.10
, for
\forall\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}},\;\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\cal H}_{0},\;\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}},
we need to verify two conditions:

(i)

the upper bound on the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}}
, and

(ii)

the lower bound of
L_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})+L_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})
for
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{v}
and
\boldsymbol{u}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0,\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0
.

We calculate the Kullback-Leibler divergence first. For
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\{0,1\}^{r}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}}={\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\times_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{m},\;\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}})=\epsilon\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\;\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\big({\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\;-{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\;\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\big)\in{\cal H}_{0}.

and consider the distribution

.
Then the Kullback-Leibler divergence between

and

\displaystyle{\rm KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\vect({\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}})-\vect({\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}})-\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{c^{2}r}{2n}.

\displaystyle L_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})+L_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\left\lVert\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}})-\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\right\rVert_{2}

So far we have verified the aforementioned conditions (i) and (ii). Lemma

immediately implies that, there exists some constant

\inf_{\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})\geq C_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\sqrt{\frac{r}{n}}\right)\geq 1-\alpha

(45)

Combining (
45
) and (

), we have

\inf_{\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\frac{r}{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(46)

Similarly, for each
{\cal H}_{m}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}_{m}}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\frac{d_{m}r_{m}}{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(47)

Finally combining (
46
) and (

\inf_{\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(48)

This implies that if

for some

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\alpha}\cdot\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

On the other hand, if
\Delta\to\infty
as

\vartheta>0

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}}\mathbb{P}\left(R_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta}_{\rm tucker})-R_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\alpha}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{1}{8}+\vartheta\right)\Delta^{2}\right\}\frac{\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r_{m}+r}{n}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(b). Consider the incomplete data case with

\displaystyle{\cal S}_{i,{\cal I}}=1,i=1,...,n_{0},\ \text{and}\ {\cal S}_{i,{\cal I}}=0,i=n_{0}+1,...,n,\forall{\cal I}=(i_{1},...,i_{M}),1\leq i_{m}\leq d_{m},\text{with probability 1}.

\Sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{m}}

For any unit vector

\mathbb{E}\check{\Sigma}_{m,jk}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m,jk}=0

\displaystyle v^{\top}\left(\check{\Sigma}_{m}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m}\right)v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d_{-m}}\sumop\displaylimits_{j,k=1}^{d_{m}}\sumop\displaylimits_{l=1}^{d_{-m}}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{X}_{i,kl}\frac{v_{j}v_{k}\boldsymbol{S}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i,kl}}{n_{0,jl,kl}}-\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{X}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{X}_{i,kl}\frac{v_{j}v_{k}\boldsymbol{S}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i,kl}}{n_{0,jl,kl}}\right)

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i)}\vec 1(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})-\mathbb{E}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i)}\vec 1(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{B}\otimes\boldsymbol{A})\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{B}\otimes\boldsymbol{A})\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\top}\left(\check{\Sigma}_{m}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m}\right)v\geq t\right)

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{16\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{(i)}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}},\frac{t}{4\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}\max_{i}\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{16\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}^{2}n/(n_{0}^{2}d_{-m})},\frac{t}{4\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}/(n_{0}d_{-m})}\right\}\right).

Similarly, consider
\Delta_{2}
. For any unit vector

\displaystyle v^{\top}\left(\Delta_{2}-\mathbb{E}\Delta_{2}\right)v

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{j,k=1}^{d_{m}}\Delta_{2,jk}v_{j}v_{k}-\mathbb{E}\Delta_{2,jk}v_{j}v_{k}

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{n}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}\vec 1(\boldsymbol{X}_{i^{\prime}})-\mathbb{E}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}\vec 1(\boldsymbol{X}_{i^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{n}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}(\boldsymbol{B}\otimes\boldsymbol{A})\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}{\vec 1}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i})(\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}^{\top})\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}(\boldsymbol{B}\otimes\boldsymbol{A})\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z}_{i}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}_{jl,kl}=v_{j}v_{k}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{S}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i,kl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i^{\prime},kl}}{d_{-m}n_{0,jl,kl}n_{0,kl}}+\frac{\boldsymbol{S}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i,kl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i^{\prime},jl}}{d_{-m}n_{0,jl,kl}n_{0,kl}}-\frac{\boldsymbol{S}_{i,jl}\boldsymbol{S}_{i^{\prime},kl}}{d_{-m}n_{0,jl}n_{0,kl}}\right),\quad\text{and}\ \boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}_{jl,kl^{\prime}}=0\ \text{for }l\neq l^{\prime}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(v^{\top}\left(\Delta_{2}-\mathbb{E}\Delta_{2}\right)v\geq t\right)

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{16\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}},\frac{t}{4\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}\max_{i,i^{\prime}}\left\|\boldsymbol{M}^{(i,i^{\prime})}\right\|_{2}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle 2\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{16\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}^{2}n^{2}/(n_{0}^{4}d_{-m})},\frac{t}{4\left\|\Sigma_{-m}\right\|_{2}\left\|\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}/(n_{0}^{2}d_{-m})}\right\}\right).

By
\varepsilon-net
argument, there exist unit vectors

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m}\right\|_{2}\geq x)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(4\max_{i\leq 5^{d_{m}}}\left|v_{i}^{\top}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m}\right)v_{i}\right|\geq x\right)\leq 5^{d_{m}}\mathbb{P}\left(4\left|v_{i}^{\top}\left(\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m}\right)v_{i}\right|\geq x\right).

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}-\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}=-\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}+\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1/2}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(-\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1/2}\Delta_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1/2}\right)^{k}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1/2}\Delta_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}

\Omega

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1/2}\Delta_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1/2}\right\|_{2}\leq CC_{m,\sigma}^{-1}\|\Sigma_{-m}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_{m}}{n_{0}d_{-m}}}.

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq CC_{m,\sigma}^{-1}\|\Sigma_{-m}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_{m}}{n_{0}d_{-m}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}C_{m,\sigma}^{-1}.

\Omega

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}-\widetilde{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq CC_{m,\sigma}^{-2}\|\Sigma_{m}^{-1}\|_{2}\|\Sigma_{-m}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_{m}}{n_{0}d_{-m}}}+CC_{m,\sigma}^{-3}\left(\|\Sigma_{-m}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_{m}}{n_{0}d_{-m}}}\right)^{2}\cdot C_{m,\sigma}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq C(C_{m,\sigma}^{-2}\vee C_{m,\sigma}^{-4})\|\Sigma_{m}^{-1}\|_{2}\|\Sigma_{-m}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{d_{m}}{n_{0}d_{-m}}}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\widehat{\Sigma}_{m,11}-C_{m,\sigma}\Sigma_{m,11}|\geq C\Sigma_{m,11}\|\otimes_{k\neq m}\Sigma_{k}\|_{2}\sqrt{\frac{t_{1}}{n_{0}d_{-m}}}\right)\leq\exp(-c_{1}t_{1}).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\widehat{\rm Var}({\cal X}_{1\cdots 1})-\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m,11}\right|\geq C\prodop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m,11}\sqrt{\frac{t_{2}}{n_{0}}}\right)\leq\exp(-c_{1}t_{2}).

The following lemma presents the tail bound for the spectral norm of a Gaussian random matrix.

Lemma B.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}
be an
p_{1}\times p_{2}
random matrix with
\boldsymbol{E}\sim{\cal M}{\cal N}_{p_{1}\times p_{2}}(0,\;\Sigma_{1},\;\Sigma_{2}).
Then for any
t>0
, with constant
C>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)\right)\leq\exp(-t^{2})

(58)

Let
\mathbb{O}_{p_{1},r}=\{\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times r},\;\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\}
be the set of all
p_{1}\times r
orthonormal columns and let
\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}
be the orthogonal complement of
\boldsymbol{U}
. Denote
\boldsymbol{E}_{12}=\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp},\;\boldsymbol{E}_{21}=\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{V}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{O}_{p_{1}\times r},\;\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{O}_{p_{2}\times r}.
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{21}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+t)\right)\leq\exp(-t^{2})

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{12}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)\right)\leq\exp(-t^{2})

(60)

Proof.

Similar to (
57
), using
\varepsilon
-net argument for unit ball (see e.g. Lemma 5 in
Cai and Zhang, (2018)
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\geq 3u\right)\leq 7^{p_{1}+p_{2}}\cdot\max_{\|x\|_{2}\leq 1,\|y\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}y|\geq u\right).

Decompose
\boldsymbol{E}=\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}
, where
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}},\;\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}N(0,1).

Then,

\displaystyle x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}y=\left(y^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\otimes x^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\right)\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z})\sim N\left(0,y^{\top}\Sigma_{2}y\cdot x^{\top}\Sigma_{1}x\right).

By Chernoff bound of Gaussian random variables,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}y|\geq u\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{y^{\top}\Sigma_{2}y\cdot x^{\top}\Sigma_{1}x}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}}\right).

Setting
u\asymp\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)
, for certain
C>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)\right)\leq 2\cdot 7^{p_{1}+p_{2}}\exp(-c(p_{1}+p_{2})-t^{2})\leq\exp(-t^{2}).

For
\boldsymbol{E}_{21}
, following the same
\epsilon
-net arguments, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{21}\|_{2}\geq 3u\right)\leq 7^{(p_{1}-r)+r}\cdot\max_{\|x\|_{2}\leq 1,\|y\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}_{21}y|\geq u\right).

Note that

\displaystyle x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}_{21}y

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{V}y=\left(y^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\otimes x^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\right)\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z})

\displaystyle\sim N\left(0,y^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\Sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{V}y\cdot x^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}x\right).

By Chernoff bound of Gaussian random variables, setting
u\asymp\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+t)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{21}\|\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+t)\right)\leq 2\cdot 7^{p_{1}}\exp(-c(p_{1})-t^{2})\leq\exp(-t^{2}).

Similarly, we can derive the tail bound of
\|\boldsymbol{E}_{12}\|_{2}
.
∎

The following lemma characterizes the maximum of norms for zero-mean Gaussian tensors after any projections.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
{\cal E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times d_{3}}
be a Gaussian tensor,
{\cal E}\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}(0;\;\frac{1}{n}\Sigma_{1}^{-1},\Sigma_{2}^{-1},\Sigma_{3}^{-1})
, where there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that
C_{0}^{-1}\leq\mathop{\min}\limits_{m\in\{1,2,3\}}\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{m})\leq\mathop{\max}\limits_{m\in\{1,2,3\}}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{m})\leq C_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

The following lemma gives an inequality in terms of Frobenius norm between two tensors.

Lemma B.7
.

For two
M
-th order tensors
\gamma,\;\widehat{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
, if
\left\lVert\gamma-\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=o\left(\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\right)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\geq c
for some constant
c>0
, then when
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\cdot\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}-\langle\gamma,\;\widehat{\gamma}\rangle\asymp\left\lVert\gamma-\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
{\cal E}=\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma
, when
\left\lVert\gamma-\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=o(\|\gamma\|_{F})
and
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\geq c,
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\cdot\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}-\langle\gamma,\;\widehat{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\cdot\left\lVert\gamma+{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}-\langle\gamma,\;\gamma+{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\sqrt{\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}+2\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle+\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}-\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}-\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle+\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}{\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}}-\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}-\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle\asymp\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\big(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle+\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}{\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}\big)-\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}-\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}{2}\asymp\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}.

∎

{\cal Z}\sim\frac{1}{2}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{1};\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\frac{1}{2}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{2};\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma})

Lemma B.9
.

Let
\theta=({\cal M},\;-{\cal M},\;[I_{d_{m}}]_{m=1}^{M})
and
\tilde{\theta}=(\tilde{{\cal M}},\;-\tilde{{\cal M}},\;[I_{d_{m}}]_{m=1}^{M})
with
\left\lVert{\cal M}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=\left\lVert\tilde{{\cal M}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=\Delta/2
. For any classifier
\delta
, if
\left\lVert{\cal M}-\tilde{{\cal M}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=o(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then for sufficiently large n,

\displaystyle L_{\theta}(\delta)+L_{\tilde{\theta}}(\delta)\geq\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\left\lVert{\cal M}-\tilde{{\cal M}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}.

Although
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
is not a distance function and does not satisfy an exact triangle inequality, the following lemma provides a variant of Fano’s lemma. It suggests that it suffices to provide a lower bound for
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
, and
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
satisfies an approximate triangle inequality (Lemma
B.9
).

Lemma B.10

(
Tsybakov, (2009)
)
.

Let
N\geq 2
and
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}\in\Theta_{d}
. For some constants
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1/8),\gamma>0
and any classifier
\widehat{\delta}
, if
{\rm KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}},\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{j}})\leq\alpha_{0}\log N/n
for all
1\leq i\leq N
, and
L_{\theta_{i}}(\widehat{\delta})<\gamma
implies
L_{\theta_{j}}(\widehat{\delta})\geq\gamma
for all
0\leq i\neq j\leq N
, then

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\delta}}\sup_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}}(L_{\theta_{i}}(\widehat{\delta}))\geq\gamma)\geq\frac{\sqrt{N}}{1+\sqrt{N}}(1-2\alpha_{0}-\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\log N}})>0.

Lemma B.11

(Varshamov-Gilbert Bound,
Tsybakov, (2009)
)
.

Consider the set of all binary sequences of length m:
\Omega=\big\{\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}),\omega_{i}\in\{0,1\}\big\}=\{0,1\}^{m}
. Let
m\geq 8
, then there exists a subset
\{\omega^{(0)},\omega^{(1)},

\ldots,\omega^{(N)}\}
of
\Omega
such that
\omega^{(0)}=(0,\ldots,0)
,
\rho_{H}(\omega^{(i)},\omega^{(j)})\geq m/8,\forall 0\leq i<j\leq N
, and
N\geq 2^{m/8}
, where
\rho_{H}
denotes the Hamming distance.
