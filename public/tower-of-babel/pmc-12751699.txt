Gannan rare earth elements cohort: study design, methods, and baseline characteristics of the cohort
Authors: Wei Liu, Lei Huang, Jie Wang, Yuyang Lei, Fen Yan, Shuting Cao, Zhiyu Jiang, Jiabin Xu, Yi He, Feijie Wang, Jiaqiang Gui, You Guo, Weihong Chen, Xiaokang Zhang, Yanfang Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Gannan region, known as the "Kingdom of Rare Earths", supplies over 70% of the global demand for medium and heavy rare earth elements (REEs). However, large-scale mining has led to environmental REEs contamination, posing potential health risks to residents. The Gannan REEs Cohort (GREEC) was established to investigate the long-term health effects of REEs exposure.MethodsGREEC is an ongoing community-based prospective cohort study conducted in two counties of Ganzhou, China. We enrolled 1062 residents aged 18–80 years who had lived in the area for at least five years. At baseline, we collected comprehensive data via questionnaires, physical examinations, and biological samples. Urinary concentrations of 15 REEs were measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. Follow-up surveys, including repeated physical examinations and validation of chronic disease endpoints, are planned every three years.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 50 years, with 78.81% being female. The prevalence of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes were 34.09%, 31.26%, and 6.40%, respectively. Detectable rates of all rare earth elements in urine were over 75%, with median concentrations ranging from 0.20 ng/L (Tm) to 28.54 ng/L (Nd). Light REEs (La, Ce, Pr, Nd) showed strong positive correlations (e.g., La and Ce: ρ = 0.85), whereas heavy REEs exhibited more complex correlation patterns.ConclusionAs the first community-based cohort focusing on residents in heavy REE mining areas, GREEC provides critical baseline data on REEs exposure and cardiometabolic risk profiles. Its longitudinal design and comprehensive data offer a unique platform for elucidating REEs-related health effects and underlying mechanisms.Keywords: Rare earth elements, Cohort study, Environmental health, Prospective study, Cohort profile

Introduction

Rare earth elements (REEs) are core materials supporting modern industry, medicine, and high-tech industries. Global demand has increased by more than eight times in the past 30 years. China acts as the leading supplier of REE, and its large-scale mining has led to the introduction of REEs into the ecosystem through environmental emissions, widely distributed in soil, water, and atmospheric particulate matter [1]. People can be exposed through food chain enrichment, drinking water, respiratory exposure (such as PM2.5, cigarette smoke) and other ways [2]. Studies have shown that there is a significant positive correlation between environmental REE concentration and blood/urine REE concentration in residents from industrial activity areas, and long-term accumulation may induce chronic damage to multiple systems, such as respiratory, reproductive, digestive, and nervous system, etc. [3, 4].

The Gannan region, as the core distribution area of global ion-type heavy rare earth resources, is known as the "Kingdom of Rare Earths". Its reserves of heavy rare earths account for more than 90% of similar resources in China [5]. Compared with other typical REEs production areas, such as Inner Mongolia in China, Mount Pass in the United States, and Wilder Hill in Australia, the Gannan mining area holds a dominant position in medium and heavy REEs, supplying over 70% of the global demand for medium and heavy rare earths [1, 6]. Heavy REEs usually exhibit a higher bioaccumulation risk and stronger toxicity than light REEs [7–9]. Such environmental and toxicological characteristics of REEs in Gannan region, where intensive mining occurs, have driven significant human exposure and potential health impacts. Previous studies have found that the levels of REEs in soil, water, and vegetables in rare earth mining areas in Gannan region are much higher than those in general areas, resulting in the estimated daily intake (EDI) of REE exceeding the threshold of subclinical damage, which may pose a health risk [2, 10]. It has been reported that high rare earth exposure in the southern part of Jiangxi Province may affect blood biochemical indicators [11] and may be related to the risk of hypertension [12]. However, epidemiological evidence, particularly evidence from systematic prospective cohort studies remains scarce regarding the health impacts of REEs exposure on residents in the Gannan region and even worldwide.

Gannan REEs Cohort (GREEC) is an ongoing cohort study aimed at investigating the relationship between long-term exposure to REEs and the health and corresponding pathogenic patterns of community residents in areas with high REEs exposure. The biomarkers of REEs exposure were determined in the study. In addition to taking common chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, chronic lung diseases, and cancer as the follow-up endpoints, changes in some physiological indicators, such as lung function, blood pressure, physical constitution, liver and kidney functions, etc., are all within the scope of outcome investigation. The study also intends to explore the potential pathways by which REEs affect health through multi-omics methods such as transcriptomics, metabolomics and proteomics.

Methods

Study area and population

The GREEC study was conducted in two counties of Ganzhou, China (Fig. 1) in 2024. Ganzhou, the largest city in Jiangxi Province, is a global hub for REE mining. Two communities in Ganzhou were selected by a stratified cluster sampling method: According to the differences between urban and rural areas, the research area was stratified to form a representative sampling framework; subsequently, communities or administrative villages from each level were randomly selected as cluster units; finally, all residents meeting the basic conditions in each selected cluster were invited to participate. This design aims to efficiently construct a cohort that can represent the general population of the REE mining area, while also considering the diversity of the sample in key environmental exposure dimensions and living types, laying the foundation for subsequent health effect studies. Residents in the communities aged from 18 to 80 years who have lived in the communities for at least five years and meet the following conditions were included in the GREEC study: not suffering from serious diseases that impaired their ability to take care of themselves, especially those who were unable to complete our questionnaires, physical examinations, and sample collection procedures successfully; non-menstrual, pregnant, or lactating women; residents who can communicate effectively and are willing to participate in the research.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University (Identified No. 22SC-2025343). All participants were well informed and provided written informed consent.

Study design

The GREEC study is a community-based, prospective cohort of resident aged 18 to 80 years living in Gnana area, with the aim of investigating the impact of REE exposure on health. Participants were invited to attend local community health centers to complete a questionnaire survey, a physical examination, and biological samples collections after an overnight fast. Baseliner characteristics were collected by trained interviewers via a semi-structured questionnaire. The physical examination was performed by trained physicians. Fasting peripheral venous blood and fasting midstream morning urine were collected. Some of the samples were used to determine biochemical traits, and some were aliquot, frozen, and then stored for future use. The follow-up is planned to be conducted every three years in the future.

Data collection

Questionnaires

The setting of the questionnaire referred to previous cohort studies of Chinese community residents [13]. Demographics, socioeconomic, lifestyle, medical history, family disease history, living conditions, and environmental exposure were collected via the questionnaire. Sleep quality was evaluated by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index Scale [14]. The questionnaire investigated the frequency and duration of different exercise intensities of the participants in the past half year. According to the Physical Activity Guidelines for the Chinese population, engaging in 150 to 300 min of moderate-intensity (3 to 6 METs) or 75 to 150 min of higher-intensity (> 6 METs) exercise per week, or an equivalent combination of the two intensities of exercise, is defined as physical actively [15]. The short web-based versions of Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-4) was used to assess the participants' self-awareness of stress [16]. Subjective cognitive decline was evaluated by the validated Subjective Cognitive Decline 9-item (SCD-9) [17]. Measurement of loneliness was performed by the UCLA Loneliness Scale (ULS-8) [18].

Quality control includes the following measures: the "Investigation Manual" was formulated, and unified training for each investigator was conducted in advance; supervisors conducted on-site inspections and verifications, and investigators need to promptly self-verify the collected information and then hand it over to the supervisors for verification; after the work of the day is completed, all staff members should promptly report the progress and submit work reports which should summarize and improve the investigation work every day in accordance with the provisions and standards of the manual; the data should be entered in a double-entry manner and the entered results should be verified again.

Physical examination

Physical examination included anthropometry measures, routine internal and surgical examinations, blood pressure measure, and pulmonary function. Anthropometry measures including standing height, weight, waist circumference and hip circumference were measured when participants stood and took off their shoes while wearing light indoor clothing. Routine internal and surgical examinations were performed by physicians. Heart rate and blood pressure were measured on right arm via a validated electric blood pressure monitor (OMRON HEM-7135, OMRON (Dalian) Co., Ltd, Dalian, China). The determination was conducted using a digital pulmonary function instrument (Chestgraph HI-101, CHEST MI, Inc, Tokyo, Japan), and the operation and quality control were carried out in accordance with the guidelines issued by the American Thoracic Society [19].

Urine sampling

Each participant was required to provide no less than 20 ml of fasting morning urine. One milliliter of urine sample was taken for routine urine analysis on the day of the physical examination. After entering the human body through various pathways, REEs are mainly excreted through urine. The REEs in urine are reliable biomarkers for exposure to REEs. The remaining urine samples were aliquot and stored at −20 °C for the determination of REE and other biomarkers.

Fasting blood sampling

A total of 15 ml of fasting venous blood samples were collected from each subject [2 × 5 ml of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) anticoagulant tubes and 1 × 5 ml of serum coagulant tubes]. Blood biochemical indicators are immediately determined in the clinical laboratory. Measure the blood cell count. The remaining blood was separated into plasma, serum, and whole blood cells, and stored in equal layers at −80 °C for future use. The genomic DNA of each participant was extracted from whole blood cells and stored at −80 °C for future research.

REE measurement

The research team has established a reliable ICP-MS method, which can accurately quantify the levels of 15 urinary REEs, including ​​lanthanum (La)​​, ​​cerium (Ce)​​, ​​praseodymium (Pr)​​, ​​neodymium (Nd)​​, ​​samarium (Sm)​​, ​​europium (Eu)​​, ​​gadolinium (Gd)​​, ​​terbium (Tb)​​, ​​dysprosium (Dy)​​, ​​holmium (Ho)​​, ​​erbium (Er)​​, ​​thulium (Tm)​​, ​​ytterbium (Yb)​​, ​​lutetium (Lu)​​, and ​​yttrium (Y), in the study population. Thaw the urine sample at room temperature and add 1 mL of urine from the supernatant to a 15 mL polypropylene conical centrifuge tube. Next, 3% HNO3 was added to the final volume of 5 mL for overnight nitrification reaction. The resulting sample was ultrasonically digested at 40 °C for 1 h. The samples were subjected to rare earth analysis by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, NexION 350; Perkinelmer Technologies). The operating conditions of ICP-MS are as follows: radio frequency power 1550 W, plasma gas flow rate 15.00 L/min, auxiliary gas flow rate 0.9 L/min, carrier gas flow rate 0.25 L/min, and resolution (peak height 10%) 0.65–0.80 amu. The improved sample volume was 0.4 mL/min and the single-peak retention time was 0.1 s. For quality control, the measurement values of REEs were repeated three times, and the average of the three measurement results was used for statistical analysis. Standard reference substance Human urine (SRM2670a Toxic elements in urine; The instrument performance is evaluated as external quality control for each batch by the National Institute of Standards and Technology in Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA. If the concentration differs from the calibrated value of SRM2670a by more than 5%, recalibrate the instrument and reanalyze the previous batch of samples. Sample testing was conducted on the basis of the standard curve R2 > 0.998. Each group of samples to be tested includes 31 urine samples to be tested and 5 quality control samples (parallel samples, blank samples, no spiked samples, low concentration spiked samples, and high concentration spiked samples). If all of the following quality control requirements are met, the test results of the 31 urine samples to be tested in this group will be accepted. Otherwise, the pretreatment and testing of this group of samples need to be redone until all quality control requirements are met: A) No target compound is detected in the blank sample; B) The relative standard deviation of the parallel samples is less than 10%; C) The spiked recovery rates of each quality control sample were within the range of 100 ± 15%; D) The recovery rate of the internal standard is within the range of 100 ± 20%; E) For samples with concentration values higher than the highest point of the standard curve in the test results, the dilution ratio needs to be increased and they should be reprocessed before being tested on the machine.

Endpoints during follow-up

The physical examinations and laboratory tests were repeatedly performed every 3 years. Self-reported chronic diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), and cancer et al., were repeatedly collected during follow-up and verified by medical records.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage). Continuous variables were expressed as median (first quartile, third quartile) deviation or mean ± standard depending on the normality of the data. Pairwise correlations among urinary REEs were evaluated by spearman correlation coefficients with coefficient > 0.70 regarding as strong correlation [20]. All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.1.0).

The correlations between light REEs and heavy REEs are mostly weak, such as La and Lu (ρ = 0.36), Ce and Tm (ρ = 0.35), and Nd and Yb (ρ = 0.37). However, the correlations between La and Yb (ρ = 0.42) and Ce and Lu (ρ = 0.40) are slightly higher than those of other light-heavy combinations. Furthermore, there are significant differences in the correlations among elements. For instance, Gd shows extremely weak correlation with Tb (ρ = 0.21) and Dy shows extremely weak correlation with Tb (ρ = 0.09), while Sm shows moderate correlations with Ho (ρ = 0.58) and Tm shows moderate correlation with Ho (ρ = 0.55).

Discussion

The GREEC study has established a key prospective framework for exploring the health effects of long-term exposure to REEs among residents in important global rare earth mining areas. As the first community population cohort focusing on heavy rare earth mining areas, this study provides unique biological surveillance data and baseline characteristics, laying the foundation for future etiological research.

The baseline characteristics of the cohort highlighted the significant risk burden of cardiometabolic diseases in the population: the prevalence rates of hypertension, dyslipidemia and diabetes were 34.09%, 31.26% and 6.40%, respectively. Although this cross-sectional analysis could not infer the causal relationship between rare earth exposure and diseases, the prospective design of GREEC (integrating physical examinations, laboratory tests, and validated disease endpoint) will support a rigorous assessment of the expose-disease relationship. It is notable that the study included detailed covariate data (such as physical activity levels evaluated based on the Chinese guidelines, PSS-4 stress score, SCD-9 cognitive function and ULS-8 loneliness scale), providing a comprehensive guarantee for the adjustment of confounding factors in future analyses.

At present, data on REE exposure among community residents is extremely scarce. This study enriched the large-sample detection data for REE exposure provided by community residents. Compared with the urban residents in Guangxi, China, another region rich in REEs, the population in this study had higher median levels of all REEs except La [21]. Compared with the non-occupationally exposed population of the another study conducted biological monitoring of REEs in the Inner Mongolia region of China, another area rich in REEs [22], the median levels of REEs in the urine of the population in this study were also much higher [22]. Moreover, compared with the occupationally exposed population in the Inner Mongolia region of China, the median urinary levels of all heavy REEs except Gd in the population of this study were also higher [22], this might be due to the differences in the types of REE minerals between the Inner Mongolia region and the Gannan region. Based on the available data, it can be seen that the community residents in Gannan region have a relatively high exposure to rare earth elements. Therefore, it is necessary to pay attention to the health risks resulting from long-term high-dose exposure to rare earth elements.

Some limitations of the GREEC study should be taken into consideration: (1) regional focus restricts the extrapolation of results to non-mining area populations; (2) rare earth measurements at a single time point cannot analyze intra-individual exposure variations. (3) self-reported disease outcomes need to be verified through medical records during follow-up. Nevertheless, GREEC's core advantages, including its forward-looking design, systematic biological monitoring of 15 rare earth elements, and abundant covariate data, make it an indispensable platform for clarifying the health risks associated with rare earths. In the future, by utilizing biological sample banks, analyses of associations and mediating effects, and incorporate evidence from toxicological experiment to explore the relationship between REEs exposure and health outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this study establishes the GREEC as a foundational resource for investigating the health effects of REEs exposure. The baseline findings reveal widespread detectable levels of REEs in participants, distinct correlation patterns between light and heavy REEs. This study underscores the potential public health significance of long-term REE exposure, particularly in mining-intensive regions. Future analyses will leverage longitudinal follow-up data to examine relationships between REE mixtures and chronic diseases, integrate multi-omics platforms to elucidate underlying biological mechanisms, and explore effect modified by genetic and lifestyle factors. Together, these efforts will advance our understanding of REE-related health risks and support evidence-based prevention strategies.
