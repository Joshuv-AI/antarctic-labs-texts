Introduction

Research background

Airports function as distinctive transportation hubs where emergency medical services operate under conditions of high unpredictability, diverse clinical presentations, and a critical need for multi-departmental coordination. Ineffective emergency response in such settings may result in treatment delays, disorganized resource use, higher risks to patient safety, and subsequently, greater public health and economic costs [1, 2]. Yet, current frameworks still fall short in evaluating nursing competencies specific to airport emergencies. Training remains largely unstructured, lacking clear tiered benchmarks, which leads to poorly tailored programs and difficulties in measuring outcomes.

Only by establishing a scientifically grounded core competency framework and implementing it through targeted education and training can the effectiveness of airport emergency medical response be systematically enhanced.

Research problem

Given this context, no systematic and practical tool currently exists to guide the development, assessment, and management of nursing competencies in airport emergency centers. Reliance on generalized emergency protocols or subjective judgment fails to address the precise demands of this specialized setting.

Current research status

Significant work has been done around core nursing competency models, with widespread use of theoretical frameworks such as competency models and the Onion Model. These have helped outline necessary knowledge, skills, and traits—especially in conventional settings like hospital emergency departments and ICUs [3, 4]. Related assessment tools have also been developed [5, 6]. Studies further agree on fundamental dimensions of nursing competency—such as clinical assessment, emergency intervention, communication, teamwork, and critical thinking—laying a foundation for training and evaluation [7].

Gaps and limitations in existing research

When directed toward the distinct and complex pre-hospital environment of airport emergencies, current research exhibits notable shortcomings. First, prevailing nursing competency models and their assessment tools are chiefly designed for hospital-based care. Consequently, they frequently miss critical competencies unique to airport response, including initial triage and command during mass casualty incidents, rescue operations in confined airport spaces like aircraft cabins or runways, and effective coordination with diverse non-medical entities such as air traffic control and security personnel. Context-specific procedures for public health emergencies, such as infection control, are also often underrepresented.

A dedicated research gap exists. Literature shows few attempts to systematically build a core competency indicator system for nursing teams within international airport medical centers. Available studies in aviation medicine or transport nursing typically concentrate on flight nurses’ specialized skills or in-flight equipment, rather than the comprehensive competency framework needed for ground-based airport emergency staff—encompassing prevention, response, management, and patient transport throughout the emergency process [8, 9].

The lack of authoritative, detailed standards in this setting has practical consequences. Training, assessment, and management at airport emergency centers often fall back on generic guidelines or institutional experience. This reliance contributes to training that poorly matches actual airport scenarios, highly subjective competency evaluations, and an underdeveloped evidence base for guiding nurses’ professional growth [10].

Methods

Onion model: theoretical foundation

The Onion Model is a multi-layered framework for understanding competence that characterizes professional competence as a hierarchical, dynamic structure with four interconnected levels.

[11]. Unlike traditional static competency models, it emphasizes the progressive development of competence from surface-level knowledge to deep-seated professional attributes, making it well-suited for complex, specialized settings like airport emergency care [12].

The four layers of the model directly align with the competency requirements of airport emergency nursing:

Theoretical knowledge (outermost layer): Encompasses specialized knowledge such as aviation medicine, emergency protocols, and transport procedures, forming the foundational basis for airport emergency nursing practice.

Professional Skills (second layer): Reflects the application of theoretical knowledge in practical scenarios, including BLS/ALS, triage, and confined-space rescue, which are critical for airport emergency response.

Professional Skills (second layer): Reflects the application of theoretical knowledge in practical scenarios, including BLS/ALS, triage, and confined-space rescue, which are critical for airport emergency response.

Professional Competence (third layer): Encompasses internalized professional values, ethical standards, and teamwork spirit, guiding nursing behaviors in high-pressure airport emergency scenarios.

Professional Development (core layer): Represents long-term professional growth, including adaptive decision-making and multi-agency coordination, which are essential for addressing the unpredictable nature of airport emergencies.

This theoretical framework emphasizes the dynamic, integrated, and multidimensional nature of competence, making it particularly suitable for assessing nursing competencies in complex, specialized settings like airport emergency centers. It provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for the systematic analysis and continuous improvement of nursing competencies, supporting the development of a scientific, rational competency evaluation system and promoting standardized, refined nursing management. To construct an Onion Model-based core competency indicator system for airport emergency centers nurses, an expert panel was formed, consisting of 17 experts: 2 held senior clinical nursing titles, 5 were engaged in clinical nursing practice, 4 in nursing management, 6 in clinical medicine, and 2 in administration. The final framework was developed through an evidence-based approach and Delphi expert consultation.

Literature review

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in Chinese databases (CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP) and English databases (Medline, PubMed, Web of Science). The search strategy employed keywords including “onion model,” “airport emergency center,” “nursing,” “core competency,” “competence,” “indicator,” and “framework.” The search period spanned from the inception of each database to February 2024. The inclusion criteria were: ① literature in Chinese or English; ② all publication types were considered. The exclusion criterion was: unavailability of the full text. Based on the findings of this literature review, a preliminary draft of the Onion Model-based core competency evaluation indicators for airport emergency centers nurses was constructed.

Expert panel meetings

A research team of 10 experts reviewed the preliminary draft of the core competency indicators. Considering the unique clinical context of airports, the competency levels were restructured. For items in the draft that were contentious, Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) were organized. The “Scenario Anchoring Method” was employed, where experts provided modification suggestions based on real-world cases. Through a voting process, items with an agreement rate of ≥ 90% were retained, ultimately finalizing the content of the core competency system.

Delphi phase

Delphi technique phase

According to the standard requirements for expert selection in the Delphi technique, the panel typically comprises 15 to 50 individuals [13], who should possess extensive professional knowledge and practical experience in the relevant field. For this study, seventeen experts were selected.

The criteria for expert inclusion were as follows:

being a healthcare professional engaged in work related to civilian airport emergency centers or familiar with the current status and development of the industry;

being a healthcare professional engaged in work related to civilian airport emergency centers or familiar with the current status and development of the industry;

having over 10 years of work experience in this field;

having over 10 years of work experience in this field;

holding an intermediate professional title or higher;

holding an intermediate professional title or higher;

voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion was the failure to return the consultation questionnaire in a timely manner during the research period.

voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study. The exclusion criterion was the failure to return the consultation questionnaire in a timely manner during the research period.

Notably, all experts involved in this study were from China, and the panel was primarily composed of clinical and nursing practitioners with direct frontline experience in airport emergency care, thereby ensuring that the indicators accurately reflect the practical needs of frontline nursing work. The exclusive inclusion of Chinese experts may limit the generalizability of the findings to international airport emergency settings, and future research should incorporate experts from aviation operations, EMS coordination, operational command, and educational design to mitigate potential disciplinary bias.

Expert questionnaire

The expert consultation questionnaire used in this phase consisted of four main sections: (1) the background, objectives, and research content of the study; (2) general demographic information of the experts; (3) the expert’s familiarity with the content of the survey and the basis for their evaluation of the items [14]; and (4) a request for experts to rate the importance of each indicator using a 5-point Likert scale, where a score of 1 indicated “not important” and 5 indicated “very important.” Experts were also invited to provide suggestions for adding, removing, or modifying items in the remarks section.

Delphi implementation

Two Delphi rounds were conducted via email from June to September 2025, each lasting two weeks. Questionnaires were distributed to the selected experts, who were reminded to provide feedback in a timely manner. Each round of the Delphi consultation was conducted anonymously, and the process was concluded when a high degree of consensus among the experts was achieved. After Round 1, we revised materials based on expert feedback. Retention criteria required indicators to simultaneously satisfy: (1) mean importance score > 3.5, (2) coefficient of variation < 0.25, and (3) full-score proportion > 20% [15]. These criteria were adopted based on a systematic review of Delphi studies, which identified these thresholds as the standard for consensus determination in competency framework research [15].Indicators with CV > 0.25 were removed because a high coefficient of variation indicates a high degree of divergence among expert ratings, meaning the experts did not reach a consistent consensus on the importance of these indicators. This decision was made by the research team after reviewing the expert feedback and statistical results, in line with common practices in Delphi studies to ensure the reliability and consensus of the indicator system.

Data analysis

Data entry and statistical analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. General characteristics of the experts were described using frequencies, percentages (%), and mean ± standard deviation. The response rate of the questionnaires was used to represent the enthusiasm coefficient of the experts. The authority degree of the experts (Cr) was determined by the judgment coefficient (Ca) and familiarity coefficient (Cs) [16], The degree of consensus among the experts was assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) and the coefficient of variation (CV). In this study, the criteria for retaining indicators included a CV < 0.25 and a mean score ≥ 3.5. Finally, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was applied to determine the weights of indicators at all levels based on their importance ratings [17]。.

Expert response rates and authority coefficient

Both Delphi rounds achieved a 100% response rate, with an expert authority coefficient (Cr) of 0.8323 (judgment coefficient Ca = 0.9235, familiarity coefficient Cs = 0.7412), demonstrating high expert engagement and panel credibility in airport emergency care.

The coefficient of variation (CV) and Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) were used to assess the degree of divergence among expert ratings for each indicator and to evaluate the reliability of each round of scoring. Kendall’s W reflects the level of agreement among the 17 participating experts regarding the weights assigned to indicators at each level. Because calculating Kendall’s W can be complex, it was computed using SPSS software. W values range between 0 and 1; higher values indicate better agreement among experts, while lower values indicate poorer agreement. A P-value of less than 0.05, corresponding to the W value, suggests that the experts’ ratings of the indicator system were statistically consistent. Weighting results and interpretation

Based on the expert consultation scores from the second round, the weights of the indicator system were determined. The weights reflect the relative importance of each indicator in the overall competency framework for airport emergency nursing.

The weighting process was validated via the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), with consistency ratios (CR) calculated for all judgment matrices to ensure mathematical reliability. For example, the CR value for the first-level indicator matrix was 0.023 (as presented in Table 3, CR < 0.1), demonstrating acceptable consistency. All other judgment matrices also met the consistency threshold, confirming that the assigned weights are statistically robust and reflect expert consensus without significant cognitive bias. The allocation of higher weights to “Theoretical Knowledge” (0.489) and “Professional Skills” (0.346) emphasizes the foundational role of these two domains in supporting effective emergency response capabilities, which aligns with the educational principle that a solid theoretical foundation and proficient practical skills are prerequisites for nurses to perform competently in high-stress airport emergency environments.

The relatively lower weights assigned to “Professional Attitudes” (0.082) and “Professional Behaviors” (0.082) do not indicate that these domains are less critical; instead, this distribution reflects expert consensus that these competencies are developed and integrated based on the foundational theoretical knowledge and professional skills, and they are manifested in practical nursing performance rather than being independent competency domains. From an educational perspective, this suggests that while professional competence and development are critical for long-term career growth and adaptability, they are not the primary focus in the initial stages of training for airport emergency nursing. It should be noted that these weights reflect expert consensus on the relative importance of competency indicators, which can be used to prioritize training content. Future empirical studies will validate the correlation between these weights and actual on-the-job performance to confirm their practical effectiveness. Discussion

Scientificity and innovativeness of the indicator system construction

The Delphi method employed in this study is a well-established approach widely used in similar research contexts. The reliability of its results is generally evaluated based on three aspects: expert authority, expert consensus, and participation rate. As reported in Sect. 3.2, this study achieved a 100% expert response rate and a high authority coefficient (0.8323), which supports the reliability of the findings.

While the statistical results demonstrate methodological rigor, it is crucial to critically examine how these metrics align with the underlying principles of competency-based medical education (CBME). CBME emphasizes the assessment of real-world performance and the development of individualized learning paths, which may not be fully captured by consensus-driven methods alone. The coefficients of variation (CV), which indicate the degree of dispersion in expert ratings, ranged from 0 to 0.258 in the first round and 0 to 0.248 in the second round. Additionally, Kendall’s W values for the different indicator levels ranged from 0.413 to 0.509, demonstrating a high degree of consensus and stability in expert opinions. However, the consensus achieved must be balanced against the potential for groupthink and the suppression of dissenting views, which could limit the framework’s applicability in diverse and dynamic emergency settings. These results further underscore the reliability of the study outcomes. This study represents the first phase of competency framework development, focusing on the construction and consensus-building of the indicator system. As outlined in the Limitations section, the practical effectiveness, predictive validity, and criterion validity of this framework will be tested in subsequent empirical studies, which will include longitudinal field implementation and performance correlation analysis to validate the framework’s real-world utility. Within the framework of competency-based medical education (CBME), the Onion Model provides a structured approach to competency development that aligns with the goals of assessing and improving real-world performance [18]. Its hierarchical, progressive structure offers a clear pathway for developing nursing competencies and improving nursing quality, featuring a systematic design that navigates complex operational environments and incorporates dynamic adjustment mechanisms in response to contextual shifts. In the specific setting of airport emergency care, the Model facilitates the enhancement of cross-cultural communication skills, emergency decision-making abilities, and teamwork cohesion, thereby contributing to more efficient emergency response and strengthened collaborative performance [19]. Its innovative application highlights the potential for integrating CBME principles into specialized emergency medical contexts, yet al.so underscores the need for ongoing validation and refinement through empirical research.

Its innovative application highlights the multidimensional and adaptive nature of nursing competency frameworks. Particularly in culturally diverse environments such as airports, nursing staff can improve their communication competence by understanding varying cultural perspectives on medical services and health, thereby reducing potential misunderstandings and conflicts. Furthermore, through strengthened emergency decision-making and team collaboration—supported by simulation training and team exercises—nursing personnel demonstrate significantly improved performance under high-pressure conditions and greater teamwork efficiency [20].

Practicality and replicability of the constructed indicator system

This study developed a core competency indicator system for nursing in airport emergency centers based on the “Onion Model” The system is characterized by high practicality and broad replicability. These attributes ensure the system provides clear guidance for capacity building in airport emergency centers and serves as a valuable reference for medical security systems in similar facilities. Regarding practicality, the system demonstrates a high degree of contextual fit. Its construction strictly adhered to the “from surface to core” logic of the Onion Theory and was closely integrated with the distinctive environment of airports. This includes responding to mass casualty incidents, managing various injuries and illnesses, operating under intense time pressure, and collaborating across multiple departments. Consequently, each indicator directly addresses core needs in real-world operational contexts. For instance, indicators like “Knowledge of Aviation Medical Emergency Management” and “Inter-departmental (e.g., Security, Ground Services) Collaborative Emergency Response Capability” represent critical elements that distinguish airport emergency care from conventional pre-hospital or in-hospital emergency care. This approach aligns with the study by Kyong Hee Park et al. [21], which identified and integrated nurses’ core competencies across multiple roles and responsibilities through systematic review and network analysis, optimizing the indicator structure to fit specific nursing contexts.

In terms of structure and operability, the hierarchical “onion” framework provides a clear pathway for competency assessment and development [22]. Hospital and airport emergency centers managers can utilize indicators from the “Theoretical knowledge layer” and “Professional Skills layer” for initial competency screening and standardized foundational training, ensuring that nursing staff master the essential theoretical knowledge and practical skills for airport emergency care. Indicators from the “Professional Competence layer” can be used to guide the cultivation of professional values and ethical awareness through targeted education and cultural building activities. Additionally, indicators from the “Professional Development layer” (e.g., on-site emergency decision-making, adaptive response to complex scenarios) can inform the design of advanced simulation drills and scenario-based training programs, enabling the evaluation of integrated nursing performance in real-world-like emergency settings. This stratified logic facilitates the targeted allocation of resources, development of training programs, and establishment of performance evaluation criteria. The foundational layer primarily encompasses general emergency care competencies, including Basic Life Support (BLS) and Advanced Life Support (ALS) skills. These are essential capabilities for all emergency nursing personnel [23]. This layered capacity-building model supports the comprehensive enhancement of the integrated emergency response capabilities of airport emergency nurses, thereby increasing their adaptability and responsiveness within the complex and dynamic airport emergency environment [24].

Theoretical rationale and practical implications of the weight allocation

The core competency framework for nursing staff in airport emergency centers developed in this study is grounded in the Onion Model. The weight distribution—Theoretical Knowledge (0.489), Professional Skills (0.346), Professional Competence (0.082), and Professional Development (0.082)—represents expert consensus on the hierarchical and foundational nature of competency elements, rather than underestimating the importance of non-technical competencies. A solid grounding in aviation medicine, emergency protocols, and proficiency in first aid techniques and equipment operation are essential for on-site leadership, multi-agency coordination, and emergency response [25]. The concentrated weight distribution reflects expert consensus that without adequate knowledge and skill support, deeper adaptive, decision-making, and coordination abilities cannot be effectively exercised in complex emergencies. Airports, as densely populated public spaces, are increasingly subject to scrutiny in the age of social media. Inappropriate behavior, even if minor, can be recorded and disseminated, potentially triggering public skepticism or reputational harm. This underscores the importance of systematic theoretical and skills training, which influences not only emergency response effectiveness but also organizational reputation and public trust. The weight distribution identified in this study suggests that training programs should prioritize the internalization of knowledge and skills to facilitate the transformation of surface-level competencies into deeper capabilities. For example, integrating theoretical knowledge into emergency decision-making simulations can enhance trainees’ leadership and coordination abilities in dynamic scenarios.

Limitations

Several limitations merit consideration in interpreting this study’s findings. The expert panel was composed primarily of frontline clinicians and nursing practitioners from similar institutional backgrounds within China, with limited representation from stakeholders in aviation administration, EMS coordination, operational command, or educational design. This composition was chosen to prioritize the practical needs of frontline nursing work, but it may have skewed the framework toward individual clinical skills at the expense of system-level coordination and pedagogical aspects, thereby introducing a potential disciplinary bias.

As a consensus-based approach, the Delphi method has inherent subjectivity, as the results are based on expert judgment rather than empirical clinical data, which may limit the generalizability of the framework to non-Chinese airport emergency settings. Even after multiple rounds, substantial divergence persisted in interpreting certain dimensions—such as “professional competence” (SD > 0.15)—indicating that the resulting indicators reflect expert judgment rather than empirical data.

The proposed framework remains theoretical; its practical effectiveness, training utility, and real-world applicability await validation through field implementation and longitudinal evaluation. Additionally, the implementation of the competency framework in real-world training may face cultural limitations, such as differences in emergency care practices and professional values across regions, and practical limitations. Practically, resource constraints in some airport emergency centers—such as limited access to high-fidelity simulation equipment, insufficient training budgets, or staffing shortages—may hinder the delivery of targeted, scenario-based training programs consistent with the framework. Moreover, the fast-paced and unpredictable nature of airport emergency work may limit the time available for nursing staff to participate in structured training, thereby further challenging the implementation of the competency system.

Moreover, the clinical predominance in the panel could have favored measurable technical skills over less tangible competencies like coordination and psychological adaptability. Grounded primarily in a nursing-clinical perspective, the framework may also require adaptation to support interdisciplinary collaboration and to fit varying cultural or EMS governance contexts [26].

Conclusion

This study constructed a core competency indicator system for nursing staff in airport emergency centers using the Delphi method. The system comprises 4 first-level indicators, 13 s-level indicators, and 56 third-level indicators. The developed system demonstrates scientific rigor and practical applicability, as it is based on a methodologically sound approach and a comprehensive, rational indicator framework. These findings provide a scientific basis for developing targeted emergency training programs for nursing personnel in airport emergency centers. However, the selection of experts may have introduced regional biases, which represents a potential limitation of this study. Given that this study is confined to a single setting and specific national context, this framework has not been validated across different sites or cultural contexts, thereby limiting its generalizability to international or non-Chinese airport emergency settings. Therefore, future research should prioritize cross-site and cross-cultural validation, as well as practical application and empirical testing, to refine and improve the proposed framework to enhance its applicability to diverse institutional and cultural contexts.
