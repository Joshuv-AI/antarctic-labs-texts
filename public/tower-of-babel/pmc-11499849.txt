Gene co-expression analysis reveals conserved and distinct gene networks between resistant and susceptible Lens ervoides challenged by hemibiotrophic and necrotrophic pathogens
Authors: Zhe Cao, Sabine Banniza
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs field crops are likely to be challenged by multiple pathogens during their development, the investigation of broad-spectrum resistance in the host is of great interest for crop genetic enhancement. In this study, we attempted to address this question by adopting a weighed gene co-expression approach to study the temporal transcriptome dynamics of resistant and susceptible recombinant inbred lines (RILs) derived from an intraspecific Len ervoides cross during the infection process with either the necrotrophic pathogens Ascochyta lentis or Stemphylium botryosum, or the hemibiotrophic pathogen Colletotrichum lentis. By comparing networks of resistant and susceptible RILs, seven network module pairs were found to possess high correlation coefficients (R > 0.70) and large number of overlapping genes (n > 100). The conserved co-regulation of genes in these network module pairs were involved in plant cell wall synthesis, cell division, cytoskeleton organization, and protein ubiquitin related processes and appeared to be common disease responses against these pathogens. On the other hand, we also identified eight modules with low correlation between resistance and susceptibility networks. Among those, a stronger gene co-expression in R-genes and small RNA processes in the resistant hosts may be enhancing L. ervoides resistance against A. lentis, C. lentis, and S. botryosum, whereas the higher level of synergistic regulation in the synthesis of arginine and glutamine and phospholipid and glycerophospholipids in the susceptible hosts may contribute to increased susceptibility in L. ervoides.Subject terms: Plant sciences, Plant stress responses

Introduction

Pathogenic fungi are the largest group of plant pathogens accounting for a wide range of plant diseases and significant economic losses. According to their lifestyles in hosts, these pathogens have been primarily categorized into biotrophs, necrotrophs, and hemibiotrophs. During the infection process, biotrophic fungi survive, draw nutrients, and proliferate only in living host cells, whereas necrotrophic fungi actively kill host cells and take up nutrients from dead tissues. Hemibiotrophic fungi combine aspects of both of these lifestyles by first parasitizing the living host tissue (biotrophic phase) and then inducing host cell death (necrotrophic phase) and proliferating in dead tissue1. These fundamental lifestyle differences among pathogens underpin a diversity of pathogenesis strategies. In response, plants have evolved sophisticated immune mechanisms to defend themselves from a multitude of pathogen infection strategies.

Necrotrophic pathogens can recruit a massive number of non-selective virulence factors including a series of cell wall degradation enzymes (CWDEs), effectors, ethylene inducing-like peptides, and toxins to disrupt reactive oxygen species (ROS) and trigger susceptibility in hosts2–4. Host resistance towards necrotrophs tends to be quantitative, depending on the plant’s ability to restrict or arrest invasion by the pathogen. The response of host plants to hemibiotrophic pathogens is more complex due to the presence of two distinct infectious phases. For example, Bos et al.5 reported that the hemibiotroph oomycete Phytophthora infestans could upregulate an AVR3a (a RXLR-WY effector) to bind potato resistance protein R3a, thereby suppressing the INF1-induced host cell death during the early biotrophic phase. When infection proceeded to the necrotrophic phase, this pathogen downregulated AVR3a and thus activated INF1 and Nep1-like proteins to accelerate plant cell death6. The host defense mechanisms against biotrophs can therefore act as either susceptibility or resistance factors against hemibiotrophs based on their infectious phases. The nuanced regulation of these mechanisms, including their timing and intensity, plays a crucial role in determining whether a plant will exhibit resistance or susceptibility to hemibiotrophic infections (reviewed by Yin et al.7).

In a field setting, crops are likely to be challenged by multiple destructive pathogens, so a useful approach for genetic improvement of these crops is to understand the broad-spectrum resistance mechanisms employed against the most prevalent pathogens causing severe epidemics8. In Arabidopsis, Sanchez et al.9 showed that bacterial rhamnolipids elicited defense responses involving salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, and ethylene pathways, which were specifically modulated in repsonse to hemibiotrophic Pseudomonas syringae, necrotrophic Botrytis cinerea and biotrophic Hyaloperonospora arabidopsidis. Similarly, antimicrobial peptides enhanced host protection to Fusarium solani (hemibiotroph) and Ascochyta pisi (necrotroph) in pea10,11. Gomez-Ariza et al.12 found that the accumulation of sucrose in rice increased host resistance not only against the hemibiotrophs Magnaporthe oryzae, Fusarium verticillioides and Erwinia chrysanthemi, but also to the necrotrophic Cochliobolus miyabeanus.

The plant immune response is a complex system involving gene collaborations that form co-expression networks to crosslink various defense mechanisms including pathogen recognition, signal transduction, change of cellular redox, initiation of the hypersensitive response, and secretion of antimicrobial compounds13. Variations within and among these gene co-expression networks could result in different resistance phenotypes, hence an investigation of these networks will increase our understanding of how synergistic gene expression patterns affect the level of host resistance14. For example, a recent study into the interaction among three Arabidopsis genotypes and 96 Botrytis cinerea isolates showed that pathogen sesquiterpene botryoidal and host camalexin regulatory networks were strongly and positively correlated in the resistant wildtype Col-0 genotype, but not in in the susceptible npr1-1 host, indicating that camalexin production played an important role in consistently strengthening the host’s defense system against these isolates of B. cinerea15. While the involvement of conserved pathways, such as the salicylic acid pathway, and their hallmark genes, such as NPR1 have been well established, information on gene networks among hosts with different levels of resistances are still scarce, and even more so for hosts challenged with different pathogens.

In this study, we adopted a gene co-expression approach to investigate transcriptomic dynamics among recombinant inbred lines (RILs) in the F9 generation derived from an intraspecific Lens ervoides population challenged with the economically important necrotrophic pathogens Ascochyta lentis (causal agent of asochyta blight) and Stemphylium botryosum (causal agent of stemphylium blight), and the hemibiotroph Colletotrichum lentis (causal agent of anthracnose). For each pathogen, the transcriptomes of the most resistant and susceptible RIL were analyzed before and after infection in a time course series using a weighed gene co-expression network approach (WGCNA) which is a powerful method to extract biologically meaningful information across treatments, timepoints, and hosts16. Gene co-expression networks were constructed for the resistant and susceptible RILs and compared to identify the most and least preserved networks. Results revealed partial overlap among some of the gene networks involved in microtubule, cell wall biosynthesis, cell division, and protein ubiquitination related biological processes between infected resistant and susceptible RILs. On the other hand, we also observed variable gene co-expression patterns between resistant and susceptible networks. It can be speculated that the stronger co-regulation of R genes and small RNA processes in the resistant hosts enhances resistance against A. lentis, C. lentis, and S. botryosum, whereas the higher level of synergistic synthesis in arginine and glutamine and phospholipid and glycerophospholipids in the susceptible hosts increases susceptibility against these pathogens.

Materials and methods

Plant and pathogen materials

L. ervoides accession L01-827a is a single-plant selection from accession IG 72847, which was categorized as L. orientalis by ICARDA. IG 72847 and IG 72815 are accessions of the ICARDA germplasm collection and were requested by the lentil breeding program of the Crop Development Centre, University of Saskatchewan, from ICARDA in the late 1990s. Permissions or licenses were not required. Both accessions have been used extensively in research on lentil genetics and genomics17–19. L. ervoides RILs were selected from an F9 recombinant inbred line population, LR-66, which was derived from a cross of L01-827a × IG 72815, based on their most contrasting resistance levels against Saskatchewan field isolates of A. lentis (isolate AL-61), S. botryosum (isolate SB-19), and C. lentis (isolate CT-30)20. For each pathogen, the most resistant (LR-66–629 for A. lentis, LR-66–637 for S. botryosum, LR-66–528 for C. lentis) and susceptible (LR-66–570 for A. lentis, LR-66–577 for S. botryosum, and LR-66–524 for C. lentis) RILs were selected.

Inoculation experiments

Four-week-old plants of RILs LR-66–629 (resistant to A. lentis) and LR-66–570 (susceptible to A. lentis) were inoculated with A. lentis at 5 × 105 conidia mL−1, RILs LR-66–637 (resistant to S. botryosum) and LR-66–577 (susceptible to S. botryosum) with S. botryosum at 1 × 105 conidia mL−1, and LR-66–528 (resistant to C. lentis) and LR-66–524 (susceptible to C. lentis) with C. lentis at 5 × 104 conidia mL−1, using approximately 2−3 mL per plant. Mock-inoculated plants of the same age and lines were sprayed with pure water. The two mock-inoculated RILs (LR-66–577 and LR-66–637) in the S. botryosum experiment and those in C. lentis experiment (LR-66–524 and LR-66–528) were sampled immediately after spraying. Mock-inoculated samples for the two RILs (LR-66–570 and LR-66–629) in the A. lentis experiment were collected 24 h post-spraying. For each RIL, four plants grown in the same pot were harvested together to represent a biological replicate. A total of three biological replicates were prepared for this study. All plants were maintained in growth chambers with constant temperatures of 21 °C (AL-61), 22 °C (CT-30), or 23°C (SB-19) based on the pathogen species, with a consistent 16-h photoperiod. For the initial 48 h post inoculation (hpi), all inoculated hosts were kept at 100% humidity to promote spore germination and penetration. After 48 h, inoculated plants were bagged to maintain the ambient humidity. Single leaflets of C. lentis-infected plants were collected at 24, 48, 72, and 96 hpi, of A. lentis-infected plants at 24, 96, and 192 hpi, and of S. botryosum at 48, 96, and 144 hpi, based on previous histopathological and qPCR results22,23,24. Mock inoculated samples were also collected. All samples were immediately immersed in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80°C for future uses.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

Transcriptome sequencing of the total 74 samples (Supplementary Table S1, Sheet: ‘exp_setup’) was previously completed21–23. Starting from inoculation, we assessed germination, host tissue penetration and the fungus/plant biomass ratio (estimated through RT-qPCR) over time until significant defoliation occurred. The earliest timepoints showing differences between resistant and susceptible RILs in the histopathological parameters or biomass ratios were selected for transcriptome sequencing. Briefly, RNA extraction was performed on deep-frozen samples using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Cat no. 74904, Qiagen Company, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of RNA samples was examined in an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only RNA samples with high quality (RNA integrity number > 7) were processed for library construction using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). High quality RNA libraries were fed into a HiSeq 2500 system using TruSeq SBS KIT-HS V4 (Illumina) for 125 bp pair-end sequencing at Canada’s Michael Smith Genome Sciences Center (BCGSC, Vancouver, BC, Canada).

Raw data processing

Raw data were submitted to Trimmomatic (version 0.36) for quality control by removing sequencing adaptors and low-quality reads under the following parameters: TruSeq3-PE-2.fa:2:30:10, leading:3, trailing:3, slidingwindow:4:15, and minlen:3624. In contrast to Cao et al.21 and Bawa et al.23 trimmed reads were aligned to the Lens ervoides genome IG 72815 (version 1.0), the paternal progenitor of these recombinant inbred lines (RILs), using the STAR software (version 2.6.1a, default settings) (https://github.com/alexdobin/STAR) (Supplementary Figure S1). This genome was retrieved from the KnowPulse database (https://knowpulse.usask.ca/genome-assembly/Ler.1DRT). Gene expression quantification in the format of raw counts was retrieved by using -quantmode parameter in STAR during the mapping process (Supplementary Table S1, Sheet: ‘raw count’). Gene expression outliers were defined as values falling more than two interquartile ranges (IQRs) below the first quartile (Q1) or above the third quartile (Q3). These outliers were replaced with ‘NA’ to exclude them from further analysis. To remove background noise introduced by lowly expressed genes in the subsequent analyses, we also exclude all genes that did not reach an average TMM value of 25 for all samples. After data cleaning, gene expression variability of samples was visualized with the t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) method.

Weighed gene co-expression analysis

The network construction for resistant and susceptible RILs was performed according to the following settings: A signed adjacency matrix was constructed using a soft threshold power of 20. Based on adjacency matrix, the corresponding topological overlap matrix (TOM) was calculated using the TOMsimilarity function in the WGCNA package. Hierarchical clustering using the flashcluster (method = ‘ward’) function was conducted to develop a gene tree based on the topological overlap dissimilarity matrix (1-TOM). A function of cutreedynamic (deepSplit = 4 and minClusterSize = 25) was used to identify network modules. If a correlation coefficient of 0.85 or greater was observed between modules, these modules were regarded as highly correlated in their gene expression trends and were therefore merged into a larger module. The value of eigengene (first principal component) was extracted to represent the overall expression trend of the corresponding module. To assess if the overall gene expression of a given module was significantly affected by inoculation process, we then performed one-way ANOVA to evaluate the effect of hpi on eigengenes for gene networks of resistant and susceptible RILs inoculated with A. lentis (mock, 24, 96, 192 hpi), C. lentis (mock, 24, 48, 72, 96 hpi), and S. botryosum (mock, 48, 96, 144 hpi). To identify the consensus part of networks between resistant and susceptible RILs, we performed multivariate Pearson’s correlation analysis on eigengene values of all modules between resistance and susceptibility networks. The significance of correspondence between a given module pair from two networks was assessed following the default settings as suggested by Langfelder et al.25 We adopted a balanced approach by setting the correlation threshold at 0.7 and the module size at 100 to efficiently identify relevant modules without excess noise or insufficient data. These genes were first converted to their known Medicago truncatula orthologs which were subsequently mapped to Gene Ontology (GO) database using the R package clusterProfiler (https://guangchuangyu.github.io/software/clusterProfiler/)(Supplementary Table S1, Sheet: ‘ID conversion’).

To further discern if gene co-expression networks in resistant hosts can be reproduced in susceptible hosts, we employed the network constructed from the resistant hosts as a reference network, recruited a resistance-centric susceptibility network developed from susceptible hosts as a test network (i.e., module and gene assignment in the susceptibility network was derived from the structure of resistance network), and compared the preservation between these two networks. The construction of the reference resistance network was configured with specific parameters: a soft threshold power of 20 for constructing the signed adjacency matrix to obtain a strong gene network with a scale-free fit index greater than 0.8, the ‘ward’ method for hierarchical clustering (flashClust function), settings of deepSplit = 4 and minClusterSize = 25 for module assignment (cutreeDynamic function), and a correlation threshold of 0.85 for merging modules with similar gene expression patterns. Subsequently, we also performed the same comparison in reverse order by using a network constructed from the susceptible hosts as a reference network to compare with a susceptibility-centric resistance network developed from the resistant hosts as a test network.

This susceptibility network was constructed using the same settings as the resistance network, with the only exception that the soft threshold power was adjusted to 30 to obtain a scale-free fit index greater than 0.8. The preservation indexes of zsummary, median rank, and module size were used to reflect the conservation level of modules between resistance and susceptibility networks25. A module that ranks within the bottom 10 in median rank and possesses a zsummary score below 10 is classified as a lowly conserved module. Qualified lowly preserved modules between networks were processed for GO analysis with the R package clusterProfiler and the top five hub genes with high intramodular connectivity in these modules were identified with the R package igraph (https://r.igraph.org/articles/igraph.html).

Results

Overview of transcriptional variability among samples

We first recruited t-SNE analysis to visualize the overall transcriptional variability among A. lentis-, C. lentis-, S. botryosum-, and water- (mock) treated samples. After data normalization using a TMM method and removal of lowly expressed genes and outliers, t-SNE analysis based on the remaining 10,004 genes clearly separated samples by different pathogen treatments (Fig. 1). As all water-sprayed samples from different experiments were clustered together, the impact of batch effect among experiment was considered small and we decided not to conduct batch normalization which carries the risk of confounding real biological difference. In addition, hosts inoculated with C. lentis exhibited a stronger temporal gene expression trend when compared with that of RIL-pathogen systems involving A. lentis or S. botryosum.

t-SNE analysis of temporal gene expression of resistant and susceptible Lens ervoides recombinant inbred lines inoculated with water (mock), Ascochyta lentis isolate AL-61 [24, 96, and 192 h post-inoculation (hpi)], Stemphylium botryosum isolate SB-19 (48, 96, and 144 hpi), or Colletotrichum lentis isolate CT-30 (24, 48, 72, and 96 hpi). Evidence for a temporal gene expression pattern is only seen for C. lentis challenged lines.

Gene co-expression network construction for resistant and susceptible RILs

After removing lowly expressed genes and outliers, gene co-expression networks were constructed from the 10,004 genes of resistant and susceptible RILs. As a result, we constructed 24 modules for the former and 25 modules for the latter. The eigengenes for these modules were extracted and subjected to ANOVA analysis to evaluate their responses to inoculation with AL-61, CT-30, and SB-19, comparing scenarios with and without the inclusion of mock samples (Fig. 2). Including mock samples allows for a better analysis of gene expression transitions from pre-infection conditions to active disease states, emphasizing the underlying biological processes from the healthy state to the response phase. On the other hand, excluding mock samples focuses on alterations in gene expression that occur after the activation of the disease response, directly evaluating the effects of pathogen exposure without reference to the pre-infection state. Exploring these scenarios revealed that inclusion of mock samples led to the identification of more significant responses for most modules compared to excluding mock samples across all host–pathogen systems analyzed. For samples exposed to CT-30, genes within all modules of both resistance and susceptibility networks exhibited stronger temporal responses than those challenged by AL-61 and SB-19. This marked reaction was consistent, whether mock samples were part of the analysis or not. The eigengene expression patterns for modules in both networks were then plotted in line graphs for a more comprehensive view (Supplementary Figure S2). After the inclusion of mock (pre-infection) samples in the analysis, a marked shift in eigengene values from the baseline established by the mock samples was observed for most modules in response to CT-30 infection. An initial expression baseline, represented by the mock samples, was typically followed by a significant deviation in eigengene values, which were either increased or decreased, indicating that gene expression was activated or repressed in response to CT-30. This pattern was contrasted with the responses to AL-61 and SB-19, where the shift from the mock baseline was generally less pronounced and prone to exhibit a more gradual change or lesser degree of deviation in eigengene values, suggesting that the response to these two pathogens was in a more moderate manner.

Bar plot of P values assessed by one-way ANOVA examining the effect of hours post inoculation (with mock and without mock) on host eigengene value for 24 modules in the resistance network (R) shown in the top panel and 25 modules in the susceptibility network (S) in the bottom panel. The plot shows results for post-inoculated samples (without mock samples) in green bars and mock- and post-inoculated samples in pink bars. A threshold of P value < 0.01 (indicated by black dotted line) is used to declare modules with significant temporal eigengene patterns. Inclusion of mock samples allows analysis from the healthy to the diseased status, while excluding them focuses on changes post-disease initiation.

Identification of highly correlated module pairs between resistance and susceptibility networks

The correlation analysis between eigengene values from modules in the resistance network and those in the susceptibility network identified seven module pairs of ‘S: lightcyan—R:cyan’, ‘S: lightcyan – R: darkmagenta’, ‘S: yellow – R: cyan’, ‘S: red – R: darkred, ‘S: brown – R: brown’, ‘S: tan – R: orange’, and ‘S: greenyellow – R:orange’, that concurrently met two selection criteria of R > 0.70 and no. common genes > 100 (Fig. 3). The genes in these modules exhibited high similarity in gene co-expression trends between hosts with differing levels of disease resistance.

Out of seven module pairs, three pairs including ‘S: lightcyan—R:cyan’, ‘S: lightcyan – R: darkmagenta’, ‘S: yellow – R: cyan’ consisted of genes that were mostly downregulated in hosts during the late infection process of all three pathogens (Fig. 4; Supplementary Table S2, Sheet: ‘module pairs’). In the ‘S: lightcyan—R:cyan’ and ‘S: lightcyan – R: darkmagenta’ module pairs, genes were significantly enriched in several GO biological processes related to cell division and cytoskeleton organization such as ‘nuclear division’, ‘microtubule cytoskeleton organization’, ‘regulation of actin filament polymerization’, and ‘regulation of cytoskeleton organization’ (Supplementary Table S2, Sheet: ‘GO_enrichment’). In the 'S: yellow – R: cyan’ module pair, genes showed significant enrichment in a variety of plant cell wall biological processes, including 'xylan metabolic and biosynthetic process’, 'plant-type cell wall organization and biogenesis’, and 'cell wall polysaccharide metabolic processes and biosynthesis’.

Individual gene expression within each consensus module pair, represented by z-scores, is visualized in heatmaps in the top panel. The overall expression level of each consensus module pair in the format of eigengene value is shown in the bottom panel. The corresponding Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment by these genes (FDR < 0.1) is shown in right panel.

In contrast, genes in the remaining four module pairs of ‘S: greenyellow – R: orange’, ‘S: tan – R: orange’, ‘S: brown – R: brown’, and ‘S: red – R: darkred’ displayed varying levels of responses to the pathogens, with some responses being similar between two pathogens and different from the third, indicating a complex pattern of gene regulation tailored to each type of infection (Fig. 5). In the 'S: greenyellow – R: orange’ and 'S: tan – R: orange’ module pairs, the gene expression response in these hosts inoculated with AL-61 and CT-30 exhibited a stronger upregulation trend than the gene expression response in other hosts challenged by SB-19. The genes in the former module pair were mainly enriched in protein modification processes, including ‘protein ubiquitination’ and 'protein modification by small protein conjugation’, whereas the genes in the latter module pair were significantly enriched in a variety of plant hypersensitive responses, such as 'response to external biotic stimulus’, 'programmed cell death’, and 'plant-type hypersensitive response’ (Supplementary Table S2, Sheet: ‘GO_enrichment’). In the module pair ‘S: brown – R: brown’, genes in hosts showed an upregulation trend during inoculation process of AL-61 and SB-19, a trend not observed in RILs infected with CT-30. Infection with AL-61 and SB-19 appeared to alter the hosts’ rhythmic regulation, as genes enriched in ‘rhythmic process’ and 'regulation of circadian rhythm’ were observed. In the analysis of the module pair 'S:red – R:darkred’, we observed a complex pattern of gene regulation in hosts infected by different isolates. Specifically, in hosts infected by CT-30, genes initially showed an upregulation followed by a subsequent downregulation. In contrast, genes in AL-61 infected hosts were exclusively downregulated. Both patterns contrasted to the consistent upregulation of genes observed in hosts challenged by SB-19, highlighting the distinct gene expression responses elicited by different infections. AL-61 and CT-30 seemed to impair the transcription process and chloroplast functioning in hosts, as evidenced by the enrichment of genes in the areas of ‘mRNA processing’, ‘chloroplast metabolic and biosynthetic processes’, and ‘chloroplast organization’.

Identification of lowly preserved modules between resistance- and susceptibility- centric networks

Using the gene co-expression network for the resistant RILs as the reference network, and then the module settings and assignments of the resistance network to construct the susceptibility network as the test network, we conducted a module preservation analyses to identify the lowly preserved gene co-expression modules between resistance and susceptibility networks. As this test was centered on the resistance network, module boundaries in a multidimensional scaling plot from the susceptibility network were more ambiguous than those from the resistance network (Supplementary Figure S3). The low-coherence modules in the susceptibility network should contain genes that did not preserve a strong gene co-expression relationship as observed in the resistance network. Based on zsummary values and median rank, it appeared that ‘yellowgreen’, ‘violet’, ‘sienna3’, and ‘skyblue3’ were the lowly preserved modules between networks (Fig. 6A; Supplementary Table S3, Sheet: ‘low preserved module’). As shown in gene expression line graphs, gene co-expression trends of ‘yellowgreen’, ‘violet’, ‘sienna3’, and ‘skyblue’ modules were evident in the resistance network, but ambiguous, as expected, in the susceptibility network (Supplementary Figure S5). Biologically, the stronger co-regulation of genes in these modules may contribute to the enhanced disease resistance against A. lentis, C. lentis, and S. botryosum. Querrying genes from each of these lowly preserved modules against the GO database revealed that two modules of ‘sienna3’ and ‘skyblue3’ possessed significant GO enrichment (Fig. 7A). In the ‘sienna3’ module, genes showed significant enrichment in meiotic cell processes, particularly in areas like 'chromosome organization involved in the meiotic cell cycle’. On the other hand, the ‘skyblue3’ module’s genes were primarily enriched in a range of small RNA processes, including 'snRNA and snoRNA processing’, 'ncRNA processing’, and ‘ncRNA metabolic process’. This module also exhibited notable enrichment in several processes related with plant-microbial interaction, such as 'response to other organism’, 'response to external biotic stimulus’, and 'biological processes involved in interspecies interaction between organisms’. We also identified the top five hub genes displaying the highest intramodular connectivity for each module (Supplementary Table S3, Sheet: ‘gene connectivity’). In the ‘sienna3’ module, the key genes Ler.1DRT.2g062600, a glutathione-conjugate transporter, and Ler.1DRT.6g061650, an L-type lectin-domain receptor kinase, should play pivotal roles in the network. Interestingly, two disease resistance genes (R-genes) (Ler.1DRT.4g012110 and Ler.1DRT.3g009520), were identified as central hub genes in the ‘skyblue3’ module, showing significant connections to both, plant pathogen interaction and the regulation of ncRNA and snRNA processes.

A reciprocal module preservation analyses with the susceptible network as the reference network and the resistance network as test network (module assignments in the resistance network were derived from the susceptibility network) (Supplementary Figure S4) revealed that five modules of ‘mediumpurple3’, ‘lightsteelblue1’, ‘thistle2’ ,‘plum2’, and ‘salmon4’ were the lowly preserved module between the two networks and contained genes that were stronger co-expressed in the susceptible RILs than in the resistant RILs (Figs. 6B and S5B; Supplementary Table S3, Sheet: ‘low preserved module’). Mapping genes in these modules against the GO database revealed that the modules ‘salmon4’ and ‘thistle2’ had significantly enriched GO processes (Fig. 7B; Supplementary Table S3, sheet: ‘GO_enrichment’). The significantly enriched GO processes of ‘glutamine family amino acid biosynthetic process’ and ‘arginine metabolic and biosynthetic processes’ in the module ‘salmon4’ and other significant GO terms of ‘phospholipid metabolic and biosynthetic processes’, ‘GPI anchor metabolic and biosynthetic processes’, and ‘glycerolipid metabolic and biosynthetic processes’ in the module of ‘thistle2’ suggested that the stronger co-regulation of these biological processes may enhance host susceptibility against AL-61, SB-19, and CT-30.

Discussion

Co-evolution between plant hosts and their pathogens has resulted in the development of a diverse set of infection strategies among pathogens to which their hosts have developed a variety of finely attuned defense mechanisms26. Using RILs of a L. ervoides population infected with either A. lentis, C. lentis or S. botryosum we showed through t-SNE analysis that differential transcriptome reprogramming events were triggered by these pathogens in the RILs. As expected for a hemibiotrophic pathogen, strong temporal gene expression dynamics were observed for RILs inoculated with C. lentis, reflected in the separation of transcriptomes that could be distinguished into three sub-groups based on incubation time. To further explore beyond the t-SNE findings, we then applied WGCNA to study gene co-expression of A. lentis, C. lentis, and S. botryosum-challenged L. ervoides RILs. This method has been used to group genes possessing a similar expression trend into modules and extract their main gene expression patterns (eigengenes)16. An eigengene that describes expression pattern of a module has been broadly incorporated into ANOVA to statistically associate this with various experimental factors27–29. The analysis demonstrated that eigengene values of most modules were consistently more affected by inoculation with CT-30 compared to AL-61 and SB-19, across all groups. This includes groups with both mock-inoculation and post-inoculation samples, as well as groups with only post-inoculation samples. From mock to 48 hpi, most modules in the CT-30 infected samples exhibited significant shifts in eigengene values, contrasting with the patterns observed in samples infected with AL-61 and SB-19, where the alterations were subtle. Previously, Bhadauria et al.30 observed that the biotrophic phase of C. truncatum isolate CT-21 extended up to 44 h in a lentil variety ‘Eston’, with a subsequent shift to necrotrophy taking place between 48 and 56 h post-inoculation. Should CT-30 and CT-21 share a similar life cycle in L. ervoides, the RILs in this study would initially be exposed to the biotrophic phase of CT-30, which can last up to 44 h. Based on this timeline, it is likely that the biotrophic phase of CT-30 activated a swift and strong immune response in hosts, explaining the significant gene expression alterations observed from mock to 48 hpi in samples infected with CT-30.

Our subsequent focus was on identifying both the shared and unique disease responses between the resistant and susceptible recombinant inbred lines (RILs). Analyzing the consensus gene co-expression patterns across networks of resistance and susceptibility enabled us to uncover disease responses common to both host types. Here, genes within three module pairs of 'S: lightcyan - R: cyan’, 'S: lightcyan – R: darkmagenta’, and 'S: yellow – R: cyan’ that were consistently downregulated in the late stages of infection, played roles in biological processes such as microtubule organization, cytoskeleton structure, cell division, and cell wall metabolism. This indicates that these critical biological functions in L. ervoides, irrespective of the resistance levels, were disrupted or stimulated by pathogens A. lentis, C. lentis, and S. botryosum, particularly during the necrotrophic phase. Such disruptions have been reported to lead to heightened plant susceptibility and to impede plant growth31–33. It is probable that these biological process modifications were triggered by broad-spectrum toxins produced by the pathogens, capable of affecting all lentil genotypes, irrespective of the level of resistance. It is known that Colletotrichum spp. can produce a toxic compound, colletotrichin, which causes rapid loss of membrane integrity and leads to anthracnose in various plant species, including tobacco, cucumber, and several nightshade species34. Similarly, Ascochyta spp. are capable of secreting ascochitine, which damages a range of legumes, such as faba bean, pea, lentil, and chickpea35. So far, it remains unclear whether these broad-spectrum toxins disrupt biological processes of lentil in the present study. If they do, attempts to modify these processes to counteract the toxins may not prove effective in enhancing plant resistance. However, not all genes exhibited similar expression patterns against AL-61, SB-19, and CT-30. Specifically, genes in the module pairs "S:red – R:darkred" exhibited a distinct response pattern: they were consistently downregulated in samples infected with AL-61, consistently upregulated in samples infected by SB-19, and showed an initial upregulation followed by downregulation in samples infected with CT-30. The variations in gene expression underscore the plant’s adaptive response to various pathogenic challenges and could therefore act as disease response signatures, enabling the identification of specific pathogen infections at the transcriptional level.

The other objective here was to test if there were gene co-expression modules that were highly cohesive in one network but not in the other network. The variation in gene co-expression networks has been documented to cause variation in disease resistance. In Arabidopsis, Zhang et al.15 discovered that the resistant wild-type and two susceptible mutants deficient in jasmonate and salicylic acid defense signaling exhibited varied gene co-expression networks in response to Botrytis cinerea. Our previous study in cross-kingdom interaction between Lens culinaris and S. botryosum also showed that resistant and susceptible lentil genotypes possessed distinctly different gene co-expression networks which interacted differentially with their in-planta pathogen gene expression networks36. Here, when the resistance network was used as the reference, we found that gene networks in four modules (‘yellowgreen’, ‘violet’, ‘sienna3’, and ‘skyblue3’) were not well retained in the susceptibility network, i.e. the co-expression of these genes was evident in the resistant RILs but ambiguous in the susceptible RILs. As these genes in ‘skyblue3’ were significantly enriched in ncRNA, snRNA, and interspecific interaction related biological processes, it appeared that the stronger co-regulation of these biological processes enhanced the broad spectrum of disease resistance against all three pathogens. More specifically, the identification of two hub genes (Ler.1DRT.4g012110 and Ler.1DRT.3g009520) as R genes seemed to suggest that a closer co-regulation of R genes, ncRNA and snRNA metabolic processes would be important to confer lentil resistance against AL-61, CT-30, and SB-19. In a review paper summarizing the recent discoveries on the interaction between small RNAs and R genes in multiple plant species including Glycine max and Medicago truncatula, both legume species like lentil, López-Márquez et al.37 reported that a range of small RNAs crucially regulate NB-LRRs in a network manner, ensuring precise dosage control essential to limit excessive host cell death while ensuring a sufficient level of pathogen developmental arrest, key to plant survival. In addition, the genes within the ‘skyblue3’ module of resistant RILs did not exhibit a significant gene expression change to respond with pathogen attack, suggesting that a non-responsive manner of these genes may contribute favorably to the improved resistance against the three pathogens. Given that necrotrophic pathogens are known to hijack the plant immune system by utilizing plant resistance genes to trigger a hypersensitive response and necrosis and thereby facilitating their growth in plant hosts, we hypothesize that the susceptible RILs inherently feature an R-gene – small RNA network that is less cohesive and more vulnerable to exploitation by these necrotrophic pathogens for their benefit38–40.

When the susceptibility network was used as reference, genes of the ‘salmon4’ and ‘thistle2’ modules formed stronger gene networks in the susceptible hosts than in the resistant hosts, and these genes were mostly overrepresented in metabolism and synthesis of glutamine, arginine, phospholipid, and glycerophospholipid. Glutamine and arginine, as amino acids, play multifaceted roles in support of immune responses through nitrogen assimilation, stress signaling pathway modulation, and synthesis of defense compounds41,42. Phospholipids and glycerophospholipids can also modulate plant disease resistance in regulating cell membrane structure, signaling molecule biosynthesis, and localized defense responses such as programmed cell death and reactive oxygen species production43–46. However, the extent to which genes in these biological processes collaborate and the degree of their cooperation in altering disease resistance levels in plants remain largely unknown. Even less is understood about the effects of these variations on different pathogens. Our data shows that a more uniform gene expression in these biological processes exacerbates susceptibility in lentil against AL-61, CT-30, and SB-19. One possible explanation for the distinct gene expression responses observed could be that the stronger co-regulation of genes within these modules creates an environment that is more conducive to the growth and spread of pathogens. However, more detailed investigations are required to deepen our understanding of this scenario.

One limitation of this study is the fact that only one susceptible RIL was compared to one resistant RIL for each host–pathogen system, which does not clearly allow separating gene expressions associated with general genotypic differences from those specific to the differences in resistance. As closely related lines of a recombinant inbred line population, we could determine a genetic similarity of 0.69 to 0.81 based on 5,000 SNPs distributed across the lentil genome, which is much higher than what would be expected in studies where plant varieties with different levels of resistance rather than RILs were compared. Due to the high level of genetic relatedness between the pairs of RILs, differences in traits other than disease resistance could not be discerned based on t-SNE distribution and visual assessments of their phenotypes. Including more resistant and susceptible RILs in two distinct pools and sampling them in a time-course manner with replicates could help mitigate such nuances; however, this was beyond the scope of this project. Nevertheless, as this study emphasizes the differential synchronization of gene groups during inoculation rather than comparing absolute expression levels of individual genes, any genotypic influence (other than that associated with resistance) of these expression differences on our findings is likely minimal.
