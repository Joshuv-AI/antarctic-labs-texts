Post-spinal cesarean hypotension
Authors: Maria Piscopo, Marco Cascella, Ornella Piazza
Published: 2026

With interest, we read the recently published study: “The influence of thirty-degree leg elevation on noradrenaline requirements administered as a prophylactic variable infusion during cesarean delivery, an open-label randomized controlled trial” [1], conducted at the Cairo University Hospital by Helmy et al. which addresses a very relevant topic, since post-subarachnoid anesthesia hypotension following cesarean section is a very common occurrence. The study was conducted between the 14th of March and the 8th of May 2025 and included 80 healthy pregnant women. The study results suggest that elevating parturient’s legs by 30 degrees is a safe, simple and non-pharmacological maneuver that can reduce the use of vasopressors without compromising maternal safety. Leg elevation was associated with a lower overall incidence of hypotension (20% vs 40%) and a substantial reduction in severe hypotension (2.5% vs 20%) compared to control group, further supporting its potential usefulness in routine clinical practice. We wondered about the physiological basis of the positive results of this maneuver. How can the legs elevation alone, without shifting the uterus to the left and without aortocaval decompression, improve the venous return and consequently the hemodynamic stability of patients?

Soap guidelines [2] and Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) Society guidelines [3] strongly recommend tilting the operating table or placing a traditional wedge under the right flank to displace the uterus to reduce vena cava compression. Vena cava compression not only impedes venous return to the heart, leading to hypotension, but also causes an increase in uterine venous pressure, which further reduces uterine blood flow. The left lateral tilt (typically achieved by elevating the right hip by 15°) is considered efficacious in increasing cardiac output and blood pressure. However, Massoth et al. [4] found that a 15° tilt to either side did not increase inferior vena cava volume significantly, while they observed that an angle of at least 30° left was needed to achieve maximal inferior vena cava volume.

Can the simple legs elevation without reducing the compression of the inferior vena cava and the aorta, increase the venous return and then the left-ventricular preload?

The 2016 American Society of Anesthesiologists Practice Guidelines for Obstetric Anesthesia [2] recommend phenylephrine, a selective α₁-adrenergic receptor agonist, as the first-line vasopressor for preventing and treating spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension during cesarean delivery in uncomplicated pregnancies without maternal bradycardia, because it is associated with better fetal acid-base status than ephedrine. Nevertheless, phenylephrine, while it effectively raises maternal blood pressure, may also constrict uterine arteries, and several comparative studies have shown that noradrenaline is as effective as phenylephrine in maintaining blood pressure and it is associated with an increase in heart rate and a consequent improvement in cardiac output [5]. The direct positive chronotropic effect of noradrenaline compensates for baroreceptor-mediated reflex bradycardia, maintaining heart rate and cardiac output. However, it should be noted that these data are the result of vasopressor infusions.

About the possibility of using noradrenaline and the leftward displacement of the uterus, we spotted an earlier paper, published in 2017 from Cairo University, by Hasanin et al. [6], correlating leg elevation with a lower incidence of post-spinal hypotension during cesarean section. In this study, leg elevation was 30 cm, achieved by placing two standard pillows to raise the legs by approximately 40 degrees. A significant reduction in the incidence of hypotension and in the intraoperative requirement for vasopressors was observed in the Leg Elevation (LE) group compared with the control (C) group. In the LE group, there was a 40.9% reduction in the incidence of hypotension, and a lower rate of intraoperative ephedrine administration: 34.7% in the LE group versus 58.7% in the C group. In this study, too, there was no leftward displacement of the uterus; indeed, the authors themselves recommended a future study combining leg elevation with the use of a wedge, and the replacement of ephedrine with noradrenaline. We consider the concentration of local anesthetics administered via spinal route, very relevant for reducing spinal complication too; 0.5% bupivacaine is preferred to 1%, due to its lower neurotoxicity, a practice that has been in place in Italy for almost three decades [7].

The issue of post-spinal hypotension following cesarean section was more recently revisited by Dr Helmy et al. themselves in a study conducted in July 2025 [8], just a few months after the JAACC paper we are commenting on; a trial was conducted on the administration of caffeine 30 min prior to cesarean section. Caffeine exerts mild stimulatory effects on the cardiovascular system by increasing circulating levels of catecholamines and antagonizing adenosine receptors, thereby promoting vasoconstriction. Administration of 200 mg of caffeine resulted in a pronounced reduction in the incidence of hypotension (9% vs 33%) and a reduction in the need for ephedrine. Caffeine alone, with the patient in the supine position and without uterine manipulation, reduced this complication and it was also associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of PONV (post-operative nausea and vomiting) and PDPH (post-dural puncture headache). The safety profile of caffeine for the newborn is unknown, despite its known ability to cross the placenta. Therefore, in our opinion, caffeine’s low cost and easy availability do not justify by themselves its indiscriminate clinical use, while wedge positioning can improve post-spinal hypotension without causing harm to the newborn, while being equally cost-effective and readily available.

We appreciate any effort toward the reduction of post-spinal hypotension, since pain during cesarean delivery is too often inadequately addressed, and subarachnoid anesthesia has still the lowest incidence of intraoperative pain during cesarean section with neuroaxial anesthesia [9]. Nevertheless, we wish that in any study concerning the choice of the best anesthesia practice, both maternal and neonatal outcomes are taken into consideration.
