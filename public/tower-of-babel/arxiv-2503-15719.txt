Phase transitions and finite-size effects in integrable virial statistical models
Xin An, Francesco Giglio, Giulio Landolfi

Abstract
We analyze thermodynamic models for fluid systems in equilibrium based on a virial expansion of the internal energy in terms of the volume density. We prove that the models, formulated for finite-size systems with N N particles, are exactly solvable to any expansion order, as expectation values of physical observables (e.g., volume density) are determined from solutions to nonlinear C-integrable partial differential equations (PDEs) of hydrodynamic type. In the limit N → ∞ N\to\infty , phase transitions emerge as classical shock waves in the space of thermodynamic variables. Near critical points, we argue that the volume density exhibits a scaling behavior consistent with the Universality Conjecture in viscous transport PDEs. As an application, we employ our framework to nuclear and quark matter, constructing a global quantum chromodynamics (QCD) phase diagram that reveals critical points for the nuclear liquid-gas transition and the hadron gas–quark-gluon plasma transition. We demonstrate how finite-size effects smear critical signatures, implying their potential impact on the search for the QCD critical point.

multiple critical points and distinct ground states. Variation of a thermodynamic function, such as temperature or pressure, can suppress one phase while promoting another, unveiling a rich interplay between competing phases

Virial-like statistical models.

Phase transitions are conveniently studied within the framework of statistical mechanics following the maximum entropy principle
[
17
]
.
This principle turns into an extremity problem for the proper entropic potential
\Psi
for a specific statistical ensemble, which is related to the system’s entropy
S
—the relevant potential in the
microcanonical ensemble
whose natural variables are a set of conserved quantities denoted by vector
\Lambda
—by Legendre transform
\Psi(\tau)=\sup_{\Lambda}\,[-S(\Lambda)+\tau\Lambda]
, where
\tau\Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\tau_{j}\Lambda_{j}
is a product of
J
conjugate pairs
\{\Lambda_{j},\tau_{j}\}
. The probability of any available microscopic state labeled by
i
is weighed as
p_{i}=e^{-\tau\Lambda^{(i)}}/{\mathcal{Z}}
,
normalized by the partition function associated with the potential
\Psi(\tau)
to ensure
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
:

\mathcal{Z}(\tau)=e^{-\Psi(\tau)}=\sum_{i}e^{-\tau\Lambda^{(i)}}\propto\int d\Lambda\,e^{S(\Lambda)-\tau\Lambda}\,,

where in the last step we have taken the continuum limit of the summation over all microscopic states to the integration over
d\Lambda=d\Lambda_{1}\dots d\Lambda_{J}
and dropped a multiplicative constant. Once the partition function for a given system is assigned, all thermodynamic properties follow
[
22
]
.

For a generic hard spheres fluid system, the microcanonical entropy (up to irrelevant constants) has the well-known form

S(E,V,N)=N\ln\left[\frac{V-Nb}{N}\left(\frac{E-U(V,N)}{N}\right)^{3/2}\right]\,,

(1)

which depends on its natural (extensive) variables such as internal energy
E

V

N

identifies the minimum volume density

where the virial expansion coefficients

2

The case

U
can be seen to account for the dependence of the averaged particles interaction upon their average distance
\bar{r}\sim v^{1/3}
, where

For the case considered in this Letter, where the energy

where its natural (intensive) variables,

In terms of standard pressure

from which the partition function is defined by

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(t,x)\equiv e^{-\mathcal{G}_{N}(t,x)}=\int dEdV\,e^{S(E,V,N)-tE-xV}\,.

(3)

Since

c(t)\propto t^{-3N/2}

(4)

where

\psi(t,x;v)=-s(v)+t\epsilon(v)+xv

Generically,

\langle O_{N}\rangle=\frac{1}{Z_{N}}\int dv\,e^{-N\psi(t,x;v)}O_{N}(v)\,.

(6)

Accordingly, we focus on the configurational entropy and potential energy density given by

s(v)=\ln(v-b)\,,\qquad\epsilon(v)=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{a_{j+1}}{jv^{j}}\,,

One key observation of our work is that the partition function (
4

(m+1)-

\left(\partial_{x}^{m}\partial_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(-N)^{j+1}a_{j+1}}{j}\partial_{x}^{m-j}\right)Z_{N}(t,x)=0\,.

(8)

Eq. (
8
) is singular in TL
N\to\infty
. It is therefore convenient to identify the differential problem for the free energy density
g_{N}
via Eq. (
4

B_{m}\partial_{t}g_{N}\\
+\,\sum_{j=1}^{m}B_{m-j}\,\left[\binom{m}{j}\partial_{x}^{j}\partial_{t}g_{N}+\frac{(-N)^{j}a_{j+1}}{j}\right]=0\,,

(9)

where
B_{n}=n!\sum_{\{\lambda^{(n)}_{\bf j}\}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(i!)^{j_{i}}j_{i}!}\left(-N\partial^{i}_{x}g_{N}\right)^{j_{i}}

is the

\lambda^{(n)}_{\bf j}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{n}\}
satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{n}ij_{i}=n
. When
n=0
, one has, by definition,
B_{0}=1
. Using Eqs. (
4
), (
5
) and (
6
), the averaged volume density and energy density are simply obtained via the derivative of
g_{N}
:

\displaystyle{\rm v}_{N}\equiv\langle v_{N}\rangle=\partial_{x}g_{N}\,,

(10a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{N}\equiv\langle\epsilon_{N}\rangle=\partial_{t}g_{N}\,,

(10b)

which allows us to convert Eq. (
9
) into a PDE for
{\rm v}_{N}
. It follows immediately from Eqs. (
10
) that
\partial_{t}{\rm v}_{N}=\partial_{x}\varepsilon_{N}
.

We emphasize that the PDE (
8
) for the partition function is linear for the whole family of statistical models admitting the arbitrary virial-like expansion (
2
). Since physical observables connect to the partition function by transformations involving variable changes and derivatives such as those in Eqs. (
10
), the whole family of statistical models under consideration is therefore
integrable
(more precisely
C-integrable

[
27
]
). In particular, the volume density at finite
N
is immediately obtained from the partition function via [cf. Eq. (
10a
)]

{\rm v}_{N}=-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{x}\ln{Z_{N}}\,,

known as Cole–Hopf-type transformation in the language of integrable systems
[
27
]
.

Multiple phases at large
N
.

Letting
g_{N}\to g,{\rm v}_{N}\to{\rm v},\varepsilon_{N}\to\varepsilon
in TL, and inserting it into Eq. (
9
), one obtains the identity
\varepsilon=\partial_{t}g=\epsilon(\partial_{x}g)=\epsilon({\rm v})
, where Eq. (
7
) is used. Applying Eqs. (
10
), one arrives at the following local conservation law:

\partial_{t}{\rm v}-\epsilon^{\prime}({\rm v})\,\partial_{x}{\rm v}=0\,.

(11)

This is the Maxwell closure relation for the Gibbs free energy in TL. Its general solution can be obtained via the method of characteristics, yielding
x+t\epsilon^{\prime}({\rm v})-f({\rm v})=0
where
f({\rm v})
is an arbitrary function that can be specified by assigning the Cauchy condition, as for instance, if a particular isothermal curve is known in some thermodynamic regimes [such as possibly known isotherms
x=f({\rm v})
in the regime of very high temperature]. Notice that the function
f
relates to the function
s
in Eq. (
5
) as
f({\rm v})=s^{\prime}({\rm v})
.
Indeed, by employing the
Laplace’s formula
to
Z_{N}
, we get

Z_{N}(t,x)\simeq\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\ell}}}e^{-N\psi_{\ell}}\,,

(12)

where the sum runs over the local minima of the potential
\psi
defined in Eq. (
4
),
\psi_{\ell}\equiv\psi(t,x;{\rm v}_{\ell})
, with
\ell
labeling the stationary points
{\rm v}_{\ell}
at which

C({\rm v};t,x)\equiv\partial_{\rm v}\psi=x+t\epsilon^{\prime}({\rm v})-s^{\prime}({\rm v})=0\,.

(13)

Evidently, Eq. (
13
) is just the implicit solution to Eq. (
11
) upon identification
f({\rm v})=s^{\prime}({\rm v})
. The implicit solutions given by
C({\rm v})=0
represent changes of the volume density of the nonlinear-wave type, which generically undergo a gradient catastrophe
[
28
,
29
]
that emerges at the inflection points (i.e., thermodynamic critical point)
[
10
]
, identified by the conditions

C({\rm v})=C^{\prime}({\rm v})=C^{\prime\prime}({\rm v})=0\,.

(14)

As discussed in
[
10
,
11
,
30
]
, thermodynamic critical points represent the onset of classical (viscous)
shock waves
[
28
]
where order parameters and/or their derivatives experience jumps, hence unveiling the occurrence of phase transitions.
However, when multiple solutions exist, the equation of state (EOS) does not contain information on which one is the most likely to occur. From a nonlinear wave viewpoint, this problem is equivalent to that of shock fitting, i.e., replacing a multi-valued solution with a discontinuous one (a shock wave), with the discontinuity located according to some specific entropic criteria (cf. Maxwell construction)
[
31
]
.

In TL, multiple solutions of EOS (
13
), associated with different thermodynamic phases, may exist. A criterion for identifying the favored solution discerns from Eq. (
12
), which for very large
N
yields

{\rm v}_{N}(t,x)\simeq\frac{\sum_{k}{\rm v}_{k}(t,x)(\psi^{\prime\prime}_{k})^{-1/2}\,e^{-N\psi_{k}}}{\sum_{\ell}(\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\ell})^{-1/2}\,e^{-N\psi_{\ell}}}\simeq\bar{\rm v}_{m}(t,x)\,,

(15)

where
\bar{\rm v}_{m}
is the solution to
\psi^{\prime}=0
that corresponds to the lowest minimum of potential
\psi({\rm v})
. Therefore, the phase diagram is identified by selecting the global minimum for each pair of
t
, and will display coexistence curves when two minima are resonant, that is when
\psi_{j}=\psi_{k}
for two distinct
{\rm v}_{j}
and
{\rm v}_{k}
.
Reconstructing coexistence lines
\mathcal{L}_{jk}\equiv\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\,|\,\psi^{\prime}({\rm v}_{j})=\psi^{\prime}({\rm v}_{k})=0,\,\,\psi({\rm v}_{j})=\psi({\rm v}_{k})\}
for pairs
j\neq k
, is mathematically equivalent to the problem of tracking trajectories of the underlying shock wave
[
10
,
30
,
17
]
. When
L
equally deep lowest minima exist, Eq. (
15
) implies that all of them contribute to the large
N
asymptotic solution for the volume density, with those exhibiting the flattest convex shapes in
\psi
contributing the most.
Indeed, from Eq. (
15
), we get
{\rm v}_{N}\simeq\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\gamma_{\ell}{\rm v}_{\ell}
as
N\to\infty
, where the
\gamma_{k}
’s are weights defined by
\gamma_{k}=(\psi^{\prime\prime}_{k})^{-1/2}/\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}(\psi^{\prime\prime}_{\ell})^{-1/2}
. Being single valued and continuous, this asymptotic solution gives a volume density that lies on the isothermal curve, representing an actual state of the system, differently from the case for multiphase systems
[
17
]
.

Universality near the critical points.

In the large but finite
N
limit, following Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
), one finds that the leading correction to the equation for volume density
{\rm v}_{N}
can be encoded into a
1/N
viscous term [cf. Eq. (

\partial_{t}{\rm v}_{N}-\epsilon^{\prime}({\rm v}_{N})\partial_{x}{\rm v}_{N}=-\frac{1}{2N}\partial_{x}\left[\epsilon^{\prime\prime}({\rm v}_{N})\partial_{x}{\rm v}_{N}\right]\,+h.o.t.,

(16)

where the higher order [
\mathcal{O}(1/N^{k})
with

and their derivatives.

The PDE for

arising from (

Finding the generalized Burgers equation (

Following

Conclusions.

We have developed a framework for discussing fluid systems whose internal energy can be written in the virial-type form (
2
). In particular, we have determined a linear PDE for the partition function, and
C
-integrable PDEs for the free energy and order parameter (volume density in the isothermal-isobaric ensemble). Gradient catastrophe phenomena and
singular solution behaviors, implying discontinuous phase transitions, are allowed in TL
N\to\infty
. Remarkably, phase transitions with discontinuities are instead prevented when finite
N
leading corrections are retained, and a behavior in agreement with the Universality Conjecture (
17
) is expected in the vicinity of a critical region. Furthermore, our approach leads to prediction of the volume density along the coexistence lines at finite
N
in terms of the thermodynamic potential around competing phases.

The formalism has then been implemented into the phase behavior of QCD, which remains poorly understood to date
[
35
,
42
,
46
]
. Our findings on finite-size effects lead to the conclusion that the search for the QCD critical point in experiments might be even more challenging than figured insofar. Indeed, these effects, varying with the system size, may obscure key characteristic signatures such as the enhanced fluctuations of conserved quantities.
Consequently, interpreting experimental data, including those from RHIC Beam Energy Scan program
[
47
]
, NA61/SHINE experiment
[
48
]
, and HADES experiment
[
49
]
, requires careful consideration of finite-size effects to ensure reliable conclusions.

Our formalism can be complemented by extending it to ensembles described by different thermodynamic variables, such as chemical potentials and magnetic field, and incorporated into hydrodynamic simulations for dynamical processes in heavy-ion collisions
[
50
]
. The consequence of complexity due to fluctuations in effective potential shall also be explored in a finite-size system
[
43
]
. We leave these for future work.
