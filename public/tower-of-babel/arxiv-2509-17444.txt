Filling in the Clinical Gaps in Benchmark: Case for HealthBench for the Japanese medical system


Abstract
This study investigates the applicability of HealthBench, a large-scale, rubric-based medical benchmark, to the Japanese context. While robust evaluation frameworks are crucial for the safe development of medical LLMs, resources in Japanese remain limited, often relying on translated multiple-choice questions. Our research addresses this gap by first establishing a performance baseline, applying a machine-translated version of HealthBench’s 5,000 scenarios to evaluate both a high-performing multilingual model (GPT-4.1) and a Japanese-native open-source model (LLM-jp-3.1). Second, we employ an LLM-as-a-Judge approach to systematically classify the benchmark’s scenarios and rubric criteria, identifying "contextual gaps" where content is misaligned with Japan’s clinical guidelines, healthcare systems, or cultural norms. Our findings reveal a modest performance drop in GPT-4.1 due to rubric mismatches and a significant failure in the Japanese-native model, which lacked the required clinical completeness. Furthermore, our classification indicates that while the majority of scenarios are applicable, a substantial portion of the rubric criteria requires localization. This work underscores the limitations of direct benchmark translation and highlights the urgent need for a context-aware, localized adaptation—a "J-HealthBench"—to ensure the reliable and safe evaluation of medical LLMs in Japan. Keywords: LLM Evaluation, Medical AI, Cross-Lingual Adaptation, LLM-as-a-Judge

1.  Introduction

Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have enabled powerful applications in complex, open-ended domains such as healthcare.
This progress, however, has simultaneously exposed the limitations of traditional automated metrics
Liu et al. 2016
;
Yeh et al. 2021
;
Fraile Navarro et al. 2025
and created a pressing need for more sophisticated methods to evaluate the quality and safety of LLM-generated text.
To meet this challenge, the field has increasingly shifted towards using LLMs themselves as evaluators, a paradigm known as LLM-as-a-Judge
Liu et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2023
.
This approach allows for reference-free assessment of qualitative aspects like coherence and relevance, which older metrics like BLEU or ROUGE could not capture
Schluter 2017
.

A key advancement within this paradigm is rubric-based evaluation
Hashemi et al. 2024
.
This method decomposes the complex, often subjective task of assessing quality into a set of specific, fine-grained criteria defined by human experts.
This approach offers two major advantages.
First, it provides detailed, multi-dimensional feedback on specific strengths and weaknesses of a response.
Second, by aggregating these criteria, it enables the calculation of a robust overall score, allowing for fair and consistent comparison across different models and outputs.

The need for such robust evaluation is particularly acute in the medical domain, where LLMs are expected to broaden access to reliable health information and to support healthcare professionals in clinical decision-making
Singhal et al. 2025

The HealthBench benchmark
Arora et al. 2025

It evaluates LLM responses to realistic, multi-turn medical conversations using physician-defined rubrics, which is specific, contextual criteria developed by a large cohort of medical experts to assess aspects like clinical accuracy, communication quality, and patient safety.
By employing a detailed scoring mechanism with both additive and subtractive points, HealthBench prioritizes realism and trustworthiness in its evaluation, moving beyond simple right-or-wrong assessments.

The need for comprehensive evaluation frameworks is also acute in Japan, where the availability of high-quality Japanese medical evaluation datasets remains limited.
The only large-scale original resource is IgakuQA
Kasai et al. 2023
, based on Japanese medical licensing exam questions, while recent benchmarks such as JMedBench
Jiang et al. 2025
rely predominantly on datasets translated from English.

However, this translation-based approach faces inherent limitations, as it may fail to fully capture local context, cultural nuances, or Japan-specific clinical standards, such as differences in approved pharmaceuticals, healthcare resource allocation, and regional disease prevalence..
Despite these challenges, constructing a large-scale, physician-annotated, and comprehensive Japanese medical evaluation benchmark from scratch is a significant undertaking that is not easily achieved in the short term.
Thus, a pragmatic interim approach is to leverage machine translation and systematically analyze its limitations as a step toward building more localized resources.

This work serves not as a definitive solution, but as a data-driven call to action, highlighting an immediate and pressing need for Japan-centric adaptations of robust evaluation frameworks such as HealthBench, essential for the safe and effective integration of LLMs into Japanese healthcare contexts.

2.2.  Methodological Trends in LLM Evaluation: Towards Rubric-Based Assessment

Traditional multiple-choice question or reference-based metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE are efficient and scalable, making them popular in medical benchmarks
Jin et al. 2019
;
Jin et al. 2020
;
Kim et al. 2024
.
However, these metrics are inadequate for evaluating LLMs in realistic usage scenarios, particularly in open-ended dialogue or medical advice
Abbasian et al. 2024
;
Fraile Navarro et al. 2025
.
In practical settings, LLMs function as chatbots or assistants, generating free-text responses rather than selecting an answer from a fixed list
Arias-Duart et al. 2025
.
Such formats demand evaluation of qualities like semantic depth, coherence, accuracy, empathy, and adherence to patient safety level metrics.

To address these shortcomings, the field has increasingly turned to using LLMs themselves as evaluators:
G
-
Eval
Liu et al. 2023
employs GPT
-
4 in a chain-of-thought, form-filling approach to perform absolute (reference-free) assessment of generated text.
It significantly improves correlation with human ratings in summarization and dialogue tasks by semantically evaluating content rather than relying on overlap metrics.
Zheng et al
Zheng et al. 2023
demonstrated that GPT
-
4 as an evaluator achieves over 80% agreement with human preferences in chatbot benchmarking (MT-Bench, Chatbot Arena), showcasing that LLMs can operate as reliable judges rather than answer generators .
These methods support scalable, reference-free evaluation and reflect real-world LLM use cases more faithfully than multiple-choice questions.

However, as LLM-as-a-Judge techniques have gained popularity, their limitations have become apparent. Challenges such as bias, unreliability, and inconsistency have been identified in recent studies
Saito et al. 2023
;
Park et al. 2024
;
Zheng et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
.
Recent developments in LLM evaluation frameworks
Gu et al. 2025
have emphasized the need to move beyond single-dimensional or holistic assessments with issues of ambiguity and potential bias in LLM-as-a-Judge systems..
This has led to the adoption of multi-dimensional, rubric-based evaluation, where complex tasks are decomposed into fine-grained criteria reflecting different aspects of response quality.

A central challenge in rubric-based evaluation is how to aggregate or calibrate these detailed criteria into a single, interpretable overall score that is comparable across responses and systems.

Different frameworks address this in distinct ways:
Hashemi et al.
Hashemi et al. 2024
propose a calibrated approach, in which an LLM (or a lightweight neural model) learns to predict overall user satisfaction by integrating multiple rubric dimensions, such as naturalness, coherence, and citation quality.
Their model is trained to align the aggregated rubric scores with human overall judgments, thereby ensuring that the final score is both reliable and human-aligned.

In contrast, HealthBench
Arora et al. 2025
employs a system where conversation-specific rubrics are created by domain experts.
Here, domain experts define a set of additive or subtractive rubric criteria for each scenario.
The model or evaluator judges whether each criterion is satisfied, with the total score calculated by summing the points for met criteria and normalizing by the maximum possible positive score.
This mirrors traditional manual scoring in professional and educational contexts, where each fulfilled criterion directly contributes to the overall score.

This process not only enables nuanced and multidimensional evaluation but also ensures that results are actionable, comparable and aligned with human professional, whether for benchmarking LLMs or providing feedback in real-world applications.

In summary, there is a pressing need to develop Japanese datasets with detailed, expert-defined evaluation criteria, enabling systematic comparison between LLM-generated responses and expert judgments.
As a first step toward this goal, our study investigates the feasibility and limitations of a translation-based approach using HealthBench, adapting its comprehensive rubric framework for the evaluation of Japanese medical LLMs.

3.  Method

Our methodology is designed to systematically investigate the applicability of the international HealthBench benchmark to the Japanese medical context.
The approach comprises two primary objectives: first, to establish a performance baseline for leading Japanese-language Large Language Models (LLMs) using a translated version of the benchmark; and second, to systematically identify and classify the contextual gaps that limit its direct application.

3.1.  Core Framework: Adapting HealthBench for Japan

Our research is built upon the HealthBench framework
Arora et al. 2025
, a large-scale benchmark designed to evaluate LLMs in realistic healthcare scenarios.
Unlike traditional multiple-choice exams, HealthBench assesses free-form responses to 5,000 multi-turn conversations and evaluates them using rubric-based criteria defined by physicians.
This system uses a detailed scoring mechanism with both additive and subtractive points to capture the nuances of response quality, including clinical accuracy, communication, and patient safety.
We selected HealthBench as our foundational framework due to its comprehensive scope and its focus on trustworthiness in a high-stakes domain.

To apply this framework to the Japanese context, we employed a machine translation-based approach as a pragmatic first step.
We uniformly translated the multi-turn conversation scenarios for all 5,000 examples into Japanese using GPT-4.1

While this direct translation has inherent limitations, it allows us to establish an initial performance baseline and systematically investigate the contextual gaps, which is a central goal of this study.

3.1.1.  Performance Evaluation of Japanese LLMs

The first phase of our methodology aims to establish a quantitative performance baseline for Japanese-language LLMs.
This evaluation provides an initial measure of how these models perform on a broad range of medical scenarios when presented in Japanese.

The evaluation framework involves a cross-lingual assessment. Japanese LLMs are prompted with the translated Japanese conversation scenarios to generate responses in Japanese.
Subsequently, these Japanese responses are graded against the original English rubric criteria.
This approach is consistent with the HealthBench methodology, which applied a common set of English rubrics to its multilingual scenarios to ensure a standardized evaluation.
The final score for each response is calculated according to the standard HealthBench methodology, which sums the additive and subtractive points awarded for each rubric item and normalizes the result.

3.1.2.  Identifying Contextual Gaps

The second phase of our methodology aims to systematically identify and classify conversation scenarios within HealthBench that are poorly suited to the Japanese clinical, cultural, and systemic context.
To achieve this, we developed a three-tiered classification framework to categorize the applicability of each scenario.
This classification is crucial for understanding the nature and extent of the benchmark’s limitations.
The classification framework consists of the following three categories:

•

Directly Applicable
: Scenarios based on universal medical principles and globally consistent standards of care.
They can be used directly in the Japanese context with little to no modification.

•

Applicable with Adjustments
: Scenarios where the core medical or ethical principle is sound, but specific details require localization.
Adjustments may include updating regional information (e.g., hotlines) or adjusting for epidemiological or cultural differences.

•

Inapplicable
: Scenarios that are fundamentally incompatible with the Japanese context due to systemic, legal, or clinical guideline differences (e.g., billing codes, prescription laws). These require a complete redesign to be applicable.

This classification serves a dual purpose.
First, it enables a qualitative analysis to identify recurring themes of contextual mismatch within the "Inapplicable" and "Applicable with Modifications" categories.
Second, by partitioning the dataset into these three subsets, we can perform a more nuanced quantitative analysis.
This allows us to investigate the extent to which any observed performance degradation in Japanese LLMs is attributable to genuine capability gaps versus the inherent limitations of a non-localized, cross-lingual benchmark.

Unlike the original paper which clipped scores to a [0, 1] range, we report the raw scores, including any negative values.

5.1.  Performance Comparison

We evaluated the performance of GPT-4.1 on both the original English HealthBench tasks and their translated Japanese equivalents, alongside a leading open-source Japanese model, LLM-jp-3.1.

The results for GPT-4.1 reveal a consistent, though modest, performance gap between the two languages.
As shown in Table
2
, the overall score for the Japanese responses was 0.446, a decrease from the 0.479 achieved by the English responses.
This performance reduction was observed across all seven thematic categories. The largest performance gaps were identified in "Global health" (0.398 for English vs. 0.338 for Japanese) and "Context seeking" (0.377 for English vs. 0.329 for Japanese).

In stark contrast, the performance of LLM-jp-3.1 was substantially lower, achieving an overall score of -0.062.
As detailed in Table
2
, this model yielded negative average scores across all thematic categories, indicating that its responses were, on average, graded as more harmful or incorrect than helpful.

A breakdown of the scores by evaluation axis, presented in Table
3
, provides further insight.
For GPT-4.1, the most significant degradation in the translated version was observed in "Instruction following" (a drop from 0.631 to 0.395) and "Communication Quality" (from 0.762 to 0.586).
In contrast, the "Accuracy" axis remained relatively stable (0.604 to 0.536).
For LLM-jp-3.1, performance was extremely low across all axes, with a notably negative score in "Completeness" (-0.223).
This suggests that the model’s responses were not merely incomplete but were often penalized for providing harmful or irrelevant information, which triggered subtractive rubric points.

5.2.  Contextual Gaps and Applicablity to Japan

To investigate the potential reasons for the performance gaps observed, we performed an LLM-based classification of the benchmark’s content.
The results of this classification, which categorized each of the 5,000 scenarios based on their applicability to the Japanese medical context, are summarized in Table

Our analysis found that while the majority of scenarios, 4,106 out of 5,000 (82.1%), were deemed Directly Applicable, a substantial portion required intervention.
Specifically, 716 scenarios (14.3%) were classified as "Applicable with Adjustments," indicating a need for localization of specific details.
Furthermore, 133 scenarios (2.7%) were found to be "Inapplicable," as their core premises are fundamentally incompatible with the Japanese healthcare system.

In total, these findings highlight that 849 scenarios—nearly 17% of the benchmark—contain elements that do not directly align with the Japanese context.
This provides a quantitative basis for understanding the performance degradation observed and underscores the challenges of cross-lingual benchmark application.

6.  Discussion

Our study explored the applicability of the HealthBench benchmark to the Japanese medical context by evaluating LLM performance on translated scenarios. The results yielded three primary findings. First, a modest but consistent performance degradation was observed for GPT-4.1 when responding to Japanese translations compared to the original English scenarios. Second, the open-source Japanese model, LLM-jp-3.1, exhibited significantly poor performance, with an overall negative score, suggesting its responses were often counterproductive. Third, our analysis of the benchmark itself revealed that a substantial portion of the scenarios (nearly 17%) contain contextual gaps, categorized as either requiring adjustments or being fundamentally inapplicable to the Japanese healthcare system. This chapter discusses the interpretation and implications of these findings.

6.1.  GPT-4.1’s Performance Gap

The analysis by evaluation axis (Table

The resilience of the Accuracy score suggests that GPT-4.1 retains its core medical knowledge when processing and responding in Japanese.
However, the sharp decline in Instruction following and Communication Quality indicates a critical mismatch between the Japanese LLM’s response patterns and the expectations embedded in the original English rubrics.
The model may generate clinically correct information, but fail to structure it or phrase it in the specific manner that the English-language criteria demand.

Although the original HealthBench scenarios were designed for multilingual contexts, our uniform translation into Japanese appears to have created a subtle disconnect between the nuances of the Japanese responses and the original English rubric criteria.
Because the grader model evaluates responses by interpreting these English rubrics literally, it often flagged appropriate, nuanced Japanese responses as problematic simply because they differed from the strict wording of the criteria.

This suggests that the observed performance gap is less about a decline in clinical reasoning and more about the friction between localized advice and a literal interpretation of global evaluation standards.

6.2.  Interpreting the Performance of a Japanese-Native LLM

The strikingly poor performance of LLM-jp-3.1 warrants a deeper analysis.
A qualitative review indicates that the model generally succeeded in understanding the translated scenarios and generating relevant responses. The issue does not appear to be a fundamental failure of comprehension.

This is strongly reflected in its extremely low score on the "Completeness" axis (-0.223). While the model may lack sufficient medical knowledge in some areas, its core failure is in providing holistic, safety-critical information.

6.3.  Contextual Gaps in HealthBench

Our LLM-based classification of the 5,000 scenarios and their rubric criteria provides critical insight into the nature of the contextual gaps within HealthBench.
This classification sorted both scenarios and rubric criteria into three distinct categories: These are ’Directly Applicable’, alongside those requiring ’Adjustments’ and those deemed ’Inapplicable’.

The "Applicable with Adjustments" category includes cases that, while medically sound in principle, contain specific details misaligned with the Japanese context.
At the scenario level, this includes references to diseases that are epidemiologically rare in Japan, such as Cystic Fibrosis.
At the rubric criteria level, the underlying medical principles are often sound, but the specific details are misaligned; for example, a criterion might require providing a U.S. or Russian hotline number for a user with suicidal ideation.
Although these issues could be resolved with content replacement, making them unusable for evaluating Japanese responses in their current form.

In contrast, the "Inapplicable" category contains scenarios and criteria that are fundamentally incompatible with the Japanese healthcare system.
At the scenario level, this includes situations premised on foreign medical or insurance systems, such as U.S. Medicare billing. More critically, at the rubric criteria level, this category includes principles that are medically inappropriate or dangerous in Japan.
More critically, at the rubric criteria level, this category includes standards unacceptable under the Japanese medical system.
For example, as illustrated in Table
6
, it rewards dangerous advice such as recommending the unsupervised use of a potent, prescription-only steroid (clobetasol) for psoriasis.

Finally, it is crucial to emphasize that our classification also confirms that the majority of the benchmark is categorized as ’Directly Applicable’.
These scenarios are built on universal medical and ethical principles that are not contingent on specific national systems.
A prime example is a consultation for pre-travel vaccinations, where the advice to consider destination-specific vaccines and consult a specialized travel clinic is a global standard of care that aligns perfectly with clinical practice in Japan.
Thus, despite the identified gaps, HealthBench contains a substantial core of high-quality scenarios that can be used without modification, underscoring its value as a foundational resource for evaluating medical LLMs in Japan.

6.4.  Toward J-Healthbench

Our findings offer several critical implications for the development and deployment of medical LLMs, particularly within non-English contexts like Japan.

Our findings underscore that direct machine translation of the senarios is insufficient for adapting localized context.
It is essential to have a systematic process for localising rubric criteria in order to align them with local clinical guidelines, legal regulations and cultural norms.
Without such a process, the ’contextual gaps’ between the benchmark and the local context will prevent a proper evaluation of a model’s true capabilities.

Frameworks like HealthBench are valuable in defining the necessary capabilities for conversational clinical AI. As similar AI tools are expected to become more prevalent in Japan, a strategic discussion is necessary to first define how they will be integrated into the Japanese clinical workflow. Only after such a discussion can the specific scenarios and evaluation criteria for a Japan-original HealthBench be effectively designed. This is where our analysis of contextual gaps becomes critical, as it provides a roadmap for distinguishing which international standards can be adapted and where uniquely Japanese criteria must be developed.

Furthermore, this study served as an early test of the HealthBench framework itself. Its LLM-as-a-Judge approach, guided by detailed rubric criteria, enables stable evaluation that reflects expert-defined priorities. Crucially, this method moves beyond assessing factual accuracy to evaluate what must not be said and what cannot be omitted, using subtractive scoring to penalize critical failures in safety and completeness. This nuanced evaluation addresses a critical dimension of LLM performance not covered by existing benchmarks.

7.  conclusion

This study investigated the applicability of the international medical benchmark, HealthBench, to the Japanese context through a multi-faceted evaluation.

Our analysis yielded three principal findings: (1) The high-performing multilingual model, GPT-4.1, exhibited a modest but consistent performance degradation on Japanese-translated scenarios, primarily due to contextual gaps between culturally adapted responses and the literal interpretation of the original English rubrics.

(2) The Japanese-native open-source model, LLM-jp-3.1, performed poorly not from a lack of comprehension, but from an inability to provide the comprehensive, safety-critical advice demanded by the benchmark.

In conclusion, this research demonstrates that adopting a sophisticated, high-stakes benchmark like HealthBench by simply translating its scenarios is insufficient for its effective deployment in a new linguistic and cultural context.

The subtle yet critical discrepancies in clinical norms, healthcare systems, and patient communication can lead to an inaccurate assessment of an LLM’s true capabilities and safety.

Therefore, our work underscores the urgent need for a context-aware adaptation of such frameworks.

The path toward a reliable evaluation of medical LLMs in Japan—and likely other non-English speaking regions—requires a strategic effort to first define the intended role of AI in the local clinical workflow, and then to develop or adapt benchmarks accordingly.
While HealthBench provides an invaluable foundation with its robust, rubric-based methodology, realizing its full potential in Japan necessitates the development of a localized "J-HealthBench," thoughtfully adapted to the nation’s unique medical landscape.

8.  limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

First, the HealthBench framework is computationally expensive and time-consuming. This constrained our study to a single evaluation run per question-answer pair, which limits the analysis of result variance and reproducibility, in contrast to the multiple runs performed in the original paper.

Second, our methodology relied on automated processes without validation by Japanese medical experts. The conversation scenarios were machine-translated, which may not have perfectly captured all clinical nuances. Similarly, the LLM-as-a-Judge approach used for both performance grading and contextual gap classification was not cross-validated by human experts, introducing potential for automated biases.

Finally, our evaluation of Japanese-native models was limited to a single open-source model. The findings for LLM-jp-3.1 may not be generalizable to all Japanese LLMs, including other commercial or open-source alternatives.
