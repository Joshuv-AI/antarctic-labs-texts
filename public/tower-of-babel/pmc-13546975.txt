Commercial Payors More Likely to Reimburse Unlisted Hip Arthroscopy Procedures Compared With Managed Care but With Similar Overall Dollar Amount
Authors: Eric V. Neufeld, Matthew Zinner, Michael J. Meade, Randy M. Cohn, Andrew D. Goodwillie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the reimbursement practices of public managed care (MC) insurance versus private commercial insurance (COM) for hip arthroscopy using the current procedural terminology (CPT) code 29999.MethodsThe CPT codes and corresponding reimbursements of hip arthroscopies that employed code 29999 performed from January 2020 to December 2024 in a multihospital health system were analyzed. Patients’ insurance carriers were identified and grouped into either MC or COM. MC consisted of Medicaid and Medicare. The outcomes analyzed were as follows: whether any reimbursement was obtained, total dollar amount paid, percentage of the billing that was reimbursed, and the percentage of CPT 29999 charge that was reimbursed.ResultsOne hundred thirty‐seven hip arthroscopies met the inclusion criteria. COM fulfilled at least part of the reimbursement (i.e., not completely deny the entire claim) in 100% of cases, whereas MC provided at least some payment in 94.1% of claims (P = .02). However, there was no significant difference in the total dollar amount ($3191.18 vs $3563.02; P = .109) or percentage of the total charge (20.0% vs 21.7%; P = .220) reimbursed by MC relative to COM, respectively. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the percentage of CPT code 29999 reimbursed between MC and COM (25.0 vs 21.2%; P = .185).ConclusionsCOM was less likely to completely deny a claim relative to MC and reimbursed approximately 10% higher than MC. However, there was no significant difference between payors in the percentage of the billing that was reimbursed or the percentage of reimbursement specifically for CPT code 29999. Regardless of insurance type, there is a low percentage of reimbursement for unlisted hip arthroscopy procedures.Clinical RelevanceAs hip arthroscopy becomes more common, it is important to understand reimbursement patterns to ensure patients receive appropriate care and surgeons are compensated. This study provides insights that help surgeons navigate these dynamics and deliver high‐quality care, regardless of a patient's insurance provider.

Over the past 2 decades, the prevalence of hip arthroscopy has risen substantially. From 2015 to 2023 alone, the number of hip arthroscopies in the United States increased by 97%. 1 In 2011, this rapid growth led to the addition of dedicated current procedural terminology (CPT) codes for labral repair, femoroplasty, and acetabuloplasty. Within 3 years, the number of procedures billed using these newly introduced “expanded codes” nearly doubled the traditional codes (i.e., CPT 29860‐29863). 2 From 2011 to 2018, the use of hip arthroscopy for femoroacetabular impingement syndrome alone increased by 85%—marking a continuation of the growth witnessed in preceding years. 3 , 4 This rapid development underscores the difficulties billing infrastructure faces to maintain pace with medical advances. Despite the additional hip arthroscopy codes added in 2011, improvements in imaging capabilities and surgical techniques have expanded the indications for hip arthroscopy. The lag time between the development of new surgical techniques and CPT codes forces many surgeons to bill for unlisted procedures, such as repair of gluteus medius or minimus tears, using the generic CPT code 29999 which may not adequately capture the procedure performed. 5

Another crucial element in surgeon compensation is the difference in reimbursement rates between public managed care (MC) plans—consisting of Medicare and Medicaid—versus commercial payers (COM). The reimbursement rates for MC plans are established by the Center for Medicare and Medicaid services. Medicare and Medicaid are the primary public health care options in the United States and together provide insurance for 40% of Americans. 6 Unfortunately, the reimbursements for these patients may not be adequate, with 67% of practices reporting MC rates did not cover the cost of providing care to the beneficiaries. 7 In addition to setting the reimbursement for these patients, there is strong evidence showing that private COM reimbursement is directly linked to the rates set by MC plans. 8 This has led to orthopaedic surgeons making 38% less than they did in 2000 while accounting for inflation. 9 This shows why it is crucial that surgeons understand the details of how they are compensated, yet, regardless of payor, the intricacies of coding and billing are often poorly understood by the average orthopaedic surgeon. 10

The purpose of this study was to compare the reimbursement practices of MC versus COM for hip arthroscopy using the CPT code 29999. We hypothesized that COM was more likely to reimburse for hip arthroscopy, would reimburse at a higher rate, and would be more likely to reimburse for unlisted procedures.

METHODS

The CPT codes and corresponding reimbursements of hip arthroscopies performed between January 2020 and December 2024 at a large, multihospital academic health system in New York were analyzed. The inclusion criteria featured all patients who underwent a hip arthroscopy procedure using the CPT code 29999. All claims that contained this CPT code were included in this study. There were no exclusion criteria. Definitions of each CPT code are denoted in Table 1. The charges for each procedure were obtained from the hospital billing department. The distinct insurance carriers were recorded and grouped into either MC n = 51 or COM n = 86 per the health system's financial operations department. MC and COM were comprised of 14 and 30 distinct carriers, respectively. The outcomes analyzed were as follows: whether any reimbursement was obtained, total dollar amount paid, percentage of the total billing charges that were collected, and the percentage of CPT code 29999 charges that were reimbursed (regardless of whether any other portion of the claim was reimbursed). Each claim was reviewed by a dedicated billing team to ensure payment was accurate relative to the contract. Claims were considered unpaid after the maximum number of appeals was exhausted. Statistical analysis was performed in SPSS version 22 (IBM, Armonk, NY) using χ 2 and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests followed by a Bonferroni correction to control the familywise error rate. Normality was first assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk tests. Continuous data are presented as median and interquartile range given their significant deviation from normality.

RESULTS

One hundred thirty‐seven patients met the inclusion criteria during the time period studied. No patients were excluded. There were 51 patients in the MC group and 86 in the COM group. The MC and COM groups were comprised of 14 and 30 distinct carriers, respectively. A summary of all procedures performed during the study period is provided in Table 2. Outcomes from between‐group comparisons are depicted in Table 3. COM was more likely to reimburse some dollar amount for CPT code 29999 (i.e., not entirely deny the claim) compared with MC (100% vs 94.1%; adjusted residual 2.3, P = .023). MC reimbursed $3191.18 (1742.85) per claim which is 10.4% less than $3563.02 (2727.16) from COM, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .109). There was no difference in the percentage of the total charge (20.0% vs 21.7%; P = .220) reimbursed by MC relative to COM, respectively. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the percentage of CPT code 29999 reimbursed between MC and COM (25.0 vs 21.2%; P = .185).

DISCUSSION

In this study, COM reimbursed at least some portion of the charge in 100% of cases. In contrast, MC fulfilled at least part of the reimbursement 94.1% of the time; that is, there was $0 reimbursed in just under 6% of cases. Additionally, MC reimbursed over 10% less than COM, although this difference did not reach statistical significance.

This absolute difference in total dollar amount reimbursed for patients with COM versus MC is in contrast with other studies that found significantly higher reimbursement for COM. 9 , 12 A previous analysis reported that COM provided higher overall payments and were more likely to be billed traditional codes, whereas CPT code 29999 was more likely to be associated with Medicare. 2 Because reimbursements can vary significantly between hospitals and states or regions, it is difficult to ascertain the precise reason for this finding. It is possible that the healthcare system in this investigation has different contracts and established payment systems with MC compared with the hospitals featured in earlier studies.

It is well known that patients with both Medicare and Medicaid are more likely to experience difficulty accessing orthopaedic surgeons. 13 , 14 , 15 One of the most cited reasons for the relative paucity of orthopaedic providers accepting MC is the decreased physician reimbursement. 15 Although our results suggest there is a 10% difference in overall reimbursement between MC and COM, this is a much smaller difference than that seen with other procedures. In a recent study of common procedures across orthopaedic subspecialties Medicare reimbursed 57% less than commercial plans. 11 This is particularly important given that only a small percentage of orthopaedist perform hip arthroscopy. 17 , 18 Therefore, these findings should be reassuring for patient access to hip arthroscopy. Hip arthroscopist can be confident that even when performing procedures using CPT 29999, they are likely to be reimbursed, at least 94% of the time, regardless of MC versus COM insurance. Additionally, the difference in reimbursement between patients with MC versus COM is not significant, so surgeons should be less incentivized to prioritize COM patients as they are with other procedures.

At a time when compensation for orthopaedic surgeons is decreasing across the board, it is important that surgeons are aware of the expected reimbursement for increasingly popular hip arthroscopy. 11 Furthermore, there is often a paucity of information on medical coding taught to orthopaedic residents, so increasing the presence of financial‐related literature should aid trainees as they begin their practices. 8 , 19 In this period of economic uncertainty, this study shows that hip arthroscopist can be confident they will be reimbursed for some portion of the charge for unlisted procedures and that the overall compensation does not differ significantly between MC and COM patients.

Limitations

This investigation is not without limitations. First, this study included a relatively small sample size of 137 patients. Medicare and Medicaid are very different but were pooled into 1 entity (MC) to maximize the power of our analysis, and the study is not adequately powered to do a subgroup analysis. Although multiple hospitals were queried, all were members of a single health system with the same billing infrastructure. Second, only the insurance providers of the 137 patients were included with a limited number of claims from each individual company. Therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other populations or health systems.

CONCLUSIONS

COM was less likely to completely deny a claim relative to MC and reimbursed approximately 10% higher than MC. However, there was no significant difference between payors in the percentage of the billing that was reimbursed or the percentage of reimbursement specifically for CPT code 29999. Regardless of insurance type, there is a low percentage of reimbursement for unlisted hip arthroscopy procedures.
