Open Boundary Conditions for Nonlinear Initial Boundary Value Problems
Jan Nordström

Abstract
We present a straightforward energy stable weak implementation procedure of open boundary conditions for nonlinear initial boundary value problems. It simplifies previous work and its practical implementation.

2
Short summary of previous results

Following
[
9
]
we consider the nonlinear IBVP posed on the domain
\Omega
with open boundary
\partial\Omega

PU_{t}+(A_{i}(U)U)_{x_{i}}+A_{i}(U)^{T}U_{x_{i}}+C(U)U=\epsilon(D_{i}(U))_{x_{i}},\quad t\geq 0,\quad\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{k})\in\Omega

(2.1)

augmented with the initial condition
U(\vec{x},0)=F(\vec{x})
in
\Omega
and the non-homogeneous boundary condition

B(U)U-G(\vec{x},t)=0,\quad t\geq 0,\quad\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},..,x_{k})\in\partial\Omega.

(2.2)

In (
2.2
),
B
is the nonlinear boundary operator and
G
the boundary data. In (
2.1
), Einsteins summation convention is used and
P
is a symmetric positive definite (or semi-definite) time-independent matrix that defines an energy norm (or semi-norm)
\|U\|^{2}_{P}=\int_{\Omega}U^{T}PUd\Omega
.
The
n\times n
matrices
A_{i},C
and the boundary operator
B
are smooth functions of the smooth
n
component vector
U
. The viscous fluxes (or stresses) with first derivatives are included in
D_{i}
which satisfies
U_{x_{i}}^{T}D_{i}\geq 0
while
\epsilon

is a non-negative constant parameter.

Remark 2.1
.

The formulation (
2.1
) can be derived for the shallow water, Euler and Navier-Stokes equations
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. It enables the direct use of the Green-Gauss theorem leading to an energy-rate as shown in
[
9
]
. However, we stress that the new boundary treatment that we will present below can be applied to any nonlinear problem as long as it’s energy rate only depends the nonlinear surface term.

2.1
The energy rates

For completeness and clarity we repeat the proof (from
[
9
]
) of the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

The IBVP (
2.1
) with
C+C^{T}=0
has an energy rate that only depends on (
2.2
).

Proof.

The energy method applied to (
2.1
) and Green-Gauss formula leads to the following energy rate

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|U\|^{2}_{P}+\epsilon\int\limits_{\Omega}U_{x_{i}}^{T}D_{i}d\Omega+\oint\limits_{\partial\Omega}U^{T}(n_{i}A_{i})\\
\ U-\epsilon U^{T}(n_{i}D_{i})\\
\ ds=\int\limits_{\Omega}(U_{x_{i}}^{T}A_{i}U-U^{T}A_{i}^{T}U_{x_{i}})-U^{T}CU\\
\ d\Omega,

(2.3)

where
\vec{n}=(n_{1},..,n_{k})^{T}
is the outward pointing unit normal. The first volume term on the right-hand side of (
2.3
) cancel by the skew-symmetric formulation and the second one by the condition
C+C^{T}=0
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Since the right-hand side of (
2.3
) vanish and
U_{x_{i}}^{T}D_{i}\geq 0
provide dissipation, Proposition
2.2
show that an energy-estimate is obtained if the surface terms are bounded by external data as shown in
[
9
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

We stress again that the new boundary treatment that we will present in Section 3, is not specifically dependent on the formulation (
2.1
). It can be applied to any nonlinear problem as long as one can derive an energy rate with the structure in (
2.3
), i.e. which depends only on a nonlinear surface term.

2.2
The nonlinear boundary conditions

We start with the case
\epsilon=0
and consider the surface term
U^{T}(n_{i}A_{i})U=U^{T}\tilde{A}U

where
\tilde{A}
is symmetric (the skew-symmetric part vanish).
Next, we transform
\tilde{A}
to diagonal form as
T^{T}\tilde{A}T=\Lambda=diag(\lambda_{i})
giving

U^{T}\tilde{A}U=W^{T}\Lambda W=(W^{+})^{T}\Lambda^{+}W^{+}+(W^{-})^{T}\Lambda^{-}W^{-}\,\ \text{where}\,\ W(U)=T^{-1}U.

(2.4)

The matrix
T
could be the standard eigenvector matrix
or
another non-singular matrix that transform
\tilde{A}
to diagonal form. In the latter case, Sylvester’s Criterion
[
14
]
guarantee that the number of positive and negative diagonal entries are the same as the number of positive and negative eigenvalues.
In the eigenvalue case
T^{-1}=T^{T}
, but not so in the general case.

\Lambda^{+}
and
\Lambda^{-}
are the positive and negative parts of
\Lambda
while
W^{+}
and
W^{-}
are the corresponding variables. Possible zero diagonal entries are included in
\Lambda^{+}
.

Note that the diagonal matrix
\Lambda=\Lambda(U)
is solution dependent and not a priori bounded (as for linear IBVPs). For linear problems, the number of boundary conditions at a surface position is equal to the number of eigenvalues (or diagonal entries) of
\tilde{A}
with the wrong (in this case negative) sign
[
15
]
.
In the nonlinear case, it is more complicated (and still not known) since multiple forms of
W^{T}\Lambda W
may exist
[
8
,
11
,
12
]
.

Remark 2.5
.

The nonlinear boundary procedure based on (
2.4
) has similarities with the linear characteristic boundary procedure
[
16
,
17
]
. Hence, with a slight abuse of notation we will refer to
\Lambda(U)
as eigenvalues and to the variables
W(U)
as characteristic variables, even in the general transformation case.

3
Previous and updated new general formulation of nonlinear boundary conditions

The starting point for the derivation of nonlinear
boundary conditions
is the diagonal form (
2.4
) of the boundary term. First we find the formulation (
2.4
) with a
minimal
number of entries in
\Lambda^{-}
(as mentioned above, there might be more than one formulation). Next, we specify the transformed characteristic variables
W^{-}
in terms of
W^{+}
and external data and
add a scaling possibility. The previous formulation in
[
9
]
read

BU-G=S^{-1}(\sqrt{|\Lambda^{-}|}W^{-}-R\sqrt{\Lambda^{+}}W^{+})-G=0\,\ \text{or equivalently}\,\ \sqrt{|\Lambda^{-}|}W^{-}-R\sqrt{\Lambda^{+}}W^{+}-SG=0

(3.1)

where
R
is a matrix combining values of
W^{-}
and
W^{+}
,
S
an non-singular scaling matrix and
G
is the external data.
The boundary condition (
3.1
) where we used the notation
|\Lambda|=diag(|\lambda_{i}|)
and
\sqrt{|\Lambda|}=diag(\sqrt{|\lambda_{i}|})
is general in the sense that it can include all components of
W
suitably combined by the matrices
S
and
R
.

We will impose the boundary conditions both strongly and weakly. For the weak imposition we introduce a lifting operator
L_{C}

that enforce the boundary conditions weakly in our governing equation (
2.1
)
as

PU_{t}+(A_{i}U)_{x_{i}}+A^{T}_{i}U_{x_{i}}+CU+L_{C}(\tilde{\Sigma}(BU-G))=\epsilon(D_{i})_{x_{i}}.

(3.2)

The lifting operator exist only at the surface of the domain and for two smooth vector functions
\phi,\psi
it satisfies
\int\limits\phi^{T}L_{C}(\psi)d\Omega=\oint\limits\phi^{T}\psi ds

and enables development of the numerical boundary procedure in the continuous setting
[
18
,
10
]
.
The previous weak implementation of (
3.1
) using a lifting operator was given by

L_{C}(\tilde{\Sigma}(BU-G))=L_{C}(2(I^{-}T^{-1})^{T}\Sigma(\sqrt{|\Lambda^{-}|}W^{-}-R\sqrt{\Lambda^{+}}W^{+}-SG)),

(3.3)

where

\tilde{\Sigma}=2(I^{-}T^{-1})^{T}\Sigma S
is a penalty matrix parametrized by the matrix coefficient
S,\Sigma
and
I^{-}W=W^{-}
.
In
[
9
]
, the following steps were required in order to determine the unknowns
R,S,\Sigma
in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
).

1.

Boundedness for strong homogeneous (
G=0
) boundary conditions lead to conditions on
R
.

2.

Boundedness for strong inhomogeneous (
G\neq 0
) boundary conditions lead to conditions on
S
.

3.

Boundedness for weak homogeneous (
G=0
) boundary conditions lead to conditions on
\Sigma
.

4.

Boundedness for weak inhomogeneous (
G\neq 0
) boundary conditions followed from conditions 1-3.

We will simplify the previous formulation (
3.3
), and start by rewriting the boundary term in (
2.4
) as

U^{T}\tilde{A}\\
\ U=(A^{+}U)^{T}(A^{+}U)-(A^{-}U)^{T}(A^{-}U)

(3.4)

where
A^{+}=\sqrt{\Lambda^{+}}T^{-1}
and
A^{-}=\sqrt{|\Lambda^{-}|}T^{-1}
. This reformulation simplifies the boundary condition (
3.1
) to

BU-G=S^{-1}(A^{-}-RA^{+})U-G=0\,\ \text{or equivalently}\,\ (A^{-}-RA^{+})U-SG=0

(3.5)

Note that the new boundary condition (
3.5
) is identical to the old one in (
3.1
), with the boundary operator
B
reformulated. Also the lifting operator in (
3.3
) simplifies by using
\tilde{\Sigma}=2(A^{-})^{T}S
to yield

L_{C}(\tilde{\Sigma}(BU-G))=L_{C}(2(A^{-})^{T}((A^{-}-RA^{+})U-SG)).

(3.6)

Remark 3.1
.

The new boundary procedure simplifies the previous one by
i)
reducing the algebra in (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) to (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) respectively,
ii)
reducing the number of parameters from three (
R,S,\Sigma
) to two (
R,S
) and
iii)
by operating with
B(U)
explicitly on the original dependent variable
U
.

The following Lemma is the main result of this note and it replaces Lemma 3.1 in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider the boundary term (
3.4
), the boundary condition (
3.5
) and the lifting operator (
3.6
).

The boundary term augmented with
1. strong nonlinear homogeneous boundary conditions
is positive semi-definite if the matrix
R
is such that

I-R^{T}R\geq 0.

(3.7)

The boundary term augmented with
2. strong nonlinear inhomogeneous boundary conditions
is bounded by the data
G
if
the matrix
R
satisfies (
3.7
) with strict inequality and the matrix
S
is such that

I-S^{T}S-(R^{T}S)^{T}(I-R^{T}R)^{-1}(R^{T}S)\geq 0\quad\text{where}\quad(I-R^{T}R)^{-1}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}(R^{T}R)^{k}.

(3.8)

The boundary term augmented with
3. weak nonlinear homogeneous boundary conditions
is positive semi-definite if the matrix
R

satisfies (
3.7
).

The boundary term augmented with
4. weak nonlinear inhomogeneous boundary conditions
is bounded by the data
G
if
the matrix
R
satisfies (
3.7
) with strict inequality and the matrix
S
satisfies (
3.8
).

Remark 3.3
.

We have used the notation
A\geq 0
to indicate that the matrix
A
is positive semi-definite above.

Proof.

The proof of Lemma
3.2
is step-by-step identical to the proof of Lemma 3.1 in
[
9
]
by replacing
\sqrt{|\Lambda^{-}|}W^{-},\sqrt{\Lambda^{+}}W^{+}
with
A^{-}U,A^{+}U
respectively and
\tilde{\Sigma}=2(I^{-}T^{-1})^{T}\Sigma S
with
\tilde{\Sigma}=2(A^{-})^{T}S
.
∎

3.1
Extension to include viscous terms

The boundary terms to consider in the case when
\epsilon>0
are given in Proposition
2.2
. The argument in the surface integral
can be reformulated into the first derivative setting. By introducing the notation
n_{i}D_{i}/2=\tilde{F}
for the viscous flux we rewrite the boundary terms as

U^{T}(n_{i}A_{i})U-\epsilon U^{T}(n_{i}D_{i})=U^{T}\tilde{A}U-\epsilon U^{T}\tilde{F}-\epsilon\tilde{F}^{T}U=\begin{bmatrix}U\\
\epsilon\tilde{F}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{A}&-I\\
-I&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U\\
\epsilon\tilde{F}\end{bmatrix}

(3.9)

where
I
is the identity matrix. We can now formally diagonalise the boundary terms in (
3.9
), apply the boundary condition (
3.5
) in combination with (
3.6
) and Lemma
3.2
to obtain energy bounds.

4
Summary

We have simplified and removed unnecessary parameter dependencies in previous work on nonlinear boundary conditions in
[
9
]
. This will remove complexities from its practical numerical implementation.
