Improving structural heart disease screening: AI-ECG and novice AI-guided focused cardiac ultrasound
Authors: Méabh M. Killalea, Jordan Borgeson, Conor J. Kane, Jude Kovac, Christie Greason, Mayari A. Gulati, Daniel A. Schonfeld, Maria M. Crestanello, Jessica Zacher, Gal Tsaban, Eunjung Lee, Jeffrey G. Malins, Jwan A. Naser, Timothy J. Poterucha, Paul A. Friedman, Francisco Lopez-Jimenez, Garvan C. Kane, Zachi I. Attia, Jeremy J. ABSTRACT
Structural heart disease (SHD) causes substantial morbidity, yet early detection is limited. In this prospective study, AI-enhanced ECG and AI-guided focused cardiac ultrasound (AI-FoCUS) were assessed for detecting SHD (left ventricular EF < 40%, ≥moderate aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or cardiac amyloidosis) in 995 adults against comprehensive echocardiography performed within 30 min of AI-FoCUS. Nine complete novices used handheld ultrasound (Philips Lumify) with real-time AI guidance (UltraSight) with images interpreted by blinded experts. AI-FoCUS was feasible in 97.3%. Prevalence was 5.0% for EF < 40%, 3.5% for ≥moderate aortic stenosis, and 8.8% for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or amyloidosis. AI-ECG alone yielded NPV 95.8% but PPV 29.6%, limiting standalone screening. AI-FoCUS alone achieved PPV 95.4% and NPV 98.0%, however requires imaging in all. A two-step strategy (positive AI-ECG followed by AI-FoCUS) maintained high PPV/NPV (96.1%/95.6%) but limited FoCUS imaging to 47%.In this echo referral cohort, novice AI-guided FoCUS acquisition with blinded expert interpretation was feasible and accurate for detecting selected SHD phenotypes. A two-step AI-ECG then AI-FoCUS strategy reduced FoCUS utilization, but lowered sensitivity compared with AI-FoCUS for all. However, both FoCUS-based strategies were superior to AI-ECG based screening alone. This work requires further assessment and validation in lower prevalence populations.

Introduction

Cardiovascular (CV) disease, including structural heart disease (SHD), is highly prevalent and remains a leading cause of death in the United States and the European Union1. In 2020 the economic burden of cardiovascular disease in the US was $400 billion, and this is projected to triple to $1490 billion by 2050, underscoring the urgent need for the implementation of low-resource screening programs and preventative medicine1. The most common CV diseases such as reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) and aortic valve stenosis (AS), are often preceded by a phase of asymptomatic structural impairment2–10. Early detection and diagnosis of SHD are necessary for effective intervention and improved survival2–12. Yet, patients with asymptomatic SHD often go undetected and hence miss the benefits of early intervention.

Currently, there is no cost-effective screening method for SHD. Formal echocardiography performed by trained sonographers is too resource- and time-intensive for screening asymptomatic populations. Emerging literature supports artificial intelligence-enhanced 12-lead electrocardiography (AI-ECG) as a non-invasive, scalable, and inexpensive alternative test. A convolutional neural network (CNN) applied to an ECG detects subtle patterns in electrical signals, enabling detection of SHD conditions such as moderate or greater AS, left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and cardiac amyloidosis13–16. Despite considerable optimism regarding this technology due to very high negative predictive values, lower specificities and positive predictive values may limit the use of AI-ECG as a standalone tool due to false positives and unnecessary referrals for diagnostic testing such as echocardiography.

Focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) with AI-guidance software is emerging as a method for less-experienced users (or potentially users without any clinical or imaging experience) to capture basic cardiac images17. The AI guides the user in real time to position the ultrasound probe, optimize the image, and indicates when an optimal image is present to allow for the ability to obtain high quality cardiac ultrasound images. Compared to a formal echocardiogram performed by trained sonographers, this technology is relatively inexpensive, accessible, and potentially scalable for SHD screening in resource-limited settings. However, due to limited clinical data, it is unclear whether novice users guided by AI can obtain satisfactory FoCUS images for SHD screening. It is also uncertain whether these images can be effectively integrated with AI-ECG to reduce false positives and improve diagnostic accuracy.

Our study hypothesized that the addition of AI-guidance would allow individuals without any prior imaging or clinical experience (truly novice users) to obtain AI-FoCUS to accurately detect abnormalities of SHD. The aim was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the AI-FoCUS scan to identify either (i) LVSD, (ii) moderate or greater AS, (iii) hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or (iv) cardiac amyloidosis. We secondarily assessed the diagnostic performance of the Mayo Clinic AI-ECG algorithms to identify the same 4 pathologies as well as a two-step combined test where the 4 AI-ECG algorithms were first applied, and if positive for one pathology, then the results of the AI-FoCUS scan were considered.

Results

Study cohort

The cohort comprised 995 prospectively enrolled patients who underwent an AI-FoCUS within 30 days of an AI-ECG. They also all underwent a comprehensive diagnostic echocardiogram within 30 min of their FoCUS (Fig. 1). The cohort included individuals with a broad spectrum of left ventricular pathology, as well as various forms of valvular heart disease (Table 1). All patients underwent a 12-lead AI-ECG within 30 days of the AI-FOCUS and echocardiogram, and the majority (84%) had the AI-ECG within 48 h (median of 0, IQR of 0–1). As detailed in Table 1, the cohort exhibited considerable variability in age, weight, body mass index (BMI), and echocardiographic findings. The median age was 69 years (interquartile range: 58–77), with females accounting for 39% of the population. Feasibility of AI-FoCUS scan

Among the 995 patients in the study, 27 (2.7%) individuals had AI-FOCUS examinations in which no diagnostic images could be obtained. In the remaining cohort of subjects with feasible AI-FoCUS studies, the median scan duration was 4 min (interquartile range: 3–7 min). Image quality and feasibility varied across the six standard imaging views, as illustrated in Fig. 2, data summarized in Table 2. The parasternal long-axis (PLAX) and parasternal short-axis (PSAX) views demonstrated the highest feasibility, followed by the apical four-chamber (A4C) view, which was moderately feasible. The apical three-chamber (A3C) and apical two-chamber (A2C) views were the least feasible. LV EF classification by AI-ECG and AI-FoCUS

Both the AI-ECG and the AI-FoCUS were targeted to assess for a threshold of an LVEF < 40%. Of the 995 patients, 50 had a LVEF < 40% for a prevalence of 5.0% by standard comprehensive echocardiography. The AI-ECG had a sensitivity of 86.0% and a specificity of 88.6% for the detection of an LVEF < 40%. Of the 108 false positive AI-ECGs, 41 had an LVEF between 40% and 49%, and 67 an LVEF ≥ 50% on comprehensive echocardiography. In contrast, only 2 AI-FoCUS scans were false positive, and both had LVEF 40–49% and were near the diagnostic threshold. The AI-FoCUS had a sensitivity of 92.0% and a specificity of 99.9% for the detection of an LVEF < 40%. Aortic stenosis classification by AI-ECG and AI-FoCUS

Both the AI-ECG and the AI-FoCUS were targeted to assess for a threshold of moderate or greater AS. 108 patients of the 995 had an aortic valve prosthesis. These patients were excluded from the AS analysis. Of those patients with a native aortic valve, 35 had moderate or greater AS (14 moderate, 7 moderate-severe, and 14 severe) for a prevalence of 3.9%. The AI-ECG had a sensitivity of 68.6% and a specificity of 69.5% for the detection of moderate or greater AS. Of the 262 false positive AI-ECG analyses for AS, 20 had mild AS and 242 did not have AS. AI-FoCUS reduced these to just 3 false positives, all of whom had mild AS by reference echocardiography. The AI-FoCUS had a sensitivity of 94.3% and a specificity of 99.6% for the detection of moderate or greater AS. The PPVs and NPVs were 8.4% and 98.2% for the AI-ECG and 91.7% and 99.7% for the AI-FoCUS (Table 3).

HCM/amyloidosis classification by AI-ECG and AI-FoCUS

Of the 995 patients, 87 had a formal diagnosis of either hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (n = 63) or cardiac amyloidosis (n = 25) for a prevalence of 8.8%. The AI-ECG had a sensitivity of 82.8% and a specificity of 77.3% for HCM or amyloid. The AI-FoCUS had a sensitivity of 84.9% and a specificity of 99.4% for HCM or amyloid. For either HCM or cardiac amyloid, the PPVs and NPVs were 25.9% and 97.9% for the AI-ECG and 93.6% and 98.6% for the AI-FoCUS (Table 3). ROC curves with 95% confidence intervals for each of the AI-ECG outputs are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 and Calibration plots in Supplementary Fig. 2.

The prevalence of SHD was not different between those patients scanned within 48 h (<2 days) of their ECG or 2–30 days between ECG and FoCUS. Furthermore, in a sensitivity analysis restricting AI-ECG to those obtained within 48 h, performance estimates were essentially unchanged, supporting minimal bias from the longer screening window. Operator-level image quality varied across novice imagers for both the apical 4-chamber and parasternal long-axis views. For the apical four chamber view, the distribution of poor/missing, adequate, and good/excellent images differed by operator (χ² = 40.17, df = 16, p = 0.0007). For the parasternal long axis view, between-operator heterogeneity was more pronounced (χ² = 68.42, df = 16, p < 0.0001). However, study subject BMI also differed across operators, a factor known to be associated with image quality. After adjustment for BMI, difference in operator remained associated with image quality (likelihood-ratio χ² = 71.7, df = 16, p < 0.0001), and higher BMI was independently associated with lower image quality (likelihood-ratio χ² = 33.8, df = 2, p < 0.0001). Discussion

As the population ages with an increasing prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes, the global burden of SHD rises1. Early detection of SHD may provide an opportunity for interventions, including risk factor modification or pharmacotherapies that may prevent sudden cardiac death or irreversible cardiac damage, as in the case of valvular heart disease2–12. The field has therefore focused on identifying feasible strategies to screen for SHD at scale. The application of AI to the 12-lead ECG has resulted in a tool capable of detecting a wide range of SHDs with a high sensitivity, allowing for ruling out the disease in those with a negative AI ECG13–16,18–20. However, the limitations of AI-ECG as a screening tool include modest specificity and, in low-prevalence settings, low positive predictive value (PPV), which would reduce the need for referring all AI-ECG positive patients for comprehensive echocardiography. Given the high clinical demand and cost of diagnostic echocardiography, there is a need for an intermediate, scalable test that can more efficiently identify which patients with an abnormal AI-ECG should proceed to a formal echocardiogram.

Here in this prospective study of 995 adults undergoing clinically indicated echocardiography, we evaluated the ability of AI-ECG and AI-FoCUS performed by novices to detect four forms of SHD. Our results confirmed that the AI-ECG, while having excellent sensitivity for the detection of SHD, the technology in isolation is limited by poor specificity. AI-FoCUS obtained by novices with results reviewed by experts is a superior screening strategy for SHD with very high sensitivity (90.1%), specificity (99.1%), and positive and negative predictive values (95.4% and 98.0%), but requires FoCUS imaging in all. AI-FoCUS also accurately reclassifies most AI-ECG false positives. This underscores the complementary roles of the two modalities: AI-ECG offers broad, sensitive initial screening, while AI-FoCUS effectively eliminates most false positives and hence could be positioned as a potential interim screen between the AI-ECG and a diagnostic clinical echocardiogram.

The simulated two-step strategy tested in this study of initial AI-ECG followed by AI-FoCUS only in those with at least one positive AI-ECG algorithm provides a pragmatic compromise between diagnostic performance and resource usage. In this framework, only 47% of patients would undergo AI-FoCUS, yet the combined strategy maintains a high PPV (96.1%) and NPV (95.6%) for the composite SHD endpoint, with specificity of 99.4% and sensitivity of 77.0%. Compared with AI-FoCUS for all strategy, this approach reduces the number of handheld ultrasound exams and downstream comprehensive echocardiograms while preserving high accuracy among those ultimately referred. However, this approach occurs at the expense of lower sensitivity, with a modest number of significant cases of SHD missed, including 6 cases of severe AS.

Ultimately, the choice of screening modality for SHD would likely vary between institutions and screening locations, which have differences in resource availability. In resource-rich settings with ample handheld devices and personnel trained in AI-guided FoCUS, performing AI-FoCUS in all patients would likely be preferrable due to the superior diagnostic accuracy over both the AI-ECG screening and combined 2-step diagnostic processes. In contrast, in settings where handheld ultrasound devices or trained operators are limited, a two-step pipeline using AI-ECG to initially gate access to AI-FoCUS may offer an attractive balance between sensitivity and resource utilization.

Ultrasound has the capacity to answer simple diagnostic questions in real-time in a host of clinical care settings, and advances in technology have provided the capacity for images to be obtained on handheld devices. However, a major barrier to handheld imaging is operator variability. There is emerging evidence that AI has significant potential in guiding less experienced users to acquire diagnostic-quality ultrasound images. A multicenter validation study demonstrated that healthcare professionals with minimal lung ultrasound experience, when assisted by AI, were able to obtain “expert-level” diagnostic images21. The AI system provided real-time guidance during image acquisition, automatically captured clips once quality thresholds were met, and annotated key features such as B-lines21. Almost all of the AI-assisted studies met diagnostic quality standards, comparable to those obtained by expert sonographers21. A separate study evaluating gestational age estimation found that novice users with no prior sonography training, using an AI-enabled device, achieved accuracy equivalent to credentialed sonographers using traditional equipment22. Other studies have evaluated cardiac imaging, specifically reporting the ability to assess LV ejection fraction by novice users with good diagnostic quality17,23–25. However, unlike in our study, the novice users in these studies may be better described as less experienced, as they are often nurses or cardiology fellows who had experience in pathology, cardiac views, or prior ultrasound scanning23,24. Some studies have been performed on cart-based rather than handheld equipment17,23,24 and frequently have exclusion criteria for subject recruitment based on image quality, patient position, or body size17,25. Here, we evaluate truly novice users by studying individuals with no clinical experience or imaging training apart from the four-hour introductory workshop explaining the device, use of the software for imaging, patient position, location of the appropriate acoustic window, and the setting of the appropriate gain, depth, and use of ultrasound gel. In addition, patient characteristics such as body size or image quality were not factors in patient selection.

An important clarification is that, in the present study, AI was used solely for image acquisition guidance in the AI-FoCUS workflow, with all diagnostic interpretation of FoCUS images being performed by expert echocardiographers blinded to AI-ECG and clinical data. As such, AI-FoCUS as implemented here does not yet represent a fully automated imaging solution, and the performance we report reflects the combination of novice acquisition plus expert interpretation. The higher performance of AI-FoCUS relative to AI-ECG should be interpreted in the context that the AI-FoCUS pathway combined AI-guided acquisition with expert image interpretation, whereas the AI-ECG pathway was fully automated, which may degrade test performance characteristics. The superior performance of AI-FoCUS relative to AI-ECG is therefore not unexpected, given that echocardiography is the reference imaging modality for SHD and that human experts reviewed the ultrasound images. Nonetheless, our results demonstrate that novice-acquired, AI-guided images can support expert-level diagnosis with excellent reproducibility, an essential prerequisite for future integration with automated interpretation algorithms26–29.

Both AI-ECG and AI-FoCUS have tremendous potential, and our findings underscore several practical considerations for clinical implementation. Handheld ultrasound devices and AI-guidance software require initial investment, yet they are significantly less expensive and more portable than conventional echocardiography systems. This portability enables deployment in primary care clinics, community hospitals, and remote locations, which are already well equipped to perform standardized AI-ECG for screening. Training for novice operators in this study was limited to a single standardized 4-h session, indicating that with the AI-guidance software tested here, competency for the acquisition of basic images may be attainable. Integration of both AI-ECG and AI-FoCUS systems into existing clinical workflows and electronic health records is essential. Although formal cost-effectiveness analyses comparing AI-ECG only, AI-FoCUS only, and two-step strategies were beyond the scope of this study, such analyses will be critical for informing policy and reimbursement decisions.

The advent of AI-guided FoCUS together with the potential development of AI tools for diagnostic interpretation of FoCUS images26–30 may reduce the burden of having formal interpretation of echocardiographic images by an expert reviewer and opens up the possibility of a new paradigm in patient care (Fig. 8). Although in this study we have used standard 12-lead ECG as a source for the AI, it has previously shown that AI-ECG derived from one-lead smartwatch/smartphone-based devices has reasonable sensitivity and specificity for SHD detection31,32. Taking this a step further, one could even envisage patients performing their own ECG on wearable devices and those with positive SHD findings being sent handheld devices for AI-guided FoCUS examination by family or self-imaged at the home. Data may then remotely be interpreted in a medical center or by AI, and patients in whom the SHD screening is positive could be triaged for need and priority for formal outpatient evaluation. However, this expanded concept of patient/family member scanning was not tested here, and the current study evaluated feasibility in non-clinical novice users, not patients performing their own scans. Our study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a tertiary care academic center among patients undergoing clinically indicated echocardiography for known or suspected cardiovascular disease. While this was needed to have a reference echocardiogram for comparison as the “gold standard” examination, subjects likely had higher pre-test probability of SHD than would be seen in asymptomatic community screening programs, and some had known SHD diagnoses at the time of enrollment. This referral-population design may inflate PPV and overall accuracy estimates relative to those expected in lower-risk populations and introduces spectrum bias. Our cohort also had limited racial and ethnic diversity, and we were underpowered to perform robust performance analyses across racial/ethnic subgroups. Lastly, while we modelled the accuracy of the AI-FoCUS and AI-ECG as screening tests, our actual population all underwent diagnostic echocardiography as part of clinical care. Future work in broader, more diverse, and truly asymptomatic populations is required to establish generalizability and to refine the role of the two-step and AI-FoCUS for all strategies in screening contexts and assess the impact of these strategies on clinical decision making, patient outcomes, or health-care utilization.

Second, although AI-FoCUS was performed by nine novice operators with no formal clinical or sonography training, they were young adults with high technical literacy and volunteered for a research study, which may not fully reflect the range of potential real-world users. While modest differences in image quality were seen between users, these were not associated with differences in diagnostic accuracy. We also did not design the study to compare scans obtained by different novices in the same subject. It remains uncertain how performance would change over time with experience, or how reproducible acquisitions would be across a larger and more heterogeneous group of novice users. Moreover, although the overall feasibility of AI-FoCUS was high, image quality varied by view, and a small proportion of patients (2.7%) had inadequate diagnostic images to assess for all SHD pathologies. In patients with partial examinations, experts relied on available views to adjudicate SHD status, reflecting real-world FoCUS practice but potentially underestimating disease in some cases.

Third, the study focused on four SHD phenotypes for which validated AI-ECG algorithms are available at our institution; left ventricular systolic dysfunction, moderate or greater aortic stenosis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and cardiac amyloidosis. We did not evaluate other common structural abnormalities such as right ventricular dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, or mitral and tricuspid regurgitation. Standard comprehensive echocardiography incorporates color and spectral Doppler imaging in addition to 2D B-mode to evaluate these conditions, whereas our FoCUS protocol relied solely on 2D views. Whether simplified imaging with AI-guided FoCUS can accurately detect a broader range of SHD requires further study, and parallel development of AI-ECG and AI-echo algorithms for these indications is ongoing26–30.

Finally, the AI-FoCUS images in this study required interpretation by an expert echocardiographer. Although review time was brief due to the small number of images (<3 min per study, including image-quality grading), reliance on human experts may still limit scalability in regions with severe shortages of interpreting echocardiographers. AI-interpretative algorithms for cardiac ultrasound images, which are in development, have the potential to reduce the reliance on human experts26–30. We also acknowledge that we used standard 12-lead ECGs for this study, and it remains uncertain whether similar results could be achieved using simplified systems, such as a 6-lead or single-lead ECG, which may be more feasible in resource-limited settings. Future experiments may explore whether the different types of ECG could be analyzed by AI to provide the same level of diagnostic information as the 12-lead ECG.

In conclusion, in this echo referral cohort, AI-guided FoCUS acquired by novice users and interpreted by experts was feasible and accurate. Performing AI-FoCUS in all participants provided the highest sensitivity, whereas a two-step AI-ECG then AI-FoCUS workflow conserved imaging resources at the cost of additional missed SHD cases. The preferred strategy will therefore depend on whether a given setting prioritizes maximal case detection (AI-FoCUS alone without any AI-ECG) or conservation of limited imaging resources (two step with AI-ECG first, then AI-FoCUS if AI-ECG abnormal).

Methods

Study population

We prospectively enrolled 995 adult patients to undergo AI-FoCUS within 30 days of an AI-ECG and undergo a clinically indicated comprehensive diagnostic echocardiogram for the evaluation of known or suspected cardiovascular disease (echocardiogram performed within 30 min of their AI-FoCUS) (Fig. 1). The only exclusion criteria were complex congenital heart disease or the inability to consent. Specifically, patients were not excluded based on any weight or body mass index threshold or on the presence of poor acoustic windows. The study was conducted at a tertiary care academic medical center among patients undergoing clinically indicated echocardiography ordered for a range of indications (e.g., dyspnea, suspected cardiomyopathy, murmurs, arrhythmias), as listed in Table 1. Some participants did have pre-existing diagnoses of SHD or cardiovascular disease at the time of inclusion (e.g., prior heart failure or known HCM/amyloidosis), with the clinically indicated echocardiogram often obtained for follow-up or further evaluation. Patients with a prior aortic valve replacement were excluded from the AS evaluation but included for the analysis of LVSD, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or cardiac amyloidosis. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 24-005529). All participants provided informed consent for participation in the study.

SHD

For this study, “structural heart disease” refers to four clinically important conditions: (i) left ventricular systolic dysfunction as defined as the presence of an LVEF < 40%33, (ii) moderate or greater aortic valvular stenosis8, (iii) hypertrophic cardiomyopathy11, and (iv) cardiac amyloidosis12.

AI-ECG

12-lead electrocardiograms were acquired as part of clinical care. AI-ECG data was generated through validated algorithms using solely input data of digital ECG signals. Each AI-ECG algorithm generates a continuous probability score (0–1). A fixed, pre-specified threshold, established in prior derivation and validation studies for each algorithm, was applied to categorize each case as positive or negative for (i) LVEF < 40%, (ii) ≥moderate aortic stenosis, (iii) hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and (iv) cardiac amyloidosis as previously described13–16. For the purposes of the analysis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and cardiac amyloidosis, if either AI-ECG model was positive, the case was deemed positive, if both models negative, the case was deemed negative.

AI-FoCUS and data analysis

For the purposes of this study AI-FoCUS referred to a FoCUS study that was performed by one of nine novice operators (college students without formal sonography or clinical medical experience), containing six standard cardiac views, each guided in real-time by AI software (UltraSight Ltd. Inc.) which was integrated into a commercial Lumify hand-held ultrasound device (Fig. 2). This FDA-approved AI-software instructs users how to maneuver the probe for each cardiac view while providing an indicator of quality. Prior to the study, novice operators received very focused training in the use of the software for imaging (standardized 4-hour session that covered patient position, location of the appropriate acoustic window, and the setting of the appropriate gain, depth, and use of ultrasound gel). The novice AI-FoCUS operators were not provided with AI-ECG outputs and had minimal clinical information prior to scanning subjects, with their task limited to following the AI guidance to obtain the required views.

No images were interpreted by AI. All AI-FoCUS images were recorded on a secure server for evaluation by an expert (board-certified cardiologists with advanced echocardiography training (e.g., level III) and more than 10 years of experience), blinded to the AI-ECG results, patient history, and to the original clinical echocardiogram images and report. The experts reviewed only the stored FoCUS images. Findings were assessed qualitatively. LVSD was rated as follows: (i) normal LVEF ≥ 50%; (ii) LVEF 35–49% and (iii) LVEF < 35%. The aortic valve was graded as: (i) normal; (ii) less than moderate stenosis or (iii) moderate or greater stenosis. The visual appearance suggestive of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or cardiac amyloidosis was graded as absent, possible, or present. For the purposes of analysis, the grading of “possible” or “present” for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or cardiac amyloidosis was combined as “present”. For patients with partial exams (i.e., some views rated “missing”), the expert interpreted SHD status using the available views, consistent with real-world FoCUS practice, and the test was considered interpretable if sufficient views were present to judge the four SHD categories. The ECG and AI-FoCUS results were then compared to the reference standard of the formal diagnostic echocardiogram, performed according to American Society of Echocardiography guidelines33.

Quality score

All AI-FoCUS images obtained by novice users were also scored for quality by the expert. The best image(s) for each of 6 standard views were scored as: Excellent (diagnostic quality equivalent for those obtained by an expert sonographer with a diagnostic echocardiography machine); Good (less than typical diagnostic echo quality obtained by an expert sonographer; however, all structures in view could be assessed including adequate quality to qualitatively assess relevant structures and function); Adequate (while sub-optimal for all cardiac structures in the view, adequate quality to qualitatively assess relevant structures and function); Poor (some structures present but sub-optimal for assessment of relevant structures and function). If no interpretable image from a particular view was present, it was defined as missing.

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 18.0. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (interquartile range), or count (percentage), as appropriate. Diagnostic performance was summarized using sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, likelihood ratios, and accuracy, each with 95% confidence intervals. For paired comparisons among AI-ECG alone, AI-FoCUS for all, and the two-step AI-ECG, first, then AI-FoCUS strategy, analyses were performed on the common cohort with interpretable AI-FoCUS examinations. Differences in sensitivity and specificity between paired strategies were assessed using McNemar tests in the diseased and non-diseased subsets, respectively. For the continuous AI-ECG outputs, discrimination was assessed using ROC curves and AUC with 95% confidence intervals by DeLong’s method, and calibration was assessed using calibration plots, calibration intercept, calibration slope, and Brier score. Prevalence-adjusted PPV and NPV scenario analyses were calculated using Bayes’ theorem. Decision-curve analysis was used to estimate net benefit for referral to comprehensive echocardiography. Operator-level learning effects were evaluated using mixed-effects models with operator as a random intercept. Two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Some figures were created in https://BioRender.com.
