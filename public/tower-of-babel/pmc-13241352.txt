Exposure relevance and translational limitations in a study on 4-hexylresorcinol and intestinal repair
Authors: Seong-Gon Kim
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with interest the article by Fan et al. [1]., entitled “The food additive 4-hexylresorcinol impairs intestinal stem cell function and exacerbates inflammatory bowel disease in Drosophila melanogaster via suppression of MAPK signaling pathway.” The authors investigated the effects of 4-hexylresorcinol (4-HR) on intestinal stem cell proliferation and epithelial repair using Drosophila and mouse colitis models. The study addresses an important topic, as the biological effects of food additives on intestinal repair mechanisms deserve careful evaluation. However, several aspects of the experimental design and interpretation appear to limit the strength of the conclusions.

A major concern is the exposure relevance of the 4-HR concentrations used in the Drosophila feeding experiments. The authors treated flies with 0.1, 1, and 10 mM 4-HR, corresponding approximately to 19.4, 194, and 1,943 mg/L, respectively, based on the molecular weight of 4-HR [1]. These concentrations are markedly above food-relevant residue levels. Codex GSFA lists 4-HR for use in crustaceans at 50 mg/kg in raw or frozen crustacean-related categories, with restrictions indicating use in crustaceans immediately after harvesting or capture [2]. However, the expected residue level in the edible portion is much lower. The EU food-additive database lists a maximum level of 2 mg/kg for 4-HR as a residue in the meat of fresh, frozen, or deep-frozen crustaceans [3]. Similarly, JECFA concluded that treatment of crustacea at concentrations up to 50 mg/L results in residue levels of approximately 1 mg/kg in the edible portion and was not of toxicological concern [4]. EFSA also concluded that 4-HR is toxicologically acceptable for preventing melanosis in shrimps and related crustaceans, provided that residues in crustacean meat do not exceed 2 mg/kg [5]. Although direct conversion between fly-food concentration and human edible-residue exposure is imperfect, comparison with realistic edible-residue levels of approximately 1–2 mg/kg indicates a substantial order-of-magnitude discrepancy. Even the lowest Drosophila dose, 0.1 mM, is approximately 10–20 times higher than expected edible-residue levels, whereas 1 and 10 mM are approximately two to three orders of magnitude higher. These high concentrations raise the possibility that the observed effects reflect toxicological exposure rather than realistic dietary exposure. High-concentration 4-HR has previously been studied in experimental anticancer contexts in which cytotoxic, apoptosis-related, or growth-modulatory effects were evaluated [6]. This further supports the need to distinguish pharmacologic high-dose exposure from realistic food-residue exposure. Without clear justification of the selected concentrations, measurement of actual 4-HR intake, or assessment of tissue exposure, extrapolation of these findings to human dietary IBD risk remains premature.

A second concern is that the manuscript repeatedly emphasizes the possible long-term or chronic harmful effects of 4-HR. However, the experimental models do not adequately address chronic exposure. In the Drosophila injury model, 4-HR was administered during short recovery periods following bleomycin-induced injury, and the mouse experiment used a 7-day DSS-induced acute colitis model. These designs are appropriate for assessing short-term effects on chemically induced injury and epithelial repair, but they do not establish the consequences of chronic dietary exposure. Therefore, statements regarding long-term safety, chronic exposure, or dietary risk in susceptible populations should be substantially moderated unless supported by prolonged low-dose feeding, repeated injury-recovery cycles, or chronic colitis models.

The mechanistic conclusion that 4-HR exacerbates intestinal injury by directly targeting MAPK1/ERK2 also requires caution. Molecular docking and bio-layer interferometry support a possible interaction between 4-HR and MAPK1, and the tissue data show reduced p-ERK signals following 4-HR treatment. However, these findings do not definitively establish direct kinase inhibition in vivo. Reduced p-ERK could result from upstream changes in EGFR signaling, altered injury responses, reduced proliferative cell populations, or nonspecific impairment of epithelial regeneration. Additional experiments, such as in vitro ERK kinase assays, dose-dependent inhibition curves, total ERK normalization, and stronger rescue experiments, would be needed to support direct functional inhibition of MAPK1/ERK2.

Finally, the translational conclusion that 4-HR is a significant dietary environmental contributor to IBD pathogenesis appears stronger than the available evidence allows. Bleomycin-induced intestinal injury in Drosophila and acute DSS colitis in mice are useful experimental systems, but they do not fully recapitulate human IBD, which involves complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, adaptive immunity, epithelial barrier function, microbiota, and chronic relapsing inflammation. The current data more conservatively support the conclusion that 4-HR, at the tested concentrations and under chemically injured conditions, can impair epithelial repair responses in experimental models.

In summary, the study provides interesting evidence that 4-HR may interfere with epithelial repair signaling under intestinal injury conditions. However, the exposure levels, absence of a chronic exposure model, and indirect mechanistic evidence weaken the broader claims regarding long-term dietary safety and IBD pathogenesis. A more cautious interpretation would improve the translational balance of the article.

Sincerely,

Seong-Gon Kim, DDS, MSD, PhD
