Gyrotropic magnetic effect in metallic chiral magnets
Nisarga Paul, Takamori Park, Jung Hoon Han, Leon Balents

Abstract
We study the gyrotropic magnetic effect (GME), the low-frequency limit of optical gyrotropy, in metals and semimetals coupled to chiral spin textures. In these systems, the chiral spin texture which lacks inversion symmetry can imprint itself upon the electronic structure through Hund’s coupling, leading to novel low-frequency optical activity. Using perturbation theory and numerical diagonalization of both relativistic and non-relativistic models of conduction electrons coupled to spin textures, we analyze how the GME manifests in both single- q q and multi- q q textures. Analytical expressions for the rotatory power are derived in terms of universal scaling functions. Estimates based on realistic material parameters reveal an experimentally viable range of values for the rotatory power. The GME arises from the orbital and spin magnetic moments of conduction electrons, with the orbital part closely tied to Berry curvature and playing a significant role in relativistic metals but not so in non-relativistic metals where there is no inherent Berry curvature. The spin contribution to the GME can be significant in non-relativistic metals with a large Fermi energy. Our work shows that the GME can be a sensitive probe of magnetic chirality and symmetry breaking in metallic chiral magnets.

Model.
— A minimal model describing electrons coupled to a spin texture is given by

H=H_{0}(\mathbf{p})+J\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}

(1)

where
H_{0}
captures the electronic dispersion,
J>0
is the Hund’s coupling to a static local spin texture
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}
, and
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y},\sigma^{z})
are the Pauli operators for itinerant electron spins. This type of model has served as the canonical means of studying the properties of chiral magnets coupled to itinerant electrons in the past
[
30
,
11
]

H_{0}^{\text{NR}}={\bf p}^{2}/2m,\qquad H_{0}^{\text{R}}=\sum_{w}\chi_{w}v_{F}(\mathbf{p}\cdot\bm{\sigma})_{w}.

(2)

For
H_{0}^{\text{NR}}
,
m
is an effective mass and
{\bf p}
is measured from the band bottom. For
H_{0}^{\text{R}}
,
v_{F}
is the Fermi velocity at the Weyl points,
\sum_{w}
is a direct sum over Weyl points with chiralities
\chi_{w}
, and
{\bf p}
is measured from the location of respective Weyl point. For simplicity we will restrict our analysis to a pair of
\mathcal{P}
-conjugate Weyl points, located at
{\bf k}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{P}}{\bf k}_{0}=-{\bf k}_{0}
with respective chiralities
\chi_{w}=\pm 1
. Moreover, we will assume that the spin texture is periodic:
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
where
\mathbf{G}
belongs to a reciprocal lattice
\Lambda
, allowing us to work in the momentum space Brillouin zone associated to
\Lambda
.

Optical rotation.
— When linearly polarized light passes through an optically active medium, its polarization is rotated by an angle
\phi
proportional to the thickness
d
. The angle
\phi
depends on the GME tensor, which at low frequencies
\hbar\omega\ll\varepsilon_{\text{gap}}
(where
\varepsilon_{\text{gap}}
is the gap to remote bands) takes the form

\alpha^{\text{GME}}_{ij}=\frac{i\omega\tau e}{1-i\omega\tau}\sum_{n}\int[d\mathbf{k}]\frac{\partial f}{\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}}v_{\mathbf{k}n,i}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j},

(3)

capturing intraband contributions for a metal with relaxation time
\tau
. The integral is over the Brillouin zone with
[d\mathbf{k}]=d^{3}\mathbf{k}/(2\pi)^{3}
,
f=f(\varepsilon_{n\mathbf{k}})
is the distribution function, and
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n},\mathbf{v}_{\mathbf{k}n}=\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}/\hbar\partial\mathbf{k}
, and
\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}n}
are band energies, velocities, and magnetic moments, respectively. The magnetic moments are
\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}n}=\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{spin}}+\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{orb}}
with

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{spin}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{eg_{s}}{2m_{e}}\langle u_{\mathbf{k}n}|\frac{\hbar\bm{\sigma}}{2}|u_{\mathbf{k}n}\rangle

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}n}^{\text{orb}}

\displaystyle=-i\frac{e}{2\hbar}\langle\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}n}|\times(H_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n})|\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}n}\rangle

(4b)

for Bloch states
|u_{\mathbf{k}n}\rangle
, where
g_{s}\approx 2
is the electron
g
-factor and
m_{e}
is the bare electron mass. The
rotatory power
is then

\rho=\frac{\phi}{d}=\frac{1}{2c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}}\real\left(\alpha^{\text{GME}}_{ij}\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j}-\bar{\alpha}^{\text{GME}}\right)

(5)

where
\bar{\alpha}^{\text{GME}}=\Tr\alpha^{\text{GME}}
, for light propagating along the unit wave normal
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
. Here
c
is the speed of light and
\varepsilon_{0}
is the vacuum permittivity. As an example,
\rho\sim 0.328
rad/mm in quartz for light of wavelength
\lambda=0.63\mu
m
[
31
]
. The rotatory power vanishes as
\omega^{2}
in insulators, molecules, and dirty metals, while in clean metals (
\omega\tau\gg 1
) it goes to a constant at low frequencies
[
39
]
.

Results.
— When the Hund’s coupling
J
is weak compared to the characteristic kinetic energy of the electrons, the GME tensor can be calculated with perturbation theory as outlined in
[
39
]
. We separate the orbital and spin contributions to the GME tensor, writing

\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,D}}=\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,D,orb}}+\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,D,spin}}

(6)

for either dispersion, D = R or NR. The leading contribution to the GME tensor occurs at
O(J^{2})
and takes the form

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,NR,spin}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\omega\tau}{1-i\omega\tau}\alpha_{0}\frac{g_{s}J}{4m_{e}(v_{F}^{\rm NR})^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq 0}\,f_{\mathbf{G},ij}g^{\text{NR}}(\xi)\,\,+\cdots\quad

(7a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,R,orb}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\omega\tau}{1-i\omega\tau}\alpha_{0}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq 0}\frac{J}{\hbar v_{F}G}f_{\mathbf{G}}\quantity[g_{\perp}^\textrm{R,orb}\qty(\xi) P^\perp_{\vec G , ij}
+g_{\|}^\textrm{R,orb}\qty(\xi) P^\parallel_{\vec G , ij} ]+\cdots

(7b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,R,spin}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\omega\tau}{1-i\omega\tau}\alpha_{0}\frac{g_{s}J}{4m_{e}v_{F}^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq 0}\bigg(f_{\mathbf{G}}\quantity[g_{\perp}^\textrm{R,spin}\qty(\xi) P^\perp_{\vec G , ij}
+g_{\|}^\textrm{R,spin}\qty(\xi) P^\parallel_{\vec G , ij} ]+f^{\prime}_{\mathbf{G},ij}g_{L}\quantity(\xi)\bigg)+\cdots.

(7c)

The prefactor
\alpha_{0}=\frac{e^{2}}{h^{2}}J
carries the dimension of the GME tensor. We introduced the non-relativistic velocity
v_{F}^{\rm NR}=\hbar k_{F}/m
in terms of the Fermi momentum
k_{F}
(Fermi energy
E_{F}=\hbar^{2}k_{F}^{2}/2m
) and the renormalized electron mass
m
. The Fermi momentum
k_{F}
is related to the Fermi energy
E_{F}
in the relativistic case as
E_{F}=\hbar v_{F}k_{F}
. In deriving the above formulas, the dimensionless quantity
\xi=2k_{F}/G
(
G=|{\bf G}|
) is assumed to satisfy
|\xi|<1
to rule out scattering among the same-energy states and the opening of the minigap. The assumption enables us to focus on the lowest-energy bands in the non-relativistic case or the bands closest to the Weyl node as being active. Terms in
\cdots
are subleading in
J/E_{K}^{\text{NR}},J/E_{K}^{\text{R}},
and
J/m_{e}v_{F}^{2}
, where
E_{K}^{\text{NR}}=\hbar^{2}G^{2}/2m
or
E_{K}^{\text{R}}=\hbar v_{F}G
is the characteristic electronic kinetic energy. We refer to these quantities as
E_{K}
when the context is clear.

Form factors
f_{\mathbf{G},ij}=i\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}(\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}}\times\bm{S}_{-\mathbf{G}})_{j}
and
f_{\bf G}=\Tr[f_{\mathbf{G},ij}]
are introduced in Eq. (
7
) in terms of
\mathbf{\hat{G}}=\mathbf{G}/G
, where
G=2\pi/\lambda
is related to the spin texture wavelength
\lambda
. Transverse and longitudinal projectors are defined by
P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G},ij}=\delta_{ij}-\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{j}
and
P^{\parallel}_{\mathbf{G},ij}=\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{j}
. The form factor
f^{\prime}_{\mathbf{G},ij}\equiv f_{\mathbf{G},ij}-f_{\mathbf{G}}P^{\parallel}_{\mathbf{G},ij}
appearing in the last equation is equivalent to
i\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}\quantity[{\bm S}_{\vec G} \times{\bm S}_{-\vec G} ]^{\perp}_{j}
where
\quantity[ {\bm S}_{\vec G} \times{\bm S}_{-\vec G} ]^{\perp}={\bm{S}}_{\mathbf{G}}\times{\bm{S}}_{-\mathbf{G}}-\mathbf{\hat{G}}\cdot\quantity( {\bm S}_{\vec G} \times{\bm S}_{-\vec G} )\mathbf{\hat{G}}
refers to the transverse component. The form factor
f_{\mathbf{G}}
shows up only in spin spirals rotating in the plane
perpendicular
to the propagation vector
\mathbf{G}
. Spin cycloids with spins rotating in the plane containing
{\bf G}
may only contribute to
\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,R,spin}}
. The non-relativistic orbital magnetic moment and
\alpha_{ij}^{\text{GME,NR,orb}}
are highly suppressed, presumably due to the absence of spin-orbit coupling and the lack of Berry curvature in the unperturbed bands.

A number of dimensionless scaling functions are introduced, which are given by

\displaystyle g^{\text{NR}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle 2\xi^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi^{2}-1}+\tanh^{-1}\xi\right)

(8a)

\displaystyle g_{\perp}^{\textrm{R,orb}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle 2\quantity(1-\xi^2)\quantity(\xi-\tanh^{-1}\xi)/\xi^{3}

(8b)

\displaystyle g_{\|}^{\textrm{R,orb}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle 2\frac{\xi(\xi^{4}-4\xi^{2}+2)-(3\xi^{4}-5\xi^{2}+2)\tanh^{-1}\xi}{\xi^{3}(\xi^{2}-1)}

(8c)

\displaystyle g_{\perp}^{\textrm{R,spin}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle(\xi-\xi^{3}-\tanh^{-1}\xi)/\xi^{2}

(8d)

\displaystyle g_{\|}^{\textrm{R,spin}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\frac{(2-3\xi^{2})(\xi+(\xi^{2}-1)\tanh^{-1}\xi)}{\xi^{2}(\xi^{2}-1)}

(8e)

\displaystyle g_{L}(\xi)=

\displaystyle-\xi+\tanh^{-1}\xi

(8f)

We plot them in Fig.
2
a.
In general, the orbital part of the

\xi

\xi=0

Discussion.
— Through analytic and numerical model calculations we established that a finite, observable rotatory power is generic in metallic chiral magnets without an inversion center. Non-centrosymmetric metals such as MnSi and FeGe posessing the B20 crystal structure and spiral-spin ground state
[
30
]
satisfy the symmetry requirements to exhibit the GME. The same compounds host a skyrmion crystal phase upon application of mild magnetic fields, and the transition can be picked up through the sudden change in the rotation angle of light passing through. Kerr rotation of light reflecting off such skyrmion crystals has been observed recently in Gd
2
PdSi
3

[
20
]
and CrVI
6

[
24
]
. Instead of a reflection experiment, a transmission-type optical probe such as performed in
[
26
]
should reveal the existence of the GME (but no Faraday effect) in the spiral phases of such compounds.

The helical Weyl semimetals SmAlSi

Distinguishing the GME and the Faraday effect

Optical activity is distinct from the Faraday effect, which also describes rotation of plane-polarized light but occurs due to broken
\mathcal{T}
and does not require
\mathbf{q}
-dependence of
\varepsilon_{ij}
. Let us briefly recap the Faraday effect. It is useful to write
\varepsilon_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}^{\prime}+i\varepsilon_{ij}^{\prime\prime}
and
\epsilon_{ij}^{-1}=\eta_{ij}=\eta_{ij}^{\prime}+i\eta_{ij}^{\prime\prime}
. The axial vector dual to the antisymmetric form
\eta_{ij}^{\prime\prime}
is defined as
G_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}\eta_{jk}^{\prime\prime}
(where
\varepsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita symbol) and the gyration vector
g_{j}
is defined via
G_{i}=-\frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\varepsilon_{ij}^{\prime}g_{j}
, where
|\varepsilon|=\det\varepsilon_{ij}
. The Faraday rotation per unit length is then given by
\tilde{\Theta}_{F}/d=(\omega g/2cn_{0})\cos\theta
, where
n_{0}
is the refractive index and
\theta
is the angle between the direction of light propagation
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
and the gyration vector
[
23
]
. In thin films, the Faraday rotation is simply related to the optical conductivity as
[
Han2022May
,
shimano2011terahertz
]

\tilde{\Theta}_{F}(\omega)/d=\sigma_{xy}(\omega)/(d\sigma_{xx}(\omega)+(n_{0}+1)/Z_{0})

(10)

where

is the vacuum impedance and it is assumed that
\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\hat{z}
. Hence the low-frequency limit of Faraday rotation is simply related to the Berry curvature of Bloch bands in the ultraclean limit where the effects of impurities can be ignored, and more generally Faraday rotation is related to the optical Hall conductivity. In contrast, the low-frequency limit of optical activity probes the magnetic moments of the Bloch bands. The related effect corresponding to a rotation of plane-polarized light upon
reflection
due to broken
\mathcal{T}
is the Kerr effect.

Optical activity and the Faraday effect can be distinguished by measuring optical rotation angles
\theta_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
and
\theta_{-\hat{\mathbf{n}}}
along two opposite directions
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
and
-\hat{\mathbf{n}}
. Optical activity corresponds to the sum
(\theta_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}+\theta_{-\hat{\mathbf{n}}})/2
while the Faraday effect corresponds to the difference
(\theta_{\hat{\mathbf{n}}}-\theta_{-\hat{\mathbf{n}}})/2

While broken

\mathcal{T}

A=T_{a/2}\mathcal{T}
, where
T_{a/2}
is a half-period translation along the wavevector of the cycloid or spiral and
\mathcal{T}=i\sigma^{y}K
is physical time-reversal.
A
preserves the kinetic terms
H_{0}^{\text{NR}}=p^{2}/2m

or

. Due to the symmetry
A

\Omega(\mathbf{k})=-\Omega(-\mathbf{k})
and the Hall conductivity must vanish; indeed, the optical Hall conductivity must vanish at all frequencies. This implies that the Faraday rotation vanishes in these cases. (In fact, for the nonrelativistic case, a stronger statement holds; upon a spin-rotation, we can arrange that
S^{y}=0

Spin spirals and cycloids (i.e. Bloch spirals and Néel spirals) are common ground states in chiral magnets even in the absence of a magnetic field, arising for instance due to the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction, RKKY interaction, or frustration. The GME therefore provides a unique transmission-based optical probe for this broad and physically important class of magnetic ground states in chiral systems.

In summary, there are two transmission-type effects which cause the rotation of plane-polarized light: optical activity (whose low-frequency limit is the GME) and the Faraday effect. Optical activity requires broken
\mathcal{P}
, while the Faraday effect requires broken
\mathcal{T}
. However, broken
\mathcal{T}
is not sufficient for the Faraday effect, as in the case of electrons coupled to a spin cycloid or spin spiral texture, as summarized by Table
2
. Finally, the contributions of optical activity and the Faraday effect to optical rotation can be easily separated by measuring along opposite directions and taking the sum and difference.

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\text{GME},(0)}

\displaystyle=0

(11a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\int[d\mathbf{k}]f^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(0)})\left(\hbar v_{\mathbf{k}n,i}^{(0)}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j}^{(1)}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(1)}\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j}^{(0)}\right)

(11b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\text{GME},(2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{\hbar}\sum_{n}\int[d\mathbf{k}]f^{\prime}(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(0)})\left(\hbar v_{\mathbf{k}n,i}^{(0)}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j}^{(2)}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(1)}\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j}^{(1)}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(2)}\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j}^{(0)}\right)-\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(0)})(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}n}^{(1)})^{2}\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k}n,j}^{(0)}

Relativistic case.
In the relativistic case, the unperturbed Hamiltonian and the perturbation are given by
H_{0}^{\text{R}}=\sum_{w}\chi_{w}v_{f}(\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{\sigma})_{w}
and

H^{\prime}=J\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}
, respectively. In order to calculate the perturbative corrections to the gyrotropic response, we first calculate the perturbative corrections to the energies and the eigenstates. We reformulate standard nondegenerate perturbation theory using projection operators of states
\rho_{{\mathbf{q}},\alpha}\equiv\outerproduct{\psi_{\q,\alpha}}{\psi_{\q,\alpha}}
instead of the states
\ket{\psi_{\q,\alpha}}
. The reason for this is simply that the projection operator for the eigenstate of
H_{0}^{\text{R}}
has a simpler representation then the eigenstate itself, and no information is lost by using the projection operator.

First, we present the the general formulas for nondegenerate perturbation theory using projection operators. Let
\{\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}\}
and
\{\rho^{(0)}_{n}\}
denote the complete set of eigenvalues and eigenstate projection operators of the unperturbed Hamiltonian. Then, it’s straightforward to see that the standard nondegenerate perturbation theory formulas for energy correction can be expressed as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle\tr[\rho^{(0)}_{n}H^{\prime}],

(12a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{n}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\tr[\rho^{(0)}_{n}H^{\prime}\rho_{m}^{(0)}H^{\prime}]}{\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(0)}}.

(12b)

The corrections to the wavefunctions becomes corrections to the eigenstate projection operators.

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{(1)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\rho^{(0)}_{m}H^{\prime}\rho_{n}^{(0)}}{\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(0)}}+\textrm{H.c.}

(13a)

\displaystyle\rho_{n}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m,l\neq n}\frac{\rho^{(0)}_{m}H^{\prime}\rho^{(0)}_{n}H^{\prime}\rho^{(0)}_{l}}{(\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(0)})(\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{l}^{(0)})}+\sum_{m,l\neq n}\frac{\rho^{(0)}_{m}H^{\prime}\rho^{(0)}_{l}H^{\prime}\rho^{(0)}_{n}}{(\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(0)})(\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{l}^{(0)})}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\rho_{m}^{(0)}H^{\prime}\rho_{n}^{(0)}H^{\prime}\rho_{n}^{(0)}}{(\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(0)})^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{n}^{(0)}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\tr[\rho_{m}^{(0)}H^{\prime}\rho_{n}^{(0)}H^{\prime}]}{(\varepsilon_{n}^{(0)}-\varepsilon_{m}^{(0)})^{2}}+\textrm{H.c.}

(13b)

It’s easy to verify that both corrections are traceless,
\tr[\rho_{n}^{(1)}]=\tr[\rho_{n}^{(2)}]=0
.

Final results.
Here we reproduce the perturbative results for the GME tensors:

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,NR,spin}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\omega\tau}{1-i\omega\tau}\alpha_{0}\frac{g_{s}J}{4m_{e}(v_{F}^{\rm NR})^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq 0}\,f_{\mathbf{G},ij}g^{\text{NR}}(\xi)\,\,+\cdots\quad

(21a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,R,orb}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\omega\tau}{1-i\omega\tau}\alpha_{0}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq 0}\frac{J}{\hbar v_{F}G}f_{\mathbf{G}}\quantity[g_{\perp}^\textrm{R,orb}\qty(\xi) P^\perp_{\vec G , ij}
+g_{\|}^\textrm{R,orb}\qty(\xi) P^\parallel_{\vec G , ij} ]+\cdots

(21b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,R,spin}}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\omega\tau}{1-i\omega\tau}\alpha_{0}\frac{g_{s}J}{4m_{e}v_{F}^{2}}\sum_{\mathbf{G}\neq 0}\bigg(f_{\mathbf{G}}\quantity[g_{\perp}^\textrm{R,spin}\qty(\xi) P^\perp_{\vec G , ij}
+g_{\|}^\textrm{R,spin}\qty(\xi) P^\parallel_{\vec G , ij} ]+f^{\prime}_{\mathbf{G},ij}g_{L}\quantity(\xi)\bigg)+\cdots

(21c)

The prefactor
\alpha_{0}=\frac{e^{2}}{h^{2}}J
carries the dimension of the GME tensor. We introduced the non-relativistic velocity
v_{F}^{\rm NR}=\hbar k_{F}/m
in terms of the Fermi momentum
k_{F}
(Fermi energy
E_{F}=\hbar^{2}k_{F}^{2}/2m
) and the renormalized electron mass
m
. The Fermi momentum
k_{F}
is related to the Fermi energy
E_{F}
in the relativistic case as
E_{F}=\hbar v_{F}k_{F}
. In deriving the above formulas, the dimensionless quantity
\xi=2k_{F}/G
(
G=|{\bf G}|
) is assumed to satisfy
|\xi|<1
to rule out scattering among the same-energy states and the opening of the minigap. Terms in
\cdots
are subleading in
J/E_{K}^{\text{NR}},J/E_{K}^{\text{R}},
and
J/m_{e}v_{F}^{2}
, where
E_{K}^{\text{NR}}=\hbar^{2}G^{2}/2m
or
E_{K}^{\text{R}}=\hbar v_{F}G
is the characteristic electronic kinetic energy. We refer to these quantities as
E_{K}
when the context is clear. The symmetric part of
\alpha_{ij}
contributes to the optical activity, while the antisymmetric part contributes to a “transverse GME” effect
[
49
]
.

Form factors
f_{\mathbf{G},ij}=i\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}(\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}}\times\bm{S}_{-\mathbf{G}})_{j}
and
f_{\bf G}=\Tr[f_{\mathbf{G},ij}]
are introduced in Eq. (
21
) in terms of
\mathbf{\hat{G}}=\mathbf{G}/G
, where
G=2\pi/\lambda
is related to the spin texture wavelength
\lambda
. Transverse and longitudinal projectors are defined by
P^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G},ij}=\delta_{ij}-\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{j}
and
P^{\parallel}_{\mathbf{G},ij}=\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{j}
. The form factor
f^{\prime}_{\mathbf{G},ij}\equiv f_{\mathbf{G},ij}-f_{\mathbf{G}}P^{\parallel}_{\mathbf{G},ij}
appearing in the last equation is equivalent to
i\mathbf{\hat{G}}_{i}\quantity[{\bm S}_{\vec G} \times{\bm S}_{-\vec G} ]^{\perp}_{j}
where
\quantity[ {\bm S}_{\vec G} \times{\bm S}_{-\vec G} ]^{\perp}={\bm{S}}_{\mathbf{G}}\times{\bm{S}}_{-\mathbf{G}}-\mathbf{\hat{G}}\cdot\quantity( {\bm S}_{\vec G} \times{\bm S}_{-\vec G} )\mathbf{\hat{G}}
refers to the transverse component. The form factor
f_{\mathbf{G}}
shows up only in spin spirals rotating in the plane
perpendicular
to the propagation vector
\mathbf{G}
. Spin cycloids with spins rotating in the plane containing
{\bf G}
may only contribute to
\alpha_{ij}^{\textrm{GME,R,spin}}
. The non-relativistic orbital magnetic moment and
\alpha_{ij}^{\text{GME,NR,orb}}
are highly suppressed, presumably due to the absence of spin-orbit coupling and the lack of Berry curvature in the unperturbed bands.

The scaling functions are given by

\displaystyle g^{\text{NR}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle 2\xi^{2}\left(\frac{\xi}{\xi^{2}-1}+\tanh^{-1}\xi\right)

(22a)

\displaystyle g_{\perp}^{\textrm{R,orb}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle 2\quantity(1-\xi^2)\quantity(\xi-\tanh^{-1}\xi)/\xi^{3}

(22b)

\displaystyle g_{\|}^{\textrm{R,orb}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle 2\frac{\xi(\xi^{4}-4\xi^{2}+2)-(3\xi^{4}-5\xi^{2}+2)\tanh^{-1}\xi}{\xi^{3}(\xi^{2}-1)}

(22c)

\displaystyle g_{\perp}^{\textrm{R,spin}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle(\xi-\xi^{3}-\tanh^{-1}\xi)/\xi^{2}

(22d)

\displaystyle g_{\|}^{\textrm{R,spin}}(\xi)=

\displaystyle\frac{(2-3\xi^{2})(\xi+(\xi^{2}-1)\tanh^{-1}\xi)}{\xi^{2}(\xi^{2}-1)}

(22e)

\displaystyle g_{L}(\xi)=

\displaystyle-\xi+\tanh^{-1}\xi

(22f)

which are plotted in Fig. 2a of the main text. In general, the orbital part of the
scaling functions are even in
\xi
and finite at
\xi=0
. The spin parts are odd in
\xi
and vanish when
\xi=0
. Only the
\xi\geq 0
part is relevant for the non-relativistic scaling function
g^{\text{NR}}(\xi)
, whereas both signs of
\xi
are meaningful for relativistic electrons since the Fermi energy can be of both signs. Spin contributions vanish for the Weyl electrons at charge neutrality (
E_{F}=0
) while for non-relativistic electrons they remain finite since
\xi>0
. The high-density region (
\xi>1
) not captured by the scaling formulas can be treated by solving the Hamiltonian numerically.

IV
Details of numerical approach

Here we summarize the numerical calculations, starting with the numerical solution to the bandstructure. We are solving

H=H_{0}(\mathbf{p})+J\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma}

(23)

for two cases,
H_{0}^{\text{NR}}=p^{2}/2m
and
H_{0}^{\text{R}}=\sum_{w}\chi_{w}v_{F}(\mathbf{p}\cdot\bm{\sigma})_{w}
. For the relativistic case, we will suppress the sum over Weyl nodes. Final quantities are summed over two Weyl nodes of opposite chirality. We assume
\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{a}_{i}}=\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}
for some primitive vectors
\mathbf{a}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{2},\mathbf{a}_{3}
which generate a lattice
\Gamma
in the case of the multi-spiral textures and a single vector
\mathbf{a}_{1}
in the case of the single spiral or cycloid. Let
\mathbf{G}_{i}
satisfy
\mathbf{G}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
, and let
\Lambda
be the reciprocal lattice generated by
\mathbf{G}_{i}
. Let
\mathbf{k}
belong to the first Brillouin zone (BZ) of
\Lambda
. By Bloch’s theorem, a solution to
H\psi=E\psi
can be expressed

\psi(\mathbf{r})=e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})

(24)

where
H_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}=Eu_{\mathbf{k}}
and
H_{\mathbf{k}}=e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}He^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
is the Bloch Hamiltonian at
\mathbf{k}
. We let

u_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}\in\Lambda}u_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}},\qquad\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}=\sum_{\mathbf{G}\in\Lambda}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}.

(25)

For the non-relativistic and relativistic cases, respectively, we have

\displaystyle H^{\text{NR}}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\hbar^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})^{2}/2m+J\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}e^{i\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}})u_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

(26a)

\displaystyle H^{\text{R}}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime},w}(\hbar\chi_{w}v_{f}\delta_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})+J\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}e^{i\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{r}})\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}\,\,u_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

(26b)

which may be written as
[\mathsf{H}^{\text{NR}/\text{R}}_{\mathbf{k}}]_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}u_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}=E_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}}
with

\displaystyle[\mathsf{H}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{NR}}]_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}\frac{\hbar^{2}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})^{2}}{2m}+J\mathbf{\sum}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{G}}\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}

(27a)

\displaystyle[\mathsf{H}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{R}}]_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w}(\hbar\chi_{w}v_{f}\delta_{\mathbf{G},\mathbf{G}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})+J\mathbf{\sum}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}}\delta_{\mathbf{G}^{\prime}+\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime},\mathbf{G}})\cdot\mathbf{\sigma}.

(27b)

We solve this in a plane wave expansion and impose a cutoff
|\mathbf{G}|<G_{max}
. For convergence, we ensure
\hbar^{2}G_{max}^{2}/2m\gg J
and
\hbar v_{f}G_{max}\gg J
, respectively.

The
\mathcal{P}
(
\mathcal{T}
) operation connects Weyl points of the opposite (same) chirality
[
chan16
]
, so a single pair of Weyl points is allowed only in materials with broken
{\mathcal{T}}
-symmetry, where Faraday/Kerr rotations of light are also expected
[
trivedi15
]
. Both the GME and Faraday rotation refer to the rotation of the light polarization through the medium, though their origins are quite different.

We augment the single pair of Weyl points with a second pair related to the first by
\mathcal{T}
. The Hall conductivities from the two pairs then cancel out, but their contributions to GME remain additive as the effect is even under
\mathcal{T}
. Although the coupling to the magnetic texture will eventually break
\mathcal{T}
, there will be no accompanying Faraday rotation as long as the coupling does not result in finite Hall conductivity. Though a minimum of two pairs of Weyl points are required to preserve
\mathcal{T}
, the GME calculation itself can be performed for a single pair and the final result multiplied by the total number of Weyl pairs.

IV.1
Gyrotropic magnetic effect

We recall the formula for the
\alpha^{\text{GME}}
tensor:

\alpha^{\text{GME}}_{ij}=\frac{i\omega\tau e}{1-i\omega\tau}\int[d\mathbf{k}](\partial f/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})v_{\mathbf{k},i}m_{\mathbf{k},j}

(28)

where we drop the band index for convenience. The magnetic moment is

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{spin}}+\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{orb}}

(29a)

\displaystyle=-\frac{eg_{s}}{2m_{e}}\bm{s}_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{e}{2\hbar}\text{Im}\langle\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}|\times(H_{\mathbf{k}}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})|\bm{\partial}_{\mathbf{k}}u_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle

(29b)

where
\bm{s}_{\mathbf{k}}=(\hbar/2)\langle u_{\bf k}|\mathbf{\sigma}|u_{\bf k}\rangle
and
f(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})
is the occupation factor. At low temperatures,
\partial f/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\approx-\delta(k-k_{F})/\hbar|v_{\mathbf{k}}|
and Eq. (
28
) can be expressed as a surface integral. However, we find it useful to keep it as a volume integral for an easier numerical integration. Noting that
(\partial f/\partial\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})v_{\mathbf{k},i}=\hbar^{-1}(\partial f/\partial k_{i})
and integrating by parts, we have

\alpha^{\text{GME}}_{ij}=-\frac{i\omega\tau e}{1-i\omega\tau}\frac{1}{\hbar}\int[d\mathbf{k}]f(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k},j}

(30)

where
\partial_{i}\equiv\partial/\partial k_{i}
. Next, we put the magnetic moments into convenient forms. It is useful to define the quantity

\displaystyle T_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\partial_{\alpha}u_{n,\mathbf{k}}|(H_{\mathbf{k}}-\epsilon_{n})|\partial_{\beta}u_{n,\mathbf{k}}\rangle=\sum_{m}\innerproduct{\partial_\alpha u_{n,\vec k}}{u_m}\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}}{\partial_\beta u_{n,\vec k}}(\varepsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})

(31a)

Using

\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}}{\partial_\alpha u_{n,\vec k}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}}{\partial_\alpha H_{\vec k}|u_{n,\vec k}}}{(\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon_{m})}&n\neq m\\
0&n=m\end{cases}

we find

T_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec k}}{\partial_\alpha H_{\vec k}|u_{m,\vec k}}\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}}{\partial_\beta H_{\vec k}|u_{n,\vec k}}}{(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})}.

Next, we define

\displaystyle S_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\equiv T_{\alpha\beta}-T_{\beta\alpha}=2\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\imaginary[\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec k}}{\partial_\alpha H_{\vec k}|u_{m,\vec k}}\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}}{\partial_\beta H_{\vec k}|u_{n,\vec k}}]}{(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})}.

(32a)

For the non-relativistic case,

S_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{NR}}=2(\hbar^{2}/m)^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\text{Im}[\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec k}}{G_\alpha|u_{m,\vec k}}\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}}{G_\beta|u_{n,\vec k}}]}{(\varepsilon_{m}-\varepsilon_{n})}.

(33)

The action of
G_{\alpha}
on the Bloch state
u_{n,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}c_{n,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
is understood as
\sum_{\mathbf{G}}c_{n,\mathbf{k},\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{G}e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{r}}=-i\nabla_{\mathbf{r}}u_{n,\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
.

For the relativistic case,

S_{\alpha\beta}^{\text{R}}=2(\hbar v_{f})^{2}\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\imaginary[\innerproduct{u_{n,\vec k}|\sigma^\alpha}{u_{m,\vec k}}\innerproduct{u_{m,\vec k}|\sigma^\beta}{u_{n,\vec k}}]}{(\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n})}

(34)

The orbital magnetic moment is

m_{{\mathbf{k}},\alpha}^{\text{orb.}}=\frac{e}{4\hbar}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}S_{\beta\gamma}^{\text{NR/R}},

(35)

the spin magnetic moment in both cases is

m_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}^{\text{spin}}=-\frac{e\hbar g_{s}}{4m_{e}}\langle u_{\mathbf{k}}|\sigma^{\alpha}|u_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle.

(36)

We evaluate
\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k},j}
numerically by finite difference, checking for convergence. We could also compute
\alpha^{\text{GME}}
using Eq. (
28
), in which case it is useful to note
v_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}^{\text{NR}}=\frac{\hbar}{m}\innerproduct{u_{\vec k}}{ k_\alpha+ G_\alpha|u_{\vec k}}
and
v_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}^{\text{R}}=\chi_{w}v_{f}\hbar\langle u_{\mathbf{k}}|\sigma^{\alpha}|u_{\mathbf{k}}\rangle
. The magnetic moments are calculated from using the complete set of Bloch eigenstates at each
\mathbf{k}
, obtained by diagonalizing the Hamiltonian in the plane wave basis with a momentum cutoff
|\mathbf{G}|<G_{max}
(
\hbar^{2}G_{max}^{2}/2m\gg J
and
\hbar v_{f}G_{max}\gg J
) to ensure convergence. A useful benchmark for the Weyl case is
J=0
, for which
m^{\text{orb.}}_{\mathbf{k},\alpha}=-e\chi_{w}v_{f}k_{\alpha}/2k^{2}
and
\alpha^{\text{GME,orb.}}_{ij}=-\chi_{w}\frac{1}{3}\frac{e^{2}}{h^{2}}\varepsilon_{f}\delta_{ij}
implying a rotatory power
\rho^{\text{orb.}}=\chi_{w}\frac{e^{2}}{3h^{2}c^{2}\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon_{f}
.

In general, care must be taken when integrating by parts to produce Eq. (
30
). Suppose for instance
f(k)=\Theta(a-k)
defines a spherical Fermi surface of radius
a
. Suppose also that
\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}}=c\hat{\mathbf{k}}/k^{b}
, for some power
b
and constant
c
in
d
dimensions, and consider the integral

I_{ij}\equiv\int d^{d}\mathbf{k}\,\partial_{i}f\,m_{\mathbf{k},j}=-\frac{c}{d}\Omega_{d-1}\delta_{ij}a^{d-1-b}

(37)

where
\Omega_{d}
is the volume of the unit
d
-sphere. Naïvely integrating by parts, we obtain

I_{ij}^{*}\equiv-\int d^{d}\mathbf{k}\,f\,\partial_{i}m_{\mathbf{k},j}=-\frac{c}{d}\Omega_{d-1}\delta_{ij}a^{d-1-b}\qquad\text{if }b<d

(38)

which diverges if
b\geq d
. One workaround is to split
I_{ij}
into two integrals, the first over an
\epsilon
-ball centered at the origin and the second over its complement. The first can be treated exactly using Eq. (
37
) while the second can be treated exactly using integration by parts, keeping the boundary term. This procedure agrees with Eq. (
38
) if
b<d
, in which case
\epsilon
can be taken to
0
. In all integrals we study, the analog of
I^{*}_{ij}
converges (i.e.
b<d
), so we do not worry about this subtlety. Nevertheless, it is sometimes useful to remove an
\epsilon
-ball from the origin to aid numerical stability and Eq. (
37
) quantifies the corresponding error.

Moreover, when the support of the distribution function
f
is unbounded (as happens for the helical spin spiral with a hole-like Fermi surface), it is convenient to replace
f
with
-(1-f)
, which manifestly preserves Eq. (
37
) and simplifies numerical calculation of Eq. (
38
). These considerations are mainly useful for the Weyl semimetal setting.

We list here some other numerical details. For the non-relativistic case, in the regime of small
J/E_{K}
, the band bottom varies as
\varepsilon_{\text{min}}\approx-J
due to the Hunds coupling. Therefore we fix
E_{F}-\varepsilon_{\text{min}}
(the natural notion of Fermi energy) rather than
E_{F}
itself. For the Weyl semimetal in a spin spiral, we excise a small
\epsilon
-ball around
k_{\perp}=0
and replace
f
with
-(1-f)
for the hole-like Fermi surface case to improve numerical convergence. For the same case, we found numerical convergence to be slow with the number
N_{k}^{3}
of momentum grid points so finite-size scaling was employed. For this, linear fits were obtained to the data as a function of
1/N_{k}^{3}
extrapolated to
N_{k}=\infty
. In other cases, finite-size scaling was not necessary. Results were checked for convergence with respect to
G_{max}
and
N_{k}
. Temperature was set to
k_{B}T=1
meV.

The GME occurs in materials with broken
\mathcal{P}
while Faraday rotation occurs when the broken
\mathcal{T}
results in finite Hall conductivity. Here we focus on the GME, augmenting the single pair of Weyl points with a second pair related to the first by
\mathcal{T}
. The Hall conductivities from the two pairs then cancel out, but their contributions to GME remain additive as the effect is even under
\mathcal{T}
. Although the coupling to the magnetic texture will eventually break
\mathcal{T}
, there will be no accompanying Faraday rotation as long as the coupling does not result in finite Hall conductivity. Though a minimum of two pairs of Weyl points are required to preserve
\mathcal{T}
, the GME calculation itself can be performed for a single pair and the final result multiplied by the total number of Weyl pairs.

VII
Analytical approach to the WSM coupled to the spin spiral

VII.1
Zero modes

The Weyl semimetal coupled to the spin spiral admits an approximate analytical solution. Without loss of generality, we suppose the spiral propagates along
\hat{\mathbf{G}}=\hat{z}
and write this model as

H=\sum_{w}\chi_{w}v_{F}(\mathbf{p}\cdot\bm{\sigma})_{w}+J\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\bm{\sigma},\quad\bm{S}_{\mathbf{r}}=(\cos Gz,\chi_{s}\sin Gz,0)

(40)

with
J>0
. We assume two Weyl nodes
w=\pm
with
\chi_{\pm}=\pm 1
. We treat transverse momentum as a quantum number
{\bf k}_{\perp}=(k_{x},k_{y})
and write the Hamiltonian, keeping the
\sum_{w}
implicit, as

H=-i\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}\sigma^{z}\partial_{z}+M_{x}\sigma^{x}+M_{y}\sigma^{y}

(41)

where
{\bm{M}}=(M_{x},M_{y})=\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}{\bf k}_{\perp}+J(S_{\mathbf{r}}^{x},S_{\mathbf{r}}^{y})
is a Dirac mass. We make progress by linearizing around the minima of

M^{2}=(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp})^{2}+J^{2}+2\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}J\cos\left(\theta_{\perp}+\chi_{s}Gz\right)

(42)

where
k_{x}+ik_{y}=k_{\perp}e^{i\theta_{\perp}}
. The minima occur at
Gz_{n}=2\pi\left(n+\frac{\delta_{\chi_{w},1}}{2}\right)-\theta_{\perp}\chi_{s}
. Near each position
z_{n}
, localized modes may be described to first order in
z-z_{n}
by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H\approx-i\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}\sigma^{z}\partial_{z}+(\chi_{w}e^{i\theta_{\perp}}\{\hbar v_{f}k_{\perp}-J[1+i\chi_{s}G(z-z_{n})]\}\sigma^{-}+\text{H.c.}).

(43)

The phase factors
e^{\pm i\theta_{\perp}}
can be absorbed through rotation
\sigma^{\pm}\rightarrow\sigma^{\pm}e^{\pm i\theta_{\perp}}
:

\displaystyle H\approx\chi_{w}\Bigl[-i\hbar v_{F}\sigma^{z}\partial_{z}+(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J)\sigma^{x}-\chi_{s}JG(z-z_{n})\sigma^{y}\Bigr].

(44)

Next, we assume that the eigenstates of
H
are simultaneous eigenstates of
\sigma^{x}
with eigenvalue
s
:

\displaystyle H\approx i\chi_{w}\sigma^{z}\Bigl[-\hbar v_{F}\partial_{z}+s\chi_{s}JG(z-z_{n})\Bigr]+s\chi_{w}(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J).

(45)

For
s=-\chi_{s}
, we find Jackiw-Rebbi-type
[
17
]
solutions

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=(\pi l_{1}^{2})^{-1/4}e^{-(z-z_{n})^{2}/2l_{1}^{2}}\hat{\chi},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k_{\perp}}

\displaystyle=-\chi_{w}\chi_{s}(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J).

(46)

where
l_{1}^{2}=\hbar v_{F}/GJ
and
\hat{\chi}
is the appropriate normalized spinor. The higher bands are obtained in the same approximation by squaring Eq. (
44
) to get

H^{2}=-(\hbar v_{F})^{2}\partial_{z}^{2}+(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J)^{2}+(JG)^{2}(z-z_{n})^{2}+\chi_{s}JG\hbar v_{F}\sigma^{x}

(47)

and solving the resulting harmonic oscillator problem. Letting
\sigma^{x}=s=\pm 1
, the (squared) energy levels are

\epsilon_{n,s}^{2}=2\hbar v_{F}JG(n+(1+s\chi_{s})/2)+(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J)^{2}.

(48)

This solution reduces to the previous case when the first term vanishes. The low-lying spectrum is decently borne out by the exact numerical solution in the large
J/E_{K}
limit, as we show in Fig.
1
. It is interesting to note that the
z
-positions of these modes are “locked” to
\theta_{\perp}
.

Despite these attractive features, the solutions we obtained in (
46
) do not obey a certain “gauge invariance” property and should be treated with caution. It is perfectly conceivable that a unitary transformation
U(\alpha)=e^{i\alpha\chi_{w}\chi_{s}Gz\sigma^{z}/2}
with arbitrary phase
\alpha
be implemented on the Hamiltonian
H\rightarrow U(\alpha)HU^{\dagger}(\alpha)
before the linearization. Taking
\alpha=1
,
U\equiv U(1)
, gives

\displaystyle\tilde{H}

\displaystyle=UHU^{\dagger}=-i\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}\sigma^{z}\partial_{z}-J\sigma^{x}+\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}\left[e^{i(\chi_{s}Gz-\theta_{\perp})}\sigma^{+}+\text{H.c.}\right]-\chi_{s}\chi_{w}\hbar v_{F}G/2.

(49)

However, proceeding as before (expanding to linear order around the minima of the Dirac mass), we find instead the localized modes and dispersion

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=(\pi l_{2}^{2})^{-1/4}e^{-(z-z_{n})^{2}/2l_{2}^{2}}\hat{\chi},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k_{\perp}}

\displaystyle=-\chi_{w}\chi_{s}(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J)-\chi_{w}\chi_{s}\hbar v_{F}G/2

(50)

where
l_{2}^{2}=1/Gk_{\perp}
, in contrast with (
46
). The lack of equivalence between the two solutions obtained is a consequence of the noncommutativity of the gauge transformation and linearization step. Interestingly, we find that the numerically obtained eigenfunctions are well fit (over several-orders-of-magnitude ranges for
k_{\perp}
and
J
) by

\phi_{n}(z)=(\pi l^{2})^{-1/4}e^{-(z-z_{n})^{2}/2l^{2}}\hat{\chi}

(51)

where
l=c(l_{1}l_{2})^{1/2}
is proportional to the
geometric mean
of
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
(and
c\approx 0.5
). The numerically obtained energy is well-fit by both Eq. (
46
) and Eq. (
50
) up to corrections which are small compared to
\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}
and
J
.

VII.2
Magnetic moments and large
-J/E_{K}
rotatory power

For the WSM coupled to the spin spiral, excellent numerical fits were found to the following forms of spin and orbital magnetic moment in the large
J/E_{K}
regime and
\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}\lesssim J
for the linearly dispersing band discussed in the previous section. They are:

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{spin}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{-eg_{s}}{2m_{e}}\frac{\hbar}{2}\chi_{s}\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\text{orb}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{4}\chi_{w}ev_{f}\frac{\hat{\mathbf{k}}_{\perp}}{(J/\hbar v_{F})^{1/3}k_{\perp}^{2/3}}

Moreover, the dispersion
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}=-\chi_{w}\chi_{s}(\hbar v_{F}k_{\perp}-J)
fits well with the numerical solution up to subleading terms which are safe to ignore in this regime. We will calculate the rotatory power assuming this dispersion and Eq. (
52
) (i.e. ignoring subleading corrections). Noting
\partial_{i}f(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})=\partial_{i}\Theta[\chi_{s}\chi_{w}(k-k_{f})]=\hat{k}_{i}\,\chi_{w}\chi_{s}\delta(k-k_{f})
at zero temperature, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\text{GME,R,spin}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{\hbar}\sum_{w}\int[d\mathbf{k}]\partial_{i}f(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})m_{\mathbf{k},j}^{\text{spin}}

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{eG}{2(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{eg_{s}}{2m_{e}}\sum_{w}\chi_{w}\int\,k_{\perp}\,dk_{\perp}\,d\theta\,\delta(k_{\perp}-k_{f})\hat{k}_{\perp,i}\hat{k}_{\perp,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\chi_{s}e^{2}Gg_{s}\varepsilon_{f}}{8\pi m_{e}hv_{f}}\hat{\delta}_{ij}

where
\hat{\delta}_{ij}=\text{diag}(1,1,0)
. The rotatory power is

\displaystyle\rho_{\|}^{\text{R,spin}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{s}\alpha_{\text{FS}}\frac{g_{s}E_{F}G}{4\pi m_{e}v_{F}c}

(54)

and
\rho_{\perp}^{\text{R,spin}}=\rho_{\|}^{\text{R,spin}}/2
. Meanwhile,

\displaystyle\alpha_{ij}^{\text{GME,R,orb}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e}{\hbar}\sum_{\chi_{w}}\int[d\mathbf{k}]\partial_{i}f(\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}})m_{\mathbf{k},j}^{\text{orb}}

(55)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e^{2}\chi_{s}v_{f}G}{4\hbar(J/\hbar v_{f})^{1/3}(2\pi)^{3}}\sum_{\chi_{w}}\int k_{\perp}^{1/3}\,dk_{\perp}\,d\theta\,\delta(k-k_{f})\hat{k}_{\perp,i}\hat{k}_{\perp,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\chi_{s}}{4}\frac{e^{2}}{h^{2}}(\hbar v_{f}G)\hat{\delta}_{ij}+O(E_{F}^{2}/J^{2})

and the rotatory power is

\displaystyle\rho_{\|}^{\text{R,orb}}

\displaystyle=\chi_{s}\alpha_{\text{FS}}\frac{v_{F}G}{4\pi c}

(56)

assuming
E_{F}\ll J
, with
\rho_{\perp}^{\text{R,orb}}=\rho_{\|}^{\text{R,orb}}/2
.

VIII
Materials & estimates

In this section we identify a few material candidates for unambiguous observation of the GME induced by chiral magnetic textures. In order to unambiguously attribute the GME to the chiral magnetic texture, it is desirable to find candidates which are centrosymmetric in their paramagnetic phases and only become globally non-centrosymmetric upon magnetic ordering. Examples of such materials are centrosymmetric skyrmion materials or centrosymmetric helimagnets. The origin of chiral spin textures in centrosymmetric materials is a frustrated longer-range spin-spin interactions such the RKKY interaction induced by itinerant electrons, rather than the Dzyaloshinskii–Moriya interaction (DMI) common to non-centrosymmetric magnets. In conclusion, frustrated centrosymmetric magnets are ideal candidates for a sharp demonstration of the GME. By global symmetry considerations, the GME vanishes identically in the centrosymmetric phases at high temperature and field and only becomes nonvanishing in the chiral magnet phases (see Fig.
