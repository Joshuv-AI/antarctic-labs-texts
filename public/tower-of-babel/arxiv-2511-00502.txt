Impact of Antenna Arrays Misalignment on the Near Field Distance in Terahertz Communications
Peng Zhang, Vitaly Petrov, Emil Björnson

Abstract
The extremely short wavelength of terahertz (THz) communications leads to an extended radiative near-field region, in which some canonical far-field assumptions fail. Existing near-field boundary formulations ( Fraunhofer distance) for uniform linear/planar array (ULA/UPA) configurations assume ideal alignment between transceivers, overlooking practical misalignments caused by mobility or mechanical imperfections. This paper addresses this critical gap by analyzing the impact of spatial misalignment on near-field distance calculations in THz systems. We derive exact analytical expressions and simplified approximations for the near-field boundary in both ULA–ULA and UPA–UPA configurations under arbitrary misalignment offsets. Through numerical simulations, we validate our theoretical models and quantify how misalignment reshapes the near-field region. These findings provide essential guidelines for optimizing THz system deployment in realistic scenarios.



I
Introduction

Terahertz (THz,
300
GHz–
3
THz) wireless communications are considered a promising solution for enabling highly-directional ultra-high-capacity links in future networks
[
1
]
. However, due to a combination of (i) extremely short wavelength of the THz signal and (ii) the use of cm-scale antennas to achieve the desired communication range, the so-called “radiative near-field region” in THz communications may expand over several tens (or, ultimately, hundreds) of meters
[
2
,
3
]
. Within the radiative near field, conventional far-field approximations (such as the “plane wave assumption”
[
4
]
) do not hold, requiring both near-field-specific approaches to model the signal propagation, as well as novel near-field specific communication techniques
[
5
]
. Hence, an accurate establishment of the near-field region to far-field region boundaries for a prospective deployment becomes essential.

Traditionally, the boundary between the radiative near field and the far field is established based on ensuring the maximum allowable phase difference between the planar wave model and spherical wave model does not exceed
\pi/8
. This criterion is often referred to as the
“near-field distance”
or, specifically,
Fraunhofer
distance
[
4
,
6
]
. The expression for such near-field distance,
d_{\text{F}}
, has been originally established for a link between a point-source transmitter (i.e., an access point, AP) and a fixed-aperture receiver (i.e., a user equipment, UE). Since then, different expressions for the near-field distance have been proposed for various geometric setups involving uniform linear antenna arrays (ULAs) or uniform planar antenna arrays (UPAs). Specifically, the authors in
[
7
]
(among others) derived the near-field distance for a ULA–ULA link, while the authors in
[
8
]
(among others) analyzed a UPA–UPA setup.

Beyond phase-based criteria, alternative definitions of the near-field boundary have been proposed from power, multiplexing, capacity, and finite-depth beamfocusing perspectives
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
. These include the critical distance and uniform-power distance
[
9
,
10
]
, the effective Rayleigh and Björnson distances for maintaining beamforming gain
[
11
,
12
]
, the effective multiplexing distance and eigenvalue-based thresholds for supporting spatial streams
[
13
,
14
]
, and capacity-based definitions comparing spherical and planar wave models
[
15
]
.

However, we observe an important gap in the prior studies.
Specifically, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, all the prior studies deriving the near-field distance for the link between two ULAs or two UPAs (including
[
7
,
8
,
10
]
, among others) assume perfect alignment between the antennas. Meanwhile, in realistic THz communication scenarios, both the AP and the UE will be equipped with antenna arrays that can often be misaligned due to the UE mobility. We therefore aim to address this essential gap in the present study.

A specific challenge arises here since a purely geometric approach (used in
[
8
,
12
]
, among others) is not directly applicable under misalignment. This is because, in such cases, the geometrically longest path between the antennas is not always the one with the largest phase shift in the received signal. Instead, the delay impact from the phase shifters must be accounted for as well, as further discussed in the paper.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study deriving closed-form expressions for near-field distance between
two misaligned antenna arrays
(ULAs and UPAs) under UE rotations.
The key contributions of this work thus are:

•

Closed-form exact and approximate
expressions for the near-field distance
in wireless communications between two ULAs or two UPAs
under various UE rotations
.

•

The near-field distance expressions get
numerically elaborated
to: (i) verify them through simulations, and (ii) quantify the impact of antenna misalignment on the near-field to far-field boundary in THz communications.

2

and

\phi

\mathbf{R}_{z}(\phi)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi&0\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

III
Near-Field Distance Calculation

We derive the near-field distance for the ULA misalignment scenario in Sec.
III-A
. We later analyze the UPA misalignment scenario with the UE rotation in only one plane (Sec.
III-B
) and the UE rotation in two planes (Sec.

III-A

ULA Setup with Antenna Misalignment

Fig. 2
:
ULA scenario with antenna misalignment (analyzed in Sec.
III-A
).

Consider the THz-ULA scenario in Fig.
2
, where the UE misalignment is characterized by the rotation angle
\theta\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
. According to the geometric free space propagation assumption
[
17
]
, for each AP-UE antenna element pair, the phase
\phi_{n_{2},n_{1}}
,
\forall n_{1}=1,\dots,N_{1},n_{2}=1,\dots,N_{2}
between the
n_{1}
-th element of the AP and the
n_{2}
-th element is

\phi_{n_{2},n_{1}}=-2\pi r_{n_{2},n_{1}}/\lambda,

(3)

where
r_{n_{2},n_{1}}
is the distance between the
n_{1}
-th antenna element of the AP and the
n_{2}
-th antenna element of the UE.

To steer the beam at an angle
\theta
off the boresight (positive
\theta
indicates rightward steering and counterclockwise UE rotation, while negative
\theta
indicates leftward steering and clockwise rotation
[
18
]
), the phase difference between two adjacent UE antenna elements,
\Delta\phi
, should satisfy

\Delta\phi=\tilde{\phi}_{n_{2}+1}-\tilde{\phi}_{n_{2}}=\left(2\pi/\lambda\right)\left(\lambda\sin(\theta)/2\right),

(4)

where
\tilde{\phi}_{n_{2}},\forall n_{2}={1,\dots,N_{2}-1}
denotes the phase compensation required for the
n_{2}
-th antenna element.
Therefore, the antenna element
n_{2}=N_{2}
requires the least phase compensation, i.e.,
\tilde{\phi}_{N_{2}}=0
, while the antenna element
n_{2}=0
requires the most phase compensation to align with the desired beam direction. Based on the recursive relationship in (
4
), the phase compensation for the
n_{2}
-th antenna element can be given as

\tilde{\phi}_{n_{2}}=-2\pi n_{2}D_{2}\sin(\theta)/(N_{2}\lambda).

(5)

Therefore, the adjusted phase between the
n_{1}
-th element of the AP and the
n_{2}
-th element of the UE can be expressed as

\tilde{\phi}_{n_{2},n_{1}}=\phi_{n_{2},n_{1}}+\tilde{\phi}_{n_{2}}=-2\pi\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}/\lambda,

(6)

where

\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}=r_{n_{2},n_{1}}+\underset{\triangleq r_{\mathrm{add},n_{2}}}{\underbrace{n_{2}D_{2}\sin(\theta)/N_{2}}}.

(7)

From (
6
) and (
7
), phase compensation can be equivalently interpreted as distance compensation. To determine the near-field distance, the maximum phase difference must not exceed
\pi/8
, which corresponds to a maximum
effective distance
variation of
\lambda/16
. Thus, the near-field distance be formulated as

d=d_{\text{F}}^{\text{(ULA)}},\quad\mathrm{s}.\mathrm{t}.\max_{n_{2},n_{1}}\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}-\min_{n_{2},n_{1}}\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}=\lambda/16.

(8)

To obtain the maximum and minimum effective distances, we introduce two projected positions of the UE along the steered beam direction, denoted as
\text{UE}^{\prime}
and
\text{UE}^{\prime\prime}
, as in Fig.
2
. Let
r^{\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}
and
r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}
denote the distances from the
n_{2}
-th element of
\text{UE}^{\prime}
and
\text{UE}^{\prime\prime}
to the
n_{1}
-th element of the AP, respectively. Additionally, we define
r_{\text{sub},n_{2}}
to represent the “reverse” phase compensation of the
n_{2}
-th element of the UE, which satisfies the following relationship with
r_{\text{add},n_{2}}

r_{\text{add},n_{2}}+r_{\text{sub},n_{2}}\equiv D_{2}|\sin\theta|,\quad\forall n_{2}=1,\dots,N_{2}.

(9)

According to the triangle inequality in Fig.
2
, we obtain

\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}=r_{n_{2},n_{1}}+r_{\text{add},n_{2}}\geq r^{\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}},

(10)

and

r_{n_{2},n_{1}}\leq r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}+r_{\text{sub},n_{2}}.

(11)

By adding
r_{\text{add},n_{2}}
to both sides of (
11
), we obtain

\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\leq r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}+r_{\text{sub},n_{2}}+r_{\text{add},n_{2}}=r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}+D_{2}|\sin\theta|.

(12)

Therefore, the range of
\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}
is given by

r^{\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\leq\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\leq r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}+D_{2}|\sin\theta|.

(13)

According to the geometric relationships (Fig.
2
), the maximum value of
\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}
is attained when
n_{1}=1,n_{2}=N_{2}
, hence

\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\max}=\left(r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\max}+D_{2}|\sin\theta|,

(14)

where

\left(r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\max}\!=\!\sqrt{\left(\frac{D_{1}}{2}\!+\!\frac{D_{2}}{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}\!+\!\left(d\!-\!\frac{D_{2}}{2}\left|\sin\theta\right|\right)^{2}}.

(15)

and the minimum value of
\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}
is attained when
n_{1}=n_{2}+(D_{1}-D_{2})/2
. Without loss of generality, we set
n_{1}=N_{1}/2
and
n_{2}=N_{2}/2
,
\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\min}
can then be expressed as

\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\min}=d+D_{2}|\sin\theta|/2,

(16)

By defining
\tilde{d}\triangleq d-D_{2}|\sin\theta|/2
and utilizing the first-order Taylor expansion
\sqrt{1+x}\approx 1+x/2+\mathcal{O}(x)
, we have

\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\max}\approx\tilde{d}+\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}/(8\tilde{d})+D_{2}|\sin\theta|.

(17)

Therefore, the equation in (
8
) can be rewritten as

\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\max}-\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\min}=\frac{\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}}{8\tilde{d}}=\frac{\lambda}{16}.

(18)

Substituting
\tilde{d}\triangleq d-D_{2}|\sin\theta|/2
,
d=d_{\text{F}}^{\text{(ULA)}}
into (
18
), the near-field distance can be expressed as

d_{\text{F}}^{\text{(ULA)}}=2\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}/\lambda+D_{2}\left|\sin\theta\right|/2.

(19)

By symmetry, this equation also holds for the case of
\theta<0
. Moreover, at THz frequencies, where
\lambda
(typically
0.1
–
1
mm) is much smaller than the physical aperture sizes
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
(usually several millimeters to a few centimeters), the first term in the expression dominates, while the second term, being independent of
\lambda
, can be approximated as negligible.
Thus, the near-field distance for the ULA–ULA case with the UE rotation angle
\theta
is approximated as

d_{\text{F,approx}}^{\text{(ULA)}}=2\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}/\lambda.

(20)

Substituting
\theta=0
, the expression reduces to the case without misalignment, given by

d_{\text{F}}=2(D_{1}+D_{2})^{2}/\lambda
, which is consistent with the expression previously given in
[
7
]
.

III-B

UPA Misalignment with UE Rotation over One Plane

Fig. 3
:
UPA setup with the UE rotation over one plane (Sec.
III-B
).

Consider the THz-UPA scenario in Fig.
3
, where the UE misalignment is characterized by a rotation angle
\theta
around the
x
-axis. Let
r_{m,n}^{i,j}
and
\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j}
,
\forall m,n=1,\dots,N_{2},\;i,j=1,\dots,N_{1}
, denote the actual and effective distances between the
(m,n)
-th element of the UE and the
(i,j)
-th element of the AP, respectively. The near-field distance is then

d=d_{\text{F}}^{\text{(UPA,1)}},\quad\mathrm{s}.\mathrm{t}.\max_{m,n,i,j}\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j}-\min_{m,n,i,j}\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j}=\lambda/16.

(21)

Inspired by Sec.
III-A
, when fixing
n
and
j
, the original problem reduces to finding the maximum
effective distance
difference between
N_{2}
ULA-UEs and
N_{1}
ULA-APs. Define
r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n}
and
r^{\prime\prime i,j}_{m,n}
as the distances from the
n
-th ULA-UE to the
j
-th ULA-AP along the steered beam direction, as in Fig.
2
, the condition in (
21
) can be rewritten as

\begin{split}&\max_{n,j}\max_{m,i}\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j}-\min_{n,j}\min_{m,i}\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j}=\max_{n,j}\left(r^{\prime\prime 1,j}_{N_{2},n}\right)\\
&-\min_{n,j}\left(r^{\prime{N_{1}/2},j}_{{N_{2}/2},n}\right)+D_{2}\left|\sin\theta\right|=\lambda/16.\end{split}

(22)

According to the geometry in Fig.
3
, the maximum value is attained when
j=N_{1},n=N_{2}
, which is

(\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j})_{\max}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{D_{1}}{2}+\frac{D_{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(r^{\prime\prime}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\max}^{2}}+D_{2}\left|\sin\theta\right|,

(23)

and the minimum value is attained when
n=j+(D_{1}-D_{2})/2
. Without loss of generality, setting
j=N_{1}/2,n=N_{2}/2
, the minimum value is given by

(\tilde{r}_{m,n}^{i,j})_{\min}=\left(\tilde{r}_{n_{2},n_{1}}\right)_{\min}=d+D_{2}|\sin\theta|/2.

(24)

Substituting
\tilde{d}\triangleq d-D_{2}|\sin\theta|/2
, (
22
) and (
23
) into the equation in (
21
), we have

\left(\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\right)^{2}+\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}\right)/(8\tilde{d})=\lambda/16.

(25)

Substituting
d=d_{\text{F}}^{\text{(UPA,1)}}
into (
25
), the near-field distance is

d_{\mathrm{F}}^{\left(\mathrm{UPA},1\right)}=\frac{2\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\right)^{2}}{\lambda}+\frac{2\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}}{\lambda}+\frac{D_{2}}{2}\left|\sin\theta\right|.

(26)

Similarly to the path from (
19
) to (
20
),
the near-field distance for the UPA–UPA case with one UE rotation angle
\theta
is approximated as

d_{\mathrm{F,\text{approx}}}^{\left(\text{UPA},1\right)}=2\left(\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\right)^{2}+\left(D_{1}+D_{2}\cos\theta\right)^{2}\right)/\lambda.

(27)

Compared to (
20
), (
27
) provides an intuitive interpretation of the near-field boundary for the UPA case. The first term is the Fraunhofer distance in the vertical (non-misaligned) direction, while the second term captures the horizontal component, influenced by the UE misalignment through the
\cos\theta
factor.

III-C

UPA Misalignment with UE Rotation over Both Planes

Fig. 4
:
UPA setup with the UE rotation over both planes (Sec.
III-C
).

Consider now the
full-scale THz-UPA scenario
, as in Fig.
1
, where UE misalignment is characterized by two rotation angles
(\theta,\phi)
. Let
\mathbf{r}_{m,n}^{(\mathrm{local})}
, for
m,n=1,\dots,N_{2}
, denote the initial position of the
(m,n)
-th antenna element on the UE. After applying the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
, the transformed global position is given by
\mathbf{r}_{m,n}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{r}_{m,n}^{(\mathrm{local})}
. To steer the beam towards the AP which is aligned with the unit direction along the
y
-axis, i.e.,
\mathbf{n}_{y}=(0,1,0)
, the required phase compensation for the
(m,n)
-th element is given by

\Delta\phi_{m,n}=-2\pi(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y})/\lambda=-2\pi\left(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{r}_{m,n}^{(\mathrm{local})}\right)\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y}/\lambda.

(28)

Inspired by Sec.
III-A
, phase compensation can be interpreted as shifting the points
\mathbf{r}_{m,n}
on the UPA plane along
-\mathbf{n}_{y}
by a distance
d_{m,n}=\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y}
, referred to as the compensation distance. The compensated position is given by

\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{m,n}=\mathbf{r}_{m,n}-(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y})\mathbf{n}_{y},

(29)

which satisfies
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y}=0
. Thus, all compensated positions
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{m,n}
lie on the same plane with normal vector
\mathbf{n}_{y}
.

In practical beam steering, phase adjustments correspond to relative delays, typically defined with respect to a reference point. Hence, we introduce the effective compensation distance

\Delta r_{m,n}=d_{m,n}+|\min_{m,n}(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y})|.

(30)

Then, the applied phase compensation can be given by
\Delta\phi^{\prime}_{m,n}=-2\pi\Delta r_{m,n}/\lambda=-2\pi\left(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y}+\Delta d\right)/\lambda,
where
\Delta d\triangleq|\min_{m,n}(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y})|
. Redefining the compensated element position
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{m,n}
with the offset
\Delta d
, we obtain

\mathbf{r}^{\prime}_{m,n}=\mathbf{r}_{m,n}-d^{\prime}_{m,n}\mathbf{n}_{y}=\mathbf{r}_{m,n}-(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y})\mathbf{n}_{y}-\Delta d\mathbf{n}_{y}.

(31)

Since
\mathbf{r}_{m,n}-(\mathbf{r}_{m,n}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y})\mathbf{n}_{y}
lies in the plane
\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{y}=0
, and
-\Delta d\mathbf{n}_{y}
is a uniform shift along
\mathbf{n}_{y}
, the normal vector remains unchanged as
\mathbf{n}_{y}
. We refer to this shifted plane as the
effective plane
. Inspired by Sec.
III-A
and Sec.
III-B
, we approximate the solution by computing the near-field distance between the effective plane
\text{UE}^{\prime}
and the AP. A proof of the validity of this approximation is given in Appendix.

Redrawing Fig.
1
, the updated diagram in Fig.
4
illustrates the relationship between the effective plane
\text{UE}^{\prime}
, the original UE, and the AP, along with the distances
r^{i,j}_{m,n}
,
r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n}
, and
\Delta r_{m,n}
. Without loss of generality, the AP element coordinates are defined as
P=(x_{\text{AP}},d,z_{\text{AP}})
, where
x_{\text{AP}},z_{\text{AP}}\in\left[-D_{1}/2,D_{1}/2\right]
, and the UE element coordinates (before rotation) are given by
(x,0,z)
, where
x,z\in\left[-D_{2}/2,D_{2}/2\right]
. After rotation, the UE element coordinates
E=(x_{\text{UE}},y_{\text{UE}},x_{\text{UE}})
can be derived using the rotation matrix
\mathbf{R}
as
x_{\text{UE}}=x\cos\phi+z\sin\phi\sin\theta
,
y_{\text{UE}}=x\sin\phi-z\cos\phi\sin\theta
, and
z_{\text{UE}}=z\cos\theta
.

.

According to (
31
), the coordinates on the effective plane can be expressed as
E^{\prime}=(x_{\text{UE}},-\Delta d,z_{\text{UE}})
, where
\Delta d={D_{2}}\left(|\cos\phi\sin\theta|+|\sin\phi|\right)/2
. Given the parallel relationship between the
\text{UE}^{\prime}
and AP planes, the minimum distance can be expressed as

(r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n})_{\min}=d+\Delta d.

(32)

Furthermore, the maximum distance can be written as

(r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n})_{\max}=\sqrt{(r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n})^{2}_{\min}+d_{e}^{2}},

(33)

where
d_{e}\triangleq\max_{E^{\prime\prime},P}d(E^{\prime\prime},P)
denotes the maximum distance between the projection of
\text{UE}^{\prime}
on the AP, denoted as
E^{\prime\prime}=(x_{\text{UE}},d,z_{\text{UE}})
, and a point
P
on the AP. Applying the first-order Taylor expansion
\sqrt{1+x}\approx 1+x/2+\mathcal{O}(x^{2})
to

(r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n})_{\max}\approx r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n}\left(1+\frac{d_{e}^{2}}{2(r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n})^{2}}\right)=r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n}+\frac{d_{e}^{2}}{2r^{\prime i,j}_{m,n}}.

(34)

Substituting (
32
) and (
34
) into (
21
), and further approximating with
d\gg\Delta d
, we obtain

d_{\text{F,approx}}^{\text{(UPA,2)}}=8d_{e}^{2}/\lambda.

(35)

According to the geometric relationships in Fig.
4
, we define
x_{\text{UE}}=D_{2}\operatorname{sgn}(\cos\phi)/2
,
z_{\text{UE}}=D_{2}\operatorname{sgn}(\sin\phi\cos\theta)/2
,
x_{\text{AP}}=-D_{1}\operatorname{sgn}(x_{\text{UE}})/2,
and
z_{\text{AP}}=-D_{1}\operatorname{sgn}(z_{\text{UE}})/2
, where
\operatorname{sgn}(\cdot)
is the sign function. Hence, the maximum distance
d(E^{\prime\prime},P)
is

d_{e}=\sqrt{\left(D_{1}/2+|x_{\text{UE}}|\right)^{2}+\left(D_{1}/2+|z_{\text{UE}}|\right)^{2}},

(36)

where

V
Conclusion

In this paper, closed-form exact and approximate expressions are derived for the near-field distance in THz communications with different antenna array types (ULA and UPA) and under various rotations of the UE. Unlike prior alignment-based models, our analysis incorporates realistic spatial deviations caused by the UE rotation. An accurate estimation of the near-field distance is essential to use the most appropriate and efficient (near-field versus far-field) wavefront engineering methods in next-generation wireless communication systems.

Our numerical study concludes that the array misalignment significantly affects the near-field distance in THz communications. Specifically, the UE rotation decreases the near-field distance by
\leq
55.6\%
for ULAs and
\leq
27.8\%
for UPAs, leading to up to tens of meters of mismatch with the state-of-the-art alignment-based formulas. Importantly, our analytical framework is not bounded to the THz band and can be further extended to capture the UE mobility away from the broadside of the AP, as well as other near-field to far-field boundary criteria besides the canonical
<
\pi/8
Fraunhofer phase mismatch.
