The Spectrum of Diagnostic Findings in Postcardiotomy Tamponade
Authors: Wali Kamran, Yash Rao, Kutaiba Nazif, Michael Milligan, Muhammad Aamir, Timothy Misselbeck, Nael Hawwa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Postcardiotomy tamponade can result from a localized fluid collection exerting external compression on a cardiac chamber. A high index of suspicion is required as these cases pose unique diagnostic challenges compared to typical circumferential pericardial effusions. We present 2 cases: (1) in case 1 the localized fluid collection compressed the right atrium (RA) causing functional tricuspid stenosis (TS) and mimicking the right atrial chamber itself, (2) in case 2 the fluid collection compressed the right ventricle (RV) that was supported by a right ventricular assist device (RVAD) and expanded to the extent of partial left ventricular compression.

Case Presentation 1

A 62-year-old woman with a history of severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was admitted with bilateral leg weakness and shortness of breath in the setting of Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, T3-6 osteomyelitis with an epidural abscess, and mitral valve (MV) endocarditis with severe mitral regurgitation. Emergent laminectomy and abscess evacuation followed by urgent bioprosthetic MV replacement was performed. On postoperative day (POD) 6 the patient developed a change in clinical status with shortness of breath, diaphoresis, and hypotension requiring addition of phenylephrine 60 μg/min. Vital signs revealed a heart rate of 109 bpm, blood pressure 104/65 mm Hg, respiratory rate 22 per minute, and oxygen saturation 98% on high flow nasal cannula with 40% FiO2 at 40 L/min. Physical examination revealed slight discomfort although conversant, no jugular venous distension, normal heart sounds, and diminished lung sounds at the bases. Based upon these findings, our working differential diagnosis included pulmonary embolism, postoperative ventricular failure, complications related to the MV prosthesis, cardiac tamponade, and pneumonia.

Laboratory tests showed lactate 5.5 mmol/L (normal range, 0.5-2.2) and creatinine 1.81 mg/dL (normal range, 0.4-1.1). Transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed normal left ventricular size and function, normal right ventricular size and mildly reduced function, normal prosthetic MV function, dilated and noncollapsible inferior vena cava, and what initially appeared to be an abnormal narrow tract of flow adjacent to the RA (Figure 1, Video 1, Video 2, Video 3). An intracardiac shunt was possible in the setting of the invasive endocarditis. Urgent computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest showed a localized fluid collection almost completely compressing the RA (Figure 2). On rereview of the TTE it became apparent that what had appeared to be the RA was a localized fluid collection, what had appeared to be an abnormal tract was a slit of the RA, and the flow acceleration on color flow Doppler was severe functional TS with a mean gradient of 11 mm Hg. Invasive hemodynamic waveform analysis of preoperative right heart catheterization at the time of subsequent surgery showed an RA waveform with giant “A” waves to 26 mm Hg, RV pressure 18/8 mm Hg, pulmonary artery pressure 22/16 mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure 16 mm Hg, Fick cardiac output 2.85, and Fick cardiac index 1.61. There was a diastolic gradient of 12 mm Hg between the RA and RV (Figure 3). The patient underwent emergent reexploration with sternotomy and evacuation of localized serous fluid. The fluid originated from the right pleural space and was no longer communicating with the existing pleural drain due to loculation. The shock immediately improved, and organ malperfusion promptly resolved by the following day. Hospital course was complicated by infectious and respiratory issues related to underlying chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. The patient was eventually discharged to a rehabilitation facility with residual paraparesis.

Case Presentation 2

A 45-year-old woman with a history of remote repair of atrial septal defect and endocardial cushion defect, as well as obesity with body mass index 49 kg/m2, developed progressive symptomatic severe primary mitral regurgitation as well as mild stenosis. The patient underwent elective mechanical MV replacement. Intraoperative course was challenging due to dense mediastinal adhesions and a significant requirement of blood products. Postoperative course was complicated by respiratory failure, volume overload, hemodynamic RV failure, and acute kidney injury. On POD 3 the patient underwent a temporary RVAD using a dual-lumen internal jugular cannula (Figure 4) with a centrifugal extracorporeal pump. Clinical status improved. However, on POD 10, the patient developed acute shock and respiratory failure. Vital signs revealed a heart rate of 102 bpm, blood pressure 81/54 mm Hg, respiratory rate 27 per minute, and oxygen saturation 95% on high flow nasal cannula with 60% FiO2 at 60 L/min. Physical examination revealed acute distress, tachypnea, and distant heart and lung sounds with very limited and challenging examination given clinical status. They required escalation to 4 vasoactive medications within a few hours, including norepinephrine 30 μg/min, phenylephrine 150 μg/min, vasopressin 0.04 units/min, and angiotensin II 80 ng/kg/min. The RVAD flows remained relatively stable at ∼ 3.0-3.3 L/min (pump speed, 3,300 RPM) with only a mild initial drop of 0.3 L/min. The patient was being treated with meropenem for extended-spectrum beta-lactamase Escherichia coli urinary tract infection for a few days prior to decompensation. Blood cultures were normal at that time. Based upon these findings, our working differential diagnosis included septic shock, pulmonary embolism, ventricular failure, cardiac tamponade, and thrombotic complication of the mechanical MV. The patient was emergently intubated due to progressive clinical deterioration. The TTE had very poor acoustic transmission, and therefore a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) was emergently performed to evaluate possible causes of cardiogenic shock. The TEE revealed distorted anatomy without clear visualization of right-sided chambers. A large anterior hematoma was seen. This had both an echodense and an echolucent septated component (Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Video 4, Video 5, Video 6). It exerted near-complete compression on the RV. The inflow ports of the dual-lumen cannula appeared patent, possibly explaining the relatively stable RVAD flows. Outflow ports in the pulmonary artery were not interrogated given the critical setting. Despite its anterior location, the hematoma had expanded to the extent of compressing the LV. The combination of these clinical and echocardiographic findings suggested a hemodynamically significant fluid collection. No other culprit causes were identified on TEE. The patient underwent emergent reexploration with sternotomy. Immediate improvement in blood pressure was noted as soon as the sternum was elevated. A large, solidified hematoma in addition to 900 mL of liquefied blood collection was removed with almost immediate weaning off all vasoactive medications. The bleeding was attributed to a small pulsatile arterial bleeder adjacent to the mediastinal fat that became apparent after hematoma evacuation and was ligated with metal clips and sternal bone marrow bleeding that was managed with topical hemostatic. These bleeding sources were not evident at the time of initial cardiotomy and were likely exacerbated by the postoperative coagulopathy in the ensuing days followed by the need for heparin infusion. Clinical status and organ perfusion improved over the following days. The RVAD was decannulated 3 days later. Patient was eventually discharged home in stable condition.

Discussion

Pericardial effusions causing tamponade are usually free-flowing and readily identifiable on TTE. In the postcardiotomy setting, a fluid collection can cause localized tamponade that is more difficult to identify due to (1) poor acoustic transmission on TTE, (2) the noncircumferential, localized nature of the fluid collection, (3) the fluid collection being mistaken for the chamber it compresses and occupies in extreme cases of near-complete compression of an isolated chamber, and (4) the presence of temporary or permanent left- or right-sided ventricular assist devices distorting the physiologic manifestation of tamponade. As such, postcardiotomy tamponade requires a high index of suspicion as even a slight delay in diagnosis can be life-threatening.

In classic cardiac tamponade, ventricular interdependence is related to the circumferential nature of the pericardial effusion. This physiology may be lacking in localized tamponade. Consequently, some echocardiographic features may be absent, including variations in mitral and tricuspid inflow. The focus in these situations is careful examination of two-dimensional (2D) TTE imaging for local chamber collapse. However, limitations exist in the acoustic transmission of TTE in the critically ill postcardiotomy patient for numerous reasons including pain at the surgical site precluding typical manipulation of the probe, image acquisition in a supine position, difficulty with breath-hold maneuvers, and mechanical ventilation. Moreover, an anterior mediastinal fluid collection further impairs visualization of the heart. These challenges may be overcome with the following: (1) endocardial border definition can be enhanced with an ultrasound-enhancing agent to better visualize all chambers including the atria that may be the source of localized compression, or agitated saline to better visualize right-sided chambers; (2) color-flow Doppler acceleration can highlight turbulent flow through a compressed chamber; (3) the echocardiogram can be performed and interpreted in the context of the specific clinical question and presentation, and (4) a TEE can be performed when the quality of TTE is nondiagnostic. Finally, even in the absence of imaging confirmation, urgent surgical reexploration is warranted and required if clinical suspicion is present in the setting of acute decompensation. There is no role for percutaneous pericardiocentesis in the postoperative setting.

In a single-center study of 2,297 patients who underwent cardiac surgery, 148 (6.4%) required reexploration for bleeding and pericardial effusion.1 In this study, patients were divided into those that presented early and late (<72 hours or >72 hours). Compared to the late presenters, the early presenters had a higher chance of being missed on TTE (60% vs 3%), having smaller size effusion (160 vs 640 mL), having localized effusion (92% vs 23%), and lacking classic echocardiographic signs of tamponade (79% vs 30%). Although hypotension was the most common manifestation in early cases, it was almost never present in the late cases. Both our patients presented later (POD 6 and 10, respectively), but hypotension was the prominent feature. An additional retrospective review of 29 patients with postcardiotomy tamponade from 3 institutions demonstrated that 19 of the patients had localized tamponade, with only 3 presenting with hypotension.2 In this study the average time from surgery to drainage was much longer, at a mean of 55 days. Posterior effusions were more common in this series, with LV diastolic collapse being the most common chamber involved. Our patients both had anterior fluid collection.

We present 2 patients that showcase the array of diagnostic modalities for localized postcardiotomy tamponade, including TTE (2D imaging, color-flow, and continuous-wave Doppler), TEE (2D imaging), right heart catheterization waveform analysis, and CT chest. Most patients are too critically ill to safely transport for CT scan.

Case 1 demonstrated a localized fluid collection causing RA compression and severe functional TS seen on invasive and noninvasive hemodynamics. It was difficult to identify the localized fluid on TTE as the right atrial wall was completely compressed and the localized effusion mimicked the RA in appearance. In the largest series to date,3 10 patients with RA tamponade were identified over a 3.5-year period at 2 institutions, and this represented 0.15% of total open-heart surgeries. These patients’ presentations occurred on POD 1 to 13. Although 3 cases required TEE, the remaining were diagnosed with TTE. Hypotension was a consistent feature in all patients, and some had respiratory distress. Two patients died, one due to brain stem infarction and one due to coagulopathy.

Case 2 demonstrated an anterior hematoma with severe compression of the right-sided chambers. The patient was on a heparin infusion for mechanical valve. In the presence of left or right ventricular assist device support, a significant drop in flow is usually the best and earliest sign of cardiac tamponade as these devices are preload dependent.4 Interestingly, our patient had no significant change in RVAD flows possibly related to the anatomic location of the hematoma sparing the inflow port as seen on the TEE bicaval view. Although the RVAD maintained right-sided flow, the hematoma continued to expand undetected until it reached an extreme size compromising and partially compressing the LV. This further reinforces the importance of including cardiac tamponade in the differential diagnosis of any patient with acute deterioration in the postcardiotomy setting, given the unconventional ways this may present.

Conclusion

Postcardiotomy localized fluid collections may result in cardiac chamber compression and cause clinical tamponade. This requires prompt diagnosis and evacuation given the rapid nature of clinical deterioration. The diagnosis should be included in the differential of postcardiotomy shock. A high level of awareness and a high index of suspicion are required. Urgent surgical reexploration is warranted even if imaging is inadequate. A localized near-complete compression of a cardiac chamber, especially the RA, is more difficult to visualize than partial compression as the fluid can mimic the compressed chamber. The presence of a ventricular assist device can alter the conventional clinical presentation of these patients. The clinician must utilize the entire array of diagnostic tools at their disposal, including a closer analysis of TTE for a masquerading fluid collection, utilization of ultrasound-enhancing agent or agitated saline for better endocardial definition, color-flow Doppler and continuous-wave Doppler for evaluating chamber compression and functional stenosis across the tricuspid or mitral valve, performing and interpreting echocardiography in clinical context, and a low threshold for TEE if TTE is nondiagnostic as transportation to CT scan may not be feasible.
