Abstract
. We give a unified treatment of the countable dense homogeneity of products of Polish spaces, with a focus on uncountable products. Our main result states that a product of fewer than 𝔭 \mathfrak{p} Polish spaces is countable dense homogeneous if the following conditions hold: • Each factor is strongly locally homogeneous, • Each factor is strongly n n -homogeneous for every n ∈ ω n\in\omega , • Every countable subset of the product can be brought in general position. For example, using the above theorem, one can show that 2 κ 2^{\kappa} , ω κ \omega^{\kappa} , ℝ κ \mathbb{R}^{\kappa} and [ 0 , 1 ] κ [0,1]^{\kappa} are countable dense homogeneous for every infinite κ < 𝔭 \kappa<\mathfrak{p} (these results are due to Steprāns and Zhou, except for the one concerning ω κ \omega^{\kappa} ). In fact, as a new application, we will show that every product of fewer than 𝔭 \mathfrak{p} connected manifolds with boundary is countable dense homogeneous, provided that none or infinitely many of the boundaries are non-empty. This generalizes a result of Yang. Along the way, we will discuss and employ several results concerning the general position of countable sets. Finally, we will show that our main result and its corollaries are optimal.

3.
The inductive convergence criterion

In the proof of Theorem
7.1
, we will construct various sequences of homeomorphisms (one for each coordinate). To make sure that all of these sequences converge to homeomorphisms, we will apply the so-called
Inductive Convergence Criterion
, as given by Theorem
3.1
. The intuition behind this criterion is that an “infinite composition” of homeomorphisms
\cdots\circ h_{1}\circ h_{0}
will yield a homeomorphism if each
h_{n+1}
causes “very small changes” with respect to a complete metric on the underlying space. One important thing to notice about this result is that the first homeomorphism in the sequence can be chosen without any restriction.

Several versions of this criterion can be found in the literature, but the earliest one seems to be due to Anderson and Bing
[
AB
, Theorem 4.2]
(see also
[
ACvM
, Lemma 5.1]
, or
[
vM1
, Exercise 1.6.1 and its solution on pages 533-534]
). Although all these results accomplish more or less the same thing, here we will follow the approach of
[
Ya
, page 195]
, since it is the most direct (that is, it makes no mention of open covers). However, we feel that the proof given in
[
Ya
]
does not give enough details as to why
H^{\ast}
should be the inverse of
H
(these functions are defined exactly as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
).

.

(H_{n}:n\in\omega)

Proof.

Proposition
2.3
and condition
(1)
ensure that

converges uniformly to some

. Similary, Proposition

Claim 1.

x_{m,n}\to x
.

Proof.
Pick
\varepsilon>0
. Fix
K\in\omega
such that
2^{-K}+2^{-(K+1)}+\cdots<\varepsilon
. Assume that

, and set

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(x_{m,n},x)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{d}\big(H_{m}^{-1}(y),H_{n}^{-1}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{d}\big(H_{m}^{-1}(y),H_{m-1}^{-1}(y)\big)+\cdots+\mathsf{d}\big(H_{n+1}^{-1}(y),H_{n}^{-1}(y)\big)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-(m-1)}+\cdots+2^{-n}

\displaystyle<\varepsilon

\blacksquare

Claim 2.

x_{m,n}\to H^{\ast}\big(H(x)\big)
.

Proof.
Pick
\varepsilon>0

is continuous at

, we can fix
\delta>0
such that

\mathsf{d}\Big(H^{\ast}\big(H(x)\big),H^{\ast}(y)\Big)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ whenever }y\in X\text{ and }\mathsf{d}\big(H(x),y\big)<\delta.

4.
General position: general facts

As it often happens with mathematical ideas, the broad concept of general position can be specified in many different ways, depending on the context. The following definition is the one that will be useful in the present context. Ultimately, in the proof of Theorem
7.1
, general position will permit us to construct the desired homeomorphism of a product by working coordinate-wise (so that it will be possible to apply Theorem
3.1
).

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\kappa
be a cardinal, and let
X_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\kappa
be spaces. Set
X=\prod_{\alpha\in\kappa}X_{\alpha}
. A set
D\subseteq X
is in
general position
(or is
very thin
) if

\{\alpha\in\kappa:d(\alpha)\neq e(\alpha)\}=\kappa\text{ for every }\{d,e\}\in[D]^{2}.

We will say that
X
has the
general position property
(
\mathsf{GPP}
) if for every countable
D\subseteq X
there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}(X)
such that
h[D]
is in general position.

Trivially, every product of
\kappa
factors has the
\mathsf{GPP}
if
\kappa\leq 1
. Furthermore, it should be clear that the
\mathsf{GPP}
is not a topological property, but it depends on the product structure. For example, set
X=2\times 2^{\omega}
and
Y=2^{\omega}
, where we view
Y
as a product with only one factor. It is obvious that
X
and
Y
are homeomorphic, but no subset of
X
containing more than
2
points is in general position.

The
\mathsf{GPP}
will be one of the items in the “checklist” that guarantees the countable dense homogeneity of a product (see Theorem
7.1
). In fact, the main results of this section are Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
, which reduce the
\mathsf{GPP}
of a large product to the
\mathsf{GPP}
of certain countably infinite subproducts, plus the existence of certain pairs of functions (see Definition
4.5
).

We begin, however, with two more fundamental facts. Proposition
4.3
shows that, under rather mild assumptions, the
\mathsf{GPP}
is a weakening of countable dense homogeneity; this proposition follows easily from Lemma
4.2
. According to
[
GNP
]
, the first part of Lemma
4.2
is a particular case of a result from
[
Pi
]
. The second part of Lemma

Next, we introduce the auxiliary concepts of weak general position and convenient pair. As Lemma

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\kappa
be a cardinal, and let
X_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\kappa
be spaces. Set
X=\prod_{\alpha\in\kappa}X_{\alpha}
. A set
D\subseteq X
is in
weak general position
if

|\{\alpha\in\kappa:d(\alpha)\neq e(\alpha)\}|=\kappa\text{ for every }\{d,e\}\in[D]^{2}.

We will say that

has the
weak general position property
(
\mathsf{WGPP}
) if for every countable

there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}(X)
such that
h[D]
is in weak general position.

It is clear that every set in general position is in weak general position, and that the two notions are equivalent in the case of finite products.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be spaces. We will say that
(s,t)
is a
convenient pair for
(X,Y)
if the following conditions hold:

•

s,t\in\mathcal{C}(X\times Y,X)
,

•

s\big(t(x,y),y\big)=x
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
,

•

t\big(s(x,y),y\big)=x
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
,

Given
A\subseteq X
and
B\subseteq Y
, we will say that convenient pair
(s,t)
for
(X,Y)
is
focused on
(A,B)
if the following additional condition holds:

•

If
x\in A
and

are such that
y\neq y^{\prime}
, then
s(x,y)\neq s(x,y^{\prime})
.

Notice that letting
s=t=\pi
, where
\pi
is the projection on the first coordinate, gives a trivial example of convenient pair. However, this convenient pair will only be focused on
(A,B)
for trivial choices of
(A,B)
, hence it would not be possible to apply Lemma
4.6
to it. So, one might want to look for convenient pairs that are “perturbations” of the projection. This is the intuition behind the results of Section
6
, where we will construct non-trivial convenient pairs in the context of manifolds with boundary. On the other hand, as we will see in Section

, non-trivial convenient pairs are readily available in the context of topological groups.

After constructing suitable convenient pairs, the following two lemmas will allow us to obtain the desired results (see Theorems

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\kappa
be an infinite cardinal, and let
X_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\kappa
be spaces. Set
X=\prod_{\alpha\in\kappa}X_{\alpha}
, and let
D\subseteq X
be countable. Assume that the following conditions hold:

•

\pi_{0}\upharpoonright D
is injective,

•

For every
\alpha\in\kappa\setminus\{0\}
there exists a convenient pair for
(X_{\alpha},X_{0})
focused on
(\pi_{\alpha}[D],\pi_{0}[D])
.

Then there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}(X)
such that
h[D]
is in weak general position.

Proof.

Given
p=\{d,e\}\in[D]^{2}
, define

\Omega_{p}=\{\alpha\in\kappa:d(\alpha)\neq e(\alpha)\}.

Also set
P=\{p\in[D]^{2}:|\Omega_{p}|=\kappa\}
.

Claim.
There exists
\Omega\in[\kappa]^{\kappa}
such that
|\Omega_{p}\setminus\Omega|=\kappa
for every
p\in P
.

Proof.
If
P=\varnothing
then set
\Omega=\kappa
. Now assume that
P\neq\varnothing
. Begin by fixing pairwise disjoint
\Omega^{\prime}_{p}\in[\Omega_{p}]^{\kappa}
for
p\in P
. Then choose
\Omega^{0}_{p},\Omega^{1}_{p}\in[\Omega^{\prime}_{p}]^{\kappa}
for
p\in P
such that
\Omega^{0}_{p}\cap\Omega^{1}_{p}=\varnothing
. Finally, set
\Omega=\bigcup_{p\in P}\Omega^{0}_{p}
. It is clear that
\Omega
will be as desired.
\blacksquare

Let
\Omega
be as in the above claim, and assume without loss of generality that
0\notin\Omega
. By assumption, for every
\alpha\in\Omega
we can fix a convenient pair
(s_{\alpha},t_{\alpha})
for
(X_{\alpha},X_{0})
focused on
(\pi_{\alpha}[D],\pi_{0}[D])
. Given
x\in X
and
\alpha\in\kappa
, make the following definitions:

\left.\begin{array}[]{lcl}&&h(x)(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x(\alpha)&\textrm{if }\alpha\notin\Omega,\\
s_{\alpha}\big(x(\alpha),x(0)\big)&\textrm{if }\alpha\in\Omega,\end{array}\right.\end{array}\right.

\left.\begin{array}[]{lcl}&&h^{\ast}(x)(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x(\alpha)&\textrm{if }\alpha\notin\Omega,\\
t_{\alpha}\big(x(\alpha),x(0)\big)&\textrm{if }\alpha\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.\end{array}\right.

Using the fact that each
(s_{\alpha},t_{\alpha})
is a convenient pair, one sees that
h,h^{\ast}\in\mathcal{C}(X,X)
and
h^{\ast}=h^{-1}
. Hence
h\in\mathcal{H}(X)
.

It remains to show that
h[D]
is in weak general position. Clearly, this is equivalent to showing that
|\{\alpha\in\kappa:h(d)(\alpha)\neq h(e)(\alpha)\}|=\kappa
for every
\{d,e\}\in[D]^{2}
. So pick
p=\{d,e\}\in[D]^{2}
. First assume that
p\notin P
. Observe that
d(0)\neq e(0)
by the injectivity of
\pi_{0}\upharpoonright D
. Therefore

h(d)(\alpha)=s_{\alpha}\big(d(\alpha),d(0)\big)\neq s_{\alpha}\big(e(\alpha),e(0)\big)=h(e)(\alpha)

for every
\alpha\in\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}
, because
(s_{\alpha},t_{\alpha})
is focused on
(\pi_{\alpha}[D],\pi_{0}[D])
and
d(\alpha)=e(\alpha)
for these values of
\alpha
. But
|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{p}|=\kappa
because
|\Omega|=\kappa
and
p\notin P
, so the proof is concluded in this case. Finally, assume that
p\in P
. In this case, it suffices to observe that

h(d)(\alpha)=d(\alpha)\neq e(\alpha)=h(e)(\alpha)

for every
\alpha\in\Omega_{p}\setminus\Omega
, since this set has size
\kappa
by our choice of
\Omega
.
∎

\Omega^{\ast}=\varnothing
; the more general version given below will be needed in the proof of Theorem

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\kappa
be a cardinal, let
X_{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in\kappa
be spaces, and let
\Omega^{\ast}\subseteq\kappa
be either empty or infinite. Set
X=\prod_{\alpha\in\kappa}X_{\alpha}
. Assume that the following conditions hold:

•

X
has the
\mathsf{WGPP}
,

•

X_{\Omega}
has the
\mathsf{GPP}
for every
\Omega\in[\kappa]^{\omega}
such that
\Omega\cap\Omega^{\ast}
is empty or infinite.

Then
X
has the
\mathsf{GPP}
.

Proof.

The lemma is trivial if
\kappa\leq\omega
, so assume that
\kappa
is uncountable. Set
\lambda=|\Omega^{\ast}|
, and fix
\Omega^{\ast}_{\alpha}\in[\Omega^{\ast}]^{\omega}
for
\alpha\in\lambda
such that
\Omega^{\ast}_{\alpha}\cap\Omega^{\ast}_{\beta}=\varnothing
whenever
\alpha\neq\beta
and
\bigcup_{\alpha\in\lambda}\Omega^{\ast}_{\alpha}=\Omega^{\ast}
. Let
D
be a countable subset of
X
. Since
X
has the
\mathsf{WGPP}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
D
is in weak general position. Using this assumption together with the uncountability of
\kappa
, one can easily find
\Omega_{\alpha}\in[\kappa]^{\omega}
for
\alpha\in\kappa
such that the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

\Omega_{\alpha}\cap\Omega_{\beta}=\varnothing
whenever
\alpha\neq\beta
,

(2)

\alpha\in\bigcup_{\beta\leq\alpha}\Omega_{\beta}
for every
\alpha\in\kappa
,

(3)

Each
\tau_{\Omega_{\alpha}}\upharpoonright D
is injective,

(4)

If
\Omega_{\alpha}\cap\Omega^{\ast}_{\beta}\neq\varnothing
for some
\alpha\in\kappa
and
\beta\in\lambda
then
\Omega^{\ast}_{\beta}\subseteq\Omega_{\alpha}
.

Notice that each
\Omega_{\alpha}\cap\Omega^{\ast}_{\beta}
is either empty or infinite by condition
(4)
. Therefore, each
X_{\Omega_{\alpha}}
has the
\mathsf{GPP}
. Pick
h_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}(X_{\Omega_{\alpha}})
for
\alpha\in\kappa
such that each
h_{\alpha}\big[\tau_{\Omega_{\alpha}}[D]\big]
is in general position (in
X_{\Omega_{\alpha}}
), then set
h=\prod_{\alpha\in\kappa}h_{\alpha}
. Since condition
(1)
holds and
\bigcup_{\alpha\in\kappa}\Omega_{\alpha}=\kappa
by condition
(2)
, one sees that
h\in\mathcal{H}(X)
. Finally, using condition
(3)
, it is straightforward to verify that
h[D]
is in general position.
∎

6.
General position: manifolds with boundary

The purpose of this section is to show that, under rather mild assumptions, a product of manifolds with boundary has the
\mathsf{GPP}
(see Theorem
6.8
). The strategy of the proof is to apply Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
, where the
\mathsf{GPP}
of countably infinite subproducts will be given by Theorem
1.2
. To this end, we will construct convenient pairs for discs (equivalently, for the closures of suitable open subspaces of the given manifolds), then glue them together. To ensure that the gluing process will yield a continuous function, we will need to be able to choose these convenient pairs so that they are arbitrarily close to the projection on the first coordinate. For this reason, we will construct a uniformly convergent sequence of convenient pairs instead of a single one. Throughout this section, we will assume that
1\leq n<m<\omega
.

We begin with several auxiliary definitions. Set

\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\ast}=\{(x/2)-(1/2,0,\ldots,0):x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\},

and observe that
(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\ast}
and that
||v||\leq 1
for every
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\ast}
. By “wrapping the disc around the sphere,” it is possible to obtain a continuous
\psi:\mathbb{D}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}_{\ast}
such that
\psi\upharpoonright\mathbb{B}^{n}
is injective and
\psi\upharpoonright\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is constant with value
(0,\ldots,0)
(see
[
Si
, Example 7.1.7]
for an explicit expression). Since
n<m
, we can let
i:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be the embedding that appends
m-(n+1)
zeros to each vector of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. In conclusion, by setting
\varphi=i\circ\psi
, we obtain a continuous
\varphi:\mathbb{D}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the following properties:

•

\varphi\upharpoonright\mathbb{B}^{n}
is injective,

•

\varphi\upharpoonright\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is constant with value
(0,\ldots,0)
,

•

||\varphi(y)||\leq 1
for every
y\in\mathbb{D}^{n}
.

Given
k\in\omega
, define
f_{k},g_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
by setting

•

f_{k}(x,y)=x+2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y)
,

•

g_{k}(x,y)=x-2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y)
.

Set
h(x)=x/(1-||x||)
for
x\in\mathbb{B}^{m}
, and observe that
h:\mathbb{B}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a homeomorphism whose inverse is described by
h^{-1}(x)=x/(1+||x||)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Given
k\in\omega
, define
s_{k},t_{k}:\mathbb{D}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}^{m}
by setting

s_{k}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h^{-1}\Big(f_{k}\big(h(x),y\big)\Big)&\textrm{if }(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n},\\
x&\textrm{if }(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\times\mathbb{D}^{n},\end{array}\right.

t_{k}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}h^{-1}\Big(g_{k}\big(h(x),y\big)\Big)&\textrm{if }(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n},\\
x&\textrm{if }(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

We begin by showing that the functions
s_{k}
and
t_{k}
are continuous. In fact, we will need to prove something stronger (see Lemma
6.3
). For this purpose, the following two calculus exercises will be useful.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exist
R>0
and
\delta>0
such that the following holds for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

||h^{-1}(z)-x_{0}||<\varepsilon\text{ whenever }||z||>R\text{ and }\left|\left|\frac{z}{||z||}-x_{0}\right|\right|<\delta.

Proof.

Simply observe that

h^{-1}(z)=\frac{z}{||z||}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{1}{||z||}+1}

for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{(0,\ldots,0)\}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exist
R>0
and
\delta>0
such that the following holds for every
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

\left|\left|\frac{u+v}{||u+v||}-x_{0}\right|\right|<\varepsilon\text{ whenever }||u||>R\text{, }||v||\leq 1\text{ and }\left|\left|\frac{u}{||u||}-x_{0}\right|\right|<\delta.

Proof.

Start by choosing
R^{\prime}\geq 1
such that

\frac{||v||}{||u+v||}\leq\frac{1}{||u||-1}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

whenever
||u||>R^{\prime}
and
||v||\leq 1
. Notice that

\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{||u||}}=\frac{||u||}{||u||+1}\leq\frac{||u||}{||u+v||}\leq\frac{||u||}{||u||-1}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{||u||}}

whenever
||u||>1
and
||v||\leq 1
. Therefore, we can find
R\geq R^{\prime}
such that

\left|\left|\frac{u}{||u+v||}-\frac{u}{||u||}\right|\right|=\left|\frac{||u||}{||u+v||}-1\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

whenever
||u||>R
and
||v||\leq 1
. Finally, set
\delta=\varepsilon/3
. Since

\left|\left|\frac{u+v}{||u+v||}-x_{0}\right|\right|\leq\frac{||v||}{||u+v||}+\left|\left|\frac{u}{||u+v||}-\frac{u}{||u||}\right|\right|+\left|\left|\frac{u}{||u||}-x_{0}\right|\right|,

it is clear that
R
and
\delta
are as desired.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Set
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{k}:k\in\omega\}
and
\mathcal{T}=\{t_{k}:k\in\omega\}

\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
are equicontinuous.

We will only show that
\mathcal{S}
is equicontinuous, since the proof that
\mathcal{T}
is equicontinuous is almost identical. So pick
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\mathbb{D}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}
. First assume that
x_{0}\in\mathbb{B}^{m}
. It is straightforward to check that the sequence
(f_{k}:k\in\omega)
converges uniformly to the projection on the first coordinate
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In particular, this sequence converges uniformly on
K\times\mathbb{D}^{n}
for every compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.6
, the sequence
(s_{k}:k\in\omega)
converges uniformly on
K\times\mathbb{D}^{n}
for every compact
K\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{m}
. By letting
K
be the closure of a sufficiently small neighborhood of
x_{0}
and applying Proposition
2.4
, one sees that
\mathcal{S}
is equicontinuous at
(x_{0},y_{0})
.

To conclude the proof, assume that
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
6.1
, we can fix
R>0
and
\delta>0
such that

||h^{-1}(z)-x_{0}||<\varepsilon\text{ whenever }||z||>R\text{ and }\left|\left|\frac{z}{||z||}-x_{0}\right|\right|<\delta.

By Lemma
6.2
, we can fix
R^{\prime}>0
and
\delta^{\prime}>0
such that

\left|\left|\frac{u+v}{||u+v||}-x_{0}\right|\right|<\delta\text{ whenever }||u||>R^{\prime}\text{, }||v||\leq 1\text{ and }\left|\left|\frac{u}{||u||}-x_{0}\right|\right|<\delta^{\prime}.

Also assume without loss of generality that
||u+v||>R
whenever
||u||>R^{\prime}
and
||v||\leq 1
. Finally, fix a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
in
\mathbb{D}^{m}
such that
||h(x)||>R^{\prime}
for every
x\in U\cap\mathbb{B}^{m}
and
||(x/||x||)-x_{0}||<\delta^{\prime}
for every

. By setting

,
v=2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y)
and

, one can easily check that

||s_{k}(x,y)-s_{k}(x_{0},y_{0})||=||s_{k}(x,y)-x_{0}||<\varepsilon

for every

and

.
∎

At this point, it will be easy to obtain the uniform convergence mentioned at the beginning of this section.

Lemma 6.4
.

The sequences
(s_{k}:k\in\omega)
and
(t_{k}:k\in\omega)
both converge uniformly to the projection on the first coordinate
\pi:\mathbb{D}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}^{m}
.

Proof.

Simply combine Theorem
2.5
and Lemma
6.3
.
∎

Next, we will show that each
(s_{k},t_{k})
is a convenient pair which behaves nicely on the boundary.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
k\in\omega
. Then
(s_{k},t_{k})
is a convenient pair for
(\mathbb{D}^{m},\mathbb{D}^{n})
focused on
(\mathbb{B}^{m},\mathbb{B}^{n})
. Furthermore
s_{k}(x,y)=t_{k}(x,y)=x
for every
(x,y)\in\partial(\mathbb{D}^{m}\times\mathbb{D}^{n})
.

Proof.

The continuity requirement follows from Lemma
6.3
. If
(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\times\mathbb{D}^{n}
then
s_{k}(x,y)=t_{k}(x,y)=x
by definition. On the other hand, if
(x,y)\in\mathbb{D}^{m}\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
then
s_{k}(x,y)=t_{k}(x,y)=x
because
\varphi(y)=0
. To complete the proof that
(s_{k},t_{k})
is a convenient pair for
(\mathbb{D}^{m},\mathbb{D}^{n})
, pick
(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}^{m}\times\mathbb{B}^{n}
. By unwinding the various definitions, one sees that

\displaystyle s_{k}\big(t_{k}(x,y),y\big)

\displaystyle=s_{k}\bigg(h^{-1}\Big(g_{k}\big(h(x),y\big)\Big),y\bigg)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\left(f_{k}\Bigg(h\bigg(h^{-1}\Big(g_{k}\big(h(x),y\big)\Big)\bigg),y\Bigg)\right)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\bigg(f_{k}\Big(g_{k}\big(h(x),y\big),y\Big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\Big(f_{k}\big(h(x)-2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y),y\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\big(h(x)-2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y)+2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y)\big)

\displaystyle=h^{-1}\big(h(x)\big)

\displaystyle=x.

Similarly, one sees that
t_{k}\big(s_{k}(x,y),y\big)=x
for every
(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}^{m}\times\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

It remains to show that
(s_{k},t_{k})
is focused on
(\mathbb{B}^{m},\mathbb{B}^{n})
. So pick
x\in\mathbb{B}^{m}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
, and assume that
s_{k}(x,y)=s_{k}(x,y^{\prime})
. Since
h^{-1}
is injective, we must have

h(x)+2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y)=h(x)+2^{-k}\cdot\varphi(y^{\prime}),

hence
\varphi(y)=\varphi(y^{\prime})
. But
\varphi\upharpoonright\mathbb{B}^{n}
is injective by our choice of
\varphi
, therefore
y=y^{\prime}
, as desired.
∎

Next, by gluing (local) convenient pairs that are sufficiently close to the projection on the first coordinate, we will obtain a (global) convenient pair for a pair of manifolds with boundary.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
1\leq n<m<\omega
, let
X
and
Y
be non-empty manifolds with boundary such that
\mathsf{dim}(X)=m
and
\mathsf{dim}(Y)=n
, and let

be countable. Assume that

. Then there exists a convenient pair
(s,t)
for
(X,Y)
focused on
(\pi_{X}[D],\pi_{Y}[D])
, where we denote by
\pi_{X}:X\times Y\longrightarrow X
and
\pi_{Y}:X\times Y\longrightarrow Y
the natural projections.

Proof.

Throughout this proof, we will use
\mathsf{cl}
to denote closure in
X
,
Y
or
X\times Y
(it will be clear from the context which one). Begin by fixing open subsets

of

(1)

\mathsf{cl}(W_{i})\cap\mathsf{cl}(W_{j})=\varnothing

(2)

,

(3)

Each

, where

is an open subset of

and
V_{k}
is an open subset of

,

(4)

Each
\mathsf{cl}(U_{k})
is homeomorphic to

via a homeomorphism that maps

onto
\mathbb{B}^{m}
,

(5)

Each
\mathsf{cl}(V_{k})
is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{D}^{n}
via a homeomorphism that maps
V_{k}
onto
\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

The sets
W_{k}
can easily be obtained by recursion, making sure to include at every stage the minimal element of

(in some fixed well-ordering of type
\omega
) that has not been covered yet. Conditions
(4)
and
(5)
can be satisfied because of the assumption that

.

Now fix an admissible metric

on
X
, but recall that, by Theorem

, uniform convergence in

for a compact subset

of

will not depend on the choice of metric. Using this observation and Lemma

, one can obtain
(s_{k},t_{k})
for

such that the following conditions are satisfied for each

:

(6)

(s_{k},t_{k})
is a convenient pair for
\big(\mathsf{cl}(U_{k}),\mathsf{cl}(V_{k})\big)
,

(7)

(s_{k},t_{k})
is focused on
(U_{k},V_{k})
,

(8)

s_{k}(x,y)=x
for every

(9)

t_{k}(x,y)=x
for every

(10)

\mathsf{d}\big(s_{k}(x,y),x\big)\leq 2^{-k}

,

(11)

\mathsf{d}\big(t_{k}(x,y),x\big)\leq 2^{-k}
for every

Finally, define

by setting

Using the fact that each

is continuous, plus conditions

(8)
and

, it is not hard to show that

(8)
and

Lemma 6.7
.

Theorem 6.8

Let
\kappa\geq 1
be a cardinal, and let
