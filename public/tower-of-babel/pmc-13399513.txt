The CaM1‐CBP60b‐MYB77 Transcriptional Cascade Regulates K+ Homeostasis and Salt Tolerance in Barley
Authors: Yunfeng Xu, Haoran Sun, Lijun Liu, Qiyu Liang, Ling Shen, Ailing Han, Liangbo Fu, Fengyue Wang, Shanggeng Xie, Jiangyan Xiong, Yishan Tu, Zhengyuan Xu, Kangfeng Cai, Lingzhen Ye, Shengguan Cai, Zhong‐Hua Chen, Dezhi Wu, Guoping Zhang, Qiufang Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSoil salinity is a major abiotic stress that threatens global crop productivity. Calcium (Ca2+) is a pivotal second messenger in plant stress signalling, but how Ca2+ sensors such as calmodulin (CaM) transcriptionally control ion homeostasis and salt tolerance remains largely understood in crops. Here, we identified a novel calmodulin‐binding protein HvCBP60b, which is a negative regulator of salt resilience in salt‐tolerant barley. We showed that HvCBP60b interacts with Ca2+ sensor HvCaM1 via its C‐terminal domain in a Ca2+‐dependent manner. Loss of either HvCBP60b or HvCaM1 promoted the uptake of K+, increased the K+/Na+ ratio, and markedly enhanced both growth performance and grain yield under salinity stress. Furthermore, HvCBP60b binds to the promoter of R2R3‐type MYB transcription factor HvMYB77 through its N‐terminal domain. HvMYB77 then transcriptionally reduces the expression of HvHAK5, a high‐affinity K+ transporter essential for maintaining K+ homeostasis under salinity. Mutations in HvMYB77 or HvHAK5 compromise salt tolerance, whereas the salt‐sensitive phenotype of hvhak5 is rescued in hvcbp60b‐hvhak5 double mutants. Therefore, we uncover a novel Ca2+‐responsive regulatory module CaM1‐CBP60b‐MYB77‐HAK5 essential for K+ homeostasis and salt tolerance in barley, highlighting new targets of Ca2+ signalling for improving salt resilience in cereal crops.

Introduction

Globally, soil salinity affects more than one‐third of irrigated land and is a major abiotic stress limiting crop productivity (Peck and Mittler 2020; Zhao et al. 2020). The detrimental effects of salinity arise primarily from excessive sodium ions (Na+) accumulation, which induces osmotic shock, ionic imbalance and oxidative damage (Munns and Tester 2008; Lu et al. 2025). To cope with these challenges, plants deploy multiple strategies, among which maintaining potassium and sodium ions (K+/Na+) homeostasis is one of the central mechanisms (Shabala et al. 2016). Barley ( Hordeum vulgare ), one of the most salt‐tolerant cereal crops, provides a powerful and ideal genetic model for elucidating regulatory pathways that underlie ion homeostasis and salt response (Munns and Tester 2008; Mascher et al. 2017; Flowers et al. 2015; Jayakodi et al. 2020; Feng, Hussain, et al. 2025). Although substantial progresses has been made in characterizing Na+ transport, extrusion and sequestration (Zeng et al. 2018; Fu et al. 2022; Kuang et al. 2025), the regulatory networks controlling K+ uptake, particularly those mediated by high‐affinity K+ transporters (HAK/KUP/KT), remain inadequately explored.

Enhancing root K+ acquisition is essential for plant salt tolerance, and HAK/KUP/KT transporters play pivotal roles in this process across diverse plant species (Shen et al. 2015; Qin, Bai, et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2020; Song et al. 2021; Zeng et al. 2024; Feng, Pidon, et al. 2025). In rice, OsHAK5 mediates K+ uptake and long‐distance transport under K+ limiting conditions (Yang et al. 2014), whereas in barley, HvHAK1 has been characterized as a dual‐function transporter capable of high‐affinity K+ uptake and low‐affinity Na+ transport (Santa‐María et al. 1997; Feng et al. 2020). Despite extensive characterization of HAK‐mediated K+ transport in multiple crops, the upstream regulatory networks governing their activation and function remain poorly defined. For instance, Arabidopsis AtHAK5 is recognized as an essential root K+ transporter under low external K+ concentration (< 10 μM) (Dodd et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2014), which is activated through phosphorylation by AtCIPK23 and transcriptionally regulated by AtARF2 and AtMYB77 (Ragel et al. 2015; Zhao et al. 2016; Feng et al. 2020). These findings highlight that HAKs in salt‐sensitive plants contribute critically to K+/Na+ homeostasis, but it remains elusive how HAK transporters are integrated into upstream signalling cascades in salt‐tolerant cereals such as barley.

Calcium (Ca2+) acts as a ubiquitous second messenger in plant responses to abiotic and biotic stresses, generating stimulus‐specific cellular Ca2+ signatures (Zhu 2016; Dong et al. 2022; Ma et al. 2025). These Ca2+ signals are decoded by a suite of Ca2+ sensors, including calmodulin (CaM) or calmodulin‐like protein (CML), calcium‐dependent protein kinase (CDPK), calcineurin B‐like protein (CBL), and CBL‐interacting protein kinase (CIPK), which transduce Ca2+ signals to downstream effectors through Ca2+‐dependent protein–protein interactions (Boudsocq and Sheen 2013; Zhu, Cheng, et al. 2015; Delormel and Boudsocq 2019; Tang et al. 2020). The CBL‐CIPK module is well established as a core regulator of plant salt responses, particularly within the salt overly sensitive (SOS) pathway (Luan 2009; Zhu 2016; Yang et al. 2019). Beyond this canonical signalling module, CaMs orchestrate additional Ca2+‐responsive pathways by interacting with diverse calmodulin‐binding proteins (CBPs), among which the CBP60 family has emerged as a multifunctional group of transcriptional regulators (Kim et al. 2007; Sun et al. 2022). However, it remains unclear whether any CBP60 proteins interact with CaM‐mediated signalling to regulate plant responses to salinity.

The CBP60 transcription factor family is defined by a conserved CaM‐binding domain and exhibits regulatory functions primarily in plant immunity. In Arabidopsis, eight CBP60 members (AtCBP60a‐g and AtSARD1) (Zhang et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2011) regulate pattern‐triggered, effector‐triggered immunity, and salicylic acid synthesis through direct transcriptional control of genes, such as ICS1, ALD1, WRKY70, NUDT6, MLO2, BON1 and BAP (Truman et al. 2013; Sun et al. 2016, 2022; Lu et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2021). Recent studies also suggest that CBP60s participate in abiotic stress tolerance. For instance, GmCBP60A‐1 in soybean ( Glycine max ) responds to salt and drought stresses (Yu et al. 2021), and OsSARD1 improves chilling tolerance in rice ( Oryza sativa ) by activating DREB transcription factors OsDREB1A/1B/1H (Ji et al. 2025). However, in‐depth functional analyses of the mechanisms by which CBP60 proteins regulate salt stress responses remain limited.

We identified a novel HvCBP60b, binding to a salt negative regulator HvCaM1 through yeast two‐hybrid screening (Shen et al. 2020), but its functional significance and regulatory pathway of HvCaM1‐CBP60b have not been investigated. It was hypothesized that CBP60b functions as a molecular switch for Ca2+ signalling and K+ homeostasis under high‐Na+ and low‐K+ conditions. Here, we show that CBP60b interacts with CaM1 to repress the expression of transcriptional factor MYB77, leading to down‐regulation of the key high‐affinity K+ transporter HvHAK5 and disruption of K+ homeostasis under salt stress. Therefore, we uncover a novel Ca2+‐responsive CaM1‐CBP60b‐MYB77‐HAK5 transcriptional cascade that links Ca2+ signalling to K+ transport in barley, which are promising targets for improving K+ acquisition and salt tolerance in crops on marginal lands.

Results

A C‐Terminal CaM‐Binding Domain in HvCBP60b Interacts With HvCaM1 in a Ca2+‐Dependent Manner

HvCBP60b was identified with unknown function as a potential interacting partner of HvCaM1 through yeast‐two‐hybrid (Y2H) assay in our previous study (Shen et al. 2020). HvCBP60b contains a conserved domain and a predicted calmodulin‐binding domain (CaMBD) as a HvCaM1‐interacting protein (Figure 1A,B). Structural predictions generated by AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al. 2024) indicated that the C‐terminal region of HvCBP60b is the primary interface for HvCaM1 binding (Figure 1A). Furthermore, Y2H assay, firefly luciferase complementation imaging (FLCI), bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and GST pull‐down assays all confirmed the interaction between HvCaM1 and HvCBP60b in vivo and in vitro (Figure 1B,C,E; Figure S1A). HvCBP60b‐GFP alone was predominantly localized to the nucleus, whereas co‐expression with HvCaM1‐mScarlet3 redistributed HvCBP60b to the cytoplasm and plasma membrane (Figure 1D). HvCaM1 contains two Ca2+‐binding EF‐hand motifs. To assess whether Ca2+ is required for HvCBP60b‐HvCaM1 interaction, we generated a Ca2+‐binding‐deficient HvCaM1 variant (mHvCaM1) harboring mutations in each EF‐hand (Figure 1F; Figure S2). Neither Y2H nor LUC complementation assays detected interaction between HvCBP60b and mHvCaM1 (Figure 1G,H). Consistently, FLCI analysis demonstrated that CaCl2 markedly enhances, whereas EGTA (a Ca2+ chelator) significantly reduces the HvCBP60b‐HvCaM1 interaction (Figure 1I; Figure S1B). Together, these findings establish that HvCBP60b interacts with HvCaM1 through a C‐terminal CaM‐binding domain in a Ca2+‐dependent manner.

HvCBP60b Negatively Regulates Salt Tolerance by Affecting K+/Na+ Homeostasis

To investigate the function of HvCBP60b under salt stress, we generated both CRISPR/Cas9 knockout and RNA interference knockdown barley lines (Figure 2A; Figure 2G; Figure S3). When grown under control conditions, cbp60b knockout mutants exhibited growth and K+/Na+ ratio comparable to those of the wild type (WT) (Figure 2B; Figure S4). However, after 4 weeks of treatment with 200 mM NaCl, the two cbp60b mutant lines displayed enhanced growth, with a substantial increase in both fresh and dry weights relative to the WT plants (Figure 2C; Figure S5A,B), closely resembling those observed in cam1 mutants (Figure 2C; Figure S5). Element analysis revealed that loss of HvCBP60b significantly reduced Na+ accumulation while increasing K+ concentration and shoot K+/Na+ ratios under salt stress (Figure 2E–G). Similarly, RNAi lines of HvCBP60b also showed enhanced biomass and improved ion homeostasis relative to WT under salinity (Figure 2H–K; Figure S6).

Given that HvCBP60b‐HvCaM1 interaction is Ca2+‐dependent, we next examined Ca2+ signalling using Ca2+ ratio imaging. Under the control condition, cbp60b mutants and WT exhibited similar cytosolic Ca2+ concentration ([Ca2+]cyt) (Figure 2M). Under salt stress, however, cbp60b knockout lines showed a significantly lower root [Ca2+]cyt than WT plants(Figure 2N,O), indicating that HvCBP60b contributes to the salt‐induced changes of Ca2+ signals.

HvCBP60b and HvCaM1 Knockout Mutations Improve Barley Grain Yield Under Salt Stress

Because loss of either HvCaM1 or HvCBP60b enhanced barley salt tolerance (Figure 2A–L), we next examined agronomic performance of cam1, cbp60b, and WT plants grown under control and salt treatment. Under salt treatment, both cam1 and cbp60b mutants exhibited markedly improved growth at maturity relative to WT (Figure S7A). Notably, shoot biomass, grains per panicle, total grain weight and 1000‐grains weight were significantly increased in the mutants under salinity (Figure S7B–E), whereas no differences were detected among genotypes under the control (Figure S7C–E). After salt stress, cbp60b mutants exhibited significantly larger total grain weight, shorter grain length and wider grain width than cam1 and WT (Figure S7F–H).

Haplotype analysis of 300 barley accessions representing wild, landrace and cultivated germplasm revealed no nonsynonymous mutations in the coding sequences (CDS) of HvCaM1 or HvCBP60b. These findings offer potentially useful targets by knockout or suppression of HvCBP60b and HvCaM1 to improve barley yield under salinity stress.

HvCBP60b Represses HvMYB77 Expression via Promoter Binding

To further elucidate how HvCBP60b modulates salt tolerance, we examined its expression, subcellular dynamics and downstream transcriptional network. RT‐qPCR analysis showed that HvCBP60b is expressed in both roots and shoots, with the highest transcript level in the root elongation zone (Figure 3A; Figure S8A). HvCBP60b was predominantly expressed in roots. Its transcript levels peaked at 6 h under short‐term salt stress and after 3 weeks of long‐term salinity exposure (Figure 3A,B; Figure S8B). Consistent with its predicted role as a transcription factor, HvCBP60b localized mainly to the nucleus under control condition but re‐localized to the cytosol and plasma membrane under salt stress (Figure 3C; Figure S8C,D). In contrast, HvCaM1 exhibited constitutive nuclear and cytosolic localization under both control and salt conditions (Figure S9). Notably, in the hvcam1 mutant, HvCBP60b remained in the nucleus even under salt stress (Figure S9), indicating that HvCaM1 is essential for its salt‐induced nuclear export. Collectively, these results suggest that HvCaM1 functions as a stress sensor that triggers HvCBP60b re‐localization, facilitating its interaction with cytosolic partners to regulate salt tolerance.

To investigate the transcriptional regulatory network of HvCBP60b under salt stress, we performed root transcriptome analysis of three independent cbp60b knockout lines under both control and salt conditions. Compared with wild‐type plants, cbp60b mutants showed 262 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; 145 upregulated, 117 downregulated) under control condition, and 331 DEGs (224 upregulated, 107 downregulated) under salt stress (Figure S10). Notably, salt stress induced a larger number of DEGs, with 217 DEGs (126 upregulated, 91 downregulated) uniquely altered in the cbp60b mutants under salt stress, whereas 114 DEGs were shared between control and salt conditions (Figure 3D). Because CBP60 proteins bind to the G(A/T)AATT(T/G) motif (Truman et al. 2013; Sun et al. 2016; Li et al. 2021), we examined the promoter regions of salt‐responsive DEGs regulated by HvCBP60b (i.e., 217 DEGs identified under salt stress) for the presence of this cis‐element. A total of 133 candidate genes harboured this motif in their promoters (Dataset S1), representing potential direct targets of HvCBP60b. GO enrichment revealed significant overrepresentation of genes in the ‘binding’ category (GO:0005488) (Figure 3E). Sixteen genes with [log2 (fold‐change)] ≥ 3 were considered as potential HvCBP60b‐regulated targets (Figure 3G). Three MYB transcription factor genes showed dramatic expression changes, including HvMYB77 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0393680, 151.2‐fold), HvMYB101 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.5HG0441760, 20.7‐fold) and HvMYB37 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0415430, 0.1‐fold) (Figure 3G,H).

Interestingly, HvCBP60b had no transcriptional activation activity in yeast (Figure 4A) and acted as a transcriptional repressor in LUC assays (Figure 4B). Dual‐luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that HvCBP60b specifically represses HvMYB77 expression, but does not bind to HvMYB37 or HvMYB101 promoters (Figure 4C; Figure S11). Electrophoretic mobility shift (EMSA) and yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays further confirmed the direct binding of HvCBP60b to the ‘GTAATT’ motif within a 270‐bp cis‐regulatory region of the HvMYB77 promoter (Figure 4E,F; Figure S12A). We further showed that the N‐terminal of HvCBP60b binds to the promoter region of HvMYB77 (Figure 4G).

Given that HvCaM1 acts upstream of HvCBP60b, we tested whether it modulates the HvCBP60b repression of HvMYB77. Co‐expression of HvCaM1 enhanced HvCBP60b‐mediated repression of HvMYB77 in LUC assays (Figure 4D), indicating that CaM1‐CBP60b interaction enhances transcriptional inhibition. In addition, HvMYB77 did not interact with either HvCBP60 or HvCaM1 proteins directly (Figure S13).

To investigate whether HvMYB77 is required for barley salt tolerance, we generated two CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines (Figure 4H). Importantly, myb77 mutants exhibited salt sensitivity with significantly lower dry biomass under salinity compared with WT (Figure 4I,J), establishing that HvMYB77 is a positive regulator in salt tolerance. Notably, under salt treatment, myb77 mutants exhibited more reduction in K+ concentration, whereas Na+ levels remained comparable to those of WT plants, resulting in a significantly decreased K+/Na+ ratio (Figure 4K–M). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the WT and myb77 mutants in terms of dry weight, fresh weight, Na+ or K+ concentration in the control (Figure S14). These results indicate that HvMYB77 functions as a positive regulator of salt tolerance, which modulates K+ uptake.

HvCBP60b Regulates HvMYB77 to Transcriptionally Repress HvHAK5 and Thereby Reduces Salt Tolerance

To reveal the downstream components of the CaM1‐CBP60b‐MYB77 module, we examined how HvMYB77 modulates K+ transport under salinity stress. HvMYB77 is a typical R2R3‐type MYB member containing two DNA binding domain (DBD) repeats at the N‐terminal (Figure S12B), and its homologues in Arabidopsis positively regulate the expression of HAK5 (Feng et al. 2021). We found that HvMYB77 is a nucleus‐localized transcriptional activator via yeast transcriptional activity and subcellular localization assays (Figure 5A; Figure S12C). We also identified 22 MYB binding motifs within the promoter of HvHAK5 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407500) (−2000‐ATG), including the MYB core (CNGTTR) and the MYB recognition element (MRE; AACC) (Figure S15A). EMSA and dual‐LUC assays confirmed that HvMYB77 directly binds to and activates the HvHAK5 promoter (Figure 5B,C; Figure S15B). Expression analysis reveals opposing transcriptional activities within the pathway. Under salt stress, HvHAK5 expression was elevated in cbp60b mutants compared to WT plants, consistent with the release of transcriptional repression, whereas HvHAK5 expression was significantly reduced in myb77 mutants, reflecting loss of transcriptional activation (Figure 5D,E). Notably, HvCBP60b, HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 displayed congruent expression profiles, with the highest transcript abundance in roots (Figure S16), suggesting the involvement of a coordinated HvCBP60b‐MYB77‐HAK5 transcriptional cascade in ion homeostasis.

Moreover, HvHAK5 encodes a plasma membrane‐localized high‐affinity K+ transporter (Figure S17A,B). In the K+‐defective yeast R5421 (trk1▲ and trk2▲), HvHAK5 expression rescued its growth under low K+ conditions (Figure 5F). In Xenopus laevis , HvHAK5 expression significantly enhanced K+ influx into the oocytes compared to the control (No HvHAK5 cRNA injection), while as determined by noninvasive micro‐test technology (NMT) (Figure 5G,H). Expression of HvHAK5 also significantly promoted K+ and rubidium (Rb+) uptake into K+‐starved oocytes (Figure 5I–L). These results indicate that HvCBP60b likely reduces salt tolerance by suppressing HvMYB77‐mediated activation of HvHAK5, thereby impairing K+ acquisition and homeostasis under salt treatment.

HvMYB77 and HAK5 Exhibit Natural Variation for Salt Tolerance in Barley

To access the functional role of HvHAK5 in salt tolerance, we generated CRISPR/Cas9 knockout lines (Figure 6A). Salinity stress severely reduced biomass, K+ concentration, and K+/Na+ ratio in both shoots and roots of the hak5 mutants in comparison to WT, whereas Na+ accumulation was unchanged (Figure 6B–F; Figure S18). In contrast, no significant differences were observed between the WT and hak5 mutants in biomass, K+ concentration, or Na+ concentration under normal conditions (Figure 6B; Figure S19), indicating that HvHAK5 is required for K+ homeostasis under salt stress. Moreover, cbp60b‐hak5 double mutant showed similar growth performance and ion contents compared to the WT in both shoots and roots under salt stress (Figure 6G–L; Figure S20). Comparatively, crossing the salt tolerant cbp60b and salt sensitive hak5 knockout mutants surprisingly recovered the salt tolerance of cbp60b‐hak5 double mutant to the WT levels, indicating that HvCBP60b acts upstream of HvHAK5 and the improved salt tolerance in cbp60b mutants depends on HvHAK5 function in K+ transport (Figure 6M–O; Figure S18A–I). The similar tissue‐specific expression patterns of these three genes provide useful insights into root responses and ion transport (Figure S16). These findings uncover a novel role for HvHAK5 in efficient K+ homeostasis under salinity, modulated through the HvCBP60b‐HvMYB77 transcriptional cascade. To investigate whether other K+ transporters compensate for the loss of HvHAK5 in the double mutants, we examined the expression levels of several additional HAK family members (HvHAK1A, HvHAK2, HvHAK3, HvHAK6 and HvHAK27) in the cbp60b‐hak5 double mutants and WT plants under both control and salt conditions. Under salt stress, HvHAK2 expression was significantly higher in the double mutant than in WT, whereas no significant difference was observed under control conditions (Figure S21). This result suggests that HvCBP60b may negatively regulate HAK2 expression, and that upregulation of HvHAK2 in cbp60b‐hak5 mutants partially compensates for the loss of HvHAK5.

To explore natural allelic variation within the HvMYB77 and HvHAK5, we examined the diversity of their CDS in a global barley panel of 300 accessions. Two single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in CDS of HvMYB77 generated three nonsynonymous haplotypes (Hap_1‐Hap_3), representing 75.7%, 16.7% and 7.6% of barley accessions, respectively (Figure S22A,B). Among the identified haplotypes of HvMYB77, Hap_3 was enriched in landraces (52%) but absent from wild barley accessions. In contrast, Hap_1 and Hap_2 were distributed across cultivated barley, landraces and wild barley (Figure S22B). Geographically, Hap_1 and Hap_2 exhibited a global distribution, whereas Hap_3 was largely restricted to Eurasia and Australia (Figure S22C).

HvHAK5 exhibited greater haplotype diversity, with four nonsynonymous haplotypes (Hap_1‐Hap_4), comprising 3%, 31.7%, 62% and 3.3% of accessions, respectively (Figure S23A,B). Hap_1 represented a potential demonstration selection from wild accessions to landraces that were mainly distributed in West and South Asia. Hap_2 was found in landraces and cultivated barley and distributed across Asia, Europe and North America. Hap_3 predominated in cultivated varieties (64.5%) and was distributed globally, whereas Hap_4 was associated with landraces and wild accessions from arid and semiarid regions of South Asia, West Asia and North Africa (Figure S23C,D). The geographical enrichment of HvHAK5 haplotypes suggests that allelic variation within the MYB77 and HAK5 contributes to ecological adaptation.

Discussion

Ca2+ acts as a universal secondary messenger in plant responses to various environmental stresses, including salinity (Zhu 2016; Tian et al. 2020). Stress conditions trigger rapid elevations of cytoplasmic Ca2+, initiating complex signalling cascades mediated by an array of Ca2+ sensors that regulate growth, immunity and abiotic stress tolerance (Sun et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024). However, the mechanisms by which Ca2+ signals are decoded into specific transcriptional programs for plant salinity stress responses remain largely underexplored in plants. Here, we demonstrated that HvCBP60b functions as a key regulator of K+ uptake under salt stress via a Ca2+‐dependent pathway, primarily through its interaction with the negative salt tolerance regulator HvCaM1 (Figure 1; Figure S1). The HvCaM1‐CBP60b module orchestrates transcriptional regulation of the HvMYB77‐HAK5 cascade, maintaining cellular ion homeostasis under salt stress.

Barley HvCBP60b translocates from the nucleus to the cytosol and plasma membrane under salinity, facilitating its direct interaction with cytosolic HvCaM1. This association may enhance DNA‐binding activity of the HvCBP60b N‐terminal domain to the HvMYB77 promoter (Figure 4). Most CBP60 proteins, except SARD1, harbour a conserved N‐terminal DNA‐binding domain (DBD) and a C‐terminal calmodulin‐binding domain (CaMBD) (Li et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2009, 2011). HvCBP60b contains an N‐terminal DBD that recognizes the GTAATTT motif in the HvMYB77 promoter, and a C‐terminal CaMBD mediates its interaction with HvCaM1, providing mechanistic insights into Ca2+‐dependent transcriptional repression. It was suggested that CaM1/4/6 mediate phosphorylation of CBP60g, supporting functional linkage of homologous proteins in plant species (Sun et al. 2022). However, the two CaM1 homologues, barley HvCaM1 and rice OsCaM1‐1, show functional diversification in salt tolerance (Shen et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2025). Moreover, we identified no natural variation among barley accessions in coding regions of HvCaM1 and HvCBP60b, indicating that the CaM1‐CBP60b module may represent one specific regulatory mechanism that distinguishes salt‐tolerant and salt‐sensitive species.

Arabidopsis CBP60b acts as a transcriptional activator (Huang et al. 2021; Li et al. 2021), while HvCBP60b functions as a transcriptional repressor (Figure 4A,B), indicating functional divergence between these homologues. Similar interspecies functional variation is observed in CaM1 and HKT1;5 proteins. For instance, OsCaM1 and OsHKT1;5 positively regulate salt tolerance in rice, whereas HvCaM1 and HvHKT1;5 negatively regulate salt tolerance in barley (Kobayashi et al. 2017; Huang et al. 2020; Shen et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2025). Even within barley, CBP60 family members exhibit functional diversification. HvCBP60‐8 enhances salt tolerance and participates in Na+ transport (Xu et al. 2025), contrasting with the negative regulatory role of HvCBP60b in K+ homeostasis through its direct interaction with HvCaM1. Notably, phylogenetic analysis of the HvCBP60 family (Xu et al. 2025) placed HvCBP60b (a negative regulator) and HvCBP60‐8 (a positive regulator) into two distantly related branches (Branch 1 and Branch 7, respectively), indicating that functional diversification correlates with evolutionary divergence within this gene family. Given the significant differences in salt tolerance between barley and other cereal crops, it is essential to investigate why certain salt‐tolerant homologues, including CaM1, HKT1;5 and CBP60 members, exhibit opposite roles across plant species.

R2R3‐type MYB transcription factors, such as Arabidopsis AtMYB77, are well known for their roles in auxin signalling and root development (Shin et al. 2007; Zhao et al. 2014; Guo et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2020). In wheat, TaMYB77 promotes drought and salt tolerance by modulating osmolyte accumulation, stomatal closure, root architecture and reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis (Hou et al. 2025). In rice, OsMYB91 coordinates plant growth and salt tolerance, while overexpression of GhMYB73 enhances salt tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis (Zhu, Dunand, et al. 2015; Zhao, Lin, et al. 2019; Zhao, Yang, et al. 2019). However, upstream transcriptional regulators of MYB77 are largely uncharacterized. We identify HvCBP60b as an upstream transcriptional repressor of HvMYB77 (Figure 4C,E,F), suggesting the potential conservation of such a MYB77‐HAK5 regulatory module in barley and Arabidopsis (Mito et al. 2011; Feng et al. 2020). Both HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 play positive roles in salt tolerance (Figure 4H–J; Figure 6), with HvMYB77 transcriptionally up‐regulating HvHAK5 to enhance shoot K+ concentration and K+/Na+ ratio (Figure 6A–F), thereby improving salt tolerance and K+ utilization efficiency. In addition, knockout of HvCBP60b also affects the expression of the genes related to other K+ or Na+ transporters, such as HKT1;5, NHX1, NHX2 and NHX4 (Figure S24). Moreover, significant natural variations in coding regions of HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 among barley accessions demonstrated that, as the downstream component of the core CaM1‐CBP60b module, HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 are functional genes that may have been under selection pressure during the domestication of barley in favour of yield and quality under optimal growth conditions over stress tolerance.

In summary, we comprehensively demonstrate that barley HvCBP60b is a negative regulator of K+ uptake and salt tolerance via a Ca2+‐responsive HvCaM1‐CBP60b‐MYB77‐HAK5 transcriptional cascade (Figure 7). Our novel discovery represents a new function of CBP60s from plant immunity to ion homeostasis, offering gene‐editing targets for enhancing crop salinity tolerance. More importantly, we reveal a framework for decoding Ca2+‐mediated signalling networks that controls ion homeostasis and stress adaptation in salt‐tolerant barley. This opens new avenues for Ca2+‐dependent regulatory networks in improving crop salinity tolerance and nutrient utilization efficiency for marginal land across the world.

A working model depicting HvCBP60b‐centered regulatory network in response to salt stress. Under normal conditions, the expression of HAK5 is activated by MYB77 due to the interaction between CBP60b and CaM1 weakening, thus the repression on MYB77 by CBP60b is decreased. Salt stress elevates cytosolic Ca2+ concentration, and Ca2+ binding to CaM1 induces its interaction with CBP60b. This interaction enhances the inhibitory effect of CBP60b on the expression of MYB77 and further represses the transcription of HAK5 by MYB77, thus decreasing the expression of HAK5.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Barley variety ‘Golden Promise’ (cv. GP) was used for genetic transformation and expression analysis. Seeds were immersed in 2% (v/v) H2O2 for sterilization and germinated onto a filter paper at 22°C, 14 h/10 h day/night and 18°C in a growth chamber. Seven‐day‐old seedlings were grown in 15 L containers with 1/2 Hoagland hydroponic solution for 7 days, according to Shen et al. (2020). For gene expression in response to salt concentration, barley plants were grown in medium containing 200 mM NaCl and were sampled at 0, 100, 200, 300, 400 or 500 mM NaCl for 3 h. For gene expression analysis in response to salt duration, barley roots were sampled after 100 mM NaCl for the first 24 h (time points 0, 1, 3, 6 and 24 h) and then with 200 mM NaCl from 24 h onward (time points 24, 25, 27, 30 and 48 h) in a short‐term period, and 1, 2, 3, 4 w of 200 mM NaCl exposure in a long‐term period. The roots of 14‐d‐old seedlings exposed to 200 mM NaCl were divided into 0–5, 6–10, 11–15, 16–20, > 20 mm from the apex to basal roots. 6–20 mm refer to the elongation zone from root tip. In the hydroponic experiment, seedlings were harvested after normal (0 mM NaCl) or salt (200 mM NaCl) treatment for 3 or 4 weeks, at the time point when the difference in growth performance became evident. Hydroponic solutions were renewed every 3 days. In the soil experiment, seedlings were planted in 1‐L containers filled with artificial soil and treated with 5‰ (w/v) NaCl at heading stage. Spikes per plant, grains per spike and 1000‐grains weight were recorded.

Plasmid Construction and Plant Transformation

HvCBP60b (HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0569990), HvCaM1 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.1HG0069750), HvMYB77 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG039368) and HvHAK5 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0407500) were cloned from GP genome. HvCBP60b mutants were generated through CRISPR/Cas9 and RNAi techniques (Shen et al. 2020; Fu et al. 2022). Briefly, guide RNA of target genes was designed using CRISPR‐GE and fused into the pTaU6:Cas9 vector by BsaI enzyme (New England Biolabs). RNAi plasmid was constructed after recombining its 389‐bp cDNA fragment of HvCBP60b with the pANDA vector through gateway method (cat No. 11789 and 11791, Invitrogen) (Pyo et al. 2010; Shen et al. 2020). Genetic transformation was performed with Agrobacterium tumefaciens AGL1 containing recombinant plasmids of pTaU6‐sgHvCBP60b and pANDA‐HvCBP60b in barley immature embryos. More than ten individual lines were obtained, and three independent positive HvCBP60b knockout (cbp60b‐1, cbp60b‐2, cbp60b‐4) and RNAi knockdown (R1, R2 and R3) lines from T1 or T2 generations were used for phenotypic observation. Similarly, HvCaM1 (cam1), HvMYB77 (myb77‐1, myb77‐2) and HvHAK5 (hak5‐7, hak5‐13) knockout lines were generated. The double mutants of cbp60‐hak5 were generated in cbp60b‐4 mutants with pTaU6‐sgHvHAK5. Genetic modified lines were verified by PCR, RT‐qPCR and DNA sequencing. All gene‐edited mutants mentioned in this paper resulted in premature protein termination. All primers used were listed in Dataset S2.

Measurements of Na+ and K+ Concentrations

Fresh weight (FW) of roots and shoots was determined and then dried in an oven at 80°C for 3 days to record dry weight (DW). Dried samples were digested by HNO3 using microwave digestion equipment (Multiwave 3000; Anton Paar). Then the concentrations of Na+ and K+ were determined by an inductively coupled plasma‐optical emission spectrometer (iCAP 6000 series; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The relative water content, K+/Na+ ratio and transportation rate were calculated accordingly (Kuang et al. 2025).

Dynamic Change of Calcium

The changes in transient [Ca2+]cyt among cbp60b‐1 and WT plants were detected using Ca2+ indicator Fura‐2 am (Thermo Fisher, F1221). Roots of 3‐d‐old seedlings exposed to 0 mM and 100 mM NaCl were sampled and immersed in 5 μM Fura‐2 am working solution (pH 6.5 adjusted with 1 M KOH) for 2 h (Krebs et al. 2012). Root samples were washed with MES solution for 3 times, fixed in a fluorescent permeable petri dish with biogel, and immersed in MES solution. Ca2+ fluorescence signals were detected with a confocal laser microscope (Eclipse Ti‐u, Nikon) at 340 nm and 380 nm. Meanwhile, samples from 1 cm of the apical tip were mashed and placed on Elisa plates to detect fluorescence intensity on a Synergy H1 microplate reader (H1MD Take3 Trio, BioTek). Six biological replicates were used.

Transcriptional Activity Assay

To analyse the transcriptional activities of HvCBP60b and HvMYB77, their coding sequences were cloned into the pGBKD7 vector to obtain pGBKD7‐HvCBP60b and pGBKD7‐HvMYB77 plasmids, and then introduced into the yeast strain YH109 (WEIDI, Shanghai, China). The plasmids of pGBKD7‐GAL4 and pGBKD7 alone were served as positive and negative controls, respectively. The yeast cells were inoculated on SD/−Trp medium and transferred onto selective medium SD/−Trp/‐His with X‐α‐gal for 3 days at 30°C to detect transcriptional activity. Five times of GAL4 sequences and one time of TATA sequence were inserted into ahead LUC within pGreenII0800‐LUC vector to construct a reporter vector. HvCBP60b and HvMYB77 were cloned into a modified vector pSKBD containing GAL4 binding domain to generate the effectors. pSKBD and pSKBD‐VP16 were used as negative and positive control, respectively. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 (WEIDI, Shanghai, China) and co‐expressed in tobacco (N. benthamiana) leaves for 2–3 days. The intensity of luciferase was determined by an imaging device (Tanon 5200).

RNA Extraction and qRT‐PCR Analysis

Total RNA of barley roots and shoots was extracted by the MiniBEST kit and then reversely transcribed using reverse transcriptase with universal oligo (dT) primers (9769 and RR037A, Takara). qRT‐PCR analysis was performed by SYBR Green Supermix (RR820, Takara) on a Roche LightCycler 480 instrument according to the manual. To analyse the expression of HvCBP60b, HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 genes, an internal reference gene α‐Tubulin was used for normalization by the 2−△△CT method. Four biological replicates and three technical repeats were conducted.

RNA Sequencing Analysis

The fresh roots of cbp60b‐1, cbp60b‐2, cbp60b‐4 and WT seedlings under normal and 200 mM NaCl treatment for 1 week were used to obtain total RNA. RNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (LC‐Bio Technology Co. Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Raw data in fastq format were used for quality control by FastQC (0.12.0), while clean reads were mapped to the reference barley genome of Morex (Mascher et al. 2017) using the HISAT2(v2.2.1) according to the default parameters (Pertea et al. 2016). Transcriptome assembly was performed by StringTie (v2.2.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with the criteria of a two‐fold difference and an adjusted p < 0.05 by using DESeq2 (v1.30.0). Promoter analysis and GO enrichment analysis were performed using TBtools (Chen et al. 2020).

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assay (Y2H)

The CDS of HvCaM1 and HvCBP60b were amplified from GP cDNA and then cloned into pGADT7 (AD) and pGBKT7 (BD), respectively. The combination of pGBKT7‐53 and pGADT7‐T was used as the positive control. Meanwhile, the combinations of BD and AD, BD‐Lam and AD‐T, BD‐HvCBP60b and AD, BD and AD‐HvCaM1 were regarded as the negative controls. These combinations were co‐transformed into the yeast strain AH109 (Weidi company, Shanghai, China) using lithium acetate‐based protocol and plated on SD/−Trp/−Leu medium and selective SD/−Trp/−Leu/‐His medium with X‐α‐gal or SD/−Trp/−Leu/‐His/−Ade medium with X‐α‐gal at 30°C for 2–3 days. The detection of protein interaction between HvmCaM1 and HvCBP60b, HvCaM1 and HvCBP60b‐N, HvCaM1 and HvCBP60b‐C, HvCBP60b and HvMYB77, HvCaM1 and HvMYB77, were also performed accordingly.

Luciferase Assay (LUC)

HvCBP60b and HvCaM1 were cloned into N‐terminal or C‐terminal fragment of LUC vectors (Qin, Wu, and Wang 2019) driven by the CaMV‐35S promoter. The 2‐kb promoters of HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 were amplified and cloned into the pGreenII‐0800‐LUC vector, respectively. The recombinant plasmids were injected in the leaves of N. benthamiana. to analyse the transcriptional regulation cascades of HvCBP60b on HvMYB77 and HvMYB77 on HvHAK5. The CDS of HvCBP60b and HvMYB77 were cloned into the pGreenII62‐SK vectors. To investigate the effect of HvCaM1 on HvCBP60b‐triggered transcriptional repression on HvMYB77, the CDS of HvCaM1 was cloned into the pRI101‐AN vector. The combinations of plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 (WEIDI, Shanghai, China) and co‐expressed in the tobacco (N. benthamiana) leaves for 2–3 days, accordingly. The tobacco leaves were sprayed with 1 mM D‐Luciferin free acid for 5 min and imaged by a low‐light cooled charge‐coupled apparatus device (Tanon 5200). The luciferase activity was analysed by using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Subcellular Location Assay

The CDS (without the stop codon) of HvCBP60b, HvMYB77 and HvHAK5 was cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector fused with the GFP coding region and then delivered into the barley protoplast, tobacco leaves, or onion ( Allium cepa ) epidermal cells using biolistic PDS‐1000/He particle device (Bio‐Rad). Nucleus‐, plasma membrane‐ and whole cellular‐localized RFP markers were used as described previously (Fu et al. 2022). The recombined plasmids were also conducted in barley protoplasts through the PEG‐6000 method and tobacco leaves by GV3101, respectively. To determine the co‐localization of HvCBP60b and HvCaM1, 35S::HvCBP60b‐GFP and 35S::HvCaM1‐mScarlets were co‐expressed in barley protoplasts. GFP and RFP fluorescence signals were detected by a confocal laser microscope (LSM780, Zeiss) at 488/490 to 540 nm and 561/580 to 630 nm, respectively.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation Assay (BiFC)

The CDS of HvCBP60b and HvCaM1 were inserted into pSAT1‐cEYFP and pSAT1‐nEYFP vectors to generate HvCBP60b‐cEYFP and nEYFP‐HvCaM1 plasmids, and transiently expressed in barley protoplasts. Co‐expression of HvSOS3 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.1HG0081480, CBL4) and HvSOS2 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.7HG0720050, CIPK24) in the SOS pathway was used as the positive control, whereas HvCBP60b‐cEYFP and nEYFP were served as the negative control. The YFP fluorescence signalling was measured by a confocal laser microscope at 514/520 to 550 nm.

GST Pull‐Down Assay

The CDS of HvCBP60b and HvCaM1 were inserted into pTac‐His‐MBP and pGEX‐4 T‐1 to construct MBP‐CBP60b and GST‐CaM1 plasmids, respectively. The recombinant vectors were transformed into BL21 (DE3) strain independently. Glutathione agarose beads were firstly washed with 1 × PBS buffer for three times, and then combined with GST‐CaM1 fusion protein to pull down MBP‐CBP60b fused protein. GST and MBP protein were used as the negative control. The protein combinations were incubated at 4°C for 2 h. After washing with 1 × PBS buffer, the beads were detected using monoclonal anti‐GST (YEASEN) and anti‐MBP (Proteintech) antibodies, respectively.

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay (Y1H)

The 270‐bp promoter sequence of HvMYB77 was cloned into the pAbAi vector as the bait vector. The CDS of HvCBP60b, HvCBP60b‐N and HvCBP60b‐C were inserted into pGADT7 as the prey vector. The prey and bait were combined and co‐expressed in the yeast strain Y1H Gold (Weidi, Shanghai, China). The combination of p53‐AbAi and AD‐p53 plasmids was used as the positive control, while the combination of bait and prey vectors was used as the negative control. The yeasts containing combined plasmids were cultured on SD/‐Ura and SD/−Leu with or without aureobasidin A (AbA).

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA)

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was performed by using the EMSA/Gel‐Shift Kit (Beyotime), accordingly. The CDS of HvCBP60b and HvMYB77 were cloned into pTac‐His‐MBP and pGEX‐4 T‐1 to construct MBP‐CBP60b and GST‐MYB77, respectively. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli (Rosetta). The amount of 5 μg purified fused proteins was incubated with biotin whose 5′‐end labelled DNA probes. Empty GST protein, mutant probes and competitive probes were served as the negative controls.

Yeast Complementation Assay

The CDS of HvHAK5 and AtAKT1 were cloned in the pYES2 vector. The plasmids were transformed into the yeast strain R5421 (trk1▲, trk2▲) lacking two endogenous K+ transporter genes (TRK1 and TRK2). Yeast strain R5421 with pYES2 empty vector was used as negative control, and R5421 containing AtAKT1 (Pyo et al. 2010) was used as positive control. The yeast cells were cultured on SD/‐Ura medium containing 100 mM K+, and then transferred onto AP medium containing 0, 1, 10 and 100 mM KCl at 30°C for 2–3 days.

Measurement of K+ Flux

Noninvasive micro‐test technology (NMT) was used to determine K+ flux as affected by HvHAK5. The oocytes of Xenopus laevis were injected with 25 ng of HvHAK5 cRNA or an equal amount of ddH2O for 2 d. Then the injected oocytes were cultured in sodium‐free or potassium‐free ND96 for 6 h for element starvation. The oocytes were equilibrated in a test solution (950 μM KCl, 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 5 mM MES/Tris, pH 5.8) for 30 min. The continuous transient K+ fluxes were recorded for 3 min. For K+ uptake experiment, the oocytes were immersed in solution containing 1 mM NaCl or 50 μM RbCl for 2 h after 2‐h starvation. Then the oocytes were dried for the measurement of K+ and Rb+ by ICP‐MS. More than six individuals were determined.

Natural Variations and Population Genetics Analysis

Natural variation and haplotype analysis of target genes were performed in 300 worldwide barley accessions, downloaded and analysed on the BarleyOmics database (https://www.barleyomics.cn/home) (Zhao et al. 2025), according to our previous study (Xu et al. 2025).

Statistical Analysis

The different significance of all measured traits among the treatments and between mutant lines and WT was analysed by one‐way ANOVA or independent Student's t‐test using SPSS 27.0 software (IBM SPSS Statistics). The difference at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 was defined as significant and highly significant, respectively.
