Environmental Impact of a Novel UV‐C Disinfection Machine for Non‐Channel Flexible Laryngoscopes
Authors: Abhishek Mahesh, Paul C. Bryson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesInfection prevention in hospitals is a highly discussed and regulated process for endoscopy in Otolaryngology. The current standard for endoscopes involves high level disinfection (HLD). The UV‐SMART D60 disinfection machine utilizes Impelux UV‐C light technology. This study aimed to evaluate the environmental impact of the D60 machine within an otolaryngology department in comparison to current HLD protocols.MethodsThree equipment, materials, and disposables audits were conducted where the turnover and HLD process of a single non‐channeled laryngoscope was analyzed. Every item involved in the cleaning process was documented for both standard HLD and D60 workflows. Water quantity was documented and a power meter was used to determine the wattage of the machinery used. Additionally, scope turnover time was documented and CO2 emissions were calculated from known quantities.ResultsResults demonstrated a significant reduction in material usage with the D60 including reduced PPE. The total water usage was about 45 L more per scope for traditional HLD. Power analysis demonstrated a reduction of 250 watts per scope. Additionally, the D60 reduced CO2 emissions by over a factor of 3500. Scope turnover time was 109 min less for the D60.ConclusionsEnvironmental analysis demonstrates a significant amount of usage of disposables, water, and energy per non‐channeled nasolaryngoscope with current HLD protocols. UV‐C HLD resulted in less consumption of these resources. The marked reduction in turnover time and scope handling represents a significant opportunity for clinical efficiency, cost reduction, and resource consumption and will merit further investigation when FDA approval is granted.Level of EvidenceN/A.

Introduction

High‐level disinfection (HLD) is the gold standard utilized widely for the re‐processing of semi‐critical instruments including both flexible and rigid laryngoscopes. HLD is defined as the complete elimination of microorganisms in/on an instrument except for small quantities of bacterial spores [1]. Due to their contact with mucous membranes, rigid and flexible laryngoscopes are classified as semi‐critical instruments on the Spaulding classification scheme which indicates that infection risk from devices within this class is moderate. The Spaulding classification scheme defines the risk of infection based on the exposures, clinical application, and level of sterilization required for the device [2]. It is important to note that the Spaulding classification has never been tested specifically for flexible, non‐channeled laryngoscopes. With regards to flexible laryngoscopy, it is a common diagnostic procedure performed by otolaryngologists to visualize the nasal cavities, laryngopharynx, and airway [3]. These scopes are used routinely in otolaryngology clinics and operating rooms, often multiple times per day, necessitating consistent and effective reprocessing and HLD. Previous studies have demonstrated that both rigid and flexible laryngoscopes harbor extensive bacterial growth following clinical use ranging from bacterial contamination rates of 13% up to 50% in certain studies [4, 5]. Additional data has shown an incidence of 0.646 nasopharyngoscope device failures per month with 34.1% of these cases involving contamination using the US FDA database [6]. As such, the necessity for effective HLD is vital to reduce iatrogenic events and maintain patient safety.

The current standard for HLD with regards to laryngoscopes includes the use of CDC recommended chemical disinfectants including alkaline glutaraldehyde, ortho‐phthalaldehyde and peracetic acid utilized within an automated endoscope reprocessor (AER) [7]. The typical workflow for laryngoscope HLD includes enzymatic pre‐cleaning, followed by AER disinfection utilizing one of the aforementioned chemical detergents, and finally the drying process. These approved detergents have been shown to provide broad anti‐microbial coverage and are effective in achieving HLD, thus remaining central to laryngoscope reprocessing workflows across institutions [8].

Despite their effectiveness, the limitations of these detergents are increasingly recognized. The literature has reported adverse effects including respiratory irritation, anaphylaxis, and asthma. This has especially been documented in relation to alkaline glutaraldehyde‐based detergents [9, 10]. Additionally, Cidex‐OPA (ortho‐phthalaldehyde)‐related anaphylaxis post‐scope treatment has been reported [11]. Various studies evaluating endoscope and laryngoscope reprocessing have also demonstrated persistent contamination even after standard HLD, with residual microbial growth identified in up to 5%–15% of samples following reprocessing [12]. This illustrates potential concerns regarding the real‐world reliability of HLD practices.

In conjunction with adverse effects associated with the chemical detergents and potential documented clinical ineffectiveness, there is an immense environmental burden associated with standard HLD practices. Chemical disinfectants such as glutaraldehyde and ortho‐phthalaldehyde are typically used in volumes of 2–5 L per reprocessing basin, with replacement required every 7–14 days or sooner depending on use and dilution. In moderate‐ to high‐volume settings, this translates to 10–20 L of disinfectant waste generated per week for each AER. Each reprocessing cycle also contains multiple rinsing steps, which often require between 2 and 10 L of water per device, resulting in cumulative water usage that may exceed 50–100 L per day in settings with high patient throughput. These rinsing processes generate aqueous effluent with residual disinfectant‐based compounds, which can enter hospital wastewater systems without being completely neutralized and contribute to potential ecotoxicity [13]. Typical HLD workflows are also associated with high levels of solid waste generation with each reprocessing cycle requiring the use of personal protective equipment (PPE) such as gloves, gowns, shoe covers, and eye protection [14]. The healthcare sector is a major contributor to environmental pollution accounting for about 8%–10% of all greenhouse gas emissions in the United States [15]. This makes it vital for efforts to be made within the field of healthcare to reduce environmental burden, of which HLD plays a role.

Finally, along with the environmental burden that typical HLD workflows can produce for laryngoscope disinfection, they also present a time‐burden that can impact clinical workflow. Standard immersion protocols range from approximately 20 to 90 min per cycle with in addition to initial manual disinfection, leak testing, and drying can take upwards of 2 h for a used laryngoscope to be ready for clinical application again [7].

These drawbacks to standard HLD protocols have led to the introduction of alternative reprocessing strategies including UV‐based disinfection methodologies which have been shown to decrease chemical exposure while also providing non‐inferior disinfection to standard HLD. In addition, these technologies are postulated to reduce water consumption to virtually nothing and drastically reduce the usage of raw materials and resources. A study conducted by Rudhart et al. documented a reduction in bacterial contamination equivalent to that achieved by standard HLD upon the examination of 50 endoscopes with Impelux UV‐C light based technology [16]. A forthcoming publication from these authors provides further non‐inferiority data validating UV‐C light technology.

This study aimed to evaluate the environmental impact of the UV‐SMART D60 disinfection machine which utilizes Impelux UV‐C light technology as compared to standard HLD workflows with regards to solid waste, water consumption, energy consumption, and the time burden.

Methods

This study was conducted at a tertiary outpatient otolaryngology clinic through the use of equipment, material, and disposable audits. Exempt status was granted by the Cleveland Clinic IRB as no human subjects or protected health information was utilized in this study. Three audits were conducted for both the standard HLD workflow as well as the UV D60 disinfection workflow. Quantities of resources used were measured on a per‐laryngoscope basis. Non‐channeled flexible laryngoscopes were utilized for the analysis. Laryngoscope models involved in the study included the Olympus ENF‐P4, the Pentax VNL‐1190STK, the Pentax VLS‐1190STK, the Pentax VNL11‐J10, the Pentax VNL‐8‐J10, and the Pentax FNL‐10RP3.

Each audit consisted of two individuals engaged in a complete shadowing of the disinfection process. For the standard HLD workflow, this included documentation and visualization from the enzymatic pre‐cleaning stage through the sterile processing department (SPD) and finally through the return of the scope back onto the floor (Table 1). Meanwhile for the UV‐C D60 disinfection machine, this involved documentation of the workflow from the enzymatic pre‐cleaning stage to the UV chamber and then back to the floor (Table 2).

In addition to solid waste, water consumption in liters was also measured on a per scope basis as well as the type of enzymatic cleaner and chemical detergent utilized within the AER and the quantity in liters. To achieve the total of three audits conducted for a given laryngoscope, each one was performed on a different day to reduce the effect of potential confounding factors on total resource consumption.

The time burden involved with scope disinfection was calculated from the initiation of the pre‐cleaning process until the return of the scope back from the respective disinfection area onto the patient floor. For the D60, since these scopes were not actually utilized for clinical use until further standard HLD was conducted, time burden was calculated until the completion of the disinfection process within the UV‐C chamber. Energy consumption for both the D60 chamber and the standard HLD AER was measured using the P4400 P3 International Kill‐A‐Watt electricity usage monitor and was reported as a power output in watts.

CO2 emission values were obtained through calculation of the amount of CO2 produced from associated water and energy consumption. These values were summated on a per‐scope analysis and then multiplied by the sum of total scopes performed on a yearly basis to obtain yearly estimates. The number of laryngoscopies performed on a yearly basis at this clinic was estimated to be approximately 7000 [17].

Finally, cost savings estimates for standard HLD versus UV‐C based disinfection were obtained using regional monetary estimates per kWh of power and per L of water with $0.09–$0.16 per kWh and $4.76–$6.35 per m3 of water which is equivalent to $18–$24 per 1000 gal of water.

Results

Results demonstrated extensive production of solid waste with classical HLD as compared to the D60. This included an increased amount of PPE, linens, transport materials, and materials used for initial and centralized disinfection. The standard HLD protocol includes many steps that were not part of the UV‐C disinfection protocol, and thus led to the generation of more solid waste. With regard to PPE usage, materials such as gowns, gloves, shoe covers, masks, etc. were multiplied two‐fold or more through the complete standard HLD process with certain waste products not involved in the D60 protocol at all (Table 4). This was largely due to the two‐step disinfection process required for the standard HLD protocol. Additionally, this was combined with the PPE utilized during the pre‐cleaning process which was standard across both disinfection methodologies.

In addition to decreased PPE usage, the D60 also demonstrated no production of solid waste products isolated to the SPD that were included in the standard HLD protocol. This included the use of Super‐Sani Cloth germicidal wipes (n = 4 per scope), table covers (n = 2 per scope), disposable absorbent towels (n = 12 per scope), and cleaning sponges (n = 1 per scope).

In terms of water consumption, the standard HLD process utilizes an initial quantity of 12 L of water per scope, with each scope requiring 2 basins of water each with 6 L for the disinfection process in the SPD, prior to being placed into the AER.

There were two primary types of AER's utilized for scope disinfection: the Steris Medivators Advantage Plus and the Steris System 1E. The two AER types differed in their water usage for a complete disinfection cycle. The Medivators uses a dual basin system which allows for two laryngoscopes to be disinfected at once. When combining the water quantity used for disinfection, detergent wash, and additional wash steps, the total comes out to 68 L per two laryngoscopes for a per‐scope water consumption of 34 L per scope. This coupled with the 12 L of water used prior to AER disinfection leads to a total per‐scope water usage of 46 L. In contrast, the System 1E utilizes a fixed 33 L of water for the disinfection of a single laryngoscope, making the total water usage come out to 45 L (Table 5).

In contrast, the D60 does not require any quantity of water, with the chamber functioning independently of the use of an AER (Table 5).

Each AER differed slightly in the type and quantity of chemical detergent utilized for disinfection. The Medivators AER systems use a dual‐chemistry workflow consisting of a low‐concentration detergent (0.5%) followed by a lower‐concentration peracetic acid disinfectant (~850 ppm), whereas the Steris System 1E relies on a single high‐concentration peracetic acid sterilant (~1800–2000 ppm) without an additional integrated cleaning phase.

Energy consumption for the standard HLD workflow was determined to average 900 watts per scope. In contrast, energy consumption for the D60 was found to be 650 watts per laryngoscope for a net reduction of 250 watts for the D60. With a 1 h time‐frame for AER disinfection, this comes out to 9 kWh per scope for standard HLD. Next, accounting for a 1 min time‐frame for D60 disinfection, this comes out to 0.011 kWh per scope leading to a decrease in kWh of 8.989 kWh per scope.

CO2 emissions were calculated from the determined water and power usage with known quantities from the literature of 0.3 kg of CO2 per L of water and 0.46 kg of CO2 per kWh. The amount of water used during the standard HLD process was averaged between the two AER devices for a total of 45.5 L.

Calculated values demonstrated a 3500× reduction in CO2 emissions with the use of the D60 as compared to standard HLD (Tables 6 and 7). This amounts to CO2 savings of over 124,000 kg per year with use of the D60.

Laryngoscope turnover time was found to average around 2 h (115 min) for disinfection of a single scope with the standard HLD process as compared to 6 min for UV‐C disinfection with the D60. This amounts to a reduction in time burden of 109 min for each scope. This coupled with the average number of laryngoscopes utilized and disinfected at the clinic on a yearly basis, illustrates a reduction in time burden of 76,300 min or 53 days.

Finally, with an annual reduction in energy consumption of 63,000 kWh, this comes out to $5700–$10,000. Next, an annual reduction in water usage of 315,000 L leads to monetary savings of $1500–$2000. Thus, combining both values, use of the D60 over a 1 year period is estimated to yield savings of around $7200–$12,000.

Discussion

The healthcare sector is one of the major waste‐producing industries in the United States and contributes an immense carbon footprint. The healthcare sector is responsible for around 8% of all greenhouse gas emissions in the US and around 5% of all emissions globally, which includes carbon dioxide, ozone, and methane primarily [18, 19]. Greenhouse gas emissions play a major role in the prevalence of global warming, which represents a major threat to overall population health. Global warming induces negative outcomes in all sectors including increasing the risk for wildfires, promoting the spread of infection, more frequent droughts, and increasing air pollution. Global warming is considered a hazard‐multiplier and intensifies climate‐sensitive negative outcomes [20].

The severity of impact that global warming produces and will continue to produce at intensifying levels points to the need to mitigate waste production and greenhouse gas emissions across the primary waste‐producing sectors which include healthcare. Although a large proportion of the carbon footprint from the healthcare sector arises from the supply chain process, waste production and energy usage also contribute sizable portions with energy consumption accounting for over 25% of the total carbon footprint [21].

Within otolaryngology, laryngoscope disinfection represents a major component of the waste generation and energy usage produced on a daily basis. Studies have illustrated anywhere from 5000 to 16,000 laryngoscopies being performed for a single clinic on a yearly basis [22, 23]. With laryngoscopies being such a common procedure, this leads to the need for large quantities of resources in order to disinfect the scopes for re‐use. It also illustrates the generation of high quantities of solid waste due to constant disinfection as well.

Ultraviolet‐based disinfection represents a novel methodology that has gained popularity in the past decade as a mechanism to disinfect semi‐critical instruments including laryngoscopes. The promotion of UV disinfection has been driven largely due to the negative environmental impacts that standard HLD yields.

This led to the need to ascertain the exact impact of UV disinfection on environmental output as compared to standard HLD protocols including analyzing water consumption, energy usage, solid waste generation, and CO2 emissions.

Results demonstrated major reductions in solid waste generation, primarily as a result of decreased PPE necessary for the D60. This included reduced usage of nitrile gloves, gowns, shoe covers, scrub caps, and so forth. There was a major reduction in water consumption noted for the D60 as compared to standard HLD with the typical AER disinfection process requiring 45–46 L of water per laryngoscope. In contrast, the D60 did not require any water for disinfection. This reduction in water consumption of over 45 L per laryngoscope represents a significant reduction in resource utilization. On a yearly basis, this comes out to over 315,000 L of water saved with UV‐based disinfection. In terms of energy usage, the D60 was found to have a power output 250 watts less than the traditional AER's and due to the 1 min time‐span for disinfection, this led to a kWh reduction of 8.989 kWh. Finally, calculated CO2 emissions were greatly reduced for the D60 as compared to standard HLD with a 3500‐fold reduction and CO2 savings of over 124,000 kg per year.

These results provide clear evidence towards the environmental benefit that UV‐based disinfection with the D60 provides as compared to standard methodologies. With solid waste, water usage, and energy consumption all directly linked to the carbon footprint of the healthcare sector, this study provides data promoting its use as a management strategy to reduce the environmental impact of the healthcare sector within the realm of otolaryngology specifically.

In addition, results also demonstrated an immense reduction in scope turnover time with use of the D60 with a reduction of 109 min. Turnover time can serve as a major drag for clinical efficacy and requires capital expenditure for large scope fleets. With the use of the D60, this effect can largely be mitigated with scopes being immediately ready for re‐use and not requiring transport to and from an SPD which can be far from the clinic.

Finally, an annual cost benefit of $7200–$12,000 was calculated based on reduced resource consumption, illustrating a monetary benefit towards adapting to UV‐C based disinfection protocols. In addition, with the reduction in scope turnover time, this provides an additional avenue for cost reduction as new laryngoscopes would be purchased less frequently.

This project serves as a pilot study analyzing the impact that UV‐based disinfection can provide on environmental output and warrants further analysis. Activation of this technology will allow for a number of important economic and environmental questions to be answered.

Certain limitations to the study include that it was performed at a single otolaryngology clinic. The study could have benefitted from a multi‐institutional analysis to obtain average values closer to a national mean for the environmental impact of UV‐C disinfection. This however was difficult due to the limited nature of obtaining the D60 machine, as FDA approval had yet to be granted at the time of this submission. Furthermore, we acknowledge that cost savings provided are estimates based on our region and that they may vary. With future FDA approval, further analysis of the D60 chamber can be conducted across multiple institutions.

Conclusion

Ultraviolet‐based disinfection of non‐channeled laryngoscopes is a novel emerging option for HLD with significant reduction in medical and environmental waste, CO2, energy, and clinical operational turnover time. This could have a profound impact on clinical operations and merits further study.
