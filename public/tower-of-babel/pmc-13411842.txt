Some performance characteristics of Caucasian pheasants (Phasianus colchicus colchicus) raised without their natural habitat

Background

Caucasian pheasants (Phasianus colchicus colchicus [1]) are an important subspecies within the family Phasianidae. They represent a true pheasant variety that naturally inhabits the coastal regions of Türkiye’s Marmara and Black Sea areas, as well as the Caucasus region and its surroundings. This subspecies is of considerable importance both in its natural habitats and in organized breeding programs. In terms of meat quality and egg production, Caucasian pheasants exhibit a high production potential for breeders [2].

In recent years, pheasants have been reared for a variety of purposes, including game release programs, breeding stock, hobby production, alongside commercial egg and meat production [2, 3]. Pheasants typically begin egg production at 35–40 weeks of age. Under natural conditions, the laying season generally starts in March or April. In intensive production systems, unless artificial lighting is applied, laying commences during the same period and may continue until late August [4]. Eggs of ring-necked pheasants are commonly brown, blue, white, or olive green in color and have an average weight of approximately 33.0 g [5, 6].

Cetin et al. [7], who investigated egg production and hatching characteristics of pheasants, examined the effects of intensive rearing conditions and a continental climate. In this study, it was demonstrated that, although no breeding or selection programs had been applied to pheasants, they could be raised quite successfully under Central Anatolian poultry housing conditions when optimal environmental conditions were provided. Furthermore, it was concluded that more comprehensive and multidisciplinary studies on this species would make significant contributions to the widespread adoption and sectoral implementation of pheasant farming.

It has been recommended that all pheasant eggs be utilized for incubation [8]. The standard incubation period for ring-necked pheasant eggs is 25–26 days. During this time, maintaining optimal humidity levels is critical. Specifically, the initial 21 days require a relative humidity of approximately 65%, while 85–90% humidity is essential during the hatching phase [5]. However, the hatching performance of pheasants is generally lower than that observed in other poultry species [9, 10].

Kırıkcı et al. [9] reported hatchability rates of 49.55% and 29.46% for eggs obtained from caged and free-mated pheasants, respectively, after seven days of storage. Cetin et al. [7] documented a hatchability rate of 62.03% in pheasants maintained in mating cages at a male-to-female ratio of 1:5. Similarly, Tepeli et al. [11] documented hatchability values ranging from 39.48% to 54.09% under various lighting programs.

The reported fertility rates of pheasants exhibit significant variation, with estimates ranging from 78.4% to 89.3% [12], 89% to 92.7% [13], 67.75% [14], 83.9% [15], 78% to 94.5% [16], 86.2% to 89.7% [17], and 85.1% to 91.1% [18]. However, other studies have reported significantly lower rates, with estimates ranging from 45 to 82% [19]. The hatchability of fertile eggs has been documented to range between 45% and 76.9% [13], [15].

The Haugh unit values of pheasant eggs have been documented to range from 79.51 to 83.24 [20], from 81.47 to 83.96 depending on shell color [6], and up to 96.33 [9]. Kuzniacka et al. [21] reported Haugh unit values between 77.2 and 82.0 for pheasants of different ages.

It was concluded that Caucasian pheasants can be rearing in the Anatolian region as well, provided that suitable management conditions provided, including appropriate feeding and housing. Concurrently, the conservation of natural habitats and the implementation of sustainable breeding practices remain imperative for ensuring species continuity and the attainment of long-term economic benefits.

Therefore, this study was conducted at the Prof. Dr. Hümeyra Özgen Research and Practice Farm, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Selcuk University in Konya, Türkiye, to determine egg production, egg quality characteristics and hatchability performance of Caucasian pheasants.

Discussion

Egg production

In the present study, the laying performance of Caucasian pheasants was relatively high compared with many values reported in the literature. Previous literature indicates considerable variability in laying rates across genotypes, housing systems and management environments [4, 9, 22–25]. Such disparities are well recognized in game bird production, where photoperiod, behavioral restriction and nutritional adequacy exert a marked influence on reproductive output. Several studies have evaluated the impact of lighting programmes on the reproductive activity of pheasant hens. These studies have highlighted the importance of extended or ahemeral schedules in promoting reproductive activity in pheasant hens [16]. This factor may have contributed to the favorable outcomes observed in the present flock, although this interpretation is not directly supported by the present data.

In addition to environmental regulation, dietary optimization is another significant factor determining reproductive efficiency. Improvements in egg production have been associated with balanced protein sources and functional feed components [26], which supports the view that the nutritional strategy implemented here was conducive to enhanced laying. In addition, genetic and subspecies-level characteristics must be considered. Earlier reports have indicated that Caucasian pheasants demonstrate relatively strong reproductive performance [27], suggesting an inherent capacity that may help explain the high laying rate recorded in this study. Collectively, these observations align with the general consensus that well-managed Caucasian pheasant flocks can surpass the productivity of standard ring-necked lines [9, 23], and they further emphasize the combined influence of genotype, photoperiod control and nutritional support in maximizing laying performance.

External egg quality characteristics

The external egg characteristics observed in this study were consistent with findings reported in previous studies. Earlier research has demonstrated that factors such as hen age, genotype and environmental conditions contribute substantially to variation in egg size and other quality characteristics [4, 17, 19, 21, 30, 33, 37, 38,]. The egg weight values exhibit variability within a comparable range. However, subtle disparities have been observed, contingent on factors such as laying period and diet profile. Furthermore, these variations may be further influenced by age-related physiological changes. Günlü et al. [20] reported that egg weight and several egg quality characteristics show measurable shifts as pheasants age. In consideration of the aforementioned sources of variation, the mean egg weight obtained in the present study can be regarded as consistent with the documented ranges reported for pheasant eggs in the existing literature.

Shape index values also reflected stable egg morphology and were compatible with prior studies evaluating pheasant egg geometry [6, 9, 24, 28]. The observed variability in shape index among flocks has been attributed to genotype-related structural characteristics and flock age dynamics. These factors may influence the proportional allocation of egg length and width. The external characteristics observed in this study reflect an adequate physiological status in the birds and demonstrate that management practices effectively supported shell development and egg formation.

Internal egg quality characteristics

An analysis of the internal quality characteristics revealed that the albumen exhibited integrity and freshness. The observed values of the yolk index, albumen index and Haugh unit are consistent with the ranges typically reported for young or early-lay game bird breeders. As indicated by previous studies, the sensitivity of these characteristics to hen age, management system and storage duration has been demonstrated [6, 9, 20, 21, 24]. Specifically, the structural properties of the yolk undergo progressive changes as hens advance through the laying cycle. In contrast, albumen viscosity typically declines with advancing age or long-term storage. The elevated Haugh unit values documented in this study are consistent with observations from younger pheasant flocks, supporting the beneficial impact of timely egg collection and appropriate handling.

Although specific dietary interventions have recently been associated with enhancements in albumen height and yolk composition [26], the balanced 18% protein diet used in this study appears to have provided sufficient support for the development of sound internal quality characteristics. The congruence of our findings with those of preceding research underscores the significance of breeder age, management precision and nutritional adequacy in shaping internal egg quality across pheasant flocks.

Proportions of egg components

The proportional distribution of albumen, yolk and shell observed in the present study aligns closely with prior reports on pheasant egg composition, although some minor differences were apparent. Earlier investigations have demonstrated that component ratios vary with laying period, diet profile and shell pigmentation [6, 21, 29]. Increases in albumen proportion and parallel reductions in yolk fraction have been documented in older hens or in eggs collected later in the laying cycle, a pattern linked to gradual shifts in reproductive physiology [20]. Similar trends have been associated with differences in dietary protein quality and mineral composition.

The shell ratio in the present study exceeded some earlier values reported in ring-necked and Caucasian pheasants [6, 21, 29]. These differences may reflect variations in shell mineralization efficiency or subtle strain-specific variations in shell gland activity. Notably, the overall distribution of components remained within normal biological limits, aligning with observations reported in other pheasant breeds under controlled conditions. These results further illustrate the combined influence of flock age, feeding management and genetic background on egg structural composition.

Hatching of Caucasian pheasants

In Caucasian pheasants, hatchability was calculated as 65.75%, fertility rate as 90%, hatchability of fertile eggs as 73.34%, and embryonic mortality rate as 24% (Table 4).

These values differ from those reported in the literature [4, 22], Ristic et al. 2013, [30]. The hatchability, fertility rate and hatchability of fertile eggs reported in this study differ from the values of 62.03%, 81.63%, and 75.99% reported by Cetin et al. [7], they are similar to the values of 65.49%, 89.63%, and 73.06% reported by Arslan et al. [27]. The embryonic mortality rate reported in this study was lower than the average embryonic mortality rate of 28% reported by Ristic et al. [31], who studied embryonic mortality according to the shell color of pheasants. The differences between the studies may be due to the incubator, environmental variability, and breeding feeding.

In pheasants, the recommended egg storage period is no longer than 8 days, as extended storage durations have been reported to cause significant declines in hatchability. Indeed, in the study by Kożuszek et al. [32], hatchability of pheasant eggs collected on the day of lay ranged from 74.5% to 92.7% depending on eggshell color,however, this rate decreased to 46.9–74.0% after 4 days of storage and further declined to 6.2–26.6% after 10 days of storage.

In addition to storage duration, several other factors have been reported to influence hatching performance in pheasants, including nutrition [26, 33, 34], male-to-female ratio [34, 35], eggshell color [31, 32, 36, 37], age [22, 38], and rearing system [39].

Conclusion

The findings of this study clearly demonstrate that Caucasian pheasants successfully adapt to the environmental conditions of the Anatolian region and exhibit high productive performance under regional farming systems.

The results of this study suggest that, with an average of 91 eggs per hen and a 65% egg production rate, this subspecies may be suitable for commercial pheasant production in the Anatolian region. Feeding and management programs, lighting programs, and hatching parameters are effective factor to economical and sustainable Caucasian pheasants production. Therefore, these factors are important to substantial economic returns through high fertility, satisfactory hatchability, and valuable egg.

Consequently, Caucasian pheasants have the potential for high egg yield and production under farm rearing conditions outside their natural habitat.

Materials and methods

Animals and housing

This study was conducted at the Research and Application Unit of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Selcuk University (38°02′12.5"N 32°30′19.1"E), Türkiye, using 40 female and 4 male Caucasian pheasants (Phasianus colchicus colchicus) as experimental material. The birds were housed in four open pens measuring 5.0 × 5.0 × 2.5 m, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:10. During the breeding period, pheasants were fed ad libitum with a layer-type diet containing 18% crude protein (CP), and water was supplied via automatic drinkers. Nesting boxes and perches were provided in each pen.

The pheasants used in this study were obtained from the Prof. Dr. Hümeyra Özgen Research and Application Farm of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Selcuk University, Türkiye (Decision number: 2024/164, Meeting date: 31.10.2024).

Lighting program

No artificial lighting was applied until the onset of egg laying. After the first egg was obtained, artificial lighting was added to natural daylight, and the photoperiod was maintained at 16 h light and 8 h dark (16L:8D) throughout the laying season.

Egg collection and egg quality measurements

Eggs were collected twice daily at 10:00 and 16:00 for 140 consecutive days. Every 15 days, 10 eggs were randomly selected for egg quality analyses, resulting in a total of 80 eggs evaluated. Egg weight and shell weight were measured using a precision balance with a sensitivity of 0.01 g. Egg length and width were measured using a digital caliper with 0.01 mm precision.

Egg storage and incubation condition

The remaining eggs were stored for 7 days at 15 °C and 75% relative humidity in egg storage machine (HD-960L-3in1) and subsequently fumigated. During the storage period, eggs were placed in a vertical position with the pointed end facing downward, and no egg turning was applied. Eggs were fumigated with formaldehyde vapor (formalin and potassium permanganate) for 10 min under standard hatchery programme.

Following fumigation, eggs were incubated in setter machines at 37.7 °C and 60% relative humidity for 23 days. During incubation, eggs were turned hourly at 45° to both sides following standard hatchery practice.

After which they were transferred to a hatcher (Cimuka-T1600-C) operating at 37.2 °C and 85% relative humidity until hatching. At this stage, the eggs were transferred to incubator baskets and no turning was applied.

Hatching characteristics and embryonic mortality

Pheasant eggs were collected twice daily between April and June. The collected eggs were stored for 7 days before being placed in the incubator. Each egg set for incubation was individually counted and recorded. The eggs were incubated for a total of 23 days and then transferred to the hatcher for the last 3 days to complete hatching. After hatching, unhatched eggs were examined macroscopically to determine fertility status and hatchability characteristics. Hatchability was calculated based on the number of chicks obtained relative to the total number of eggs set in the incubator. The number of infertile eggs was determined, and the fertility rate was calculated accordingly. Hatchability of fertile eggs was calculated based on the number of fertile eggs.

Statistical analysis

Data were recorded in Microsoft Excel and statistical analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were calculated for egg production, egg quality characteristics and hatching performance parameters. Results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE).
