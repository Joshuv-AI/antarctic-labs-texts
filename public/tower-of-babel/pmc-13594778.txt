The Alzheimer's disease risk gene SORL1 is a regulator of excitatory neuronal function
Authors: C. Andrew Williams, Shannon E. Rose, Vera Stamenkovic, Aquene N. Reid, Kyle J. Travaglini, Stephen E. P. Smith, Jessica E. Young
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONSynaptic dysfunction is an early feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and proper localization of proteins involved in pre‐ and post‐synaptic composition is dependent on endosomal recycling and trafficking, cellular processes involving the AD risk gene sortilin‐related receptor 1 (SORL1).METHODSWe examined SORL1's role in synaptic protein composition and neuronal function in human excitatory cortical neurons. Synaptic protein interactions were analyzed using a mesoscale proteomics assay. Immunocytochemistry was used to visualize synaptic proteins and α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid receptor subunits. Neuronal function was measured with multi‐electrode arrays.RESULTSLoss of SORL1 expression significantly changes many synaptic protein–protein interactions and patterns of expression. SORL1‐deficient neurons exhibit hyperactivity that is primarily amyloid beta independent. SORL1‐deficient neurons also have impaired network plasticity.DISCUSSIONThese findings further support a growing body of literature implicating early endosomal recycling defects as drivers of AD pathogenesis.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is characterized by progressive synapse loss and dysfunction that precedes widespread neuronal death and correlates strongly with cognitive decline. 1 Synaptic alterations include changes in synapse morphology, impaired synaptic plasticity, and disrupted neurotransmission, particularly affecting glutamatergic neurons in the cerebral cortex. 2 Hallmark AD pathogenic proteins amyloid beta (Aβ) and hyper‐phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) interfere with synaptic function by impairing long‐term potentiation and disrupting synaptic transmission 3 but how AD risk genes impact synapses outside of changes to Aβ and p‐tau is largely unknown.

Pathogenic changes to neuronal synapses and disruption of signaling can lead to aberrant neuronal activity and larger scale network activity dysfunction. Neuronal hyperactivity is associated with early disease state, followed by hypoactivity with advanced disease status and neurodegeneration. Disease‐associated hyperactivity can manifest as an increased prevalence of seizures and epileptiform brain activity 4 and is associated with faster rates of cognitive decline. Neuronal hyperexcitability is seen in multiple models of AD 5 and has emerged as a significant contributor to synaptic dysfunction. This hyperexcitability manifests as increased spontaneous neural activity; altered calcium homeostasis; and enhanced susceptibility to excitotoxicity, altered glutamate receptors, and changes to excitatory‐inhibitory balance. 6 , 7 Ultimately, this cascade of events can lead to synaptic exhaustion and degeneration through excessive metabolic demands and increased cellular stress. 8 , 9

Deficiencies in endosomal recycling can contribute to aberrant synaptic function. Reduced expression of the retromer complex, a multiprotein complex heavily involved in endosomal trafficking and recycling, leads to loss of cell‐surface glutamatergic α‐amino‐3‐hydroxy‐5‐methyl‐4‐isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors and impaired long‐term potentiation. 10 , 11 The sortilin‐related receptor 1 (SORL1/SORLA) directly interacts with retromers to facilitate endosomal trafficking through the recycling, retrograde, and degradative pathways. 12 SORL1 is an established AD risk gene, implicated by multiple genome‐wide association studies 13 , 14 , 15 and by exome studies 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 showing that loss of function variants in SORL1 protein are causal for early‐onset AD. Furthermore, SORL1 protein levels are decreased in AD human brains and specifically decreased in the AD‐susceptible transentorhinal cortex. 11 We have previously established models of SORL1 deficiency using human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)–derived neurons and glia. 21 , 22 , 23 In cortical neurons with loss of SORL1 expression we have observed altered trafficking of key proteins involved in neuronal synaptic function including the glutamate receptor 1 (GLUR1) subunit of the AMPA receptor and the brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) receptor tropomyosin receptor kinase B. However, how SORL1 expression shapes neuronal function and synapses remains underexplored.

Here, we used SORL1‐deficient human cortical neurons to investigate the role SORL1 plays in synaptic protein composition and neuronal activity. We report that SORL1 knock‐out (KO) neurons have disrupted synaptic protein interactions and alterations of excitatory postsynaptic proteins and glutamate receptors. Using multi‐electrode arrays, we observe that SORL1‐deficient neurons are hyperexcitable relative to isogenic wild‐type neurons, that the increased neuronal activity is driven in part by glutamatergic excitatory neurotransmission, and that SORL1‐dependent hyperexcitability is not solely due to increased secretion of soluble Aβ peptides, which in addition to driving hyperactivity have been shown to be synaptogenic. 24 We also demonstrate the SORL1‐deficient neurons have impaired network activity after long‐term potentiation induction, demonstrating, for the first time, that SORL1 plays a critical role in active modification of network activity. Together, our study shows that our human neuron model of SORL1 deficiency recapitulates an early phenotype of AD pathology and suggests that SORL1‐dependent alterations of synaptic protein levels leads to neuronal dysfunction in the disease. These results will open pathways to new therapeutic development.

Systematic review: We reviewed the literature using traditional sources (e.g., PubMed) and found that deficiencies in endosomal trafficking pathways lead to synaptic dysfunction, an early feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD). Sortilin‐related receptor 1 (SORL1) is a strong AD risk gene that functions in endo‐lysosomal trafficking. Loss of SORL1 is evident in neurons of patients with AD. However, no studies have looked at the mechanisms by which loss of SORL1 might influence neuronal synaptic dysfunction. Interpretation: Using our established human induced pluripotent stem cell neuronal model of SORL1 deficiency, we found that loss of SORL1 induced widespread changes in synaptic protein interactions, neuronal hyperexcitability, and loss of synaptic plasticity. Future directions: Our study corroborates a growing body of literature implicating early endosomal recycling defects as drivers of AD pathogenesis and supports exploring the SORL1 pathway for therapeutic development in AD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human cell lines and CRISPR‐Cas9 genome editing

All cell lines used in this study are human and have been previously published. 21 , 22 , 25 , 26 All cell lines used are isogenic and generated from the published and characterized CV cell line (GM2530, Coriell). 27 , 28 This cell line is male with an apolipoprotein E ε3/ε4 genotype and publicly available. CRISPR‐Cas9 gene editing was used to generate SORL1 KO clones and APP Swe+/+ as described previously. 29 All clones demonstrated normal karyotypes and regularly tested for mycoplasma (MycoScope, AMS Bio).

Neuronal differentiation

Isogenic hiPSCs were differentiated into excitatory cortical neurons using dual‐SMAD inhibition, as described previously. 21 , 22 , 25 , 30 Briefly, neural precursor cells (NPCs) were differentiated from hiPSCs using dual‐SMAD inhibition. NPCs were then passaged and expanded for neuronal differentiations. The dual‐SMAD differentiation protocol used in this study results in cultures of primarily excitatory neurons, with low levels of astrocyte and inhibitory neurons (Figure S1 in supporting information) and other glial subtypes being absent completely. After differentiation, neurons were then passaged and replated according to experimental needs. See Supplementary Materials and Methods in supporting information for detailed descriptions. All cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Quantitative multiplex immunoprecipitation

After differentiation, seven million hiPSC neurons were plated on Matrigel‐coated 6‐well culture plates and multiple‐electrode arrays (see Supplementary Materials and Methods) for monitoring of network bursting activity. Upon network bursting detection, ≈ 30 days post plating (DPP) after neuronal differentiation, parallel 6‐well cultures were harvested for quantitative multiplex immunoprecipitation (QMI) analysis.

Multiplexed immunoprecipitation (IP) is achieved using antibody‐based IPs onto color‐coded Luminex beads, followed by probing for co‐associated proteins using fluorophore‐coupled antibodies and quantification on a flow cytometer, as described previously. 31 , 32 , 33 Briefly, samples were lysed in NP40 lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris [pH 7.4], 1% NP‐40, 10 mM NaF, 2 mM sodium orthovanadate + protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktails [Sigma]) and protein concentrations were measured by bicinchoninic acid assay (Pierce) and normalized between samples. A master mix containing 20 classes of antibody‐coupled Luminex beads was prepared and added to lysates. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C on a rotator. The next day, beads were washed with cold FlyP buffer (50 mM tris [pH7.4], 100 mM NaCl, 1% bovine serum albumin, and 0.02% sodium azide) and distributed into 40 wells of a 96‐well plate for technical replicates. One of 20 biotinylated probe antibodies was added to each well, in duplicate, and the plate was incubated at 4°C with gentle agitation for 1 hour. After incubation, the resulting bead–probe complexes were washed three times using an automatic plate washer with cold FlyP buffer, incubated with streptavidin–phycoerythrin at 4°C for 30 minutes with gentle agitation, and washed an additional three times. Finally, samples were resuspended in 125 µl of ice‐cold Fly‐P buffer and bead fluorescence was measured from a mean of 60 beads/class/well using a customized refrigerated Bio‐Plex 200. For comprehensive video protocol, analysis software code for both R and MATLAB, refer to Brown et al. 34 QMI validation, including specific validation of each antibody pair, was described previously. 31 The full list of IP–probe antibody pairs used in the study is provided in Table S1 in supporting information. QMI analysis was performed as previously described; 31 , 33 , 34 see Supplementary Materials and Methods for a detailed description.

Immunocytochemistry

After differentiation, hiPSC neurons were passaged to Matrigel‐coated #1 glass coverslips in 24‐well culture plates. Neurons were plated at a density of 500,000 cells per coverslip. In the first week post‐plating, cultures were transitioned from neural maintenance medium (see Supplementary Methods) containing BDNF, glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), and dibutyryl cyclic adenosine monophosphate (db‐cAMP) to Brainphys neuronal medium supplemented with 1% B27, 0.5% N2, 0.2 µg/ml BDNF, 0.2 µg/ml GDNF, 500 µM db‐cAMP, and 1 µg/ml laminin. Two thirds of well volume was changed twice a week. At ≈ DPP 30, cell medium was removed, and coverslips were fixed and stained to visualize Homer protein homolog 1 (HOMER1), SYNAPSIN1/2, GLUR1, and GLUR2. See Supplementary Materials and Methods for further protocol details and antibody information.

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

Neurons were imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope with a 63x oil immersion objective. All images were captured with the experimenter blinded to cell line. See Supplementary Materials and Methods for further confocal microscope setting details. Image analysis of HOMER1/SYNAPSIN and GLUR1/GLUR2 immunostained neurons was performed using Bitplane Imaris software (Oxford Instruments). Similar pipelines were created for both synaptic and glutamate receptor analyses, as follows: The surface object workflow was used to define the Microtubule‐associated protein 2 (MAP2)+ neuron regions of interest (whole cell, soma, dendrites) and for the creation of masked HOMER1/SYNAPSIN and GLUR1/GLUR2 channels within the neuron regions of interest. The surface object workflows were then created to quantify HOMER1/SYNAPSIN and GLUR1/GLUR2 puncta volume, number, and fluorescent intensity. GLUR1/GLUR2 volume, intensity, and frequency data were normalized to HOMER1 volumes to account for differing postsynaptic density volumes. Colocalization of HOMER1 and SYNAPSIN was analyzed using the ImarisColoc function.

Multiple electrode array culture

Differentiated hiPSC neurons were generated as described above and were passaged to Matrigel‐coated multiple electrode array (MEA) culture plates in high‐density droplets. Six‐well and 48‐well MEA plates were used for these experiments (Axion Biosystems cats. #M384‐tMEA‐6 W and #M768‐tMEA‐48 W). See Supplementary Materials and Methods for a detailed description.

MEA recordings and analysis

Extracellular electrical activity of hiPSC neurons plated on MEA plates was recorded twice a week using an Axion Biosystems Maestro Pro system. Recordings were taken prior to medium changes to minimize physical perturbation of neurons. MEA culture plates were acclimated to the MEA reader for a minimum of 10 minutes and activity was verified to have stabilized prior to the onset of recording and the environment was controlled at 37°C and 5% CO2. Recordings were ≈ 4 minutes in duration. Signals were recorded at a sampling frequency of 12.5 kHz with a 3 kHz Kaiser window low‐pass filter and a 200 Hz high‐pass filter. Spikes were detected using the adaptive threshold crossing method of the Axion Axis Navigator software. The Axion Neural Metric Tool and custom Matlab (Mathworks) scripts were used to quantify firing and network activity. See a detailed description in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Neurotransmitter receptor antagonist experiments

Blockade of AMPA, N‐methyl‐D‐aspartic acid (NMDA), and GABAA neurotransmitter receptors were performed pharmacologically with the addition of 2,3‐dioxo‐6‐nitro‐7‐sulfamoyl‐benzo[f]quinoxaline (NBQX; Tocris, cat. #0373), D‐AP5 (APV, Tocris cat. #0106), and SR 95531 hydrobromide (Gabazine, Tocris cat. #1262), respectively. A full media change was performed the day before antagonist application. Antagonists were diluted in Brainphys medium at 10x the final concentration. Baseline recordings were performed as described above. After determination of baseline activity, one tenth of MEA well medium was replaced with an equal volume of 10x antagonist‐containing medium for a final antagonist concentration of NBQX 10 µM, APV 50 µM, or Gabazine 10 µM. Recordings were then immediately taken and wells were washed with three full volume medium changes of Brainphys neuronal medium. Approximately 2 hours later, washout condition recordings were performed.

Chemical long‐term potentiation

Chemical long‐term potentiation (cLTP) methods were adapted from Pré et al. 35 After maturation and development of network bursting, MEA well medium was fully exchanged for Brainphys medium excluding BDNF, GDNF, and db‐cAMP. The next day, medium was again fully replaced with medium excluding supplemental neurotrophic factors. After 2 hours, baseline recordings were performed. cLTP induction was performed by the addition of cAMP second messenger pathway modulators forskolin (LC Laboratories cat. #F‐9929) and rolipram (Tocris cat. #0905). Medium containing 10x concentrations of forskolin and rolipram were added to MEA wells to give final concentrations of forskolin (50 µM) and rolipram (0.5 µM). Wells treated with dimethyl sulfoxide vehicle were subjected to identical medium changes as cLTP wells. Thirty minutes after cLTP induction, MEA plates were recorded. After the 30‐minute time point, wells were washed with three full medium changes with Brainphys medium without BDNF, GDNF, and db‐cAMP. Recordings were performed 4, 24, 48, and 72 hours post‐cLTP induction. Two thirds of MEA well medium was removed and replaced after the 48‐hour time point recording. cLTP MEA recordings were processed as described above. Due to large changes in intensity and frequency of network bursts, all recordings were reviewed to ensure accurate detection of network bursts. In some cases, manual thresholding of the spike histograms was performed to accurately detect high‐frequency and low‐intensity network bursting.

Measurements of secreted Aβ1‐40 and Aβ1‐42

Concentrations of secreted Aβ1‐40 and Aβ1‐42 were measured from conditioned medium collected from 48‐well MEA culture plates. Medium was conditioned for 96 hours prior to collection and ≈ 150 µl was harvested. MEA electrical activity was recorded and monitored for neuronal firing and network burst development. DPP 14 time point was used for preburst Aβ measurements and DPP 35 was used for postbursting Aβ analysis. Secreted Aβ levels were measured using an MSD Aβ V‐PLEX enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (MesoScale Discovery #151200E‐2). Two technical replicates per MEA well were measured and final Aβ1‐40 and Aβ1‐42 concentrations were reported as the average of the two replicates.

Single‐cell RNA sequencing

SORL1 KO and isogenic wild‐type control neurons were generated as described above. Two wild‐type clones and two SORL1 KO clones were used for single‐cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) experiments. Neurons were passaged onto Matrigel‐coated 12‐well culture plates at a density of 350,000. Neurons were disassociated with Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies cat. #AT‐104) and filtered through 40 µm cell strainers (Stellar Scientific cat. TC70‐SWM‐40) to obtain single cell suspensions. cDNA libraries were generated using the Parse Evercode WT v3 (Parse Biosciences cat. ECWT3300) system. In brief, single‐cell suspensions were fixed and permeabilized according to manufacturer's specifications (Evercode Cell Fixation v3, Parse Biosciences cat. ECFC3300). cDNA libraries were generated according to manufacturer's specifications and quality control was performed with Qubit 4 fluorometer (ThermoFisher) and Agilent Tapestation (Agilent Technologies) system. cDNA libraries were sequenced using an Ultima UG100 system to obtain 100,000 reads per cell. Gene counts were generated by aligning sequencing reads to the GRCh38 human reference genome using Parse Biosciences’ Trailmaker pipeline module. All downstream data processing, analysis, and visualization was performed in R using Seurat version 5.5.0. Initial quality control excluded cells with < 1000 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), < 1000 detected genes, or > 10% mitochondrial gene content. Subsequent ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX, 36 and doublets were identified using Scrublet. 37 After quality control, 50,109 remained, with an average of 3482 genes per cell. Cell‐type and neuronal subtype marker gene expression values were derived from log‐normalized RNA counts.

Statistical testing and plotting

Statistical testing was conducted in GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software). Dataset normality was determined with the D'Agostino and Pearson test. Parametric data were tested using two‐tailed unpaired t tests, paired t tests, and one‐way, two‐way, or three‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests. Non‐parametric data were compared using Mann–Whitney rank testing. MEA time course data were tested using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) mixed‐effects model. MEA culture wells were treated as a random effect and time point, genotype, and time point x genotype were tested as fixed effects. MEA network potentiation experimental results were tested with a repeated‐measures three‐way ANOVA test (factors: genotype, cLTP treatment, and time point). Plots were generated in R statistical software or GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as appropriate. Representative immunocytochemistry images of analyzed neurons were generated in Imaris and signal brightness levels were optimized for signal visibility. MEA spike time histogram and raster plot traces were generated using Matlab.

RESULTS

Widespread disruption of protein interactions among AMPA receptors, NMDA receptors, scaffolds, and effectors in SORL1 KO excitatory cortical neurons

To understand the impact of AD‐related SORL1 loss in excitatory neurons, we queried the Seattle Alzheimer's Disease Brain Cell Atlas data set, an open‐data resource aimed to help identify molecular and cellular pathologies that underlie the disease. 38 , 39 When we specifically queried SORL1, we observed high expression in excitatory cortical cells with a significant loss in the disease state (Figure S2 in supporting information). Based on our previous observation of reduced cell surface GLUR1 in SORL1 KO neurons, we hypothesized that altered endosomal recycling due to SORL1 deficiency 22 alters the composition of synaptic proteins in excitatory neurons. After synapse‐dependent network bursting activity was confirmed in parallel cultures by MEA, ≈ DPP 35, cultures were lysed and subjected to QMI, a mesoscale proteomics assay that reports the relative abundance of ≈ 400 binary interactions among 21 proteins expressed at the excitatory glutamate synapse 31 , 34 (see Table S1 for proteins and antibodies). Principal component analysis of QMI data matrices showed clear separation of wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons across principal component 1, with additional effects of both batch (two separate differentiations) and cell line (two clonal cell lines in duplicate per differentiation; Figure 1B). To identify the specific interactions responsible for this effect, QMI data were processed using two independent statistical approaches: (1) adaptive non‐parametric test with adjustable alpha cutoff (ANC) was designed specifically for QMI datasets 40 and compares bead distributions for each of 400 interactions while correcting for multiple comparisons; while (2) correlation network analysis (CNA) 41 clusters interactions by similar behavior across all experimental samples, and asks if any co‐regulated clusters, termed modules, correlate with experimental variables (Figure 1A). Only interactions that meet both CNA and ANC significance thresholds are reported hereafter. CNA identified four modules that correlated with genotype, arbitrarily color‐coded Blue (correlation coefficient [CC] = 0.86, p = 2 × 10−5), Magenta (CC = 0.67, p = 0.005), Turquoise (CC = 0.57, p = 0.02), and Black (CC = 0.56, p = 0.03). Note, however, that Magenta and Black also correlated strongly with a batch effect (CC = 0.93, p = 3 × 10−7 and CC = 0.8, p = 2 × 10−4, respectively) and Turquoise correlated with a batch x cell line effect (CC = 0.94, p = 4 × 10−7). Blue was the only module not confounded by batch and/or cell line. A heatmap of interactions that were ANC‐significant for either batch 1 or 2, and a member of the four genotype‐significant CNA modules (Figure 1C) demonstrates these effects. Interactions that were ANC‐significant across both batches are in bold/italic. Immunoprecipitation target GLUR1, probe GLUR1 (abbreviated GLUR1_GLUR1) was the interaction most significantly correlated to the Blue module and was consistently reduced in SORL1 KO cells across both batches (Figure 1D). Interactions in which the IP and probe are for the same protein can indicate changes in abundance, multimerization, or changes in other co‐associated proteins that may occlude antibody binding sites; 33 prior published data showing reduced protein localization of GLUR1 supports the former interpretation. 22 GLUR2_GLUR2 was significantly correlated to the Black module, and was only significant by ANC for batch 2 (Figure 1E). These differences, combined with changes in GLUR1_GLUR2 in batch 2, suggest a shift in AMPA receptor subunit usage due to known endosomal GLUR1 trafficking deficits in SORL1 mutants. In addition, alterations in HOMER1_HOMER1 (Figure 1F, Shank1_panShank (Figure 1G), and Homer_GLUR2 (Figure 1H) indicate alterations to the Homer‐Shank scaffolding network that supports AMPA receptor localization. Extensive changes to downstream effector molecules such as the RAS‐GTPase SynGAP (exemplified by NMDAR2A_SynGAP, Figure 1C) or the SRC‐family kinase FYN (Figure 1I) indicate altered signal transduction downstream of synaptic activity that could affect glutamate receptor properties or synaptic plasticity. A node‐edge diagram limited to interactions that were ANC‐CNA significant in both batches demonstrates the synapse‐wide scope of the differences observed (Figure 1J). These data supported further investigations into neuronal activity in SORL1 KO neurons.

Loss of SORL1 changes pre‐synaptic and post‐synaptic protein expression

QMI analysis demonstrated changes in multiple categories of excitatory synaptic proteins and their interactions. We next investigated if these widespread changes were reflected in alterations of pre‐ or post‐synaptic proteins using immunocytochemistry. Wild‐type and SORL1 KO neuronal cultures were matured, fixed, and stained for SYNAPSIN and HOMER1 to identify pre‐ and post‐synaptic structures, respectively. We performed three‐dimensional analysis of pre‐ and post‐synaptic markers. We found clear expression of SYNAPSIN and HOMER1 in the MAP2‐positive somatodendritic compartments of wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons (Figure 2A). Colocalization of the pre‐ and post‐synaptic markers suggests that both neuronal genotypes form complete synaptic structures (Figure 2A, lower panels, arrows). Several differences were detected in SORL1 KO neurons. Consistent with QMI analysis, we detected increased expression of post‐synaptic scaffolding protein HOMER1. SORL1 KO neuron HOMER1 puncta were larger and showed a greater mean fluorescence intensity (Figure 2B). Staining of the presynaptic marker SYNAPSIN (SYN) revealed an increased fluorescence intensity and puncta volume in SORL1 KO (Figure 2C), an analogous increase in the pre‐synaptic compartment alongside the post‐synaptic structure. We found more HOMER1 puncta in SORL1 KO neurons (Figure 2B) and no difference in the number of SYN puncta, suggesting that loss of SORL1 influences the post‐synaptic density greater than the pre‐synaptic. Three‐dimensional colocalization analysis (Figure 2D) revealed no difference in the number of colocalized HOMER1 and SYNAPSIN positive puncta, suggesting that the number of putative synapses does not differ between SORL1 KO neurons and isogenic controls (Figure 2E, right). The volume of HOMER1 and SYNAPSIN colocalizations were larger in SORL1 KO neurons, intimating larger synaptic structures (Figure 2E, left). Taken together, these data indicate that altered synaptic structures are found on both sides of the synapse in SORL1 KO neurons. We next compared pre‐ and post‐synaptic protein signals in the somatic and dendritic compartments (Figure S3 in supporting information). HOMER1 intensity, volume, and frequency were greater in the dendrites of SORL1 KO neurons (Figure S3C). In contrast, we observed no differences in the somatic compartments (Figure S3A). The frequency of SYN was lower in the cell bodies of SORL1 KO neurons but, like HOMER1, the intensity and volume of SYN signal was greater in the dendrites of SORL1‐deficient neurons. Together, the observed differences in whole‐cell synaptic marker analysis were driven principally by changes in the dendritic compartments. SORL1 KO neurons show clear differences in pre‐ and post‐synaptic proteins, recapitulating the increased HOMER1 interactions identified by QMI (Figure 1).

SORL1 KO neurons have altered AMPA receptor subunit expression

We have shown previously that SORL1 KO neurons have impaired trafficking of the glutamate AMPA receptor subunit GLUR1 22 and here we find altered interactions of GLUR1 and GLUR2 subunits in SORL1 KO neurons with QMI analysis (Figure 1). We performed an immunocytochemical assay of GLUR1 and GLUR2 expression in matured wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons to further examine the expression profiles of these two AMPA receptor subunits in single neurons. We found GLUR1 and GLUR2 expression in wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons (Figure 3A). To account for the larger post‐synaptic densities in SORL1 KO neurons (Figure 2), we normalized GLUR quantifications to genotype‐specific HOMER1 volume. SORL1 KO neurons expressed smaller GLUR1 puncta and less signal intensity but we found no difference in frequency (Figure 3B). Conversely, SORL1 KO neurons had, on average, GLUR2 puncta with greater mean fluorescence intensities than isogenic wild‐type control neurons but similar volumes and frequency of GLUR2 signals (Figure 3C). By both volume and intensity measurements, SORL1 KO neurons exhibited decreased GLUR1/GLUR2 ratios (Figure 3D, E). These patterns of expression align with GLUR1 and GLUR2 interaction changes observed in QMI (Figure 1) and may reflect a shift in the balance of GLUR1 and GLUR2 subunit expression profiles when SORL1‐dependent trafficking is disrupted. GLUR1 and GLUR2 expression in somatic and dendritic compartments was analyzed and we found little difference compared to whole‐cell analysis (Figure S4 in supporting information). Similar ratios of GLUR1/GLUR2 were also detected in both the soma and dendrites of SORL1 KO neurons. These data show that differences in AMPA receptor subunit expression are occurring cell wide and not differently in somatic and dendritic compartments and add to our previous findings of GLUR1 trafficking being disrupted.

SORL1‐deficient neurons are hyperexcitable

Broad changes in synaptic protein composition suggest physiological consequences of loss of SORL1. Laboratory models of AD repeatedly demonstrate hyperexcitable neuronal phenotypes at the network and cellular level. 5 , 6 , 7 , 42 Here, to assess how SORL1 affects neuronal function, we measured the spiking activity of SORL1 KO and isogenic control hiPSC‐derived neurons on MEA plates for 60 days post‐differentiation. Over this time period SORL1 KO cultures exhibited several hyperexcitable phenotypes (Figure 4). SORL1 KO neurons generated more action potentials than isogenic controls, with SORL1 KO cultures generating greater mean firing rates (Figure 4B). The timing of KO neurons spiking was also less regular, as demonstrated by the higher coefficient of variation of the inter‐spike interval in SORL1 KO neuron cultures (Figure 4C). After ≈ 3 weeks, KO and control neuronal cultures began to exhibit coordinated activity indicative of neurotransmission‐driven network firing, or network bursting, consistent with the detection of colocalizations of pre‐ and post‐synaptic proteins (Figures 2, 4A). SORL1 KO neuronal hyperexcitability was conserved at the network level and KO cultures generated more network bursts compared to isogenic controls (Figure 4D). SORL1 KO culture network bursting also showed more irregularity in the timing of the inter‐burst interval (IBI), reflected by an increased coefficient of variation in the IBI (Figure 4E). We next examined the rates of spiking inside and outside the network burst window to quantify firing during the periods of synaptically driven network activity. Surprisingly, we found no difference between genotypes in the mean firing rate outside of coordinated bursting (Figure 4G). Within the duration of the burst, however, SORL1 KO neurons fired at an increased rate (Figure 4F). These results indicate that SORL1 KO neuron hyperexcitability is occurring primarily during coordinated network bursts.

Hyperexcitability in SORL1‐deficient neurons is glutamate dependent

In vitro cultures of hiPSC excitatory neurons developing synaptically driven, coordinated network bursting is an established phenomenon. 43 , 44 The hyperactivity of SORL1 KO network activity and changes observed in excitatory synapse proteins suggest changes to synapses could be driving SORL1 KO neuron hyperexcitability. Here, we tested if SORL1 KO hyperexcitability persisted with blockade of fast ionotropic glutamatergic AMPA and NMDA receptors. After the development of network bursting, wild‐type and SORL1 KO neuronal cultures were treated with 10 µM NBQX and 50 µM APV to block AMPA‐ and NMDA‐mediated neurotransmission. Network bursting was completely abolished in both wild‐type and SORL1 KO cultures with treatment (Figure 5A). Mean firing rates and coefficient of variation of inter‐spike intervals also decreased with antagonist application in both genotypes (Figure 5B,C). Notably, the differences in these metrics between wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons were eliminated when glutamate neurotransmission was blocked and restored after antagonist washout, suggesting that excitatory synaptic activity is a principal driver of the network activity differences we observed. Inhibitory neurotransmission shapes network activity 44 , 45 , 46 and dual‐SMAD neuronal differentiation can lead to the expression of inhibitory neuron genes and proteins. 47 While our differentiation protocol yields very low numbers of inhibitory neurons (Figure S1), to investigate the contribution of GABAA receptor activity to the network, we applied 10 µM gabazine, which led to small, non‐significant increases in firing rates of wild‐type and SORL1 KO neuronal cultures (Figure 5D and Figure S5 in supporting information). No changes were observed in network bursting rate (Figure S5B) or coefficient of variation of the inter‐burst interval (Figure S5C) with gabazine treatment. While not sufficient to rule out inhibitory neuron influence on the observed hyperactivity phenotypes, these results suggest that they play a smaller role than excitatory neurotransmission in this hiPSC neuron model. Wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons showed similar levels and patterns of spiking when glutamate receptors were blocked, suggesting that the hyperexcitability phenotypes we found are the result of abnormal excitatory glutamate neurotransmission.

Hyperexcitability in SORL1 KO neurons is not solely due to increases in secreted Aβ

SORL1 KO and SORL1 loss‐of‐function variant hiPSC neurons secrete more soluble Aβ as a result of impaired amyloid precursor protein (APP) trafficking. 21 , 25 Soluble Aβ causes increased neuronal activity and impaired synaptic plasticity in hiPSC neurons and other laboratory models. 42 , 48 , 49 , 50 To examine whether increased Aβ could explain the physiological phenotypes we observe in SORL1 KO neurons, we compared neurophysiological activity of neural cultures using MEA and harvested medium from MEA wells to measure secreted Aβ protein concentrations of SORL1 KO neurons, an isogenic line carrying homozygous APP mutations KM670 and 671NL, the APP Swedish mutations (APP Swe+/+), and an isogenic wild‐type control. APP Swe+/+ neurons secreted much higher levels of Aβ1–40 and 1–42 than wild‐type control and SORL1 KO neurons 26 (Figure 6A–D). Consistent with prior studies, 24 APP Swe+/+ neurons were hyperactive compared to isogenic wild‐type controls and developed coordinated network bursting (Figure 6E,F). Interestingly, mean firing rates of APP Swe+/+ neurons were similar to SORL1 KO neurons prior to the development of network bursting (Figure 6E). After network bursting was established, APP Swe+/+ neurons continued to fire more than isogenic controls but significantly less than SORL1 KO neurons (Figure 6F). All cell lines generated more Aβ1–42 after developing bursting and wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons also secreted significantly more Aβ1–40 (Figure S6 in supporting information). Network bursting SORL1 KO neurons generated significantly more spiking than APP Swe+/+ neurons (Figure 6G). Despite the increased level of neuronal spiking observed prior to and after network bursting in APP Swe+/+ neurons compared to control neurons, APP Swe+/+ neurons did not generate more network bursts compared to WT neuronal cultures, in contrast with SORL1 KO cultures, which maintained their hyperactivity at the network level (Figure 6G). These results further support a SORL1‐dependent physiological mechanism that, when disrupted, leads to neuronal hyperactivity. When firing rates and concentrations of secreted Aβ are compared directly, we found that the hyperactivity observed in SORL1 KO neuronal cultures is not due solely to increased Aβ secretion (Figure 6H).

Loss of SORL1 inhibits network activity potentiation

Long‐term potentiation (LTP) is a form of synaptic plasticity in which GLUR1‐containing AMPA receptors are rapidly trafficked to the postsynaptic membrane in response to high levels of presynaptic activity. 51 Inhibited LTP has been linked to AD in several models, and the SORL1 binding partner retromer is a trafficking complex that is necessary for LTP. 10 , 11 Here, we used a cLTP_protocol optimized for iPSC neurons described in Pré et al. 35 to examine the network activity of SORL1 KO neuronal cultures that have undergone LTP induction. The adenylyl cyclase activator forskolin was used in combination with the phosphodiesterase inhibitor rolipram to stimulate the cAMP second messenger pathway and prevent cAMP degradation. We induced cLTP in wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons plated on MEA culture plates and assayed the electrical activity for 72 hours post induction. Wild‐type neurons displayed increases in network bursting rate that lasted up to 72 hours post induction (Figure 7A,B). In contrast, SORL1 KO neurons failed to recover network bursting activity for up to 24 hours post induction (Figure 7A,B), with significantly lower fold‐change bursting rates compared to wild‐type cultures. Mean firing rates measured from MEA cultures increased in wild‐type cultures more slowly than the increase in network bursting, similar to what has been described in dopaminergic neuronal network cLTP responses. 35 cLTP‐treated SORL1 KO neurons showed no change in mean firing rates compared to pretreatment baseline conditions (Figure 7C). Interestingly, SORL1 KO neurons do exhibit an increased network bursting rate once network activity has resumed and the differences between wild‐type and SORL1 KO network bursting fades at the 48‐hour time point post induction (Figure 7A,B). These data demonstrate that SORL1 is an important component of shifts of network activity strength.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we report that loss of SORL1 expression affects excitatory synapse protein composition and expression patterns, neuronal function, and the induction of network‐wide potentiation. Given the high expression of SORL1 and vulnerability of excitatory neurons in AD, we chose to use a model that would allow for an isolated examination of their function 40 , 41 (Figure S2). Synaptic dysfunction is a very early event in AD progression 52 and the main contributor to cognitive decline; 53 thus, preventing this impairment at an early stage would be a beneficial therapeutic option for AD.

In AD, hyperexcitability has been linked to numerous mechanisms, including neuropathological hallmarks Aβ and p‐tau. 54 Increases in Aβ molecules can directly depolarize neurons and disrupt inhibitory circuits. 55 , 56 In addition, soluble Aβ may be synaptogenic. 24 p‐tau pathology can also impair axonal transport and destabilize neuronal networks. 52 Impaired endosomal recycling has emerged as a hypothesis that is upstream of both pathogenic Aβ and p‐tau and can contribute to both pathologies. 57 Multiple proteins necessary for normal synaptic composition are trafficked through the endosomal network. 58 , 59 Based on our previous work, 22 we hypothesized that impaired endosomal recycling in SORL1‐deficient neurons would alter synaptic protein composition and interactions. Using an unbiased approach, QMI, we show that multiple synaptic proteins, including receptors and scaffolding proteins, show altered protein–protein interactions.

HOMER1 and PSD95 postsynaptic scaffolding proteins both demonstrate increased interactions in SORL1 KO neurons, suggesting post‐synaptic density alterations. This is supported by our immunostaining results showing larger volumes and intensities of HOMER1 signal (Figure 2). We also observed increased signal intensity and volume of SYNAPSIN puncta. Interestingly, we found similar numbers of pre‐ and post‐synaptic protein colocalizations in wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons (Figure 2), suggesting that while SORL1 contributes to the maintenance of proteins at synapses, its absence does not result in compensatory formation of new synapses. Both QMI and HOMER1 ICC results point toward structural alterations in the post‐synaptic structure but whether synaptic morphology is significantly altered remains to be explored.

We also observe alterations in AMPA receptor subunits GLUR1 and GLUR2 both by QMI and by immunostaining. There is a significant decrease in GLUR1–GLUR1 interactions and GLUR1/GLUR2 ratios in SORL1 KO neurons, which corroborates our previous findings of reduced GLUR1 subunits trafficked to the neuronal membrane. Loss of GLUR1 on neuronal membranes has also been seen in models of retromer deficiency, 10 , 60 suggesting that SORL1–retromer trafficking is a key mechanism for maintaining surface AMPA receptor levels. We observe increased GLUR2_GLUR2 interactions and increased intensity in GLUR2 immunostaining in SORL1 KO neurons. While GLUR2 is also trafficked via retromer, 61 its interaction with SORL1 is not clear. In a mouse neuronal model of VPS35 deficiency, decreased synaptic transmission could be rescued by GLUR2 overexpression. Thus, increased GLUR2 levels in our model may be a compensatory effect. GLUR2 subunit incorporation into AMPA receptors prolongs the decay time of synaptic currents, and leads to more efficient excitatory postsynaptic potential–action potential coupling, 62 which could contribute to the increased bursting we observed. In addition, GLUR2 subunits control the calcium permeability of AMPA receptors and block calcium influx after post transcriptional Q/R site editing. Decreased GLUR2 editing in the neurodegenerative disease amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) contributes to excitotoxicity in motor neurons. 63 Similar mechanisms may be occurring here.

Blocking glutamatergic neurotransmission and synchronized network bursting normalizes SORL1 KO neuron hyperexcitability demonstrating that excitatory synaptic activity contributes to the aberrant firing we observe in our model. Wild‐type and SORL1 KO neuron firing rates are similar outside of synchronized network bursts, adding further support for a functional consequence of the synaptic protein shifts we observed. The hyperexcitability phenotypes we find here are consistent with other recent work performed on SORL1 KO neurons. 64 There, the authors find hyperactivity in not only SORL1 KO neurons but also the missense variant p.Y1816C. These results build upon other reports of detrimental effects of SORL1 pathogenic variants 25 , 65 , 66 , 67 and demonstrate that neuronal function is dependent upon functional SORL1 protein and not affected solely by its complete loss of protein expression. Additionally, their measurements are taken from a different parental cell line and generated using a transcription factor induced differentiation protocol, distinct from the dual‐SMAD inhibition protocol used here, emphasizing that the critical component in our studies to be missing or malfunctioning SORL1. Together, these results demonstrate the robustness of SORL1’s importance for healthy neuronal function and contributions to early disease pathology.

Our findings demonstrate that SORL1 deficiency disrupts neuronal network activity through a mechanism that is largely independent of soluble Aβ. While increases in secreted Aβ due to loss of SORL1 are documented 21 and may contribute to some of the hyperexcitability we observe, neurons with the APP Swedish mutation secrete more than 5X the amount of Aβ and are less hyperactive after the onset of synchronized, synaptically driven network activity. This suggests that the primary driver of neuronal hyperexcitability in SORL1‐deficient neurons is due to other mechanisms. Additionally, increased levels of soluble Aβ are synaptogenic 24 and SORL1 KO neurons’ increased Aβ secretion may contribute to some of the changes to HOMER1 and SYNAPSIN we observe here. It is likely that a pathological increase of Aβ secretion and the downstream effects act synergistically with other mechanisms of SORL1‐dependent hyperactivity and require further examination to tease apart.

Synaptic plasticity is dependent on the movement of receptors and proteins at the synapse, and deficits in LTP and cortical plasticity have been demonstrated in AD models and human brains 68 , 69 and SORLA's binding partner retromer. 10 , 11 Here, we report the novel finding that loss of SORL1 inhibits the ability of hiPSC neurons to shift network activity in response to chemical stimulation. Recycling endosomes near the post‐synaptic structure hold and shuttle AMPA receptors for LTP induction 58 and SORL1‐deficient neurons demonstrate recycling endosome swelling and increased co‐localization with GLUR1 AMPA subunits. 22 The lowered levels of excitatory neurotransmission‐driven activity after cLTP induction we see here are possibly due to a failure in the movement of GLUR1‐containing AMPA receptors in response to induction, but direct measurements of post‐synaptic potentials are required for confirmation. Interestingly, we do see a slow response to cLTP induction in SORL1 KO neurons, showing that the ability to induce long‐lasting changes in firing isn't completely lost.

Limitations of this study

While the data reported here show evidence for both altered excitatory synapses and hyperactivity in SORL1 KO neurons, we cannot isolate changes to synaptic currents or intrinsic neuronal excitability as these features cannot be directly addressed with multi‐electrode arrays. Further, direct measurements of these electrophysiological properties are warranted. In our previous study, 22 bulk RNA sequencing showed increases in ion channel expression in SORL1 KO neurons, which may be contributing to hyperexcitability independently of changes to synaptic proteins. Similarly, the shifts in GLUR1/2 ratios we observe in SORL1 KO neurons suggests altered AMPA receptor composition and additional electrophysiological experiments are needed to conclusively determine whether such a shift is occurring. Changes in excitatory–inhibitory balance has been implicated in AD‐driven hyperactivity in laboratory models and in AD patients. 6 , 7 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 While we find no effect of blocking GABAA inhibitory receptors (Figures 5, S5) beyond a small, non‐significant increase in firing rate in both wild‐type and SORL1 KO neurons, we cannot eliminate changes to inhibitory neurons as a possible mechanism of SORL1’s physiological effects. GAD1‐ and GAD2‐expressing neurons are present at low levels with the method of neuronal differentiation used here (Figure S1).

In addition to inhibitory contributions, neural network activity is supported and shaped by glia. The hiPSC neuron model used here generates a predominantly excitatory neural culture, with only small numbers of glial markers detected (Figure S1), limiting interpretations of the neuronal and network hyperexcitability we observe. As SORL1 is expressed in astrocytes (Figure S2), we cannot rule out their influence on network activity. Astrocytic glutamate reuptake through the excitatory amino‐acid transporter 2 (EEAT2) is critical for glutamate clearance at excitatory synapses in the brain and EEAT2 activity is inhibited by Aβ, 48 but any interactions between EEAT2 and SORL1 remain unexplored. Microglia express high levels of SORL1 and depletion of SORL1 negatively impacts lysosomal function and increases phagocytic activity, 23 which likely leads to altered pruning of synaptic networks and the resulting activity. Microglia are not present in the cultures used in this study but the combinatorial effects of SORL1‐deficient neurons and microglia is an important step forward in understanding SORL1–AD biology. While all human studies are limited, further examination using co‐cultures are required to better understand the role of SORL1 in a more complete human model.

CONCLUSION

Our work supports a model in which synaptic trafficking defects represent an early and potentially targetable mechanism in AD pathogenesis. These insights suggest that therapeutic interventions aimed at restoring proper endosomal function or correcting synaptic protein trafficking may offer effective strategies for preventing cognitive decline in AD.
