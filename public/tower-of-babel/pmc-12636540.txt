A question of risk: how do undergraduate GP teachers experience entrustment decision-making in primary care?
Authors: Jayne Victoria Cullen, Hugh Alberti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe decision to trust a learner to actively participate in care is a fundamental part of clinical training. However, concerns about patient safety mean that clinical practice often presents newly qualified doctors with situations they are unable to manage independently. ‘Entrustable professional activities’ (EPAs) have been proposed as a possible solution to this unpreparedness. Understanding how undergraduate GP teachers make entrustment decisions, which is a gap in the existing literature, may be an appropriate first step towards understanding whether EPAs can be applied to undergraduate primary care. To explore teachers’ experiences of entrustment decision-making in an undergraduate primary care context. This is an interpretive phenomenological study. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with final year GP teachers. Interviews were audio recorded, transcribed and subjected to thematic analysis. Five GP teachers were interviewed. Five themes and twenty-four subthemes were produced. These captured participants’ experiences of entrustment decision-making, as well as the factors influencing these decisions. The teacher's personal approach to risk was a key consideration. The experience of GP teachers is also shaped by their context and challenges such as managing complexity and uncertainty that are inherent to the GP role. Entrustment decision-making is complex and is experienced individually by different teachers, resulting in a range of approaches to supervision. Despite these differences there are various common factors that impact the decision of whether to entrust. This study allows several recommendations to be made that may enable us to move towards entrustment of undergraduate students in a primary care context.Keywords: Entrustment, entrustable professional activities, supervision, undergraduate, primary care

Background

Workplace learning poses many challenges for clinical teachers, perhaps the greatest of which is balancing learning and patient care. The decision to trust a learner to actively participate in care is a fundamental part of clinical training. However, in recent years the patient safety agenda has made many less accepting of this. As a result, clinical practice often presents newly qualified doctors with situations they are unable to manage safely without supervision [1]. The concurrent rise of competency based medical education (CBME), in part driven by a desire to ‘quality-assure’ competence before students take any responsibility for healthcare delivery [1,2], may also contribute to unpreparedness for independent practice. There is an urgent need to ‘close the gap’ between what is taught to undergraduate medical students and what they are expected to do on day one as graduates [3]. This desire to improve preparedness is a strong driver for change.

In postgraduate training, the concept of entrustable professional activities (EPAs) as a means of safely translating competencies into the tasks of professional practice has been met with widespread enthusiasm [4]. Originally described by ten Cate in 2005, EPAs can be defined as ‘the core activities of physicians’ work that are observable and measurable and can be entrusted by a supervisor to a trainee with a certain amount of autonomy’ (ten Cate, in [5], p. 752). It has been argued that there is a strong case for the use of EPAs in undergraduate medical education [6] and that such an approach may bridge the gap between undergraduate and postgraduate training and go some way towards tackling unpreparedness. Significant progress has been made in the United States, and following completion of the Association of American Medical Colleges 10 school pilot [7], EPAs now exist at an undergraduate level in all clinical specialties including primary care.

Many of the factors influencing entrustment decision-making are well described. However, the literature on the development and implementation of EPAs in undergraduates remains sparse. In their review, Shorey and colleagues [4] identified only nine papers where the target learners were undergraduates. Of note, only four studies in this review were in a ‘family medicine’ context. At the time of writing there were no studies specifically examining entrustment decision-making in an undergraduate primary care context, and thus we aimed to explore undergraduate GP teachers’ experiences of entrustment decision-making. Specifically, we set out to answer how undergraduate GP teachers experience entrustment decision-making in clinical settings, what factors influence the GP teacher in their decision to entrust, and what evidence is used to inform this decision.

Methods

The purpose of this study was to explore and understand GP teachers’ experiences of entrustment decision-making, aligning with a relativist ontology and interpretivist paradigm. Phenomenology aims to provide rich, complex descriptions of the subjective ‘lived experience’ of individuals [8] and was congruous with the research question and paradigm stance. Semi-structured interviews were selected as they are described as the ‘exemplary method’ for phenomenological research [9], allowing researcher and participant to engage in dialogue that is modified in light of the participants’ responses in order to explore their experiences in depth. An interview schedule was created based on the literature, the researcher's expectations and the research questions, and use of this schedule as part of the questioning technique was reviewed after each interview. Participants were encouraged to discuss their experiences with as little interruption as possible, but prompts were prepared in the event that this proved difficult, which is important where the topic of discussion is complex [9].

Purposive sampling was used, which is a common approach in studies of this type. This allows the researcher to choose participants that will satisfy the aims of the study [10]. Potential participants were identified through the medical school office and an invitation email sent to all GP teachers responsible for regularly supervising final year medical students from one UK medical school. The study information sheet specified that they should have had recent experience of conducting parallel surgeries, in the hope that they would have experience of the phenomenon under study. Maximum variation sampling was then used to select participants with a range of clinical and educational experience from those that responded. It was hoped that, by doing so, the researcher would capture differing points of view and thus allow ‘diversification’ of the data [11]. A total of five GP teachers were interviewed. Each interview was 40−60 minutes long. Interviews were recorded and transcribed by a professional transcription company.

Data collection and analysis were conducted in parallel to allow an iterative approach to be taken, so the researcher could reflect on the process, refine it, and begin ‘dwelling with the data’ [12, p. 186], analyzing its meaning whilst it was still being gathered. Thematic analysis was chosen as the method of analysis. Although sometimes criticized as poorly demarcated and not always acknowledged as a method in its own right, this is a ‘theoretically flexible’ approach in that it can be applied across a range of theoretical and epistemological approaches [13, p. 78] and is considered coherent with an interpretivist paradigm. It is widely used to identify, analyze and report patterns (themes) within qualitative data [13, p. 79]. The six-phase approach described by Braun and Clarke [13] was used as a guide and is outlined below.

Phase 1- familiarizing yourself with your data

Data was transcribed using an online transcription service for pragmatic reasons. The transcripts produced were checked for accuracy against the audio-recordings and required extensive editing, which was helpful in allowing the researcher to become familiar with and immersed in the data. This was further achieved through repeated re-reading and noting down very early ideas.

Phase 2- generating initial codes

Next, codes were generated across the entire data set systematically. An inductive, data-driven approach was taken. This was done initially by JC, by writing notes in the margins of the transcripts and highlighting potential patterns or interesting sections of text. The codes were then collated by copying extracts from individual transcripts and shared and agreed with HA. As suggested in Braun and Clarke's approach, the data set was coded for as many potential patterns as possible and some of the surrounding text was retained so that the context was not lost. As part of this phase, participants were sent a section of their transcript with initial codes and asked if they felt the coding was a true representation of the data.

Phase 3- searching for themes

Phase 3 began with a list of all the codes identified across the entire data set, which were then collated into potential themes proposed by JC and modified after discussion with HA. This was achieved with the help of a visual representation, where codes were written on ‘post-it’ notes and sorted into theme-piles. As this phase progressed, some initial codes became themes and others sub-themes.

Phase 4- reviewing the themes

Level 1-The extracts related to each theme were read to determine if they formed a coherent pattern. In some cases, this meant moving an extract to another already-existing theme or creating a new one. An early thematic map was then produced.

Level 2-The thematic map was considered in terms of the data set as a whole, to ensure it accurately reflected the meanings therein. A small number of additional codes were added when the whole data-set was re-read and agreed with by both authors.

Phase 5- defining and naming themes

This phase involves identifying the ‘essence’ of each theme, or what aspect of the data it captures. This was achieved by returning to the collated data extracts, refining the themes, and organizing them into a coherent narrative. Each theme was also considered in relation to the others.

Phase 6- producing the report

In the final phase, example extracts were chosen for inclusion in the final write-up. The analysis is then linked back to the original research question and the relevant literature, which is presented in the discussion.

Frambach's quality principles [14] were followed to ensure rigor including member checking, a thick description, a clear audit trail, iterative data collection and analysis, peer debriefing and reflexivity.

Results

Five GP teachers of variable genders, ages and teaching experiences were interviewed (See Appendix 1).

Context

At the time this study was undertaken, all final year students at Newcastle University undertook a three-week essential senior rotation (ESR) in General Practice. Prior to this they were exposed to primary care through visits in year 1 and 2 and weekly sessions throughout year 3. Parallel surgeries are one of the key components of the ESR. The following description is taken from the study guide.

Parallel surgeries – following appropriate time observing your GP tutor and other GPs and health care professionals, you will be allowed an opportunity to consult with patients while being observed by your tutor. Following this you will have a number of parallel surgeries where patients are booked in with and see you first, before they are presented to the GP to decide on the management of the patient. This allows the development of consulting skills, presenting skills as well as real life discussions on differential diagnosis, investigations and management. This component has received outstanding feedback from previous students.

This was considered to be an appropriate context in which to explore entrustment.

It should also be noted that the study took place during the Covid−19 pandemic and that a greater proportion of consultations were being conducted remotely than previously.

Development of the themes and subthemes

Thematic analysis of the interview transcripts was undertaken iteratively as described previously. 189 initial codes were generated across the entire data set. These codes were then collated into potential themes and a very early thematic map was produced. The themes were then reviewed and refined by repeatedly returning to the collated data extracts for each theme. Some subthemes from the early thematic map overlapped and were combined (communication skills, clinical ability and knowledge became one subtheme named ‘competence’, for example) and others were discarded as there was insufficient data to support them (such as assessment and doctor-patient relationship). The theme initially labeled ‘context’ was most extensively re-worked, and some of its subthemes were moved to already existing themes that were a better fit (‘nature of the task’, for example, was a more coherent fit with the theme ‘patient factors’). This process resulted in five main themes and twenty-four subthemes (Table 1).

Theme 1-teacher factors

This theme is about the aspects of a teacher's identity and personal experiences that affect their views on workplace learning and hence their propensity to entrust. All the participants felt that entrustment involved an unavoidable element of risk, both to the patient and to the supervising clinician, though the perception of level of risk varied significantly. This perception was not static; student and patient factors also influenced it. With this in mind, the teacher's personal approach to (or tolerance of) risk and uncertainty in general was a key subtheme. This was linked to personality and consultation style. The doctor's level of experience and fear of litigation also influenced their approach to risk. The views of relevant regulators such as the GMC were also perceived as key.

‘I think I'm quite a careful doctor. And so I do check things. So, you know, I feel uncomfortable taking unnecessary or exceptional risk with clinical cases.’ P5

Level of teaching experience was considered to be a significant factor: prior experiences with students informed decision-making and also had an impact on self-efficacy. Faculty development and support were also considered key. Clear guidance and expectations for teachers was considered important: teachers wanted to know that they were acting in a similar way to their peers.

‘You want to be sure that you're sort of not being a maverick.’ P2

Participants described how their personal views on workplace learning influenced their practice, informing their assessment of risk and determining the type of activities entrusted to students.

For example, P1 felt that, in reality, very few tasks are truly entrusted to final year students. She described feeling that this was not allowed, inappropriate, or too soon, and that she did not feel she had permission to hand tasks over without supervision.

‘I suppose I'm thinking, what tasks are you thinking that I'm actually handing over to them? And I don't think I'm handing over really any tasks. There isn't really very much that I'm giving them responsibility for the care of that patient, as a student.’ P1

In contrast, P3 described a huge range of tasks she was comfortable to entrust. This participant went on to say that she valued the student's contributions, such as their role as information gatherer, and that this helped them to accomplish other tasks if busy. She referred to her extensive prior experiences with entrustment and the absence of patient safety concerns, significant events or other negative repercussions. She also referred to the literature on the importance of providing authentic experiences in general practice and the influence of this on future career intentions as another strong argument for entrustment. Awareness of the relevant educational theory and literature was felt to help teachers overcome some of the barriers to entrustment, and also motivated them to give students more active roles.

‘In 16 years of teaching, I've never run into problems by giving fifth years more to do.’ P3

The teacher's personal life and stressors such as complaints were also perceived as important. Sense of wellbeing and resilience impacted the teacher's tolerance of risk, as well as impacting self-efficacy and confidence.

‘I suppose at the start of the first lockdown, I found the telephone consultations incredibly stressful. And I found (I’m quite a risk averse person), I found it very difficult. I suppose if I had had students in April, I think I may not have trusted them.’ P2

Theme 2-nature of general practice

Participants described several factors about the GP role that were perceived to make entrustment more challenging. This included part-time working, which could sometimes make it more difficult to ‘make that connection’ (P1) with a student which takes time to develop, the level of complexity and variety, and the unpredictable nature of general practice. Managing undifferentiated problems, spotting serious pathology, risk of deterioration and dealing with uncertainty were also perceived as challenges inherent to general practice that have a huge impact on entrustment.

‘We see undifferentiated patients where most people are absolutely fine but a small number aren’t, … people can become more unwell and there is definitely more individual risk there.’ P5

Competing demands, ever increasing workload and time pressure (often attributed to administrative tasks) all had a negative impact on propensity to entrust. Teachers sometimes felt guilty about the additional time taken or running late and were mindful that this needed to be acceptable to patients. Practice culture and ethos was considered vitally important. It was felt that students were much more likely to be given active roles if the whole team were supportive of this. Leadership and role modeling was also felt to be a factor, modulated by each teacher's own level of comfort.

‘We do feel a little bit like an educational institution.’ P3

The interviews took place during the Covid−19 pandemic, and all the participants mentioned the way in which this had impacted their experience of entrustment. This included lack of informal opportunities to meet with students and colleagues, both of which were important for forming and refining an impression about the student and the degree of autonomy that would be appropriate for them, as well as some discomfort or lack of familiarity with consulting remotely that was perceived by some as a barrier to entrustment.

The short length of the current final year placement (3 weeks at the time of the study) was mentioned multiple times as a factor negatively impacting entrustment. This was in part because of the length of the student-teacher relationship, and the feeling that trust took time to develop.

Theme 3-student factors

Several of the participants felt that professionalism and interpersonal skills of the student were key factors in their decision-making, and in some cases more important than their clinical knowledge or ability. Part of the rationale for this was the perception of a link between this factor and the likelihood of complaints.

‘If you feel like they're going to be pleasant and polite and professional, then you would trust them to speak to patients, and then whatever, clinically, they've got right or wrong, you can, you know, guide and change afterwards, can’t you? But you can't change that personal experience that they've given the patient.’ P2

The majority of participants felt that enthusiasm about being in practice, level of engagement and willingness to take on an active role were key considerations, with some students taking on responsibilities beyond those expected of a final year because they actively sought them out and wanted to contribute in ways that were helpful for the practice team. In turn, the process of entrustment and encouraging a person-centered approach were felt to increase motivation.

Communication skills including sensitivity and ability to develop rapport were considered vitally important. Missing cues or lack of empathy, especially if associated with a lack of self-awareness or responsiveness to feedback, were considered barriers to entrustment and caused some discomfort for the supervising doctor, sometimes prompting them to ‘take over’.

Clinical ability was mentioned by participants and included the ability to take a thorough history without significant omissions, to suggest a differential diagnosis, to identify red flags and to demonstrate the expected level of clinical reasoning.

All of the participants felt that being confident that the student knew when to ask for help and demonstrated awareness of their own limitations and learning needs supported the granting of entrustment. This had a significant impact on the perceived level of risk and was also linked to professionalism. Honesty and ability to share uncertainty and articulate clinical reasoning were also valued in students.

Theme 4-patient factors

All the participants felt patient group and problem type were significant factors in their decision of whether or not to entrust. Some groups or problems were perceived as more challenging for students, including mental health, children, ‘heart-sink’ patients, persistent physical symptoms, those who have previously been abusive, frequent attenders, and those considered vulnerable.

‘There are obviously people, I just think, ‘Oh, God, I can’t bear a student seeing them, because it'll just open everything up and just get too far too complicated, and they’ll just go up the wrong tree’.’ P3

However, some participants did emphasize that a degree of challenge and complexity was important and educationally valuable.

The level of suspicion of serious illness or pathology and overall assessment of acuity was also a key factor. Participants described the importance of their initial ‘end of the bed’ assessment and felt that they might accept student findings only if the situation was considered ‘low stakes’ or further examination would be unlikely to alter their management.

Overall acceptability to the patient and their willingness to see students was perceived to be a key factor by all the participants. Whilst some ‘professional patients’ were known to enjoy participating in education, it was felt that others would view the student as ‘second best’, and this might be less acceptable to those the teacher felt were particularly demanding or ‘consumerist’. This was a barrier to entrustment.

In contrast, some of the participants identified a therapeutic element to student consultations. Examinations were often perceived as more thorough by the patient and there were other benefits of a longer consultation that motivated the participants to entrust.

The nature of the task itself was a key factor for all the participants. That said, there was no consensus about which tasks were too challenging to entrust to students. For example, giving an explanation or ‘rounding-up’ was felt by one participant to be too difficult, and something students were rarely ready for, whereas another teacher was very comfortable with this.

Theme 5- sources of information

The final theme is about how the student factors already discussed are determined by the teacher. This was felt to occur through a combination of instinct, direct observation and careful consideration of the impression of the wider team, both clinical and non-clinical. First impressions were considered important, even if non-clinical. Instinct and intuition were perceived to play a part, and subjective, subtle evidence was valued. This ability to rely on instinct was linked to the teacher's level of experience. Sometimes prior knowledge of the student helped with rapidly forming an impression and making a connection.

‘And she just was a really good student.just meeting her in the coffee room I immediately thought, yeah, she's fine. Even though I hadn't seen her with the patients, if you know what I mean.’ P1

Direct observation of the student whilst ‘hot seating’ and case discussions were considered an important source of information by all the participants. An incremental approach was taken based on students demonstrating competence over time. Some of the participants felt that the decision to entrust was a collective one and that the responsibility was shared. Looking for consensus within the team was considered important. It was felt that this helped to ensure patient safety in the context of part time working and helped to mitigate any perceived lack of experience.

Discussion

Entrustment decision-making is experienced very differently by different teachers, resulting in a vast range of approaches to supervision. Some expressed uncertainty about whether it was appropriate in an undergraduate context at all, and, even if considered appropriate, there was a lack of consensus about the types of task that were appropriate to entrust. Others had the opposite view and were enthusiastic about the educational benefits of entrustment, as well as the possible benefits for their patients.

Despite these differences there are some common factors that impact the decision of whether to entrust. These are manifold and interlinked but a central consideration is the teacher's personal approach to and tolerance of risk. This in itself is complex, and is influenced by level of experience, views on workplace learning and personal wellbeing. Perhaps understandably, sharing the responsibility with the rest of the team was considered helpful and, importantly, teacher development can have an impact here.

The experience of GP teachers is also shaped by their context, and challenges such as managing complexity, variety and uncertainty that are considered inherent to the GP role. Sufficient protected time for teaching is vital and length of attachment also has an impact. Propensity to entrust is not static and there are also multiple interacting patient and student factors that must be balanced. Different teachers weighted these differently, but suspicion of serious illness in the patient and self-awareness in the student were given particular emphasis, perhaps because of their perceived impact on risk.

Comparison with previous studies and models

The themes and subthemes identified in this study are broadly similar to those described in the literature in a postgraduate context in various clinical settings [15,16] and to the factors identified in relevant reviews [4,17]. The relationship between student and teacher, identified as a fifth factor in the latter review, was less prominent here, perhaps because the current final year rotation is only three weeks long.

The participants in this study had very diverse views on entrustment that in turn had a significant impact on the level of supervision they considered appropriate for undergraduate students in primary care. Some authors have attempted to produce models to account for this variability in supervision style [18,19]. These models capture the tensions between a perception of personal responsibility for patient care, service provision, patient safety and learner autonomy that were also apparent in this study. The experience of entrustment decision-making is complex and personal to each GP teacher, resulting in a range of approaches to supervision.

Ten Cate and colleagues [20] propose three modes of trust in supervisor-trainee relationships: presumptive trust, initial trust, and grounded trust. The importance of instinct and first impressions were confirmed by this study. Both were identified as important sources of information by all the participants. Peters and colleagues [21] suggest that we should listen to and try to understand gut feelings about a learner and that these sorts of heuristics can sometimes outperform deep analysis in terms of quality of decision-making. Ad hoc entrustment decisions are often taken without much time to carefully deliberate and with limited information. This does not make them inaccurate, and it is acknowledged that not everything that grounds an entrustment decision can be captured using scales or in words [22].

Implications for practice and recommendations

Increase rotation length of undergraduate GP placements

One of the barriers frequently mentioned here was the length of the final year rotation. Sufficient acquaintance with a learner is critical if supervisors are to make valid entrustment decisions [23], and short rotations may not allow them to build meaningful relationships with students in order to discern the factors identified as important in this study. Both supervisors and learners need ‘time to trust’ [23] so longitudinal relationships are key.

Peters and colleagues [21] also suggest that curriculum leaders should aim for more consistent supervisor-learner pairings and longer working relationships to facilitate the development of grounded trust. Where this is difficult, learner handovers from one supervisor to the next or the use of student portfolios may be helpful.

Support GP practices to ensure sufficient protected time for teaching

Running a parallel surgery is time consuming and all the participants felt that protected time for teaching was vital and that without it many teachers wouldn’t have the capacity to give students an active role in patient care. Chen and colleagues’ [24] psychological framework for trust highlights the importance of addressing lower level needs to enable teachers to focus on facilitating entrustment.

Targeted GP teacher training and development to include open discussions about entrustment decision-making

Targeted teacher development is crucial. This may include training around the use of supervisory scales for assessment and establishing communities of practice for entrustment in undergraduate primary care. The importance of clear guidance for teachers is echoed by Favreau and colleagues [25].

Share the factors that facilitate entrustment with students

Peters and colleagues [21] suggest that supervisors need to talk explicitly about trust with learners and make it clear that it will influence participation. It follows that we should aim to share the factors identified with students so that they are aware that qualities such as enthusiasm, self-awareness and professionalism are considered just as important as clinical ability. Brown and colleagues [26] argue that we must develop mechanisms to enable students to meet expectations for entrustment and sharing this information may begin to address this.

Self-awareness was the most prominent student factor in this study. All the participants felt that being confident that the student knew when to ask for help supported the granting of entrustment. This finding is echoed in the literature [27]. These authors also suggest that establishing shared expectations is crucial, and that graduated autonomy is a shared responsibility between learner and supervisor.

Identify leaders and role models to help facilitate change

A desire for clear guidance, permission to reduce supervision, reassurance from peers and the need for strong role models appeared pivotal in this study and may help to change the views of those teachers less comfortable with entrustment of undergraduate students. The transition to an entrustment-based system should begin with leaders representing their value to stakeholders [28]. Ten Cate [29] argues that true entrustment requires that faculty, patients and regulators understand that doctors in training can be ready to bear full responsibility for specific tasks, and tasks us with the challenge: ‘be an advocate for a new view on certification and licensing’ [29, pg. 262]. Identifying teachers able to act as role models and share the educational benefits of entrustment in an undergraduate context will be key.

We need to challenge and move away from a binary approach and recognize that a learner progresses through many steps on the way to achieving full entrustment and should be rewarded with increasing autonomy at each ‘pre-entrustable step’ [21].

Strengths and limitations of the study

Entrustment is relevant to practice, and very topical. The study addresses an identified gap in the literature, the research aim and questions are well aligned, and the chosen methodology and methods are coherent with the aim and the phenomenon under study.

The study is intentionally small and contextual. The interpretive or hermeneutic phenomenological approach focuses on ‘understanding, explaining, and making meaning of the phenomenon’ [30]. Understanding meaning and context is key to the aims of this study, so this is the approach that was adopted. Phenomenological studies are typically conducted with a small number of participants to allow a sufficiently detailed analysis of the included cases [9]. Data saturation was of less importance here because ‘there is no saturation point with respect to phenomenological meaning’ [8, pg. 5].

Although the final themes are similar to those reported elsewhere in the literature, a data driven approach was taken and the final themes that were chosen were carefully considered to best capture the meaning of the data therein.

Finally, it is likely that there are unconscious biases that impact entrustment decisions - judgments that form the foundation of trust can be influenced by deeply ingrained, unconscious biases regarding traits such as gender, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic class, sexual orientation, physical abilities and age - [31] that are not explored here.

Conclusions

This study explored entrustment decision-making as experienced by clinical teachers in undergraduate primary care, in the hope that this might help us better balance learning and patient safety, tackle unpreparedness for practice, and provide more opportunities for students to contribute to care in this setting.

The findings suggest that the experience is complex and personal to each GP teacher. We make several recommendations including increasing rotation length, supporting practices to ensure sufficient protected time for teaching, targeted teacher development, sharing the factors that facilitate entrustment with students and identifying leaders and role models to help facilitate change.

Further work is needed to explore student's experiences of entrustment, and to identify which aspects of a teacher's behavior help/hinder the development of trust and the perceived impact of this on gaining practical experience and preparedness for independent practice. In this study, acceptability and benefits to the patient as perceived by the teacher were important factors. Further work is needed to explore patients’ views on factors that facilitate entrustment. In order to implement entrustment in an undergraduate primary care context, further work would also be needed to define speciality specific EPAs and map them to existing competencies.

We would recommend all medical schools consider implementing entrustment and EPAs in undergraduate GP placements and further work to evaluate the impact of this.
