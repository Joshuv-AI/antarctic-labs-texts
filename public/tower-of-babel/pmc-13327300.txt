Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity secondary to hypoglycemic encephalopathy: A case report
Authors: Yushi Kohzuki, Junpei Yamamoto, Yuki Kaminaga, Yoko Kayaba, Hiroshi Onodera, Masahiro Usui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 48‐year‐old male was admitted to our hospital due to a hypoglycemic coma. Although his blood glucose level was increased after glucose administration, his impaired consciousness persisted. Magnetic resonance imaging indicated hypoglycemic encephalopathy. On hospital Day 4, he exhibited paroxysmal hypertension, tachycardia, hyperthermia, tachypnea, excessive diaphoresis, and hyperglycemia. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis and electroencephalography were nonspecific, suggesting paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity (PSH) secondary to hypoglycemic encephalopathy. Although PSH following hypoglycemic encephalopathy is rare, clinicians should be aware of its possibility to enable timely intervention and improve prognosis.

INTRODUCTION

A comprehensive understanding of hypoglycemia is essential for the safe and effective management of diabetes mellitus. Glucose deficiency rapidly impairs neuronal function, with gray matter particularly vulnerable. Severe hypoglycemia can cause neurological damage and metabolic abnormalities 1 . Sympathetic activation is a physiological compensatory response to restore blood glucose levels 2 ; however, excessive activation may alter neural excitability and induce maladaptive physical responses. Paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity (PSH) is characterized by synchronous sympathetic hyperactivity following severe acquired brain injury 3 . Traumatic brain injury is the most common cause of PSH, whereas hypoglycemia is rarely reported. This case report describes the onset of PSH on Day 4 in a patient with hypoglycemic encephalopathy.

CASE REPORT

A 48‐year‐old male with a 20‐year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, treated with three daily subcutaneous injections of insulin aspart (20 units at breakfast, 20 units at lunch, and 15 units at dinner) and teneligliptin 20 mg daily. He was found unconscious at his home 12 h after he was last seen well at work. Emergency medical services assessed him, exhibited impaired consciousness with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 6 and a blood glucose level of 38 mg/dL. He was administered 20 g of intravenous glucose in the ambulance. His medical history included hypertension and dyslipidemia, with no other medications or supplements that predisposed him to hypoglycemia other than insulin. Upon arrival at the emergency department, his GCS score improved to 9, and his plasma glucose level was 130 mg/dL. His vital signs were as follows: body temperature 37.4°C, heart rate 99 beats per minute, blood pressure 119/82 mmHg, respiratory rate 19 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation 95% on room air. His body mass index was 25.6 kg/m2. His pupils measured 4 mm bilaterally, exhibiting brisk reflexes, and there was no evidence of nuchal rigidity or gross motor paralysis. He appeared agitated, frequently shaking his head and making efforts to sit up. Laboratory tests revealed elevated levels of inflammatory markers and creatine phosphokinase, which were presumed to result from prolonged recumbency and physical agitation (Table 1). The brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed hyperintensity on diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) and low apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values in the bilateral deep white matter and the splenium of the corpus callosum, consistent with hypoglycemic encephalopathy (Figure 1).

After hospitalization, hypoglycemia did not recur, and his blood glucose levels were maintained above 200 mg/dL. However, his consciousness did not improve (GCS 8–12), and he remained unable to follow commands. He showed no obvious convulsions or hemiplegia. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis on Day 3 revealed no abnormal findings indicative of meningitis or encephalitis. On Day 4, he developed paroxysmal elevations in blood pressure, heart rate, body temperature, and respiratory rate, along with excessive diaphoresis (Figure 2). He frequently exhibited heat intolerance, orthostatic posturing, and limb flailing. The total daily insulin dose (TDD) required to control his blood glucose levels also increased (Figure 2). These symptoms were presumed to be due to sympathetic hyperactivity, and we administered beta‐adrenergic blockers to suppress the sympathetic overactivation. The electroencephalography (EEG) on Day 8 showed no spike waves, and generalized slow waves were observed. The brain MRI on Day 9 showed partial resolution of DWI hyperintensity. Notably, urinary adrenaline concentration was elevated to 64.4 μg/day on day 15 (Table 2). Based on these findings, he was strongly suspected of having developed PSH caused by hypoglycemic encephalopathy.

Titrated beta‐blockers achieved sympathetic hyperactivity control; thus, we deemed gabapentin unnecessary. Urinary adrenaline concentration on Day 22 also decreased to 16.7 μg/day (Table 2). His consciousness also gradually improved, allowing him to answer easy questions on Day 27. Symptoms of sympathetic overactivity disappeared over time, leading to the discontinuation of the alpha‐adrenergic blocker on Day 44. The urinary adrenaline concentration on Day 49 decreased to 4.9 μg/day (Table 2). The EEG on Day 49 showed a predominance of alpha waves, indicating improved cerebral function.

The TDD, peaking at approximately 100 units/day, decreased alongside sympathetic improvement, eventually reaching 14 units/day on sitagliptin/metformin.

On Day 51, he was transferred for rehabilitation with persistent mild disorientation and retrograde amnesia.

DISCUSSION

Hypoglycemic encephalopathy predisposes to autonomic dysregulation. Brain injury‐induced sympathetic hyperactivity manifests as PSH—characterized by paroxysmal tachycardia, hypertension, tachypnea, hyperthermia, and diaphoresis 3 , 4 . PSH lacks definitive laboratory criteria; however, episodes often correlate with elevated catecholamines.

Approximately, 80% of PSH cases are associated with traumatic brain injury 5 , 6 . PSH secondary to hypoglycemic encephalopathy is exceptionally rare; this represents only the second global case, following a report from India 7 .

The PSH Assessment Measure (PSH‐AM), designed for early detection, comprises two components: the clinical feature scale (CFS) for assessing six core features and the diagnosis likelihood tool (DLT) for estimating likelihood based on observed features 8 . An MRI study indicates that disrupted descending inhibitory pathways from the periaqueductal gray to interneuronal networks may drive sympathetic and motor hyperactivity 9 . In this case, based on a PSH‐AM score of 29 (CFS 18, DLT 11), the patient was diagnosed with probable PSH.

Clinical features consistent with PSH emerged on hospital Day 4, coinciding with elevated catecholamines and MRI evidence of disrupted descending inhibitory pathways originating in the gray matter. Given the subsequent normalization of catecholamine levels and improvement in consciousness, these paroxysms were estimated to have persisted for 30 days from onset.

PSH may be explained by the ‘excitatory‐inhibitory ratio (EIR) model’, which postulates that central nervous system inhibitory dysfunction—specifically, disrupted periaqueductal gray inhibitory projections—triggers maladaptive excitatory interneuron activation by innocuous stimuli, resulting in excessive sympathetic and motor activity 10 .

Differential diagnosis is crucial due to overlapping clinical features. In this case, alternative conditions—including sepsis, encephalitis, nonconvulsive status epilepticus, malignant hyperthermia, hypercapnia, and thyroid or adrenal crises—were effectively ruled out.

Notably, our patient recovered with beta‐adrenergic blocker monotherapy alone. While gabapentin is reported to be effective for PSH, presumably by restoring inhibitory control in the brain and spinal cord 6 . His symptoms were successfully managed without such interventions. This contrasts with typical brain injury‐induced PSH, which often requires multidrug regimens including gabapentin, bromocriptine, and benzodiazepines. The therapeutic response may highlight a significant clinical distinction between PSH caused by brain injury versus hypoglycemic encephalopathy.

This case highlights that sympathetic hyperactivity during hypoglycemia may reflect a pathological response to hypoglycemia‐related cerebral injury rather than a benign autonomic reflex. Prompt recognition and intervention are crucial for favorable outcomes, underscoring the importance of vigilant clinical assessment.
