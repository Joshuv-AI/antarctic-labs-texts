Burnout among paediatric surgeons: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sebastian Kirdar-Smith, Ricardo Twumasi, Charlotte Capon, Callum Pearse, Vasilisa Smychkovich, Alec Knight
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout is increasingly identified as a factor affecting physician welfare, and subsequently patient care. Surgeons are known to experience higher rates of burnout compared with the general population. No review has focused exclusively on burnout prevalence among paediatric surgeons. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to determine the prevalence of burnout among paediatric surgeons, alongside identifying and analysing potential influences.MethodsUsing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses and Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines, following our published PROSPERO protocol (CRD42025640570), 719 records were identified from inception through 13 January 2025. Included studies produced primary data reporting paediatric surgeon burnout prevalence, using validated burnout metrics. Main exclusion criteria were: study or population irrelevance, study type and duplicates. All available data were extracted; authors were contacted for original data. Random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum likelihood estimation estimation was conducted to estimate a pooled burnout prevalence, further subgroup analyses and meta-regressions were performed.ResultsMeta-analysis of 15 studies (2757 paediatric surgeons) revealed a pooled burnout prevalence of 29.4% (95% CI 20.3% to 40.5%), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=96.4%). Burnout rates ranged from 7.8% to 73.7%. Studies using the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) reported significantly lower burnout (22.4%) compared with non-MBI tools (44.9%; p=0.025). No publication bias was detected. Study quality and measurement tool choice explained 35.3% and 24·5% of heterogeneity, respectively.ConclusionsThis study establishes that nearly one in three paediatric surgeons experience burnout, with a pooled prevalence of 29.4% (95% CI 20.3% to 40.5%). This rate, while concerning, is notably lower than other surgical disciplines. Strong personal accomplishment, resulting from emotionally rewarding work with children, appears protective, alongside structurally embedded practices such as family-centred care and multidisciplinary collaboration. Personality traits associated with increased agreeableness, conscientiousness and emotional stability may further reduce susceptibility to burnout. Specialty-specific interventions, supported by structured mentorship, are critical to sustainable well-being throughout paediatric surgical practice.Keywords: Children, Health Policy, Psychology, Qualitative research

Introduction

Burnout is an occupational syndrome resulting from unmanaged workplace stress, presenting as feelings of exhaustion, cynicism towards one’s job and a reduction in professional efficacy.1 The most common tool used for assessing burnout is the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), typically a 22-item instrument assessing the burnout.2 Burnout is most commonly measured via the assessment of three dimensions; emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalisation (DP) and reduced sense of personal accomplishment (PA).3 However, the consequences of burnout extend far beyond a simple score on a test or arbitrary cut-off point.

Factors commonly associated with the aetiology of burnout are overwork, a lack of autonomy, insufficient rewards such as a lack of institutional recognition for work, breakdown in community resulting in interpersonal discord and values conflicts, manifesting as forms of dissension between personal and professional duties.4

It is estimated that approximately one in three physicians experiences burnout at any given time5, with surgeons commonly found to be the most burnt out demographic compared with their peers.6 Outside of the surgical profession, burnout rates are reported to be as high as 54% in doctors, a stark disparity when compared with the 10–15% statistic seen in the general population.7

Studies imply that burnout in surgeons is associated with aspects of emotional lability, including lower patient satisfaction and, most critically, an increased risk of mistakes, such as higher rates of preventable human error. A combination of these factors ultimately leads to decreased patient safety.8 This demonstrates that burnout is not only important to understand on an individual level for surgeons, but also on an institutional level, as it retains the potential to have a direct consequence on patient safety.

Among surgical specialties, paediatric surgeons are generally reported to have lower rates of burnout, presenting with higher rates of career satisfaction and lower depression rates.9 These low rates, nevertheless, do not signify an absence of burnout within the specialty9 Research has found the variables of work hours, admin tasks and issues with work-life balance as factors contributing to the presence of burnout in paediatric specialties.10 However, despite this, there have been no systematic reviews or meta-analyses exclusively focusing on the specialty of paediatric surgery in relation to burnout.

This study, therefore, aims to provide the first systematic review and meta-analysis to determine the pooled prevalence of burnout among paediatric surgeons and to compare paediatric surgery to other surgical fields.11 Furthermore, we seek to explore the institutional, professional and personal factors that may moderate burnout in this population, providing a data-driven foundation for promoting well-being in paediatric surgeons.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

For this systematic review and meta-analysis, three authors (CC, CP and VMS) independently screened and identified studies that reported the prevalence of burnout in paediatric surgeons, including subspecialties, that were published up to and including 13 January 2025. The literature search consisted of three main steps: searching databases, hand-searching journals and cross-referencing. For the database search, Ovid was used to search PubMed (MEDLINE), EMBASE and PsycINFO, with hand-searching journals and cross-referencing yielding additional records. Post automated deduplication, studies were identified for further consideration, with the most common reason for exclusion being an irrelevant title/abstract, irrelevant target intervention, exposure or population. All searches were conducted within the same date range (database inception through 13 January 2025). Further limitations to searches included the exclusion of grey literature and unpublished studies. In the event of any conflicting opinions, a two-stage discrepancy resolution and arbitration system was implemented, as outlined in the protocol.12 This study was conducted in accordance with the published protocol (CRD4202564057012), while following the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (online supplemental material figure 3) protocol, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist (online supplemental materials figure 4). For the database searches, combinations and variations of MeSH terms, title words, text words and keywords relating to burnout and paediatric surgeons were employed. Particular attention was given to the variations in the spelling and phrasing, including the Americanisation, of words. As per our protocol12, included studies must have been published studies, available in the English language, including paediatric surgeons, producing primary data and with a valid burnout score measured via a verified burnout tool.

Data analysis

Three authors (CC, CP and VMS) extracted data from the included studies in accordance with our protocol.12 Extracted data included paediatric specialty, total number of study participants, total number of paediatric surgeons, burnout tool used, burnout prevalence (including subscales where reported), relevant statistics and demographic data. Where CIs or SD were missing, authors were subsequently contacted, of whom 5 of 15 authors responded. When authors responded and provided full data sets, any relevant data was extracted, including SD. Where authors did not respond to the initial request, a follow-up request was sent.

Two authors (CC and VMS) independently evaluated the risk of bias (RoB) of the included studies using a Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) adjusted for cross-sectional studies, assessing studies according to three domains: selection, comparability and outcome. The NOS tool allowed us to assess the methodological quality and calculate RoB scores out of nine, enabling sensitivity analyses based on study quality. As per our RoB table (online supplemental materials), no included studies were at high RoB, while the vast majority (n=14) were at moderate RoB, with one study assessed to be at low RoB.

Meta-analyses were completed in R (V.4.4.2) by one author (RT with code checked by SK-S) using the metafor package.13 Effect sizes were calculated using logit-transformed proportions to normalise the data distribution. Logit proportions were back-transformed to raw proportions when reported for clinical interpretation. Inverse variance weighting was applied in all analyses.

A random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation with Knapp-Hartung14 adjustment (which outperforms the DerSimonian-Laird method)15 for test statistics to account for uncertainty in heterogeneity estimation. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistics and Cochran’s Q test16, with I² ≥75% indicating very high heterogeneity.

Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot visualisation, Egger’s regression test17 for asymmetry, Begg’s rank correlation test18 and Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill19 method to estimate potentially missing studies.

Subgroup analyses examined burnout tool type, study quality (NOS categories), sample size (median split) and publication year (recent vs older studies) using mixed-effects models. Sources of heterogeneity were explored through univariate and multivariable meta-regression, including continuous variables for publication year, sample size, NOS scores and female surgeon percentage (available for 12 of 15 studies). Model selection used Akaike information criterion20 comparison. P values were adjusted for the false discovery rate using a family wise Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, as multiple post hoc comparisons were added after peer review.

Sensitivity analyses included leave-one-out analysis and cumulative meta-analysis by publication year. Influence was assessed using Cook’s distance21 and DFFITS (Difference From Fits) statistics.22

Results

The PRISMA flowchart, as shown in figure 1, illustrates the study selection process. A total of 719 records were identified through the literature search, consisting of: searching databases (n=631), hand-searching journals (n=34) and cross-referencing (n=54), with 581 remaining after deduplication. Following the application of eligibility criteria created for this review, 544 records were excluded, primarily due to the lack of inclusion of paediatric surgeons, irrelevant study type or unresolved duplicates. The remaining records (n=37) were eligible for extra consideration and underwent rigorous screening by three independent reviewers. Ultimately, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria for quantitative synthesis and subsequent meta-analysis.

PRISMA flowchart. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

The 15 included studies collectively sampled a total of 2757 paediatric surgeons, with studies surveying an average of 184 subjects. 10 of the studies exclusively included paediatric surgeons, while the other 5 analysed paediatric surgeons alongside other specialties. 14 of the papers were published in the last decade, with 9 published within the last 5 years, highlighting a growing academic interest in burnout among paediatric surgeons. 11 studies used the MBI or modified versions of it. The remaining four studies employed other validated tools including the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory and the Compassion Satisfaction Fatigue Test, or Likert scale-based surveys. 10 studies focused solely on a subspecialty within paediatric surgery such as spine, urology, orthopaedics and general surgery, while 5 studies did not specify a subspecialty. Meta-analysis revealed a pooled burnout prevalence of 29.4% (95% CI 20.3% to 40.5%) as shown in the forest plot (figure 2) among paediatric surgeons. Individual study burnout rates demonstrated wide variation, ranging from 7.8% (95% CI 4.0% to 14.0%) in Bratu et al,23 2019 to 73.7% (95% CI 70.0% to 77.0%) in Liang et al,24 2021. The meta-analysis demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I²=96.4%, τ²=0.85, Q=454.05, p<0.001), indicating significant between-study variability beyond sampling error. The 95% prediction interval (5.0% to 76.0%) suggested that true burnout prevalence in future similar studies could vary widely. The prediction interval is wider than the 95% CI as this incorporates between-study heterogeneity.

Forest plot of burnout prevalence in paediatric surgeons.

Subgroup analysis by burnout measurement tool revealed significant differences (QM=5.04, p=0.025). Studies employing MBI-based instruments reported lower pooled burnout prevalence (22.4%, 95% CI 15.3% to 31.6%) compared with non-MBI tools (44.9%, 95% CI 25.4% to 66.0%), with measurement tool explaining 24.5% of heterogeneity (R²=24.5%). Study quality emerged as another significant moderator, with moderate-quality studies demonstrating significantly lower burnout rates than the single high-quality study (coefficient=−2.05, p=0.008, R²=35.3%). Multiple post hoc comparisons were carried out and additional subgroup analyses were performed. An analysis of included studies by country economic status was performed (online supplemental materials figure 1), which showed the burnout rates of paediatric surgeons in high-income countries to be 25.7% (95% CI 15.9% to 38.7%). A subgroup analysis was also run on included studies by COVID status (online supplemental materials figure 2), which found no statistically significant difference between studies run before, during or after the COVID pandemic.

Meta-regression analyses revealed no significant associations between burnout prevalence and publication year (p=0.831), sample size (p=0.071) or overall NOS scores (p=0.353). Among 12 studies reporting gender demographics, female representation ranged from 6.7% to 71.2%, with no significant association between gender composition and burnout rates (p=0.328). Assessment of publication bias revealed no significant asymmetry. Egger’s regression test (t=-1.88, p=0.082) and Begg’s rank correlation test (τ=0.124, p=0.559) both suggested absence of small-study effects. Trim-and-fill analysis (figure 4) identified two potentially missing studies on the right side of the funnel plot, with adjusted prevalence of 33.4% (95% CI 23.3% to 45.4%).

Publication bias analyses.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated overall stability of results. The most influential study (Liang et al, 2021) when removed, reduced the pooled estimate from 29.4% to 26.5%, with all other exclusions producing changes <3%. Cumulative meta-analysis by publication year showed relatively stable estimates after 2019, suggesting temporal consistency in findings.

Discussion

This meta-analysis reveals that 29.4% (95% CI 20.3% to 40.5%) of paediatric surgeons experience burnout, though rates vary substantially across studies and measurement tools. The significant influence of burnout assessment instruments on reported prevalence rates highlights a critical methodological challenge in burnout research, with non-MBI tools yielding prevalence estimates nearly double those of MBI-based studies. The extensive unexplained heterogeneity (>60% after accounting for moderators) suggests that unmeasured factors such as healthcare system characteristics, work environment, training level and cultural contexts may substantially influence burnout risk, as well as other social factors, such as economic status of countries—a factor which other papers examining burnout have also found.25 These findings underscore the urgent need for standardised burnout assessment protocols and comprehensive investigation of system-level factors to enable meaningful cross-study comparisons and inform targeted interventions for this surgical population. While 29.4% is an unacceptably high prevalence of burnout, this is much lower than other surgical specialties (figure 5), such as the significant 60.0% burnout rate seen in trauma surgery.26

Paediatric surgical departments consistently demonstrate a stronger institutional emphasis on family-centred care (FCC) and multidisciplinary teamwork than other surgical specialties, which may be protective against burnout. FCC is widely regarded as a core principle in paediatric healthcare27, actively involving parents in decision-making from diagnosis through postoperative care, particularly in congenital cases requiring antenatal counselling.28 This model has been associated with improved patient and family satisfaction, reduced medical errors, fewer malpractice claims and increased professional fulfilment29 —all of which may contribute to enhanced PA, a key measure inversely related to burnout according to the MBI.30 Multidisciplinary collaboration is integral and emphasised as the standard approach31 with neonatologists, radiologists, anaesthesiologists and other specialists commonly working alongside paediatric surgeons.32 As in other clinical professions, MDT (multidisciplinary team) involvement not only improves clinical outcomes33, but fosters a collaborative and supportive working environment34 that can help reduce EE and DP. These are two additional core components of burnout, as defined by the MBI.30 The consistent integration of these structural features in paediatric surgery may ultimately contribute to the lower rates of burnout observed in this specialty.

Working with children routinely emerged as a protective factor against burnout among paediatric surgeons. Surgeons consistently report strong senses of PA, with one study stating that 77% of paediatric surgeons were satisfied with their career.35 Patient satisfaction and the reward associated with helping others (85%) emerged as the principal motivating factor among paediatric surgeons.35 Paediatric surgeons frequently demonstrate the lowest rates of burnout among surgical specialties.11 The combination of higher job satisfaction alongside greater workload and work-home conflict11 could point to a strong intrinsic motivation among paediatric surgeons to pursue and remain in their field, potentially reflecting the fulfilment gained from caring for children. Treating paediatric patients often elicits strong emotional responses and gratitude from families, who value reassurance, inclusion and compassionate communication from surgeons.36 37 Positive feedback regarding patient recovery and clinical care experience, reported in 82% of responses in one paper37, provides emotional reinforcement, enhancing PA.

Personality profiles of paediatric surgeons suggest a selection bias favouring traits protective against burnout, independent of job-related factors. Studies have found that paediatric surgery applicants scored higher than both general surgery residents and community norms in agreeableness, conscientiousness and emotional stability on a statistically significant level.38 Higher agreeableness and conscientiousness correlate with increased PA,39 a variable reflecting low burnout.30 Studies identify neuroticism, the inverse of emotional stability, as positively correlated with EE and DP.39 Integrating these findings indicates that individuals who exhibit higher emotional stability, and therefore lower levels of neuroticism, demonstrate greater protection against these core components of burnout.39 Collectively, these data strongly support the proposition that paediatric surgeons possess an intrinsic psychological profile that reduces their susceptibility to burnout.

Characteristics of the paediatric surgical patient population may contribute to lower burnout by facilitating more rewarding clinical outcomes. Paediatric patients typically present with fewer comorbidities than adult patients40 41, reducing perioperative risk and improving prognoses.42 43 In contrast, adult surgical outcomes are often compromised by comorbid chronic conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease and chronic lung pathology, which correlate with higher postoperative morbidity and mortality.42 43 Survival rates following comparable surgeries are consistently higher in paediatric populations44, even for complex procedures, such as cardiac surgery, where children demonstrate better postoperative outcomes and fewer neurological complications.45 Such outcomes increase professional satisfaction,35 which may reinforce PA, a core dimension of the MBI reflecting low burnout.30

Children are also less likely to engage in harmful lifestyle behaviours such as smoking46 and alcohol consumption.47 As a result, they are less predisposed46 47 to chronic conditions more common in adult cohorts, such as alcohol-related liver disease or tobacco-related malignancies, which may require interventions such as transplant surgery.48 Although paediatric surgery can include complex cases, particularly those involving congenital anomalies, such cases occur relatively infrequently49 and are typically elective procedures.50 51 This allows for more comprehensive preoperative planning and reduces exposure to the acute stressors commonly associated with emergency surgical care.52 In contrast, adult patients are more frequently involved in high-mortality trauma53 and emergency surgeries54 55, especially overnight53, which are strongly associated with increased burnout due to sleep disruption, fatigue and increased risk of errors.56 Paediatric emergency procedures are generally less complex, such as abscess drainage57 or appendectomy58, and uncomplicated (non-perforated) appendectomies can often be safely delayed until daylight hours, allowing for better decision making and working conditions.59 This reduction in overnight surgical burden and associated fatigue contributes to more positive clinical outcomes56, further mitigating key contributors to burnout.

Collectively, these factors highlight how the clinical profile of paediatric patients of: a healthier baseline status, more favourable outcomes and less frequent exposure to complex emergency or high-risk overnight surgeries supports the association between paediatric surgery, enhanced PA and lower burnout rates.

This study demonstrates several strengths, among which is the generalisability and robustness of findings. The inclusion of a variety of paediatric surgical subspecialties allowed the investigation of confounding variables in relation to burnout in paediatric surgery, enhancing reliability and internal validity. The included studies themselves show a high degree of relevance due to a wide variety of included countries, along with a range of subspecialties, further increasing the external validity of results in addition to their generalisability. Another strength is the recency of included studies, with all but one study being published in the last decade. This ensures our analysis reflects the current landscape within paediatric surgery, demonstrating pertinence and showing alignment with topical issues within the specialty.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The use of the MBI can be perceived as both a strength and limitation. While the MBI is the most common burnout metric, there is no evidence suggesting it to be the most accurate.2 This implies that while its widespread use is helpful for interstudy comparability, the validity of such a self-reported instrument has the potential to be influenced through cognitive biases, predominantly recency and survivorship bias. There is the potential for some selection bias in the literature search, as we were only able to include studies that were available in the English language. In included studies, response rate and cohort sample size were two consistently low scoring criteria, with no included study reporting a response rate above 95% and only one including a cohort size larger than 400 participants. Additionally, our included paediatric surgery subspecialties are limited to spine, urology, orthopaedic and general surgery, bringing up constraints in generalisability of study results across other paediatric subspecialties. Included studies showed a heavy male predominance, with 1844 male subjects, compared with 522 female participants. This questions the external validity of results, as it is unclear whether the majority male response will be representative of the overall paediatric surgeon population.

This study highlights the increasingly prevalent issue of burnout among paediatric surgeons, which threatens patient safety60, staff retention61 and workforce sustainability. Organisations prioritising employee well-being can improve quality of care and the patient experience, while enhancing retention and productivity.62 This can manifest as the provision of psychological support such as Schwartz rounds, but also ensuring adequate rota coverage and regular monitoring of burnout prevalence. Interventions, such as a positive psychology coaching programme, have been shown to improve trainee paediatric surgeons’ levels of burnout, among other positive improvements. Further interventions building on this concept are vital to mitigating burnout within paediatric surgery. Professional bodies must take a leading role in addressing burnout by endorsing routine well-being assessments and developing mentorship programmes. Such bodies should support training initiatives to build emotional resilience, such as webinar series, mindfulness training and structured peer support groups, to identify at-risk individuals and guide interventions. Enhanced stress resilience training can improve executive functioning, burnout and psychological distress, with the potential for tailoring to optimise these effects63 and has been shown to significantly reduce high stress rates and increase mindfulness, specifically in surgical trainees, with a potential reduction of burnout.64 It would also be appropriate for psychological resilience training to be built into existing paediatric surgery training programmes, which could be achieved through workshops delivered at annual review points with burnout monitoring, as well as peer-facilitated reflective practices.

Further research into burnout within paediatric surgery, and within a broader context, is critical. However, the nature of this research should concentrate on interventions, including those previously described, with practical implementation recommendations. These interventional studies should target both individual (eg, resilience training) and systemic (eg, psychologically safe environments, team function) factors that are needed to inform evidence-based strategies for burnout prevention and mitigation within paediatric surgical practice. Interventions must be evidence-based, focus on the individual surgeon, reduce mental health stigma and ensure that wider organisations take responsibility for the well-being of their staff. Future research should also examine the longitudinal course of burnout across training and specialist practice. The onus is on healthcare organisations to create psychologically safe environments that promote open communication without fear of reprisal. The prevalence of burnout in paediatric surgeons highlights the necessity for specialty-specific interventions, which must be tailored to the needs of groups with common occupational risk factors for maximal impact.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis establishes that nearly one in three paediatric surgeons experiences burnout, with a pooled prevalence of 29.4% (95% CI 20.3% to 40.5%). This rate, while concerning, is notably lower than other surgical disciplines. Our analysis elucidates critical protective factors uniquely inherent to paediatric surgery, including the foundational principles of FCC, strong multidisciplinary work environments and working with paediatric patients. Our findings mandate integrated, evidence-based, bottom-up interventions in order to protect paediatric surgeons and build and maintain a sustainable workforce that is able to most help their patients without putting themselves and their well-being at risk. Such strategies include embedding psychological resilience into surgical training, longitudinal burnout monitoring and other specialty-specific initiatives. Professional bodies bear the responsibility for these reforms through clear policies, mentorship programmes and rigorous investment in research. In this way, we can and must secure a future for paediatric surgeons, and more widely surgical training, where well-being and clinical excellence are mutually reinforcing pillars.
