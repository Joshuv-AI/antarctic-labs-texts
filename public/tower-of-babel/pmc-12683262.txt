Barriers to communication about complementary and alternative medicine between oncology patients and healthcare professionals in Croatia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Aleksandar Racz, Vanja Vasiljev, Tomislav Rukavina, Andrea Armano, Aneta Perak, Martina Trncevic, Ana Marija Hošnjak, Ljerka Armano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeComplementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is increasingly integrated into oncology care, yet significant communication gaps persist between patients and healthcare professionals concerning its use. This study aimed to identify the barriers that hinder the exchange of information between oncology patients and healthcare professionals.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2022 to May 2023. The sample consisted of 832 respondents, comprising 411 oncology patients and 421 healthcare professionals, including 100 physicians and 321 nurses. Data was collected using a survey questionnaire based on modified CAM Health Belief Questionnaire and Integrative Medicine Attitude Questionnaire instruments. Descriptive and inferential statistics methods were applied, including one-way analysis of variance and Tukey’s test to identify differences among groups.ResultsOnly 23.5% of respondents reported discussing or planning to discuss CAM with a healthcare professional. Primary communication barriers included a lack of initiative from healthcare professionals (70.2%), perceived insufficient knowledge about CAM (37%), and patients’ fear of adverse effects from medical staff (15.1%). Statistically significant differences in attitudes were identified between patients and healthcare professionals regarding information sources, trust in CAM, and willingness to discuss the topic (p<0.05).ConclusionThe findings underscore the need to improve healthcare professionals’ education on alternative medicine and promote open communication with patients. Integrating CAM into medical education and development guidelines for its incorporation into clinical practice could enhance patient awareness and safety.

Introduction

Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is experiencing a significant increase in popularity among patients seeking new and alternative approaches to managing their health conditions [1,2]. In this study, we defined CAM in line with the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) as a group of diverse medical and health care practices and products that are not generally considered part of conventional medicine. This includes, but is not limited to, herbal medicine, homeopathy, acupuncture, bioenergy therapies, massage, and spiritual healing. This definition was provided to all respondents at the beginning of the questionnaire. In the past, patients were utterly dependent on their physicians and their knowledge, which strengthened mutual trust and reinforced the physician's position of authority. With the development of the Internet, the concept of an active and educated patient is gaining prevalence, enabling individuals to access medical information more efficiently [3]. However, differences in health literacy and challenges in finding reliable information underscore the importance of effective communication between patients and healthcare professionals [4]. At the same time, the availability of medical information on the Internet can change the dynamics of communication between patients and physicians. Clear and effective communication between patients and healthcare professionals is essential for understanding and correctly applying medical information [5]. The reasons for the lack of open, two-way, and confidential communication between health professionals and patients are numerous and utterly unexplored in the available literature in Croatia. Through more precise guidelines, open dialogue, and education, patients can and must be provided with tools for critical evaluation and proper use of available health resources.

Communication between patients and physicians is crucial in making treatment decisions, especially when patients refuse recommended therapy and consider using CAM. This is confirmed by one of the few studies on barriers to communication between healthcare professionals and cancer patients who use or are considering using CAM, a study conducted in Sweden, which reveals the complexity of mutual understanding and challenges in communication. While physicians primarily rely on medical reasons, cancer patients often make their decisions based on personal values, which can lead to misunderstandings and tensions in their interactions [6]. A similar study was conducted among 84 cancer patients in Turkey. The results showed that the main barriers were a lack of sufficient information from healthcare professionals, difficulties in understanding medical terminology, and insufficient support from physicians in interpreting the data found [7]. It is essential to realize that across Europe, the education about the CAM remain highly heterogeneous. Countries such as Germany, Austria, and Switzerland have integrated CAM into medical curricula, with formal training in acupuncture, phytotherapy, and homeopathy available for physicians and pharmacists, and certain therapies even reimbursed by public health insurance under specific indications. Portugal has gone further by regulating seven CAM disciplines through national legislation, requiring 4-year undergraduate programs and coordinated licensure with the Ministries of Health and Education. In contrast, Central and Eastern European countries show more limited integration: Hungary and Slovakia have formal regulations and specialist training programs, particularly in acupuncture, Slovenia and Serbia recognize CAM legally, yet education is mainly confined to short courses and services are predominantly self-financed. In Croatia, official partial education is limited to acupuncture, herbal medicines, and dietary supplements, with education offered through professional associations rather than universities. Italy regulates CAM at the regional level, creating significant disparities in access and training, while Bosnia and Herzegovina and Montenegro remain at an early stage, with fragmented private training and no systematic coverage. Overall, while some European countries—particularly in Central and Western Europe—are moving toward structured integration of CAM into health and education systems, others, especially in the Balkans, continue to rely on ad hoc training, underscoring the uneven development of CAM across the continent [8].

1. Aim and purpose of the research

Since the use of CAM therapies and preparations in Croatia is a taboo topic with almost no published research, even though in Croatia, the use of CAM has steadily increased since the late 1990s, partly driven by patients’ dissatisfaction with conventional medicine, the growing burden of chronic diseases, and the increasing availability of online information. However, the CAM landscape in Croatia remains underregulated and fragmented. Only a few CAM practices, such as acupuncture, homeopathy, and herbal therapy, are legally recognized under national law, and there is no comprehensive institutional integration of CAM into the healthcare system. While some aspects of CAM are available in private practice, public healthcare institutions rarely offer or officially endorse them. General awareness of CAM is moderate, with noticeable differences between urban and rural populations. CAM use is often self-directed and seldom discussed with physicians, which reflects broader communication barriers in the cancer care environment. Despite the growing interest in integrative oncology worldwide, Croatia still lacks national guidelines for CAM in cancer treatment, and communication between patients and healthcare professionals regarding CAM remains sporadic and unstructured.

CAM practices in Croatia are partially regulated, particularly regarding herbal and homeopathic medicinal products, which are subject to European Union (EU) directives on market authorization. Acupuncture and biofeedback are covered explicitly by compulsory health insurance, but only when provided by licensed physicians. Other CAM modalities—such as naturopathy, homeopathy (when practiced outside pharmacies), and manual therapies—lack formal institutional integration, are not reimbursed, and are typically available only in private practice settings. Currently, there is no national legislation governing CAM providers or standardized guidelines for the use of CAM in oncology care. Consequently, although CAM is accessible to patients, its application remains unstructured, mainly, and communication about CAM options between patients and healthcare professionals is unregulated and heterogeneous.

Additionally, in Croatia, formal CAM education remains minimal. Most medical schools do not include CAM content in their undergraduate curricula, although occasional elective modules or seminars are offered at the postgraduate level. As a result, physicians and other healthcare professionals typically acquire CAM knowledge through online resources, self-study, or participation in continuing medical education (CME) programs, often provided by professional associations or international platforms. For example, CME courses in complementary medicine are available via national medical societies and through international providers, including the US NCCIH.

This study aimed to identify and analyze the obstacles and reasons that contribute to the avoidance of communication between healthcare professionals and oncology patients about CAM, including professional, educational, and perceptual factors.

2. Questionnaire

For this research, two questionnaires were used—one for healthcare professionals and one for oncology patients—based on the CAM Health Belief Model, with minor modifications. The instruments included the CAM Health Belief Questionnaire (CHBQ) [9] and the Integrative Medicine Attitude Questionnaire (IMAQ) [10].

Methods

The study was conducted at a single tertiary-level hospital in Croatia, which has approximately 850 inpatient beds and serves both urban and rural oncology patients. This setting provides a representative sample of typical oncology care within the national healthcare context, although the results may not be generalizable to other institutions.

This cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2022 and May 2023. A proportionally stratified random sample included 411 oncology patients and 421 healthcare professionals (100 physicians and 321 nurses), covering both those directly and indirectly involved in oncology care.

The modified CHBQ used in our study consisted of 10 items, while the modified IMAQ included 29 items. Both instruments were assessed using a 7-point Likert scale. On average, outpatient visits for cancer patients receiving anticancer treatment in the hospital studied last approximately 12 to 15 minutes, including medical assessment, discussion of symptoms, and treatment planning. This limited timeframe may contribute to communication barriers regarding topics perceived as secondary, such as CAM. As mentioned, data collection involved face-to-face surveys for patients and self-administered questionnaires for healthcare workers, distributed in unmarked envelopes and returned anonymously.

To minimize individual variation in the understanding of what constitutes CAM, the questionnaire began with a standardized definition of CAM based on the NCCIH classification. This included natural products (e.g., herbs, vitamins), mind–body practices (e.g., acupuncture, meditation), and whole-system approaches (e.g., traditional Chinese medicine, homeopathy). All respondents read this definition before completing the survey.

Attitudinal statements were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). The same items were used to produce frequency distributions (Table 1), mean scores with standard deviations (Table 2), and group comparisons via analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s post hoc tests (Table 3).

The research results were structured according to the predefined objectives and presented in both textual and tabular formats, offering insights into respondents’ attitudes, preferences, and behavioral patterns related to CAM. Descriptive analysis included tabulated data with absolute frequencies, percentages, and measures of central tendency (arithmetic mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum). To compare mean values across three or more groups with normally distributed data, one-way ANOVA was applied. For more detailed comparisons, a post hoc Tukey test was conducted to identify specific intergroup differences. Data processing and statistical analysis were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA).

This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of UHC Sestre Milosrdnice (Class: 003-06/21-02/001, Reg. No.: 251-29-11/1-2l-01-9) and conducted by ethical standards and guidelines for human research. Ethical principles of autonomy, justice, beneficence, and non-maleficence were upheld, by the Nuremberg Code, the Declaration of Helsinki, and relevant Croatian legislation, including the Health Care Act and the Patients’ Rights Act. Data protection complied with the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR; Regulation 2016/679). Before participating, all respondents received written information about the study and signed informed consent forms under the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical guidelines

Results

1. Sample description

A total of 832 participants were included, comprising 29.4% males and 70.6% females. Among oncology patients, 42.6% were men, while healthcare workers included physicians (32% men) and nurses (11.8% men). The most represented age groups were those between 41 and 60 years. Most oncology patients were over 60 years old, while healthcare workers were predominantly aged 31–50 years. A majority (70.3%) were married or in a relationship. Regarding education, 40.6% had completed secondary education, and 58.3% held higher education degrees; all physicians and 73.2% of nurses had higher education. Most respondents (72.1%) lived in urban areas, and a high proportion of respondents (78%–85%) identified with a religious affiliation across all groups. Most physicians earned over €1,000 monthly, while half of the oncology patients had incomes below that level. The majority of healthcare workers had 16–25 years of experience, and 30.7% worked directly in oncology.

2. Communication between healthcare workers and patients about CAM

Communication about CAM is analyzed through several aspects, including information exchange, communication barriers, and perceptions of healthcare professionals and patients regarding the importance of dialogue about CAM. Tables 1, 2, and 3 present data on respondents’ attitudes toward openness to discussing CAM, reasons for avoiding communication, and experiences related to obtaining information about CAM from healthcare professionals or other sources.

Table 1 outlines the reasons why patients avoid discussing CAM with healthcare professionals. Most respondents (76.5%) reported never having discussed or being unwilling to discuss CAM, primarily because no one asked them. A common perception is that healthcare professionals lack sufficient CAM knowledge, a view shared by both patients and professionals. While fear of ridicule and negative attitudes exist, it is not predominant. Concerns that disclosing CAM use could affect medical treatment are minimal, and although some respondents feel discomfort discussing CAM, most do not. Notably, many patients believe it is unnecessary to inform physicians about CAM use, and some reported that healthcare professionals seemed unwilling to engage in such discussions.

Table 2 presents the mean values and standard deviations of beliefs and attitudes among physicians, nurses, and oncology patients regarding reasons for avoiding discussions about CAM. All groups strongly agreed with the statement that no one had asked them about CAM, with oncology patients expressing the highest level of agreement. The belief that healthcare professionals lack sufficient CAM knowledge was widespread. Physicians were more likely than others to believe that healthcare professionals disapprove of CAM. Fear of ridicule was minimal, and concerns about treatment being affected by CAM use were low, particularly among patients. Discomfort discussing CAM was most common among physicians, while nurses and patients were more open. The belief that informing physicians about CAM is unnecessary was more accepted among physicians. Notably, all groups reported a low willingness to engage in CAM discussions, indicating a general communication gap in clinical practice. These findings highlight differing perceptions and hesitancy surrounding CAM dialogue within the healthcare system.

Table 3 presents the Tukey test results, highlighting statistically significant differences among physicians, nurses, and patients regarding reasons for avoiding discussions about CAM.

Patients were significantly more likely than healthcare professionals to report that no one asked them about CAM, with no difference between physicians and nurses. Both patients and nurses were more inclined than physicians to believe it is unnecessary to inform healthcare professionals about CAM use. Patients also differed from nurses in expressing fear that CAM disclosure might lead to treatment delays, while no difference was found between physicians and patients. Physicians were more strongly inclined to believe that healthcare professionals disapprove of CAM, compared to the other groups. In terms of discomfort discussing CAM, patients were least affected, followed by physicians, with nurses in between, suggesting a greater professional distance from the topic among healthcare providers. No significant group differences emerged regarding perceptions of insufficient CAM knowledge, preparedness to discuss CAM, or prior attempts to initiate such discussions. Overall, patients appear to perceive greater barriers to CAM communication than healthcare professionals do.

The figure presents the mean agreement levels (on a 5-point Likert scale) for four key reasons why communication about CAM may be avoided, as reported by patients, nurses, and physicians (Fig. 1). The statements include: “No one asked me about CAM,” “Disclosure could delay/deny treatment,” “Health professionals do not approve of CAM,” and “I do not feel comfortable talking about CAM.” Statistically significant differences between groups were identified using Tukey post hoc tests and are annotated with p-values. Furthermore, the results illustrate clear patterns in attitudes across professional roles: patients expressed the highest openness, whereas physicians exhibited the highest levels of discomfort and skepticism regarding CAM.

The results presented have shown that of the 832 respondents, only 23.5% (n=181) reported having discussed or planning to discuss CAM with a healthcare professional, while 76.5% (n=588) indicated they had not. The most frequently cited reason for avoiding CAM communication was that “no one has ever asked me about CAM,” reported by 74.5% of patients (4.08±0.95) compared with 66.4% of healthcare workers (3.57±1.34); this difference was statistically significant (p<0.001). Physicians expressed the most tremendous discomfort in discussing CAM (3.26±0.91), significantly higher than both nurses (2.78±1.14, p<0.001) and patients (2.55±0.95, p<0.001). Perceptions that healthcare professionals disapprove of CAM were more pronounced among physicians (3.45±0.83) than among nurses (2.92±1.12, p<0.001) and patients (3.03±0.89, p<0.001). These findings highlight that patients perceive more barriers to CAM dialogue than healthcare providers do, with statistically significant differences confirmed in Tukey post hoc tests.

Beyond the numerical findings, the results point toward systemic implications for medical and nursing education. The high proportion of respondents who perceived healthcare professionals as lacking sufficient CAM knowledge suggests an educational gap that directly influences communication patterns. This perception was consistent across all professional groups, with no statistically significant differences, indicating that the lack of formal training in CAM is a widespread issue rather than confined to a single professional subgroup. Furthermore, as mentioned above, physicians reported significantly greater discomfort in discussing what may reflect the limited presence of CAM-related communication skills in medical curricula, where structured guidance on patient-centered dialogue regarding non-conventional therapies is rarely included. Nurses, while somewhat more open, also expressed uncertainty, consistent with their limited opportunities for formal CAM education.

Taken together, these results reveal that both knowledge-related and communication-related barriers to CAM discussion are closely tied to educational and training processes. The consistency of these barriers across groups highlights a need for structured educational interventions, such as curriculum integration, targeted CME, and interprofessional training programs, to equip healthcare providers with the competence and confidence to address CAM in oncology settings.

Discussion

In Croatia, CAM began to gain visibility in the late 1990s, primarily through the influence of Western integrative medicine models and increasing patient dissatisfaction with conventional care. Over the past 2 decades, public interest has steadily grown, driven by the media, online platforms, and individual experiences. However, awareness and acceptance remain uneven, being more prevalent in urban areas and among younger, more educated populations, while skepticism persists among older generations and healthcare professionals. Despite this interest, the formal integration of CAM into mainstream medical practice remains limited.

Effective communication between patients and healthcare professionals regarding the use of CAM is crucial for aligning therapeutic approaches, fostering trust, and ensuring comprehensive care. However, this communication is often insufficient and hampered by prejudice, lack of knowledge, and fears on both sides. Understanding the reasons behind this pattern of non-communication may contribute to improving the relationship between patients and healthcare professionals, thereby enabling the better integration of CAM into formal healthcare practices.

Research consistently shows that patients often do not report their use of CAM to healthcare professionals [11,12]. The reasons why patients do not report their use of CAM to their physicians most often include a lack of interest and proactive inquiries from healthcare professionals, a belief that physicians are not open to CAM, a perception that healthcare professionals do not have sufficient knowledge about these methods, and a perception that their use of CAM is not clinically relevant [13,14]. Additionally, some patients believe that CAM does not interact with conventional treatment, which is why they do not see the need to inform their physicians about it. However, research shows that patients who discussed CAM with their physicians perceived an improved quality of the relationship, and healthcare professionals expressed greater satisfaction with the communication [11]. These results align with our study's findings, in which 37% of respondents cited a lack of knowledge about CAM from their physicians as one of the reasons for not reporting CAM. Additionally, 48.7% of respondents were unable to assess the attitude of healthcare professionals towards CAM, while 22.4% believed they were generally not supportive of these methods. Additionally, 15.1% of patients reported that the fear of ridicule was a barrier to open communication with physicians about CAM. These data indicate that the perception of physicians’ attitudes, combined with their knowledge and approach to CAM, significantly influences patients’ decisions to report the use of these methods, thereby confirming the importance of educating health professionals and adopting a proactive approach in discussions about CAM. Similarly, in the study by Chang et al. [15], only 30.1% of CAM users voluntarily informed their physicians about the use of these methods. Among those who did not initiate the conversation themselves, only 5.9% were directly asked by their physicians. The main reasons for non-reporting, as cited by patients, were a lack of medical interest (34.6%), a belief that physicians would not understand CAM (3.7%), and fear of possible disapproval (5.9%) [15].

Sanford, using comprehensive national research data, states that the use of CAM, at 29.3%, was not discovered by physicians, with the main reasons being that physicians are perceived as not interested (57.4%) or that they do not need to be aware of such therapy (47.4%). Additionally, 8.5% of patients believe that physicians lack sufficient knowledge about CAM, while a minor part expressed concern due to an adverse reaction or a lack of time during the consultation [16]. These observations align with our results, which also indicate that patients with low communication skills use CAM less frequently with physicians.

The limited communication about CAM in clinical practice is confirmed by the research by Juraskova et al. [17], which showed that CAM was rarely mentioned during examinations in public hospitals and that patients were most often the ones who initiated such discussions. Similar patterns were observed in the research by Roter et al. [18], where CAM was the topic of debate in only 11% of the examinations. In most cases, it was the patients who initiated the conversation. Stub et al. [12] in 2016 distinguish two basic communication approaches of physicians towards CAM: traditional and supportive. The first is characterized by a distanced, technical speech and often a negative attitude towards CAM, which can create a feeling of misunderstanding in patients and even lead to rejection of conventional treatment. On the other hand, physicians who use a supportive approach, although they do not recommend CAM, allow for a more open discussion, which gives patients a sense of appreciation and can increase their cooperation in implementing standard therapy. The authors further emphasize that a lack of communication about CAM may compromise patient safety, particularly for patients who combine conventional and complementary therapies [12]. A study conducted in Switzerland on a sample of 132 oncology patients further confirms the low level of communication between patients and medical staff regarding the use of CAM methods. Most patients (86%) reported that their oncologists had never inquired about their use of CAM. In comparison, 89% stated that their physicians had not mentioned the possibility of incorporating CAM into their treatment. Similar trends were observed in communication with nurses, with as many as 90% of patients stating that medical staff had not inquired about their use of CAM, and 92% of patients saying that they had not been offered CAM as part of their healthcare. However, 58% of patients using CAM spontaneously informed their oncologists about this practice themselves, while 38% did so in communication with nurses. Interestingly, oncologists who were informed about the use of CAM supported patients in continuing their use in 63% of cases, while in 34% of cases, they remained neutral and made no recommendations. Among patients who did not report CAM use to their physicians, the main reason was the belief that this information was not clinically relevant and did not fall within the area of interest of their oncologist [19].

While most studies show results like ours, confirming low levels of communication about CAM between patients and physicians [20,21], some studies record significantly higher levels of reporting of CAM use, with half or more than half of patients openly informing their physicians about their use of these methods [22-24].

Although research on CAM in Croatia is scarce, it primarily provides insights into reporting patterns of CAM use, including aspects of communication between patients and healthcare professionals.

Our results are consistent with those of Marijanović et al. [25], who found that 36.1% of oncology patients discussed CAM with their physicians. Kust et al. [26], in a high-volume cancer center, reported that 72% of patients used CAM during treatment, although only around 36% disclosed it, highlighting similar disclosure gaps despite differences in prevalence. Unlike previous studies, our research included both patients and healthcare professionals, allowing for a cross-stakeholder comparison of attitudes and communication practices.

At the same time, studies by Marijanović et al. [25] and Kust et al. [26] indicate a relatively low level of communication, with only 36% of patients consulting an oncologist about the use of CAM. On the other hand, patients emphasize the high level of interest in the integration of CAM into the therapeutic process and the need for more open communication between patients and physicians, which confirms the results of research in which more than half of patients (59.6%) informed yours physicians about the use of CAM, and a large most respondents (81.7%) expressed a desire to talk about CAM with their family physician [27].

The study yielded approximately similar results, in which 52.3% of respondents informed their physician or medical nurse about using CAM, while those who did not stated reasons, including unavailability and disinterest from medical staff on the topic of CAM. Patients said that physicians “do not have time,” “they do not want to talk about anything except chemotherapy,” or that “they are not informed enough about CAM.” Some participants expressed discomfort or fear of a negative reaction, as noted in comments such as “I’m having a hard time starting this conversation” or “I don’t think they approve of it.”

Although research on CAM in Croatia is not primarily focused on communication between patients and physicians, it still contains significant data on this topic. While some results indicate a lack of information on the use of CAM by physicians, other research shows that a substantial proportion of patients openly discuss CAM methods.

Our results suggest that the communication gap is bidirectional. On the physician side, barriers include time constraints, lack of training on CAM, and discomfort with therapies outside the biomedical framework. On the patient side, reluctance is often driven by fear of ridicule, low confidence in initiating “non‑medical” topics, or the assumption that physicians are uninterested. While 70.2% of patients reported that no one had asked them about CAM, 15.1% also expressed discomfort or fear of adverse reactions. These findings underscore the need for institutional efforts on both sides—empowering patients while also training physicians to initiate non-judgmental conversations. These differences reflect the complexity of communication about CAM, which depends on patients’ attitudes, level of trust in physicians, and their interest in CAM. Given this, it is crucial to identify ways to improve this communication and ensure better understanding between patients and healthcare professionals.

Our results are in concordance with previous studies that have shown that communication about CAM is often absent or strained in oncology, with both physicians and patients hesitant to initiate dialogue. Evidence suggests that the silence around CAM is often mutual: patients may fear judgment or dismissal, while physicians may avoid the topic due to lack of knowledge or time constraints [28-30].

Our study aligns with numerous studies that have emphasized the importance of open dialogue about CAM between physicians and cancer patients, as it can undermine trust and shared decision-making, potentially affecting treatment adherence and satisfaction [31,32].

Beyond its national context, this study contributes to the broader global discourse on integrating CAM into medical curricula as part of core professional competencies. The finding that both patients and healthcare professionals in Croatia perceive insufficient CAM knowledge as a major barrier mirrors international trends, where lack of formal education has been consistently identified as a limiting factor in open communication. By documenting these gaps within an under-researched healthcare system, our results reinforce calls from international organizations and academic bodies to include CAM-related content in medical and nursing education, not only as theoretical knowledge but also as a communication skill essential for patient-centered care. Integrating CAM into curricula could strengthen physicians’ and nurses’ confidence to engage in evidence-informed discussions with patients, thereby enhancing trust, safety, and shared decision-making. In this way, our study aligns with ongoing efforts in Europe, North America, and Asia to establish CAM literacy as a critical global competency for healthcare professionals. Recent scoping reviews demonstrate that CAM teaching remains inconsistently embedded in undergraduate medical curricula, with persistent gaps in clearly defined graduate competencies for CAM knowledge and communication skills [33].

In addition to European and North American contexts, evidence from the southern hemisphere and Asia highlights how communication about CAM is more openly integrated into healthcare practice and education. In Australia, for instance, more than 50% of medical and nursing schools incorporate some CAM content, reflecting both high patient demand and government-supported initiatives to regulate integrative health education. Similarly, in China and Korea, traditional medicine systems are formally embedded in national health policies, with structured curricula that prepare physicians to engage in informed discussions about CAM [34-36].

In India, Ayurveda and other traditional systems are recognized within a parallel medical education track, which fosters greater openness in patient–physician communication about CAM [37].

These examples illustrate how regional policies and cultural expectations shape communication patterns, suggesting that Croatia and other countries with limited CAM integration could benefit from adapting models developed in Asia and the southern hemisphere.

1. Research limitations

The study has several limitations. First, the sample was collected within a single hospital setting, which may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader population of oncology patients and healthcare professionals in Croatia. Furthermore, there is a possibility of socially desirable responses, especially given the sensitivity of the CAM topic, which may influence the expression of actual attitudes and practices. Additionally, data collection occurred during the COVID-19 (coronavirus disease 2019) pandemic, which may have influenced both communication patterns and attitudes toward CAM. Reduced face-to-face interaction in healthcare settings may have further discouraged discussions related to CAM. At the same time, a greater reliance on digital sources likely increased patients’ exposure to CAM information online. These contextual factors should be considered when interpreting the findings.

In contrast, some respondents may have been insufficiently informed about CAM, which could have affected the accuracy of their responses. Methodological limitations include selection bias and the possibility of recall error bias, particularly among oncology patients and healthcare professionals, who may have selectively interpreted their experiences. Finally, the complexity of the questionnaire may have made participation difficult for older respondents and those with lower levels of education. At the same time, professional norms and institutional expectations may have influenced some responses.

Given the identified barriers and differences in perceptions of CAM among patients and healthcare professionals, future research should focus on the impact of effective communication strategies to enhance dialogue about CAM. This research should evaluate various approaches, including educational materials, workshops, and digital platforms, to promote more open communication about CAM between patients and healthcare professionals. Research should be directed towards deepening the understanding of the dynamics between healthcare professionals and patients regarding CAM, reducing the perception gap, and enabling better quality healthcare based on information and collaboration.

Future research should also explore how targeted communication interventions, such as structured discussion protocols or digital decision aids, might enhance the quality of CAM dialogue in oncology care. Furthermore, longitudinal studies are necessary to evaluate the impact of improved communication on CAM on treatment adherence, patient satisfaction, and clinical outcomes. Comparative studies across healthcare and educational systems with varying levels of CAM integration would also provide valuable insights [33-37].

It is especially essential because regional differences in CAM practices and professional education are substantial. In countries such as Germany, Switzerland, and Canada, CAM is partially integrated into medical education and clinical guidelines. Conversely, in much of Southern and Eastern Europe, including Croatia, CAM remains peripheral mainly to medical training, with minimal curricular content and no standardized certification. These disparities suggest that educational strategies should be tailored to regional healthcare cultures while promoting patient-centered communication skills. Recognizing these differences may also foster international collaboration in developing evidence-based integrative care models.

2. Conclusion

This study identified key structural, professional, and perceptual barriers that limit communication about CAM between oncology patients and healthcare professionals. Only 23.5% of respondents reported discussing CAM, with the main obstacles including a lack of professional initiative, limited CAM knowledge, and fear of adverse reactions. Significant differences in perceptions between patients and professionals reject the null hypothesis, underscoring the need for enhanced communication. To address these gaps, healthcare systems should promote CAM-related education, establish clinical guidelines, and integrate CAM into medical training. Open and informed communication is essential for safe and effective use of CAM in oncology care.
