Optimization and physicochemical & antioxidant properties of leek (Allium porrum L.) dried by foam-mat drying
Authors: Yasemin Yikilkan, Hojjat Pashazadeh, Sarhan Mohammed, Ilkay Koca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The foam drying method has evolved as a novel food drying technique. In the present study, the foam-mat drying technique is used to produce powder from leek (Allium porrum L.). The proportions of foaming agents were systematically optimized using the response surface methodology (RSM) and central composite design model. The optimized formulation was identified as 1.50% carboxymethyl cellulose, 14.37% maltodextrin, and 8.93% egg white. The optimal composition was adjusted for achieving high foam stability, high volume, and low density. This improved formula was then freeze-dried and dried using hot air at 50 °C and 70 °C, and its physical and chemical properties, antioxidant abilities, and overall quality were analyzed afterward. Based on the data obtained, the Midilli model was identified as the most suitable drying model. The TPC values for the samples dried at 50 °C, 70 °C, and freeze-dried were 1340.31, 1368.87, and 1349.83 mg/kg, while the TFC values were 208.78, 225.61, and 225.61 mg/kg. The antioxidant activities measured using the ABTS, DPPH, and FRAP tests were 8.58, 16.18, and 13.83 mmol/g for ABTS; 3.43, 4.56, and 1.91 mmol/g for DPPH; and 1.04, 1.32, and 1.27 mmol/g for FRAP, respectively. Considering the types of volatile compounds and the qualities of the powder, the samples dried at 50 °C using the convective method showed the best outcomes.

Introduction

Allium species, a member of the Allieaceae family, are among the oldest cultivated vegetables and include onion, garlic, and leek1,2. It was used for medicinal purposes, as food, or as ornamental plants for more than 4000 years3. Most of these plants constituents have been shown to have rich nutritional and aromatic properties4.

Leek (Allium porrum L.) is a common vegetable species belonging to the Allium genus. Its prevalence has surged as a result of the accumulation of knowledge5. Leeks exhibit a broad geographical distribution from Mediterranean countries, Mediterranean islands, Asia, Europe, North America, and North Africa to Southern England6. Renowned for their nutritional content and biological activities, they are among the most widely consumed vegetables globally. In addition to their nutritional value, they also take their place among herbal medicines7. Their abundance of bioactive compounds, including organic acids, phenolic compounds, carbohydrates, amino acids, and organosulfur compounds (OSB), are linked to their therapeutic qualities. Allium vegetables are rich in organosulfur compounds. They are believed to provide advantageous effects on health. Allium vegetables are highlighted for their use in pharmaceuticals and nutraceuticals. It demonstrates a wide range of therapeutic applications for its antihypertensive, antithrombotic, hypocholesterolemic, antimicrobial, antiviral, antidiabetic, anticarcinogenic, and antimutagenic properties8.

Leeks’ composition and quality have been the subject of a great deal of research up to this point. Owing to its aromatic properties, it has been incorporated into pasta9, bread10, edible11, meat12 and cookies13 as a flavor enhancer or preservative. To guarantee the sustainable use of leeks, various contemporary drying techniques, including infrared14, microwave15, convective methods16, and freeze-drying17, were employed. To the best of our knowledge, there is no published research on foam mat drying leeks. Foam mat drying is a method that differs from conventional techniques employed to dehydrate liquid and semi-liquid food products. The materials are combined with diverse foaming agents and stabilizers, followed by drying post-foam production. It is important to highlight that various methods are available for drying the generated foam. As of now, numerous materials, including banana18, papaya19, guava20, apple juice21, honey22, coconut milk23, and fruit juice24, have been dehydrated using the foam mat drying technique.

Through the use of leeks (Allium porrum L.), the purpose of this research is to develop a foam mat drying method. The experiment comprises two phases. Initially, the ratios of foaming agents and stabilizers were optimized through response surface methodology to ascertain the suitable proportions for drying conditions. In the subsequent phase, the chosen ratios are utilized to generate high-quality powder. Various analyses were conducted to determine total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), antioxidant activity, and FTIR spectra. Volatile compounds using GC-MS, color, dry matter, water activity, powder properties, and drying kinetics were also investigated.

Material and method

Physicochemical analysis of fresh Leek pulp

A collection of leeks (Allium porrum L.) was acquired from Samsun, Turkey. Initially, the leek was cleaned and washed. Subsequently, the leaf portion was separated, and the white portions were crushed using a household blender (Arzum, Turkey) to obtain pulp. Various parameters were measured for the samples, including color, pH, total dry matter, total soluble solids (TSS, °Bx), water activity, total phenolic and flavonoid content, and antioxidant capacity25.

Preparation of leek pulp for foam drying

The pH of the resultant pulp was modified to 4.0 utilizing a citric acid solution. After being held at 60 °C for 5 min, it was quickly cooled. These specific time and temperature conditions help preserve the aroma and contribute to a more homogeneous pulp texture. Central composite design and surface response approach optimized wet foams with 50% leek pulp and varying proportions of egg white, maltodextrin, and carboxymethyl cellulose based on foam qualities. Foaming agents were whipped with a hand mixer for 3 min under optimum foaming conditions (8.93% egg white, 14.37% maltodextrin, and 1.50% CMC), and then leek pulp was added and whipped for another 3 min. The obtained foam pulp mixture was spread on trays at a thickness of 0.5 cm and dried by both freeze-drying and convective drying methods.

Drying of foam pulp mixture

Samples were freeze-dried for 48 h in a Labconco Freezone 12 Plus lyophilizer at −80 °C and 13 Pa.s. For the drying system, a laboratory-scale cabinet dryer was employed (EKSIS, Turkey). The drying process was carried out at 50 °C and 70 °C with an air speed of 0.5 m/s. The dried samples were then ground into powder using a Sinbo SCM 2934 grinder.

Physicochemical analysis of the dried powders

The color characteristics, pH, and dry matter of the dried powders were assessed. As for the color analysis, the L* (lightness/darkness), a* (redness/greenness), and b* (yellowness/blueness) values were measured using a colorimeter.

Determination of total flavonoids

1 mL of the sample was combined with 0.3 mL of 5% w/v NaNO₂. After 5 min, 0.5 milliliters of 2% w/v AlCl₃ were added. The mixture was, then, neutralized with 0.5 mL of a 1 mol/L NaOH solution after 6 min. The mixture was maintained at room temperature for 10 min, after which absorbance was assessed at 510 nm27.

Assessment of total phenolic content (TPC)

The TPC analysis was conducted utilizing the Folin-Ciocalteu method28. In this procedure, 150 µL of the diluted sample was mixed with 750 µL of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, and after a 5-min interval, 600 µL of 7.5% sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) was incorporated. The absorbance of the mixture was measured at 760 nm via a UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Spectronic) after being incubated in the dark for 2 h. The total phenolic content (TPC) was reported as mg gallic acid equivalent (mg GAE/g) per gram.

Determination of volatile components

The volatile compounds were identified using a gas chromatography mass spectrometer (GC-MS). In a 20 mL vial, 1 g of powdered samples was weighed and combined with 1.5 mL of pure water for sample preparation. The syringe was immersed in the vial and kept in a water bath at 55–60 °C for 60 min. After 60 min, it was transferred to the GC-MS apparatus (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). The MS library was used to determine the volatile components.

Determination of individual phenolic compounds

The powder sample extracts were transferred to vials after passing through a 0.45 μm syringe filter. An LC-MS/MS system from Thermo Scientific identified each phenolic compound. The instrument parameters included a binary gradient of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (methanol). The solvent flow rate was maintained at 0.7 mL/min, and the column oven temperature was set to 30 °C. The column used was an ODS HYPERSIL with dimensions 4.6 × 250 mm and a particle size of 5 μm. The injection volume was 20 µL, and the total analysis time was 34 min. The MS/MS instrument was operated with a capillary temperature of 300 °C and a vaporizer temperature of 350 °C. The sheath gas pressure and auxiliary gas pressure were set to 30 and 13 psi, respectively. For positive mode operation, the spray voltage was 4000 V, while in negative mode, it was 2500 V. A discharge current of 4 µA was employed.

Antioxidant activity

The DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging activity was performed using a modified method28. 1 mL of DPPH solution (0.06 mM in 80% methanol) was mixed with 50 µL of the sample, and the mixture was shaken. It was then left to stand in the dark for 1 h until the reaction was completed. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Spectronic). The DPPH radical scavenging activity for each extract was calculated using a calibration curve, with Trolox as the standard. The results were expressed as mmol Trolox equivalent (TE) per gram dry weight (dw).

In FRAP analysis, a 50 µL sample was mixed with 950 mM FRAP solution containing 100 mM acetate buffer, 10 mM FeCl₃, and 10 mM 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine (TPTZ). The mixture was shaken for approximately 5 min, and the optical density was measured at 593 nm against a blank. FRAP values were expressed as mmol Trolox equivalents (TE) per gram dry weight (g⁻¹ DW). The FRAP values of the extracts were calculated by constructing a calibration curve using FeSO₄ as a standard. The results were expressed as mmol FeSO4 equivalents (mmol ISE g-1 dw)29.

In the ABTS assay, a 2.45 mM aqueous solution of potassium persulfate was mixed with a 7 mM ABTS solution to generate ABTS. The ABTS was incubated in darkness for 12 h. Subsequently, 10 µl of samples were combined with 1 mL of working solution in a vessel and vortexed for 1 min. The optical density was measured at 734 nm with a UV-vis spectrophotometer. Furthermore, the blank consisted of phosphate-buffered saline at a concentration of 5 mM. The results were expressed as mg TE/g, based on the Trolox standard curve30.

Characterization of the dried powders

The microstructure of the dried powders was determined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JEOL JSM-7001 F). A small amount of powder samples were placed on a double-sided stainless-steel stub and coated for 2 min at 10 nm with the help of an argon and plasma flow sputter coater. The obtained images were recorded at acceleration voltages of 10 kV and 15 kV31. An FTIR spectrometer was used to analyze the powder samples and get the spectra from 4000 to 800 cm⁻¹ (Perkin Elmer, Spectrum Two, USA, PEService 35).

Response surface methodology experimental design

The foam drying of leek pulp was determined using the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) for the suitable foaming agent, egg white (albumin), foam stabilizer carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), and maltodextrin ratios. The central composite design (CCD), in which foam density (FD), foam volume (FE), and foam stability (FS) were taken as dependent variables, was carried out using Design-Expert 10.0 software (Stat-Ease Inc., USA). 20 experiments were performed at the central point with 6 replications. Three independent variables were evaluated at 5 coded levels, including − 1.68, − 1, 0, + 1, and + 1.68 (Table 1). The maltodextrin and egg white concentration varied from 1.59% at the lowest level (− 1.68) to 18.41% at the highest level (+ 1.68). The central point being 10.0%. CMC was tested at lower levels, ranging from 0.159% (− 1.68) to 1.84% (+ 1.68), with intermediate levels at 0.5%, 1.0%, and 1.5%. Statistical analysis

The Design-Expert software 10.0 (Stat-Ease Inc., USA) was used to design the optimization process and to generate the models and 3D graphics. The ANOVA of response surface methodology was applied for the statistical significance. The coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted coefficient of determination (adj. R2), coefficient of variation (CV), and Fisher’s test value (F-value) were used for the validation of the models. The analyses were conducted in triplicate, and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical significance level was accepted as p < 0.05. The optimum conditions were determined based on the desirability function. Other analyses were carried out in three replicates, the one-way ANOVA followed by a Duncan’s multiple range test (SPSS, version 22) was implemented to determine the effect of various drying conditions on the quality parameters of the obtained leek powder.

Discussion

Physicochemical analysis of fresh Leek pulp

The pH of the fresh leeks was 6.51. A significant pH drop was observed in 50 °C dried powder, whereas pH changes in lyophilized powder were the lowest among the three drying methods. A high proportion of white components in utilized samples, resulting in a marked change in an L* value in the color analysis compared to other leek studies documented in the literature. The changes in the color values were observed between three drying methods. An increase in drying temperature resulted in a slight decrease in lightness (L*) and an increased a* value. Biernacka et al.44 conducted a study on the white and the green parts of leek using freeze-drying; the color parameters (L*, a*, b*) were reported as 85.04, −3.09, 21.44, and 73.58, −8.96, and 29.05, respectively. As expected, fresh leeks showed a higher capacity of antioxidant activity than the dehydrated samples. Conversely, in foam mat drying samples, TPC and TF were found to be significantly high. The total phenolic content was 526.41 mg kg− 1 in the fresh leeks and 1353 mg kg⁻¹ in the dried samples. According to Čeryová (2024), the total polyphenol content in the white parts ranged from 446.30 to 1580.55 mg GAE/kg, whereas in green parts it was 748.09 to 2172.78 mg GAE/kg DM3245. As in Table 3, the highest antioxidant activity was found for fresh leeks. Consistent with the findings of this results, temperature significantly influenced heat-sensitive compounds, which resulted in significant losses in physicochemical properties and alterations in color46,33.

Optimizing foam production

Overall, R² and corrected R² expressions range between 0 and 1. The fact that these expressions are close to 1 and to each other means that the model is suitable. The data observed in the dependent variables demonstrated suitability for the model. The coefficient of variation (%C.V.) shows how much the model’s estimate varies and compares the model’s consistency to the average value. The fact that the coefficient of variation is 10% or lower provides information about the reliability of the data. In the study, it was observed that the coefficient of variation of the foam density and stability was 7.08 and 5.38, respectively, while the foam volume was above this limit at 11.97. Figure 4 presents the graph that illustrates the impact of independent variables on foam density and foam stability. As seen in Fig. 4a, the interaction of egg white and CMC ratios had a significant effect on density. As egg white increased, the density first decreased and then increased. The density rose as the CMC ratio dropped. As seen in Fig. 4b, as the maltodextrin and CMC ratio increased, foam stability increased.Fig. 4FTIR spectra of 50 °C, 70 °C, control (only lyophilized foam), and fresh leek samples.

FTIR spectra of 50 °C, 70 °C, control (only lyophilized foam), and fresh leek samples.

The optimization study revealed that, aside from expansion, the program’s predicted values closely aligned with the experimentally obtained values. Consequently, the response surface methodology is a reliable and reproducible tool for determining the agent ratio in the foam drying process. A wide range of foods have been the subject of optimization research thus far. In a study about drying peaches with foam, researchers used Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to find the best conditions, and the results showed that the amount of foaming agents greatly influenced the drying process47. The foam drying study on shrimp and papaya recorded foam densities ranging from 0.47 to 0.61 and 52 to 65 g/cm³, respectively48,49. In the yogurt study, methyl cellulose and egg white were employed in varying proportions. The sample that contained 4% egg white exhibited the highest foam expansion, which was 70.0%50.

Drying kinetics

The drying kinetics were modeled using thirteen thin-layer drying models, and the results demonstrated a strong temperature-dependent behavior. As shown in Table 7, the Midilli model provided the best fit for both temperatures with exceptionally high coefficients of determination (R² = 0.9988 at 50 °C and 0.9995 at 70 °C), indicating that moisture removal during foam-mat drying follows a combined diffusion-controlled and empirical exponential pattern. The increase in the drying rate constant (k) at 70 °C reflects the accelerated internal moisture diffusion and external mass transfer driven by higher thermal energy. Foam drying at appropriate foaming agent levels and temperature results in enhanced drying performance. The logarithmic and Midilli models effectively characterize the drying behavior, as evidenced by high R² values that signify strong predictive accuracy of moisture loss over time. Kadam and Balasubramanian (2011) identified 10% egg albumin as the optimal foaming agent for foam-dried tomato pulp at 60 °C, 65 °C, and 70 °C, and drying kinetics yielded the best R² value (0.985) for the logarithmic model51. In foam-dried mango pulp, a cabinet-type dryer at 60 °C was identified as the optimal temperature52. The Midilli model, exhibiting an R² value of 0.9945, most accurately characterized the drying process for assessing the adaptability of whey to foam drying. The moisture ratio was accurately predicted as a function of time and temperature, evidenced by a high coefficient of determination. Furthermore, it was concluded that the samples dried at 60 °C produced superior results relative to the other temperatures53.

Physical analyses of powder samples

As seen in Table 8, there was an increase in the b(+) (yellowness) value as temperature rose. The rise is attributed to the intensified Maillard reaction resulting from elevated temperatures and prolonged drying durations54. In the study by Biernacka et al., the color measurements (L*, a*, b*) for the white parts of freeze-dried leek samples were 85.04, −3.09, and 21.44, while the white parts of samples dried with hot air had measurements of 84.62, −1.24, and 17.35. Oxygen during hot air drying was found to oxidize the unsaturated pigments. Accordingly, it was determined that vacuum-drying or freeze-drying are better options for preserving pigment and color stability55. Studies indicate that maltodextrin positively influences color, whereas egg white powder can expedite the browning reaction. The foam’s structure may induce alterations in the resultant powder, presumably attributable to pigment segregation56. A study investigating the physicochemical properties of leeks dried using various techniques revealed that samples boiled in salt water and subsequently dried in a microwave, along with those dried in a tray dryer, yielded the highest pH value of 5.8457.

For a powdered food to fulfill its nutritional purposes, its ingredients must be highly soluble58. Scientists who used foam drying to make acai powder found that emulsifier-containing samples were less dense and more soluble. The research highlighted that sustaining the emulsifier concentration beneath 0.7 could avert aggregation59. Powdered food flowability and stickiness are measured by the Carr index (CI) and Hausner ratio (HR). The Carr index demonstrated that the flowability value varied between 18% and 28%, with no notable difference detected across the temperatures. Nonetheless, the research conducted by Hajiaghaei and Sharifi categorized the samples dried at 50 °C as good according to the Carr index results, whereas it deemed those dried at 70 °C and through lyophilization as relatively good26.

Determination of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds in the samples were identified using GC-MS, as presented in Table 12. A total of 55, 50, and 70 volatile compounds were identified in the 50 °C, 70 °C, and freeze-dried powders, respectively. In the control group samples, 68 volatile compounds were identified, while 31 were identified in the fresh leek. It is important to note that the 70 °C dried samples contained 2,4-dimethylfuran, 2-furancarboxaldehyde (CAS), and furfural, which were not present at 50 °C. Furan compounds are generated through Maillard reactions and caramelization, both of which are affected by the presence of saccharides60. The evidence indicates that the powders dried at 50 °C are preferable.

Volatile compound results (expressed as %) for powders dried at 50 °C, 70 °C, lyophilized powders, Leek pulp, and control (foam lyophilized without Leek).

The powders dried at 50 °C displayed the highest variety of compounds among the samples. Notably, as the temperature increased, the convective dried samples showed a rise in alcohol content and a fall in acid number. Dipropyl trisulfide was found in both the convective and freeze-dried powders, which was anticipated since it is the main sulfur compound in leek pulp. However, in the control group, dipropyl disulfide was detected. Both compounds were present in elevated concentrations in leek pulp; however, their levels diminished with rising drying temperatures, resulting in an increase in trans-propenyl propyl disulfide. Consequently, they were comparatively preserved in the freeze-dried powders. In alignment with our study, the untreated leek sample revealed the presence of 7 aldehyde compounds, 2 sulfur compounds, 1 alcohol, and 1 ketone, as identified through headspace-solid phase microextraction-gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS)61.

In general, saturated straight-chain aldehydes, like hexanal and heptanal, have unpleasant, spicy odors, whereas unsaturated aldehydes tend to smell fresh and grassy62. The alcohol and aldehyde levels in convectively dried leek samples were more varied than in fresh leek, possibly due to the breakdown or oxidation of other compounds at high temperatures. Moreover, the breakdown of plant cells could result in the emission of volatile compounds, or certain compounds could be produced through isomerization, degradation, or reorganization processes62. Aldehydes like heptanal, hexanal, butanal, decanal, propanal, nonanal, and their related compounds were often found in the samples in different amounts. When comparing the volatile components of the powders to fresh leek, significant differences were observed. These include acids, alcohols, and esters, which were not detected in fresh leeks but were likely formed in the powders due to the effects of temperature and foaming agents.

Determination of individual phenolic compounds

Individual phenolic compounds in the samples were presented in Table 10. In one study on apple pomace, drying at 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C and 80 °C showed significant reductions in total phenolic and flavonoid contents and antioxidant capacity compared to fresh material — in particular, drying un-blanched pomace at temperatures above 60 °C caused marked reductions in individual compounds such as epicatechin and p-coumaric acid63. A separate study found that drying pears at 60 °C and 70 °C resulted in approximately a 40% decrease in both total phenolic compounds and antioxidant activity for each temperature64. A study examining the thermal stability of specific phenolic compounds indicated that exposure to 60 °C and 80 °C for 4 h led to a decrease of 15% in gallic acid and 25% in vanillic acid. Furthermore, at 100 °C, gallic acid, vanillic acid, and catechin exhibited reductions of 30%, 37%, and 32%, respectively65. In contrast to these results, a separate investigation of foam-dried mango demonstrated that the concentration of gallic acid and chlorogenic acid increased as the drying temperature was increased66. Based on these findings, it seems that the agents utilized in foam drying could enhance the preservation of phenolic compounds. A separate study analyzed the antioxidant and phenolic compound profile of leek by drying the white and green sections through various methods. The green portion of the leek was determined to possess a higher phenolic content than the white part. Besides that, p-coumaric acid in convectively dried samples was higher than in freeze-dried leeks and vacuum-dried samples44. It is important to note that vanillin and cinnamic acid were not found in the lyophilized and 50 °C-dried powders. This absence may be attributed to the binding of these compounds to cell-wall matrices, which requires higher thermal energy for their release. At 70 °C, the increased temperature likely aided in the breakdown of conjugated phenolic structures, leading to the detection of free cinnamic acid at a concentration of 58.50 µg/100 g. Similar temperature-dependent liberation of bound phenolics has been reported in convective drying studies44.

Antioxidant properties of powders

Significant differences in DPPH radical activity were observed between lyophilized and temperature-dried samples. Increased drying temperatures expedite non-enzymatic browning reactions, and certain reaction products may demonstrate antioxidant properties67. The Maillard reaction is a complex process occurring when proteins and carbohydrates interact, resulting in substances recognized for their antioxidant properties31. Conversely, samples dried at 50 °C demonstrated the lowest antioxidant activity by the FRAP method. After drying leeks in microwaves and tray dryers, researchers found that their total phenolic content decreased57.

Characterization of the dried powders

A study on shrimp powder production via foam drying revealed that elevated temperatures improved porosity and pore size distribution48. An irregular morphological structure consists of voids created by air bubbles during foaming, as shown in Fig. 2, which was also revealed in the foam drying process of peach fruit47. In foam drying processes, high concentrations of maltodextrin facilitate the development of smooth, spherical particle morphologies. This phenomenon is ascribed to maltodextrin’s capacity to form a soft and porous surface structure68.

Reports indicate that single bonds with hydrogen are influenced within the range of 4000 to 2000 cm⁻¹, while triple bonds are affected between 2300 and 2200 cm⁻¹. Double bonds show influence in the range of 1950 to 1550 cm⁻¹, and single bonds are impacted from 1300 to 800 cm⁻¹69. Furthermore, several peaks within the 1300–1700 cm⁻¹ range correlate with the presence of protein31. The presence of protein in egg white, utilized as a foaming agent in the foam drying method, is thereby confirmed. The extensive peak identified in the 3600–3000 cm⁻¹ range is linked to the stretching vibrations of –OH hydroxyl groups and also signifies the N–H stretching vibrations of unbound amino acids70.

Conclusions

The feasibility of the foam drying technique for leek vegetables was examined in this study. Carboxymethyl cellulose, maltodextrin, and egg white were chosen as foam agents and stabilizers, with respective usage rates of 1.50%, 14.37%, and 8.39%. The foam had stability of 95.32 ± 0.95%, expansion of 145.95 ± 11.09%, and density of 40.86 ± 3.19 g/cm³. The properties of the foam facilitated the creation of superior-quality powder. The Midilli model was selected as the most appropriate drying model for this study after the drying kinetics parameters were examined. The results for antioxidant activity and total phenolic compounds indicated TPC values of 1340.31, 1368.87, and 1349.83 mg/kg; TF values of 208.78, 225.61, and 225.61 mg/kg; ABTS values of 8.58, 16.18, and 13.83 mmol/g; DPPH values of 3.43, 4.56, and 1.91 mmol/g; and FRAP values of 1.04, 1.32, and 1.27 mmol/g for the samples dried at 50 °C and 70 °C and those that were freeze-dried, respectively. The optimal drying temperature for the preservation of volatile and phenolic compounds was determined to be 50 °C.
