Influence of HLA‐G 3′ Untranslated Region Haplotypes and SNP +3422 Gene Variants as Host Genetic Factors on the Outcomes of SARS‐CoV‐2 Infection During Acute and Post‐Acute Phases in a German Cohort
Authors: Hana Rohn, Fynn Elischer, Louisa Larbig, Sarah Jansen, Sabine Schramm, Mona Otte, Margarethe Konik, Krystallenia Paniskaki, Peter Weber, Johanna Reinold, Anja Gäckler, Adalbert Krawczyk, Benjamin Wilde, Mirko Trilling, Rafael T. Michita, Birte Möhlendick, Winfried Siffert, Thorsten Brenner, Hannah Dinse, Eva M. Skoda, Peter A. Horn, Oliver Witzke, Vera Rebmann
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHLA‐G, an important immune‐checkpoint (IC) molecule that exerts inhibitory signalling on immune effector cells, has been suggested to represent a key player in regulating the immune response to Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus Type 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2). Since specific single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) in the HLA‐G 3′untranslated region (UTR), which arrange as haplotypes, are crucial for the regulation of HLA‐G expression, we analysed the contribution of these genetic variants as host factors in SARS‐CoV‐2 infection during acute and post‐acute phases. HLA‐G gene polymorphisms in the 3′UTR were investigated by sequencing in an unvaccinated Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) cohort during acute SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (N = 505) and in the post‐acute phase (N = 253). The HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype known as UTR‐3 (p = 0.002) and the variant rs17875408 (also known as +3422) T variant (p = 0.004) are independent prognostic risk factors for fatal COVID‐19. The +3422T variant (p = 0.006) predicted also the early loss of neutralising SARS‐CoV‐2 antibodies. In addition, the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype UTR‐7 (p = 0.023) emerged as an independent prognostic factor for increased susceptibility to Long‐COVID symptoms after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Our study highlights that due to the variability of the 3′UTR genetic background, HLA‐G has the potential to contribute to the progression of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, extending to the development of Long‐COVID symptoms, despite the likely alterations in the microenvironment and associated HLA‐G‐specific regulatory elements over the course of the disease. By spotlighting HLA‐G, the importance of the genetic background of IC and their pivotal role in modulating immune responses during and after COVID‐19 are emphasised.

Introduction

In the pathogenesis of COVID‐19, the interplay between direct virus‐mediated damage and host responses is crucial, but the molecular mechanisms underlying immune dysregulation remain incompletely understood. Understanding the complex interplay of these factors is central for predicting disease outcomes and developing effective personalised therapeutic strategies. Although vaccines against COVID‐19 have effectively mitigating the worst outcomes of the pandemic, the continuous emergence of novel variants raises concern about their continued effectiveness, particularly in preventing non‐life‐threatening re‐infections [1, 2]. Additionally, the post‐acute sequelae of COVID‐19 (Long COVID) affecting a significant percentage of patients, requires further investigation.

The global urgency of the COVID‐19 pandemic prompted unique collaboration among researchers worldwide to accelerate discoveries related to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and severe COVID‐19. In the field of genetics, the COVID‐19 Host Genetics Initiative (https://www.covid19hg.org) has played a pivotal role, providing valuable data to uncover the genetic determinants of COVID‐19 susceptibility, severity, and outcomes [3, 4, 5]. One of the first genome‐wide association studies identified strong associations between COVID‐19 severity and specific genetic variations within the HLA‐G 3′UTR [4]. In addition, enhanced expression of the immunomodulatory HLA‐G in SARS‐CoV‐2‐infected patients was found in peripheral blood as well as on COVID‐19‐damaged lung epithelial cells [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Thus, it has been hypothesised that HLA‐G plays a dual role in COVID‐19 [13]: on the one hand potentially promoting viral escape from immune surveillance while on the other hand modulating the hyperinflammatory response triggered by the virus, thereby preventing excessive tissue damage [13]. HLA‐G is an important immune‐checkpoint molecule that exerts inhibitory signalling on immune effector cells by interacting with several receptors including the Ig‐like transcript 2 (ILT‐2). HLA‐G cell‐surface expression is mainly restricted to the maternal‐foetal interface and to immune privileged adult tissues, where it facilitates immune tolerance [14, 15]. However, in various pathological situations, aberrant HLA‐G expression, which is influenced by genetic variations, has been linked to immune evasion [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. We and others showed a substantial increase in the levels of membrane‐bound and soluble HLA‐G among COVID‐19 patients, and have linked the differential HLA‐G abundance to COVID‐19 courses [6, 7, 8, 9, 21]. These findings suggest that HLA‐G may be a key player in regulating the immune response to SARS‐CoV‐2, and that genetic variations in the HLA‐G gene may influence individual susceptibilities to severe COVID‐19. The polymorphic 3′untranslated region (UTR) plays a central role in regulating HLA‐G expression [17]. Within this region, certain single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and the HLA‐G 14‐base pair (bp) insertion/deletion (INS/DEL) (rs66554220) polymorphism have been identified, which may alter the binding affinity of sequence‐specific gene expression regulators for post‐transcriptional factors [22, 23, 24, 25]. These polymorphisms define distinct haplotypes. The functional disparities among these haplotypes within the 3′UTR can significantly influence individual susceptibility to viral diseases [22, 26]. For example, certain polymorphisms within the 3′UTR have been associated with increased susceptibility for Human papilloma virus (HPV) infection [27, 28]. In addition, HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype UTR‐2 carriers have demonstrated reduced susceptibility to Hepatitis C virus infection [29], while the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype UTR‐3 was more prevalent among patients with chronic Hepatitis C [30]. In living‐donor kidney transplantation the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype UTR‐4 of donors or recipients was associated with occurrence of BK polyomavirus replication or nephropathy [16].

To date, few studies have explored the association and biological significance between COVID‐19 and the potential underlying regulatory mechanisms of HLA‐G polymorphisms, and there is only one study on the relevance of HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes and COVID‐19 severity [31]. However, this study was conducted during the SARS‐CoV‐2 Delta wave and did not assess patient mortality [32]. Our cohort was recruited in the first and second waves of SARS‐CoV‐2 in Germany, which offer a greater chance of being able to define genetic determinants of the host without having to consider pre‐existing immunity and viral genetic diversity that occur in later SARS‐CoV‐2 waves. Understanding the factors that drive effective antiviral responses at the population level across different regions is essential. Analysing regional cohorts can uncover specific, often overlooked features. This knowledge is particularly important for identifying vulnerable and at‐risk individuals. To introduce the prognostic importance of HLA‐G as host genetic factors during SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and its consequences, we examined 3′UTR haplotypes in a total of 505 COVID‐19 patients in a German cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Patients were recruited for this monocentric study between May 2020 and March 2021 at the Department of Infectious Diseases, University Hospital Essen. Patients were assessed for eligibility based on a positive reverse‐transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) assay for SARS‐CoV‐2, conducted using respiratory tract samples tested either by the local Center for Disease Control (CDC) or by a designated diagnostic laboratory. To be eligible, individuals had to be 18 years of age or older and have a diagnostic specimen that tested positive for SARS‐CoV‐2 using RT‐PCR. Additionally, all participants were required to provide written informed consent. Recruitment was performed consecutively and randomly among eligible individuals. Exclusion criteria for the study included individuals who were under 18 years of age, had a prior history of COVID‐19 and those who did not provide written informed consent. In total, 505 patients were included in the study. The study period spanned the first and second waves of SARS‐CoV‐2 in Germany, characterised by the wild type of the virus. It is worth noting that during this time there was no SARS‐CoV‐2 vaccine available thus none of the patients included had been vaccinated against SARS‐CoV‐2 at time point of infection or had a prior history of COVID‐19. Patients were classified with the World Health Organisation (WHO) progression scale according to their worst clinical presentation [33]. Comprehensive demographic and clinical data were documented for all patients. All patients were offered an appointment at the outpatients clinic. Of the total cohort, 253 of the patients attended the post‐COVID outpatient clinic, completing outcome measures questionnaires about symptoms persisting after recovery. Long‐COVID was defined according to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence Guidelines. Additionally, 254 unrelated healthy subjects served as a control panel for genotyping. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Local Ethics Committees (17‐7859‐BO/20‐9374‐BO) and was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

HLA‐G 3′UTR Analysis

Desoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) isolation and HLA‐G 3′UTR typing were performed as previously described [16, 17, 34, 35]. Typing of the HLA‐G 3′UTR–associated SNP +3422C/T (rs17875408) was determined with a Taqman assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Flow Cytometry

Lymphocyte subsets were analysed with the AQUIOS CL Flow Cytometer System (Beckman Coulter), along with AQUIOS Tetra reagents, according to the manufacturer's instructions. In addition, surface expression of HLA‐G on different peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was analysed using an antibody cocktail containing fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies recognising cluster of differentiation (CD)3 (PB450, SK7, Biolegend, Germany), CD19 (BD Horizon APC‐R700, HIB19, BD Bioscience, Germany), CD14 (KO525, Beckman Coulter, Germany) and HLA‐G (APC, MEM‐G/9, Exbio, Praha, Czech Republic). Isotype‐matched antibodies from Beckman Coulter, BD Bioscience or BioLegend served as negative controls. Samples were subjected to multicolor flow cytometry using a CytoFlexS cytometer (Beckman Coulter). Data acquisition of at least 200,000 events was performed using CytExpert Version 2.1 software (Beckman Coulter) and analysed with Kaluza Analysis 2.1 software. General gating strategy for flow cytometric analysis is visualised in Figure S1.

sHLA‐G Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Soluble HLA‐G was quantified as previously described, using anti‐HLA‐G MEM‐G/09 (Exbio, Praha, Czech Republic) as capture antibody and anti‐β2‐microglobulin antibody (Dako, Denmark) as secondary antibody conjugated with peroxidase [18, 36].

Virus Neutralising Assay

The neutralising capacity of serum samples was determined by a standard end‐point dilution assay, as previously described [37].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, New York, USA), GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software), or R (Rstudio). Data were presented as median and range. After assessment for Gaussian distribution, differences between two or more groups were evaluated by Mann–Whitney test. Allele and genotype frequencies of polymorphic sites were determined with two‐sided Fisher's exact test. Genetic variants were associated to clinical parameters either by two‐sided Chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test. Multiple Mantel–Haenszel‐Zimmermann tests with Bonferroni corrections of p‐values (pc) were performed with BIAS 11.10 software (http://www.bias‐online.de/). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was evaluated by the Haploview 4.2 software through inspections of D′ and (LD) plots [38, 39]. Haplotype phasing from all individuals were assessed by PHASE 2.1 software using default parameters [16, 17]. Kaplan–Meier curve analysis and log‐rank tests were performed by survminer R package version 0.4.9 (https://CRAN.R‐project.org/package=survminer). Either stepwise multivariate Cox regression, in accordance with the proportional hazards assumption, or binomial logistic regression was used to identify independent prognostic factors. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

HLA‐G 3′UTR Haplotype Frequencies in COVID‐19 Patients and Healthy Control Subjects Do Not Differ

To define the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes, we analysed 9 previously described polymorphic variations in the HLA‐G 3′UTR, along with the associated SNP +3422 C/T variant downstream of the HLA‐G 3′UTR. All 10 polymorphic sites displayed similar pattern of linkage disequilibrium (LD) in both COVID‐19 patients and control subjects, as well as in the combined dataset (Figure 1) All HLA‐G 3′UTR SNP frequencies were in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (data not shown). Haplotype phasing revealed eight distinct haplotypes with frequencies exceeding 1% (Table 2) with UTR‐1 and UTR‐2 being the most frequent haplotypes as described in other populations by Castelli et al. [41] The HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes known as UTR‐10, UTR‐13 and UTR‐17 displayed haplotype frequencies below 1%. Overall, there were no statistically significant differences between patients and controls in HLA‐G 3′UTR SNPs frequencies, haplotype frequencies or distribution or in the distribution of the SNP +3422C/T variant (Supporting Information). We compared our HLA‐G haplotype and SNP frequencies with previously published data from Castelli et al. [41], which provides a global overview of HLA‐G haplotype and LD distribution, and observed similar frequencies within pooled European cohorts.

All outcome parameters were analysed for the individual HLA‐G 3´UTR SNPs and UTR haplotypes. Aside from the +3422 C/T variant, there were no associations found for the individual SNPs (data not shown); associations were observed only for the UTR haplotypes. Therefore, the following results section will focus on the findings related to the +3422 C/T variant and the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes.

Differential Association of +3422 C/T Variant With HLA‐G 3´UTR Haplotypes

When the SNP rs17875408 C/T at position +3422 downstream of the HLA‐G 3′UTR region was included in haplotype phasing, it became evident that haplotype UTR‐3 and the rare haplotype UTR‐13 are exclusively associated with the +3422T allelic variant. In contrast, the +3422C allelic variant demonstrated a wider UTR association. Only UTR‐5 and UTR‐6 were associated with both allelic variants (Figure 2).

Association between the SNP +3422C/T and the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes: The +3422C variant was associated with the 7 UTR haplotypes found in our patient cohort (UTR‐1, UTR‐2, UTR‐4, UTR‐7, UTR‐10, UTR‐17 and UTR‐18), whereas both the +3422C variant and the T variant were associated with UTR5 and 6. Notably, UTR 3 and the rare UTR‐13 were exclusively linked to the +3422T variant.

T Cell Counts and Association With the UTR‐1, ‐3 and ‐7 Haplotypes and the +3422T Variant

T cells are important for controlling most viral infections, including SARS‐CoV‐2. Thus, the association of HLA‐G 3′UTRs with absolute counts of CD3+, CD4+ or CD8+ T cells, along with the CD4/CD8 ratio, in acute and post–COVID‐19 phases was analysed (Figure 3). During acute COVID‐19, no direct association was observed between a certain HLA‐G 3´UTR haplotype and T cell counts. The post‐acute COVID‐19 phase, however, highlighted reductions in CD3+ (p = 0.009) and CD8+ T cells (p = 0.028) among UTR‐1–positive patients compared to the UTR‐1 negative ones (Mann–Whitney test). A similar trend was observed in UTR‐7‐positive patients; however, an association was only reached for CD8+ T cells (p = 0.016). Conversely, patients carrying the haplotype UTR‐3 or the +3422T variant showed increases in CD3+ (UTR‐3: p = 0.06; +3422T: p = 0.03) and CD8+ T‐cell numbers (UTR‐3: p = 0.017; +3422T: p = 0.002; Mann–Whitney test) as compared to the UTR‐3 and +3422T negative ones, respectively. Data for CD4+ T cells are not shown, because only UTR‐1 was linked to lower counts in the post‐acute COVID‐19 situation.

No significant differences were found between the acute COVID‐19 period and the post‐COVID‐19 situation in the CD4/CD8 ratio and its association with UTR‐1. In contrast to UTR‐1 carriers, UTR‐7 carriers exhibited a higher CD4/CD8 ratio during the post–COVID‐19 situation than did non‐carriers (p = 0.019, Mann–Whitney test).

During acute COVID‐19, UTR‐3 or +3422T carriers exhibited a higher CD4/CD8 ratio than non‐carriers (UTR‐3: p = 0.013; +3422T: p = 0.021; Mann–Whitney test). However, in the post‐acute COVID‐19 phase, an inverse association emerged, indicating a lower CD4/CD8 ratio for UTR‐3 (p = 0.08) or +3422T carriers (p = 0.006).

Frequencies of HLA‐G Expressing CD3 or CD19 Cells Are Associated With UTR‐2, UTR‐5 Haplotypes and SNP +3422T Variant in Post COVID‐19 Phase

Several studies have demonstrated associations between HLA‐G 3′UTR polymorphic sites and sHLA‐G concentrations [42, 43, 44]. However, in our cohort, we found no association between certain UTRs and sHLA‐G levels either during COVID‐19 or in the post‐sequel phase (data not shown).

As previously reported HLA‐G frequencies on CD3 T cells, B cells, and monocytes in patients with acute COVID‐19 did not significantly differ from convalescent patients post COVID‐19 [6]. Regarding association of HLA‐G UTR Haplotypes and +3422 variants, post COVID‐19 patients with UTR‐5 haplotype (n = 4) showed higher frequencies (median [range]%) of HLA‐G‐positive CD3 (2.0 [1.4–9.6]; p = 0.003) and CD19 cells (2.2 [1.8–8.8]; p = 0.04) than 27 UTR5‐negative patients (CD3: 1.1 [0.5–2.1] and CD19: 1.6 [0.7–4.4]; Mann–Whitney test). Similarly, frequencies of HLA‐G‐positive CD3 (1.6 [1.2–9.6]; p = 0.004) and CD19 cells (2.2 [1.6–8.8]; p = 0.014) were increased in post‐COVID19 patients having +3422T SNP allele (N = 8) compared to patients (N = 23) without +3422T SNP variant (CD3: 0.9 [0.5–2.1] and CD19: 1.6 [0.7–4.4]). In contrast, only the UTR‐2 haplotype was associated with lower frequencies of HLA‐G‐positive CD19‐positive cells (1.4 [0.7–3.1], N = 18 vs. 1.9 [0.9–5.8], N = 13; p = 0.013). For monocytes, no association between HLA‐G‐UTR haplotypes or +3422T‐SNP was observed by the Mann–Whitney test during acute and post COVID‐19.

HLA‐G UTR‐3 and SNP +3422T Carrier Status Are Prognostic Covariates for 30‐Day Mortality

Among SARS‐CoV‐2 infected patients, only UTR‐3 or +3422T carriers exhibited an increased 30‐day mortality rate (WHO 10 on the COVID‐19 progression scale) after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection (Table 3). Of note, both were exclusively associated with fatal outcome by multiple Mantel–Haenszel‐Zimmermann comparisons. For the remaining HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes, we found no association with COVID‐19 severity or 30‐day mortality.

Discussion

We demonstrated for the first time that the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype UTR‐3 and the SNP +3422T variant are independent prognostic factors for fatal COVID‐19. Regarding post–SARS‐CoV‐2 infection events, SNP +3422T is associated with nAb loss, whereas the HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotype UTR‐7 is associated with Long‐COVID symptoms in both univariate and multivariate analyses.

Numerous studies have evaluated the clinical and immunological aspects of COVID‐19, revealing its complexity as a result of viral factors and various host‐related characteristics involving genetic, demographic, and immunological aspects. Specific host genetic factors have been identified as contributors to both susceptibility to and severity of COVID‐19 [45, 46, 47]. However, there is limited information available regarding the biological and clinical significance, as well as the potential underlying regulatory mechanisms, of HLA‐G expression.

HLA‐G is an endogenous immunosuppressive molecule with a pivotal role in immune system regulation. Previous research has highlighted the influence of HLA‐G gene variations in modulating HLA‐G expression levels and thus has identified HLA‐G as a prognostic factor in a range of medical conditions [15, 48, 49, 50]. These investigations have underscored the significance of HLA‐G as an immune‐checkpoint [18, 51, 52, 53].

The HLA‐G molecule however, has a dual role, exerting either beneficial or harmful effects depending on the context. Identifying individuals who are genetically predisposed to different HLA‐G expression patterns could help develop new strategies to modulate immune responses and tailor therapies accordingly.

HLA‐G expression modulation has been consistently observed in various viral infections, including SARS‐CoV‐2 [13]. It was hypothesised that SARS‐CoV‐2 may exploit HLA‐G as part of its immune escape mechanism. This concept gained support from a genome‐wide association study showing a strong genetic association between the presence of SNP +3187G (rs9380142) within the HLA‐G 3′UTR region and the severity of COVID‐19 [4].

The polymorphic nature of the 3′UTR is pivotal in modulating HLA‐G expression levels. HLA‐G expression levels are regulated by post‐transcriptional mechanisms, including alternative splicing and microRNAs (miRNAs) interaction. miRNAs can modulate gene expression through mRNA degradation or by inhibiting translation. Key miRNAs involved in regulating HLA‐G expression include miR‐148a, miR‐148b, miR‐152, miR‐133a, miR‐628‐5p and miR‐548q. Variations in the target sequences of these miRNAs can influence their binding strength, thereby affecting gene expression. The 3′UTR, as the primary target for miRNA binding, contains polymorphic sites critical for the post‐transcriptional regulation of HLA‐G expression [17]. For instance, the +3010 variant affects mRNA stability through alternative splicing, while the +3142 SNP alters miRNA binding affinity. Additionally, the +3187 variant impacts mRNA stability. Although these non‐coding variants do not change the protein sequence, they significantly influence immune response modulation. Increased HLA‐G expression can enhance immune tolerance, which may benefit pathogens while hindering host defence, which may lead to poorer treatment outcomes.

However, the role of genetic variations in HLA‐G 3′UTR in COVID‐19 has been a subject of conflicting findings, a fact that emphasises the multifaceted relationship between genetics and COVID‐19 outcomes. Patients carrying a 14‐bp del/del genotype have been described to be at risk of contracting COVID‐19 [54]. Conversely, a study conducted in Italy described the 14‐bp DEL carrier status and the haplotype UTR‐1, which contains both, the 14‐bp del and the SNP +3187G allele, as protective genetic markers against severe COVID‐19 [32]. At variance to these studies, we performed a more detailed stratification into severe and fatal COVID‐19 in our study. In addition, our cohort is unique as it was recruited during the early stages of the pandemic. This timing offers a greater opportunity to identify host genetic determinants independent of pre‐existing immunity and in the absence of viral genetic diversity, which can be influenced by pre‐existing immunity and affect outcomes. At the same time, our cohort is representative in terms of both outcomes and risk factors. Because our patients presented the entire spectrum of outcomes, from oligosymptomatic to fatal, we were able to compare severely affected patients to those mildly affected. In line with previous studies, our study found that advanced age, obesity, diabetes and hypertension were associated with an elevated mortality risk. It has to be accnoleged that healthcare systems vary across regions.

It is important to acknowledge that healthcare and pandemic management and individual risk factors vary across regions. integrating clinical parameters enriches our findings by contextualising the genetic data within the broader spectrum of risk factors. There are significant differences in the regional and ancestral frequency distribution of HLA‐G 3′UTR variants among different populations [41]. Therefore, it is crucial to consider genetic diversity across regions when investigating these associations. Interestingly, UTR‐3 and UTR‐7 variants have previously been showed to exhibit varying worldwide prevalence, with haplotype UTR‐3 being more common in individuals with African and Asian ancestry, while UTR‐7 has a low frequency in Europe and being nearly absent in Africa [55]. In our study, we did not assessed ancestry; however, we found that the distribution of HLA‐G 3′UTR variants in our patient cohort was consistent with that of our control cohort and aligned with the previously published pooled European cohorts.

In our study we analysed HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes and SNP +3422C/T located downstream of 3′UTR, each of which are non‐coding but impact HLA‐G expression, as host genetic factors for COVID‐19 outcomes.

This led to the identification of the UTR‐3 haplotype as an independent risk factor for 30‐day mortality in COVID‐19 patients. So far, no specific mechanisms resulting in SARS‐CoV‐2‐mediated HLA‐G up‐regulation have been identified. However, in severe cases of COVID‐19, a cytokine storm can occur characterised by a hyperinflammatory response and an excessive release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Elevated levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and interleukin‐1β (IL‐1β) have been associated with this phenomenon and it has been suggesting that these factors might be involved in COVID‐19‐mediated enhanced HLA‐G expression within the microenviroment of body compartments [21].

There is the hypothesis that other polymorphic sites present in the 3′UTR may counterbalance the effect of the specific variation site [44]. The UTR‐3 haplotype has been linked to intermediate levels of soluble HLA‐G (sHLA‐G) expression [44]. Notably, this haplotype contains 14 bp Del/+3142 C variation sites being both associated with increased HLA‐G expression, it also harbours the +3187A SNP, associated with reduced mRNA structural stability, which results in lower HLA‐G levels [24, 56]. In addition, UTR‐3 has been associated with the high sHLA‐G producing allele HLA‐G*01:04 [57]. However, in a cohort of lung transplant recipients the combination of HLA‐G*01:04∼UTR3 haplotype was associated with lower levels of sHLA‐G additionally indicating differential expression mechanisms upon physiological and pathological conditions [58].

Besides the UTR‐3 haplotype, the HLA‐G*01:04 also harbours the SNP +3422T variant, which emerged as an independent covariant for 30‐day mortality, potentially indicating it as an expression quantitative trait locus. Of note +3422T is associated (though not exclusively) to UTR‐3. The +3422 SNP, while not located within the 3′UTR of the HLA‐G gene, remains of considerable interest due to its potential regulatory effects. Although functional experiments directly assessing the impact of this SNP are currently lacking, data from RegulomeDB indicate that it resides in a region highly accessible to DNase I digestion in specific cell types, including vaginal tissue, the sigmoid colon, and stimulated activated effector memory CD8 T cells. This accessibility suggests that the SNP may play a role in gene regulation. Moreover, the +3422 SNP is annotated as an expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL), further implying its potential influence on gene expression. Sequence analysis of transcription factor (TF) motifs indicates that this SNP may alter the binding affinities of two transcription factors, SOX13 and SOX6. Although the precise functional implications of the +3422 SNP have yet to be fully elucidated, these findings support the hypothesis that it may modulate HLA‐G expression by affecting transcription factor binding.

Earlier studies considered this variant a HLA‐G 3′UTR SNP [59, 60]. However, because this particular SNP is situated downstream beyond exon 8 of HLA‐G; it does not fulfil the criteria set by Castelli [22], who defined the HLA‐G 3′UTR as extending to the HLA‐G position +3292 within the last exon. This definition suggests that the SNP is not transcribed into mature mRNA. SNP +3422 C/T is also in LD with the SNP +755A/C (non‐synonymous) that defines the G*01:04 allele. Recent study reported an association between SNP +3422C/T genotype and an increased risk of cervical cancer, a disease linked to human papillomavirus infection [59].

To further elucidate the biological significance of SNP +3422C/T, the Ldlink‐NIH‐Database revealed an LD with SNPs located on chromosome 6 and associated with severe COVID‐19 (rs2523404, rs2735059) [61], positive response to SARS‐CoV‐2‐vaccination (rs1737060) [62], and CD8 expression on CD8+ central‐memory T cells (rs9260013) [63]. The observed associations, especially in terms of mortality and nAb loss within our cohort, may find their explanation through LD. However, the complex HLA locus poses challenges in gene studies because of its high gene density. This highly polymorphic region exhibits extensive LD among variants, making it challenging to precisely detect specific genes or alleles associated with observed outcomes. Consequently, the spectrum of genetic predisposition factors within the HLA‐locus in reported studies is notably heterogeneous [64, 65]. Of note, in our study, SNP +3422T was significantly associated with increased incidence of HLA‐G positive CD3 and CD19 in convalescents patients after COVID‐19 potential indicating it as an expression quantitative trait locus. The expression of HLA‐G on CD3 and CD19 cells raise the question, whether these cells display immune regulative capacities affecting T and B functions.

An effective immune response against viral infections depends on the activation of cytotoxic T cells that can clear infection by killing virus‐infected cells. HLA‐G can trigger CD8+ T‐cell apoptosis [66]. To draw conclusions regarding T‐cell populations, we investigated the correlation between HLA‐G 3′UTR haplotypes and T‐cell count and ratio. Our findings showed a statistically significant association exclusively within convalescent patients. This finding strongly suggests that various compartments within the microenvironment in the body and immunological pathways exert different influences on HLA‐G and, consequently, on immune effector cells, depending on different pathological and physiological conditions. This entire process occurs within the context of diverse genetic backgrounds. Similar to haplotype UTR‐3, the UTR‐7 haplotype is characterised by carrying +3010C/+3187A and has been associated with the lowest sHLA‐G levels according to studies [44]. UTR‐7 has previously been associated with sepsis‐related mortality. In our cohort, the UTR‐7 haplotype was present in 14% of Long‐COVID patients, with a likelihood ratio greater than 10.

Although this study was conducted at a single centre, the variations in treatment strategies observed during the early pandemic phase may have contributed to heterogeneity in outcomes. Nevertheless, our findings underscore the concept that the immune‐modulatory HLA‐G influences the immunopathogenesis of both COVID‐19 and Long‐COVID. Additional investigations of SNP +3422C/T in larger cohorts and across other infectious diseases are warranted to evaluate its potential as a risk‐stratification marker that would allow the development of easy‐to‐determine predictive algorithms.

Conclusion

To our knowledge this is the first study defining 3′UTR haplotypes of the immune‐checkpoint HLA‐G and the associated SNP +3422C/T variant in context of COVID‐19‐associated mortality, the loss of nAb after SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and the presence of Long‐COVID symptoms. The genetic background of the HLA‐G 3′UTR and regulatory elements in the microenvironment targeting this region highlight their influence on the time‐dependent course of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Thus, our study on HLA‐G emphasises the relevance of immune‐checkpoints in COVID‐19 and may introduce additional immune‐checkpoint axes for consideration in ongoing research into the immunological and genetic aspects of the pathogenesis of COVID‐19 and Long‐COVID. Identifying individuals genetically predisposed to different HLA‐G expression patterns could help develop new strategies to modulate immune responses and tailor therapies accordingly.
