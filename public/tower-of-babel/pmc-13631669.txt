Eutectogel with superior adhesion and high drug loading for transdermal delivery of loxoprofen against arthritis induced in rats
Authors: Yimeng Zhang, Man Li, Jiayue Zhang, Xinyue Zhang, Zhuopeng Du, Nanxi Zhao, Manli Wang, Zheng Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transdermal drug delivery represents an important therapeutic approach for arthritis. However, conventional hydrogels for transdermal drug delivery face several challenges, including insufficient adhesion, low drug-loading capacity for hydrophobic drugs, and poor environmental stability, which hinder their ability to meet clinical needs. In this study, a novel eutectogel was developed using poly(acrylic acid-co-4-hydroxyphenyl methacrylate-co-2-hydroxyethyl acrylate) as the network and zirconium ions as the crosslinking agent for high-performance transdermal drug delivery. The prepared eutectogel demonstrated excellent adhesion, mechanical strength, and environmental stability. The deep eutectic solvent and the polyacrylate network showed synergistic effects, leading to significant improvement of the solubility of loxoprofen in eutectogel. Infrared spectroscopy and molecular docking confirmed that the drug binds to the eutectogel via multiple hydrogen bonds, resulting in stable and controlled transdermal release. Furthermore, the eutectogel displayed excellent biocompatibility and distinct therapeutic efficacy against arthritis in animal studies. The eutectogel offers a novel transdermal drug delivery carrier for arthritis therapy, providing superior performance, a well-defined mechanism of action, and proven safety and efficacy.

Introduction

Arthritis, a common chronic inflammatory disease, is mainly characterized by joint pain, swelling, and functional impairment, which has a profound effect on the quality of life of patients (Smolen et al., 2016). Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), the first-line clinical agents (Thakur et al., 2018), inhibit cyclooxygenase activity and reduce prostaglandin synthesis to significantly alleviate inflammation and pain (Bua et al., 2020). Loxoprofen (LOX), as a prodrug, displays potent anti-inflammatory and analgesic activity upon metabolic conversion into active metabolites in the body and is extensively used to treat arthritis (Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2020). However, its long-term oral administration may cause systemic adverse effects and gastrointestinal damage, which greatly restricts its long-term clinical application (Smolen et al., 2016). Moreover, the current treatment methods for arthritis still have several common problems, such as limited efficacy, significant side effects, and prominent drug resistance (Shams et al., 2026). Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDSs) have emerged as the best option for treating arthritis locally because of their capacity to achieve targeted drug delivery to the joints, maintain stable local drug concentrations, and improve patient compliance (Bajaj et al., 2011; Gu and Chen, 2018).

Hydrogels have emerged as a novel excipient and delivery platform, attracting significant interest in the field of transdermal drug delivery due to their highly hydrated three-dimensional network structure, good stretchability, and excellent biocompatibility (Ning et al., 2026). Recently, polyacrylate, polyurethane, and natural polysaccharide-based composite hydrogel patches have been widely used to treat pain, as anti-inflammatory therapy, and for the topical treatment of chronic diseases (Bahmani et al., 2025; Cai et al., 2024; Trinh et al., 2026). However, hydrogels exhibit poor adhesive properties, which can be further reduced by moisture loss during prolonged use or storage on the skin (Lei et al., 2023). Moreover, hydrogels are suitable for water-soluble drugs. NSAIDs such as LOX are highly lipophilic, yet display very poor aqueous solubility. As they must penetrate the lipophilic skin barrier, TDDSs require the vast majority of candidate drugs to be lipophilic (Phatale et al., 2022). This may result in uneven drug loading, phase separation, and crystallization in conventional hydrogel matrices, limiting the ability to achieve effective local therapeutic concentrations. Guan et al. demonstrated in their study on chitosan methacrylate photocrosslinked hydrogels that unmodified LOX, following direct embedding, showed poor drug loading efficiency and significant burst release. Poor solubility makes it difficult to achieve stable local drug delivery (Guan et al., 2024). In a chondroitin sulfate-based hydrogel, Khalid et al. noted that the poor aqueous solubility of hydrophobic drugs directly limits the drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency (Khalid et al., 2018). Similarly, Ahmad et al. demonstrated that, due to the poor aqueous solubility of LOX in thermosensitive chitosan hydrogels, conventional hydrogels make it challenging to attain high drug loading and sustained-release properties (Ahmad et al., 2019).

Deep eutectic solvents (DESs) represent a new class of green solvents that exhibit significant potential in the field of drug delivery (Pedro et al., 2022; Smith et al., 2014). It is commonly formed through hydrogen-bond interactions between hydrogen-bond acceptors (such as choline chloride) and hydrogen-bond donors (such as polyols and organic acids), providing benefits including excellent design flexibility, easy preparation, and strong biocompatibility. In contrast to conventional solvents, DES possess a unique hydrogen-bond network structure, improving drug solubility and significantly increasing the drug-loading capacity of formulations (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2022). Furthermore, DES demonstrates specific characteristics that improve transdermal penetration by reversibly raising skin permeability through the disruption of the ordered arrangement of stratum corneum lipids (Banerjee et al., 2017; Tanner et al., 2018). Eutectogels represent a new class of materials in which DES functions as the continuous phase with polymers or small-molecule gelling agents forming a three-dimensional network. Eutectogels have been widely studied and applied in fields such as biomedicine and multifunctional materials. DES-based eutectogels combine the properties of DES and hydrogels, providing advantages like good biocompatibility, high drug solubility, high mechanical strength, strong adhesion, and broad temperature stability.

This study reports a TDDS using a eutectogel with high adhesion and high drug-loading capacity. DES was prepared using choline chloride (ChCl) and glycerol (Gly), with poly(acrylic acid-co-4-hydroxyphenyl methacrylate-co-2-hydroxyethyl acrylate) as the polymer network and zirconium ions (Zr4+) as the crosslinking agent to form the eutectogel (Fig. 1and Fig. S1), designated as PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel. By adjusting the monomer ratio, strong mechanical characteristics and good adhesion were attained, demonstrating significant benefits over the gels used in previous studies, as shown in Fig. 1c and Table S1 (Fang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Tong et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). By utilizing synergistic benefits such as solubilization, controlled release, and improved skin permeation through multiple hydrogen-bonding and metal-coordination interactions, this method solves problems with LOX transdermal delivery and offers a novel carrier choice for the efficient transdermal delivery of poorly soluble NSAIDs. Materials and methods

Materials

Acrylic acid (AA, ≥ 99%,CAS:79–10-7), hydroxyethyl acrylate (HEA,96%, CAS:818–61-1), 4-hydroxyphenyl methacrylate (HP, 97%, CAS: 31480–93-0), diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphine oxide (TPO,CAS:75980–60-8), Zirconium chloride octahydrate (ZrOCl₂·8H₂O, 98%, CAS: 13520–92-8), ChCl (Analytical Reagent, 98%, CAS: 67–48-1), Gly ((Analytical Reagent, 99%, CAS: 56–81-5), and LOX (≥98%, CAS: 68767–14-6). All reagents were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.

Japanese White Long-Eared rabbits (male, 2 kg) were obtained from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Wistar rats (male, 200 ± 20 g) and SD rats (male, 200 ± 20 g) were both obtained from Changchun Yisi Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Beihua University (Approval No.: BHU-IACUC-C2022–4–20).

Preparation of eutectogel and drug-loaded patches

The ChCl-Gly DES with a molar ratio of 2:1 was selected in this study due to its excellent biocompatibility, negligible skin irritation, stable hydrogen-bonded network, and superior solubilization capacity for hydrophobic drugs. This classic ratio can construct sufficient and reversible intermolecular hydrogen bonds, effectively improving the solubility of LOX, optimizing the microscopic pore structure of eutectogel, and assisting transdermal penetration. In addition, the 2:1 ChCl/Gly system possesses appropriate viscosity, excellent thermodynamic stability and no phase separation, which is highly suitable for the fabrication of high-performance drug-loaded eutectogels for topical transdermal delivery (Zhang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022; Turner and Holbrey, 2019).

DES was prepared by heating a mixture of ChCl and Gly in a 2:1 M ratio in a 90 °C water bath until a transparent, clear solution formed. Using a one-pot method, 3 mL of DES was taken, and the monomers AA, HP, and HEA, along with ZrOCl₂·8H₂O (40 mg), were added. Finally, the photoinitiator TPO was added, and the mixture was stirred until completely dissolved. The eutectogel precursor solution was poured into a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) mold and irradiated with UV light at 365 nm for 5 min.

For the lox-loaded patches, LOX was added to the eutectogel precursor solution and stirred until completely dissolved. The mixture was then poured into a PTFE mold and irradiated with UV light at 365 nm for 5 min.

Characterization

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The eutectogel was placed in a beaker and fully immersed in deionized water at 25 °C to replace the DES solvent. The deionized water was refreshed every 6 h with a total soaking duration of 24 h for complete solvent exchange. After thorough replacement, the sample was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for 30 min, immediately vacuum freeze-dried at −50 °C for 12 h, and then fractured with liquid nitrogen to expose the flat cross-section. After gold coating under vacuum, the sample was examined using a scanning electron microscope (ZEISS Gemini SEM 300, Germany) at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV. The distribution of Zr4+ in the eutectogel was analyzed using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS, Oxford Xplore 30, UK).

Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR)

FT-IR was used to characterize the structure of the samples. The measurement range was 4000–400 cm−1, with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)

The chemical structure of the samples was analyzed by 1H NMR. An appropriate amount of sample was dissolved in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO‑d₆), and the chemical shifts and peak shapes were measured and recorded at room temperature.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

XPS was used to analyze the elemental composition and chemical valence states of the sample surface. Al Kα was used as the radiation source, with a full-scan energy range of 0–1200 eV. High-resolution spectra were used to perform peak fitting on characteristic peaks such as C 1 s and O 1 s, with charge correction performed using C 1 s (284.8 eV).

Differential scanning calorimeter (DSC)

DSC was used to analyze the thermal behavior of the samples. A 3–5 mg sample of freeze-dried eutectogel was placed in an aluminum crucible and heated from −20 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere. The heat flow curve was recorded to observe the glass transition and phase transition behavior.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

Thermal stability of the samples was analyzed by means of TGA. A sample of 3–5 mg was placed in an aluminum crucible and heated from 30 °C to 100 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under a nitrogen atmosphere, and the curve of mass change versus temperature was recorded.

Mechanical properties

Tensile tests were conducted using a ZQ990B universal testing machine (Dongguan Zhiqu Precision Instrument Co., Ltd.) equipped with 50 N/500 N load cells. Samples were prepared into strip-shaped specimens (3.0 cm × 1.0 cm × 0.1 cm) and tested at room temperature at a tensile speed of 100 mm/min, with stress-strain curves recorded. Each set of parallel tests was conducted at least three times, and the average value was calculated.

Rheological studies

Strain sweep

Under a fixed frequency of 1 Hz, a strain sweep was performed on the sample over a strain range of 0.01% to 100% to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVE) of the sample.

Frequency sweep

Within the linear viscoelastic region, a fixed strain is selected, and a frequency scan is performed within the range of 0.1–100 rad/s. The changes in the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") with frequency are recorded to evaluate the sample's viscoelastic properties and network structure strength.

Stress sweep

With a fixed frequency of 1 Hz, perform a stress scan applying stresses ranging from 1 to 10,000 Pa. Measure the changes in G' and G“ with respect to stress to determine the sample's yield stress—the stress value at which G" begins to decrease significantly.

Creep test

Select a constant stress within the linear viscoelastic region and conduct a creep test at 32 °C. First, apply a constant stress of 100 Pa to the sample and maintain it for 60 s, recording the change in strain over time to obtain the creep curve. Subsequently, the stress is removed, and the strain recovery curve is recorded. The sample's creep resistance and viscoelasticity are evaluated based on the creep and recovery rates.

Temperature sweep

Under fixed strain and frequency conditions within the linear viscoelastic region, a temperature scan is performed at a heating rate of 2 °C/min. The changes in G' and G" with temperature are monitored to evaluate the sample's thermal stability.

Adhesion properties

The tack adhesion was determined using a CZY-G type tack adhesion tester (Labthink, Jinan, China) via a rolling ball stop test. In accordance with the provisions of the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (2025 Edition), a hard steel ball was rolled down from the top of an inclined plate set at a 30° angle. The value of the rolling ball adhesion test was recorded when the ball came to a stop after contacting the eutectogel.

The 180° peel adhesion test was conducted in accordance with the method for determining the peel strength of patches. A eutectogel patch (50 mm × 25 mm × 1 mm) was firmly affixed to the test plate. The eutectogel patch was then folded 180°, and one end of the folded patch was secured in the test fixture. Use the testing instrument to perform a continuous peel process at a constant speed of 100 mm/min, and record the curve of peel force versus time.

We conducted shear tests on porcine skin bridge specimens. After processing, the porcine skin was cut into standard specimens measuring 2 cm × 10 cm. The test employed a bridge configuration, one end of each of the two porcine skin specimens was overlapped, and eutectogel was evenly applied to the overlapping area. A roller was used to apply uniform pressure, and the specimens were left to stand for 20 min to allow the eutectogel to bond fully with the porcine skin, simulating the actual application process. The prepared porcine skin bridge specimens were secured in the upper and lower grips of a universal testing machine. Tensile shear testing was performed at a speed of 5 mm/min. The maximum shear force at eutectogel failure was recorded, and the bridge shear strength was calculated. Concurrently, the adhesion state and peeling behavior of the eutectogel on the porcine skin surface were observed.

Cold flow test

The cold flow properties of the samples were tested using the static loading method. A circular eutectogel (10 mm in diameter) was placed in the center of a smooth glass plate, and a 500-g weight was uniformly applied above the sample, which was then left undisturbed at room temperature for 24 h. The sample morphology was then photographed. The formula for calculating the percentage change in cold flow weight is as follows:Cold flow weight change rate%=Wt−W0W0×100%where W₀ is the initial weight, and Wt is the weight at time t.

Drug release and drug skin permeation study

Drug release experiments

In vitro drug release experiments were conducted using a horizontal Franz diffusion cell with an effective diffusion area of 0.785 cm2 and a receiving chamber volume of 4.0 mL. The experimental temperature was maintained at 32 °C (simulating human skin surface temperature). Drug-loaded eutectogel patches (1.0 cm in diameter, 1.0 mm in thickness) were securely fixed between the donor and receiver chambers. The receiving medium was phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4. Samples of 4.0 mL of the receiving solution were withdrawn at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h, and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS preheated to 32 °C. The withdrawn samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to determine drug concentration. The detection wavelength for LOX was set at 222 nm. The mobile phase was a mixture of methanol, water, phosphoric acid, and triethylamine with a volume ratio of 75:25:0.1:0.1 (v/v), and the flow rate was controlled at 1.0 mL/min. The cumulative drug release was calculated, and the release data were fitted using zero-order and first-order kinetic equations to elucidate the drug release mechanism.

Drug skin permeation study

Male Wistar rats (6–8 weeks old, 180–220 g) were selected, euthanized, and the abdominal skin was excised. Subcutaneous fat was removed, and the skin was washed and stored at −80 °C for later use. Prior to the experiment, the skin was allowed to thaw naturally and fixed between the supply and receiving chambers of a Franz diffusion cell with the stratum corneum facing upward. Drug-loaded eutectogel patches were applied to the skin surface. The receiving chamber was filled with PBS (pH 7.4) at 32 °C, and a magnetic stirrer was set to 300 r/min. Samples of 2 mL were collected at 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 h. Fresh receiving solution was replenished using the same method. In vitro skin permeation studies were performed using Franz diffusion cells with a diffusion area diameter of 1.5 cm, and the receptor compartment contained 4 mL of receptor medium. A eutectogel patch (diameter = 1 cm, thickness = 1 mm) was placed onto the surface of skin for permeation evaluation. Drug concentrations were determined by HPLC, and the cumulative transdermal delivery per unit area, Q (μg/cm2), was calculated to evaluate the transdermal permeation capacity of the eutectogel. The Q value was calculated using the following equation:Q=∑i=2n2.5Ci−0.5Ci−2Ai=246……where Q is the cumulative skin permeation amount (μg cm−2), Ci is the drug concentration in the receptor solution (μg mL−1), and A is the effective permeation area (0.785 cm2).

Adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) model establishment and treatment

Male SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g) were selected. Adjuvant-induced arthritis (AIA) was established by intradermal injection of 0.15 mL of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA) into the right hind paw. Seven days after modeling, rats exhibiting marked plantar swelling and significant joint inflammation were selected and randomly divided into three groups (5 rats per group). They were respectively the blank group, the AIA model group and the eutectogel group.

For the eutectogel group, LOX-loaded eutectogel patch was applied to the abdomen and replaced every 2 days, with a dosage of 0.15 mg/kg. The AIA model group and the blank group received no drug treatment. Treatment continued for 21 days. Every 3 days, the swelling of the rat's metatarsophalangeal and ankle joints was measured using a Vernier caliper, and arthritis scores were recorded.

At the end of the experiment, rats were euthanized under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia, and ankle joint tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Sections were stained with H&E, eosin-gallus green, toluidine blue, and anti-tartrate acid phosphatase (TRAP) to observe pathological damage to joint tissues and the repair of cartilage and bone tissues.

Biosafety evaluation

Male Japanese White rabbits (2.0 kg) were selected. Abdominal hair was shaved 24 h prior to the experiment to prevent skin damage. The rabbits were randomly divided into three groups: blank group, positive group (10% sodium dodecyl sulfate, SDS), and eutectogel group.

The eutectogel was applied to the rabbits' abdominal skin, secured, and left in place for 24 h. The erythema index (EI) was measured using a skin erythema meter (Mexameter) before application (EI₀) and 24 h after removal of the sample (EI₂₄). The erythema index ratio was calculated as EI₂₄/EI₀ to evaluate the degree of erythema stimulation. The ΔEI value was calculated using the following equation:ΔEI=EI24hEI0hwhere EI0 is the EI value at 0 h, and EI24h is the EI value at 24 h.

After 24 h, the rabbits were euthanized. Skin tissue from the application site was collected, fixed in 10% neutral formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E. The skin tissue morphology, inflammatory cell infiltration, and tissue damage were observed under a light microscope to comprehensively evaluate the skin safety of the eutectogel.

Molecular docking

This study employed molecular docking technology to further investigate the interaction strength between LOX and binary polymers (HP-HEA) and ternary polymers (HP-AA-HEA). Two polymer carriers were selected as the research models to evaluate the binding effect between the drug and the polymer matrix. The molecular structure of LOX was derived from the Chemical Book database and was drawn and organized using Chem Draw 22.0.0 software. The molecular docking simulation calculation was carried out using the Materials Studio 7.0 software (San Diego, California, Corrys Company, USA). Firstly, the structure of each molecular component was optimized using the Forcite module, and then the molecular docking calculation was completed through the Blend module. The resulting complex system was optimized for the final geometric structure using the COMPASS II force field.

Data analysis

All experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and analyzed statistically using SPSS software. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, while comparisons between two groups were performed using t-tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Characterization

The SEM results revealed that the prepared PAE—Zr4+ exhibits a uniform and continuous three-dimensional porous network structure. The pore distribution is regular, with no obvious agglomeration or pore blockage. The pore sizes are uniform, and the pores are interconnected, forming a complete porous network system. To verify the successful formation of the PAE—Zr4+ network and the intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions, the PAE—Zr4+ samples were analyzed by FT-IR. The results of the infrared spectroscopy analysis showed that the characteristic absorption peak of the hydroxyl stretching vibration of pure Gly appeared at 3217 cm−1. When Gly and ChCl were eutectically mixed in a specific molar ratio to form DES, the characteristic hydroxyl stretching vibration peak of Gly shifted to 3300 cm−1, showing a remarkable blue shift. The characteristic C—N stretching vibration peak corresponding to the quaternary ammonium cation (1470 cm−1) exhibited a slight blue shift, along with broadening of the peak and a slight decrease in peak intensity, indicating hydrogen bonding between the chloride ion and the hydroxyl groups of Gly (Fig. 2b). The double bond peaks of HP, AA, and HEA, located at 1442.01 cm−1, 1634.85 cm−1, and 1637.26 cm−1 respectively, all disappeared after polymerization into a eutectogel, indicating that the monomers had successfully polymerized to form the eutectogel. The phenolic hydroxyl peak of HP is at 3459.20 cm−1, and that of HEA is at 3432.01 cm−1. After eutectogel formation, both shifted to 3296.10 cm−1, indicating hydrogen bonding interactions between the hydroxyl groups and the phenolic hydroxyl groups (Fig. 2c). After the addition of Zr4+, the hydroxyl peak of the PAE eutectogel shifted to 3287.12 cm−1, indicating that addition of Zr4+ formed a coordination bonds with the hydroxyl group. The –COOH stretching vibration of AA shifted from 1693.76 cm−1 to 1721.63 cm−1, indicating that it interacts with hydroxyl groups or phenolic hydroxyl groups via hydrogen bonding. After the addition of Zr4+, it shifted to 1725.014, indicating a coordination interaction between the Zr4+ and the carboxyl groups.

1H NMR was used to further confirme the success of the polymerization reaction and the presence of intermolecular hydrogen bonding. Fig. 2d shows that in the ChCl-Gly low-melting-point system, chloride ions act as strong hydrogen bond acceptors and preferentially form stable hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups of Gly. Consequently, the hydrogen of the Gly hydroxyl group shifts to a lower field by 0.09 ppm, confirming the hydrogen bonding between them (Fig. S2). After gelation, the carboxyl hydrogen of AA shifted from 12.37 to 12.32 ppm, the phenolic hydroxyl of HP shifted from 9.49 to 9.54 ppm, and the alcohol hydroxyl of HEA shifted from 4.85 to 5.49 ppm, indicating that a multi-component (multi-intensity) synergistic hydrogen-bonding network had formed in the eutectogel system (Fig. 2e). Upon the introduction of Zr4+ into the eutectogel system, the chemical shift of the AA carboxyl proton shifted from 12.32 ppm to 12.30 ppm in the high-field region (Fig. 2f). This is attributed to the formation of a stable coordination structure between the carboxyl group and Zr4+, where the electron cloud of the oxygen atom shifts toward the metal center, thereby enhancing the shielding effect of the carboxyl hydrogen. HP exhibited a slight shift, confirming its weaker coordination interaction with the metal ion. The shift of the HEA hydroxyl group decreased from 5.49 ppm to 5.47 ppm, as Zr4+ coordination competition weakened the hydrogen bonding of the hydroxyl group. These results confirm that the specific coordination of Zr4+ with carboxyl and hydroxyl groups constructs a novel dynamic cross-linking network, forming a cross-linking network that combines multiple hydrogen bonding interactions with coordination interactions.

As shown in Fig. 2g–h, the 531.85 and 533.11 peaks in the PAE oxygen spectrum correspond to CO and C—O, respectively. After the addition of Zr4+, the CO and C—O peaks shifted to 531.54 and 532.42, respectively, and a new peak at 533.13 appeared, which is primarily attributed to the Zr—O coordinating oxygen. Mechanical properties

Despite joint flexion, extension, and associated friction during movement, the patch retained its eutectogel morphology and interfacial adhesion, resulting in superior mechanical properties. To investigate the mechanical properties of the eutectogel, screening experiments were systematically carried out by optimizing the contents of AA, HP, and Zr4+. As the AA content increased, both strain and tensile strength increased (Fig. 3a), but showed a decreasing trend once the AA content exceeded 20%. This demonstrated that the carboxyl groups introduced by the addition of AA improved hydrogen-bonding interactions within the eutectogel system, increasing crosslinking density, leading to improved mechanical properties. Because of the phenolic hydroxyl groups and benzene rings, a suitable amount of HP increases the intermolecular forces and the network rigidity. Upon exceeding 8% content, the excessive rigidity significantly decreased the deformation ability (Fig. 3b). Due to a relatively weak intermolecular hydrogen bonding in the eutectogel system, Zr4+ was introduced to strengthen its mechanical properties. As displayed in Fig. 3c, tensile stress was linearly increased with Zr4+ content, which can be attributed to Zr4+ acting as a crosslinking agent to form ionic coordination bonds with carboxyl groups and working synergistically with hydrogen bonding within the system to improve the molecular binding force, hence increasing the eutectogel's tensile strength. At low Zr4+ concentrations, a uniform and flexible eutectogel network was formed by moderate ionic cross-linking, which gradually increased the tensile strain. However, as the Zr4+ content exceeded 0.8%, the eutectogel network became overly dense and rigid with excessive ionic cross-linking, limiting molecular segment movement and hence decreasing tensile strain. Therefore, an optimal Zr4+ content improved the integrity and stability of the eutectogel network structure, facilitating the regulation of its mechanical properties. The viscoelastic properties of the eutectogel were investigated using rheological tests. Table S2 presents the detailed formulation of different types of eutectogels and hydrogel. According to frequency sweeping results, the values of G' and G“ increased significantly with the addition of AA and Zr4+, indicating the formation of a denser and more robust synergistic network, leading to significantly improved intermolecular interactions, improving the dynamic mechanical properties of the eutectogel (Fig. 3d). The mechanical response of eutectogels were investigated using stress sweeping analysis under varying shear stresses. The PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel demonstrated the highest and most stable values of G' and G", indicating no yielding, network breakdown, or structural relaxation behavior, and could stably withstand shear forces within the tested stress range, highlighting the stable mechanical performance (Fig. 3e). The eutectogel exhibited a dense and strong synergistic network structure arising from hydrogen bonds and Zr4+ coordination, with uniformly distributed cross-linking points and strong intermolecular interactions. Furthermore, the network structure showed no fracture or deformation and remained intact under varying shear stresses. Creep test results demonstrated that the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel had the smallest creep deformation and the slowest creep rate, reflecting a dense and robust network, with strong creep resistance (Fig. 3f). By comparing the tensile and creep tests of the eutectogel with a single hydrogen bond, and the eutectogel without Zr4+ coordination cross-linking, it can be seen that hydrogen bonds mainly contribute to energy dissipation and enhance the eutectogel's ductility, while the Zr4+ coordination bonds are responsible for strengthening the network rigidity and inhibiting long-term creep deformation. These two forces perform their respective functions and jointly construct the high strength and anti-creep characteristics of the eutectogel. Compared to hydrogel, the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel exhibited significantly superior mechanical performance, which can be ascribed to the presence of multiple hydrogen-bonding interactions and metal coordination within the eutectogel.

The dynamic hydrogen-bonding behavior of the eutectogel network was characterized using variable-temperature FTIR (VT FTIR) and two-dimensional correlation infrared spectroscopy (2DCOS) (Fig. 3h) (Jin et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). The eutectogel displayed a characteristic broad peak in the hydroxyl stretching region (3000–3600 cm−1), which likely arises from a multi-level hydrogen-bonding system. As the temperature increased, the characteristic peak of hydroxyl gradually shifted toward higher wavenumbers (blue shift), with a significant increase in the intensity of the free hydroxyl peak, revealing that hydrogen bonds dissociate progressively rather than simultaneously upon heating, confirming the presence of a hierarchical, multi-intensity hydrogen-bonding network within the eutectogel. The carbonyl group of the hydrogen-bonded state in the acrylic units showed a blue shift upon increasing temperature at 1600–1750 cm−1, although the magnitude of the shift was negligible, suggesting strong coordination between Zr4+ and the carboxyl groups, which significantly improved the thermal stability of the network and suppressed the rapid dissociation of carbonyl hydrogen bonds.

2DCOS analysis demonstrated characteristics auto-peaks at 3500 and 3200 cm−1 in the synchronous spectrum, signifying the coordinated variation of the two types of hydrogen-bonding sites in the system (Fig. 3i). According to the Noda's rule, the asynchronous spectrum provided a clear sequence of group responses (Fig. 3j), consistent with the stepwise dissociation behavior aligned with the hierarchical multi-intensity hydrogen bond hinge mechanism, conferring excellent dynamic adaptability, energy dissipation capacity, and structural stability to the eutectogel network. In the 1600–1700 cm−1 range, 2DCOS analysis suggested that carbonyl shifts occurred in a distinct sequence: formation of free carboxyl groups first, followed by the dissociation of carboxylic acid dimers, and finally Zr4+-carboxyl coordination structure (Fig. S5). The synergistic evolution of the hydroxyl and carbonyl regions collectively verified a characteristic hierarchical multi-strength supramolecular network in the eutectogel. Preferential dissociation of weak and medium-strength hydrogen bonds facilitated effective energy dissipation, whereas strong hydrogen bonds and metal coordination bonds, acting as the network backbone, maintained structural integrity, establishing the structural basis for the eutectogel's excellent mechanical properties, interfacial adhesion, and dynamic adaptability (Fig. 3k).

Adhesive properties

Adhesive properties are critical for enabling the transdermal patches to adhere to the skin and remain in place for an extended period, ensuring tight adhesion over the joint, working synergistically with mechanical properties to reduce detachment and substrate slippage, stabilizing the drug delivery interface, and ultimately determining the patch's drug delivery efficiency and safety. The analysis on the effect of HEA content on adhesion revealed a significant improvement in tack adhesion, 180° peel strength, and adhesion strength with increasing HEA content. However, content greater than 10% displayed a downward trend (Fig. 4a). The incorporation of HEA increased the surface density of exposed adhesive chemical groups on the eutectogel and improved intermolecular forces with the substrate. Excessive HEA may result in incomplete molecular polymerization and an increased number of short-chain molecules, thus decreasing adhesion strength and increasing the tendency of eutectogel breakage and residue during peeling. The comparative analysis showed that HEA improved the adhesion, whereas metal ions increased cohesive strength, leading to further optimization of the overall adhesion performance of the eutectogel. We also investigated the effect of Zr4+ on adhesion. The results showed that a 0.8% Zr4+ content resulted in the strongest adhesion. This might be due to the addition of an appropriate amount of Zr4+, which optimized the three-dimensional network structure of the eutectogel, enhanced its mechanical stability, and strengthened the eutectogel's cohesion. At the same time, the eutectogel surface also retained a large number of hydrogen bond active sites, ensuring that the eutectogel has excellent hydrophilicity and interfacial adhesion ability. This led to a balance between the eutectogel's cohesion and surface adhesion, resulting in a relatively strong overall adhesion (Fig. S6). Relative to hydrogel, the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel demonstrated superior adhesive performance (Fig. 4b). The adhesion performance of the biological interface was simulated using the porcine skin shear test. The PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel reached a shear force of 5 N, with an adhesion distance of 12.91 mm, and adhered closely to the porcine skin with uniform distribution, demonstrating excellent adhesion stability (Fig. 4c-d). Environmental stability and biosafety evaluation

The environmental stability of eutectogels is a critical prerequisite for the practical application of transdermal patches, as it enables them to withstand external environmental disturbances, maintain their inherent structure and morphology, and ensure stable performance during storage and use. Currently, hydrogel patches available on the market tend to dehydrate during daily use, which affects drug release and transdermal delivery. Therefore, we conducted environmental stability tests on the prepared PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel to investigate its stability. DSC testing revealed that the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel did not undergo phase transitions between −30 °C and 40 °C, whereas the hydrogel exhibited a phase transition at 0 °C (Fig. 5a). TGA results indicated that the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel showed almost no change between 30 and 100 °C, whereas the hydrogel lost 40% of its weight (Fig. 5b). Additionally, we tested the weight change of the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel over 30 days to investigate its long-term stability (Fig. 5c). The results show that the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel maintained stable weight over 30 days, with no significant changes in adhesion. Biocompatibility is a prerequisite for the application of topical eutectogel patches, good biocompatibility reduces the risk of skin irritation and meets the safety requirements for long-term application on joint areas. Therefore, we conducted skin irritation tests to investigate the safety of the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel as a transdermal drug delivery matrix. Patches were applied to rabbit skin for 24 h, and skin irritation was assessed through visual inspection and histological evaluation. After 24 h of observation, the positive control group treated with 10% SDS solution exhibited obvious skin irritation symptoms such as erythema and edema in the application area, whereas neither the eutectogel group nor the blank group showed any signs of skin damage, erythema, or edema (Fig. 5d). The EI value of the eutectogel group did not show significant changes compared to normal skin (Fig. 5e). H&E staining results showed extensive epidermal thickening in the positive control group, accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration, while no histopathological changes were observed in the eutectogel group. This further confirms that the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel exhibits good safety and is suitable for use in transdermal drug delivery.

Adhesion performance of eutectogel after drug loading

Tensile property tests were performed to evaluate the adhesion performance of eutectogel after drug loading. Compared to the blank eutectogel matrix, LOX incorporation leads to a slight reduction in the elongation at break and tensile strength of the eutectogel (Fig. 6a), as the embedding of drug molecules partially interferes with the interactions between the polymer chains of the eutectogel, however, the eutectogel still retains overall mechanical properties. Rheological studies were conducted to further investigate the internal structure and rheological behavior of eutectogel. Based on the frequency sweeping results, LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel showed slightly decreased G' and G“ values, accompanied by reduced viscoelasticity (Fig. 6b), compared to the blank eutectogel. Moreover, the drug-loaded eutectogel satisfied G' > G” within the tested frequency range, and the modulus increased with frequency, demonstrating characteristic frequency-dependent behavior, suggesting that LOX incorporation only slightly changed the eutectogel cross-linked network without destroying the stability of the three-dimensional network structure. The creep test indicated that, although the creep deformation slightly increased, the drug-loaded eutectogel still exhibited an excellent deformation recovery performance, suggesting that drug loading had no substantial adverse effect on its deformation recovery ability (Fig. 6c). As confirmed by the strain sweeping results, the linear viscoelastic range of the drug-loaded eutectogel decreased from 250% to 215%, with a slight decrease in strain limit, while still retaining a broad linear viscoelastic range and favorable structural stability (Fig. 6d). In the temperature sweeping test, the modulus variation trend of the drug-loaded eutectogel was consistent with that of the blank eutectogel. Increasing temperature enhanced the molecular thermal motion, leading to relaxation of the eutectogel network, resulting in decreases in the mechanical properties and viscoelasticity (Fig. 6e). Across the investigated temperature range, G' was consistently higher than G", indicating structural stability and confirming suitability for the temperature requirements of transdermal patches. The results of the adhesion performance test showed that the shear adhesion strength of the drug-loaded eutectogel reached 4.65 N, with drug loading causing no significant impact on the adhesion performance and maintaining excellent adhesion strength (Fig. 6f–g). Porcine skin contains polar groups such as hydroxyl and amino groups and possesses a porous and rough surface, making it an ideal model for evaluating bioadhesive properties of eutectogels. Benefiting from the high polarity and water retention properties of DES, the eutectogel effectively absorbed free water on the skin surface, removed the interfacial water layer, and completely wetted the skin pores, thus achieving a close fit. Similarly, the low volatility of DES maintained long-term interfacial hydration and flexibility, minimizing drying and detachment. The multiple functional groups within the polymer established hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π-π stacking interactions with the skin, significantly improving the interfacial bonding force. The synergistic effect of multiple actions established a favorable balance between the adhesion and cohesion of the eutectogel, effectively preventing detachment and internal structure damage, conferring excellent adhesion ability (Fig. 6i).

Drug loading, drug release, and drug skin permeation study

Drug solubility tests were performed to analyze the potential of PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel as a drug carrier for LOX. The solubility of LOX was compared in the prepared DES solvent and water. LOX exhibits extremely low aqueous solubility due to its strong hydrophobicity. DES can improve the dissolution performance of LOX through hydrogen bonding interactions, whereas pure liquid DES possesses a low drug-loading ceiling and limited solubilization capacity. As shown in Fig. 7a-b, LOX exhibited insolubility in water, whereas DES facilitated the dissolution of 0.3% of LOX. Despite a significant increase in solubility induced by DES, it remained limited. The HP monomers adopted in this study is poorly water-soluble, but it shows excellent compatibility with DES. After the formation of eutectogel via Zr4+ coordination crosslinking, the DES and polymer network exert a prominent synergistic solubilization effect. Owing to an inherent hydrogen bond network and the hydrogen bonding competition effect, DES only forms a few weakly binding single hydrogen bonds with LOX, preventing stable drug dispersion. The PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel contained abundant polar active groups, such as carboxyl, alcohol hydroxyl, phenolic hydroxyl, and ester groups, that can preferentially displace DES and strongly interact with LOX, leading to the formation of a dense hydrogen-bonding network on the surface of the drug molecule. This interaction directly enhanced the affinity between the drug molecule and the acrylate polymer and effectively “blocked” LOX from forming dimers or higher-order aggregates via carboxyl groups, breaking the self-aggregation forces between LOX molecules (Kong et al., 2023; Ohyagi et al., 2017). Meanwhile, Zr4+ coordination crosslinking constructs a stable three-dimensional porous skeleton, which immobilizes liquid DES within the eutectogel pores and forms continuous and stable drug-solubilizing microenvironments, further maximizing the solubilization advantages of DES. The PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel had hydrophobic benzene rings, hydrophilic polar functional groups, and an alkyl chain structure, allowing the formation of an amphiphilic dispersion structure in DES, where the hydrophilic end connects the hydrogen bond network of DES, and the hydrophobic structure wraps the hydrophobic backbone of LOX, preventing local over-saturation and aggregation of the drug (Wang et al., 2026). The solubility of LOX in acrylate polymer was significantly improved under the combined influence of multiple hydrogen bonding and amphiphilic dispersion stability.

Fig. 7bii further verifies the water insolubility of the polymer monomers. DES not only significantly improves the solubility of hydrophobic LOX but also effectively dissolves the hydrophobic acrylate monomers for eutectogel fabrication. Both DES and acrylate polymer play a significant role in improving the solubility of LOX. DES showed higher solubility for acrylate monomers, expanding the range of polymer monomers available for eutectogel formulation and enhancing the potential for optimizing eutectogel performance. In summary, DES provides a suitable solvent environment to solve the poor water solubility of both drugs and monomers, while the polymer crosslinked network further strengthens drug dispersion and dissolution stability through multiple non-covalent interactions. The synergistic effect of the DES and acrylate polymer significantly improves the solubility and loading efficiency of LOX in eutectogels.

LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel patches were then prepared to investigate their drug delivery capabilities, skin barrier penetration, and release characteristics. The main routes of drug penetration through the skin for transdermal administration can be classified as the intercellular lipid pathway in the stratum corneum, the pathway across keratinocytes, and the pathway through skin appendages (Bouwstra and Ponec, 2006). The stratum corneum is the core barrier that hinders drug penetration, and it is also the rate-limiting layer for transdermal absorption. Small molecules with moderate lipid solubility (log P ranging from 1.0 to 3.0) can effectively penetrate this barrier and reach the deeper layers of the skin. LOX is a lipid-soluble small molecule with log P within this suitable range, possessing the molecular conditions for transdermal absorption. To evaluate the feasibility of PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel as a drug carrier, in vitro drug release and skin penetration tests were conducted using the SYT1 multifunctional transdermal diffusion apparatus and diffusion cells (Fig. 7c). Over 24 h, the cumulative release of LOX from the LOX@PAE-Zr4+ hydrogel patch was 1699.08 ± 67.12 μg/cm2, showing steady-state sustained release characteristics without a significant burst release. Fitting of the drug release curves for the hydrogels and eutectogels revealed that both conformed to a first-order model (Fig. 7d), suggesting that PAE—Zr4+ eutectogels are capable of providing sustained, stable, and non-spikey drug release behavior. Transdermal testing results demonstrated that the drug in LOX@PAE-Zr4+ effectively penetrated the stratum corneum at a significantly higher rate than the hydrogel matrix (Fig. 7e), confirming that the LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel patch significantly improves transdermal drug penetration efficiency, satisfying the needs for local anti-inflammatory and analgesic drug delivery. Further, the release behavior was quantitatively analyzed based on the cumulative drug release percentage. The results showed that the cumulative drug release percentage of the LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel reached 55.30 ± 3.18% within 24 h, which was higher than 52.15 ± 3.21% of the hydrogel (Fig. S8). At the same time, during the first 12 h of drug release and skin permeability, the drug release was slow, providing a certain controlled-release effect on the drug (Fig. S9). Overall, it effectively improved the release efficiency and transdermal efficiency of the drug. Both of these results jointly confirmed the advantages of the eutectogel system in transdermal drug delivery. DES can disrupt the ordered structure of the lipid bilayer in the skin's stratum corneum, weakening the skin's barrier resistance, and creating a stable transdermal penetration pathway for LOX. At the same time, the eutectogel has good skin adhesion, can closely adhere to the epidermis, eliminate interface gaps, and continuously maintain a high drug concentration gradient at the administration site, driving the drug to continuously diffuse into the skin interior. Moreover, the hydrogen bond interaction formed between the matrix and LOX can regulate the drug release rate, preventing a large amount of drug from being lost in a short period of time, and ultimately achieving a long-lasting and stable transdermal delivery effect.

The interaction between drug and eutectogel

The intermolecular interactions between LOX and the eutectogel were investigated using FT-IR spectroscopy. The FT-IR spectra of LOX, PAE—Zr4+, and LOX@PAE-Zr4+ are provided in Fig. 7f. LOX showed a characteristic absorption peak at 2966.95 cm−1 corresponding to the alkyl C—H stretching vibration and a distinct carbonyl stretching vibration peak at 1730.80 and 1698.02 cm−1, which correspond to the ester and carboxyl groups in its molecular structure, respectively (Rajamohan et al., 2024).

The LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel spectrum did not exhibit new characteristic absorption peaks; however, significant shifts were observed in the peak positions of key functional groups, suggesting that the chemical structure of the eutectogel remained intact during the drug-loading process. The blank eutectogel spectrum showed a substantial broadening of the peak shape and a shift from 3287.32 to 3311.04 cm−1, which directly demonstrates the formation of hydrogen-bonding interactions between LOX and the eutectogel. Furthermore, the characteristic carbonyl peak in LOX@PAE-Zr4+ shifted to 1721.44 cm−1, indicating evident variations from the 1730.80/1698.02 cm−1 of pure LOX and the 1723.76 cm−1 of the PAE—Zr4+ eutectogel, further verifying the presence of strong intermolecular interactions between the drug molecules and the eutectogel matrix. These results confirmed the successful loading of LOX into the eutectogel system, with both components interacting at the molecular level through non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, supporting the enhanced solubility and sustained-release characteristics of the drug-loaded eutectogel.

LOX was docked with the binary polymer (HP-co-HEA) and the ternary polymer (HP-co-AA-co-HEA) molecules, respectively (Fig. 7g–h). The ternary polymer established two hydrogen bonds with LOX, with corresponding hydrogen-bond distances of 2.342 and 3.340 Å, respectively, whereas the binary polymer formed only a single hydrogen bond with the LOX, with a hydrogen-bond distance of 2.375 Å. The LOX and the binary polymer carrier had a binding energy of −0.976 kcal/mol, while the ternary polymer exhibited a binding energy of −1.171 kcal/mol. The ternary polymer showed lower binding energy and established double hydrogen-bonding interactions with the drug, suggesting the formation of a more stable ternary polymer-drug complex, improving drug loading and controlled release capabilities, which is better suited for controlling drug release and transdermal behavior in TDDS. Incorporation of AA into the ternary polymer introduced highly polar carboxyl groups capable of forming double hydrogen bonding with the carboxyl groups in LOX, which may be essential for attaining controlled release and stable drug loading.

The FT-IR results confirmed that there is a stable hydrogen bond interaction between the LOX molecule and the eutectogel matrix, which can fix the drug in the eutectogel network and effectively inhibit the burst release behavior of the drug. The molecular docking results verified that LOX can form a stable conformation with the eutectogel base with low binding energy. The uniform dispersion characteristics of the drug and the stable carrier-drug interaction make the drug release rate mainly depend on the slow diffusion of the molecule in the eutectogel network, presenting the typical first-order release kinetics characteristic, which is consistent with the drug release results.

Efficacy of NSAID treatment with eutectogel patches in the AIA animal model

An AIA model was developed and subjected to different intervention treatments to investigate the therapeutic effects of LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel patches on local arthritis (Fig. 8a). At the end of the experiment, the paw thickness and ankle joint diameter in the treatment group were nearly identical to those in the control group and substantially smaller than those in the model group (Fig. S10). Compared to the AIA group, rats in the LOX@PAE-Zr4+ eutectogel group showed noticeably less swelling in the ankle joints and paw pads, along with relief from joint redness and swelling (Fig. 8b–c). Furthermore, the pathological changes of the joints were investigated using histopathological staining (Fig. 8d). At the same time, a histopathological scoring system was used to conduct semi-quantitative assessment of the joint tissue damage (Fig. S11) (Yang et al., 2024; He et al., 2022; Qaiser et al., 2026). The results of H&E staining indicated that eutectogel treatment reduced tissue damage and inflammatory infiltration while greatly improving the pathological morphology of the joints. Safranin O-fast green staining and toluidine blue staining confirmed cartilage-protective effect, and TRAP staining further verified the inhibition of bone destruction, indicating that the drug delivery system could exert distinct anti-inflammatory, anti-edematous, chondroprotective, and osteoprotective effects via the stable transdermal release of LOX. Despite the satisfactory drug loading, adhesion performance, and sustained transdermal release behavior of the optimized eutectogel, several limitations still exist in this study. First, the current work only adopted LOX as a single model drug, and the universality of this eutectogel platform for different therapeutic agents requires further verification. Second, although the eutectogel exhibits favorable transdermal delivery capability, the skin penetration efficiency can be further improved through the rational introduction and optimization of penetration enhancers. Third, only a fixed DES system with a specific ratio was investigated in this study; more systematic exploration of different DES types and component ratios is still needed to clarify the structure–activity relationship. Conclusion

In this study, a novel eutectogel was successfully developed employing DES and poly(acrylic acid-co-4-hydroxyphenyl methacrylate-co-2-hydroxyethyl acrylate) as the matrix, with Zr4+ as the cross-linking agent. The structure and properties of the eutectogel were thoroughly examined, and its potential for transdermal delivery in treating arthritis was investigated. The eutectogel showed excellent synergistic properties in terms of environmental stability and skin adherence, successfully tackling the fundamental issues of hydrogels, such as low drug-loading capacity and poor environmental stability. This study provides an innovative, safe, and highly effective novel carrier for the transdermal delivery of drugs for arthritis, providing encouraging prospects for clinical application.
