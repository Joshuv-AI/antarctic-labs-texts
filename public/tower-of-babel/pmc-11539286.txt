Brainstem hemorrhage associated with venous hypertensive myelopathy without dural arteriovenous fistula: illustrative case
Authors: Sho Hanai, Kiyoyuki Yanaka, Ken Akimoto, Aiki Marushima, Kazuhiro Nakamura, Nobuyuki Takahashi, Yuji Matsumaru, Eiichi Ishikawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDVenous hypertensive myelopathy (VHM), formerly known as “Foix-Alajouanine syndrome,” is a spinal cord dysfunction caused by impaired perfusion of the spinal cord. Most cases are attributed to spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs), but there are scattered reports of VHM without concomitant dAVF. Furthermore, no cases of VHM associated with intracranial hemorrhage exist.OBSERVATIONSA 68-year-old man with a history of hypertension presented with a sudden headache, proximal paresis of the left upper extremity, impaired pain and temperature sensation in the right upper extremity, dysphagia, and dysarthria. Computed tomography scans showed intraparenchymal hemorrhage in the left medulla oblongata and a linear, continuous high-density area extending from the medulla oblongata to the cervical spinal cord. Magnetic resonance images showed cervical spondylosis at the C5–6 and C6–7 levels, with high signal intensity changes from the medulla oblongata to the lower cervical cord on T2-weighted images. Cerebral angiography showed no abnormal vessels. Conservative treatment gradually improved symptoms and the high signal intensity areas.LESSONSThis case highlights intracranial hemorrhage occurring from extracranial causes and the possibility of VHM due to cervical spondylosis. When hemorrhagic lesions of the craniovertebral junction or spinal parenchymal lesions are encountered, the underlying pathology should be investigated thoroughly and systematically. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24441

Intracerebral hemorrhage can be caused by hypertension, amyloid angiopathy, antithrombotic treatment, cavernous hemangioma, and arteriovenous malformation.1 Most hemorrhages are arterial in origin, although hemorrhages due to venous congestion from dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) have also been reported.2 In such cases, subarachnoid hemorrhages are common, with a few causing intraparenchymal hemorrhages.2

Venous hypertensive myelopathy (VHM) or venous congestive myelopathy, formerly known as “Foix-Alajouanine syndrome,” is a spinal cord dysfunction caused by spinal cord venous perfusion disorder.3–5 VHM is a condition in which increased venous pressure due to spinal venous congestion causes edema in the spinal cord, resulting in reduced spinal perfusion and ischemic damage.3–5 Symptoms of VHM often progress relatively rapidly and include motor paralysis, sensory disturbance, defecation problems, and sexual dysfunction.6 Most cases of VHM are caused by spinal dAVF, while other etiologies are rare.3–8 Herein, we report a case of brainstem hemorrhage that showed a high signal intensity area on T2-weighted magnetic resonance (MR) images of the spinal cord without dAVF.

Illustrative Case

A 68-year-old hypertensive man presented to our hospital with a sudden headache and paresis in the left upper extremity. On admission, the patient was awake but had proximal paresis in the left upper extremity, impaired temperature and pain perception in the right upper extremity, dysphagia, and dysarthria. Blood pressure was elevated at 251/126 mm Hg. The results of laboratory tests were largely within the normal ranges, including those for coagulation tests (prothrombin time, 11.5 seconds; activated partial thromboplastin time, 23.5 seconds; d-dimer, 0.5 μg/dl).

Computed tomography (CT) scans and MR images showed hemorrhage on the left side of the medulla oblongata but revealed no subarachnoid hemorrhage (Fig. 1). MR angiograms showed no abnormal vessels. However, the upper motor paresis was confined to the left extremity, and the brainstem hemorrhage did not appear to involve the pyramidal tract. Therefore, considering the possibility of a cervical lesion, cervical MR imaging (MRI) was performed, which showed a high-intensity area extending from the medulla oblongata to the C6 level on T2-weighted images. In addition, a cervical deformity was observed at the C5–6 and C6–7 levels, and the spinal cord appeared to be mildly compressed (Fig. 2A–C). Cervical MR images revealed a high-intensity area corresponding to the high-density region on CT. This area appeared linear and continuous, extending from the medulla oblongata to the cervical spinal cord, suggesting a thrombosed spinal vein (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, venous congestion due to venous thrombosis of the spinal cord was suspected. Surgical decompression was initially considered, but venous congestion and acute inflammatory processes, such as transverse myelitis and demyelinating diseases, had to be considered rather than cervical spondylotic myelopathy. Hence, the patient underwent conservative management and close observation, including rehabilitation. On the 3rd day of onset, the patient complained of mild dysuria, and cervical MRI showed an enlarged high-intensity area on T2-weighted images (Fig. 2D). However, the symptoms gradually began to improve, and by the 10th day after onset, the high-intensity area had reduced (Fig. 2E). The patient was discharged from the hospital on the 29th day of onset with slight muscle weakness in the left upper extremity. Before discharge, an MRI examination of the whole spine was performed considering the presence of a dAVF at a remote site, but no significant findings were obtained. About 3 years after discharge, the symptoms had disappeared almost completely, and no recurrence was observed on the MR images.

Discussion

Observations

We reported a case of brainstem hemorrhage associated with VHM without dAVF. The patient presented with very high blood pressure upon admission, which does not completely rule out hypertensive brainstem hemorrhage. However, hypertensive hemorrhage in the medulla oblongata is relatively rare. Therefore, further investigation for other causes, such as microaneurysms or vascular malformations,9, 10 revealed a rare association with VHM. Furthermore, this is the first case in which symptoms improved with conservative treatment.

In cases of subarachnoid hemorrhage, dAVF has often been diagnosed, but with recent advances in diagnostic imaging, more cases with symptoms due to venous congestion are being diagnosed. In dAVF, the artery and vein are directly connected, and arterial pressure is applied directly to the vein, resulting in vessel rupture and hemorrhage. On the other hand, symptoms due to venous congestion can appear even when hemorrhage does not occur. Increased venous pressure due to the direct connection between arteries and veins causes intraparenchymal arteries to dilate in order to maintain tissue perfusion pressure. These changes also lead to progressive vascular reactivity and autoregulation loss, leading to increased tissue perfusion pressure. Eventually, intraparenchymal edema develops, leading to hypoperfusion pressure and ischemic injury.8 Most dAVFs in the spinal cord and craniovertebral junction develop with congestive symptoms or subarachnoid hemorrhage, but reports of intramedullary hemorrhage are rare.2, 11, 12 Reports show that varices in the draining venous tract could be involved in intraparenchymal hemorrhage.11, 12 However, reports also show that venous congestion could be accentuated and that hemorrhage could occur when there are few connections in the draining venous tract, even in the absence of varices.2 In our case, there was no dAVF and no varix; however, it is possible that venous congestion was exacerbated by insufficient development of the draining venous tract, leading to hemorrhage, as in the latter theory.

There are few reports of VHM without dAVF or other vascular malformations.4, 8, 13 Matsuo et al. reported autopsy cases of VHM due to a spinal intramedullary tumor and multiple sclerosis.13 Auler et al. also reported a case of posttraumatic mediastinal hematoma compressing the brachiocephalic vein and causing VHM.4 However, there are only a few cases in which a cause can be speculated upon, and in most cases, occult dAVF is assumed to be the underlying etiology. Matsubara et al. summarized 15 previously reported cases of VHM without spinal dAVF.8 These authors concluded that it was more likely that dAVF was not discovered; rather, it was genuinely absent. It was hypothesized that possible reasons for failure to detect dAVF included insufficient examination, the dAVF being located far away from the site of spinal cord intensity change, and the dAVF not being visualized due to thrombosis of the draining vein.14 It is possible that in our case, for reasons similar to those mentioned above, the presence of the dAVF was not detected. On the other hand, the occurrence of VHM in patients with cervical disc herniation and cervical spondylosis has been reported.3, 5, 6, 13, 15, 16 The lack of valves in the internal vertebral venous plexus and its susceptibility to pressure-induced regurgitation and stasis can also contribute to the occurrence of VHM.5, 17

Eight cases of VHM associated with degenerative spinal diseases, including ours, have been reported and are summarized in Table 1.3, 5, 6, 13, 15, 16, 18 The condition is more common in men and occurs after middle age. Unlike Foix-Alajouanine syndrome, which is more commonly located below the thoracic spinal cord, it can occur in the cervical spinal cord. In some cases, as in our case, there are no preceding symptoms, and the symptoms appear suddenly. On the other hand, there are cases in which VHM is added to a list of long-standing mild spinal symptoms and suddenly worsens. This point seems to differ from VHM caused by dAVF. The hyperintense areas on T2-weighted MR images tend to expand around the spinal deformity and disappear quickly with surgical decompression. In the present case, cervical spondylosis and mild spinal cord compression were seen at the C5–6 and C6–7 levels. In addition, a continuous linear hyperintense structure was identified on the left lateral anterior aspect of the spinal cord, which appeared to be a thrombosed anterolateral vein. In this case, the improvement of symptoms without decompression surgery might have occurred either because the thrombosed vein was not the main trunk of the spinal drainage system or because the thrombosis could have opened another drainage pathway that had not been functioning previously. A review showed that even cases with vascular malformations, such as dAVF at the craniovertebral junction, can be managed conservatively.19 Therefore, conservative treatment might be a viable option in cases similar to ours. However, individual differences in spinal cord draining system development could result in missed opportunities for recovery in individuals whose ancillary pathways are underdeveloped. Unlike in our case, surgical decompression was performed in the other seven cases, and T2 high-intensity areas in the spinal cord were immediately improved. Therefore, if conservative treatment is selected, the progression of symptoms and changes on MR images might need to be carefully monitored. Lessons

To our knowledge, this is the first case of brainstem hemorrhage caused by VHM without dAVF. The VHM caused by cervical spondylosis appeared to have affected the perfusion of the brainstem, leading to hemorrhage. When encountering such a case, a detailed neurological assessment and a thorough imaging evaluation are essential. In addition, further case studies are needed to elucidate the pathophysiology of this condition.
