Beyond Discreteness: Sample Complexity Analysis of Straight-Through Estimator for 1-bit Quantization
Halyun Jeong Jack Xin Penghang Yin

Abstract
Training quantized neural networks requires addressing the non-differentiable and discrete nature of the underlying optimization problem. To tackle this challenge, the straight-through estimator (STE) has become the most widely adopted heuristic, allowing backpropagation through discrete operations by introducing biased yet valid surrogate gradients. However, its theoretical properties remain largely unexplored, with few existing analyses focus on the generalization error by assuming an infinite amount of training data. In contrast, this work presents the first sample complexity analysis of STE in the context of neural network quantization. Our theoretical results highlight the critical role of sample size in the success of STE, a key insight absent from existing studies. Specifically, by analyzing the quantization-aware training of a two-layer neural network with binary weights and activations, we derive the sample complexity bounds in terms of the data dimensionality that guarantee the convergence of STE-based optimization to the global minimum for both ergodic and non-ergodic analyses. Moreover, in the presence of label noises, we prove an intriguing recurrence property of STE-gradient method, where the iterate repeatedly escape from and return to the optimal binary weights. Finally, we empirically demonstrate that STE fails for general non-Gaussian data but its effectiveness can be restored through normalization, underscoring its practical importance in effective quantization.

quantization, straight-through estimator, 1-bit compressed sensing

1.1
Contributions

In this paper, we present the theoretical analysis of the straight-through estimator (STE) for quantization-aware training, addressing an optimization problem that is discrete in both its objective and constraint. Specifically, our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We provide the first analysis of the sample complexity that guarantees the effectiveness of STE. For training a two-layer binarized neural net with random Gaussian inputs, we show that

O(n^{2})
samples suffice for convergence of the ergodic (averaged) iterates to the optimal solution, while
O(n^{4})
samples are sufficient for the non-ergodic (last iterate) convergence, where
n
is the dimension of data.
Moreover, we empirically validate that our
O(n^{2})
bound for the ergodic convergence case is tight.

•

For the non-ergodic convergence analysis, we discover a surprising recurrence behavior of the STE gradient algorithm in the presence of label noise: the iterates repeatedly hit exactly and escape from the optimal binary weights. This stands in contrast to classical problems like linear regression and compressed sensing, where exact recovery of the underlying parameters is typically impossible under observation noise.

•

We empirically show that convergence guarantees fail to hold for general sub-Gaussian distributions. Nevertheless, we demonstrate that applying a normalization procedure can improve the performance of STE when the data deviate from normality, underscoring the practical importance of normalization in the effective use of STE.

•

To support the analysis, we leverage techniques from the context of 1-bit compressed sensing
[
15
]
, revealing important connections between these two domains. Additionally, we introduce a novel application of occupation time analysis, a tool traditionally used in dynamical systems, to rigorously analyze the drift dynamics of the iterates.

Background and Related Work

Straight-Through Estimator.

In the classical sense, STE is simply an identity mapping used in the backward pass to replace the a.e. zero derivative of the Heaviside step function
\theta(x):=\mathds{1}_{\{x\geq 0\}}
. Specifically, this approach treats
\theta^{\prime}(x)
as 1, backpropagating as if

\theta(x)
were the identity function
[
18
]
.
This original idea can be traced back to the celebrated perceptron algorithm
[
45
,
46
]
for training single-layer perceptrons, and is now commonly referred to as the identity STE. More generally, for a composite function
l\circ\theta(\boldsymbol{x})
, where
l:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and
\theta
is the Heaviside function applied element-wise to the input
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by overloading the notation "
\approx
", the identity STE modifies the chain-rule to evaluate

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\,(l\circ\theta)(\boldsymbol{x})=\theta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x})\odot\nabla\,l(\theta(\boldsymbol{x}))\approx\nabla\,l(\theta(\boldsymbol{x})),

where
\nabla\,l(\theta(\boldsymbol{x}))
denotes the gradient of
l
evaluated at
\theta(\boldsymbol{x})
. Later, variants of the STE heuristic for replacing
\theta^{\prime}
have been proposed, including the derivative of sigmoid function
[
5
,
19
]
. In an orthogonal direction, the expressiveness and approximation power of neural nets with binary weights or activations were investigated in
[
16
,
47
,
48
,
53
]
. More recently, the use of STE has been extended to facilitate backpropagation through multi-bit quantized (multi-step) functions in the quantization of deep neural networks, where more sophisticated STEs such as the derivatives of ReLU, log-tailed ReLU, and clipped ReLU have been adopted
[
7
,
10
,
20
,
25
,
62
]
.

Theoretical underpinnings of STE were initially developed in the context of weight-only quantization, focusing on minimizing general objective functions with quantized parameters using the identity STE
[
29
,
61
]
. A key advancement in understanding why STE works was made in
[
60
]
, which analyzed the expected neural network loss over Gaussian inputs with binary activations, enabling a fine-grained study of more sophisticated STEs beyond the identity STE. Notably,
[
31
,
60
]
also demonstrated that effective STE choices for activation quantization are typically not unique. However, all prior works either consider general loss functions
[
1
,
24
,
29
,
30
,
61
]
or population network losses
[
31
,
32
,
56
,
60
]
, in contrast to the finite-sample setting addressed in this work.

1-bit Compressed Sensing.

The 1-bit compressed sensing problem was first introduced by
[
6
]
. The objective is to recover an
s
-sparse signal
\boldsymbol{x}
from its 1-bit measurements, given by
\boldsymbol{b}=\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x})
, which are the signs of the linear measurements, where
\boldsymbol{A}
is typically a random matrix such as Gaussian. While 1-bit compressed sensing offers a practical approach to discretizing measurements for efficient sparse recovery, due to this discontinuous nature, it has been posing several interesting challenges.
Numerous approaches have been proposed to address the 1-bit compressed sensing problem, including linear programming methods
[
41
]
, convex relaxations
[
40
]
, and the generalized Lasso
[
42
]
, among others. However, these methods provide theoretical guarantees with only sub-optimal reconstruction error at most
O(\sqrt{s/N})
, where
N
is the number of samples.
[
22
]
proposed Normalized Binary Iterative Hard Thresholding (NBIHT), and it has been known for its strong empirical performance for 1-bit compressed sensing. Despite the analytical difficulties arising from the discrete and non-smooth loss induced by quantization, recent breakthroughs have established NBIHT’s near-optimal sample complexity.
[
15
]
introduced Restricted Approximate Invertibility Condition (RAIC), resembling the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) for conventional compressed sensing
[
4
,
8
,
13
,
14
]
. Subsequent works
[
9
,
34
]
have further affirmed its optimality concerning the sparsity level
s
, thereby showing the optimal reconstruction error of
O(s\log N/N)
, matching the lower bound of reconstruction error found in
[
22
]
.

Dynamical Systems and Ergodic Theory.

The argument we used to establish our ergodic theorem, which is based on occupation time analysis, shares a similar flavor with Kac’s Lemma or Kakutani’s skyscraper argument
[
11
,
39
]
in Ergodic Theory.
However, since our setting lacks the formal measure-theoretic structure typically assumed in ergodic theory (e.g., invariant measures or measure-preserving transformations), our analysis was developed independently. Nonetheless, its emphasis on recurrence through occupation time is reminiscent of the spirit of ergodic-theoretic arguments.

Given a sample

\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

y(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{Z}):=\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}\theta(\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}.

Here,

denotes the binary (Heaviside) activation function, and
\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{\top}
represents the
j

can be interpreted as an image patch in computer vision (CV) tasks or as a token embedding in natural language processing (NLP) applications. Accordingly,

Hence, the loss remains differentiable w.r.t. the weights of the second layer, thus not requiring the use of STE for its training. Since our work aims to analyze the convergence behavior of the STE, the quantization of the second layer is of independent interest.

(Input Data)

The row vectors of input data,

y_{\boldsymbol{Z}}:=y(\boldsymbol{w}^{*};\boldsymbol{Z})+\xi=\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w}^{*})+\xi,

(1)

which is possibly corrupted with unknown additive noise

Assumption 2

(Label Noise)
.

The label noise
\xi

K_{\xi}
.
In particular, for mean-zero Gaussian noise with variance

, we have
K_{\xi}\approx\sigma
.

Moreover, we consider the following squared loss function:

\displaystyle l(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{Z}):=\frac{1}{2}\left(y(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{Z})-y_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w})-y_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\right)^{2}.

Then given the labeled dataset

\min_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w})-y_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}}\right)^{2}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}.

(2)

Note that the above optimization problem is
discrete, both in its objective and constraint
. We define the weight quantizer
\mathcal{Q}
, which is the projection of
\boldsymbol{w}
onto
\mathbb{Q}_{1}=\left\{\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right\}^{n}

\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{w}):=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbb{Q}_{1}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol{w}).

(3)

3.1
STE-Gradient Method

Since the activation function
\theta
is piece-wise constant, the sample loss
l
has a vanished gradient:

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}l=\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}(\theta^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w})\odot\boldsymbol{v})\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w})-y_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\right)=\mathbf{0}\quad\mbox{a.e.},

which renders the standard backpropagation inapplicable.
To circumvent this issue, we consider a surrogate gradient obtained by replacing
\theta^{\prime}
with the derivative of ReLU
\mu(x):=\max\{x,0\}
, referred to as ReLU STE:

\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}l=\boldsymbol{Z}^{\top}(\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w})\odot\boldsymbol{v})\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{w})-y_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\right),

(4)

where
\mu^{\prime}(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x\geq 0\}}
. ReLU STE was proved effective for activation quantization of shallow neural nets
[
60
]
such as LeNet-5
[
28
]
.

Then we have

\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}l_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)})^{\top}(\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w})\odot\boldsymbol{v})\left(\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w})-y_{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}}\right).

(5)

In this paper, we study the most widely used quantization-aware training (QAT) algorithm
[
7
,
20
,
62
]
, which we refer to as STE-gradient method. It updates the weights using STE-induced surrogate gradient while keeping them quantized:

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{x}^{t}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})\\
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}=\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}).\end{cases}

(6)

The STE-gradient method (
6

\boldsymbol{w}^{t}=\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1}))
,
but features two key modifications that make it particularly effective for the discrete optimization problem (
2

\theta

\boldsymbol{x}
and the (STE-)gradient evaluated at
\boldsymbol{w}

allowing informative gradients to aggregate over time at small step size
\eta_{t}
without being offset by the discretization
\mathcal{Q}

Main Results

We present the first finite-sample analysis of STE for

quantization-aware training. Our main results provide global convergence guarantees for Algorithm
1
, assuming a sufficiently large sample size
N
. Specifically, we show that
O(n^{2})
samples are sufficient to ensure ergodic convergence to
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, while
O(n^{4})
samples suffice for non-ergodic convergence, where the iterates
\{\boldsymbol{w}^{t}\}
visit
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often.

4.1
Ergodic Convergence

We demonstrate that the ergodic average of the iterates of the STE-gradient method,
\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}^{t}
, converges to
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
up to an error floor that depends only on the ratio
n/N
.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
,

when the sample size

N\gtrsim\left(\tfrac{(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})n}{\,\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}\right)^{2},

for some universal constants
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
, then with high probability,
the ergodic average of iterates
\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}
generated by Algorithm
1
satisfies for a sufficiently large
T
that

\left\|\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\lesssim O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}T}\right).

Moreover, the exact recovery can be achieved by

\mathcal{Q}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\right)=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}

for sufficiently large
T
.

The sample complexity bound depends on
K_{\xi}
, the sub-Gaussian norm of noise
\xi
; heavier noise requires more samples to guarantee convergence.
Moreover, it also depends on
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}/\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}
, which can be interpreted as the effective sparsity of
\boldsymbol{v}

[
59
]

In low-noise regime where
K_{\xi}\ll\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}
, the effective sparsity primarily determines the sample complexity

. In high-noise regime where
K_{\xi}\gg\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}
, the noise level
K_{\xi}
becomes the dominant factor. This trade-off similarly applies to the non-ergodic convergence case.

Theorem 2

(Informal)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3

To understand the instability behavior of iterates

\boldsymbol{x}^{t}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1})).

When the optimal solution is achieved at
\boldsymbol{w}^{T}=\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}^{T})=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
for some
T
, the corresponding STE-gradient with
\mu^{\prime}(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x\geq 0\}}
is given by

\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}^{T}))=\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)})^{\top}(\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w}^{*})\odot\boldsymbol{v})\xi^{(i)}

is nonzero with probability
1-2^{-\|v\|_{0}N}
under the continuous nonzero-noise assumptions, where
\|v\|_{0}:=|\operatorname{supp}(\boldsymbol{v})|
. Unless
\mathrm{sign}(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})=\mathrm{sign}(-\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*}))
,
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{t}\}_{t>T}
will move in a fixed direction until
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}=Q(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\neq\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
for some
t>T
. Subsequently,
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}
will repeatedly revisit and escape from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. Similar oscillation behavior was also observed empirically in ImageNet classification towards the end of quantization-aware training using STE
[
37
]
.

However, we emphasize that this divergence behavior is
beneficial
, as it helps prevent the iterates from stalling. In contrast, even with the STE-gradient
\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L
, any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
becomes a stationary point of the following PGD-like iteration under a small
\eta_{t}>0
:

\boldsymbol{w}^{t}=\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})),

since
\mathcal{Q}
is a discrete operator. This will lead to stagnation and thus poor empirical performance.

5
Proof Ideas

This section outlines the proofs of Theorems
1
and
2
. The first ingredient is a finite-sample concentration estimate showing that the STE-gradient is uniformly close to a linear drift toward the planted binary vector
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. The second ingredient is a coordinate-wise occupation-time analysis for the latent dynamics generated by Algorithm
1
.

5.1
\ell_{\infty}
Concentration Bound for the STE-Gradient

We first record the sample-size condition used throughout the concentration argument.

Assumption 4
.

There exists some sufficiently large universal constant
C_{1}>0
such that
N\geq{C_{1}n^{2}}
.

The following theorem is the finite-sample analogue of the population STE alignment property. It shows that, uniformly over the quantized parameter space
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, the empirical STE-gradient behaves like the linear map

\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}),\qquad\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi},

up to an
\ell_{\infty}
perturbation of order
\sqrt{n/N}
. This is the restricted approximate invertibility principle that drives the convergence analysis.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Let
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then, there exist positive universal constants
C_{1},C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime},c
such that with probability at least
1-5m\exp(-cn)
,

\displaystyle\left\|{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}\leq{1\over\tau}(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

To see the mechanism behind Theorem
3
, we decompose the STE-gradient into a noiseless term and a noise term:

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w})=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)})^{\top}(\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w})\odot\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\left(\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w})-\theta(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{w}^{*})\right)}_{\text{noiseless term}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)})^{\top}\Bigl[\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\,\boldsymbol{w})\odot\boldsymbol{v}\Bigr]\,\xi^{(i)}}_{\text{noise term}}.

The signal is carried by the diagonal part of the noiseless term, where the same row
\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}
appears both in the STE factor and in the activation mismatch. The off-diagonal part is a cross term between different rows and is controlled separately. Using
\mu^{\prime}(x)=\theta(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x>0\}}
, we write

\displaystyle\mathrm{noiseless\;term}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{2}\,\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}\Bigr)\;\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}\Bigr)}_{\text{squared term}}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k=1\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{m}v_{j}\,v_{k}\,\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}\Bigr)\;\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}\Bigr)}_{\text{cross term}}.

Among these three pieces, the squared term is the informative one: it is the part that produces the linear drift toward
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. The next single-row estimate isolates this mechanism and shows why the STE-gradient behaves like a restricted approximate inverse on the quantized set
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

For the squared term, define

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}\Bigr)\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}\Bigr)\;,

so that the squared term is given by
\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{2}\,\widetilde{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{w})
. To make the notation light, define

D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\;:=\;\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}

and

\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}.

Theorem 4
.

Under the same conditions in Theorem
3
, there exist some positive universal constants
c_{7}
and
C_{4}
such that, with probability at least
1-3\exp(-c_{7}n)
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\tau\cdot\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

The following corollary of Theorem
4
gives the concentration bound of the squared term:

Corollary 1
.

Under the same conditions in Theorem
3
, there exist some positive universal constants
c_{7}
and
C_{4}
such that, with probability at least
1-3m\exp(-c_{7}n)
,

\displaystyle\left\|\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tau\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{2}\,\widetilde{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}\leq C_{4}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

Proof of Corollary
1
.

By the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\left\|\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tau\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{2}\,\widetilde{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{2}\left\|(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tau\widetilde{g}^{(j)}(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}^{2}C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}=C_{4}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}},

where the second inequality is by applying Theorem
4
to each
\widetilde{g}^{(j)}
for
j\in[m]
and by the union bound of the probabilities.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Our proof strategy for Theorem
4
adapts techniques from the NBIHT analysis for 1-bit compressed sensing
[
9
,
15
,
34
]
.
In particular, for
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{\pm\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
, we consider the following orthogonal decomposition of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}
:

\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}+\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}+\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}),

where
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. Note that
\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
are also orthogonal since
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|=1
.

Using this three-component decomposition of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}
, we then establish the bound as follows:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\tau\cdot\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\Bigr)\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\left\langle\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\left\langle\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|-{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\Bigr)\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\tau\sqrt{n\over N}+C_{3}^{\prime}\tau\sqrt{n\over N}+C_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}\,\tau\;\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

where we have applied Lemmas
1
,
2
, and
3
followed by the triangle inequality.
The common idea behind these three lemmas is to fix

\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, identify the population mean of each projected component,
and then apply sub-Gaussian concentration followed by a union bound over the
finite quantized set
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
. The first projected component
recovers the population drift in the
\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
direction, with magnitude of
order
\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|
. The second and
residual components are centered, by the orthogonality of

\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
since
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|=1
, and by projection onto the orthogonal complement.
Consequently, the deviations of all three components from their population
contributions are controlled uniformly, for the relevant

\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{\pm\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
, at order
O(\sqrt{n/N})
. For more details, see the proofs of Lemmas
1
,
2
, and
3
in the Appendix.

It remains to combine the success probabilities of the three estimates.

The success probability follows from the union bound by taking the minimum of the positive constants
c_{2},c_{2}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}
in the lemmas such that

1-\exp(-c_{2}n)-\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime}n)-\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}n)\geq 1-3\exp(-c_{7}n)
, where
c_{7}
is the minimum.

For the case
\boldsymbol{w}=-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, the same argument holds but with a simpler decomposition of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}=\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{w}),

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{w})
is orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}=-2\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. Applying the Lemmas
1
and
3
as before yields

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\tau\cdot\widetilde{g}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|_{2}-{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{b}_{i}}(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\tau\sqrt{n\over N}+C_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}\,\tau\;\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

In the case when
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, the bound in Theorem
4
holds trivially.

∎

The remaining pieces are centered. The cross term is controlled by independence between different Gaussian rows, and the noise term is controlled by the sub-Gaussian noise assumption. Their estimates are stated and proved in Appendix
A.2
and Appendix
A.3
. Combining these estimates with Corollary
1
gives the full STE-gradient concentration theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Finally, combining the bound for the squared term part in Corollary

and the cross term part in Lemma
4
, and the noisy part in Lemma
5
and applying the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tau\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}+C_{3}\,\tau\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}+C_{8}\tau K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

\displaystyle\leq(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})\sqrt{{n\over N}}\quad\small({\text{since $m\lesssim O(2^{n})$ by Assumption\penalty\ \ref{assump:data} and $\|\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}$}})\normalsize

for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
. Dividing both sides by
\tau
yields the bound in Theorem

. The success probability follows by taking the minimum of the positive constants
c_{7},c_{5},c_{6}
in the lemmas such that

1-3m\exp(-c_{7}n)-\exp(-c_{5}n)-\exp(-c_{6}n)\geq 1-5m\exp(-cn)
, where
c
is the minimum.
∎

5.2
Finite-Sample Convergence Analysis

Following Theorem
3
, the first step of the STE-gradient method can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\,{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})+\eta_{t}\left({\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}+\underbrace{\eta_{t}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})}_{\text{drift term}}+\underbrace{\eta_{t}O\left(\sqrt{n\over N}\right)}_{\text{perturbation term}},

(8)

The key to understanding the convergence behavior, both in the ergodic and non-ergodic cases, lies in analyzing the component-wise dynamics governed by (
8
), as outlined in the remainder of this section.

5.2.1
Ergodic Convergence Analysis

We first outline the proof of Theorem
1
and then give the representative constant-step-size argument in detail. This inclusion is meant to make the occupation-time mechanism explicit; the extension to general step sizes and nonzero initialization is deferred to Appendix
D
.

Drift dominates the perturbation when
N
is large.

An important observation is that since
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}=\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})
, it holds that
\mathrm{sign}(w_{j}^{t})=\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})
,
\forall\;j\in[n]
. Whenever

\mathrm{sign}(w_{j}^{t-1})=\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t-1})\neq\mathrm{sign}(w_{j}^{*})
,
the drift term actively steers the dynamics of
\{x_{j}^{t}\}
towards the desired sign,
\mathrm{sign}(w_{j}^{*})
. Note that each component of the drift term has the scale of
O(1/\sqrt{n})
.
This drift term dominates the perturbation term when the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio, denoted by
\rho=O(n/\sqrt{N})
, is sufficiently small. Crucially,
\rho
is governed by the concentration bound in Theorem
3
and scales inversely with
\sqrt{N}
, ensuring that the drift prevails over the perturbation when the sample size
N
is large enough.

Occupation time analysis.

Moreover, when the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio
\rho
is sufficiently small, each coordinate
x_{j}^{t}
exhibits a pattern of brief excursions into the incorrect sign region, followed by longer phases in the sign region of
w_{j}^{*}
. Crucially, both
w_{j}^{t}
and
x_{j}^{t}
spend proportionally more time in the correct sign region, which approximately a factor of
1/\rho
longer than in the incorrect one. As a result, the average of
w_{j}^{t}
over time
t
converges to
w_{j}^{*}
, up to an error of
O(\rho/\sqrt{n})=O(\sqrt{n/N})
. Therefore,
\|\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|_{\infty}
also has an error floor of
O(\sqrt{n/N})
. This is the mechanism formalized next in the constant-step-size setting and extended to the general step-size case in Appendix
D
.

We now make the above mechanism precise in the representative constant-step-size setting. The purpose of including this argument here is to show the core idea behind the ergodic analysis: the concentration theorem converts the STE-gradient dynamics into a deterministic drift system with a uniformly bounded perturbation; once the drift is stronger than the perturbation, incorrect-sign excursions become both short and infrequent. The full general-step-size result is obtained later in Appendix
D
by extending this same occupation-time argument beyond constant step sizes and zero initialization.

Theorem 5

(Non-Asymptotic Ergodic Convergence)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Assume the sample size
N
is large enough such

N\geq\max\left(C_{1}n^{2},\left(\frac{5n(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)

to ensure that the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio
\rho:=\frac{n(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{N}}<1/5
for some positive universal constants
C_{1},C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
.
Then, with probability at least
1-5m\exp(-cn)
for some positive universal constant
c>0
, the following statements hold:

For a constant step size
\eta_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}=0
, the ergodic average satisfies for a sufficiently large
T
:

\left\|\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+O\left(\frac{1}{T\sqrt{n}}\right).

Moreover, if the number of iterations
T
is large enough such that
T>\frac{4(1-\rho)}{1-3\rho}

the exact recovery is possible, by quantizing the ergodic average, i.e.,

\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T})=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}

Remark 1
.

Note that the bound in Theorem
5
for the ergodic average implies the bound

\left\|\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+O\left(\frac{1}{T\sqrt{n}}\right)=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}T}\right),

in Theorem
1
(the informal version) because
\rho=O\left({n\over\sqrt{N}}\right)
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

The first step of the STE-gradient method can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}+\underbrace{\eta_{t}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})}_{\text{drift term}}+\underbrace{\eta_{t}\left({\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})-\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{t-1})\right)}_{\text{perturbation term }\eta_{t}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{t}}.

(9)

The unscaled perturbation term,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{t}
, captures the approximation error given by Theorem
3
.
Suppose each component of
|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{t}|
is bounded by

\widetilde{\Delta}:={1\over\tau}(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}=O((\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}))\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

This bound holds with probability at least
1-5m\exp(-cn)
for some positive universal constant
c>0
, as established by the
\ell_{\infty}
concentration bound in Theorem
3
.

The key to understanding the convergence behavior lies in analyzing the drift of the dynamics of a single coordinate
x_{j}^{t}
governed by Equation (

).

The drift term is
\eta_{t}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}\left(w_{j}^{*}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t-1})\right)
and its magnitude is
\eta_{t}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau\sqrt{n}}
when the
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t-1})\neq w_{j}^{*}
or the sign of
x_{j}^{t-1}
differs from the sign of
w_{j}^{*}
. Thus, this justifies the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio
\rho
definition in Theorem

since

\frac{\eta_{t}\cdot\widetilde{\Delta}}{\eta_{t}\cdot 2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}/(\tau\sqrt{n})}=\frac{(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})n}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{N}}=\rho

To make the notation light and connect with the cycle analysis later, and since we consider the constant step size case in this section, we also define

a:=\frac{\eta_{0}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{\tau\sqrt{n}}={\eta_{0}\widetilde{\Delta}\over 2\rho}.

Before analyzing the zero-crossing behavior, let us recall the key parameters from our setup:
\rho
captures the ratio between the upper bounds of the perturbation and drift magnitudes, and
2a
is the size of the drift (including the step size
\eta_{0}
).

Step 1: Zero–Crossing Times.
This analysis uses the parameters
a
and the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio
\rho
, where
\rho
incorporates the perturbation bound
\widetilde{\Delta}
such that
\eta_{0}|\epsilon^{t}_{j}|\leq\eta_{0}\widetilde{\Delta}=2a\rho
.

First, assume that
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
without loss of generality. The other case follows by symmetry.
Define the sequence of zero–crossing times for coordinate
j
:

t_{1}<t_{2}<t_{3}<\cdots,

where
t_{2m-1}
is the
m
th time
x_{j}^{t}
crosses from nonnegative (
\geq 0
) to negative (
<0
), and
t_{2m}
is the
m
th time it crosses from negative back to nonnegative.
When the process crosses from nonnegative to negative at time
t_{2m-1}
, we have
x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}-1}\geq 0
. The update dynamics give:

Since
x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}-1}\geq 0
and the perturbation term is bounded as
\epsilon^{t_{2m-1}}_{j}\geq-\widetilde{\Delta}
:

x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}}\geq 0-\eta_{0}\widetilde{\Delta}=-2a\rho.

We show that the next crossing
t_{2m}
occurs immediately,
t_{2m}=t_{2m-1}+1
. At time
t=t_{2m-1}+1
, the preceding state was
x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}}
, which is negative (

). The dynamics give:

\displaystyle x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}+1}

\displaystyle=x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}}-\eta_{0}\,{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-(+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})\Bigr)+\eta_{0}\,\epsilon^{t_{2m-1}+1}_{j}

\displaystyle=x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}}+2a+\eta_{0}\,\epsilon^{t_{2m-1}+1}_{j}.

Using
x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}}\geq-2a\rho
and the perturbation bound

:

x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}+1}\geq(-2a\rho)+2a-\eta_{0}\widetilde{\Delta}=-2a\rho+2a-2a\rho=2a(1-2\rho).

Since
\rho<1/5<1/2

2a(1-2\rho)>0
. Thus
x_{j}^{t_{2m-1}+1}>0
, confirming
t_{2m}=t_{2m-1}+1
. Each negative event lasts exactly one time step.

Similarly, suppose that
w_{j}^{*}=-1/\sqrt{n}
. Then
t_{2m}
is the
m
-th time

crosses from negative (
<0
) to non-negative (
\geq 0

x_{j}^{t_{2m}-1}<0
(

). The update gives:

0\leq x_{j}^{t_{2m}}=x_{j}^{t_{2m}-1}-\eta_{0}\,{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}\Bigl(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})\Bigr)+\eta_{0}\,\epsilon^{t_{2m}}_{j}=x_{j}^{t_{2m}-1}+\eta_{0}\,\epsilon^{t_{2m}}_{j}.

Since
x_{j}^{t_{2m}-1}<0
and the perturbation term is bounded

x_{j}^{t_{2m}}\leq 0+\eta_{0}\widetilde{\Delta}=2a\rho.

Now consider the next step
t_{2m}+1
. We have
x_{j}^{t_{2m}}\geq 0

w_{j}^{t_{2m}}=1/\sqrt{n}
.

x_{j}^{t_{2m}+1}=x_{j}^{t_{2m}}-\eta_{0}\,{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\over\tau}\Bigl(+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}-(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})\Bigr)+\eta_{0}\,\epsilon^{t_{2m}+1}_{j}=x_{j}^{t_{2m}}-2a+\eta_{0}\epsilon^{t_{2m}+1}_{j}.

x_{j}^{t_{2m}}\leq 2a\rho

x_{j}^{t_{2m}+1}\leq(2a\rho)-2a+\eta_{0}\widetilde{\Delta}=2a\rho-2a+2a\rho=2a(2\rho-1)=-2a(1-2\rho).

Since

, this is strictly negative. Thus
x_{j}^{t_{2m}+1}<0
. This confirms the one-step reset property symmetrically.

In both cases, the coordinate resets to the “correct" sign region within one time step of entering the “incorrect" sign region, provided

.

Step 2: Occupation Time Analysis.
Without loss of generality, assume that
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
.
The dynamics of the
j
-th component
x_{j}^{t}
exhibit the following cycle structure. Define a cycle as:

•

A
negative phase
: one time step during which
x_{j}^{t}<0
(with
\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=-1
).

•

A
positive phase
: a block of consecutive time steps during which
x_{j}^{t}\geq 0
(with
\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=1
).

After a negative event at time
t
, we have
x_{j}^{t+1}\geq 2a(1-2\rho)>0
by the result of Step 1. To force the
j
-th component
x_{j}^{k}
from this positive level back below zero, a cumulative downward displacement of at least
2a(1-2\rho)
is required. Since each step in the positive phase may decrease
x_{j}^{k}
by at most
2a\rho
(in the worst case because the perturbation term
\eta_{0}|\epsilon^{t}_{j}|\leq 2a\rho
), it takes at least

L:=\left\lceil\frac{2a(1-2\rho)}{2a\rho}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{(1-2\rho)}{\rho}\right\rceil

steps. Thus, a complete cycle consists of one negative step followed by at least
L
positive steps, giving a minimum cycle length of
1+L
. We know that
L\geq(1-2\rho)/\rho
, which implies
1+L\geq 1+(1-2\rho)/\rho=(1-\rho)/\rho
.

If
x_{j}^{t}\geq 0
for every
t=1,\dots,T
, then
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=1
, and the desired lower bound is immediate. Hence, assume that at least one negative event occurs. Let
T_{0}=t_{1}
be the first time in
[1,T]
the iterate
x_{j}^{t}
becomes negative. By definition, this means
x_{j}^{t}\geq 0
for
t=1,\dots,T_{0}-1
, and
x_{j}^{T_{0}}<0
.
Let
\gamma
be the number of negative events (steps where
x_{j}^{t}<0
) in the interval
[T_{0}+1,T]
. Let
T^{\prime}=T-T_{0}
. Each negative step in
[T_{0}+1,T]
initiates a cycle of minimum length
1+L
. The number of full cycles starting in
[T_{0}+1,T]
is at most
\lfloor T^{\prime}/(1+L)\rfloor
. The remaining partial cycle can contain at most one negative step. Therefore,
\gamma\leq\lfloor T^{\prime}/(1+L)\rfloor+1
.
Using the inequality
1+L\geq(1-\rho)/\rho
, we have
1/(1+L)\leq\rho/(1-\rho)
. Substituting this into the bound for
\gamma
:

\gamma\leq\frac{T^{\prime}}{1+L}+1\leq T^{\prime}\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}+1

Step 3: Bounding the Average Sign.
Now, we find a lower bound for the sum of signs over the entire interval
[1,T]
:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})+\sum_{t=T_{0}+1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})

For the term part, using the definition of
T_{0}
, we have

\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=\sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}-1}(+1)+\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{T_{0}})=(T_{0}-1)+(-1)=T_{0}-2

For the second part, let
p
be the number of non-negative steps (
x_{j}^{t}\geq 0
) in the interval
[T_{0}+1,T]
. We have
\gamma+p=T^{\prime}
. The sum over this interval is given by:

\sum_{t=T_{0}+1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=\gamma\times(-1)+p\times(+1)=-\gamma+(T^{\prime}-\gamma)=T^{\prime}-2\gamma

Using the upper bound
\gamma\leq T^{\prime}\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}+1
yields

\sum_{t=T_{0}+1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=T^{\prime}-2\gamma\geq T^{\prime}-2\left(T^{\prime}\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}+1\right)=T^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}\right)-2

Combining the two parts for the total sum:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})\geq(T_{0}-2)+\left[T^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}\right)-2\right]

We substitute
T^{\prime}=T-T_{0}
:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})\geq T_{0}-2+(T-T_{0})\left(1-\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}\right)-2

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})\geq T-(T-T_{0})\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}-4

(10)

Finally, we derive the lower bound for the average sign over the interval
[1,T]
by dividing (
10
) by
T
:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})\geq\frac{1}{T}\left[T-(T-T_{0})\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}-4\right]=1-\frac{T-T_{0}}{T}\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{4}{T}

Since
1\leq T_{0}\leq T
, we have
T-T_{0}<T
, which implies
\frac{T-T_{0}}{T}<1
for all
T_{0}\geq 1
. As
\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}>0
for
0<\rho<1/2
, we can bound the term:
-\frac{T-T_{0}}{T}\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}\geq-(1)\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}
.
Therefore,

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})\geq 1-\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{4}{T}

By the same argument and symmetry for the case
w_{j}^{*}=-1/\sqrt{n}
, with the possible initial mismatch due to the convention
\operatorname{sign}(0)=1
absorbed into the
O(1/T)
term, we have

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\operatorname{sign}(x_{j}^{t})\leq-1+\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+\frac{4}{T}

Since the component-wise error bound

|w_{j}^{*}-\overline{w}_{j}^{T}|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+\frac{4}{T}\right)

holds uniformly for all components
j=1,\dots,n
, as established before, we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+\frac{4}{T}\right)

(11)

This bound shows that the ergodic average of the iterates converges to a ball around the target
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
in the
\ell_{\infty}
norm, with the radius of the ball shrinking as the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio
\rho
decreases, plus a term that vanishes as the number of iterations
T
increases.

Exact Recovery Condition.

Exact coordinate-wise sign recovery requires that the sign of the average iterate matches the target sign for each component:

\operatorname{sign}\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{j}^{t}\Bigr)\;=\;\operatorname{sign}(w_{j}^{*})\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,n,

which is equivalent to

\mathcal{Q}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}^{t}\right)\;=\;\boldsymbol{w}^{*}.

This is guaranteed if
\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}^{t}\|_{\infty}<\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
. Using the bound derived in (
11
), this condition is satisfied if
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+\frac{4}{T}\right)<\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
,
which is equivalent to the condition
\frac{2\rho}{1-\rho}+\frac{4}{T}<1
; this condition simplifies to:

\frac{4}{T}<\frac{1-3\rho}{1-\rho}

Hence, from the assumption
\rho<1/5<1/3
, the exact recovery is achieved when

T>\frac{4(1-\rho)}{1-3\rho}.

∎

When

Visit to

6.2
Non-Gaussian Data and Normalization

Although the Gaussian data assumption may seem restrictive and unrealistic, we empirically show that the STE can fail to perform reliably for general non-Gaussian distributed data, raising the question of why this heuristic remains prevalent in practical quantization. To address this, we demonstrate that applying a normalization procedure significantly enhances the STE’s performance, restoring its effectiveness across broader data distributions.

Sub-Gaussian Data.

We further examine the behavior of Algorithm
1
on sub-Gaussian data. Our experiments indicate that its convergence guarantees deteriorate significantly for general sub-Gaussian distributions. In particular, under the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}[0,1]
and the Bernoulli distribution
\mathcal{B}(0.5)
with
\mathrm{Pr}(X=0)=\mathrm{Pr}(X=1)=0.5
, both of which possess non-zero mean, the STE fails to consistently converge to an optimal solution (with a zero loss) as the sample size increases. As shown in Figure
3
, the success rates (over 100 randomized runs) remain low and do not exhibit improvement with larger sample sizes
N
. When the data are normalized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, convergence improves notably; this corresponds to
\sqrt{12}\,(X-0.5)
for
X\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1]
and
2X-1
for
X\sim\mathcal{B}(0.5)
.

MNIST Classification.

These findings emphasize the crucial role of normalization in ensuring the effectiveness of STE-based training. To further substantiate this observation beyond the weight recovery setting, we conduct experiments on MNIST classification using a binary CNN comprising two convolutional layers (Conv) with
5\times 5
kernels and a single fully connected (FC) layer:

\mathrm{Conv}(5\times 5\times 6)\to\mathrm{MaxPool}(2\times 2)\to\mathrm{Conv}(5\times 5\times 16)\to\mathrm{MaxPool}(2\times 2)\to\mathrm{FC}.

In this configuration, both the weights and activations of the convolutional layers are binarized with an adaptive multiplicative full-precision scalar, following common practice
[
43
,
62
]
, while the fully connected layer is retained in full precision. The cross-entropy loss is used, and the ReLU STE is adopted for activation quantization during training. We observe that, in the absence of normalization—applied neither to the input data (entering the first convolutional layer) nor to the intermediate features (entering the second convolutional layer), the training accuracy plateaus around 30%. In contrast, applying normalization for both the input data and intermediate features (via a non-affine batch normalization layer
1
1

[
21
,
7
]
) substantially improves performance, achieving approximately 96% accuracy.

Taken together, our results validate the practical significance of the Gaussian data assumption central to our theoretical analysis and underscore the essential role of normalization in effective quantization.

7
Concluding Remarks

We presented the first sample complexity analysis for STE-based training for quantization-aware learning, emphasizing the pivotal role of sample size in ensuring its effectiveness. Our analysis focuses on a two-layer neural network with both binary weights and binary activations. The associated STE-gradient method employs dual STEs to handle the discreteness in both the objective and the constraints. We proved that
O(n^{2})
samples are sufficient to guarantee ergodic convergence to the optimal binary weights
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, while
O(n^{4})
samples suffice for a non-ergodic guarantee, where the iterates exactly reach
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often. Notably, the use of STE generally leads the iterates to repeatedly diverge from the minimizer in weight quantization; however, we argue that this behavior is actually beneficial, as it prevents the premature stagnation of iterates. In addition, we empirically demonstrate that convergence guarantees break down under general non-Gaussian data distributions but can be restored through normalization.

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})-\frac{\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\right|\;\leq\;C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}},

or

\left|\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|-\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\right|\;\leq\;C^{\prime\prime}\,\tau\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

Let
C_{3}=C^{\prime\prime}
. Since
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
is a finite set with cardinality
|\mathbb{Q}_{1}|=2^{n}
, a union bound over all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
(at most
2^{n}-1
possibilities) gives total failure probability

<2^{n}\;\times\;2e^{-c^{\prime\prime\prime}n}\;=\;2\exp\bigl(n\ln 2-c^{\prime\prime\prime}n\bigr)\;=\;2\exp\bigl(-(c^{\prime\prime\prime}-\ln 2)\,n\bigr).

Since
c^{\prime\prime\prime}>2\ln 2
, we have
c^{\prime\prime\prime}-\ln 2>\ln 2
, and the failure probability decays as
\exp(-\Omega(n))
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\exp(-\Omega(n))
, the inequality

\left|\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|-\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\right|\;\leq\;C_{3}\,\tau\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

holds simultaneously for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
, which completes the proof.
∎

A.1.2
Concentration of the second term

Lemma 2

(Concentration of the second term)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Let
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then there exist positive universal constants
c_{2}^{\prime}
and
C_{3}^{\prime}
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime}n)
, such that

\left|{\tau\over N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle\right|\leq C_{3}^{\prime}\tau\sqrt{n\over N},

for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
. Here
D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})=\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\;=\;{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}
. Since
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{w}\neq-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, the denominator is non-zero and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Define the random variable:

Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\;=\;D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\,\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{2},

The expression inside the absolute value in the lemma statement is
\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})
.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\right]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})
, by linearity of expectation,

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})]\;=\;\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}],\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right\rangle\;=\;\left\langle{1\over 2\sqrt{2\pi}}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}),{\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\over\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\right\rangle.

Because
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|=\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|
for
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, the vectors
\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
are orthogonal:
\left\langle\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*},\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\right\rangle=\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^{2}-\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|^{2}=0
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})]=0.

Next, we establish that
Y_{i}
is sub-Gaussian. Since
|D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})|\leq 1
, we have
|Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})|\;\leq\;|\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}|
.
As
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
is a unit vector,
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, which is sub-Gaussian. Since
|Y_{i}|
is bounded by the magnitude of a standard sub-Gaussian random variable,
Y_{i}
is itself sub-Gaussian with a sub-Gaussian norm
\|Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}
uniformly bounded by an universal constant
C^{\prime}
for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
.

Since
Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})
are independent, mean-zero, sub-Gaussian random variables with uniformly bounded sub-Gaussian norm
C^{\prime}
, we apply Hoeffding’s bound
[
52
]
, for any
t>0
:

\Pr\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\right|>t\right)\;\leq\;2\exp\bigl(-c\,N\,t^{2}/(C^{\prime})^{2}\bigr),

for some universal constant
c>0
. Choose a constant
C^{\prime\prime}>0
large enough such that
c^{\prime\prime\prime}:=c(C^{\prime\prime})^{2}/(C^{\prime})^{2}>2\ln 2
. Let

t\;=\;C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

Then, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{w}
with
\boldsymbol{w}\neq-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, with probability at least
1-2e^{-c^{\prime\prime\prime}n}
:

\Bigl|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\Bigr|\;\leq\;C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

Multiplying by
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
yields:

\Bigl|\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\Bigr|\;\leq\;C^{\prime\prime}\,\tau\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

Let
C_{3}^{\prime}=C^{\prime\prime}
. To ensure the above holds uniformly for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
, we use a union bound. The total number of such vectors is less than
2^{n}
. The total failure probability is bounded by

<2^{n}\;\times\;2e^{-c^{\prime\prime\prime}n}\;=\;2\exp\bigl(n\ln 2-c^{\prime\prime\prime}n\bigr)\;=\;2\exp\bigl(-(c^{\prime\prime\prime}-\ln 2)\,n\bigr).

Since
c^{\prime\prime\prime}>2\ln 2
, let
c_{2}^{\prime}=c^{\prime\prime\prime}-\ln 2>0
. The failure probability decays as
\exp(-\Omega(n))
. The condition
N\geq C_{1}^{\prime}n^{2}
ensures
t
is appropriately scaled for the concentration regime. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime}n)
(adjusting constant for the factor of 2), the inequality

\Bigl|\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})\Bigr|\;\leq\;C_{3}^{\prime}\,\tau\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

holds simultaneously for all vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, completing the proof.
∎

A.1.3
Concentration of the third term

Recall that from the decomposition of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}
, we have
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\right\rangle\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right\rangle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
is the component of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}
orthogonal to
\mathrm{span}\{\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*},\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*})/\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}=(\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*})/\|\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|
. Recall also that
D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})=\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}
.
Define the vector
R(\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
where

R_{j}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Let
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then, there exist positive universal constants
c_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}
and
C_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}
such that with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}n)
,

\|R(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;C_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}\,\tau\;\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

holds simultaneously for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
with
\boldsymbol{w}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
with
\boldsymbol{w}\neq\pm\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. Define the scalar random variable for each coordinate
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:

S_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{w})=D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle.

The
j
-th component of the vector
R(\boldsymbol{w})
is
R_{j}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{w})
.

First, we compute the expectation of
S_{i,j}
. We know that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\,\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\right]=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}).

Since
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}=\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\right\rangle\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right\rangle\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
, by linearity we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[S_{i,j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[D(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\mathds{1}_{\bigl\{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\bigr\}}\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}\left[(D)\mathds{1}\left(\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[(D)\mathds{1}\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}],\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[(D)\mathds{1}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\right\rangle]-\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[(D)\mathds{1}\left\langle\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\right\rangle]

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}(\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}),\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle-\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[X_{i}(\boldsymbol{w}))]-\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{Z}}[Y_{i}(\boldsymbol{w})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}(w_{j}-w_{j}^{*})-\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle\cdot 0

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}(w_{j}-w_{j}^{*})-\frac{w_{j}-w^{*}_{j}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{w}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\|}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.

Next, we establish that
S_{i,j}
is sub-Gaussian. We have
|S_{i,j}|\leq|\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle|
. Since
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
is the projection of
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n})
onto the subspace orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
,
\boldsymbol{b}_{i}
is a mean-zero Gaussian vector within that subspace. The term
\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle
is the projection of this Gaussian vector onto
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
, which makes
\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle
a mean-zero Gaussian random variable with variance
\leq 1
. Since
|S_{i,j}|\leq|\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle|
and
\left\langle\boldsymbol{b}_{i},\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\right\rangle
is Gaussian with variance
\leq 1
(hence sub-Gaussian),
S_{i,j}
is also sub-Gaussian with a uniformly bounded sub-Gaussian norm
\|S_{i,j}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}
for some universal constant
C^{\prime}>0
.

Because
S_{i,j}(\boldsymbol{w})
are independent (across
i
), mean-zero, sub-Gaussian random variables with uniformly bounded sub-Gaussian norm
C^{\prime}
, we can apply Hoeffding’s bound to their sum for fixed
j,\boldsymbol{w}
:

\Pr\left(\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i,j}\right|>t\right)\;\leq\;2\exp\bigl(-c\,N\,t^{2}/(C^{\prime})^{2}\bigr).

Next, we obtain a uniform bound over
j\in[n]
and all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{\pm\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
, which are over fewer than
M=n\cdot 2^{n}
events. We choose
t
such that the failure probability for a single event is
\leq\delta/M
, where
\delta=\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}n)
is the overall target failure probability. This requires
t^{2}\gtrsim\frac{(C^{\prime})^{2}}{cN}\ln(M/\delta)\approx\frac{n}{N}
. We set
t=2\frac{(C^{\prime})^{2}}{cN}\ln(M/\delta)=C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{n/N}
for a sufficiently large constant
C^{\prime\prime}
.
By the union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta=1-\exp(-c_{2}^{\prime\prime\prime}n)
:

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i,j}\right|\leq t=C^{\prime\prime}\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}

holds simultaneously for all
j\in[n]
and all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}\setminus\{\pm\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
.
Multiplying by
\tau
and taking the maximum over
j
:

\|R(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}=\max_{j\in[n]}\left|\frac{\tau}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S_{i,j}\right|\leq C^{\prime\prime}\tau\sqrt{\frac{n}{N}}.

Setting
C_{3}^{\prime\prime\prime}=C^{\prime\prime}
yields the claimed bound.
∎

A.2
Concentration Bound for the Cross Term

Define the cross-term vector
T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose
p
-th component is given by

[T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{m}v_{j}\,v_{k}\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}\,[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}\Bigr)\;\Bigl(\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}\Bigr).

Lemma 4

(
\ell_{\infty}
Concentration of Cross Terms)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Let
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then, there exist positive universal constants
c_{5}
and
C_{3}
such that with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{5}n)
,

\|\tau T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{3}\,\tau\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

holds simultaneously for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

Proof of Lemma
4
. .

Fix
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, indices
j\neq k
from
\{1,\dots,m\}
, and a coordinate
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Let
X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})=\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}\,[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}
and
\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})=\mathds{1}_{{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}}-\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}
.
Define the single term contribution from sample
i
as
A_{ijk,p}=X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})
, and its average over
N
samples as
S_{jk,p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{ijk,p}
.
The
p
-th component of the cross-term vector is
[T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}=\sum_{j\neq k}v_{j}v_{k}S_{jk,p}
, where
\sum_{j\neq k}
is the double summation over the indices
j,k\in[m]
with
j\neq k
. We want to bound
\|T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}
.

Step 1: Expectation.

Since
j\neq k
,
\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)}
are independent random vectors. Therefore,
X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})
(which depends only on
\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}
) and
\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})
(which depends only on
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)}
) are independent random variables for fixed
\boldsymbol{w},p
. We also know from the rotational invariance of Gaussian measure that
\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})]=\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}]=1/2-1/2=0
.
Thus, the expectation of the single term is
\mathbb{E}[A_{ijk,p}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})]\mathbb{E}[\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})]=0
.
By linearity of expectation, the expectation of the average is
\mathbb{E}[S_{jk,p}]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[A_{ijk,p}]=0
.

Step 2: sub-Gaussianity of Individual Terms.

The term
\left|X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})\right|\leq\left|[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}\right|
, and
[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
is a standard Gaussian, which is sub-Gaussian. Thus,
X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})
is sub-Gaussian with a
\psi_{2}
-norm bounded by an universal constant, say
\|X_{j,p}^{(i)}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}
.
The term
\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})
is bounded:
|\Delta_{k}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})|\leq 1
.
The product
A_{ijk,p}=X_{j,p}^{(i)}\Delta_{k}^{(i)}
is the product of a sub-Gaussian variable and a bounded variable, hence it is sub-Gaussian. Its norm satisfies
\|A_{ijk,p}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\|\Delta_{k}^{(i)}\|_{\infty}\|X_{j,p}^{(i)}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq 1\cdot C^{\prime}
for some universal constant
C^{\prime}>0
, uniformly over all
i,j,k,p
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.
The terms
A_{ijk,p}
are independent across the sample index
i
because the samples
(\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{(i)},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{m}^{(i)})
are independent across
i
.

Step 3: Uniform Concentration for
S_{jk,p}
.

Since
S_{jk,p}
is the average of
N
independent, mean-zero, sub-Gaussian variables
A_{ijk,p}
(with
\psi_{2}
-norm
\leq C^{\prime}
), we can apply Hoeffding’s bound (
[
52
]
): for a fixed set of indices
(j,k,p)
and fixed vectors
\boldsymbol{w}
,

\Pr\left(\left|S_{jk,p}\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-c\,N\,t^{2}/(C^{\prime})^{2}\bigr)

for some universal constant
c>0
.
To ensure this holds uniformly over all possibilities, we apply a union bound. The number of possibilities is:

•

Pairs
(j,k)
with
j\neq k
:
m(m-1)
pairs.

•

Coordinate
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:
n
coordinates.

•

The vectors
\boldsymbol{w}
from
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
: At most
|\mathbb{Q}_{1}|=2^{n}
.

The total number of events to control is
M^{\prime}=m(m-1)\cdot n\cdot 2^{n}
.
Let
\delta=\exp(-c_{5}n)
be the target overall failure probability (for some chosen constant
c_{5}>0
). The required failure probability per event is
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/M^{\prime}
.
We need to choose
t
such that
2\exp(-cNt^{2}/(C^{\prime})^{2})\leq\delta^{\prime}
. This requires

\frac{cNt^{2}}{(C^{\prime})^{2}}\geq\ln(2/\delta^{\prime})=\ln(2M^{\prime}/\delta)\approx\ln(m^{2}n)+n\ln 2+c_{5}n.

Choosing
t
to satisfy the inequality yields:

t^{2}=2\frac{(C^{\prime})^{2}}{cN}((2\ln 2+c_{5})n+\ln(m^{2}n))

To make the notation light, we can choose a constant
C_{\mathrm{alt}}
such that setting

t=C_{\mathrm{alt}}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

(absorbing constants
C^{\prime},c,c_{5}
terms into
C_{\mathrm{alt}}
) satisfies the requirement for a sufficiently large
N
.
By the union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta=1-\exp(-c_{5}n)
, the bound

|S_{jk,p}|\leq C_{\mathrm{alt}}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

holds simultaneously for all
j\neq k
, all
p
, and all vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

Step 4: Bounding the Sum

Now we bound the target quantity

[T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}=\sum_{j\neq k}v_{j}v_{k}S_{jk,p}
using the uniform bound derived above via triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\left|[T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq k}|v_{j}v_{k}||S_{jk,p}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq k}|v_{j}v_{k}|\left(C_{alt}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j\neq k}|v_{j}v_{k}|\right)C_{alt}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

Using the inequality
\sum_{j\neq k}|v_{j}v_{k}|\leq(\sum_{j}|v_{j}|)(\sum_{k}|v_{k}|)=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}
, we get:

\left|[T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}\right|\leq C_{\mathrm{alt}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

This holds simultaneously for all
p
and relevant
(\boldsymbol{w})
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Finally, taking the maximum over
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and multiplying by
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
:

\|\tau T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}=\max_{p}|\tau[T_{\mathrm{cross}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}|\leq C_{\mathrm{alt}}\,\tau\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}

Defining the constant
C_{3}=C_{\mathrm{alt}}
(which is independent of
\boldsymbol{v},n,N
) yields the stated result.
∎

A.3
Concentration Bound for the Noise Term

Define the noise contribution vector
T_{noise}(\boldsymbol{w})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
whose components are given by

[T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}\left(\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}\right)\xi^{(i)}.

Lemma 5

(Uniform
\ell_{\infty}
Concentration of Noise Term)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Let
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\tau=2\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then, there exist positive universal constants

c_{6}
and
C_{8}
such that with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{6}n)
,

\|\tau T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{8}\tau K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}},

holds uniformly for all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

Proof of of Lemma
5
.

For fixed vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, indices
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and sample index
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, define:

•

X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})=\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}>0\}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}
.

•

A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})=X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})\xi^{(i)}
.

•

S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
.

The
p
-th component of the noise term for a given
\boldsymbol{w}
is
[T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
. We want to bound its magnitude uniformly over
p
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

Step 1: Expectation.

For any fixed
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
, since
\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}
and
\xi^{(i)}
are independent, and
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{(i)}]=0
from Assumption
2
, we have:

\mathbb{E}[A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})]=\mathbb{E}[X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})\xi^{(i)}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})]\mathbb{E}[\xi^{(i)}]=0.

By linearity,
\mathbb{E}[S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})]=0
for any fixed
\boldsymbol{w}
.

Step 2: Properties of
A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
for Bernstein’s Inequality.

The term
|X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})|\leq|[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}|
. Since
[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}
is a standard Gaussian component, it is sub-Gaussian with a uniform sub-Gaussian norm, so
\|[\boldsymbol{z}_{j}^{(i)}]_{p}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}
. Thus,
X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})
is also sub-Gaussian with
\|X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C^{\prime}
uniformly over all
i,j,p
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.
The noise term
\xi^{(i)}
is sub-Gaussian with
\|\xi^{(i)}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{\xi}
by Assumption
2
.
The term
A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})=X_{j,p}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{w})\xi^{(i)}
is the product of two independent, mean-zero sub-Gaussian random variables. Therefore,
A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
is a mean-zero sub-exponential random variable and its sub-exponential norm is bounded by
K_{A}:=C^{\prime}K_{\xi}
by Lemma 2.8.6 in
[
52
]
.
The variables
A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
are independent across the sample index
i
for any fixed
j,p,\boldsymbol{w}
.

Step 3: Uniform Concentration for
S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
.

S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
is the average of
N
independent, mean-zero, sub-exponential variables
A_{ij,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
. Applying Bernstein’s inequality
[
52
]
for sub-exponential random variables: for a fixed
\boldsymbol{w},j,p
,

\Pr\left(\left|S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-cN\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K_{A}^{2}},\frac{t}{K_{A}}\right)\right)

for some universal constant
c>0
.
Now, we want that the above holds uniformly over
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.
The total number of
(j,p,\boldsymbol{w})
tuples to control is
M^{\prime}=m\cdot n\cdot|\mathbb{Q}_{1}|=mn2^{n}
.
Let
\delta=\exp(-c_{6}n)
be the target overall failure probability (for some chosen
c_{6}>0
). The required failure probability per event is
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/M^{\prime}
.
We choose
t
such that
2\exp\left(-cN\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K_{A}^{2}},\frac{t}{K_{A}}\right)\right)\leq\delta^{\prime}
. This requires:

cN\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K_{A}^{2}},\frac{t}{K_{A}}\right)\geq\ln(2/\delta^{\prime})=\ln(2M^{\prime}/\delta)\approx\ln(mn2^{n})+c_{6}n=(\ln 2+c_{6})n+\ln(mn).

Let
R_{n,m}=(\ln 2+c_{6})n+\ln(2mn)
. We aim to find
t
satisfying

cN\min\left(\frac{t^{2}}{K_{A}^{2}},\frac{t}{K_{A}}\right)\geq R_{n,m}\quad(*).

We select
t=K_{A}\sqrt{\frac{R_{n,m}}{cN}}
. This choice corresponds to the quadratic term
\frac{t^{2}}{K_{A}^{2}}
being active in
(*)
, provided
t\leq K_{A}
, which is equivalent to
N\geq\frac{R_{n,m}}{c}
.
By Assumption
4
,
N\geq C_{1}n^{2}
for a sufficiently large universal constant
C_{1}
. Since
R_{n,m}=O(n+\ln m)
(From Assumption
1
, we have
m=O(2^{n})
implying
R_{n,m}=O(n)
), we choose
C_{1}
large enough to ensure such that
C_{1}n^{2}\geq\frac{2R_{n,m}}{c}
for all
n\geq 1
.
Thus, under Assumption
4
, our choice of
t
derived from the quadratic term is valid.

Therefore, we take:

t=K_{A}\sqrt{\frac{R_{n,m}}{cN}}=C^{\prime}K_{\xi}\sqrt{\frac{(\ln 2+c_{6})n+\ln(2mn)}{cN}}.

As
R_{n,m}=O(n+\ln m)
, we define
C_{8}
as an appropriate universal constant incorporating all numerical factors, such that:

t=C_{8}K_{\xi}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}.

By the union bound, with probability at least
1-\delta=1-\exp(-c_{6}n)
, the bound

|S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})|\leq t

holds simultaneously for all
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
,
p\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
.

Step 4: Bounding the Sum.

Now we bound the target quantity
[T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})
using the uniform bound on
|S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})|
:

\displaystyle\left|[T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{j=1}^{m}v_{j}S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}|v_{j}||S_{j,p}(\boldsymbol{w})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}|v_{j}|\left(C_{8}K_{\xi}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}\right).

Thus, we have

\left|[T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}\right|\leq C_{8}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}.

This holds simultaneously for all
p
and all
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Therefore, we have

\max_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}}\|\tau T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\infty}=\max_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{Q}_{1}}\max_{p\in[n]}|\tau[T_{\mathrm{noise}}(\boldsymbol{w})]_{p}|\leq C_{8}\tau K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}\sqrt{\frac{n+2\ln m}{N}}.

∎

Theorem 6

Suppose that Assumptions
1
and
2

over the draw of the noisy samples

\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Assume that the conditions of Theorem
5
hold.
Recall that

\rho=\frac{(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})n}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{N}}\quad\text{and}\quad L\;=\;\Bigl\lceil\tfrac{\,1-2\rho\,}{\,\rho\,}\Bigr\rceil.

A simple algebraic computation shows that the sample complexity requirement in Theorem
6
implies
\rho<1/(n+1)
, which in turn implies
L+1>n
.

The cycle dynamics, established in Steps 1–2 of the proof of Theorem
5
, govern each coordinate
x_{j}^{t}
. This ensures incorrect signs occur at most once per cycle of minimum length
L+1=1+\lceil(1-2\rho)/\rho\rceil
.

Infinite Visits to
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Let
S_{j}(T)
be the set of times up to
T
such that
w_{j}^{t}\neq w_{j}^{*}
for a given index
j
. From Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
5
, we have shown
|S_{j}(T)|\leq\lceil T/(L+1)\rceil
. Define
U_{T}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}S_{j}(T)
as the set of times up to
T
where
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}\neq\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
. Thus, by the union bound,

|U_{T}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}|S_{j}(T)|\leq n\lceil T/(L+1)\rceil.

Therefore,

|U_{T}|\leq n(T/(L+1)+1)=\frac{n}{L+1}T+n.

Let
G_{T}=\{t\in\{1,\dots,T\}:\boldsymbol{w}^{t}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\}
be the set of times where the iterate hits the target. The number of visits up to time
T
is
|G_{T}|=T-|U_{T}|
, so

|G_{T}|\geq T-\left(\frac{n}{L+1}T+n\right)=\left(1-\frac{n}{L+1}\right)T-n.

Since
L+1>n
, the factor
c_{0}:=1-n/(L+1)
is positive. Hence
|G_{T}|\geq c_{0}T-n\to\infty
as
T\to\infty
, and the iterates visit
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often.

Infinite Departures from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

We now show that, under continuous nonzero observation noise, the iterates cannot remain permanently at
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
after visiting it.
We first use the following lemma.

Lemma 6

(Symmetry and Non-vanishing Probability of the Gradient with High Probability)
.

Let

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}:=|\operatorname{supp}(\boldsymbol{v})|=|\{j\in[m]:v_{j}\neq 0\}|.

Define the random vector
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\boldsymbol{G}=-\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})
, with
p
-th component

G_{p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}v_{j^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)}]_{p}\right)\xi^{(i)}.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, and assuming in addition that the noise variables
\xi^{(i)}
are independent of the data and across samples, continuous, and not a.s. zero, we have:

1.

The vector
\boldsymbol{G}
is symmetric about zero, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-\boldsymbol{G}
. In particular, each component
G_{p}
is symmetric about zero.

2.

For each
p\in[n]
,

\mathbb{P}(G_{p}=0)=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N},\qquad\mathbb{P}(G_{p}>0)=\mathbb{P}(G_{p}<0)=\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N-1}.

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{G}=\mathbf{0})=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N}.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Vector symmetry of
\boldsymbol{G}
.

Condition on the data
\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and define

\boldsymbol{C}_{i}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}v_{j^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)}.

Then

\boldsymbol{G}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{C}_{i}\xi^{(i)}.

Since the noise variables are symmetric and independent across samples, the noise vector
(\xi^{(1)},\dots,\xi^{(N)})
is jointly symmetric; moreover, it is independent of the data. Thus the noise vector has the same distribution as its negative. Hence, conditional on the data and therefore unconditionally,
\boldsymbol{G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}-\boldsymbol{G}
.

Symmetry of
G_{p}
:

Let
G_{p}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}
, where

Y_{i}=C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})\xi^{(i)}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}v_{j^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)}]_{p}.

The terms
Y_{i}
are i.i.d. because each pair
(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)},\xi^{(i)})
is i.i.d. We show that each
Y_{i}
is symmetric about zero using its characteristic function,
\phi_{Y_{i}}(s)=\mathbb{E}[e^{isY_{i}}]
. This can be expressed as a nested expectation with the conditional expectation on
\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}
and thus on the value
c=C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})
:

\phi_{Y_{i}}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\xi^{(i)}}[e^{isc\xi^{(i)}}\mid\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}}[\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(sc)].

Since
\xi^{(i)}
is symmetric about zero, its characteristic function
\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(u)
is real-valued and even, i.e.,

\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(u)=\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(-u)
.
Thus,
\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(sc)
is real and even in
s
. Then,
\phi_{Y_{i}}(s)
is real. Also,

\phi_{Y_{i}}(-s)=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}}[\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(-sC_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}))]=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}}[\phi_{\xi^{(i)}}(sC_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)}))]=\phi_{Y_{i}}(s).

Since
\phi_{Y_{i}}(s)
is real and even,
Y_{i}
is symmetric about zero. The sum of i.i.d. symmetric random variables,
\sum_{i}Y_{i}
, is symmetric, and so is
G_{p}
.

Sign probabilities of
G_{p}
.

Let

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}:=|\operatorname{supp}(\boldsymbol{v})|=|\{j\in[m]:v_{j}\neq 0\}|.

For fixed
i
and
p
, the coefficient

C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}v_{j^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}[\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)}]_{p}

equals zero whenever all indicator terms corresponding to
j^{\prime}\in\operatorname{supp}(\boldsymbol{v})
are inactive. This event has probability
2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}}
. On the complementary event, at least one relevant indicator function is active, and
C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})
is a nontrivial continuous random variable; hence it is nonzero almost surely. Therefore,

P(C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})=0)=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}}.

By independence across samples,

P(C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})=0\ \text{for all }i=1,\dots,N)=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N}.

On the complementary event, at least one coefficient
C_{p}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(i)})
is nonzero. Conditional on the data and on all other noise variables,
G_{p}
is then an affine function of one continuous noise variable with nonzero slope. Hence the conditional probability that
G_{p}=0
is zero. Consequently,

P(G_{p}=0)=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N}.

Since
G_{p}
is symmetric about zero,

P(G_{p}>0)=P(G_{p}<0)=\frac{1-P(G_{p}=0)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N-1}.

This also gives

P(G_{p}\neq 0)=1-2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N}.

Finally, define the vector coefficients
\boldsymbol{C}_{i}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{m}v_{j^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)\top}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}>0\}}\boldsymbol{z}_{j^{\prime}}^{(i)}
.
The same argument gives
\boldsymbol{C}_{i}=\mathbf{0}
with probability
2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}}
, and
\boldsymbol{C}_{i}\neq\mathbf{0}
a.s. conditional on a nonempty active subset. If at least one
\boldsymbol{C}_{i}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then conditional on the data and all other noises, the vector
\boldsymbol{G}=N^{-1}\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{C}_{i}\xi^{(i)}
cannot equal
\mathbf{0}
except on a probability-zero event. Therefore

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{G}=\mathbf{0})=2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N}.

∎

Proof of Departure by Contradiction.

Fix any coordinate
j\in[n]
. Recall that the update rule of the STE-gradient method is

\boldsymbol{x}^{u}=\boldsymbol{x}^{u-1}-\eta_{0}\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{u-1}).

Suppose, for contradiction, that the iteration gets stuck at
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
from some time
t
onwards, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
for all
u\geq t
.

Since
\boldsymbol{G}=-\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})
, Lemma
6
implies that

P\left(w_{j}^{*}[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}>0\right)=\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N-1}.

On this event, the iterate must eventually depart from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Indeed, if
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
, then

[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}>0
. While
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, we have
x_{j}^{u}\geq 0
, and the update

x_{j}^{u+1}=x_{j}^{u}-\eta_{0}[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}

shows that
x_{j}^{u}
strictly decreases at every step. Hence
x_{j}^{u}
must eventually become negative, contradicting
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Similarly, if
w_{j}^{*}=-1/\sqrt{n}
, then

[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}<0
. While
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
, we have
x_{j}^{u}<0
, and the update

x_{j}^{u+1}=x_{j}^{u}-\eta_{0}[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}

shows that
x_{j}^{u}
strictly increases at every step. Hence
x_{j}^{u}
must eventually become nonnegative, again contradicting
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Therefore, the event ensuring departure occurs with probability at least

\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N-1}.

Thus, combining the previous infinite-visit result with this departure event, we show that the iterates visit
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often and also depart from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often with probability at least

\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{0}N-1}-5m\exp(-cn),

where the term
5m\exp(-cn)
comes from the failure probability of the finite-sample approximation bound in Theorem
3
. This confirms the recurrent, non-stable behavior stated in the theorem.
∎

Appendix D

The main-text constant-step-size ergodic argument in Section
5.2.1
and the last-iterate argument in Section

We now demonstrate that these convergence guarantees extend to a general class of non-summable step sizes under Assumption

\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\neq 0

1

Recall that the component-wise update rule is:

x_{j}^{t}=x_{j}^{t-1}-\eta_{t}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t-1})-w_{j}^{*}\right)+\eta_{t}\epsilon^{t}_{j},\qquad|\epsilon^{t}_{j}|\leq\widetilde{\Delta}.

(12)

The perturbation bound
\widetilde{\Delta}={1\over\tau}(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})\sqrt{n/N}
and the perturbation-to-drift strength ratio
\rho

\rho=\frac{\eta_{t}\cdot\widetilde{\Delta}}{\eta_{t}\cdot 2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}/(\tau\sqrt{n})}=\frac{\widetilde{\Delta}}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}/(\tau\sqrt{n})}=\frac{(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})n}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{N}}

are independent of
\eta_{t}
. We assume
N

\rho

).

D.1
Ergodic Convergence Analysis

C_{1},C^{\prime}

The ergodic average
\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\boldsymbol{w}^{t}

Proof of Theorem
7
.

The proof adapts the constant step size analysis by verifying the persistence of essential cycle properties under Assumption
3
. We define
D_{\mathrm{eff}}:=\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{\tau\sqrt{n}}
as the constant factor for the full corrective drift magnitude.

Step 1: One-Step Reset Property.

We show that for a sufficiently large
t

x_{j}^{t}
enters the incorrect sign regime,
x_{j}^{t+1}
resets to the correct sign. Assume
w_{j}^{*}=+1/\sqrt{n}

t
be when
x_{j}^{t}<0
and
x_{j}^{t-1}\geq 0
.
The update for
x_{j}^{t}
(using step size
\eta_{t}
and based on
x_{j}^{t-1}
) involved zero drift (as
\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t-1})=\mathrm{sign}(w_{j}^{*})
). Thus,
x_{j}^{t}=x_{j}^{t-1}+\eta_{t}\epsilon^{t}_{j}\geq-\eta_{t}\widetilde{\Delta}
.
The update for
x_{j}^{t+1}
(using step size
\eta_{t+1}
) involves the full corrective drift
\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}
:

x_{j}^{t+1}=x_{j}^{t}+\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}+\eta_{t+1}\epsilon^{t+1}_{j}.

Using
x_{j}^{t}\geq-\eta_{t}\widetilde{\Delta}
and
\epsilon^{t+1}_{j}\geq-\widetilde{\Delta}
:

x_{j}^{t+1}\geq-\eta_{t}\widetilde{\Delta}+\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}-\eta_{t+1}\widetilde{\Delta}.

We substitute
\widetilde{\Delta}=\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}
:

x_{j}^{t+1}\geq-\eta_{t}(\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}})+\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}-\eta_{t+1}(\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}),

which gives

x_{j}^{t+1}\geq D_{\mathrm{eff}}\left[\eta_{t+1}(1-\rho)-\eta_{t}\rho\right]=D_{\mathrm{eff}}\eta_{t}\left[\frac{\eta_{t+1}}{\eta_{t}}(1-\rho)-\rho\right].

For
x_{j}^{t+1}>0
, we need
\frac{\eta_{t+1}}{\eta_{t}}(1-\rho)-\rho>0
, or
\frac{\eta_{t+1}}{\eta_{t}}>\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}
.
By Assumption
3
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\eta_{t+1}}{\eta_{t}}=1
. Since
\rho<1/5<1/2
, we have
\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}<1
. Thus, for a sufficiently large
t
, the condition holds, ensuring
x_{j}^{t+1}>0
. A symmetric argument holds for
w_{j}^{*}=-1/\sqrt{n}
. This relies on Assumptions
3
and
\rho<1/5<1/2
.

Step 2: Cycle Structure and Occupation Time Bound.

By symmetry, assume
w_{j}^{*}=+1/\sqrt{n}
without loss of generality.

By Step 1 and Assumption
3
(
\lim_{s\to\infty}\eta_{s+1}/\eta_{s}=1
), there exists a finite
T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
such that for all
t\geq T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
:

(i)

The one-step reset condition
\eta_{t+1}/\eta_{t}>\rho/(1-\rho)
holds since
0<\rho<1/5
. In other words, if
x_{j}^{t}<0
(incorrect sign, having entered from
x_{j}^{t-1}\geq 0
), then
x_{j}^{t+1}\geq 0
(one-step reset to the correct sign).

(ii)

The ratio of consecutive step sizes is bounded from above:
\eta_{t}/\eta_{t+1}\leq M_{0}
for some chosen universal constant
M_{0}\geq 1
.

Since the initial phase for
t<T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
is finite,
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{burnin}}}\in[-R,R]
for some finite
R>0
. If
x_{j}^{t}
becomes negative during
t<T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
, the corrective drift component in the update,
x_{j}^{s+1}\geq x_{j}^{s}+\eta_{s+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)
, is positive for each subsequent step
s
while
x_{j}^{s}
remains incorrect (
x_{j}^{s}<0
). Due to the non-summability of
\{\eta_{s}\}
(Assumption
3
(2)), the sum of these positive drift contributions
\sum\eta_{s+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)
must eventually exceed any finite negative value of
x_{j}^{t^{\prime}}
, ensuring that
x_{j}^{t}
returns to the correct sign regime in some finite time.

For this coordinate
j
, let
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init},j}
be a finite time index (
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init},j}\geq T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
) by which
x_{j}^{t}
has returned to the correct sign region and conditions (i) and (ii) above reliably hold for subsequent iterations. For the vector and
\ell_{\infty}
analyses below, take the common burn-in time

T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}:=\max_{j\in[n]}T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init},j},

which is finite since
n
is finite. After this common burn-in, the reset and cycle bounds hold simultaneously for all coordinates.

For
t\geq T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
, the system exhibits a stable cyclic behavior:

•

An incorrect sign phase (e.g.,
x_{j}^{k}<0
for
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
, where
k\geq T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
and
x_{j}^{k-1}\geq 0
) lasts exactly one time step, with
x_{j}^{k+1}\geq 0
due to the one-step reset property active for
k\geq T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}\geq T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
.

•

We now determine the minimum duration,
L_{M_{0}}
, of the subsequent correct sign phase starting at
x_{j}^{k+1}
. After the reset at step
k+1
, the value is
x_{j}^{k+1}\geq D_{\mathrm{eff}}[\eta_{k+1}(1-\rho)-\eta_{k}\rho]
.
During the correct phase (steps
s=k+1,k+2,\dots
), the update is
x_{j}^{s+1}=x_{j}^{s}+\eta_{s+1}\epsilon^{s+1}_{j}
. The maximum decrease in
x_{j}
at any step
s+1
within this phase is
\eta_{s+1}\widetilde{\Delta}=\eta_{s+1}\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
By Assumption
3
,
\eta_{s+1}\leq\eta_{k+1}
for all steps
s+1
in this particular correct phase starting after
x_{j}^{k+1}
. Thus, the maximum decrease in any single step of this correct phase is bounded by
\eta_{k+1}\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}
.

Let
L_{M_{0}}
be the minimum number of steps in this correct phase. For the phase to end (i.e., for
x_{j}
to become negative again), the cumulative maximum decrease must overcome
D_{\mathrm{eff}}[\eta_{k+1}(1-\rho)-\eta_{k}\rho]
:

L_{M_{0}}\cdot(\eta_{k+1}\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}})\geq D_{\mathrm{eff}}[\eta_{k+1}(1-\rho)-\eta_{k}\rho],

or

L_{M_{0}}\geq\frac{\eta_{k+1}(1-\rho)-\eta_{k}\rho}{\eta_{k+1}\rho}=\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}-\frac{\eta_{k}}{\eta_{k+1}}.

By Assumption
3
(3),
\lim_{k\to\infty}\eta_{k}/\eta_{k+1}=1
. Since
\eta_{k}\geq\eta_{k+1}
, we have
1\leq\eta_{k}/\eta_{k+1}
. We can choose
T_{\mathrm{burnin}}
(and thus
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
) to be sufficiently large such that for all
k\geq T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
,
\eta_{k}/\eta_{k+1}\leq M_{0}
, where
M_{0}\geq 1
is a universal constant arbitrarily close to
1
. For a fixed, concrete non-asymptotic analysis for
k\geq T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
(where
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
might be chosen to guarantee a specific
M_{0}
, e.g.,
M_{0}=1.5
if we assume
\eta_{k+1}/\eta_{k}\geq 2/3
), we have:

L_{M_{0}}\geq\frac{1-\rho}{\rho}-M_{0}=\frac{1-(1+M_{0})\rho}{\rho}.

Since
L_{M_{0}}
must be an integer,
L_{M_{0}}\geq\left\lceil\frac{1-(1+M_{0})\rho}{\rho}\right\rceil
. Note that for a constant step size case, i.e.,
\eta_{k}=\eta_{k+1}
, then
M_{0}
can be set to
1
, so we recover
L\geq\left\lceil\frac{1-2\rho}{\rho}\right\rceil
, the cycle length relation and
\rho
we used for the proof (Step 2) of Theorem
5
.

Thus, for
t\geq T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
, cycles consist of one incorrect step followed by at least
L_{M_{0}}
correct steps (minimum length
1+L_{M_{0}}
). The behavior during an initial finite period (up to
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
) does not affect the asymptotic fraction of incorrect steps. Therefore, following the counting argument from Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
5
, if
\gamma
is the number of negative events in the interval
[T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}+1,T]
and
T^{\prime}=T-T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
, we have for large
T^{\prime}
(i.e., large
T
):

\gamma\leq\frac{T^{\prime}}{1+L_{M_{0}}}+1\leq T^{\prime}\frac{\rho}{1-M_{0}\cdot\rho}+1.

Step 3: Bounding the Average Sign.

From Step 2, we established that after an initial finite period
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}-1
, the fraction of time of each component exhibiting an incorrect sign is bounded by
\frac{\rho}{1-M_{0}\rho}
for the time in
[T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}},T]
for sufficiently large
T
. The initial
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}-1
steps contribute terms of order
O(T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}/T)
to the final average sign error.

Given the asymptotic upper bound on the fraction of incorrect steps, the logic from Step 3 of the proof of Theorem
5
applies. The same analysis in Step 3 of the proof of Theorem
5
yields the similar equation (
10
). Since the argument is purely algebraic and we have already presented it in Step 3 of the proof of Theorem
5
, we omit the detailed re-computation. This computation yields

\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n}\right|\leq\frac{2\rho}{1-M_{0}\rho}+O\left(\frac{T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}}{T}\right).

Since
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
is a finite constant (
\eta_{\max}
,
\rho
,
M_{0}
, but not
T
), the term
O(T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}/T)
is
O(1/T)
.
Hence, the
\ell_{\infty}
bound on
\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}
becomes:

\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left(\frac{2\rho}{1-M_{0}\rho}\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{T\sqrt{n}}\right).

Since
M_{0}\geq 1
can be chosen arbitrarily close to
1
, by choosing
M_{0}=1.1
(
T^{\prime}_{\mathrm{init}}
depends on
M_{0}
but still finite), the
L_{\infty}
bound on
\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}
and the exact recovery condition (
\rho<1/5
, for large
T
as detailed in the proof of Theorem
5
) follows.

∎

The next corollary generalizes Theorem
7
to the case when
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}
is not necessarily 0 but satisfies
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\left[-\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]^{n}
.
Note that an initialization scale of
O(1/\sqrt{n})
per component is consistent with standard practices such as Glorot normal initialization, which is commonly used for the initialization in training neural networks.

Corollary 2

(Ergodic Convergence with Bounded Initialization)
.

Suppose the conditions of Theorem
7
hold, except that the initialization
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}
is not necessarily
0
but satisfies
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\left[-\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]^{n}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
.
Then, for a sufficiently large
T
and with probability at least
1-5m\exp(-cn)
for some universal constant
c>0
, the conclusions of Theorem
7
still hold:

\left\|\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}-\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{2\rho}{1-1.1\rho}+\frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}}{T\sqrt{n}},

where
C_{init}
is a finite constant that depends on the initialization bound constant
c_{0},\|v\|
and the step size
\eta_{t}
.
Moreover, if
T
is sufficiently large, then
\mathcal{Q}\!\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\right)=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
.

Proof of Corollary
2
.

We demonstrate that a bounded non-zero initialization
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}
does not alter the asymptotic conclusions of Theorem
7
. Without loss of generality, we analyze a single component
j
and assume
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
. The other case follows by symmetry.

Case 1:
x_{j}^{0}\geq 0
.
In this scenario, the iterate component
x_{j}^{0}
starts with the correct sign. If
x_{j}^{0}\geq 0
, the argument in the proof of Theorem
7
(specifically,
T_{0}
is defined as the first time the iterate
x_{j}^{t}
becomes negative as before) is unchanged, and the subsequent cycle analysis applies.

Case 2:
x_{j}^{0}<0
.
Let
T_{\mathrm{init}}
be the first time
t\geq 1
such that
x_{j}^{t}\geq 0
.
For
t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{init}}-1]
,
x_{j}^{t}<0
, so
\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=-1
. The update rule for
x_{j}^{t+1}
is:

x_{j}^{t+1}=x_{j}^{t}-\eta_{t+1}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{\tau}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\right)+\eta_{t+1}\epsilon^{t+1}_{j}.

With
\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=-1
and
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
, the drift term is
-\eta_{t+1}\frac{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{\tau}(-\frac{2}{\sqrt{n}})=\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}
, where
D_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}{\tau\sqrt{n}}
is a constant.
The perturbation
\eta_{t+1}\epsilon^{t+1}_{j}
satisfies
\eta_{t+1}\epsilon^{t+1}_{j}\geq-\eta_{t+1}\widetilde{\Delta}
. Using
\widetilde{\Delta}=\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}
, this is
-\eta_{t+1}\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}
, where
0<\rho<1/5
from the assumption in Theorem
7
.
Thus, for
t<T_{\mathrm{init}}
, we have

x_{j}^{t+1}\geq x_{j}^{t}+\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}-\eta_{t+1}\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}=x_{j}^{t}+\eta_{t+1}D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho).

Applying this repeatedly from
t=0
to
T_{\mathrm{init}}-1
yields

x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\geq x_{j}^{0}+D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\eta_{t}.

Since
x_{j}^{0}\geq-c_{0}/\sqrt{n}
,

x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\geq-\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}+D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\eta_{t}.

Also, by definition of
T_{\mathrm{init}}
,
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}<0
and
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\geq 0
. The update for
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}
is
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}=x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}+\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}D_{\mathrm{eff}}+\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\epsilon^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}_{j}
.
Since
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}<0
and
\epsilon^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}_{j}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}=\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}
, we have:

0\leq x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}<0+\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}D_{\mathrm{eff}}+\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\rho D_{\mathrm{eff}}=\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1+\rho).

Combining these bounds on
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}
:

D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\eta_{t}\leq x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}-x_{j}^{0}<\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1+\rho)+\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\eta_{t}

\displaystyle<\frac{\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1+\rho)+\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}}{D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta_{T_{\mathrm{init}}}(1+\rho)}{(1-\rho)}+\frac{c_{0}/\sqrt{n}}{D_{\mathrm{eff}}(1-\rho)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta_{\max}(1+\rho)}{(1-\rho)}+\frac{\tau c_{0}}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}(1-\rho)},

where
\eta_{\max}
is an upper bound on the step sizes (it can be set to
\eta_{1}
since it is non-increasing).
Since
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
by Assumption
3
and the right-hand side is a finite constant
T_{\mathrm{init}}
must be finite, which depends on
c_{0},\|v\|
and the step size
\eta_{t}
.

After
T_{\mathrm{init}}
iterations,
x_{j}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}}\geq 0
, and the subsequent analysis resembles the case where the iterate starts with the correct sign. The overall average
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})
can be decomposed as below:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{init}}}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t}).

For
t\in[1,T_{\mathrm{init}}-1]
,
\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})=-1
(since
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
and
x_{j}^{0}<0
).
For the segment
[T_{\mathrm{init}},T]
of length
\widetilde{T}=T-T_{\mathrm{init}}+1
, the analysis from the proof of Theorem
7
(which adapts Theorem
5
) implies:

\left|\frac{1}{\widetilde{T}}\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{init}}}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n}\right|\leq\frac{2\rho}{1-1.1\rho}+O\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{T}}\right).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}(\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n})+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{init}}}^{T}(\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}{T}(-1-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n})+\frac{\widetilde{T}}{T}\left(\frac{1}{\widetilde{T}}\sum_{t=T_{\mathrm{init}}}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n}\right).

Since
w_{j}^{*}=1/\sqrt{n}
, the first term is
\frac{T_{\mathrm{init}}-1}{T}(-2)
.
Thus, for large
T
,

\left|\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{sign}(x_{j}^{t})-w_{j}^{*}\sqrt{n}\right|\leq\frac{2(T_{\mathrm{init}}-1)}{T}+\frac{T-T_{\mathrm{init}}+1}{T}\left(\frac{2\rho}{1-1.1\rho}+O\left(\frac{1}{T-T_{\mathrm{init}}+1}\right)\right)

\leq\frac{2\rho}{1-1.1\rho}+O\left(\frac{T_{\mathrm{init}}}{T}\right).

Therefore, the
\ell_{\infty}
bound on
\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}
stated in Theorem
7
,

\left\|\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-\overline{\boldsymbol{w}}^{T}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\frac{2\rho}{1-1.1\rho}+\frac{C_{\mathrm{init}}}{T\sqrt{n}},

and the subsequent conclusions regarding exact recovery for sufficiently large
T
, hold even if
x_{j}^{0}
starts within the specified bounded range, as the effect of the initial transient period
T_{\mathrm{init}}
is absorbed into the
C_{\mathrm{init}}/T
term of the bound.
∎

D.2
Last-Iterate Convergence Analysis

Theorem 8

(Last-Iterate Convergence)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
–
3
hold.
Assume that the sample size
N
is large enough such that

N\geq\max\left(C_{1}n^{2},\left(\frac{5(n+4)^{2}(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)

for some positive universal constants
C_{1},C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
.

Then, with probability at least
1-5m\exp(-cn)
for some positive universal constant
c>0
, the iterates
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}
generated by the STE-gradient process with
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\left[-\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]^{n}
for a given
c_{0}>0
visit the target state
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often. Furthermore, assuming the noise variables
\xi^{(i)}
are independent of the data and across samples, continuous, and not a.s. zero, then with
\|v\|_{0}:=|\operatorname{supp}(\boldsymbol{v})|=|\{j\in[m]:v_{j}\neq 0\}|
,
with probability at least

\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|v\|_{0}N-1}-5m\exp(-cn)

over the draw of the dataset and the noise
\{(\boldsymbol{Z}^{(i)},\xi^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the iterates also depart from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
infinitely often.

Proof of Theorem
8
.

We first note that the conclusions of this theorem regarding infinite visits and departures are robust to bounded non-zero initializations
\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\left[-\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}},\frac{c_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}\right]^{n}
. As shown in the analysis for Corollary
2
, any initial phase where
x_{j}^{0}
has an incorrect sign lasts for a finite time
T_{\mathrm{init}}
. The subsequent asymptotic analysis of recurrence and departure is unaffected by this finite transient period.

The proof adapts arguments from the constant step size case (Theorem
6
) using properties established under Assumption
3
.
First, the sample size requirement in Theorem
8
implies
that
\rho:=\frac{(C^{\prime}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}^{2}+C^{\prime\prime}K_{\xi}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1})n}{2\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\sqrt{N}}<1/(n+M_{0})
where
M_{0}=1.1
by an algebraic computation. This in turn implies that
L_{M_{0}}+1>n
, where
L_{M_{0}}=\lceil(1-(1+M_{0})\rho)/\rho\rceil=\lceil(1-2.1\rho)/\rho\rceil
. Here
L_{M_{0}}
, rather than the constant-step-size quantity
L
, denotes the minimum correct-phase length in the general step-size setting; the use of this
L_{M_{0}}
and
M_{0}=1.1
is justified in Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
7
.

Part 1: Infinite Visits to
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}

This relies on showing the fraction of time spent at
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
is positive. The cycle analysis presented in the proof of Theorem
7
establishes that, after a finite coordinate-dependent burn-in, each coordinate has cycles consisting of one incorrect step and at least
L_{M_{0}}
correct steps. Taking the common burn-in time
T_{\mathrm{init}}^{\max}:=\max_{j\in[n]}T_{\mathrm{init},j}
, which is finite since
n
is finite, makes this cycle structure hold simultaneously for all coordinates. Hence, up to the vanishing transient contribution
O(T_{\mathrm{init}}^{\max}/T)
, the fraction of time where
\boldsymbol{w}^{t}\neq\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
is asymptotically bounded above by
n/(L_{M_{0}}+1)
. Since the condition
\rho<1/(n+M_{0})
ensures
L_{M_{0}}+1>n
, this fraction is strictly less than 1. Therefore, the fraction of time spent exactly at
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
is asymptotically bounded below by
c=1-n/(L_{M_{0}}+1)>0
, making the rest of the argument in Part 1 of the proof of Theorem
6
applicable.

Part 2: Infinite Departures from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}

This argument demonstrates that iterates cannot remain permanently at
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
on the event

A:=\{\exists j\in[n]\text{ such that }w_{j}^{*}[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}>0\}.

By Lemma
6
, the event
A
occurs with probability at least
\frac{1-2^{-\|v\|_{0}N}}{2}
.

Assume we are in such a realization for some coordinate
j
. Suppose, for contradiction, that
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
for all
u\geq t^{\prime}
. Then
x_{j}^{k}=x_{j}^{k-1}-\eta_{k}[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}
.
Summing from
k=t^{\prime}+1
to
T+1
gives

x_{j}^{T+1}=x_{j}^{t^{\prime}}-[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}\sum_{k=t^{\prime}+1}^{T+1}\eta_{k}.

Since
[\tilde{\nabla}_{\boldsymbol{w}}L(\boldsymbol{w}^{*})]_{j}
has the same sign as
w_{j}^{*}
on
A
and
\sum_{k}\eta_{k}=\infty
by Assumption
3
, the coordinate
x_{j}^{k}
must eventually cross the quantization boundary in the direction opposite to
w_{j}^{*}
. This contradicts
\boldsymbol{w}^{u}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
for all
u\geq t^{\prime}
.

Combining the event
A
with the finite-sample approximation event from Theorem
3
, which holds with probability at least
1-5m\exp(-cn)
, gives the departure probability lower bound

\frac{1}{2}-2^{-\|v\|_{0}N-1}-5m\exp(-cn)

Since the visits to
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
are infinite, departures from
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
are also infinite on this event.

∎
