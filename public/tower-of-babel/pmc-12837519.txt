Enhanced therapeutic efficacy of repeated bone marrow–derived MSC administration in a murine model of pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Zihan Zhou, Jiawei Ding, Shuhua Han, Yuanfang Duan, Jie Chao, Jie Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSilicosis is a progressive lung fibrosis lacking effective treatment. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) show antifibrotic potential, but their survival is impaired by the early inflammatory microenvironment. The therapeutic value of repeated MSC administration remains unclear.MethodsA murine silicosis model was analyzed by single-cell RNA sequencing, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cytokine assays, and human Bone Marrow-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (hBMSCs) transcriptomics after BALF exposure. Mice received either single or repeated intratracheal hBMSCs doses. Cell retention, lung function, imaging, histology, and fibrosis markers were assessed. The role of ZC3H4 in macrophage activation was examined by in vivo expression profiling, in vitro knockdown, and functional assays.ResultsEarly silica exposure triggered strong M1 inflammation, high BALF cytokines, and hBMSCs senescence signatures. Repeated hBMSCs dosing improved cell persistence, reduced fibrosis on imaging and histology, enhanced lung function, and decreased collagen deposition compared with a single dose. Mechanistically, MSC therapy suppressed macrophage ZC3H4 expression, while ZC3H4 knockdown reduced macrophage activation and fibroblast migration.ConclusionsRepeated hBMSCs administration enhances therapeutic efficacy in silicosis by improving cell persistence and attenuating fibrosis, partly through ZC3H4-mediated regulation of macrophages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04859-5.Keywords: Pulmonary fibrosis, Silicosis, Mesenchymal stem cells, Repeated administration, Inflammatory microenvironment, Macrophage polarization, ZC3H4

Introduction

Silicosis is a chronic and irreversible interstitial lung disease caused by the prolonged inhalation of free crystalline silica (SiO2) dust. China has one of the highest rates of silicosis due to its large mining and construction sectors [1]. Once silicosis develops, the process of pulmonary fibrosis continues, leading to permanent lung function damage and respiratory impairment. Currently, there is no effective clinical treatment to reverse or halt the progression of silicosis. Treatment primarily focuses on alleviating symptoms and preventing complications, such as the use of antifibrotic drugs, oxygen therapy, and lung transplantation. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapies to treat this complex disease.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a type of cell with self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities. Preclinical experimental studies on pulmonary fibrosis have demonstrated that MSCs can home to the site of lung injury through paracrine signaling. These cells can release and secrete a variety of bioactive molecules that regulate immune responses, reduce pulmonary inflammation and nodule formation, repair damaged lung tissue, decrease lung tissue remodeling, thereby slowing the progression of pneumoconiosis [2]. However, after transplantation, MSCs encounter the pathological microenvironment induced by the disease, where various detrimental factors can compromise cell viability and function. This significantly impairs the inherent therapeutic properties of MSCs, thereby reducing the efficiency and effectiveness of the transplantation.

In our previous study, we utilized a novel gold nanoparticle suitable for CT/NIRF dual-modal imaging to label and track bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). These labeled BMSCs could be tracked for up to 21 days post-transplantation in vivo using NIRF and CT imaging. The transplanted BMSCs were observed to downregulate silica-induced macrophage activation, exhibiting anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects in a silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model [3]. Nevertheless, these therapeutic benefits are often transient, as MSCs exhibit inherent immunogenicity and limited engraftment capacity, leading to rapid clearance by host immune mechanisms regardless of the delivery route, either intravenous or local. [4]. To overcome this limitation, accumulating preclinical evidence indicates that while a single administration of MSCs can mitigate fibrosis, repeated dosing may be required to sustain and amplify therapeutic efficacy. For instance, human embryonic stem cell-derived immunity- and matrix-regulatory cells (IMRCs) exhibited marked anti-fibrotic effects following a single infusion, but these effects declined significantly by day 6 [5]. A dual-dosing regimen—administered on day 1 and day 7—resulted in improved cell persistence and greater histological and functional recovery [6]. Similarly, repeated administration of MSCs derived from adipose tissue or menstrual blood has been shown to produce superior outcomes in experimental models of pulmonary fibrosis compared to single-dose treatment [7–9]. Notably, studies in cardiac injury models have reported analogous findings: repeated stem cell delivery led to enhanced functional recovery and reduced fibrosis, even when the total cell dose was the same as in single-dose groups. These observations reinforce the concept that treatment efficacy is influenced not only by cell type and delivery route, but also by dosing strategy. Together, these findings support a repeated dosing approach as a means to achieve durable therapeutic benefit in stem cell-based interventions for pulmonary fibrosis.

Collectively, these observations suggest that therapeutic efficacy in stem cell-based interventions is determined not only by the cell source and delivery route but also by dosing strategy. They provide a strong rationale for systematically evaluating repeated MSC administration as a means to achieve durable and enhanced therapeutic benefit in pulmonary fibrosis. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms of repeated BMSC administration in a murine model of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis. We hypothesized that repeated BMSC delivery would enhance cell persistence, strengthen anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects, and improve lung function compared with a single administration. These benefits were expected to arise from regulating macrophage activation and rebalancing the inflammatory microenvironment to favor fibrosis resolution.

Methods

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Sample collection

Lung samples for scRNA-seq were collected from four groups of mouse whole lung tissues (NS-7d, SiO2-7d, NS-56d and SiO2-56d) and washed three times rapidly with precooled × *PBS.

Cell capture and cDNA synthesis

Lung tissue was cut into approximately 1 mm2 pieces, and individual cells were obtained using the Lung Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotech, 130–095–927, Germany) and suspended in PBS containing 0.04% BSA, according to the operating protocol instructions. The captured cells (approximately 1 × 104) were placed in individual GEMs for reverse transcription to obtain barcode codes. Reverse transcription was performed on an S1000TM Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) (53 °C, 45 min; 85 °C, 5 min; stored at 4 °C) to obtain cDNA. Finally, the quality was assessed using an Agilent 4200 system (Beijing BIO Biotechnology Co.).

scRNA-seq library preparation

The scRNA-seq libraries were constructed using the Single-Cell 5′ Library and Gel Bead Kit, Single Cell V(D)J Enrichment Kit, and Human T Cell (1000005) and Single Cell V(D)J Enrichment Kit according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 sequencer and required a depth of no less than 100,000 reads per spot, accompanied by a 150-bp paired end (PE150). Sequencing was performed by Beijing BIO Biotechnology Co.

scRNA-seq data pre-processing and analysis

Cell barcode filtering, comparison and UMI counting were performed using Cell Ranger 4.0.0 (https://www.10xgenomics.com/). Further analysis was conducted with the R package Seurat v3.2.2 and was based primarily on the official tutorial [10]. The double linkage rate (the nExp parameter in DoubletFinder) was estimated from the user guide for 10 × Chromium. For quality control and filtering, the R package DoubletFinder was used to detect and remove doublets [11]; cells with detected genes less than 500 or with mitochondrial gene content > 20% were excluded, and genes presented in less than 10 cells were deleted. The scRNA-seq data were normalized using Log Normalize (scale factor 10,000), two thousand highly variable genes were identified, and normalized counts were scaled by default. We performed principal component analysis (PCA) for primary dimensionality reduction with 30 dimensions, which were selected based on the elbow plot. Batch effects among four samples were alleviated with Harmony [12]. Thirty-three Clusters were identified by the FindNeighbors (based on KNN graphs) and FindClusters (based on Louvain method, resolution = 1) functions in Seurat. Harmony embeddings were used as the input for t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) [Journal of Machine Learning Research 9 (2008) 2579–2605], which allows data visualization in a two-dimensional space. Cell-type annotation was conducted with the manually curated cell type markers and 33 clusters were merged into 20 cell types. Harmonic embeddings were used as input to t-SNE, which allows for data visualisation in two dimensions. The differential expression levels of genes were calculated using the FindMarkers, which is based on the Wilcoxon ranking and test with default parameters.

Cell culture

Human BMSCs isolated from bone marrow aspirates were purchased from Nanjing Drum Tower Clinical Stem Cell Center. The cells were cultured in T75 flasks in DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and 2 mM l-glutamine at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium was changed every three days. BMSCs at P3-8 were used for all experiments.

The human monocytic cell line THP-1 (ATCC) was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, streptomycin (100 μg/mL), and penicillin (100 U/mL) in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. Then, 50 nM phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) was used to stimulate 6 × 105 THP-1 cell per well differentiation for 24 h before the experiments.

Animal experiments

All animals were maintained on a 12:12 h light/dark cycle under constant temperature (23 °C) and humidity (50%) conditions with free access to food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee at Southeast University and were performed in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Male C57BL/6 mice(6–8 weeks old, ~ 30 g) were randomly selected from cages and allocated into four groups (n = 6 per group): control, SiO₂, SiO₂ + SD BMSCs, and SiO₂ + RD BMSCs. After anesthesia induced by pentobarbital sodium (1%, 50 mg/kg), mice were intratracheally administered 100 μL of a silica suspension, except for those in the control group, which received 100 μL of saline. Mice in the control and SiO₂ groups were intratracheally administered 100 μL of sterile saline. In the SiO₂ + SD BMSCs group, 1 × 10⁶ bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) suspended in 100 μL of saline were administered via intratracheal instillation on day 7 post-silica exposure. In the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group, mice received intratracheal instillations of BMSCs (1 × 10⁶ cells in 100 μL) on both day 7 and day 28 following silica administration. On day 56 after model induction, all animals were humanely euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (≥ 150 mg/kg) to minimize the pain, and lung tissues were subsequently collected for downstream analyses. Animal experiments executed in this study were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethical Inspection Form of Southeast University (approval no. 20220228013). The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

CT imaging

CT imaging in this study was captured by a micro-CT system (Hiscan XM) followed these scanning parameters: voltage, 60 kV; electricity,133 μA; scanning resolution, 50 μm; single exposure time, 1.2 s; scanning circle, 360°; scanning angle interval, 0.5°. The reconstruction and analysis software programs were provided by Hiscan.

NIR fluorescence imaging

In vivo NIR fluorescence images of ICG (704 nm excitation and 735 nm filter) were obtained with a Maestro in Vivo Imaging System (CRi Inc., USA).

Histopathological examination

Mice were sacrificed, and the organs were quickly isolated, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in OCT and cut into 5-mm-thick sections. For histological analysis, tissue sections on glass slides were subjected to H&E staining to evaluate general pathology, and to Sirius Red and Masson's trichrome staining to assess collagen deposition and fibrosis, following the protocols provided by the manufacturers.

Fibrosis severity was quantified using the modified Ashcroft scoring system based on Sirius Red–stained lung sections. Scores ranged from 0 (normal lung structure) to 8 (severe architectural distortion with extensive collagen deposition). Two experienced pathologists, blinded to group allocation, independently evaluated ≥ 5 randomly selected fields per mouse, and the average score was used for statistical analysis. Inflammatory infiltration was assessed on H&E-stained lung sections using a semi-quantitative scale (0–4) according to inflammatory cell density and peribronchial/perivascular accumulation: 0 = no infiltration; 1 = minimal; 2 = mild; 3 = moderate; 4 = severe infiltration. A minimum of 5 randomly selected fields per mouse were scored in a blinded manner.

Pulmonary function test

The mice with anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium, a tracheal catheter was inserted and fastened to the trachea, and then the trachea was exposed to assess pulmonary function. Then, including IC, FVC, FEVand TLC were tested with the Forced Manoeuvres System (EMMS, Hants, UK). Each mouse underwent three measurements before being sacrificed for collection of lung samples for further study.

ELISA

ELISA assays were performed using commercial Mouse TNF-α, IL-6, and CXCL2 ELISA kits (Proteintech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples and standards were incubated in pre-coated plates at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated detection antibodies for 40 min at 37 °C. After TMB substrate development for 15 min at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Western blotting

Cells (or lung tissue) were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (10–30 μg per lane) were separated on 8–12% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) using a wet transfer system (300 mA, 60–90 min).

Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-FN1 (1:1000, affinity, Cat#AF5335), anti-Collagen I (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat#66761-1-1 g), anti-α-SMA (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 18985-1-AP), anti-ZC3H4 (1:1000, abcam, Cat# ab174962), β-actin (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat#66009-1-Ig), and anti-GAPDH (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# 60004-1-Ig). After TBST washing (3 × 8 min), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP, 1:2000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2; goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP, 1:2000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Tanon, China) and imaged with a Tanon 5200 Chemiluminescence Imaging System. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH) and normalized to GAPDH.

Immunofluorescence

Treated cells and lung tissue Sects. (8 μm) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After blocking with 10% normal goat serum for 2 h at room temperature, samples were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-NOS2 (1:200, Proteintech, Cat#18985-1-AP), anti-Arginase-1 (1:200, Proteintech, Cat#16001-1-AP), anti-F4/80 (1:200, Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-52664), and anti-ZC3H4 (1:200, Abcam, Cat# ab174962). After washing, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies (1:250, Invitrogen, USA) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by DAPI counterstaining for nuclear visualization.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Unpaired numerical data were compared using an unpaired t-test (two groups) or analysis of variance (ANOVA; more than two groups). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0(GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals inflammatory immune landscape in early pulmonary fibrosis

Pulmonary fibrosis is a progressive disease characterized by an early inflammatory phase, subsequent fibrotic lesion formation, and later structural remodeling with functional decline. To capture these dynamic stages, we established a murine model via intratracheal instillation of silica particles, with saline-treated mice as controls. Lung tissues were harvested at days 7 and 56 post-exposure, representing the inflammatory and fibrotic phases, respectively, and subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Given that intratracheally administered stem cells are directly exposed to the diseased pulmonary microenvironment, we first focused on characterizing the immune milieu using our previously published scRNA-seq dataset. Transcriptomic profiles from four groups—NS-7d, SiO₂-7d, NS-56d, and SiO₂-56d—were normalized, integrated, and subjected to graph-based clustering, identifying 24 distinct cellular clusters [13]. These were annotated into 20 cell types based on canonical markers from the CellMarker database and visualized via t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) (Fig. 1A).

On day 7, silica exposure led to a robust expansion of macrophages—especially M1-like subsets—as well as increased dendritic cells, while neutrophils and NK cells were reduced. By day 56, elevated levels of both M1 and M2 macrophages persisted, accompanied by an expansion of fibroblasts and IgA⁺ AT2 cells, and a marked decline in T cells, NK cells, and monocytes, consistent with a transition toward chronic inflammation and fibrosis (Fig. 1B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of inflammatory signaling pathways in the SiO₂-7d group, particularly those related to cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, and interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling (Fig. 1C). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for Molecular Function (MF) further highlighted the upregulation of pathways involved in cytokine activity, chemokine activity, and receptor binding. Notably, in the SiO₂-56d group, inflammatory and chemokine-mediated signaling pathways remained activated, alongside the upregulation of collagen metabolism pathways (Fig. 1D–E). To quantify immune activation and fibrotic remodeling at the single-cell level, we applied scoring algorithms. Immune scores highlighted M1 macrophages as the most immunologically active cells at day 7, with dendritic cells and M2 macrophages also contributing to inflammation. At day 56, the highest immune activity was observed in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myofibroblasts, suggesting a cellular shift toward tissue remodeling and fibrosis (Fig. 1F). Fibrosis scores corroborated these findings, showing marked increases in fibroblasts and IgA⁺ AT2 cells in the SiO₂-56d group (Fig. 1G). Analysis of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (Tnf, Il6, and Cxcl2) further confirmed their significant upregulation in the SiO₂ group, predominantly within newly emerged macrophage populations (Fig. 1H).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis involves a sustained yet evolving inflammatory response, peaking at day 7 and persisting through day 56. M1-polarized macrophages drive early inflammation, while fibroblasts and epithelial subsets progressively shape the fibrotic niche, outlining a dynamic immune–fibrotic landscape that may inform the timing and mechanisms of stem cell–based interventions.

Limited retention of BMSCs after single intratracheal administration

Given the limited persistence of transplanted BMSCs, the timing of administration is considered critical for therapeutic efficacy. Previous studies have shown that administering stem cell transplantation during the early inflammatory phase significantly improves the treatment efficacy for pulmonary fibrosis. However, in practical applications, the highly inflammatory and oxidative microenvironment at the early stage restricts stem cell survival and function, impairs their homing efficiency, and diminishes their therapeutic efficacy [3, 5]. To characterize the early inflammatory niche in greater detail, we quantified representative proinflammatory cytokines in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collected at 7, 28, and 56 days after silica instillation. As shown in Fig. 2A, TNF-α, IL-6, and CXCL2 levels were markedly elevated at day 7, then progressively declined at later timepoints, indicating a transient but intense inflammatory surge following silica exposure. These results corroborate the early scRNA-seq findings and confirm that transplanted stem cells are likely to encounter a highly proinflammatory environment shortly after administration.

To further elucidate how this inflammatory environment affects the function of transplanted stem cells at the molecular level, we conducted transcriptomic profiling of human Bone Marrow-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (hBMSCs) exposed to early-stage inflammatory conditions. On day 7 post-instillation, BALF was collected from SiO₂-exposed mice and applied to cultured hBMSCs in vitro. Total RNA was extracted from the treated hBMSCs and subjected to absolute quantitative transcriptome sequencing (Fig. 2B). GO enrichment analysis of the sequencing data revealed significant upregulation of pathways associated with cell division, DNA repair, and cell cycle progression. KEGG pathway analysis further confirmed the enrichment of genes involved in the cell cycle, motor proteins, and cellular senescence (Fig. 2C, D). In contrast, pathways related to signal transduction, cell adhesion, and cellular responses to hypoxia were significantly downregulated (Fig. 2E, F). These transcriptomic alterations suggest that exposure to the early inflammatory microenvironment may drive abnormal proliferation of hBMSCs and compromise their stemness, ultimately impairing their reparative function. Additionally, the downregulation of response-related pathways may limit their capacity to survive, adapt, and engraft effectively in vivo, thereby reducing therapeutic efficacy. Together, these findings highlight the limitations of single-dose administration during the early inflammatory phase and provide a rationale for implementing a repeated dosing strategy to enhance stem cell persistence and therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, we designed repeated administration strategy to overcome the limitations of early-phase transplantation, aiming to enhance stem cell persistence and improve therapeutic outcomes (Fig. 3A).

Repeated intratracheal delivery of BMSCs enhances pulmonary retention

To evaluate whether repeated intratracheal administration of BMSCs enhances their pulmonary retention, we established a dosing regimen in which silica-exposed mice received either a single BMSC dose (1 × 10⁶ cells on day 7, SD BMSCs) or repeated doses (1 × 10⁶ cells on days 7 and 28, RD BMSCs) (Fig. 3A). Mice underwent serial near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging to monitor labeled BMSC biodistribution and retention over time.

BMSCs were dual-labeled with DiO (green) and AA@ICG@PLL, a multifunctional nanoprobe providing both fluorescence and CT imaging capabilities [3]. In vivo NIRF imaging was conducted on days 7, 21, 28, 42, and 56 post-transplantation. As shown in Fig. 3B, both single-dose and repeated-dose groups exhibited strong initial ICG signals in the thoracic region, confirming successful pulmonary delivery. However, signal intensity in the SD group declined sharply over time, whereas mice receiving RD BMSCs exhibited sustained and robust pulmonary fluorescence signals through day 56. This indicates that repeated delivery significantly prolongs the intrapulmonary persistence of transplanted BMSCs.

To validate these findings at the tissue level, ex vivo lung sections were imaged for DiO and ICG fluorescence. As shown in Fig. 3C, DiO-labeled cells were sparsely detected in the lungs of the SD group, whereas lungs from the RD group displayed dense and co-localized green (DiO) and red (ICG) fluorescence, suggesting improved cell engraftment and survival following repeated administration. DAPI counterstaining confirmed that these signals were localized within lung parenchyma.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that repeated intratracheal dosing markedly improves BMSC retention in fibrotic lungs, potentially by replenishing viable cells after clearance of the initial population, thereby extending the therapeutic window for pulmonary repair.

Repeated BMSC administration confers superior therapeutic efficacy against pulmonary fibrosis compared to a single dose

Given the enhanced cell retention observed with repeated dosing, we next evaluated whether this strategy improves therapeutic outcomes in silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis. First, body weight was monitored longitudinally (Fig. 4A). Compared to the Control group, the SiO₂ group experienced a significant decline in body weight following silica exposure. While both the SiO₂ + Single Dose BMSCs (SD BMSCs) and SiO₂ + Repeated Dose BMSCs (RD BMSCs) groups showed similar trends of weight loss, no significant recovery was observed relative to the SiO₂ group. These findings suggest that BMSC therapy had limited effects on reversing systemic manifestations such as weight loss.

To evaluate the degree of pulmonary fibrosis, in vivo Micro-CT scanning was performed to obtain high-resolution axial images of the mouse lungs (Fig. 4B). The Control group exhibited well-aerated lung fields with homogeneous parenchymal attenuation and normal bronchovascular markings. In contrast, the SiO₂ group showed markedly increased parenchymal attenuation, thickened interlobular septa, and extensive diffuse reticular opacities predominantly involving the perihilar, upper, and middle lobes—radiological features consistent with progressive pulmonary fibrosis. In the SiO₂ + SD BMSCs group, these fibrotic changes were attenuated, with a visible reduction in the extent of reticulation. The SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group demonstrated a further reduction in fibrotic burden, with significantly decreased reticular densities and improved parenchymal aeration. Three-dimensional volumetric reconstruction provided a clearer spatial depiction of fibrotic lesions along the bronchial tree. Following BMSC administration, lesion volume was reduced in both treatment groups, with the most pronounced regression observed in the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group. Quantitative analysis of the air-to-tissue ratio (Fig. 4C) confirmed that BMSC treatment, particularly repeated dosing, significantly improved lung aeration. Then, the mice were sacrificed, lung tissues were harvested following PBS perfusion. As shown in Figure. S1A the lungs of SiO₂ group appeared markedly firm with reduced elasticity. The lung surfaces exhibited multiple ill-defined white nodules and patchy fibrotic lesions. In some areas, tissue shrinkage and depression were observed, and the overall lung volume appeared uneven. These gross morphological changes are indicative of advanced and persistent pulmonary fibrosis. In contrast, lungs from the Control group, as well as those treated with SD or RD BMSCs, appeared uniformly pink, soft, and smooth, without visible lesions, and retained normal structure and elasticity.

Pulmonary function testing was conducted on day 56 to assess key respiratory parameters, including inspiratory capacity (IC), total lung capacity (TLC), forced expiratory volume (FEV), and forced vital capacity (FVC). All parameters were significantly decreased in the SiO₂ group compared to controls, indicating substantial impairment of pulmonary function. Both single and repeated BMSC treatments led to improvements in IC, TLC, FEV, and FVC. Notably, the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group showed superior recovery in TLC, FEV, and FVC compared to the single-dose group (Fig. 4D). In addition, airway resistance, dynamic compliance, and chord compliance were also assessed; however, no clear trends or statistically significant differences were observed among the groups (Figure. S1B).

Together, these results demonstrate that repeated intratracheal administration of hBMSCs not only enhances pulmonary cell persistence but also mitigates fibrotic changes and yields superior structural and functional recovery compared with single-dose administration.

Enhanced histological amelioration of pulmonary fibrosis by repeated BMSC administration

To further evaluate the extent of pulmonary fibrosis, lung tissues from mice were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius Red staining (Fig. 5A). H&E staining revealed that, compared to the Control group, the SiO₂ group exhibited marked inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar structural disruption, and significant thickening of the alveolar septa. Both the SiO₂ + SD BMSCs and SiO₂ + RD BMSCs treatment groups showed alleviated collagen deposition in the lung interstitium, with a more pronounced reduction observed in the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group compared to the SiO₂ + SD BMSCs group. Masson’s trichrome and Sirius Red staining further confirmed collagen accumulation around the alveolar walls and interstitial regions in the SiO₂ group, which was notably improved following BMSC treatment, particularly in the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group. Quantitative histopathology showed that silica exposure significantly increased Ashcroft fibrosis scores compared with controls (6.80 ± 0.84 vs. 0.20 ± 0.45, p < 0.0001). Inflammation scores also rose sharply in the SiO₂ group (3.6 ± 0.55 vs. 0.20 ± 0.45, p < 0.0001). Single-dose BMSC treatment reduced fibrosis and inflammation, while repeated dosing produced a more pronounced decrease (p < 0.05 vs. single-dose), consistent with the histological observations (Fig. 5B and 5C). Additionally, polarized light microscopy of Sirius Red-stained sections (Fig. 5D) revealed scattered green and orange-red birefringent fibers—indicative of type III and type I collagen, respectively—distributed irregularly in the interstitial and perialveolar regions in the SiO₂ group. These signals were markedly reduced in the BMSC-treated groups, especially in the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group.

Finally, the expression of fibrosis-associated proteins in lung tissue was assessed (Fig. 5E and S2A). Compared with the control group, the SiO₂ group showed significantly increased expression of fibronectin (FN1), collagen I, and α-SMA. These levels were significantly reduced in both BMSC treatment groups, with the SiO₂ + RD BMSCs group exhibiting a further decrease compared to the SiO₂ + SD BMSCs group. Since hydroxyproline (HYP) content serves as an indirect indicator of collagen deposition in lung tissue, we further quantified HYP levels in lung homogenates. As shown in Fig. 5F, HYP levels were significantly elevated in the SiO₂ group compared to the control group. Following BMSC treatment, HYP levels in lung tissue were significantly reduced (P < 0.01), with the most pronounced decrease observed in the RD BMSCs group (P < 0.0001).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that repeated intratracheal administration of BMSCs effectively attenuates silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis at the histological, molecular, and biochemical levels, reinforcing the therapeutic advantage of a repeated dosing strategy.

BMSCs suppress macrophage activation via downregulation of ZC3H4 in silicosis

To further explore the mechanism by which BMSCs exert antifibrotic effects, we investigated their impact on immune cell dynamics, particularly macrophage activation, within the fibrotic lung microenvironment. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed a substantial expansion of M1-type macrophages in SiO₂-exposed lungs, especially at day 7 post-exposure (Fig. 6A–B). GO enrichment analysis of M1 macrophage clusters indicated suppressed expression of genes associated with signal transduction, GTPase activity, and actin cytoskeletal organization (Fig. 6C–D). Immune scoring confirmed that M1 macrophages displayed heightened activation in the SiO₂ group compared to controls (Fig. 6E). In line with these findings, immunofluorescence staining and qPCR analysis of lung tissue showed that the M1 marker NOS2 and the M2 marker ARG1 were markedly upregulated in SiO₂-treated mice, whereas both single and repeated BMSC administration progressively reduced their expression (Figure S2B and S2C), further supporting that BMSCs suppress macrophage activation in vivo.

Our previous work identified ZC3H4 as a regulatory factor involved in pulmonary fibrosis. Consistent with this, single-cell RNA-seq data also demonstrated elevated ZC3H4 expression in the silicosis group (Fig. S3A). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that BMSCs may modulate macrophage activation—and thus attenuate fibrosis—by regulating ZC3H4 expression.

To investigate the role of ZC3H4 in silicosis-associated macrophage activation, we first isolated primary alveolar macrophages from BALF of mice and assessed ZC3H4 protein levels. As shown in Figs. 6G and S3B, alveolar macrophages derived from silicosis mice exhibited significantly elevated ZC3H4 expression compared to those from control mice. Immunofluorescence staining of lung tissue further confirmed colocalization of the macrophage-specific marker F4/80 (red fluorescence) and ZC3H4 (green fluorescence), with increased expression of both proteins observed in SiO₂-treated lungs (Fig. 6F). To further elucidate the function of ZC3H4 in macrophage activation, we employed a CRISPR/Cas9-based plasmid system to selectively knock down ZC3H4 expression in THP-1-derived macrophages. As shown in Fig. 6 H, transfection with the ZC3H4-targeting plasmid for 48 h effectively reduced ZC3H4 protein levels by approximately 60% compared to control (Figure S3C). Subsequent exposure of these cells to SiO₂ revealed that ZC3H4 knockdown significantly attenuated the SiO₂-induced upregulation of macrophage activation markers NOS2, ARG1, and SOCS3, as confirmed by Western blotting and densitometric analysis (Figs. 6I and S3D), indicating that ZC3H4 modulates both M1 (NOS2, SOCS3) and M2 (ARG1) macrophage polarization in response to fibrogenic stimuli.

Previous studies have shown that activated macrophages secrete pro-fibrotic cytokines that stimulate fibroblast proliferation and migration, thereby accelerating fibrogenesis [14, 15]. To investigate whether ZC3H4-mediated macrophage activation influences fibroblast behavior, we collected conditioned media from THP-1-derived macrophages and applied it to GFP-labeled human pulmonary fibroblasts (GFP-HPF-a). As shown in Figs. 6J, K, conditioned media from SiO₂-stimulated macrophages significantly enhanced fibroblast migration in a wound healing assay. Notably, this effect was abolished when ZC3H4 expression was silenced in macrophages, indicating that ZC3H4 contributes to macrophage-mediated fibroblast activation.

Finally, to determine whether BMSCs therapy regulates ZC3H4 expression in vivo, we performed Western blot analysis of lung tissues from BMSCs-treated and untreated silicosis mice. BMSCs transplantation significantly reduced the elevated levels of ZC3H4 in fibrotic lung tissues. Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed reduced ZC3H4 expression in the lungs of BMSCs-treated mice compared to the untreated model group (Fig. 7A and S3E). To further validate this effect at the cellular level, we examined whether BMSCs modulate ZC3H4 expression in macrophages. Macrophages stimulated with SiO₂ were treated with BMSCs-conditioned medium (BMSCs-CM) at varying concentrations, and ZC3H4 expression was assessed. As shown in Fig. 7B and D, Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining revealed that ZC3H4 expression was significantly upregulated in macrophages upon SiO₂ stimulation compared to controls. Treatment with BMSCs-CM led to a marked reduction in ZC3H4 expression in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7C). Together, these data suggest that BMSCs downregulate ZC3H4 expression to suppress macrophage activation and reprogram inflammatory polarization. This mechanism may underlie the observed reduction in fibroblast migration and contributes to the overall antifibrotic efficacy of BMSC therapy in silicosis.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a distinct inflammatory peak during the early progression of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis that contributes to the formation of a pro-fibrotic immune microenvironment. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that during this inflammatory phase, the functional responsiveness of transplanted BMSCs—including signal transduction and environmental sensing—was markedly impaired. Consistent with this, BMSCs exhibited limited pulmonary retention after a single administration. However, this limitation was substantially mitigated by repeated cell delivery at day 28 post-modeling. While both single and repeated BMSC treatments alleviated fibrotic pathology and partially improved lung function, repeated dosing significantly enhanced BMSC retention and amplified therapeutic efficacy.

Mechanistically, the benefits of BMSC therapy appear to be largely mediated by immunomodulatory effects. Given the temporal dynamics of macrophage subtypes—where M1 macrophages dominate early inflammation and M2 macrophages contribute to fibrotic remodeling in later stages—BMSCs exert dual-phase anti-fibrotic effects by dampening macrophage activation across both time points. Notably, we identified ZC3H4, a CCCH-type zinc finger protein, as a key regulatory node in macrophage activation. BMSC treatment significantly downregulated ZC3H4 expression, thereby suppressing both M1 and M2 macrophage polarization. This suppression likely contributes to the attenuation of macrophage-driven inflammation and fibrosis. Importantly, repeated BMSC administration enhanced therapeutic benefit without observable adverse effects, likely owing to improved cell persistence and prolonged paracrine signaling within the injured lung.

MSCs are known to modulate diverse immune populations, with macrophages being among the most responsive targets. Direct MSC–macrophage interactions involve multiple mechanisms. For example, human placenta-derived MSCs (PMSCs) promote M2 polarization by secreting IL-10, IL-1β, IL-12, and macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α), as well as activation of glucocorticoid and progesterone receptors [16]. Gingiva-derived MSCs (GMSCs) upregulate the M2 marker CD206, elevate IL-10 production, and enhance phagocytic activity [17]. Moreover, MSCs can secrete insulin-like growth factor-2 (IGF-2), which preconditions macrophages during their maturation for anti-inflammatory polarization [18]. Dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), particularly those derived from inflamed pulp (I-DPSCs), suppress macrophage pro-inflammatory responses through the TNF-α/IDO axis, thereby reducing tissue inflammation [19]. In addition to intrinsic signaling, MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) promote macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-23 and IL-22 [20]. This is achieved through multiple mechanisms, including the transfer of microRNAs that target inflammatory pathways [21], the delivery of regulatory proteins like STAT3 [22], and the donation of mitochondria to enhance oxidative metabolism and phagocytic function [23]. Beyond EVs, MSC-derived apoptotic vesicles (apoVs) also contribute to immune regulation. Calreticulin exposed on apoV surfaces serves as a key “eat-me” signal, facilitating efferocytosis by macrophages. These apoVs reprogram macrophages at the transcriptional level, suppressing their accumulation and promoting an M2 phenotype [24]. Together, these findings illustrate the multifaceted ways in which MSCs orchestrate macrophage function and polarization, laying the foundation for further exploration into the molecular mediators involved in this process.

Recent studies have identified ZC3H4, a novel CCCH-type zinc finger protein, as a pivotal regulator in the pathogenesis of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis. In alveolar epithelial cells, ZC3H4 facilitates epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) via endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, with its expression modulated through the circZC3H4/miR-212 axis [25]. In macrophages, ZC3H4 contributes to proinflammatory activation and, through paracrine interactions, enhances fibroblast proliferation and migration [26]. Infiltrating monocytes exhibit reduced anti-fibrotic activity under the influence of ZC3H4, primarily due to suppressed IL-10 secretion via autophagy [27]. Meanwhile, in pulmonary fibroblasts, ZC3H4 activates a Sigmar1-dependent ER stress pathway that promotes myofibroblast activation and forms a self-reinforcing feedback loop [28]. Notably, ZC3H4 also plays a role in silica-induced endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), again mediated by ER stress and autophagy [29]. In line with these findings, our study demonstrates that bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) can attenuate macrophage activation by downregulating ZC3H4, thereby indirectly limiting fibroblast migration. Collectively, these results underscore the central role of ZC3H4 in coordinating fibrotic signaling across multiple cellular compartments and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target in silicosis.

Although single-dose MSC therapy has shown benefits in various precliniacal models, accumulating evidence supports the superiority of repeated administration. Multiple studies have shown that dual or repeated delivery of MSCs—including adipose-derived (AD-MSCs), menstrual blood-derived (MenSCs), and umbilical cord-derived cells—yields greater suppression of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic markers, restores MMP–TIMP balance, and inhibits EMT. Notably, dual administration of AD-MSCs at early and delayed time points was more effective than single doses in reducing fibrosis and preserving alveolar integrity [7, 8]. Similarly, MenSCs alleviated fibrosis by suppressing fibroblast activation and protecting epithelial cells from apoptosis through cytokine modulation [9]. These findings underscore the potential of repeated MSC therapy in targeting both inflammation and tissue remodeling in lung fibrosis. Studies in ischemic and cardiac injury models show that repeated MSC infusions—particularly when combined with local delivery—enhance engraftment, tissue repair, and immunomodulation, characterized by increased Tregs, reduced NK cell activity, and M2 macrophage polarization [30]. In dilated cardiomyopathy, dual hUCMSC dosing more effectively improved cardiac function and reduced fibrosis, partly through IDO-mediated Treg induction and anti-inflammatory effects [31]. Clinical data also indicate a dose- and frequency-dependent relationship, where higher doses of MSCs result in greater improvements in organ function, accompanied by a favorable safety profile and a marked attenuation of pulmonary function decline, likely due to cumulative paracrine effects such as anti-apoptotic, anti-fibrotic, and immunoregulatory factor secretion. Given the transient presence of MSCs in vivo, repeated infusions may act by repeatedly “educating” immune cells to sustain therapeutic effects over time [32].

The present study has certain limitations that should be considered. First, the C57BL/6 mouse model of silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of human disease, which may limit clinical translation. Second, the study mainly assessed short- to mid-term outcomes, and the long-term efficacy and safety of repeated stem cell administration remain unclear. Third, variability between batches of human stem cells could affect reproducibility and therapeutic effects. Finally, although we analyzed key immune cells and inflammatory factors, the full complexity of the pulmonary immune microenvironment was not captured. Taking these limitations into account, our results still highlight the antifibrotic potential of repeated intratracheal administration of BMSCs over 28 days in a murine model of silicosis. This therapeutic benefit was accompanied by suppression of macrophage activation and fibroblast migration, potentially mediated through BMSC-induced downregulation of ZC3H4. Collectively, these findings support the strategy of repeated BMSCs administration to boost and prolong therapeutic outcomes, particularly through reinforced immunomodulation and paracrine signaling.

Conclusion

Our data demonstrate that repeated intratracheal BMSCs administration enhances pulmonary retention, improves immunomodulatory efficacy, and more effectively suppresses fibrosis compared to single dosing. These effects are mediated, at least in part, through downregulation of ZC3H4, a central regulator of macrophage activation and fibroblast–macrophage crosstalk. These results underscore the importance of dosing strategies and molecular targeting in optimizing stem cell-based therapies for pulmonary fibrosis.
