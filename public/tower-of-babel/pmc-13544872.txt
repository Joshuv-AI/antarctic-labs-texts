Efficacy of Aerobic Versus Non-aerobic Exercise in Mild Cognitive Impairment: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Based on RCTs
Authors: Qin Zhou, Yixia Chen, Mengyi Qian, Xiaoling Feng, Wen Shi, Zeqi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:This study reviewed relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to meta-analyze the effects of aerobic versus non-aerobic exercise in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI).Methods:RCTs comparing aerobic and non-aerobic exercise on cognitive function in patients with MCI were identified through searches of four databases—PubMed, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library—from January 2000 to July 2025, in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Effect sizes were assessed using the mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence interval (CI).Results:A total of 15 studies were included, which comprised 1,192 older adults with MCI. Compared with the non-aerobic exercise group, the aerobic exercise group had greater peak oxygen uptake (MD = 3.99 ml/kg/min; 95% CI: (2.01, 5.96); P < 0.001), shorter trail making test-B (TMT-B) completion time (MD = –9.57 s; 95% CI: (–15.43, –3.70); P = 0.001), and improved delayed recall (MD = 0.90; 95% CI: (0.47, 1.33); P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) and mini-mental state examination (MMSE) scores (both P > 0.05).Conclusions:Aerobic exercise improves cognitive flexibility, cardiorespiratory fitness, and information consolidation and extraction in older adults with MCI compared with non-aerobic exercise, but it does not significantly affect overall cognitive function.

Introduction

Dementia refers to an acquired syndrome characterized by a significant decline in cognitive abilities compared with previous levels.[1] It is now the seventh leading cause of death globally and imposes a substantial burden on individuals and communities.[2] The earliest clinical features of dementia often presented as mild cognitive impairment (MCI), also referred to as mild neurocognitive disorder (mNCD), which represents a transitional state between normal aging and dementia. Patients typically exhibit cognitive decline beyond what is expected for their age but do not meet the diagnostic criteria for dementia.[3] Factors including neurodegenerative changes, vascular damage, nutritional metabolism, sex, and educational level are associated with an increased risk of dementia. The prevalence of MCI among individuals aged 60 years and older is estimated at 15%–20%,[45] with an annual progression rate to dementia of 8%–15%.[6] Therefore, early intervention is critical for slowing disease progression. Nonpharmacological interventions for MCI include noninvasive brain stimulation, computerized cognitive training, physical exercise, and health education.

In recent years, exercise has been increasingly recognized as beneficial in delaying cognitive decline; however, the relationship between exercise modality and its effectiveness remains unclear. A network meta-analysis by Huang et al.’s[7] suggested that resistance exercise (a non-aerobic modality) may be the most effective for slowing cognitive decline in patients with cognitive dysfunction; however, this was based on indirect comparisons rather than head-to-head trials. Venegas-Sanabria[8] reported that only multicomponent physical activity programs incorporating aerobic exercise could improve overall cognitive function in individuals with MCI. However, their analysis included only three relevant randomized controlled trials (RCTs), resulting in a small sample size and limited strength of evidence. Given these limitations of previous studies, the present study sought to conduct a meta-analysis including more relevant RCTs to directly evaluate the effects of aerobic exercise versus non-aerobic exercise on cognitive function in elderly individuals with MCI.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was preregistered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251047052).[9]

Search strategy and study selection

Four databases—PubMed, Web of Science, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library—were searched from January 2000 to July 2025 to identify RCTs examining the effects of aerobic exercise in comparison to non-aerobic exercise on cognitive function in older adults with MCI. The search terms utilized a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms. The search formula was: (aerobic exercises OR cardiovascular training OR mind-body exercise OR MBE OR tai chi OR pilates OR cycling OR swimming OR running OR walking) AND (non-aerobic exercises OR physical activities OR physical exercises OR physical training OR physical therapy OR resistance exercise training OR plyometric exercise OR endurance exercise OR flexibility exercise OR strength training) AND (mild cognitive impairment OR MCI OR cognitive dysfunction). In addition, Google was additionally searched to identify potentially relevant articles. The above process was conducted independently by two reviewers and disagreements were resolved by mutual agreement.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were defined according to PICOS principles: (1) participants with MCI; (2) aerobic exercise as the intervention; (3) non-aerobic exercise as the control; (4) outcome measures including cognitive function assessments such as memory, perception, and attention; and (5) RCT design.

Articles that met any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) not written in English; (2) not peer-reviewed or full text unavailable; (3) included research subjects with pathological dementia, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or Parkinson’s disease (PD); (4) duplicate or updated studies; or (5) specific data on outcome measures cannot be statistically analyzed.

Definition

Types of interventions

Included studies evaluated structured aerobic training interventions. Aerobic exercise is defined as physical activity that relies primarily on aerobic metabolism and involves major muscle groups under adequate oxygen supply.[10] Examples include walking, jogging, swimming, cycling, and traditional Chinese fitness methods such as Tai Chi and Ba Duan Jin.

Types of controls

Control groups involved non-aerobic exercise, defined as any structured physical activity other than aerobic exercise, including strength training, resistance training, stretching and relaxation, functional training, gait or balance training, and physical therapy.[1112]

Cognitive function assessment

The Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) is a single-page, 30-point test completed within 10 minutes.[13] It assesses eight cognitive domains, including memory, visuospatial ability, executive function, and verbal fluency. The cut-off score is generally set at 26 points (25 points or below indicates cognitive impairment), with higher scores indicating more normal cognitive function.

The mini-mental state examination (MMSE) is a simplified 30-point scale assessing cognitive mental status, excluding questions related to mood, abnormal psychological experiences, and thought patterns.[14] A higher score indicates better cognitive function.

The trail making test (TMT), consisting of Parts A and B, is administered in a paper-and-pencil format. Participants are required to connect a series of markers according to specific rules, with timing often measured in seconds. This test quantifies visual search, attention, and executive function. TMT-A reflects attention, visual scanning, and motor speed, whereas TMT-B reflects cognitive flexibility, working memory, and executive control function. Longer completion times indicate poorer performance.

Peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) refers to the maximum oxygen uptake achieved by a subject during a rapid incremental exercise test. The average value of the last 20 seconds or 3–5 breaths before the end of the exercise is taken and reported as an absolute value (ml·min⁻¹ or l·min⁻¹) and a weight-indexed value (ml·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹).[15] VO₂peak is the gold standard for assessing cardiorespiratory function, with higher values indicating better cardiorespiratory endurance in the subject.

The Stroop color-word test (SCWT) is a widely used test in the field of neuropsychology, designed to assess an individual’s ability to inhibit cognitive interference. The interference resistance score is calculated as total time + ((total time/100) × number of errors).[16] The longer the time taken and the greater the number of errors, the higher the interference resistance score, indicating a weaker ability to inhibit interference.

Delayed recall evaluates the ability to freely recall learned information after an interval of time (usually 5–30 minutes). Lower scores indicate a higher risk of progression from MCI to AD.

The auditory verbal learning test (AVLT) is a classic procedural memory assessment tool used to evaluate an individual’s ability to learn, retain, and retrieve verbal information. The two most commonly cited measures are the AVLT-learning score and the AVLT-IR.[17] The AVLT-learning score is calculated by summing the number of words correctly recalled by the participant across the five learning trials (Trials 1–5). A higher score indicates stronger encoding and cumulative learning abilities. AVLT-IR indicates the number of words accurately recalled by the participant during free recall immediately following Trial 5. A higher score indicates greater working memory capacity and immediate retrieval efficiency.

Data extraction

Based on predesigned forms, two researchers independently extracted study characteristics, including authors, year, country, intervention measures, control measures, and patient characteristics (such as age, gender, cognitive status), and measurement results.

Risk of bias within studies

Risk bias was assessed using the Cochrane tool. For RCTs, the Cochrane Collaboration guidelines specify six areas: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, participant blinding, assessor blinding, incomplete outcome data, and selective outcome reporting. The potential risk of bias in each area was classified as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear.”

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.3. Continuous variables were analyzed using mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) to assess the effects of aerobic exercise on cognitive function in patients with MCI. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Chi-square test and quantified with the I² statistic. Values of I² <50% indicated low heterogeneity, 50%–75% moderate heterogeneity, and >75% high heterogeneity. Given potential variability across studies (e.g. baseline characteristics, exercise intensity, and frequency), a random-effects model was used to enhance the reliability of the results. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, and sensitivity analyses were conducted by sequentially excluding individual studies. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Study selection process

The screening process is illustrated in Figure 1. The initial search identified 6,521 studies. After removing duplicates using EndNote, 4,581 studies remained. By reviewing the titles and abstracts, 4,550 studies with low relevance to this meta-analysis were excluded, leaving 31 studies for full-text review. Of these, 16 were excluded due to not meeting RCT criteria (n = 1), unavailable full text (n = 1), insufficient outcome data (n = 5), or being updated RCTs (n = 9). Quality assessment

All 15 studies met high-quality criteria. Most studies clearly outlined their allocation concealment and used standardized methods for random sequence generation, minimizing selection bias. Twelve studies employed blinded outcome assessment, resulting in an overall low risk of detection bias. All studies reported reasons for withdrawal, indicating low attrition bias. Except for one study, all reported planned outcomes, suggesting a low risk of reporting bias. Detailed information on bias assessment is provided in Supplementary Figures 1 and 2.

Effects of aerobic and non-aerobic exercise on MCI

Global cognition

A total of nine trials assessed global cognitive function using different assessment tools. The results were analyzed in groups based on the assessment tool. Four studies using MoCA [Figure 2] showed no significant difference between aerobic exercise and non-aerobic exercise (MD = 0.51; 95% CI: (−1.00, 2.03); P = 0.51; I2 = 79%). Five studies using the MMSE [Figure 3] also showed no statistically significant difference in MMSE scores between the two groups (MD = 0.29; 95% CI: (–0.39, 0.97); P = 0.40; I2 = 67%).

Executive function

TMT-A or TMT-B was used to assess executive function in elderly participants with MCI before and after the intervention [Figure 4]. Subgroup analysis showed no significant effect on TMT-A performance, and overall heterogeneity between studies was low (MD = –1.13; 95%CI: (–5.80, 3.53); P = 0.63; I2 = 38%). However, for TMT-B, the aerobic exercise group completed the task in a shorter time and performed better overall than the non-aerobic exercise group (MD = –9.57S; 95% CI: (–15.43, –3.70); P = 0.001; I2 = 0%).

Cardiorespiratory function

Cardiorespiratory fitness was measured by VO2peak [Supplementary Figure 7]. Pooled analysis of three studies showed significantly higher VO2peak in the aerobic exercise group compared with the control group (MD = 3.99 ml/kg/min; 95% CI: (2.01, 5.96); P < 0.001; I2 = 0%).

Information extraction capability

The delayed recall task was used to assess participants’ information consolidation and retrieval abilities [Supplementary Figure 8]. The pooled analysis results showed that, compared with the non-aerobic exercise control group, the aerobic exercise intervention group performed better (MD = 0.90; 95% CI: (0.47, 1.33); P < 0.001; I2 = 0%).

Interference resistance and memory checking ability

The SCWT assessed participants’ ability to inhibit cognitive interference [Supplementary Figure 3]. The results showed no significant differences in SCWT scores between the control and intervention groups. The AVLT assessed episodic memory [Supplementary Figure 4]. There were no significant differences in either AVLT-IR or AVLT-learning scores between groups.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analyses for the MoCA and MMSE scales, conducted by excluding each study individually, showed stable results. Funnel plots for MoCA [Supplementary Figure 5] and MMSE [Supplementary Figure 6] were approximately symmetrical, indicating no significant publication bias.

Discussion

Through this meta-analysis, we confirmed that aerobic exercise, compared with non-aerobic exercise, did not significantly improve overall cognitive function (assessed using the MoCA and MMSE scales) in older adults with MCI. Subgroup analysis showed no significant improvement in sustained attention (assessed via TMT-A). However, aerobic exercise significantly enhanced cognitive flexibility (assessed via TMT-B). The aerobic exercise group also demonstrated significantly improved cardiopulmonary fitness (quantified via VO₂peak) and enhanced information consolidation and retrieval (assessed via delayed recall). Both exercise forms showed no significant differences in participants’ ability to inhibit cognitive interference (assessed via SCWT) or episodic memory (assessed via AVLT).

Brain neuroplasticity underpins cognitive function. Regular exercise promotes neurogenesis, improves cerebral blood flow and vascular health, regulates systemic metabolism, and suppresses inflammation. Aerobic exercise primarily relies on aerobic metabolism to supply energy during activity, involving rhythmic, moderate- to low-intensity movements of major muscle groups under conditions of adequate oxygen availability.[10] Non-aerobic exercise (predominantly anaerobic) encompasses structured physical activities focused on targeted, full-cycle interventions to alleviate or improve physical functional impairments. Its core distinction from aerobic exercise lies in differing metabolic pathways for energy supply, often relying on rapid energy delivery independent of oxygen.[33] This metabolic distinction directly determines the differences between the two exercise forms.

MCI often serves as a precursor in the early stages of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD. Patients exhibit measurable cognitive deficits but do not meet diagnostic criteria for dementia. Research indicates that the onset of MCI can be explained by hypotheses including the β-amyloid (Aβ) hypothesis, hyperphosphorylated tau protein hypothesis, and oxidative stress hypothesis. Aβ is generated through cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein by β-secretase and γ-secretase.[34] Under normal conditions, production and clearance of Aβ are balanced. However, disruption leads to Aβ accumulation in plasma.[35] These aggregates cross the blood–brain barrier mediated by abnormally expressed receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), forming amyloid plaques that cause neuronal dysfunction and death. Persistent neuronal dysfunction and synaptic loss, along with reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF) increase the risk of progression to AD by approximately seven times that of age-matched healthy individuals.[36]

Animal studies show that exercise upregulates mRNA expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF) in rat brains.[37] BDNF binds to the TrkB receptor, activating signaling pathways such as phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) and extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK). This activation promotes synaptic remodeling and neurogenesis in the hippocampus, influences functional brain connectivity, and maintains cognitive function. NGF activates the PI3K/Akt pathway and the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, promoting the aggregation of cytoskeletal proteins, mediating axonal growth and branching of neurons, and regulating synaptic plasticity. Both aerobic and non-aerobic exercise can upregulate neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and NGF, leading to improvements in overall cognitive function. Ultimately, no significant difference was observed in the effects of the two types of exercise on the cognitive function of elderly patients with MCI. Another possible explanation for these findings is that both exercise groups utilized only a single exercise modality. This narrower stimulus profile compared to mixed exercise may not have provided the optimal microenvironment for newly generated neurons, thereby insufficiently activating structural plasticity. As a result, Aβ clearance may not have reached equilibrium, resulting in no significant improvement in overall cognitive function among participants.

Inhibitory control relies on the synergistic interaction between the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC).[38] Previous studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging have observed increased activation in the DLPFC during dual-task conditions before and after training. However, neurostimulation targeting repetitive motor tasks without complex cognitive training fails to cover the prefrontal network. This limitation may prevent precise stimulation of the network, thereby hindering synergistic effects between the two regions. Consequently, such approaches may not enhance performance on highly prefrontal-dependent executive tasks (e.g. SCWT).[39]

Previous research indicates that exercise-induced cognitive improvements exhibit load dependency. Although gains in the visual cortex–thalamus–prefrontal basal neural circuit underlying sustained attention (TMT-A) are limited, enhancements in higher-cognitive-load executive functions (TMT-B) are more pronounced.[40] Furthermore, all participants in this study were elderly, so they were significantly influenced by noncognitive factors. The decline in basic sensorimotor efficiency, such as natural visual deterioration and hand movement slowness, is irreversible. Consequently, the effects of exercise were minimal, with no significant difference observed between the aerobic and non-aerobic exercise groups.

Thus, the present study demonstrated that aerobic exercise did not significantly improve overall cognitive function, the ability to inhibit cognitive interference, or attention persistence in older adults with MCI compared to non-aerobic exercise.

Furthermore, MCI patients often exhibit a 5%–10% reduction in hippocampal volume compared to healthy elderly individuals. The hippocampus, a crucial component of the medial temporal lobe, contains pyramidal cells involved in memory encoding.[41] The loss of these cells contributes to patients’ relative impairment in episodic memory. Xu et al.[42] assessed cognitive function in rats before and after exercise intervention using the Morris water maze test. They observed increased escape latency and platform crossings post-intervention, alongside upregulation of BDNF and TrkB expression. This indicates enhanced learning and memory capacity through activation of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway. Wang et al.’s[43] research demonstrated that prolonged voluntary exercise increased the total number of dendritic spines in the mouse hippocampus while suppressing microglial activation to protect hippocampal synapses. Based on these studies, both exercise regimens promote neurogenesis by enhancing BDNF, activating relevant pathways, and delaying further pyramidal cell loss. Therefore, both approaches yield comparable improvements in episodic memory function among elderly MCI patients, with no significant differences observed. An alternative explanation is that exercise alone cannot reverse neuronal death, rendering it insufficient to restore verbal memory retrieval efficiency (as measured by the AVLT) to normal levels. For instance, Ferrier et al.’s[44] research demonstrated that exercise-treated rats following cardiac arrest failed to reverse hippocampal neuronal death compared to sham-exercise controls.

VO2peak reflects an individual’s oxygen delivery potential; a stronger oxygen supply capacity indicates better cardiopulmonary function. Long-term moderate-intensity aerobic exercise activates the IGF-1/PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in the myocardial cells of MCI patients, promoting the synthesis of cardiac contractile proteins (actin and myosin) and increasing myocardial mitochondrial number and density to enhance energy supply efficiency and cardiac contractility, thereby increasing stroke volume.[45] Concurrently, it regulates heart rate by enhancing vagal tone and suppressing sympathetic nervous system excitability, thereby improving cardiopulmonary function on multiple levels. The intensity and duration of exercise determine that moderate-intensity aerobic exercise outperforms non-aerobic exercise in enhancing cardiopulmonary function. The present study revealed that VO2peak improved in the aerobic exercise group, consistent with previous findings from animal model experiments.

Clinically, cerebral blood supply is often insufficient in patients with MCI.[46] CBF continuously delivers glucose and oxygen to sustain neural activity—specifically, synaptic transmission—enabling the brain to encode, transmit, and integrate information. Widespread reduction in CBF inhibits aerobic respiration in neuronal mitochondria, triggering energy depletion that compromises synaptic function. As synapses, the fundamental units of cognition, fail, leading to overall cognitive decline and resulting in impaired memory, attention deficits, reduced verbal fluency, and diminished executive function. Aerobic exercise enhances nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability through its aerobic components, achieving sustained dilation of cerebral arterioles and elevating steady-state CBF.[47] By contrast, non-aerobic activities such as resistance training provide only intermittent high-pressure stimulation to vascular walls, leading to a short-term increase in CBF.

Aerobic exercise promotes the upregulation of BDNF while significantly increasing CBF, delivering ample oxygen and nutrients. This downregulation of stress hormones creates favorable conditions for BDNF to cross the blood–brain barrier. Upon binding to TrkB receptors on neuronal surfaces, BDNF targets and activates the BDNF–cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) signaling pathway, enhancing the hippocampus–medial temporal lobe system. This brain region forms the core of the intact memory chain, responsible for memory encoding and regulating memory retrieval. Research indicates that after 2 weeks of aerobic exercise, adult mice exhibited a significant increase in new neurons within the hippocampal dentate gyrus.[48] These new neurons specifically integrated into memory networks, significantly enhancing delayed recall ability—consistent with our findings. In contrast, non-aerobic exercises like strength training, while slightly elevating BDNF levels, struggle to effectively cross the blood–brain barrier to act on the hippocampus. One study revealed that after 16 weeks of strength training, hippocampal BDNF levels in elderly individuals with MCI showed no significant improvement. Conversely, the aerobic exercise group experienced an 18% increase in hippocampal BDNF and a 22% improvement in delayed recall accuracy.[49]

The prefrontal–parietal network, critical for cognitive flexibility (TMT-B), is a brain region vulnerable to early damage in MCI yet highly amenable to exercise-induced repair. This cortical area exhibits heightened sensitivity to ischemia, which can readily lead to delayed neural signal transmission. Karakilic et al.[50] revealed that aerobic exercise elevates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels in the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles of rats. Muscle VEGF levels positively correlate with hippocampal VEGF levels, suggesting that aerobic exercise boosts VEGF levels, promotes cerebral capillary neogenesis, and increases resting blood flow perfusion by approximately 20%–30%, while simultaneously improving mitochondrial function and reducing oxidative stress damage in prefrontal neurons. As previously noted, aerobic exercise also enhances cardiopulmonary function and activity-related CBF in MCI patients, thereby contributing to cognitive maintenance.

Our study systematically evaluated and compared the effects of aerobic and non-aerobic exercise on cognitive function in older adults with MCI. Reviewing data from the literature over the past two decades and using RCT-based meta-analysis, we not only elucidated the differences between different types of exercise but also provided a reference basis for nonpharmacological interventions for patients with MCI. Previous meta-analyses were mostly network meta-analyses that included AD patients with low-level evidence and insufficient outcome measures for assessing the effects of different exercise modalities on cognitive function, whereas this study rigorously excluded articles involving patients with pathological dementia such as AD, only enrolled studies focused on MCI patients, and employed multiple outcome measures, including the MoCA and MMSE for quantitative assessment, thereby strengthening the credibility of the study.

However, it is important to highlight that the studies included in this research faced high risks of blinding and performance bias. In addition, there was considerable variability in patient characteristics, exercise intervention regimens, and outcome measures. This heterogeneity hindered the ability to draw definitive results or recommendations on exercise protocols aimed at improving cognitive outcomes. This undermines the credibility of the findings. Furthermore, the included RCTs enrolled participants from both Western countries and China, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the local population.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that aerobic exercise is more effective than non-aerobic exercise in improving cognitive flexibility, cardiopulmonary fitness, and information consolidation and retrieval in older adults with MCI. However, no significant differences were observed between the two exercise types in overall cognitive function, sustained attention, inhibitory control, or episodic memory performance.
