Increased Accuracy of Acetabular Cup Positioning with an Automatic Impaction Device in Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Hyeongmin Song, Chan Young Lee, Taek-Rim Yoon, Kyung-Soon Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditionally, cementless total hip arthroplasty (THA) has been primarily performed using a manual technique involving a hammer and an impactor. Traditionally, cementless total hip arthroplasty (THA) has been primarily performed using a manual technique involving a hammer and an impactor1) (Fig. 1A). Even with the manual technique, no major complications have been reported, and generally favorable outcomes have been achieved.1) However, during cup insertion into the acetabulum, the manual technique may cause temporary limitations in visualization, discrepancies in the applied force, and imbalances in the force vector.2) These factors can result in variations in cup positioning and stability, particularly in achieving the targeted anteversion and inclination angles. Such issues may be more pronounced among less experienced orthopedic surgeons, as it is difficult to maintain consistent force and direction during cup insertion.

Surgical instrumentation for acetabular cup insertion in total hip arthroplasty. (A) Manual hammer and impactor. (B) Automatic impaction device.

Recently, automatic impaction devices have been developed and adopted for acetabular cup insertion, replacing the traditional manual technique3) (Fig. 1B). Using these devices, consistent force and direction can be maintained throughout the cup insertion process.4) Acetabular cup positioning in primary THA is closely related to postoperative complications, including dislocation, impingement, accelerated polyethylene wear, and component loosening.5,6,7,8,9,10,11) Dislocation is the second most common cause of revision surgery after primary THA, and inaccurate acetabular cup positioning is a major risk factor for dislocation.5,12,13)

It remains unclear whether the manual technique or the automatic impaction device provides greater accuracy in acetabular cup insertion. Therefore, this study was designed to compare the accuracy of acetabular cup positioning in primary THA between the manual technique and the automatic impaction device by asking the following research questions: (1) Is there a difference in the accuracy of acetabular cup positioning between the manual technique and the automatic impaction device? and (2) Is there a difference in the rate of intraoperative and postoperative complications when using the automatic impaction device compared with the manual technique? We hypothesized that the automatic impaction device that can provide consistent force and direction would position the acetabular cup within the Lewinnek safe zone more accurately than the manual technique.

METHODS

The retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hwasun Hospital (IRB No. CNUHH-2025-212). This study was conducted as a retrospective study, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

Study Design

Patients who underwent THA at a tertiary hospital were retrospectively evaluated. From May 2009 to March 2023, a total of 77 patients who underwent primary THA with the PINNACLE 1-mm press-fit cementless cup (DePuy Synthes), performed by a single surgeon with specialized training in hip surgery (KSP), were initially identified. There were no restrictions on underlying disease or preoperative diagnosis, and cases included osteoarthritis, osteonecrosis, femoral neck fractures, and sequelae of developmental dysplasia of the hip. Patients were excluded if they underwent revision or conversion from bipolar hemiarthroplasty to THA. In addition, 10 cases that used other surgical approaches, including the direct anterior, posterolateral, or posterior approaches, were excluded, as this study included only patients who underwent THA using the anterolateral approach.14)

Based on these inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 67 cases were included in the study. The first 36 hips were assigned to the manual group, undergoing surgery with the manual technique, while the remaining 31 hips, operated using the automatic impaction device, were assigned to the automatic group. Two researchers (HS and CYL) reviewed the electronic medical record for each case and measured the anteversion and inclination angles of the cup on 1-month postoperative radiographs. There are 6 established methods for measuring radiographic cup anteversion on plain radiographs. Using each method, the proportion of cups positioned within the Lewinnek safe zone was compared between the 2 groups.

In this study, placement of the acetabular cup within the targeted Lewinnek safe zone was considered indicative of high accuracy. The accuracy of cup positioning, the incidence of acetabular screw augmentation, and the occurrence of intraoperative and postoperative complications up to 1 year after surgery were compared between the 2 groups. Intraoperative complications included intraoperative acetabular fracture and femoral fracture, whereas postoperative complications included dislocation, periprosthetic fracture, infection, hematoma, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary thromboembolism.

Surgical Method

Apart from the impaction process, there were no procedural differences between the 2 groups. In the manual technique, the cup was attached to an impactor and inserted by the surgeon through direct hammering, whereas with the automatic impaction device, the cup was connected to an insertion handle and implanted using an automated system (Fig. 2). In this study, the KINCISE system (DePuy Synthes) was used. Unlike the manual technique, the automatic impaction device can provide a consistent direction and magnitude of force to the same location, enabling co-linear impaction, whereas variability during manual impaction may result in off-axis strikes.2,3)

Intraoperative scenes of acetabular cup insertion using the manual technique and the automatic impaction device. (A) Manual technique. (B) Automatic impaction device.

The intraoperative target angles were identical in both groups, with a target anteversion of 15° and a target inclination angle of 40°. Acetabular screw augmentation was additionally performed when adequate press-fit fixation was uncertain. There were no differences between the 2 groups in the subsequent steps, including femoral component placement, liner and head insertion, and stem reduction after full seating of the cup.

Radiologic Evaluation

A standardized protocol was used to acquire 2 types of radiographs for radiological evaluation: anteroposterior (AP) hip radiographs and cross-table lateral radiographs. The acetabular component anteversion was measured using multiple established methods, including those described by Liaw et al.,15) Lewinnek et al.,6) Widmer,16) Hassan et al.,17) and Ackland et al.18) on AP radiographs, and the Woo and Morrey19) method on cross-table lateral radiographs.20) All images were digitally archived and managed using a picture archiving and communication system (PACS).

Acetabular anteversion and inclination angle21) were measured on plain radiographs, as illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4, respectively. Measurements were performed independently by 2 orthopedic surgeons (HS and CYL). Each surgeon measured the values twice at separate time points, and the mean of the 2 measurements was used for analysis.

Measurement of acetabular anteversion on anteroposterior (A-E) and lateral (F) plain radiographs. (A) The method of Liaw et al.15) version: sin‒1 tan α. (B) The method of Lewinnek et al.6) version: arcsin (b / a). (C) Widmer’s method.16) version: arcsin (b / r): 48.05 × (b / r) – 0.3 (if 0.2 < b /r < 0.6). Measurement of acetabular inclination angle on an anteroposterior plain radiograph. Cup inclination is the angle between the ischial tuberosity line and the acetabular opening (γ angle).

Data Analyses

All data were managed and analyzed using Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS statistical software version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were analyzed using the t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were evaluated with Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the automatic group demonstrated a higher proportion of cases positioned within the targeted Lewinnek safe zone, indicating greater accuracy. Moreover, except for the Woo and Morrey method, the automatic group showed smaller standard deviations across the other anteversion measurement methods as well as in inclination angle measurements. Together with the more concentrated clustering of cups within the Lewinnek safe zone observed in Fig. 5, these findings collectively indicate that the automatic group demonstrated superior precision.

There are 3 types of anteversion: radiographic, anatomic, and operative.21) Among these, radiographic anteversion is widely used due to its cost-effectiveness and accessibility, as postoperative x-rays are routinely available. Postoperative computed tomography (CT) is not routinely performed in our hospital. Measurements of cup orientation on plain radiographs, particularly anteversion, may be affected by factors such as pelvic tilt and patient positioning. Although CT-based assessment would have provided more accurate measurements, these data were unavailable in this study. Therefore, because postoperative pain may affect patient positioning during radiographic acquisition, radiographs obtained 1 month after surgery were used for analysis, and only appropriately positioned AP and lateral radiographs were included. As previously noted, 6 methods are commonly used to measure anteversion; however, their comparative accuracy remains controversial. Some studies have reported that the Woo and Morrey method is less reliable because it does not account for spinal deformity or pelvic tilt, whereas the Liaw method is considered the most accurate.20,22) Because no universally accepted standard currently exists for radiographic anteversion measurement on plain radiographs, and because measured anteversion may vary according to pelvic tilt and other positional factors, all 6 radiographic measurement methods were used in this study in an attempt to minimize measurement bias and improve the robustness of the radiographic assessment.

The clinical significance of acetabular component positioning in THA has been widely investigated. A notable contribution came from Lewinnek et al.6) in 1978, who established a radiographic safe zone for acetabular position, specifying an anteversion of 15° (± 10°) and an inclination angle of 40° (± 10°).6) Their findings demonstrated an association between anterior dislocations in THA and increased cup anteversion, which has since been corroborated by multiple subsequent studies.6) Cup placement outside the Lewinnek safe zone has been associated with higher rates of dislocation.6) Dislocation is the second most common reason for revision after primary THA, and as dislocations increase in frequency, the likelihood of undergoing revision THA also rises.5,12,13) Therefore, positioning of the acetabular cup within the Lewinnek safe zone was targeted during surgery.

While manual techniques have traditionally yielded excellent outcomes in primary THA, the introduction of the automatic impaction device holds promise for improving operative efficiency and reducing surgeon workload.1,23) However, the potential advantages of the automatic impaction device over the manual technique have not been thoroughly investigated. In the present study, the automatic impaction device positioned the acetabular cup within the targeted Lewinnek safe zone more accurately during cup placement, resulting in a higher proportion of cases meeting this target compared with the manual technique. This improved accuracy may be attributable to the temporary visual limitations, variability in applied force, and inconsistencies in force vectors that are inherent to manual impaction. Therefore, the use of an automatic impaction device may reduce inter-surgeon variability, and its benefits could be particularly valuable for less experienced surgeons.

In this study, cases performed between 2009 and 2023 were included, representing a relatively long study period. Because the automatic impaction device was introduced more recently, the manual group consisted predominantly of cases performed earlier than those in the automatic group. The inclusion period became prolonged because only cases using a single implant system were included in order to minimize confounding factors. Although all procedures were performed by the same surgeon, the potential influence of the surgeon’s learning curve over time cannot be completely excluded. However, the surgeon had performed more than 200 hip surgeries annually since 2008, and there were no substantial changes in surgical technique, instrumentation, or perioperative environment during the study period. Therefore, the influence of temporal bias related to surgeon experience or perioperative factors was considered minimal.

This study had several limitations. First, because it was conducted as a retrospective study, it is subject to inherent selection and information biases, and unmeasured confounding factors could not be fully controlled. Second, the study included a relatively small cohort of 67 cases. Although no significant differences in complication rates were observed between the 2 groups, the small sample size may have limited the statistical power of the analysis. In addition, marginal differences in age and ASA classification were observed between the groups in Table 1. However, because this study focused primarily on the device effect based on radiographic findings rather than differences in clinical outcomes, the influence of these baseline differences was considered minimal. Third, the study was performed at a single center; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other surgeons using different implants or surgical approaches. Fourth, long-term follow-up was not available. Additional cases and further studies involving other surgical approaches or techniques are needed.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. First, potential confounding factors were minimized by using a single surgeon, a single implant system, and a single surgical approach. Second, this study provides a novel comparison of the accuracy of the manual technique and the automatic impaction device, which has not been previously investigated.

The use of an automatic impaction device can improve the accuracy and precision of acetabular cup positioning in primary THA compared with the manual technique. By providing consistent force and direction during cup insertion, automatic impaction devices may facilitate more reliable achievement of the surgeon’s intended anteversion and inclination angles, reduce inter-surgeon variability, and offer particular benefits for less experienced surgeons.
