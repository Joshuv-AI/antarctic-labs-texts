Structural insights into antibody-antibody interactions in sandwich ELISA: implications for assay development and performance
Authors: Yue Su, Bo Zhao, Youmi Moon, Yue Fu, Elif Karayusuf, Emily Ressegue, Xiaojiao Xue, Joshua Zylstra, Christine Grimaldi, Bojie Zhang, Hui Xiao, Ning Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is a widely utilized immunoassay technique in biopharmaceutical drug development due to its high sensitivity and specificity. Optimizing assay performance necessitates addressing challenges that arise during method development, particularly the selection of compatible monoclonal antibody (mAb) pairs for use as capture and detection antibodies. A common approach to measure human mAb therapeutics in nonclinical studies is to use generic human-specific mAb reagents that specifically recognize human immunoglobulin constant regions. In this study, we investigated the antibody-antibody interactions for a panel of reagent mAbs that bind to distinct epitopes in the constant regions of the human IgG heavy and light chains that may contribute to diminished signal and elevated background in the sandwich ELISA format. Using both in-solution and on-plate characterization methods including hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS), native size exclusion chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (nSEC-MS), and biolayer interferometry (BLI), we identified signal attenuation as a result of overlapping binding sites, leading to competitive or steric interference that hinders detection antibody binding. Additionally, elevated background signals were attributed to cross-reactivity between capture and detection antibodies. Furthermore, our results also reveal that antibody interactions at distinct epitopes induce different extents of steric hindrance, which significantly impacts the degree of oligomerization within the resulting product complexes. These insights enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms affecting assay performance and offer strategies for improving immunoassay design. Introduction

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is widely employed to identify drug targets, evaluate drug efficacy, and study pharmacokinetics (pK) of antibody-based therapeutics during biopharmaceutical drug development [1–6]. Among which, the commonly used sandwich ELISA format is performed by capturing the antigen of interest between two antibodies: a capture antibody immobilized on a solid surface and a detection antibody present in solution. The detection antibody, or a subsequently added third anti-species antibody targeting the detection antibody, is conjugated to an enzyme that facilitates the generation of a detectable signal. The sandwich ELISA leverages the binding of the target antigen at distinct epitopes by two antibodies, ensuring specific antigen recognition directly from complex biological samples without prior purification steps, thus achieving high specificity and sensitivity [7, 8]. As a result, it is a powerful immunological method for detecting and quantifying antigens, including proteins, peptides, antibodies, and hormones in various stages of drug development [7–10].

To ensure performance and robustness, ELISA methods must be meticulously developed and thoroughly evaluated for various targets [11–14]. The development typically begins with the selection of reagent antibodies that bind to the target antigen with high specificity and affinity [7, 15–17]. These antibodies are then thoroughly screened in sandwich ELISA configurations to identify pairs capable of generating robust dose–response reactivity [17–22]. Non-specific interactions or cross-reactivity of capture and detection antibodies, which may result in false-positive outcomes, are carefully excluded from further analysis [22–24]. This strict selection process ensures the reliability and sensitivity of the assay, while driving advancements in immunoassay technologies.

To avoid the use of mismatched antibodies and ensure the functionality of sandwich ELISA assays for detecting antibody-based therapeutics, it is crucial to understand the binding interactions among the capture antibody, detection antibody, and the antigen monoclonal antibody (mAb). Such binding interactions can be rigorously characterized using both solution-based and solid phase-based methodologies [25–29]. Epitope mapping techniques, for instance, are critical for precisely identifying and characterizing the specific binding sites on the antigen that interact with antibodies [30]. Among these, hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) provides detailed insights into protein conformation, dynamics, and interactions by measuring the exchange of backbone amide proton with deuterium [31–34]. Another solution-based technique, size exclusion chromatography coupled with native mass spectrometry (nSEC-MS), offers a robust approach for studying protein complexes [35, 36]. This technique separates protein complexes based on their native conformational sizes while utilizing gentle ionization by mass spectrometry to detect and characterize higher-order structures. Furthermore, biolayer interferometry (BLI), a plate-based technique, detects binding interactions by measuring shifts in light interference patterns caused by changes in the optical thickness of the biosensor surface, enabling real-time and high-sensitivity analysis of molecular interactions [37–39]. By integrating these approaches, researchers can leverage the strengths of each technique to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of abnormal trimeric antibody interactions in sandwich ELISA assays.

In this study, we explored the mechanisms behind antibody-antibody interactions for a panel of human constant region specific reagent mAbs in sandwich ELISA assays for therapeutic antibody detection. We identified undesired interactions that contributed to signal attenuation and during the methods development of specific mAb pairs. Epitope mapping using HDX-MS revealed the binding interface of each reagent mAb. Signal attenuation, caused by overlapping binding sites of reagent antibodies, was further validated through nSEC-MS and BLI. Binding interactions between reagent mAbs in the absence of the human therapeutic mAb (analyte) was confirmed to result in high background signals in ELISA. Additionally, our results showed that antibody-antibody binding at distinct epitopes may be limited by varying levels of steric hindrance, which eventually influence the degree of oligomerization, and thus the assay signal.

Materials and methods

Materials

All reagents were commercially available and used as received unless specified otherwise. Chromatography solvents were LC/MS grade and purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). Therapeutic mAbs Ag, Ag2 and mouse anti-human constant region reagent mAbs Rg1-5 were generated at Regeneron (Tarrytown, NY). Deuterium oxide (D2O), sodium phosphate, sodium chloride, guanidine hydrochloride, tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP-HCl), iodoacetamide, and urea were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). FabRICATOR was purchased from Genovis (Cambridge, MA). Monkey serum was purchased from BioIVT (Westbury, NY). No animals were handled, sampled, or subjected to experimental procedures by the authors as part of this study.

Sandwich ELISA for mAb antigen detection

Anti-constant region mAb pair combinations were tested by ELISA. The 96-well microtiter plate was coated overnight at 4 °C with purified capturing Rg mAb in pH 7.2 phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (100 μL/well). Next, the plate was washed and then blocked with 5% BSA blocking buffer (300 μL/well) overnight at 4 °C, and followed by washing. Serial dilutions of the human Ag mAb (analytes) as standards were prepared in monkey serum and diluted in assay diluent buffer (0.5% BSA and 0.05% Tween-20 in 1× DPBS) and then were added to the wells (100 μL/well) for 1-h incubation. The microplate was washed again prior to the addition of the second biotinylated anti-human constant region mAb in assay diluent buffer (100 μL/well). After another plate washing, NeutrAvidin-HRP prepared in assay diluent buffer was added to the wells (100 μL/well) followed by 1-h incubation and plate washing. Then the substrate solution prepared by combining equal volumes of SuperSignal ELISA Pico luminol/enhancer solution and SuperSignal ELISA Pico stable peroxide solution was added to the wells for a 20-min incubation. The chemiluminescence was recorded by a microplate luminometer. All incubations were conducted at room temperature (RT) with shaking (200 rpm) on the microplate shaker unless otherwise specified. Each washing step involved five washings with imidazole-buffered saline and Tween® 20 (300 μL/well) using the plate washer.

Each reagent pair was evaluated in both reciprocal orientations (A as capture/B as detection and B as capture/A as detection). In Fig. 1, each orientation was tested against Ag using a single measurement. To confirm the findings, a selected panel of reagent pairs was subsequently evaluated using a second antigen (Ag2). These confirmation experiments were performed in duplicate, and the reported RLU values represent the mean of the two replicate measurements.

Measurement of relative light units (RLU) to evaluate varying concentrations of antigen mAb using different reagent mAb pairs as capture and detection antibodies in sandwich ELISA

Reagent pairs that consistently demonstrate robust performance (e.g., Rg1/Rg4 and Rg4/Rg5) were used as assay controls. In the absence of antigen, these pairs serve as negative controls to help identify reagent pairs that exhibit non-specific interactions. At antigen concentrations greater than zero, the same reagent pairs function as positive controls by confirming effective signal generation and assay performance. They also provide a benchmark for identifying reagent pairs that fail to exhibit the expected increase in signal with increasing antigen concentration.

Native SEC-MS

Native SEC chromatography was conducted on an UltiMate 3000UPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with an Acquity BEH200 SEC column (200 Å, 1.7 µm, 4.6 mm × 300 mm, Waters, Milford, MA) with the column compartment set to 30 °C. An isocratic flow of 150 mM ammonium acetate at 0.2 mL/min was applied to separate bound and unbound mAbs. UV absorption at 280 nm and a Thermo Q Exactive UHMR was used for native mass analysis. The resolution was set at 12,500, the capillary spray voltage was set at 3.0 kV and the capillary temperature was set at 350 °C. Mass spectra were acquired with an m/z range of 2000–15,000 [36].

Biolayer interferometry

Binding competition assays were conducted using the Octet RED98 system (Sartorius). Biotinylated Rg1 (5 μg/mL) was captured onto streptavidin biosensors (SA, Sartorius) for 60 s. Rg1-loaded biosensors were subsequently dipped in wells containing Ag and Rg2 (29 μg/mL) for 600 s. The Ag-saturated biosensors were transferred into separate wells containing Rg1, Rg2, or Rg4 (29 μg/mL each) for an additional 600 s. A parallel experiment was performed wherein biotinylated Rg2 (5 μg/mL) was initially captured on streptavidin biosensors, followed by Ag and Rg1 saturation step. Lastly Ag-saturated biosensors were dipped into wells containing Rg1, Rg2, or Rg4 (29 μg/mL each) for 600 s.

To monitor interactions within each reagent mAb pair, one biotinylated reagent mAb (5 µg/mL) was captured on streptavidin biosensors (SA, Sartorius) for 60 s. The biotin-mAb-captured biosensors were then dipped into wells containing the other reagent mAb (29 µg/mL) and the response at the end of association phase (600 s) was measured.

HDX-MS

HDX-MS experiments were conducted following the procedures reported previously [33]. Deuterium labeling was conducted using a LEAP PAL3 HDX automation system (Trajan Scientific and Medical, Morrisville, NC). Samples consisting of 8 μL of 3 mg/mL were labeled in 72 μL of labeling buffer (1× PBS in D2O, pH 7.4) for each specified labeling time (30, 240, 1800, and 14,400 s) at 25 °C. Following labeling, 50 μL of each labeled sample was quenched with 50 μL of quenching buffer (200 mM sodium phosphate, 4 M guanidine hydrochloride, and 500 mM TCEP, pH 2.3, in water) at 1 °C. Subsequently, 50 μL of the quenched sample was injected into a chromatography system housed in a cold box connected to a Waters Acquity UPLC instrument (Waters, Milford, MA). The cold box and LC solvent precooler were maintained at 0 °C throughout the experiments. Injected samples were passed through an immobilized pepsin column (2.1 × 30 mm, NovaBioAssays, Woburn, MA) at a flow rate of 100 μL/min for 120 s, using mobile phase A (95% H2O, 5% acetonitrile, and 0.5% formic acid). The resulting peptic peptides were captured and desalted on a Symmetry Shield C8 trap column (3.9 × 20 mm, Waters, Milford, MA). The digested peptides were then separated on an Acquity BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm, 130 Å, Waters, Milford, MA) over a 25-min linear gradient of mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile), ranging from 0.1 to 30.0%. A Thermo Q Exactive™ Plus mass spectrometer was utilized to measure the masses of the deuterium-labeled peptides.

HDX-MS data analysis

HDX-MS data analysis was performed as previously described [33]. Raw files from both unbound Ag and bound Ag complex samples were processed using Protein Metrics ByosⓇ with the HDX workflow, followed by refinement with in-house software designed to select high-quality peptides with a redundancy value close to 4. The refined peptide list was manually validated within the Protein Metrics software, and chromatographic peak integration limits were adjusted as needed. Sequence alignment of mouse IgG Rg1-5 against human IgG Ag was conducted (Figure S1). Peptides from Ag overlapped with at least one Rg were identified and removed manually. After data processing, statistical testing using a “volcano plot” [40] was performed to identify peptides exhibiting significantly different deuterium uptake at each labeling time.

Antibody subunit generation

Antigen mAb Ag was diluted to a concentration of 10 mg/mL in 1× PBS buffer at pH = 7.4. Each sample was then digested with Genovis FabRICATOR enzyme at a ratio of 1 unit/μg of substrate at a temperature of 37 °C for 2 h. The subunits were collected using the above-mentioned nSEC conditions.

Results and discussion

ELISA signals with a panel of human constant region reagent mAbs

A panel of anti-human constant region mouse antibodies was generated in-house as reagent mAbs (Rg1-5), which are currently utilized for measuring therapeutic human antibodies in ELISA. As detailed in Table 1, these reagent mAbs exhibit specificity toward human IgG constant domains, with Rg1-3 targeting the human IgG4 Fc domain and Rg4-5 specifically binding to the human Ig kappa light chain (κLC). All 5 Rg have strong binding affinity for human IgG constant domain with KD ranging from 52 to 490 picomolar. Their performance in a sandwich ELISA format was assessed by testing various combinations as capture and detection reagents against the human antibody such as Ag.

Figure 1 illustrates the signal response when different reagent mAb pairs were used to detect varying concentrations of human Ag mAb. For each pair, the capture and detection reagents were swapped, and the identical experiments were conducted in parallel to validate the results. As expected, the Rg4–Rg1 and Rg5–Rg4 pairs showed concentration-dependent increases in signal, confirming their compatibility in this assay format. By contrast, the Rg1–Rg2 pair exhibited no clear dose response across the tested Ag concentrations, irrespective of whether they served as capture or detection reagents, likely reflecting overlapping or identical epitopes that limit signal generation through competition or steric hindrance. The Rg1–Rg3 pair produced a strong concentration-dependent response but also yielded high background in the absence of analyte, regardless of orientation. This elevated background signal may result from unintended interactions between Rg1 and Rg3 independent of antigen. When Ag2, which shares the same constant domain as Ag, was used, the same pairs again showed signal loss and increased background (Figure S2), which confirms the observations. It should be noted that these ELISA studies were intended as qualitative assessments to identify unexpected interactions among reagents during method development. The analyses were not designed to provide quantitative assay performance metrics, and comprehensive ELISA method qualification or validation was not included as part of this work.

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying binding competition and elevated background, and to avoid selecting unsuitable pairs for sandwich ELISAs, we conducted an in-depth characterization of antibody-antibody interactions using Ag (a representative human IgG4 with a kappa light chain) with each reagent mAb listed in Table 1.

Epitope mapping by HDX-MS

Identifying the binding sites on antigen mAbs targeted by reagent mAbs is crucial for studying antibody-antibody interactions. While ELISA techniques utilizing antibody subunits, such as F(ab′)2 and Fc fragments, can provide approximate binding domain specificities, HDX-MS was employed for more precise identification of the binding interface. Since epitope mapping by HDX-MS relies on the differential uptake of deuterium by peptides to pinpoint binding sites, it is essential to analyze peptides unique to the antigen to ensure accuracy. Given that both the antigen and reagent are mAbs, homologous sequences between them could introduce ambiguity. To address this, sequence alignment, shown in Figure S1, was performed to ensure that sufficient unique peptides for human antigen mAbs were selected. Additionally, manual verification was conducted to exclude any sequence overlaps with Rg1-5. This rigorous approach ensures sufficient coverage and the reliability of HDX-MS for epitope mapping.

Determining the stoichiometric ratio between human constant region-specific reagent mAbs and the human therapeutic mAbs is also critical for achieving saturation of binding sites. To this end, a titration-type experiment was conducted and evaluated using nSEC-MS and HDX-MS. For example, as shown in Figure S3, varying amounts of a κLC binder, Rg4, were introduced to Ag. Starting from 1:1 antigen-to-reagent ratio, the Ag monomer peak became negligible, and only the heterodimer peak was observed, indicating that all Ag molecules were bound to Rg4. At this ratio, only one of the two epitopes in Ag and one of the two paratopes in Rg4 were occupied. When an excess amount of Rg4 was introduced, its corresponding monomer peak emerged and increased proportionally, suggesting saturation of binding.

The same set of samples was analyzed using HDX-MS to assess deuterium incorporation. Figure S4 illustrates the differential deuterium uptake after 1800 s of exchange time, compared to non-bound Ag. An increasing trend in protection was observed with higher ratios of Rg4. Specifically, a mixing ratio of 1:2 yielded a saturated protection signal, indicating its adequacy for maximizing protection at this time point. Therefore, a slightly over-dosed ratio of 1:2.2 was adopted for all subsequent HDX-MS experiments to ensure optimal protection of Ag during the analysis.

HDX-MS was performed to investigate the binding dynamics of Ag with reagent mAbs Rg1-5 across four labeling time points ranging from 30 to 14,400 s, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Statistical analysis was subsequently conducted to identify peptides with significant differences in deuterium uptake, visualized using volcano plots [40] at a 99% confidence level. Compared to unbound Ag, regions with differential deuterium uptake values below −0.25 Da were identified as undergoing structural protection upon binding, while regions with values above 0.25 Da were considered as exposed. The affected regions were visualized in homology model, where darker colors indicated tight binding sites with high confidence, and light color represented surrounding areas undergoing dynamic changes likely caused by the bindings.

As shown in Fig. 2a, binding by Rg1 resulted in substantial protection close to the “bottom” of CH3 region located in the Fc domain of Ag. A small area adjacent to the binding site was observed to become more exposed, highlighted in cyan. Consistent with this hypothesis, Rg2 binds to nearly the same epitope as Rg1, although it exhibits a slightly smaller reduction in deuterium uptake in heavy chain peptide 408–423 (Figure S5) and does not induce increased deuterium uptake in adjacent regions, as shown in Fig. 2b. Meanwhile, Rg3 was found to bind Ag at the CH2 domain of the heavy chain, introducing certain dynamic changes near the CH2-CH3 interface. Furthermore, Rg4 binds to a region on the upper region of constant domain of κLC, facing “downward” and proximate to the VL-CL interface in Fig. 2c. Interestingly, in addition to the areas adjacent to binding sites, low-level protection was also observed in CH3 domain, which is distant from the primary binding site, as illustrated in Fig. 2d. Last but not least, Rg5 binds to κLC constant domain as well, specifically closer to the hinge region, on the opposite side relative to the binding site of Rg4. The deuterium uptake kinetic plots for peptide sequences representing the highlighted regions are provided in Figures S6–S10. Overall, these HDX-MS results pinpointed individual epitopes of all four reagent mAbs, which largely improve the understanding of reagent specificities in ELISA.

Binding competition between reagent mAbs

To account for potential differences associated with experimental conditions, sandwiched antibody complexes formed under in-solution and on-plate conditions were characterized using nSEC-MS and BLI, respectively. In the in-solution format, it is preferable to achieve complete complexation between the first reagent antibody and the antigen (eliminating unbound monomers) before introducing the second competing reagent. Residual unbound monomers from the initial step could otherwise complicate the following nSEC-MS analysis. As shown in Figure S11, the monomer peak became negligible at a 1:1 reagent-to-antigen ratio, indicating successful binding between the antigen and reagent mAbs. Therefore, this condition was then selected for sequential binding studies.

Next, the non-competing (Rg1 and Rg4) as well as the competing (Rg1 and Rg2) reagent pairs were sequentially introduced into the Ag solution. As illustrated in Fig. 3, no monomer peak remained in the SEC chromatogram when a single reagent was added. Among the three reagent mAbs, Rg4 was observed to only form heterodimer with Ag (Fig. 3a, second panel), while Rg1 and Rg2 formed tetrameric and higher-order oligomeric complexes with Ag (Fig. 3b, top two panels). Native MS analysis confirmed that the tetramer peak consisted of two antigen mAbs and two reagent mAbs. As a positive control experiment, the sequential addition of non-competing binders predominantly produced peaks of oligomers beyond tetramers (Fig. 3a, bottom two panels). Although direct mass measurement of the larger oligomers was difficult to acquire, the overall reduced retention times of main peaks strongly suggested the formation of oligomers beyond tetramers. Note that these final oligomers (n > 4) formed in solution may not represent the desired sandwich structure (n = 3). But such shifted peak profile and the absence of any monomers indeed confirmed successful binding between both reagents to the antigen.

Normalized absorbance in nSEC-MS for products formed when (a) non-competing binders and (b) competing binders were added to the antigen Ag in varying sequences. In the top two panels, a single reagent mAb was added to Ag. In the bottom two panels, two reagent mAbs were sequentially added to Ag. Peaks are color-coded: blue for oligomers beyond tetramer, gray for tetramers, green for dimers, and purple for monomers

On the contrary, the sequential addition of competing binders to Ag yielded a significant monomer peak (Fig. 3b, bottom two panels). Interestingly, mass analysis confirmed that the monomer peak observed consisted entirely of Rg2, regardless of the order in which the reagents were added. When Rg1 is initially complexed with the antigen, Rg2 is unable to bind to the target due to the binding site being occupied, leaving Rg2 in solution as a monomer. Conversely, when Rg2 is initially complexed with the antigen, the addition of Rg1 disrupts the pre-existing equilibrium. Consequently, Rg2 is replaced by Rg1 from the protein complexes and remains in monomeric form. Given that both binders target similar epitopes with comparable binding affinities, we hypothesize that Rg1 may engage Ag with a distinct orientation or induce a slightly different conformational stabilization relative to Rg2. This is supported by the marginally broader affected region (highlighted in cyan) observed in Fig. 2a and the greater degree of protection in deuterium uptake, as shown in Figure S5.

As an orthogonal method, on-plate competing BLI experiments were also conducted to mimic the experimental condition of sandwich ELISA and validate the findings from in-solution bindings. As shown in Fig. 4a, coating the plate with Rg1 resulted in an increase in BLI signal, confirming its capture on-plate. The subsequent addition of Rg2 did not produce a noticeable increase in signal (purple trace), suggesting no cross-reactivity between both reagent mAbs. When Ag was added, a significant increase in thickness was observed, suggesting successful binding of Ag to Rg1. Further addition of Rg1 elicited no additional increase in signal (red trace), indicating that the binding sites on Ag were saturated by Rg1. In a parallel experiment, the addition of Rg2 fails to increase thickness as well (black trace), affirming that there are binding competitions between Rg2 and the previously coated Rg1. However, as a positive control experiment, the addition of Rg4 to Rg1-captured Ag contributed to a significant signal increase (blue trace), demonstrating successful binding when there is no binding competition. Figure 4b shows similar results from a reversed addition of reagent, where Rg2 was initially coated on-plate. The BLI results further confirmed competitive binding between Rg1 and Rg2, with no observable displacement regardless of which reagent was immobilized first. The lack of detectable replacement likely reflects intrinsic limitations of surface-based formats: the high surface density of the immobilized capturing reagent can enhance apparent avidity and reduce antigen dissociation, while bivalent IgG interactions promote rebinding and kinetically obscure exchange.

High background in the absence of antigen

In addition to competition, the non-specific interaction between the Rg1 and Rg3 pair in the absence of the human mAb was also investigated. As shown in Fig. 5, the BLI signal for the high background pair (Rg1 and Rg3) was considerably higher than that observed for the other pairs. This suggests that weak binding between Rg1 and Rg3 does occur at the current mAb concentration (~ 0.2 µM). While non-specific interactions between reagents may theoretically be influenced by concentration, all ELISA and BLI experiments were conducted using the same concentrations for the corresponding reagent pairs. As shown in Fig. 5, all combinations other than Rg3/Rg1 served as negative controls and were tested under identical BLI conditions. Consequently, the observed responses of the different reagent pairs at the tested concentrations should be interpreted as arising from non-specific binding rather than from differences in assay setup or reagent concentration. However, due to its low abundance and the potential dissociation issue during dilution, this heterodimer is difficult to observe and isolate using SEC. As a result, pinpointing their exact binding sites remains challenging.

Steric hindered higher-order oligomer formation

Due to the symmetrical structure of antibodies, each antigen mAb contains two epitopes available for binding, and each reagent mAb carries two paratopes capable of binding antigens. When these antibodies interact, they can form polymeric structures resembling a “handholding” arrangement, termed “paper dolling” [41–43]. As shown in Figure S10, one of the CH3-binding reagent antibodies, Rg1, forms trimers, tetramers, and larger oligomers with Ag. Interestingly, the other CH3-binding antibody, Rg2, predominantly forms tetramers and beyond with Ag. Even though these two reagents target similar epitope, the resulting binding products still remain different. In contrast, the κLC binder, Rg4, only forms heterodimers and fails to generate higher-order oligomers, as illustrated in Figure S3. We hypothesize that steric hindrance, either from the Fc region of the antigen mAb or from the bulky structure of reagent mAbs, plays a critical role in limiting the formation of higher-order oligomers.

To investigate whether paper dolling is influenced by steric hindrance from the antigen mAb Fc domain, Ag subunits were generated using FabRICATOR digestion. Figure 6 compares complexes formed by Rg1 and Rg4 when bound to either full-length Ag or its subunit. As expected, the removal of the F(ab′)2 region did not significantly alter the product distribution of the Ag Fc domain in complex with Rg1, as shown in the SEC chromatogram in Fig. 6a. Mass analysis confirmed that the products remained as tetramers or higher-order oligomers, similar to those formed with full-length Ag.

Products formed by mixing full-length antigen Ag (top) and its subunit (bottom) with (a) Rg1 and (b) Rg4 at molar ratio of 1:1

Contrary to expectations, the removal of the Fc region did not facilitate oligomer formation between Rg4 and Ag F(ab′)2. The products predominantly remained as heterodimers, with no significant improvement in forming a “paper dolling” structure. This suggests that Fc domain hindrance is not the exclusive factor inhibiting paper dolling in this case. A hypothetical scheme illustrating the prohibited co-binding of two Rg4 molecules to Ag F(ab′)2 is presented in Figure S12. Two Rg4 molecules were still unable to bind simultaneously to Ag F(ab′)2 in the absence of the Fc domain. This limitation may arise from the spatial orientation of the Rg4-Ag complexes and the inherent flexibility of the F(ab′)2 region due to its hinge structure.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed characterization of antibody-antibody interactions for reagents intended for the measurement of human mAbs by ELISA, offering critical insights into the molecular mechanisms affecting assay performance. Two common issues with incompatible reagent mAbs pair were investigated: one leading to signal attenuation due to overlapping epitopes and competitive binding, and the other causing elevated background signals due to cross-reactivity.

Leveraging appropriate and complementary analytical techniques can be useful for achieving a multi-dimensional understanding of the complex issues inherent in the sandwich ELISA format, where three different antibodies form higher-order protein complexes. Epitope mapping using HDX-MS provides detailed insights into the binding interfaces of each reagent mAb, offering a structural basis to interpret the abnormalities observed in ELISA. Meanwhile, nSEC-MS effectively characterizes the in-solution analogs of sandwich antibody complexes, enabling accurate identification of protein composition and binding stoichiometry. Additionally, sequential addition of two competing reagent mAbs to the antigen mAb revealed an intriguing reagent replacement phenomenon, potentially indicating differential binding affinities between the two binders. Using an orthogonal method, BLI, the stepwise monitoring of on-plate binding behaviors successfully validated binding competition and confirmed cross-reactivity between the two reagent mAbs, which contributes to the high background issue. Furthermore, our findings reveal that antibody interactions at distinct epitopes significantly influence the degree of oligomerization in the resulting complexes. This effect may be attributed to the binding orientation of the reagents to the antigen and the structural dynamics of the antibodies. To address these challenging questions, other advanced techniques like CryoEM and computational modeling could provide more insights into the structural and dynamic properties of these interactions.

Moreover, we observed differences between antibody complexes formed in-solution and on-plate. Compared to on-plate that has more restricted accessibility or degree of binding, antibodies tend to generate “paper-dolling” effect in-solution due to repeated epitopes and paratopes from antigen and reagent mAbs, respectively. Furthermore, the replacement of competitive binders was solely observed under in-solution condition, but not under on-plate condition. This indicates the differential reaction equilibration built with different format, despite the same reactants being mixed. These results reiterate the significance of implementing complementary methods to obtain thorough understanding of the issue.

In addition to the primary binding site on the κLC of Ag, we observed a slight dynamic change in the Fc domain upon binding with Rg4. Although differences in deuterium uptake were only detected at the 14,400-s time point, they were statistically significant and confirmed by multiple overlapping peptides, as shown in Figure S13. Given that the primary binding site for Rg4 is on the κ constant light (CL) region facing the Fc domain of Ag (Fig. 2d), we hypothesize that the bulky full-length Rg4 may form additional weak interactions with the Fc domain of Ag. These interactions could potentially be mediated by the flexible hinge region structures of both mAbs. In contrast, due to distinct epitopes and spatial orientations of other reagent mAbs, these antibodies do not form crowded higher-order structures like Rg4. Consequently, no distant “cross-talk” between different domains was observed for other reagent mAbs. Notably, HDX-MS stands out for its ability to detect cross-talk interactions, as it can characterize full-length antibodies—an advantage not shared by many other techniques. Future studies using accurate computational docking experiments could further validate this hypothesis and delve deeper into the mechanisms governing weak interactions and their influence on antibody functionality.

In summary, our study highlights the critical importance of selecting and validating compatible mAb pairs for use as capture and detection antibodies in sandwich ELISA assays. Employing antibodies with well-defined binding sites and carefully characterized relative affinities enables researchers to make informed decisions that optimize assay design, ultimately enhancing both sensitivity and specificity. The insights gained from this characterization advance our understanding of deviations in sandwich ELISA assays and contribute to the development of more robust immunoassay applications in biochemical research.
