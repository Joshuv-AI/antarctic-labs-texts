Analysis framework for higher-order temporal correlations with applications to human heartbeats
Authors: Tibebe Birhanu, Hang-Hyun Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We propose a time series analysis framework focused on higher-order temporal correlations in the event sequence beyond the interevent time distribution by employing the burst-tree decomposition method. Bursts are clustered events that rapidly occur within shorter time periods, and they are separated by relatively longer inactive periods. The burst-tree decomposition method exactly maps the event sequence onto a tree, called a burst tree, in which each internal node represents a merge of consecutive bursts at the timescale separating those bursts. The burst tree fully reveals the hierarchical structure of bursts, hence the higher-order temporal correlations for the entire range of timescales. Those correlations are quantified using novel and existing measures derived from the burst tree, such as the burst complexity, memory coefficient for bursts, and principal and secondary cross sections of the burst-merging kernel. We apply our framework to the heartbeat time series of healthy people and of those with heart disease to reveal distinct multiscale temporal properties in physiological time series.

Introduction

A complex system can be characterized by the collection of interactions between its components, which determines the system’s overall behavior and dynamics1. The dynamics of complex systems often shows intermittent temporal activity, which can be depicted as a bursty sequence of events. The bursty temporal pattern implies rapidly occurring events in short time periods interlaced by long inactive periods2–4. Examples include event sequences from solar flare activity5,6 and earthquakes7,8 to neural dynamics9–11 and human social interactions2,3,12–20. As these temporal patterns also show 1/f noise, some of such systems have been analyzed in the framework of self-organized criticality21–23. To understand the underlying mechanisms behind the complex dynamics of systems, it is of utmost importance to devise effective methods or tools characterizing temporal correlations in the data. For this, various measures and quantities have been introduced, such as the interevent time distribution, burstiness parameter, memory coefficient, burst size distribution, and autocorrelation function to name a few2–4,14,24.

The interevent time (IET) is the time interval between two consecutive events and its distribution is often found to be heavy tailed, implying the deviation from the memoryless, Poisson process25. The memory coefficient measures the correlation between two consecutive IETs14, while the notion of the burst size captures the correlation between an arbitrary number of consecutive IETs24. That is, for a given timescale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, events in the sequence are clustered into multiple bursts such that the IETs within bursts are smaller than or equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, but IETs between bursts are greater than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. The number of events in the burst is called a burst size and heavy-tailed distributions of burst sizes were empirically found, indicating the presence of higher-order temporal correlations beyond IET distributions24,26.

For a more systematic approach to the analysis of empirical event sequences, the burst-tree decomposition method has been proposed19. By scanning the entire range of the timescale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, the method exactly maps the event sequence onto a tree, called the burst tree. Thus, the burst tree reveals the hierarchical structure of bursts across multiple timescales. In particular, each internal node of the burst tree denotes a merge of consecutive bursts as the timescale defining bursts increases. Such a merging pattern has been captured by some selection rule called a burst-merging kernel19,27,28. The burst-merging kernel dictates which bursts are merged together as the timescale increases, hence this kernel plays a crucial role in understanding the underlying mechanism of higher-order temporal correlations, often characterized by heavy-tailed burst size distributions and positive correlations between consecutive burst sizes.

In this work, we propose a time series analysis framework focused on higher-order temporal correlations beyond the IET distribution by employing the burst-tree decomposition method. Those correlations are quantified using novel and existing measures derived from the burst tree, such as the burst complexity, memory coefficient for bursts, and principal and secondary cross sections of the burst-merging kernel. For demonstration, we apply our framework to the publicly available data of heartbeat time series in healthy subjects exhibiting normal sinus rhythm (NSR) as well as individuals diagnosed with cardiovascular diseases such as congestive heart failure (CHF) and atrial fibrillation (AF). The burst trees derived from these data allow us to represent and analyze the complex multiscale temporal dynamics of heartbeat time series, providing a robust method for understanding the underlying physiological processes. Consequently, our study may shed light on the differences in heartbeat dynamics between healthy individuals and those with heart failure.

We brief some previous works on the heartbeat time series analysis using other methods such as the detrended fluctuation analysis (DFA), multiscale entropy (MSE), and visibility graph (VG) algorithm. These methods analyze the time series of RR intervals (RRI or interbeat intervals), i.e., the IET sequence, whereas our approach is to analyze the event sequence directly, yet the event sequence and the IET sequence are easily interchangeable. Peng et al.29 proposed the DFA method, which essentially quantifies the power-law scaling of the root-mean-squared fluctuation as a function of the timescale for detecting the local trend. Using the DFA method30 it was found that heartbeat time series from the CHF group show different scaling behaviors than those from healthy people. Recently, a dynamical DFA (DDFA) was introduced to detect real-time changes in highly nonstationary RRI time series31. Pukkila et al.32 adopted the DFA and DDFA to extract features from the RRI sequences. Such features were used as inputs to machine learning methods to distinguish the NSR, CHF, and AF groups. Regarding the classification or detection of subjects with heart diseases, we also find more practical machine learning methods33,34. The MSE method based on the sample entropy35 has been applied to the RRI time series to detect long-range temporal correlations across multiple timescales36,37. It was found that the MSE curves as a function of the timescale show different behaviors depending on whether people are healthy or have heart diseases. The VG algorithm has also been applied to physiological time series38. The VG algorithm transforms the time series to the network of nodes and links, enabling to apply various characterizations in network science to the time series analysis.

Although all the mentioned methods have shown significant results when applied to the RRI time series, they do not always provide the underlying mechanisms behind such observed temporal correlations, making their results hard to understand and interpret in terms of the building blocks of time series, that is, RRIs or IETs. In contrast, our proposed framework is entirely based on the statistics of IETs and the correlations between IETs; the latter has been successfully characterized by burst sizes and their hierarchical structure19. Therefore our framework enables us to deepen the IET-based understanding of multiscale temporal dynamics in various time series data.

Materials and methods

Data

We analyze heartbeat time series from the datasets downloaded from the physiological data repository, PhysioNet39. Every dataset has a unique identity, such as nsr2db for normal sinus rhythm (NSR), chfdb and chf2db for congestive heart failure (CHF), and afdb and ltafdb for atrial fibrillation (AF). We take three distinct groups of people for the analysis. The group of the NSR contains 54 healthy individuals from nsr2db dataset. Precisely, they are 30 males with an age distribution ranging from 28 to 76 years, and 24 females with an age distribution from 58 to 73 years. The CHF group consists of 44 individuals with CHF from chfdb and chf2db datasets, of which 19 are male and 6 are female. The age distribution for males ranges from 22 to 71 years, while for females, it is from 54 to 63 years. The gender of the rest 19 subjects is not known. Finally, the AF group we consider consists of 109 individuals with AF from afdb and ltafdb datasets, in which subjects’ age and sex data are not available. The duration of recording is approximately 24 hours for the NSR, CHF, and AF from ltafdb datasets, while it is around 10 hours for the AF from the afdb dataset. The estimated values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A\approx -0.64$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau \approx 0.98$$\end{document} for the entire period of the NSR subject 1’s event sequence are shown in Fig. 1d. Although A and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document} are already informative in understanding temporal correlations, they are not enough to study the higher-order temporal correlations in the time series, requiring more systematic approaches.

Burst-tree decomposition method

To fully characterize the higher-order temporal correlations beyond the IET distribution, the burst-tree decomposition method has been proposed19, which can map the event sequence into a burst tree. This method reveals the hierarchical structure of bursts of events for a wide range of timescale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. For a given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, a burst is defined as a set of consecutive events such that the IET between each pair of two consecutive events in the burst is less than or equal to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, while the IETs between bursts are greater than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}24. We call the number of events in each burst as the burst size, and it is denoted by b. Any successive bursts separated by some IET, say \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}, are supposed to merge when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} increases to become \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}. By scanning \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _\textrm{min}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _\textrm{max}$$\end{document}, the burst-tree decomposition method captures such merging process, hence the hierarchical structure of bursts without losing any information in the original event sequence.

We describe how the burst tree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} is constructed by varying the timescale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. Initially, we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} to be smaller than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _\textrm{min}$$\end{document}; each event is considered a burst of size one, depicted as empty red circles in Fig. 2a. The merging process starts when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} continuously increases from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _\textrm{min}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _\textrm{max}$$\end{document}. Accordingly, the consecutive bursts are sequentially merged to form bigger bursts, depicted as filled red circles in Fig. 2a. Finally, the merging process ends when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t \ge \tau _\textrm{max}$$\end{document}, when all events belong to one giant burst of size n. Such merging pattern can be visualized by a dendrogram in Fig. 2a, also called a burst tree; individual events, merged bursts, and the giant burst of size n are represented as leaf nodes, internal nodes, and the root node in the burst tree, respectively. The numbers next to the filled circles in Fig. 2a are burst sizes of internal nodes. For example, the burst tree derived from the sample event sequence in Fig. 1a is shown in Fig. 1b. Then, for any given \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, a burst size sequence can be obtained directly from the burst tree, and it is denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {B}_{\Delta t}=\{b_1,\ldots , b_m\}$$\end{document}, where m is the number of bursts detected using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. Note that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sum _{j=1}^m b_j=n$$\end{document} for any \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. Using the burst size sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {B}_{\Delta t}$$\end{document}, one can characterize the higher-order temporal correlations in terms of the burst size distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{\Delta t}(b)$$\end{document}, the burst complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document}, and the memory coefficient for bursts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document}, which will be defined below.

The burst size distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{\Delta t}(b)$$\end{document} obtained from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {B}_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} often shows a heavy tail3,19,24, clearly indicating the presence of long-term correlations between a number of consecutive IETs. It is obviously beyond the correlations characterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\tau )$$\end{document}, A, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document}. Similarly to the burstiness measure in Eq. (2), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} has a value in the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-1,1]$$\end{document}. If all burst sizes are identical, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r_b=0$$\end{document}, hence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}=-1$$\end{document}. When all IETs are uncorrelated with each other, the burst size distribution becomes exponential, leading to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}\approx 0$$\end{document}. Finally, when IETs are strongly correlated with each other, the complex structure of bursts emerges, as evidenced by the heavy-tailed burst size distributions, hence one may find that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}>0$$\end{document}. Note that the number of bursts m decreases with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}, and eventually m will decrease to 1; we calculate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} as long as at least 2 burst sizes are detected.

Next, we adopt the memory coefficient for bursts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document}, quantifying the correlation between two consecutive burst \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu '_1$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \begin{document}$$\sigma '_1$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma '_2$$\end{document}) are the average and standard deviation of burst sizes in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {B}_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} excluding \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_m$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_1$$\end{document}), respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} has a value in the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-1,1]$$\end{document}; the positive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} and it is negative for the opposite tendency, while it is around zero for the uncorrelated burst sizes. We calculate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} as long as at least 3 burst sizes are detected, when both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma '_1$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma '_2$$\end{document} can be calculated.

We discuss some possible patterns of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} regarding burst trees. As \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} increases, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} is expected to have non-trivial, positive values, in particular, when the burst size distribution develops a heavy tail. In contrast, one cannot tell about the general tendency of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} values. Finally, if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document} is sufficiently large, there remain only few, big bursts, lowering \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} again. First, the increasing behavior of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} for small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} Ordinal burst tree and the burst-merging kernel

Following the original burst-tree decomposition method, we assume that the burst tree is binary, implying that each merged burst consists of only two bursts. In reality, the merged bursts can be made of more than two bursts, which however is not very common19. Let us consider two successive bursts, indexed by v (earlier burst) and w (later burst), that are separated by an IET \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}. At the moment of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t=\tau$$\end{document}, bursts v and w merge together to become another burst indexed by u. Then bursts u, v, and w are called a parent, a left child, and a right child, and their sizes have the relation as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_u=b_v+b_w$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_\alpha$$\end{document} is the burst size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in \{u,v,w\}$$\end{document}. Denoting the IET separating v and w by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\tau }_u$$\end{document}, we sort those IETs in a descending way. Then the index u is determined as the rank of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\tau }_u$$\end{document} in the ordered IETs; e.g., since the root node is associated with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _\textrm{max}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{\tau }_1=\tau _\textrm{max}$$\end{document}, the index of the root node is 1. Finally, one can represent each merged burst by a tuple \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(u,v,w,\hat{\tau }_u)$$\end{document}. Therefore, the burst tree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} is fully described by the set of these tuples \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{(u,v,w,\hat{\tau }_u)\}$$\end{document}. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} can be decomposed into the ordinal burst tree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}\equiv \{(u,v,w)\}$$\end{document} and the set of IETs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{\hat{\tau }_u\}$$\end{document}, which is equivalent to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\tau )$$\end{document} in Eq. (1). It is remarkable that the ordinal burst tree is separable from the IET distribution, hence it can be associated with an arbitrary IET distribution.

Although the ordinal burst tree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}$$\end{document} carries the detailed information on the given event sequence, one often needs to summarize it for the simpler representation. For this, from the observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}$$\end{document} we estimate the burst-merging kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(b,b')$$\end{document} with b and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b'$$\end{document} denoting burst sizes of the left and right children in the burst tree, respectively. Kernels are versatile tools used in various fields. In network science kernels guide for shaping the structure of growing networks over time42–49, while in the coagulation process kernels determine how likely two existing clusters are chosen for coalescence or merge27,50–58. In our work, we consider the observed \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {G}$$\end{document} as an instance of the underlying stochastic merging process driven by the burst-merging kernel19,27,28. That is, the individual events are sequentially merged to become clusters of events, i.e., bursts. The merging process is governed by the burst-merging kernel such that two bursts of sizes b and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b'$$\end{document} are chosen with a probability proportional to the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(b,b')$$\end{document}, and they are merged to become another burst of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b+b'$$\end{document}. This procedure is repeated until all events belong to a single burst.

To describe the stochastic merging process more precisely, let us denote an auxiliary time step by s, which counts the number of merges and has nothing to do with the real time. At each time step s, there are exactly \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n-s$$\end{document} bursts. Among them two bursts of sizes b and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b'$$\end{document} are randomly chosen with a probability proportional to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(b,b')$$\end{document}, and they merge together to become another burst of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b+b'$$\end{document}. Figure 1g shows these diagonals \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_1(b)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_2(b)$$\end{document}. We find that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_1(b)$$\end{document} overall increases with b, while \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_2(b)$$\end{document} shows an overall symmetric behavior with respect to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b=\sqrt{1500}\approx 39$$\end{document}, implying the time-reversal symmetry in terms of the burst sizes.

In conclusion, we propose the analysis framework for a given event sequence by characterizing the entire orders of temporal correlations in the event sequence. We begin by measuring the IET distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(\tau )$$\end{document}, the burstiness measure A, and the memory coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document}, then after mapping the event sequence to the burst tree we measure the burst size distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Q_{\Delta t}(b)$$\end{document}, the burst complexity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{\Delta t}$$\end{document}, and the memory coefficient for bursts \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\Delta t}$$\end{document} for the entire range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t$$\end{document}. Finally, we estimate the burst-merging kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(b,b')$$\end{document} summarizing the ordinal burst tree and take the principal and secondary cross sections of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K(b,b')$$\end{document} as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_1(b)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_2(b)$$\end{document}, making it easier to compare these quantities across subjects and groups.

In addition, to compare the distributions of quantities, such as A and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document}, across groups of NSR, CHF, and AF, we conduct the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test comparing those distributions. By using the ks_2samp function from the scipy.stats library in Python, we calculate the p-values and report them as appropriate.

Classification of individuals using SVM

Finally, we adopt the support vector machine (SVM)59 to test if one can classify individual subjects into three groups of NSR, CHF, and AF. Since the SVM has originally been for binary classification, we employ the one-vs-rest method for multi-class classification using the sklearn library in Python. As for input features, we use the burstiness measure A in Eq. (2), memory coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_{\tau }$$\end{document} in Eq. (3), timescale interval \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta$$\end{document} in Eq. (6), and peak timescale \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t_\textrm{peak}$$\end{document} in Eq. (7). Since values of these features are on different scales, we normalize them in terms of Z-score. We employ the radial basis function kernel and perform grid search using 5-fold cross-validation to determine hyperparameters, i.e., the regularization parameter that controls the trade-off between low training and testing errors, and the kernel parameter that defines the influence of a single training example, with higher values leading to more complex decision boundaries. We perform the classification 100 times or runs; in each run, we randomly choose \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$80\%$$\end{document} of samples for the train set, and the rest \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\%$$\end{document} as the test set.

Results

We apply our analysis framework proposed in the previous section to the heartbeat time series of 207 subjects in three groups of the normal sinus rhythm (NSR), the congestive heart failure (CHF), and the atrial fibrillation (AF) to see whether there are significant differences between these groups in terms of temporal correlations of all relevant orders.

For each subject, we first estimate the values of the burstiness measure A in Eq. (2) and the memory coefficient \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document} in Eq. (3). Figure 3a,b show the distributions of those results in the form of box plots. We observe that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$A<-0.5$$\end{document} for all subjects as the heartbeats are mostly regular rather than bursty; the range of A values for the NSR group is higher than that for the CHF group, implying that the subjects with CHF show more regular heartbeats than the healthy subjects. The values of A for the AF group are most dispersed, though their median is closer to that for the NSR group than for the CHF group. The differences between groups get more pronounced in the distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document}; the values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document} are all very close to 1 for the NSR group, while those for the CHF group are obviously smaller than those of the NSR group. The values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$M_\tau$$\end{document} for the AF group are overall smallest and again most dispersed, while overlapping with those of the CHF group. Our observations are supported by the p-values estimated between groups using the KS test. Finally, we generate a scatter plot of the results in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(M_\tau ,A)$$\end{document} space in Fig. 3c to see if the groups can be distinguished from each other. Three groups occupy overall different regions but still with some overlaps.

For the qualitative comparison, we visualize burst trees derived from three selected subjects for each group, each of which contains exactly 70 consecutive events, as shown in Fig. 4. Conclusion

We have proposed a novel analysis framework for high-order temporal correlations in event sequences mainly based on the burst-tree decomposition method19; we have successfully applied our framework to human heartbeat time series of 207 people from different groups of the normal sinus rhythm (NSR), the congestive heart failure (CHF), and the atrial fibrillation (AF)39. By measuring various existing and novel measures from each subject’s time series, we could compare the statistical properties of such time series across groups. In particular, we explore the possibility that higher-order quantities derived from the burst-tree structure, such as the burst complexity and memory coefficient for bursts, can be used to distinguish different groups of people. We adopt one of commonly used machine learning techniques, i.e., the support vector machine, to achieve the reasonable accuracy for the classification task.

We remark that our analysis framework is entirely based on the statistics of IETs and the correlations between IETs, which makes out approach distinguished from the previous methods based on the detrended fluctuation analysis, multiscale entropy, and visibility graph algorithms. In sum, our work highlights the potential of the burst-tree decomposition method and our analysis framework based on the method to improve the understanding of complex physiological time series data, paving the way for further research in the fields of complex systems and nonlinear time series analysis.
