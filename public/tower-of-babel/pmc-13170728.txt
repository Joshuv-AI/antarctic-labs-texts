High-Dose Sucralose Is Associated With Lacrimal Circadian Remodeling, Reduced Immunometabolic Signatures, and Reduced Tear Secretion
Authors: Di Qi, Tingting Yang, Xiaoting Pei, Dingli Lu, Shenzhen Huang, Liu Yang, Jingwen Yang, Shuting Xuan, Mengru Ba, Wenxiao Zhang, Xiaohui Liu, Shufan Yu, Zhijie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine whether high-dose sucralose (SUC) exposure alters circadian organization of the mouse extraorbital lacrimal gland, immunometabolic pathways, and tear film function using multi-omics and functional assays.MethodsMale C57BL/6J mice received SUC (0.72 mg/mL in drinking water) or water under a 12:12 hour light–dark cycle for 30 days. Extraorbital lacrimal glands were collected every 3 hours across 24 hours (n = 3/time point/group) for RNA sequencing and 4D–data-independent acquisition proteomics. Rhythms were identified with JTK_CYCLE, differential expression with DESeq2/MSstats followed by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis. Oil Red O and CD4/CD8 immunohistochemistry assessed lipid and immune signatures. Tear secretion, tear film breakup time, and corneal fluorescein staining were measured at ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18.ResultsSUC increased rhythmic transcripts (3074 to 4600) and proteins (378 to 984), redistributing acrophases (transcript peaks shifted toward ZT3–ZT6; protein peaks toward ZT21–ZT24), indicating clock phase remodeling. Both omics layers converged at the pathway level, showing downshifts in metabolic and immune programs, including complement- and T-cell–related signatures. SUC elevated water intake and body weight without altering glucose tolerance. Functionally, SUC-treated mice showed reduced stimulated tear secretion, whereas tear film breakup time and corneal fluorescein staining did not differ significantly.ConclusionsUnder this high-exposure paradigm, SUC was associated with lacrimal circadian remodeling, reduced metabolic and immune pathway signatures, and lower stimulated tear secretion, identifying candidate pathways for mechanistic testing without establishing direct causality.

Nonsugar sweeteners (NSSs, also referred to as non-nutritive sweeteners) are increasingly used in beverages, foods, and pharmaceuticals owing to their high sweetness intensity and negligible caloric content, serving as popular substitutes for dietary sugar reduction.1 In 2023, the World Health Organization issued a conditional recommendation against the use of NSS for long-term body weight control, emphasizing the need for a cautious interpretation of their physiological effects within a risk–benefit framework.2,3 Common NSSs include aspartame, acesulfame-K, steviol glycosides, erythritol, mogrosides, and sucralose (SUC)—the latter being widely adopted owing to its sucrose-like taste and excellent physicochemical stability in diverse formulations.4–6

SUC is a selectively chlorinated derivative of sucrose, first identified through collaborative academic–industrial research at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, in the mid 1970s, leading to subsequent patent development.7 It is approximately several hundred times sweeter than sucrose, stable across a wide pH range, and compatible with various manufacturing processes; however, concerns remain regarding its thermal degradation and potential generation of chlorinated byproducts during high-temperature cooking.4,8 Importantly, SUC has been detected in human breast milk after maternal ingestion, and such exposure has been associated with increased vomiting risk in infants.9,10 At the metabolic level, several studies have suggested that concurrent ingestion of SUC and carbohydrates can transiently impair insulin or glucose tolerance, although later methodological reassessments have challenged this conclusion, leaving the metabolic implications of SUC unresolved.11,12

Beyond its metabolic effects, recent mechanistic investigations have revealed that high-dose SUC exposure can modulate the immune system. In mice, SUC restricts T-cell proliferation and differentiation, resulting in a marked immunosuppressive phenotype, while human randomized trials demonstrate that different NSS—including SUC—induce individualized, microbiome-dependent glycemic responses.13–15 These findings extend the biological scope of NSS from simple caloric substitutes to bioactive compounds influencing the immune–metabolic–microbiome axis. However, most of these studies have focused on systemic or intestinal physiology, leaving potential effects on extraintestinal organs—particularly the ocular adnexal glands—largely unexplored.

The circadian rhythm is governed by cell-autonomous transcription–translation feedback loops composed of core clock regulators such as CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, and CRY. These molecular oscillators maintain 24-hour periodicity, orchestrating rhythmic control of metabolism, immunity, and secretion across peripheral tissues.16–18 The ocular surface system exhibits robust circadian characteristics: the tear proteome displays diurnal variability, tear secretion and blinking follow rhythmic patterns, and perturbations of external timing cues—such as chronic jet lag or sensory nerve injury—can disrupt the homeostatic balance of the ocular surface–lacrimal axis.19–21 These observations highlight the critical interdependence between circadian phase alignment and functional homeostasis in ocular surface physiology and suggest that lacrimal clocks are key regulators of tear film quantity and quality.

Emerging evidence further indicates that both environmental circadian disruption (sleep loss) and nutritional challenges (high-fat nutrition and high-fructose intake) can remodel lacrimal gland biology and precipitate dry eye–like phenotypes.22–24 Experimental manipulations of clock genes in ocular tissues, as well as chronic misalignment of light–dark cycles, have been linked to altered mucin production, epithelial barrier compromise, and ocular surface inflammation, and high-sugar or high-fructose diets can reprogram lacrimal transcriptional programs and perturb tear homeostasis. Together, these studies position the lacrimal gland as a peripheral, immunometabolic clock hub that integrates systemic and local timing cues to support ocular surface defense.

Despite accumulating evidence that environmental and nutritional cues can modulate peripheral clocks, it remains unknown whether non-sugar sweeteners, particularly SUC, whose daily exposure levels are steadily increasing25 can alter the temporal organization of the lacrimal gland, thereby reshaping its metabolic–immune network and influencing tear film function. To address this gap, we established a controlled high-dose SUC mouse model under a 12:12 hour light–dark cycle and performed integrated 24-hour multi-omics profiling of the extraorbital lacrimal gland, including transcriptomics and proteomics at 3-hour resolution, complemented by histological, immunological, and ocular surface functional assessments. Our aim was to determine whether high-dose SUC exposure is associated with remodeling of lacrimal circadian organization, altered metabolic and immune pathway signatures, and changes in tear-related phenotypes, while defining candidate molecular pathways and temporal associations without implying direct causality.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old; specific pathogen free) were used in this initial mechanistic study to reduce additional biological variability related to estrous cycle stage in a design involving dense circadian sampling and multi-omics profiling. Mice were obtained from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China) and maintained at 24 ± 1°C, 40% to 60% humidity, under a 12:12 hour light–dark cycle (lights on at 07:00 = zeitgeber time (ZT) 0; off at 19:00 = ZT12) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. After a 2-week acclimation, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups with balanced cage distribution, and health status was monitored daily. All experimental procedures complied with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Henan Provincial People's Hospital (Approval No. HNEECA-2022-20). Euthanasia was performed by intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (≥100 mg/kg) followed by cervical dislocation to ensure humane end points. The study design was guided by the 3Rs principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement). Reduction was achieved by allocating one extraorbital lacrimal gland (ELG) from each mouse to RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and the contralateral ELG to proteomics, thereby maximizing data yield per animal, and by obtaining multiple physiological and ocular surface readouts from the same animals where appropriate. Refinement included acclimation before experimentation, daily health monitoring, controlled housing conditions, anesthesia for relevant procedures, and humane euthanasia. Replacement was considered; however, the present aims required an intact in vivo model to assess circadian timing, drinking behavior, tear secretion, immune–metabolic interactions, and whole organ lacrimal physiology across the light–dark cycle, which could not be adequately reproduced in vitro.

Overall Study Design

This study was designed to determine whether chronic high-dose SUC exposure is associated with alterations in the circadian organization and homeostatic functions of the ELG. The experimental workflow (Fig. 1) combined a controlled in vivo exposure protocol with time-resolved multi-omics profiling and histological/immunological validation.

Experimental design and analytical workflow. (A–E) Mice were randomized to NC (water) or SUC (0.72 mg/mL in water) groups and maintained under a 12:12 hour light–dark (LD) cycle (ZT0 = 07:00; ZT12 = 19:00). After 30 days, ELGs were collected every 3 hours across 24 hours, with one gland used for RNA-seq and the contralateral gland for 4D–data-independent acquisition (4D-DIA) proteomics. (F–H) Overview of analytical workflow: JTK_CYCLE rhythmicity screening, PCA, differential analyses (DESeq2, MSstats), KEGG, and GSEA enrichment, time-series clustering. Histological and immunological validation included Oil Red O and immunohistochemistry (IHC) (CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells) and CFS.

After a 2-week acclimation period, male C57BL/6J mice were randomly assigned to a normal control (NC) group or a SUC-treated group under identical housing conditions with ad libitum access to standard chow. The NC group received regular drinking water, whereas the SUC group received water containing 0.72 mg/mL SUC13,26 (Cat. No. 69293, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Based on the measured mean daily water intake per mouse in the SUC group and the corresponding mean body weight, the estimated actual SUC intake was 184.5 mg/kg/d (estimated intake = SUC concentration [mg/mL] × water intake [mL/d]/body weight [kg]). Using standard body-surface-area conversion (mouse Km = 3; human Km = 37), this corresponds with a human-equivalent dose of 15.0 mg/kg/d, or approximately 900 mg/d for a 60-kg adult. This exposure is therefore close to the current European Food Safety Authority acceptable daily intake for SUC (15 mg/kg/d), but remains above typical real-world dietary exposure; accordingly, it was used here as a mechanistic high-exposure paradigm rather than to model average human consumption. All animals were maintained under a 12:12 hour light–dark cycle (lights on at 07:00 = ZT0; lights off at 19:00 = ZT12) for 30 days (Fig. 1A).

To capture circadian dynamics of the ELG, tissues were collected every 3 hours across a full 24-hour cycle (ZT0–ZT21; eight ZTs). For the omics arm, n = 3 mice per ZT per group were used (Fig. 1B). In each mouse, one ELG was allocated to RNA-seq and the contralateral gland to 4D–data-independent acquisition proteomics, thereby maximizing information yield per animal and enabling paired transcriptome–proteome integration (Figs. 1C–E).

Bioinformatic analyses were structured to first define circadian architecture and then quantify condition-dependent shifts.

Expression Analysis

Circadian rhythmicity of gene and protein expression was assessed with the Jonckheere–Terpstra–Kendall (JTK_CYCLE) algorithm (period fixed at 24 hours) to identify oscillatory features. Global variation and group separation were visualized by principal component analysis (PCA), volcano plots, rose plots and heatmaps. Differential expression analyses were performed using DESeq2 for RNA-seq data and MSstats for proteomic data, with multiple-testing control. Time-series clustering with Mfuzz was applied to JTK_CYCLE–defined rhythmic features to resolve recurrent temporal expression modules and to compare condition-specific rhythmic modes (Fig. 1F).

Functional Analysis

Gene/protein sets were subjected to functional enrichment using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to identify significantly affected biological pathways (Fig. 1G).

Histological and Immunological Validation

For histological and immunological validation, neutral lipid accumulation in ELGs was evaluated by Oil Red O staining, and immune cell infiltration was assessed by quantifying CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells by immunohistochemistry and corneal epithelial integrity was assessed via corneal fluorescein staining (CFS) (Fig. 1H).

Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT) and Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT)

Glucose homeostasis was assessed in the same cohort of mice using GTTs and ITTs, separated by a 1-week washout period. All procedures were performed at a fixed ZT under a 12:12 hour light–dark cycle to minimize circadian variability.

For the GTT,27,28 mice were fasted for 16 hours with ad libitum access to water, followed by an oral gavage of glucose (2 g/kg body weight). Blood glucose levels were measured from the tail vein at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes using a portable glucometer (Yuwell 720, Yuwell, Danyang, Jiangsu, China).

After a 1-week recovery, the ITT was conducted after a 6-hour fast. Baseline glucose was recorded before intraperitoneal administration of human insulin (1 U/kg; Novo Nordisk, Beijing, China). Tail vein glucose was subsequently measured at the same time points (0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 minutes).29

ELG Collection

After 30 days of SUC (0.72 mg/mL) exposure, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (≥100 mg/kg), followed by cervical dislocation, and ELGs were dissected within 2 minutes postmortem to preserve molecular integrity. Tissue collection was performed at 3-hour intervals across a full 24-hour light–dark cycle (ZT0–ZT21) to capture circadian dynamics. Immediately after excision, each gland was rinsed in ice-cold PBS, blotted dry, and transferred into prechilled cryogenic tubes. Samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further processing for RNA-seq and proteomic analyses.

RNA-Seq

RNA-seq was performed as previously described.30,31 Briefly, ELGs were minced on ice, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), the RNA was then extracted twice with chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (24:1), precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 75% ethanol, and finally dissolved the precipitate in 20 to 200 µL RNase-free water to obtain high-purity RNA. Qualitative and quantitative analysis of total RNA was performed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

mRNA was enriched using oligo (dT) magnetic beads and fragmented in the presence of Mg²⁺ to an average size of <200 bp. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand synthesis using dNTPs, RNase, and DNA polymerase I. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified, end-repaired, adenylated at the 3′ ends, and ligated to sequencing adapters. Adapter-ligated fragments were amplified and circularized by Phi29 polymerase to generate DNA nanoballs. After being adsorbed onto silicon chips, DNA nanoballs were loaded into the MGISEQ-2000 platform for sequencing. The raw sequencing data were filtered using SOAPnuke software (GitHub, https://github.com/BGIflexlab/SOAPnuke, version 1.5.2)32 to obtain clean reads. Subsequently, the data were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2 software (SourceForge, https://sourceforge.net/projects/bowtie-bio, version 2.2.5).33 Finally, gene expression was quantified using the RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM, version 1.2.12) tool.

Tissue Lysis and Protein Extraction

ELGs were transferred to 2-mL prechilled microtubes containing two 5-mm stainless-steel beads and kept on ice throughout the procedure. The tissue was lysed in an appropriate amount of protein lysis buffer containing SDS and 1X Cocktail with a final concentration of EDTA. Homogenization was then performed using a bead mill homogenizer on ice.

To reduce disulfide bonds, dithiothreitol was added to a final concentration of 10 mM and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes, followed by the addition of iodoacetamide (55 mM) for 45 minutes in the dark. Proteins were precipitated by adding five volumes of prechilled acetone, incubating at −20°C for 2 hours, and centrifuging at 25,000×g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The resulting pellet was air-dried briefly and resuspended in SDS-free lysis buffer, then rehomogenized using an automated tissue disruptor.

After a final centrifugation (25,000×g, 15 minutes, 4°C), the supernatant was collected as the soluble protein fraction. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and all samples were stored at −80°C until further proteomic analysis. The proteins that passed quality inspection were enzymatically digested, followed by rapid scanning and mass spectrometry data acquisition using a timsTOF Pro liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). Finally, data analysis and quality control were performed with the mProphet algorithm to obtain quantitative results of the detected proteins.

Differential Expression Analysis

Differential transcriptomic analysis was conducted in R (v4.2.1) using the DESeq2 package (Bioconductor, version 1.38.3). Raw read count matrices from all samples were imported and normalized with the default median of ratios method to correct for library size and sequencing depth. Dispersion estimates and model fitting were performed using the negative binomial generalized linear model framework implemented in DESeq2.

Genes showing an absolute fold change >1.2 or <1.0/1.2 and an adjusted P value of <0.05 were considered differentially expressed (differentially expressed genes (DEGs)). Exploratory transcript-level subset analyses used a more permissive threshold (absolute fold change of >1.2 or <1.0/1.2 and P < 0.05) for pathway-focused annotation only and were not intended as independent estimates of global transcript–protein concordance.34

The resulting DEG lists were exported for downstream analyses, including PCA, heatmap visualization, functional enrichment (KEGG, GSEA), and cross-omics integration with the proteomic dataset.

Differential Protein Abundance Analysis

Differential protein quantification was conducted using the MSstats package (Bioconductor, version 4.6.2) in R (v4.2.1). Systematic error correction, data normalization, and statistical significance testing were conducted for all samples. Proteins were considered differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) if they exhibited an absolute fold change of >1.2 or <1.0/1.2, and an adjusted P value of <0.05.

The resulting DAP dataset was used for downstream functional enrichment (KEGG, GSEA) and multi-omics integration with the transcriptomic data.

Circadian Gene and Protein Rhythmicity Analysis

Circadian rhythmicity of gene and protein expression was analyzed in R (v4.2.1) using the JTK_CYCLE algorithm (v3.1) to identify oscillatory features across the 24-hour light–dark cycle. Transcriptomic data were expressed as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads, and proteomic data as normalized abundance values. Data from the NC and SUC groups were analyzed separately to assess group-specific rhythmic profiles.

A fixed period of 24 hours was specified, corresponding with the experimental sampling design (eight time points at 3-hour intervals, ZT0–ZT21). For each gene or protein, the algorithm estimated the phase (acrophase), amplitude, and adjusted P value using the nonparametric Kendall's tau correlation method implemented in JTK_CYCLE.

Rhythmic features were defined by the following thresholds: for the transcriptome, adjusted P < 0.05, fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads sum > 0.1, and period = 24 hours; for the proteome, adjusted P < 0.05 and period = 24 hours. The resulting rhythmic genes and proteins were subjected to downstream analyses, including phase distribution profiling, heatmap visualization, and KEGG pathway enrichment to characterize circadian functional architecture in each group. JTK_CYCLE was used for rhythmic calling.

KEGG-Based Functional Annotation and Pathway Enrichment

Functional annotation and pathway enrichment analyses were performed for both transcriptomic and proteomic datasets using the KEGG database (Release 81.0). Gene and protein identifiers were mapped to the NCBI RefSeq GRCm38.p5 genome build, which served as the background universe for enrichment calculations. Significantly enriched pathways were defined as those with Q < 0.05 for transcript-level analyses and adjusted P < 0.05 for protein-level analyses.35

GSEA

GSEA was performed using the GSEA software suite (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org). Expression matrices and phenotype annotation files were formatted according to the developer's guidelines. The M5.go.bp.v2025.1.Mm.symbols.gmt gene set database, encompassing Gene Ontology Biological Process categories, was used as the reference collection.

To enhance sensitivity for differential expression between groups, the signal-to-noise ratio was applied as the gene-ranking metric. Gene sets containing <15 genes or >500 genes were excluded to minimize bias from very small or large categories. Each analysis was conducted with 100 permutations to ensure statistical robustness of enrichment scores.

Significance thresholds followed established standards: gene sets with a false discovery rate (FDR) of <0.25 were considered significantly enriched. Core outputs included the enrichment score, normalized enrichment score, nominal P value, and FDR. Enrichment results were visualized using ridge plots and heatmaps to illustrate the temporal and pathway-level differences between groups.

Time-Series Clustering Analysis

Time-series clustering was conducted in R (v4.2.1) using the Mfuzz package (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/Mfuzz.html) to identify genes with similar temporal expression profiles. The analysis used the fuzzy c-means clustering algorithm, which allows each gene to belong to multiple clusters with varying degrees of membership, thereby preserving the inherent biological continuity of circadian expression data.

Before clustering, rhythmic genes (identified by JTK_CYCLE, adjusted P < 0.05) were standardized using z-score transformation to ensure comparability across time points. The number of clusters (c) was empirically set to 4 based on within-cluster variance optimization. The minimum standard deviation (min.std) threshold was set to 0, and the core threshold was defined as 0.7, ensuring that only genes with strong temporal coherence were retained within each cluster. Separate clustering analyses were performed for the NC and SUC groups to explore the differences in gene expression patterns between the two groups.

Immunohistochemistry and Oil Red O Staining

Paraffin-embedded sections of mouse ELGs were prepared for immunohistochemical analysis. Sections were deparaffinized using environmentally friendly clearing solution followed by rehydration through graded ethanol to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) at 95°C for 30 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 minutes, and nonspecific binding was prevented by incubation with 3% BSA for 30 minutes at room temperature.

Primary antibodies were applied as follows: anti-CD4 (1:200, Cat. No. GB15064, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and anti-CD8 (1:500, Cat. No. GB15068, Servicebio). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber, rinsed with PBS, and subsequently incubated with species-appropriate horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies for 50 minutes at room temperature. Visualization was achieved using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Slides were dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted for microscopic evaluation.

For Oil Red O staining, ELG tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound for cryosectioning. NC and SUC sections were processed in parallel in the same staining run. Sections were post-fixed for 15 minutes, immersed in freshly prepared and filtered Oil Red O working solution for 10 minutes, and differentiated in 60% isopropanol to remove background staining. After rinsing in distilled water, sections were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted using glycerol gelatin. Lipid droplets and immune cell distributions were examined under a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and quantitative analyses were performed using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). Oil Red O–positive lipid droplets were segmented using a fixed threshold applied identically to all images, followed by particle analysis with predefined size and circularity filters to exclude diffuse background staining. Because these histological measurements were obtained from independent animals collected at different ZTs, the data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA and independent samples t test.

Tear Secretion Measurement

Tear secretion was quantified as previously described36–38 using the phenol red thread test under light anesthesia. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane vapor (1%–2%), and pilocarpine hydrochloride (4.5 mg/kg, intraperitoneal; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was administered to induce reflex tear secretion. After 10 minutes, a sterile phenol red thread (Cat. No. 30059010; Tianjin Jingming New Technology Development Co., Tianjin, China) was gently placed in the inferior conjunctival fornix at the medial canthus for 20 seconds.

The wetted length (mm) of the thread, indicated by color change from yellow to red, was immediately measured under ambient light and recorded as the tear secretion index. The mean value of both eyes for each animal was used for statistical analysis.

CFS

Corneal epithelial integrity was evaluated by fluorescein sodium staining as described previously.39,40 Under light isoflurane anesthesia, a 1% fluorescein sodium solution (Tianjin Jingming, China) was instilled into the inferior conjunctival fornix. After 30 seconds, excess dye was rinsed with sterile saline, and images were captured 2 minutes later under cobalt blue illumination using a slit-lamp biomicroscope (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan).

The punctate epithelial staining was graded using a blinded method. Each cornea was divided into four quadrants and scored separately (0 = none; 1 = mild (<30 dots); 2 = moderate (>30 dots non-diffuse); 3 = diffuse without plaques; and 4 = diffuse with plaques). The total CFS score (0–16) was obtained by summing quadrant scores; higher values indicated greater epithelial damage.

Tear Film Breakup Time (TBUT)

Tear film stability was assessed by measuring TBUT as previously described.39 Mice were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane, and a 5-µL drop of 1% sodium fluorescein solution was instilled into the lower conjunctival sac. After 30 seconds, excess dye was rinsed with sterile saline, and residual fluid around the eyelids was gently blotted dry. Under cobalt blue illumination using a slit-lamp biomicroscope (Topcon, Tokyo, Japan), the interval between the last blink and the appearance of the first dry spot on the corneal surface was recorded as the TBUT. Each eye was measured three times, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis and Software

Bar charts and line graphs in this study were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Analysis of similarities and differences in circadian genes between the NC and SUC groups was presented using the Venn Diagram Plotter tool (https://jvenn.toulouse.inrae.fr/app/example.html). Heatmaps were generated using the pheatmap package in R software (version 4.2.1). PCA was performed using the SangerBox website (http://vip.sangerbox.com/home.html) by importing gene expression tables and grouping tables for plotting. For longitudinal datasets obtained repeatedly from the same experimental unit (e.g., food intake, water intake, body weight, GTT, and ITT), normality was assessed before parametric testing. Repeated-measures ANOVA was used to evaluate differences across time points and between groups for normally distributed data, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post hoc independent-samples t tests where appropriate. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test was used, as appropriate.

For histological and immunohistochemical datasets obtained from independent animals sacrificed at different ZTs, before parametric testing, normality was assessed. Two-way ANOVA was used. Under the assumption of homogeneity of variances, if the group-by-time interaction was not significant, the main effect of time was examined, with a P value of <0.05 indicating statistically significant differences between time points. If the interaction was significant, post hoc independent samples t tests were performed with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical data are presented as mean ± SEM, with a P value of <0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.

Results

Systemic Metabolic Effects of High-Dose SUC

To evaluate the systemic metabolic and behavioral effects of high-dose SUC intake in mice, daily food intake, water intake, body weight dynamics, blood glucose levels, GTTs, and ITTs were systematically measured. In terms of daily food intake, the SUC-treated group showed slight changes compared with the NC group during the first 16 days, suggesting an early adjustment in feeding behavior. However, this difference diminished in the later stages, indicating that high-dose SUC intake had no significant effect on food consumption (repeated-measures ANOVA: intersubject effect test: F = 2.434; P = 0.17) (Fig. 2A). Conversely, daily water intake was significantly higher in the SUC-treated group throughout the experiment, suggesting a sustained increase in water consumption driven by SUC intake (repeated-measures ANOVA: intersubject effect test: F = 200.374; P < 0.001; t test: t5 = −10.401, P5 < 0.001; t9 = −25.496, P9 < 0.001; t13 = −10.769, P13 = 0.001; t16 = −12.658, P16 < 0.001; t19 = −9.938, P19 < 0.001; t23 = −10.563, P23 < 0.001; t26 = −10.693, P26 = 0.001; t30 = −8.252, P30 < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

(B) Changes in daily water intake (mL) after high-dose SUC administration, assessing the impact on drinking behavior. n = 4 (cage). (C) Body weight dynamics in mice subjected to high-dose SUC intake, compared with the NC group. n = 10 (mouse). (D) Blood glucose levels after 30 days of high-dose SUC intake. n = 10 (mouse). Body weight monitoring revealed similar trends in both groups during the early stages of the experiment. However, by day 16, the SUC-treated group exhibited a significant increase in body weight compared with the NC group (repeated-measures ANOVA: intersubject effect test: F = 32.133; P < 0.001; t test: t0 = 0.456, P0 = 0.654; t5 = −0.599, P5 = 0.557; t9 = 1.148, P9 = 0.266; t13 = 0.698, P13 = 0.186; t16 = −4.248, P16 = 0.001; t19 = −1.888, P19 = 0.075; t23 = −3.011, P23 = 0.007; t26 = −2.379, P26 = 0.029; t30 = −3.324, P30 = 0.004) (Fig. 2C). This finding suggests that SUC intake may promote weight gain, potentially through effects on energy metabolism or other physiological mechanisms. Despite these changes, blood glucose levels measured at the end of the 30-day period showed no significant differences between the SUC-treated and NC groups (repeated-measures ANOVA: intersubject effect test: F = 0.009; P = 0.927; intrasubject effect test: F = 0.411; P = 0.529) (Fig. 2D). This result indicates minimal effects of SUC on basal glucose homeostasis.

To further explore the metabolic effects of SUC, GTTs and ITTs were conducted. The results revealed no significant differences between the SUC-treated and NC groups in either GTT (between-subjects effects: F = 0.014; P = 0.907) (Fig. 2E) or ITT (repeated-measures ANOVA: intersubject effect test: F = 0.026; P = 0.873) (Fig. 2F) curves. These findings suggest that, although SUC intake alters water consumption and body weight, it does not impair glucose metabolism or insulin sensitivity.

In conclusion, long-term high-dose SUC intake has no significant adverse effects on food consumption, blood glucose levels, glucose metabolism, or insulin sensitivity in mice. However, the observed increases in water intake and body weight highlight specific behavioral and physiological changes associated with SUC intake.

Reprogramming of Lacrimal Circadian Transcriptome and Proteome

Using 24-hour sampling at 3-hour resolution (n = 3 biological replicates per ZT per group), we next asked whether high-dose SUC reprograms the circadian organization of the ELG transcriptome and proteome. We detected 19,117 genes in the ELG transcriptome. By JTK (period = 24 hours; adjusted P < 0.05), the number of rhythmic genes was 3074 in NC (16.1% of detected) and 4600 in SUC (24.1%) (Fig. 3A). The phase distribution of rhythmic genes was shifted: rhythmic genes in NC concentrated at ZT9 to ZT13, whereas in SUC they concentrated at ZT3 to ZT7 (Fig. 3B). The overlap comprised 1503 shared genes (48.9% of NC rhythmic; 32.7% of SUC rhythmic), with 1571 NC-unique and 3097 SUC-unique rhythmic genes (Fig. 3C). Multi-Omics Integration Reveals Changes in Metabolic–Immune Pathways

To characterize time-of-day expression patterns and link them to overall expression differences between conditions, rhythmic features (JTK_CYCLE, period = 24 hours; adjusted P < 0.05) were clustered with Mfuzz (c = 4) using standardized abundance across ZT0, ZT3, ZT6, ZT9, ZT12, ZT15, ZT18, and ZT21 (n = 3 biological replicates per ZT per group).

In the rhythmic transcriptome, the four clusters captured distinct 24-hour profiles (Figs. 4A–H). Cluster 1 peaked near the light–dark transition and contained 970 genes in NC (31.6%) and 1137 in SUC (24.7%). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that both the NC and SUC groups were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways; NC genes were concentrated in carbohydrate metabolism, whereas SUC genes shifted toward carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism. Cluster 2 showed a trough at the light–dark boundary with 226 (7.4%) and 974 (21.2%) genes in NC and SUC, respectively. No KEGG pathway enrichment was detected for this trend in NC, whereas SUC genes were enriched in disease- and immune-related pathways. Cluster 3 exhibited light-phase peaks and dark-phase troughs with 952 (31.0%) genes in NC and 1813 (39.4%) in SUC. Here, NC genes were predominantly enriched in metabolism-related pathways, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, biosynthesis of amino acids, and biosynthesis of cofactors, whereas SUC genes were mainly enriched in cellular process pathways such as autophagy. Cluster 4 displayed the reciprocal temporal pattern with 926 (30.1%) genes in NC and 676 (14.7%) in SUC; in both groups, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated similar enrichment in disease-related pathways, including fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis and lipid and atherosclerosis (Figs. 4A–H).

High-Dose SUC Intake Reduces Tear Secretion in Mice

Finally, to determine whether the molecular and cellular changes in the lacrimal gland translate into measurable ocular surface phenotypes, ocular surface endpoints were quantified in mice receiving water (NC) or 0.72 mg/mL SUC for 30 days (n = 8/group). Stimulated tear secretion measured by the phenol-red thread test (mm) was lower in SUC than NC at four ZT time points (ZT0, ZT6, ZT12, and ZT18) (two-way ANOVA: Levene's test: F = 0.285; P = 0.957; intersubject effect test: P time × group = 0.779; t test: t0 = 3.438, P0 = 0.004; t6 = 5.187, P6 < 0.001; t12 = 3.762, P12 = 0.002; t18 = 3.719, P18 = 0.002; one-way ANOVA: FNC = 2.666, PNC = 0.067; Fsuc = 4.65, Psuc = 0.009, P0 vs.6 = 0.295, P0 vs.12 = 0.988, P0 vs.18 = 0.243, P6 vs.12 = 0.171, P6 vs.18 = 0.005, P12 vs.18 = 0.397) (Figs. 8A, 8B). These results indicate that the ingestion of high concentrations of SUC was associated with reduced stimulated tear secretion but did not produce detectable corneal epithelial damage under the present conditions.

Discussion

Using 24-hour time-resolved transcriptomics and 4D–data-independent acquisition proteomics, we found that chronic high-dose SUC exposure was accompanied by remodeling of the rhythmic landscape in the mouse ELG. SUC increased the number of rhythmic transcripts and proteins and redistributed their phase distributions, and integrated multi-omics analyses pointed to coordinated suppression of metabolic and immune–inflammatory pathways. These molecular changes coincided with increased water intake and body weight, whereas glucose tolerance remained largely preserved in this paradigm. Functionally, SUC-treated mice showed reduced stimulated tear secretion across the four sampled ZTs. Taken together, our findings support a working model in which high-dose SUC exposure is associated with remodeling of peripheral lacrimal timing, metabolic, and immune signatures, together with reduced tear secretion. Whether SUC acts directly on peripheral timing systems or indirectly through behavioral, endocrine, microbial, or other systemic pathways requires direct testing.

Relationship to Recent Work

The immunosuppressive profile observed here is consistent with recent evidence that high-dose SUC suppresses adaptive immunity by limiting T-cell proliferation and differentiation in mice13,41 and with controlled human data showing that non-nutritive sweeteners induce individualized, microbiome-dependent glycemic responses,14,42 supporting a gut–microbiome–immune axis rather than an inert sweetener model and motivating a putative gut–immune–lacrimal link. With respect to circadian organization, sweet taste inputs can serve as nonphotic zeitgebers, as sucrose and SUC accelerate behavioral re-entrainment in jet-lagged mice via peripheral clock modulation,43 and long-term high-fructose intake reprograms lacrimal circadian transcription and disrupts glandular homeostasis.24 We found that SUC, a non-nutritive sweeteners similarly reconfigured lacrimal clock and immunometabolic programs, thereby extending these dietary and temporal influences to the ocular adnexa and tear secretion.

Proposed Mechanistic Model

Our time-resolved multi-omics data suggest that high-dose SUC exposure is associated with a coordinated shift in lacrimal circadian organization and immunometabolic state. In the lacrimal gland, we observed an expanded set of rhythmic transcripts and proteins with redistributed phase distributions, together with broad downregulation of metabolic and immune–inflammatory pathways (including complement– and T-cell–associated features). These tissue-level signatures coincided with reduced tear secretion, indicating that lacrimal circadian and immunometabolic programs are candidate correlates of the ocular surface phenotype in this model.

The upstream drivers of this lacrimal remodeling are not resolved by the present dataset. Sweet taste inputs have been reported to accelerate behavioral re-entrainment in jet-lagged mice via peripheral clock modulation, raising the possibility that sweetener-related cues can influence peripheral timing.43 However, because we did not measure intake timing, locomotor activity, or SCN phase, we cannot distinguish a direct zeitgeber effect from indirect behavioral and systemic influences (e.g., altered drinking patterns) on lacrimal clocks.

Downstream of circadian organization, several processes relevant to tear film homeostasis are known to be clock linked, including BMAL1-dependent mucin biology at the ocular surface,44 lacrimal remodeling under dietary sugar stress,24 and ductal water handling and barrier regulation involving aquaporins and junctional components.45–47 Although we did not directly assess AQP5 abundance/localization or junctional permeability, these pathways provide focused candidates for how circadian remodeling could accompany reduced tear secretion.

Finally, human data indicate that responses to non-nutritive sweeteners can be microbiome dependent14 and peripheral sweet taste receptors (TAS1R3/TRPM5) can couple nutrient sensing to hormonal and neural outputs.48 These observations provide context for a gut–systemic route to lacrimal signaling, but microbiome and endocrine intermediates were not measured here. Overall, our results support a working model in which high-dose SUC exposure is associated with lacrimal circadian remodeling and immunometabolic suppression that accompany reduced tear secretion, while the upstream timing signals and downstream secretory effectors remain to be defined.

Translational and Public Health

The World Health Organization's 2023 guideline advises against using NSSs for long-term weight control, reflecting uncertainty in long-term benefit and potential cardiometabolic risk.2 The present study used a mechanistic, high-exposure paradigm (0.72 mg/mL SUC in drinking water) that likely exceeds typical human intake and was chosen to probe biological potential rather than population-level risk; similar supra–acceptable daily intake models show that SUC at or >0.3 mg/mL can alter gut microbiota, induce oxidative stress, and promote adipogenic signaling.49–51 Within this boundary, the data nominate clock remodeling, metabolic suppression, and immune pathway downshifts as pathways that can be tested for relevance to lacrimal function.

Future translational work should confirm internal exposure by quantifying SUC and its metabolites in plasma, tear fluid, or lacrimal tissue, given evidence that dietary-level SUC inhibits P-glycoprotein–mediated detoxification,52 and should establish dose–response relationships within acceptable daily intake ranges, as even low-dose exposure modifies gut microbial composition and immune tone.49 It will also be important to assess time-of-day effects on tear film physiology, because circadian phase strongly modulates metabolic and endocrine responses to sweeteners,53,54 and to perform comparative studies under iso-sweetness conditions with caloric sugars and other NSS; randomized and long-term data show heterogeneous outcomes ranging from insulin elevation to neutral or beneficial effects.55–57 Prior work indicates that the metabolic consequences of SUC can vary with exposure conditions, including dose and intake timing, microbiome state, and concurrent nutrient intake.58 Extending this exposure–context framework to the lacrimal gland, our data suggest that both the amount and timing of SUC consumption—together with the host's microbial and immunometabolic milieu—may influence tear secretion.

Clinically, several proteomic studies in patients with dry eye disease and meibomian gland dysfunction have reported increased levels of complement components and inflammatory mediators in tear fluid.59 In contrast, high-dose SUC suppressed complement (C1qa, C4b) and T-cell–associated proteins in the lacrimal gland. Because we did not profile tear proteomes or tear cytokines, we cannot infer how these tissue programs translate to the immune composition of the tear film. A more conservative interpretation is that SUC exposure is associated with altered immune-related programs in the lacrimal gland, and that paired lacrimal–tear profiling will be required to determine whether complement– and T-cell–related changes are reflected in tear film immunity.

Our findings also intersect with chrononutrition, which emphasizes that the timing of intake can be as relevant as total dose. If SUC intake is clustered at specific times of day, it could differentially influence peripheral timing cues and lacrimal programs, given the circadian gating of endocrine and autonomic outputs. Future studies should therefore systematically vary the timing of SUC exposure relative to the light–dark cycle and feeding schedule, and assess whether time-restricted intake mitigates or amplifies lacrimal and ocular surface phenotypes.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, we studied only male mice under a single, mechanistic high-dose regimen; whether females, other metabolic states, or lower doses within the acceptable daily intake range exhibit similar lacrimal sensitivity remains unknown. Second, we did not manipulate clock genes (e.g., Bmal1, Rev-erbα) or T-cell/complement pathways in a gain- or loss-of-function manner,60–62 so causal links between specific nodes and SUC-induced phenotypes remain inferential. Third, because ocular surface end points were sampled at four ZTs, these measurements provide time-of-day–sampled group comparisons but are not sufficient to establish circadian rhythmic parameters or to robustly test group × time interactions for tear film metrics. Fourth, we did not quantify intake timing, locomotor activity, or SCN phase, which limits inference about whether SUC acts as a zeitgeber vs. indirectly reshaping peripheral timing cues through behavior and systemic signals. Fifth, under the present exposure conditions, the estimated human-equivalent dose was approximately 15 mg/kg/d, which is close to the current EFSA acceptable maximum daily intake for SUC, whereas typical population exposure estimates remain below this level. Therefore, the present model should be interpreted as a mechanistic high-exposure paradigm rather than a simulation of average human intake. Sixth, a notable limitation of the integrated analysis is the modest overlap between differentially expressed transcripts and proteins. This finding may reflect differences in transcript and protein stability, time lags, post-transcriptional regulation, and depth of analysis within the circadian sampling design. Consequently, our conclusions are confined to pathway-level convergence and should be interpreted as hypothesis generating before direct validation. Finally, we did not profile tear fluid metabolites, lipids, or the ocular and gut microbiomes, even though metabolomic and microbiome signatures are likely to mediate sweetener effects along a gut–microbiome–immune–lacrimal axis.

Future Directions

Looking ahead, four lines of investigation appear most informative. First, direct perturbation of key clock and immune/complement nodes should test the necessity and reversibility of SUC-induced phenotypes, for example, by pharmacological or genetic targeting of the REV-ERB/ROR axis, which governs Bmal1 transcription and metabolic–immune coordination,63,64 and by exploiting models in which REV-ERBα deletion elevates C3/C4b and enhances microglial and macrophage activation65,66 to probe clock-controlled complement rhythms in lacrimal immune homeostasis. Second, microbiome-focused studies are needed to define a gut–microbiome–lacrimal axis in sweetener-induced clock remodeling; REV-ERB/ROR regulation of innate lymphoid cells links circadian timing, short chain fatty acid metabolism, and mucosal immunity67 and provides a framework for combining microbiome/metabolite profiling with gnotobiotic and fecal transfer approaches.

Third, dose–response, sex-stratified, and time-of-day–resolved paradigms will be essential to define the exposure thresholds, recovery dynamics, and host contexts under which SUC reshapes lacrimal clocks and tear film homeostasis. Future research should explore whether these findings are sex specific and can be extended to female mice, ideally coupled with tear metabolomics, lipidomics, and microbiome profiling. Finally, this study can be compared with future research on other artificial sweeteners (such as acesulfame-K, because it is widely used, chemically distinct from SUC, and often coformulated with other NSSs in beverages and processed foods) to determine whether the currently observed circadian rhythm and immunometabolic changes in the lacrimal gland are unique to SUC or reflect a broader, more general effect of sweeteners.

Conclusions

Under this mechanistic high-exposure paradigm, high-dose SUC exposure was associated with alterations in lacrimal circadian organization, reduced metabolic and immune-associated pathway signatures, and lower stimulated tear secretion. These findings extend current discussion of non-nutritive sweeteners from systemic metabolism and gut microbiome biology to the ocular adnexa and identify candidate timing and immunometabolic pathways for future causal testing at more diet-relevant exposure levels (Fig. 9).

Working model linking high-dose SUC exposure to lacrimal circadian remodeling and reduced tear secretion. Schematic summary of the working model derived from the present data. Oral SUC intake is associated with remodeling of the intrinsic lacrimal circadian network. This remodeling coincides with three concurrent molecular signatures in the gland: (1) altered RNA and protein expression programs; (2) downregulation of immune-related signatures, including complement- and T cell–associated modules (e.g., C1qa and C4b); and (3) metabolic alterations accompanied by altered neutral lipid handling. These lacrimal signatures co-occur with reduced tear secretion in this high-dose paradigm. Whether the changes in lacrimal gland programs translate into alterations in tear film composition or ocular surface homeostasis remains to be determined. Orange arrows indicate hypothesized rather than established relationships.
