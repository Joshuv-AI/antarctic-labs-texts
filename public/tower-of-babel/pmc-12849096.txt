A novel potent autophagy inhibitor HM-013 limits production of infectious dengue virus particles

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue virus (DENV) infects millions of individuals annually, yet no specific antiviral therapy exists. Autophagy, a conserved cellular degradation pathway, is activated during DENV infection and supports the production of infectious virions. Although autophagy modulation has emerged as a potential antiviral strategy, few small-molecule autophagy inhibitors with both potent anti-dengue activity and low cytotoxicity have been reported. Thus, identifying novel, safe, and effective autophagy-targeting compounds remains an important unmet need for host-directed antiviral development.MethodsWe performed a high-content imaging screen of a natural product-derived compound library to identify autophagy inhibitors. Candidate compounds were evaluated for autophagy inhibition and anti-dengue activity using LC3 puncta quantification and plaque assays. The most potent compound was further characterized by immunoblotting to assess autophagy inhibition. Its effects on DENV genome replication, viral protein expression, and infectious particle production were examined by qRT-PCR, immunofluorescence imaging, flow cytometry, and plaque assays. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the MTS assay.ResultsThe screen identified HM-013, a 1,4-naphthoquinone derivative structurally related to lawsone, lapachol, juglone, and plumbagin, as a potent autophagy inhibitor. Mechanistically, HM-013 did not inhibit viral genome replication but instead blocked a late stage of the viral life cycle. Background

Dengue virus (DENV), a member of the Flaviviridae family, is responsible for dengue fever and severe forms of the disease such as dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome [1]. Transmission occurs primarily through the bite of infected Aedes mosquitoes and approximately 400 million cases and 22,000 deaths occur due to dengue worldwide each year [2]. DENV primarily targets monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells and the virus initially replicates at the site of inoculation and then disseminates to regional lymph nodes and the bloodstream [3]. The virus has four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4), and infection with one serotype provides lifelong immunity to that serotype but not to the others [4]. A second infection with a different serotype significantly increases the risk of severe disease due to antibody-dependent enhancement, where non-neutralizing antibodies facilitate viral entry into host cells resulting in increased viremia, exacerbated immune responses and vascular dysfunction [5]. DENV has been reported in more than 100 countries in tropical and subtropical regions [2]. Currently there is no specific drug for dengue, underscoring the need for effective therapeutic strategies.

Autophagy is a highly regulated cellular degradation pathway essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis under basal and stress conditions [6]. During autophagy, cytoplasmic substrates including damaged organelles and intracellular pathogens are sequestered into double-membrane autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes for cargo degradation [7]. In viral infections, autophagy can exert either antiviral or pro-viral effects depending on the virus and cell type, reflecting the pathway’s inherently dual nature. For DENV, however, multiple studies have demonstrated that the virus predominantly exploits autophagy to enhance its replication. Autophagosomes have been implicated as sites for DENV genome replication and virion maturation [8–12]. DENV also induces lipophagy to mobilize lipid droplets and generate free fatty acids that support efficient viral replication [13]. Changes in lipid metabolism additionally promote membrane fluidity that facilitates nucleocapsid envelopment during virion maturation [14]. Furthermore, recent studies have highlighted the role of secretory autophagosomes in facilitating the release and dissemination of DENV from infected host cells [15]. Unlike the traditional autophagy pathway, which culminates in lysosomal-mediated degradation, secretory autophagosomes bypass lysosomal fusion and instead fuse with the plasma membrane to release a single-membrane autophagosome containing cargo into the extracellular environment [16]. DENV exploits this unconventional autophagy pathway to evade from neutralizing antibodies during transport, thereby promoting infection of distant cells [15]. Together, these studies indicate that although autophagy can be antiviral in other viral contexts, DENV infection primarily co-opts autophagy for pro-viral functions.

Based on the above information, inhibition of autophagy is considered as an attractive strategy to treat DENV infection. In this work, we utilised high-content imaging-based screening to identify autophagy inhibitory compounds synthesised from or inspired by natural products and their precursors. HM-013, a derivative of 1,4-naphthoquinone, a core structure found in some natural products such as lawsone, lapachol, juglone and plumbagin, was identified from our screen to effectively decrease the number of autophagosomes in cells undergoing autophagy induction. Further characterization showed that HM-013 dose-dependently inhibits autophagy at low micromolar concentrations. It also potently inhibits DENV infectious virion production in a concentration-dependent manner at low micromolar concentrations. Mechanistically, HM-013 does not inhibit DENV genome replication but instead acts at a late stage of DENV infection, impairing infectious viral particle formation and/or release from host cells. Altogether, these data indicated the potential development of HM-013 to be used for dengue treatment.

Methods

Cells, inhibitors and antibodies

HepG2 cells (ATCC) and CRISPR-Cas9 GFP-LC3 knock-in (KI) HeLa cells [17] were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 4 mM L-glutamine (Hyclone) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. CRISPR-Cas9 GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells [17] were maintained in IMDM (Gibco) containing 10% FBS and 4 mM L-glutamine under the same incubation conditions. Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS; Sigma) was used as the starvation medium. Bafilomycin A1 (Baf; LC Laboratories) was used at a final concentration of 0.1 µM. For immunoblotting, the following antibodies were used: polyclonal anti-p62 (Progen) at a 1:3,000 dilution, polyclonal anti-LC3 (MBL International) at a 1:2,000 dilution, and monoclonal anti-Actin (Abcam) at a 1:10,000 dilution. For immunofluorescence staining, the following reagents were used: Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen) at a 1:500 dilution, monoclonal anti-DENV E (clone 4G2; ATCC) at a 1:100 dilution, Alexa568-conjugated polyclonal anti-mouse IgG at a 1:400 dilution. For flow cytometry, the following antibodies were used: polyclonal anti-NS1 (GeneTex) at a 1:100 dilution, rabbit IgG isotype control (Invitrogen) at a 1:100 dilution, and Alexa568-conjugated polyclonal anti-rabbit IgG at a 1:500 dilution.

Compound screening

Natural product-derived compounds and their derivatives were subjected to high-throughput screening to evaluate their autophagy inhibitory activity using the Operetta high-content imaging system (PerkinElmer). The assay quantified GFP-LC3+ puncta, an established marker of autophagosomes, in GFP-LC3 KI HeLa cells. Briefly, 6 × 103 cells were seeded per well in 96-well black plates and incubated overnight. Autophagy was induced by replacing the culture medium with Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS) following three PBS washes. Cells treated with DMSO served as the negative control. Each tested compound was added at a final concentration of 50 µM in EBSS and incubated for 2 h. A total of 54 compounds were screened. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Hoechst 33342 to visualize nuclei. High-content image analysis was performed to quantify the number of GFP-LC3+ puncta per cell, as previously described [17]. Compounds that reduced autophagosome numbers below the mean value observed in the DMSO control group were designated as candidate autophagy inhibitors.

SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in buffer containing 62.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.01% bromophenol blue. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE using 15% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham Biosciences). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting LC3, p62 or Actin. Following four washes with 0.1% PBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and washed again. Protein bands were visualised using chemiluminescence detection (Roche Diagnostics). The resulting images were analysed using ImageJ software, and band intensities were quantified. Expression levels of target proteins were normalised to Actin, which served as the loading control.

Plaque and virucidal assays

Plaque assay was conducted to evaluate the impact of the tested compounds on the DENV infectious virion production. Briefly, 5 × 105 GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were seeded into each well of 12-well culture plates. After a 16-hour incubation, cells were infected with DENV-2 strain 16681 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 in IMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. Following 1.5 h of viral adsorption, the inoculum was removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS. Infected cells were then cultured in IMDM supplemented with 5% FBS and various concentrations of the tested compounds. Control wells received 1% DMSO without compound. After 48 h, culture supernatants were collected. Plaque quantification was then determined using Vero cells. For virucidal assay, HM-013 (6.25 µM) was incubated with DENV-2 strain 16681 in DMEM containing 5% FBS at 4 °C for 0, 2, 4 and 6 h. The supernatants were collected followed by plaque quantification using Vero cells.

Real-time PCR

GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were either mock-infected or infected with DENV-2 strain 16681 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 for 1.5 h, followed by treatment with 6.25 μM HM-013. DMSO serves as the negative control. At 24 h post-infection, infected cells or culture supernatants were collected. DENV-2 genomic RNA levels were then quantified by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR Kit (2X Master Mix; Kapa Biosystems Inc.). Total RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and cDNAs were synthesised with random hexamer primers and AMV reverse transcriptase (Promega). Amplification was performed using DENV-2-specific primers: forward (5′-CAATATGCTGAAACGCGAGAG-3′) and reverse (5′-GTGGGATTGTTAGGAAACGAA-3′). Viral RNA copy numbers were determined from the threshold cycle (Ct) values and normalised to GAPDH expression. Relative DENV-2 RNA levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, comparing target gene expression in treated samples to DMSO-treated controls: ΔΔCt = ΔCt (DV2 target gene) - ΔCt (DMSO control).

Immunofluorescence assay

GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were seeded into black 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight. The following day, cells were infected with DENV-2 strain 16681 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 5 for 1.5 h. Post-infection, cells were washed and treated with the specified compounds for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed using ice-cold methanol/acetone and immunostained with a monoclonal antibody against DENV envelope (E) protein (clone 4G2) at a 1:100 dilution. This was followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated secondary antibody at a 1:400 dilution, alongside nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342 at a 1:500 dilution. High-content imaging was performed to quantify autophagosomes and viral protein expression.

Time-of-addition experiments

GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 106 cells per well and allowed to equilibrate overnight. Cells were treated with DMSO or HM-013 (6.25 µM) at various time points relative to DENV-2 strain 16681 infection at an MOI of 1: 2 h prior to infection (pre-infection), during the 1.5 h viral adsorption period (during infection), 0-4 h post-infection, 12-24 h post-infection, and 0-24 h post-infection. At 24 h after treatment, culture supernatants were collected for plaque assay quantification, and cells were harvested for flow cytometry and qRT-PCR analyses.

Flow cytometry

Harvested cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min on ice, washed twice with cold PBS, and stored at 4 °C until staining. Cells were permeabilized with Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (BD Biosciences) for 20 min at room temperature, followed by two washes with Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences). Cells were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature with either a polyclonal anti-NS1 antibody or the corresponding isotype control, diluted in Perm/Wash buffer. After two additional washes, cells were stained with an Alexa568-conjugated secondary antibody prepared in Perm/Wash buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, cells were washed twice, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Unless specified otherwise, all experiments were independently performed at least three times, and the resulting data were pooled to calculate the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparison test was employed to assess differences among groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High-content image screening for autophagy inhibitory compounds

As stated above, autophagy is activated during DENV infection and is essential for efficient viral replication [8–12]. Given the traditional use of Thai herbal and natural products in disease treatment, we screened a compound library to identify autophagy inhibitory molecules synthesised from or inspired by natural products using fluorescently-based high-content imaging as previously described [17]. In brief, the previously generated CRISPR-Cas9-engineered GFP-LC3 KI HeLa cells were used. Of note, LC3 is a well-established marker of autophagic vesicles [18], following translation, it is cleaved to form cytoplasmic LC3-I, which is subsequently lipidated to LC3-II and recruited to both the inner and outer membranes of autophagosomes [19]. Cells were treated for 2 h with DMSO (negative control), starvation (EBSS; autophagy induction control), or starvation plus 50 µM of individual tested compounds. The number of GFP-LC3+ puncta per cell was quantified. From this screen, compounds including HM-002, HM-003, HM-004, HM-005, HM-010, HM-013, HM-015, HM-020 and HM-021 were identified as the most effective in reducing GFP-LC3+ puncta per cell (Figures 1a, b).

The following day, cells were treated for 2 h under three conditions: (i) DMEM with 0.1% DMSO (negative control), (ii) Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS) with 0.1% DMSO (autophagy induction control), or (iii) EBSS containing 50 μM of each test compound. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. We next evaluated the autophagy-inhibitory activity of the candidate compounds in a dose-dependent manner to narrow down the candidates. GFP-LC3 KI HeLa cells were treated for 2 h with either DMSO (negative control), wortmannin (standard autophagy inhibitory control), or increasing concentrations of the aforementioned compounds in starvation medium. Autophagosome accumulation was then quantified via high-content imaging. Among the compounds tested, HM-013, HM-020, and HM-021 exhibited the most pronounced dose-dependent inhibition of autophagy (Supplementary Figure 1). Subsequently, these three compounds were screened for their antiviral activity against DENV. To do this, the previously generated CRISPR-Cas9-engineered GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells, used as a host cell model for DENV infection, were infected with DENV-2 at MOI of 1 for 1.5 h. Following infection, cells were washed and treated with serial dilutions of each compound for 48 h. Culture supernatants were collected and analysed via plaque assay to quantify infectious viral particles. Among the candidates, HM-013 demonstrated the strongest dose-dependent inhibition of DENV infectious virion production in GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells, outperforming both HM-020 and HM-021 (Supplementary Figure 2). Based on these findings, HM-013 was selected for further characterization.

HM-013 is a 5,8-dihydro-1,4-naphthoquinone exhibiting potent autophagy inhibitory activity

The structure of HM-013, a 5,8-dihydro-1,4-naphthoquinone, was depicted is Figure 2a. HM-013 could be efficiently prepared via the Diels-Alder reaction of the corresponding synthetic aryldiene and commercially available 1,4-benzoquinone. The detailed preparation followed our developed synthetic method reported previously [20, 21]. The compound was obtained in 24% yield. The structure of HM-013 was confirmed by spectroscopic methods (1H-NMR, 13C-NMR and MS analysis; Figure 2b, c) (Please see Methods for more information).

To validate the autophagy-inhibitory effect of HM-013 and determine its effective concentrations, we conducted a detailed analysis of GFP-LC3 puncta formation in GFP-LC3 KI HeLa cells under starvation conditions. Treatment with HM-013 resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of GFP-LC3+ puncta per cell when compared to that of the starvation control (Figure 3a, b), indicating inhibition of autophagosome formation. Notably, significant inhibition was observed at concentration 10 μM (9.08 ± 0.96 puncta/cell), with maximal suppression at 100 μM (7.25 ± 1.93 puncta/cell), compared to the starvation control (23.98 ± 2.53 puncta/cell), reducing autophagic vacuole numbers to levels comparable to that seen in non-starved cells. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of HM-013 for autophagy inhibition was calculated to be 2.82 ± 1.52 μM.​ Further confirmation was obtained through immunoblot analysis of autophagy markers. GFP-LC3 KI HeLa cells were treated with DMSO (negative control), Baf (autophagic flux inhibitor control), or 6.25 μM HM-013 (IC90 concentration) in both complete and starvation media for 2 h. As expected, Baf treatment led to increased levels of P62 and LC3-II proteins (Figure 3c), reflecting inhibited autophagic flux. In contrast, HM-013 treatment resulted in decreased LC3-II levels without corresponding marked increase in P62 levels, suggesting that HM-013 inhibits autophagosome formation rather than autophagic flux. These findings establish HM-013 as a potent autophagy inhibitor that acts by preventing autophagosome formation.

HM-013 potently inhibits production of DENV infectious particles

To validate the DENV inhibitory effect of HM-013 and determine its effective concentrations, we further investigated the antiviral efficacy of HM-013 in THP-1 cells. To determine this, GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were infected with DENV-2 at MOI of 1 for 1.5 h. Post-infection, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular virus and then treated with serial dilutions of HM-013. After 48 h post infection, supernatants were collected, and the quantity of infectious virions was determined via plaque assay using Vero cells. HM-013 demonstrated a concentration-dependent inhibition of DENV infectious virion production, with low micromolar concentrations (IC50 of 3.53 ± 0.33 μM) (Figure 4a, b). These results confirmed that HM-013 possesses a potent DENV inhibitory activity resulting in DENV restriction in THP-1 cells.

HM-013 does not inhibit DENV genome replication but instead suppresses the late stage of DENV life cycle

To further elucidate HM-013’s DENV-inhibitory properties, we conducted qRT-PCR analyses on both extracellular and intracellular DENV RNA copy numbers in GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells treated with 6.25 μM HM-013 for 24 h. The treatment led to a significant reduction in DENV RNA copy numbers in the supernatant when compared to that of the DMSO-treated control cells (0.54 ± 0.03 fold decrease) (Figure 5a), in agreement with the results obtained from the plaque assay (Figure 4a, b). Surprisingly, when we determined the DENV RNA copy numbers inside the infected cells, a significant accumulation of DENV RNA copy numbers when compared to that of the DMSO-treated control cells was observed (1.99 ± 0.30 fold increase) (Figure 5a). These findings suggested that HM-013 does not act by inhibiting DENV genome replication in THP-1 cells but rather interferes with subsequent steps in the virus life cycle.

We then performed immunofluorescence assay to further investigate the activity of HM-013 in DENV restriction. GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were infected with DENV-2 and treated with DMSO, Baf or 6.25 μM HM-013 for 24 h. Cells were then stained with anti-DENV E protein antibodies followed by high-content imaging analyses. The results showed that DENV infection increased GFP-LC3+ puncta formation as previously described [17] (uninfected: 0.03 ±0.01 puncta/infected cell vs DMSO: 1.00 ±0.16 puncta/infected cell) (Figure 5b, c). Baf treatment further amplified this effect (3.73 ± 0.43 puncta/infected cell), while HM-013 treatment significantly reduced GFP-LC3+ puncta (0.99 ± 0.11 puncta/infected cell) to the levels seen in DMSO-treated control cells. Interestingly, we observed a significant accumulation of DENV E protein mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) inside the infected cells upon treatment with HM-013 when compared to that of the DMSO-treated cells (DMSO: 2.28 ± 0.42 MFI/infected cell vs HM013: 7.99 ± 0.80 MFI/infected cell) (Figure 5c, d). These findings suggested that HM-013 inhibits at the late stage of the virus life cycle.

We next performed virucidal and time-of-addition assays to define the stage of the DENV life cycle targeted by HM-013. To assess whether HM-013 directly inactivates the virus, DENV-2 was incubated with DMSO or HM-013 (6.25 µM) at 4 °C for 0, 2, 4, or 6 h before plaque quantification. Viral titers were comparable between HM-013-treated and DMSO-treated cells (Figure 6a), indicating that HM-013 does not exhibit virucidal activity. We then evaluated the temporal requirement for HM-013 antiviral activity. GFP-LC3 KI THP-1 cells were treated with either DMSO or HM-013 (6.25 µM) at various time points relative to DENV-2 infection: 2 h before infection (pre-infection), during the 1.5 h viral adsorption period (during infection), 0-4 h post-infection, 12-24 h post-infection, or continuously from 0-24 h post-infection. After 24 h, cell culture supernatants were collected for plaque assays, and cells were harvested for flow cytometry and qRT-PCR analyses. The results showed that HM-013 treatment during the 0-24 or 12-24 h post-infection led to intracellular accumulation of DENV NS1 protein (0-24 h post-infection: 36.99 ± 0.73 × 103 MFI/infected cell; 12–24 h post-infection: 35.15 ± 0.80 × 103 MFI/infected cell) compared with DMSO controls (15.99 ± 0.50 × 103 MFI/infected cell) (Figure 6b). Correspondingly, intracellular viral RNA levels were increased in these same treatment conditions (0-24 h post-infection: 1.94 ± 0.31 fold increase; 12-24 h post-infection: 1.42 ± 0.16 fold increase) relative to DMSO-treated cells (Figure 6c). Furthermore, plaque assays of culture supernatants revealed reduced infectious titers only when HM-013 was added during 0-24 or 12-24 h post-infection (0-24 h post-infection: 61.00 ± 10.97 × 103 PFU/mL; 12-24 h post-infection: 85.00 ± 14.43 × 103 PFU/mL) compared with DMSO-treated cells (DMSO: 119.33 ± 20.28 × 103 PFU/mL) (Figure 6d). Together, these results indicated that HM-013 acts during the late stage of the DENV life cycle, likely impairing infectious virion assembly and/or release. Importantly, HM-013 displayed low cytotoxicity toward human HepG2 and THP-1 cells, with CC50 values of 86.06 ± 26.66 µM and 78.11 ± 2.93 µM, respectively (Supplementary Figure 3). These findings highlighted HM-013 as a promising lead compound for further optimization and development as a potential dengue therapeutic.

Discussion

Autophagy can exert antiviral or pro-viral effects depending on the viral species, stage of infection, and host cell type, reflecting the pathway’s context-dependent nature [6, 7]. In the case of DENV, numerous studies have shown that the virus primarily leverages autophagy to support its replication cycle. Autophagosomes facilitate DENV genome replication and virion maturation [8–12]. In addition, lipophagy-mediated lipid mobilization provides metabolic substrates essential for DENV replication [13], and alterations in lipid homeostasis enhance membrane fluidity that is important for nucleocapsid envelopment [14]. Furthermore, secretory autophagy has been implicated in DENV egress and dissemination while enabling virions to avoid antibody neutralization [15].

Our findings are consistent with this pro-viral model. HM-013, a derivative of 1,4-naphthoquinone, a scaffold present in natural compounds such as lawsone, lapachol, juglone, and plumbagin, was shown in this study to be an effective inhibitor of autophagosome formation at low micromolar concentrations. It also significantly suppresses the production of infectious DENV particles at similar concentrations. Mechanistic studies revealed that HM-013 does not inhibit DENV genome replication but rather interferes with the late stage of the virus life cycle, such as the formation and/or release of infectious viral particles, as demonstrated by the time-of-addition experiments. Of note, virucidal assay results showed that HM-013 does not directly inhibit DENV. Moreover, HM-013 exhibits low cytotoxicity towards human liver and monocytic cells, highlighting its potential as a promising candidate for dengue antiviral development.

As mentioned above, HM-013 strongly inhibited autophagosome formation yet did not suppress DENV genome replication. This is similar to prior observations that autophagy inhibition can have a larger impact on virion maturation than on viral RNA replication [10]. Our findings showed that HM-013 caused intracellular accumulation of viral RNA and DENV E protein along with a marked reduction in infectious virion release, suggesting that blocking autophagosome formation disproportionately affects late steps of the DENV life cycle, consistent with our time-of-addition experiments. It is recognized that inhibition of autophagic flux during DENV infection can alter both viral RNA and infectious particle output, and these effects can be distinguished by separately measuring intracellular and extracellular RNA levels [10]. These observations suggest that genome replication (reflected by cell-associated RNA) and virion assembly/release (reflected by extracellular RNA and infectious titers) are regulated by distinct autophagy-dependent processes. Indeed, in the case of DENV, autophagy appears to support virion maturation and secretion more than genome replication [10]. Consistent with this, HM-013 treatment resulted in increased intracellular RNA but reduced extracellular RNA, supporting the notion that autophagy inhibition may impair virion assembly or release rather than upstream RNA synthesis. Of note, few studies explicitly compare intracellular versus extracellular RNA by qRT-PCR in DENV [10, 22, 23]. Going forward, inclusion of both intracellular/extracellular RNA and infectious particle assays will be essential for interpreting how compounds affect distinct steps of the viral life cycle.

As discussed above, the roles of autophagy in facilitating DENV propagation are well-documented. DENV utilizes autophagosomes as a platform for viral genome replication and particle maturation [8–12]. In addition, DENV manipulates host lipophagy to degrade lipid droplets into free fatty acids to generate ATP required for efficient genome replication [13]. Altered lipid metabolism also modulates membrane fluidity, thereby facilitating efficient nucleocapsid envelopment during virion maturation [14]. Furthermore, DENV exploits secretory autophagy to promote its egress and spread from infected cells [15]. Being enclosed in single-membrane vesicles allows DENV to evade neutralizing antibodies during transport, thereby enhancing its ability to infect distal cells [15]. Our findings demonstrate that while HM-013 strongly inhibits autophagosome formation, it does not impair DENV genome replication, as reflected by the accumulation of intracellular viral RNA in HM-013-treated cells. This indicates that viral RNA levels are not the limiting factor for infectious particle production. In contrast, a significant reduction in the release of infectious virions was observed, accompanied by an increase in intracellular DENV E protein. These results suggest that HM-013, by inhibiting autophagosome formation, may disrupt late stages of the DENV life cycle through (i) reduced virion assembly and maturation as a result of decreased autophagosome formation and the accumulation of lipid droplets, which may compromise membrane fluidity necessary for virion assembly and maturation, and/or (ii) impaired infectious virion release due to decreased formation of secretory autophagosomes, thereby limiting viral egress. Further investigations are required to clarify the precise mechanisms underlying HM-013 antiviral activity.

HM-013 is a derivative of 1,4-naphthoquinone, the core structure that is present in natural compounds such as lawsone, lapachol, juglone, and plumbagin. 1,4-Naphthoquinones represent a versatile class of synthetic compounds exhibiting a broad spectrum of biological activities[24–27]. Previous studies have shown that 1,4-naphthoquinones can directly inhibit DNA viruses such as human Herpesviruses type 1 and 2 and RNA viruses including SARS-CoV-2, HIV-1, poliovirus, and DENV [24, 26, 28, 29]. For example, compound NQ4 has demonstrated potent and selective antiviral activity by disrupting viral attachmen t[24], while juglone and its derivatives, particularly 2-acetyl-8-methoxy-1,4-naphthoquinone, have shown promise as inhibitors of the SARS-CoV-2 main protease [28]. In the context of DENV, NQ4 was shown to exert its antiviral effect primarily during viral entry, as it has no activity when administered after infection[24]. However, the precise mechanism remains unclear. In contrast, our study demonstrated that HM-013 exhibits clear post-infection antiviral activity, particularly at the late stage of the virus life cycle, as confirmed by our time-of-addition experiments. In addition, virucidal assay results from this study showed that HM-013 does not directly inhibit the virus.

Beyond antiviral activities, 1,4-naphthoquinones have also been reported to exhibit antibacterial, antiparasitic, antifungal, and antioxidant properties [24–26]. Given that ROS are required for autophagosome formation [30], it is plausible that the autophagy-inhibitory effect of HM-013 may be mediated through ROS inhibition. Further investigation is needed to elucidate this potential mechanism. Interestingly, a study demonstrated that black carbon nanoparticles coated with a 1,4-naphthoquinone inhibit autophagy in THP-1 cells, not by preventing autophagosome formation, but by inducing lysosomal damage, thereby impairing autolysosome fusion and leading to the accumulation of autophagosomes and undegraded substrates [31]. Additionally, certain 1,4-naphthoquinone derivatives have been shown to induce autophagy in cancer cell lines [32, 33]. These divergent modulatory effects on autophagy may be attributed to structural variations among the 1,4-naphthoquinone derivatives, such as the presence and arrangement of functional groups, or may reflect cell type-dependent responses resulting in distinct mechanisms of action.

Conclusions

In conclusion, HM-013 emerges as a promising autophagy inhibitor with potent anti-DENV activity and low cytotoxicity, underscoring its potential for development as a dengue therapeutic. Further studies are warranted to identify its molecular targets and refine its structure to improve efficacy.
