Physicochemical characterization and industrial use of Awzet Kaolin deposit in Northwestern Ethiopian plateau
Authors: Mohammed Yesuf Ali, Daniel Meshesha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to elucidate the mineralogy, geochemistry, genesis, and potential industrial applications of the Awzet kaolin deposit in northwestern Ethiopian Plateau. New geological, mineralogical, and physicochemical data were utilized to enhance understanding of its formation and uses. The primary lithologic units included unwelded tuff and basalt, with the parent rock identified as unwelded tuff. Several laboratory analyses were conducted using various techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD) for mineral identification and crystallinty characterization, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to analyze functional groups and crystallinity, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) for morphological assessments, and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) methods for geochemical analysis. Additionally, a range of physical tests were performed, including assessments of color, pH, Atterberg limits, grain size, bulk density, and specific gravity, to evaluate the suitability of the kaolin for various applications. Results indicated that the deposit primarily comprises kaolinite and quartz, formed through intense chemical weathering, as evidenced by high Chemical Index of Alteration (89.08%) and Chemical Index of Weathering (93.92%) values. Elevated concentrations of Ce + Y + La and Cr + Nb, along with the absence of quartz veining, suggest a supergene weathering process. Samples from the upper part of the deposit (LK2 and AKM) exhibited higher silt and clay contents of 76.8% and 80%, respectively, while bottom samples (LK1 and LKS2) showed lower fractions of 74.4% and 71.2%. The lower bulk density (average 1.25 g/cm3) and grain size distribution further support the supergene kaolinization process. Awzet kaolin samples demonstrated low linear shrinkage values (1.1%–3.5%), a pH range of 6.71–7.43, and preliminary colors of light greenish gray and pale yellow, indicating their potential suitability for applications in brick manufacturing, paper coating, fillers, ceramics, and agriculture.Keywords: Awzet, Ethiopia, Genesis, Industrial application, Kaolin, SupergeneSubject terms: Geochemistry, Mineralogy

Introduction

Clays are among nature’s most abundant and versatile materials, with a wide range of applications1,2. One of the most important clay minerals, kaolin, has played a significant role in the advancement of human civilization2,3. The term “kaolin” refers both to the minerals and the rocks containing kaolinite4. Specifically, it denotes a group of minerals including kaolinite, dickite, nacrite, and halloysite, with kaolinite being the predominant component of kaolin5. Kaolin crystals are classified as dioctahedral 1:1 phyllosilicates, composed of a sheet of silicon atoms tetrahedrally coordinated with four oxygen atoms, paired with a sheet of aluminum atoms octahedrally coordinated with two oxygen atoms and four hydroxide groups6. In this structure, the apical oxygen of the silica tetrahedron is shared with aluminum, while the basal oxygen atoms form one crystal face. The remaining oxygen atoms in the octahedral sheet are shared with hydroxide groups, forming the opposite crystal face. The structural formula for kaolinite is Al4Si4O10(OH)8, with a theoretical chemical composition of SiO2 (46.54%), Al2O3 (39.50%), and H2O (13.96%)4.

Kaolin can be classified into primary (in-situ) and secondary (sedimentary) categories based on its origin and geological occurrence2,5,7. Primary (in-situ) deposits include residual weathering horizons, hydrothermal alteration zones, and fracture networks. These deposits are typically found in feldspathic igneous and metamorphic rocks, where the clay fraction is sometimes formed through a combination of processes and is hosted within a matrix of non-clay minerals at the site of formation8. Secondary (sedimentary) deposits form from clays that have been transported from areas where in-situ deposits have weathered and are hosted in siliciclastic sedimentary rocks2. The physical characteristics of individual kaolin deposits vary, influencing their end-use applications9–11. Kaolin is suitable for a wide range of uses depending on its mineralogical and physicochemical properties, including applications in paper, porcelain, paint, rubber, fiberglass, and cement.

Ethiopia possesses abundant kaolin deposits, with previous research identifying occurrences in various regions12,13. The volcanic rocks of the Northwestern Ethiopian Plateau (NWEP) serve as one of the protoliths for these kaolin deposits12. Researchers (e.g12,13., have studied the kaolin occurrences found in this region. According to13, the kaolin’s in the Northwestern Ethiopian Plateau exhibit varying degrees of weathering, which influence their physicochemical properties. Subsequently12, conducted mineralogical and geochemical characterization on a regional scale by combining different kaolin occurrences in the northwestern Ethiopian plateau as single kaolin at regional scale.Current research has not thoroughly investigated the physicochemical characteristics and quality of these deposits, often relying on narrow methodologies that fail to capture the full range of kaolin’s properties and applications. A more comprehensive approach using different analytical techniques is essential for understanding its suitability across various industries, especially in sectors like ceramics, paper, and plastics, where the region has yet to fully realize kaolin’s economic potential.

Previous researchers have recommended detailed mineralogical and physicochemical analyses to characterize kaolin’s genesis and quality. There is a clear need for further studies, including FTIR analysis, morphological assessments, pH testing, preliminary color evaluations, fired color analysis, Atterberg limits, and linear shrinkage tests. This study focuses on the Awzet kaolin deposit, aiming to unravel its origins and assess its potential for industrial utilization. By conducting these in-depth analyses, we can better understand kaolin’s value for both domestic and international markets, ultimately maximizing its potential and promoting sustainable growth in the region’s mineral resources. This comprehensive research effort will be crucial for unlocking the full potential of kaolin in the area.

Geologic setting of the area

The Cenozoic Ethiopian continental flood basalt province is situated at the convergence of the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, and East African continental rift systems14. The Ethiopian flood basalts, also known as traps, cover an area exceeding 600,000 km² and comprise both basaltic and felsic volcanic rocks14. This trap series, which includes ignimbrites and flood basalts, ranges in thickness from 500 to 1,500 m, occasionally reaching up to 3,000 m1. Volcanic formations in this region primarily exhibit a bimodal composition of basalt and rhyolite, with a notable absence of intermediate types15. The volcanic plateau is influenced by rift faulting and is asymmetrically distributed around the Afar and Main Ethiopian Rift (MER). The western side of the rift is designated as the Western Ethiopian Plateau (WEP), while the eastern side is known as the Southeastern Ethiopian Plateau (SEP). The WEP encompasses the northern, central, and southwestern regions, whereas the SEP includes the eastern, southeastern, and southern parts of the Ethiopian flood volcanic province14,16. In the northwestern Ethiopian Plateau, volcanic layers overlie nearly horizontal Mesozoic sedimentary rocks. This area differs from the typical characteristics of a continental flood basalt province, as it lacks a thick, homogeneous layer of rapidly erupted, undisturbed tholeiitic basalts. Instead, it features several distinct volcanic centers, each with unique magmatic properties and a wide range of ages1414. classifies the volcanic formations of the northwestern Ethiopian Plateau into four categories: (1) Oligocene flood volcanics (trap series), consisting of Oligocene to Miocene rhyolites and basalts; (2) Miocene to Pliocene shield volcanoes; (3) volcanic plugs and domes; and (4) Quaternary volcanics and sediments (Fig. 1).

Methodology

Each lithological unit was systematically described based on selected profiles throughout the study area. To ensure precise location data, GPS measurements were meticulously recorded at each sampling site. This geospatial information was then mapped onto topographic maps and documented in field notes, facilitating a comprehensive spatial analysis of the lithological units.

For laboratory analysis, six representative kaolin samples, each weighting between 1 and 1.5 kg, were collected from both vertical and horizontal sections, along with three volcanic rock samples. Each sample was carefully labeled and sealed in plastic bags to prevent contamination and preserve its integrity for subsequent analysis.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to analyze the mineralogy of six kaolin samples at Adama Science and Technology University, providing detailed information about their mineralogical composition. The diffractometer was equipped with a copper tube and operated at 40 kV and 30 mA, scanning from 10° to 80° 2θ for approximately one hour per sample. Match! 3 and Origin Pro software were used for qualitative phase identification and quantitative mineralogical determination. To further investigate the morphology of the Awzet kaolin deposit, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized at Addis Ababa Science and Technology University, providing clear images of kaolinite surface features. Complementing these analyses, a Thermo Scientific iS50 ATR-FTIR Fourier transform infrared spectrometer was also used at Addis Ababa Science and Technology University to evaluate the functional groups and crystallinity of five kaolin samples, offering valuable insights into their chemical properties and structural characteristics.

The Australian Laboratory Services (ALS) in Ireland received nine samples: three unwelded tuff rocks and six kaolin samples for major and trace element analysis using Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) and Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS), respectively. A weight-based sequential testing approach was used to calculate the samples’ weight loss upon ignition at 1000 degrees Celsius. Samples for whole-rock geochemical analysis were prepared at the ALS facility in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The materials were crushed into chips to determine their geochemical composition; 85% of the samples passed through a 75 μm sieve, and 70% of the chips were smaller than 2 mm (fine crushing). The chips were then further ground into powder, with a total mass of up to 250 g.

pH of kaolin was measured using the methods described by17. Atterberg limit tests—including linear shrinkage, plastic limit, and liquid limit—were conducted in accordance with ASTM D4318 and ASTM C356 standards. The plasticity index (PI) was calculated as the difference between the liquid limit (LL) and the plastic limit (PL). The kaolin samples were initially visually assessed for color by comparison with the Munsell soil color chart at the Ethiopian Geological Survey. Awzet kaolin samples were analyzed by the Ethiopian Construction Design and Supervision Works Corporation using hydrometer testing in compliance with ASTM D422, employing hydrometer number 152 H. Additionally, the specific gravity and bulk density of the Awzet kaolin deposit were evaluated for industrial applications.

Results and discussion

Geology of the Kaolin deposit

The primary lithologic units exposed in the study area include unwelded tuff and basalt (Fig. 2). Unwelded tuff is the most abundant lithological unit, covering the majority of the area. Fresh unwelded tuff displays a gray to light bluish color, whereas the weathered portions appear dark gray.

Geological map of the study area (a) and cross-section along profile A–B (b).

The other rock unit found in the study area is basalt. It occurs in the southwestern part of the study area, often associated with small hills and superficial deposits. Fresh outcrops appear dark, while weathered exposures display gray and reddish hues. The basalt exhibits a porphyritic texture, containing visible plagioclase phenocrysts.

Awzet kaolin deposit (Fig. 3) is associated with an unwelded tuff unit located within the study area. The overburden covering the kaolin deposit in the Awzet area varies in thickness from 0.5 to 5 m. Beneath this overburden, kaolin exposures with thicknesses ranging from 5 to 15 m were identified. The upper portion of the kaolin primarily consists of silt- and clay-sized particles, in contrast to the kaolin in the lower section. In the field, the Awzet kaolin deposit exhibits a whitish color.

Exposure of the Awzet kaolin deposit along with its overburden materials.

Mineralogy and crystallinity of Awzet Kaolin deposit

The mineralogical composition of the Awzet kaolin deposits was analyzed using X-ray Diffraction (XRD) and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) techniques. These methods were employed to identify the mineral phases present in the Awzet kaolin and to characterize its crystallinity.

XRD

The degree of kaolin crystallinity is crucial for understanding the presence of defects and the formation of the deposit18. A high degree of crystallinity corresponds to residual kaolin formed under low-temperature and low-pressure conditions. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Awzet kaolin samples show considerable variation, which is attributed to differences in mineralogy and the degree of kaolinite crystallinity. In addition to kaolinite, quartz was identified in the Awzet kaolin samples (Fig. 4; Table 1). The sharp XRD peaks in these samples indicate a higher degree of crystallinity.

Major oxide geochemistry of Awzet Kaolin

Alteration phenomena of unwelded tuff rocks and Awzet Kaolin deposit

Major oxides were used to interpret alteration phenomena and know the possible industrial application of Awzet kaolin. Moreover, LOI data were used to interpret the alteration and weathering phenomena of Awzet kaolin.

The weathering product (kaolin) and unaltered parent rock (unwelded tuff) display significant differences in major oxide composition (Table 3). The LOI value of the parent rock (3.26–4.34 wt%) is lower than that of the weathering product (kaolin) (13.35–16.15 wt%). The kaolinized tuff sample shows a higher LOI (13.5 wt%) value than the unaltered parent rock sample, though it has a slightly lower value than the average LOI value of kaolin samples (14.63 wt%). As weathering intensity increases, the amount of Al2O3 and LOI increases23. These phenomena were observed in Awzet kaolin samples and unaltered parent rocks.

The unwelded tuff is relatively enriched in Na2O, K2O, and CaO and depleted in Al2O3 and LOI compared to the kaolin samples (Table 3). As weathering increases, mobile compounds such as Na2O, K2O, and CaO are leached out, and the enrichment of immobile compounds (Al2O3) continues. A major oxide binary plot (Fig. 7) of Awzet kaolin samples shows a depletion of Na2O and K2O and an enrichment of Al2O3. The indirect relationship between Na2O and K2O with Al2O3 and LOI indicates the breakdown of feldspars during weathering.

Binary plot of Na2O vs. Al2O3 (a), K2O vs. Al2O3 (b).

In addition, the ternary systems A-CN-K [(Al2O3- (CaO + Na2O) - K2O)] and A-CNK-F [(Al2O3− (CaO + Na2O + K2O) - Fe2O3] are used to examine the weathering and alteration phenomena of the kaolin. In both the A-CN-K and A-CNK-F ternary diagrams, the weathered product (kaolin) samples are plotted very near to the Al2O3 (Fig. 8). Genesis of Awzet Kaolin deposit

Evidence from geological, mineralogical, geochemical, and physical test data strongly supports that supergene weathering processes play the main role in the genesis of Awzet kaolin. The volcanic rocks and kaolin samples of the Awzet area are not well exposed except by streams and road cuts due to thick soil overburden in many localities. Thick soil overburden and the absence of quartz veins indicate that the kaolinization process was taking place by supergene fluid.

Kaolinite and quartz minerals were identified from XRD, FTIR, and SEM analyses of Awzet kaolin samples (Figs. 4, 5 and 6). Therefore, the presence of only kaolinite and quartz minerals strengthens the supergene origin of Awzet kaolin.

Awzet kaolin samples collected from the upper section of the kaolin contain more silt and clay-size fractions than Awzet kaolin collected from the lower section of the kaolin (Fig. 13). This fining-upward grain size distribution of the Awzet kaolin sample depicts that the weathering process took place downward, from top to bottom, by supergene fluids. Furthermore, the dried bulk density of kaolin samples was used as a means of discriminating supergene kaolin from hypogene kaolin. Kaolin formed by the hydrothermal process shows higher densities (> 2 g/cm3) than kaolin formed by the supergene process (< 2 g/cm3)28. The lower density of Awzet kaolin samples (average of 1.25 g/cm3) (Table 4), which is less than 2 g/cm3, indicates that supergene weathering was the responsible mechanism for the genesis of Awzet kaolin.

Industrial properties of Awzet Kaolin deposit

In most industries, the physicochemical properties of kaolin are very important. Therefore, assessing the physicochemical attributes is useful to determine the suitability of kaolin for different industrial applications3,5. Kaolin quality varies depending on its mineralogical and physicochemical attributes. Different attributes of kaolin can constrain its ability to meet the standard specifications of different kaolin-consuming industries. To assess the quality of Awzet kaolin, various tests, such as pH, preliminary color, fired color, linear shrinkage, grain size, Atterberg limit test (plastic limit, liquid limit, and plasticity index), bulk density, and specific gravity tests, have been conducted (Table 4).

Color assessment

Color is one of the most important properties in the economic evaluation of kaolin31. In most high-value applications of kaolin, whiteness plays a key role17.Color can be assessed either by comparing kaolin samples visually with the Munsell soil color chart or by fully quantitative spectrophotometric methods17. However, fully quantitative spectrophotometric methods of color assessment are not appropriate for assessing raw kaolin17. Therefore, a preliminary color assessment by comparing Awzet kaolin samples visually with the Munsell soil color chart was conducted at the Central Geoscience Laboratory of the Geological Survey of Ethiopia. Color can be expressed in three dimensions: hue (name of a color), value (amount of light in a color), and chroma (degree of strength in a color). The three dimensions of color are written as hue, value/chroma order to express the kaolin color based on the Munsell soil color chart. The preliminary color assessment results show that Awzet kaolin has whitish, light greenish-gray and pale-yellow colors (Table 4). From the analyzed samples, three samples (AKM, LKS2, and LK2) have a light greenish-gray preliminary color, and AKM has a white preliminary color, whereas the other two samples (LK1 and LK3) have a pale-yellow preliminary color on the Munsell soil color chart (Table 4). In addition to the preliminary color assessment of kaolin, assessing the fired color of kaolin is important for industrial applications. The properties of a fired kaolin output provide further information on the suitability of the kaolin for ceramics and pottery applications17. Awzet kaolin samples changed into creamy-white and reddish-yellow colors after being fired at 900 °C. The changes in the fired color of Awzet kaolin samples are attributed to its elevated concentration of Fe2O3 (an average of 2.51 wt%) and TiO2 (an average of 0.56 wt%).

PH of Awzet Kaolin deposit

Kaolin has inert properties throughout a broad pH range (4–9)31. While extenders in paint require a pH of 4.5–9.5, kaolin, used as a white pigment in the paper industry, has a pH of 5–7.5 as coating clays and 6.5–7.5 as a white pigment3. Awzet kaolin has a pH range of 6.71–7.43, which is slightly acidic, to an average of 7.07, which is slightly basic. This means that it can be used with coating clays, paint extenders, and white pigment.

Atterberg limit

The Atterberg limit test was used to characterize Awzet kaolin because its workability is determined by its plasticity characteristics. When comparing kaolin samples with higher Al2O3, silt, and clay fractions with lower Al2O3, silt, and clay fractions, the plastic limit of the former is somewhat larger. Furthermore, based on the clay workability chart (Fig. 12), Awzet kaolin samples can be used for bricks and pottery.

Linear shrinkage

A crucial characteristic to evaluate kaolin’s viability for various applications is shrinkage. The water content of plasticity and fineness is directly correlated with drying (linear) shrinkage32. Awzet kaolin samples (1.1%–3.5%) have lower linear shrinkage values (Table 4), which makes them appropriate for industrial use.

Grain size distribution

Apart from color, grain size distributions are an important feature for evaluating kaolin17,33. One of the main factors influencing how kaolin is used in various industries is its fineness. The majority of Awzet kaolin samples are composed of silt- and clay-size fractions (Fig. 13). The grain size distribution curve of Awzet kaolin samples shows an enrichment of silt and clay fractions at the upper part of the kaolin and a decrease in silt and clay fractions towards the bottom of the kaolin. Kaolin samples collected from the upper part (LK2 and AKM) show higher silt- and clay-size particles, 76.8% and 80%, whereas the bottom of the kaolin (LK1 and LKS2) shows relatively lower silt- and clay-size fractions, 74.4% and 71.2%, respectively (Fig. 13).

Conclusion

Information obtained from geological fieldwork, mineralogy, geochemistry, and physical testing leads to the following conclusions. These data indicate that in situ supergene weathering is the underlying process responsible for the formation of Awzet kaolin.

Based on field observations, the supergene origin of Awzet kaolin is supported by a thick soil cover and the lack of quartz veins.
