Predicting general practice trainee summative fellowship exam performance using commencement-of-training formative assessments: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Alison Fielding, Irena Patsan, Elizabeth Holliday, Jason Dizon, Jean Ball, Amanda Tapley, Ashley Blowes, Alexandria Turner, Linda Klein, Graham Lee, Parker Magin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFellowship examinations in Australian vocational general practice training assess competency for unsupervised practice. Early identification of trainees at risk of failing these summative examinations facilitates pro-active remediation. This study aimed to explore the utility of formative pre-general practice assessments (PGAs), undertaken at the commencement of training, for predicting final summative examination performance.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study. The PGAs included a written, short-answer, five-case Key Feature Problems test (‘PGA-KFP’) and an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (‘PGA-OSCE’). Multivariable regression was used to examine the relationship between these PGAs and standardized scores on first-attempts at three summative fellowship examinations (an Applied Knowledge Test (‘Fellowship-AKT’), a KFP (‘Fellowship-KFP’) and an OSCE (‘Fellowship-OSCE’); and passing all three (versus failing at least one) examination on first attempt. Registrar demographics and training-related factors were included as covariates.ResultsHigher PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores were univariably associated with higher Fellowship-AKT, Fellowship-KFP, and Fellowship-OSCE scores (all p-values ≤ 0.002) and with passing all three examinations on first attempt (p < 0.006). On multivariable analyses, higher PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores were significantly associated with higher Fellowship-AKT, Fellowship-KFP (p values ≤ 0.016) but not Fellowship-OSCE scores. Higher PGA-KFP scores were also predictive of passing all three Fellowship exams on first attempt (p < 0.001).ConclusionsThis study found intensive formative assessments conducted at the commencement of training to be predictive of final fellowship examination performance in an apprenticeship-based model of general practice training. This suggests utility of these assessments for early-identification of trainees in need of proactive remediation both in Australia and internationally. Further research is required to explore predictive utility of less resource-intensive PGAs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09530-y.

Background

Summative examinations within specialist medical training are critical for assuring fitness of practitioners for unsupervised practice. Within Australian general practice (family medicine) training, final summative examinations provide a benchmark for safe and competent practice. Passing these examinations is a final requirement for attaining vocational general practice Fellowship [1] and licensure for specialist general practice.

Although most general practice trainees complete program requirements without failure, some will experience difficulties that culminate in failing high stakes final summative examinations [1]. The cause of summative exam failure in postgraduate medical education is thought to be multifaceted, and may be due to poor clinical judgment, knowledge and/or skills of trainees [2, 3].

Examination failure in Australian general practice vocational training often requires extended training time for individualized remediation [4]. Remediation of medical trainees is typically resource-intensive [5–7] and may affect the trainee emotionally and financially, while the additional medical education requirements impose burden on the training organization [6, 7]. These adverse outcomes are potentially avoidable if appropriate and tailored pro-active intervention strategies are implemented early in training [8, 9]. Thus, the importance of early identification lies in early intervention (remediation) [10].

Though the resource and emotional costs of summative examination failure are considerable, identification of trainee deficits and subsequent remediation is directed not only at examination performance but at overall clinical competence and clinical performance [11].

Hauer et al. have proposed a model for remediation of performance deficits in medical students, trainees and practising physicians [11]. The model recommends four core components: initial assessment (or screening) using multiple assessment tools to identify deficiencies; diagnosis of problems and development of an individualized learning plan; provision of instruction that includes deliberate practice, feedback, and reflection; and reassessment and certification of competence [11]. Within this framework, formative in-training assessments have potential utility for the ‘identification’ and ‘diagnosis’ of performance deficits, and for informing learning plan development for under-performing trainees at risk of failing [11]. Identification of trainees with deficiencies requires multiple, reliable, and valid assessment tools [11]. It follows that each of these assessment tools requires individual validation.

Assessments conducted at the commencement of training may be best placed for identifying, diagnosing, and informing tailored remediation plans with a maximal intervention window. Formative assessments conducted during training may not offer sufficient time for delivery of remediation or support, especially in relatively short training programs such as Australia’s 18-month GP vocational training program. Similarly, while examination-style formative assessments conducted proximate to summative examinations may provide beneficial ‘examination training’, they generally occur too late for substantive intervention to remedy under-performance [12].

While early identification of undergraduate medical underperformance has been well examined [13–17], similar early identification at the level of commencement of vocational general practice training remains relatively under-researched.

In studies of UK physician (internal medicine) trainees, selection assessments conducted well before commencement of specialist training were found to be predictive of summative Fellowship examination performance [18] as well as in-training performance [19]. A situational judgement Test (SJT) and a clinical problem-solving test have also shown to be predictive of final summative assessments in the UK GP training program [20]. Patterson et al. [21] similarly found that an SJT and a multiple-mini-interview (MMI) performed as entry-to-training assessments for Australian general practice to be predictive of summative final examination performance. Another recent study also assessing predictive utility of selection scores (including SJT and MMI) in Australian vocational general practice training, identified moderate correlations between SJT and MMI performance and performance in final summative exams [22].

Despite such evidence for predictive utility of selection-into-training assessments, just as formative assessments throughout training may be too late for identifying trainees at risk of failure for appropriate early intervention, selection-into-training assessments may be premature for the optimal identification and diagnosis of deficits for informing an individualized learning plan as per Hauer’s remediation model [11]. In Australia, selection assessments are typically delivered six-to-eighteen months, or more, prior to commencing general practice training terms. During that period, trainees are exposed to a great deal of hospital-based learning and experience, and individual trainee’s entry-to-training assessments may not reflect their status at commencement of general practice terms. Thus, while selection-into-training scores have merit as early performance indicators, they cannot be used as stand-alone identification and diagnostic indicators of underperformance and have very limited scope for informing the subsequent remediation learning plan.

Although commencement-of-training assessments may therefore be ideally placed to help diagnose and inform individualised remediation learning plans as per Hauer’s model [11], few studies have examined the utility of formative pre-general practice assessments (PGA) (i.e., undertaken immediately prior to trainees commencing their community-based clinical general practice) for predicting summative examination performance. An Australian retrospective cohort study [23] found that a ‘basic clinical knowledge’ multiple-choice question (MCQ) examination focusing on ‘not-to-miss’ diagnoses was predictive of Fellowship examination performance. Another Australian study explored the predictive ability of four PGA components (a 65-item MCQ, a short answer questionnaire, observed simulated consultations, and a written clinical reasoning (CR) paper) [22]. The MCQ was found to be predictive of all three summative licensing examinations segments, and the CR paper was predictive of the key feature problems (KFP) examination only, though with limited adjustment for potential confounding in the analyses [22]. Collectively, an implication of these findings is that trainees at higher risk of summative examination failure can be detected very early in vocational training, offering an ideal window for proactive remediation.

This study aims to investigate whether a multi-component PGA (consisting of KFP-style and objective structured clinical examination (OSCE)-style components) is predictive of performance in final summative Fellowship examinations in Australian specialist general practice, both considering the PGA result in isolation and when adjusted for potential confounding by other factors. This predictive ability will be framed within Hauer et al’s model of remediation’s first component, ‘initial assessment (or screening) using multiple assessment tools to identify deficiencies’; and the initial aspect of the second component, that is, ‘diagnosis of problems’.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study utilising routinely collected data from GP Synergy, the not-for-profit, geographically-defined Regional Training Organization (RTO) responsible for delivering the Australian General Practice Training Program in New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory from 2016. At the time of PGA data collection (2011–2015), GP Synergy’s training region encompassed Central, Eastern, and South-western Sydney and the New England area of New South Wales.

Study population

Participants included vocational general practice trainees (hereafter ‘registrars’) who completed PGAs between 2011 and 2015 and subsequently undertook Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) Fellowship examinations between 2012 and 2019. The large majority of GP Synergy registrars were enrolled in the RACGP Fellowship pathway, reflecting the fact that the RACGP Fellowship was pursued by approximately 95% of vocationally registered GPs in Australia [24]. Fellowship can be awarded to registrars when all RTO educational and Fellowship examination requirements are successfully completed [1].

Inclusion criteria for registrars were: completed the PGA between 2011 and 2015; were enrolled in the RACGP Fellowship curriculum; completed all components of GP training with GP Synergy; and had RACGP Fellowship examination results available at the time of analysis.

Registrars were not eligible for inclusion if they: withdrew from GP training or permanently transferred to another training provider; were an Australian Defence Force registrar (whose training experience is substantially different due to deployment disruptions and, therefore, non-generalisable to other registrars); or were an Australian College of Rural and Remote Medicine registrar (who undertake a different curriculum and Fellowship examinations, and for who there were insufficient data for analysis).

Outcome factors

The primary outcome measures were standardized scores for first attempt at each of the three RACGP Fellowship examination components, each of which is intended to test a different aspect of Miller’s framework for assessing clinical competence:

i.An Applied Knowledge Test (‘Fellowship-AKT’) consisting of multiple-choice questions (assessing ‘knows’);ii.A Key Features Problems (‘Fellowship-KFP’) consisting of short answer written responses (assessing ‘knows how’); and.iii.An Objective Structured Clinical Examination (‘Fellowship-OSCE’) consisting of clinical ‘stations’ with patient presentations/role-playing (assessing ‘shows how’) [25].

Fellowship examinations have been conducted since 1968 [26] and various iterations of the examinations have been demonstrated to be reliable and have content validity [12, 27–30].

Raw scores for the Fellowship-AKT, Fellowship-KFP and Fellowship-OSCE were standardized by test and year using the z-score formula: ((raw exam score – national mean)/ national standard deviation). This standardization was performed to remove variation attributable to year-to-year examination score variation.

The secondary outcome measure for the study was having passed all three Fellowship examination components on first attempt, versus failing one or more of the components on first attempt.

Study factors

Study factors were registrars’ outcomes from two formative assessments, the ‘PGA-KFP style’ and ‘PGA-OSCE style’ assessments. Both assessments were designed by a team of experienced medical educators, with content determined by their clinical and educational expertise. The purpose of the PGAs was to assess trainee skills immediately prior to the commencement of their first general practice training term, with view to inform individual trainee learning needs to assist with the transition from the hospital to community general practice setting.

The PGA-KFP assessed clinical knowledge and reasoning skills in a written short question and answer case study format. The PGA-KFP score comprised 11 items, used to create 5 sub-scores which were summed to create the overall PGA-KFP score (total score out of 50). PGA-KFP questions were consistent across all years included within the study. A marking rubric for each PGA-KFP item was established by an experienced GP/GP medical educator (co-author, PM). PGA-KFP marking was completed by a medically-qualified research assistant according to the devised marking rubric and under the supervision of an experienced GP (co-author, PM).

The PGA-OSCE involved one station, for which trainees undertook a standardised simulated patient consult within a traditional OSCE format. The consultation was assessed by a single trained medical educator using a standardised assessment form, which incorporated assessment of: communication skills (8 items), history taking (6 items), physical examination (3 items), problem listing (3 items), management (4 items) medical recording (3 items) and “X-factors” (reflecting more advanced competencies than expected at the commencement of training) (5 items) (total score out of 70).

Missing data handling

Due to minor year-to-year variations in a subset of the PGA-OSCE assessor forms (where some items were not included) and incomplete assessor marking, some PGA-OSCE observations had missing item values. Observations missing all items were excluded. For observations missing some but not all items, missing values were replaced with the sample mean of all non-missing values for that item. This approach provided unbiased estimates under a missing completely at random (MCAR) assumption, which was judged reasonable for the data. After performing the imputation, PGA-OSCE total score was summed using scores from each PGA-OSCE item.

Data collection

PGA records and routinely collected demographic and training data were extracted from GP Synergy’s administrative databases.

Fellowship examination data were routinely provided to GP Synergy by the RACGP after each examination round. Selection score band data were provided to GP Synergy by the Australian Department of Health in accordance with selection processes at the time.

Statistical analysis

Primary outcome analyses

To assess the relationships between each of the three Fellowship examination outcomes and the separate PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores, univariable and multivariable linear regression models were fitted. Estimates from these models are presented as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The regression coefficients represent the estimate of the change in the dependent variable (standardized Fellowship-AKT, Fellowship-KFP, or Fellowship-OSCE score) for a one-unit increase in the predictor variable (PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores, respectively), if all other variables in the model are held constant.

Pearson’s correlation coefficients were also calculated for the relationships of standardized Fellowship-AKT, Fellowship-KFP, or Fellowship-OSCE scores with PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores, respectively.

Descriptive statistics for the continuous outcomes are presented as a mean score with standard deviation (SD) within levels of categorical explanatory variables, and as regression coefficients with 95% CI for continuous explanatory variables.

Secondary outcome analyses

To assess the relationship between each of PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores and the secondary outcome (pass all/fail on any of the three Fellowship exams), univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression was performed. Estimates from these models are presented as odds ratios with 95% CI. The beta coefficients from logistic regression represent the estimated change in the log odds for a one unit increase in the predictor variable. The exponentiated form (odds ratios), represent the estimated multiplicative increase in the odds of the binary response (pass/fail) for a one-unit change in the predictor (variable (PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores, respectively).

Registrars were included in these analyses if they attempted all three exam components and passed all three on first attempt (‘pass all’) or if they had attempted any of the three examination components and failed one or more component without necessarily having attempted all three components (‘fail on any’).

Cohen’s d was also estimated as a measure of effect size of the difference in each of PGA-KFP scores and PGA-OSCE scores for registrars who (overall) passed versus those who (overall) failed their RACGP-examinations. We also calculated sensitivity, specificity, and numbers of registrars who failed an RACGP Fellowship examination (versus passed all Fellowship examinations) at a range of cut scores for the PGA-KF and PGA-OSCE.

Descriptive statistics for this binary outcome include frequencies for categorical variables and mean with SD for continuous variables.

Model selection

Model selection was performed using full multivariable models as a starting point via an augmented backwards selection process. There were seventeen covariates (Table 1) considered for inclusion in each model alongside the study factor of interest (i.e. PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores, respectively). Variables in the model with p-values > 0.2 were tested for removal, starting from the variable with the highest p-value. A variable was removed if the resulting model did not have substantively different beta coefficients than the previous model. A substantively different beta coefficient was defined as being more than 10% different to its value in the previous model. If the variable was retained, the variable with the next highest p-value > 0.2 was tested for removal. This iterative process was performed until all variables with p-values > 0.2 had been assessed and retained or removed.

Model fit

For logistic models, diagnostic tests to assess goodness of fit included the Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test. The logistic model assumption of linearity in the log-odds for continuous variables was also checked. Goodness of fit for linear models was assessed using visual inspection of residual plots to verify homoscedasticity and normality.

For linear regression models, predictive accuracy was quantified using the model R-squared to estimate the proportion of the variance in the respective Fellowship examination scores that is explained by the variables in the particular linear regression model. For logistic regression models, pseudo-R-squared and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) were used.

All analyses were programmed and conducted using STATA 14.0 and SAS V9.4. Statistical significance was declared at the conventional 0.05 level, with the magnitude and precision of effect estimates also used to interpret results.

Ethics approval

Approval to undertake this study as a quality assurance project was obtained from the University of Newcastle Human Research Ethics Committee (reference number QA147). Registrars provided written informed consent for data routinely collected as part of the GP Synergy educational program to be used for quality assurance purposes.

Discussion

Summary of the main findings

Scores on two PGAs at the time of Australian GP registrars’ commencing their general practice-based training placements were predictive of performance in the summative Fellowship examinations. The univariable associations of scores on each PGA assessment with scores on each Fellowship examination, and with failure on any examination, were all statistically significant (at the p < 0.05 level). For multivariable associations, all were significant except for the PGA-OSCE score predicting Fellowship-OSCE score, and PGA-OSCE score predicting examination failure, where there was some evidence for association (p-values 0.06 and 0.07, respectively).

Effect sizes

Pearson’s correlation coefficients for the relationships of scores on the two PGAs with scores on three Fellowship examination components ranged from 0.17 to 0.32. These represent small-moderate to moderate effect sizes [31]. All six multivariable models showed explanatory utility for examination scores. (R-square/pseudo-R-square values 0.27 to 0.34));

The Cohen’s d for pass-fail and scores on the PGA-KFP (0.59) and PGA-OSCE (0.35) represent moderate and small-moderate effect sizes [31], respectively. AUC-ROC values of 0.81 and 0.78) suggest considerable discrimination for these models (an AUC-ROC value of 0.80–0.90 is considered ‘excellent’ [32], and a value of 0.70–0.80 is considered ‘acceptable’ discrimination) [32].

These various effect sizes should be considered in the context of prediction of examination performance greater than 12 months in the future (during which time registrars are subject to a steep learning curve).

Comparisons with previous research

There is limited previous research on early prediction of GP trainee summative examination performance.

Evidence of predictive ability of general practice PGAs

Stewart et al. performed a study that also examined the predictive ability of a PGA for Fellowship examination performance and found similarly strong predictive utility of a PGA for RACGP-KFP and RACGP-OSCE performance [23]. Their findings are not entirely comparable with our analysis, given that ours used PGA scores from KFP and OSCE assessments whereas Stewart et al. used a pass/fail PGA predictor variable derived from a multiple-choice question assessment. Additionally, our findings of PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE being strongly predictive of Fellowship-AKT performance differ from Stewart et al., who failed to find an association of their MCQ PGA and Fellowship-AKT performance. This is surprising as the MCQ PGA format is similar to that of Fellowship-AKT, whereas the formats of the PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE are dissimilar to the Fellowship AKT format.

Heggarty et al. also examined the use of several PGA formats, including an MCQ, a short answer questionnaire (SAQ), clinical skills simulations, and a clinical reasoning (CR) paper; finding their MCQ PGAs to be most predictive of RACGP fellowship examination performance [22] It is difficult to compare linear regression analysis findings between our study and Heggarty et al., given that our analyses examined predictive utility of PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores in separate models, whereas Heggarty et al. combined each of their PGA components (and another later in-training MCQ assessment) into one predictive model of each RACGP exam segment [22]. We also considered additional potential confounders in our multivariable analyses, whereas Heggerty et al. only adjusted for gender and IMG status.

Our pass/fail findings are not directly comparable with Heggarty et al., given that different measures of failure were used between studies (Heggarty et al. examined failure on each fellowship examination segment separately, whereas we analysed passing all three examinations versus failing one or more examination on first attempt)), and adjustment for potential confounders in Heggarty et al. was limited to gender and IMG status. Interestingly, Heggarty et al. did not find gender or IMG status to be significant in any models, which is not consistent with some of our present findings nor some of those of Stewart et al.’s previous Australian study [23].

Evidence of predictive ability of selection-to-training modalities

Tiffin et al. recently performed a study on UK-based GP-training recruitment data, which evaluated the use of Multi-Specialty Recruitment Assessment (MSRA) and a Selection Centre (SC) scores on prediction of Fellowship examinations [33]. As in our study, the authors demonstrated statistically significant relationships between the selection process scores and performance on the Clinical Skills Assessment (CSA) at the completion of training (p < 0.001) [33].

It is noteworthy that selection band (derived from MMI and SJT results), though not our study factor, was included as a covariate in our multivariable models and was predictive of examination performance in all models (with strong statistical significance). This finding is consistent with evidence from other Australian and United Kingdom vocational GP studies that have also identified significant correlations with selection scores and subsequent examination performance [21, 22].

Implications for policy/practice/future research

Predictive utility

Univariable findings and pragmatic ‘stand-alone’ utility

Our univariable findings indicate that formative training commencement KFP- and OSCE-format tests may have ‘stand-alone’ utility as educational tools to assist with the early ‘flagging’ of registrars that may be at risk of poor performance and/or failure of Fellowship exams. Given that PGAs are conducted at the very outset of registrars’ general practice experience, this early flagging is of considerable practical relevance to training providers and educators considering Australia-wide Fellowship examination failure rates are approximately 20% for RACGP-AKT and KFP and 15–20% for the RACGP OSCE/Clinical Competency Exam [1].

Multivariable findings and added utility beyond known predictors

Our multivariable findings suggest that the PGA results continue to offer value for predicting Fellowship examination outcomes while controlling for other significant covariates including selection score. That is, they provide information on risk of failure beyond other known predictors. Our models’ R-square values indicate explanation of a considerable amount of variance [34] in our Fellowship examination scores (acknowledging the complexity of explaining these examination scores). Thus, educators can have some confidence in applying these findings to their practice across the range of registrar demographics (recognizing also that between the time of the PGA assessments and Fellowship examination, trainees have undergone 12 + months of full-time equivalent practice-based clinical general practice training, completed a series of out-of-practice educational workshops, and undergone/undertaken multiple other progressive formative assessments).

Relative utility of different PGA modalities for predicting summative Fellowship examination performance

It is also important to establish the relative predictive utility of different formats of PGA compared to other predictors of summative examination performance Existing evidence for relative utility of different PGA formats in this respect is inconsistent. The findings of Heggarty et al. [22] suggest an MCQ format to have strong predictive capacity for Fellowship examination performance. However, the predictive capacity of our PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE tests may have increased predictive capacity compared with an MCQ-format in the Stewart et al. study [23]. Any increased predictive capacity of PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE tests must be balanced against the greater resource demands in conducting, and marking these assessments compared to MCQ-format assessments.

Relative utility of PGAs and selection-into-training assessments for predicting summative Fellowship examination performance

We can also compare the predictive utility of tests conducted at selection-into-training and tests done at commencement of the first general practice training term (that is, well before training commences and early in training). The comparability is limited by the very different kinds of assessment employed [21] and by the fact that the means of comparison (correlation coefficients) does not adjust for other covariates in the relationship. The comparison, though, suggests similar predictive ability for pragmatic ‘red flag’ identification of registrars at risk of poor examination performance. And, again, resource implications must be considered. Selection-into-training test results, while very resource-intensive in themselves, are available without further resource investment by the training organization.

Specificity of PGA modality prediction of Fellowship examination performance by Fellowship examination modality

There is evidence that formative OSCE assessments predict medical students’ summative OSCE performance [35] and that Canadian internal medicine trainees’ OSCE progress test scores are predictive of future performance on summative OSCE examination [16]. However, some aspects of our findings, and those of others [22, 23] in similar GP registrar populations, suggest that PGA predictive utility lacks specificity. Utility to flag risk of failure in a specific component of the Fellowship examinations may be limited. In our study, PGA-OSCE and PGA-KFP scores were both highly predictive of Fellowship-AKT score, though the three examination components are designed to assess different aspects of registrars’ competencies. In the Stewart et al. study [23], a PGA-MCQ assessment was strongly predictive of Fellowship-KFP and Fellowship-OSCE scores, but there was no association with Fellowship-AKT scores (p = 0.92) despite the similar MCQ/AKT formats. Similarly, Heggarty et al. suggests stronger relationship between a MCQ PGA performance and performance in all three final examination segments, despite their other PGA modalities (consultation simulations, CR paper and SAQ) conceptually linking more closely with competencies assessed by the RACGP-KFP and RACGP-OSCE [22].

This raises the possibility that these various assessments reflect general, non-specific competency rather than competencies at the specific levels of Miller’s pyramid of assessment that the Fellowship-AKT, -KFP and -OSCE are designed to address. Or even that performance on the PGA assessments and Fellowship examinations share a common registrar facility with ‘examination technique’.

Implications of lack of specificity for ‘diagnosis’ in Hauer’s remediation model

Thus, it should be considered that PGA-style assessments may address the first stage of Hauer’s [11] remediation model, ‘identification’, for risk of examination failure, but may not be particularly specific as to which examination component they are at risk of failure. This will affect the second remediation stage, ‘diagnosis’, and would apply especially to MCQ-based assessments. To address this possibility and considering further issues regarding ‘diagnostic’ utility (outlined below), future research should evaluate the predictive ability for summative examination performance of in-practice work-based assessments. These assessment modalities may be particularly suited to a ‘diagnosis’ function and, in combination with PGA-style assessments, fulfill the Hauer-model ideal of multiple ‘identification’ assessment tools. A study of the predictive ability of one in-practice work-based assessment (observed practice via ‘External Clinical Teaching Visit’) found this to be predictive of RACGP Fellowship examination performance with some granularity of ‘diagnostic’ features [12].

As per Hauer’s suggestion for multiple assessment tools, further research investigating optimal combinations of assessment modalities for predicting summative examination performance for general practice training programs is also indicated. This could include assessment of incremental validity of concurrently conducted assessments [13]. While we have adjusted for multiple potential confounding variables in our analyses, we have tested the PGA KFP and PGA OSCE in separate models.

Consideration of findings regarding independent variables in our models

There were a number of independent variables associated with Fellowship examination performance in our multivariable models (age, gender, country of medical graduation, selection band). Application of these findings to practice (along with PGA results) could potentially underpin more granular individualized risk stratification. This, though, is premature as our study was not specifically designed to address this question. Specific research exploring this issue is indicated. Further research could consider building and validating a ‘predictive profile’ for identifying trainees most at risk of underperformance, combining both other assessments and demographic data. This would be an adjunct to Hauer’s ‘multiple, reliable, and valid assessment tools’ and would not preclude qualitative synthesis of individual registrars’ programmatic assessment components to guide remediation (or other training) decisions.

Informing remediation

As well as ‘identification’, we can view our findings with reference to the ‘diagnosis’ stage of Hauer’s remediation model [11]. This will facilitate tailoring of remediation to identified deficits through individual analysis and feedback of assessment [9]. In-house PGA assessments (or other assessments such as work-based assessments, above), compared to nationally delivered selection-into-training assessments, have the potential utility of educators being able to qualitatively assess specifics of examination responses (for purposes of ‘diagnosis’), not just to ‘flag’ registrars on the basis of summary scores. This may be especially so for KFP- and OSCE-format tests, with their more complex, nuanced structures compared to AKT/MCQ-format tests.

Also, clinically-oriented PGA-style assessments will have very different relevance for remediation to assessments such as SJTs (where generic problem-solving and clinical reasoning skills are examined [13, 36]). Further research should explore the capacity of early formative assessments to inform remediation content (in addition to flagging the need for remediation) and to assess the effect of remediation on clinical and professional competency and on examination performance [9]. That is, to explore the latter stages of Hauer’s remediation model.

Strengths and limitations

This study addresses an important and under-researched area within general practice vocational training. Robust statistical analysis methods were utilized, adjusting for a comprehensive range of demographic and in-training variables that may confound analyses of exam performance.

Further strengths for this study include generalizability to Australian vocational training and to similar general practice training programs internationally, like the UK, New Zealand and Canada [37].Data was obtained from one of the largest GP vocational training organizations in Australia and included five cohorts of trainees with demographic characteristics similar to the national cohort [24]. The outcome study factors are well established and reliable [27, 28].

There are, however, some limitations arising from the retrospective study design with use of routinely collected training data (that is, data collected for purposes other than research on this topic). The resultant issue of some missing data for our study factors, PGA-OSCE and PGA-KFP, was addressed with imputation of data. Importantly, there were no missing data for our outcome factors of performance on Fellowship examinations.

Also, we had limited case content of our PGAs (a 5-case KFP, and a 1-station OSCE). Despite this, the PGAs predictive ability was robust and the applicability and practicability of the only modestly resource-intensive PGAs in other settings seems good. It is likely, however, that careful curation of the limited content may be vital to the PGAs’ efficacy.

We have examined the predictive validity of extant formative assessment tools. A limitation is that these assessment tools have not been the subject of psychometric evaluation. As we have in our study demonstrated their considerable predictive utility, psychometric evaluation to refine the assessment tools may be indicated to potentially further enhance their predictive validity.

A further limitation is that, while our outcome factor, examination failure, entails considerable consequences for both registrar and training organization, it doesn’t necessarily capture clinical competence. Further work in the Australian registrar population has shown a measure of unobserved clinical performance to be predictive of RACGP examination performance, including examination pass/failure [30]. While remediation must address being at-risk for examination failure, it must also address the deficit in clinical competence which the PGA performance likely also reflects.

Conclusion

Our findings of higher PGA-KFP and PGA-OSCE scores being associated with higher Fellowship examination scores for the AKT and KFP and OSCE and greater likelihood of passing all three exams suggest an intensive commencement-of-training assessment, similar in format to subsequent summative examinations, has utility in the early identification of risk of failure in those summative examinations. It may, thus, facilitate early intervention. How learners’ individual performance in the assessments can inform individual remediation, and whether remediation can improve summative examination performance and clinical performance are questions for further research.
