Stability of the Shape for Circular Vortex Filaments under Non-Symmetric Perturbations
Masashi Aiki, Mitsuo Higaki

Abstract
We establish the nonlinear orbital stability of circular vortex filaments governed by the Localized Induction Equation (LIE) under non-symmetric perturbations, within the framework of Tani-Nishiyama [ 9 ] . This result extends the first author’s recent work [ 1 ] by removing symmetry assumptions on perturbations. While the circular filaments are known to be Lyapunov unstable due to linear growth of the translational mode, we prove that their shape remains globally stable modulo spatial translations and rotations about the symmetry axis. The crucial ingredient is a geometric stability lemma derived from the conservation of vector fluid impulse, which constrains the low-frequency modulations that are not covered by the relative energy. Finally, we relate the fluid impulse to an isoperimetric inequality, yielding a geometric constraint for closed filaments.



1
Introduction

We consider the motion of a closed vortex filament in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
governed by

\bm{x}_{t}=\bm{x}_{s}\times\bm{x}_{ss}\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0mu\mathbb{T}\times(0,\infty).

(LIE)

Here,
\bm{x}(s,t)
describes the filament position parameterized by the arc-length
s
at time
t
, subscripts denote differentiations with the respective variables, and
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{T}_{L}=\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
denotes the flat torus of length
L
. The system (
LIE
) is called the Localized Induction Equation (LIE) and is invariant under spatial translations and rotations.

In this paper, we fix a radius
R>0
, so that
L=2\pi R
, and focus on the stability of the circular vortex filament. Let
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
be the stationary circular profile given by

\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)=R\cos\Big(\frac{s}{R}\Big)\bm{e}_{1}+R\sin\Big(\frac{s}{R}\Big)\bm{e}_{2},

where
\{\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2},\bm{e}_{3}\}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It is well known that (
LIE
) admits an exact solution describing a circular filament moving with constant speed in the
\bm{e}_{3}
-direction:

\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)=\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)+\frac{t}{R}\bm{e}_{3}.

Our goal is to analyze the behavior of solutions
\bm{x}
near the manifold generated by the circular filaments
\bm{x}^{R}
. However, since the equation is invariant under spatial translations and rotations around the
z
-axis, perturbations generally induce secular drifts along the orbits of these symmetries. This means that, even if the shape remains stable, the solution
\bm{x}
itself may deviate from the reference profile
\bm{x}^{R}
by shifting its position or its phase over time.

Therefore, to establish the shape stability, it is indispensable to introduce a metric that explicitly factors out these spatial symmetries. Inspired by the study of solitons
[
10
]
, we define the orbital distance to the manifold
\Sigma
generated by the profile
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
and the symmetries:

\Sigma=\{Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(\cdot)+\tau~|~\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\mkern 9.0mu\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}\subset H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3},

(1.1)

where
Q^{\alpha}_{z}
denotes the rotation matrix around the
z
-axis by angle
\alpha
. We then investigate the uniform global-in-time bound of the distance between
\bm{x}(t)
and
\Sigma
:

\begin{split}\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)&=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{x}(t)-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T})}.\end{split}

(1.2)

This definition allows us to capture the stability of the circular filament by minimizing the error over translation and rotation, thereby absorbing the secular drifts inherent to the problem. This can be interpreted from a physical point of view as follows. To investigate the stability of the shape of the filament, translation can be disregarded since it doesn’t change the shape. Furthermore, rotating the circular filament
\bm{x}^{R}
around the
z
-axis results in a non-zero perturbation, but the resulting curve is the exact same. This motivates us to define the orbital stability as in (
1.2
) to quantify the essential change in the shape of the filament. On the other hand, the long-time behavior of the translation of the filament is also of interest, hence we also investigate the behavior of the translational mode. Namely, we prove the estimate (
1.5
) below.

Since the present work is a direct extension of
[
1
]
, we keep the discussion of the physical background and the literature brief, and refer to
[
1
]
for a detailed list of related works. For the reader’s convenience, a quick summary of the new ingredients in this paper is as follows:

•

Removal of the symmetry assumptions in
[
1
]
;

•

Introduction of an orbital distance modulo translations and axial rotations;

•

Impulse-based lemma (Lemma
2.6
) controlling the low-frequency modes.

Related nonlinear orbital stability results for Hill’s vortex in the axisymmetric Euler equations are obtained by Choi
[
4
]
; see also Choi-Jeong
[
5
]
for linear-in-time filamentation near Hill’s vortex. While these works concern the Euler setting and rely on different techniques,
they are conceptually connected to the present paper through the role of impulse-type conserved quantities in controlling translational/low-frequency modulations.

Our result is stated as follows. Denote the Fourier series expansion of
\bm{v}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
by

\bm{v}(s)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\bm{v}}_{n}e^{\mathrm{i}ns/R},\qquad\hat{\bm{v}}_{n}:=\frac{1}{2\pi R}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bm{v}(s)e^{-\mathrm{i}ns/R}\,ds,

and the projection onto the
n
-mode by

\mathcal{P}_{n}\bm{v}(s)=\hat{\bm{v}}_{n}e^{\mathrm{i}ns/R}.

Moreover, for
m\neq 0

\mathcal{P}_{\pm m}=\mathcal{P}_{m}+\mathcal{P}_{-m},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{|n|\geq m}=\sum_{|k|\geq m}\mathcal{P}_{k},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{|n|\leq m}=\sum_{|k|\leq m}\mathcal{P}_{k}.

In addition, denote the fluid impulse of solutions
\bm{x}
of (
LIE
) by

\bm{P}[\bm{x}(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bm{x}(s,t)\times\bm{x}_{s}(s,t)\,ds.

The following theorem states the orbital stability of
\bm{x}^{R}
under a structural condition on the initial impulse and a smallness condition on the higher-frequency modes.

Theorem 1.1

The circular vortex filament
\bm{x}^{R}
is orbitally stable in the following sense. There exist positive constants
C,\delta_{0}
such that for any
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
and any initial filament
\bm{x}_{0}\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
satisfying

|\bm{P}[\bm{x}_{0}]-\pi R^{2}\bm{e}_{3}|\leq\delta^{2},\qquad\|\mathcal{P}_{|n|\geq 2}\bm{x}_{0}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\delta,

(1.3)

and
|\bm{x}_{0s}(s)|=1
for all
s\in\mathbb{T}
,
the unique global-in-time solution
\bm{x}
of (
LIE
) in
[
9
]

satisfies

\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)\leq C\delta,\quad t\geq 0.

(1.4)

Moreover,
\bm{x}=\bm{x}(t)
stays in an
O(\delta^{2}t)
-neighborhood of
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
globally in the sense that

|\mathcal{P}_{0}(\bm{x}(t)-\bm{x}^{R}(t)-\bm{x}_{0})|\leq C\delta^{2}t,\quad t\geq 0.

(1.5)

Remark 1.2

(i)

It is important to distinguish between Lyapunov stability and orbital stability. As discussed in
[
1
]
, the circular filament
\bm{x}^{R}
is Lyapunov unstable due to the linear growth of the zero mode. Theorem
1.1
shows orbital stability by factoring out this translation and
z
-axis rotation via
\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x},\Sigma)
. Consequently, we prove the stability of the filament’s
shape
without assuming the symmetry conditions in
[
1
]
.

(ii)

The quantity
\bm{P}[\bm{x}]
, often referred to as the fluid impulse, is analyzed by Arms-Hama
[
2
]
and Fukumoto
[
6
]
. The fluid impulse is conserved in time for closed curve solutions of (
LIE
). The assumption (
1.3
) ensures that the initial impulse
\bm{P}[\bm{x}_{0}]
of the solution
\bm{x}
is sufficiently close to the reference value
\bm{P}[\bm{x}^{R}_{0}]=\pi R^{2}\bm{e}_{3}
. In fact, this assumption is connected with an isoperimetric inequality; see Appendix
A
. We emphasize that, even if the inequality in Appendix
A
holds, Theorem
1.1
remains nontrivial: it is a nonlinear stability of solutions to (
LIE
) and requires a dynamical control of the low-frequency modulation parameters along the evolution.

(iii)

The regularity assumption
\bm{x}_{0}\in H^{4}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
is imposed to ensure the uniqueness of the global solution, as established in Tani-Nishiyama
[
9
]
. While the existence of weak solutions in
H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
is known by Nishiyama-Tani
[
8
, Remark 4.1]
, to the best of our knowledge, the uniqueness of solutions in this class remains an open problem.

(iv)

The linear stability of various closed vortex filaments, including circular filaments, with respect to closure-preserving periodic perturbations has been investigated via the Hasimoto map; see Calini-Keith-Lafortune
[
3
, Section 6]
. However, proving the nonlinear orbital stability of a vortex filament against more general perturbations requires a different approach. Extending the analysis in
[
1
]
, our current result establishes this stability by using the conservation of fluid impulse.

(v)

In the geometric framework of Langer-Perline
[
7
]
, elastic curves arise as critical points of the bending energy with a length-type constraint and yield rigid motion solutions of the LIE. In the closed filament setting, the circular vortex filament is an example of such an elastic curve; see
[
7
, Remarks]
for
the observation that one could equally consider closed curves. In this setting, the circle (hence the circular filament) is the simplest example. However, their analysis does not explicitly involve the vector-valued fluid impulse, which is crucial for controlling the
\pm 1
modes. Our proof utilizes this conserved quantity to show that the circular filament is not merely a critical point, but a (constrained) local minimum.

2
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.1
. For a solution
\bm{x}
of (
LIE
), define the perturbation
\bm{\varphi}
by

\bm{x}(s,t)=\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)+\bm{\varphi}(s,t).

Substituting this decomposition into (
LIE
) yields the nonlinear equation for
\bm{\varphi}
:

\bm{\varphi}_{t}=\bm{x}^{R}_{s}\times\bm{\varphi}_{ss}+\bm{\varphi}_{s}\times\bm{x}^{R}_{ss}+\bm{\varphi}_{s}\times\bm{\varphi}_{ss}\quad\text{in}\mkern 9.0mu\mathbb{T}\times(0,\infty).

Before proceeding, recall the conservation laws for solutions of (
LIE
).

Lemma 2.1

For a solution
\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{R}+\bm{\varphi}
of (
LIE
), the following
conservation laws hold:

(i)

The preservation of arc length parameter:
|\bm{x}_{s}(s,t)|=1

for all
(s,t)\in\mathbb{T}\times(0,\infty)
.

(ii)

Conservation of the fluid impulse:
\bm{P}[\bm{x}(t)]=\bm{P}[\bm{x_{0}}]
.

(iii)

Conservation of the relative energy

E[\bm{\varphi}(t)]:=\|\bm{\varphi}_{ss}(t)\|^{2}-(1/R^{2})\|\bm{\varphi}_{s}(t)\|^{2}
:

E[\bm{\varphi}(t)]=E[\bm{\varphi}(0)]

Proof.

The conservation laws (
i
) and (
ii
) are well-known; see e.g.
[
9
, Lemma 2.2]
and
[
2
,
6
]
, respectively. For the conservation of (
iii
), we refer to
[
1
, Proof of Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

We begin the proof by decomposing
\bm{\varphi}
into Fourier modes. Set

\bm{g}=\mathcal{P}_{0}\bm{\varphi},\qquad\bm{\psi}=\mathcal{P}_{\pm 1}\bm{\varphi},\qquad\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}=\mathcal{P}_{|n|\geq 2}\bm{\varphi}.

(2.1)

We then reduce the proof of Theorem
1.1
to the estimates of these components. Since the circular filament
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
consists solely of the
\pm 1
modes, for any rotation angle
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and translation vector
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the profile
Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau
doesn’t possess any frequency mode satisfying
|n|\geq 2
. By the orthogonality of Fourier modes, the distance to the manifold
\Sigma
satisfies

\begin{split}&\operatorname{dist}(\bm{x}(t),\Sigma)^{2}\\
&=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{x}^{R}(t)+\bm{\varphi}(t)-(Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\tau)\|_{2}^{2}\\
&=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R},\,\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\Big(\|(t/R)\bm{e}_{3}+\bm{g}(t)-\tau\|_{2}^{2}+\|\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\bm{\psi}(t)-Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\Big)\\
&=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\|\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\bm{\psi}(t)-Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(2.2)

Here, the infimum over
\tau
is attained by choosing
\tau=(t/R)\bm{e}_{3}+\bm{g}(t)
, which eliminates the zero-mode contribution. Therefore, the first assertion of Theorem
1.1
, the orbital stability, follows from the analysis of the modulation for the
\pm 1
modes
\bm{\psi}(t)
and the uniform bounds on
\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}
. The second assertion of the theorem, the estimate for the deviation from
\bm{x}^{R}(t)
, requires the estimate of the zero mode
\bm{g}(t)
itself, since the infimum over
\tau
is not taken in that context.

As detailed in Section
2.1
below, the high-frequency modes
\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}
are estimated by utilizing the conservation law for
E[\bm{\varphi}(t)]
in Lemma
2.1
. In contrast, the
\pm 1
modes
\bm{\psi}
and the zero mode
\bm{g}
require separate analysis. This distinction is essential because the relative energy
E[\bm{\varphi}(t)]
degenerates on these lower modes.
The detailed analysis of
\bm{\psi}
will be performed in Section
2.2
, while
\bm{g}
in Section
2.3
.

2.1
Estimates for the High-Frequency
|n|\geq 2
Modes

In this section, we provide the global-in-time smallness of the high-frequency
|n|\geq 2
modes
\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}
. Define the bilinear form for the relative energy by

\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{E}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bigg(\bm{u}_{ss}\cdot\bm{v}_{ss}-\frac{1}{R^{2}}\bm{u}_{s}\cdot\bm{v}_{s}\bigg)\,ds,\quad\bm{u},\bm{v}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3},

and the corresponding energy by

\|\bm{u}\|_{E}=\sqrt{\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle_{E}},\quad\bm{u}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3}.

The estimate of
\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}
is a corollary of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2

Let
\bm{u}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
satisfy
\mathcal{P}_{|n|\leq 1}\bm{u}=0
. Then,

\|\bm{u}\|_{E}\approx\|\bm{u}\|_{2}.

Proof.

By Parseval’s identity,

\|\bm{u}\|_{E}^{2}=\frac{1}{R^{4}}\sum_{|n|\geq 2}n^{2}(n^{2}-1)|\hat{\bm{u}}_{n}|^{2}.

Thus
\|\bm{u}\|_{E}\lesssim\|\bm{u}\|_{2}
. Using the inequality
n^{2}(n^{2}-1)\geq(3/4)n^{4}
for
|n|\geq 2
,

\|\bm{u}\|^{2}_{E}\geq\frac{3}{4R^{4}}\sum_{|n|\geq 2}n^{4}|\hat{\bm{u}}_{n}|^{2}.

Thus
\|\bm{u}\|_{2}\lesssim\|\bm{u}\|_{E}
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Corollary 2.3

Let
\bm{x}=\bm{x}^{R}+\bm{\varphi}
be a solution of (
LIE
) and
\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}
defined as in (
2.1
). Then,

\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}\approx\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(0)\|_{2}.

Hence, under the assumption (
1.3
), we have the global-in-time smallness of
\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}
:

\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}\lesssim\delta,\quad t\geq 0.

(2.3)

2.2
Analysis of the
\pm 1
Modes

In this section, we provide the orbital stability of the
\pm 1
modes
\bm{\psi}
. Recall from (
2.2
) that the stability of the low-frequency part relies on the bound for

\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\|\bm{x}^{R}_{0}+\bm{\psi}(t)-Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=\inf_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}\|\mathcal{P}_{\pm 1}\bm{x}(t)-Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since the reference solution
\bm{x}^{R}_{0}
describes a circle in the
xy
-plane, the spatial rotation
Q^{\alpha}_{z}
is equivalent to a phase shift in the parametrization. Specifically, by setting
\sigma=R\alpha
,

Q^{\alpha}_{z}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)=\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s+\sigma).

Motivated by this symmetry and the equivalence

\|\mathcal{P}_{\pm 1}u\|_{k}\approx\|\mathcal{P}_{\pm 1}u\|,\quad k\geq 0,\mkern 9.0muu\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}),

(2.4)

we define the distance for the
\pm 1
modes as

\operatorname{dist}_{\pm 1}(t)=\inf_{\sigma\in\mathbb{T}}\|\mathcal{P}_{\pm 1}\bm{x}(\cdot,t)-\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(\cdot+\sigma)\|.

(2.5)

Let the solution
\bm{x}
of (
LIE
) be expanded in the Fourier series as

\bm{x}(s,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\bm{x}_{n}(t)e^{\mathrm{i}ns/R}.

(2.6)

Using the explicit form

\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)=\bm{C}_{1}e^{\mathrm{i}s/R}+\bm{C}_{-1}e^{-\mathrm{i}s/R},

(2.7)

with

\bm{C}_{1}=\frac{R}{2}(\bm{e}_{1}-\mathrm{i}\bm{e}_{2}),\qquad\bm{C}_{-1}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,

(2.8)

we can rewrite the distance
\operatorname{dist}_{\pm 1}(t)
in terms of Fourier coefficients. Indeed, by Parseval’s identity and the conjugate relations
\bm{x}_{-1}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
and
\bm{C}_{-1}=\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, we have

\operatorname{dist}_{\pm 1}(t)\approx\min_{\sigma\in\mathbb{T}}|\bm{x}_{1}(t)-e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma/R}\bm{C}_{1}|.

(2.9)

Our goal is to verify that the right-hand side remains small globally in time, provided that the initial perturbation and the fluid impulse are close to those of the reference state.

First, we characterize the optimal phase and simplify the distance function.

Lemma 2.4

Let
\bm{a},\bm{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. The minimum of
|\bm{a}-e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\bm{c}|
over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
is attained when

\theta=\operatorname{arg}(\bm{a}\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{c}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)
,
and the minimum value is given by

\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}/(2\pi\mathbb{Z})}|\bm{a}-e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\bm{c}|^{2}=(|\bm{a}|-|\bm{c}|)^{2}+2\big(|\bm{a}||\bm{c}|-|\bm{a}\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{c}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu|\big).

(2.10)

Proof.

The proof is elementary and thus omitted.
∎

Remark 2.5

The phase
\sigma=\sigma(t)
that minimizes the right-hand side of (
2.9
) is given by

\sigma(t)=R\operatorname{arg}\big(\bm{x}_{1}(t)\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\big).

(2.11)

Thanks to Lemma
2.4
, by setting

\varepsilon_{\parallel}(t)=\big(|\bm{x}_{1}(t)|-|\bm{C}_{1}|\big)^{2},\qquad\varepsilon_{\perp}(t)=2\big(|\bm{x}_{1}(t)||\bm{C}_{1}|-|\bm{x}_{1}(t)\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu|\big),

we have

\operatorname{dist}_{\pm 1}(t)^{2}\approx\varepsilon_{\parallel}(t)+\varepsilon_{\perp}(t).

(2.12)

The norm error
\varepsilon_{\parallel}(t)
is controlled by the conservation of the quantity
|\bm{x}_{s}|=1
due to Lemma
2.1
(
i
). Indeed, Parseval’s identity for
\bm{x}_{s}
implies that

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2}|\bm{x}_{n}(t)|^{2}=R^{2},\quad t\geq 0.

Since
|\bm{C}_{1}|^{2}=R^{2}/2
, we obtain

2|\bm{x}_{1}(t)|^{2}-2|\bm{C}_{1}|^{2}=\sum_{|n|=1}n^{2}|\bm{x}_{n}(t)|^{2}-R^{2}=-\sum_{|n|\geq 2}n^{2}|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}_{n}(t)|^{2}.

(2.13)

Hence, using the smallness of
\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}
from (
2.3
),

\varepsilon_{\parallel}(t)\lesssim\big||\bm{x}_{1}(t)|-|\bm{C}_{1}|\big|\lesssim\|\tilde{\bm{\varphi}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2}.

(2.14)

However, the angular error
\varepsilon_{\perp}(t)
requires more structural information. To this end, we utilize the conservation of the fluid impulse
\bm{P}[\bm{x}]
due to Lemma
2.1
(
ii
). We decompose
\bm{x}_{1}(t)
with respect to the basis

\{\bm{C}_{1},\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,\bm{e}_{3}\}

of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
into

\bm{x}_{1}(t)=\lambda(t)\bm{C}_{1}+\mu(t)\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\nu(t)|\bm{C}_{1}|\bm{e}_{3},

(2.15)

where
\lambda(t),\mu(t),\nu(t)\in\mathbb{C}
are time-dependent coefficients. Note that

\bm{C}_{1}\cdot\bm{C}_{1}=0,\qquad\bm{C}_{1}\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=|\bm{C}_{1}|^{2}=\frac{R^{2}}{2},\qquad\bm{C}_{1}\cdot\bm{e}_{3}=0,

(2.16)

and that

\bm{C}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\mathrm{i}|\bm{C}_{1}|^{2}\bm{e}_{3},\qquad\bm{C}_{1}\times\bm{e}_{3}=-\mathrm{i}\bm{C}_{1},\qquad\overline{\bm{C}_{1}}\times\bm{e}_{3}=\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.

(2.17)

The following geometric stability lemma plays a crucial role in the proof.

Lemma 2.6

(Geometric Stability Lemma)

Under the assumption (
1.3
),

\big||\lambda(t)|-1\big|+|\mu(t)|+|\nu(t)|\lesssim\delta,\quad t\geq 0.

Proof.

Using (
2.16
), we have

|\bm{x}_{1}|^{2}=|\bm{C}_{1}|^{2}\big(|\lambda|^{2}+|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2}\big).

(2.18)

Substituting this into (
2.13
), we see from (
2.3
) that

\big||\lambda|^{2}+|\mu|^{2}+|\nu|^{2}-1\big|\lesssim\delta^{2}.

(2.19)

Next, we invoke the conservation of
\bm{P}[\bm{x}]
. By the Fourier series expansion,

\bm{P}[\bm{x}]=-\mathrm{i}\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\bm{x}_{n}\times\bm{x}_{-n}=-\mathrm{i}\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n\bm{x}_{n}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.

(2.20)

Applying Lemma
2.1
(
ii
) and the embedding

\begin{split}&\bigg|-\mathrm{i}\pi\sum_{|n|=1}n\bm{x}_{n}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\pi R^{2}\bm{e}_{3}\bigg|\\

Thus, by the assumption (
1.3
) and the smallness of

\begin{split}\bigg|-\mathrm{i}\pi\sum_{|n|=1}n\bm{x}_{n}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\pi R^{2}\bm{e}_{3}\bigg|\lesssim\delta^{2}.\end{split}

(2.21)

The summation term simplifies to

-\mathrm{i}\pi\sum_{|n|=1}n\bm{x}_{n}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{n}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=-\mathrm{i}\pi(\bm{x}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)=2\pi\Im(\bm{x}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu).

Using (
2.17
), we have

\begin{split}\bm{x}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu&=(\lambda\bm{C}_{1}+\mu\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\nu|\bm{C}_{1}|\bm{e}_{3})\times(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\lambda\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu+\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}+\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\nu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu|\bm{C}_{1}|\bm{e}_{3})\\
&=\mathrm{i}|\bm{C}_{1}|^{2}(|\lambda|^{2}-|\mu|^{2})\bm{e}_{3}+\bm{v},\end{split}

with
\bm{v}
satisfying
\bm{v}\cdot\bm{e}_{3}=0
:

\bm{v}:=\mathrm{i}|\bm{C}_{1}|(\nu\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\lambda\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\nu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\bm{C}_{1}+\mathrm{i}|\bm{C}_{1}|\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu(\nu\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\lambda\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\nu\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{C}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu.

Hence, since

2\pi\Im(\bm{x}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\cdot\bm{e}_{3}=2\pi\Im\big((\bm{x}_{1}\times\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\bm{x}_{1}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu)\cdot\bm{e}_{3}\big)=\pi R^{2}(|\lambda|^{2}-|\mu|^{2}),

which combined with (

\big||\lambda|^{2}-|\mu|^{2}-1\big|\lesssim\delta^{2}.

(2.22)

The assertion follows from (
2.19
) and (
2.22

This completes the proof of the lemma.

Before closing this paper, we present a concrete example exhibiting linear-in-time drift from
\bm{x}^{R}
. This example confirms the optimality of the estimate (

). Let

Q^{\alpha}_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&0&\sin\alpha\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\alpha&0&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

We define the tilted solution
\bm{x}^{\alpha}
from
\bm{x}^{R}
by

\bm{x}^{\alpha}(s,t)=Q^{\alpha}_{y}\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)=Q^{\alpha}_{y}\bm{x}^{R}_{0}(s)+\frac{t}{R}Q^{\alpha}_{y}\bm{e}_{3}.

Since (

\bm{x}^{\alpha}
is a solution for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

\bm{P}[\bm{x}^{\alpha}(t)]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}Q^{\alpha}_{y}\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)\times Q^{\alpha}_{y}\bm{x}^{R}_{s}(s,t)\,ds=Q^{\alpha}_{y}\bm{P}[\bm{x}^{R}(t)]=Q^{\alpha}_{y}(\pi R^{2}\bm{e}_{3}).

The explicit form of
\bm{x}^{\alpha}
is given by

\begin{split}\bm{x}^{\alpha}(s,t)&=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&0&\sin\alpha\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\alpha&0&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}R\cos(s/R)\\
R\sin(s/R)\\
t/R\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}(R\cos\alpha)\cos(s/R)+(\sin\alpha)t/R\\
R\sin(s/R)\\
-(R\sin\alpha)\cos(s/R)+(\cos\alpha)t/R\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

Thus,

\bm{x}^{\alpha}(s,t)=\bm{x}^{R}(s,t)+\frac{t}{R}\begin{pmatrix}\sin\alpha\\
0\\
\cos\alpha-1\end{pmatrix}+R\cos\Big(\frac{s}{R}\Big)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha-1\\
0\\
-\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

Then the perturbation
\bm{\varphi}:=\bm{x}^{\alpha}-\bm{x}^{R}

\bm{\varphi}(s,t)=\frac{t}{R}\begin{pmatrix}\sin\alpha\\

Appendix A
Fluid Impulse and Isoperimetric Inequality

In this appendix, we explain a geometric meaning of the assumption on fluid impulse by using an isoperimetric inequality. Recall that
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/L\mathbb{Z}
with
L=2\pi R
.

Proposition A.1

Let
\bm{x}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})^{3}
be a closed curve parameterized by the arc-length
s
, i.e.,
|\bm{x}_{s}(s)|=1
for all
s\in\mathbb{T}
. Then
\bm{P}[\bm{x}]\cdot\bm{e}_{3}\leq\pi R^{2}
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\bm{x}(\mathbb{T})
is a circle of radius
R
on a plane parallel to the
xy
-plane.

Proof.

Denote
\bm{x}(s)=(x_{1}(s),x_{2}(s),x_{3}(s))
. By definition,

P_{3}:=\bm{P}[\bm{x}]\cdot\bm{e}_{3}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi R}(x_{1}(s)x_{2s}(s)-x_{2}(s)x_{1s}(s))\,ds.

We see from Green’s theorem that
P_{3}
equals the signed area enclosed by the projected 2D closed curve
\gamma(s):=(x_{1}(s),x_{2}(s))
in the
xy
-plane. Let
\ell
denote the length of
\gamma
:

\ell=\int_{0}^{2\pi R}\sqrt{x_{1s}(s)^{2}+x_{2s}(s)^{2}}\,ds.

The 2D isoperimetric inequality gives

4\pi|P_{3}|\leq\ell^{2}.

(A.1)

On the other hand, the condition
|\bm{x}_{s}(s)|=1
trivially implies

\ell\leq\int_{0}^{2\pi R}1\,ds=2\pi R.

(A.2)

Combining (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we obtain the upper bound for
P_{3}
:

4\pi P_{3}\leq\ell^{2}\leq(2\pi R)^{2}=4\pi^{2}R^{2},

(A.3)

which implies the first assertion
P_{3}\leq\pi R^{2}
.

Next we prove the characterization of the equality case. It is clear that
P_{3}=\pi R^{2}
holds when
\bm{x}(\mathbb{T})
is a circle of radius
R
lying in a plane parallel to the
xy
-plane. Conversely, assume that
P_{3}=\pi R^{2}
. Then (
A.3
) forces that
\ell=2\pi R
. The equality in (
A.2
) implies that
\sqrt{x_{1s}(s)^{2}+x_{2s}(s)^{2}}=1
for all
s\in\mathbb{T}
. Together with
|\bm{x}_{s}|=1
, this gives
x_{3s}=0
, hence
x_{3}
is constant and the curve is contained in a plane parallel to the
xy
-plane. Moreover, equality in (
A.1
) implies that the planar curve
\gamma
is a circle. Since
\ell=2\pi R
, the radius of this circle is
R
. Therefore,
\bm{x}(\mathbb{T})
is a circle of radius
R
in a plane parallel to the
xy
-plane, up to translations and reparameterizations by shifts of
s
.
∎
