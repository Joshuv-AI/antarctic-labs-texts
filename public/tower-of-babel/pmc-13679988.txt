Managing inappropriate sexual behaviour in people with dementia
Authors: Natasha E Lane, Dallas Seitz, Jennifer A Watt
Published: 2026

As many as 25% of people with dementia demonstrate inappropriate sexual behaviour

Such behaviour encompasses a range of sexual expressions representing a change from baseline behaviours or context, including inappropriate sexual comments, self-exposure, public masturbation, and unwanted sexual touching. Patients with any dementia subtype can exhibit these behaviours, which can cause substantial distress for patients, families, and staff, but stigma and uncertainty about management can result in under-reporting. 1 Roughly 15% of reported cases occur among females.2

Not all sexual expression in people with dementia is inappropriate

Appropriateness of sexual behaviours is subjective and often specific to the setting or caregiver.3 Clinicians should consider people’s cultural and relational context (i.e., what is appropriate in one setting may be inappropriate in another), their orientation to context, and capacity to consent when determining if the behaviour is inappropriate.3

New-onset inappropriate sexual behaviour requires a structured assessment, including medication review

Medical triggers (e.g., delirium) and comorbid behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia should be assessed.4 Clinicians should review medications and deprescribe potential triggers (e.g., dopamine agonists).2 Unmet needs for intimacy, social connection, environmental stimulation, or personal care should be explored.3

Helpful first-line treatments include behavioural and environmental interventions5

Behavioural strategies (e.g., caregiver education, gentle redirection), environmental modification (e.g., private space, caregiver change), and distracting activities often improve these behaviours and are preferred and well tolerated by patients.4

When pharmacotherapy is needed, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and hormonal agents have the best evidence

Evidence for use of medications is limited, but SSRIs and — for male patients — hormonal agents (e.g., finasteride) are the best tolerated options and most frequently associated with symptom improvement in case studies (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260633/tab-related-content).2 Pharmacotherapy that may benefit comorbid symptoms (e.g., SSRIs in patients with comorbid agitation) minimizes polypharmacy. Physicians should consider referral to a psychiatrist or geriatric specialist if behaviours are refractory to initial behavioural and pharmacologic interventions.
