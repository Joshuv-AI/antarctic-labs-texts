Posted Pricing for Online Selection: Limited Price Changes and Risk Sensitivity
Hossein Nekouyan, Bo Sun, Raouf Boutaba, Xiaoqi Tan

Abstract
Posted-price mechanisms (PPMs) are a widely adopted strategy for online resource allocation due to their simplicity, intuitive nature, and incentive compatibility. To manage the uncertainty inherent in online settings, PPMs commonly employ dynamically increasing prices. While this adaptive pricing achieves strong performance, it introduces practical challenges: dynamically changing prices can lead to fairness concerns stemming from price discrimination and incur operational costs associated with frequent updates. This paper addresses these issues by investigating posted pricing constrained by a limited, pre-specified number of allowed price changes, denoted by Δ \Delta . We further extend this framework by incorporating a second critical dimension: risk sensitivity. Instead of evaluating performance based solely on expectation, we utilize a tail-risk objective—specifically, the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) of the total social welfare, parameterized by a risk level δ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \delta\in[0,1] . We formally introduce a novel problem class kSelection- ( δ , Δ ) (\delta,\Delta) in online adversarial selection and propose a correlated PPM that utilizes a single random seed to correlate posted prices. This correlation scheme is designed to address both the limited price changes and simultaneously enhance the tail performance of the online algorithm. Our subsequent analysis provides performance guarantees under these joint constraints, revealing a clear trade-off between the number of allowed price changes and the algorithm’s risk sensitivity. We also establish optimality results for several important special cases of the problem.

Such mechanisms are common in real-world systems like airline ticket sales, ride-sharing platforms, and online advertising.

1.1
Our Contribution and Techniques

In the following, we elaborate on the contributions of this work to the existing literature on online optimization and resource allocation through posted pricing.
We emphasize that the
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
problem is newly introduced in this paper. However, several of its special cases have been studied in prior work.
To build toward our main results, we first analyze these special cases and then generalize our findings to the most comprehensive form of the problem.

We begin with the risk-neutral setting (i.e.,
\delta=1
), where the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
metric reduces to
expected
efficiency. In this case, our goal is to focus solely on the price-change cap and design PPMs with arbitrary caps
\Delta
that achieve optimal competitiveness. Prior work
[
30
]
provides an optimal PPM for the special case
\Delta=0
, but their techniques do not extend to settings with more permitted price changes. In contrast, we develop a
family
of PPMs that achieve the
optimal
competitive ratio for every price-change cap
\Delta\in\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
. The techniques introduced in this section form the basis for extending the price-change cap constraint to the risk-sensitive regime.

We then turn to the risk-sensitive setting (i.e.,
\delta\in(0,1)
), beginning with the fully-static case
\Delta=0
. This simple setting helps build toward the most general case. Recent work
[
8
]
studies the 1-max search problem—a close variant of
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
when
k=1
—and introduces a single-threshold randomized algorithm. Although their model differs slightly from ours and does not allow a direct comparison, their algorithm is not optimal. In contrast, for
\Delta=0
, we derive the
optimal risk-sensitive static price
, achieving the tightest
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
-competitive ratio among all online algorithms that use a single static price for every
\delta\in(0,1)
. Next, we examine the special case
\Delta=k-1
, which permits a fully-dynamic pricing scheme. This setting removes the price-change cap and isolates the challenge of designing risk-sensitive algorithms under the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
metric. Here, we design a PPM that achieves
exact optimality in the large-inventory limit
(i.e., when
k\to\infty
) for every
\delta\in(0,1)
. Finally, synthesizing insights from these two extreme cases, we present a
general
\Delta
-level pricing design
that accommodates any capped number of price changes for arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
. This framework yields monotonic improvements in
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
-competitiveness as the allowed number of price changes
\Delta
increases or as the tail probability
\delta
increases. While we cannot establish optimality in this general setting, the design closely mirrors the optimal constructions derived for the previous two cases. We thus conjecture that, with a more refined analysis, optimality can also be demonstrated here.

From a technical standpoint, our contributions are twofold.

Correlated Posted Pricing.

We design a PPM in which all pricing levels are correlated through a single random seed drawn from a uniform distribution over
[0,1]
. This seed jointly determines the posted prices at every level. From a risk perspective, such correlation induces a family of pricing profiles that remain more balanced across different realizations of randomness, thereby reducing the variability of the algorithm’s total welfare (or revenue) across sample paths. When evaluating the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
objective—namely, the expected performance over the worst
\delta
-fraction of outcomes—the algorithm loses little performance because the remaining
(1-\delta)
-fraction of sample paths does not diverge significantly from the worst
\delta
-fraction. Moreover, correlating the pricing levels enforces smoother price changes across realizations, leading to less variability in the number of sold units across different sample paths of the randomized algorithm, which as a result also generates a more balanced objective value across different sample paths.

In the special case
\Delta=k-1
, we further show that this correlation scheme yields randomized integral decisions that closely track the fractional decisions of an algorithm that uses the same pricing functions in the fractional version of the
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
problem. In this sense, the correlation mechanism can be interpreted as a rounding scheme that employs correlation to convert fractional allocations into integral ones without loss. Viewed this way, our design contributes to a growing line of work on online rounding schemes that leverage correlation, including
[
17
,
22
,
15
]
. We also demonstrate that both the correlation mechanism and the proof technique developed for the
\Delta=k-1
case extend to the open question posed in
[
20
]
: constructing a PPM that achieves optimal performance in both small and large inventory regimes for the online selection variant studied in that work.

Risk-Sensitive Randomized Online Primal-Dual.

From a methodological standpoint, to establish the competitiveness of our algorithm, we introduce a dual linear program specifically tailored to the
\Delta
-capped setting. This dual program includes one variable per price
level
(a total of
\Delta{+}1
variables), where each variable intuitively corresponds to the price associated with that level of price change. To analyze the algorithm’s performance, we develop a randomized online primal–dual (R-OPD) framework adapted to the risk-sensitive setting. The R-OPD approach constructs dual variables as functions of the algorithm’s randomized decisions. In standard online settings
[
13
,
10
]
, update rules for dual variables depend on random decisions along each sample path, and the final dual values are obtained by taking expectations over all realizations of randomness. In the risk-sensitive setting, however, this procedure must be modified so that dual updates are performed
only over the worst
\delta
-fraction of sample paths
. These updates are designed to ensure two key properties: (i) the expected dual objective matches the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
performance of the algorithm, and (ii) the dual feasibility constraints hold in expectation. This tail-focused adaptation of the R-OPD method, combined with the
\Delta
-level dual structure, allows us to establish
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
competitiveness guarantees for all results derived in this work.
Our R-OPD framework also reveals some interesting insights in the design of risk-sensitive online algorithms in general. More specifically, to balance the uncertainty inherent in the
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
problem, our algorithms are randomized and become controllably, yet progressively, more aggressive along certain sample paths. Because the worst
(1-\delta)
-fraction of paths is disregarded under the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
metric, the design of the pricing functions naturally leads to a system of delay differential equations
[
28
]
parameterized by the risk level
\delta
. These equations induce a
memory effect
in the algorithm’s behavior, determining how rapidly the pricing functions adapt based on previously posted and accepted prices, while effectively ignoring the
(1-\delta)
-fraction of paths where performance is minimized.

More Related Work

Single-leg Revenue Management.

In the operations research literature, the
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
model can be interpreted as a single-leg revenue management problem, wherein a seller allocates a fixed capacity of a single resource (e.g., airline seats or advertising impressions). A substantial body of work (e.g.,
[
24
,
1
,
2
,
25
]
) analyzes these single-leg revenue management problems using competitive analysis, developing booking-limit and posted-price controls that yield provably optimal or near-optimal worst-case guarantees. Our framework fits naturally within this literature. We treat
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
as a single-leg revenue management problem and utilize PPMs that employ reserved resource units at different price levels to obtain tight competitive guarantees relative to the offline optimum.

Pricing with Limited Price Changes.

Within the operations research literature,
[
6
]
investigate dynamic pricing with unknown demand under a limited number of allowable price levels. They design policies that strategically schedule a small number of exploratory prices before transitioning to exploitation, achieving near-optimal performance while respecting a cap on the number of price changes. They motivate this setting by observing that in numerous markets, firms are unable to change prices freely—a constraint attributed to operational frictions, adverse customer reactions, or the additional costs associated with price adjustments, as discussed in
[
35
]
—which effectively limits the number of feasible price updates. Motivated by this perspective, several subsequent works have explored similar price-change-capped settings in both theoretical and applied contexts (e.g.,
[
5
,
4
]
).

Risk-Aware Randomized Algorithms.

As discussed earlier, recent work by
[
12
]
studies the online ski rental problem under a tail-risk constraint specified by the pair
(\gamma,\delta)
, designing online algorithms such that the probability of the worst-case competitive ratio exceeding
\gamma
is at most
\delta
. Another recent work,
[
8
]
, investigates a range of online problems—such as ski rental and 1-max search—under the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) metric. Moreover, risk-aware algorithm design has gained considerable attention in the learning theory community, where CVaR has been explored in various settings, including Markov decision processes
[
11
,
7
]
, online submodular optimization
[
29
]
, and multi-armed bandit problems
[
32
,
3
]
.

2
Problem Setting and Main Results

We introduce the problem of
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
as follows: A seller has
k
identical units of an item, and faces
T
buyers arriving one by one. When buyer
t
arrives, the seller posts a price
p_{t}
. Buyer
t
has a private value
v_{t}
and accepted the price if
v_{t}\geq p_{t}
; otherwise the buyer leaves. The price may change at most
\Delta
times over the horizon, i.e.,
\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{p_{t}\neq p_{t+1}\}}\leq\Delta
, where
\Delta\in\{0,1,\cdots,k-1\}
.

Let
x_{t}\in\{0,1\}
indicate whether buyer
t
purchases an item.
Buyer
t
’s utility is given by
u_{t}=(v_{t}-p_{t})x_{t}
, the seller’s revenue is
r=\sum_{t=1}^{T}p_{t}x_{t}
, and the total social welfare which is defined as the sum of buyer utilities and seller revenue—is

r+\sum_{t=1}^{T}u_{t}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}x_{t}
.
Let
P=\{p_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
denote the vector of prices posted by an online algorithm
ALG
.
In the online setting, the seller must determine each price
p_{t}
without knowledge of future valuations
\{v_{t^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}>t}
or even the total number of arrivals
T
.
Since the algorithm may randomize its pricing decisions to manage uncertainty in buyer valuations and total demand,
P
is treated as a random vector.

For an instance
I=\{v_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
of
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
, let
\texttt{ALG}(I,P)
denote the random variable representing the total social welfare achieved by algorithm
ALG
on instance
I
under the random price vector
P
. Let
F_{\texttt{ALG}(I,P)}
be the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of this random variable.
We use CVaR as our risk metric as it is tail-sensitive, coherent, and convex, which makes it tractable for optimization
[
16
,
27
]
.
Following the standard definition in
[
16
,
27
]
, we define
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
as follows:

Definition 1
.

Given
\delta\in(0,1]
and for a reward-type random variable
X
, we define
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}[X]=\sup_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{\tau-\tfrac{1}{\delta}\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\tau-X)_{+}\big]\Big\},

where
\delta
specifies the risk level (tail probability) and
(x)_{+}=\max\{x,0\}
.

We adopt the reward-based formulation of
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
since the objective in this work represents a reward (social welfare) rather than a loss.
Moreover, if the CDF
F_{\texttt{ALG}(I,P)}
corresponding to the random objective value of an online algorithm
ALG
is strictly increasing and continuous, the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
performance of that algorithm is given by

\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}[\texttt{ALG}(I,P)]=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{\delta}F_{\texttt{ALG}(I,P)}^{-1}(\eta)\,d\eta,
where
F^{-1}
denotes the inverse cumulative distribution (quantile) function.
Intuitively,
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
measures the algorithm’s expected performance over the worst
\delta
-fraction of its sample paths.

The objective is to design an online algorithm that minimizes its
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
-competitive ratio, denoted by
\textsf{CVaR}_{\delta}\textsf{-CR}
, defined as

\displaystyle\textsf{CVaR}_{\delta}\textsf{-CR}(\texttt{ALG})=\sup_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\frac{\texttt{OPT}(I)}{\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}[\texttt{ALG}(I,P)]},

where
\texttt{OPT}(I)
denotes the offline clairvoyant optimum computed as the summation of the
k
highest valuations, i.e.,
\texttt{OPT}(I)=\max_{x_{t}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}x_{t}
, subject to
\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_{t}\leq k,\;x_{t}\in\{0,1\},\;\forall\,t
.

Without any additional information regarding the buyers valuations, no online algorithm attains a bounded competitive ratio for
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
. We therefore assume that buyer valuations are bounded within a range whose bounds are known the decision maker.

Assumption 1
.

In
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)

, buyer valuations satisfy
v_{t}\in[L,U]
for all
t\in[T]
.

Correlated PPM with Pricing Functions

In contrast to the algorithm proposed in
[
20
]
, which samples an independent random seed for each unit of the item—resulting in independently sampled prices across different units—Algorithm
1
employs a single random seed to correlate the prices across all levels. This correlation ensures that the prices vary more smoothly across different sample paths of the randomized algorithm. Specifically, Algorithm
1
generates pricing profiles
\{\phi_{j}(R)\}_{j\in[\Delta+1]}
that become gradually more aggressive as the value of the random seed
R
increases from
0
to
1
. This design inherently balances the trade-off between serving lower-valued and higher-valued buyers. The smooth evolution of prices across sample paths also induces a desirable monotonic behavior in the algorithm’s resource utilization, which simplifies analysis by making it easier to determine the probability that each buyer’s request is accepted or rejected. Crucially, this correlation helps to synchronize posted prices such that the total welfare of the algorithm varies only slightly across sample paths. As a direct consequence, when evaluating the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
objective—i.e., by computing the expected performance over the worst
\delta
-fraction of sample paths—the algorithm incurs a minimal loss. This is because the remaining
(1-\delta)
-fraction of outcomes does not yield significantly better total welfare than the worst
\delta
-fraction.

3.1
Intuition Behind the Design in Theorem
1

To elaborate on the intuition behind the pricing design in Theorem
1
, consider the fractional relaxation of
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
, where the integrality constraint
x_{t}\in\{0,1\}
is relaxed to
\hat{x}_{t}\in[0,1]
. In this relaxation, the optimal online algorithm proposed by
[
36
]
determines the fractional allocation
\hat{x}_{t}
for buyer
t
, given their valuation
v_{t}
, according to the following utility maximization rule:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t}=\arg\max_{x\in[0,1]}\Biggl\{v_{t}x-k\int_{\hat{y}_{t}/k}^{(\hat{y}_{t}+x)/k}\phi(\eta)\,d\eta\Biggr\},\quad\text{where }\phi(x)=\begin{cases}L,&\text{if }x\in\bigl[0,\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\bigr),\\
L\cdot\exp(\alpha x-1),&\text{if }x\in\bigl[\tfrac{1}{\alpha},1\bigr].\end{cases}

(5)

Here,
\hat{y}_{t}:=\sum_{s<t}\hat{x}_{s}
denotes the algorithm’s cumulative (fractional) allocation just before buyer
t
arrives.
Intuitively,
\phi(\eta)
represents the marginal price at the normalized utilization level
\eta\in[0,1]
, and the integral term captures the total cost of allocating an additional
x
units when the current utilization is
\hat{y}_{t}/k
.

To see how pricing design arise naturally from this perspective, partition the inventory into two quotas,
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, such that
q_{1}+q_{2}=k
. There exists an equivalent form of the above maximization expression that yields the same fractional decision. By an appropriate change of variables, one can verify that
\hat{x}_{t}
can also be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{t}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{x\in[0,1]}\bigg\{v_{t}x-q_{1}\cdot\int_{\min\{\hat{y}_{t}/q_{1},\,1\}}^{\min\{1,(\hat{y}_{t}+x)/q_{1}\}}\phi\big((q_{1}/k)\,\eta\big)\,d\eta-

\displaystyle\qquad\mathbf{1}\{\hat{y}_{t}+x>q_{1}\}\cdot q_{2}\cdot\int_{\max\{0,\frac{\hat{y}_{t}-q_{1}}{q_{2}}\}}^{\min\{1,(\hat{y}_{t}+x-q_{1})_{+}/q_{2}\}}\phi\big((q_{1}/k)+(q_{2}/k)\,\eta\big)\,d\eta\bigg\}.

The first integral prices the portion of the allocation that lies within the first
q_{1}
units, using the curve
\eta\mapsto\phi\big((q_{1}/k)\eta\big)
. Once the cumulative allocation reaches
q_{1}
(i.e., when
\hat{y}_{t}\geq q_{1}
), this term becomes inactive. The second integral prices the spillover into the next
q_{2}
units using a shifted curve
\eta\mapsto\phi\big((q_{1}/k)+(q_{2}/k)\eta\big)
, which only contributes when the decision interval
(\hat{y}_{t},\hat{y}_{t}+x)
crosses the boundary at
q_{1}
. Moreover, the pricing of each segment of the items is normalized so that the fraction of that segment lies within the unit interval
[0,1]
. For example, for the first
q_{1}
units, the integration over the curve
\eta\mapsto\phi\big((q_{1}/k)\eta\big)
is normalized to the range
[0,1]
. Consequently, the design of the pricing functions in Theorem
1
is inspired by this structure—where the pricing for the first
q_{1}
units and the subsequent
q_{2}
units follows directly from the pricing functions used in utility-maximization formulation described above.

is defined as the general inverse of the

Proof Overview.

We first establish two key structural properties of Algorithm
1
.
The first is a monotonicity property concerning the number of items sold, and the second provides a lower bound on the utilization level of the algorithm across all realizations of the random seed
R
.
We then formulate a dual linear program whose optimal objective value serves as an upper bound on the offline optimal welfare.
Next, following a randomized online primal-dual (R-OPD) approach, we define dual variable updates as functions of the realized random seed and construct a candidate dual solution.
We then show that the dual constraints are
\alpha^{\star}
-feasible in expectation under these updates and that each buyer contributes at most
v_{t}
in expectation to the dual objective.
By weak duality, this implies that the algorithm achieves at least a

We now proceed with the detailed proof of Theorem

Proof.

Let us fix an input instance

kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
problem and prove Algorithm
1

\alpha^{\star}=1+\ln(U/L)
-competitive on this instance of the problem. Although Algorithm

In other words, the total number of sold units remains close to the maximum
\sum_{i=1}^{i^{*}}q_{i}
achieved across all realizations of the random seed.
This observation is made precise through the following two properties.

Monotonic Utilization.

We establish a monotonicity property for the number of units sold by Algorithm

as a function of the realized random seed

Specifically, we show that the utilization of the algorithm by the arrival of the

This monotonicity arises from the fact that the pricing profiles posted by the algorithm become progressively more aggressive as the random seed

Lemma 1

For any

The proof of above lemma can be found in Appendix

Thus, following the above lemma, we can see that

Given the constraint on the reservation vector

Lemma 2

If

under the reservation-vector constraint

The proof of above lemma can be found in Appendix
B

Consider the following dual linear program (LP), which upper bounds the offline optimum:

\displaystyle\min_{{u_{t}},{\lambda_{j}}}\quad\sum_{t\in[T]}u_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta+1}\lambda_{j}\cdot q_{j}\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad v_{t}\leq u_{t}+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\Delta+1}\lambda_{j}\cdot q_{j},\quad\forall t\in[T].

(6)

It can be verified that the optimal objective value of this LP provides an upper bound on the performance of the offline clairvoyant algorithm.
Following the economic interpretation of the randomized primal-dual framework presented in
[

]

as the

Following the R-OPD framework, we construct, for each realization of the random seed

The final dual variables are then defined as their expectations over the random seed such that

Initialize all dual variables to zero. Then for a realization of the random seed

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}\phi_{i}(r),&i\in\{1,2,\dots,i^{*}-1\},\\[2.0pt]
\phi_{i}(r),&i=i^{*},\,r\in[0,\,r^{*}],\\[2.0pt]
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

Furthermore, if buyer

\displaystyle u_{t}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}v_{t}-\phi_{i}(r),&\text{if }i<i^{*}\text{ or }(i=i^{*}\text{ and }r\leq r^{*}),\\[2.0pt]
v_{t},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

The update in Eq. (

4.1.1
Proof of Proposition
3
: A Risk-Sensitive R-OPD Approach

The proof uses a modified version of the R-OPD method. The central idea of R-OPD for problems that aim to maximize the expected reward is to specify, for each sample path of the randomized algorithm, an update rule for the dual variables of the LP as a function of the algorithm’s decisions along that path. These path-wise dual updates are chosen so that the dual objective accumulated on each realized path is equal to the social welfare achieved by the online algorithm on that same path.

Key Idea of Risk-Sensitive R-OPD.

In the
kSelection-
(\delta,\Delta)
problem, where the objective is to maximize the
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
of the algorithm’s social welfare, the standard R-OPD update scheme is modified.
Specifically, dual updates are performed only along the worst
\delta
-fraction of sample paths—those realizations of the algorithm’s randomization for which the objective value is minimized and smaller than that of all other sample paths.
Each sample path of Algorithm
1
corresponds to a particular realization of the random seed
R

Once
R
is realized as
R=r
, the algorithm’s performance
\texttt{ALG}^{(r)}
becomes deterministic.
Accordingly, to adapt the R-OPD approach for establishing the
\textsf{CVaR}_{\delta}\textsf{-CR}
-competitiveness of Algorithm
1

R=r
that belong to the set
\mathcal{S}
.
When the function

(\delta,\Delta)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\big\{\,r\in[0,1]\;\big|\;F_{\texttt{ALG}(I)}(\texttt{ALG}^{(r)})\leq\delta\,\big\},

where
\delta\in(0,1)

However, if this function is not continuous, we must carefully handle potential discontinuities of the CDF around the tail probability
\delta

In that case, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\big\{\,r\in[0,\tilde{r}]\;\big|\;F_{\texttt{ALG}(I)}\left((\texttt{ALG}^{(r)})^{-}\right)\leq\delta\,\big\},

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{r}=\inf_{r\in[0,1]}\left\{\int_{x=0}^{r}\mathbf{1}_{\big\{\,F_{\texttt{ALG}(I)}\left((\texttt{ALG}^{(x)})^{-}\right)\leq\delta\,\big\}}\,dx\geq\delta\right\}.

Revisiting Theorem

In this section, we revisit the pricing design for
cPPM
-
\boldsymbol{\phi}
given in Theorem

\Delta

\{1,2,\dots,k-1\}

So far, we have analyzed the case of

that achieves the optimal
\textsf{CVaR}_{\delta}\textsf{-CR}

We then examined two extreme cases: in the first, where

\Delta=k-1

The construction of pricing functions at each level follows a system of delayed differential equations, where each function includes a delayed term that depends on the tail probability

In what follows, we present the intuition behind the design of these pricing functions.

Intuition of Theorem

.

Let
r^{*}
,
i^{*}
, and

denote the instance-dependent parameters that characterize the performance of
cPPM
-
\boldsymbol{\phi}

Following the pricing design in Theorem

Under these properties, the algorithm fully allocates all units up to the

-th price level for all realizations of the random seed within the interval

.
Additionally, using the lower bound from Lemma

, we know that for any realization of the random seed

(i^{*}-1)
-th price level. These units are allocated at the corresponding prices

These two properties together allow us to construct a nontrivial lower bound on the algorithm’s revenue across all sample paths.
Because the performance of
cPPM

\boldsymbol{\phi}

Combining these insights yields a system of delayed differential equations, parameterized by

Crucially, the dual updates are applied only to the worst

Proof Sketch of Theorem
4
.

The proof generally follows the risk-sensitive R-OPD framework used in the proof of

3
, adapted to the CVaR objective. We first utilize the dual LP established in the proof of Theorem
1
to upper-bound
\texttt{OPT}(I)
.
Within the risk-sensitive R-OPD, we then define update rules for the dual variables only on the
\delta
-worst fraction of outcomes of
the algorithm: let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of seeds

corresponding to the
lower
\delta
-tail of
\texttt{ALG}^{(R)}(I)
, as in the proof of

and Eq. (

For

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}\frac{\phi_{i}(r)}{2\delta},&i<i^{*}\text{ or }(i=i^{*},r\leq r^{*}),\\

Furthermore, for any buyer

under the random seed

\displaystyle u_{t}^{(r)}=\begin{cases}\frac{v_{t}-\phi_{i}(r)/2}{\delta},&i<i^{*}\text{ or }(i=i^{*},r\leq r^{*}),\\
\frac{v_{t}}{\delta},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Using the monotonicity and lower-bound lemmas, one checks that for every

\sum_{t}u_{t}^{(r)}+\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}^{(r)}q_{j}=\texttt{ALG}^{(r)}(I).

\boldsymbol{\phi}

5
Conclusion

We investigated the design of posted-price mechanisms (PPMs) for online adversarial selection subject to dual constraints: a limited number of price changes (denoted by
\Delta
) and a risk-sensitive
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
objective, where
\delta
captures the tail risk. Our primary contribution is the development of
cPPM
-
\boldsymbol{\phi}
, a novel correlated posted-pricing scheme that simultaneously manages both the limited price changes and the tail risk by utilizing a single random seed to correlate posted prices. Our theoretical analysis provides performance guarantees for this mechanism and formally establishes the inherent trade-off between the number of allowable price updates, the degree of risk sensitivity, and the worst-case competitive performance.

Several open problems remain for future research. One direction is to perform a tighter analysis of the
\textsf{CVaR}_{\delta}\textsf{-CR}
competitiveness of
cPPM
-
\boldsymbol{\phi}
in the most general case of the problem; we conjecture that the
\textsf{CVaR}_{\delta}\textsf{-CR}
induced by the pricing design in Theorem
4
can be improved to establish optimality. Another promising direction involves extending this risk-sensitive, price-change-constrained framework to other fundamental online problems, such as online matching and combinatorial auctions. Furthermore, exploring alternative correlation/rounding schemes or other classes of risk-sensitive objectives could yield further insights. Finally, an important practical extension is to study these problems in a data-driven setting, where buyer value distributions are unknown and must be learned over time.

Since

Appendix G
Proof of Theorem

We first derive the closed-form expression of the pricing function
\phi
using the design specified in Proposition
3
for an arbitrary value of
\alpha
, and in particular for the smallest possible value according to that design which is
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
.
We then apply Proposition
2
to establish that
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
serves as a lower bound on the performance of any online algorithm.

Closed-form Design of
\phi
for the Upper-Bound
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
.

We begin by defining the parameters
\tau=1-\delta,\;c=\frac{\alpha}{\delta}
. Consider the function
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, defined implicitly based on Proposition
3
as follows:

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\begin{cases}L,&x\in[0,b],\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\int_{0}^{x-\tau}\phi(\eta)d\eta,&x\in[b,1],\end{cases}

where the breakpoint
b=1-\delta+\frac{\delta}{\alpha}
.

For
x\in[b,1]
, the function
\phi
satisfies the following delayed differential equation (DDE):

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\phi(x-\tau)=c\phi(x-\tau).

(14)

This is a linear DDE with constant delay
\tau
and constant history
\phi(x)=L
for all
x\leq b
.
We use the method of steps to derive an explicit closed-form expression for
\phi(x)
. Define the
delay exponential function
as

\displaystyle E_{c}(t)=1+\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor}\frac{c^{j}}{j!}(t-j\tau)^{j},\qquad(t\geq 0).

Given the constant history
\phi=L
on
(-\infty,b]
, the unique solution of Eq. (
14
) on
[b,1]
is

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\phi(b)E_{c}(x-b)=LE_{c}(x-b).

Expanding
E_{c}
yields a finite, piecewise-polynomial form:

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\begin{cases}L,&0\leq x\leq b,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle L\left[1+c(x-b)+\frac{c^{2}}{2!}\big(x-b-\tau\big)_{+}^{2}+\cdots+\frac{c^{n}}{n!}\big(x-b-(n-1)\tau\big)_{+}^{n}\right],&b\leq x\leq 1,\end{cases}

where
(u)_{+}=\max\{u,0\}
and
n=\Big\lfloor\frac{x-b}{\tau}\Big\rfloor+1
, ensuring the summation is finite for every
x
.

To determine the optimal value of
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
for which a feasible design of the pricing function
\phi
exists according to the construction in Proposition
3
,
we impose the boundary condition
\phi(1)\geq U
.
Hence,
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
is defined as the smallest value of
\alpha
satisfying this condition.
By substituting
x=1
into the expression for
\phi(x)
, and noting that the right-hand side of the equation is monotonically increasing in
\alpha
,
we obtain that
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
is the solution to the following equation:

\displaystyle\phi(1)=L\left[1+\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\frac{\delta(1-1/\alpha)}{1-\delta}\right\rfloor}\frac{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\delta}\right)^{j}}{j!}\big(\delta(1-\frac{1}{\alpha})-j(1-\delta)\big)^{j}\right]=U.

Thus,
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
is the smallest value of
\alpha
that satisfies the above equation.

Lower Bound on
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
.

Based on Proposition
2
and the definition of the function
\psi
, we can, without loss of generality, assume that
\psi
is strictly increasing.
Using a change of variables, we obtain the following constraint on the design of
\psi^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\nu=0}^{\delta-1+\psi(v)}\Big[\psi^{-1}(\nu)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\geq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}+L\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\leq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}\Big]d\nu\geq\frac{v}{\alpha},\qquad\forall v\in[L,U].

For a given
\alpha
, if there exists a feasible design of
\psi
that satisfies the above inequality, then there must exist a corresponding inverse function
\psi^{-1}
.
Without loss of generality, we can also assume that
\psi^{-1}(1)=U
.
Rewriting the above constraints yields:

\displaystyle\int_{\nu=0}^{\delta-1+x}\Big[\psi^{-1}(\nu)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\geq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}+L\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\leq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}\Big]d\nu\geq\frac{\psi(x)}{\alpha},\qquad\forall x\in[\psi(L),1].

(15)

From this, we can upper-bound
\psi(1)
as:

\displaystyle U=\psi(1)\leq\alpha\cdot\int_{\nu=0}^{\delta}\Big[\psi^{-1}(\nu)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\geq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}+L\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\leq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}\Big]d\nu,

which leads to the following lower bound on
\alpha
:

\displaystyle\frac{U}{\int_{\nu=0}^{\delta}\Big[\psi^{-1}(\nu)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\geq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}+L\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\leq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}\Big]d\nu}\leq\alpha.

(16)

Computing the above lower bound requires knowledge of
\psi^{-1}
within the range
[0,\delta]
.
By substituting the upper bound implied by the constraint in Eq. (
15
), for the values of
\psi^{-1}
, we can derive the lower bound. Thus, let us consider the
\psi^{*-1}
function for which the inequalities in Eq. (
15
) are tight. Thus, the design for
\psi^{*-1}
is as follows:

\displaystyle\psi^{*-1}(x)=\begin{cases}L,&x\in[0,1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor],\\[4.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\nu=0}^{\delta}\Big[\psi^{*-1}(\nu)\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\geq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}+L\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\nu\leq 1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor\}\Big]d\nu,&x\in[1-\delta+\delta\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor,1].\end{cases}

Constructing
\psi^{*-1}
according to the above and substituting into Eq. (
16
) yields a lower bound on
\alpha
achievable by any online algorithm.
It can be verified that this design of
\psi^{*-1}
is equivalent to the pricing function
\phi
characterized in Proposition
3
, and the resulting expression in Eq. (
16
) produces the a lower-bound value equal to
\alpha_{\delta}^{\textsf{SP}}
following the same procedure as before.

Proof of Proposition

In the following, we will prove that for values of
\alpha\geq 1+\ln(\frac{U}{L})
, according to the pricing design in Theorem
4
, we will have
\phi_{k}(1)\geq U
, and thus there exists a design that obtains
1+\ln(\frac{U}{L})
-
\mathsf{CVaR}_{\delta}
competitive. Following, the well-established lower-bound

, the proof of above theorem follows.

Let us set
m=\lfloor\frac{k}{\alpha}\rfloor
and for
i\geq m+2
define

\displaystyle S_{i}:=\sum_{r=1}^{i}\int_{0}^{\delta}\phi_{r}(\eta)d\eta,\qquad b_{i}=\phi_{i}(0),\qquad c_{i}=\phi_{i}(1).

Evaluating
\phi_{i}
at
x=0
and
x=1
gives

b_{i}=\frac{\alpha}{k\delta}S_{i-1},\qquad c_{i}=\frac{\alpha}{k\delta}S_{i}\qquad(i\geq m+2),

(17)

and since
\phi_{r}=L
for
1\leq r\leq m
, we have the base mass

S_{m+1}\ \geq\ \sum_{r=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{\delta}Ld\eta\ =\ L\cdot m\delta.

(18)

We claim each
\phi_{i}
is nondecreasing on
[0,1]
. This is clear for
i\leq m
; for
i\geq m+1
it follows by induction: if all
\phi_{r}
with
r<i
are nondecreasing, then in case
x+\delta\leq 1
, then we will have:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{i}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{k\cdot\delta}\cdot\left(\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}\phi_{r}(x+\delta)-\phi_{r}(x)\right)>0,

where in above the inequality follows from the induction hypothesis and thus
\phi_{i}(x)
for

is nondecreasing. Furthermore, for
x+\delta>1
, we will have:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{i}(x)=\frac{\alpha}{k\cdot\delta}\cdot\left(\sum_{r=1}^{i}\phi_{r}(x+\delta-1)-\sum_{r=1}^{i-1}\phi_{r}(x)\right)>0,

where the inequality follows from the fact that
\phi_{r}(1)=\phi_{r+1}(0)
and the induction hypothesis.
Thus,
\phi_{i}(x)

If

is nondecreasing, then for every

,

\int_{x}^{\min\{1,x+\delta\}}g(\eta)d\eta\ +\ \int_{0}^{\max\{0,\delta-1+x\}}g(\eta)d\eta\ \ \geq\ \ \int_{0}^{\delta}g(\eta)d\eta.

(19)
