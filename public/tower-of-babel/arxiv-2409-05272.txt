Decomposition of global solutions for a class of nonlinear wave equations
Georgios Mavrogiannis, Avy Soffer, Xiaoxu Wu

Abstract
. In the present paper we consider global solutions of a class of non-linear wave equations of the form □ ​ u = N ⁡ ( x , t , u ) ​ u , \Box u=N(x,t,u)u, where the nonlinearity N ⁡ ( x , t , u ) ​ u N(x,t,u)u is assumed to satisfy appropriate boundedness assumptions. Under these appropriate assumptions we prove that the free channel wave operator exists. Moreover, if the interaction term N ⁡ ( x , t , u ) ​ u N(x,t,u)u is localised, then we prove that the global solution of the full nonlinear equation can be decomposed into a ‘free’ part and a ‘localised’ part.

1.1.
Background and the aim of this paper

We are considering Nonlinear wave equations (WE) with general interaction terms of the form (
1.1
). We allow such interactions to be both linear and nonlinear, and may explicitly depend on space and time.

The interest and importance of wave equations is well known, and have many applications in Physics and Geometry. The classical examples are vibrations of strings and membranes, light waves and acoustic wave propagation as well as the dynamics of space-time itself, as in General Relativity. We are particularly interested in looking for the properties of solutions of the wave equation (
1.1
), with general interactions. This analysis is relevant to the analysis of
systems of equations
.

The aim of this work is to study the large time behavior of global solutions of wave equations with general interactions, under several general assumptions on the nonlinearity and the global behavior of the solution. In particular if we assume that the interaction is localized around the origin, we will prove that global solutions of equations of the form (
1.1
) break, as time goes to infinity, to a part that evolves like a solution of the free wave equation, and a (weakly) localized part around the support of the interaction.

This is known as the key step in Scattering theory, leading to the property of Asymptotic Completeness (AC) which, in laypersons terms, is the generic statement that all asymptotic states of systems are composed of a set of subsystems with known (simpler) dynamics, who are moving independently as time goes to infinity.

Under our assumptions, we show that the free wave part separates from the rest of the solution for a large class of nonlinearities. When the number of such subsystems is more than one then this corresponds to a multi-channel scattering problem. To rigorously formulate multichannel scattering problems one needs to use the notion of Channel wave operators, see e.g.
[
4
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
21
,
20
,
22
]
and cited references. The multichannel scattering problem is intrinsic when the interaction terms can create localized solutions, and allow free waves at the same time. For this, the interaction terms should have attractive forces (focusing). Note that in the present paper we construct the free channel wave operator, which can be seen as the first step of studying a multi-channel scattering problem. See also
[
23
]
for a proof of dispersive estimates for Schrödinger equations with time-quasi-periodic potentials, utilizing the concept of channel wave operators.

In the case when the solution scatters to a free wave only (one channel case), a lot of results are known, going back to early results of Morawetz
[
12
]
and Strauss
[
24
]
in the early 1960’s. The development of Strichartz estimates
[
25
]
then enables to get a fairly complete and general understanding of one channel problems. That includes small data cases as well as defocusing non-linear terms. See also
[
5
]
for the application of Fredholm analysis to time-dependent Schrödinger equations.

1.2.
Discussion on our results

In both our Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
, we prove the existence of the free channel wave operator. In particular, we do not need radial symmetry assumptions in order to construct the free channel. The assumption of spherical symmetry or localization of the interaction terms is used only to control the localization properties of the non-free part. Clearly, with this kind of generality, one cannot expect to have the standard a-priori estimates. The approach is then to prove directly the existence of a free wave at infinity that separates from the rest of the solution. This is done by proving that, in a suitably defined Hilbert space, as time goes to infinity the solution converges in the strong topology to a free wave plus an asymptotically orthogonal part.
To achieve this, we project the full solution on a subspace of the phase-space (we microlocalize) where only the free solution can concentrate.

In our Theorem
2.1
we in fact prove more, by assuming an appropriate localization property for the nonlinearity. In particular, we prove that the solution can be decomposed to a free part and a localized part.

Throughout this paper,
C
will denote a constant and may vary from one line to another. We write
\lesssim
or
\gtrsim
whenever
A\leq CB
or
CA\geq B
for some constant
C>0
. We write
A\lesssim_{a}B
or
A\gtrsim_{a}B
if
A\leq C_{a}B
or
C_{a}A\geq B
for some constant
C_{a}>0
which depends on parameter
a
.

To elaborate further, we prove that the localized part is localized in a weighted integrated sense:

\|\langle x\rangle^{\delta}\nabla u_{lc}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\langle x\rangle^{\delta}\partial_{t}u_{lc}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C,

for some positive
\delta
and
C
. Specifically, see already inequality (
2.5
) of Theorem
2.1
.

1.3.
Comparison with relevant work

It is instructive to compare our results to the works on wave equations with general initial data in
\dot{H}^{1}
in the energy critical case.

Kenig and Merle pioneered the study of the wave equation with energy-critical nonlinearity in
[
11
]
. Building on this foundation, a series of subsequent works, including significant collaborations with Collot and Duyckaerts
[
2
,
1
,
3
]
for dimensions 6, 3, and all odd spatial dimensions, as well as references cited in
[
1
]
, have advanced the field. Complementing these efforts, the insightful work by Jendrej and Lawrie
[
9
]
in dimensions 4 and higher has played a key role in fully resolving the asymptotic dynamics of the wave equation with an energy-critical interaction term. Together, these contributions cover all dimensions 3 and above for spherically symmetric initial data in
\dot{H}^{1}
.

It is shown that any solution of the equation that does not blowup in a finite time, converges (in
\dot{H}^{1}
) to a free wave plus possibly the (unique) ground state soliton of the equations and plus self similar solutions, all of which with envelope given by the ground state soliton. The blowup solutions are also self-similar, with the same soliton serving as their envelope. Here, we adopt a generalized notion of self-similarity: a function is considered self-similar if it can be expressed as a function of
x/t^{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\neq 0
, or as a function of
x/\lambda(t)
, where
\lambda(t)
is a real-valued function. A similar progress was achieved in the study of the wave map dynamics, see e.g.
[
8
,
7
]

and also in the case of potential perturbations of the energy critical wave equation
[
10
]
.

In contrast, we consider the wave equation (
1.1
) with initial data in
\dot{H}^{1}
but with a general type of interaction term, which satisfies several assumptions (that differ from the assumptions of the papers mentioned above). Our class of allowed interactions is defined in terms of abstract properties, and allows for space and time dependence of the interaction. Yet, our assumptions preclude some cases, in particular the energy critical case.
In Theorem
2.1
we prove that any global solution of equation (
1.1
) converges in
\dot{H}^{1}
to a sum of a free wave and a
localized in space
solution, under our assumptions. However, we do not prove that these localized solutions are time independent. Any such solution, if it is time independent, has to be a soliton. In contrast, in the works mentioned above, on the critical case, a subtle theorem is proved that excludes the possibility of time dependent localized solutions (so-called breathers) without the presence of a free wave.
Proving/disproving the existence of breathers in higher dimensions for general type of equations is a major, fundamental problem which is still open.

Furthermore, our notion of blow-up is qualitative; it only means that the
\dot{H}^{1}
semi-norm of the solution grows to infinity with time (at a sufficiently fast rate). If the localized part is time independent (as is conjectured for NLWEs in higher dimensions), then this localized part is a soliton (a solution of the time independent equation).

3.1.
The free wave equation

In the following lemma we taylor already known results in the language of the present paper.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\vec{u}_{0}(t)=(u_{0}(t),\dot{u}_{0}(t))
be a solution to the free wave equation

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\Box u_{0}(t)=0\\
\vec{u}_{0}(0)=\vec{u}(0)=(u(x,0),\dot{u}(x,0))\in\mathcal{H}\end{cases},\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then, the following hold

(a)

Let
\alpha
and
\sigma

2.1
and

(3.3)

F_{1}(|p|,t):=F_{1}(t^{\alpha/2}|p|\geq 1)F_{1}(|p|\leq t^{\alpha/2}).

There exists a constant

such that we have the following decay estimate for the solution
u_{0}

\displaystyle\|\langle x\rangle^{-\sigma}F_{1}(|p|,t)|p|u_{0}(t+s)\|^{2}+\|\langle x\rangle^{-\sigma}F_{1}(|p|,t)\dot{u}_{0}(t+s)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{C}{|t|^{2\sigma}}\left(\|\langle x\rangle^{\sigma}|p|u_{0}(s)\|^{2}+\|\langle x\rangle^{\sigma}\dot{u}_{0}(s)\|^{2}\right)

for all
|t|\geq 1
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

Let
\alpha
and
F_{\alpha}(x,p,t)
be as in Theorem
2.2
. If

and
\dot{u}(\cdot,0)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\|F_{\alpha}(x,p,t)|p|u_{0}(-t)\|\leq\frac{C\log_{2}|t|}{|t|^{(n-1)/2-(n+1/2)\alpha}}\|\dot{u}(x,0)\|_{L^{1}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\|F_{\alpha}(x,p,t)\dot{u}_{0}(-t)\|\leq\frac{C\log_{2}|t|}{|t|^{(n-1)/2-(n+1/2)\alpha}}\|\dot{u}(x,0)\|_{L^{1}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

for all
|t|\geq 2
.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that (
3.4
) is equivalent to the following statement

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|\begin{pmatrix}|p|^{-1}\langle x\rangle^{-\sigma}|p|F_{1}(|p|,t)&0\\

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{C\log_{2}|t|}{|t|^{(n-1)/2-(n+1/2)\alpha}}\sup\limits_{s\geq 0}\|N(u(s))u(s)\|_{L^{1}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\in L^{1}_{t}[2,\infty).

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

We begin with the proof of part (a). It suffices to check the case when
s=0
. Let
H_{0}=-\Delta_{x}
. Then, we obtain that

and
\dot{u}_{0}(t)
can be represented as

(3.9)

\displaystyle u_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\cos(t\sqrt{H_{0}})u(0)+\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{H_{0}})}{\sqrt{H_{0}}}\dot{u}(0)

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sin(t\sqrt{H_{0}})\sqrt{H_{0}}u(0)+\cos(t\sqrt{H_{0}})\dot{u}(0),

(3.12)

\partial_{t}[\vec{u}_{0}(t)]=-A_{0}\vec{u}_{0}(t).

We solve for
\vec{u}_{0}(t)

(3.13)

\vec{u}_{0}(t)=e^{-tA_{0}}\vec{u}(0),

where the evolution operator

(3.15)

(3.18)

).

3.2.
Duhamel formulas

We outline some fundamental properties of the solutions to the perturbed wave equation.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(u(t),\dot{u}(t))
be a solution to the perturbed wave equation

(3.29)

\begin{cases}\Box u(t)=-V(x,t)u(t)\\
\vec{u}(0)=(u(x,0),\dot{u}(x,0))\in\mathcal{H}\end{cases},\quad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}.

Then, the solution
\vec{u}(t)
can be represented by the following formula:

(3.30)

\vec{u}(t)=U_{0}(t,0)\vec{u}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}U_{0}(t,s)\mathcal{V}(x,s)\vec{u}(s)ds,

where the matrix
\mathcal{V}(x,t)
is defined by

(3.31)

\mathcal{V}(x,t):=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
V(x,t)&0\end{pmatrix},

U_{0}(t,0)=e^{-tA_{0}}
and
U_{0}(t,s)=U_{0}(t-s,0)
.

Proof.

We can reformulate Equation (
3.29
) as follows

(3.32)

\partial_{t}[\vec{u}(t)]=-(A_{0}+\mathcal{V}(x,t))\vec{u}(t),

or equivalently as follows

(3.33)

\partial_{t}[U(t,0)\vec{u}(0)]=-(A_{0}+\mathcal{V}(x,t))U(t,0)\vec{u}(0).

Utilizing Equations (
3.14
) and (
3.33
), we can derive Duhamel’s formula for
\vec{u}(t)
:

(3.34)

\displaystyle\vec{u}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{0}(t,0)\vec{u}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}U_{0}(t,s)\mathcal{V}(x,s)\vec{u}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}u(t)\\
\dot{u}(t)\end{pmatrix}.

∎

Similarly, using Equation (
3.34
), we derive Duhamel’s formula for
\Omega(t)^{*}\vec{u}(0)\equiv U_{0}(0,t)\vec{u}(t)
:

(3.35)

\displaystyle\Omega(t)^{*}\vec{u}(0)

\displaystyle=\vec{u}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}U_{0}(0,s)\mathcal{V}(x,s)\vec{u}(s)ds.

Consequently, with
\Omega(t)^{*}\vec{u}(0)\equiv(u_{\Omega}(t),\dot{u}_{\Omega}(t))
, the expressions for
u_{\Omega}(t)
and
\dot{u}_{\Omega}(t)
are given by:

(3.36)

\displaystyle u_{\Omega}(t)

\displaystyle=u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin{(s\sqrt{H_{0}})}}{\sqrt{H_{0}}}V(s)u(s)ds,

(3.37)

\displaystyle\dot{u}_{\Omega}(t)

\displaystyle=\dot{u}(0)-\int_{0}^{t}\cos(s\sqrt{H_{0}})V(s)u(s)ds.

Preliminary Commutator estimate

We have the following commutator estimate.

Lemma 4.1
.

Define
F_{1}^{l}(k):=\frac{d^{l}F_{1}}{dk^{l}},
where
l=0,1
. For
t\geq 1
,
\alpha>0
and
\alpha>\beta

(4.1)

\|[F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1),F_{1}^{(l)}(t^{\beta}|p|>1)]\|\lesssim_{n}\frac{1}{t^{\alpha-\beta}}.

In this proof, we write
F_{c}=F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)
and
F_{1}^{(l)}=F_{1}^{(l)}(t^{\beta}|p|>1)
for simplicity. We use the Fourier transform and write
[F_{c},F_{1}^{(l)}]
as follows

(4.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int d^{n}\xi\hat{F}_{1}^{(l)}(\xi)e^{it^{b}p\cdot\xi}\times\left[e^{-it^{\beta}p\cdot\xi}F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)e^{it^{\beta}p\cdot\xi}-F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int d^{n}\xi\hat{F}_{1}^{(l)}(\xi)e^{it^{\beta}p\cdot\xi}(F_{c}(\frac{|x-t^{\beta}\xi|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)-F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)).

(4.3)

\dfrac{\left|F_{c}(\frac{|x-t^{\beta}\xi|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)-F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)\right|}{t^{\beta-\alpha}|\xi|}\lesssim\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}|F_{c}^{\prime}(|x|\leq 1)|\lesssim 1,

we can deduce that for each

we obtain

(4.4)

\|[F_{c}(\frac{|x|}{t^{\alpha}}\leq 1)),F_{1}(t^{\beta}|p|>1)]\psi\|\leq\frac{1}{t^{\alpha-\beta}}\int d^{n}\xi|\hat{F}_{1}^{(l)}(\xi)|\xi|\|\psi(x)\|\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{\alpha-\beta}}\|\psi\|,

∎

4.2.
Definitions of (Relative) Propagation observables

We define the following propagation observables, inspired by
[
21
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

(
Propagation observable
)
Given a class of matrix operators
\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
with

(4.5)

B(t)=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}(t)&0\\
0&B_{2}(t)\end{pmatrix},

we define the time-dependent scalar product as follows

(4.6)

\displaystyle\langle B(t),\vec{u}(t)\rangle_{t}:=(|p|u_{1}(t),B_{1}(t)|p|u_{1}(t))_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+(u_{2}(t),B_{2}(t)u_{2}(t))_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where
\vec{u}(t)
denotes the solution to (
1.1
). The family
\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
is termed a Propagation Observable if it satisfies the following condition: For a family of self-adjoint operators
B(t)
, the time derivative satisfies: there exists
L\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
such that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle B(t),\vec{u}(t)\rangle_{t}=\pm\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}(\vec{u}(t),C_{l}^{*}(t)C_{l}(t)\vec{u}(t))_{\mathcal{H}}+g(t)

\displaystyle g(t)\in L^{1}_{t}[1,\infty),\quad C_{l}^{*}(t)C_{l}(t)\geq 0,\quad l=1,\cdots,L.

Integrating (
4.7
) over time, we derive the
Propagation Estimate
:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|C_{l}(t)\vec{u}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\pm\langle B(t),\vec{u}(t)\rangle_{t}|_{t=t_{0}}^{t=T}\mp\int_{t_{0}}^{T}g(s)ds

(4.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\geq t_{0}}\left|\langle B(t),\vec{u}(t)\rangle_{t}\right|+C_{g},

where
C_{g}:=\|g(t)\|_{L^{1}_{t}[1,\infty)}.

Second, we define the relative propagation observables.

Definition 4.2
.

(
Relative Propagation observables
)
Consider a class of matrix operators
\{\tilde{B}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
with

(4.9)

\tilde{B}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{B}_{1}(t)&0\\

(4.10)

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{B}:\vec{v}(t)\rangle_{t}:=(|p|v_{1}(t),\tilde{B}_{1}(t)|p|v_{1}(t))_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+(v_{2}(t),\tilde{B}_{2}(t)v_{2}(t))_{L^{2}_{x}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

\vec{v}(t)
is not necessarily the solution to (

(4.13)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\|C_{l}(t)\vec{v}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\sup\limits_{t\geq t_{0}}\left|\langle\tilde{B}(t):\vec{v}(t)\rangle_{t}\right|+C_{g},

.

as
