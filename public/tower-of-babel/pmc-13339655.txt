Gait Alterations Due to DCC Gene Variants in Individuals with Congenital Mirror Movements
Authors: Nok‐Yeung Law, Shanie Desrosiers, Sinthiya Sivanesan, Helga Tonkov, Frédéric Charron, Frédéric Bretzner, Myriam Srour, Dorothy Barthélemy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital Mirror Movement Syndrome (CMMS) involves involuntary movements on one side of the body while voluntary movements are performed on the other side. They disrupt left–right coordination and can be caused by a pathogenic variant in the DCC gene. CMMS have been extensively studied in the upper limb, but its impact on lower limb during gait in humans is unclear.ObjectivesDetermine the impact of CMMS on locomotor patterns in individuals with a DCC pathogenic variant.MethodsThe gait pattern of seven individuals with a DCC pathogenic variant and ten healthy controls was assessed using 3D motion capture (kinematics), electromyographic recordings (EMG) of the soleus (SOL) and tibialis anterior (TA) muscles and foot pressure distribution during comfortable walking, fast walking, and running.ResultsIn comparison to controls, the DCC group exhibited prolonged soleus (SOL) EMG activity and overlapping left–right SOL activation at a comfortable walking speed. No change was noted in TA. Both groups demonstrated increased pressure at the toe during the push‐off phase; however, individuals with the most severe CMMS also displayed an early stance‐phase pressure peak, reflecting contralateral push‐off. These features diminished at higher walking speeds. Kinematic analysis revealed delayed knee flexion and prolonged double‐limb support in the DCC group. Group differences were more pronounced when comparing control participants to DCC individuals with observable CMMS (Wood and Tauber scale).Conclusions DCC variants impair left–right coordination, particularly affecting distal extensor muscle timing during gait. These disruptions are most evident during slow walking and correlate with mirror movement severity.

Mirror movements (MMs) are involuntary movements that occur simultaneously on one side of the body while voluntary movements are performed on the contralateral side. 1 MMs can be observed physiologically in children before the maturation of the central nervous system 2 or pathologically in patients with movement disorders, such as dystonia, 3 Parkinson's disease, 4 and essential tremor. 5 MMs are also associated with congenital pathologies of the nervous system, such as Klippel–Feil syndrome, 6 Kallmann's syndrome, 7 and congenital hemiplegia. 8 Additionally, MM can also occur in individuals without other significant neurological abnormalities, either sporadically or through autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance, in a condition known as Congenital Mirror Movement Syndrome (CMMS). 9

In one‐third of CMMS cases, genetic etiology is identified, with heterozygous pathogenic variants in the DCC, RAD51, NTN1, or ARHGEF7 genes, all of which play essential roles in the normal midline decussation of axons from ipsilateral neurons to their contralateral targets.1, 10, 11, 12, 13 While the impact of CMMS has been extensively studied in the upper limb, research on its effects on the lower limb has not been investigated. Given that walking requires bilateral coordination, MMs could potentially alter walking patterns.

Locomotion is enabled through the locomotor central pattern generator (CPG), a neuronal network in the spinal cord responsible for the left–right alternation and flexor‐extensor coordination of limbs during walking. 14 The commissural interneurons in the spinal cord, as part of this CPG, enable communication between ipsilateral and contralateral neurons across the midline; and supraspinal command, including from the descending corticospinal tract, can modulate its output. 15 Although these neural networks may be altered by DCC gene mutations, little is known about locomotor control in individuals with the pathogenic DCC variant.

It has been demonstrated that Dcc and its ligand, Netrin‐1, are crucial for the normal development of afferent connections within the dorsal spinal cord. At birth, Dcc and Netrin‐1 facilitate midline guidance and play an essential role in axon pathfinding and neuronal migration. 14 In mice with null mutations in Netrin‐1 or Dcc, V0 commissural interneurons in the spinal cord are significantly reduced; however, V3 commissural interneurons remain largely unaffected in Netrin‐1 mutants. 14 Consequently, the disorganization of coupling between the left and right limbs could result from the reduction of V0 and V3 commissural interneurons. Animal studies showed that the loss of lateralization due to Dcc gene mutations leads to altered gait patterns in mice, such as hopping gait. 16 Adult mice with selective Dcc mutations in spinal interneurons exhibit a hopping gait. When required to increase walking speed, these mice struggle to lengthen their strides and instead compensate by increasing their stride frequency, highlighting that their hopping gait stems from defects in the local spinal cord. Although Dcc heterozygous mutant mice do not show overt bilateral impairments, they exhibit subtle motor deficits, such as decreased occurrence of out‐of‐phase walking and increased duty cycle during the stance phase at slow speeds. 17

Despite interspecies differences and the absence of hopping gait in humans with a pathogenic DCC gene variant, the spinal and supraspinal mechanisms controlling basic locomotion are comparable between humans and rodents. Therefore, the DCC pathogenic variant could impact human gait, akin to observations in Dcc heterozygous mice.17, 18 In the current study, we aimed to characterize the gait patterns of individuals with CMMS walking at different speeds. We hypothesized that disturbances in left–right coordination, as reported in animal models, could be observed, and that MM might modify temporal gait parameters.

Materials and Methods

The experimental protocol was approved by the local ethics committee, the Centre de Recherche Interdisciplinaire en réadaptation du Montréal métropolitain (CRIR), and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Oral and written information about the experiment were given to the participants, who provided their written consent to participate in the study.

Participants

Sixteen individuals were included in this study: seven related individuals with a heterozygous pathogenic nonsense variant (NM_005215.3: c.4009C>T; p.Arg1337*) in DCC individuals (age: 36.9 ± 20.8 years; mean ± SD) and ten control individuals (CTRL; age: 36.8 ± 15.3 years; mean ± SD). The characteristics of the DCC and CTRL individuals are summarized in Table S1–S3. All individuals completed three questionnaires to assess lateral dominance: the Waterloo Footedness Questionnaire, 19 the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, 20 and the Laterality Preference Inventory. 21 These tests confirmed laterality in both hands and feet. Additionally, a clinical assessment was performed in DCC individuals by a neurologist to determine the presence and score severity of MMs using the Woods and Teuber Scale. The scores are presented in Table S2. Individuals with other neurophysiological or orthopedic pathologies that could alter gait patterns were excluded from the study.

Task: Walking on a Treadmill

Walking was assessed on a split‐belt treadmill (Force Link BV, TechnoGym, Capelle aan den Ijssel, The Netherlands) with belts matched at the same speed. After 1–2 min of practice, individuals walked at three speeds: comfortable, fast, and running. To assess potential order effects, early and late strides within each speed condition were compared, and no systematic drift in gait metrics was observed. Comfortable Speed was set by increasing the speed in 0.2 km/h increments until it felt too fast, then lowering by 0.1 km/h to a comfortable pace, followed by 5 min of walking. Fast speed was set by increasing the treadmill speed from comfortable walking until running occurred, then reducing it by 0.1 km/h to maintain walking, followed by 1 minute of walking (50 s for DCC #6). Running Speed was reached by further increasing speed and maintained for 1 min. A safety harness (Maine Anti‐Gravity Systems) was available; only DCC #1 used it. EMG, pressure sensors, and kinematic analysis were used for data collection.

Foot Pressure Data Acquisition

Pressure sensors connected to a DMC7/10 Pedestrian contact box were taped to the soles of individuals’ running shoes under the first metatarsophalangeal joint and the heel of the right foot (or of both feet). The pressure sensors, less than 1 mm thick, helped to identify toe‐off and heel contact, delineating the gait cycle phases.

EMG Data Acquisition

Surface EMG was used to record the activity of the tibialis anterior (TA) and soleus (SOL) muscles in the left and right lower limbs. 6 Discoidal Ag | Ag‐Cl surface electrodes (1 cm2, inter‐electrode distance of 2 cm) were placed according to SENIAM guidelines. 22 Signals were amplified (1000–5000), bandpass filtered (10–1000 Hz), and digitized and sampled (2 kHz) on a computer using a Micro1401 interface (Spike and Signal software; Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK).

Parameters of EMG Analysis

EMG signals from TA and SOL were rectified and analyzed using a custom MATLAB script. An analysis target of 60 gait cycles per condition was set; if fewer were available, the full recording was used. Data from DCC individual #6 during running was excluded due to lack of heel strikes, preventing gait cycle synchronization. This forefoot running pattern can also occur in healthy individuals 23 and is not specific to DCC variants. The following outcome measures were collected:

(A) Gait cycle duration was defined as the time between two successive right heel contacts, which can vary with speed, anthropometrics, sex, and age. 24 (B) For each muscle, EMG burst duration was calculated per gait cycle. Burst onset was when EMG activity exceeded baseline (over 200 ms) by two standard deviations for at least 30 ms,25, 26 and offset was when it returned to baseline. Duration (in ms) 7 was also normalized by gait cycle duration to allow comparisons between individuals. (C) Bilateral SOL EMG overlap was quantified during late stance phases by measuring: (1) the gap between right SOL offset and left SOL onset, and (2) the gap between left SOL offset and right SOL onset. (D) Coupling between left and right SOL activations was determined by the difference between their burst onsets. A coupling of 0.5 indicates right SOL onset at 0% and left SOL onset at 50% of the cycle. (E) Left–right SOL symmetry was assessed by the ratio of right to left SOL EMG burst durations, with 1 indicating perfect symmetry.

Kinematics Data Acquisition and Analysis

Three‐dimensional kinematic data during treadmill walking were recorded using the Vicon Motion System Ltd. (Vicon Motion Systems Limited, Oxford, UK). Participant anthropometric measurements were entered into Vicon Nexus software (Version 2.11.0). Thirty‐nine Vicon reflective markers (14.0 mm diameter) were placed on the body following the Plug‐In‐Gait Full‐Body model (Fig. 1). 27 Ten 100 Hz infrared cameras (Vicon Bonita 10, Vicon Motion Systems Ltd, Oxford, UK) and two Bonita video cameras (Vicon Bonita 720c, Vicon motion Systems Ltd, Oxford, UK) recorded the gait. Custom Python and MATLAB scripts were used to analyze the following kinematic parameters:

Experimental setup. Participants walked on a treadmill with reflective markers for full‐body motion capture. Kinematic and EMG data, heel contact, and toe‐off events were recorded. An avatar was generated to analyze hip, knee, and ankle movements. Kinematic markers were placed on the following anatomical landmarks: back of the head (1), front of the head (2), cervical vertebra C7 (3), clavicle (4), thoracic vertebra T10 (5), sternum (6), shoulder (7), upper arm (8), elbow (9), forearm (10), wrists (11, 12), finger (13), anterior superior iliac spine (14), thigh (15), knee (16), tibia (17), ankle (18), heel (19), and toe (20).

(A) The Range of motion of the joints included the calculation of the total angular excursion, maximum and minimum amplitudes, and time of occurrence during a walking cycle for hip, knee, and ankle joints in the sagittal plane. 24 (B) The Temporal parameters were determined using left and right heel strike and toe‐off data from pressure sensors. Stance and swing phase durations, double‐limb support periods, and walking cadence were calculated. (C) Stride length was calculated by multiplying the swing duration by the treadmill speed.28, 29, 30, 31

Statistical Analysis

Group means ± standard errors were calculated for the DCC and CTRL groups. EMG and kinematic parameters were compared using Student's t tests or Mann–Whitney tests, with Welch's t test applied when variances were unequal. For joint angle and timing variables, independent‐samples t‐tests compared DCC and CTRL groups separately for the right and left legs and paired‐samples t‐tests assessed left–right leg differences within each group. Speed effects across comfortable walking, fast walking, and running were evaluated using repeated‐measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. To examine potential differences between DCC individuals with and without MM, the two DCC participants without clinically observable MM were removed, and group comparisons between DCC individuals with MM and CTRLs were performed using t‐tests or non‐parametric tests as appropriate. A post hoc power analysis was conducted in G*Power using effect sizes (Cohen's d) and group sample sizes (DCC: n = 7; CTRL: n = 10), based on key independent‐samples t‐tests. For variables such as left SOL activity duration and end of right stance overlap, Cohen's d was 2.42, corresponding to a power estimate greater than 0.99. Additionally, sensitivity analyses using repeated‐measures ANCOVA with body mass as a covariate ensured that differences in body mass did not influence the outcomes. These analyses confirmed that the main effects of group remained robust. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v29.0.2.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) with significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Electromyographic (EMG) Analysis

Characterization of the Gait Pattern at a Comfortable Speed in DCC Individuals

We compared the locomotor pattern at a comfortable speed for representative control (CTRL) and DCC individuals (Fig. 2A,B). Two main differences were observed: First, overlap in EMG signals of left and right SOL was observed at the end of the left and right foot stance phases in the representative DCC individual, whereas no EMG overlap was observed in the CTRL participant. Second, only one pressure peak was observed in the sensor placed underneath the toes at the end of the stance phase (see R toe signal) in the CTRL. That peak corresponds to the push‐off phase when force is exerted on the forefoot before the swing phase. 32 In the representative DCC individual presented in Figure 2B, this peak was also present but was preceded by another pressure peak in the early stance. Thus, the toes exerted two distinct pressure peaks on the ground during the stance phase in this DCC individual.

Gait pattern at comfortable speed in CTRL and DCC individuals. Panels show heel/toe pressure and SOL EMG in representative individuals (A–B), modeled group data (C–D), and bilateral SOL overlap during late stance phases of the right foot (E) or the left foot (F). In panel D, illustrating heel/toe pressure and SOL EMG activation duration, R toe* refers to subjects with double bursts: the first (early) peak (red) and the second (later) peak (black) of the two toe pressure peaks are observed in some participants (n = 2). R toe refers to subjects with only the primary burst, excluding those with double bursts. Negative values in panels E and F, represent absence of EMG activity.

The two main differences in locomotor patterns were consistently observed across the group data. Figure 2C,D represents the modeled graphs of the average gait pattern of CTRL and DCC individuals, respectively. First, DCC individuals showed an EMG overlap in bilateral SOL, which was observed at the end of the right foot stance phase for a duration of 9.2 ± 5.8% of the gait cycle. In contrast, no overlap in SOL EMG activation was observed in CTRL, and rather an absence of EMG activation in either left or right SOL was observed for a duration of −5.8 ± 7.7% of the gait cycle (P < 0.001; Fig. 2E; the negative value indicates the absence of EMG activity in either left or right muscle). A similar difference was observed at the end of the left foot stance phase (Fig. 2F). No overlap between left and right TA EMG activation was observed in DCC individuals.

Second, two subpopulations within the DCC individuals could be identified: during the stance phase, the majority of DCC participants (5/7) had only one pressure peak on the toe sensor, as observed in the CTRL group, whereas two of the seven DCC individuals had two distinct peaks. These two individuals had the highest scores on the Woods and Teuber Scale (Table S2), reflecting more severe MMs. The first peak of pressure occurred at 6.3 ± 0.6% of the gait cycle for a duration of 22.5 ± 9.2% of the cycle and mirrored the actual push‐off occurring simultaneously in the contralateral leg. The second peak in toe pressure corresponded to the expected “push‐off” at the end of the stance phase. No difference was observed between the CTRL and DCC groups regarding the timing of pressure exerted on the heel at the onset of the stance (P = 0.905). Characterization of participants revealed a significant difference in weight between the DCC and CTRL groups (Table S1). To determine whether the gait analysis results could be influenced by body weight, a repeated‐measures ANCOVA with body weight as a covariate was ran and showed that the second peak in toe pressure remained unchanged. This indicated that the observed group pattern was not influenced by anthropometric differences.

Overlap in Activation of Left and Right SOL Results from Prolonged SOL EMG Burst

The average duration of the right SOL activity of the DCC group (57.8 ± 8.4% of the gait cycle; expressed in percentage of gait cycle; Fig. 3A,B) was significantly different from that of the CTRL group (47.2 ± 0.4%; P = 0.008). The mean duration of left SOL activity was also longer in the DCC group (59.1 ± 5.5% of the gait cycle) than in the CTRL group (43.0 ± 7.6%; P < 0.001).

Analysis of SOL EMG bursts. Duration of right (A) and left (B) SOL activity, average gait cycle duration (C), coupling of SOLs (D), and symmetry of SOL activity duration (E) were compared between the DCC and CTRL groups. * = P < 0.05.

No Differences in Cycle Duration, Left–Right Alternation, or Symmetry between Groups

For individuals walking at comfortable walking speeds ranging between 3.0 and 4.0 km/h, the mean gait cycle duration of DCC individuals was not significantly different from CTRL individuals (P = 0.596; Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the coupling of left versus right SOL activation was similar between groups (P = 0.114, Fig. 3D), and no statistical difference was observed in the symmetry of left and right SOL duration between the DCC and CTRL group (P = 0.072; Fig. 3E).

Effect of Speed on the Gait Characteristics of DCC Individuals

While no overlap in bilateral SOL activation was observed in CTRLs (Fig. 4A–C), the overlap present at a comfortable speed in DCC individuals decreased at fast walking speeds and disappeared when running (Fig. 4D–F). The double peak in toe pressure observed in two of the seven DCC individuals at a comfortable speed was also present at a fast speed but was absent during running (Fig. 4D and 4E vs. 4F). Figure 5 A‐B shows that at the comfortable walking speed, the duration of left and right SOL overlap was significantly higher for the DCC group compared to the CTRL group, and this was also observed at fast walking speed at the end of the right stance phase. For running, both groups showed an absence of overlap at the end of the left and right stance phase. This absence of overlap was longer in CTRL than in DCC at the end of the left stance phase. More specifically, a significant decrease in SOL overlap duration was observed between the comfortable speed and running (P = 0.015), and fast speed and running (P = 0.039). After controlling for weight/BMI, ANCOVA results indicated that the group difference in SOL overlap remained significant (P = 0.012, η2p = .487), showing that the increased SOL overlap in the DCC group was not attributable to body‐mass differences.

End of right (A) and left (B) stance phase duration, right (C) and left (D) SOL activity duration, and symmetry of SOL activity duration (E) are shown for CTRL and DCC groups. *P < 0.05 compared between DCC and CTRL groups across walking and running speeds. * = P < 0.05.

Effect of Speed on SOL EMG Duration and Left–Right Symmetry

In the DCC group, normalized SOL EMG duration significantly decreased with increasing speed (Right P = 0.03; Left P = 0.005; Fig. 5C,D). Specifically, decreases were observed between comfortable speed and running (Right P = 0.05; Left P = 0.008) and between fast speed and running (only in Left leg P = 0.019; Right P = 0.088), but not between comfortable and fast speeds (both P = 1.000). In CTRLs, SOL EMG duration also decreased, but was statistically significant only in the right leg (P < 0.001), mainly between fast speed and running (P = 0.002) and between comfortable speed and running (P = 0.003). Across all walking conditions, SOL EMG duration was significantly longer in DCC than CTRL group at comfortable (Right P = 0.008; Left P = 0.001) and fast speeds (Right P = 0.015; Left P = 0.011), and for the right SOL during running (P = 0.021). Speed increases did not affect SOL symmetry in either group (P = 0.092; Fig. 5E).

Kinematic Analysis

The kinematic analysis revealed subtle changes in the gait pattern that corresponded with observations from the EMG analysis. No change in the angular excursion of the right hip, knee, or ankle was observed between the DCC and CTRL groups (Fig. 6; Table S4–S6). During the swing phase, maximum knee flexion occurred later in the gait cycle for the DCC group compared to the CTRL group (Fig. 6K, P = 0.008). This observation is consistent with a prolonged duration of SOL EMG and a prolonged period of double‐limb support (Fig. 7A) in the DCC group when walking at a comfortable speed (DCC individuals: 17.4 ± 3.7% of the gait cycle; CTRL individuals: 13.3 ± 2.7%; P = 0.036). After controlling for weight/BMI, ANCOVA results confirmed that the group difference in knee‐flexion timing remained significant (P = 0.011, η2p = .426), indicating that the delayed knee flexion observed in the DCC group was independent of body mass.

Temporal gait characteristics at different speeds. Duration of double‐limb support (A), stance (B), swing (C), cadence (D), and stride length (E) during walking and running in DCC and CTRL groups. Error bars: SD. P < 0.05.

Effect of Speed on Phase Duration, Cadence, and Stride Length

As gait speed increased, stance phase duration decreased, and swing phase duration increased in both DCC and CTRL individuals (Fig. 7B,C; Table S7 and S8). In CTRLs and DCC individuals, cadence significantly increased across all speeds (all P < 0.001; Fig. 7D; Table S9). Within each group, stride length differs significantly across walking speeds (repeated‐measures ANOVA: CTRL, P < 0.001; DCC, P = 0.002), with all pairwise speed comparisons reaching significance in both groups (CTRL: P ≤ 0.003; DCC: P ≤ 0.014; Fig. 7E). Interestingly, DCC individuals had shorter strides than CTRLs at fast speed (Right: DCC = 0.52 ± 0.08 m vs CTRL = 0.63 ± 0.08 m; P = 0.026).

Difference between DCC Individuals with and without MM

Of the seven DCC individuals tested, five exhibited MM per the Woods Teuber Scale (Table S2), while two scored 0/4. Although no formal statistics were run on the two without MM, their key outcomes were compared (Table S10). This approach was intended to provide a clearer illustration of gait differences attributable specifically to clinically manifest MM. Bilateral SOL EMG overlap was higher in DCC participants with MM (mean 10.6%) than in those without MM (6.7% and 4.8% for each participant), whereas CTRLs showed no overlap (−5.8%). Similarly, maximum knee flexion during swing was delayed in DCC individuals with MM (75% of the gait cycle) compared to 71% and 69% in the two DCC individuals without MM and 70% in CTRLs. Given these differences, the two DCC individuals without MM were excluded of the following subgroup analysis, and comparisons focused on DCC individuals with MM (DCC‐MM) versus CTRL.

At comfortable speed, key differences noted in the EMG, foot pressure and kinematic analysis persisted, but the duration of double limb support became non‐significant in the DCC‐MM group (P = 0.036 → 0.066). Apart from this, new differences emerged: longer stance phase and shorter swing phase in DCC‐MM were observed (P = 0.028 each), and stride length became significantly shorter in DCC‐MM during running (P = 0.018). Overall, DCC individuals with MM showed more gait differences compared to CTRLs.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of DCC pathogenic variants on lower limb locomotor patterns. Our results show that individuals with a DCC variant exhibit distinct gait changes: (1) overlap in left and right SOL activation at comfortable and fast speeds due to prolonged EMG bursts; (2) two toe pressure peaks during stance (early and late) in individuals with severe MM, versus only a late, single pressure peak in CTRLs and less‐affected DCC individuals. The earlier pressure peak mirrored the push‐off phase of the contralateral limb; (3) prolonged double‐limb support delaying maximal knee flexion during swing; (4) stride length was shorter in DCC individuals at fast speed. Importantly, after adjusting for weight/BMI, the group differences in SOL overlap, toe‐pressure profiles, and knee‐flexion timing remained significant, indicating that these effects were robust to anthropometric variation. These changes were maintained when comparing control with only the DCC individuals that displayed MM (DCC‐MM), except for double‐limb support that became no longer significant. Additional findings in the DCC‐MM group include (5) longer stance and shorter swing phases at comfortable speed, and shorter stride length at both fast speed and running. Overall, although these differences are significant, they are small, aligning with the relatively “normal” gait appearance of DCC individuals. Furthermore, variability in the gait characteristics across DCC individuals is likely resulting from changes in the penetrance of the pathogenic DCC variant. To note, distal EMG and plantar pressure were recorded, but proximal muscle activity and medio‐lateral control were not assessed; thus, no conclusion can be reached on these aspects.

Similarities and Differences Observed in Animal Models

Prolonged SOL EMG activation contributed to overlapping activation and likely increased double‐limb support. Such prolonged extensor activity and double‐limb support have been previously reported in aging adults and in patients at high risk of falling, who tend to walk more slowly to enhance stability.33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39 However, in DCC individuals, prolonged SOL activation occurred on a wide range of speeds, suggesting neurologic changes rather than balance deficits. In support to that, mouse studies have previously shown that loss of Dcc in the spinal cord alone induces hopping gait and reduces the number of commissural interneurons coordinating left and right limbs in Dcc sc mice. 16 Although there is no information about the number of commissural interneurons in heterozygous Dcc +/− mutant mice, they also exhibit an increased stance duty cycle without altered left–right coupling, 17 as DCC individuals.

Another difference lies in the preferred strategy to increase gait speed. When increasing gait speed, two strategies are mainly used: increasing step length and increasing cadence.29, 31, 40, 41 DCC individuals used increased stride length when transitioning from comfortable to faster speeds and to running, similarly as what is observed in CTRLs. This is different from Dcc sc mice as increasing cadence was the preferred strategy due to the hopping gait that prevented increasing stride length. 16 In contrast, in both heterozygous Dcc mice 17 and DCC individuals the bilateral coupling was not altered. Mechanisms underlying these gait changes have been identified in mice, notably a considerable reduction in commissural axons crossing the midline to reach contralateral targets in Dcc sc mice. 16 While similar mechanisms could potentially occur in DCC individuals, direct evidence is lacking, and further studies are needed to explore this possibility.

Limits

The DCC/pMM group comprises related individuals carrying the same nonsense variant (p.Arg1337*), whereas controls are unrelated. Shared familial traits could influence gait independent of genotype and may contribute to some of the observed group differences. 24 Future studies should include participants from multiple families to preclude this potential confounding factor and better identify MM gait markers. Although our study focused on adult locomotion; assessing young DCC mutation carriers could reveal different impairments before developmental compensation.

In this study, a secondary, exploratory analysis excluded two DCC carriers without clinically observable mirror movements (0/4 on the Wood and Teuber scale) to compare DCC‐MM participants versus controls. This was done to illustrate gait differences specifically associated with clinically manifest MM, as including asymptomatic carriers could dilute group effects. While this approach clarifies the biomechanical impact of MM, it reduces generalizability and may overstate group differences. Interpretation of these subgroup results should therefore be cautious, as the study's primary aim was to assess the influence of familial DCC mutations on gait. EMG recordings were limited to distal muscles (TA, SOL), and plantar pressure was measured only under the great toe and heel. Hence, proximal (hip) contributions cannot be inferred from the available dataset. Future work could include EMG and kinematic analyses of the proximal and intermediate leg joints to provide a more comprehensive understanding of intra‐ and inter‐limb coordination. Given the small sample size, post‐hoc power analyses indicated that the study was underpowered to detect small effects, although it was adequately powered to identify medium‐to‐large effects observed in the main outcomes.

Individuals with a pathogenic variant in the DCC gene showed discrete gait alterations, specifically overlapping left and right SOL activation and prolonged double‐limb support, both contributing to delayed knee flexion during swing. These functional changes could reflect neuroanatomical alternations, as observed in mutant mice, but may also result from developmental compensation. Further investigations will be necessary to elucidate these functional changes.
