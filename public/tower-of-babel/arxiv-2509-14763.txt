Large-order perturbation theory of linear eigenvalue problems
S. Jonathan Chapman

Abstract
We consider a class of linear eigenvalue problems depending on a small parameter ϵ \epsilon in which the series expansion for the eigenvalue in powers of ϵ \epsilon is divergent. We develop a new technique to determine the precise nature of this divergence. We illustrate the technique through its application to four examples: the anharmonic oscillator, a simplified model of equitorially-trapped Rossby waves, and two simplified models based on quasinormal modes of Reissner-Normström de Sitterblack holes.



Keywords

Exponential asymptotics; Stokes
phenomenon; divergent expansion; WKB theory; quantum mechanics.

1
Introduction

Linear eigenvalue problems of the form

L_{\epsilon}g=\lambda g,

where
L_{\epsilon}
is a differential operator (depending on a parameter

\epsilon>0
), are ubiquitous in applied mathematics and
theoretical physics. The eigenvalue
\lambda
might correspond to an energy
level, the frequency of a normal mode of oscillation, or the growth
rate in a linear stability analysis.
Often
\epsilon
is a small parameter, in which case it
is common to develop the perturbation series for the eigenvalue

\lambda
in powers of
\epsilon
,

\lambda\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\lambda_{n}.

Sometimes this series diverges, and it is
of interest to determine the nature of this divergence. Here we provide a
straightforward approach to determining the precise asymptotic
behaviour of
\lambda_{n}
for large
n
.

One of the earliest examples of such a problem is the quantum
anharmonic oscillator
[
2
,
3
]
, which we revisit in
Section
3
. This problem has a long history; it
was the first
non-exactly-solvable problem tackled by the newly-written Schrodinger
equation in 1926, has practical applications ranging
from quantum chemistry and atomic-molecular physics to crystal
lattice vibrations in solid-state theory, and serves as a simple
model for quantum field theory
[
11
]
.
The seminal work by Bender and Wu
[
2
,
3
]
established
nature of the divergence of the perturbation series for the ground
state energy. This work became the prototype for similar analyses in
many other quantum mechanical systems, in what is now known as
large-order perturbation theory
[
1
,
8
]
.

The main technique of Bender and Wu is to analytically continue in

\epsilon
(typically until
\epsilon
is negative), and then solve the
resulting problem by
combination of Liouville-Green (WKB) and matched asymptotic approximations. This is a
delicate procedure, since the goal is to identify an exponentially
small component of
\lambda
beyond-all-orders of the divergent asymptotic
series. Cauchy’s integral formula is then used to determine the
coefficients in the power series expansion of
\lambda
on the positive real

\epsilon
axis in terms of the values of

\lambda
on either side of the negative real
\epsilon
axis. This technique
is ingenious, and has proved successful, but the details can be very
complicated. The present work aims to present a simpler alternative
method, which we hope will be useful.

In the applied mathematics literature an early example of such a
problem occurs in the work of Boyd and Natarov
[
4
]
, who
consider a model problem for an equitorially-trapped Rossby wave in a
shear flow in the ocean or atmosphere. There the main interest is in
the imaginary part of the eigenvalue (corresponding to the growth rate
of instability)—the divergent perturbation series is purely real,
but there is an exponentially small imaginary part beyond all orders.
In
[
10
]
this problem is attacked in almost the reverse
direction to Bender and Wu—the divergent series is first found, and
used to determine the exponentially small imaginary component of the
eigenvalue via optimal truncation and Stokes phenomenon, rather than
the other way round. Simplifying and extending
the procedure from

[
10
]
forms the basis of the present work.

We present our procedure through its application to four examples.
Each follows the same general framework, which we hope will allow the
interested reader to adapt the method to their own particular problem,
but the final part of the analysis differs slightly in each case.

2
Example 1: Simplified black holes

We consider the model problem

2(1-\epsilon x)(-\omega g+xg^{\prime})+g+(xg^{\prime})^{\prime}=0,\qquad-\infty<x<0,

(1)

with

g(0)=1
and
g(x)=o(\mathrm{e}^{-x})
as
x\rightarrow-\infty
, where
0<\epsilon\ll 1
. This is a much-simplified version of the problem in
[
7
]
concerning
quasinormal modes of Reissner-Normström de Sitter black holes; the
eigenvalue
\omega
is the frequency of the mode.

As
x\rightarrow-\infty
the two possible behaviours are

g(x)\sim\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon x^{2}},\qquad g(x)\sim x^{\omega},

while as
x\rightarrow 0
the two possible behaviours are

g(x)\sim 1,\qquad g(x)\sim\log x.

The boundary conditions at
x=0
and
x=-\infty
each remove one
degree of freedom, so that there is a nonzero solution only if

\omega
takes particular values. The goal is to find the asymptotic
expansion of the leading eigenvalue,

\omega\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\omega_{n},

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, and in particular the form of the divergence of

\omega_{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

2.1
Inner region

We start with

2(1-\epsilon x)(-\omega g+xg^{\prime})+g+(xg^{\prime})^{\prime}=0.

We expand

g=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}g_{n},\qquad\omega=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\omega_{n},

(2)

to give at leading order

2(-\omega_{0}g_{0}+xg_{0}^{\prime})+g_{0}+(xg_{0}^{\prime})^{\prime}=0.

The solution which is regular at the origin is

g_{0}=L_{\omega_{0}-1/2}(-2x),

where
L_{n}(z)
is the Laguerre function.
To avoid exponential growth as
x\rightarrow-\infty
we need the Laguerre function to be a polynomial, i.e. we need
\omega_{0}-1/2
to be a non-negative integer.
Choosing the first of these,
n=0
, gives the solution
g_{0}=1
,
\omega_{0}=1/2
.
At next order

2xg_{1}^{\prime}+(xg_{1}^{\prime})^{\prime}-2\omega_{1}=-x.

The solution which is regular at
x=0
and does not grow exponentially at minus infinity is

g_{1}=-\frac{x}{2},\qquad\omega_{1}=-\frac{1}{4}.

In general

2xg_{n}^{\prime}+(xg_{n}^{\prime})^{\prime}-2\omega_{n}=-2x(\omega_{n-1}g_{0}+\cdots+\omega_{0}g_{n-1}-xg_{n-1}^{\prime})+2(\omega_{n-1}g_{1}+\cdots\omega_{1}g_{n-1}),

(3)

and the solution is of the form

g_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ni}x^{i},

with

\displaystyle 2ja_{n,j}+(j+1)^{2}a_{n,j+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{k=j-1}^{n-1}\omega_{n-1-k}a_{k,j-1}+2(j-1)a_{n-1,j-1}+2\sum_{k=j}^{n-1}\omega_{n-k}a_{k,j},

(4)

\displaystyle\mbox{ }\hskip 113.81102pt\qquad\mbox{ for }j=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle a_{n1}-2\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(5)

We can iterate to find
\omega_{n}
numerically. Figure
1
(a)
shows
|\omega_{n}|^{1/n}
as a function of
n
; the linear growth in
n
is consistent with
factorial growth in
\omega_{n}
at large
n
.
In principle we could extract the asymptotic behaviour as
n\rightarrow\infty
from (
4
)-(
5
), but this is not so
straightforward.
The method we now highlight determines
\omega_{n}
for large
n

without the need to analyse (
4
)-(
5
).

2.2
Outer region

The expansion (
2
) is not uniform in
x
—it rearranges
when
x
is large. In this section we develop the corresponding
expansion valid for large
x
.

To this end we set
\epsilon x=X
to give

2(1-X)(-\omega g+Xg^{\prime})+g+\epsilon(Xg^{\prime})^{\prime}=0.

Now expanding

g=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}g_{n}

(6)

gives, at leading order,

2(1-X)(-\omega_{0}g_{0}+Xg_{0}^{\prime})+g_{0}=0,

so that

g_{0}=B(1-X)^{1/2}X^{\omega_{0}-1/2},

for some constant
B
.
For there to be no singularity at
X=0
we require
\omega_{0}=1/2
, in
agreement with §
2.1
.
To match with the inner expansion as
X\rightarrow 0
we require
g_{0}\rightarrow 1
so that
B=1
.
In general, equating coefficients of
\epsilon^{n}
,

X(g_{n}+2(1-X)g_{n}^{\prime})=-(Xg_{n-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}+2(1-X)(\omega_{1}g_{n-1}+\cdots+\omega_{n}g_{0}).

(7)

We need to determine the late terms in the expansion, that is, the
behaviour of
g_{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
There are two sources of divergence in
g_{n}
: the usual
factorial/power divergence driven by differentiating
g_{n-1}
, and a
factorial/constant divergence driven by
\omega_{n}
.
For the first, we follow the usual procedure
[
5
]
by
supposing that

g_{n}\sim\frac{G\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{\chi^{n+\gamma}}

(8)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
G
and
\chi
are functions of
x
and

\gamma
is constant.
Then, equating coefficients of powers of
n
gives, at leading order,

\chi^{\prime}=2(1-X).

Since this divergence is driven by the singularity in
g_{0}
at
X=1
,
we have
\chi(1)=0
,
so that

\chi=-(1-X)^{2}.

At next order we find

(2-5X)G+2X(1-X)G^{\prime}=0,

giving

G=\frac{\Lambda}{X(1-X)^{3/2}},

for some constant
\Lambda
.
Thus (absorbing
(-1)^{-\gamma}
into
\Lambda
) this part of
g_{n}
satisfies

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{X(1-X)^{3/2}(1-X)^{2n+2\gamma}}.

As
X\rightarrow 1
,

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{(1-X)^{3/2}(1-X)^{2n+2\gamma}}.

Comparing powers of
1-X
with
g_{0}
gives

-\frac{3}{2}-2\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\gamma=-1,

so that

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n-1)}{X(1-X)^{2n-1/2}}.

(9)

The other part of
g_{n}
, driven by the divergence of
\omega_{n}
, is
given by
g_{n}\sim Q\omega_{n}
where

X(Q+2(1-X)Q^{\prime})=2(1-X)g_{0}=2(1-X)^{3/2},

so that

Q=(1-X)^{1/2}(\log X+C).

The presence of
\log X
here means we need to modify slightly the ansatz
g_{n}\sim Q\omega_{n}
(essentially we need
C
to include a term proportional to
\log n
). If we set instead
g_{n}=(Q_{0}\log n+Q_{1})\omega_{n}
then

\displaystyle X(Q_{0}-2(1-X)Q_{0}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle X(Q_{1}-2(1-X)Q_{1}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(1-X)^{3/2},

so that

Q_{0}\log n+Q_{1}=(1-X)^{1/2}\left(C_{0}\log n+C_{1}+\log X\right).

Putting the two parts of
g_{n}
together gives

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n-1)}{X(1-X)^{2n-1/2}}+(1-X)^{1/2}(C_{0}\log n+C_{1}+\log X)\omega_{n}.

(10)

To determine
\Lambda
we need to match with an inner region in the
vicinity of the singularity at
X=1
.

2.3
Inner region near
X=1

Motivated by both
g_{0}(X)=\sqrt{1-X}
and by (
9
) we set
X=1-\epsilon^{1/2}\hat{x}
,
g=\epsilon^{1/4}\hat{g}
to give

(1-2\hat{x}\epsilon^{1/2}\omega)\hat{g}+(2\hat{x}(-1+\epsilon^{1/2}\hat{x})-\epsilon^{1/2})\hat{g}^{\prime}+(1-\epsilon^{1/2}\hat{x})\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}=0.

(11)

In terms of the inner variable

\displaystyle g_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\epsilon^{1/4}\hat{x}^{1/2},

(12)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{n}g_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{1/4}\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n-1)}{\hat{x}^{2n-1/2}}.

(13)

At leading order in (
11
),

\hat{g}_{0}-2\hat{x}\hat{g}_{0}^{\prime}+\hat{g}_{0}^{\prime\prime}=0.

Writing

\hat{g}_{0}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{1/2-2n},

we find

c_{n}=-\frac{(2n-5/2)(2n-3/2)c_{n-1}}{4n},\qquad c_{0}=1,

where the latter equation comes from matching with (
12
).
Thus

c_{n}=-(-1)^{n}\frac{(3/4)_{n-1}(5/4)_{n-1}}{16(2)_{n-1}}.

Matching with (
13
) gives

\Lambda=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}c_{n}}{\Gamma(n-1)}=-\frac{1}{16\Gamma(3/4)\Gamma(5/4)}=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}\,\pi}.

2.4
Boundary layer in the late terms near
X=0

So far everything we have done has followed the standard approach to
finding the late terms of the expansion, as described in
[
5
]
, for example.
In this section we make one crucial observation, which extends this
standard approach, and allows us link the two parts of the expansion
in (
10
) and determine
\omega_{n}
.

This observation is that the large-
n
asymptotic approximation for
g_{n}
in
the outer region is non-uniform, and rearranges when
X
is small.
We can see this directly from the asymptotic behaviour (
9
), which is singular at

X=0
, while we know that
g_{n}
is in fact regular at
X=0
.

Thus there is another inner region near the origin, now not in the small-
\epsilon
expansion of
g
, but in the large-
n

expansion of
g_{n}
. To examine this inner region we rescale
X
by
setting
X=\xi/n
. Then the equation for
g_{n}
, equation (
7
),
becomes

\frac{\xi}{n}g_{n}+2\xi\left(1-\frac{\xi}{n}\right)g_{n}^{\prime}=-n\left(\xi g_{n-1}^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+2\left(1-\frac{\xi}{n}\right)(\omega_{1}g_{n-1}+\cdots+\omega_{n}g_{0}),

(14)

where
′
is now
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\xi
.
Writing
X=\xi/n
in (
10
), the inner limit of the outer is

\displaystyle g_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda(-1)^{n}n\Gamma(n-1)}{\xi(1-\xi/n)^{2n-1/2}}+(1-\xi/n)^{1/2}(C_{0}\log n+C_{1}+\log\xi/n)\omega_{n}

(15)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2\xi}}{\xi}+((C_{0}-1)\log n+C_{1}+\log\xi)\omega_{n}.

This motivates writing

\omega_{n}\sim\Omega(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n),\qquad g_{n}\sim H(\xi)\Omega(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n),

which, on substituting into (
14
), gives, at leading order,

-(\xi H^{\prime})^{\prime}+2\xi H^{\prime}=2.

Thus

H=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\Ei(2\xi)+\log\xi,

(16)

where

\Ei(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{z}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{t}}{t}\,\mathrm{d}t

is the exponential integal. Now
g_{n}
should be regular as
\xi\rightarrow 0
. Since

\Ei(2\xi)\sim\log\xi
as
\xi\rightarrow 0
,
we need

\alpha_{2}=-1

to remove the logarithmic singularity at
\xi=0
.
Since
\Ei(2\xi)
exhibits Stokes phenomenon for large
\xi
there
will be a switch in the behaviour of the late terms depending on the
argument of
\xi
—this is what is known as the higher-order Stokes
phenomenon, a Stokes phenemonon not in the asymptotic expansion of
g

as a function of
\epsilon
, but in the late-term approximation of

g_{n}

[
6
,
9
]
.
There is a higher-order Stokes line when
\xi
crosses the
positive real axis, across which the constant contribution to the
large-
\xi
approximation of
H
(i.e. in the outer limit of the inner
expansion) changes.
Note that there is no Stokes phonomenon associated with the particular
solution
\log\xi
, so that the coefficient of
\mathrm{e}^{2\xi}/\xi
is fixed. This will
not be the case in our other examples.

To complete the analysis and determine
\Omega
we need to match
(
16
) with (
15
).
As
\xi\rightarrow\infty

\Ei(2\xi)\sim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2\xi}}{2\xi}.

Matching with (
15
) gives
C_{0}=1
and
\Omega=-2\Lambda

so that

\omega_{n}\sim-2\Lambda(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n)=\frac{(-1)^{n}\Gamma(n)}{2\sqrt{2}\,\pi},

(17)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Much of the analysis follows the same framework, though the details of

Consider

3.2
Outer region

As before, the expansion (
19
) is not uniform in
x
—it rearranges
when
x
is large. In this section we develop the corresponding
expansion valid for large
x
.
We subtract off the leading-order eigenvalue by writing

\lambda=\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon\bar{\lambda}.

(25)

We rescale into the far field by setting
\epsilon^{1/2}x=X
to give

-\epsilon^{2}g^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon Xg^{\prime}+\frac{X^{4}g}{4}-\epsilon^{2}\bar{\lambda}g=0.

(26)

The solution this time is of Liouville-Green (WKB) form,

g=\mathrm{e}^{\phi/\epsilon}A,\qquad A\sim\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}A_{n}.

(27)

Substituting (
27
) into (
26
) and equating

\epsilon
gives, at leading order, the eikonal equation

-(\phi^{\prime})^{2}+X\phi^{\prime}+\frac{X^{4}}{4}=0.

The solution which decays at infinity is

\phi^{\prime}=\frac{X}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{1+X^{2}}\right)

so that

\phi=\frac{1}{6}+\frac{X^{2}}{4}-\frac{(1+X^{2})^{3/2}}{6}

\phi(0)=0
.
The amplitude equation is

X(1+X^{2})^{1/2}A^{\prime}+\left((1+X^{2})^{1/2}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(1+X^{2})^{1/2}}\right)A-\epsilon A^{\prime\prime}-\epsilon\bar{\lambda}A=0.

Expanding

\epsilon

A_{0}=\frac{B_{0}}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}},

.

X(1+X^{2})^{1/2}A_{n}^{\prime}+\left((1+X^{2})^{1/2}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(1+X^{2})^{1/2}}\right)A_{n}-A_{n-1}^{\prime\prime}\\
-\bar{\lambda}_{0}A_{n-1}-\bar{\lambda}_{1}A_{n-2}-\cdots-\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}A_{0}=0.

A_{n}
: the usual factorial/power
from repeated differentiation of the singularity in
A_{0}
, and a

\bar{\lambda}_{n}

For the first, we use the usual ansatz
[
5

A_{n}\sim\frac{G\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{\chi^{n+\gamma}},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-X(1+X^{2})^{1/2}G^{\prime}+\left(-(1+X^{2})^{1/2}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2(1+X^{2})^{1/2}}\right)G,

Thus this part of

A_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}}\frac{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{(1+X^{2})^{3(n+\gamma)/2}}.

A_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{(\pm 2\mathrm{i})^{1/4}(X\mp\mathrm{i})^{1/4}}\frac{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{(\pm 2\mathrm{i})^{3(n+\gamma)/2}(X\mp\mathrm{i})^{3(n+\gamma)/2}}.

\gamma=0

giving, finally

A_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}}\frac{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n)}{(1+X^{2})^{3n/2}}.

(29)

The other part of
A_{n}
satisfies

X(1+X^{2})^{1/2}A_{n}^{\prime}+\left((1+X^{2})^{1/2}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(1+X^{2})^{1/2}}\right)A_{n}\sim\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}A_{0},

giving

A_{n}\sim\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}}\left(C-\tanh^{-1}\sqrt{1+X^{2}}\right).

Again, the presence of a logarithm here
means we need to modify slightly the large
n
ansatz to allow for

\log n
terms, which is the same as allowing the constant
C
to
depend on
\log n
. Doing so gives

A_{n}=\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}}\left(C_{0}\log n+C_{1}-\tanh^{-1}\sqrt{1+X^{2}}\right).

Together

A_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}}\frac{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n)}{(1+X^{2})^{3n/2}}\\
+\frac{\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}}{(1+X^{2})^{1/4}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}}\left(C_{0}\log n+C_{1}-\tanh^{-1}\sqrt{1+X^{2}}\right).

(30)

To determine
\Lambda
we need to match with an inner region in the
vicinity of either
X=\mathrm{i}
or
X=-\mathrm{i}
.
This problem is slightly unusual in that there are two singularities
in the leading-order solution, but they each produce a late-term
behaviour with the same singulant
\chi
, so that there is only one
factorial/power divergence in the late terms.

3.3
Inner region near
X=\mathrm{i}

We set
X=\mathrm{i}-\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{2/3}\hat{x}/2
,
A=\epsilon^{-1/6}\hat{A}
to
give, at leading order,

-2\hat{x}^{1/2}\hat{A}^{\prime}-\frac{\hat{A}}{2\hat{x}^{1/2}}+4\hat{A}^{\prime\prime}=0.

To match with (
28
) requires

\hat{A}\sim\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\hat{x}^{1/4}}\qquad\mbox{ as }\hat{x}\rightarrow\infty.

(31)

Writing

\hat{A}=\sqrt{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{g_{n}}{\hat{x}^{1/4+3n/2}},

(32)

gives

g_{n}=-\frac{(6n-1)(6n-5)g_{n-1}}{12n},\qquad g_{0}=1,

where the latter condition comes from (
31
).
Thus

g_{n}=\frac{5(-1)^{n}3^{n-2}(7/6)_{n-1}(11/6)_{n-1}}{4(2)_{n-1}}.

(33)

The inner limit of (
29
) is

\epsilon^{n}A_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda}{\epsilon^{1/6}\hat{x}^{1/4}}\frac{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n)}{\hat{x}^{3n/2}}.

(34)

Matching (
34
) with (
32
) gives

\Lambda=\sqrt{2}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{g_{n}}{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n)}=\frac{5\sqrt{2}}{36\Gamma(7/6)\Gamma(11/6)}=\frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2}}.

3.4
Boundary layer in the late terms near
X=0

Here we come to the key step.
The large
n
asymptotic series for
A_{n}
in the outer region
is nonuniform, and rearranges when
X
is small—there is another inner region near the origin.
We emphasize again that this is a boundary layer not in the
small-
\epsilon
expansion of
A
, but in the large
n
-expansion of

A_{n}
. Again, this nonuniformity is evident because the asymptotic
formula (
30
) is singular at
X=0
, while
A_{n}
should be
regular there.
This time the appropriate scaling for the inner region is
X=\xi/n^{1/2}
(so that
XA_{n}^{\prime}
balances with
A_{n-1}^{\prime\prime}
), giving

\xi A_{n}^{\prime}+\frac{3\xi^{2}}{4n}A_{n}-nA_{n-1}^{\prime\prime}+\cdots-\bar{\lambda}_{0}A_{n-1}-\bar{\lambda}_{1}A_{n-2}-\cdots-\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}A_{0}=0.

(35)

As
X\rightarrow 0
in (
30
),

\epsilon^{n}A_{n}\sim\frac{\sqrt{2}\,\Lambda}{-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{X^{2}}}\frac{(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n)}{(1+X^{2})^{3n/2}}+\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}\left(C_{0}\log n+C_{1}\pm\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}-\log 2+\log X\right).

(36)

Note that there are two choices of branch to be made here—one for

\sqrt{X^{2}}
arising from
\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}
and one for the constant
\pm\mathrm{i}\pi/2
arising from

\tanh^{-1}\sqrt{1+X^{2}}
.
In particular note that when matching to find
\Lambda
we took
\sqrt{1-\sqrt{1+X^{2}}}
to be real and
positive when
X
approached
\pm\mathrm{i}
, which means we need
-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{X^{2}}
to be real and positive when
X
is on the imaginary axis;
in turn this means we need
\sqrt{X^{2}}=X
when
X
is positive
imaginary, and
\sqrt{X^{2}}=-X
when
X
is negative
imaginary.
We will return to this choice and the
position of the branch cuts shortly, when we match with the inner
solution.

With
X=\xi/n^{1/2}
, (
36
) is

\epsilon^{n}A_{n}\sim\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,\Lambda n^{1/2}(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-3\xi^{2}/2}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}}}+\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}\left((C_{0}-1/2)\log n+C_{1}\pm\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}-\log 2+\log\xi\right).

(37)

This motivates setting
A_{n}\sim H\Omega(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n+1/2)
,

\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}\sim\Omega(-1)^{n}3^{n}\Gamma(n+1/2)
. Using this
ansatz in (
35
) gives

3\xi H^{\prime}+H^{\prime\prime}-3=0,

so that

H=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\int_{0}^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-3u^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}u+3\int_{0}^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{-3t^{2}/2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{3u^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}t.

(38)

Both the particular integral and the complementary function exhibit Stokes’ phenomenon for large
\xi
, so that there
will be a switch in the behaviour of the late terms depending on the
argument of
\xi
, corresponding to the higher-order Stokes’ phenomenon.
There is a higher-order Stokes line due to the particular integral when
\xi
crosses the
real axis, across which the coefficient of
\mathrm{e}^{-\xi^{2}}/\xi
in
the far field (i.e. in the outer limit of the inner expansion)
changes. The branch cut associated with
\sqrt{\xi^{2}}
in
(
37
) must be chosen
to line up with this higher-order Stokes line.
In addition, there is a higher-order Stokes line on the imaginary axis,
across which the constant in the far field changes. The branch cut
associated with
\pm\mathrm{i}\pi/2
must be chosen to align with this
higher-order Stokes line.
As
\xi\rightarrow\infty
in the first quadrant,

H\sim\alpha_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{2}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}+\left(-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-3\xi^{2}/2}}{\xi}+\cdots+\log\xi+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{E}+\log 6)+\cdots.

(39)

where
\gamma_{E}
is the Euler gamma.
As
\xi\rightarrow\infty
in the third quadrant,

H\sim\alpha_{1}-\frac{\alpha_{2}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}+\left(-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{3}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}\right)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-3\xi^{2}/2}}{\xi}+\cdots+\log\xi++\frac{1}{2}(\gamma_{E}+\log 6-\mathrm{i}\pi)+\cdots.

(40)

In the model problem of Section
2
the key coefficient

\alpha_{2}
was determined by imposing that
H
was regular at

\xi=0
. In this case (
38
) is regular at the origin for all

\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}
, and it is matching with the outer solution
which determines
\alpha_{2}
.

Matching (
39
) with (
37
) as
\xi\rightarrow\infty

in the first quadrant gives

\left(-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{3}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}\right)\Omega=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\Lambda,\qquad C_{0}=1/2,\quad C_{1}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}-\log 2=\alpha_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{2}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{\gamma_{E}}{2}+\log 6.

Matching (
40
) with (
37
) as
\xi\rightarrow\infty

in the third quadrant gives

\left(-\frac{\alpha_{2}}{3}+\frac{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}\right)\Omega=-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\Lambda,\qquad C_{0}=1/2,\quad C_{1}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi}{2}-\log 2=\alpha_{1}-\frac{\alpha_{2}\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{6}}+\frac{\gamma_{E}}{2}+\log 6-\mathrm{i}\pi.

Thus
\alpha_{2}=0
and

\Omega=-\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Lambda=-\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{3/2}}.

This gives, finally,

\bar{\lambda}_{n-1}=\lambda_{n}\sim\frac{(-1)^{n+1}\sqrt{6}}{\pi^{3/2}}\,3^{n}\Gamma(n+1/2),

(41)

in agreement with
[
3
]
.

\end{overpic}

4
Example 3: Simplified Rossby waves

Our third example is a simplified version of the model problem for an
equitorially-trapped Rossby wave considered in
[
10
]
.
Consider

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi/\mathrm{d}x^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi/\mathrm{d}x^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi/\mathrm{d}x^{2}}-2x\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}x}}{\mathrm{d}\psi/\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\psi/\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}\psi/\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}\psi}{1+\epsilon x}=\lambda\psi,\qquad\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\psi\rightarrow 0\quad\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow\pm\infty,

with
\psi(0)=1
. Essentially the problem considered in

[
10
]
has the
\epsilon^{2}
in the third term replaced with

\epsilon
. The switch to
\epsilon^{2}
makes the inner region below more complicated, but significantly
simplifies all the other regions of the analysis.

4.1
Inner region

We will see that the expansion in powers of
\epsilon
proceeds as

\psi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\psi_{n},\qquad\lambda=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2n}\lambda_{n}.

(42)

At leading order

\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}-2x\psi_{0}^{\prime}=\lambda_{0}\psi_{0}.

In order for
\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\psi(y)
to decay as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
we need
\psi_{0}
to be a
Hermite polynomial.
The leading eigenvalue therefore has
\psi_{0}=1
,
\lambda_{0}=0
.
In general

\psi_{n}^{\prime\prime}-2x\psi_{n}^{\prime}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}(-x)^{k-2}\psi_{n-k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\lambda_{k}\psi_{n-2k},\qquad n\geq 1.

In particular, we find
\psi_{1}=0
, while

\psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}-2x\psi_{2}^{\prime}+1=\lambda_{1},

so that
\psi_{2}=0
,
\lambda_{1}=1
. Separating even and odd indices,

\displaystyle\psi_{2n+1}^{\prime\prime}-2x\psi_{2n+1}^{\prime}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-x)^{2k-2}\psi_{2(n-k)+1}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-x)^{2k-1}\psi_{2(n-k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\lambda_{k}\psi_{2(n-k)+1},

\displaystyle\psi_{2n}^{\prime\prime}-2x\psi_{2n}^{\prime}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-x)^{2k-2}\psi_{2(n-k)}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(-x)^{2k-1}\psi_{2(n-k)-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\lambda_{k}\psi_{2(n-k)}.

The solutions are of the form

\psi_{2n+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{2n+1,k}x^{2k-1},\qquad\psi_{2n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{2n,k}x^{2k},

(43)

with

\displaystyle 2(2k-1)a_{2n+1,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2k(2k+1)a_{2n+1,k+1}+\sum_{m=1}^{n}a_{2(n-m)+1,k-m+1}

(44)

\displaystyle\mbox{ }-\sum_{m=1}^{n}a_{2(n-m),k-m}-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\lambda_{m}a_{2(n-m)+1,k},

\displaystyle 4ka_{2n,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2k+2)(2k+1)a_{2n,k+1}+\sum_{m=1}^{n}a_{2(n-m),k-m+1}

(45)

\displaystyle\mbox{ }-\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}a_{2(n-m)-1,k-m+1}-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\lambda_{m}a_{2(n-m),k},

with
a_{n,0}=0
for
n>0
and the convention that
a_{2n+1,k}=0
and
a_{2n,k}=0
if
k>n
or
k<1
. For each
n
equations (
44
)-(
45
) may be solved iteratively stepping down from
k=n
.
The solvability condition determining
\lambda_{n}
comes from setting
k=0
in (
45
), giving
\lambda_{n}=2a_{2n,1}
. We now follow the procedure of §
2
to determine the asymptotic behaviour of
\lambda_{n}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

4.2
Outer region

As usual, the expansion (
42
) is not uniform in
x
and rearranges when
x
is large.
We set
\epsilon x=X
to give

\epsilon^{2}\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi}{\mathrm{d}X^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}-2X\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi}{\mathrm{d}X}}{\mathrm{d}\psi/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi/\mathrm{d}X}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}\psi}{1+X}=\lambda\psi.

The outer expansion now proceeds straightforwardly in powers of
\epsilon^{2}
as

\psi=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2n}\psi_{n},\qquad\lambda=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{2n}\lambda_{n}.

(46)

At leading order

-2X\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{0}}{\mathrm{d}X}}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{0}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{0}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{0}/\mathrm{d}X}=\lambda_{0}\psi_{0},

with solution

\psi_{0}=B_{0}X^{-\lambda_{0}/2}.

For there to be no singularity at
X=0
requires
\lambda_{0}/2
to be a non-positive integer, in agreement with the inner analysis in §
4.1
. The leading eigenvalue therefore has
\lambda_{0}=0
,
\psi_{0}=1
.
At next order

-2X\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{1}}{\mathrm{d}X}}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{1}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{1}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{1}/\mathrm{d}X}+\frac{1}{1+X}=\lambda_{1},

with solution

\psi_{1}=B_{1}+\frac{(1-\lambda_{1})}{2}\log X-\frac{1}{2}\log(1+X).

For there to be no singularity at
X=0
we require
\lambda_{1}=1
, in agreement with §
4.1
. The boundary condition
\psi_{1}(0)=0
(more properly a matching condition with the inner region) gives
B_{1}=0
.
In general

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}X^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}-2X\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}}{\mathrm{d}X}}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}X}+\frac{\psi_{n-1}}{1+X}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\psi_{n-k}.

(47)

As usual, there are two types of divergence: a factorial/power from repeated differentiation of the singularity
\log(1+X)
in
\psi_{1}
, and a factorial/constant from
\lambda_{n}
.
For the first, we use the usual ansatz

\psi_{n}\sim\frac{G\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{\chi^{n+\gamma}}.

(48)

At leading order in
n
this gives

\chi^{\prime}=-2X

so that

\chi=1-X^{2}

since
\chi=0
at
X=-1
.
At next order,

\displaystyle G+XG^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

so that

G=\frac{\Lambda}{X}.

Thus this part of
\psi_{n}
satisfies

\psi_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{X(1-X^{2})^{n+\gamma}}.

As
X\rightarrow-1
,

\psi_{n}\sim-\frac{\Lambda\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{2^{n+\gamma}(X+1)^{n+\gamma}}.

Comparing powers of
X+1
with the early terms gives
\gamma=-1
, so that

\psi_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda\Gamma(n-1)}{X(1-X^{2})^{n-1}}.

(49)

Here we see a curious feature of this example—the late term
behaviour
\psi_{n}
was driven by a singularity at
X=-1
, but the
singulant vanishes also at
X=+1
.
Whereas in the anharmonic oscillator problem of §
3
both
singularities
X=\pm\mathrm{i}
were present in the early terms, here only
X=-1
is
present in the early terms. The resolution of this apparent paradox,
as described in
[
10
]
, is a higher-order Stokes line which
turns off the contribution (
49
) in a region enclosing

X=1
. We will return to this point later when
matching with an inner region near
X=0
.

The other part of
\psi_{n}
satisfies
\psi_{n}\sim Q\lambda_{n}
where

-2XQ^{\prime}=1,

giving

Q=C-\frac{1}{2}\log X.

As usual, the presence of a logarithm
means we need to modify the large
n
ansatz to essentially allow
C
to depend on
\log n
. Putting both parts of
\psi_{n}
together we have

\psi_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda\Gamma(n-1)}{X(1-X^{2})^{n-1}}+\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log X+C_{0}\log n+C_{1}\right)\lambda_{n}.

(50)

The next step is to determine the constant
\Lambda
, by matching with an inner region near the singularity at
X=-1
.

4.3
Inner region near
X=-1

We set
X=-1+\epsilon^{2}\hat{x}
.
Then the inner limit of the outer expansion satisfies

\displaystyle\psi_{0}+\epsilon^{2}\psi_{1}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle 1-\epsilon^{2}\log\epsilon-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\log\hat{x},

(51)

\displaystyle\epsilon^{2n}\psi_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{\epsilon^{2}\Lambda\Gamma(n-1)}{(2\hat{x})^{n-1}}.

(52)

Equation (
51
) motivates writing
\psi=1-\epsilon^{2}\log\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}\hat{\psi}
to give the inner equation as

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}-2(-1+\epsilon^{2}\hat{x})\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}+\frac{(1-\epsilon^{2}\log\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}\hat{\psi})}{\hat{x}}=\lambda(1-\epsilon^{2}\log\epsilon+\epsilon^{2}\hat{\psi}).

At leading order

\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}^{2}}+2\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}_{0}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}{\mathrm{d}\hat{\psi}_{0}/\mathrm{d}\hat{x}}+\frac{1}{\hat{x}}=0.

Thus

\hat{\psi}_{0}=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\hat{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\hat{x}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\hat{x}}\Ei(2\hat{x})=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-2\hat{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\log\hat{x}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{(2\hat{x})^{n+1}}.

Matching with (
52
) gives

\Lambda=-\frac{1}{2}.

4.4
Boundary layer in the late terms near
X=0

As usual, the large
n
asymptotic series for
\psi_{n}
in the outer region
rearranges when
X
is small.
This is again clear from the fact that the asymptotic approximation for
\psi_{n}
, (
50
), is singular at
X=0
, while
\psi_{n}
is not.
There is a boundary layer near the origin in the large
n

expansion of
\psi_{n}
.
The appropriate scaling of this inner region is
X=\xi/n^{1/2}
, so that
\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}X^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}X^{2}}
balances
X\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}}{\mathrm{d}X}}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}X}
, so that (
47
) becomes

n\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}}{\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\psi_{n-1}/\mathrm{d}\xi^{2}}-2\xi\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}\xi}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}\xi}{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}/\mathrm{d}\xi}+\frac{\psi_{n-1}}{1+\xi/n^{1/2}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\psi_{n-k}.

(53)

The inner limit of the outer expansion (
50
) is, for
X<0
,

\displaystyle\psi_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma(n-1)n^{1/2}}{2\xi(1-\xi^{2}/n)^{n-1}}+\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log(\xi)+C\right)\lambda_{n}

(54)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma(n-1/2)}{2}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}}}{\xi}+\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log(\xi)+C_{0}\log n+C_{1}\right)\lambda_{n}.

This motivates setting
\lambda_{n}=\Omega\Gamma(n-1/2)
,
\psi_{n}\sim H\Omega\Gamma(n-1/2)
in (
53
), to give

H^{\prime\prime}-2\xi H^{\prime}=1,

where
{}^{\prime}\equiv\mathchoice{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}}{\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\xi}{\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\xi}{\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\xi}
, so that

H=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}\int_{0}^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{\xi}\mathrm{e}^{t^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-p^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}t.

Both the particular integral and the complementary function exhibit
Stokes’ phenomenon for large
\xi
, corresponding to the higher-order
Stokes phenomenon in
\psi
.
There is a higher-order Stokes line due to the particular integral when
\xi
crosses the
imaginary axis, across which the coefficient of
\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}}/\xi
in
the far field changes.
In addition, there is a higher-order Stokes line on the real axis,
across which the constant in the far field changes.
For real
\xi

while as
\xi\rightarrow+\infty

where

Matching with the outer

The outer limit of the inner is

\displaystyle\psi_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\right)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}}}{\xi}\Gamma(n-1/2)+\cdots-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}\left(\log\xi+\cdots\right)\qquad\mbox{ as }\xi\rightarrow-\infty,

\displaystyle\psi_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\Omega\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\right)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\xi^{2}}}{\xi}\Gamma(n-1/2)+\cdots-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}\left(\log\xi+\cdots\right)\qquad\mbox{ as }\xi\rightarrow+\infty.

) as

For

5
Example 4: Divergence driven by two singularities

Our final example is chosen to illustrate that the divergence of the
eigenvalue can be driven by more than one singularity in the outer
solution, leading to more exotic behaviour. This is exactly what
happens in the model in
[
7
]
concerning
quasinormal modes of Reissner-Normström de Sitter black holes.
The form of this divergence is more difficult to pick up with other
methods, and the interaction between two singularities makes it
difficult to guess the form of the divergence from numerical
calculations of the leading terms in the series.

Consider, as a model problem,

\frac{b^{2}+(c+\epsilon x)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+\epsilon x)}\left(-\omega g+xg^{\prime}\right)+g+(xg^{\prime})^{\prime}=0,\qquad-\infty<x<0,

(56)

with

g(0)=1,\qquad g(x)=o(\mathrm{e}^{-x})\mbox{ as }x\rightarrow\infty.

The relationship with (
1
) is clear—the coefficient of
the first term has been modified to generate an outer solution with
two singularities.

5.1
Inner region

We expand

g=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}g_{n},\qquad\omega=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\omega_{n},

to give at leading order

-\omega_{0}g_{0}+xg_{0}^{\prime}+g_{0}+(xg_{0}^{\prime})^{\prime}=0,

with solution
g_{0}=1
,
\omega_{0}=1
.
At next order

-\frac{cx}{b^{2}+c^{2}}+\left(-\omega_{1}+xg_{1}^{\prime}\right)+(xg_{1}^{\prime})^{\prime}=0,

with solution

g_{1}=-\frac{cx}{b^{2}+c^{2}},\qquad\omega_{1}=\frac{c}{b^{2}+c^{2}}.

In general

g_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{ni}x^{i}.

with

ia_{ni}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{k}a_{n-k,i}-(i+1)^{2}a_{n,i+1}\\
-\frac{c}{(b^{2}+c^{2})}\left(-2\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\omega_{k}a_{n-k-1,i-1}+2(i-1)a_{n-1,i-1}+a_{n-1,i-1}+i^{2}a_{n-1,i}\right)\\
-\frac{1}{(b^{2}+c^{2})}\left(-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\omega_{k}a_{n-2-k,i-2}+(i-2)a_{n-2,i-2}\right),

(57)

and
\omega_{n}=a_{n,1}
.

57
) numerically. Figure

interaction of the contributions from the two

5.2
Outer region

We set
\epsilon x=X
to give

\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}\left(-\omega g+Xg^{\prime}\right)+g+\epsilon(Xg^{\prime})^{\prime}=0.

Expanding

g=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}g_{n},\qquad\omega=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\epsilon^{n}\omega_{n},

(58)

and using
\omega_{0}=1
gives

\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}\left(-g_{0}+Xg_{0}^{\prime}\right)+g_{0}=0,

with solution

g_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}}{\sqrt{b^{2}+c^{2}}},

(59)

where we have used the fact that
g_{0}\rightarrow 1
as
X\rightarrow 0
.
We see that
g_{0}
has singularities when
b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}=0
, i.e.
X=-c\pm\mathrm{i}b
(of course, the coefficient of the first term in (
56
)
was chosen to make this the case).
In general

\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}(-g_{n}+Xg_{n}^{\prime})+g_{n}=-(Xg_{n-1}^{\prime})^{\prime}+\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}(\omega_{1}g_{n-1}+\cdots+\omega_{n}g_{0}).

As usual there are two types of divergence: a factorial/power from the differentiation, and a factorial/constant from the
\omega_{n}
.
For the first, we use the usual ansatz

g_{n}=\frac{G\Gamma(n+\gamma)}{\chi^{n+\gamma}}.

(60)

At leading order in
n
this gives

-\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}=-\chi^{\prime}

so that

\chi=\frac{(c+X)(cX+c^{2}-2b^{2})}{2c^{2}}+\frac{b^{2}(b^{2}+c^{2})\log(b^{2}+c^{2}+cX)}{c^{3}}+\mbox{const.}

This time there are two possible late term divergences, one
corresponding to each of the two singularities of the leading order
solution:

\displaystyle\chi_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\mathrm{i}b^{3}+c(c+X)^{2}-b^{2}(c+2X)}{2c^{2}}+\frac{b^{2}(b^{2}+c^{2})}{c^{3}}\log\left(\frac{b^{2}+c(c+X)}{b^{2}+\mathrm{i}bc}\right),

\displaystyle\chi_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-2\mathrm{i}b^{3}+c(c+X)^{2}-b^{2}(c+2X)}{2c^{2}}+\frac{b^{2}(b^{2}+c^{2})}{c^{3}}\log\left(\frac{b^{2}+c(c+X)}{b^{2}-\mathrm{i}bc}\right).

At next order,

\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}(-G+XG^{\prime})+G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G\chi^{\prime}+X(2G^{\prime}\chi^{\prime}+G\chi^{\prime\prime}).

Thus

\frac{(b^{4}+c(c+X)^{2}(c+3X)+b^{2}(2c^{2}+5cX+4X^{2}))}{(b^{2}+c(c+X))^{2}}G+\frac{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}G^{\prime}=0,

giving

G=\frac{\Lambda(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}.

Thus this part of
g_{n}
satisfies

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda_{1}(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(n+\gamma_{1})}{\chi_{1}^{n+\gamma_{1}}}+\frac{\Lambda_{2}(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(n+\gamma_{2})}{\chi_{2}^{n+\gamma_{2}}}.

To determine
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
we match the order of the
singularity as
X\rightarrow-c\pm\mathrm{i}b
with the early terms.
As
X\rightarrow-c+\mathrm{i}b
,

\chi_{1}\sim\frac{(X+c-\mathrm{i}b)^{2}}{c-\mathrm{i}b},

g_{n}\sim-\frac{\mathrm{i}b}{2\sqrt{2}(\mathrm{i}b)^{3/2}(X+c-\mathrm{i}b)^{3/2}}\frac{(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{n+\gamma_{1}}}{(X+c-\mathrm{i}b)^{2n+2\gamma_{1}}}\Lambda_{1}\Gamma(n+\gamma_{1}).

Comparing powers of
X+c-\mathrm{i}b
with
g_{0}
gives

-\frac{3}{2}-2\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\gamma_{1}=-1.

A similar comparison as
X\rightarrow-c-\mathrm{i}b
gives
\gamma_{2}=-1
also, so that

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda_{1}(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(n-1)}{\chi_{1}^{n-1}}+\frac{\Lambda_{2}(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(n-1)}{\chi_{2}^{n-1}}.

The other part of
g_{n}
satisfies
g_{n}=(Q_{0}\log n+Q_{1})\omega_{n}
where

\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}(-Q_{0}+XQ_{0}^{\prime})+Q_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}(-Q_{1}+XQ_{1}^{\prime})+Q_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}{b^{2}+c(c+X)}g_{0},

giving

Q_{0}\log n+Q_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}}{b^{2}+c^{2}}\left(\log X+C_{0}\log n+C_{1}\right).

Together

g_{n}\sim\frac{\Lambda_{1}(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(n-1)}{\chi_{1}^{n-1}}+\frac{\Lambda_{2}(b^{2}+c(c+X))}{X(b^{2}+(c+X)^{2})^{3/2}}\frac{\Gamma(n-1)}{\chi_{2}^{n-1}}\\
+\frac{\sqrt{b^{2}+(c+X)^{2}}}{b^{2}+c^{2}}\left(\log X+C_{0}\log n+C_{1}\right)\omega_{n}.

(61)

The next step is to determin
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
through
matching with inner regions near
X=-c\pm\mathrm{i}b

5.3
Inner region near
X=-c+\mathrm{i}b

To determine
\Lambda_{1}
we look near
X=-c+\mathrm{i}b
.
We set
X=-c+\mathrm{i}b+\epsilon^{1/2}(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}\hat{x}
,
g=\epsilon^{1/4}\hat{g}
to give

\frac{2\mathrm{i}\epsilon^{1/2}(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}\hat{x}}{b+\mathrm{i}c}\left(-\omega\hat{g}+\frac{1}{(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}(-c+\mathrm{i}b+\epsilon^{1/2}(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}\hat{x})\hat{g}^{\prime}\right)\\
+\hat{g}+\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}\hat{g}^{\prime}+(-c+\mathrm{i}b+(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}\epsilon^{1/2}\hat{x})\frac{1}{(c-\mathrm{i}b)\epsilon}\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}\right)=0.

At leading order

-2\hat{x}\hat{g}_{0}^{\prime}+\hat{g}_{0}-\hat{g}_{0}^{\prime\prime}=0.

Writing

\hat{g}_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{2}(\mathrm{i}b)^{1/2}(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{b^{2}+c^{2}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}x^{1/2-2n},

gives

Thus

The inner limit of the outer expansion is

\displaystyle\epsilon^{n}g_{n}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{i}b\epsilon^{n}}{2\sqrt{2}(\mathrm{i}b)^{3/2}(X+c-\mathrm{i}b)^{3/2}}\frac{(c-\mathrm{i}b)^{n-1}}{(X+c-\mathrm{i}b)^{2n-2}}\Lambda_{1}\Gamma(n-1)

6
Conclusion

Through four examples, we have demonstrated a systematic procedure for
calculating the precise asymptotic behaviour of the late terms of the
asymptotic expansion of the eigenvalue in a variety of linear
eigenvalue problems. The framework in each of our examples is the
same.

After a regular perturbation expansion the eigenfunction at each order
is a polynomial, leading to a set of recurrence relations for the
coefficients of these polynomials and
the coefficients of the eigenvalue expansion.
While these relations are easy to iterate numerically to get the
leading terms of the eigenvalue expansion, it is hard to extract the
late term behaviour from them.

This regular perturbation expansion is nonuniform, and rearranges when

x
is large. Rescaling to an outer variable the corresponding outer
solution can be found, again as an asymptotic power series. This
series has a standard factorial/power divergence driven by
singularities in the leading-order approximation, and an additional
divergence driven by the divergent eigenvalue expansion. In contrast
to the original expansion, the late terms in this outer asymptotic
expansion are easy to find, using the usual factorial/power ansatz,
but the divergence of the eigenvalue is still undetermined.

However, the late term approximation of this outer expansion also
non-uniform, now not as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
but as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By
introducing a local variable in the equation for the late terms of
the outer expansion, a new inner expansion is generated in which the two
parts of the diverence become coupled, and the eigenvalue is
determined.

We hope our framework provides a template by which similar problems of
interest may be solved.
