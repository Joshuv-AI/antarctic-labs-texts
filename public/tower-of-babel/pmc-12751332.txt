Associations of serum bile acids and amino acids with low skeletal muscle mass among Chinese population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ting Zhang, Ning Chen, Ting Liu, Mingyu Zhu, Hongxia Liu, Tingting Han, Yurong Weng, Rong Huang, Yaomin Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSarcopenia, characterized by progressive muscle loss, burdens aging populations. While bile acids (BAs) in skeletal muscle mass (SMM) are poorly understood, the role of amino acids (AAs) remains debated. This study investigated the associations between serum BAs, AAs, and low SMM in Chinese adults.MethodsAmong 2170 initially enrolled participants, 702 were finally analyzed. Participants were classified into low and normal SMM groups. Serum BAs and AAs were quantified by targeted LC-MS/MS. Nonlinear relationships were assessed using restricted cubic spline analysis. Mediation analysis was used to assess the mediating effects of metabolic factors.ResultsThe prevalence of low SMM was 14.5% (females: 21.1% vs. males: 10.2%). Multivariable logistic regression identified that increased glycocholic acid (GCA, OR = 0.561, p = 0.030), glycolithocholic acid (GLCA, OR = 0.255, p < 0.001) and deoxycholic acid (DCA, OR = 0.444, p = 0.003) were independently associated with lower risk of low SMM in the fully adjusted model. Valine (OR = 0.646, p = 0.010) and leucine (OR = 0.680, p = 0.024) showed consistent protective effects against low SMM at physiological concentrations. Mediation analysis showed BMI mediated protective effects of GCA, valine and leucine (all p < 0.01), while creatinine/cystatin C ratio mediated leucine’s effect (p = 0.030). Notably, GLCA exhibited direct protection independent of metabolic mediators (both p < 0.01).ConclusionsThis study identified specific BAs and AAs significantly correlated with preserved SMM, suggesting that modulating BA metabolism (particularly elevating GLCA) and maintaining optimal BCAA levels may be effective strategies for low SMM. GLCA’s direct protective effect supports exploring dietary or microbiota-targeted interventions. Individual metabolic profiles, such as BMI, should be incorporated into personalized sarcopenia management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09353-w.Keywords: Bile acid, Amino acid, Skeletal muscle mass, Sarcopenia, Glycolithocholic acid

Introduction

In the era of population ageing, sarcopenia, recognized as a muscle disease [1], has become a global health challenge and attracted considerable attention [2]. It is characterized by a progressive and systemic loss of skeletal muscle mass (SMM) and a decline in skeletal muscle strength or physical function. Sarcopenia has been reported to affect approximately 25% of individuals under the age of 70 and 40% of people under the age of 80 [3]. The loss of muscle mass and function can lead to an increased incidence of common ageing-related syndromes such as falls and frailty, along with an elevated risk of adverse events such as reduced quality of life, physical disability, and even mortality, ultimately resulting in higher healthcare expenditures [3]. Sarcopenia is therefore a major health problem and a significant burden on healthcare systems across the world. Current management for sarcopenia relies primarily on evidence-based foundational strategies such as structured exercise (particularly resistance training) and nutritional support (e.g., protein and amino acid supplementation). However, despite the efficacy of these approaches, the therapeutic options remain limited and lacks targeted pharmacological interventions [4].

According to contemporary diagnostic consensuses [1], the diagnosis of sarcopenia typically requires the presence of both low muscle strength and low muscle mass, with poor physical performance further indicating severity. While muscle strength is a primary indicator for case finding, low SMM remains a fundamental component for the confirmation of sarcopenia. Given that the decline in SMM is a primary driver of the metabolic and functional consequences of the condition, and considering the specific focus of our available data, this study specifically investigated low SMM as the key outcome. Our aim is to identify metabolic markers associated with this core component of the disease.

The metabolites bile acid (BA) and amino acid (AA) are pivotal for the host and considered to be closely correlated with ageing-related pathological conditions [5]. BAs are critical metabolites that regulate muscle metabolism through receptor-mediated signaling pathways. Emerging evidence suggests that altered BA profiles were associated with sarcopenia, particularly in conditions like liver cirrhosis [6]. BAs exert their biological effects primarily via the nuclear receptor farnesoid X receptor (FXR) and the membrane-bound G protein-coupled receptor TGR5 [7, 8]. TGR5 activation has been shown to promote muscle cell differentiation and hypertrophy, with transgenic overexpression enhancing muscle strength in both young and old mice [9]. Conversely, some studies report that hydrophobic BAs, such as cholic acid (CA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA), may induce muscle protein degradation via TGR5 under pathological conditions [10]. These divergent findings highlight the context-dependent roles of BAs in muscle homeostasis, necessitating further investigation into their physiological effects on SMM.

Amino acids (AAs), particularly branched-chain AAs (BCAAs) like valine and leucine, are equally vital in maintaining muscle protein synthesis and mitigating age-related muscle loss [11, 12]. BCAAs activate the mTOR pathway, a key regulator of protein synthesis [13], and their deficiency is linked to reduced SMM in clinical and experimental settings. While some studies, including one in cirrhotic populations, have reported that BCAA supplementation is associated with improved muscle mass [14], the overall evidence remains controversial. A key methodological limitation is the frequent lack of control for concomitant physical activity, an important stimulus for muscle growth. Therefore, the observed benefits may be attributable to the combined effects of supplementation and exercise rather than to supplementation alone. While both BAs and AAs influence muscle metabolism, their specific roles, and the contextual factors that modulate their efficacy, in the development of SMM loss deserve further in-depth exploration.

This study aims to investigate the relationships between serum BAs (including primary, secondary and conjugated species), AAs, and low SMM in a Chinese cohort. While previous studies have established a general link between these metabolites and muscle mass, several limitations remain. First, most existing evidence comes from pathological populations (e.g., liver cirrhosis), and their roles within physiological ranges in middle-aged and older adults are poorly evaluated. Second, the association of SMM with BAs and AAs, and the potential mediating role of metabolic factors remain unexplored. We aimed to comprehensively profile a wide spectrum of BAs and AAs and to evaluate potential metabolic mediators. Our findings will clarify the distinct physiological roles of these BA- and AA-related metabolites in sarcopenia, thereby contributing to the development of more targeted and effective intervention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was carried out in the Ren Ji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine from December 2020 to March 2023. Participants included into this study met the following criteria: aged 18 years or older; completed the bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA, 50 kHz, 0.8 mA); completed a test for serum concentrations of BAs and AAs. Exclusion criteria were: (1) chronic conditions that may affect bile acid or amino acid metabolism, including hepatobiliary diseases such as liver cirrhosis, NAFLD, PBC and biliary obstruction; gastrointestinal diseases such as IBD; as well as cancer; (2) cardiopulmonary failure, severe cognitive impairment, or neuromuscular diseases; (3) musculoskeletal conditions severely affecting mobility, including acute/chronic osteoarthritis and a history of vertebral surgery or fractures; (4) peripheral edema or recent diuretic use (within one month); and (5) contraindications for the Inbody test, such as cardiac pacemaker implantation. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ren Ji Hospital, School of Medicine, Shanghai Jiao Tong University according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all the study participants.

Assessment of skeletal muscle mass and definition of low SMM

The SMM was assessed using BIA (Euromedix, Leuven, Belgium). All BIA assessments were performed under standardized conditions. Participants were instructed to adhere to the following protocols: (1) fast for at least 8 h overnight; (2) refrain from strenuous physical activity for at least 24 h prior to testing; (3) maintain normal hydration without excessive fluid intake immediately before the test. All measurements were conducted in the morning to minimize the influence of diurnal variation. These standardized procedures are in line with established guidelines for BIA assessment [15]. Among the 547 individuals who did not undergo valid BIA assessment, the primary reasons included: (1) the presence of pre-defined exclusion criteria that directly interfere with BIA measurement accuracy, such as peripheral edema or recent diuretic use; (2) medical contraindications for the test, including cardiac pacemaker implantation; and (3) participant-related factors, such as refusal or inability to comply with the testing protocol.

The skeletal muscle index (SMI) was calculated as SMM (kg)/height (m²). According to the AWGS criteria [16], low SMM was defined as BIA-SMI < 7.0 kg/m2 in males and < 5.7 kg/m2 in females. Based on this cutoff, all participants were categorized into normal SMM group and low SMM group. For each patient, the following data were collected: age; sex; height; weight; waistline; hip circumference; calf circumference; T12-cross-sectional area (CSA); T12-skeletal muscle radiodensity (SMD); L3-CSA; L3-SMD; and blood test including hemoglobin (Hb), serum albumin, creatinine, cystatin C, eGFR-EPI, superoxide dismutase (SOD), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c). The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2).

Determination of serum BAs and AAs

To investigate the relationship between BAs and skeletal muscle mass (SMM), we systematically selected representative BAs for analysis: cholic acid (CA) as a primary BA; deoxycholic acid (DCA), ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), and lithocholic acid (LCA) as secondary BA; and glycocholic acid (GCA) and glycolithocholic acid (GLCA) as conjugated BAs. For AAs, we focused on valine, leucine, alanine, and lysine based on their established biological relevance to muscle function. Serum concentrations of these BAs and AAs were qualified using targeted liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). We followed the reference standard procedures as outlined by Mayo Clinic Laboratories [17]. To specifically examine the effects of physiologically normal concentrations on SMM, we excluded values outside the following reference intervals: Bile acids: CA ≤ 2740 nmol/L, DCA ≤ 2840 nmol/L, UDCA ≤ 640 nmol/L, LCA ≤ 90 nmol/L, GCA ≤ 2170 nmol/L, GLCA ≤ 110 nmol/L; Amino acids (adults ≥ 18 years): valine 136–309 µmol/L, leucine 68–183 µmol/L, alanine 200–579 µmol/L, lysine 103–255 µmol/L.

For analytical purposes: BA concentrations (CA, DCA, UDCA, GCA) were dichotomized into High-Med (above median) and Low-Med (below median) groups, as many values approached the lower detection limit. LCA and GLCA were categorized as High-Cut (detectable) versus Low-Cut (below detection limit) groups, as > 50% of measurements fell below the detection threshold. This stratification approach enabled robust comparison while maintaining all analyzed values within clinically normal ranges. The median-based categorization was particularly appropriate given the non-normal distribution of many BA concentrations.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis was performed by SPSS version 24.0. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the comparison between two groups was performed by unpaired Student’s t test. Continuous variables that did not conform to normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile (IQR), and Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparison between groups. The chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables across groups. Binary logistic regression analysis was used to analyze independent factors affecting muscle mass. All models were adjusted for age, sex, albumin, eGFR-EPI, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), smoking history, drinking history, number of chronic diseases, percentage body fat (PBF), gamma-glutamyl transferase (γ-GT), urea, creatine kinase, vitamin A, folate and vitamin B12. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models were performed to estimate linear and nonlinear associations of the concentrations of AAs with risks of low SMM. There were three points located at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of AA concentrations, and the median was used as reference in the RCS models. The mediating roles of BMI and Cr/CysC on bile acid/amino acid associations with low SMM were examined using mediation analysis. The “mediation” package in R was used for the mediation analysis. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

A total of 2170 subjects were recruited during this study period (Additional file 1). And 702 participants were assessed and included into the final analysis, 14.5% of whom were classified into the low SMM group. Males were less likely to have low SMM (p < 0.001). Of the 422 male patients, 10.2% were classified into the low SMM group, while of the 280 female patients, 21.1% were classified into the low SMM group (Additional file 1). As shown in Table 1, participants with low SMM showed significantly different anthropometric measures compared to those with normal SMM, including lower BMI (p < 0.001), smaller waist circumference (p < 0.001), smaller hip circumference (P < 0.001), and reduced calf circumference (p < 0.001). Notably, these differences in waist circumference, hip circumference and the waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) remained statistically significant when the analysis was stratified by sex (all p < 0.05 for both males and females, Table 1). Muscle cross-sectional area at both T12 and L3 levels was significantly reduced in the low SMM group (both p < 0.001). Biochemical markers including hemoglobin (p < 0.001) and creatinine (p < 0.001) were also significantly lower in participants with low SMM. In addition, age, serum albumin, skeletal muscle radiodensity at both T12 and L3 levels were comparable between subjects with and without loss of muscle mass (P > 0.05).

Differential concentrations of BAs and AAs in low and normal SMM group

Differential concentration patterns between low and normal SMM groups were observed for all measured bile acids and amino acids within normal reference ranges (Fig. 1), with particularly marked differences for GCA, GLCA, valine, leucine, alanine, and lysine (all p < 0.01). As shown in Fig. 1, compared with the normal SMM group, serum concentrations of GCA, GLCA, valine, leucine, alanine and lysine in the low SMM group were significantly reduced (all p < 0.05). The levels of CA (p = 0.056), DCA (p = 0.067), and UDCA (p = 0.070) also showed a decreased trend.

Associations between BAs/AAs within normal reference ranges and low SMM

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated significant associations between specific BAs and AAs with low SMM (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), after adjustment for demographic, metabolic, lifestyle and nutritional factors, higher levels of DCA, GCA and GLCA were strongly associated with a reduced risk of low SMM within physiological reference ranges. The protective association was most pronounced for GLCA (OR = 0.255, 95% CI: 0.120–0.538, adjusted p < 0.001), followed by DCA (OR = 0.444, 95% CI: 0.259–0.762, adjusted p = 0.003) and GCA (OR = 0.561, 95% CI: 0.333–0.945, adjusted p = 0.030). Similarly, among AAs within clinically normal ranges, higher levels of valine (OR = 0.646, 95% CI: 0.464–0.900, adjusted p = 0.010) and leucine (OR = 0.680, 95% CI: 0.486–0.951, adjusted p = 0.024) maintained significant independent inverse associations with low SMM. Notably, consistent inverse associations were observed across all three models, underscoring the robustness of these findings.

Nonlinear relationships between AA concentrations and low SMM risk

To comprehensively evaluate the relationship between AA concentrations and SMM, we performed RCS analysis across both full-range and normal-range concentrations (Fig. 2). The full-range analysis revealed a nonlinear, U-shaped association between serum valine and low SMM risk (overall p = 0.017, nonlinear p = 0.010). Increased valine was associated with a significantly reduced risk of low SMM at lower serum concentrations, whereas increased valine showed a nonsignificant trend toward an elevated risk of low SMM at higher concentrations. Within the physiological range, both valine and leucine exhibited consistent protective effects against low SMM (overall p = 0.015 and p = 0.037, respectively). Therefore, maintaining optimal levels of valine and leucine is especially important for muscle health.

Mediation analysis of BMI and creatinine/cystatin C ratio

Mediation analysis demonstrated that BMI significantly mediated the protective effects of GCA (ACME = −0.0452, 95% CI: −0.0592, 0.0053, p = 0.007) and branched-chain amino acids (valine: ACME = −0.0258, 95% CI: −0.0479, −0.0097, p = 0.004; leucine: ACME = −0.0385, 95% CI: −0.0593, −0.0149, p < 0.001) against low SMM (Table 3). For these metabolites, the ADEs were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05). In contrast, for DCA and GLCA, the ADEs remained significant (DCA: ADE = −0.0609, p = 0.002; GLCA: ADE = −0.0622, p = 0.007) while their ACME through BMI were not significant. In the mediation pathways through Cr/CysC, a significant ACME was found only for leucine (ACME = 0.0120, p = 0.030). For DCA, GCA, GLCA and valine, significant ADEs were observed (all p < 0.05).

Discussion

This study finally identified a distinct profile of BA- and AA-associated metabolic markers strongly related to SMM. The altered BA and AA metabolic profile emerged as an important feature of low SMM. We reported a low SMM prevalence of 14.5% in this Chinese cohort. However, the reported prevalence of sarcopenia varies greatly (10–50%) between studies [18, 19], which may be due to wide variations in the diagnostic criteria, evaluation methods and country of origin. Additionally, we found that female subjects tend to have a higher prevalence of low SMM compared to males, with a rate of 22.1% in women and 12.9% in men. Consistently, Li et al. reported a prevalence of sarcopenia of 10.3% in men and 12.4% in women [18]. Another study also documented sarcopenia presented in 9.7% of male subjects and 11.3% of female subjects [19]. The sex-specific sterol hormones should be an important factor to facilitate these differences in SMM [20]. Further, the sex-based differences in SMM may partly explain why women showed a higher incidence of hip fractures compared to men [21]. Correspondingly, patients with low SMM exhibited significant reductions in BMI, WHR, calf circumference and muscle cross-sectional area at T12 and L3 levels.

The BA is an important class of metabolites involved in muscle metabolism. Emerging evidence indicated that alterations in BA profiles were associated with sarcopenia development in patients with liver cirrhosis [6]. In the low SMM group, we observed a significantly lower level of serum GCA and GLCA, while a decreasing trend of CA, DCA and UDCA. And further multivariate analysis in the fully adjusted model (Model 3) showed that higher levels of DCA (OR = 0.444), GCA (OR = 0.561) and GLCA (OR = 0.255) were independent predictors for a reduced risk of low SMM.

BAs are potent signaling molecules that exert various effects through BA-activated receptors [7, 8], including FXR and TGR5. The potential interplay between BAs and SMM also relies on the activation of BA receptors [9, 10, 22, 23]. Skeletal muscle exhibits sensitivity to BAs through TGR5 receptor expression. Using both gain- and loss-of-function approaches, Sasaki et al. found that TGR5 activation promoted muscle cell differentiation and hypertrophy [9]. Notably, transgenic mice with muscle-specific TGR5 overexpression displayed enhanced muscle strength in both young and aged animals [9]. Moreover, muscle-specific overexpression of TGR5 can improve glucose clearance in glucose-intolerant mice [22]. Both GCA and DCA serve as physiological ligands for TGR5 [20]. These mechanisms align with our findings, where reduced DCA and GCA may be associated with TGR5 downregulation, potentially contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic dysregulation, and subsequent muscle loss.

In contrast to our findings, CA and DCA have been reported to induce skeletal muscle protein degradation and mitochondrial dysfunction in mice through the activation of TGR5 [10]. Cholestasis could induce a mouse model of sarcopenia, which also depends on the activation of TGR5. Recent evidence indicate that CA was hydrophobic and cytotoxic, and its activation of TGR5 led to the breakdown of muscle proteins in mice, and further loss of SMM [24, 25]. In contrast, we observed significantly reduced levels of DCA, GCA, and GLCA in subjects with low SMM. This suggests that at physiological concentrations, these BAs may mediate muscle mass accretion. Discrepancies with previous studies could be attributed to selection of normal concentration ranges, patient selection criteria, and ethnic variations in BA metabolism.

Another BA receptor that links BAs to muscle physiology is FXR. Mice with FXR gene inhibition exhibited increased SMM loss, accompanied by alterations in protein synthesis pathways [26]. Activation of ileal FXR-FGF15/19 signaling has been reported to improve SMM loss in aged mice, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for sarcopenia [27]. A recent study found that bovine lactopontin intervention increased the abundance of gut microbiota, such as Parabacteroides, promoted GLCA synthesis, and activated the FXR-Runx2-BMP signaling pathway, thereby regulating osteoblast proliferation and differentiation and promoting bone development in growing rats [28]. The above findings underscore the crucial role of gut microbiota in modulating GLCA production and subsequent metabolic effects. Another recent study demonstrated that supplementation with GCA improved the growth performance of Pelteobagrus fulvidraco and alleviated liver and intestinal tissue damage by activating the FXR receptor [29]. These findings indicate that a physiological increase in the levels of BAs such as DCA, GCA, and GLCA, acting as ligands for FXR and TGR5 receptors, improves glucose homeostasis and skeletal muscle function, thereby benefiting muscle health. However, further studies using human skeletal muscle cell lines are needed to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying the interaction between BAs and SMM.

In addition to BAs, AAs also play an essential role in maintaining SMM, especially the essential AAs such as valine, leucine and lysine [30]. Our findings demonstrate significantly reduced levels of these AAs and alanine (which has established SMM associations [31, 32] ) in the low SMM group. Multivariate analysis identified valine and leucine as independent protective factors, showing consistent beneficial associations with low SMM across physiological ranges. The benefits of BCAAs in muscle maintenance are well-established. Following BCAA stimulation, skeletal muscle primary satellite cells could accelerate the process of mTOR phosphorylation. Mice with knockout of mTOR demonstrated significantly reduced SMM [13]. Clinically, BCAA supplementation improved muscle mass in elderly populations [33] and cirrhotic patients with sarcopenia [14], consistent with observed BCAA deficiencies in sarcopenic individuals [6].

However, our results suggest important distinctions: while physiological AA levels are protective, restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant U-shaped association between valine and low SMM risk across the full concentration range (nonlinear p = 0.010), indicating potential adverse effects at higher levels. This is supported by recent work showing elevated circulating BCAA levels were positively linked to greater intramuscular lipid deposition [34]. Further, Zuo et al.’s multi-omics analysis identified impaired BCAA catabolism as both a key pathogenic mechanism and therapeutic target in sarcopenia [35]. Therefore, our results not only confirm the essential role of BCAA homeostasis in muscle health, but also highlight the need for precision in BCAA supplementation strategies - maintaining optimal physiological levels appears crucial for maximizing benefits while avoiding potential adverse metabolic effects.

In the mediation analysis, we found that BMI mediated the protective effects of GCA, valine and leucine against low SMM, suggesting that these protective associations are largely explained by their relationship with overall body composition. This implies that in individuals with higher BMI, the independent muscle-protective efficacy of these metabolites might be less pronounced. Furthermore, the creatinine/cystatin C ratio significantly mediated leucine’s association with SMM (ACME = 0.0120, p = 0.030). Notably, the direction of this mediation effect was positive, suggesting that leucine’s protective association with SMM might be partially attributed to its positive correlation with the Cr/CysC ratio, a marker often reflecting higher muscle mass relative to renal function. Clinical monitoring of this ratio could help identify patients most likely to benefit from leucine therapy. Notably, GLCA demonstrated direct protective effects independent of mediation of BMI or creatinine/cystatin C pathways, likely through pathways such as FXR activation. GLCA has also been reported to increase the frequency of circulating regulatory T cells [36], thereby alleviating postmenopausal osteoporosis. These findings highlight GLCA as a promising therapeutic target whose benefits appear to operate through distinct, mediator-independent pathways.

Strengths of this study included the well-characterized clinical cohort, which allowed us to investigate the associations of BAs and AAs with SMM while controlling for a range of clinical and metabolic covariates. The associations of BAs and AAs with SMM were investigated under physiological conditions rather than in pathological states. Furthermore, our comprehensive approach, including multivariable adjustment, exploration of nonlinear relationships and mediation analysis, provides a deeper understanding of these complex relationships. Finally, a novel and significant finding is that GLCA exerts a direct protective effect against low SMM, independent of the metabolic mediators examined.

Considering the potential impact of unmeasured confounders, particularly dietary intake and physical activity, could influence both metabolite levels and muscle mass. This is a common challenge in observational nutritional metabolomics studies. However, we wish to highlight several aspects that support the robustness of our findings. First, the associations we report for key metabolites like GLCA, DCA, valine, and leucine persisted and strengthened through progressively adjusted models that included a comprehensive panel of clinical and metabolic covariates (Table 2). These adjustments are closely linked to both nutrition and overall metabolic health. Second, the identification of GLCA’s direct protective effect, which was independent of the metabolic mediators we tested (including BMI), suggests that its association with muscle mass may involve pathways not fully accounted for by conventional lifestyle confounders. While we cannot rule out residual confounding, the consistency and specificity of our findings provide a reasonable basis for their relevance and warrant further investigation.

This study reserves several limitations. First, we lacked direct data on dietary intake and physical activity levels, which are important factors that influence circulating metabolite levels and muscle mass. Although we adjusted for several nutritional biomarkers and metabolic health indicators that may indirectly reflect aspects of nutritional status and energy balance, the absence of direct measurements remains a limitation for causal inference. Second, the incomplete coverage of metabolite data for certain BCAA and essential AA subclasses limited our ability to examine class-specific associations with SMM. Additionally, all study participants belonged to the same ethnic group, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnicities. Finally, this is a retrospective cross-sectional analysis and future prospective studies that incorporate detailed dietary and activity assessments are needed to establish the causal relationship between SMM and both BAs and AAs.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identified serum GCA, GLCA, DCA, valine and leucine as potential metabolic markers for low SMM. Our findings suggest that increased physiological levels of these metabolites were independently associated with a lower risk of low SMM. A key finding is the direct, mediator-independent protective effect of GLCA, highlighting it as a particularly promising target for interventions. For BCAAs, our results confirm their essential role while revealing a U-shaped relationship, underscoring that supplementation strategies must aim for optimal physiological levels to maximize benefits and avoid potential risks.

The protective effects of GCA, valine and leucine were at least partially mediated by BMI, suggesting that individual metabolic profiles should be considered for personalized strategies. Future longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to establish causality and explore therapeutic interventions, such as modulating gut microbiota to elevate GLCA or implementing precision nutrition for BCAAs.
