Efficacy and Safety of Sonidegib in the Treatment of Multiple Facial Cutaneous Basal Cell Carcinomas with Chronic Renal Insufficiency: A Case Report
Authors: Wenwen Jing, Junwu Yang, Zhicheng Gong, Weiqi Lei, Zhenhua Zhang, Wen Bin Rao, Yong Ai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBasal cell carcinoma (BCC) is one of the most common malignant skin tumors. Most BCCs can be effectively treated with surgical resection. Hedgehog signaling pathway inhibitors (HHIs) are effective systemic treatment options for patients with locally advanced or metastatic BCC who are unsuitable for radical surgery. However, evidence-based guidelines for managing advanced or metastatic BCC are limited, particularly regarding treatment safety in patients with chronic renal insufficiency. Here, we report a case of a patient with multiple facial BCCs complicated by chronic renal insufficiency who was treated with sonidegib. This case demonstrates that sonidegib may be a safe and effective therapeutic option for inoperable cutaneous BCC in patients with chronic renal insufficiency.Keywords: Basal cell carcinoma, Hedgehog signaling pathway inhibitors, multiple, chronic renal insufficiency

Introduction

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) worldwide. Current data indicate that the incidence of BCC continues to increase annually.1,2 BCC typically occurs in individuals older than 50 years, with risk factors including ultraviolet radiation exposure, advanced age, immunosuppression, male sex, White race, and a history of prior non-melanoma skin cancer.3,4 In addition to histopathological classification, BCC is clinically categorized as localized, locally advanced (laBCC), or metastatic (mBCC).5,6 Treatment options for localized tumors include standard surgical excision, Mohs microsurgery, cryosurgery, laser therapy, photodynamic therapy, and radiotherapy. However, laBCC and mBCC are often challenging to manage and typically require systemic treatment.7

Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (HHIs) suppress the expression of genes involved in BCC pathogenesis and are recommended for patients with laBCC who are not candidates for conventional therapeutic treatments.8 Currently, two HHIs are approved for laBCC treatment: sonidegib (200 mg once daily) and vismodegib (150 mg once daily). To date, systemic exposure and clinical safety data for sonidegib in patients with renal insufficiency are limited, and its efficacy in patients with chronic renal insufficiency has not been well documented. Here, we report the therapeutic outcome of sonidegib in a patient with multiple facial BCCs and chronic renal insufficiency.

Case Report

History of Disease

A 65-year-old male farmer presented with multiple black facial papules that had gradually increased in size over 12 years, accompanied by itching and pain. The patient sought outpatient treatment at our hospital. Over the previous year, the skin lesions had repeatedly ulcerated and bled, with poor response to topical antibiotic ointments. On physical examination, black plaques were observed in the preauricular region of the right ear, right cheek, right temporal region, and left cheek near the nasal dorsum, measuring approximately 4 cm × 3 cm, 1 cm × 1 cm, 3 cm × 3 cm, and 1 cm × 0.5 cm, respectively. The surfaces of the skin lesions in the preauricular and right temporal region were ulcerated, erosive, and had bloody exudation. Histopathological examination revealed dermal tumors composed of nests of basaloid cells, some connected to the epidermis. Peripheral palisading and retraction clefts were observed. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with nodular basal cell carcinoma involving the right preauricular, right cheek, right temporal region, and left cheek near the nasal dorsum. The patient had a more than 20-year history of chronic renal insufficiency, hyperuricemia, and hyperkalemia and was undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. After stabilization, he was treated with oral niaoduqing granules, sodium bicarbonate tablets, and febuxostat tablets. Serum potassium levels were regularly monitored, and potassium-lowering therapy was administered as needed.

Related Examinations

Laboratory findings included hemoglobin (Hb) 87 g/l (↓), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) 19.79 mmol/L (↑), creatinine (Cr) 235.4 μmol/L (↑), uric acid (UA) 289umol/L, serum potassium (K+) 5.04 mmol/L, and creatine kinase (CK) 86U/L. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed gallstones and a right renal cyst, with no significant abnormalities in the liver, spleen, pancreas, left kidney, or ureter. Brain and chest computed tomography (CT) showed low-density areas in both frontal lobes consistent with age-related changes, scattered chronic inflammatory lesions in the middle and lower lobes of the right and left lung, left lower lung and right middle and lower lung nodules requiring follow-up, aortic and coronary artery calcifications, left ventricular enlargement, scoliosis, and gallstones. No other significant abnormalities were detected.

Given the multiplicity and size of the lesions, particularly those in the preauricular and right temporal regions, surgical resection was associated with extensive trauma and significant cosmetic concerns. After discussion with the patient and his family, oral sonidegib was administered at 200 mg once daily on April 15th, 2025. After 1 month of treatment, the patient’s CK level was 299 U/L. After 2 months of treatment, the patient developed an acute upper respiratory tract infection, accompanied by a further increase in CK to 712 U/L. Laboratory tests at that time showed BUN12.18 mmol/L, Cr 170.2umol/L, UA 289umol/L, K+ 5.04 mmol/L, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 35 U/L, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 37 U/L. Symptomatic treatment was provided at a local hospital, and oral sonidegib was temporarily discontinued. On July 14th, 2025, CK levels decreased to 335 U/L, and sonidegib was resumed at 200 mg once every 2 days. Changes in various laboratory parameters during treatment are shown in Figure 1. The patient was reexamined on October 8, 2025. The facial skin lesions had resolved, leaving residual pigmentation at the original sites. Biopsy specimens obtained from areas with the most prominent pigmentation showed normal epidermal architecture, visible pigment granules, superficial dermal lymphocyte infiltration, and no evidence of tumor cell proliferation. Adverse Reactions

Adverse reactions were monitored throughout the study, graded for toxicity using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4 03. No significant adverse events were observed during the early treatment period. Four months after treatment initiation, the patient developed Grade 2 muscle soreness and intermittent lower limb muscle spasms. After 5 months of treatment, Grade 1 nausea, loss of appetite, Grade 2 taste disorder, and Grade 2 alopecia were noted, without significant weight loss.

Follow-up

According to the drug instructions for sonidegib, sonidegib can cause an increase in CK values. During the oral administration of sonidegib, CK values need to be monitored once a month. If the CK values exceed the upper limit of the normal range, treatment should be suspended. Then, the CK values should be monitored once a week. If the CK values gradually return to the baseline level, treatment can be resumed, with 200 mg once every 2 days.

The patient received oral sonidegib for 6 months, the patient achieved complete response, after which histopathological examination confirmed complete tumor eradication with residual pigmentation at the primary lesion site. Renal function remained relatively stable throughout treatment. A transient increase in UA occurred during an episode of upper respiratory tract infection, but subsequently returned to baseline. CK levels were 325 U/L on October 8, 2025, increased to 634 U/L on October 22, 2025, and decreased to 206 U/L on November 20, 2025, after temporary drug discontinuation. Sonidigib was then resumed at 200 mg once every 2 days as maintenance therapy. The patient remained under regular follow-up. Monitor the CK value monthly, conduct follow-ups every 3 months, for a total of 2 years, and then follow-ups will be conducted every 6 months thereafter.

Discussion

The patient presented with multiple facial BCCs. To determine whether the patient had nevus-like basal cell carcinoma syndrome (NBCCS, Gorlin-Goltz syndrome), relevant examinations were performed. No odontogenic keratocysts were detected on brain CT, no obvious bone abnormalities were observed on chest CT, only mild scoliosis was identified, and no palmoplantar pits were found on physical examination. Therefore, the patient did not meet the diagnostic criteria for NBCCS.9 Previous studies have shown that the development of BCC is associated with gene mutations that upregulate the normally inactive Hedgehog (Hh) pathway, allowing aberrant inhibition of suppressor genes or enhanced transcription of activating genes.8,10 Bonilla et al conducted genetic testing on 236 patients with BCC and found that 85% had mutations in the Hh pathway.11 Sonidegib and vismodegib are the only HHIs approved for advanced BCC to date, although several other HHIs, such as patidegib,12,13 taladegib14 are currently under investigation.

The patient also had chronic renal insufficiency and was diagnosed with stage 4 chronic kidney disease based on renal function indices, the numerical values of the patient’s glomerular filtration rate (GFR) calculated according to the formula are shown in Figure 1.15 According to the prescribing information for sonidegib, the drug is primarily metabolized in the liver and is not renally excreted; therefore, dose adjustment is not required in patients with renal insufficiency. Accordingly, sonidegib was considered an appropriate treatment option. Our findings indicate that sonidegib did not adversely affect hepatic or renal function and was safe in this patient with chronic renal insufficiency. During treatment, only CK levels increased, and mild-to-moderate adverse reactions were observed. Notably, the onset of adverse reactions occurred later than previously reported.16,17 Previous studies have reported that the median time to muscle spasms was 2.3 months; in this patient, it was 4 months. This might be related to individual differences. Overall, the treatment was well tolerated. Temporary treatment interruption and dose reduction did not compromise therapeutic efficacy. Vismodegib, another HHI inhibitor, may have similar safety in patients with chronic renal insufficiency. However, up to now, there have been no reports of HHI inhibitors being used in patients with chronic renal insufficiency.

In our report, after one month of treatment, the patient’s skin lesions have significantly shrunk, and the ulceration and erosion of the lesions have improved markedly compared to before. After 6 months of treatment, the patient achieved complete response, histopathological examination revealed no residual tumor cells. Pigmentation persisted at the original lesion sites, which may have led to clinical misinterpretation, suggesting incomplete tumor regression. Sonidegib is mainly metabolized by Cytochrome P450 3A4 enzyme (CYP3A4). When Sonidegib is used in combination with potent inhibitors or inducers of CYP3A4, it can significantly increase or decrease the concentration of Sonidegib. This patient has a history of hyperuricemia and is being treated with febuxostat tablets. The main metabolic pathway of febuxostat tablets is glucuronidation reaction. According to the drug instructions of febuxostat tablets, it has not been found that it has inhibitory or inducing effects on CYP3A4. Therefore, febuxostat tablets should have no impact on the metabolism of Sonidegib in the patient’s body.

Limitations

This report describes a single case with a limited sample size; therefore, larger prospective studies are needed to confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of sonidegib in patients with chronic renal insufficiency. In addition, multipoint sampling was not performed during histopathological examination after 6 months of treatment. During follow-up, skin lesions should be closely monitored, and if necessary, multipoint biopsies and concurrent skin CT examinations should be performed.

Conclusion

Sonidegib appears to be a safe and effective treatment for inoperable cutaneous BCC complicated by chronic renal insufficiency. It is associated with rapid regression of skin lesions, tolerable adverse reactions, and no apparent impairment of hepatic or renal function. Sonidegib may also facilitate eligibility for other potentially curative treatments, particularly in patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease who are unsuitable for surgery or radiotherapy and have concomitant chronic renal insufficiency.
