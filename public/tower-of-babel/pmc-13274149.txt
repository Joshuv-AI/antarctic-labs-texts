Colorectal adenoma recurrence: an analysis of risk factors and gut microbiota characteristics
Authors: Yujie Chen, Xueru Gao, Li Wang, Chunlin Zhang, Yuhai Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal adenoma (CRA) is precancerous lesion of colorectal cancer (CRC). Early detection and endoscopic resection of CRA are the most effective methods for preventing CRC, yet there is a significant recurrence risk. We aim to analyze the risk factors for CRA recurrence and explore the gut microbiota characteristics in individuals with recurrent CRA, in order to provide new insights for risk stratification and intervention.MethodsWe recruited 30 participants with recurrent CRA and 15 participants without CRA recurrence. Demographic information, baseline characteristics of adenomas, and other clinical data were collected from each participant. Univariate analysis and Firth Logistic regression analysis were used to analyze the risk factors for CRA recurrence. Meanwhile, fecal samples were collected from all participants and 16s rRNA sequencing was performed to analyze the composition, structure, and taxonomic differences of the gut microbiota, as well as to predict potential functional capacities. Correlation analysis is employed to explore the associations between differential gut microbiota and functional pathways.ResultsUnivariate analysis revealed that there were statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) between the two groups in terms of age, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, smoking history, adenoma size, adenoma number, and advanced adenomas. Firth Logistic analysis indicated that adenoma number and smoking history were independent clinical risk factors for CRA recurrence. Microbiomic analysis showed no significant difference (p > 0.05) in the α-diversity and β-diversity of the gut microbiota between the two groups. Taxonomic analysis revealed the abundances of potential pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia-Shigella and Klebsiella were significantly increased in the recurrence group, while the abundances of Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium were significantly reduced. Furthermore, Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis indicated that the non-recurrence group was enriched with genera such as Megasphaera. Functional prediction analysis suggested that pathways related to fatty acid metabolic and biosynthetic process were more active in the non-recurrence group. Correlation analysis uncovered the genera enriched in the recurrence group were positively correlated with the functional pathways of styrene degradation and fat digestion and absorption, whereas the genera with reduced abundance were positively correlated with fat digestion and absorption and MicroRNAs in cance pathways.ConclusionThis study not only elucidates the clinical risk factors for CRA recurrence but also reveals that CRA recurrence may be closely associated with the gut microbiota dysbiosis. Combining clinical risk factors with gut microbial biomarkers is expected to construct a more precise predictive model for adenoma recurrence and provide crucial theoretical support for future intervention strategies targeting the gut microbiota.

Introduction

According to 2020 statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO), the incidence and mortality rates of colorectal cancer (CRC) rank third and second globally respectively [1, 2], posing a severe threat to public health. Colorectal adenomas (CRA), defined as benign tumors originating from the colorectal mucosa [3, 4], are recognized as precursors of CRC [5]. Approximately 70% to 80% of CRC cases develop from CRA [1, 6]. Currently, endoscopic resection of CRA is the most effective approach to reducing the incidence and mortality of CRC [7, 8].

However, approximately 20% to 50% of patients with CRA experience recurrence after endoscopic resection [9, 10], with this proportion increasing to 87% when surveillance exceeds five years [1]. In recent years, numerous domestic and international studies have revealed that CRA recurrence is closely associated with factors such as gender, age, body mass index (BMI), dietary habits, smoking and drinking history, hypertension, coronary heart disease, adenoma size, number of adenomas, and pathological subtypes of adenomas [8, 11]. Additionally, pharmacological prevention has garnered widespread attention. Studies have found that low-dose aspirin [3], metformin [12], folic acid [13], calcium supplements [14] and Chinese herbal medicines [1, 9] (such as Shenbai granules and berberine) can effectively reduce the recurrence rate of CRA. However, due to safety concerns and inconsistent outcomes, no guidelines currently recommend pharmacological prevention strategies for CRA. Therefore, exploring the influencing factors of CRA recurrence and exploring safer and more effective approaches to prevent its recurrence represent urgent issues that need to be addressed.

The gastrointestinal tract is the largest microbial reservoir in the human body, containing approximately 100 trillion gut microorganisms which encompass over 1,000 species and ten times the total number of human cells [6]. The gut microbiota not only participates in the breakdown, digestion, and absorption of amino acids, sugars, and fats but also provides the host with various essential nutrients and promotes the growth and development of intestinal epithelium, besides, the gut microbiota also plays critical roles in suppressing pathogenic bacteria, resisting pathogen invasion, regulating intestinal mucosal immune responses and maintaining intestinal homeostasis [10, 15]. Once this balance is disrupted, adverse effects on the individuals may occur. Numerous studies have shown that gut dysbiosis plays a significant role in the development of various gastrointestinal diseases, such as CRC, colorectal polyps, inflammatory bowel disease, and irritable bowel syndrome [16–18]. Dysbiosis is characterized by a reduction in beneficial bacteria and an increase in pathogenic bacteria, which may contribute to disease development and progression by fostering an inflammatory environment in the gut [10]. However, the intrinsic connection between the gut microbiota and CRA recurrence remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to explore the gut microbiota characteristics of CRA recurrence, seek new biological predictive indicators, and use this as a non-invasive method to assess the risk of CRA recurrence. At the same time, it aims to provide potential therapeutic targets for more effective and safer reduction of CRA recurrence.

Materials and methods

Subjects and study design

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Dongli Hospital (2024041702). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to study initiation. A total of 45 participants were included in this study, comprising 30 participants in the CRA recurrence group (Recur group) and 15 participants in the non-recurrence group (NR group). All patients were diagnosed and treated under the care of the gastroenterology department in Tianjin Dongli Hospital.

The inclusion criteria for our study were as follows: (1) participants had undergone a high-quality colonoscopy with adequate intestinal preparation, completion of cecal intubation and the withdrawal time exceeding 6 min [9] at least six months prior, during which CRA were histologically confirmed, and all detected adenomas were completely removed and evaluated; (2) the medical history and clinical data are complete.The exclusion criteria for our study were as follows: (1) participants with familial adenomatous polyposis, hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer, or other concomitant intestinal diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease; (2) those with coexisting hematological diseases, severe psychiatric disorders (such as schizophrenia), severe underlying diseases including cerebrovascular diseases, cardiopulmonary insufficiency, renal failure, or highly suspected gastrointestinal perforation; (3) administration of antibiotics, immunosuppressants, corticosteroids, or probiotics within three months prior to sample collection.

Participants underwent approximately 1 to 2 years surveillance colonoscopy after endoscopic resection of CRA. Subgroups were established based on the colonoscopy findings. Recur group: recurrence was defined as the diagnosis of colorectal adenoma according to the results of surveillance colonoscopy and histology, regardless of whether it was a recurrence at the original resection site or a newly developed adenoma at a different location; NR group: this group included participants in whom no colorectal adenomas or other intestinal diseases were diagnosed during the surveillance. To reduce the risk of missed polyps, all participating endoscopists were experienced specialists with more than 2,000 colonoscopy procedures, and holding a professional title of associate chief physician or higher. Furthermore, at the beginning of the study, Tianjin Dongli Hospital provided comprehensive training and guidance on the study protocol and operational procedures to all involved endoscopists and pathologists.

Sample and data collection

All participants provided a fresh fecal sample (≥ 300 mg) prior to undergoing follow-up colonoscopy. Each collected sample was aliquoted, immediately stored at 4 °C, and transferred to -80 °C storage within 12 h of collection. Subsequently, all samples were transported on dry ice to LC-Bio Technologies (Hangzhou, China) for gut microbiota analysis via 16 S rRNA sequencing. In addition, we collected the following baseline information for each participant: demographic data (sex, age, BMI), general health information (smoking history, alcohol consumption history, comorbidities), laboratory test results including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, and baseline characteristics of the CRA, including size, location, number, morphology, and pathological type.

BMI is calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. The location of CRA was classified into the left colon (descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum) and the right colon (cecum, ascending colon, and transverse colon). Adenomas were histologically classified based on the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of the Digestive System [19]. Advanced colorectal adenoma need meet one or more of the following criteria [20]: (1) Diameter ≥ 10 mm; (2) Villous component > 25% (in villous or mixed adenomas); (3) Presence of high-grade dysplasia.

Microbiome analysis

The microbial community composition was determined by 16 S sequencing targeting the variable V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene. Fecal samples were stored at -80 °C until further analysis. A selected extraction kit was used to extract total microbial DNA from the microbiome samples. The hypervariable V3-V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using the forward primer (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and the reverse primer (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG). PCR products were purified with AMPure XP beads(Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA) and quantified using Qubit (Invitrogen, USA). The purified PCR products were assessed with a library quantification kit from Illumina(KapaBiosciences, Woburn, MA, USA) and qualified libraries were required to have a concentration above 2 nM. Finally, high-throughput sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ-G99 sequencer using the DNBSEQ-G99RS High-Throughput Sequencing Reagent Kit (G99 App-D FCL PE300).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 software. Measurement data following a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and were compared using the t test. Measurement data with a skewed distribution are expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)] and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Count data are presented as numbers and percentages which were compared using the χ2 test. Firth Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify influencing factors. Microbiota analysis involved cluster analysis and taxonomic analysis, including species annotation, composition analysis, α diversity analysis, β diversity analysis, differential species analysis, and functional prediction analysis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To investigate the potential associations between differential gut microbiota and differential functional pathways, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficients between the relative abundances of the top 20 differential gut microbiota genera and differential KEGG pathway. Correlations with |r| > 0.4 and p < 0.05 are considered statistically significant. The results are presented in the form of a heatmap. The correlation heatmap was drawn based on the R (https://www.rproject.org/) on the OmicStudio platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool).

Results

Subject characteristics

The study population included 45 participants, of which 30 were Recur group and 15 were NR group. The detailed characteristics of all participants are presented in Table 1 (at the end of the document text file). Univariate analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of gender, hypertension, coronary heart disease, history of alcohol consumption, CEA, adenoma location, adenoma pathological type, and whether the adenoma was pedunculated (all p > 0.05). However, the Recur group was significantly older (p = 0.023) and had a significantly higher BMI (p = 0.034) compared to the NR group. Additionally, the proportion of participants with a history of diabetes (p = 0.033) and smoking (p = 0.035) was significantly higher in the Recur group. Furthermore, the number of adenomas was significantly greater in the Recur group than in the NR group (p = 0.002), and the proportion of participants with advanced adenomas was also significantly higher in the Recur group (p = 0.044).

Differences in Clinical Data Between the Two Groups

Firth Logistic regression analysis of CRA recurrence

Using CRA recurrence as the dependent variable and factors showing statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis as independent variables, a further Firth Logistic regression analysis was performed. The results revealed that smoking history (p = 0.033) and the number of adenomas (p = 0.005) were independent risk factors for CRA recurrence (Table 2). Specifically, patients with smoking history had an 7.248-fold higher risk of postoperative recurrence compared to those with non-smoking history. For each additional adenoma, the risk of postoperative recurrence increased by 27.8%.

Gut microbiota diversity analysis

Diversity analysis of gut microbiota across groups was performed by clustering all samples into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). The distribution of ASVs is presented in a Venn diagram (Fig. 1). A total of 945 ASVs were shared between the Recur group and NR group. The NR group harbored 949 unique ASVs, whereas the Recur group possessed a markedly higher number of unique ASVs (2,075). This substantial set of specific ASVs of Recur group may include potential conditional pathogens or pro-inflammatory genera, suggesting that their proliferation could disrupt the normal ecological balance and be linked to disease pathogenesis.

To investigate microbial dysbiosis associated with CRA recurrence, we compared α and β diversity metrics between NR and Recur groups. α diversity was assessed using the Chao1, Simpson, and Shannon indices. We observed that compared with the NR group, the Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 indices in the Recur group were all decreased, although the differences in Shannon, Simpson, and Chao1 indices were not statistically signiffcant (p > 0.05). It still suggested a trend toward reduced species richness, diversity, and distribution uniformity of gut microbiota in the Recur group (Fig. 2A-C).

Taxonomic composition anaiysis of the gut microbiota

We assessed the overall taxonomic composition of the gut microbiota across multiple taxonomic levels in both NR and Recur groups. At the phylum level, the dominant microbiota in all samples mainly consisted of five major groups: Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Verrucomicrobiota (Fig. 4A-B). However, compared to the NR group, the Recur group exhibited an increased relative abundance of Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota and Verrucomicrobiota, alongside a decreased relative abundance of Bacillota and Bacteroidota. At the family level, the Recur group exhibited an increased abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, and Akkermansiaceae, and a decreased abundance of Ruminococcaceae, Bacteroidaceae, Streptococcaceae, and Veillonellaceae (Fig. 4C-D). Zooming into the genus level, the differences were more specific: the Recur group had an increased abundance of Escherichia-Shigella, Bifidobacterium, Akkermansia, Klebsiella, and Segatella, while the abundance of Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, Streptococcus, and Dialister decreased (Fig. 4E-F).

Gut microbiota differential analysis

To further identify microbial taxa with statistically significant differences between the NR group and Recur group, we employed Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis. we observed the enrichment of ten genera, including Citrobacter, Megasphaera, Acetitomaculum, Fannyhessea, [Eubacterium] ventriosum, Sutterella, as well as one family (Sphingobacteriaceae) in the NR group (Fig. 5A). The bar chart visually demonstrates that NR group exhibited a greater number of significantly enriched bacterial genera, notably including Citrobacter. At the species level, Citrobacter koseri was enriched in the NR group, and numerous “unclassified” species also exhibited significant abundance differences between the two groups (Fig. 5B). This finding not only highlights the limitations of current microbial reference databases but also suggests important, yet uncharacterized taxa may still play a key role in the dysbiosis associated with CRA recurrence.

Predicted functional profiles of the gut microbiota

To infer the potential functional capacities of the gut microbiota, we performed a prediction analysis based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data using PICRUSt2 against the KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and GO (Gene Ontology) database. GO database was used to classify their biological processes, cellular components and molecular functions. As to the biological processes, fatty acid metabolic process, fatty acid biosynthetic process, chitin metabolic process and chitin catabolic process were more enriched in NR group, while cleavage involved in rRNA processing, glycyl−tRNA aminoacylation, stringent response, and interaction with host were more enriched in Recur group (p < 0.05). As to the molecular functions, glucan 1,4 − α − glucosidase activity was more enriched in NR group, while 2 − isopropylmalate synthase activity, UMP kinase activity, adenyl deoxyribonucleotide binding, deoxyribonucleotide binding and so on were more enriched in Recur group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6).

To further explore the signaling pathways related to the different intestinal flora, KEGG pathway analysis was employed. At Level 3 of the KEGG hierarchy, fat digestion and absorption, styrene degradation, xylene degradation were more enriched in NR group than Recur group (p < 0.05), while microRNAs in cancer was more enriched in Recur group than NR group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7). Based on these results, it is speculated that gut microbiota dysbiosis may promote the recurrence of CRA by affecting metabolic processes, catabolic and biosynthetic pathways, enzymatic activity, abnormal RNA expression, and so on.

Correlation Analysis of Differential Gut Microbiota and Functional Pathways

To further investigate the potential correlations between key microbial taxa and differential functional pathways, we performed Spearman correlation analysis. The result (Fig. 8) showed that at the genus level, the potentially pro-inflammatory genera Escherichia-Shigella and Klebsiella, which increased in abundance in the Recur group, were positively correlated with the styrene degradation and fat digestion and absorption pathways. These associations suggest that the increase in these bacteria may reduce the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are essential for maintaining gut barrier integrity and immune regulation. Conversely, the genera with decreased abundance in the Recur group, Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium, were positively correlated with the fat digestion and absorption pathway and the microRNAs in cancer pathway.

Discussion

Colorectal polyps are benign growths on the inner lining of the colon or rectum, which are classified into four types: adenomatous, inflammatory, hyperplastic, and hamartomatous polyps according to the WHO [21]. Compared with other types of polyps, adenomatous polyps, especially advanced CRA, carry a higher risk of developing into CRC and recurrence after endoscopic resection [5, 22]. Thus, detecting recurrent adenomas following CRA polypectomy is critical for reducing the incidence of CRC. This study found that age, BMI, diabetes, smoking history, adenoma size, adenoma number, and advanced adenomas are closely associated with the recurrence of CRA. Among these factors, adenoma number and advanced adenomas are independent risk factors for CRA recurrence.

This study demonstrates that the Recur group had a higher proportion of individuals aged over 60 years, which is consistent with the findings of many previous studies [23]. The reason may be that older adults have a higher prevalence of colonic dysfunction and constipation, making the colon more susceptible to irritation and leading to chronic inflammation. Meanwhile, the capacity for repair and regeneration of intestinal mucosal cells declines with advancing age, which further contributes to the increased recurrence rate of CRA in elderly patients [23, 24]. Furthermore, this study also found a significant association between BMI and the CRA recurrence, with a higher proportion of individuals in the Recur group having a BMI ≥ 24 kg/m². This finding also aligns with previous research results [22, 23]. The underlying mechanism may be that obesity promotes the expression of insulin and insulin-like growth factor-1, which can facilitate the transformation of non-adenomatous polyps into adenomatous polyps and increasing the risk of recurrence [2, 21]. In this study, we also discovered patients with a history of diabetes had a significantly higher risk of CRA recurrence compared to those without diabetes, which is also consistent with previous studies [25]. Diabetes can slow gastrointestinal motility, which may subsequently lead to constipation and difficulty in defecation [26]. The prolonged retention of intestinal feces increases the exposure of the intestinal mucosa to toxins and carcinogens, damages the intestinal mucosal barrier, and ultimately promotes CRA formation [26]. Smoking history was also found to be associated with CRA recurrence in this study, serving as an independent risk factor for recurrence. Cigarettes contain numerous carcinogenic components, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, heterocyclic amines, and nitrosamines [23]. Previous research also indicates that smoking is an independent potential risk factor for CRA recurrence, as it induces oxidative stress and DNA damage, and generate various carcinogens [8]. Moreover, patients with a smoking history over 35 years show a significantly increased recurrence rate of CRA [23].

The characteristics of adenomas are also closely associated with the risk of CRA recurrence. Our study found that adenoma number and size were significantly correlated with CRA recurrence, with adenoma number being an independent risk factor for recurrence. Each additional adenoma increased the recurrence risk by 27.8%, which is consistent with previous findings. Previous research indicates that patients with three or more CRA have a significantly higher recurrence rate, and adenoma number is an independent risk factor for CRA recurrence [21]. Furthermore, adenomas with a diameter ≥ 1 cm and those containing more villous components tend to develop faster and recur more readily, whereas patients with adenomas ≤ 5 mm in diameter have a lower recurrence rate [21, 27]. Our study also revealed that a higher proportion of individuals with advanced CRA in the Recur group. Previous studies have also reported that advanced CRA have a higher recurrence rate compared to non-advanced adenomas, especially in advanced CRA with a diameter of ≥ 15 mm (the recurrence rate is approximately 57.9%) [28, 29].

In summary, this study not only elucidates the impact of patient demographic factors on recurrence but also reveals the influence of adenoma characteristics on recurrence. However, growing evidence indicates that the gut microbiota participates in the development of colorectal tumors by modulating mucosal immunity, metabolizing bile acids, and producing pro-inflammatory substances [30]. Therefore, we hypothesize that alterations in the gut microbiota may serve as a key microenvironmental factor driving CRA recurrence. Based on this, our study further compared and analyzed the gut microbiota characteristics between Recur group and NR group. This approach not only contributes to a deeper understanding of the disease pathogenesis but also provides support for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we found no significant changes in species richness or community diversity between Recur group and NR group. Consistent with previous research, endoscopic resection of CRA did not alter the overall structure of the gut microbiota, while the abundance of protective bacterial groups showed an increasing trend and tumor associated bacteria exhibited a decreasing trend [4]. Similarly, in this study, taxonomic analysis at the phylum, family, and genus levels revealed significant dysbiosis in the gut microbiota of participants with CRA recurrence. In the Recur group, the abundances of Klebsiella and Escherichia-Shigella were markedly increased. Studies have shown that the pathogenic bacteria Klebsiella, a Gram-negative organism, which is associated with intestinal infections and antibiotic resistance. Klebsiella can produce toxic substances that disrupt intestinal barrier function, trigger local inflammatory responses, and promote the development of colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, and colonic polyps [10, 31]. Escherichia-Shigella has been also confirmed to be associated with intestinal inflammation, immune activation, epithelial cell damage and endotoxin production [32]. These findings suggest that Klebsiella and Escherichia-Shigella may play a potential role in the formation and recurrence of CRA. Conversely, probiotics including Faecalibacterium and Bacteroides were significantly reduced in patients with CRA recurrence. A decrease in the abundance of probiotics may impair intestinal barrier function, increase intestinal inflammation, and thereby promote the formation and recurrence of polyps [10]. Faecalibacterium is a strictly anaerobic, highly oxygen-sensitive, Gram-positive, rod-shaped, non-motile, and non-spore-forming bacterial genus [33]. Numerous studies have indicated that the abundance of Faecalibacterium is reduced in various diseases, including CRC, inflammatory bowel disease, and depression. Faecalibacterium can alleviate intestinal inflammation by modulating the immune system, promoting anti-inflammatory factors expression (such as IL-10), and inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors expression (such as IL-12). Meanwhile, consistent with the results of this correlation analysis, Faecalibacterium can also promote the production of SCFAs (such as butyrate), suppress harmful bacteria growth, and enhance intestinal barrier and anti-inflammatory functions [33].

Based on LEfSe analysis, further differences in the gut microbiota between NR group and Recur group were revealed. The results indicated that the NR group exhibited a greater number of significantly enriched bacterial genera, such as Megasphaera.Megasphaera, belonging to the family Veillonellaceae, is a gram-negative anaerobic cocci type [34]. V.D. Bamol et al. has been reported Megasphaera is more enriched in healthy individuals compared to CRC patients [35]. Additionally, previous studies have also found that Megasphaera is present in the tumors of long-term survivors of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and can enhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy by inducing the production of SCFAs such as butyrate and valerate, and modulating CD8 + T cell responses [36]. However, other researchers have observed high levels of Megasphaera in patients with inflammatory bowel disease and CRC, where it reshapes colonic immunity by triggering dendritic cell‑mediated Th1 and Th17 inflammatory responses [34]. These findings suggest that intestinal microbial dysbiosis is a significant risk factor for CRA recurrence. Enhancing probiotic levels and targeting specific pathogenic bacteria may represent important strategies for preventing CRA recurrence.

Based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing data, GO and KEGG functional prediction analyses provided important clues for a deeper understanding of the potential functions of gut microbiota in patients with recurrent and non‑recurrent CRA. GO functional analysis revealed that the microbiota in the NR group was significantly enriched in fatty acid metabolic and biosynthetic process. Previous studies have shown that the levels of SCFAs, such as butyrate and propionate, are higher in the gut of healthy individuals, and SCFAs play important roles in maintaining intestinal health and mitigating intestinal inflammation. In contrast, patients with CRA exhibit reduced levels of SCFAs along with elevated levels of amine metabolites and bile acids, which may further exacerbate intestinal inflammatory responses and promote CRA progression [10, 37]. KEGG pathway analysis further validated and expanded the aforementioned functional differences at the signaling pathway level. It also revealed that the gut microbiota in the NR group was significantly enriched in the fat digestion and absorption pathway, which closely aligns with the enrichment of “fatty acid metabolic and biosynthetic process” identified in the GO analysis of the same group. Furthermore, the enrichment of the NR group in xenobiotic metabolic pathways such as styrene degradation and xylene degradation is particularly noteworthy. This strongly suggests that the gut microbiota in the NR group may possess an ability to detoxify or degrade potentially harmful environmental compounds. We suppose the function could indirectly protect the intestinal mucosa and reduce chronic inflammation or genotoxic damage by decreasing the intestinal load of endogenous toxins or chemical irritants, which potentially provide a microbial barrier mechanism for preventing CRA recurrence. This hypothesis need further validation in future studies. Conversely, microRNAs (miRNA) associated with cancer were significantly enriched in the Recur group which is of great significance in microbial function prediction. MiRNAs are non-coding RNA molecules of 18–25 nucleotides that regulate the expression of target genes by binding to partially complementary recognition sequences in target mRNAs, and alterations in miRNA expression play a critical role in human cancers [38]. Previous studies have shown that gut microbiota and their metabolites can directly or indirectly influence the expression of certain microRNAs, creating a microenvironment more conducive to development or recurrence of tumor, for instance, low expression of miR-194 has been identified as an independent predictor of recurrence in CRA [39].

This study still has several limitations. Firstly, the sample size is relatively limited and it is a single-center study, therefore validation in larger and multi-center cohorts is necessary in future research. Secondly, the functional prediction based on 16 S sequencing and databases in this study has limitations, and further validation through metagenomics, metatranscriptomics and metabolomics is required to elucidate the precise mechanisms of these pathways in CRA recurrence.

Conclusion

This study not only identifies risk factors for CRA recurrence but also provides new insights into the mechanisms of CRA recurrence from a microbiome perspective. The gut microbiota is not merely an observer, but may actively participate in and regulate the microenvironmental homeostasis of the colorectal mucosal by influencing immunity, metabolite production, and the integrity of the mucosal barrier. Future research could focus on functional validation and metabolomic studies of these differential microbial genera, exploring their potential as predictive microbial biomarkers for CRA recurrence, thereby providing novel targets for clinical intervention.
