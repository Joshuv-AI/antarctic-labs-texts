Collapsing in polygonal dynamics
Jean-Baptiste Stiegler

Abstract
Polygonal dynamics is a family of dynamical systems containing many studied systems, like the famous pentagram map. Similar collapsing phenomena seem to occur in most of these systems. We give a unifying, general definition of polygonal dynamics, and conjecture that a generic orbit collapses towards a predictable point. For the special case of “closed polygons”, we show that the collapse point depends algebraicly on the vertices of the starting polygon, using tools called scaling symmetry and infinitesimal monodromy. This holds regardless of the validity of the conjecture. As a corollary, this generalises previous results about the pentagram map. Then, we investigate the case of polygonal dynamics in ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}^{1} for which we give an explicit polynomial equation satisfied by the collapse point. Based on previous works, we define a new dynamical system, the “staircase” cross-ratio dynamics, for which we study particular configurations.

Let

Since

3.2
and

Lemma 2.2.

Let
k
be an algebraicly closed, complete field for the metric coming from a non-trivial valuation.

Then a generic element of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}(k)
has one attractive and one repelling fixed point.

Proof.

Let
M\in\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}(k)
. The set of diagonalisable matrices of
\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}(k)
is Zariski dense; indeed,
M
is diagonalisable if its characteristic polynomial
\chi_{M}(X)
have distinct roots. This happens if the discriminant, which is a polynomial in the coefficients
\Delta(a_{1,1},\dots,a_{d+1,d+1})
, doesn’t vanish. Since this is not the zero polynomial, we obtain a Zariski-open set. It is not empty since it contains the identity.

So up to conjugation we can assume that:

M=diag(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{d+1}),

with
|\lambda_{1}|\leq\dots\leq|\lambda_{d+1}|
. Since the valuation is non-trivial, then out of genericity we can assume that the inequalities are in fact strict. In
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}
, we can take the representant with
\lambda_{d+1}=1
. So:

\lim_{N\to\infty}M^{N}=E_{d+1,d+1}.

Thus
M
has an attractive fixed point in
\mathbb{P}^{d}
, being
[0:\dots:0:1]
(the origin).

We can do similarly for
M^{-1}
and take a representant with
\lambda_{1}=1
. This yields:

\lim_{N\to\infty}M^{-N}=E_{1,1},

and the repelling fixed point of

is
[1:0:\dots:0]
(“the” point at infinity).
∎

The following fact is classical but we’ll prove it anyway. If we apply it in
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}

Lemma 2.3.

Let

be two commuting matrices such that all the eigenvalues of

are distinct. Then

and
M^{\prime}
share the same eigenlines.

Proof.

Let

be such that
Mx=\lambda x
. Hence we have:

MM^{\prime}x=M^{\prime}Mx=\lambda M^{\prime}x,

so

is an eigenvector of

. Since the eigenvalues are all distinct,
M^{\prime}x
and

lie on the same eigenline of
M
, and thus

is an eigenvector of
M^{\prime}
.
∎

Definition 2.4.

A dynamical system

3.1
A collapse conjecture for twisted polygons

A simple observation shows what are the potential collapse points given the monodromy matrix
M
. Generically, there will be only a finite number of candidates.

Lemma 3.1.

Let
P=((p_{1},\dots,p_{n}),M)
be a twisted
n
-gon. Suppose that under the iteration of
T
, the vertices of P all collapse towards the same value
q
. Then
q
has to be a fixed point of
M
.

Proof.

Write
P^{(N)}:=T^{N}(P)
. Since
T
preserves the monodromy, we have that for all
N\in\mathbb{Z}
:

p_{i+n}^{(N)}=Mp_{i}^{(N)}.

But since
M
is continuous, by taking the limit we get:

q=Mq.

∎

Now we can state the conjecture. It’s inspired by a question raised by Schwartz in his original paper
[
26
, §4]
. He stated in our private communication that no progress have been achieved since 1992.

Conjecture 3.2.

Let
k
be an algebraicly closed, complete field for the metric coming from a non-trivial valuation.
Let
T
be a polygonal transformation taking place in
\mathbb{P}^{d}(k)
, such that the dynamic on the moduli space is discretely integrable.

Then for a generic choice of twisted
n
-gon
P=((p_{1},\dots,p_{n}),M)
, the vertices of
P
collapse under the iteration of
T
in the past/future towards fixed points of
M
.

We only managed to prove the conjecture in the case of periodic motion on the moduli space. We know that such a periodic motion is possible for the pentagram map (see
[
18
]
and the link with Poncelet polygons) and for the staircase cross ratio dynamics (see subsection
6.2
).

Partial proof.

Let
P=((p_{1},\dots,p_{n}),M)
be twisted
n
-gon and
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
be such that
T^{N}([P])=[P]
. In the initial space, this means that there exists
\tilde{M}\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}
such that
T^{N}(P)=\tilde{M}(P)
. By lemma
2.2
, we can assume that
\tilde{M}
generically has an attractive and a repelling fixed point. Since
T
preserves the monodromy of
P
, we obtain:

M=\tilde{M}M\tilde{M}^{-1},

hence
M
and
\tilde{M}
commute. Out of genericity we can assume that
M
has distinct fixed points, and lemma
2.3
grants that
\tilde{M}
share the same fixed points.

Since
T
commutes with the action of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}
, we have:

T^{2N}(P)=T^{N}(\tilde{M}(P))=\tilde{M}(T^{N}(P))=\tilde{M}^{2}(P).

By recurrence (that we can also apply backwards), we get:

k\in\mathbb{Z},\penalty\ T^{kN}(P)=\tilde{M}^{k}(P).

We also have that for all
1\leq q<N,\penalty\ k\in\mathbb{{Z}}
:

T^{kN+q}(P)=T^{q}(T^{kN}(P))=T^{q}(\tilde{M}^{k}(P))=\tilde{M}^{k}(T^{q}(P)).

We can assume that none of the

contains a vertex which is fixed by
\tilde{M}
. If we denote by
q_{\pm}
the attractive/repelling fixed points of
\tilde{M}
, then:

\lim_{k\to\pm\infty}T^{k}(P)=((q_{\pm},\dots,q_{\pm}),M)

∎

Now the problem is: how to prove it for quasi-periodic motion, which is the truly generic case ? Since the motion is recurrent on the moduli space, one could try to mimic the case of periodic motion to say that some subsequence of the iterate “behaves asymptotically the same” as the iteration of a projective transformation.

The meaning of “behaving asymptotically the same” is yet unclear. One strategy could be to use generalized schwarzian derivatives (see
[
23
]
), but we haven’t succeeded.

Corollaries for closed polygons

The previous conjecture used the fact that the monodromy of a polygon has
d+1
distinct fixed points (which is generic in
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}
). However, the original questions were formulated for closed polygons, and
\mathrm{Id}
fixes every point.

However, if the system we’re interested in admits a scaling symmetry, we can use the infinitesimal monodromy of a polygon to retrieve the “true fixed points” of the identity; using the previous work for twisted polygons, we can find the potential collapse points for the closed ones. Regardless of the validity of conjecture
3.2
, the next theorem gives us a non-trivial analogue of lemma
3.1
.

Theorem 3.3.

Let
T
be a polygonal transformation taking place in
\mathbb{P}^{d}(k)
where
k
is a complete field, such that the dynamic admits a scaling symmetry. Let
P
be a closed polygon and
M^{\prime}
be its infinitesimal monodromy.

If the vertices of
P
collapse to a point
q
under the iteration of
T
, then
q
has to be an eigenline of
M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Suppose that the vertices of
P
collapse to the point
q
under the iteration of
T
. Then, since the scaling symmetry conjugates the dynamic, we also have that the vertices of the twisted polygons
P_{z}=((p_{1}(z),\dots,p_{n}(z)),M_{z})
collapse to some points
q_{\pm}(z)
. Because of lemma
3.1
, they have to be fixed points of
M_{z}
. For
z\sim 1
we have

M_{z}=\mathrm{Id}+(z-1)M^{\prime}+o(z-1),

so the fixed points of
M_{z}
tend towards the ones of
M^{\prime}
. Since
q=q(1)
, we have the result.
∎

If the scaling symmetry acts by birational transformations, which is the case in every encountered situation so far, we can give a generalization of
[
14
, Thm. 1.1]
.

Corollary 3.4.

Let
P
be a closed
n
-gon in
\mathbb{P}^{d}
and
(p_{i}=[p_{i}^{(0)}:\dots:p_{i}^{(d)}])_{i=1,\dots,n}
be its vertices. Let
q=[q^{(0)}:\dots:q^{(d)}]
be a potential collapse point predicted by theorem
3.3
. If the scaling symmetry acts by birational transformations, then
q^{(0)},\dots,q^{(d)}
are contained in a field extension of
\mathbb{Q}\big((p_{i}^{(j)})_{i=1,\dots,n}^{j=0,\dots,d}\big)
of degree at most
d+1
.

Proof.

If the scaling symmetry acts by birational transformations, then the coefficients of the infinitesimal monodromy are rational functions in
p_{i}^{(j)}
. Because of theorem
3.3
, a potential collapse point
q=[q^{(0)}:\dots:q^{(d)}]
is an eigenline of this
(d+1)\times(d+1)
matrix. To find it, one needs to solve a linear system with
(d+1)
equations involving the coefficient of the matrix, hence the result.
∎

In the case of the pentagram map and its generalizations, we can apply both the theorem and the corollary. Note that it holds for non-convex, and even non-real polygons. This answers the questions raised by Schwartz in
[
28
, §9.2]
.

Corollary 3.5.

Let
k
be a complete field. For the pentagram map and its generalisations in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(k)
, the only possible collapse points, in the future and the past, are eigenlines of Glick’s operator.

Proof.

Aboud and Izosimov proved in
[
1
]
that Glick’s operator coincides with the infinitesimal monodromy. Their argument in fact holds on any complete field, hence the result. In fact, their computation of the infinitesimal monodromy coupled with our theorem
3.3
allows the circumvent the nice work made by Glick in
[
14
]
.
∎

Let’s conclude this section with another conjecture about the generic collapse of closed polygons. That would be in fact a consequence of the conjecture
3.2
, which concerns generic twisted polygons (and hence doesn’t include the closed polygons).

If the conjecture
3.2
for twisted polygons is true, we can use the scaling symmetry and apply theorem
3.3
to get the following for closed polygons. So far, all numerical experiments suggest it to be true (see figure
4
for instance).

Conjecture 3.6.

Let
k
be an algebraicly closed, complete field for the metric coming from a non-trivial valuation.
Let
T
be a polygonal transformation in
\mathbb{P}^{d}(k)
, such that the dynamic admits a scaling symmetry and is discretely integrable on the moduli space.

Let
P
be a generic closed polygon, and
M^{\prime}
be its infinitesimal monodromy. Then there exist
q_{-}
and
q_{+}
two fixed points of
M^{\prime}
such that, under the iteration of
T
, the vertices of
P
collapse in the past/future towards
q_{-}
and
q_{+}
respectively.

Remark 3.

It would be interesting to consider a similar statement in the setting of polygonal dynamics on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the group
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For instance in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Benoist and Hulin prove in
[
9
]
that when
n=4
, the iteration of some element of the folding group is generically conjugated, on the moduli space, to a translation on an elliptic curve. This matches definition
2.4
we gave for integrability. The authors conclude that, on the initial space, the polygons drifts towards a point on the line at infinity, following some “drifting vector”. When seen on the projective plane, the fixed points of positive isometries are on the line at infinity, which is coherent with our conjecture.

4
Polygonal dynamics in
\mathbb{P}^{1}

For any polygonal dynamic in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, we can make a precise version of theorem
3.3
and corollary
3.4
. This is presented is theorem
4.2
.

We will apply it to three kinds of polygonal dynamics: the leapfrog map, the “flat” cross-ratio and the newly defined “stair-case” cross-ratio dynamics (the only ones we know). The two last ones are linked to the theory of discrete holomorphic functions (see
[
4
, §6]
) and are endowed with a
discrete curvature
, which is an
n
-periodic sequence
\mu=(\mu_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where each
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{G}_{m}
should be thought to live on the edge linking
p_{i}
and
p_{i+1}
. They are left unchanged by the action of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}
, but can be modified by the scaling symmetry.

For any
n
-gon in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, define its cross-ratio coordinates to be:

c_{i}:=[p_{i},p_{i+1},p_{i-1},p_{i+2}]=\frac{p_{i}-p_{i-1}}{p_{i}-p_{i+2}}\frac{p_{i+1}-p_{i+2}}{p_{i+1}-p_{i-1}},\quad i\in\mathbb{Z}.

They are
n
-periodic and invariant under projective transformations.

Remark 4.

Sometimes another convention is taken for the cross-ratio, which is:

(a,b,c,d)=\frac{a-b}{b-c}\frac{c-d}{d-a}.

We have the relation:

[a,b,c,d]=1-(a,b,c,d).

In order to stay coherent with
[
6
]
, we’ll stick to the first convention.

Here’s a lemma that retrieves the monodromy of any polygon in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Its proof can be found in the original paper.

Lemma 4.1 –
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Let
P=((p_{1},\dots,p_{n}),M)
be a
n
-gon in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
c_{i}=[p_{i},p_{i+1},p_{i-1},p_{i+2}]
be its cross-ratio coordinates. Up to conjugation, suppose that
p_{0}=1,\penalty\ p_{1}=\infty,\penalty\ p_{2}=0
. Then in this projective chart, the monodromy matrix is:

M=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{1}\\
-1&1\\
\end{array}}\right)\dots\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{n}\\
-1&1\\
\end{array}}\right).

When we have a scaling symmetry, this gives us a formula for the infinitesimal monodromy. Combined with theorem
3.3
, this tells us what can be the collapse points for the dynamics.

Theorem 4.2.

Suppose to have a polygonal dynamic on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
with a scaling symmetry
\rho
. Let
P
be a closed
n
-gon, and
c_{i}(z)
the cross-ratio coordinates of
P_{z}
. Then the infinitesimal monodromy of
P
is

M^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}n/2+J&-K\\
I&n/2-J\end{pmatrix},

where

I=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{1}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}},\quad J=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{p_{i}+p_{i+1}}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}},\quad K=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{p_{i}p_{i+1}}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}.

Furthermore, its eigenvectors
(X,Y)^{T}
satisfy the polynomial equation

\chi_{P}(X,Y):=IX^{2}-2JXY+KY^{2}=0.

Remark 5.

A similar matrix already appeared in
[
6
, §6.2.1]
. This answers the question asked at the end of
[
5
]
, about the link between the pentagram map and cross-ratio dynamics.

Remark 6.

As we’ll see in the proof, the infinitesimal monodromy and the polynomial
\chi_{P}
rewrite themselves as:

\displaystyle M^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{1}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}\begin{pmatrix}p_{i}&-p_{i}p_{i+1}\\
1&-p_{i+1}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\chi_{P}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{(X-p_{i}Y)(X-p_{i+1}Y)}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}.

Remark 7.

The dynamic of any closed 3-gon is trivial on the moduli space for any system in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, since the action of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}
is 3-transitive. So on the initial space, the iteration of
T
is equivalent to the iteration of a projective transformation.

In some systems (like the flat cross-ratio dynamics
[
6
, Lemma 5.3]
), the iteration of
T^{2}
on closed 4-gon is also trivial on the moduli space. This is reminiscent of classical results on the pentagram map (see
[
26
, §2]
). It provides one more evidence in favour of conjecture
3.2
.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of
[
1
, Prop. 4.1]
.
By applying the scaling symmetry, we deform
M
into

M_{z}=\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{1}(z)\\
-1&1\\
\end{array}}\right)\dots\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{n}(z)\\
-1&1\\
\end{array}}\right).

For
1\leq i\leq n
, define
M_{z,i}
to be the product of the first
i
matrices (so
M_{z,n}=M_{z}
), and set
M_{z,0}=\mathrm{Id}
. Note that

\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{i}(z)\\
-1&1\\
\end{array}}\right)\dots\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{n}(z)\\
-1&1\\
\end{array}}\right)=M_{z,i-1}^{-1}M_{z}.

This gives that

\frac{dM_{z}}{dz}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_{z,i-1}\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{i}^{\prime}(z)\\
0&0\\
\end{array}}\right)M_{z,i}^{-1}\right)M_{z}.

Since
M_{1}=\mathrm{Id}
, then the infinitesimal monodromy is

M^{\prime}:=\left.\frac{dM_{z}}{dz}\right|_{z=1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_{i},\quad\text{where }S_{i}:=M_{1,i-1}\left({\begin{array}[]{cc}0&c_{i}^{\prime}(1)\\
0&0\\
\end{array}}\right)M_{1,i}^{-1}.

Since we chose the projective chart where
p_{0}=1,p_{1}=\infty,p_{2}=0
, we obtain the lifts
V_{i}\in k^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
such that

V_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
1\end{pmatrix},V_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},V_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

By proceeding like in
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
, we get that the matrices act this way:

M_{1}:\begin{cases}V_{0}&\mapsto c_{1}V_{1}\\
V_{1}&\mapsto-V_{2}\\
V_{2}&\mapsto V_{3}\\
\begin{pmatrix}c_{2}\\
1\end{pmatrix}&\mapsto V_{4}\\
\end{cases}.

Indeed, the first three lines are given by direct computation, and the last one comes from the observation that:

c_{2}=[1,\infty,0,c_{2}]=[p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},M_{1}(c_{2})].

By definition of
c_{2}
we obtain that
M_{1}(c_{2})=p_{4}
, which means that:

M_{1}\begin{pmatrix}c_{2}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=V_{4}.

Similarly, we get by recurrence that for
2\leq i\leq n
:

M_{i}:\begin{cases}V_{0}&\mapsto-c_{i}V_{i}\\
V_{1}&\mapsto-V_{i+1}\\
V_{2}&\mapsto V_{i+2}\\
\begin{pmatrix}c_{i+1}\\
1\end{pmatrix}&\mapsto V_{i+3}\end{cases}.

Since
p_{i}\neq p_{i+1}
, then
(V_{i},V_{i+1})
forms a basis of
k^{2}
. By a simple computation we get:

S_{i}:\begin{cases}V_{i}&\mapsto\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}V_{i}\\
V_{i+1}&\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\end{pmatrix}\end{cases}.

We can decompose any
V\in k^{2}
as

V=\frac{|V,V_{i+1}|}{|V_{i},V_{i+1}|}V_{i}+\frac{|V_{i},V|}{|V_{i},V_{i+1}|}V_{i+1},

where
|,|
denotes the determinant of the
2\times 2
matrix made out the column vectors. In this basis, the linear map associated to
S_{i}
is:

V\mapsto\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{|V,V_{i+1}|}{|V_{i},V_{i+1}|}V_{i}.

Notice that this linear map commutes with the diagonal action of
\mathrm{GL}_{d+1}
on the vectors. Indeed, the ratio of the determinants remains unchanged. Moreover, the previous linear map is independent of the choice of lifts
V_{i}
’s. So up to a projective transformation, we can suppose that
p_{i}\neq\infty
for all
i
and choose the lifts:

V_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{i}\\
1\end{pmatrix}\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

In this choice of affine chart, the matrix expression of the linear map is:

S_{i}=\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{1}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}\begin{pmatrix}p_{i}&-p_{i}p_{i+1}\\
1&-p_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}.

By summing them, we conclude that:

M^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{1}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}\begin{pmatrix}p_{i}&-p_{i}p_{i+1}\\
1&-p_{i+1}\end{pmatrix}.

A small computation makes the quantities
I,J,K
appear. Note that the eigenvectors of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}n/2+J&-K\\
I&n/2-J\end{pmatrix}

are the same that the ones of

\begin{pmatrix}J&-K\\
I&-J\end{pmatrix}.

The eigenvalues change, but it doesn’t matter for our purpose. Let
\begin{pmatrix}X\\
Y\end{pmatrix}
be an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, then:

\begin{cases}JX-KY&=\lambda X\\
IX-JY&=\lambda Y\end{cases}.

By multiplying the first line by
Y
and the second by
X
, we get by subtracting them:

IX^{2}-2JXY+KY^{2}=0.

This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.3.

The quantities
I,J,K
are invariant by the dynamic. Furthermore, the quantity
\Delta=:J^{2}-IK
is invariant under the action of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}
, but the quantities
I,J,K
are not. They change under the action of the generators as the following:

\displaystyle(z\mapsto z-\lambda)\cdot[I,2J,K]

\displaystyle=[I,-(2I\lambda-2J),I\lambda^{2}-2J\lambda+K]

\displaystyle=[I,-\chi^{\prime}_{P}(\lambda,1),\chi_{P}(\lambda,1)],\qquad\penalty\ \lambda\in k,

\displaystyle(z\mapsto\lambda z)\cdot[I,2J,K]

\displaystyle=[I\lambda^{-1},2J,K\lambda],\qquad\qquad\qquad\lambda\in k^{*},

\displaystyle(z\mapsto\frac{-1}{z})\cdot[I,2J,K]

\displaystyle=[K,-2J,I].

Proof.

The invariance of
I,J,K
under the dynamic simply comes from the invariance of the infinitesimal monodromy.

The quantity
\Delta=J^{2}-IK
is the determinant of the polynomial
\chi_{P}
, and so it is projectively invariant.

The action of projective transformations does not change the cross-ratio coordinates, but only the
p_{i}
’s. The modifications of
I,J,K
under the action of the generators are given by a simple computation.
∎

Some objects keep reappearing in each polygonal dynamic in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, but we couldn’t make a unifying statement. Let’s conclude this section with open questions.

Open question 1.

Define the following 1-form and (usually presymplectic) 2-form:

\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{dp_{i}+dp_{i+1}}{p_{i+1}-p_{i}},\quad\Omega=d\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}\frac{dp_{i}\wedge dp_{i+1}}{(p_{i}-p_{i+1})^{2}}.

Long specific computations show that in each system, the
\Omega
is invariant through the dynamic, meaning
T^{*}\Omega=\Omega
(see
[
6
, Thm. 15]
and
[
13
, Cor. 5.15]
). Could we prove it for any polygonal dynamic in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
?

Moreover, if we consider the generators of the infinitesimal action of
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}},

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}p_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}},

\displaystyle w=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}p_{k}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{k}},

then a little computation similar to the one done in
[
6
, §6.2]
shows that

\displaystyle i_{u}\Omega

\displaystyle=dI,

\displaystyle i_{v}\Omega

\displaystyle=dJ,

\displaystyle i_{w}\Omega

\displaystyle=dK+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}(\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}-\frac{c_{i-1}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i-1}})dp_{i}.

If all the
\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}
are equal

, then the last sum vanish and this tells us that
I,J,K
are the hamiltonians of respectively
u,v,w
.

Open question 2.

For
1\leq k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, consider the quantities

G_{k}(P)=\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}}\frac{c_{i_{1}}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i_{1}}}\dots\frac{c_{i_{k}}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i_{k}}}\frac{(p_{i_{1}}-p_{i_{k}+1})(p_{i_{2}}-p_{i_{1}+1})\dots(p_{i_{k}}-p_{i_{k-1}+1})}{(p_{i_{1}}-p_{i_{1}+1})(p_{i_{2}}-p_{i_{2}+1})\dots(p_{i_{k}}-p_{i_{k}+1})}.

They are projective invariant, since they are sums of multiratios. They are introduced for the flat cross-ratio dynamics in
[

, where the authors prove the invariance through the dynamic. They also link them to the concept of
\lambda
-length, and to logarithms of alternated perimeters coming from ideal polygons.

A long and painful computation (not reproduced here) shows that these quantities are also invariant for the staircase cross-ratio dynamic. But for the general case of polygonal dynamic over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, we didn’t manage to prove the invariance. Maybe the answer could come from the

\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}=\left.\frac{d\mathrm{ln}(c_{i}(z))}{dz}\right|_{z=1}.

5
Two examples of dynamics in
\mathbb{P}^{1}

In this section, we study two polygonal dynamics previously defined in other papers. For any of them, we start by deriving their scaling symmetries, and then apply theorem
4.2
to get their infinitesimal monodromies. From this, we get what are their possible collapse points.

5.1
The leapfrog map

The leapfrog map is defined in
[
13
, §5.2]
as an analogue of the pentagram map in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and the authors notice that it seems to be the hyperbolic counterpart of the polygon recutting defined in
[
2
]
(which would be the euclidean case). For a matter of internal coherence, the notations of
[
13
]
are changed to fit the ones from here.

The dynamic involves two
n
-gons

S^{-}=(s_{i}^{-})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}},\quad S=(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}},

with the same monodromy, which we can intertwine this way

\dots,s_{i}^{-},s_{i},s_{i+1}^{-},s_{i+1},\dots

to form a
2n
-gon denoted by
P
. The leapfrog map is defined by

\Phi:(S^{-},S)\mapsto(S,S^{+}),

where
S^{+}
is given by applying local “leapfrog” moves. Such a move maps
s_{i}^{-}
to
s_{i}^{+}
via the only projective transformation that fixes
s_{i}
and interchanges
s_{i-1}
with
s_{i+1}
. Note that such moves are involutions, and that we could study the group generated by them, like we will do afterwards for the staircase cross-ratio dynamics. However, it is not the point here.

Because of the indexation, it is more convenient work with

o_{i}^{-}=[s_{i}^{-},s_{i},s_{i-1},s_{i+1}^{-}],\quad o_{i}=[s_{i},s_{i+1}^{-},s_{i}^{-},s_{i+1}],

instead of the usual
c_{i}
. This gives a fairly simple scaling symmetry.

Proposition 5.1.

There is a scaling symmetry acting by:

o_{i}^{-}(t)=to_{i}^{-},\qquad o_{i}(t)=o_{i}.

It is well defined for any
t\in\mathbb{G}_{m}
.

Proof.

First of all, it is direct to check that this is indeed a group action from
\mathbb{G}_{m}
.

We will express the system of coordinates
(o^{-},o)
with the help of two other systems, and then get the result. According to
[
13
, Prop. 5.12]
, we have the coordinate systems
(x,y)
and
(p,r)
, defined by:

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=[s_{i+1},s_{i+1}^{-},s_{i+2}^{-},s_{i}^{-}],

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s_{i+1}^{-}-s_{i+1})(s_{i+2}^{-}-s_{i+2})(s_{i}^{-}-s_{i+1}^{-})}{(s_{i+1}^{-}-s_{i+2})(s_{i}^{-}-s_{i+1})(s_{i+1}^{-}-s_{i+2}^{-})},

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=[s_{i+1}^{-},s_{i+2}^{-},s_{i+1},s_{i+2}],

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=[s_{i},s_{i+1},s_{i+1}^{-},s_{i+2}^{-}].

They are linked by the following relations
7
7

7

The original paper forgot the minus signs, but it doesn’t impact their reasoning.
:

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{y_{i}}{x_{i}},

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{x_{i-1}x_{i}}{x_{i-1}(1-x_{i})+y_{i-1}}.

Now, because of the permutation of the variables of the cross-ratio and the previous relation, we see that:

\displaystyle o_{i}^{-}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{i-1}}{r_{i-1}-1}=\frac{x_{i-2}x_{i-1}}{x_{i-2}+y_{i-2}},

\displaystyle o_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{i-1}}{p_{i-1}-1}=\frac{y_{i-1}}{x_{i-1}+y_{i-1}}.

But according to
[
13
, Rmk. 3.5]
, there is a scaling symmetry (actually working for all of their generalised pentagram maps) defined for any
t\in\mathbb{G}_{m}
by

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=tx_{i},

\displaystyle y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=ty_{i}.

Plugging it back to our formula linking
(o^{-},o)
to
(x,y)
, we get the result.
∎

Now we derive the infinitesimal monodromy and potential collapse points for the leapfrog map.

Proposition 5.2.

The infinitesimal monodromy for the leapfrog map is

M^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{s_{i}^{-}-s_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}s_{i}^{-}&-s_{i}^{-}s_{i}\\
1&-s_{i}\end{pmatrix},

and its eigenvectors
(X,Y)^{T}
are roots of the polynomial

\chi_{P}(X,Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(X-s_{i}^{-}Y)(X-s_{i}Y)}{s_{i}^{-}-s_{i}}.

Proof.

We have from proposition
5.1
that

\frac{o_{i}^{-}\penalty\ {}^{\prime}(1)}{o_{i}^{-}}=1,\qquad\frac{o_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{o_{i}}=0,

and by remembering that they correspond to the cross-ratio coordinates
c_{i}
, we get the result from applying theorem
4.2
.
∎

Flat cross-ratio dynamics

The flat cross-ratio dynamic was studied in
[

]
and
[

]
for constant discrete curvature
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and for varying
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
in
[
3

. Hamiltonian integrability was proved in both situations.

Definition 5.3.

Let
n\geq 3
and
P=((p_{1},\dots,p_{n}),M)
be an
n
-gon in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
such that
p_{i}\neq p_{i+1},p_{i+2}
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Enrich it with a discrete curvature
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})
such that
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{G}_{m}\setminus\{1\}
for each
i\in\mathbb{Z}
.

We say that two
n
-gons
P
and

are
\alpha
-related if they share the same monodromy, the same discrete curvature, and verify

\alpha_{i}=[p_{i},p_{i+1},q_{i},q_{i+1}]\quad\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}.

In a more compact way, we write
P\overset{\alpha}{\sim}Q
.

From
[
6
, Cor. 2.7]
we get that for an
n
-gon

, there are generically only two
n
-gon
Q^{+},Q^{-}
that are
\alpha
-related to it. This gives an evolution operator in the futur/past, called the
flat cross-ratio dynamics
.

Next lemma is an adaptation of
[
6
, Lemma 4.4]
for non-constant
\alpha_{i}

Lemma 5.4.

Let
P
and
Q
be two
n
-gons such that
P\overset{\alpha}{\sim}Q
. For all

, set

\displaystyle c_{i}

\displaystyle=[p_{i},p_{i+1},p_{i-1},p_{i+2}],

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=[p_{i},p_{i+1},p_{i-1},q_{i}],

\displaystyle d_{i}

\displaystyle=[q_{i},q_{i+1},q_{i-1},q_{i+2}],

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=[q_{i},q_{i+1},q_{i-1},p_{i}].

Then we have

\begin{split}c_{i}=\alpha_{i}x_{i}(1-x_{i+1}),\quad d_{i}=\alpha_{i}y_{i}(1-y_{i+1}),\\
\quad 1=x_{i}+y_{i}+x_{i}y_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{1-\alpha_{i-1}}-1\right).\end{split}

(1)

Conversely, for any
(x_{i})\in(\mathbb{G}_{m}\setminus\{1\})^{n}
and any
(\alpha_{i})\in(\mathbb{G}_{m}\setminus\{1\})^{n}
, the
(c_{i})
and
(d_{i})
given by the previous formulas define a pair
P\overset{\alpha}{\sim}Q
, unique up to a simultaneous projective transformation.

Proof.

Again, it is similar to
[
6
]
. By using the “Chasles’ relation of the cross-ratio”, we have:

c_{i}=[p_{i},p_{i+1},p_{i-1},q_{i}][p_{i},p_{i+1},q_{i},q_{i+1}][p_{i},p_{i+1},q_{i+1},p_{i+2}]=x_{i}\alpha_{i}(1-x_{i+1}),

and similarly we get the same one for
d_{i}
by exchanging the
p_{i}
’s with
q_{i}
’s, and
x_{i}
’s with
y_{i}
’s. We also have

\displaystyle[p_{i},q_{i},p_{i-1},p_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=[p_{i},q_{i},p_{i-1},q_{i-1}][p_{i},q_{i},q_{i-1},q_{i+1}][p_{i},q_{i},q_{i+1},p_{i+1}]

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{i-1})[p_{i},q_{i},q_{i-1},q_{i+1}]\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{i}}

so by referring to the permutations of the variables of the cross-ratio, we get:

\frac{x_{i}}{1-x_{i}}(1-\alpha_{i})=\frac{1-y_{i}}{y_{i}}(1-\alpha_{i-1}).

This rewrites as:

1=x_{i}+y_{i}+x_{i}y_{i}\left(\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{1-\alpha_{i-1}}-1\right).

Conversely, the data of
x
and
\alpha
allows to reconstruct

,
c
and

. We extend them to get
n
-periodic sequences. From

, we can choose representant

such

. Because of lemma

, we know its monodromy

. Similarly we construct a polygon

, with monodromy

. We observe that

[p_{i},p_{i+1},p_{i-1},q_{i}]=x_{i}=x_{i+n}=[M_{P}(p_{i}),M_{P}(p_{i+1}),M_{P}(p_{i-1}),M_{Q}(q_{i})],

so by a classical property of the cross-ratio and projective transformations, we necessary have
M_{P}=M_{Q}
. Because of the computation that gave us

we recognize that
[p_{i},p_{i+1},q_{i},q_{i+1}]=\alpha_{i}
, so we have indeed

.
∎

This crucial lemma allows us to define a scaling symmetry. This was already observed in
[
6
]
for constant
\alpha_{i}
, although the scaling was much simpler and the concept of scaling symmetry was not named.

Proposition 5.5.

We have a scaling symmetry
\rho
that acts both on the
c_{i}
’s and the
\alpha_{i}
’s, in the following way:

\alpha_{i}(t)=1+\kappa_{i}(t\alpha_{1}-1),\quad c_{i}(t)=c_{i}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t)}{\alpha_{i}},\qquad\text{where }\kappa_{i}=\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{1-\alpha_{1}}.

This is well defined for

t\neq\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}},\frac{\kappa_{i}-1}{\kappa_{i}\alpha_{1}}
.

In any case, this scaling symmetry is well defined around
t=1
. Indeed,
1/\alpha_{1}\neq 1
and
(\kappa_{i}-1)/\kappa_{i}\alpha_{1}=1
would imply
\alpha_{i}=0
, which is not the case.

Remark 8.

If all the
\alpha_{i}
are equal, we get back the scaling symmetry form
[
6
, Lemma 4.4]
.

Proof.

Let’s check first that
\rho
is indeed a group action. First, we have

\alpha_{i}(1)=1+\frac{\alpha_{i}-1}{\alpha_{1}-1}(\alpha_{1}-1)=\alpha_{i},

and so
c_{i}(1)=c_{i}
. Furthermore, we note that

\kappa_{i}(t)=\frac{1-\alpha_{i}(t)}{1-\alpha_{1}(t)}=\frac{\kappa_{i}(t\alpha_{1}-1)}{(t\alpha_{1}-1)}=\kappa_{i},

so
\kappa_{i}(t)
is constant for each
i
(
\rho
was in fact designed from that property). Then we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(t_{1})(t_{2})

\displaystyle=1+\kappa_{i}(t_{2}\alpha_{1}(t_{1})-1)

\displaystyle=1+\kappa_{i}(t_{2}t_{1}\alpha_{1}-1)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}(t_{1}t_{2}),

and from this we obtain

\displaystyle c_{i}(t_{1})(t_{2})

\displaystyle=c_{i}(t_{1})\frac{\alpha_{i}(t_{1})(t_{2})}{\alpha_{i}(t_{1})}

\displaystyle=c_{i}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t_{1})}{\alpha_{i}}\frac{\alpha_{i}(t_{1}t_{2})}{\alpha_{i}(t_{1})}

\displaystyle=c_{i}(t_{1}t_{2}).

So
\rho
is indeed a group action. It is well defined if
\alpha_{i}(t)\neq 0,1
for each
i
, which corresponds to
t\neq\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}},\frac{\kappa_{i}-1}{\kappa_{i}\alpha_{1}}
.

Let’s now see why it commutes with the evolution operator. Take
c,d,x,y
as in lemma
5.4
. They are linked by equation
1
. Note that
\frac{1-\alpha_{i}}{1-\alpha_{i-1}}
is equal to
\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa_{i-1}}
, so it stays constant when applying
\rho
. In fact, applying
\rho
modifies
c,d,\alpha
, but leaves
x
and
y
unchanged. The equation
1
still holds for
c_{i}(t),d_{i}(t),\alpha_{i}(t),x_{i},y_{i}
and so by lemma
5.4
we have
P(t)\overset{\alpha(t)}{\sim}Q(t)
. Hence
\rho
is indeed a scaling symmetry.
∎

Proposition 5.6.

The infinitesimal monodromy for the flat cross-ratio dynamics is

M^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1-\alpha_{i})\alpha_{1}}{(1-\alpha_{1})\alpha_{i}}\frac{1}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}\begin{pmatrix}p_{i}&-p_{i}p_{i+1}\\
1&-p_{i+1}\end{pmatrix},

and its eigenvectors
(X,Y)^{T}
are roots of the polynomial

\chi_{P}(X,Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1-\alpha_{i})}{\alpha_{i}}\frac{(X-p_{i}Y)(X-p_{i+1}Y)}{p_{i}-p_{i+1}}.

Proof.

From proposition
5.5
we see that

\frac{c_{i}^{\prime}(1)}{c_{i}}=\frac{(1-\alpha_{i})\alpha_{1}}{(1-\alpha_{1})\alpha_{i}},

so by theorem
4.2
we get the formula for the infinitesimal monodromy. Still from the same theorem, we obtain that an eigenvector
(X,Y)^{T}
is a root of the polynomial
\chi_{P}(X,Y)
. Up to multiplying it by
\frac{\alpha_{1}}{(1-\alpha_{1})}
, which we do only for cosmetic purpose, we get desired formula for the polynomial.
∎

6
A group dynamic in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
: staircase cross-ratio dynamics

We define a new polygonal dynamic in
\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by a group of polygonal transformations. As said in the introduction, it is a generalization of other systems coming from the continuation of discrete holomorphic functions (see
[
4
, §6]
and references therein).

6.1
Definition and results

Before defining the group, let’s start with a few preliminaries.

Definition 6.1.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{G}_{m}
. Two couples of distinct points
(a,c),(b,d)
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
form a
\bm{\alpha/\beta}
-quadrangle
when
[a,c,b,d]=\alpha/\beta
. The point
c
is called the
\bm{\alpha/\beta}
-conjugate
of
a
with respect to
(b,d)
, and we write
c=h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}(a)
. When
\alpha/\beta=-1
, the quadrangle said to be harmonic.

Proposition 6.2.

(a)

Projective transformations send
\alpha/\beta
-quadrangles to
\alpha/\beta
-quadrangles. Moreover, we have:

g\circ h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}=h^{\alpha/\beta}_{g(b),g(d)}\circ g,\quad\text{for all }g\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}.

(b)

The
\alpha/\beta
-conjugation with respect to
(b,d)
is a projective transformation
h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}(z)=\frac{(\beta d-\alpha b)z-(\beta-\alpha)bd}{(\beta-\alpha)z-(\beta b-\alpha d)}
, which fixes
b,d,
and has characteristic constant
\alpha/\beta
. Its inverse is
h^{\alpha/\beta}_{d,b}
.

(c)

If
(b,d)
is fixed, then for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{G}_{m}
, we have
h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}\circ h^{\gamma/\delta}_{b,d}=h^{\alpha\gamma/\beta\delta}_{b,d}
. Hence
h^{1}_{b,d}=id
and
(h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d})^{-1}=h^{\beta/\alpha}_{b,d}
.

Proof.

(a)

Let
(a,c),(b,d)
be an
\alpha/\beta
-quadrangle and
g\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}
. The cross-ratio is
\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}
-invariant, so

[g(a),g(h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}(a)),g(b),g(d)]=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}=[g(a),h^{\alpha/\beta}_{g(b),g(d)}(g(a)),g(b),g(d)],

which gives the result.

(b)

The formula of
h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}
is the result of direct calculation. To see the characteristic constant, it suffices to conjugate by a projective transformation that sends
(b,d)
to
(\infty,0)
, use
(a)
and by the formula we see that
h^{\alpha/\beta}_{\infty,0}:z\mapsto\frac{\alpha}{\beta}z
.

Considering
(c,a),(d,b)
also gives a
\alpha/\beta
-quadrangle, because this permutation doesn’t modify the cross-ratio. So we get

a=h^{\alpha/\beta}_{d,b}(c)=h^{\alpha/\beta}_{d,b}\circ h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}(a),

hence
h^{\alpha/\beta}_{d,b}=(h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d})^{-1}
.

(c)

Let
g\in\mathbb{P}\mathrm{GL}_{2}
which sends
(b,d)
to
(\infty,0)
. Using the
(a)
and
(b)
, we obtain

\displaystyle h^{\alpha/\beta}_{b,d}\circ h^{\gamma/\delta}_{b,d}

\displaystyle=g^{-1}\circ h^{\alpha/\beta}_{\infty,0}\circ g\circ g^{-1}\circ h^{\gamma/\delta}_{\infty,0}\circ g

\displaystyle=g^{-1}\circ h^{\alpha\gamma/\beta\delta}_{\infty,0}\circ g

\displaystyle=h^{\alpha\gamma/\beta\delta}_{b,d}.

∎

We can now define the dynamic.

Definition 6.3.

Let

and

n

\mathbb{P}^{1}

Here, we investigate some specific cases of staircase cross-ratio dynamics for which we can write explicitly the dynamic. Their motion on the moduli space is periodic, meaning that it amounts on the initial space to iterate a projective transformation (of any kind). They can be seen as a special case of discrete holomorphic functions, depicted in figure
5
. The idea comes from
[
21
]
, and it holds on any algebraicly closed field of characteristic
0
. For an example of generic orbit in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{C)}
, see the simulation in figure
4
.

First, let’s see that how a parabolic transformation can appear. Let’s consider a closed
n
-gon
P=(0,1,\dots,n-1)
with discrete curvature
(1,\dots,1,\lambda)
, such that after applying
\phi_{n}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{1}
, we get
\tilde{P}=(n,n+1,\dots,2n-1)
. This happens if the cross-ratio on any square is:

\lambda=\frac{(k-(k+n-1)(k+n-(k+1))}{(k+n-1-(k+n))(k+1-k)}=(n-1)^{2}.

Hence, we get the Möbius transformation
z\mapsto z+n
, which is parabolic. The dynamic converges in the future and past towards the double point
\infty
.

Now, let’s do the same for a closed
n
-gon
P=(1,q,\dots,q^{n-1})
with discrete curvature
(1,\dots,1,\lambda)
, such that after applying
\phi_{n}\circ\dots\circ\phi_{1}
we get
\tilde{P}=(q^{n},q^{n+1},\dots,q^{2n-1})
. Again, we have to check the cross-ratio condition for every square:

\lambda=\frac{(q^{k}-q^{k+n-1})(q^{k+n}-q^{k+1})}{(q^{k+n-1}-q^{k+n})(q^{k+1}-q^{k})}=\frac{(1-q^{n-1})(q^{n}-q)}{(q^{n-1}-q^{n})(q-1)}.

By remembering that
\frac{q^{n-1}-1}{q-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}q^{k}
and doing some simplifications, we get that
q
is a root of the polynomial equation

Q_{n}(q,\lambda)=(\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}q^{k})^{2}-\lambda q^{n-2}=0.

The polynomial
Q_{n}
is of degree
2n-4
and palindromic with respect to
q
, which implies that
1/q
is also a root. So we can suppose
|q|\geq 1
. Note that if
q=1
, we get back
\lambda=(n-1)^{2}
as in the parabolic case.

Let’s go back to conjecture
3.2
. In the case of periodic motion of the moduli space, any type of Möbius transformation can appear.

1.

If
|q|\neq 0,1
, we get a convergence towards
\infty
in the future and
0
in the past (loxodromic transformation, the generic case);

2.

If
q
is a root of unity, then the dynamic is periodic (finite order elliptic transformation);

3.

If
|q|=1
but is not a root of unity, then the dynamic is recurrent (infinite order elliptic transformation);

4.

Otherwise, we get a convergence in the past and future towards
\infty
(parabolic transformation).

In all of this cases, we can deform the polygons via the scaling symmetry to get families of twisted
n
-gons. The generic case is the first, which is coherent with conjectures
3.2
and
3.6
, but the other ones remind us that they won’t hold everywhere.
