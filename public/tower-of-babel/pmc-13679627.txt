Clinicopathologic and molecular features of primary effusion lymphoma and fluid overload-associated large B-cell lymphoma: A retrospective study
Authors: Taesung Jeon, Young-Hyeh Ko, You-Na Sung, Harim Oh, Hyunsung Kim, Seongsik Bang, Ka-Won Kang, Changgon Kim, Kunye Kwak, Yeseul Kim, Sangjeong Ahn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights●PEL and FO-LBCL showed overlapping morphology but distinct biologic features.●PEL lacked the B-cell marker CD20 and often expressed aberrant T-cell markers.●FO-LBCL retained pan-B-cell markers and harbored recurrent CD79B, PIM1, and MYD88 mutations.●A subset of FO-LBCL showed partial molecular overlap with molecular features characteristic of the MCD subtype of DLBCL.●Integrated pathologic and molecular analysis supports diagnosis of effusion-based B-cell lymphomas.

Introduction

Primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) and fluid overload–associated large B-cell lymphoma (FO-LBCL) are rare B-cell neoplasms that present as serous effusions without detectable tumor masses [1,2]. Although PEL and FO-LBCL have overlapping cytomorphologies, these entities differ in their virologic associations, immunophenotypes, and clinical courses. PEL is uniformly associated with human herpesvirus-8 (HHV-8) and frequently shows a loss of pan–B-cell markers with aberrant expression of T-cell antigens [3,4]. In contrast, FO-LBCL is consistently HHV-8 negative, retains B-cell lineage markers, and generally demonstrates a more indolent clinical behavior [2,5].

In parallel, advancements in genomic profiling have refined the molecular taxonomy of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), leading to the recognition of distinct genetic subtypes. Among these, the MYD88/CD79B (MCD) subtype—defined by co-occurring MYD88L265P and CD79B mutations—has been particularly associated with the non-germinal center/activated B-cell phenotype and a predilection for immune-privileged sites, such as the central nervous system and testes [[6], [7], [8]]. Although FO-LBCL has not been formally assigned to this framework, limited genomic reports have identified MYD88 and CD79B mutations in some cases [9,10] suggesting a potential biological link to the MCD subtype.

Despite these observations, PEL and FO-LBCL have not been fully defined at the molecular level, and to the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have integrated their genetic differences with the underlying pathogenesis or contextualized them within a broader DLBCL spectrum. Given the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with effusion-based lymphomas, a better understanding of their molecular basis is essential for elucidating their cellular origin and pathogenic mechanisms, which may ultimately improve clinical management. We hypothesized that PEL and FO-LBCL, despite their morphologic similarity, represent biologically distinct entities with differing pathogenetic mechanisms and genetic profiles. To address this, we performed an integrated clinicopathological and molecular analysis to delineate their biological differences and provide insights for future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Case selection

All cases of PEL and FO-LBCL were retrospectively collected from Korea University Anam Hospital and Guro Hospital between 2022 and 2024. A total of three PEL cases (PEL group) and seven FO-LBCL cases (FO-LBCL group) were included. Cases were selected if they presented with body cavity effusion without detectable solid masses and had sufficient cytologic or histologic material for immunophenotypic and molecular analyses. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Hospital (approval no. 2024AN0166) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Clinical data were retrieved from electronic medical records, and pathological factors were reviewed independently by two pathologists (T. Jeon and Y. Kim). Cases of secondary lymphoma involvement of effusion were excluded based on clinical and imaging evaluations.

Immunohistochemistry and in situ hybridization (ISH)

Immunocytochemical studies were performed on cell block sections using the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (Ventana Medical Systems, Arizona, United States), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The primary antibodies used included CD2, CD3, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, CD20, CD79a, PAX5, BOB1, OCT2, CD30, CD10, BCL6, BCL2, MYC, MUM-1, CD138, Ki-67, and HHV-8 (Table S1). Epstein–Barr virus–encoded RNA (EBER) and kappa and lambda light chains were assessed using ISH.

Immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) and T-cell receptor (TCR) clonality assay

Polymerase chain reaction assays were performed to detect IGH, TCR beta (TRB), and TCR gamma (TRG) gene rearrangements following the BIOMED-2 concerted action protocols, as described previously [11].

DNA extraction and next-generation sequencing

Targeted sequencing was performed using SolidAccu-Lymphoma Panel v.2.0. on the NextSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA was extracted from the dissected cell block sections and assessed for quality. Following bioinformatics analysis, all detected variants were manually reviewed. All output files were integrated and visualized using R version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A list of the 432 genes included in the panel is presented in Table S2.

Data analysis

An oncoPrint of select genes known to be relevant to lymphoma and mutated in this cohort was created using the ComplexHeatmap R package (version 4.5.0; Gu, Z., The Comprehensive R Archive Network [CRAN], Vienna, Austria) [12].

T-cell and B-cell receptor clonality assay

Eight patients were evaluated for B-cell and T-cell clonality. Clonal IGH rearrangement was detected in all eight samples. Among the PEL cases, two showed monoclonal TRB gene rearrangements, whereas the remaining case showed a low signal peak. In the FO-LBCL group, low signal peaks in TRB were observed in two of five cases. A positive TRG gene rearrangement was identified in only one PEL case.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS)

NGS was performed in 5 of the 10 cases (Fig. 4). In the PEL group, TJP1 mutations and copy number variations in IKBKB, CTCF, and MDM2 were observed in both PEL cases. In contrast, the FO-LBCL group demonstrated mutations in CD79B and PIM1, as well as mutations or copy number variations involving POU2F2; these were found in three FO-LBCL cases but were not detected in the PEL group. Additionally, two FO-LBCL cases harbored mutations in CUX1, MYD88, and TRRAP that were not observed in PEL, along with copy number variations in CD37, CD79A, LILRB1, NKG7, and PTKD2. Discussion

PEL and FO-LBCL (also known as HHV-8-negative PEL or PEL-like effusion lymphoma) are rare lymphomas, with PEL exhibiting more aggressive behavior and poorer clinical outcomes than FO-LBCL [13,14]. Despite their distinct clinical behavior, direct comparative studies of the clinicopathologic and molecular features of these two entities remain limited. In this preliminary retrospective case series, we compared the morphologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular features of PEL and FO-LBCL, with targeted sequencing performed in a subset of cases.

Although PEL and FO-LBCL showed no consistent morphologic differences, distinct differences were observed in their immunohistochemical features, with T-cell lineage infidelity being more frequently observed in PEL. Furthermore, FO-LBCL showed recurrent alterations involving CD79B, PIM1, and MYD88, suggesting partial molecular overlap with the MCD-like molecular pattern reported in subsets of activated B-cell-like (ABC) DLBCL [7].

In our study, both PEL and FO-LBCL exhibited male predominance, with all three PEL cases occurring in male patients and a male-to-female ratio of 2.5:1 observed in the FO-LBCL group. The median age of patients in the FO-LBCL group (83 years) was higher than that in the PEL group (72 years). This age difference may reflect the distinct pathogenesis of the two entities: HHV-8–associated oncogenesis in PEL and fluid-overload–related malignant transformation in older individuals with FO-LBCL [2,15]. These findings were consistent with those of previous studies [16,17]. These distinct pathogenetic mechanisms may also contribute to differences in clinical outcomes. In our cohort, all patients with PEL died due to the disease within 2–14 months of diagnosis. In contrast, FO-LBCL appeared to have a comparatively more favorable clinical course in this cohort: 3 of 7 patients (43 %) remained alive, 3 of 7 patients (43 %) died due to the disease, and one patient (14 %) died due to an unrelated cause.

Interestingly, patients with FO-LBCL showing pericardial involvement (Cases #6, #8, and #10) appeared to have more favorable clinical outcomes than those with pleural involvement. This observation aligns with the findings of Kubota et al., who reported a significantly better overall survival in patients with pericardial effusion (P = 0.045) [17].

Another notable finding was that 67 % (4/6) of patients with FO-LBCL who had available follow-up cytology had negative results for subsequent cytologic specimens. Among these, half of them achieved spontaneous remission despite not receiving systemic treatment because of poor general condition or other factors. Except for one case in which IGH clonality testing failed owing to insufficient material, all spontaneously regressed cases were confirmed to be monoclonal. Moreover, two of these cases underwent targeted sequencing, revealing that CD79B and PIM1 mutations were commonly associated with DLBCL [7,18]. These findings further support the notion that, despite its relatively favorable clinical course, FO-LBCL is a true hematologic malignancy harboring known driver mutations.

Ashihara et al. previously suggested that external factors such as fluid overload may trigger malignant transformation within effusion cavities, a mechanism proposed in previous reports of HHV-8-negative effusion-based lymphoma [19]. However, as demonstrated in Cases #4 and #10, such lymphomas can also arise in the absence of any apparent fluid overload, suggesting that alternative mechanisms, such as immunosenescence, may contribute to their pathogenesis.

Molecular studies have suggested that PEL and FO-LBCL arise through partially distinct oncogenic mechanisms despite their shared presentation as cavity-based lymphomas. PEL is driven primarily by HHV-8-associated viral oncogenesis. HHV-8-encoded proteins promote constitutive activation of multiple prosurvival signaling pathways, including NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and PI3K/AKT, while viral-mediated dysregulation of p53 signaling further contributes to lymphomagenesis [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]].

Within the limits of our cohort, IKBKB copy number amplification was identified in the PEL group. This alteration may be biologically relevant because HHV-8 promotes persistent NF-κB pathway activation via the viral FLICE inhibitory protein, a known viral oncoprotein [20,21]. Functional dependence on MDM2 has also been reported in PEL, suggesting that suppression of p53 activity may contribute to tumor-cell survival [22,23]. HHV-8-mediated activation of NF-κB together with dysregulation of p53-related signaling has been implicated in the biology of PEL [[20], [21], [22], [23],25].

TJP1, a tight junction protein, is not widely recognized as a recurrently mutated gene in lymphoma. However, studies on leiomyosarcoma, lung cancer, and bladder cancer have shown that TJP1 overexpression promotes cell aggregation and tumor progression and is often associated with poor prognosis [[26], [27], [28]]. Our findings raise the possibility that alterations in adhesion-related genes such as TJP1 may contribute to the unique biology of PEL, including its effusion-based growth pattern.

In contrast, the limited molecular data available for FO-LBCL have identified recurrent alterations involving genes implicated in B-cell receptor signaling and other lymphoma-associated pathways. Mendeville et al. detected MYD88 mutations in four of eight HHV-8-negative effusion-based lymphomas, including three cases with the canonical L265P variant, and identified a concurrent CD79B mutation in one case [9]. Subsequent reports have described recurrent alterations involving MYD88 and CD79B, together with mutations in PIM1 and other lymphoma-associated genes, although the number of molecularly characterized cases remains small [10,[29], [30], [31], [32]]. In our cohort, MYD88 mutations were detected in two of three FO-LBCL cases, although neither involved the L265P variant, and all three cases carried mutations in both CD79B and PIM1. Together, these recurrent alterations suggest partial molecular overlap between FO-LBCL and the molecular features characteristic of the MCD subtype of ABC-DLBCL, with involvement of chronic active B-cell receptor signaling and NF-κB pathway activation [33,34].

Given these molecular features and their overlap with those reported previously, it is possible that FO-LBCL shares certain microenvironmental features with MCD-type DLBCLs that show a predilection for immune-privileged sites such as the central nervous system and testes [7,35]. Although FO-LBCL arises in serous cavities, such as the pleura or pericardium, these spaces may similarly support compartmentalized lymphoma growth in which local environmental factors contribute to tumor persistence. As anatomically confined spaces with potentially limited immune cell infiltration, serous cavities may permit malignant cells to proliferate under reduced immune pressure. Consistent with this concept, some FO-LBCL cases have shown tumor regression after simple fluid drainage, suggesting that the serous cavity milieu can play an important role in sustaining lymphoma growth [2,36,37]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which the effusion environment supports tumor cell survival and expansion remain to be clarified.

In the present study, IGH monoclonality was confirmed in all eight evaluable cases of PEL and FO-LBCL, excluding two FO-LBCL cases in which the analysis failed owing to insufficient specimen quantity. Interestingly, three cases of PEL demonstrated monoclonality or low signal intensity in the TRB, with one also showing TRG positivity. In contrast, among the FO-LBCL cases, only two showed low TRB signals. Two PEL cases expressed T-cell markers, namely, CD3 and CD4, exhibiting both dual genotypes and dual phenotypes of B-cell and T-cell lineages. These findings are occasionally observed in hematological malignancies and may arise because of lineage infidelity, particularly in the presence of EBV infection [[38], [39], [40]]. One hypothesis is that EBV proteins induce lineage infidelity during malignant transformation by interfering with the expression of certain host genes and activating others, such as those encoding T-cell antigens [39] while another suggests that transformation at the progenitor cell level, accompanied by the release of genetic repression, drives the neoplastic expansion of a subset of B-cell populations expressing T-cell antigens [38]. Notably, one EBER-positive FO-LBCL case also showed a low TRB signal was detected. All four PEL- and FO-LBCL cases with EBER infection demonstrated TRB rearrangement or a low TRB signal.

Few studies have directly compared the molecular genetic features of FO-LBCL and PEL, and our findings provide additional support for a partial molecular overlap between FO-LBCL and the MCD-like genetic pattern reported in subsets of DLBCL. However, this study has some limitations. First, the study cohort included only Korean patients, and the clinicopathological characteristics of PEL and FO-LBCL may vary across different ethnic backgrounds. Second, genomic profiling was performed in only five cases, limiting the statistical power of our findings. Larger-scale studies are warranted to validate these results. Finally, the retrospective design may have introduced a selection bias, particularly regarding treatment response and outcome data. Accordingly, the molecular observations presented here should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating pending validation in larger cohorts.

In conclusion, this preliminary retrospective case series characterized the clinicopathological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features of PEL and FO-LBCL through direct comparison of their genetic profiles. Our findings suggest partial molecular overlap between FO-LBCL and the molecular features characteristic of the MCD subtype of DLBCL in a subset of cases. Although the two entities share similar morphological features that make histologic distinction challenging, they can be reliably differentiated using established immunohistochemical markers.

These findings enhance diagnostic precision and underscore the importance of integrating morphologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular data for accurate classification of effusion-based B-cell lymphomas. Clinically, the identification of recurrent alterations involving BCR/NF-κB–related pathways in FO-LBCL may provide a basis for future studies exploring targeted therapeutic approaches such as BTK inhibitors.

The distinct genetic alterations observed in each group likely reflect differences in pathogenesis that contribute to their divergent clinical behavior. Future studies involving larger, multi-institutional cohorts with integrated molecular analyses are warranted to further clarify the biological relationships among PEL, FO-LBCL, and immune-privileged site DLBCL.
