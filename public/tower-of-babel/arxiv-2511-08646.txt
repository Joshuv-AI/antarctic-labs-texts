Schrödinger equation is ℛ \mathcal{R} -separable in toroidal coordinates Preprint: APS/123-QED
Matheus E. Pereira, Alexandre G. M. Schmidt

Abstract
We present, for the first time, exact solutions for the Schrödinger equation in Moon and Spencer’s toroidal coordinates, and in the electromagnetic toroidal–poloidal coordinate systems. Curiously, both systems present a fractional angular momentum, because of the torus’s hole. We achieve these novel solutions using the irregular ℛ \mathcal{R} -separation of variables, an unexplored approach in Physics, which results in a wavefunction with fractional angular momentum eigenvalues. Numerous solutions for the Schrödinger equation in a variety of external potentials are shown, including an external magnetic field. A plane-wave expansion and a Green function are also presented, setting the stage for future progress in this area.



I
Introduction

Usually, one of the steps to solve Schrödinger equation using simple separation of variables is to consult Moon and Spencer’s handbook
32
and find if the equation is said to be separable in a given coordinate system. Historically, it has been shown
33
;
30
;
31
;
11
;
12
that the free Schrödinger (Helmholtz) equation is simply separable in exactly eleven coordinate systems, while
\mathcal{R}
-separation is possible only for rotational coordinate systems, like spherical and spheroidal. Therefore, the challenge to solve the Helmholtz equation in any coordinate system that defies Moon and Spencer’s impossibility theorems remained open for decades. While most textbook problems revolve around symmetric configurations, which are contemplated by one of those eleven coordinate systems
4
, real-life physical applications are seldom trivial
40
;
6
;
26
. In particular, we found important contributions in the solution of the Laplace and hydrodynamic equations in bispherical coordinates
50
;
44
;
27
;
17
. The theory of regular and irregular
\mathcal{R}
-separation can be found in the seminal works of Kalnins and Miller
22
;
8
;
23
;
20
;
21
.

The toroidal geometry is widely known, for example, for shaping both donuts and tokamaks
36
;
18
, which are devices designed to manipulate plasma at extremely high temperatures (hundreds of thousands of kelvins)
45
;
7
. Because of its deceptively simple and symmetric shape, attempts to solve Helmholtz equation using Moon and Spencer’s toroidal coordinates or the electromagnetic toroidal–poloidal coordinates via simple separation of variables only resulted in failure.

In the previous two decades, there have been several attempts to investigate the Schrödinger equation in toroidal coordinates, either on the torus surface or in a toroidal quantum well. Encinosa and collaborators studied numerically
15
bound states induced by curvature and in the presence of a magnetic field
16
. Schulze-Halberg
42
;
43
also tackled the problem of a quantum particle adhered to a toroidal surface; however, the azimuthal quantum number was taken to be zero, and the geometrical potential was not taken into account. Encinosa also studied the quantum mechanical problem in an elliptical torus
13
and the role of hard and soft boundary conditions
14
, both using a numerical approach. Atanasov and collaborators also made an important contribution where they considered the geometrical potential, however, their results are valid for weak magnetic fields and certain approximations in the geometrical parameters
5
. In our paper we do not resort to approximations in these geometrical parameters, nor in particular choices of quantum numbers.
The next section reveals the only known closed-form solution to the Helmholtz equation in Moon and Spencer’s toroidal coordinate system. Additionally, we present an alternative coordinate system in the same section to serve as a basis to the ensuing discussions. Solutions for a free particle and for a particle in an infinite potential well are shown in section
III
. The first is concisely written in terms of Bessel and exponential functions. The last are written using Heun functions
41
. Section
IV
presents a Green function and the plane-wave expansion. We conclude with a discussion and our final remarks in section
V
.

II
Preliminary considerations and an alternative toroidal coordinates

II.1
A toroidal trouble

Consider Moon and Spencer’s toroidal coordinate system

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{R\sinh\tau\cos\phi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\frac{R\sinh\tau\sin\phi}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=\frac{R\sin\theta}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}.

Here,
R
is a constant that describes the distance from the cartesian origin to a ring of radius
R
at the
xy
plane — the focal ring. More details can be found in the works of Moon and Spencer and of Weston
30
;
31
;
33
;
47
;
48
;
46
. Now consider the Helmholtz equation in these coordinates
32
,

\frac{(\cosh\tau-\cos\theta)^{3}}{R^{2}\sinh\tau}\Bigg[\sinh\tau\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\left(\frac{\sinh\tau}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\tau}\right)\\
+\frac{1}{\sinh\tau(\cosh\tau-\cos\theta)}\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial\phi^{2}}\Bigg]+k^{2}\psi=0.

(1)

Ever since Moon and Spencer laid down their well-known works on separation of variables, it has been believed that a separable solution was impossible to achieve for such an equation. This is still true. However, more recent investigations by Kalnins, Miller, Prus and Sym
39
introduced a new type of solutions for partial differential equations, known as an irregular
\mathcal{R}
-separation of variables. Following the results of Prus and Sym, calculations revealed

\displaystyle\psi(\tau,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=N_{\pm}\exp{\left(\pm ikR\frac{\sinh\tau}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}\right)}\sqrt{e^{i\phi}\frac{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}{\sinh\tau}}

(2)

\displaystyle=e^{i\phi/2}\left[c_{1}J_{1/2}\left(kR\frac{\sinh\tau}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}\right)+c_{2}H_{1/2}^{(1)}\left(kR\frac{\sinh\tau}{\cosh\tau-\cos\theta}\right)\right]

is the solution of Helmholtz equation (
1
) in toroidal coordinates using irregular
\mathcal{R}
-separation of variables
39
, where
J_{\nu}
is the ordinary Bessel function of order
\nu
and
H_{\nu}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind with order
\nu
. A simple investigation reveals a troubling fact though, that this solution cannot be used for solving physical problems, in agreement with
39
. Indeed, consider the problem of a particle in an infinite toroidal well of minor radius
a
and major radius
R
. Because
(0,0,0)
is actually outside the well, we have
c_{2}
may not be zero. For integer
n
, we get

\psi(a,\theta,\phi)=0\quad\rightarrow\quad kR\frac{\sinh a}{\cosh a-\cos\theta}=n\pi\quad\rightarrow\quad\cos\theta=\cosh a-\left(\frac{kR}{n\pi}\right)\sinh a,

(3)

revealing the well does not trap the particle except where
\cos\theta
obeys the last expression, which is not a valid condition for the particle in a well problem.

II.2
The toroidal–poloidal coordinate system

To remedy the situation explained in the previous section, consider the Dupin cyclide parametric equations,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{2}\cos v\cosh\eta+w(c\cosh\eta-a\cos v)}{a\cosh\eta-c\cos v},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=s_{y}\frac{b\sin v(a\cosh\eta-w)}{a\cosh\eta-c\cos v},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=s_{z}\frac{b\sinh\eta(w-c\cos v)}{a\cosh\eta-c\cos v}.

where
s_{z}
and
s_{y}
are dimensionless scaling quantities and
c^{2}=a^{2}-b^{2}
. Prus and Sym
39
studied the irregular
\mathcal{R}
-separation in this coordinate system, achieving some exact solutions for Helmholtz equation. We lift most of their restrictions, allowing the Dupin cyclide to reduce to a very special case. If
a=b=R
, these equations describe a ring circular torus whose minor and major radii are measured by
w
and
R=b
respectively. Namely,

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=(R-w\sech\eta)\cos v,

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=s_{y}(R-w\sech\eta)\sin v,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=s_{z}w\tanh\eta.

This is not a good choice of coordinates, because it lacks periodicity in
\eta
, which is a necessary feature of toroidal symmetry. Morever, Prus and Sym
39
did solve the Helmholtz equation for the more general case, and their solution is not appropriate for physical problems, as they recognize. Therefore, we must make a dramatic change in these coordinates, in order to recover periodicity. Following Maxwell
28
, by transforming
\cosh\eta\rightarrow\sec u
and
\sinh{\eta}\rightarrow\tan u
we acquire

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=(R+w\cos u)\cos v,

(4)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=s_{y}(R+w\cos u)\sin v,

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=s_{z}w\sin u,

where
s_{z}=\pm 1,s_{y}=\pm 1
and we made
w\rightarrow-w
for convenience. These are the parametric equations of a regular circular ring torus, which also happen to describe a right-handed orthogonal coordinate system if
s_{z}=1
,
s_{y}=-1
. They define the so-called
toroidal and poloidal coordinate system
, where
0\leq w<\infty
,
0\leq u,v\leq 2\pi
. Incidentally, they are an alternative to the usual toroidal coordinate system, and can also be obtained from the conformal mapping

z=x+iy=e^{w}+R,

(5)

where
w=u+iv
. This means that the toroidal-poloidal coordinates are conformal, thus angle-preserving. Using a position vector
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
, the scale factors can be calculated from (
4
) as

h_{w}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial w}\right|=1,\quad h_{u}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial u}\right|=w,\quad h_{v}=\left|\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial v}\right|=(R+w\cos u).

(6)

Consequently, the determinant of the metric tensor is

\sqrt{\det g}=\sqrt{g}=h_{w}h_{u}h_{v}=w(R+w\cos u).

(7)

Thus, the volume element
dV=\sqrt{g}\;dw\;du\;dv
and surface element
dS=\sqrt{g}|_{w=w^{\prime}}\;du\;dv
are given by,

dV=w(R+w\cos u)\;dw\;du\;dv,\qquad dS=w^{\prime}(R+w^{\prime}\cos u)du\;dv.

(8)

To gain insight on this system, we notice that for constant
z
,
\rho=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
defines circles in the
xy
plane, where we define
\tan u=y/x
. We obtain cone-like surfaces if
u
is a constant, which degenerate to disks for
u=0
. On the other hand, surfaces of constant
v
are all disks centered at
w=0
. Finally, constant
w
draws toroidal surfaces, as we see in Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Level surfaces of the toroidal/poloidal coordinate system. We see a yellow torus, as a surface of constant
w
, cyan cones, of constant
u
, and magenta disks, setting
v
constant.

We choose
s_{z}=-1
, thus relating our coordinates to Cartesian coordinates via

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(\rho-R)^{2}+z^{2}},

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{-z}{\rho-R}\right),

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=\arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right).

Consider a point
P
in space like Figure
2
. We notice that

\displaystyle\tilde{w}(w,u)=\sqrt{w^{2}+4R^{2}-4Rw\cos u},

(9)

\displaystyle w(\tilde{w},\tilde{u})=\sqrt{\tilde{w}^{2}+4R^{2}-4R\tilde{w}\cos\tilde{u}},

(10)

and

\displaystyle\cos\tilde{u}=\frac{2R+w\cos u}{\tilde{w}(w,u)}.

(11)

Also, this figure shows a troubling fact about this coordinate system. Any point with Cartesian coordinates
P(x,y,z)
has two representations in toroidal-poloidal coordinates. In other words, we are essentially using two coordinate systems at once by employing (
4
), which is well-known in the mathematics community as the
double covering
of the torus.

Figure 2
:
Cross section of a torus with minor radius
a
, major radius
R
for a fixed
v
, where the plane
\rho
is defined by
z=0
and
\rho=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
.

While from the point
(R\cos v,R\sin v,0)
the distance to
P
is
w
, from the point
-(R\cos v,R\sin v,0)
, which is its antipodal, located at
(w,u,v+\pi)
, this distance is
\tilde{w}
. This reveals a curious characteristic of this system: even if our wavefunction is regular at a given point, there will be another wavefunction that will be evaluated at the same point, but with a different result.

III
The free particle and the toroidal well

III.1
Free particle

The time-independent Schrödinger equation for a free particle can be written as a Helmholtz equation with
k^{2}=2m^{*}E/\hbar^{2}
, where
m^{*}
is the mass,
E
is the energy and
\hbar
is the reduced Planck’s constant. In three-dimensional orthogonal curvilinear coordinates, this is

\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{g}}{g_{ii}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\right)+\left[k^{2}+V(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\right]\varphi=0.

(12)

Substituting the metric tensor and its determinant, we easily have

\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial w^{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{w}+\frac{\cos u}{R+w\cos u}\right)\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial w}+\frac{1}{w^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial u^{2}}-\frac{\sin u}{w(R+w\cos u)}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial u}+\frac{1}{(R+w\cos u)^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial v^{2}}\\
+V(w,u,v)\varphi+k^{2}\varphi=0.

(13)

Considering
V(w,u,v)=0
for the free particle and applying a
F-
homotopic transformation, we introduce the
ansatz

\varphi(w,u,v)=w^{A}(R+w\cos u)^{B}f(w)e^{i\alpha u}e^{Cv},

(14)

where the constants
A,B
,
C
and
\alpha
have to be chosen such that equation (
13
) is (non-regularly and non-Stäckel)
\mathcal{R}
-separable
39
. The periodicity condition in
u
implies
\alpha=m
, where
m
is an integer. Taking
B=-1/2
reveals a Bessel equation for
f(w)
and also determines
C
, since
C=\pm i|B|
. Thus,

\frac{d^{2}f}{dw^{2}}+\left(\frac{1+2A}{w}\right)\frac{df}{dw}+\left(k^{2}+\frac{A^{2}-m^{2}}{w^{2}}\right)f(w)=0.

(15)

Solutions of this equation can be written in terms of Bessel and Hankel functions, namely,
f(w)=w^{-A}\left[c_{1}J_{m}(kw)+c_{2}H^{(1)}_{m}(kw)\right]
. Thus, substituting into (
14
), we obtain the free solution of the Schrödinger equation

\varphi_{m}(w,u,v)=A_{m}\frac{J_{m}(kw)e^{imu}\exp{\left(-iv/2\right)}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}},

(16)

where we dropped the irregular solution, and
A_{m}
is a constant. The wave number
k
is continuous for the free particle, but otherwise must be calculated considering appropriate boundary conditions. It is most extraordinary to realize that the wavefunction
is not
2\pi
periodic in
v
. Indeed,

\varphi_{m}(w,u,v+2\pi)=e^{-i\pi}\varphi_{m}(w,u,v).

(17)

The appearance of this geometrical phase is explained by the fact that the irregular
\mathcal{R}
-separability removes one quantum number, which is reflected in the
\mathcal{R}
-factor. Notice we can write

\mathcal{R}(w,u,v)=\left[(R+w\cos u)e^{iv}\right]^{-1/2}=(x+iy)^{-1/2}.

(18)

As we circle the Cartesian origin, the multi-valued nature of the wavefunction becomes evident: every time
x=0
, the square root of an imaginary number brings a phase
e^{-i\pi/2}
. This results in the wavefunction jumping to its other branch once the rotation completes
2\pi
, leaving a phase
e^{-i\pi}
behind.
\varphi
returns to the original branch upon a
4\pi
rotation. The reason is that since the wavefunction will be singular at
x+iy=0
and we use a sine angular function to enforce regularity at these points,
we have effectively excised the
z
-axis
from the configuration space. Indeed, this remarkable change means that

\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{(0,0,z)\mid z\in\mathbb{R}\}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}\simeq S^{1},

that is, the three-dimensional Euclidean space without the
z
axis is homotopically equivalent
34
to a plane without the origin, which is homotopically equivalent to a circle. Because now the configuration space is multiply connected, a new topology comes about, which implies the phase
24
in (
17
) to be a
topological, or geometrical phase
, much like the historical Aharonov–Bohm effect.
While our wavefunction (
16
) is suitable to understand the dynamics of a quantum particle trapped in a toroidal well, that is, restricting the radial-like domain, this is still a multivalued solution if one wishes to treat a free particle. As we mentioned, the coordinate system is not injective.

In order to gain insight about this problem, we plot four normalized probability densities calculated using (
16
). Since the toroidal well is infinite, we impose that the wave function vanishes at its wall, namely
\varphi_{m}(R,u,v)=0
, i.e., the wave number must be a zero of the ordinary Bessel function. In figures
3
and
4
we restrict the
v
angle to
[0,\pi]
in order to see inside the well.

III.2
Toroidal well with external potentials

Toroidal geometries have been extensively studied for applications ranging from nuclear fusion (tokamaks) to nanodevices
40
;
1
;
2
;
29
;
49
;
9
. There even exists a toroidal black hole
19
. However, as we mentioned, it was thought to be impossible to analytically solve the scalar wave equation using separation of variables, which is one of the most useful techniques for approaching the problem. Now, with the solution within our grasp, we show a variety of external potentials for which the Schrödinger equation is still exactly solvable in toroidal-poloidal coordinates using this irregular
{\cal R}-
separation method.

III.2.1
Case 1:
V_{ext}=V^{(1)}_{ext}(w,u)

Let us consider an external potential that depends on
w
and
u
as,

V^{(1)}_{ext}(w,u)=4U_{1}w+4U_{2}w^{2}+\frac{V_{1}}{w}+\frac{V_{2}}{w^{2}}+\frac{T_{2}}{4(R+w\cos u)^{2}},

(19)

where the parameters
U_{1},U_{2},V_{1},V_{2},
and
T_{2}
represent real coupling constants. Substitution of this potential into (
12
) results in the non-normalized eigenfunctions

\psi^{(1)}_{m}(w,u,v)=c_{m}\exp{\left[\frac{U_{2}(U_{1}+U_{2}w)w}{(-U_{2})^{3/2}}+imu-\frac{iv\sqrt{1+T_{2}}}{2}\right]}\frac{w^{\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}}}\;{\rm HeunB}(\{1\};w)}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}},

(20)

where
\{1\}
represents the set of five parameters of the bi-confluent Heun’s function
41
;
25
,

\{1\}=\left\{q_{1},k^{2}-\frac{U_{1}^{2}}{U_{2}}+4\sqrt{-U_{2}}\left(1+\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}}\right),1+2\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}},-\frac{2U_{1}}{\sqrt{-U_{2}}},4\sqrt{-U_{2}}\right\}.

(21)

We define the accessory parameter as

q_{1}=-V_{1}+\left(\frac{1+2\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}}}{\sqrt{-U_{2}}}\right)U_{1}.

(22)

The bi-confluent Heun’s function
y(z)={\rm HeunB}(q,\alpha,\gamma,\delta,\varepsilon;z)
satisfies the differential equation
35
,

zy^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+\left(\gamma+\delta z+\varepsilon z^{2}\right)y^{\prime}+(\alpha z-q)y=0,

(23)

where
\gamma
can not be zero nor a negative integer.

The second solution reads,

\psi^{(2)}_{m}(w,u,v)=d_{m}\exp{\left[\frac{U_{2}(U_{1}+U_{2}w)w}{(-U_{2})^{3/2}}+imu-\frac{iv\sqrt{1+T_{2}}}{2}\right]}\frac{w^{-\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}}}\;{\rm HeunB}(\{2\};w)}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}},

(24)

where
\{2\}
represents another set of five parameters of the bi-confluent Heun’s function
41
;
25
,

\{2\}=\left\{q_{2},k^{2}+4\sqrt{-U_{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}}\right),1-2\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}},0,4\sqrt{-U_{2}}\right\}.

(25)

where the accessory parameter is slightly changed,

q_{2}=-V_{1}+\left(\frac{1-2\sqrt{m^{2}-V_{2}}}{\sqrt{-U_{2}}}\right)U_{1}.

(26)

The bi-confluent Heun’s equation has a regular singularity at
w=0
, and an irregular singularity at
\infty
of rank
2
. It has five parameters. One of them, usually represented as
q
, the so-called accessory parameter. Its solutions are known as bi-confluent Heun’s functions, which on their turn, are entire functions. In a previous paper
38
we have shown how to obtain polynomial solutions of this equation.

Case 2:
V_{ext}=V^{(2)}_{ext}(w)

V^{(2)}_{ext}(w)=V_{0}+\frac{V_{1}}{w}+\frac{V_{2}}{w^{2}}+\frac{V_{3}}{w^{3}}+\frac{V_{4}}{4w^{4}},

(27)

where
V_{i}

w=0
and

\psi^{(1)}_{m}(w,u,v)=c_{m}\exp{\left(imu-\frac{iv}{2}-i\kappa w+\frac{i\sqrt{V_{4}}}{2w}\right)}\frac{w^{\frac{1}{2}-ip}\;{\rm HeunD}(\{1\};w)}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}},

(29)

The double confluent Heun’s function

III.3
Particle in a weak uniform magnetic field

Immersed in a constant magnetic field
\mathbf{B}=B_{0}\hat{k}
, a charged particle has a corresponding Schrödinger equation that reads

\displaystyle E\psi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m^{*}}\left(-i\hbar\nabla-q\mathbf{A}\right)^{2}\psi

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m^{*}}\left(-\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}\psi+q^{2}\mathbf{A}^{2}\psi+2i\hbar q\mathbf{A}\cdot\nabla\psi+i\hbar q\psi\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}\right),

where
m^{*}
is the particle’s mass and
q
is the electric charge. The choice of magnetic field implies
\mathbf{A}=(y\hat{i}-x\hat{j})B_{0}/2=\hat{v}(R+w\cos u)B_{0}/2
, using the symmetric gauge, which in turn means
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}=0
. Substituting the vector potential, we obtain, in the weak field approximation,

-\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}\psi+i\hbar qB_{0}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial v}=2m^{*}E\psi.

(35)

It is straightforward to solve (
35
). Setting
k^{2}=2m^{*}E/\hbar^{2}
, one finds

\psi_{m}(w,u,v)=B_{m}\frac{J_{m}(\kappa w)e^{imu}\exp{\left(-iv/2\right)}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}},

(36)

where
\kappa^{2}=k^{2}+qB_{0}/2\hbar
, i.e., a toroidal wavefunction with shifted energy eigenvalues.

IV
The Green’s function and a plane-wave expansion

In order to study the quantum scattering of a non-relativistic scalar particle, it is fundamental that we have a practical form of the Green function. As it is known
3
, the Green function is the solution to

\nabla^{2}G+k^{2}G=-\delta(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}).

(37)

In toroidal-poloidal coordinates, we write the delta function as

\delta(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime})=\frac{\delta(w-w^{\prime})\delta(u-u^{\prime})\delta(v-v^{\prime})}{w(R+w\cos u)}.

(38)

An eigenfunction expansion can be achieved using

\displaystyle\delta(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle\psi(\mathbf{w^{\prime}})|\psi(\mathbf{w})\rangle

(39)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\int_{0}^{\infty}dkkJ_{m}(kw)J_{m}(kw^{\prime})\frac{e^{im(u-u^{\prime})}e^{-i(v-v^{\prime})/2}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}\sqrt{R+w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(w-w^{\prime})}{w}\sum_{m}\frac{e^{im(u-u^{\prime})}e^{-i(v-v^{\prime})/2}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}\sqrt{R+w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime}}}

A noteworthy feature of our basis is that it is not possible to construct an actual delta function of
v
using only the two solutions we have,
e^{iv/2}
and
e^{-iv/2}
. However, a simple calculation shows that our delta function does work as a filter regardless of this fact. We take inspiration from the eigenfunctions of our Schrödinger equation to write the bilinear form of our Green function
37
;
10
;
3
as

G^{+}_{0}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{w}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{-i(v-v^{\prime})/2}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}\sqrt{R+w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime}}}\sum_{m}g_{m}(w|w^{\prime})e^{im(u-u^{\prime})}

(40)

Then, substitution of (
40
) into the differential equation (
37
) reduces it to

\frac{d^{2}g_{m}}{dw^{2}}+\frac{1}{w}\frac{dg_{m}}{dw}+\left(k^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{w^{2}}\right)g_{m}(w|w^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta(w-w^{\prime})}{w}.

(41)

We can suppress the index
m
and present two solutions,

\displaystyle g(w|w^{\prime})=\begin{cases}g_{1}(w|w^{\prime})=a_{m}(w^{\prime})J_{m}(kw)+b_{m}(w^{\prime})H_{m}^{(1)}(kw),\quad\text{if}\quad w<w^{\prime}\\
g_{2}(w|w^{\prime})=c_{m}(w^{\prime})J_{m}(kw)+d_{m}(w^{\prime})H_{m}^{(1)}(kw),\quad\text{if}\quad w>w^{\prime}\end{cases}

(42)

The boundary conditions are: the Green function must be continuous across the boundary
w=w^{\prime}
, while its derivative is not. Additionally, the function is finite at
w=0
, and we require that Sommerfeld’s radiation condition be satisfied, that is, we want propagating waves from the origin to infinity, and not the opposite. The last two conditions are crucial, and they imply
b_{m}(w^{\prime})=c_{m}(w^{\prime})=0
. The continuity condition at
w=w^{\prime}
ensures that

a_{m}(w^{\prime})J_{m}(kw^{\prime})=d_{m}(w^{\prime})H_{m}^{(1)}(kw^{\prime}),

(43)

while the discontinuity of the derivative of the radial Green function at
w=w^{\prime}
can be investigated by integrating equation (
41
). We get

\lim_{w\rightarrow w^{\prime}_{+}}\frac{dg_{2}}{dw}-\lim_{w\rightarrow w^{\prime}_{-}}\frac{dg_{1}}{dw}=\frac{-1}{w^{\prime}},

which translates to

-a_{m}(w^{\prime})J^{\prime}_{m}(kw^{\prime})+d_{m}(w^{\prime})H_{m}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}(kw^{\prime})=\frac{-1}{kw^{\prime}}.

(44)

We find that

a_{m}=\frac{i\pi}{2}H_{m}^{(1)}(kw^{\prime}),\qquad d_{m}=\frac{i\pi}{2}J_{m}(kw^{\prime}),

(45)

so using the traditional notation, we write the Green function of the Helmholtz equation in the toroidal/poloidal coordinate system

G^{+}_{0}(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{w}^{\prime})=\frac{i\pi}{2}\frac{e^{i(v^{\prime}-v)/2}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}\sqrt{R+w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime}}}\sum_{m}J_{m}(kw_{<})H_{m}^{(1)}(kw_{>})e^{im(u-u^{\prime})},

(46)

with the notation
w_{<}=\min(w,w^{\prime})
,
w_{>}=\max(w,w^{\prime})
. To obtain a closed form of the Green function in these coordinates, notice that for a toroidally symmetric system with source at
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}=0
, equation (
37
) reads

\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial w^{2}}+\left(\frac{1}{w}+\frac{\cos u}{R+w\cos u}\right)\frac{\partial G}{\partial w}+\frac{1}{(R+w\cos u)^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial v^{2}}+k^{2}G=-\frac{\delta(w-w^{\prime})}{w}.

(47)

Therefore, for
w\neq w^{\prime}
, the incoming wave solution is

G(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{w}^{\prime})=B_{0}\frac{H_{0}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}|)}{\sqrt{(R+w\cos u)e^{iv}}\sqrt{(R+w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime})e^{iv^{\prime}}}}.

Taking
w^{\prime}\to 0
and using

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\oint\frac{\partial G}{\partial w}\biggr\rvert_{w=\epsilon}dS_{\epsilon}=-1,

one calculates
B_{0}=-1/16
. Thus we obtain,

G(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{w}^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{16}\frac{H_{0}^{(1)}(k|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}|)}{\sqrt{(R+w\cos u)e^{iv}}\sqrt{(R+w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime})e^{iv^{\prime}}}}.

(48)

This closed form of our Green function is symmetric, and we can use it to calculate an eigenfunction expansion for the plane wave.

IV.1
The Plane Wave

In scattering problems, it is useful to use a plane wave as an impinging wave because of its simplicity and versatility. To calculate an eigenfunction expansion of a plane wave, notice that if we naively try

e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{m}B_{m}\frac{J_{m}(kw)e^{imu}e^{-iv/2}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}}

we will end up facing seemingly innocent integrals that will result in expressions involving elliptic functions. Instead, contemplate taking the source to a position very far from our field point using equation (
46
). The asymptotic behavior of our Hankel function is, with
z\to\infty
,

H_{m}^{(1)}(z)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}}\exp{\left[i\left(z-\frac{m\pi}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\right]}.

Since
w^{\prime}\to\infty
, we also notice
|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{\prime}|\approx w^{\prime}-\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{w}
. With this,

G(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\approx\frac{i\pi}{2}\frac{e^{i(v^{\prime}-v)/2}}{\sqrt{R+w\cos u}\sqrt{w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{k\pi w^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}e^{ikw^{\prime}}\sum_{m}(-i)^{m}J_{m}(kw)e^{im(u-u^{\prime})},

(49)

Simultaneously, as we take
w^{\prime}\to\infty
to observe the closed form (
48
) in its asymptotic behavior, we get

G(\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{w}^{\prime})\approx-\frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{1}{k\pi w^{\prime}}}\frac{e^{ikw^{\prime}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{w}}}{\sqrt{w^{\prime}\cos u^{\prime}e^{iv^{\prime}}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(R+w\cos u)e^{iv}}}.

(50)

Comparing this with (
49
) results in

e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{w}}=-8\pi i\sum_{m}i^{m}J_{m}(kw)e^{im(u-u_{k})}e^{-iv_{k}}.

(51)

which has a surprisingly simple form.

V
Final remarks

We obtained, for the first time, the solution of the Helmholtz equation in Moon and Spencer’s toroidal coordinates using a
\mathcal{R}
-separable solution, namely (
2
). This represents a progress in the longstanding problem of analytically solving the Helmholtz equation in unorthodox coordinate systems. A similar approach was used in the toroidal–poloidal case, yielding a closed-form solution, (
16
), in terms of Bessel’s functions. This time, however, the problem is that the coordinate system only describes half of a torus, while covering the whole
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Despite this, we went on to find the Green function and a plane-wave expansion. We also studied the quantum mechanics of a particle interacting with quite complicated potentials, (
19
) and (
27
), and presented exact solutions for the eigenfunctions in terms of bi-confluent and double-confluent Heun’s functions.

The inherent properties of this misleading geometry took long time to be understood, but we presented two solutions which may push this area further. We expect future research to continue from this point, bringing physical and engineering applications to this problem.
