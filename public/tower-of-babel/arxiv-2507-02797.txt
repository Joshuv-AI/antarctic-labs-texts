A Highly Carbon-Rich Dayside and Disequilibrium Chemistry in the Ultra-Hot Jupiter WASP-19b
Suman Saha, James S. Jenkins

Abstract
Ultra-hot Jupiters (UHJs) offer exceptional opportunities for detailed atmospheric characterization via emission spectroscopy. Here we present a comprehensive analysis of the dayside atmosphere of WASP-19b—one of the shortest-period UHJs—using archival JWST NIRSpec/PRISM observations spanning 0.6-5.3 μ \mu m. We report robust detections of H 2 O (16.44 σ \sigma ), CO (5.47 σ \sigma ), and CO 2 (10.72 σ \sigma ), along with marginal detections of CH 4 (3.76 σ \sigma ) and C 2 H 2 (2.45 σ \sigma ). The retrieved composition reveals a highly carbon-rich atmosphere with a tightly constrained super-solar C/O ratio of 0.94 ± \pm 0.03. Elevated abundances of carbon-bearing species provide strong evidence (11.69 σ \sigma ) for disequilibrium chemistry. We also detect condensate clouds, likely Al 2 O 3 (c), at high significance (17.28 σ \sigma ), and constrain the atmospheric metallicity to 1.7 + 1.2 − 0.7 {}_{-0.7}^{+1.2} × \times solar. These results establish a precise benchmark for modeling dayside conditions in extreme irradiated atmospheres and demonstrate JWST’s transformative capabilities for exoplanet science.



Emission spectroscopy of transiting exoplanets is rapidly emerging as one of the most powerful techniques for probing the physical structures and chemical compositions of their atmospheres. Ultra-hot Jupiters (UHJs), with day-side temperatures exceeding 2000K, are particularly well-suited for such studies due to their strong thermal emission and extended atmospheres
[

,

,

]
. Near-to mid-infrared emission spectra of these extreme worlds can reveal the precise atmospheric abundances of key molecular species—such as H
2
O, CO, CO
2
, and SiO—as well as place constraints on cloud composition and vertical structure
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Since UHJs are often thought to have formed through unique evolutionary pathways, such atmospheric characterization may also offer valuable insights into their origins and dynamical histories
[
7
,

]
.

WASP-19b
[
9
]
, which was the shortest-period transiting exoplanet known at the time of its discovery, orbits its G8V-type host star in just
\sim
0.79 days. With an equilibrium temperature of 2113K, and a radius of 1.41 R
J

[
10
]
, it lies well within the UHJ population, while orbiting the coolest host star among them. These characteristics make WASP-19b a particularly compelling target for atmospheric follow-up studies. Several previous attempts have been made to characterize its atmosphere using both ground- as well as space-based observations, despite the challenge posed by its slightly fainter host star (V
mag

\approx
12.25).

Previous studies using HST and Spitzer have reported the detection of water vapor in the atmosphere of WASP-19b, along with the presence of hazes absorbing at shorter wavelengths

. Ground-based optical to near-infrared observations using VLT/FORS2, Magellan/IMACS, and VLT/ESPRESSO have also suggested the possible presence of TiO and reaffirmed the existence of high-altitude clouds and hazes
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. However, these earlier studies were unable to robustly constrain key chemical properties such as the metallicity or carbon-to-oxygen (C/O) ratio, largely due to instrumental limitations and the relative faintness of the host star.

The C/O ratio, in particular, has been widely regarded as a key tracer of the formation and migration history of giant exoplanets
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
. Super-solar C/O ratios inferred for several UHJs
[
24
,
25
,
26
]
suggest formation beyond the snow line, followed by inward migration. However, the relatively large uncertainties in these estimates have so far limited their ability to robustly constrain such formation pathways. The C/O ratio has also been proposed as a diagnostic for distinguishing between formation via core accretion and gravitational collapse
[
27
,
28
]
. Additionally, oxygen depletion through sequestration into refractory condensates has been suggested as a mechanism that may artificially elevate the observed C/O ratios in gas-giant atmospheres
[
29
]
.

Robust estimation of the C/O ratio in planetary atmospheres requires precise measurements of the abundances of both carbon- and oxygen-bearing molecules. Since many of these species exhibit strong absorption features in the near- to mid-infrared, earlier ground-based instruments—limited both in sensitivity and spectral coverage in these wavelengths—have struggled to provide the necessary precision. However, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)
[
30
]
, with its broad and high-precision spectroscopic capabilities spanning this critical wavelength range, marks a major advancement. It enables the accurate detection of multiple key molecular species, thereby allowing unprecedented precision in the retrieval of atmospheric abundances, C/O ratios, and metallicities.

]
in the Bright Object Time Series (BOTS)

and exoTEDRF (formerly supreme-SPOON)

We then binned the data into 200 near-equally spaced wavelength bins to produce spectrally resolved 1D lightcurves, which were modeled using our custom-built pipeline, ExoELF (ExoplanEts Lightcurves Fitter). The emission spectra resulting from both reductions are in excellent statistical agreement, with a mean absolute difference of
\sim 1\sigma
(Figure

Lightcurves Modeling

Retrieving Atmospheric Properties

We conducted atmospheric retrievals on the observed emission spectra to identify the key molecular features. Synthetic spectra were generated using petitRADTRANS (pRT)
[
35
]
, incorporating both free and equilibrium chemistry. Our models included key molecular opacities expected at the relevant temperatures, such as H
2
O, CO, CO
2
, SiO, CH
4
, H
2
S, C
2
H
2
, HCN, PH
3
, SO
2
, TiO, and VO, along with condensate species like Fe(s), MgSiO
3
(s), Al
2
O
3
(s), and SiO
2
(s). Retrievals were performed using the pRT retrieval routines
[
36
]
, which leverage PyMultinest
[
37
,
38
]
for Bayesian inference.

The high S/N emission spectra from our observations, combined with the fact that emission spectral features are more prominent in ultra-hot atmospheres (compared to transmission spectra), allowed our free chemistry retrievals to efficiently fit the data. The retrieval on the Eureka! spectra yielded highly constrained estimations of the abundances of several key molecular species—H
2
O, CO, CO
2
, CH
4
, and C
2
H
2
(see Table
1
)—while also marginally constraining the SiO abundance. The reflection-dominated part of the spectra was also well fitted by our cloud models, primarily indicating the presence of Al
2
O
3
(s).

To assess the statistical significance of our molecular detections, we also performed fits with those species removed from our models (see Figure
2
and Table
1
). We report statistically significant detections of H
2
O, CO, and CO
2
at 16.44
\sigma
, 5.47
\sigma
and 10.72
\sigma
, respectively, along with a strong detection of CH
4
(3.76
\sigma
) and a marginal detection of C
2
H
2
(2.45
\sigma
). Interestingly, we do not detect TiO or VO. The detection of multiple key C- and O-bearing molecules, along with precise abundance estimates, is particularly valuable in the exoplanetary context, as it enables us to constrain the C/O ratio and the metallicity with high accuracy. Additionally, we detect the presence of clouds in the atmosphere at 17.28
\sigma
. Our best-fit temperature-pressure profiles reveal a strong thermal inversion near 0.01 bar, though its lower boundary remained unconstrained due to the presence of high-pressure cloud layers (also see Figure
S4
).

Performing the free chemistry retrieval on the exoTEDRF spectra also resulted in the precise abundance estimates of H
2
O, CO, CO
2
and CH
4
, all of which are in close statistical agreement with those from the Eureka! spectra. However, it was unable to constrain C
2
H
2
, confirming only a marginal detection from the Eureka! modeling. Nonetheless, the strong statistical agreement between the two independent analyses further verifies the robustness of our key findings.

Next we performed equilibrium chemistry modeling on our observed spectra, which failed to reproduce some key spectral features (see Figure
S7
), even though it yields a reasonable overall fit to the data. For the Eureka! spectra, we found that the equilibrium chemistry model was 11.69
\sigma
less significant than the free chemistry model fit. Further investigation revealed that the equilibrium chemistry models could not support the much higher abundances of several carbon-bearing species—such as CO, CO
2
and C
2
H
2
—retrieved from the free chemistry analysis (see Table
1
). This provides statistically significant observational evidence of disequilibrium chemistry phenomena in this atmosphere. While the possibility of detecting disequilibrium chemistry signatures in exoplanet atmospheres has been explored in previous theoretical studies
[
39
,
40
]
, only a handful of such detections have been reported in literature
[
41
,
42
]
, and none with significant statistical evidence. Additionally, we find that existing models may be too limited to account for all key factors that could explain such a strong observational detection. Thus, our findings provide a benchmark for testing and refining disequilibrium chemistry models, incorporating factors that have yet to be implemented.

A Precisely Measured Super-Solar C/O Ratio

Using the precisely estimated abundances of the confidently detected molecular species from the free chemistry retrievals (Eureka!)—i.e., H
2
O, CO, CO
2
, and CH
4
—we constrained the C/O ratio to a highly precise super-solar value of 0.937
\pm
0.032. This is considerably more precise than the well-constrained C/O ratios reported for various exoplanets in the literature (see Figure
3
, where the C/O ratio values are adopted from
[
43
,
44
,
26
,
25
,
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
24
,
50
]
, and some equilibrium temperatures adopted from
[
51
,
52
]
). Including the marginally detected C
2
H
2
abundance yields a C/O ratio of 1.18
\pm
0.25, which remains statistically consistent with the previous value, though the broader uncertainties reflect the lower confidence in the C
2
H
2
abundance.

The exceptional precision of the C/O ratio, directly estimated from the molecular abundances, can be attributed to the unparalleled capability of JWST in precisely measuring the abundances of key molecular species, as well as the effectiveness of emission spectroscopy in probing UHJ atmospheres. The comparison suggests that super-solar C/O ratios may be more common within the UHJ population, with an emerging trend between the C/O ratio and equilibrium temperature (see Figure
3
). To explore this potential correlation, we have modeled C/O ratios as a quadratic function of equilibrium temperature:
\mathrm{C/O}=\mathrm{a}\,\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{2}+\mathrm{b}\,\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{eq}}+\mathrm{c}
, with the estimated coefficients
\mathrm{a}=0.62\pm 0.29
,
\mathrm{b}=(-5.90\pm 4.05)\times 10^{-4}
, and

\mathrm{c}=(2.76\pm 1.26)\times 10^{-7}
. However, aside from this work, the other C/O ratios for UHJs shown are not based on JWST observations. Additionally, the overall uncertainties in the previous studies are too large to draw any confident conclusion. Given JWST’s ability to provide more reliable C/O ratio estimates, especially when combined with emission spectroscopy, we emphasize the need for additional similar studies covering a broader range of UHJ and other planetary populations. An expanded catalog of precisely estimated C/O ratios across a larger exoplanet population would facilitate global analyses, connecting these values to planetary and host star properties, ultimately leading to a clearer picture of their formation and evolution.

From the estimated abundances of key molecular species, we also precisely constrained the metallicity to 1.72
{}_{-0.69}^{+1.19}

\times
solar, which falls within the expected range for typical gas giants (e.g., those in our Solar System). This suggests less vigorous photoevaporation of non-metals in the atmosphere
[
53
]
. The smaller G8-type host star—resulting in lower levels of highly energetic irradiation—may have contributed to this, despite the planet’s close-in orbit. Furthermore, the atmosphere’s highly carbon-rich atmosphere, reflected in a C/O ratio significantly higher than the solar value, indicates that the planet likely formed far beyond the ice line, before migrating inwards to its current orbit
[
8
,
54
]
. However, due to possible accretion during the migration, distinguishing between core accretion and gravitational collapse as the formation mechanism remains challenging
[
27
,
28
]
. Additionally, as previously suggested
[
29
]
, the elevated C/O ratio could result from oxygen depletion through sequestration into refractory condensates. Thus, our precise estimations provide an ideal testbed for developing next-generation planet formation and evolution models, enabling deeper insights into the extreme processes that shape UHJ formations.
