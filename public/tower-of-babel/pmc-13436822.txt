Optimism and mindfulness are associated with decreased abdominal pain among adolescents with inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Meghan Z Benchimol, C Meghan McMurtry, Paula Forgeron, Jennifer N Stinson, Amanda Ricciuto, Jason D Rights, Sara Ahola Kohut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic relapsing and remitting disease, frequently causing abdominal pain. The gut-brain axis provides an extensive framework to understand the relationship between IBD and psychological well-being. Accumulating evidence indicates that prolonged psychological stress may worsen IBD symptoms and recurrence. Resilience factors (eg, optimism, self-efficacy, mindfulness) are associated with improved outcomes in other populations with recurrent pain; however, it has not been investigated in adolescent IBD populations.AimsTo examine the association between resilience factors and abdominal pain intensity in adolescents with IBD.MethodsA cross-sectional study of 70 adolescents (aged 12-17 years) with IBD (51% male, 47% in clinical remission) was conducted. Multivariable hurdle models were used to predict the presence and degree of pain. In conditional models, all variables were adjusted for age and sex.ResultsIn all models, disease activity and optimism were significant predictors of pain presence (OR 0.92 [95% CI, 0.85-0.99]). In unconditional models, sex was associated with degree of pain. Predicted pain intensity was 1.37 times greater for females than males. Sex was not significantly associated with pain presence. Increased clinical disease activity, lower optimism, and lower mindfulness scores were associated with pain presence in adolescents with IBD.ConclusionsOur findings support an association between clinical disease activity, optimism, mindfulness, and pain. These results highlight the potential benefits of incorporating resilience-based pain management strategies alongside clinical disease assessment and treatment for adolescents with IBD.Keywords: inflammatory bowel disease, paediatrics, psychology, pain, mindfulness, optimism

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is an umbrella term that consists of 2 main chronic inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract, Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). Some patients may exhibit overlapping features of CD and UC, leading to a third classification known as inflammatory bowel disease unclassified (IBD-U).1 Increasing evidence suggests a strong association between the gastrointestinal system and the nervous system, often referred to as the gut-brain axis. The gut-brain axis represents a complex and bidirectional communication network, combining signalling routes of the enteric and central nervous systems.2 The gut-brain axis has offered additional insight into the biopsychosocial model, providing an extensive framework to understand the relationship between IBD and psychological well-being through the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors.3 Based on the concept of the gut-brain axis, accumulating evidence indicates that prolonged psychological stress may worsen IBD symptoms and lead to IBD flares. Furthermore, patients with IBD are more likely to experience symptoms of anxiety and depression compared to healthy individuals.4 The precise mechanism of this association remains partially unknown.1 Other psychological attributes, both positive (eg, optimism, mindfulness) and negative (eg, anxiety, catastrophizing, shame), have been shown to impact the lived experience of IBD including pain and quality of life.3,5-7

Abdominal pain is a key symptom of IBD. Multiple factors can account for pain, including physical, psychological, and social factors, supporting a multidimensional biopsychosocial model for the experience of pain in IBD.8 A multidimensional view on pain recognizes that pain is a subjective experience, thereby allowing it to be a target for psychological interventions.9 For example, pain catastrophizing has been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in overall health, functioning, and quality of life across multiple populations/samples.10 Psychological interventions of pain often attempt to improve functioning by increasing self-management behaviours and decreasing catastrophizing behaviours.9 Recent research has explored resilience-based coping as a supplement to existing disease management approaches. One example is the Resilience5 model, which targets individuals living with IBD and highlights 5 key positive psychological attributes that support disease-interfering self-management behaviours: self-efficacy, disease acceptance, self-regulation, social support, and optimism. Individuals high in the Resilience5 components are likely confident in their ability to take necessary action to effectively manage their condition, while adhering to treatment plans recommended by healthcare providers.11 Approximately 10%-20% of individuals newly diagnosed with IBD are under the age of 18, in a pivotal developmental stage.12 Early identification and intervention of IBD, in physical and psychological mechanisms, may assist adolescents in developing resilience, while establishing effective coping mechanisms.13,14

The aim of this study was to examine the association between resilience factors and abdominal pain in adolescences with IBD. Based on the Resilience5 core components, 4 resilience factors (optimism, self-efficacy, mindfulness, and psychological flexibility) and 2 social factors (friendship satisfaction, family functioning) were selected for assessment.11,15-17 We hypothesized that higher levels of resilience factors would be associated with the absence of abdominal pain or a decreased degree of pain. Through identifying resilience factors or specific resilience skills associated with pain, our research aimed to identify treatment targets for minimizing the impact of abdominal pain in an IBD sample.

Methods

Ethical considerations

Approval was granted by the SickKids Research Ethics Board (#1000056622). All procedures adhered to institutional review board guidelines and all participants provided written informed consent.

Design

Data were collected as a part of a larger cross-sectional mixed method study on resilience in adolescent IBD at an academic teaching hospital (The Hospital for Sick Children [SickKids], Toronto, Canada) between February and July 2019.18 Question­naire selection was informed by patient, caregiver, healthcare provider interviews.

Participants

We recruited patients aged 12-17 years with a minimum of 6 months since diagnosis of IBD (CD, UC, or IBDU), as well as proficiency in speaking and understanding English. Diagnosis with IBD was made by clinicians using standard criteria.19 Participants admitted to hospital at time of screening were ineligible, to avoid confounding results from medication changes and feeling of overwhelm.

Procedure

Participants were recruited during routine visits to the IBD clinic. A total of 95 potential participants were approached consecutively; however due to patient flow within the clinic, some potential participants may have been missed. Of the approached potential participants, 20 declined to participate. Participants completed a series of self-report questionnaires online via REDCap on their demographic and resilience information at a single time point, within a week of their IBD visit. Medical records were reviewed and data collected for disease and health-related information.

Measures

Demographic and medical information

The following demographic and medical information were collected from the medical chart: age, sex, self-reported ethnicity, IBD subtype, current IBD disease activity, current IBD therapies, months since diagnosis, presence of an ostomy. Pain intensity and unpleasantness were collected as a part of the questionnaire on REDCap. All questionnaires demonstrated reliability and validity in adolescent populations, and, when possible, in populations with IBD.20-24 Disease activity was classified using the Paediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index for participants with CD and the Paediatric Ulcerative Colitis Activity Index for the participants with UC/IBD-U.25,26 Using established criteria, the continuous disease activity ratings were converted into a categorical 4-level variable ­representing increasing levels of disease severity (none, mild, moderate, severe).25,27,28

Pain: presence, intensity, unpleasantness

Pain presence and intensity was measured using questions 7 through 10 of the Wisconsin Brief Pain Questionnaire, a validated measure of pain (Cronbach’s α = .88).29 Participants were asked to indicate if they had experienced pain within the previous month. They were then prompted to self-report their average levels of pain intensity and unpleasantness using a scale from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable) over that time. The work of Stinson et al30 was adapted to be used as the measure for pain unpleasantness.

Self-efficacy

Inflammatory Bowel Disease Self-Efficacy Scale—Adolescent (IBD-SES-A) is a 13-item questionnaire that assesses an individual’s confidence in managing IBD-related challenges across multiple domains: managing medical care, managing daily activities, managing emotions, and managing the future. A total score reflects the ability of an individual to effectively manage a combination of all 4 domains, with higher scores reflecting increased self-efficacy. IBD-SES-A demonstrated a high level of internal consistency in our sample (Cronbach’s α = .73).20

Mindfulness

The Children’s Acceptance and Mindfulness Measure (CAMM) is a 10-item questionnaire that measures characteristics of mindfulness and internal awareness across 3 domains: The degree to which an individual attends to internal phenomena (thoughts, feelings, and physical sensations) maintains present-moment awareness, and presents openness and acceptance to experiencing a full range of internal events without judgment. Higher scores reflect a higher degree of mindfulness. CAMM is a reliable method of assessing mindfulness (Cronbach’s α = .82).21

Psychological flexibility

The Avoidance and Fusion Questionnaire for Youth (AFQ-Y8) is an 8-item questionnaire that measures psychological inflexibility in 2 domains: experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion. Higher scores represent an individual that is more psychologically inflexible. AFQ-Y8 is a reliable method of assessing psychological flexibility within our sample (Cronbach’s α = .87).22

Optimism

The Youth Life Orientation Test (YLOT) is a 16-item questionnaire that assess dispositional optimism and pessimism in youth. The present study used a 6-item dispositional optimism subscale to reduce participant burden and align with the goals of the overall study. Higher scores reflect higher optimism. The YLOT demonstrated internal consistency for assessing optimism in our sample (Cronbach’s α = .88).23

Friendship satisfaction

Friendship satisfaction was assessed using a 3-item questionnaire designed for this study. Participants were asked to assess their satisfaction on a scale of 1-5 (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied) in 3 domains: (1) “How satisfied are you with the number of friends you have?,” (2) “How satisfied are you with the quality of your friendships?,” and (3) “How satisfied are you with how your friendships are going?” Measures of friendship satisfaction demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = .92).

Family functioning

The Brief Family Assessment Measure—General Scale (Brief FAM-GS) is a 14-item questionnaire that assesses the strengths and weaknesses of a family unit from the child’s perspective. Higher scores reflect improved family function. Brief FAM-GS is a reliable method of assessing family functioning (Cronbach’s α = .90).24

Statistical analysis

Means, standard deviations, ranges, and percentages were reported to describe sample characteristics. Pearson correlations were conducted to examine correlations among biopsychosocial variables. Exploratory analyses were conducted, examining differences between the group reporting no pain and the group reporting any pain with independent samples’ Student t-tests. The primary analysis used hurdle models with pain intensity as the dependent variable. Hurdle models contain 2 submodels—a logistic regression predicting whether the dependent variable is 0 or non-0, and a truncated Poisson regression predicting the counts for non-0 values. Thus, these models allowed us to simultaneously examine predictors of the presence of pain and the intensity of pain and accounted for the zero inflation and positive skew of average pain ratings. Given the exploratory nature of this study, we conducted both conditional models that contain all aforementioned clinically relevant predictors, and unconditional models that contain a single predictor at a time. Given the small number of individuals with a diagnosis of IBD-U (n = 2), this categorical variable was collapsed with UC, and, similarly, the small group of severe disease activity (n = 4) was collapsed with the moderate group (analysis of models without collapsing lead to an identical pattern of statistical significance).

Results

Participant demographics

The study sample consisted of 75 individuals aged 12-17 years. However, 5 individuals had to be excluded for not completing the online questionnaires, leaving 70 individuals (mean age 15.17 years ±1.62 SD). Of 70 included participants, there was 0.5% missing data, where imputed means were inserted. Please see Table 1 for summary of demographic variables. The duration since IBD diagnosis among participants was mean 3.9 years (±0.14 years) with a range from 0.5 to 15.5 years. The sample was nearly evenly split by sex (female n = 34, 49%, male n = 36, 51%) and was predominantly Caucasian (n = 51, 68%). Most individuals had CD (n = 46, 66%) and few individuals had an ostomy (n = 7, 10%). Most participants were on biologic medications (n = 44, 63%) and just under half were in clinical remission (n = 33, 47%).

Presence and degree of pain

Of those reporting pain (n = 46, 53%), the average pain intensity was 4.52 (SD 1.81). Females reported higher degree of pain compared to males (mean female 3.65 ± 2.74, mean male 2.40 ± 2.34; 2-sided P-value: .034). When comparing pain and no pain groups (pain intensity = 0, pain intensity > 0), no significant differences were found in age, sex, disease subtype, time since diagnosis. Clinical remission rates in the no-pain group (n = 18, 78.3%) were significantly higher than in the pain group (n = 15, 31.9%; P < .001). In self-reported variables, mindfulness and optimism were significantly different between groups (Table 2).

Correlational analyses

Pain intensity and unpleasantness were both initially included as outcomes; however, as a result of their strong correlation (r = 0.932), pain unpleasantness was removed from subsequent analyses to maintain parsimony. As shown in Table 3, mindfulness and psychological inflexibility were inversely correlated (r = − 0.727), showing a moderate negative association. There was a moderate negative correlation between family functioning and self-efficacy (r = − 0.626), indicating that higher levels of function within families are associated with lower levels of self-efficacy. Optimism was correlated with all resilience variables (r = ± 0.287 to 0.461) and family functioning (r = − 0.541). Pain intensity was correlated with optimism and mindfulness (optimism: r = − 0.405, mindfulness: r = −0.318).

Predicting presence and degree of pain

In the logistic regression portion of the hurdle model predicting presence of pain, at alpha = 0.05, only disease activity and optimism were significant predictors (optimism conditional OR 0.74 [95% CI, 0.58-0.94]; mild versus inactive/remission conditional OR 12.134 [95% CI, 2.00-73.74]; moderate/severe versus inactive/remission conditional OR 27.339 [95% CI, 1.916-390.075]). Mindfulness was not statistically significant at P = .107 in the conditional model, whereas it was found to be a significant predictor in the unconditional model (Tables 4 and 5). Holding all other predictors constant, the predicted odds of having pain versus no pain decreases by 26% for each unit increase in optimism (OR =0.74; P = .01). In the unconditional model, the predicted odds of having pain versus no pain decreases by 8% for each unit increase in mindfulness (OR =0.92, P = .035). Thus, lower levels of optimism and mindfulness were related to the presence of pain. In the unconditional regression submodels predicting intensity of pain, only biological sex was a significant predictor (Table 4). Predicted pain intensity was 1.37 times greater for females than males (unconditional OR = 1.37; P = .028).

Discussion

This study examined the association between resilience factors and self-reported abdominal pain intensity in adolescents with IBD. Increased clinical disease activity and lower optimism were significantly associated with of the presence of pain. Decreased mindfulness was significantly associated with pain presence in only the unconditional model. Sex was found to be associated with the degree of pain, but not the presence of pain in an unconditional model. No other variables were found to be significantly associated with pain in our study.

Optimism is considered to be a general anticipation of a favourable outcome, closely associated with the behaviour or health of an individual. It enables individuals to navigate negative events or challenging situations with an enhanced sense of hope and determination.31 Consistent with the literature, our findings have revealed that lower levels of optimism were associated with the presence of pain. While this may be due to approximately half of our sample being in clinical remission and reporting no pain indicating favourable IBD symptom control, optimism was also correlated with lower pain and higher levels of resilience factors.32-34 According to Hanssen et al,32 optimism has been linked to improved pain coping mechanisms and decreased pain sensitivity in healthy samples. Furthermore, studies have shown that individuals with increased levels of optimism have more favourable chronic pain outcomes as they may be less attentive to their pain and more inclined to adjust to cope with their condition.6,35-39 Optimistic individuals have a greater pain threshold, enabling them to endure more pain prior to distress or functional disability.35,39 This may be a result of an increased probability of using positive coping strategies instead of unhealthy mechanisms in individual with greater levels of optimism.6,35-38 Of note, given the limitations of existing conceptualizations and measurements of coping, future research should explore relationships between coping and pain over time in a more nuanced manner and considering individual goals.40 Overall, these findings are in line with literature in other chronic conditions showing optimism is a protective factor for pain.41,42

Optimism has also been shown to be an effective strategy in minimizing the impact of stress. Stress is a well-known trigger for IBD flare-ups and many other chronic conditions, worsening symptoms and heightening pain experience.1,43 Murphy et al44 found that the presence and degree of pain was predicted by adolescent internalizing factors such as pain catastrophizing and fear of pain. Integrating an optimistic disposition could assist in mitigating the negative consequences of internalizing on pain. Taking this into account, inclusion of optimism training as a component of pre-existing stress management intervention may support adolescents living with IBD, both to bolster resilience to pain and to mitigates the harmful consequences of stress on their health.45,46

Lower mindfulness was associated with pain presence, but not degree of pain in the unconditional model. This is in contrast to literature and clinical practice whereby mindfulness is a core therapeutic approach in chronic pain populations for its ability to decrease pain intensity. Adolescents with IBD encounter unique challenges such as dietary restrictions, frequent medical appointments, and unpredictable symptoms, influencing their engagement in mindfulness practices.47-49 An adolescent study on a mindfulness-based intervention in IBD indicated improvements in emotional functioning, and perhaps peer interactions, symptoms of depression, and mindfulness skills.47 Mindfulness-based interventions are also effective in adults with IBD to reduce stress, anxiety, depression, and improve quality of life.50-52 As a result of this connection, further research should examine the mechanism of mindfulness influencing pain perception in adolescent IBD.

Females in this study reported a higher degree of pain intensity compared with males (Cohen’s d = 0.52). The literature demonstrated either women endorsing pain more frequently than men, or no reported differences by biological sex in population and clinical data.53-55 Our study was split relatively evenly between males (51%) and females (49%), thus providing further evidence in binary sex differences of pain reporting in adolescent IBD samples. Given our sample did not have any participants select or identify as non-binary, further research is needed to explore beyond the male/female dichotomy.

Family functioning and friendship satisfaction were not associated with pain in this sample. This is in contrast with the literature as social factors have been well established to play a role in expression, perception, and management of pain.56 This finding may be unique to IBD as the symptoms experienced are often stigmatized and viewed as socially embarrassing, potentially impacting adolescents’ willingness to engage in social supports.57 Furthermore, adolescents with IBD are more likely to accommodate their GI symptoms at the expense of social connection (eg, avoiding public bathrooms and social outings).58-65 Future research would benefit from more nuanced and validated measures of social factors in adolescent IBD, such as friendship quality (eg, Friendship Quality Scale, McGill Friendship Questionnaire) or Procidano and Heller’s measures of perceived social support from friends.66-68 Positive family functioning was not associated with pain in our study; however, our previous work (n = 100) found that parent miscarried helping (eg, overprotective caregiver behaviours) was associated with increased pain ratings.58 Perhaps, supporting anxious caregivers may be an effective family approach to pain management in IBD.

Psychological flexibility and IBD self-efficacy were not associated with presence or intensity of pain, contrasting the literature linking both to positive outcomes in paediatric and adult IBD samples.35,59,64,69-71 Some studies have indicated that successful IBD self-management therapies may be most helpful when they boost self-efficacy; however, the exact nature of this association is unclear.64,69-71 Taking into account the correlation between optimism, psychological flexibility, and self-efficacy, these variables could be interrelated in ways that complicate individual effects on pain perception.

In line with the biopsychosocial model of pain, both disease-related (disease activity) and psychological (optimism) factors were associated with the presence of pain. Given the relapsing and remitting nature of IBD, it would be important for future research to explore adolescents’ pain experience across various levels of clinical disease activity and how this interacts with optimism. Perhaps youth who have experienced periods of remission have increased optimism about the duration of future IBD-flares. Future research may also wish to consider frequency, length, and severity of IBD flares on pain experience and coping in longitudinal work.

The results of our study highlight the potential advantages of resilience-based pain management strategies for adolescents with IBD through clinical application. Given that optimism and mindfulness are trainable and teachable skills, brief interventions can be integrated into the routine care of adolescents with IBD. The development of resilience factors may be encouraged through language in clinical settings. This may be implemented through emphasis on the patient’s strengths and capabilities, while reframing challenges to focus on growth and problem-solving. In order to properly understand the long-term advantages on pain-management and overall quality of life, conducting a longitudinal study to examine the development and implementation of resilience skills in adolescents with IBD is needed.

Our study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Our sample was limited to 70 participants from a single centre and consequently it may be underpowered. Half of the sample was in clinical remission and reporting little to no pain. This is not atypical in IBD samples from routine clinic visits as medical treatments are often successful. Future pain research in IBD may wish to consider targeting those with ongoing pain or difficult to treat IBD. Although variables were modelled as predictors of pain, the cross-sectional design of this study cannot support causal or directional inferences. It is plausible that increased pain intensity and disease severity hinder resilience factors. Thus, except for fixed characteristics, the identified predictors should be interpreted as correlational associations rather than causal. Furthermore, the cross-sectional nature of this research did not allow examination of how the association between variables and pain may change over time. Future longitudinal research is warranted to clarify these relationships. Although prior research indicates it may affect resilience factors, coping skills, and functioning with pain, socioeconomic status was not assessed and may have influenced outcomes.72 Lastly, insufficient multicultural and gender diverse individuals in the study restricted the ability of the results to be applied and generalized across various racial, ethnocultural, and gender groups.

Overall, this study highlights the significance of resilience factors, such as optimism and mindfulness, in addition to disease activity in predicting the presence of pain in adolescents with IBD. While sex was found as a predictor of the degree of pain, no significant associations were found between pain and psychological flexibility, self-efficacy, family functioning, friendship satisfaction, or duration after diagnosis. The findings align with the existing literature on the biopsychosocial model in pain management. Future research with larger, diverse samples and longitudinal designs are needed to better understand these associations and create effective, resilience-based pain management interventions for this unique population.
