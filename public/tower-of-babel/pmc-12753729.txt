National assessment of transit electrification in Canada: infrastructure costs, energy demand, and greenhouse gas reduction potential
Authors: Hatem Abdelaty, Moataz Mohamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Battery electric buses (BEBs) offer a scalable solution for decarbonizing public transit; however, comprehensive national-level assessments remain limited. This study presents the first bottom-up evaluation of BEB adoption across Canada, using open-source data from 102 transit providers. We quantify fleet requirements, infrastructure needs, electricity demand, and the cost-effectiveness of greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions under a fully electrified bus transit system. Our analysis indicates a required 17% increase in fleet size and an additional 1.255 TWh of electricity annually, representing just 0.20% of Canada’s total generation. GHG emissions would decline by over 92% to approximately 130,000 tonnes annually, with the social cost of carbon falling by a similar margin (92.68%). These findings demonstrate that nationwide BEB deployment is technically feasible, economically manageable, and environmentally impactful. This study offers reproducible, open-source-based, critical evidence to guide energy planning, policy decisions, and investments in a sustainable, zero-emission transit future.

Introduction

Transportation electrification has emerged as a cornerstone of sustainable urban mobility. The shift from fossil fuel to clean energy alternatives enables cities to cut greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and reduce dependence on fossil fuel, fostering healthier urban environments supporting global decarbonization goals1–6. In public transit applications and beyond environmental impacts, electrifying bus transit aligns with long-term economic and social sustainability goals (SDG-11), offering substantial cost savings through reduced maintenance and reliance on stable, increasingly renewable energy sources. Electric bus (e-bus) technology is advancing rapidly with notable cost reductions. It is indeed a viable economic choice for many transit systems7,8.

Supportive policies, standards, and pilot programs have been introduced globally to accelerate the adoption of e-buses9. However, such transition poses serious challenges attributed to the complexities of converting transit systems to new technology, entailing substantial upfront investments in e-buses, related ancillary infrastructure, and energy pathways10,11. In Canada, federal, provincial, and municipal governments are providing substantial incentives to deploy zero-emission transit nationwide, aligning with the long-term goal of net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 205012.

Acknowledging the significant progress that has been made in advancing e-bus adoption through targeted research and governmental support, there remains a critical need to explore the broader implications of e-bus adoption at the national level. The need for a comprehensive national-level understanding is echoed globally13,14, where current research on transit electrification primarily focuses on system-level analyses across three areas. (1) Technical performance and operational aspects, including battery sizing15, system configuration16–18, charging infrastructure allocation19,20, energy consumption21,22, and route optimization21,23,24 to improve efficiency and reliability under varied operating conditions. (2) Economic feasibility, entailing an assessment of the total cost of ownership to capture initial investments, maintenance, and fuel savings and to gauge the financial viability of e-buses compared to other alternatives8,25,26. (3) Environmental impact to analyze emission reductions and air quality improvements that align with sustainability goals3,27,28.

Previous studies provide valuable insights into the localized impacts of transit electrification as well as operational strategies. However, a comprehensive national assessment that quantifies infrastructure costs, power demands, and associated costs for GHG reductions remains lacking29. This gap hinders the comprehension of the multifaceted challenges and benefits of the national shift to zero-emission transit solutions.

As such, the primary aim of the present study is to assess the national-level impact of transit electrification across fleet costs, energy demand outlook, infrastructure planning, and associated environmental consequences.

Literature review: studies on electrification in the transportation sector.

Rodrigues30 examined the economic feasibility of adopting battery electric buses (BEBs) in Brazil between 2023 and 2030, focusing on the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) and the trade-offs between battery sizes and charging strategies. The results indicate that by 2030, BEBs are projected to have a lower TCO than diesel buses, particularly with overnight charging, estimated at 19.22 Brazilian Riyal/km compared to 24.65 Brazilian Riyal/km for diesel buses. However, opportunity charging involves higher infrastructure costs, comprising 10.45% of the TCO. Notably, the study did not assess emissions or energy outlooks related to different charging strategies and fleet configurations, leaving room for further exploration.

Liu31 investigated the benefits of transforming public transport depots into profitable energy hubs in Beijing, China, under scenarios aligned with China’s net-zero emissions target for 2050. Using solar photovoltaic (PV) and energy storage systems (ESS), the study presents solutions for reducing energy loads and improving efficiency, achieving CO2 emissions reductions of 1.4% to 5.7% compared to a baseline scenario where BEBs replace all other fuel-type buses. The marginal abatement costs for solar PV range from US$259 to US$223 per ton of CO2, while combining solar PV with ESS results in slightly reduced profitability, with costs ranging from US$220 to US$193 per ton of CO2. However, given their scope, the study did not consider the associated GHG emission potentials or the electrified fleet cost in their model.

In the EV literature, several studies provide valuable insights into the challenges and opportunities at a national level. Strobel37 assessed the integration of 10 million EVs in Germany by 2030, highlighting that conventional charging methods could lead to significant peak loads and inefficient energy utilization, whereas optimized charging strategies enhance energy distribution and reduce grid strain37.

In South Korea, Kim38 assessed the replacement of one million internal combustion engine vehicles (ICEVs) with EVs by 2030. The results show a projection to reduce GHG emissions by 55%, equivalent to 13–14 million tons. This reduction represents 6.0–6.4% of Korea’s national GHG targets and over half of the transportation sector’s goals38. Similarly, Moon35 analyzed the GHG emissions of EVs and ICEVs in South Korea, focusing on the impact of energy sources and production methods. Their findings show that EVs emit 1.57 tons of GHG, compared to 2.24 tons for ICEVs. However, the relatively high emissions from EVs are attributed to South Korea’s electricity generation profile, which relies on natural gas, highlighting the influence of energy generation on emissions. Hedegaard39 assessed the large-scale adoption of EVs across North European countries, estimating GHG emission reductions of 3%–28% with EV adoption of up to 53% by 2030. However, their findings highlight that these reductions are more costly and limited in the early stages, with total system impacts ranging from -0.9% to 3.1% depending on fuel and CO2 price fluctuations39.

From a cost perspective, Riesz40 assessed the Australian car fleet, comparing a rapid EV transition with continued ICEV use. They found that transitioning to EVs by 2025 would increase total costs by approximately 25%, assuming a slow decline in EV and battery costs and low fuel prices. Meanwhile, Nieto41 examined a 100% EV transition in the UK by 2050, highlighting macroeconomic benefits such as improved energy efficiency and economic growth. They found that energy inputs, including efficiency and rebound effects, boost economic growth to 2.25% annually, compared to 1.71% under a baseline scenario with ICE-based transport. However, the transition entails substantial costs for infrastructure, vehicle production, and energy system upgrades. In addition, Guo34 evaluated the emission reduction and cost-effectiveness of EVs in China, finding that EVs reduce lifecycle carbon emissions by 47% but are economically less competitive than gasoline vehicles due to high initial purchase costs. However, their marginal abatement costs could become negative if gasoline prices exceed 1.372 USD/Litre. Oliveira Soares33 assessed the TCO and green premium of electric and conventional vehicles in Brazil, analyzing cost dynamics and proposing a roadmap to enhance EV adoption. The results show that EVs have significantly lower annual costs, up to 73% less than conventional vehicles, but have 96.5% higher initial acquisition costs. Despite this, EVs offer long-term savings due to greater energy efficiency and reduced emissions.

From a policy and market perspective, recent literature highlights the critical role of financial incentives and infrastructure in accelerating EV adoption. Sheldon and Dua42 assessed the cost-effectiveness of PEV purchase incentives in 23 countries using counterfactual simulations, estimating additional vehicle uptake and translating it into cost per avoided ton of CO₂ and per gallon of gasoline displaced. They found that the cost per additional PEV ranged from USD 14,857 to 62,443, improving by nearly 50% when medium-term effects such as adoption spillovers were considered. While Münzel43 reviewed EV adoption across Europe and showed that well-designed purchase subsidies and incentives increased EV market share by up to 7%, especially in countries with supportive infrastructure and low electricity costs. In addition, Sierzchula44 used cross-country regression to examine factors influencing EV adoption and found that charging infrastructure was the strongest predictor, followed by financial incentives and domestic EV manufacturing presence.

Results

Energy outlook

Canada’s roadmap to a fully electrified public bus transit system will require a notable expansion in fleet size, particularly under a depot-only charging strategy, as shown in Fig. 1. Currently, there are approximately 12,191 conventional buses (including diesel, hybrid, and natural gas) operating across Canada. The transition to full electrification through the deployment of BEBs would require 14,628 BEBs, reflecting a 17% increase in total fleet size to accommodate charging requirements and operational demands. Cost assessment

The total estimated annual cost to fully electrify Canada’s bus transit fleet amounts to approximately $1.94 billion, encompassing both capital and operational expenditures. The majority of this cost stems from fleet procurement, totaling over $1.73 billion. This is followed by electricity costs ($153.20 million) and emissions-related costs ($12.33 million). Among all provinces, Ontario incurs the highest total cost at over $821.16 million, driven primarily by its large fleet and the corresponding capital investment needs. This is followed by Quebec and British Columbia, with total costs of $377.80 million and $349.21 million, respectively. Charging station costs remain relatively minor across all provinces, comprising less than 1% of total expenditure. Operationally, electricity and emissions costs represent a small share of total costs but are important considerations for long-term sustainability.

The operational costs, comprising electricity and emissions, amount to approximately $165.5 million annually across Canada. Electricity costs total $153.20 million, with the highest values observed in Ontario ($65.19 million), Quebec ($36.88 million), and British Columbia ($25.36 million), reflecting larger fleet sizes and higher energy use. Emissions costs, totaling $12.33 million, vary by province based on grid carbon intensity. Notably, Alberta ($6.91 million) and Saskatchewan ($1.20 million) incur higher emissions-related expenses, while Quebec and Prince Edward Island maintain near-negligible levels due to their cleaner electricity generation. These results highlight the operational advantages of low-carbon grids in minimizing long-term electrification costs.

The carbon pricing and electricity costs are based on 2025 reference values, while electricity emissions intensity reflects the most recent available data (2020–2021) published by the Government of Canada.

In contrast, provinces with carbon-intense electricity production, such as Alberta and Nova Scotia, continue to report relatively higher emissions even under full electrification. Nationally, the shift to BEBs offers a transformative opportunity for decarbonizing public transit systems and advancing climate targets.

Cost of decarbonization

The second approach is a global Sobol sensitivity analysis, applied to 89 cities using 1,108 scenarios per city, resulting in a total of 98,612 simulations. This method captures the combined effects of varying key parameters based on three main components: (a) City-specific operational factors, including Vehicle Kilometers Traveled (VKT), battery sizing, fleet size, and charger requirements, which were tailored to each city using realistic ranges derived from real-world transit agency observations; (b) Provincial variability, including electricity prices and GHG emission intensity, which differ significantly across provinces and affect TCO and emissions outcomes; and (c) Scenario-based assumptions, covering battery prices, charger costs, and emissions pricing, which were systematically varied according to the six predefined sensitivity scenarios discussed in the first approach.

The results show that across all provinces, battery price consistently demonstrate the highest Sobol indices, with total-order effects approaching or exceeding 0.9 in most provinces, underscoring its dominant role in shaping the TCO. These findings highlight that while battery costs remain a universal driver of BEB economics, local electricity pricing and operational energy efficiency can have region-specific implications, reinforcing the value of localized planning in national-scale decarbonization strategies.

Policy context and institutional considerations

The transition to BEBs in Canada is supported by multiple policy efforts across federal, provincial, and municipal levels. The federal government has launched the Zero Emission Transit Fund (ZETF), allocating $2.75 billion to support the procurement of BEBs, charging infrastructure, and related facility upgrades46. At the provincial level, programs such as British Columbia’s CleanBC roadmap47 and Québec’s Green Economy Plan48 offer targeted incentives to accelerate fleet electrification and reduce GHG emissions. While municipalities have also set ambitious targets to begin large-scale BEB deployments with clear electrification targets. For example, Toronto plans to fully electrify its bus fleet by 2040 through the TTC’s Green Bus Program49. Similarly, Brampton has launched the largest interoperable BEB fleet in North America, marking the first phase of a 10-year electrification strategy. This initiative aims to replace Brampton Transit’s entire diesel-powered bus fleet with up to 1,000 BEB, making it the largest zero-emission transit project in Canada to date50.

Despite these efforts, several institutional, logistical, and workforce-related challenges remain. A major limitation is Canada’s restricted domestic manufacturing capacity for BEBs and batteries, leading to reliance on international supply chains and extended lead times for vehicle and component delivery. Procurement is further hindered by high upfront capital costs and limited supplier availability. Institutional capacity varies widely across jurisdictions; with many municipalities lacking the technical or administrative expertise needed for large-scale fleet transitions.

Besides, the workforce readiness presents a critical barrier. The shortage of mechanics and technicians trained in electric drivetrains and high-voltage systems may increase operational costs or delay deployment. Labor-related factors, such as collective agreements, scheduling constraints, and retraining requirements, further limit operational flexibility. These constraints are compounded by the preference to rely on depot-only charging, driven by centralized maintenance practices and labor-related considerations. Finally, coordination with electric utilities remains uncertain in some regions, particularly where grid-side infrastructure upgrades (e.g., transformers, feeders) may be required to accommodate fleet charging demands.

Addressing these institutional, logistical, and labor challenges is essential to ensure the practical scalability of BEB deployment. Complementing the techno-economic analysis presented in this study, these factors underscore the need for integrated planning, intergovernmental coordination, and sustained investment in workforce development to support Canada’s long-term transit decarbonization goals.

Benchmarking BEB costs against global case studies

This section compares our total cost of ownership (TCO) per service kilometer (US $/km) with recent national-level BEB assessments in Brazil and Germany. To maintain consistency, values were converted to US dollars and grouped into three components: BEB fleet procurement, charging infrastructure, and operational costs.

Rodrigues30 implemented a phased deployment approach in Brazil over eight years. Their 2025 projection reported a total cost of ownership (TCO) of approximately $4.35/km for overnight charging and $5.20/km for opportunity charging. The cost breakdowns included infrastructure at $0.75/km, BEB fleet procurement at $2.70/km, and operational costs at $0.90/km. In a separate multi-city study in Germany, Sistig32 reported partial BEB adoption results with infrastructure at $0.12/km, fleet costs around $1.60/km, and operational costs of $1.10/km.

These differences become evident when comparing our study’s cost breakdown, $0.01/km for infrastructure, $1.73/km for fleet procurement, and $0.17/km for operation costs, with the higher values reported in other assessments. The variation arises from factors such as phased deployment strategy, elevated electricity prices, and differing capital expenditure assumptions. Our lower costs are largely driven by our strategy of full upfront capital investment for complete fleet electrification across Canada using a depot-only charging approach, which enables economies of scale, avoids cost escalation over time, and simplifies infrastructure planning.

Notably, the overnight charging scenario, done by Rodrigues30, is functionally aligned with our depot-only strategy, proved considerably more cost-effective than their opportunity charging alternative, underscoring the economic advantages of centralized depot charging, particularly under favorable off-peak electricity conditions.

This comparative insight reinforces the technical and economic viability of large-scale electrification in Canada under a centralized, depot-based planning model. Our full upfront investment approach stands in contrast to the phased or partial deployment strategies observed in other studies, highlighting key factors that contribute to lower costs and helping to contextualize Canada’s cost profile within broader international practices.

Conclusion

A bottom-up approach is crucial for understanding the detailed, region-specific dynamics involved in the transition to Battery Electric Bus (BEB) systems in Canada. The approach begins by analyzing the bus transit networks across the country, represented by 87 transit operators, utilizing their GTFS data, energy consumption, and emissions profiles. These findings are then progressively aggregated, starting at the provincial level and ultimately reaching the national level, providing a comprehensive and scalable outlook on the feasibility of BEB adoption across Canada.

The transition to the BEB transit system is evaluated using three key performance indicators (KPIs): (1) energy demand outlook to assess whether the local power grid can accommodate the increased demand; (2) cost evaluation, focusing on the total costs of electrifying the fleet, including both capital and operational expenses; and (3) GHG emissions reduction, which evaluates the expected reduction in greenhouse gas emissions resulting from the transition to BEBs, highlighting the environmental benefits. Together, these KPIs provide a comprehensive assessment of the technical, financial, and environmental impacts of electrification across Canada.

This approach relies on a depot-only charging strategy that reflects operational constraints commonly observed in Canadian transit agencies, even though it may lead to conservative estimates of fleet size and cost. These constraints include labor agreement limitations, reduced scheduling flexibility, operator safety concerns in extreme weather, and institutional preferences for centralized maintenance and energy management. Such factors have been consistently identified by transit agencies and unions as significant barriers to the adoption of en-route/opportunity charging. While the integration of en-route charging is beyond the scope of this analysis, future research will evaluate its potential as a complementary strategy, particularly for high-frequency urban routes, to assess its impact on cost efficiency.

The results indicate that electrifying Canada’s bus fleet will require a 17% increase in fleet size to meet operational demands. This will result in an additional electricity demand of about 1.255 terawatt-hours (TWh) annually, which represents just 0.20% of Canada’s total electricity generation. Considering that Canada has an average annual net electricity export of around 27.6 TWh, this demonstrates the technical feasibility of electrifying the bus fleet from an energy supply standpoint.

From an economic perspective, the total estimated annual cost to fully electrify Canada’s bus fleet is approximately $1.94 billion, with the majority of this cost attributed to fleet procurement. While the cost of decarbonization is significant, it remains manageable, with the national average cost for decarbonization standing at $1,179.61 per unit of GHG reduction. This transition will result in a dramatic reduction in GHG emissions, with national emissions decreasing from approximately 1.77 million tonnes per year to just 129,747 tonnes per year, a reduction of over 92.68%. Similarly, the SCC will experience a significant decline of 92.68%, dropping from $480 million to $35 million.

The comprehensive sensitivity analysis revealed that battery price is the dominant driver of TCO variability across Canada, showing the highest first- and total-order indices. The global Sobol analyses consistently showed that while parameters such as energy consumption rate, electricity price, and GHG emission intensity exhibited lower first-order impacts, their higher total-order effects (0.374, 0.348, and 0.308) suggest substantial interaction with other variables. These findings highlight the importance of considering both individual parameter sensitivity and combined interactions when evaluating BEB deployment costs, especially in regionally diverse contexts like Canada.

These results demonstrate that the adoption of BEBs is not only technically feasible but also provides a financially efficient strategy for decarbonizing Canada’s public transit system. This transition offers significant environmental and economic benefits on a national scale, helping Canada achieve its climate targets while promoting sustainable, low-carbon public transit.

However, although the analysis provides a comprehensive national-level assessment, certain assumptions and exclusions should be noted. Specifically, the study does not account for potential grid-side infrastructure upgrades, such as transformer and feeder reinforcements, which may be necessary for large-scale depot charging. These aspects were excluded due to data limitations and are recommended for future investigation.

Modeling parameters.

The GTFS is processed for trip assignments followed by Vehicle Kilometers Traveled (VKT) estimation21. These metrics are used as inputs for the estimation of energy consumption, battery sizing, and fleet requirements. Subsequent steps include the evaluation of energy demand, charging power, and associated costs, such as charging station infrastructure, electricity, and maintenance expenses. The framework also integrates GHG emissions and their associated costs, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the energy outlook, cost assessment, and environmental performance of BEB operations.

This study utilized GTFS data from 87 transit operators (102 transit networks) representing all Canadian transit systems at the national level. The data was sourced through open-source feeds58, and Remix, a transit planning and data analysis platform59, was used to display and derive key operational metrics from the GTFS data, providing detailed information on transit agencies, bus routes, trips, stops, and timetables.

Trip assignment

Despite its detailed scope, the GTFS dataset lacks information on the number of trips each bus completes on a specific route, known as the vehicle assignment model. To bridge this gap, a trip assignment process is applied to systematically allocate the total trips among the available buses for each route.

The process follows the FIFO (First-In, First-Out) principle to construct bus blocks. Each block represents a sequence of trips that a single bus completes during the operational period, and it starts at the depot with the first assigned departure and then continues to the next feasible departure at the other endpoint based on the timetable60.

Energy outlook

The charging strategy implemented in this paper focuses on depot charging, relying solely on charging during non-operational hours31. On-route charging is excluded due to logistical, union, and infrastructure challenges. Depot charging simplifies operations by centralizing energy supply, optimizing battery health through controlled state-of-charge management, and reducing service disruptions. Additionally, it addresses grid capacity constraints by avoiding costly upgrades to substations and transmission lines. It also benefits from utilizing off-peak energy with reduced prices and lower GHG intensity. This approach offers a practical and cost-effective solution for fleet electrification without straining the power network.
