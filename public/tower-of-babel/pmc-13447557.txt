Endovascular exclusion of an aortobronchial fistula 38 years following a blunt thoracic aortic injury
Authors: Tiffany Lian, Nallely Saldana-Ruiz, Jayden Moore, Paul Pottinger, Sherene Shalhub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aortobronchial fistula is a rare but life-threatening cause of hemoptysis. We report the case of a 68-year-old man who developed an aortobronchial fistula 38 years after undergoing open repair of a blunt thoracic aortic injury from a plane crash. Initial thoracic endovascular aortic repair was performed covering the area of the previous open repair (zones III/IV) where fistulas typically form; however, persistent symptoms required extension with subsequent resolution of hemoptysis. Six years later, a suspected endograft infection following bronchitis was managed with antibiotics and chronic suppressive therapy. At 9-year follow-up, he remains stable. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges and late infection risk and supports thoracic endovascular aortic repair as a treatment option in selected high-risk patients.

Aortobronchial fistula (ABF) is a rare but life-threatening condition characterized by abnormal communication between the aorta and central airway.1,2 Primary etiologies include aortic aneurysms, trauma, malignancy, and infectious or inflammatory processes, whereas secondary ABF typically stems as a late complication of previous aortic intervention.3 Hemoptysis is the hallmark presentation and may rapidly progress to respiratory failure, hemorrhagic shock, and death.1,4 Historically, open surgical repair has been the standard treatment but carries perioperative mortality rates as high as 41%.3 Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR), first described for ABF in 1996, is considered first line in the temporization of hemodynamically unstable patients with ABFs because of its less invasive nature and favorable short-term outcomes.5, 6, 7

We report a case of delayed ABF occurring 38 years after open repair for blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI), initially presumed to be related to the previous repair but ultimately found to be anatomically distinct. The patient was initially treated with TEVAR but required repeat intervention for a persistent fistula, with durable symptom resolution at 9-year follow-up. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report and any accompanying images.

Case presentation

A 68-year-old man presented with a 6-month history of intermittent hemoptysis. His medical history was notable for open repair of a BTAI sustained in an airplane crash in 1977, after which he remained asymptomatic for almost 40 years. At the start of his hemoptysis in April 2015, initial evaluation by his primary care provider included a normal chest radiograph. Four months later, he experienced recurrence with increasingly frequent intermittent episodes (Supplementary Fig, online only). Evaluation by otolaryngology and pulmonary medicine included videoscopy, bronchoscopy, esophagram, and upper endoscopy, which was unrevealing. Chest computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a calcified soft tissue mass associated with subsegmental bronchi bordering the lateral aspect of the proximal descending aorta (Fig 1, A) without clear aortobronchial communication. Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging was negative (Fig 1, B and C). There was also noted to be additional distal calcifications in the distal thoracic aorta distal to the site of open repair (Fig 1, A). Despite the absence of bronchoscopic or CT imaging confirmation, there was persistent concern for ABF given ongoing hemoptysis, most likely at the anastomosis of his previous graft. Subsequently, he underwent a TEVAR using a 28 × 10 cm Gore TAG device with coverage extending across the entire length of the earlier interposition graft (zones III-IV) (Fig 2, A and B). Two months later, he again developed recurrent hemoptysis. Repeat bronchoscopy and CT angiography (CTA) were unrevealing, and he was discharged but soon returned with recurrent large-volume hemoptysis. Another bronchoscopy revealed active hemorrhage from the posterior left lower lobe corresponding to the previously noted distal calcified lesion. Owing to concern for persistent ABF, the TEVAR repair was extended using a second 28 × 10 cm Gore TAG device to cover zones IV-V where the irregularity was noted to border the aorta. He recovered uneventfully and received perioperative antibiotics without chronic suppressive therapy.

The patient remained asymptomatic for 6 years. Surveillance CTA in 2022 confirmed a stable aortic repair without infection or endoleak (Fig 3). However, in April 2023, following an episode of acute bronchitis treated with antibiotics, he developed acute on chronic back pain. CTA demonstrated new periaortic stranding at the site of his stent graft repair concerning for graft infection (Fig 4, A). Although blood cultures were negative, he was admitted and treated empirically with antibiotics. Repeat CTA on hospital day 2 was stable, and vancomycin was discontinued after negative blood cultures. During his hospitalization, his erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein remained elevated, but he had no fever or white blood cell count elevation. Given this, all antibiotics were discontinued and he was discharged on hospital day 5. Follow-up PET/CT scan in July 2023 showed uptake at the aneurysm repair site, confirming endograft infection. He was thus placed on chronic suppressive therapy with Augmentin and doxycycline (Fig 4, B and C). Discussion

ABFs most commonly involve the descending thoracic aorta (zones II-IV), with 88.4% reported in this region because of its proximity to the bronchial tree.8 Time to presentation after aortic surgery varies widely, ranging from months to decades. Although data were limited, existing data suggest that ABFs tend to develop earlier following TEVAR compared with open surgical repair.8

Although open repair has historically been the gold standard for ABF, TEVAR is now well established as an effective bridge to definitive repair, with a 30-day mortality of 5.9%, an overall success rate of 93.3%, and a mean follow-up of 17.4 months.3,9,10 Despite these promising results, usage as long-term definitive therapy remains incompletely defined.11, 12, 13, 14 This case, however, supports the use of TEVAR as a viable long-term solution in carefully selected patients who are not candidates for open repair.

Stent graft infection remains a primary concern in TEVAR as aortobronchial communication exposes the graft to respiratory flora. Scarce data exist on the risk of infection after TEVAR from ABF, though one study estimates mortality at 2.2%.4 In contrast, infection of stent grafts placed for other indications has been associated with substantially higher mortality, with rates reported as high as 36%.15, 16, 17 Despite this, post-TEVAR antibiotic management strategies remain controversial and ill-defined, with management determined on a case-by-case basis by each hospital's infectious disease team, ranging from short postoperative courses to lifelong suppressive therapy.4,18, 19, 20 Although definitive management of prosthetic graft infection remains open surgical explantation with reconstruction, it was deemed prohibitively high risk in this patient given his comorbidities and the anticipated morbidity of a repeat open aortic operation, particularly in the setting of potential reinfection. As such, chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy was selected as a secondary, nonoperative strategy.

Although the patient received antibiotic treatment for the initial respiratory infection, this case underscores the need for ongoing vigilance and consideration of prolonged antibiotic therapy for respiratory infections, even years after the index procedure. As established stent graft infection may require high-risk explantation and open reconstruction, early and aggressive treatment of suspected infection is critical. In select cases, long-term or lifelong suppressive therapy may be necessary, though this must be weighed against risks such as bone marrow suppression and infectious colitis.21

Empiric therapy should include coverage of respiratory commensal organisms such as Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species, with regimens tailored to the patient's past cultured microbiology and in collaboration with infectious disease specialists based on the individual risk factors.22 Long-term follow-up is essential, as the absence of definitive source control after TEVAR often necessitates prolonged therapy supported by host immune response. Determining when to discontinue antibiotics remains challenging and relies on ongoing clinical assessment with serial imaging every 4 to 6 months until findings stabilize and infection is felt to be controlled.

The most surprising part of this case is the location of ABF formation. Secondary ABF formation typically occurs at the site of previous aortic intervention, particularly at the proximal or distal anastomotic sites, and is most commonly associated with postoperative complications such as endoleak, infection, or pseudoaneurysm formation. In this case, the ABF development unexpectedly occurred distal to the initial interposition graft in the absence of known complications of the index surgery, raising questions of its etiology. This may reflect a delayed sequela of the original BTAI, with progressive degeneration of a previously unrecognized segment of injured aorta, or represent an unrelated process related to the calcification seen on imaging of unclear etiology.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that TEVAR can serve as definitive therapy for ABF in select high-risk patients when necessary and reinforces several important principles in the diagnosis and management of ABF, including the limited sensitivity of CT and bronchoscopy, the risk of late graft infection, and the possibility of fistula formation at sites remote from earlier anastomoses. Prompt recognition, thoughtful antibiotic management, and careful long-term surveillance are essential to achieving favorable long-term outcomes in this rare but potentially fatal condition.
