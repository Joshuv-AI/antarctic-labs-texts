Reframing ageing in place: perspectives from Chinese older people
Authors: Yajing Wang, Nini Kerr, Leah Macaden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs global populations age, the concept of ageing in place (AIP), originally rooted in Western discourse, has gained international attention. However, current interpretations of ‘place’ often remain limited to physical settings such as home and community. This study explored how older people in urban China understand and experience ‘place’ in AIP.MethodsUsing Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), in-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with nine participants in Changsha, China. Data was analysed following IPA’s analytic procedures.ResultsFour key themes emerged: (1) Negotiating between two ‘homes’, with subthemes: Ageing in my own home, and Near and yet far enough from adult children’s homes, (2) Ageing in supportive social networks; (3) Ageing in a digital world; and (4) Not to be 'institutionalised'.ConclusionThis study offers a culturally nuanced understanding of AIP, challenging the notion that ‘place’ is solely a physical or geographical location. It highlights the significance of social connectedness, emotional security, digital participation, and the desire to avoid institutional care. These findings provide valuable insights for policymakers and practitioners, underscoring the need to design ageing policies and care practices that address not only physical housing but also social networks, digital inclusion, and the relational aspects of institutional care, thereby promoting autonomy, dignity, and well-being.Keywords: Ageing in place, Older people, Place, Interpretative phenomenological analysis, Qualitative research, China

Introduction

The world is experiencing an unprecedented increase in the ageing population [1]. In 2020, the global population aged 65 and over was 727 million, accounting for 9.3% of the total population. By 2050, this number is projected to more than double to over 1.5 billion, accounting for approximately 16% of the global population [2]. Moreover, the population aged 60 and above is expected to nearly reach 2 billion by 2050, representing about 22% of the world’s population [3, 4].

The rapid growth of the ageing population poses significant challenges to both socioeconomic development and health policies worldwide. Within this context, the concept of Ageing in Place (AIP), which originated in Western societies, has gained increasing global attention. The Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines AIP as the ability of older people, regardless of economic status, age, or intrinsic capacity, to live independently, safely, and comfortably in their homes and local communities [5]. Initially promoted in Western countries as an ageing strategy, the concept has since spread to many parts of the world. In China, for example, efforts to implement AIP began in the early 2000 s [6, 7].

At its core, AIP promotes the idea that older people should be supported to remain in their homes or familiar communities for as long as possible, fostering independence in later life. Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of ageing at home and/or in the community, and have consistently linked AIP with a strong sense of independence [8–15].

Beyond independence, AIP also offers older people a private space that supports autonomy and personal freedom [8, 10, 14, 16, 17]. Autonomy encompasses the ability to control one’s daily routines, organise and manage activities based on personal preferences and capacities, and make decisions without external pressure or conformity with societal expectations [14]. As highlighted in Stones and Gullifer's [14] study, ageing in one’s own home enables individuals to live in accordance with their values and express their authentic selves. Additionally, older people who value autonomy and freedom often view institutional care settings, such as nursing homes, as the antithesis of AIP, associated with diminished control and restricted decision-making [10, 14, 18].

For many older people, the home also functions as an anchor for personal history and identity, preserving a sense of continuity over time. Familiar objects and surroundings connect individuals to their past, present, and future selves, fostering emotional wellbeing and a sense of contentment [12, 15]. Even amid life transitions such as retirement or children moving out, remaining in a familiar community where one is still recognised and acknowledged helps older people experience ageing as a seamless continuation of life, rather than a disruptive shift [19].

Because of the emotional and symbolic significance of the home, many older people express a strong desire to remain in their homes and communities as long as possible, which reflects a deep sense of place attachment [10]. Place attachment refers to the affective bond formed between an individual to a particular place, shaped by the meaningful experiences that take place in that environment [20]. In Wiles et al.'s [18] study, older people’s strong sense of place attachment stemmed from familiarity with both people and places within a community. Older people felt a deep connection and warmth with and from their local surroundings, such as neighbours, familiar places, and community resources. This connection persisted even when people left and returned, as they sought the comfort of known environments and relationships [18].

Nevertheless, Yu and Rosenberg [21] argue that Western theories of AIP do not fully account for non-Western contexts such as China. In the West, AIP is closely tied to values like independence, autonomy, and self-identity. By contrast, Chinese ageing experiences remain deeply shaped by expectations of family involvement, community engagement, and state support. Cultural values rooted in collectivism and familism inform how older people in China perceive and pursue later life. Many still view the state and collective structures as essential providers of care and support. In contrast, the Western model of AIP is premised on individual choice and autonomy. As such, frameworks that centre on individualism cannot be readily transposed onto the Chinese context, where ageing is often negotiated through connectivity and interdependence [21, 22].

A qualitative study [23] in China echoes this argument, which revealed that older people in China face loneliness while AIP, social and emotional loneliness, arising from a lack of close companionship and emotional intimacy,and existential loneliness, which stems from fears about declining health, experiences of loss, and a sense of disconnection from the world. Many older people in this study expressed a strong desire for more collective activities that can help them reconnect with society and foster a sense of belonging. To mitigate these feelings of loneliness, older people would actively mobilise various social resources in constructing and maintaining their social networks [23].

Furthermore, China’s underdeveloped social welfare system poses various challenges to age at home and in one’s own community. Amid rapid demographic ageing, community-based services often fail to meet older people’s diverse needs, particularly in terms of emotional support [23]. Existing services remain limited in scope, quantity, accessibility, and flexibility [22]. Underdeveloped community support, health concerns and difficulties accessing prompt medical care are major barriers to AIP, which erode older people’s confidence in its feasibility [6, 7, 23]. In this context, dependence on children and intergenerational co-residence remains both widespread and necessary, shaped by inadequate welfare provisions and enduring Confucian values of filial piety [24].

Dalmer [25] critically argues that AIP idealises the home and local community as the most favourable settings for ageing. However, the home is not always a supportive environment, as it can also be a site of conflict and tension. Echoing this view, Buffel, Handler and Phillipson [26] suggest that communities may also become challenging, even hostile environments for older people, shaped by wider structural forces such as globalisation and urban redevelopment.

Therefore, viewing place merely as a physical space, such as the home or community, oversimplifies its meaning and risks idealising the concept of AIP. Neglecting how ageing is perceived and practiced across different sociocultural contexts leads to a fragmented and limited understanding of place. In settings like China, where collectivist and family-oriented values are deeply embedded, place spans beyond a physical or geographical space and can often encompass emotional, relational, and cultural dimensions.

Until recently, far too little attention has been paid to the meaning of place in AIP in Chinese context. This study seeks to broaden the meaning of place by examining how it is experienced and understood by older people in China. Rather than treating place as a fixed or universal concept, it explores its culturally embedded meanings as shaped by the everyday lives of older people. In doing so, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of AIP and supports the development of culturally responsive and contextually grounded ageing policies.

Methods

This study used interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) [27] to explore how older people understand the concept of ‘place’ in AIP. From December 2021 to May 2022, in-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with nine urban-dwelling older people in China. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the University of Edinburgh Ethics Committee (NUST006). All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [28].

Theoretical foundations of IPA

IPA is grounded in three theoretical foundations: phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography [29]. Phenomenology underpins IPA studies to examine individual experiences in their own terms, rather than through predefined categories [27]. This approach avoids researchers relying on interpretations and definitions of ‘place’ in AIP provided by international organizations and governments, allowing the meaning of ‘place’ to emerge directly from the participants’ perspectives. To capture participants' experiences and understanding regarding place, semi-structured interviews were used in this study.

The second foundation, hermeneutics, or the ‘art of interpretation,’ focuses on uncovering and restoring meaning [30]. It posits that phenomena exist, waiting to be revealed, and that researchers need to interpret and make sense of them [29]. Engaging with double hermeneutics, the meaning-making done by participants represents the first order of interpretation, while the analysis conducted by researchers constitutes the second-order [31]. This dual interpretative process enabled the researchers to clarify participants’ understandings of ‘place’ from their own perspectives, while also deepening these insights using the researchers’ contextual knowledge.

The third foundation, idiography, emphasizes a detailed examination of individual cases within their specific contexts. This approach prioritises the unique perspectives of participants, focusing on the particular rather than the universal [32]. By amplifying the voices of older people, idiography reveals their specific understandings of ‘place.’ In line with this principle, IPA research often involves small, purposefully selected, context-specific samples. This facilitates a thorough, idiographic exploration of each participant’s experiences before engaging in comparative analysis across cases [33].

In conclusion, these three theoretical foundations make IPA particularly well-suited for this study. By combining phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography, IPA allows for an in-depth exploration of how older people experience and interpret the concept of ‘place’ in AIP. This approach goes beyond simply giving participants a voice, enabling the researcher to capture the nuances of meaning-making, examine how experiences are understood within their specific contexts, and identify emerging themes across individual cases.

Sampling and recruitment

IPA seeks to obtain a purposive and relatively homogeneous sample to ensure that the phenomenon under investigation is both relevant and personally meaningful to all participants [27]. This approach facilitates the collection of rich, in-depth insights from individuals sharing common experiences, while also allowing exploration of both convergence and divergence in how these experiences are interpreted [27, 34]. Consistent with the idiographic foundation of IPA, a small sample size was adopted to enable a detailed and nuanced examination of individual experiences [35]. Larger samples may risk diluting the depth of analysis and complicate the identification of nuanced similarities and differences between cases [36]. Although there is no fixed rule regarding sample size in IPA research [32], Smith, Flowers and Larkin [27] suggest that six to ten interviews are typical for PhD studies. This research, as part of a PhD project, included nine participants, balancing the need for rich data, depth of analysis, time constraints, and participant availability [32].

Participants were purposively recruited from two urban communities in Changsha, China. To enhance sample homogeneity, the selected communities were similar in socioeconomic conditions. Both communities were situated in intermediate urban districts with relatively convenient transportation links. They were middle-income residential areas with a moderate proportion of older residents, well-established neighbourhood committees, and accessible community-based health and social services.

As the study is grounded in the AIP framework proposed by the CDC [5], participants aged 60 years or older were recruited to ensure they had lived experiences of ageing at home and within the community. Recruitment strategies included posters on community bulletin boards, distribution of flyers at older people’s social clubs, and snowball sampling. Potential participants were provided with between 24 h and one week to review the study information sheet and decide on participation [37]. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection

Nine older people participated in face-to-face semi-structured interviews, with each interview’s average duration being 60 min. The interview locations were chosen either at participants’ homes or private spaces within community centres based on participants’ preferences. Before the interviews, all participants provided their consent forms to confirm their voluntary participation.

At the outset of each interview, participants were asked basic demographic questions, including their age, marital status, income level, education level, and length of residence in their current community. To protect participants’ privacy, data on age and length of residence was recorded and presented as ranges. The interviews explored participants’ experiences of ageing in their homes and communities, their perceptions of their living spaces (including both their home and local community), the challenges they faced in daily life and their coping strategies, as well as their views on what constitutes an ideal place for ageing. All interviews were conducted in Chinese, audio-recorded with the participants' consent, and subsequently transcribed verbatim to ensure accuracy. After interviews, each participant was provided with 100 yuan as a token of gratitude for their time to participate in this study.

Data analysis

Four transcripts were translated from Chinese into English under the guidance of a bilingual supervisor to ensure accuracy and credibility. The remaining five were analysed directly in Chinese by the researcher. Peer debriefing and validation with two supervisors ensured the trustworthiness of the results. Data analysis was guided by IPA analysis as described by Smith, Flowers and Larkin [27]. The process involved the following five steps:

GET 1. Negotiating between two ‘homes’

GET 1 involves participants’ continuous negotiation between their own homes and those of their adult children. These two spaces represent not only physical locations but also reflect competing values such as personal autonomy and intergenerational support, which together shaped older people’s experiences of AIP in this study.

Ageing in my own home

Participants viewed their physical homes as a fundamental aspect of ‘place’, a private space offering freedom and autonomy. Within their homes, they could prepare meals according to their preferences, plan daily activities, set their own schedule, and make independent decisions:

‘How nice it is to live in your own home! You’re free! I’m very happy now. If you intend to go out, many older people will go with you… If I want to eat something specific, I can just make a phone call or order it online.’ (Yan, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 住在自己家里多好啊!自由自在!我现在非常开心。你要是想出去, 很多老人会陪你一起……我要是想吃点什么东西, 我只要打个电话或者叫个外卖就行了。).

Living apart from adult children also reduced intergenerational responsibilities and emotional strain, enabling participants to enjoy more relaxed and independent lives:

‘I often need to worry a lot when living with them. For example, I sometimes help them with taking care of my grandchildren. I don't have as much to worry about when I live on my own.’ (Du, male, 75 years old) (Chinese version: 和他们一起生活我常常要操很多心。比如, 有时候我要帮他们照看我孙子孙女。我自己一个人住的话就没那么多要操心的。).

While participants expressed overall satisfaction with their current living arrangements, all of them acknowledged that their homes were not equipped to support potential future health and care needs. Although most were living with chronic conditions, their health was generally stable. However, living alone raised concerns about safety in emergencies or during episodes of acute illness. Xu reflected on this vulnerability:

‘Sometimes when my husband goes out to play mahjong and I’m home alone, I’m quite worried. What if I suddenly have a stroke while I’m alone? What should I do then?’ (Xu, female, 66 years old) (Chinese version: 有时候我老伴出去打麻将, 我一个人在家, 我心里其实挺担心的。万一我突然中风了, 身边又没人怎么办?那种时候我该怎么办呢?).

When discussing ideal places for ageing, most participants, despite their satisfaction with homes, did not express a strong emotional attachment to their current homes. Instead, many were open to relocating in the future, particularly those whose adult children lived far away or who experienced a lack of accessible community services. Inadequate access to healthcare support in local communities contributed to a sense of insecurity about staying in their current homes long term. For many, relocation was seen as a practical strategy to ensure wellbeing and prepare for future care needs. Wu and Long shared their intentions to move closer to their children:

‘Now my daughter’s planning to take us both over to live there. Because my daughter doesn't think it's convenient and safe for only two of us to keep living here, she intends to let us live in a place near to her flat, so that she can see us whenever she wants and help us timely.’ (Wu, male, 84 years old) (Chinese version: 现在我女儿打算带着我们两个一起搬到她那里去住。因为她觉得只有我们两个人住在这里不方便也不安全, 所以她打算让我们住在一个离她家比较近的地方, 这样一来她就可以随时来看我们, 也可以第一时间帮到我们。).

‘It’s really a big problem for us. My children said that if we can't take care of ourselves one day, they would take us to xx city… I’ll choose to go to xx to live with my son, cause I’ll have no other choices then.’ (Long, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 这对我们来说确实是个大问题。我小孩说, 如果有一天我们没办法照顾好自己了, 他们就会带我们去 xx 市……我会选择去 xx 市和我的儿子一起生活, 因为那个时候我别无选择了嘛。).

A few participants also perceived relocation as a reciprocal arrangement, enabling them not only to receive care from their adult children but also to provide support in return. For instance, Yu described relocating to ‘new home’ to facilitate caregiving responsibilities for her grandchildren, while also gaining access to prompt assistance from her daughter:

‘… we decided to move here, bought a new flat in this community where my daughter lives… It’s much easier to take care of my grandchildren and get support from my daughter as well.’ (Yu, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 我们后面就决定搬到这里来了, 就在我女儿住的小区里新买了一套公寓……这样一来照顾我孙子孙女就方便多了, 而且有什么事我女儿也可以给我们搭把手。).

Near and yet far enough from adult children’s homes

When participants spoke about their own homes, their adult children's homes frequently became part of the conversation. Participants’ homes were located at varying distances from their adult children’s homes, ranging from the same community to different cities. Despite these differences, they shared a common expectation: maintaining a geographic distance close enough to foster mutual support while preserving private and personal spaces. This idea was encapsulated in the phrase ‘a distance for a bowl of hot soup’, as described by Yu:

‘I make a bowl of soup and bring it to my child’s home, and this soup doesn't even get cold.’ (Yu, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 我做一碗汤, 然后把它送我小孩家里, 到她家里这碗汤甚至还没凉。)

For Yu and Du, living separately yet within the same community with their adult children facilitated privacy and minimised intergenerational conflicts, while also enabling prompt mutual support and fostering a sense of security. As Du explained:

‘I think it's (the distance) fine. When we need my daughter, she comes immediately. If they need help, we go to her home right away to take care of their children… We don’t have many disagreements now.’ (Du, male, 75 years old) (Chinese version: 我觉得 (这种距离) 还不错。当我们需要女儿的时候, 她立马就赶过来了。如果他们需要帮助, 我们也会立马去他们那里照顾他们小孩……我们现在很少有意见不合的情况了。).

Participants whose adult children lived in other cities also found this idea appealing. The concept of ‘a distance for a bowl of hot soup’ emphasised appropriate closeness to foster reciprocal support:

‘So it's better to live a little closer. If I need something, I can just call them. If they (Yan’s son and daughter-in-law) were still in XX city, they would not be able to help me now. They now live here, and they can come to help within half an hour.’ (Yan, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 所以住得近一点会更好些。如果我有需要, 我可以直接打电话给他们。如果他们 (严的儿子和儿媳) 还在 XX 市的话, 现在他们就没办法过来帮我了。他们现在住在这里, 半小时内就能过来帮忙。).

Two primary factors drove this desire for proximity: health concerns and emotional support. Most participants frequently expressed anxiety about health issues as they aged. Health anxieties, particularly among participants with chronic conditions, made being closer to their adult children reassuring. As Long put it:

‘Both my husband and I have high blood pressure. What if something happens? If my sons were closer, I wouldn’t worry so much.’ (Long, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 我和我老伴都有高血压。万一出了什么事怎么办?要是儿子离我们近点就好了, 这样我就没必要这么担心了。).

Physical proximity also facilitated face-to-face interactions and emotional support, which participants found more emotionally fulfilling than digital communication:

‘We chat via WeChat, but sometimes it’s not enough for me. I can only see my daughter once a year during the New Year holidays, and sometimes not even that.’ (Xu, female, 66 years old) (Chinese version: 我们通过微信聊天, 但这个有时候对我来说还是不够的。我每年过年放假的时候才能见我女儿一次, 有时甚至一次都见不到。).

While proximity offered benefits, some participants stressed the importance of maintaining boundaries in intergenerational relationships. Living too close risked blurring these boundaries, potentially leading to conflicts or increased responsibilities. As Xu put it:

‘It’s not good to live too close. It’s easy to have conflicts when you’re in constant contact.’ (Xu, female, 66 years old) (Chinese version: 住得太近并不好。我们一旦经常接触, 就很容易产生矛盾。)

Proximity also sometimes resulted in increased caregiving responsibilities, which participants found mentally and physically burdensome:

‘This distance is good, but it adds mental pressure. Now that we live close, sometimes when we see them in need, we can't help but want to help them, after all we’re parents. We always feel responsible for helping them.’ (Du, male, 75 years old) (Chinese version: 这种距离还可以, 但它会带来心理上的一些压力。现在我们住得近了, 有时一看到他们有难处, 我们就忍不住想去帮他们。我们毕竟是父母嘛, 我们总觉得自己有责任去帮他们。).

The GET 1 reveals participants’ ongoing negotiation to achieve a balance between personal autonomy and familial responsibility. Their movement and negotiation between two ‘homes’ illustrates the emotional and practical tensions they experience. While they value autonomy and freedom, the limited health and social support provided by their homes and community environments led them to seek healthy proximity, a closeness that enables reciprocity without compromising personal space.

GET 2. Ageing in supportive social networks

GET 2 explores how participants experienced ageing not only in physical spaces but also within relational environments shaped by emotional and social connections. For them, AIP meant living within a relational place, a supportive environment that met emotional and psychosocial needs.

Participants actively built and maintained connections with family, friends, neighbours, the government, and society at large, illustrating their interdependence on a wide range of social networks. These relationships not only offered recognition but also affirmed their identities across multiple roles, such as parent, sibling, friend, and citizen. For example, Ma and Wan shared:

‘Our families often get together during the holidays. I’m the eldest brother, so I organise these gatherings for my siblings during Spring Festival and other occasions. As the eldest, I feel it’s my responsibility.’ (Ma, male, 68 years old) (Chinese version: 我们这一大家子在节假日的时候会经常性的聚一聚。我是家里的老大, 所以在过年还有一些其他的场合, 我都会要组织安排这些聚会活动。作为家里的老大, 我觉得这是我的责任。).

‘The service and policies are still pretty good. We get a free medical check-up every year, which would cost hundreds otherwise. The government values us older people.’ (Wan, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 服务和政策还是很不错的。我们每年可以免费做一次体检, 不然我们自己花钱体检要花好几百呢。政府还是很重视我们这些老年人的。).

Through community engagement in volunteering, Xu expressed a strong sense of purpose, significance, and self-worth in her later life. Such participation not only enabled individuals to remain socially connected but also provided a meaningful platform to exercise their abilities, contribute to the well-being of others, and reaffirm her personal value and competence as an active member of the community:

‘I’m the backbone of volunteer work in the community… I participated in all the voluntary activities in the community… Basically, the community can't do anything without our help. I just want to have fun, make myself happy, I want to be fulfilled, I don't want to be lonely.’ (Xu, female, 66 years old) (Chinese version: 我是社区志愿者工作里的中坚力量……我参与了社区里所有的志愿活动……基本上, 社区要是没有我们的帮助就无法开展任何工作。我只是想过得开心点、让自己开心, 我想感到充实点, 不想孤孤单单的一个人。).

Participants also found strength and comfort in peer networks and groups, where mutual assistance and reciprocity were common. These relationships fostered a strong sense of belonging and resilience, helping them navigate challenges such as discrimination and injustice in community life. Shen shared:

‘Since last year, we stopped playing mahjong with young people because they always tried to take advantage of us… Now, I only play with my friends in the community. We feel much better this way.’ (Shen, female, 69 years old) (Chinese version: 从去年起, 我们就不再和那些年轻人一起打麻将了, 因为他们总是想占我们便宜……现在我只和社区里的朋友们一起打。这样我们感觉好多了。).

A broadly supportive social environment further reinforced participants’ sense of security and life satisfaction. This included the stability of living in a peaceful society and receiving healthcare and economic support, such as national healthcare insurance and pensions. Du reported:

‘We’re now living without war, peacefully. Our society and economy are relatively stable. I feel safe and comfortable… The national healthcare insurance and pension we receive also gives us a sense of security in old age.’ (Du, male, 75 years old) (Chinese version: 我们现在生活在没有战争的和平年代里。我们的社会和经济状况还比较稳定。我个人感觉还是安全舒适的……我们所享受的医保和退休金也让我们对养老生活充满了安全感。).

GET 3. Ageing in a digital world

GET 3 is concerned with how digital technology has become an integral part of place in AIP, offering older people new means to stay socially connected, informed, and engaged. For many participants, AIP presented challenges of maintaining connections due to geographical distance from adult children, family, and friends. This issue became more pronounced during the COVID-19 pandemic when physical social contact was significantly limited:

‘During the COVID-19, we hardly saw each other at all, we were all in our own homes. We were still afraid to go out at times like during the pandemic.’ (Ma, male, 68 years old) (Chinese version: 在新冠疫情期间, 我们几乎没怎么见面, 都待在自己家里。有时我们还是害怕出去, 就像疫情期间那样。).

Many participants were introduced to smartphones and various social and entertainment applications by their adult children or grandchildren. With their guidance, participants gradually developed digital skills, which empowered them to engage socially and access information at homes, and manage their lives more independently:

‘My sons and grandkids taught me how to use this (smartphone). I also taught myself how use it myself. Having a smartphone really makes life a lot more convenient.’ (Wan, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 我儿子和孙子都教了我怎么用它 (智能手机) 。我自己也自学了一下怎么用。有一部智能机确实让生活变得更加方便了。).

Digital platforms provided flexible and accessible means for maintaining relationships. Social media applications such as WeChat enabled participants to stay connected both synchronously and asynchronously, helping to mitigate feelings of loneliness and isolation. As Shen put it:

‘I keep in touch with my classmates and friends through WeChat. We also have group chats where we say ‘hello’ to each other every morning. It’s part of our daily routine.’ (Shen, female, 69 years old) (Chinese version: 我用微信跟同学、朋友他们保持联系。我们还有一个微信群, 每天早上都会互相打招呼。这是我们每天生活的一部分。).

Such digital interactions cultivated a sense of emotional closeness, despite physical distance. Five participants described a feeling of presence and reassurance through video calls. As Wan shared:

‘Every time I see them on my phone, I feel like they're still around. Every video call makes me feel relaxed. Seeing them on my phone reassures me. They’re safe, and I don’t need to worry.’ (Wan, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 我每次在手机上看到他们, 都会觉得他们就在我身边。每次打视频电话也会让我感到轻松。在手机上看到他们能让我安心。知道他们很安全, 我就不需要担心什么的了。).

Nonetheless, digital communication could not entirely substitute for in-person contact. Five participants expressed a continuing desire for face-to-face interactions with their families, especially their adult children. As Wan stated:

‘Although we often have video calls, I still hope that my two sons in xx city can come back to see me in person. It’s always better.’ (Wan, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 虽然我们经常打视频电话保持联系, 但我还是希望我在 xx 市的两个儿子能多回来看我一下。这样总归还是好一些。).

Beyond communication, digital technology enriched participants’ lifestyles. All participants reported improved digital literacy and the incorporation of technology into their everyday routines, ranging from browsing news and watching short videos to online shopping and information-seeking:

‘I usually watch videos on TikTok, read news online. If I don’t know something, I’ll search it online. It’s really convenient.’ (Shen, female, 69 years old) (Chinese version: 我经常在抖音 上刷视频, 也会在网上看下新闻。如果我有不知道的事, 我就会在网上搜一下。这真的很方便。).

Digital technology also promoted health awareness. Participants accessed information on disease prevention, health promotion, and fitness, which encouraged healthier behaviours. For instance, Yu incorporated what she learned online into their daily lives:

‘I try to prepare meals as nutritious as possible, often following online guidance.’ (Yu, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 我会尽量把每餐饭弄得营养丰富一点, 经常参考网上的一些指导。)

However, increased digital engagement also came with risks. Digital technology exposed most participants to misleading information and unconfirmed news. Four participants shared information seen on social media. It was apparent that the source of some information was unverified, and some were highly misleading. However, the participants were quite convinced that the information they had read online, and never questioned, was reliable. As Wu presented:

‘I read a lot of information online every day. This information told me many things that I didn’t know before.’ (Wu, male, 84 years old) (Chinese version: 我每天都会在网上了解到很多信息。这些信息让我知道了许多之前并不清楚的事情。)

Additionally, algorithm-driven content sometimes impacted participants’ values and mindset, and reinforced their biases. For instance, negative news about nursing homes on social media intensified eight participants’ distrust and aversion towards institutional care, as well as exacerbated their sense of vulnerability and pessimism as older people in society. Long shared:

‘I’ve seen so many stories online about nursing homes being irresponsible, nurses not giving medicines on time. it’s really horrible for us…’ (Long, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 我在网上看到了很多关于养老院不负责、护士不按时给药的报道。这对我们来说真的很可怕……).

GET 4. Not to be ‘institutionalised’

GET 4 focuses on older people’s strong reluctance to be institutionalised. This reluctance was particularly directed towards places such as nursing homes, which were perceived as environments where dignity, autonomy, and personal freedom could be compromised.

Eight out of nine participants expressed strong resistance to move to nursing homes. They were distrustful, suspicious, and even averse to these facilities, fearing that caregivers would not provide the level of care they deserved despite the high costs. Such institutional settings represented an undesirable place, seen only as a last resort when ageing in place was no longer possible. Long shared her concerns:

‘Those caregivers in nursing homes won't be responsible and patient with you… They don't give medication to older people when it's time to take it… I’m just worried that after paying to live there, they won’t take care of me properly… This is my last option.’ (Long, female, 73 years old) (Chinese version: 那些养老院里的护工不会对你负责的、不会对你有耐心……他们不会在该用药的时候就给老人用药……我只是担心, 付了钱住在那里之后, 他们不会好好照顾我……这是我最后的选择了。).

For many, the fear of losing dignity was another major concern. Eight participants believed that caregivers in nursing homes treated older people impersonally, focusing on tasks rather than individual needs, which they felt would lead to a lower quality of life. Yan described her anxieties:

‘When you can’t move, you go to a nursing home, and it’s very likely you’ll be abused. Like on TV, people in nursing homes get bruised and battered. Sometimes they don’t even feed you enough.’ (Yan, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 当你动不了的时候, 你去养老院, 在那里你就很有可能会被他们虐待。就像在电视上看到的那样, 养老院里的老人会被打得遍体鳞伤。有时他们甚至不会给你足够吃的东西。).

Four participants also associated nursing homes with the loss of freedom and autonomy. They worried that life in a nursing home would be governed by strict schedules, leaving them unable to live on their own terms. Yan contrasted this with ageing in her own home:

‘In a nursing home, you rely on staff for meals, and they’re served on a strict schedule. If you’re late, the food is cold. How can you reheat it? At home, you can cook a hot meal anytime you want.’ (Yan, female, 74 years old) (Chinese version: 在养老院, 吃饭全靠那里的工作人员安排, 严格遵循固定的时间表。要是错过用餐时间, 饭菜就凉了。那怎么热饭菜呢?在家里, 你随时都可以做一顿热饭热菜。).

These negative perceptions of institutional care, shaped largely by online information and anecdotal stories, created a frightening image of nursing homes. As a result, most participants regarded institutional care as a last resort, to be avoided unless absolutely necessary. Wu expressed his significant distrust, fear and aversion in the interview:

‘I saw videos online of caregivers in nursing homes hitting older people very hard. It was terrifying. Everyone around me says we can’t go to a nursing home!’ (Wu, male, 84 years old) (Chinese version: 我在网上看到养老院护工殴打老人的视频, 场面极其吓人。身边所有人都说我们绝不能去养老院!).

In contrast, only Ma held a more balanced view, informed by both negative online information and positive experiences shared by his friends whose parents lived in nursing homes. Acknowledging the existence of both poor and high-quality facilities, Ma expressed openness to the idea:

‘Some of my friends’ parents live in a nursing home, and they’re happy there. I’m not against it. Yes, there’re caregivers who treat older people badly, but there’re also good nursing homes. My wife and I agree that if we can’t take care of ourselves in the future, we’d consider moving into one.’ (Ma, male, 68 years old) (Chinese version: 我有些朋友的父母就住在养老院里, 他们在那儿过得挺开心的。我对此并不反对。是的, 确实存在虐待老人的护工, 但也存在不错的养老院。我和我老婆都同意, 如果将来我们没办法自理了, 我们会考虑搬到养老院里去。).

Discussion

Findings from this study has helped reframe AIP and further refines the concept of 'place' within the Chinese context. For older people, aging at home and within familiar communities did offer the autonomy and freedom they sought. However, shaped by a culture rooted in collectivism and familism, their understanding of place extended beyond the notion of physical space. For the participants in this study, ‘place’ also encompassed their connections and networks with adult children, family members, friends, the community, the state, and the digital world. In articulating their understanding of place, they also voiced a desire to avoid the constraints associated with institutionalised care.

For older people in China, having a separate home of their own was considered a fundamental aspect of ‘place’ in AIP. Aging in their own homes supported their autonomy and freedom, enabling them to maintain control over daily lives and decisions within familiar environments. This finding echoes those of other studies [17, 18, 24, 39, 40]. Furthermore, the influence of globalisation and urbanisation appears to have subtly shaped individuals’ values and preferences. With greater exposure to Western ideologies, many older people were gradually influenced by individualistic lifestyles and increasingly exhibit such values in their daily lives [41].

Interestingly, most participants in this study did not exhibit a strong sense of place attachment to their current homes, despite being satisfied with their homes and ageing environment. This finding diverges from previous research which commonly reported a strong emotional attachment to home and community among older people’s AIP [12–14, 17]. Place attachment is typically understood as the emotional bond between individuals and specific geographical locations, often rooted in meaningful life experiences [20]. In this study, many participants were relatively flexible regarding the location of their homes and expressed a willingness to relocate closer to their adult children, suggesting that their attachment was more closely tied to familial presence than to physical space. At the same time, participants frequently emphasised the importance of maintaining a certain degree of distance, described metaphorically as keeping a distance for ‘a bowl of hot soup’, which enables intergenerational reciprocity and mutual support while preserving personal spaces with healthy boundaries.

Three potential explanations may account for this relatively weak sense of place attachment. First, the length of residence plays a crucial role in shaping attachment. As the duration of residence increases, individuals are more likely to develop a stronger emotional connection to their homes. The home, in this sense, becomes more than just a physical structure; it is imbued with personal memories, family histories, and symbolic significance accumulated over the life course [17]. For instance, Stones and Gullifer [14] interviewed 19 very old adults aged over 85 who had lived in their current homes for at least 15 years. These participants exhibited strong place attachment, which included both tangible objects within the home and intangible elements such as values, beliefs, and personal histories [14]. In contrast, participants in this study had often resided in their current homes for fewer than 20 years, with some having lived there for less than a decade. Within such a relatively short time frame, it is possible that they had not yet developed a deep sense of attachment to their current homes.

Secondly, China has the largest internal migrant population in the world, encompassing rural-to-urban, intercity, and interprovincial migration [42]. Due to the household registration (hukou) system, many internal migrants remain excluded from social welfare and public services in their host cities [43]. They are often characterised as a ‘floating population’, marked by high mobility and weak ties to the places in which they reside [44]. Although this study did not collect detailed migration histories, the relatively short duration of residence reported by many participants suggests a likelihood of prior internal migration. Moreover, interview data revealed that some participants were considering relocating to cities where their adult children resided to obtain more resources and support for ageing. These patterns highlight how internal migration in China, much like international migration in Western contexts, is subtly transforming the meaning and practical implementation of AIP.

Thirdly, the cultural value of familism may influence how older people form attachments to their homes. Familism, as a culturally specific form of collectivism, emphasises individual attachment to family, loyalty, reciprocity, and the prioritisation of family interests over individual needs [45]. In this study, many older people expressed that living in close proximity to their adult children enabled them to both contribute to childcare (e.g., caring for grandchildren) and receive assistance with their own aging-related needs. This form of supportive relocation underscores the reciprocal dynamics of intergenerational relationships and functions as a strategy for optimising the use of familial resources. Furthermore, familism operates as a moral framework that defines what constitutes appropriate and dignified ageing. Older people’s choices to reside near their adult children or to assist with grandchild care were not only pragmatic strategies, but also moral practices that affirmed their roles as responsible parents and contributors within the family. In this sense, familism embodies a moral order through which ageing, care, and belonging acquire culturally specific meanings. Such patterns exemplify the enduring influence of familism within the framework of AIP in the Chinese context. Older people’s attachment to ‘home’ can thus be interpreted as a form of moral and relational negotiation, through which they actively mobilise intergenerational reciprocity, spatial flexibility, and emotional distance (‘a bowl of hot soup’) to preserve autonomy and dignity within a collective context. Ultimately, AIP in China reflects a culturally situated process of moral place-making, where ageing, care, and autonomy are constantly rebalanced between independence and interdependence.

In addition to symbolising separate homes, ‘place’ was understood by older people as a relational environment shaped through their social networks and interactions. This reflects the social and relational dimensions of place in AIP. At the micro level, participants described maintaining relationships with spouses, adult children, neighbours, and old friends, forming networks of reciprocity and interdependence. At the macro level, they expressed a sense of connection to the broader society and the state, often articulating feelings of safety and stability provided by living in a supportive state.

These social networks and interactions constituted what has been broadly referred to as social capital, defined as the relationships and resources that facilitate coordination, cooperation, and mutual benefit [46, 47]. Key elements of social capital include interpersonal networks, trust, reciprocity, and participation in social life [48, 49]. In this study, such social capital emerged as a core component of an intangible sense of place, deeply influencing both the functional and emotional aspects of participants’ AIP. Physically, social networks enabled access to care resources, health information, and encouragement for healthy behaviours [48, 50]. Psychologically, participation in social activities helped reduce loneliness, fostered a sense of belonging, and enhanced self-worth and life satisfaction [23, 40, 49, 50]. Some participants also described how social capital enabled them to actively resist age-related discrimination, isolation, and marginalisation in the communities.

At the macro level, support from society and the state provided older people with a sense of security and stability, which shaped the relational dimensions of place in their daily lives. Over time, the government’s responsibility for older people in China has shifted from ensuring only basic subsistence under the planned-economy system to delivering a broader spectrum of ageing services in today’s market-oriented context, including daily care, chronic disease management, health maintenance, and psychosocial support [51]. This evolution has produced a stratified system of ageing support, with public provisions for the most disadvantaged, quasi-public services for low-income groups, and private offerings for middle- and high-income older people [51]. Such institutional arrangements interact with older people’s social networks, influencing how autonomy, interdependence, and well-being are negotiated within AIP.

With technological advancements, the concept of ‘place’ in AIP has extended beyond physical and social environments to include the digital realm. Digital platforms have enabled older people to stay connected with family, friends, and wider society, thereby enriching their daily lives. Technology helped overcome geographic and mobility barriers, improving family communication, reducing social isolation, and reinforcing intergenerational relationships [40, 52, 53]. Online interactions offered emotional support and alleviated loneliness [54, 55], although they could not fully substitute for face-to-face contact [56].

Beyond family ties, technology also supported connections with friends, neighbours, and old acquaintances, helping to rebuild shrinking physical networks and enhance social support, sense of belonging and mental well-being [57, 58]. Reconnecting with old ties countered the effects of older people’s shrinking physical networks, reducing loneliness and improving mental health and life satisfaction [52, 54].

Additionally, access to digital health information has improved health literacy and awareness, encouraging healthier behaviours and better self-management [58, 59]. Digital technologies have also greatly enhanced older people's daily lives in terms of convenience and richness [59, 60]. Through these functions, digital technologies not only supported the creation of a digital place but also sustained a relational place, enabling older people to stay socially engaged and experience a more fulfilling later life despite geographical distance.

However, technology also presented challenges. Many older people in this study demonstrated limited digital literacy and had difficulty assessing the credibility of online information [61]. This made them vulnerable to online misinformation, including exaggerated or false narratives about nursing homes. Several participants reported frequently encountering negative portrayals of institutional care on social media, which intensified their fear and mistrust. Lacking the ability to critically evaluate such information, many relied too heavily and uncritically on online sources [61, 62]. Therefore, while digital technologies helped broaden the understanding of 'place' and supported relational connections, they also exposed older people to new risks and uncertainties.

This study found that most participants held negative perceptions of nursing homes, seeing them as the last place they would consider for ageing. Their opposition often stemmed not only from concerns about care quality but from a deeper resistance to the idea of being institutionalised, an experience that, for many, symbolised the beginning of losing dignity, autonomy, freedom and purpose. Similar sentiments have been documented in other countries [17, 40, 63]. For these older people, entering a nursing home marked a shift from being active members of families and communities, parents, grandparents, contributors, to being primarily passive care recipients. This transition came with potential loss of identity, social status, and self-worth [64]. Older people's contact with their original community, friends and family is also likely to be substantially reduced, making it difficult for them to maintain their previous social networks [63, 65]. The reduced contact with family and community would lead to loneliness, disconnection, and depression [64]. Furthermore, upon entering a nursing home, many tasks are performed by caregivers, leaving the residents to engage primarily in daily activities mandated by the nursing home [64]. The freedom, autonomy and independence of older people could also be undermined with a rigid and fixed schedule. The life in nursing homes was therefore described by some older people as 'waiting house for the dying' [64]. The structured nature of institutional life, while necessary for care routines, could be experienced as restrictive and depersonalising.

In the Chinese context, this fear and resistance toward institutional care was compounded by systemic and cultural factors. Institutional care remains underdeveloped, with regional disparities in service quality, a shortage of trained professionals, and inadequate facilities, especially in less affluent regions [66]. Private nursing homes often faced significant shortcomings in facilities, training, and financial support, further eroding individual confidence in institutional care [66]. Culturally, deep-rooted norms of filial piety cast institutional care as abandonment, reinforcing the perception that placing parents in care homes or nursing homes is morally unacceptable [67, 68]. These factors combined to strengthen older people’s reluctance to consider institutional care as a viable option for ageing.

While receiving institutional care does not necessarily mean being institutionalised in the psychological or social sense, the current realities make it difficult for older people to distinguish between the two. To shift this perception, more effort is needed from institutions, society, and the state. This includes improving care quality, enhancing transparency, and promoting more diverse, community-connected models of institutional care. Technology, if used wisely, may also serve as a bridge—offering ways to maintain external social ties and preserve autonomy even within institutional settings.

Overall, ‘place’ in AIP within the Chinese context from this study extends beyond physical space to encompass social, moral, relational, institutional, and digital dimensions. Participants negotiated autonomy and interdependence within homes, families, communities, and broader institutional contexts, shaped by cultural values such as familism and stratified state support. Micro-level social networks, macro-level policies, and digital technologies collectively structured relational place, influencing well-being, security, and daily practices. These findings advance the theorisation of place by highlighting its embedding in moral, social, economic, and political systems, moving beyond simplistic narratives of underdeveloped welfare and providing a framework for understanding AIP across diverse socio-cultural contexts.

Implications and future directions

The findings, grounded in the lived experiences of older people in China, provide a nuanced understanding of ‘place’ in AIP, highlighting its territorial, relational, digital, and deinstitutionalised dimensions. While it may be unrealistic to design a universally suitable place for all older people, developing a more comprehensive and context-sensitive understanding of place is essential for policymakers. By expanding the concept beyond physical space to include relational ties, digital engagement, and resistance to institutionalisation, this study has practical implications for ageing-related policy development. Specifically, policies should move beyond infrastructure provision and address the importance of social connectivity, digital access, and respect for older people's autonomy and their resistance to institutionalised care. This broader framing can guide the development of culturally sensitive and socially responsive AIP initiatives.

At the institutional level, the findings also suggest that the core issue in formal care settings is often not the physical environment, but the sense of being institutionalised. Many older people express concern over losing dignity, autonomy, and identity. Addressing these calls for relational, person-centred approaches to care. At the organisational level, policies and management practices can play a key role in softening rigid institutional routines, fostering meaningful staff–resident relationships, and creating environments that preserve older people’s individuality and sense of belonging.

Looking ahead, the meanings of ‘place’ in AIP are unlikely to remain static. As China undergoes rapid demographic and technological transitions, the spatial, relational, and digital dimensions of later life will continue to be reconfigured. The negotiation of belonging, autonomy, and care will evolve alongside broader social changes—such as the expansion of digital infrastructures, the diversification of family forms, and shifting expectations of intergenerational support. Future research might explore how older people rearticulate their sense of place and identity within these transformations, and how new configurations of digital and relational networks shape emerging forms of ‘home’. For policymakers and practitioners, anticipating these shifts is crucial. Rather than treating ‘place’ as a fixed setting for care, it should be approached as a dynamic and evolving field of relationships, technologies, and values through which ageing itself is continuously redefined.

Limitations

This study also has limitations, which are largely a feature of its idiographic IPA approach. The sample size was intentionally small and not intended to be demographically representative, as the focus was on capturing in-depth experiences of individual participants rather than producing generalisable findings. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the diversity of experiences among older people with different genders, marital statuses, or socioeconomic backgrounds, which are particularly important in shaping AIP experiences in East Asian cultural contexts. Furthermore, all participants lived in private homes, which constrained the exploration of how older people without stable housing, such as renters or migrants, construct and interpret the meaning of place. Future research should include more diverse populations to develop a more inclusive and comprehensive understanding of place in AIP.

Conclusion

As current ageing policies promote AIP, it is necessary to provide a comprehensive understanding of ‘place’ in AIP [21]. This study examined Chinese older people’s perceptions of ‘place’ in AIP, revealing its multidimensional nature. While ‘place’ begins with the physical home, it extends to include relational connections, social interactions, digital engagement, and the rejection of institutionalisation. Older people’s experiences were shaped by socio-cultural values such as familism, as well as by broader political and economic contexts, extending current understandings of AIP beyond Western individualistic paradigms. Specifically, China’s familism, collective values, social welfare systems, and market-oriented economy together shape ageing experiences, care, and understandings of place. By broadening the concept of ‘place’ beyond its traditional physical dimensions, this study offers valuable insights for policymakers and practitioners. It highlights the need to design ageing policies that address not only physical housing but also social networks and digital inclusion, thereby promoting relational and emotional well-being. Altogether, this study contributes to fostering more inclusive and supportive environments that meet the diverse needs of older people, particularly in an increasingly globalised and digitalised world.
