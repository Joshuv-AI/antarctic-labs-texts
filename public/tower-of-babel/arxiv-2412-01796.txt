Suppression of the collisionless tearing mode by flow shear: implications for reconnection onset in the Alfvénic solar wind
A. Mallet \aff 1 \corresp, S. Eriksson \aff 2, M. Swisdak \aff 3, J. Juno \aff 4

Abstract
We analyse the collisionless tearing mode instability of a current sheet with a strong shear flow across the layer. The growth rate decreases with increasing shear flow, and is completely stabilized as the shear flow becomes Alfvénic. We also show that in the presence of strong flow shear, the tearing mode growth rate decreases with increasing background ion-to-electron temperature ratio, the opposite behaviour to the tearing mode without flow shear. We find that even a relatively small flow shear is enough to dramatically alter the scaling behaviour of the mode, because the growth rate is small compared to the shear flow across the ion scales (but large compared to shear flow across the electron scales). Our results may explain the relative absence of reconnection events in the near-Sun Alfvénic solar wind observed recently by NASA’s Parker Solar Probe.



1
Introduction

Magnetic reconnection is a fundamental plasma physics process, involving a topological rearrangement of the magnetic field, and accompanied by the conversion of magnetic energy into bulk plasma flow and heat. Reconnection occurs in a wide range of contexts, for example solar flares
(
Yan et al. 2022
)
, at planetary magnetopauses
(
Paschmann et al. 2013
)
, in the sawtooth oscillation in tokamaks
(
Kadomtsev 1975
)
and in current sheets ubiquitous in many space and astrophysical environments
(
Eriksson et al. 2022
;
Eriksson et al. 2024
)
.

Here, we are specifically motivated by observations of current sheets in the near-Sun solar wind, currently being explored
in situ
for the first time by NASA’s Parker Solar Probe (PSP) spacecraft. While such sheets are observed essentially ubiquitously
(
Vasko et al. 2022
;
Lotekar et al. 2022
)
, only a small fraction actually reconnect
(
Eriksson et al. 2022
)
: if one looks only in "Alfvénic" solar wind, where velocity and magnetic-field fluctuations are highly correlated, with
\delta\boldsymbol{u}\approx\delta\boldsymbol{b}
, (with
\delta\boldsymbol{b}=\delta\boldsymbol{B}/\sqrt{4\pi n_{i}m_{i}}
the magnetic field fluctuation in velocity units), even fewer reconnecting current sheets are observed
(
Phan et al. 2020
;
Fargette et al. 2023
;
Eriksson et al. 2024
)
. This suggests that the quasi-Alfvénic velocity shears across the current sheet are suppressing the onset of reconnection, as observed in steady-state nonlinear reconnection
(
Cassak 2011
)
. Here, we develop a theory to describe the suppression of reconnection onset by such a flow shear.

Many numerical studies of reconnection begin with a kinetic scale current sheet: for example, the GEM reconnection challenge problem
(
Birn et al. 2001
)
has a current sheet with a width comparable to
d_{i}=c/\omega_{pi}
. With this setup, reconnection proceeds rapidly from the outset. However, in many of the natural systems mentioned above, reconnection occurs as part of a bursty, two-timescale process: first a long, quiescent phase in which magnetic energy builds up in thinning current sheets, followed by an extremely rapid disruption as reconnection occurs.

Recently, new models of reconnection onset that explain this property have been developed
(
Pucci & Velli 2014
;
Uzdensky & Loureiro 2016
;
Tolman et al. 2018
)
. On large scales, ideal dynamics lead to the progressive "thinning" of the current sheet width
a
, for example via the Chapman-Kendall collapse
(
Chapman & Kendall 1963
)
, on a characteristic (ideal) timescale
T(a)
. This can be compared with the growth rate of the tearing mode (the linear stage of reconnection),
\gamma(a)
: only when
\gamma(a)T(a)\gtrsim 1

will reconnection onset occur: at which point, the ideal dynamics are disrupted and the current sheet is usually destroyed. This model has also been applied to turbulence, where
T
can be identified as the nonlinear timescale
T_{\rm nl}
, whose scaling depends on the details of the turbulence
(
Mallet et al. 2017a
;
Mallet et al. 2017b
;
Loureiro & Boldyrev 2017a
;
Loureiro & Boldyrev 2017b
;
Comisso et al. 2018
)
.

To apply this model to the highly sheared current sheets observed by PSP, we need to understand the scalings of both
\gamma
and
T
in the presence of strong flow shear, neither of which are currently well understood. Here, we focus on the former problem, and develop a new analytic theory for the collisionless tearing mode with significant flow shear,
\delta u\sim\delta b=\delta B/\sqrt{4\pi n_{i}m_{i}}
, determining the growth rate
\gamma
as a function of the physical parameters. We find that flow shear strongly suppresses the tearing instability, with the growth rate proportional to
1-\alpha^{2}
, where
\alpha=\delta u/\delta b
: as
\alpha\to 1
, corresponding to Alfvénic flow shear, the growth rate of the tearing mode vanishes (for
\alpha>1
, we would have instead the ideal Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, whose growth rate is proportional to
\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}
: we will not comment further on this instability here). Finally, we discuss how our results might apply to the PSP observations.

2
Equations

We will use the kinetic reduced electron heating model (KREHM) equations
(
Zocco & Schekochihin 2011
)
. These equations are derived from gyrokinetics (and thus assume low frequency fluctuations
\omega\ll\Omega_{i}
and a strong guide field,
\delta B/B_{0}\ll 1
) in the limit of small
\beta
, and, assuming no variations in the
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
direction, may be written

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(1-\hat{\Gamma}_{0})\Phi

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{i}^{2}\left\{{\Psi},{\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\Psi}\right\},

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\Psi-d_{e}^{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\Psi)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\Psi},{\frac{Z}{\tau}(1-\hat{\Gamma}_{0})\Phi-\frac{c}{eB_{0}}\delta T_{\parallel e}}\right\}

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{d{g_{e}}}{dt}+\frac{v_{\parallel}}{v_{A}}\left\{{\Psi},{g_{e}-\frac{\delta T_{\parallel e}}{T_{0e}}F_{0e}}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\Omega_{i}}\left(1-\frac{2v_{\parallel}^{2}}{v_{\mathrm{th}e}^{2}}\right)F_{0e}\left\{{\Psi},{\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\Psi}\right\}.

(5)

where the Poisson bracket
\{f,g\}=\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\cdot(\nabla_{\perp}f\times\nabla_{\perp}g)
,
the ion gyroradius is
\rho_{i}=v_{\mathrm{th}i}/\Omega_{i}
, with
v_{\mathrm{th}i}=\sqrt{2T_{0i}/m_{i}}
the ion thermal speed.
B_{0}
is again the (strong) guide magnetic field in the out-of-plane
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
direction,
F_{0e}
is the equilibrium Maxwellian electron distribution function, and we have written

\frac{d}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\left\{{\Phi},{\ldots}\right\}.

(6)

The equations have been written in different variables to ZS11,

\Phi=\frac{c}{B_{0}}\phi,\quad\Psi=-\frac{A_{\parallel}}{\sqrt{4\pi m_{i}n_{0i}}},

(7)

chosen to be notationally similar to reduced magnetohydrodynamics (RMHD):
\phi
is the electric potential and
A_{\parallel}
is the parallel magnetic vector potential, while the perpendicular (to
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}})
magnetic field (in velocity units) is
\boldsymbol{b}_{\perp}=\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\times\nabla_{\perp}\Psi
and the
\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}
velocity is
\boldsymbol{u}_{E\times B}=\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}\times\nabla_{\perp}\Phi
. The operator
\hat{\Gamma}_{0}
is the inverse Fourier transform of

\Gamma_{0}(k_{\perp}^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}/2)=I_{0}(k_{\perp}^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}/2)e^{-k_{\perp}^{2}\rho_{i}^{2}/2},

(8)

where
I_{0}
is the modified Bessel function: at large and small scales, we have

1-\hat{\Gamma}_{0}\approx\begin{cases}-\frac{\rho_{i}^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2},\quad&\rho_{i}^{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\ll 1\\
1,\quad&\rho_{i}^{2}\nabla_{\perp}^{2}\gg 1.\end{cases}

(9)

The electron density fluctuations are

\frac{\delta n_{e}}{n_{0e}}=-\frac{2}{\rho_{i}^{2}\Omega_{i}}(1-\hat{\Gamma}_{0})\Phi.

(10)

Equation (
5
) evolves the reduced parallel electron distribution function
g_{e}
, defined in terms of the perturbed parallel electron distribution function
\delta f_{e}
as

g_{e}=\delta f_{e}-\left[\frac{\delta n_{e}}{n_{0e}}+\frac{2v_{\parallel}u_{\parallel e}}{v_{\mathrm{th}e}^{2}}\right]F_{0e},

(11)

The parallel electron temperature fluctuation is given by the second moment,

\frac{\delta T_{\parallel e}}{T_{0e}}=\frac{1}{n_{0e}}\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{v}\frac{2v_{\parallel}^{2}}{v_{\mathrm{th}e}^{2}}g_{e}.

(12)

KREHM is designed to be a set of equations appropriate for studying reconnection: the equations contain a rigorous treatment of electron heating via the electron kinetic equation (
5
), the dispersion at the ion scales (
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{s}=\rho_{i}\sqrt{Z/2\tau}
), important for achieving fast reconnection
(
Shay et al. 2001
)
, and also the flux-unfreezing at the electron inertial scale
d_{e}
. This model cannot be expected to apply to all the current sheets observed in the solar wind: in particular, the assumptions that
\beta\ll 1
and
\delta B/B_{0}\ll 1
are often not satisfied. However, it will allow us to make progress in understanding reconnection onset in the near-Sun solar wind.

2.1
Equilibrium and linearized equations

Our chosen equilibrium is

\Phi_{0}=\alpha\Psi_{0},\quad b_{0y}=v_{\mathrm{A}y}f(x/a)=\partial_{x}\Psi_{0},

(13)

where we will be especially interested in the case where the shear flow in the
\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}
direction is quite large,
\alpha\sim 1
. As in
1.1
, we have the current sheet normal in the
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
direction and both the reconnecting field and wavenumber in the

direction. We also assume that the equilibrium length-scale is large compared to ion and electron scales,

(15)

\displaystyle(\gamma+i\alpha kv_{\mathrm{A}y}f)(\Psi-d_{e}^{2}\Psi^{\prime\prime})=ikv_{\mathrm{A}y}f\left[\left(1+\frac{Z}{\tau}(1-\hat{\Gamma}_{0})\right)\Phi-\frac{c}{eB_{0}}\delta T_{\parallel e}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle(\gamma+i\alpha kv_{\mathrm{A}y}f)g_{e}+ikv_{\mathrm{A}y}f\frac{v_{\parallel}}{v_{A}}\left(g_{e}-\frac{\delta T_{\parallel e}}{T_{0e}}F_{0e}\right)=

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\frac{1}{\Omega_{i}}ikv_{\mathrm{A}y}f\left(1-\frac{2v_{\parallel}^{2}}{v_{\mathrm{th}e}^{2}}\right)F_{0e}\left[\Psi^{\prime\prime}-k^{2}\Psi-\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{f}\Psi\right].

(22)

\zeta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\epsilon\left(\frac{i\lambda}{\xi}-\alpha\right).

(36)

The electron inertia term in (

We first solve the equations on the ion scales,

(37)

Electron layer

(44)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\epsilon y\Psi^{\prime\prime}}{1+i\alpha\epsilon y}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{1+Z/\tau}\left(\frac{i\epsilon y\Psi^{\prime\prime}}{1+i\alpha\epsilon y}\right)^{\prime\prime},

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}(1+i\alpha\epsilon y)\Psi-i\lambda^{2}\epsilon y\Phi

(47)

Anticipating

At lowest order, we have
\Psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}=0

\Psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}=0

\Psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\lambda^{2}(1+\tau/Z)}{1-\alpha^{2}}\Psi_{0e}\frac{1}{(G+\tau/Z)y^{2}+1},

\Psi_{2}=\frac{\lambda^{2}\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\Psi_{0e}\int_{0}^{y}dz\int_{0}^{z}\frac{du\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}{(G+\tau/Z)u^{2}+1}.

\Psi_{e}\to\Psi_{0e}\left[1+\frac{\lambda\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}{\epsilon(1-\alpha^{2})}I_{G}\xi-\frac{\lambda^{2}\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\log(\xi)\right],

(51)

where

I_{G}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dy\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}{(G+\tau/Z)y^{2}+1}.

(52)

If we had assumed isothermal electrons (
G=1
),
I_{G}=\pi/2
. The solution for
\Phi
can be found by integrating (
45
) twice:

\Phi=\Phi_{0e}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{1+Z/\tau}\left(\frac{i\epsilon y\Psi^{\prime\prime}}{1+i\alpha\epsilon y}\right)+i\epsilon\int_{0}^{y}dz\int_{0}^{z}du\frac{u\Psi^{\prime\prime}}{1+i\alpha\epsilon u},

(53)

The second term on the RHS, resulting from the small-scale asymptotic of
1-\hat{\Gamma}_{0}
, is dominant for
y\sim 1
, but always decays for
y\gg 1
, since
\Psi^{\prime\prime}
(e.g.
\Psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}
) is strongly peaked on the electron scales. The final term on the RHS does produce terms in the solution for
\Phi
that do not decay as
y\to\infty
: however, note that they are still a factor
\epsilon
smaller than the corresponding terms in
\Psi
. Inserting
\Psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}
into (
53
), the lowest-order piece is, as
y\to\infty
and in terms of
\xi
,

\Phi_{e}\to\Phi_{0e}+i\epsilon\int_{0}^{y}dz\int_{0}^{z}du{u\Psi_{2}^{\prime\prime}}\to\frac{i\lambda(1+\tau/Z)}{1-\alpha^{2}}\Psi_{0e}\left[\xi\log(\xi)-\xi+\text{const.}\right]+\frac{i\lambda\Psi_{0e}}{1+Z/\tau}\frac{1}{\xi},

(54)

so the non-constant piece of
\Phi_{e}
is
O(\lambda)
for
\xi\sim 1
.

Finally, if we were to solve to third order for
\Psi
, we could determine the odd part of the eigenmode in terms of the even one, and thus determine the small imaginary part of the solution: because this appears at third order, the linear term that appears in the asymptotic solution as
y\to\infty
is
\sim\lambda\xi
, showing that the imaginary part of the eigenmode is a factor of around
\lambda
smaller than the real part. However, this is not necessary to obtain the growth rate.

5
Matching and dispersion relation

We can now match the ion and electron solutions. We match the constant and linear terms from
\xi\to 0
from the ion solution (
42
and
43
) and
y\to\infty
in the electron solution (
51
and
54
), assuming
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\sim 1
. From the constant terms of
\Psi
, we obtain

\Psi_{0e}=\Psi_{\infty}+C_{i}.

(55)

As we found above, at lowest order the leading non-constant terms of
\Phi_{e}
are
O(\lambda)
, and so to lowest order, the linear term in (
43
) must be zero,

\frac{1}{2}\alpha\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\Psi_{\infty}-\frac{1}{\alpha}C_{i}\left[1-\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{1+Z/\tau}\right]=0,

(56)

whence

C_{i}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\Psi_{\infty}(1+Z/\tau)}{Z/\tau+\alpha^{2}}.

(57)

Finally, matching the linear terms for
\Psi_{i}
as
\xi\to 0
(
42
) and
\Psi_{e}
as
y\to\infty
(
51
),

\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\Psi_{\infty}-C_{i}=\frac{\lambda\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}{\epsilon(1-\alpha^{2})}I_{G}\Psi_{0e}.

(58)

Inserting (
55
) and (
57
), we obtain the dispersion relation

\lambda=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\epsilon(1-\alpha^{2})^{2}Z/\tau}{Z/\tau+\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}(1+Z/\tau)}\frac{1}{I_{G}\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}}.

(59)

5.1
Growth rate

Using the definitions of
\lambda
(
33
) and
\delta
(
27
), the growth rate is

\frac{\gamma a}{v_{\mathrm{A}y}}=\frac{k\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}^{2}\epsilon(1-\alpha^{2})^{2}}{2I_{G}\sqrt{1+\tau/Z}(1+\alpha^{2}\tau/Z+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}(1+\tau/Z))}

(60)

Inserting
\delta_{in}
(
41
), we find that,

\frac{\gamma a}{v_{\mathrm{A}y}}\sim\begin{cases}k\Delta^{\prime}\rho_{s}d_{e}\frac{\displaystyle(1-\alpha^{2})\sqrt{1+\tau/Z})}{\displaystyle 2I_{G}(1+\alpha^{2}\tau/Z)},&\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\ll 1\\
kd_{e}\frac{\displaystyle(1-\alpha^{2})^{3/2}}{\displaystyle\alpha^{2}I_{G}(1+\tau/Z)},&\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\gg 1.\end{cases}

(61)

The growth rate for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\ll 1
has the same scaling with
d_{e}
and
\rho_{s}
as the no-flow scaling (
1
), but for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\gg 1
, we have obtained the surprising result that the growth rate is independent of
\rho_{s}
: in the no-flow case, we had
\gamma_{0}\propto\rho_{s}^{2/3}d_{e}^{1/3}
. Note that the growth rate for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\gg 1
does not match smoothly onto the no-flow scaling (
1
): this is because the expansion in the ion region is invalidated for
\alpha\ll\lambda
(see Sec.
5.2
below).

The transition between these scalings occurs where they match. Inserting
\Delta^{\prime}a\propto 1/(ka)^{n}
(see Sec.
1.1
), the transition occurs roughly at

k_{\mathrm{tr}}a\sim(\rho_{s}/a)^{1/n}\frac{\alpha^{2/n}}{(1-\alpha^{2})^{1/2n}}\left(\frac{(1+\tau/Z)^{3/2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\tau/Z}\right)^{1/n}

(62)

for which the growth rate is of order

\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}a}{v_{\mathrm{A}y}}\sim\frac{\rho_{s}^{1/n}d_{e}}{a^{1+1/n}}\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})^{3/2-1/2n}}{\alpha^{2-2/n}}\frac{(1+\tau/Z)^{3/2n-1}}{(1+\alpha^{2}\tau/Z)^{1/n}}.

(63)

For
n=2
, the growth rate increases with
k
for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\gg 1
and decreases with
k
for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\ll 1
, so that
\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}
is also the maximum growth rate, occuring uniquely at
k_{\mathrm{tr}}
. For the
n=1
case,
\gamma
is independent of
k
for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\ll 1
, and equal to
\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}
for all
k>k_{\mathrm{tr}}
.

This differs in several ways from the growth rate for
\alpha=0
(Eq.
1
). First, the growth rate scales strongly with
1-\alpha^{2}
: as
\alpha\to 1
, the growth rate vanishes, as is the case for the resistive MHD tearing mode with shear
(
Hofman 1975
;
Chen & Morrison 1989
;
Boldyrev & Loureiro 2018
)
, where
\gamma_{\rm MHD}\propto(1-\alpha^{2})^{1/2}
: thus the growth rate of the tearing mode is more strongly suppressed in a collisionless plasma than in resistive MHD.
2
2

2

For
\alpha^{2}>1
, we would have instead the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability: since
a\gg\rho_{i}
this is essentially the same as the MHD case
(
Miura & Pritchett 1982
)
.
Second, the growth rate depends differently on
\tau
. For
\tau\ll 1
, the growth rate becomes independent of
\tau
, similarly to the tearing mode without flow shear. In contrast, for
\tau\gg 1
, the maximum growth rate is proportional to
\tau^{(1/2n)-1}
; the opposite dependency to the case without flow shear, for which
\gamma_{0\mathrm{tr}}\propto\tau^{1/2}
for
\tau\gg 1
. Finally, in general the growth rate also depends differently on the scales
\rho_{s}
and
d_{e}
than in the no-flow case: without shear flow,
\gamma_{0\mathrm{tr}}\propto d_{e}^{1/3+2/3n}\rho_{s}^{2/3+1/3n}
, but with shear flow,
\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}\propto d_{e}\rho_{s}^{1/n}
. For
n=1
, these scalings coincide, but for
n=2
,
\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}/\gamma_{0\mathrm{tr}}\propto\epsilon^{1/3}
, where
\epsilon=d_{e}/\rho_{s}\ll 1
.

5.2
Validity and the small shear flow limit

For the expansion in the ion region to be valid, we must have

\lambda\ll\alpha
.
For
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\ll 1
, this is easily satisfied, and indeed, the growth rate in this case smoothly joins onto the no-flow growth rate (
1
) as
\alpha\to 0
. For
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\gg 1
, the inequality is

\frac{\epsilon(1-\alpha^{2})^{2}}{\alpha^{2}(1+\tau/Z)^{3/2}}\ll\alpha,

(64)

or
\alpha
above a critical
\alpha_{c}
defined by

\frac{\alpha_{c}}{(1-\alpha_{c}^{2})^{2/3}}=\epsilon^{1/3}(1+\tau/Z)^{-1/2}.

(65)

For
\epsilon\ll 1
,
\alpha_{c}\sim\epsilon^{1/3}
, and even a relatively small shear flow can affect the growth rate scalings with
d_{e}
and
\rho_{s}
for
\Delta^{\prime}\delta_{in}\gg 1
. This is because the growth rate of the mode is slow compared to the shearing rate on the ion scales.

7
Conclusions

We have studied the collisionless tearing mode in the presence of significant shear flows, as parametrized by
\alpha=\delta u/\delta b
, where
\delta u
and
\delta b
are the amplitudes of the background velocity and magnetic field fluctuations, pointing in the
y
(or L) direction and varying in the
x
(or N) direction across the current sheet. We find that the growth rates depend strongly on
\alpha
, with a maximum growth rate given by (
63
),

\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}a}{v_{\mathrm{A}y}}\sim\frac{\rho_{s}^{1/n}d_{e}}{a^{1+1/n}}\frac{(1-\alpha^{2})^{3/2-1/2n}}{\alpha^{2-2/n}}\frac{(1+\tau/Z)^{3/2n-1}}{(1+\alpha^{2}\tau/Z)^{1/n}},

(67)

where
n=1
(e.g. for a Harris-type profile)or
n=2
(e.g. for a sinusoidal type profile). As
\alpha\to 1
(an exactly Alfvénic flow),
\gamma\to 0
. Moreover, for large ion-to-electron temperature ratios,
\tau=T_{0i}/T_{0e}
, the growth rate decreases with
\tau
,
\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}\propto\tau^{1/2n-1}
. With
n=1
for simplicity, relative to the maximum tearing mode growth rate with
\alpha=0
,
\gamma_{0\mathrm{tr}}
(see Eq.
2
), we have

\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}}{\gamma_{0\mathrm{tr}}}=\frac{1-\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}\tau/Z}.

(68)

A remarkable absence of reconnection was reported in the near-Sun solar wind observed during Parker Solar Probe’s first perihelion
(
Phan et al. 2020
)
. This has been confirmed in a comprehensive recent study of the PSP data by
Eriksson et al. 2024
, who found that reconnection was extremely rare more specifically in faster, high-ion-temperature solar wind emerging from coronal holes: this is also the wind that is typically highly Alfvénic
(
D’Amicis & Bruno 2015
;
D’Amicis et al. 2021
)
, with highly correlated velocity and magnetic field fluctuations,
\delta\boldsymbol{u}\sim\pm\delta\boldsymbol{b}
or
1-\alpha^{2}\ll 1

(
Ervin et al. 2024
)
. Typically, this wind also has large
\tau=T_{0i}/T_{0e}

(
Shi et al. 2023
)
, probably due to higher ion heating in imbalanced turbulence due to the recently discovered helicity barrier
(
Meyrand et al. 2021
;
Squire et al. 2022
)
. Similar clustering was observed at larger heliocentric distances by
Fargette et al. 2023
, possibly with the same underlying cause.

We have shown that both these parameters typical of the Alfvénic wind,
1-\alpha^{2}\ll 1
and
\tau\gtrsim 1
, suppress the tearing mode growth rate, and thus increase the reconnection onset time, comparable to
\gamma^{-1}
. We can roughly estimate a typical reconnection time from our expression for the growth rate: taking
n=1
and typical values for the PSP Alfvénic wind,
\alpha\approx 0.9
and
\tau\approx 2

(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Shi et al. 2021
;
Shi et al. 2023
;
Ervin et al. 2024
)
, we find
\gamma_{\mathrm{tr}}/\gamma_{0\mathrm{tr}}\approx 0.07
. Our theory may therefore help to explain the patchy suppression of reconnection in the PSP data.
