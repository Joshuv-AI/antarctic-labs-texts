An application of the mean motion problem to time-optimal control
Omri Dalin, Alexander Ovseevich, Michael Margaliot

Abstract
We consider time-optimal controls of a controllable linear system with a scalar control on a long time interval. It is well-known that if all the eigenvalues of the matrix describing the linear system dynamics are real then any time-optimal control has a bounded number of switching points, where the bound does not depend on the length of the time interval. We consider the case where the governing matrix has purely imaginary eigenvalues, and show that then, in the generic case, the number of switching points is bounded from below by a linear function of the length of the time interval. The proof is based on relating the switching function in the optimal control problem to the mean motion problem that dates back to Lagrange and was solved by Hermann Weyl.



I
Introduction

Consider the single-input
linear control system

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=Ax+bu,

(1)

,

,

We assume throughout that the system is controllable
[
4

(2)

(3)

Under the controllability assumption,

[

(see, e.g.
[

2

3

Typically the value of the adjoint state vector is not known explicitly, so it is natural to study an “abstract”

m(t;p,b,A):=p^{\top}e^{-At}b

(4)

p,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
The zeroes of the switching function are called the switching points.

Here, we consider the case where all the eigenvalues of the matrix

are purely imaginary. Our main contributions include the following:

•

We develop a new approach for analyzing the zeros of

using the classical, yet generally forgotten, problem of mean motion that was solved by Hermann Weyl in 1938
[
9
]
;

•

Using this new approach we show that

(5)

where

The next section reviews the mean motion problem including the Bohl–Weyl–Wintner (BWW) formula that allows to derive an explicit expression for

For the sake of completeness, we include more details

Section
III

The final section concludes, and describes some directions for further research.

II
Preliminaries

In this section, we
review several results

II-A

The problem of mean motion

The mean motion problem
goes back to Lagrange’s analysis of the secular perturbations of the major planets.
It provides a way to calculate the average rate at which an object orbits a central body.

Consider the complex function
z:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

z(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}e^{i(\lambda_{k}t+\mu_{k})},

(6)

where
a_{k},\lambda_{k},\mu_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that this may be interpreted as a weighted sum of linear oscillators with different frequencies.

Assume that
z(t)\not=0
for all
t\geq 0
.
Then
\Phi(t):=\arg(z(t))
is a continuous function.

Problem 1

(mean motion)
.

Determine whether the asymptotic angular velocity of
z(t)
, that is, the limit

\Omega:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t},

(7)

exists, and if so, find its value.

Example 1
.

Suppose that all
the
\lambda_{k}
s are equal, and we denote their common value by
\lambda
, then

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}e^{i(\lambda t+\mu_{k})}

\displaystyle=e^{i\lambda t}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}e^{i\mu_{k}},

so

\displaystyle\Phi(t)

\displaystyle=\arg(z(t))

\displaystyle=\lambda t+\arg(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}e^{i\mu_{k}}),

and
\Omega=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)}{t}=\lambda
.

II-B

Bessel functions

Bessel functions are ubiquitous in mathematics and physics
[
8
]
.
The
p
-order Bessel function
J_{p}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is defined by

J_{p}(x):=\frac{(x/2)^{p}}{\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2p}(\phi)e^{-ix\cos(\phi)}d\phi,

(8)

where
\Gamma
is the Gamma function. In the mean motion problem, we encounter two Bessel functions:

J_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-ix\cos(\phi)}d\phi,

and

J_{1}(x)=\frac{x}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}(\phi)e^{-ix\cos(\phi)}d\phi.

These functions appear
through their connection to

n
-dimensional balls and spheres. To explain this,
let
1_{B^{n}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
denote the indicator function of the
n
-dimensional ball, that is,
1_{B^{n}}(x)=1
if
|x|\leq 1
and
1_{B^{n}}(x)=0
, otherwise. The Fourier transform of
1_{B^{n}}
is

\displaystyle\hat{1}_{B^{n}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{|x|\leq 1}e^{ix^{\top}\xi}dx_{1}\dots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{|x|\leq 1}e^{-ix^{\top}\xi}dx_{1}\dots dx_{n}.

(9)

Since
1_{B^{n}}(x)
is a radial function (that is, it only depends only on
|x|
),

\hat{1}_{B^{n}}(\xi)
will depend on
|\xi|
(see, e.g.
[
5
, Chapter IV]
) and it is
enough to consider the particular
case where
\xi=\begin{bmatrix}0&\dots&0&r\end{bmatrix}^{\top}
,
with
r>0
. Then,

\displaystyle\hat{1}_{B^{n}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{|x|\leq 1}e^{-ix_{n}r}dx_{1}\dots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{n-1}}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\int_{-1}^{1}(\sqrt{1-x_{n}^{2}})^{n-1}e^{-ix_{n}r}dx_{n},

where
V_{n-1}
is the volume of the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit ball.
Setting
x_{n}=\cos(\phi)
and using the fact that
V_{d}=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(1+(d/2))}
gives

\displaystyle\hat{1}_{B^{n}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n/2}\Gamma(1+((n-1)/2))\sqrt{\pi}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{n}(\phi)e^{-i\cos(\phi)r}d\phi,

and comparing this with (
8
) yields

\hat{1}_{B^{n}}(\xi)=|\xi|^{-n/2}J_{n/2}(|\xi|).

In particular, for the special case where
n=2
, we have

\hat{1}_{B^{2}}(\xi)=|\xi|^{-1}J_{1}(|\xi|).

(10)

The Bohl–Weyl–Wintner formula

The Bohl–Weyl–Wintner (BWW) formula

Fix

Geometrically, this can be interpreted as
the position of the end-point of a multi-link robot arm where the
k
th link has length
|a_{k}|
, and the
angle between link
k

and link
(k+1)
is

(see Fig.
1
).

\mathrm{Re}
\mathrm{Im}

a_{2}

\phi_{2}
\;\;\;\;\arg(z(\phi))

The canonical (Haar) probability measure on the torus is

(11)

Let

\displaystyle W_{n}(r)=\int_{|z|\leq r}d\mu

For small values of

The next example demonstrates this.

Example 2
.

Consider the case

. Fix

.
We compute

To do so, we first determine

We have

\displaystyle|z|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(a_{1}+a_{2}\cos(\phi_{2})\big)^{2}+\big(a_{2}\sin(\phi_{2})\big)^{2}

II-D

Solution of the mean motion problem

Returning to Problem
1
,
we say that the
\lambda_{k}
s in (
6
)
are

non-resonant

if there are no non-trivial relations of the form

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\ell_{k}=0,\text{ where every }\ell_{k}\text{ is an integer}.

(16)

Hermann Weyl
[
9
]
proved that under this condition the mean motion
\Omega
in (
7
) exists, and satisfies

\Omega=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}V_{k},

(17)

where

V_{k}:=W_{n-1}(a_{k};a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},a_{k+1},\dots,a_{n}).

(18)

Furthermore,
the
V_{k}
s are non-negative, and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}V_{k}=1

[
9
]
.
In particular, if
\lambda_{k}>0
for all
k
then
\Omega>0
, and if all the
\lambda_{k}
s are equal to a common value
\lambda
then Eq. (
17
) gives

\Omega=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda V_{k}=\lambda.

Thus, the mean motion
\Omega
is a weighted average of the angular velocities
\lambda_{k}
.
The weight
V_{k}

corresponding to
\lambda_{k}

is the “volume” of the set of points
(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{k-1},\phi_{k},\dots,\phi_{n})

in an
(n-1)
-dimensional torus
such that
|{\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\not=k}}a_{\ell}e^{i\phi_{\ell}}|\leq|a_{k}|
.

.

Consider the case

, that is,

.

Note that combining

Remark 1
.

The BBW formula is a solution to a “static” problem of determining a “volume” of angles in the torus that satisfies a certain inequality. The mean motion problem is “dynamic”, as it considers the asymptotic behaviour of the time-dependent function
z(t)
. For the sake of completeness, we give an informal explanation on how these two problems are related.
Since
z(t)=|z(t)|e^{i\Phi(t)}
, we have
\Phi(t)=\operatorname{Im}(\log(z(t)))
, so

\displaystyle\dot{\Phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}(\frac{\dot{z}(t)}{z(t)})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}(\frac{\sum a_{k}\lambda_{k}ie^{i(\lambda_{k}t+\mu_{k})}}{\sum a_{k}e^{i(\lambda_{k}t+\mu_{k})}})

\displaystyle=\real(\frac{\sum a_{k}\lambda_{k}e^{i(\lambda_{k}t+\mu_{k})}}{\sum a_{k}e^{i(\lambda_{k}t+\mu_{k})}}).

This implies that we can view
\dot{\Phi}(t)
as a mapping from
(\lambda_{1}t,\dots,\lambda_{n}t)\in\mathbb{T}^{n}

to
\mathbb{R}
. Now,

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(t)-\Phi(0)}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\dot{\Phi}(s)ds,

(19)

and the non-resonance condition implies that we can calculate this integral
using a “static” integral over the torus.

.

Eq. (
17
) holds even if condition (

) is relaxed to the following almost non-resonancy condition: there are no non-trivial relations of the form

, where
the
\ell_{k}
s are integers, and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ell_{k}=0
. If
\lambda_{1}=\dots=\lambda_{n}

then the non-resonance condition fails, but the almost non-resonance condition holds.

Remark 3
.

Writing (

and noting that

implies that we can actually assume that
the

are complex numbers,
and the formula for

III
Main result

If the spectrum of
A
in (
1
) is real then it is well-known (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem 6-8]
) that
N(T)\leq n-1
for any
T>0
. We consider the case where all the eigenvalues of
A
are purely imaginary.

Assumption 1
.

The dimension
n
is even and the
eigenvalues of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are

i\lambda_{1},-i\lambda_{1},\dots,i\lambda_{n/2},-i\lambda_{n/2},

where all the
\lambda_{k}
s are real.

We can now state our main result.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
A
satisfies Assumption
1
and that the pair
(A,b)
is controllable.
Then, for generic vectors
p,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

there exists
c>0
such that
for any
T
large enough the number of zeros of the switching function
m
in (
4
) on the interval
[0,T]
satisfies

N(T)\geq cT+o(T).

The proof of this result, given below, can actually be used to
provide a closed-form
expression for
c
in terms of a solution to
the mean motion problem.

Proof:

We may assume that there exists a non-singular matrix
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that
A=H^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(B_{1},\dots,B_{n/2})H
, with

B_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda_{i}\\
-\lambda_{i}&0\end{bmatrix}.

The switching function (
4
)
becomes

\displaystyle m(t)

\displaystyle=p^{\top}H^{-1}\operatorname{diag}(\exp(B_{1}t),\dots,\exp(B_{n/2}t))Hb

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}c_{k}\cos(\lambda_{k}t)+d_{k}\sin(\lambda_{k}t),

where
c_{k},d_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
depend on the
entries of
b
,
p
, and
H
. Thus,

\displaystyle m(t)

\displaystyle=\real(\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}c_{k}e^{i\lambda_{k}t}+d_{k}e^{i(\lambda_{k}t-(\pi/2))})

\displaystyle=\real(\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}a_{k}e^{i\lambda_{k}t}),

with
a_{k}:=c_{k}+d_{k}e^{-i\pi/2}=c_{k}-id_{k}
.
Define

z(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{n/2}a_{k}e^{i\lambda_{k}t}.

Every time
t_{i}\geq 0
such that
\arg(z(t_{i}))=\pi/2
or
\arg(z(t_{i}))=3\pi/2

is a zero of the swicthing function
m(t)
. Consider the asymptotic
behavior of
\arg(z(t))
on the interval
[0,T]
, with
T
large.
The solution of the mean motion problem implies that

\ \Omega:=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{\arg(z(T))-\arg(z(0))}{T}

exists and satisfies (
17
). Thus, for
T
large enough the number of zeros of
m
on the interval
[0,T]

is bounded from below by

\frac{|\Omega|}{\pi}T+o(T),

and this completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

In this case,

\displaystyle m(t)

\displaystyle=p^{\top}e^{-At}b

\displaystyle=-p_{1}\sin(\zeta_{1}t)-p_{3}\sin(\zeta_{2}t)+p_{2}\cos(\zeta_{1}t)+p_{4}\cos(\zeta_{2}t).

This can be expressed as

\displaystyle m(t)

\displaystyle=\real(-p_{1}e^{i(\zeta_{1}t-(\pi/2))}-p_{3}e^{i(\zeta_{2}t-(\pi/2))}

\displaystyle+p_{2}e^{i\zeta_{1}t}+p_{4}e^{i\zeta_{2}t})

\displaystyle=\real(a_{1}e^{i\zeta_{1}t}+a_{2}e^{i\zeta_{2}t}),

with
a_{1}:=-p_{1}e^{-i\pi/2}+p_{2}
and
a_{2}:=-p_{3}e^{-i\pi/2}+p_{4}
.

For low values of
n
it is possible to give more accurate bounds
for
N(T)
and show that they agree with the asymptotic lower bound in Theorem
1
.
The next result demonstrates this.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the case where

\displaystyle m(t)=\real(a_{1}e^{i\lambda_{1}t}+a_{2}e^{i\lambda_{2}t}),

with
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,
and assume, without loss of generality, that

\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}.

If
a_{1}>a_{2}
then

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\pi}T\leq N(T)\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\pi}T+1,\text{ for all }T>0.

(20)

If
a_{1}<a_{2}
then

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\pi}T\leq N(T)\leq\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\pi}T,\text{ for all }T>0.

(21)

Proof:

A time
t_{i}
is zero of
m(t)
iff

\cos(\lambda_{2}t_{i})=-\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\cos(\lambda_{1}t_{i}).

Suppose that
a_{1}>a_{2}
.
Fix an integer
\ell\geq 0
, and consider the time interval

L_{1}:=[\frac{2\pi\ell}{\lambda_{1}},\frac{2\pi\ell}{\lambda_{1}}+\frac{\pi}{\lambda_{1}}].

On this time interval,
-\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\cos(\lambda_{1}t)
is monotonic, and attains all the values from
-a_{1}/a_{2}
to
a_{1}/a_{2}
and since
a_{1}>a_{2}
, we conclude that it intersects
\cos(\lambda_{2}t)
at least once. Also, since
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
there is exactly one such intersection, so
m(t)
has a single zero on
L_{1}
. A similar argument shows that
m(t)
has exactly one zero on

L_{2}:=[\frac{2\pi\ell}{\lambda_{1}}+\frac{\pi}{\lambda_{1}},\frac{2\pi(\ell+1)}{\lambda_{1}}].

Thus, there are two zeros of
m(t)
in every period of
\cos(\lambda_{1}t)
. This proves (
20
).

Now consider the case
a_{1}<a_{2}
. The upper bound on
N(T)
in (
21
) is obtained arguing similarly as above, but noting that now
|a_{1}/a_{2}|<1
.
To derive the lower bound,
fix an integer
\ell\geq 0
, and consider the time interval

\tilde{L}_{1}:=[\frac{2\pi\ell}{\lambda_{2}},\frac{2\pi\ell}{\lambda_{2}}+\frac{\pi}{\lambda_{2}}].

On this time interval,
\cos(\lambda_{2}t)
is monotonic, and attains all the values in the range
[-1,1]
. Since
-\frac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}\cos(\lambda_{1}t)
takes values inside the interval
(-1,1)
, there is at least one zero of
m(t)
in
\tilde{L}_{1}
. Arguing similarly as above on the interval

\tilde{L}_{2}:=[\frac{2\pi\ell}{\lambda_{2}}+\frac{\pi}{\lambda_{2}},\frac{2\pi(\ell+1)}{\lambda_{2}}]

gives the lower bound on
N(T)
in (
21
).
∎

Not that Proposition
1
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{2}}{\pi}T\leq N(T),\text{ for all }T>0,

and this agrees with the asymptotic
linear bound in Theorem
1
.

IV
Discussion

We studied the number of switching points
N(T)
in time-optimal controls of a single-input linear system on the interval
[0,T]
. We showed that when all the eigenvalues
of
A
are purely imaginary
the number of switching points
is lower-bounded by
cT
for large
T
.
By relating the question to the
solution of
the mean motion problem, we also provided
an explicit formula for
c
in terms of integrals of Bessel functions.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first connection between optimal control theory and the mean motion problem.

An interesting question is whether the
bound can be strengthened to

N(T)=\tilde{c}T+o(T),\text{ as }T\to\infty,

(22)

with an explicit positive parameter
\tilde{c}
.
The relation to the mean motion problem suggests that
\widetilde{c}=\frac{|\Omega|}{\pi}
, where
\Omega=\sum\lambda_{k}V_{k}
, and the probabilities

V_{k}:=W_{n-1}(a_{k};a_{1},\dots,a_{k-1},a_{k+1},\dots,a_{n})

are given by the BWW formula (
15
).
Another natural research direction is to extend the analysis to the case where
A
has a more general spectral structure.

Appendix: proof of the BWW formula

Let
\xi:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}e^{i\phi_{k}}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Fix
r>0
.
By definition,

W_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}1_{|\xi|\leq r}(\xi)\prod_{k=1}^{n}d\phi_{k}.

(23)

Writing

1_{|\xi|\leq r}(\xi)=1_{B^{2}}(\xi/r),

(24)

and applying an inverse Fourier transform to (
10
) yields

\displaystyle 1_{|\xi|\leq r}(\xi)

\displaystyle=1_{B^{2}}(\xi/r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\eta|^{-1}J_{1}(|\eta|)e^{i\operatorname{Re}(\bar{\eta}\xi/r)}d\eta.

Defining the integration variable

\theta:=\eta/r
gives

1_{|\xi|\leq r}(\xi)=\frac{r}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\theta|^{-1}J_{1}(r|\theta|)e^{i\operatorname{Re}(\bar{\theta}\xi)}d\theta.

(25)

Substituting this in (
23
) yields

\displaystyle W_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\theta|^{-1}J_{1}(r|\theta|)e^{i\operatorname{Re}(\bar{\theta}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}e^{i\phi_{k}})}d\theta

\displaystyle\times\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}d\phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\theta|^{-1}J_{1}(r|\theta|)

\displaystyle\times\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}e^{i\operatorname{Re}(\bar{\theta}a_{k}e^{i\phi_{k}})}d\phi_{k}\right)d\theta.

Write
\theta
in the polar representation
\theta=\rho e^{i\alpha}
, where
\rho:=|\theta|
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\operatorname{Re}(\bar{\theta}a_{k}e^{i\phi_{k}})}d\phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\operatorname{Re}(\rho a_{k}e^{i(\phi_{k}-\alpha)})}d\phi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\rho\operatorname{Re}(a_{k}e^{i\beta_{k}})}d\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\rho|a_{k}|\cos(\beta_{k})}d\beta_{k}

\displaystyle=J_{0}(\rho|a_{k}|).

Thus,

\displaystyle W_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}|\theta|^{-1}J_{1}(r|\theta|)\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}J_{0}(|\theta||a_{k}|)\right)d\theta.

The integrand

|\theta|^{-1}J_{1}(r|\theta|)\prod_{k=1}^{n}J_{0}(|\theta||a_{k}|)

is a radial function, and using known results on the integration of radial functions (see, e.g.,
[
6
, Chapter 6]
)
gives

\displaystyle W_{n}(r)

\displaystyle=r\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{1}(r\rho)\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}J_{0}(\rho|a_{k}|)\right)d\rho,

and this completes the proof of the BWW formula .
