Hepassocin prevents age-related liver senescence and facilitates liver regeneration by activating AMPK
Authors: Yang Yang, Hui Chen, Shensi Xiang, Yujia Wei, Limin Zhang, Aihua Sun, Xiao E, Fan Wu, Ning Luo, Fei Liang, Xiaojie Wu, Chenyu Wang, Zhuo Chen, Qizheng Zhang, Xinrui Chen, Qinlu Wu, Zixuan Han, Changyan Li, Ronghua Yin, Guangming Ren, Jingjing Li, Ke Zhao, Huiying Gao, Ruijia Wang, Miao Yu, Xiaoming Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liver aging significantly impairs hepatic function and regenerative capacity, increasing the risk of morbidity and mortality from chronic liver diseases. Identifying molecular regulators of these processes may reveal promising therapeutic targets. Although Hepassocin (HPS), a hepatokine with known hepatoprotective functions, has minimal effects on liver homeostasis in adult mice, its role in long-term liver maintenance remains unclear. In this study, we observed a decrease in circulating and intrahepatic HPS levels in both aged mice and elderly humans. Moreover, the upregulation of HPS following two-thirds partial hepatectomy (PHx) was significantly blunted in 12-month-old (aged) mice. Aged HPS-knockout (KO) mice exhibited variable hepatic steatosis, exacerbated cellular senescence, and impaired autophagy. Liver regeneration after PHx was severely compromised in aged HPS-KO mice, as indicated by increased mortality, reduced hepatocyte proliferation, delayed liver mass recovery, and worsened autophagy disruption. Mechanistically, HPS directly activated 5’-AMP-activated protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha-1 (AMPK) in hepatocytes via the Annexin A2 (ANXA2)-extracellular signal-regulated kinase 2–90 kDa ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1-liver kinase B1 (ANXA2-ERK-p90RSK-LKB1) signaling cascade. Compared with their wild-type littermates, aged HPS-KO mice presented reduced LKB1 and AMPK activation and elevated mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (mTOR) activity in both quiescent and regenerating livers. Treatment with the AMPK agonist AICAR ameliorated the liver aging phenotype and restored liver regenerative capacity in aged HPS-KO mice. Importantly, the administration of exogenous HPS enhanced regenerative outcomes in aged wild-type mice. These results establish HPS as a novel protective factor against liver senescence through AMPK-dependent mechanisms. Therapeutic strategies aimed at enhancing HPS signaling may offer a viable approach to counteract age-related liver dysfunction and regeneration failure.

Introduction

The liver plays a central role in energy metabolism, the detoxification of xenobiotics and endobiotics, molecular biosynthesis, and immune regulation. Liver disease causes about 1.2 million deaths annually, accounting for 3.5% of total deaths and affecting almost 1.5 billion people worldwide.1 Age-related alterations in hepatic structure and function, including reduced liver volume, increased dense body formation, dysregulated stress responses, downregulation of growth-regulatory genes, and impaired DNA repair, collectively contribute to a decline in functional capacity and increased susceptibility to liver diseases in elderly individuals.2 Globally, over 70% of new hepatocellular carcinoma cases and 80% of related deaths occur in individuals aged 55 years and older, with disease burden rising by 74% over recent decades.3 The prevalence of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease reaches ~50% among community-dwelling elderly Asian individuals in Taipei City (n = 1091; mean age 74.6 years).4 Moreover, individuals aged 60 years and older account for nearly 60% of drug-induced liver injury cases, with a 4–5-fold higher risk in those over 80, accompanied by a higher likelihood of progression to acute liver failure.2 The prevention and treatment of liver diseases in the elderly is becoming an important public health issue.

It is widely recognized that hepatocytes acquire a senescent phenotype with advancing age. While cellular senescence serves as a tumor-suppressive mechanism by limiting the proliferation of damaged cells, it also impairs liver function, promotes inflammation, and accelerates the progression of age-related hepatic pathologies such as cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver failure.5 For instance, metabolic dysregulation in senescent hepatocytes contributes to the increased incidence of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) in older individuals.6 Studies have shown that senescent hepatocytes exhibit markedly reduced regenerative capacity, which is correlated with significantly increased posthepatectomy mortality in patients over 70 years of age and in mice older than 12 months.7 Furthermore, senescent hepatocytes shape the tissue microenvironment through the secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors, which can induce secondary senescence in neighboring cells and exacerbate inflammation and tissue injury.8 In chronic liver diseases, persistent senescence fosters a microenvironment that supports hepatocarcinogenesis.8 Although the broad impact of cellular senescence on liver disease is generally acknowledged, the molecular mechanisms that specifically regulate age-related hepatocyte senescence remain incompletely understood.

Hepassocin (HPS), also known as fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1), is a hepatokine with pleiotropic functions, including the promotion of hepatocyte proliferation, metabolic regulation, and immunomodulation.9–13 Following acute liver injury, hepatocytes upregulate HPS expression under the control of transcription factors such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF-1α) and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3).14,15 Although the signaling pathways mediated by HPS have not been fully elucidated, existing studies indicate the presence of specific receptors for HPS on the hepatocyte membrane, which mediate hepatocyte proliferation through the epidermal growth factor receptor/tyrosine-protein kinase Src/extracellular signal-regulated kinase 2 (EGFR/Src/ERK) pathway.16 Moreover, several downstream pathways, including phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase/AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (PI3K/AKT), 5’-AMP-activated protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha-1/mechanistic target of rapamycin kinase (AMPK/mTOR), and STAT, have been reported to be associated with HPS function.17 HPS also exerts antiapoptotic effects by modulating the balance between pro- and antiapoptotic factors.13,18 Consequently, HPS confers protection against acute liver injury in multiple animal models by promoting hepatocyte proliferation and suppressing apoptosis.19,20 Genetic ablation of HPS exacerbates liver injury, underscoring its role as an endogenous cytoprotective factor.13 These properties suggest the therapeutic potential of HPS in promoting hepatic regeneration and repair. The HPS also participates in the metabolic response to liver injury.21 Hepatic metabolic stress not only provides energy support for damaged hepatocyte repair but also facilitates the metabolic remodeling of newly generated cells. HPS deficiency exacerbates two-thirds of partial hepatectomy (PHx)-induced lipid accumulation, indicating a role in regulating metabolic stress during regeneration.21 Previous works from our group have demonstrated that HPS inhibits lipid synthesis, promotes fatty acid oxidation, and improves lipid metabolism in both in vivo and in vitro models of MAFLD, suggesting that HPS may facilitate metabolic adaptation during liver repair.22 The observation that PHx upregulates hepatic HPS expression and that exogenous HPS stimulates hepatocyte proliferation after PHx further supports its involvement in liver regenerative processes.11,13 Notably, HPS loss has only minor effects on liver regeneration in young adult mice, likely because of compensatory mechanisms and redundancy among regenerative pathways.21 Intriguingly, HPS has been shown to inhibit mTOR activity in HCC models and suppress tumor development,23 suggesting that metabolic pathways may represent important downstream effectors of HPS signaling. On the basis of these findings, we hypothesized that HPS may play a critical role in mitigating liver aging and supporting regenerative capacity in aged individuals.

In this study, we investigated the contribution of HPS to liver aging and age-related regeneration deficits. We demonstrate that HPS deficiency accelerates liver aging and severely impairs regenerative capacity in aged mice. We further identified the maintenance of AMPK activity as a central mechanism through which HPS exerts its protective effects. These findings suggest that HPS is a promising therapeutic target for ameliorating age-related liver dysfunction and enhancing regenerative outcomes in elderly individuals.

Results

HPS ablation aggravates age-associated liver senescence

To determine whether HPS influences age-related liver dysfunction, we evaluated the effects of HPS ablation in 12-month-old (aged) HPS-knockout (KO) and wild-type (WT) mice under standard dietary conditions, which represent middle-aged stages. The successful deletion of HPS in liver tissue and adipose tissue was confirmed by Western blotting and qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Aged HPS-KO mice exhibited body weights comparable to those of their WT littermates and showed no overt phenotypic abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Critically, no spontaneous dermatitis or elevated levels of anti-double-stranded DNA or antinuclear antibodies, as previously reported,12 were detected in these HPS-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 1c, e, f), and peripheral blood and intrahepatic leukocyte composition remained normal (data not shown), indicating that immune dysregulation did not occur at this age. Both hepatic and plasma levels of HPS were reduced in aged mice, as determined by immunoblotting and ELISA (Fig. 1a–c). Similarly, ELISA revealed lower plasma HPS levels in older individuals than in younger individuals (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, analysis of a human hepatocellular carcinoma proteomics dataset24 revealed that HPS expression in adjacent nontumor tissues was significantly lower in subjects over 50 years of age than in those under 50 years of age (Fig. 1e). We found that HPS deficiency markedly accelerated age-related hepatic senescence, as demonstrated by increased lipofuscin accumulation (Fig. 1f), increased senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity (Fig. 1g), and upregulation of the senescence markers CDK4 inhibitor p16-INK4 (p16INK4a) and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (p21WAF1/Cip1) at the protein level (Fig. 1h), along with increased mRNA expression of SASP-related genes (Fig. 1i). Notably, adult HPS-KO mice appeared to lack an obvious liver aging phenotype, in addition to a mild increase in hepatic p21WAF1/Cip1 expression, suggesting that the loss of HPS may not be sufficient alone to drive liver aging in adult mice (Fig. 1f, h, i). Taken together, these results indicate that HPS loss promotes cellular senescence and SASP activation in the liver in an age-dependent manner.

HPS ablation aggravates age-induced liver dysfunction

Liver aging is a major contributor to metabolic syndrome, which is linked to insulin resistance and hepatic lipid accumulation.2 We found that the serum glucose and insulin levels did not significantly differ between the two groups of aged mice under fed and fasting conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h), but compared with their WT littermates, aged HPS KO mice were significantly more glucose intolerant and insulin resistant (Supplementary Fig. 1i, j), indicating that HPS deficiency causes systemic insulin resistance in aged mice. Consistently, compared with those in WT aged mice, AKT phosphorylation levels in the liver in chow-fed aged HPS-KO mice were markedly attenuated (Supplementary Fig. 1k). The serum triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol were not significantly different between the two lines of mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b), but the levels of hepatic TG (Fig. 1j), total cholesterol (Supplementary Fig. 2c), and free fatty acids (FFA) (Supplementary Fig. 2d) were elevated in HPS KO aged mice compared to their WT littermates, and the increased hepatic lipid accumulation in HPS KO aged mice was further demonstrated by histopathologic analyses with Oil Red O staining (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Analysis of key lipid metabolism genes in the liver revealed that hepatic lipid accumulation in aged HPS-KO mice likely results from enhanced fatty acid synthesis coupled with reduced fatty acid oxidation (Fig. 1k). Sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c), a key transcription factor that regulates lipid synthesis, is synthesized as an inactive precursor anchored in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane with SREBP cleavage activation protein (SCAP) and undergoes increased cleavage and activation under ER stress.25 We demonstrated that HPS deficiency induced severe hepatic ER stress, upregulated SCAP expression, and decreased the protein levels of insulin-induced gene 1 (INSIG1) and INSIG2 (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). INSIG1 and INSIG2 are established inhibitors of SREBP-1c proteolytic processing. Consistent with these findings, hepatic nuclear extracts from aged HPS-KO mice presented elevated levels of mature SREBP-1c and increased SREBP-1c DNA-binding activity (Supplementary Fig. 2h, i). These data indicate that HPS deficiency enhances SREBP-1c activity by promoting its cleavage in aged mice. Serum ALT levels were mildly but significantly elevated (Supplementary Fig. 2j), indicating minimal hepatocellular injury. Together, these findings confirm that HPS ablation exacerbates aging-induced liver dysfunction.

HPS deficiency promotes the aging-induced suppression of hepatic autophagy

We investigated the liver ultrastructure of 12-month-old mice by transmission electron microscopy. Representative images are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a. We noted that hepatocytes from aged HPS-KO mice exhibited marked changes in ER and mitochondrial morphology. Notably, we observed an increase in the number of autophagic vacuoles in the HPS-KO liver compared with that in the WT liver, suggesting that autophagic flux in the liver of aged HPS-KO mice is disturbed. Consistent with these findings, we demonstrated that compared with WT mice, livers from aged HPS-KO mice presented reduced conversion of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3β (MAP1LC3B/LC3B-I) to LC3B-II and increased accumulation of sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1/p62) (Fig. 2a). Moreover, the expression of Atg7, an essential autophagy gene that mediates LC3-phosphatidylethanolamine conjugation, decreased in aged HPS-KO livers (Supplementary Fig. 3b). QPCR revealed no significant change in p62 mRNA expression, indicating that p62 protein accumulation resulted from impaired degradation (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Chloroquine (CQ) treatment elevated LC3B-II levels in the livers of both genotypes, but the increase was lower in aged HPS-KO mice than in aged WT mice (Fig. 2a). In contrast, CQ completely abolished the difference in p62 levels between aged HPS WT and HPS-KO livers, confirming that p62 accumulation in HPS-KO mice was due to autophagy impairment (Fig. 2a). To determine whether these changes in whole-liver extracts reflect the status of parenchymal cells, we isolated hepatocytes from WT and HPS-KO mice, transfected them with an Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B adenovirus, and cultured the cells in Earle’s balanced salt solution (EBSS). Confocal microscopy revealed increased yellow fluorescence (indicating accumulated autophagosomes) and decreased red fluorescence (reflecting fewer autolysosomes) in HPS-KO hepatocytes, confirming impaired autophagosome‒lysosome fusion and degradation (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). Furthermore, the mRNA expression of multiple autophagy-related genes, including Atg5, Atg7, Atg12, Atg13, Ulk1, and LC3b, was significantly suppressed in aged HPS-KO livers (Supplementary Fig. 3c), further supporting the requirement of HPS for functional autophagy in aged livers.

HPS deficiency significantly impairs hepatectomy-induced liver regeneration in aging mice

Diminished regenerative capacity is a hallmark of liver aging.7 To assess the role of HPS in age-related liver regeneration, we performed a standard two-thirds PHx in 3-month-old (adult) and 12-month-old (aged) HPS KO and WT mice. Following PHx, plasma and hepatic HPS levels were significantly elevated in adult WT mice, as measured by ELISA, qRT‒PCR and western blotting, whereas this response was markedly blunted in aged mice (Fig. 2b–d). All the adult mice, regardless of their genotype, survived to 7 days post-PHx. With respect to aged mice, WT mice exhibited 100% survival at 72 h and 87% survival at 7 days after PHx, whereas the survival rates of HPS-KO mice were substantially reduced to 60% at 72 h and 40% at 7 days (Fig. 2e). The liver-to-body mass ratio did not differ between adult HPS-KO and WT mice from 24 to 72 h post-PHx. However, in aged mice, the liver-to-body mass ratio at 36 h after PHx was 2.0% in HPS-KO mice versus 2.5% in WT mice, and by 72 h, it reached 2.5% in KO mice compared with 3.4% in WT mice. These values corresponded to 50% and 64% recovery of the original liver mass in aged HPS-KO and WT mice, respectively, at 72 h post-PHx (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Furthermore, the quantification of bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU)-, Ki67-, or proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA)-positive hepatocytes revealed a dramatic decrease in cell proliferation in aged HPS-KO mice from 24 to 72 h after PHx (Fig. 2f & Supplementary Fig. 4b). Compared with those in WT controls, the increase in cyclin D (CCND) and cyclin A2 (CCNA) protein levels as well as PCNA protein levels induced by PHx was attenuated in HPS-KO regenerating livers (Supplementary Fig. 4c). These findings indicate that HPS is essential for liver regeneration induced by PHx in aged mice. The levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in serum (Supplementary Fig. 4d) and the mRNA expression of c-fos and c-myc in the liver (Supplementary Fig. 4e) did not differ between aged WT and HPS KO mice at 1 h after PHx, indicating that the early response to PHx is unaffected by HPS deficiency in aged animals.

Autophagy is essential for liver regeneration. Aged HPS-KO mice displayed blunted PHx-induced autophagy, as evidenced by reduced MAP1LC3B-II/I ratios, increased p62 accumulation (Fig. 2g), and downregulated expression of autophagy-related genes, including Atg5, Atg7, Atg12, Atg13, ULK1, and LC3b (Supplementary Fig. 4f). Furthermore, CQ treatment was used to confirm that the lower LC3II/LC3I ratio and increased p62 accumulation observed in the HPS-KO group were due to impaired autophagic flux (Supplementary Fig. 4g). PHx-induced transient regeneration-associated steatosis (TRAS) contributes to liver regeneration.26 We demonstrated that PHx-induced TRAS was exacerbated in aged HPS-KO livers, with elevated expression of key lipogenic genes and suppressed expression of key FAO-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). Notably, the upregulation of several PPARα-targeted genes was markedly attenuated in aged HPS-KO regenerating livers (Supplementary Fig. 5d). These data indicate that HPS is involved in the regulation of hepatic lipid metabolism following PHx in aged mice. At 36 h post-PHx, compared with aged WT mice, aged HPS-deficient mice showed no significant differences in inflammatory infiltration or necrosis, as determined by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining or serum ALT/AST levels (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). However, hepatocyte apoptosis was elevated in the regenerating livers of aged HPS-KO mice, as evidenced by increased levels of cleaved caspase-3 and a greater number of TUNEL-positive hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 5e, g, h).

Deficiency in HPS leads to LKB1/AMPK hypoactivation and mTOR hyperactivation in aged quiescent and regenerative livers

Since AMPK is involved in age-associated liver dysfunction, autophagy, and lipid metabolism,27 we investigated whether HPS deficiency affects AMPK signaling in the aged liver. We found that neither aging nor HPS deficiency significantly affected total AMPK protein expression; however, the phosphorylation levels of AMPK at Thr172, as well as those of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E) (two targets of AMPK), were significantly decreased in the livers of both genotypes of aged mice, with a greater extent in the livers of HPS-KO mice, suggesting that HPS deficiency results in reduced AMPK activation in aged mice (Fig. 3a). Accordingly, the phosphorylation of ULK1 at the AMPK-dependent activation site Ser555 was partially inhibited in aged WT livers but almost completely attenuated in aged HPS-KO livers. Compared with those in control livers, the phosphorylation levels of mTOR at Ser2448 and ULK1 at the mTOR-dependent activation site Ser757, as well as ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K), were increased in aged HPS-KO livers, indicating that HPS deficiency increases mTOR activity in aged livers (Fig. 3a). AMPK is activated mainly by the tumor suppressor kinase liver kinase B1 (LKB1) under hepatic metabolic stress conditions.28 We found that LKB1 phosphorylation at Ser431 was significantly weakened in aged HPS-KO livers (Fig. 3a). Collectively, these data indicate that HPS deficiency leads to LKB1/AMPK hypoactivation and mTOR hyperactivation in the aged liver. Intrahepatic ATP levels remained comparable between genotypes, excluding energy deficit as a contributing factor to LKB1/AMPK hypoactivation (Supplementary Fig. 6a). We further confirmed that HPS deficiency decreased AMPK activity and increased mTOR activity in the aged regenerative liver (Fig. 3b). AMPK was rapidly activated in the regenerative liver of adult WT and HPS-KO mice, but this activation was significantly diminished in aged WT mice but further decreased in aged HPS-KO mice, suggesting that HPS plays an important role in the activation of AMPK during liver regeneration in aged mice. Phosphorylation of S6K was increased in aged HPS-KO regenerative livers compared with control livers following PHx at 12 and 24 h, indicating that mTOR activation and increased protein translation occurred. The observed reduction in ULK1 phosphorylation at Ser555 (an AMPK-dependent site) coupled with increased phosphorylation at Ser757 (an mTOR-dependent site) further indicated an imbalance in AMPK/mTOR signaling and underscores autophagy dysregulation in aged HPS-KO regenerating livers. The transcription factor EB (TFEB), a master regulator of autophagy and lysosomal gene expression, is phosphorylated and inhibited by mTORC1, leading to its cytoplasmic retention. We observed an increase in phosphorylation at Ser211 of TFEB (an mTOR-dependent site) in aged HPS-KO livers compared with WT livers. Following PHx, significant TFEB dephosphorylation occurred in both the regenerating HPS-KO and WT livers; however, the extent of TFEB dephosphorylation was lower in the HPS-KO liver. Similarly, nuclear translocation of TFEB was markedly reduced in regenerating HPS-KO livers (Supplementary Fig. 6b). These results further substantiate the impairment of autophagy in the livers of aged HPS-KO mice. Given the absence of a defective liver regeneration phenotype in adult HPS-KO mice, we compared key liver regeneration signaling pathways between adult HPS-KO and WT mice. The results revealed that regenerating livers from HPS-KO mice displayed reduced phosphorylation of ERK but elevated phosphorylation of STAT3 and calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase β (CaMKKβ) (Fig. 3c). In contrast, the activation states of AMPK, AKT, STAT5 and STAT6 remained unaltered (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Since both STAT3 and the CaMKKβ/AMPKα1 axis play important roles in regeneration,30,31 these findings suggest that compensatory activation of these pathways may underlie the preserved regenerative capacity in adult HPS-KO mice.

HPS activates AMPK in hepatocytes via the ANXA2-ERK-p90RSK-LKB1 pathway

We further examined whether HPS directly activated AMPK in hepatocytes. Western blot analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in AMPK phosphorylation in primary cultured mouse hepatocytes at 60 min after treatment with HPS protein (Fig. 4a). Similar results were obtained in HepG2 cells (Fig. 4b). Moreover, treatment of primary cultured hepatocytes with HPS increased AMPK phosphorylation in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, HPS treatment rapidly induced LKB1 phosphorylation in primary cultured hepatocytes (Fig. 4c). Since HPS activates ERK in hepatocytes,13 and since ERK is reported to promote LKB1 activation via 90 kDa ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1 (p90RSK),32 we thus examined ERK and p90RSK phosphorylation following HPS treatment. We assessed the time course of HPS-induced phosphorylation in primary hepatocytes and reported that HPS rapidly activated ERK (5 min), followed by p90RSK (15 min), LKB1 (30–120 min), and ultimately AMPK and ACC (30–120 min) (Fig. 4c). Notably, the ERK inhibitor U0126 abolished p90RSK, LKB1, AMPK and ACC phosphorylation in HPS-stimulated hepatocytes (Fig. 4c), confirming the dependence on ERK. LKB1 was enriched in the cytosol and coimmunoprecipitated strongly with AMPK in HPS-treated hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). Pharmacological inhibition of LKB1, but not CaMKK-β, blocked HPS-induced AMPK phosphorylation, further supporting LKB1 as the primary upstream kinase for AMPK activation in hepatocytes following HPS stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 7c). These findings were validated in LKB1-knockdown hepatocytes. While LKB1 knockdown did not affect the HPS-induced activation of ERK or p90RSK, it markedly reduced the phosphorylation of AMPK and ACC triggered by HPS (Supplementary Fig. 7d). Similarly, the p90RSK inhibitor SL0101 impaired HPS-triggered LKB1 and AMPK phosphorylation, whereas PKA inhibition had no effect (Supplementary Fig. 7c), indicating p90RSK dependence. To confirm the presence of a dedicated hepatocyte membrane receptor, we conducted ¹²⁵I-HPS binding assays. ¹²⁵I-HPS retained biological activity equivalent to that of native HPS in stimulating hepatocyte proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Saturation binding analysis revealed typical saturation curves for the binding of ¹²⁵I-HPS to both cultured mouse hepatocytes and human hepatoma cells (Fig. 4d, e). Unlabeled HPS dose-dependently displaced the bound ¹²⁵I-HPS, whereas neither EGF nor HGF could displace HPS binding even at 1000-fold excess concentrations (Fig. 4f). These results demonstrate the presence of specific HPS receptors on hepatocyte membranes that exhibit high affinity, specificity, reversibility, and saturability.

Using pull-down coupled with mass spectrometry, we identified Annexin A2 (ANXA2) as a candidate HPS-interacting protein (Supplementary Fig. 8b). ANXA2 was selected for further investigation because of its established function as a membrane receptor that mediates EGFR/ERK activation,33 which aligns with our previous observations.16 The interaction between HPS and ANXA2 was confirmed by coimmunoprecipitation and by their colocalization at the hepatocyte membrane (Supplementary Fig. 8c, d). Functionally, knockdown of ANXA2 impaired HPS binding (Fig. 4g) and attenuated HPS-induced phosphorylation of ERK, p90RSK, LKB1, and AMPK (Fig. 4h). These results collectively suggest that ANXA2 may act as a mediator of HPS-dependent AMPK activation.

AMPK reactivation rescues hepatocyte senescence and restores aged liver regenerative capacity

To further assess whether AMPK activation defects contribute to age-associated liver dysfunction in HPS-KO mice, we tested whether AMPK reactivation could reverse aging-associated liver dysfunction and impaired regeneration. Western blot analysis revealed that treating aged HPS-KO mice with the AMPK agonist AICAR (200 mg/kg, i.p., twice daily for 7 days) did not affect the phosphorylation status of ERK, p90RSK, or LKB1 in aged HPS-KO livers. In contrast, it increased the levels of phosphorylated AMPK and ACC but decreased the level of phosphorylated S6K (Supplementary Fig. 9a). This effect was accompanied by suppressed p16INK4a and p21WAF1/Cip1 protein expression, decreased lipofuscin accumulation, and downregulated SASP-related gene mRNA levels (Fig. 5a–c). Moreover, AICAR treatment dose-dependently enhanced liver regeneration in aged HPS-KO mice after 70% PHx, as demonstrated by increased hepatocyte proliferation, as determined by the quantification of BrdU- and PCNA-positive hepatocytes (Fig. 5d), and improved liver mass recovery at 36 h and 72 h post-PHx (Fig. 5e). Accordingly, AICAR administration markedly improved the survival of aged HPS-KO mice post-PHx (Fig. 5f). AICAR treatment also reversed the autophagy impairment observed in the regenerating livers of aged HPS-KO mice (Fig. 5g, h). When autophagic flux was inhibited by CQ, the AICAR-induced increase in hepatocyte proliferation in aged HPS-KO mice following PHx was partially suppressed, as reflected by reduced numbers of PCNA-positive nuclei and lower levels of PCNA and Cyclin A2 proteins, indicating that impaired autophagy, resulting from low AMPK activity, contributes to the poor liver regeneration observed in aged HPS-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 9b, c). Exogenous HPS enhances liver regeneration in aged mice

To determine the potential application value of HPS, we examined whether exogenous HPS administration mitigates liver senescence and enhances regeneration in aged mice. Daily administration of HPS (1 mg/kg) for 7 days increased the PHx-induced phosphorylation of ERK, p90RSK, LKB1, AMPK, and ACC in aged WT livers (Fig. 6a). Furthermore, daily intraperitoneal injections of recombinant HPS at two doses (1 mg/kg and 2 mg/kg) for 7 days dose-dependently increased hepatocyte proliferation in aged WT mice at 36 h post-PHx, as quantified by BrdU and PCNA staining (Fig. 6b), and improved liver mass recovery at both 36 and 72 h after PHx (Fig. 6c). Importantly, HPS treatment dose-dependently increased the survival rate of aged HPS-KO mice at 7 days post-PHx (Fig. 6d). Compared with vehicle treatment, HPS treatment (1 mg/kg) reduced hepatic lipofuscin accumulation, suppressed p16INK4a and p21WAF1/Cip1 protein levels, and downregulated SASP-related gene expression in aged HPS-KO mice, indicating that liver senescence was attenuated (Fig. 6e–g). The pro-regenerative effect of HPS was accompanied by restored hepatic autophagy at 36 h post-PHx in both aged WT and HPS-KO mice (Fig. 6h & Supplementary Fig. 10). Discussion

This study demonstrated a critical role for HPS in protecting against age-related liver dysfunction and impaired regeneration. We showed that HPS deficiency accelerated liver aging, manifested as increased cellular senescence, metabolic dysregulation, impaired autophagy, and decreased regenerative capacity. The age-dependent decline in both circulating and hepatic HPS levels suggests that its loss may contribute to natural liver aging and compromised repair. Mechanistically, we revealed that HPS activated AMPK in hepatocytes via the ANXA2-ERK-p90RSK-LKB1 pathway, which is essential for maintaining metabolic and autophagic homeostasis and regeneration in aged livers under both steady-state and regenerative conditions. In contrast, adult HPS-KO mice exhibit normal AMPK activity and liver regeneration. This preserved regeneration correlated with elevated activation of CaMKKβ and STAT3 in adult HPS-KO livers. Since the CaMKKβ/AMPKα1 axis mediates Ca²⁺-dependent regenerative responses34 and AMPK can be activated independently of LKB1 during liver regeneration,35 together with the well-established pro-regenerative function of STAT3,36 the upregulation of these compensatory pathways likely maintains hepatic AMPK activity and supports regeneration in adult HPS-KO mice following PHx. A similar compensatory mechanism may also explain why HPS knockout alone does not induce liver aging in adult animals. Our findings thus link HPS to the regulation of nutrient-sensing pathways and autophagy under hepatic stress, providing new insight into age-related liver dysfunction. Importantly, exogenous HPS administration ameliorated senescence and restored regenerative capacity in aged livers, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting HPS signaling to improve liver outcomes in elderly individuals.

AMPK signaling, a central nutrient-sensing system that decreases with age in the liver, normally reduces oxidative stress, enhances autophagy, limits inflammation, and regulates metabolism when activated.37 We found that HPS deficiency led to AMPK hypoactivation in aged and regenerating livers. Consequently, the phosphorylation of key AMPK downstream metabolic regulators, including ACC, eIF4E, and S6K, decreased, indicating that HPS regulates age-associated metabolic reprogramming in aged livers. AMPK phosphorylates ACC to inhibit fatty acid synthesis and promote mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation.38 In aged HPS-KO livers, we observed increased lipid accumulation and synthesis, along with decreased fatty acid oxidation. This elevated lipid accumulation exacerbates lipotoxicity, likely accelerating liver aging and impairing regeneration. AMPK also influences aging by inhibiting mTOR, which regulates cell growth, protein synthesis, autophagy, and insulin sensitivity.39 We detected increased phosphorylation of mTOR (Ser2448), ULK1 (Ser757), and S6K in aged HPS-KO livers, reflecting mTOR hyperactivation. Autophagy, which is essential for hepatocyte metabolic homeostasis, organelle clearance, and protein turnover,40 was dysregulated in aged HPS-KO livers. HPS deficiency reduced ULK1 phosphorylation at Ser555 (AMPK-dependent) and increased ULK1 phosphorylation at Ser757 (mTOR-dependent), confirming that AMPK/mTOR imbalance disrupts autophagy. Such dysregulation is closely linked to ER stress, oxidative stress, and steatosis, all of which contribute to cellular senescence. Upon PHx, AMPK is rapidly activated and contributes to TNF-α- and HGF-mediated regenerative responses.41,42 In aged HPS-KO mice post-PHx, hepatic AMPK phosphorylation was markedly impaired, accompanied by high mortality, delayed mass recovery, suppressed Cyclin E1 and Cyclin A2 expression, impaired autophagy, and disrupted eNOS signaling. The interplay between AMPK and Cyclin A2/eNOS in liver regeneration in HPS-KO mice aligns with prior reports linking AMPK to HGF-mediated regenerative signaling.41 Taken together, these findings suggest that HPS prevents liver aging and maintains regenerative capacity largely by promoting cell cycle progression, regulating metabolic reprogramming, and sustaining autophagy, all of which are closely tied to AMPK activity. Reactivating AMPK with AICAR in aged HPS-KO mice reduced liver senescence and restored regenerative capacity, confirming that AMPK is the central mediator of the antiaging and proregenerative functions of HPS. The finding that blocking autophagy significantly but incompletely attenuated the pro-regenerative effect of AICAR highlights autophagy impairment as a key cause of poor liver regeneration in HPS-KO mice and further suggests that HPS acts through both autophagy-dependent and autophagy-independent mechanisms.

LKB1 is the principal upstream kinase responsible for phosphorylating AMPK in the liver under various stimuli.43 We showed that HPS-dependent AMPK activation requires LKB1, as HPS induces LKB1 phosphorylation, promotes its cytosolic translocation, and enhances its interaction with AMPK, whereas inhibition of LKB1, but not CaMKKβ, attenuated HPS-induced AMPK activation. Furthermore, HPS treatment triggered the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and the subsequent activation of p90RSK, a direct ERK substrate that phosphorylates LKB1 at Ser431. Inhibiting either ERK1/2 or p90RSK impaired the HPS-stimulated phosphorylation of both LKB1 and AMPK. These results reveal a noncanonical signaling pathway that links HPS to LKB1/AMPK activation.36 Using HPS pull-down coupled with mass spectrometry and classical ligand binding assays, we confirmed that ANXA2 likely functions as a membrane-associated receptor that mediates HPS signaling. ANXA2 is a calcium-dependent phospholipid-binding protein that forms a heterotetrameric complex with S100A10 at the cell surface and participates in various membrane-related events, including serving as a receptor for tissue plasminogen activator (tPA).44,45 Notably, ANXA2 mediates tPA-induced activation of the ERK1/2 pathway via EGFR,32 a mechanism analogous to our present findings.16 ANXA2 is also recognized as a key marker of migratory hepatocytes in both humans and mice.46 Knockdown of ANXA2 impaired the HPS-induced phosphorylation of ERK, p90RSK, LKB1, and AMPK in hepatocytes, underscoring its essential role in HPS signaling. Moreover, we ruled out the possibility that HPS deficiency affects AMPK activity by altering cellular ATP levels. Taken together, our data demonstrate that HPS activates AMPK through an ANXA2-ERK-p90RSK-LKB1 signaling axis.

The age-associated decrease in HPS levels and its correlation with reduced hepatic AMPK activation provide a mechanistic link to the progression of liver dysfunction and impaired regeneration in aged animals. AMPK reactivation can rescue phenotypes in aged HPS-KO mice, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of targeting the HPS/AMPK axis. Notably, HPS administration restored liver senescence markers and regenerative capacity in aged WT animals, indicating that the aged liver remains responsive to HPS and that increasing serum HPS levels could mitigate liver aging and regeneration deficits. We demonstrated that both circulating and intrahepatic HPS levels were decreased in elderly humans, indicating that recombinant HPS represents a promising therapeutic candidate for elderly patients undergoing liver resection or transplantation, where hepatic regenerative capacity is critical. Importantly, nonselective systemic AMPK activation may cause adverse effects, such as promoting cardiac hypertrophy, which could limit its therapeutic utility.47,48 Because HPS appears to selectively activate hepatic ERK1/2,13 it may offer a safety advantage over broad-spectrum AMPK agonists. Another benefit is that HPS targets multiple pathways simultaneously to promote protective senescence responses and combat liver disease.13,18,19,22 Our preliminary pharmacokinetic study revealed that HPS was rapidly absorbed (at a peak plasma concentration of 0.25 h), with a half-life of ~1.57 h. The subcutaneous bioavailability of HPS reached 100%.20 These data suggest a favorable pharmacokinetic profile for therapeutic use. Initial toxicity assessments indicated that a high dose (20× the effective dose) or daily administration (1 mg/kg for 7 days) caused no detectable adverse effects.20 Moreover, sustained HPS overexpression via AAV8 in mice for 2 months, resulting in high circulating levels, also induced no detectable toxicity (data not shown). These data demonstrate that HPS is well tolerated in mice.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that HPS plays a crucial role in protecting against liver aging and preserving regenerative capacity in aged mice through the activation of AMPK signaling. These results highlight the potential of the HPS pathway as a novel therapeutic target for age-related liver disorders, providing a rationale for strategies aimed at enhancing liver regeneration and alleviating age-associated hepatic dysfunction. Future studies should focus on elucidating the detailed mechanisms through which HPS modulates AMPK activity and its downstream effects on autophagy, metabolism, and regenerative processes, thereby paving the way for the development of HPS-based interventions against liver aging and related pathologies.

Materials and methods

More information about the experimental procedures is provided in the Supplementary Materials and methods.

Ethics statements

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Beijing Institute of Radiation Medicine and complied with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (IACUC-DWZX-2023-P711). Human plasma samples were obtained from the General Hospital of Xinjiang Military Command. Patient information is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC-2025RR0301) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Animal care and treatments

Total-body HPS-null mice on a C57BL/6J background were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system as previously described.22 All the mice were maintained in a temperature- and light-controlled environment with free access to food and water. For the PHx experiments, the mice were anesthetized and subjected to mid-ventral laparotomy with ~70% PHx according to the classical Higgins-Anderson model.49 Control animals underwent mid-ventral laparotomy without liver manipulation (sham treatment). The animals were sacrificed at the indicated time points after surgery. Blood was collected, and the remaining liver lobes were removed, weighed, and processed for analysis. In the HPS rescue experiments, recombinant HPS protein (1 mg·kg⁻¹ or 2 mg·kg⁻¹) was administered by intraperitoneal injection daily for 7 consecutive days before the scheduled hepatectomy. PHx was performed 24 h after the final injection. For the AICAR rescue experiments, AICAR (100 mg·kg⁻¹ or 200 mg·kg⁻¹) (MCE; HY-13417) was injected intraperitoneally twice daily for 7 days before PHx, which was conducted 24 h after the last dose. Chloroquine (50 mg∙kg-1) (MCE; HY-17589A) was administered to the mice via intraperitoneal injection to inhibit autophagic flux.

Measurement of plasma FGL1 levels

Human peripheral blood was collected by venipuncture, centrifuged to isolate the plasma, and stored at −80 °C. The plasma HPS (FGL1) levels were quantified using a Human HPS/HFREP-1 ELISA Kit (Invitrogen, EH239RB), with assay diluent (PBS supplemented with 10% FBS) as a negative control. The plasma HPS (FGL1) levels of mice were measured using a mouse FGL1 ELISA Kit (Cusabio; CSB-EL008653MO).

Cell culture and treatments

Mouse primary hepatocytes were isolated by a two-step collagenase perfusion method and cultured as described previously.50 The human hepatoma cell line HepG2 and the mouse hepatocyte line AM12 were obtained from the Cell Center of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing) and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Pharmacological treatments included 20 µM U0126 (ERK inhibitor, HY-12031, MCE), 50 µM radicicol (LKB1 inhibitor, HY-N6769, MCE), 10 µM SL0101-1 (p90RSK inhibitor, HY-15237, MCE), 20 µM H-89 (PKA inhibitor HY-15979, MCE), and 20 µM STO-609 (CaMKK inhibitor, MCE). The ANXA2 siRNA pool (sc-270151; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and LKB1 siRNA pool (sc-35817; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were transfected into AML12 cells with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
