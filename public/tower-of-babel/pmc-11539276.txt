E-dengue System Insights: Exploring the Factors Influencing Dengue-related Deaths in an Urbanized State in a Low-Middle Income Country (LMIC)
Authors: Farah Khalida Abdul Rahman, Sharifa Ezat binti Wan Puteh, Mohamad Azfar bin Zainuddin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue has emerged as a rapidly escalating health issue in low- and middle-income countries, with its burden and geographic spread increasing over the years. Malaysia, in particular, has witnessed a significant rise in dengue cases, accompanied by a spike in mortality rates. Several studies have identified various factors, primarily focusing on the 27 clinical aspects of severe dengue infection and the development of dengue-related fatalities. Expanding on this focus, this study aims to identify the demographic, clinical, and environmental factors contributing to dengue mortality, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the variables influencing dengue-related fatalities.MethodsThis study utilized a 1:2 case-control design, analyzing data from the E-dengue system database and medical records from January 2015 to December 2022, involving 219 participants (73 dengue fatalities as cases and 146 recovered patients as controls). Dengue deaths were confirmed by the Penang State Mortality Review Committee, and controls were randomly selected from laboratory-confirmed dengue cases. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, including descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression to identify predictors of dengue mortality, with variables included in the multivariable model if p < 0.05.ResultsSeveral significant predictors of dengue-related mortality, including clinical and environmental factors were identified. Key predictors were a platelet count below 50,000/µL (OR 15.70; 95% CI: 5.65–43.53), presence of one comorbid condition (OR 2.90; 95% CI: 1.22–6.90), more than two comorbid conditions (OR 10.15; 95% CI: 3.53–29.23), bronchial asthma (OR 12.00; 95% CI: 1.08-132.13), and outbreak locality status (OR 2.3; 95% CI: 1.11–4.79). An interaction was also found between locality status and platelet levels.ConclusionThe study emphasizes the need for developing risk profiles for dengue patients by integrating factors such as platelet levels, comorbidities, and locality status to improve clinical care. Nuanced protocols are needed to address the specific challenges of single-case and outbreak areas. In single-case localities, patients with low platelet counts (below 100,000/µL) should be prioritized for rapid intervention to mitigate severe outcomes. In outbreak areas, healthcare systems should bolster resources and apply comprehensive triage approaches considering platelet levels and other risk factors. Implementing predictive models that account for geographical factors can enhance resource allocation and preparedness for dengue outbreaks. These recommendations aim to empower public health personnel, healthcare providers, and communities to collectively reduce dengue-related mortality rates.Keywords: Dengue, Mortality, Death, Factors, Outbreak, Comorbidities

Background

Dengue fever (DF) is a flaviviral infection transmitted primarily by the Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes, which are common in the tropical and subtropical parts of the world [1]. The highest dengue infection rates are observed in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, with Asia bearing 70% of the disease burden [2]. It is one of the significant vector-borne diseases affecting humans, mainly in terms of worldwide morbidity and death [3]. As a result of geographical expansion into new nations and, more recently, a transition from urban to rural settings, the incidence of dengue fever has increased by a factor of 30 over the past 50 years with approximately 100 to 400 million cases occurring annually worldwide [4].

Dengue cases have markedly risen in Malaysia, a low- and middle-income country, particularly along the west coast. A total of 43,619 dengue cases and 28 fatalities were recorded as of 20 May 2023 (Epi week 20) compared to 16,144 cases and 9 fatalities in 2022. Additionally, 170% and 211% rise in dengue cases and deaths have been recorded over the same period in 2023. Following Selangor, Penang currently has among the highest number of daily reported cases in northern Malaysia with 4,146 cases and five deaths have been recorded in 2023 compared to 195 cases and no deaths that were recorded over the same period in 2022 [5].

Dengue fever may initially appear as flu-like symptoms, but in some circumstances, it may progress to a serious and potentially fatal illness that results in severe bleeding, organ malfunction, and even death [6]. Apart from supportive treatments, there is currently no cure for dengue infection. The high prevalence of dengue fever as well as its possible complications and mortality have placed a significant strain on the national healthcare system. During dengue epidemics, critical resources such as time, hospital beds, human resources, and funds are directed toward optimizing dengue management [7]. Dengue fever also has a substantial financial impact on both infected people and affected countries, particularly as the illness typically affects 77.5% of young adults who are more likely to be working and are the breadwinners for their families [8]. Therefore, surveillance of dengue infection is vital to prevent and manage infections, identify outbreaks, and assess the impact of interventions.

The case fatality rate (CFR) for dengue could be decreased to less than 1% with improved health through early diagnosis and treatment [9]. Unfortunately, dengue-related mortality has been increasing over the past ten years and some countries have experienced significant CFR during recent outbreaks [10]. While most infectious disease death rates have declined between 2005 and 2015 globally, dengue mortality has grown by roughly 50% [11]. Dengue-related death is a preventable cause of mortality and stopping the spread of such illness requires dengue prevention measures that are targeted and strengthened by coordinated efforts from various sectors and policymakers [12].

Furthermore, past studies have identified factors such as patients’ symptoms, diagnosis, and laboratory findings [13] as predictors for severe dengue and dengue-related fatality. However, many of these studies focused more on clinical aspects and limited attention given to the influence of epidemiological factors on dengue mortality, particularly in the context of Malaysia. The primary objective of this study is to examine the relationship between various sociodemographic, clinical and environmental factors and the occurrence of dengue mortality. Specifically, this study aims to identify and analyze the impact of these factors—encompassing sociodemographic variables (gender, age, ethnicity, nationality, and occupation), clinical health factors (comorbidities, platelet levels, time interval between onset and treatment, type of reporting healthcare facilities), and environmental influences (locality status, housing type, urban or rural and distance to healthcare facilities) on the risk of death from dengue fever. Ultimately, the findings will provide insights into the crucial factors contributing to dengue mortality. By identifying these key risk factors, this research seeks to inform public health policies and ensure effective interventions to prevent dengue mortality.

Methods

Study Design

This study focuses on Penang, Malaysia, which reports the highest number of dengue cases and fatalities in northern Malaysia. The state, comprising the five districts of Timur Laut, Barat Daya, Seberang Perai Utara, Seberang Perai Tengah, and Seberang Perai Selatan, has a population of 1.77 million [14]. The districts of Timur Laut and Barat Daya are located on an island, with Penang General Hospital serving as the designated referral center. In contrast, the remaining three districts are on the mainland, with Seberang Jaya Hospital serving as the referral center for these districts. According to the Department of Statistics Malaysia’s 2020 report, Timur Laut is the third most densely populated district in Malaysia, with a population density of 4,403 individuals per square kilometer [14]. Between 2015 and 2022, Penang reported a total of 24,333 dengue cases with 73 fatalities.

This study employed a 1:2 case-control design, utilizing data from the E-dengue system database and medical records spanning from January 2015 to December 2022. All confirmed dengue cases were registered in the Ministry of Health Malaysia’s dengue online registry at [http://edenguev2.moh.gov.my/]. A total of 219 participants were included, with 73 cases in the case group (representing all dengue-related deaths from 2015 to 2022) and 146 cases in the control group.

Dengue death cases were defined as deaths resulting directly from acute dengue infection, confirmed by the Penang State Mortality Review Committee. The control group consisted of dengue patients confirmed by laboratory tests or a dengue rapid test kit (either NS1, IgM, or IgG positive) who recovered from the acute disease. 146 controls were selected from the database of 24,333 registered dengue patients between January 2015 and December 2022. Simple randomization was performed using random number generator in excel file to ensure unbiased selection in the control group.

In addition to extracting data from health system databases, medical records for each case were reviewed to obtain supplementary information, including occupation, past medical history (comorbidities), and diagnoses which are not available in the online system database. A clinical record review checklist was prepared beforehand to streamline the data collection process.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 27.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were employed to explore and describe the data. For quantitative variables, means and standard deviations were calculated, while frequencies and percentages were used for qualitative variables. The chi-square test and Yates correction were used to determine associations between qualitative variables and the outcome variable. Quantitative variables with normal distribution, such as the interval between the date of onset and treatment, were analyzed using the independent t-test. Non-normally distributed variables, such as the distance to healthcare, were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U Test. Crude and adjusted odds ratios for predictors of dengue mortality were calculated using simple and multiple logistic regressions. Variables with p < 0.05 from the simple logistic regression were selected and included in the multivariable logistic regression model using the enter method. The final analysis was conducted using multiple logistic regression with forward elimination of variables. All statistical tests were conducted with a 95% confidence interval (CI) and a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analysis of patient’s characteristics, univariate, and bivariate analysis

From 2015 to 2022, Penang recorded a total of 24,333 dengue cases, including 73 fatalities. The study included 219 participants. As shown in Table 1, the majority of cases were Malaysians (96.3%), with an almost equal distribution across ethnicity (43.4% Malays and 44.3% Chinese) and gender (51.1% males and 48.9% females). Most cases were adults aged 16–60 years (69.9%), followed by children below 15 years old (17.8%) and those aged 60 years and above (12.3%). Additionally, 53% of dengue patients sought treatment from private facilities. The majority lived in urban areas (95.4%), with 47.9% residing in housing estates and 42.9% in high-rise buildings; only 9.2% lived in villages.

Both groups showed statistically significant differences in occupation and age factors (p = 0.01), with higher unemployment recorded in the case group (41.1%) compared to the control group (27.4%). About 55% of the cases had at least one comorbidity, significantly associated with dengue mortality (p = 0.001). Diabetes mellitus and hypertension were the most prevalent, with 11 patients each, but none of these patients died, resulting in a 0% mortality rate for both conditions. In contrast, patients with cancer (3 patients) and bronchial asthma (3 patients) had the highest mortality rates, with all of them succumbing to dengue, resulting in 100% mortality for both conditions. Additionally, 2 out of 3 patients with obesity died, giving a 66.7% mortality rate. The percentage of cases with comorbidities was higher among death cases (54.8%) than the control group (17.1%). An almost equal number of cases were found living in either single-case or outbreak localities, but the percentage of cases in outbreak areas was higher among dengue death cases (61.6%) compared to the control group (43.8%). Additionally, the majority of death cases (56.2%) had platelet levels less than 50,000/µL, compared to the control group (12.1%).

As shown in Table 3, the simple logistic regression analysis revealed several factors significantly associated with mortality. Increasing age was a notable factor, with those aged 46 to 60 years having an odds ratio (OR) of 6.56 (95% CI: 2.02 to 21.34) and an even higher risk for those aged over 60 years (OR: 17.5, 95% CI: 4.73 to 64.72). The presence of one comorbidity increased the risk (OR: 4.07, 95% CI: 1.94 to 8.57), with an even higher risk for those with more than two comorbidities (OR: 10.45, 95% CI: 4.08 to 26.90). Specific comorbidities also posed significant risks: bronchial asthma (OR: 12.99, 95% CI: 1.30 to 6.36), diabetes mellitus (OR: 2.87, 95% CI: 1.30 to 6.36), and heart disease (OR: 5.29, 95% CI: 1.00 to 27.98). A platelet level less than 50,000/µL was strongly associated with mortality (OR: 14.74, 95% CI: 5.97 to 36.38), as was living in an outbreak locality (OR: 2.0, 95% CI: 1.16 to 3.66). These variables were further analyzed using multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Factors associated with dengue death: Multivariable logistic regression analysis and predictive model

We conducted model diagnostics to evaluate the goodness-of-fit and checked for multicollinearity. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values, with all values falling below 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity issues. The goodness-of-fit of the final model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, confirming an adequate fit to the data.

The multivariable logistic regression analysis identified several statistically significant independent predictors for dengue mortality in Penang. These included outbreak locality status (p = 0.03, AOR: 2.30, 95% CI: 1.11–4.79), presence of one comorbidity (p = 0.02, AOR: 2.90, 95% CI: 1.22–6.90), presence of more than two comorbidities (p < 0.01, OR: 10.15, 95% CI: 3.53–29.23), bronchial asthma (p = 0.04, AOR: 12.00, 95% CI: 1.08–132.13), and a platelet level below 50,000/µL (p < 0.01, AOR: 15.7, 95% CI: 5.65–43.53) (Table 4).

Specifically, dengue patients with a platelet level less than 50,000/µL had 16 times higher odds of mortality compared to those with platelet levels between 150,000 and 400,000/µL. Dengue patients with a comorbidity had 3 times higher odds of mortality than those without comorbidities, while those with more than 2 comorbidities had 11 times higher odds of mortality. Additionally, dengue patients with bronchial asthma had a 13 times higher risk of mortality compared to those without bronchial asthma. Lastly, patients living in known outbreak areas had a 2 times higher risk of mortality than those living in single case areas. The study categorizes locality status into outbreak locality and single case locality. A dengue outbreak locality in Malaysia is defined as a location where more than one dengue case is reported in the same locality within 14 days of the notification of the first case [15].

As shown in Table 4, the only significant interaction found was between locality status and platelet level. Among those who lived in the single case areas, the odds ratio (OR) for platelet levels between 50,000 and 99,000 (µL) was 20.4 (as compared to platelet levels of more than 150 000 (µL)) with p value of 0.02. The highest OR was recorded by platelet levels of less than 50,000 (µL) which was 174 (p value of < 0.01). As platelet counts decrease, the risk significantly increases, especially in the lower platelet count groups. The interaction between single case locality and platelet count becomes more pronounced at lower levels of platelets, with statistically significant increases in risk for counts below 100,000 µL.

For those living in the outbreak areas, for platelet counts between 50,000 µL and 149,999 µL, there were no signifant difference of increased risk of mortality (as compared to platelet levels of more than 150 000 (µL)). However, there was a significant difference for the platelet levels of less than 50,000 (µL) with p value of 0.01 and OR of 5.0. This suggests that individuals in this category have a significantly higher risk of mortality among those who live in outbreak areas.

The analysis of the interaction between locality status and platelet levels in single-case and outbreak localities found a significant result, with a p-value of 0.01, among individuals with platelet levels between 150,000 and 400,000/µL residing in outbreak areas. These individuals had a 19-fold increased risk of dengue mortality compared to those in single-case localities. No significant associations were found for outbreak localities among individuals with platelet levels between 100,000 and 149,999/µL or between 50,000 and 99,000/µL. It indicates that higher platelet counts, particularly in the 150,000–400,000 µL range, are significantly associated with increased of mortality in outbreak area as compared to single case area, while lower counts do not show a significant relationship. This indirectly shows that the effect of platelet level varies between those who live in the outbreak and those in non-outbreak areas.

Discussion

Comorbidities

The study’s findings demonstrate that the presence of comorbidities significantly increases the risk of dengue-related mortality, underscoring the critical need for heightened vigilance among dengue patients with underlying health conditions. In particular, the discovery that a single comorbidity increases mortality risk threefold, and that the risk escalates to twelvefold with multiple comorbidities, highlights the compounding effect of health complications on dengue outcomes. This insight aligns with studies by Mehta colleagues and Saqib et al., who found that comorbidities exacerbate the severity and fatality of dengue infections [16, 17]. However, our findings extend beyond these observations by quantitatively establishing the risk ratios for single and multiple comorbidities, providing a clearer framework for predicting patient outcomes. Comorbidities were found to be an important risk factor for dengue mortality because they increase the disease’s severity in patients. Additionally, the physiological responses to viral infections might worsen certain pre-existing comorbidities, while some comorbidities could also contribute to the severity of these physiological responses to the infection. A study conducted in another low- and middle-income country indicates that elevated cTn-I predicts the length of hospital stay and in-hospital mortality in dengue-infected patients. Additionally, atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, low serum bicarbonate, high serum creatinine, and any abnormalities in echocardiography were linked to adverse outcomes in these patients [18]. Among individuals with diabetes, approximately 90% have type 2 diabetes, and nearly 80% reside in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the prevalence is rising most swiftly [19]. As diabetes rates increase in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which already bear a significant burden of infectious diseases, health systems must evolve to deliver integrated care to effectively treat and manage the associated comorbidities of diabetes.

Several other studies have also discovered a considerably increased risk of severe dengue due to pre-existing comorbidities, such as diabetes [20], hypertension, and renal failure [21]. However, there was a study in Mexico that found no significant association between comorbidity, such as diabetes mellitus, with dengue mortality. This could be explained by the limited sample size as compared to other studies, including one that involved 24,595 dengue fever cases from January 2020 to April 2021 [26]. Although no apparent explanation was proposed, diabetes patients with suboptimal glycemic control (HbA1c 7%) were more likely to have severe dengue than those with sufficient glycemic control and no other comorbidities [20].

Health systems, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), face unique challenges given the rising prevalence of chronic conditions like diabetes, which often coexist with infectious diseases. Therefore, the integration of dengue management with chronic disease care becomes essential, as comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases amplify dengue’s impact on the healthcare system. The data from this study provide valuable metrics for developing predictive models to identify high-risk dengue patients. For instance, healthcare providers could incorporate these findings into triage systems, where the presence of specific comorbidities raises an alert for closer monitoring and immediate treatment. Additionally, predictive algorithms could be designed to quantify mortality risk, enabling more informed decision-making in clinical settings.

Bronchial asthma

This study reveals that individuals with bronchial asthma have a significantly higher risk—12 times greater—of dengue-related mortality compared to the control group. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating that chronic illnesses like asthma predispose patients to severe forms of dengue [22–24]. The heightened risk associated with bronchial asthma emphasizes the need for specialized care protocols for asthmatic patients in dengue-prone regions.

Interestingly, contrasting evidence from Yu et al. suggests that asthma may reduce the likelihood of contracting dengue fever initially, potentially due to decreased DC-SIGN expression in dendritic cells. This molecule is a known entry point for the dengue virus, and lower expression could confer some protection. However, once infected, asthmatic patients appear more susceptible to severe complications, highlighting a complex interplay between asthma and dengue pathogenesis [25].

The association between asthma and more severe disease outcomes, such as Dengue Hemorrhagic Fever (DHF) and Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS), may be attributed to underlying immunological mechanisms. Higher IgE levels, common in secondary dengue infections, indicate a heightened immune response, which may contribute to the severe manifestations of dengue in asthmatic patients [26]. This aligns with findings from studies in Ho Chi Minh City and Pakistan, which identified allergic conditions, including asthma, as risk factors for severe dengue complications and hospital admissions. A study done in Ho Chi Minh City also demonstrated that allergic rhinitis (OR: 1.95, 95%; CI: 1.11 to 3.4, p = 0.019) was associated with dengue shock syndrome [27]. Additionally, those with bronchial asthma had a higher chance of being admitted to the hospital within 30 days after receiving a dengue diagnosis (OR: 1.34, 95%; CI: 1.13 to 1.57) [28].This is supported by another study conducted in Pakistan which advocated the association between bronchial asthma and disease severity [22]. The immune system of individuals with hypersensitivity, such as bronchial asthma, may be persistently activated with evidence of inflammation in tissues and capillaries, which could exaggerate the underlying pathophysiology of dengue illness and lead to the development of severe dengue and ultimately death.

Numerous epidemiological studies over recent decades have revealed significant variability in global asthma prevalence, with a notable increase in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), leading to substantial morbidity and mortality. Factors such as poverty, air pollution, climate change, exposure to indoor allergens, urbanization, and diet contribute to poor asthma control and adverse outcomes in these developing regions [29]. The condition is likely to worsen in the presence of dengue co-infection. Understanding the connection between asthma and dengue severity has profound implications for clinical management. Healthcare providers should monitor asthmatic dengue patients closely, given their increased risk of severe outcomes. Additionally, these findings underscore the importance of integrating asthma care with dengue management, particularly in areas where both conditions are prevalent.

Platelet level

This study identified thrombocytopenia as a significant risk factor for dengue mortality, with a platelet count below 50,000/µL associated with a 16-fold increased risk of death as compared to those with normal platelet level (p < 0.001). This finding reinforces the established role of low platelet counts as a marker for dengue severity [30, 31] This is with existing research by Mehta et al., which highlights the heightened severity of thrombocytopenia in dengue patients, particularly those with comorbidities [16]. Another study showed that mortality was higher among those with platelet counts below 50,000 (µL) versus those with over 50,000 (µL) and that in patients with abnormal coagulation profiles, disseminated intravascular coagulation that resulted in thrombotic or haemorrhagic manifestations is commonly associated with higher risk of morbidity and death [32]. A low platelet count can also increase the likelihood of organ failure and Intensive Care Unit admission, indirectly amplifying the risk of death [33]. While the association between low platelet counts and mortality is well-supported, contrasting results from another study indicated that individuals with platelet counts between 50,000 and 100,000 cells/mm³ had a lower likelihood of death [34]. However, this discrepancy might be attributed to the substantial amount of missing data in that particular study, which could have skewed the results. Such findings underscore the importance of comprehensive data collection and analysis when assessing the impact of thrombocytopenia on dengue outcomes.

Recognizing thrombocytopenia as a critical risk factor provides valuable insight into clinical management. It emphasizes the need for timely platelet monitoring in dengue patients, particularly those presenting with severe infections or comorbid conditions. Integrating regular platelet counts into standard dengue treatment protocols can enhance early detection of severe cases, potentially improving patient outcomes by allowing for prompt and targeted interventions. In the broader context of public health, these findings also highlight the importance of resource allocation in dengue-prone regions. Ensuring access to diagnostic tools and treatments that monitor and address thrombocytopenia can be instrumental in reducing dengue-related fatalities.

Locality status

This study highlights outbreak locality as a significant risk factor for dengue mortality, a relationship that, to the best of our knowledge, has not been directly addressed in prior research. While studies have established connections between outbreak localities and severe dengue cases [17, 35], our findings provide insight into how residing in an outbreak area directly influences mortality risk. A previous study showed a significant number of dengue severity in an outbreak in Karachi with 109 out of 278 patients were diagnosed with DHF/DSS (39%) with a total of 5 deaths [36]. A dengue surveillance was conducted at the Dhaka Hospital during Bangladesh’s first dengue haemorrhagic fever outbreak. From 176 individuals tested positive for dengue, 40% of them were diagnosed with dengue haemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome [37]. This may be due to increased exposure to virulent strains and higher vector density, both of which contribute to the severity of infections. A significant concern in outbreak localities is the potential for insecticide resistance, as observed in the Petaling District. An investigation of field strain mosquitos from an outbreak area in the Petaling District demonstrated that Aedes aegypti had developed resistance to all types of pesticides used (mortality less than 90%). This phenomenon raises concerns about the effectiveness of vector control in areas with constant pesticide application, a factor that could contribute to sustained high transmission rates and increased severity of dengue cases. Insecticides were used extensively to control dengue vectors; however, it led to the development of resistant populations, particularly among Aedes aegypti [38]. Moreover, changes in dengue serotypes within endemic regions further complicate the situation. In outbreak areas, shifts in circulating serotypes can lead to secondary infections with different strains, which are often more severe [39]. This dynamic heightens the risk of severe dengue and, consequently, increases mortality rates. Thus there is a need for integrated vector management strategies that include monitoring insecticide resistance and promoting alternative vector control methods to prevent resistant mosquito populations.

These findings underscore the need for targeted public health interventions in outbreak localities. The increased risk of severe dengue and associated mortality in these areas highlights the importance of strengthening dengue surveillance and prevention strategies especially in outbreak areas. Enhanced vector control measures, coupled with rapid response mechanisms, are essential for minimizing the impact of outbreaks and reducing mortality risks.

Locality status and platelet level interaction

In areas where only a single case of dengue is reported, individuals with platelet levels between 50,000 and 99,000 µL have a very high risk of mortality, with an odds ratio (OR) of 20.4 compared to those with platelet counts above 150,000 µL. The risk increased for individuals with platelet counts below 50,000 µL, with an OR of 174. This indicates that as platelet levels drop, the mortality risk increases dramatically in single case areas. Low platelet levels in dengue patients are often associated with more severe disease forms, such as dengue haemorrhagic fever, which can lead to shock and death. In single case areas, this effect is especially pronounced, possibly because healthcare resources may not be as stretched as in outbreak areas, making the platelet level a clearer marker of disease severity.

While in outbreak areas, platelet counts between 50,000 and 149,999 µL do not show a significant increase in mortality risk, suggesting that in these areas, other factor such as overwhelmed healthcare services or more widespread illness might influence mortality risk, reducing the impact of platelet levels alone. However, when platelet levels drop below 50,000 µL, the risk becomes significant (OR = 5.0), indicating that in outbreak scenarios, extremely low platelet counts still present a substantial risk for death, though the increase is not as dramatic as in single case areas.

Interestingly, people living in outbreak areas with platelet counts in the normal range (150,000–400,000 µL) have a 19 times higher mortality risk compared to those in single case areas. This suggests that even individuals who do not have critically low platelet levels may face higher mortality risk simply due to the outbreak environment, possibly because of delayed treatment, insufficient medical care, or the strain on healthcare systems during outbreaks. This data emphasizes that platelet levels play a crucial role in dengue mortality, particularly in single case areas, but during outbreaks, other factors also significantly influence mortality risk. Lower platelet levels (< 50,000 µL) are always a concern, but in outbreak areas, even normal platelet levels may not guarantee a lower risk of death due to the pressures of the outbreak environment.

Strength

This study attempted to review all dengue cases and fatalities medical record across the state from 2015 to 2022 to facilitate a more accurate diagnosis of patients according to the latest 2009 WHO classification, which addresses the severity levels of dengue infection. This aims to make the data more applicable to patient management and policy decisions in the future.

Limitation

One limitation of this study is that it was conducted by utilizing secondary data. Therefore, the findings might have been understated due to underreporting. There was also a possibility of information bias affecting the study results. Data verification was conducted through a thorough review of medical records, ensuring that classifications were as accurate and reliable as possible. The study may have encountered selection bias, particularly regarding how controls were selected. To minimise the selection bias, controls were chosen randomly. Matching did not involve age, sex and comorbidities as these were the factors being analyzed. However, to help control the confounding factors, we attempted to align controls with cases by year, to account for the seasonality of infection and the predominant circulating dengue serotypes, and by district, to address the spatial differences in clinical practices and environmental exposures. This approach was considered to ensure that comparisons between cases and controls were as fair as possible. The sample size for comorbidities such as bronchial asthma in the study was also small. Smaller samples may reduce the precision of the results and make it harder to draw definitive conclusions. For example, although only 7 patients in total had bronchial asthma, it remained a significant factor, with 100% mortality observed in the 3 patients who had it as their sole comorbidity.

Conclusion

The study underscores the necessity of developing risk profiles for dengue patients by integrating factors such as platelet levels, comorbidities, and locality status in order to prioritize and improve clinical care. It is essential to develop nuanced protocols that cater to the specific challenges of single-case and outbreak areas. In single-case localities, where the mortality risk is significantly higher for patients with low platelet counts (below 100,000/µL), healthcare providers should prioritize these patients for rapid intervention and hospitalization. This proactive approach can help mitigate severe outcomes. Conversely, in outbreak localities, where healthcare systems may be overwhelmed, even patients with normal platelet counts can face elevated mortality risks. Therefore, these areas should bolster resources, including staffing and medical supplies, and apply a more comprehensive triage approach that considers both platelet levels and other risk factors like comorbidities. The implementation of predictive models that take geographical factors into account can improve resource allocation and preparedness for dengue outbreaks in vulnerable communities. Overall, the recommendations from this study aim to empower public health personnel, healthcare providers, and communities in their collective effort against dengue, contributing to a significant reduction in mortality rates associated with this disease.
