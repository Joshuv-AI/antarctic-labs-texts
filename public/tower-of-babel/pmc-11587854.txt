A large gastric inflammatory fibroid polyp revealed by an upper gastrointestinal bleeding: a case report
Authors: Ghada Gharbi, Manel Yakoubi, Asma Ben Mohamed, Moufida Mahmoudi, Mohamed Karim M’Farrej, Mahdi Bouassida, Amal Khsiba, Mouna Medhioub, Emna Chelbi, Lamine Hamzaoui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory fibroid polyps (IFPs) are rare submucosal lesions that usually arise in the stomach. It represents 0.1% of all gastric polypoid lesions and are frequently asymptomatic. It’s usually revealed by an abdominal pain. Gastrointestinal bleeding can occur when the polyp is ulcerated. We report a case of a 72-year-old female with an IFP of the prepyloric region. The head of the polyp was thirty-five millimeters in diameter. It was revealed by an upper gastrointestinal bleeding and epigastric pain. An endoscopic resection was performed.

Introduction

The gastric inflammatory fibroid polyp (GIFP) is a rare benign tumor of the stomach. It accounts for about 0.1% of all gastric polyps [1]. It is usually asymptomatic, discovered on endoscopy or imaging performed for other causes. Sometimes, it shows nonspecific symptoms depending on the size and the location of the polyp. GIFP is a rare cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding and only few cases have been reported in the literature [2]. We report a case of a large gastric inflammatory fibroid polyp in a 72-year-old female presenting with epigastric pain and gastrointestinal bleeding.

Case presentation

A 72-year-old female with a history of hypertension and diabetes melitus, presented with a complaint of chronic epigastralgia since five months. She also reported some episodes of melena and iron deficiency anemia symptoms since three months: asthenia, headaches and palpitations. Physical examination was normal despite pale conjunctivae. Laboratory tests revealed microcytic hypochromic anemia of 4.8 g/DL. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy showed a large pedunculated polypoid lesion in the prepyloric region, protruding through the pylorus into the duodenal bulb (Figure 1). The head of the polyp was 35 mm in diameter with an 8 mm ulceration. Its stalk was long with a thickness of 15 mm (Figure 1). An open biopsy forceps was used to estimate this measurements.

A large pedunculated polypoid lesion in the prepyloric region with a head of 35 mm in diameter overcome by an 8 mm ulceration and a long stalk with a thickness of 10 mm.

We performed an endoscopic resection with a monopolar snare polypectomy after the injection of the stalk with adrenaline. Clips were applied prophylactically (Figure 2).

A monopolar snare polypectomy of the prepyloric polyp after the injection of the stalk with adrenaline and placement of a prophylactic clip.

Histopathological examination showed a fibroblastic stroma with extensive thick-walled capillary vessel, spindle cells, and an inflammatory infiltrate with numerous eosinophils and some lymphoid clusters (Figure 3). It is mainly located in the submucosa and extends through a thick and totally disorganized muscular mucosa to the mucosa (Figure 4). Both lateral and deep margins were free of tumor cells.

(A) Inflammatory fibroid polyp is composed of a loose mixture of a cytologically bland spindle-shaped stromal cells; inflammatory cells; and small, thin-walled blood vessels. Stromal cells are embedded in an edematous background and proliferate in a vaguely concentric fashion around the small blood vessels (Yellow arrow) (H&E, 100×). (B) In the inflammatory fibroid polyp, the inflammatory infiltrate is rich in eosinophils (Red arrow) (H&E, 400×).

Low-power image of the inflammatory fibroid polyp showing a predominantly submucosal, spindle-cell lesion with extension into the antral gastric mucosa (H&E, 25×).

Immunohistochemical staining was positive for CD34 (Figure 5), and negative for CD117, S100, DOG-1 and smooth muscle actin, consistent with the diagnosis of GIFP.

Immunohistochemical stain for CD34 shows characteristic diffuse positivity in the lesional cells (25×).

The patient was seen 1 month later. She was asymptomatic. The hemoglobin level was at 12.1 g/DL.

Discussion

The gastric Inflammatory fibroid polyp, also called Vanek’s tumor, is an unfrequent benign subepithelial tumor first described by Konjetzny in 1920 as a fibromatous polyp [3], then by Dr Vanek in 1949 as a granulomatous eosinophilic submucosal lesion [4]. Drs Helwig and Ranier in 1953 proposed the term of inflammatory fibroid polyp [5].

The pathogenesis of this tumor remains unclear. Many hypothesis have been proposed. A causal relationship with Helicobacter pylori has been suggested [6]. Allergic and parasitic infestations of the gastrointestinal tract have also been considered as possible etiologic factors as there is a preponderance of eosinophils in these tumors [7]. An activating mutation in the platelet derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFRA) gene have also been reported. It was first reported by Schildhaus et al in 2008 [8] and Huss et al proved its presence in 55% of IFPs [9].

The peak age incidence is between 60 and 70 years old [10] with a male predominance [3].

The presentation is various. It is usually asymptomatic, especially when the lesion is small and is localized in a large organ like the stomach [11]. In the other cases, patients present with abdominal pain, dyspeptic symptoms, vomiting, bleeding or anemia.

Gastrointestinal bleeding could seldom reveal a GIFP. In fact, the main etiologies of upper gastrointestinal bleeding are esophagitis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric cancer, and gastritis. GIFP is a rare cause of upper gastrointestinal bleeding and only few cases have been reported in the literature [2].

When its size is important, it can lead to a gastric outlet obstruction, called “ball valve syndrome” [11]. This complication is more frequent in case of an intestinal polyp causing Intussusception [12,13].

Despite the important size of the polyp and its location in the antrum, which is the most frequent site accounting on 70% of cases of IFP, our patient didn’t had obstructive signs. She presented with epigastric pain, melena and anemia. In fact, gastrointestinal bleeding is usually reported when the surface of the polyp is ulcerated. It’s often an occult bleeding leading to an iron deficiency anemia [14].

After the antrum, the other considered sites are the prepyloric region, followed by the cardia, the corpus, the pylorus and finally the fundus [14].

Endoscopy shows usually a solitary, sessile or pedunculated lesion with an intramural growth. Mucosal ulcerations or erosions can be also individualized [2]. Its size ranges usually between two and five centimeters [15].

Echoendoscopy may be useful. It shows an hypoechogenic and homogeneous lesion with inconspicuous margins, originating from within the second and third mucosal layers. Internal echoes indicate the presence of blood vessels in the tumors [16]. It helps to exclude one of the principal differential diagnosis: GIST, which has a transmural growth with well-defined margins [15].

The diagnosis is made on pathologic data which show a submucosa lesions with bland spindle cells and prominent vasculature. A concentric cuffing of vessels by the spindle cells, fibroblasts and myofibroblasts making an “onion skin” appearance, is characterized but seen only in half of cases [5]. In fact, it was absent in our case.

Immunohistochemical examination confirm the diagnosis. IFPs are positive for CD34 and vimentin, and negative for CD117, S-100 protein and cytokeratin AE1/AE3. Alpha smooth muscle actin can be either positive or negative [17].

Immunohistochemical staining are also helpful to rule out many differential diagnosis with spindle cells, such as GISTs, schwannomas and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors. In fact, all these lesions are negative for CD34. GISTs are positive for CD117. Schwannomas are positive for S100 protein, and inflammatory myofibroblastic tumors are positive for anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) [7].

Other differential diagnoses to be considered are adenomatous polyp, lipoma, lymphoma, inflammatory pseudotumor, hemangioendothelioma, hemangiopericytoma and spindle cell carcinoid [2].

Some studies suggested a neoplastic nature of IFP as it’s usually associated with PDGFRA mutations [18].

For treatment, endoscopic resection is always sufficient for polypoidal lesions. Different modalities have been used: polypectomy, submucosal dissection, snare cautery, and adrenaline plus diathermic loop [2].

In some cases, especially for sessile lesions, endoscopic resection may be incomplete or result in perforation and submucosal growth pattern could lead to recurrence. Thus, surgical treatment should be considered. The wedge resection is the gold standard for surgical resection [15].

Regardless the therapeutic modality, the resection is considered curative when the margins are negatives.

Most of the time, GIFPs don’t reoccur after local resection. Only one case of recurrence of a cardinal IFP after 12 mouths of endoscopic resection have been reported in the literature by Zinkiewicz et al. The patient underwent a proximal gastrectomy [19].

As evidence for malignant transformation and recurrence still lacking, recommendations for surveillance are not yet available.

Conclusion

Lastly, GIFP is a rare benign tumor, usually asymptomatic. However, our case report highlights the possible gastrointestinal bleeding leading to a deep iron deficiency anemia by mucosal erosion. The treatment is usually based in a margin-free resection. It’s neoplastic potential still unclear and the recurrence remains special, thus an endoscopic surveillance is not yet recommended after local resection.
