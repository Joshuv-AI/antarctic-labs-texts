Compressive spontaneous spinal epidural hematoma following cervical laminoplasty and the role of multimodal neuromonitoring: a case report
Authors: Meera M. Dhodapkar, Mohamed Sarraj, Charles A. Mechas, E. Matthew Hoffman, Brian Crum, Brett A. Freedman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical myelopathy is a clinical syndrome resulting from cord compression at the level of the cervical spine characterized by fine motor, sphincter, and gait dysfunction, for which early surgical management is the mainstay of therapy. Identification of techniques to monitor for and facilitate early management of rare but serious neurologic complications following cervical spine surgery is of the utmost importance. Intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) is one such technique that may be employed to identify neurologic injury in a timely manner including those events occurring remote to the operative levels.Case DescriptionIn this report, we describe the case of a 77-year-old male with a complicated medical history who sustained intraoperative spinal cord injury caudal to the instrumented levels during a cervical laminoplasty. Neurologic change was detected on IONM leading to emergent imaging. Spinal epidural hematoma was identified, which led to prompt surgical intervention resulting in return to preoperative neurologic baseline within 24 hours postoperatively.ConclusionsIONM facilitates early identification and allows for prompt management of potential neurologic adverse events including those caudal to operative levels during spine surgery and may reduce or reverse associated morbidity and mortality. We hope this case highlights the importance of further study into this modality to determine its optimal use in elective spine surgery.

Introduction

Cervical myelopathy is the clinical manifestation of cervical spinal cord compression and can result in loss of fine motor control, gait instability, and bowel and bladder symptoms (1). Early identification and operative intervention in some form is the mainstay of treatment to prevent functional decline and improve neurological prognosis (2-6).

While surgical intervention remains the gold standard, particularly in moderate and severe degenerative cervical myelopathy, it is not without risks (7). While risks such as dysphagia, pseudoarthrosis, wound complications, and medical complications may be more common, neurologic decline as a complication of surgery can be devastating. Spinal cord injury as a complication of cervical spine surgery is estimated to occur in approximately 5% of cases (8). Postoperative neurological injury is associated with a significant amount of morbidity and mortality (9), and therefore strategies to identify impending or early spinal cord injury are of the utmost importance.

One such method which is employed intraoperatively to identify early signs of neurological compromise is multimodal intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM), which includes somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and electromyography (EMG). Although IONM was first implemented in spinal surgery for scoliosis correction (10), it has become increasingly utilized in several surgical procedures with a higher risk of iatrogenic neurologic injury, for example, in deformity correction or decompression for cervical myelopathy. Arbeitsgemeinschaft fur Osteosynthesefragen Spine (AOSpine) guidelines for use of IONM strongly recommend use of IONM for high-risk patients. These include surgeries to address complex spinal deformity, revision of congenital deformity, conditions with cord compression and myelopathy, intramedullary spine tumors, unstable spine fractures, and ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament with associated cord compression (11).

While IONM can be influenced by several patient (e.g., presence of any baseline neurologic deficits) and intraoperative (e.g., positioning, anesthetic technique) factors, it can identify real-time changes in neurologic status both directly due to actions during surgery as well as any indirectly due to patient positioning or systemic factors (e.g., hypoperfusion). While one study demonstrated a 100% sensitivity and 98.4% specificity of multimodal IONM to predict acute-onset C5 palsy in patients undergoing cervical laminoplasty for the management of degenerative cervical myelopathy (12), another meta-analysis of neurologic outcomes in patients undergoing operative intervention for degenerative cervical spine conditions demonstrated no significant reduction in neurologic adverse events following cervical spine surgery for degenerative conditions. Nonetheless, this is an area of ongoing interest and investigation in the literature (13).

Early identification and prompt management of potential neurologic adverse events may allow for the opportunity to reduce or reverse associated morbidity and mortality. Below, we present the case of a patient with a complicated medical history who sustained intraoperative spinal cord injury caudal to the instrumented levels during a cervical laminoplasty which was detected by IONM, leading to prompt intervention. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-76/rc).

Case presentation

A 77-year-old male with a past medical history of supraventricular tachycardia, chronic diastolic heart failure, mild-moderate aortic regurgitation, severe mitral regurgitation, coronary artery disease, and multiple prior lumbar spine surgeries presented to clinic with worsening symptoms of ataxia and reduced manual dexterity. A magnetic resonance image (MRI) of the cervical spine demonstrated acquired congenital cervical canal stenosis most significant at C3–6 (Figure 1). He was deemed an appropriate candidate for cervical laminoplasty due to his preserved cervical lordosis, multi-level stenosis, and negative K-line. His preoperative platelet count was noted to be normal at 245×109/L.

Preoperative imaging of the cervical spine. AI, anterior inferior; IR, inferior right; PS, posterior superior; SL, superior left.

The beginning of his operative course was uneventful. First, C3 laminectomy was performed, followed by C7 dome laminectomy. Ligamentum flavum was taken down, during which routine epidural bleeding was noted but was not noted to be significant. Following this, laminoplasty was performed opening sequentially from C4–6. While securing the C6 laminoplasty plate, there was a sudden unexplained near-complete loss of lower limb MEPs (Figure 2). There were no traumatic events and no palpable stenosis under the leading edge of C7 or the caudal end of C2. The hinges were open with a clearly patent canal and well-positioned plates (Figure 3). In the subsequent 17 minutes, SSEP from each of the lower limbs decreased until they were absent. Upper limb MEPs and SSEPs remained stable throughout. Other sources of signal drop were investigated, namely, the patient’s hemoglobin had dropped slightly during the case, and he was transfused 1 unit of blood. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was restored to a goal of ≥85 mmHg, and no evidence of durotomy or traumatic contact to the spinal cord was present. Signals did not recover during this time, and a plan was made for completion of case and awake examination. At this point in time, partial thromboplastin time (PTT), prothrombin time (PT), internationalized normal ratio (INR), and fibrinogen were all drawn and were normal at 27 s, 12.5 s, 1.1, and 283 mg/dL, respectively.

MEPs and SSEPs during initial cervical laminectomy and laminoplasty. L, left; MEPs, motor evoked potentials; R, right; SSEPs, somatosensory evoked potentials.

Intraoperative photos of laminectomy bed and laminoplasty hardware.

Awake examination was limited by slow emergence from anesthesia and reduced compliance yet still demonstrated evidence of weakness in bilateral lower extremities. There were no long tract findings on exam. The patient was sent for immediate brain and spine computed tomography (CT), which demonstrated no evidence of hardware malalignment or structural explanation such as inappropriate bony alignment of laminoplasty block for the change in neuromonitoring status. The patient was therefore sent for immediate MRI spine which demonstrated evidence of an acute compressive epidural hematoma from C7 to roughly T9–10 although with less compression caudal to T8 (Figure 4).

Postoperative epidural hematoma on emergent MRI spine. IR, inferior right; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

The patient was brought to the operating room emergently for decompression. IONM demonstrated reliable SSEPs from all four limbs at baseline, a recovery from the end of the first surgery. While MEPs were present from all four limbs at baseline (green trial), they were significantly reduced in the bilateral lower limbs compared to pre-change MEPs from the first surgery. Skip laminectomies were expeditiously performed, with levels chosen based on extent of hematoma that was compressive that was visualized on MRI as well as the use of skip laminectomies performed in particular so as to maximize preservation of soft tissue attachments given extent of epidural hematoma and required exposure. First, T2 laminectomy was performed which demonstrated hematoma which was under pressure and which relieved with laminectomy, as well as a small amount of clot which was aspirated. Additionally, T7 laminectomy was performed which provided access to the caudal aspect of the hematoma, which was evacuated with appropriate appearance of the spinal cord following evacuation and without any residual fluid collections visible at laminoplasty site. At this time, the patient’s bilateral SSEP and MEPs were noted to be improved although not completely at baseline (Figure 5). The patient was extubated and transferred to intensive care unit (ICU) in stable condition.

Motor and SSEPs during skip laminectomy with hematoma evacuation. SSEPs, somatosensory evoked potentials.

At this time, his exam was notable for trace weakness in iliopsoas, anterior tibialis, triceps, and finger abduction bilaterally but left greater than right. It though was otherwise similar to preoperative exam and was notable for full sensation throughout. Rectal exam was notably positive only for reported slightly diminished although present perianal sensation to dull but was otherwise normal. He was initiated on spinal cord injury precautions and was started on riluzole, corticosteroids, and MAP targets of >85 mmHg with systolic blood pressure <160 mmHg. The next morning, his neurologic exam was noted to be his preoperative baseline, and MAP goals and steroids were discontinued. He continued to improve from a neurologic perspective, and once his drains met criteria for removal, he was deemed an appropriate candidate for spinal cord rehabilitation unit and was discharged on postoperative day 8. Postoperative X-ray was negative for hardware malignment (Figure 6).

Postoperative upright X-rays of the cervical spine.

At 1 month postoperative, he demonstrated full strength in bilateral lower and upper extremities as well as full sensation in all dermatomal distributions in bilateral upper and lower extremities.

Discussion

Here we present the case of a 77-year-old male who presented with cervical myelopathy and underwent cervical laminoplasty which was complicated by spontaneous near-complete loss of lower limb IONM signals and found to have an extensive cervicothoracic epidural hematoma. Following skip laminectomies and hematoma evacuation at T2 and T7/8, he returned to neurological baseline. We believe this case highlights several perioperative considerations of the role of IONM in the detection and early management of adverse intraoperative neurologic events.

Spontaneous epidural hematoma is a rare but serious complication associated with cervical spine surgery, estimated to occur in up to 3% of patients undergoing spine surgery in some studies (7,14). Previously identified risk factors for development of spinal epidural hematoma include procedures involving greater than 5 levels, INR >2.0 post-procedure, and blood loss >1 L (14). Additionally, postoperative spinal epidural hematoma remote to the site is even more rarely reported in the literature (15). Most present within 24 to 48 hours after surgery but may present later (16). In cervical or thoracic spine surgery, implementation of techniques to facilitate prompt diagnosis and management is of the utmost importance as failure to do so may result in permanent neurologic deficit.

IONM provides one such technique which may assist in early identification and management of neurological injury. In this case, the neuromonitoring alert led to expedited workup and identification of significant epidural hematoma. Of note, neuromonitoring was crucial for identification of spontaneous hematoma at a non-instrumented level and which in the absence of any hardware malalignment or more cranial hematoma at the area of laminectomy and laminoplasty was otherwise occult. Furthermore, while initial CT imaging was negative, IONM signal changes were instrumental in the decision to obtain further advanced imaging which revealed the underlying etiology. Prompt identification led to expedited hematoma evacuation and prevented any long-term neurological sequelae in this patient, who returned to neurological baseline within 24 hours following this event.

Conclusions

We believe this case highlights the value of routine multimodal IONM in mitigating and managing unexpected complications. Collaboration between surgeons, anesthesiologists, and neurophysiologists is critical for the early detection, prompt management, and optimization of outcomes after intraoperative neurologic injury. We hope this case brings awareness to this potential benefit as well as highlights areas for future study to understand the sensitivity, specificity, and cost-effectiveness of IONM in these scenarios.
