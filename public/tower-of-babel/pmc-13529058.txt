Accessible smartphone applications in visual rehabilitation: Utilization and associated factors
Authors: Manu Saini, Parul Chawla, Kulbhushan Saini, Pankaj Gupta
Published: 2026

Visual impairment remains a major public health concern worldwide, disproportionately affecting populations in low and middle-income countries. With an estimated 43 million people living with blindness and nearly 300 million with moderate-to-severe visual impairment globally,[1] the need for scalable and affordable assistive technologies is greater than ever. Smartphones, equipped with built-in accessibility features and third-party applications, have emerged as versatile assistive tools capable of enhancing independence, social participation, and quality of life among individuals with visual disabilities.[2] The present hospital-based cross-sectional study provides valuable insights into smartphone usage, utilization of accessible applications, and factors influencing their adoption among people with visual disabilities attending a vision rehabilitation clinic at a tertiary eye care center in India. These findings are pertinent, especially in light of rapid digital expansion and ongoing national initiatives to promote digital inclusion for persons with disabilities.

One of the most encouraging observations from the study is the high prevalence of smartphone users among participants. Nearly 9 out of 10 mobile phone users reported using smartphones, even among economically vulnerable and visually impaired populations. This trend likely reflects declining smartphone costs, expanding mobile networks, and supportive government initiatives such as the Assistance to Disabled Persons (ADIP) scheme and the Digital India program.[3] However, despite high levels of smartphone access, only 40% of users reported utilizing special accessible applications specifically designed for visual impairment. This notable gap between access and effective utilization underscores a critical challenge in the implementation of assistive technologies. Furthermore, nearly one-third of participants continued to depend on family members for daily activities, indicating persistent functional limitations despite technological availability.

The study identified higher educational status and being unmarried as significant predictors of accessible application use. Participants with graduate-level education or higher were substantially more likely to adopt special applications,[4] highlighting the crucial role of digital literacy and cognitive familiarity in technology uptake. These findings suggest that access alone is insufficient; users must also possess the skills, confidence, and exposure necessary to integrate technology into their daily routines. The classification of accessible applications into visual assistance apps for individuals with residual vision and visual substitution apps for those with profound vision loss appropriately acknowledges the heterogeneity of visual impairment and the need for personalized assistive solutions.

The reported use of applications such as InstaReader, KiBo, and Visor demonstrates how low-cost or freely available tools can support essential daily functions, including reading, communication, reminders, entertainment, and information access activities closely linked to autonomy and social inclusion. Notably, built-in screen readers such as TalkBack and VoiceOver support users with severe vision loss, while magnification and visual enhancement apps like Magnifier and Visor benefit those with residual vision. Text recognition and reading applications, including InstaReader and Seeing Artificial Intelligence (AI), enable access to printed and digital information through audio output.[56] Here, eye care providers and vision rehabilitation professionals play a pivotal role in translating technological availability into meaningful use.

Structured counseling, hands-on demonstrations, and app-specific training during rehabilitation visits can significantly improve awareness and confidence among users. Integrating digital assistive technology training into low vision rehabilitation programs may further empower both clinicians and patients to fully exploit smartphone capabilities.

Despite its strengths, the study has certain limitations. Being hospital-based and conducted in an urban tertiary care setting, the findings may not be fully generalizable to rural populations, women, or individuals with lower educational attainment groups that often face greater barriers to accessing rehabilitation services. Community-based and population-level studies are therefore needed to better understand contextual challenges and to explore reasons for non-use of accessible applications, including usability issues, language barriers, affordability of data services, and lack of sustained training or follow-up.

In conclusion, this study reinforces the growing evidence that smartphones and accessible applications represent powerful assistive technologies for persons with visual impairment. While smartphone ownership among individuals with visual impairment is encouragingly high, the underutilization of specialized accessible applications highlights a crucial need for targeted interventions to improve their effective use. Measures such as increasing awareness through patient education, enhancing digital literacy through structured training programs, incorporating assistive technology counseling into routine eye care, and promoting locally relevant, language-accessible applications are essential. Collaboration between healthcare providers, rehabilitation services, policymakers, and technology developers is crucial to ensure that assistive smartphone technologies are not only available but also accessible, acceptable, and effectively utilized. Strengthening these measures will help maximize the potential of smartphones as assistive devices, ultimately promoting greater independence, inclusion, and improved quality of life for individuals with visual impairment.
