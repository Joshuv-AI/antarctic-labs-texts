Boosting luminescence in oxide phosphors by the vitrifying-devitrifying approach
Authors: Mohamed A Ali, Moushira A Mohamed, Yujie Chen, Zhijie Ju, Renren Deng, Lin Gu, Boyuan Li, Lu Deng, Lei Yang, Lina Gao, Xiaofeng Liu, Yuanzheng Yue, Jianrong Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitrification-devitrification process often results in the formation of translucent/transparent bulk glass-ceramics/ceramics with functionalities comparable to their crystalline counterparts. Here we show a significant enhancement and tunability of luminescence in Mg2Al4Si5O18+1.5x:xEu2+ (MAS:xEu) powders after being subjected to a vitrification-devitrification protocol. In MAS:xEu powders, Eu2+ occupies multiple-sites in MAS hollow channels, demonstrating tunable blue-to-yellow emissions with red-afterglow. After vitrifying-devitrifying MAS:xEu powders, the derived ceramics showcase red-emissions with a near-unity luminescence efficiency, higher-thermal stability, and longer-persistent luminescence, enabling the fabrication of red-emitting lighting device with an ultrahigh brightness. The luminescence enhancement is ascribed to the increase of oxygen-related defects in the network during vitrification process that facilitates an efficient trapping-detrapping process. From structural analyses, the luminescence tunability can be attributed to the change in the heterogeneous structure of the channel wall, amending the distribution of Eu2+ multiple-sites. Thus, our approach has opened a new avenue for advancing the design of high-performance luminescent materials.Subject terms: Inorganic LEDs; Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Crystal field theory

Introduction

In materials science, vitrification and devitrification processes have been widely employed to fabricate functional glasses/glass-ceramics/ceramics for various applications, including gas separation, energy storage, bioimaging, and photonics1–6. For decades, vitrification of materials is achieved via the conventional melt-quenching technique, where raw materials are melted at elevated temperatures then rapidly quenched to room temperature1,7. In addition, advanced vitrification techniques, e.g., vapor hydration/thermal dehydration, direct synthesis, desolvation method, and structural perturbation strategy, were recently established to vitrify non-meltable structures8–10. Furthermore, additive manufacturing techniques combined with a high temperature sintering, have also been developed to enable high-throughput fabrication of glasses with complex geometries and feature sizes ranging from micrometer to centimeter scales11. Employing the melt-quenching approach, four glass families (i.e., organic, inorganic, metallic, and organic-inorganic) can be created1. These functional glasses are partially or completely devitrified into glass-ceramics or ceramics through heating the glasses near or above their crystallization temperatures (Tc)4,12. However, both solvents and pressure induce glass crystallization9,13,14. The conversion of crystalline powders into bulk glasses leads to the absence of grain boundaries and the ability to shape glasses into desired geometries, which represents significant advantages over their crystalline counterparts. This enables the construction of functional devices based on bulk glasses/glass-ceramics/ceramics, which exhibit a great potential for industrial applications2–4,15. Despite the fabrication of bulk glass/glass-ceramics/ceramics offers a solution for the shaping issue of functional crystalline powders, the vitrification of crystalline organics and organic-inorganics reduces their porosity and luminescence efficiency, while proton conductivity and lithium storage capacity of the crystalline phases are improved after their glass formation3,15–19. However, the crystallization of inorganic and organic-inorganic glasses results in the formation of ceramics with almost similar structure and properties compared to their crystalline powders or single crystals4,5,12,15,20. This implies that the vitrification-devitrification process can be utilized as a shaping technique to transform functional crystalline powders into transparent/translucent/opaque bulk glass-ceramics or ceramics5,20. Thus, materials vitrification followed by crystallization don’t significantly improve or tune their functionalities4,5,15.

In this work, we fabricate Mg2Al4Si5O18+1.5x:xEu2+ (MAS:xEu, x = 0, 0.0284, 0.1137, and 0.2842 mmol) powders, in which Eu2+ occupies multiple-sites in the hollow channels of MAS. The MAS:xEu powders demonstrate tunable blue-to-yellow emissions with an internal quantum efficiency (IQE) of 20–65% as well as red persistent luminescence (PerL) with a varied afterglow time. Upon vitrification-devitrification of MAS:xEu powders, the resultant MAS ceramics show red emissions with a near-unity IQE, higher photoluminescence (PL) thermal stability, and longer red PerL decay time. The structural investigations illustrate that the vitrification-devitrification of MAS:xEu powders results in the rearrangement of the heterogeneous structure of the channel wall, changing the distribution of multiple Eu2+ sites from blue-to-red. In addition, vitrification-devitrification process facilities the trapping-detrapping process between red-sites and traps and induces the formation of extra oxygen-related defects (e.g., oxygen vacancies VO), enhancing both IQE, PL thermal stability and decay time of luminescence. Therefore, we fabricate a red-emitting solid-state light (SSL) source by integrating a blue laser diodes (LDs) with a red-emitting MAS:xEu spectral converter, which exhibits an ultrahigh brightness. Our results suggest that the photonic functionalities of materials could be manipulated and improved via a vitrification-devitrification process, which could be further exploited for the development of luminescent materials based on strategies beyond traditional doping engineering.

Results

Synthesis of the MAS:xEu channel phosphors

In the hexagonal MAS structure (Fig. 1a), (Si/Al)O4/MgO6 units are linked to each other, forming a 3D network that contains hollow channels with a diameter of 5.6 Å along c-axis21–23. The channel wall is mainly composed of rings made of 4SiO4/2AlO4 units. In addition, there are two sites for the (Al/Si)O4 units that located outside (i.e., T1 sites) and inside (i.e., T2 sites) the channel (Fig. 1a). Based on atomic substitution engineering rules, Eu2+ (r6-coord.=1.17 Å) cannot occupy Mg2+ (r6-coord = 0.72 Å)/Al3+ (r4-coord = 0.39 Å)/Si4+ (r4-coord = 0.26 Å) sites because the large mismatch in the ionic radius ( > 62.5%), exceeding the limits of the Hume-Rothery rules for atomic substitution ( < 15%)24. Consequently, the hollow channels of MAS could be appropriate to accommodate Eu2+ ions21,25. To synthesize MAS:xEu powders, stoichiometric reactants of MAS (i.e., 2 mmol MgO, 2 mmol Al2O3, and 5 mmol SiO2) together with 0.0142, 0.0568, and 0.1421 mmol of Eu2O3 were heated at 1400 °C/2 hours under 5%H2/95%N2 atmosphere (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1). To convert MAS:xEu powders into ceramics, the prepared powders were vitrified using an aerodynamic levitation/laser heating equipment, and the resultants glass beads were heat-treated at 1200 °C (above the Tc = 1142 °C, Supplementary Fig. 1) for 1 h to ensure complete crystallization of the sample26. At x = 0, 0.0284, 0.1137, and 0.2842, the weight ratios of Eu2O3 doped to the MAS host are calculated to be 0, 0.85, 3.42, and 8.54 wt% (Supplementary Table 1), respectively. As shown in Fig. 1c and Supplementary Figs. 2a,3a, in spite of the addition of large amount of Eu2O3 to the MAS reactants, the MAS:xEu powders/ceramics still exhibit the X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks of hexagonal MAS structure with a high crystallinity, whereas MAS:0.0284Eu glass shows a characteristic broad hump as expected21–23,25,26. At x > 0.2842, unknown secondary phases were observed in the MAS:xEu powders/ceramics (Supplementary Figs. 2b,3b).

Luminescence performance of the MAS:xEu channel phosphors

Despite the MAS:xEu powders/ceramics possess a similar crystal structure, the luminescence properties are totally different21,22,25. For instance, the PL spectra of the MAS:0.0284Eu powder/ceramic (λexc = 360 nm) exhibit broad blueish-white/red emission bands centered at 450 nm/620 nm (Fig. 1b,d), respectively. However, weak green/red and blue emission peaks were observed for MAS:0.0284Eu powder and ceramic (Fig. 1d), respectively21,25. Furthermore, the difference between the peak positions of the emitted light from MAS:xEu powders and ceramics (i.e., ∆λ) steadily decreases with increasing x-value (Fig. 1e). These results suggest the multiple-site occupancy of Eu2+ in the hollow channels of MAS network, while the vitrification-devitrification process strongly influences the distribution of multiple Eu2+ sites from blue/green/red (dominant blue) to blue/red (dominant red) sites (Fig. 1f)27. The increase of x-value leads to the shift of the PL/PL excitation (PLE) peaks of powders from 450 nm/350 nm (x = 0.0284) to 500 nm/365 nm (x = 0.1137) and 520 nm/390 nm (x = 0.2842) (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 4). However, MAS:0.1137Eu/MAS:0.2842Eu powders demonstrate a shift in PLE peak from 350 nm to 460 nm, when monitored at different emission peaks ranging from 450 nm to 620 nm (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 5a). In addition, all MAS:xEu powders exhibit red emissions peaking at 620 nm (λexc = 460 nm) (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These results imply that Eu2+ ions are incorporated into multiple-sites in MAS powder, where Eu2+ site occupation preference changes from blue-to-green and red-sites as x-value increases25,27,28. The PL spectra of the MAS:xEu glasses illustrate blue and red peaks (Supplementary Figs. 6,7), which are attributed to the presence of both Eu2+ and Eu3+ in the glass structural network.

Unlike MAS:xEu powders, the PL spectra of the MAS:xEu ceramics showcase strong red emissions located at 620 nm (λexc = 460 nm, Fig. 2d and Supplementary Figs. 8,9b), while weak blue peak (460 nm) gradually increases with increasing x-value (λexc = 360 nm, Fig. 2c)21,25,27. Similar to MAS:xEu powders, MAS:0.2842Eu ceramic exhibits different PLE spectra when monitored at different emission peaks (Supplementary Fig. 9a). This observation suggests that Eu2+ red-sites are most abundant in the MAS:xEu ceramics and a small part of Eu2+ ions enters the blue-sites, implying both doping concentration and vitrification-devitrification process influence the preference of Eu2+ site occupation in MAS network. Surprisingly, the MAS:xEu ceramics possess a longer lifetime (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 10), a larger IQE/absorption efficiency (AE) (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 11), and a higher PL thermal stability (Fig. 2g,h and Supplementary Figs. 12–14) than MAS:xEu powders. The creation of more lattice defects in MAS network during vitrification-crystallization process leads to the enhancement in lifetime/IQE/PL thermal stability for ceramics compared to the powder counterparts29. Such improvement in the luminescence performance of the resultant MAS:xEu ceramics arises from the facile electron trapping-detrapping process between the traps and Eu2+ red-sites instead of the electronic exchange process with Eu2+ green and red-sites (Fig. 3a, trapping-detrapping process (1))21. However, the lifetime/IQE/PL thermal stability (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Figs. 10,14) progressively decreases with x-value as a result of the concentration quenching effect30. The IQE values (λexc = 360 nm) for MAS:xEu powders are 65%, 53%, and 20% at x = 0.0284, 0.1137, and 0.2842, which increases to 95%, 73%, and 63% (λexc = 460 nm) for the corresponding ceramics (Fig. 2f), respectively. Among the prepared samples, red-emitting MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic shows a near-unity IQE under excitation of blue light and excellent PL thermal stability (i.e., retaining 82% of PL intensity at 200 °C, Fig. 2g). This makes MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic an excellent candidate for SSL applications31. Amazingly, the time-resolved PL (TRPL) spectra of the MAS:xEu powders (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Figs. 15–17) all show microsecond decays with PL peaks shift to the 620 nm red emission, implying the energy transfer between multiple Eu2+ sites (Fig. 3a, energy transfer process (1))32,33.

Owing to the insertion of large amount of Eu2+ ions into MAS hollow channels as well as the fabrication of MAS:xEu at elevated temperature, oxygen interstitial (Oi = 1.5x)/VO defects were generated, which are associated with the observation of PerL34,35. Upon irradiating the samples by a high power 254 nm light or 460 nm laser, the as-prepared MAS:xEu phosphors/ceramics exhibit red PerL (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 18–20). The trapping-detrapping process between traps and red-sites and the energy transfer between multiple Eu2+ sites are illustrated in Fig. 3a. In the PerL mechanism, the electrons are excited from valence band to conduction band (CB) of MAS under λexc = 254 nm, which afterwards arrested in the shallow and deep traps. These arrested electrons in traps return to the CB via trapping-detrapping processes (2) and (3) as shown in Fig. 3a. The PerL starts when the CB and shallow traps transfer their electrons to the excited states of Eu2+ ions at multiple-sites (Fig. 3a, energy transfer process (2)). Therefore, the as-fabricated MAS:xEu ceramics show a red PerL. In contrast, the observed red PerL in MAS:xEu powders is due to the energy transfer between multiple Eu2+ sites (Fig. 3a, energy transfer process (1))32,33. The PerL decay time of the prepared MAS:xEu powders/ceramics (Supplementary Figs. 21a,b) increases with x-value because of the increase of Oi content in the MAS channels. Moreover, the prepared ceramics demonstrate a longer decay time compared to powders (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Figs. 21c,d), which can reach up to 5 min for MAS:0.2842Eu ceramic. This result indicates that the melting of MAS:xEu powders at elevated temperature leads to the formations of extra oxygen-related defects in ceramics (e.g., VO)36. This observation highlights the importance of vitrification-devitrification for MAS:xEu powders, which not only shifts the PL peak from blue-to-red and improve the IQE/thermal stability of luminescence but also prolong the PerL decay time35,36.

The distribution of traps (i.e., Oi/VO) in MAS:xEu powders/ceramics was examined by thermoluminescence (TL) measurement (Supplementary Fig. 22a,b), revealing a broad TL peak centered at 220 °C (deep traps) and a weak shoulder at 110 °C (shallow traps). As x-value increases, the intensity of the TL peak related to shallow traps (depth of 0.76 eV) steadily increases in the MAS:xEu powders/ceramics, however, the TL peak of the deep traps shifts to higher temperatures for MAS:xEu ceramics (depth of 0.95–1.05 eV)21,37. By comparing the TL curves of the MAS:xEu powders/ceramics (Fig. 3d,e and Supplementary Figs. 22c,d), it is found that the concentration/depth of the shallow and deep traps in the ceramics are higher/deeper than that in powders. This suggests that the traps capacity increases with x-value and after vitrification-crystallization process. The rise in traps capacity and density prolongs the trapping-detrapping process, thereby increasing the intensity and period of the PerL36. This could explain why PerL decay time increases with doping concentration and after the vitrification-devitrification process.

The electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurement endorses the presence of multiple Eu2+ sites and Oi/VO defects in MAS:xEu structures, which matches well with the PL and PerL data. The EPR curve of the MAS:0.0284Eu powder shows resonance peaks at applied magnetic field strengths of 3450 G, 2455 G, 1310 G (Fig. 3f), which are attributed to the presence of Eu2+ in weak (i.e., blue-sites), medium (i.e., green-sites), and strong (i.e., red-sites) crystal fields38–41. However, after converting MAS:0.0284Eu powder into ceramic, the intensity of the EPR peak for Eu2+ red-sites remarkably increases compared to that of the Eu2+ blue-sites, but the EPR peaks of green-sites almost disappeared. The MAS:0.0284Eu powder showcases weak resonance peaks related to Oi and VO defects with g-factors of 4.78/2.34/2.28 and 1.99/1.92 (Supplementary Fig. 23), respectively36. After vitrification-devitrification, the MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic also illustrates weak EPR peaks associated with Oi and VO defects at g = 5.57/4.65 and g = 1.99/1.92, respectively. As x-value increases, the EPR peaks of red-sites and Oi/VO defects significantly increases for the ceramic samples, while the related EPR peaks for Oi defects and blue/green/red-sites of the MAS:0.2842Eu powder become weakened because of the strong dipole interactions between Eu2+ ions at high doping concentration (Supplementary Fig. 24)36,38.

Origin of multiple Eu2+ sites in MAS channel phosphor

In order to understand the observed luminescence tunability in MAS:xEu, the location of Eu2+ and its surrounding environment were evaluated by high angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM), positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS), and cross polarization magic angle spinning nuclear magnetic resonance (CPMAS-NMR) techniques. As shown in Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Figs. 25–29, we succeeded in imaging the MAS:xEu powders and ceramics along c-axis via a HAADF-STEM equipment. Owing to the low doping concentration (i.e., low filling degree of the channel by Eu2+), the images of MAS:0.0284Eu powder and ceramic exhibit a homogeneous distribution of vacant channels without any obvious sign for filling the channels by Eu2+ ions (Supplementary Figs. 25,26). However, the STEM images of the MAS:0.2842Eu powder and ceramic demonstrate both empty and filled channels by white dots, which are distributed randomly in the channels (Fig. 4a,b and Supplementary Figs. 27–29)42–46. We also find that the brightness of these white dots is varied from channel to channel, suggesting the difference in the depth of the Eu2+ ions in the channel. Fascinatingly, the line scan HAADF intensity profile (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 29c) reveals consecutive hollow and filled channels for MAS:0.2842Eu powder and ceramic together with ordered sharp crests separated by hollow channels. The diameter of these channels ranges from 3.5 to 6.5 Å, while the white dots at the center of the channels illustrate strong peaks with width of 2.34 Å, which matches the diameter of Eu2+ (D6-coord.=2.34 Å). These results suggest both the occupation of Eu2+ at the hollow channels of MAS powders and ceramics with octahedral coordination and the unchanged location of Eu2+ in MAS network after vitrification-devitrification process42,43, contradicting with some recent studies21–23,25. By analyzing the characteristics of channels in MAS:xEu using STEM technique, it is found that the channel diameter distribution of MAS:xEu powders remains almost unchanged after vitrification-devitrification process (Supplementary Fig. 30). On the other hand, the steady filling of the channels by large amount of Eu2+ and Oi (∼ 8.54 wt% of MAS at x = 0.2842) leads to the slightly enlargement of the channels diameter of MAS powder/ceramic from 4.25 Å to 5.25 Å as x-value rises from 0.0284 to 0.2842 (Supplementary Figs. 31,32), respectively. Although the as-prepared MAS:0.2842Eu samples possess similar channel diameters ranging from 4 Å to 6.5 Å, most of Eu2+ ions selectively occupy channels with diameters of 4–5.5 Å in powder and ceramic samples (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 33). This excludes the contribution of channel diameter effect to the change of luminescence properties of MAS:xEu upon vitrification-devitrification process.

PALS analyses also endorse the slight change in the channel diameter distribution upon converting MAS:xEu powders into ceramics (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Figs. 34, Supplementary Table 2), as well as the channel expansion of MAS powders and ceramics with increasing the Eu2+ doping level (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Figs. 35–36, Supplementary Tables 3–4))47. Moreover, blocking the hollow channels of MAS powder and ceramic by Eu2+ and Oi was indirectly confirmed by PALS analysis, where the fractional free volume (FFV) gradually decreases with x-value as shown in Fig. 4g48. Remarkably, PALS data shows the channel diameter of undoped MAS glass is 4.38 Å (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Fig. 34, and Supplementary Table 2), approving the preservation of hollow channels in glassy state49.

The 27Al and 29Si CPMAS-NMR spectra of the MAS:xEu samples exhibit representative resonance peaks for (Si/Al)O4 units in T1 and T2 sites (Fig. 4h,i and Supplementary Fig. 37)50–52. By calculating the integrated area under relevant peaks, the percentage of Si (i.e., SiT1 and SiT2) and Al (i.e., AlT1 and AlT2) and the number of Si (i.e., N(Si,T1) and N(Si,T2)) and Al (i.e., N(Al,T1) and N(Al,T2)) in T1 and T2 sites could be estimated (Supplementary Figs. 38–45 and Supplementary Table 5)51,52. Theoretically, the fully ordered MAS channel has a N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio in T2 sites of 0.5 because the ring of the channel is composed of 4SiO4 and 2AlO4 units51. However, the measured N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio in T2 sites based on NMR data is 0.69 for MAS:0.0284Eu powder, which increases to 0.83 (MAS:0.2842Eu powder), 1.03 (MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic), and 0.89 (MAS:0.2842Eu ceramic) (Fig. 4j). This implies the heterogeneous structure of the channel wall, which is sensitive to x-value and the vitrification-devitrification process50,51,53. Accordingly, the distribution of the SiO4 tetrahedrons with different number of bridging oxygen (BO) (i.e., Qn, n is the number of BO) were changed in T2 sites (i.e., reducing Q3 units and increasing Q0/Q1/Q2 units) after increasing the doping concentration or converting the MAS:xEu powders into ceramics (Supplementary Figs. 46,47 and Supplementary Table 6)50,54. The NMR results indicate that there is a strong relationship between the observed tunable luminescence in MAS:xEu and the heterogeneous structure of the channel wall (i.e., N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio, Fig. 4j)50. In addition, the variation in the N(Al,T1)/N(Si,T1) ratio (Supplementary Table 5) has no contribution in the luminescence tunability because the (Al/Si)O4 units in T1 sites are the second neighbor shell for Eu2+ ions. Due to the partial retaining of the channels in MAS glass as confirmed by PALS analysis, the glassy samples show broader and weaker 27Al and 29Si resonance peaks in T2 sites compared to the crystalline MAS:xEu powders (Fig. 4h,i and Supplementary Fig. 37). In addition, the molecular dynamic (MD) simulations (Supplementary Figs. 48,49) of the 20MgO-20Al2O3-50SiO2-10EuO (mol%) glass also confirm that Eu2+ is incorporated into a pore-like structure in MAS glass (Fig. 4k) and that most Al3+ and Si4+ sites retain a coordination number (Cn) of 4 (Fig. 4l). This further endorses the incomplete preservation of MAS structure after vitrification49. The scanning electron microscope and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) mapping of MAS:0.2842Eu powder and ceramic reveal a homogeneous elemental distribution for Mg2+/Al3+/Si4+/O2-/Eu2+ within the samples with elemental weight percentages very close to the nominal concentrations of the samples (Supplementary Figs. 50,51 and Supplementary Table 7).

To realize luminescence tunability in Eu2+-activated phosphors, several strategies were developed to greatly increase the crystal field around Eu2+ ions, shifting the PL peak from blue to red or even to near-infrared spectral regions55,56. According to the crystal field theory, the crystal field strength at Eu2+ site is sensitive to the bond length, bond angle, coordination number, anion type, and the degree of site symmetry55–58. In the literature, MAS:xEu phosphors exhibited blue and red luminescence, which relies on the synthetic technique21,22,25. For instance, solid-state reaction and sol-gel methods result in the development of blue-emitting and dual blue-red-emitting MAS:xEu phosphors, respectively22,25. However, the crystallization of non-stoichiometric 2MgO–Al2O3–3SiO2 glass led to the formation of efficient and stable red-emitting MAS:Eu composite phosphor with a high crystallinity of 95.8 wt%21. The researchers attributed the blue and red emissions to the incorporation of Eu2+ into Mg2+ and hollow channel sites without providing convincing evidence. In this study, we synthesized high purity MAS:xEu powders and ceramics, which demonstrated luminescence tunability, energy transfer between multiple Eu2+ sites, and red PerL. Based on the PL, STEM, PALS, and NMR analyses, we have verified that Eu2+ ions have entered the hollow channels of MAS powders/ceramics and octahedrally coordinated with the oxygen sites at the channel wall with assuming that Eu‒O bond length remains unchanged. This observation contradicts with the previous work, according to which the blue emission originates from the substitution of Eu2+ ions for Mg2+ sites22. Due to the heterogeneous structure of the channel wall, the Eu‒O‒Al/Si bond angle varies, inducing the variation in the crystal-field around Eu2+ from weak to strong strength (i.e., creation of blue/green/red-sites). As x-value increases in MAS:xEu powders or upon vitrification-devitrification, the N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio in the channel wall increases from 0.69 (MAS:0.0284Eu powder) to 0.83 (MAS:0.2842Eu powder)/1.03 (MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic)/0.89 (MAS:0.2842Eu ceramic), resulting in a disordered atomic arrangement in the channel wall50,51,53. This means that the disordered atomic arrangement is ascribed to the partial replacement of small Si4+ (r4-coord = 0.26 Å) by large Al3+ (r4-coord = 0.39 Å) in the channel wall. Such structural perturbation in the channel wall leads to the increase of the crystal field around Eu2+ and thus induces the formation of more Eu2+ green/red-sites rather than blue-sites and vice versa. The observed structural change contributes to the shift in the PL peak of the MAS:0.0284Eu powder from 450 nm to 520 nm for MAS:0.2842Eu powder and to 620 nm for the MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic. In addition, the observed red-shift in the PL peak of the MAS:0.2842Eu powder from 520 nm to 620 nm after the vitrification-devitrification process, is due to the increase of the N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio from 0.83 to 0.89. While, the drop in the N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio from 1.03 (x = 0.0284) to 0.89 (x = 0.2842) with doping concentration, induces the formation of more Eu2+ blue-sites in ceramics. Owing to the growth in the N(Al,T2)/N(Si,T2) ratio (i.e., more Si‒O‒Al bonds) in the channel wall of the MAS:xEu powders after increasing the x-value (i.e., more Eu‒O‒Si/Al bonds) or after vitrification-crystallization process, extra Si‒O‒Al/Eu bonds are formed, significantly reducing the density of the Q3 units in the channel wall51,52,54. After vitrifying the MAS:xEu powders, a part of the channel structure and their (Si/Al)O4 tetrahedrons were preserved, which facilities the restoration of the MAS:xEu structure during the crystallization of glass with more disordered channel wall compared to that of the parent powder. This result suggests that the observed tunable emission from blue-to-red in MAS:xEu structure originates from the increase in the degree of the structural disorder of the channel wall, which is caused by the doping concentration and/or vitrification-devitrification50,54.

Application of MAS:xEu ceramics for laser lighting

To demonstrate the high power lighting application for the as-prepared phosphors, we fabricate red-emitting SSL devices by coupling MAS:xEu ceramics with a 445 nm blue LDs (Fig. 5a). At x = 0.0284, as the incident laser power density (LPD) increases from 0.05 to 7.04 W mm-2, the luminous flux (LF), luminous efficacy (LE), and output power (P) of the as-fabricated device gradually increases till saturation at 1084 lm (LPD = 6.42 W mm-2), 53 lm W-1 (LPD = 4.55 W mm-2), and 2.19 W (LPD = 6.42 W mm-2) (Fig. 5b–e and Supplementary Figs. 52,53), respectively. Owing to the drop in IQE of the MAS:xEu ceramics with x-value, the LFmax/LEmax/Pmax values of the devices steadily decline to 605 lm/45 lm W-1/1.24 W (x = 0.1137) and 205 lm/32 lm W-1/0.42 W (x = 0.2842) (Supplementary Fig. 53). In contrast to the fabricated SSL devices using various red-emitting phosphors, (Supplementary Table 8), the as-constructed SSL device based on high purity MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic showcases an ultrahigh brightness, recording LFmax = 1084 lm/LEmax = 53 lm W-1/Pmax = 2.19 W21,59–63. All those results suggest that the prepared MAS:0.0284Eu ceramic is a promising red-emitting material that enables the fabrication of high power lighting device21,31,48.

Discussion

We have demonstrated the fabrication of efficient and multicolor MAS:xEu phosphors, in which Eu2+ occupy multiple-sites in the vacant channels of MAS network. The as-prepared MAS:xEu powders exhibit tunable PL spectra covering the blue-yellow visible range with IQE = 20–65% and red PerL. However, the conversion of MAS:xEu powders into ceramics via the vitrification-devitrification process yields red emissions with a near-unity IQE and enhancement in the PL thermal stability and PerL decay time. The significant improvement in the luminescence performance of MAS:xEu after vitrification-devitrification results from the facile trapping-detrapping between red-sites and traps as well as the formation of extra VO defects in the network during glass formation. The observed red PerL in MAS:xEu powders is due to the energy transfer between multiple Eu2+ sites. The formation of multiple Eu2+ sites originates from the random bonding of Eu2+ ions with the heterogeneous channel wall. The incorporation of Eu2+ ions into the hollow channels of MAS is confirmed by STEM and PALS analyses, leading to the expansion of the channel. The solid-state NMR results suggest that the heterogeneous structure of the channel wall depends on the doping concentration of Eu2+ and the preparation method of MAS. This results in the modification in the distribution of multiple Eu2+ sites and thus tunable luminescence was observed. The vitrification-devitrification of MAS:xEu structure shifts the PL peak from blue to red spectral region with a significant enhancement in IQE/PL thermal stability and the red PerL decay time. Therefore, the as-constructed high power red-emitting SSL devices based on MAS:xEu ceramics showcase an ultrahigh brightness with LFmax = 1084 lm/LEmax = 53 lm W-1/Pmax = 2.19 W. These results provide new insights into the luminescence mechanism. The strategy for enhancing the luminescence in MAS:xEu through the vitrification-crystallization protocol may open the gate to engineer the optical performance of other phosphors for different photonic applications.

Methods

Reagents

MgO (Aladdin, 99.99 %), Al2O3 (Aladdin, 99.99 %), SiO2 (Aladdin, 99.99 %) and Eu2O3 (Aladdin, 99.99 %) were purchased and used for preparing the MAS:xEu powders, glasses and ceramics.

Preparation of the MAS:xEu powders, glasses, and ceramics

To fabricate the MAS:xEu powders (x = 0, 0.0284, 0.1137, and 0.2842 mmol), 2 mmol MgO, 2 mmol Al2O3, and 5 mmol SiO2, doped with 0, 0.0142, 0.0568, and 0.1421 mmol of Eu2O3 respectively, were separately grinded for 1 hour in an agate mortar with the assistant of ethanol. After drying the mixtures at 100 °C for 1 hour, the resultant powders were sintered at 1400 °C for 2 h under 5%H2/95%N2 reducing atmosphere. To prepare the MAS:xEu glasses, the as-prepared MAS:xEu powders were converted into glasses by a melt-quenching process using the aerodynamic levitation/laser heating equipment. The as-quenched glass beads were then processed by a pressureless glass crystallization process at 1200 °C for 1 h in 5%H2/95%N2 atmosphere to obtain the MAS:xEu ceramics.

Characterizations

XRD patterns were collected using a XPERT-PRO-PANalytical system (2θ = 5°-90° with a resolution of 0.02°). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurement was performed in air using a TA instrument Q200 (TGA) at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The HAADF-STEM images were recorded using a JEM-ARM300F2 GRAND ARM™2 atomic resolution analytical microscope. The SEM/EDS images were captured with a Hitachi SU-8010 microscope. EPR measurement was performed at 100 K using a Bruker EMXplus-9.5/12 equipment.

All simulations are performed using the LAMMPS software. The glass structure was obtained by the melt-quench method. The initial random structure of 48000 atoms (20MgO-20Al₂O₃-50SiO₂-10EuO) was generated in a cubic periodic boundary simulation box. The system was then energy minimized to eliminate unphysical atomic overlaps from the initial random configuration. Subsequently, the structure was equilibrated at 300 K, heated to 4500 K, and then held at this temperature to ensure complete melting. After this, the system was cooled to 300 K at a cooling rate of 5 K/ps. All preceding simulations were carried out in the NVT ensemble. Finally, volume relaxation was conducted under the NPT ensemble, followed by the NVE ensemble to obtain the final glass structure.

Optical measurements

The PL, PLE, TL, PL decay, PerL decay curves, and temperature-dependent PL spectra were recorded on a spectrofluorometer (iHR 320, Jobin-Yvon, France) equipped with a xenon arc lamp as excitation source, where the temperature was controlled by a high temperature fluorescence test device (TAP-02, Orient KOJI). The TRPL spectra were recorded using a Horiba iHR550 spectrometer equipped with Picoquant PMA 192 (photomultiplier detector) and Time Harp 260 PICO (a time-correlated single photon counting and multichannel scaling board). The samples were excited by a 360 nm TTL modulated continuous-wave diode laser. The TRPL spectra were acquired by measuring the luminescence decay at different wavelengths with intervals of 10 nm. IQE and AE of the as-fabricated samples were measured using a Quantaurus-QY Plus C13534-35 system (Hamamatsu Corp., Shizuoka, Japan).

Fabrication of high power red-emitting SSL device

High power red-emitting SSL devices were fabricated by combining a blue 445 nm LDs with the MAS:xEu pellets (0.4 mm in thickness). The optical properties of the as-fabricated SSL devices including the luminescence spectra, LF, LE, and P values under different LPD, were measured using an integrating sphere measurement system (LHS-1000, EVERFINE Corporation, China) equipped with an array spectrophotometer (350-1100 nm, HAAS-2000) and an integrating sphere (SPEKTRON R98, Φ 50 cm). The measured LE is defined as the LF of the emitted red light divided by the LPD. The total radiant power measured from the as-fabricated SSL device comprises the incident blue LDs and the resulting red emission from the MAS:xEu ceramic.
