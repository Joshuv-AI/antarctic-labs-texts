A Very Rare Side Effect of Sirolimus in a Child with Autoimmune Hemolytic Anemia: Lymphedema
Authors: Fatma Ünal, Turan Bayhan, Neşe Yaralı
Published: 2025

Sirolimus is an immunosuppressive agent with antiproliferative properties that inhibits the mTOR pathway by interrupting the cell cycle at the G1-S phase.1 It is commonly used to prevent graft rejection in organ transplantation and to treat certain autoimmune diseases.2 Known side effects of sirolimus include hyperlipidemia, thrombocytopenia, diarrhea, poor wound healing, and lymphedema. Although lymphedema has been documented in patients using sirolimus for organ transplant rejection, it has not been reported in pediatric patients treated with sirolimus for autoimmune hemolytic anemia.1,3,4 Here, we present a pediatric case where lymphedema developed as a rare side effect of sirolimus in a child diagnosed with resistant autoimmune hemolytic anemia.

A 2-year-old male patient had been under follow-up since the age of 2 months for autoimmune hemolytic anemia and immunodeficiency. At the time of diagnosis, direct and indirect Coombs tests were highly positive. The immunoglobulin G level was below the normal range (2.96 g/L), and the patient was started on monthly intravenous immunoglobulin treatment for immune deficiency. Despite treatments with methylprednisolone, intravenous immunoglobulin, cyclosporine, and mycophenolate mofetil, the patient continued to experience hemolytic anemia episodes. Consequently, sirolimus treatment was initiated, and therapeutic levels were maintained. Under sirolimus therapy, the patient experienced no hemolysis episodes, and hemoglobin levels remained within the normal range. However, after the first year of sirolimus treatment, edema was observed in the patient’s left foot. Over the following month, the edema increased in size. Physical examination revealed a 3 × 5 cm edema. Ultrasound imaging indicated that the skin and subcutaneous tissues of the left foot were thickened, heterogeneous, and edematous, suggesting infection or inflammation. Lymphoscintigraphy revealed second-degree lymphatic insufficiency in the left lower extremity. Despite initiating physical therapy for lymphedema, the edema continued progressing (Figure 1). Suspecting sirolimus as the potential cause, the drug was discontinued after 1.5 years of treatment, and the patient was switched to tacrolimus. Within 1 month of discontinuing sirolimus, a significant reduction in lymphedema was observed. Five months later, the edema had regressed further (Figure 2). Tacrolimus treatment was discontinued after 6 months, with no recurrence of hemolytic anemia during follow-up. By the 17th month after stopping sirolimus, the patient’s lymphedema had completely resolved (Figure 3).

A limited number of cases suggest that sirolimus may be effective in treating resistant autoimmune hemolytic anemia.4 In our patient, sirolimus maintained therapeutic levels, and hemolytic anemia resolved rapidly. However, lymphedema developed during the first year of treatment as a rare side effect. Lymphedema occurs when the lymphatic load exceeds the carrying capacity of the lymphatic system, leading to fluid accumulation in the interstitial space. This can be caused by congenital abnormalities of the lymphatic system or damage that reduces the number or flow of lymphatic channels. Although the exact mechanism by which mTOR inhibitors like sirolimus cause lymphedema is unclear, it is known that these drugs can inhibit lymphatic proliferation by blocking the VEGF-C and VEGF-D pathways.1 While cases of lymphedema associated with sirolimus use have been reported in the literature, this is the first documented case in a pediatric patient with autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Although it is challenging to definitively attribute the cause of lymphedema to sirolimus, the complete resolution following drug discontinuation and the lack of other causes suggest a strong association. Clinicians should be aware of lymphedema as a potential rare side effect of sirolimus in pediatric patients with autoimmune hemolytic anemia and immune deficiency and consider discontinuing the drug early to prevent irreversible damage.
