Controllable Patching for Compute-Adaptive Surrogate Modeling of Partial Differential Equations
Payel Mukhopadhyay, Michael McCabe, Ruben Ohana, Miles Cranmer

Abstract
Patch-based transformer surrogates have become increasingly effective for modeling spatiotemporal dynamics, but the fixed patch size is a major limitation for budget-conscience deployment in production. We introduce two lightweight, architecture-agnostic modules—the Convolutional Kernel Modulator (CKM) and Convolutional Stride Modulator (CSM)—that enable dynamic patch size control at inference in patch-based models, without retraining or accuracy loss. Combined with a cyclic patch-size rollout, our method mitigates patch artifacts and improves long-term stability for video-like prediction tasks. Applied to a range of challenging 2D and 3D PDE benchmarks, our approach improves rollout fidelity and runtime efficiency. To our knowledge, this is the first framework to enable inference-time patch-size tunability in patch-based PDE surrogates. Its plug-and-play design makes it broadly applicable across architectures—establishing a general foundation for compute-adaptive modeling in PDE surrogate tasks 1 1 1 Full codebase will be made publicly available upon publication in this repository. .

Introduction

Numerical methods have long been the gold standard for simulating spatio-temporal dynamics in systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). Numerical solvers offer convergence guarantees, exploit problem sparsity, and provide fine-grained control of the accuracy-compute trade-off through configurable resolution
[
1
,
2
]
. This last aspect is of
vital importance as it is well-known that certain aspects
of physics can only be resolved with sufficient resolution
so that the choice of resolution is driven by both physical necessity and the availability of compute
[
3
,
4
]
.

In recent years, deep learning and particularly transformers have emerged as a powerful framework for training surrogate models
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
Often derived from computer vision methods such as the Vision Transformer (ViT
[
9
]
), these models segment discretized fields into distinct, non-overlapping patches, converting them into tokens for the transformer. This results in a downsampling of the input, an essential step as self-attention cost scales quadratically with token count. While initially expensive to train, this cost can be amortized by repeated usage in problems such as forecasting
[
10
]
, PDE-constrained optimization
[
11
]
, and parameter inference
[
12
,
13
]
.

However, the inflexible computational cost of the ViT-based framework can become an undue burden as models are deployed in production. At a fixed input resolution, a ViT-based model incurs a fixed cost, regardless of problem complexity or the budget of the user. This problem is not unique to physical simulation - notably, NLP researchers have explored variable-cost solutions, such as switching between models of different expense
[
14
]
, adjusting hidden dimension size
[
15
]
, or other test-time scaling methods
[
16
]
.

While the problem is not unique to physical settings, the physics domain does offer more natural paths toward adaptive compute. It is well understood in physical modeling that there are often hard resolution thresholds required for resolving fine-scale features like shocks or wave-fronts
[
17
]
. In many problems, these small scales can influence larger scales overtime and thus resolving them can lead to significant accuracy gains. For ViT-style models, the internal resolution is entirely determined by the patch size used for tokenization. This can influence both model accuracy and computational cost
[
9
,
18
]
. Smaller patches act to reduce the level of downsampling, allowing the transformer to resolve finer details with greater accuracy (Fig.
3
) but at significant cost. Thus, we seek a compute-adaptive approach for balancing the accuracy and budget demands of users by controlling the model’s internal patch resolution.

To address this issue, this paper introduces two different strategies for adjusting downsampling rates in patch-based tokenization blocks enabling
controllable resolution transformers
and giving users more precise control over their desired location on compute-accuracy frontier.
Convolutional Kernel Modulation (CKM)
utilizes the patch-resizing of FlexiViT
[
19
]
in both the encoder and decoder while
Convolutional Stride Modulation (CSM)
utilizes a static convolutional kernel but adjusts the compression rate directly by modulating the stride. These two strategies are visualized in Fig.
1
.

Crucially, this flexibility opens up new modeling capabilities that are simply unavailable to static-patch architectures. For example, we show that during autoregressive rollout — where errors can accumulate over time — alternating patch sizes across timesteps mitigates patch-induced artifacts such as harmonic spectral spikes (see Fig.
2
). This artifact reduction highlights the emergent benefits of enabling patch/stride diversity at inference, leading to broader implications for stability and robustness in long-horizon surrogate modeling.

Contributions.

We propose a general framework for enabling compute-adaptive inference in transformer-based PDE surrogates. Our contributions span model design, architectural generality, and empirical insights:

1.

Controllable Tokenization:
We introduce
Convolutional Kernel Modulation (CKM)
and
Convolutional Stride Modulation (CSM)
as two approaches for controllable tokenization. Used with randomized compression rates during training, they allow test-time compute-adaptive accuracy.

2.

Architecture-agnostic deployment:
These flexible patching and striding strategies operate at the tokenization level of the encoder and decoder, and are compatible with a broad class of transformer architectures. We show this generality using two different types of standard base transformer processors – vanilla ViT
[
9
]
with full attention and axial ViT
[
21
]
.

3.

Competitive to fixed patch size baselines:
Across a diverse suite of 2D and 3D PDE datasets from the Well collection
[
20
]
, CKM and CSM consistently match or outperform fixed-patch size surrogates at multiple token counts—despite being trained
once
. This holds for both next-step prediction and long autoregressive rollouts. Our models are also shown to be competitive with a range of state-of-the-art non-patch-based baselines, underscoring their broader importance.

4.

Patch Artifact Reduction:
Leveraging the CKM and CSM models mentioned above, we introduce a novel rollout strategy that mitigates patch-induced artifacts (spikes at harmonics of the inverse patch size, see Fig.
2
). This significantly enhances the quality of long-term predictions, especially in capturing finer-scale physical features. This artifact reduction emerges naturally from the ability to modulate tokenization over time—demonstrating an additional benefit of flexible inference.

Background and related work

Notations.
We focus on autoregressive surrogate models for time-evolving PDE systems, where the goal is to predict the next state of a discretized physical field from a short temporal context. This setup mirrors video prediction on a uniform grid. We refer to our proposed CKM/CSM models as
compute-elastic
, meaning they support tunable patch or stride resolution at inference time. Unless otherwise stated, scalability refers to this token-level flexibility, not mesh-level resolution changes.

Architecture-agnostic tokenization for PDE surrogates.

CKM and CSM are architecture-agnostic tokenization strategies applied at the encoder and decoder stages. They control the patch/stride configuration for spatial downsampling while remaining independent of the transformer architecture. We demonstrate compatibility with standard vanilla ViTs (full attention)
[
9
]
and axial ViTs
[
22
]
. This modularity makes CKM and CSM flexible plug-ins for a wide range of transformer-based PDE surrogates, enabling adaptive deployment without redesign. To our knowledge, this is the first work to propose such flexible, architecture-agnostic tokenization for PDE surrogates.

Flexible encoder & decoder frameworks

Overview.
CKM and CSM operate as encoder-decoder modules within a ViT based architecture for autoregressive PDE prediction. The transformer core applies a sequence of temporal attention, spatial attention, and MLP blocks (App.
B.2
). Crucially, our framework is architecture-agnostic—we test it with axial attention and vanilla ViTs (and also a recent model– Continuous ViT
[
63
]
in App.
D
).

Utility of compute-elastic models.
As illustrated in Fig.
3
, increasing the number of tokens—equivalent to using smaller patch sizes in models with fixed patches—enhances the accuracy of autoregressive prediction tasks, but at a higher computational cost. The cost for models trained with fixed patch sizes cannot be controlled at inference time. This limits the utility of these models in budget-aware production settings and motivate the exploration of compute-elastic models for PDE modeling with ViT-inspired architectures.

Research in the broader machine learning community has extensively demonstrated the value of scale
[
64
,
65
]
. As transformer-based surrogate models similarly scale in parameter count, ensuring inference-time scalability is crucial, particularly to ensure these models are able to be used in compute-constrained environments. To avoid any confusion, by inference scalability, we mean patching/striding scalability or tunability, and not scalability in mesh resolution. Training and maintaining multiple fixed-patch models is a burden especially as very large models, such as large pretrained or foundation models are being built
[
7
,
8
,
66
]
. In such a scenario, if our compute-elastic strategies can enable training of a
single
model without retraining for multiple patch resolutions without any loss in accuracy, these strategies can form the building blocks for larger scale foundation models for PDEs.

Patching.

Given an input image with height
H
and width
W
, ViTs will divide the image into non-overlapping patches of size
k\times k
, producing

N=\lfloor H/k\rfloor\cdot\lfloor W/k\rfloor

tokens. For global attention, transformer cost scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
, and for axial attention, as
\mathcal{O}(N\sqrt{N})

[
22
]
. In convolutional tokenization, patches are extracted via kernels of size
k
and stride
s
, resulting in a token count

N\coloneqq N_{h}\cdot N_{w},\quad\text{for}\quad N_{h}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{H-k}{s}\right\rfloor+1,\quad N_{w}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{W-k}{s}\right\rfloor+1,\quad

(1)

While most ViT based architectures use
k=s
, our CKM and CSM modules decouple these parameters to flexibly control token count at inference.

Convolutional Kernel Modulator (CKM).

CKM introduces dynamic patch size selection within
convolutional encoder and decoder blocks, enabling a model to tokenize input fields using patch sizes

k

\in
{4, 8, 16} at each forward pass. These powers-of-two sizes reflect standard discretizations in
PDE data, and contrast with most ViT-based surrogates that fix patch size at 16 throughout training and inference
[
7
,
66
]
.

Consider input
x\in\mathcal{X}^{T}
for some input space
\mathcal{X}^{T}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{M\times H\times W\times T\times C}
, with
M,T
and
C
being the batch size, time context and input channels respectively. CKM resizes a base convolutional kernel
w^{\text{base}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\text{base}}\times k_{\text{base}}\times C\times C^{\prime}}
using PI–resize
[
19
]
(App.
A
):

w=B^{\mathsf{T}\dagger}w^{\text{base}},\quad x_{\text{enc}}=\operatorname{Conv}(x,w,\texttt{stride}=k),

(2)

where
B\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k_{\text{base}}}
is a bicubic interpolation matrix and
B^{\mathsf{T}\dagger}
its pseudoinverse. Tokens are processed by the transformer processor (this transformer can have any architecture), producing latents
x_{\text{lat}}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{lat}}
for some latent space
\mathcal{X}_{\text{lat}}
. The decoder reuses the same
k
and
w
to reconstruct:

\hat{x}=\operatorname{ConvTranspose}(x_{\text{lat}},w,\texttt{stride}=k),

(3)

yielding a next-step prediction
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{B\times H\times W\times 1\times C}
. Pseudocode is in App.
A.5
.

Comparison with FlexiViT.
While FlexiViT introduced flexible patching in image classification via a single-stage encoder
[
19
]
, it lacked a decoder and was not designed for spatiotemporal forecasting. CKM generalizes this idea, enabling PI–resize modulation across
any
convolutional stage—encoder or decoder, single- or multi-stage—as long as convolutional weights are available. FlexiViT is thus a special case within the broader CKM framework.

Modular Applicability.
In our vanilla ViT and Axial ViT models, we adopt two-stage convolutional encoders and decoders following the hMLP architecture
[
67
]
, which has recently been adopted for large-scale PDE surrogates
[
66
,
7
]
, and apply CKM independently at each stage.

Convolutional Stride Modulator (CSM).

CSM enables flexible downsampling by sampling the stride
s\in\{4,8,16\}
at each forward pass. Given input
x\in\mathcal{X}^{T}
, we pad the input with learned tokens (informed by boundary conditions) to avoid edge artifacts. Using a fixed kernel
k^{\text{base}}
, the encoder produces:

x_{\text{enc}}=\operatorname{Conv}(x,k^{\text{base}},\texttt{stride}=s),

(4)

which is passed through a transformer to obtain
x_{\text{lat}}
. The decoder applies a transposed convolution:

\hat{x}=\operatorname{ConvTranspose}(x_{\text{lat}},k^{\text{base}},\texttt{stride}=s),

(5)

to generate the autoregressive prediction
\hat{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{1}
. Like CKM, CSM is compatible with both single-stage and multi-stage patching schemes. Together, CKM and CSM offer general strategies for flexifying convolutional tokenization in ViTs. Pseudocode is in App.
A.6
.

Novel rollout strategy.

In traditional autoregressive forecasting, patch size is fixed across time. Our compute-elastic modules unlock a novel strategy: alternating patching during rollout. At each step, the model cycles through
k\in\{4,8,16\}
for CKM (or
s\in\{4,8,16\}
for CSM). This test-time scheduling is entirely decoupled from training and enables new temporal patterns in tokenization.

Surprisingly, we find this alternating rollout
suppresses patch artifacts
—periodic errors that appear as harmonics in the spectral residuals (see Fig.
2
). These artifacts are ubiquitous in fixed-patch ViT based architectures, regardless of attention type (vanilla, axial, Swin), and cannot be removed through training alone (App.
E
). Alternating rollouts yield cleaner, more stable long-horizon predictions. Importantly, this discovery was only possible due to the test-time flexibility provided by our framework—a capability entirely absent from prior PDE surrogates.

Across all datasets and token configurations, CKM and CSM consistently match or outperform their fixed-patch counterparts—despite being single, compute-elastic models. Across all datasets, we observe a consistent trend: increasing the number of tokens at inference—by using smaller patch sizes (CKM) or strides (CSM)—leads to improved VRMSE, but it is more expensive computationally. \sim
2.

These results reinforce that token count serves as a natural proxy for compute-accuracy tradeoff in patch-based PDE surrogates. Importantly, CKM and CSM allow a single model to be flexibly deployed at different compute budgets, without retraining, unlike fixed-patch baselines. We additionally evaluate performance across spatial frequencies using the binned spectral normalized mean squared error (BSNMSE). Across datasets, CKM and CSM consistently match or outperform fixed patch models in all frequency bands, highlighting their ability to preserve both coarse and fine-scale features. [

This ability to dynamically scale computational resources at inference is, to our knowledge, new in the PDE emulator literature. While large language models leverage inference-time optimizations such as entropy-based techniques, PDE surrogate models typically lack such flexibility. Our compute-elastic models address this gap by enabling adaptive patches and strides, allowing dynamic resource allocation to balance accuracy and efficiency at inference.

4.3
Improved rollouts with compute elastic models

A key advantage of compute-elastic models is their ability to enable novel inference-time rollout strategies that are inaccessible to fixed-architecture surrogates. We introduce one such strategy: an alternating-patch rollout, where CKM and CSM models dynamically cycle through patch or stride sizes of 4, 8, and 16 at each prediction step. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration in the PDE surrogate modeling literature where inference-time patching is not only treated as a tunable knob, but actively used to improve long-horizon prediction fidelity.

This strategy significantly reduces a persistent issue in patch-based architectures:
patch artifacts
—grid-like distortions that commonly arise in autoregressive rollouts, especially for fixed patching. These artifacts, prevalent across ViT variants (vanilla, axial, Swin), stem not from training instability or data quality, but from the limitations of static tokenization (App.
E
for more extended evidence).

Across both axial and vanilla transformer backbones, compute-elastic models consistently outperform fixed patch models—often by large margins—demonstrating greater long-horizon stability. Conclusion

We introduced compute-elastic encoder/decoder modules—CKM and CSM —that allow inference-time control over patch and stride sizes in transformer-based PDE surrogates. Across challenging 2D and 3D benchmarks, these flexible models consistently match or exceed fixed-patch baselines while enabling real-time trade-offs between accuracy and computational cost. This adaptability is critical as surrogate models scale toward general-purpose, deployable architectures.

Beyond accuracy–cost control, token-level modulation enables new rollout strategies. Cyclically varying patch/stride sizes during rollout significantly reduces long-horizon artifacts—a persistent issue in ViT-based surrogates—unlocking capabilities previously unavailable in PDE modeling. Our rollout strategy exemplifies how such control can expose patch-sensitive behaviors, and could pave the way for discovering other dynamics that static architectures obscure.

Future directions.
If compute resources were not a limiting factor, our study could be extended along several directions. For example, we could test our CKM/CSM modules for flexifying non-ViT but patch-based architectures, such as AFNO

We anticipate that our flexible patching modules can generalize across ViT and non-ViT backbones alike. Our study provides a foundation for future exploration of flexible, compute-aware modeling strategies in the PDE surrogate literature, and opens the door for building next-generation surrogate models with tunable inference costs, modular components, and improved generalization.

Appendix A
Derivation of the PI-Resize Matrix for Convolutional Kernel Modulators

A.1
Motivation for Kernel Resizing

The CKM-based model dynamically adjusts convolutional kernel sizes at each forward pass while maintaining a fixed architecture. This enables variable patch sizes (
4,8,16
), which is crucial for handling different spatial resolutions while ensuring compatibility with the ViT.

However, this flexibility introduces a problem: the CNN encoder, which extracts ViT tokens, is trained with a
fixed base convolutional kernel
. To ensure that features extracted at different patch sizes remain aligned, we use a
resize transformation
, which projects the base kernel to a dynamically selected kernel size
[
19
]
.

A.2
Mathematical Formulation

Let:

•

W^{\text{base}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k^{\text{base}}\times k^{\text{base}}\times c_{\text{in}}\times c_{\text{out}}}
be the learned CNN weights for the
fixed base kernel size

k^{\text{base}}
.

•

W\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k\times c_{\text{in}}\times c_{\text{out}}}
be the resized kernel for a dynamically selected kernel size
k
.

•

B
be an
interpolation matrix
(e.g., bilinear, bicubic) that resizes
input patches
from size
k
to size
k^{\text{base}}
.

•

x\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times k\times k\times c_{\text{in}}}
be an
input patch
, where:

–

B
is the batch size.

–

k\times k
is the
local receptive field
defined by the kernel.

–

c_{\text{in}}
is the number of input channels.

Intuitively, the goal is to find a new set of patch-
embedding weights
W
such that the tokens of the resized
patch match the tokens of the original patch

W^{\text{base}}\ast x\approx W\ast(Bx),

(6)

where
\ast
denotes convolution. The goal is to solve for
W
. Mathematically, this is an optimization problem.

A.3
Least-Squares Optimization for PI-Resize

A.3.1
Expanding the Objective

We aim to expand the expectation:

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\left[(x^{T}W^{\text{base}}-x^{T}B^{T}W)^{2}\right].

(7)

Since inner products can be rewritten as matrix-vector multiplications, we note that:

\langle x,W^{\text{base}}\rangle=x^{T}W^{\text{base}}.

Step 1: Expand the Square

Using the identity
(a-b)^{2}=a^{2}-2ab+b^{2}
, we expand:

\displaystyle(x^{T}W^{\text{base}}-x^{T}B^{T}W)^{2}

\displaystyle=(x^{T}W^{\text{base}})^{2}-2x^{T}W^{\text{base}}(x^{T}B^{T}W)+(x^{T}B^{T}W)^{2}.

(8)

Step 2: Take the Expectation

Now, we take expectation over
x\sim\mathcal{X}
:

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\left[(x^{T}W^{\text{base}})^{2}-2x^{T}W^{\text{base}}(x^{T}B^{T}W)+(x^{T}B^{T}W)^{2}\right].

(9)

Using the definition of the covariance matrix:

\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}[xx^{T}],

(10)

we apply the linearity of expectation to each term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[(x^{T}W^{\text{base}})^{2}]

\displaystyle=W^{\text{base},T}\Sigma W^{\text{base}},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[(x^{T}B^{T}W)^{2}]

\displaystyle=W^{T}B\Sigma B^{T}W,

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}[x^{T}W^{\text{base}}(x^{T}B^{T}W)]

\displaystyle=W^{\text{base},T}\Sigma B^{T}W.

(13)

Step 3: Write in Matrix Form

Substituting these into the expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}\left[(x^{T}W^{\text{base}}-x^{T}B^{T}W)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=W^{\text{base},T}\Sigma W^{\text{base}}-2W^{\text{base},T}\Sigma B^{T}W+W^{T}B\Sigma B^{T}W.

(14)

The above expression can be re-written as:

\|W^{base}-B^{T}W\|^{2}_{\Sigma}=(W^{base,T}-W^{T}B)\Sigma(W^{base}-B^{T}W).

(15)

Conclusion

This result expresses the squared error between the transformed weight matrix
W
and the resized weight matrix
B^{T}W^{\text{base}}
, weighted by the covariance matrix
\Sigma
. The quadratic form measures the deviation in terms of feature space alignment, ensuring that the transformation remains consistent with the learned base kernel.

The optimal solution minimizing Eq.
15
is given by

W=\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}B^{T}\right)^{\dagger}\sqrt{\Sigma}W^{base}

(16)

where
\left(\sqrt{\Sigma}B^{T}\right)^{\dagger}\sqrt{\Sigma}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse matrix.

A.4
Final Transformation and Implementation

Thus, at each forward pass, we apply the following steps:

1.

Randomly select a kernel size

k\in\{4,8,16\}
.

2.

Compute the interpolation matrix

B
that resizes the input patch from
k
to
k^{\text{base}}
.

3.

Transform the kernel weights
using:

W=(B^{T})^{\dagger}W^{\text{base}}.

(17)

4.

Apply the resized kernel

W
for convolution.

This ensures that dynamically changing kernel sizes maintain compatibility with the fixed architecture while enabling flexible patch sizes.

B.4
VRMSE metric

The variance scaled mean squared error (VMSE): it is the MSE normalized by the variance of the truth. It has been used for the benchmarking of the Well
[
20
]
.

\mathrm{VMSE}(u,v)=\frac{\langle\lvert u-v\rvert^{2}\rangle}{(\langle\lvert u-\bar{u}\rvert^{2}\rangle+\epsilon)}.

We chose to report its square root variant, the VRMSE:

\mathrm{VRMSE}(u,v)=\frac{\langle\lvert u-v\rvert^{2}\rangle^{1/2}}{(\langle\lvert u-\bar{u}\rvert^{2}\rangle+\epsilon)^{1/2}}.

Note that, since
\mathrm{VRMSE}(u,\bar{u})\approx 1
, having
\mathrm{VRMSE}>1
indicates worse results than an accurate estimation of the spatial mean
\bar{u}
.

Appendix C
Binned spectral error analysis

C.1
Definitions

We investigate another metric for the robustness of our results, namely the binned spectral mean squared error (BSMSE). BSNMSE, introduced in
[
20
]
, quantifies the mean squared error after applying a bandpass filter to the input fields over a specific frequency band
\mathcal{B}
. It is defined as:

\mathrm{BSMSE}_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)=\langle\lvert u_{\mathcal{B}}-v_{\mathcal{B}}\rvert^{2}\rangle,

(18)

where the bandpass-filtered field
u_{\mathcal{B}}
is given by:

u_{\mathcal{B}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\mathcal{F}\left[u\right]\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}\right].

(19)

Here,
\mathcal{F}
represents the discrete Fourier transform, and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
is an indicator function that selects frequencies within the band
\mathcal{B}
.

For each dataset, we define three disjoint frequency bands,
\mathcal{B}_{1}
,
\mathcal{B}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{B}_{3}
, which correspond to low, intermediate, and high spatial frequencies, respectively. These bands are determined by partitioning the wavenumber magnitudes evenly on a logarithmic scale.

The
binned spectral normalized mean squared error (BSNMSE)
is a normalized variant of the BSMSE metric, adjusting for the energy within each frequency band:

\mathrm{BSNMSE}_{\mathcal{B}}(u,v)=\frac{\langle\lvert u_{\mathcal{B}}-v_{\mathcal{B}}\rvert^{2}\rangle}{\langle|v_{\mathcal{B}}|^{2}\rangle}.

(20)

A
\mathrm{BSNMSE}
value of 1 or greater indicates that the model performs worse than simply predicting zero coefficients at that scale.

CViT experiments.
We further evaluate our flexible patching strategy within the Continuous Vision Transformer (CViT) architecture
[
63
]
, a recent vision transformer-based model tailored for PDE surrogate modeling. CViT features a patch-based transformer encoder coupled with a novel, grid-based continuous decoder that directly queries spatiotemporal locations. As the decoder operates without patch-based tokenization, we apply our kernel modulation (CKM) only to the encoder, leaving the rest of the architecture unchanged.

A flexified CViT therefore refers to the encoder being a CKM. For CViT
[
63
]
, whose original design features a single-stage encoder (instead of multi-stage hMLP we used for our vanilla ViT and axial ViT experiments) and a decoder based on grid-based querying, CKM is applied only to the encoder, for the single stage; because the idea is to see if flexification on top of the base fixed patch architecture helps or not. These use cases demonstrate CKM’s modularity and adaptability: it can flexify arbitrary convolutional patching pipelines, without requiring architectural changes or any overengineering to the base convolutional or attention mechanism or task head.

Since CViT is designed for 2D problems, we restrict our evaluation to 2D datasets, ensuring a fair and focused test of patch flexibility without altering any other model components. This experiment demonstrates that our flexible tokenization strategy is architecture-agnostic and can be modularly integrated into advanced PDE models like CViT.

Table
6
shows that a flexified CViT consistently outperforms fixed patch size CViT models across four diverse 2D PDE datasets. As in earlier experiments, reducing patch size (e.g., from 16 to 4) increases token count and compute, but yields significantly improved accuracy. Importantly, a flexified CViT enables this accuracy-compute trade-off at inference without retraining, demonstrating the modularity and architectural agnosticism of our approach. This result also underscores how adaptive patching can be extended to complex hybrid architectures like CViT, unlocking new modes of inference-time flexibility in scientific surrogate modeling.

CViT Patch Embeddings.

The ViT encoder takes as input a gridded representation of the input function
u
, yielding a spatio-temporal data tensor
\mathbf{u}\in\mathrm{R}^{T\times H\times W\times D}
with
D
channels. The input is patchified into tokens
\mathbf{u}_{p}\in\mathrm{R}^{T\times\frac{H}{P}\times\frac{W}{P}\times C}
by tokenizing each 2D spatial frame independently, following the process used in standard Vision Transformers
[
9
]
. The patching process involves a single convolutional downsampling layer. Trainable 1D temporal and 2D spatial positional embeddings are added to each token. These are absolute position encodings, unlike the RoPE encodings we used for vanilla and axial ViT experiments.

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{pe}=\mathbf{u}_{p}+\text{PE}_{t}+\text{PE}_{s},\quad\text{PE}_{t}\in\mathrm{R}^{T\times 1\times 1\times C},\quad\text{PE}_{s}\in\mathrm{R}^{1\times\frac{H}{P}\times\frac{W}{P}\times C}.

(21)

The above description of CViT has already been detailed in the original paper
[
63
]
. Our main experiments were to flexify this CViT architecture and show that under a given compute budget, training a flexible CViT is much more advantageous than training multiple fixed patch CViT models. To this end, we adapt CKM to flexify the convolutional layer of CViT, randomizing the patch size at the encoding stage. Additionally, since base CViT has absolute learned position embedding, we resized the position embedding at each step of the forward pass as well using bilinear interpolation to make it compatible with the randomized patch size.

Goals with flexified CViT:
Our goal with the CViT experiments is to show that, similar to the axial ViT and vanilla ViT results, CViT also benefits from flexification. In other words, given a fixed compute budget, it is much more beneficial to train a flexible CViT model than to train and maintain multiple static resolution CViT models. This leads to the creation of a flexible CViT, similar to the flexible versions of axial and vanilla ViTs we created earlier.

First we show that similar to axial and vanilla ViT, CViT also consistently gains in accuracy as the patch size, aka token counts is decreased (Table 6). Additionally, we show that our alternating patch strategy for CViT leads to dramatically better accuracies than the fixed patch models (Table 7). Through these experiments, we enable the enable the flexification of this recent architecture. This experiment further demonstrates the architecture agnostic–ness of our methods; meaning flexification can be made compatible with a range of base architectures, as exemplified by our results in the main text, augmented the flexification of this advanced hybrid architecture like CViT. Flexified CViT is compute-adaptive meaning it can adapt to various downstream compute/accuracy requirements at inference after being trained only
once
, eliminating the need to train and maintain multiple models.

Training details for CViT experiments.

We follow a consistent training protocol to ensure a fair comparison between fixed-patch and flexified CViT models. All models use the CViT-S architecture from
[
63
]
, configured with 5 encoder layers, an embedding dimension of 384, MLP width 384, and 6 attention heads. The spatial embedding grid size is matched to the input resolution of each dataset.

We fix the learning rate at
10^{-4}
, chosen after a hyperparameter search over
\{10^{-2},10^{-3},10^{-4}\}
. Weight decay is set to
10^{-5}
. Batch size is chosen to fully utilize GPU memory for each dataset. Training is conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPU.

Due to compute limitations, training epochs vary across datasets, but remain consistent within each dataset. Concretely:

•

For
shear_flow
, each fixed-patch model (patch sizes 4, 8, and 16) is trained for 30 epochs.

•

For
active_matter
, each is trained for 40 epochs.

•

For
turbulent_radiative_layer_2D
, each is trained for 160 epochs.

The corresponding flexified CViT models (i.e., with CKM) are trained using the combined compute budget of all three fixed-patch models. This allows a direct, compute-equivalent comparison. As described in Sec. 5.2 of the main paper, this setup addresses a practical question: under a fixed training budget, is it better to train multiple static models or one flexible model? If the flexible model enables training without any loss in validation accuracy, we have a flexible model by training only once eliminating the need to train and maintain multiple fixed patch models.

Patch artifacts are widely present in patch based PDE surrogates. In this section, we will provide extended evidence for the existence of these artifacts. Note that these patch artifacts are a problem for the architectures where both the encoder and decoder are patch based, so this is not a problem for an architecture like CViT.

SineNet:

This model consists of multiple sequentially connected U-shaped network blocks, referred to as
waves

[
70
]
. While traditional U-Net architectures use skip connections to support multi-scale feature processing, the authors argue that forcing features to evolve across layers leads to temporal misalignment in these skip connections, ultimately limiting model performance. In contrast, SineNet progressively evolves high-resolution features across multiple stages, reducing feature misalignment within each stage and improving temporal coherence. This model has achieved SOTA performance on a range of complex PDE tasks. Refer to the original paper for more details.

We used their open source code from:
https://github.com/divelab/AIRS/blob/main/OpenPDE/SineNet/pdearena/pdearena/modules/sinenet_dual.py

2\times 10^{-4}

10^{-4},2\times 10^{-4},5\times 10^{-4},10^{-3}

F-FNO:

Fourier neural operators
[
72

[
69

which improves over the original FNO by learning features

\sim

Transolver:

In this model
[
71

The model was found to achieve SOTA performance in a range of benchmarks including large-scale industrial simulations, including car and airfoil designs. We use their open source codebase and adapt it to train on the Well. We used 8 hidden layers, 8 heads, 256 hidden channels, and 128 slices. This amounted to a model of size 11M. This configuration matches the configuration presented in their main experiments. Training was performed on a single Nvidia A100-80GB GPU. Batch size was set to be 16.

Appendix H
Neural operator based methods benefitting from smaller patch sizes

To show that patch size is a critical parameter for some neural operator based methods as well, we conducted a preliminary experiment of the AFNO
[
74
]
baseline provided with the Well package. We took the
turbulent_radiative_layer_2D
dataset and trained the AFNO model with a patch size of 16 and 4. Overall, we found that reducing AFNO’s patch size from 16 to 4 improves test VRMSE from 0.298 to 0.211 on the this dataset. Additionally, patch size was found to be a critical parameter in a recent DPOT
[
75
]
model which performed large-scale pretraining on PDEs.

Overall, these show that patch size is an important parameter in setups other than the ViT frameworks we considered in this paper. These findings suggest our flexible models can be valuable for extending compute-adaptive capabilities to a broader class of neural operator architectures, enabling task-aware inference-time control in both pretrained and task-specific surrogate models.
