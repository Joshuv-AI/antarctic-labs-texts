Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation in patients with traumatic cardiac arrest during the acute phase following injury: a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Romana Erblich, Justyna Swol, Ben Singer, Elizabeth Powell, Jürgen Lipusch, Marius Knöll, Karim Brohi, Jens Meier, Martin W Dünser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is considered infeasible in patients with traumatic cardiac arrest (TCA).MethodsWe conducted a systematic literature review applying a multi-layered search strategy to summarise and meta-analyse published evidence on the use of ECPR in TCA patients. We focused on TCA occurring during the pre-hospital and early in-hospital phase approximately corresponding to the first six hours following injury.ResultsNine studies (retrospective study, n = 4; case series, n = 2; case report, n = 3) including 84 TCA patients undergoing ECPR were identified. All ECPR procedures were performed in-hospital. The median age was 39 (29–51) years. Two patients (2%) were < 18 years. Sixty-seven (80%) patients were male. The injury mechanism was blunt in 54% and penetrating in 46%. Thirty-three (39%) patients survived to hospital discharge. All survivors recovered completely or with minor to moderate neurological disability. Survivors more frequently sustained TCA due to respiratory failure (56 vs. 18%, p = 0.008), experienced TCA out-of-hospital (36 vs. 14%, p = 0.04), had a shorter duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation before ECPR [43 (25–71) vs. 78 (54–103) minutes, p = 0.02] and underwent surgery following ECPR more often (100 vs. 0%, p = 0.001) than non-survivors. While all survivors sustained chest injuries, no patient with traumatic brain injuries survived. The median initial extracorporeal blood flow reported in twenty-six patients was 3.6 (2.8–4.0) L/min. One study reported infeasibility to maintain adequate extracorporeal blood flows in four of eleven (36%) patients. While 21 of 27 patients (74%) received no initial anticoagulation, unfractionated heparin was administered in the remaining six patients. Of the 32 patients, in whom complications were reported, 18 subjects (56%) experienced one or more complications. Complications related to the extracorporeal circuit (25%) and neurological (22%), renal (16%), haemorrhagic (16%) and cardiovascular (16%) complications were reported most frequently.ConclusionsThis comprehensive systematic review of the literature identified 84 patients, who were treated with ECPR because of a TCA during the acute phase following injury. The results of this meta-analysis are hypothesis-generating and suggest that ECPR may be feasible and potentially beneficial in selected patients with TCA. Future research on ECPR in TCA is warranted. Entry of respective cases into international registries appears essential.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-025-01534-9.Keywords: Traumatic cardiac arrest, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, ECPR, Trauma, Survival

Background

The majority of deaths from traumatic injuries occurs during the acute phase following trauma, often before hospital admission [1, 2]. Cardiac arrest from traumatic aetiologies carries high mortality rates approaching 95–100% in some cohorts [3, 4]. While some injuries are unsurvivable, TCA may result from non-cardiac causes (e.g., haemorrhage, severe traumatic brain injury, respiratory failure including tension pneumothorax) or direct cardiac trauma (e.g., cardiac tamponade, massive cardiac contusion) [3, 4]. Contemporary guidelines for the management of patients with TCA focus on haemorrhage control, airway protection, ventilation with 100% oxygen, reversal of hypovolaemia, and relief of tension pneumothorax or cardiac tamponade [5]. However, real-life data indicate that TCA often remains refractory, even if reversible causes have been addressed [4]. Various advanced interventions are currently under investigation to increase the chances of survival from TCA. These comprise resuscitative thoracotomy [6], resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta [7], selective aortic arch perfusion as well as emergency preservation and resuscitation [8].

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) has evolved as a promising therapeutic modality in non-traumatic cardiac arrest with survival rates and favourable neurological outcomes around 30% in selected patients [9, 10]. Many ECPR programs, however, consider TCA a contraindication to ECPR because of the unknown feasibility to establish adequate extracorporeal blood flow and bleeding risks. Hypothetically, ECPR could be a viable treatment option for patients in TCA such as those with direct cardiac injuries or refractory respiratory failure. Furthermore, even TCA patients, in whom intractable haemorrhagic shock has resulted in global myocardial ischaemia and/or severe electrolyte shifts – both conditions refractory to fluid resuscitation or vasopressor therapy – may theoretically benefit from ECPR. Understanding more about feasibility, complications and outcome effects of ECPR in TCA could therefore help to elucidate the potential of this intervention to improve the dismal chances of survival in this mostly young and healthy patient population.

In this review, we sought to systematically search, summarise and whenever possible meta-analyse published evidence on the use of ECPR in patients with TCA. Based on the identified evidence we aimed to find answers to the following questions: 1) Which patients with TCA underwent ECPR? 2) How feasible was ECPR in patients with TCA? 3) What were the outcomes of patients who underwent ECPR because of TCA? 4) How did survivors differ from non-survivors? 5) What were complications associated with ECPR in patients with TCA? 6) What were practical considerations of ECPR in patients with TCA?

Definitions

We considered TCA as the clinical phenotype of a non-palpable central pulse in an unconscious patient with trauma [defined as tissue damage due to physical (e.g., blunt, penetrating or deceleration force) energy transfer] as the underlying aetiology [4]. Although the majority of TCAs are encountered at the scene of injury, cardiac arrest due to traumatic causes may also arise during the first hours after injury [3, 4]. While unsurvivable body destruction, severe traumatic brain injury and exsanguination from major vessel rupture are common causes of TCA occurring immediately after injury, uncontrolled haemorrhage, respiratory failure and obstructive shock including tension pneumo- and haemothorax as well as cardiac tamponade may develop at any time during the acute phase following injury [11]. To acknowledge this and evaluate the potential effects of ECPR not only at the scene of injury but during the acute phase following trauma, we focused on cardiac arrest from traumatic aetiology occurring during the prehospital and early in-hospital phase including treatment in the emergency department and operating room approximately corresponding to the first six hours following injury. In line with definitions by the Extracorporeal Life Support Organisation [12], we defined ECPR as the application of rapid-deployment veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation to provide circulatory support in patients, in whom conventional CPR was unsuccessful in achieving sustained return of spontaneous circulation.

Methods

This analysis was designed as a comprehensive systematic review of the literature. We conducted two separate searches of the literature aiming to identify all published cases of ECPR in patients with TCA. The first search was designed to identify studies including patients with TCA who underwent ECPR. The second search was designed to identify patients with TCA who had been included in ECPR studies not specifically focused on patients with TCA. The methodology of the first search is outlined below. The methodology of the second search is summarised in Electronic Supplementary Material Table E1. The 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) checklist guided the drafting of this manuscript (Electronic Supplementary Material Table E2) [13].

Systematic review to identify published cases on ECPR in patients with TCA

This systematic review was conducted according to a pre-published protocol, which had been registered at an international systematic review registry (PROSPERO; https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251112961). We considered publications applying one of the following designs as eligible for inclusion: original, peer-reviewed research articles including randomised, controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, case series, and case reports. We excluded trial protocols, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, or studies only published as abstracts or posters, as well as publications in non-peer reviewed journals. All studies reporting ECPR in trauma patients (without age restrictions) experiencing a TCA during the acute phase following injury were included. We did not include studies, which reported the use of extracorporeal life support in trauma patients with non-traumatic cardiac arrest or for other indications than cardiac arrest.

The National Library of Medicine MEDLINE was used as an information source and searched through PubMed® (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) applying predefined search terms (Electronic Supplementary Material Table E3). Bibliographic references and citations of included studies as well as review articles on the topic were reviewed to identify further potentially eligible publications. Research articles published from inception of the National Library of Medicine MEDLINE (January 1, 1966) until July 31, 2025 were reviewed. On December 8, 2025, the literature search was updated and the end date of the search period extended to November 30, 2025. No language restrictions were imposed. Two authors (RE, MK) independently screened all publications. In a first step, identified records were screened by title and abstract for relevance. Subsequently, full-texts of studies as selected by the title and abstract search were reviewed by the same pair of authors. Following an in-depth analysis of full-texts and application of eligibility criteria, the agreement of both authors was necessary to include the study into the analysis. In case of disagreement, consensus was achieved by discussion among the two initial reviewers (RE, MK) or decision making by a third author (MWD). Reasons for full-text exclusion were documented.

Data from selected studies were extracted independently by two authors (RE, MWD) using an electronic data extraction form. In order to answer the questions stimulating this systematic review, the following data were extracted from all identified records whenever available: name of the first author, publication year, publication type, country of origin, number of patients included, sex, age, injury mechanism (blunt vs. penetrating), details of injury mechanism, the Injury Severity Score, body regions injured, cause of TCA, the duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation before ECPR, cannulation site and technique, first electrocardiography rhythm, initial extracorporeal blood flow, transfusion requirements, surgery performed before and/or after ECPR, anticoagulation strategy, ECPR-associated complications, duration of extracorporeal life support, as well as survival and functional status at hospital discharge or at the last time point of follow-up. In case of missing data, we attempted to obtain the information directly from the study authors. We used the Newcastle–Ottawa scale [14] to assess the risk of bias of included retrospective studies. Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools [15] were applied to assess the risk of bias of included case series and case reports.

As we could not identify any controlled trials or studies comparing ECPR with standard TCA resuscitation, we did not apply random-effects models as indicated in the pre-published protocol, but used descriptive statistical methods to summarise and meta-analyse extracted data. Categorical data were presented as absolute values with percentages. Continuous data were presented as median values with interquartile ranges. In order to identify differences between ECPR survivors and ECPR non-survivors, Chi-square (for categorical variables) and Mann–Whitney-U (for continuous variables) tests were used to compare data between patients who were alive or dead at hospital discharge. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

The first literature search of this systematic review rendered 778 records, of which nine studies were selected (Fig. 1). One excluded study [16] was notable, as veno-venous ECMO was used during TCA from respiratory failure due to complete tracheal rupture in a 39-year-old male. The patient regained spontaneous circulation one minute after initiation of extracorporeal blood flow and survived. The second search identified 121 records (Fig. 2). Although one [17] out of five randomised, controlled trials [18–21] considered cardiac arrest patients without multi-injury trauma to be eligible for enrolment, no trauma patient was randomised. Out of 116 non-randomised studies evaluating ECPR, 29 studies specifically excluded trauma patients and 77 did not mention nor include trauma patients. In three studies, we could not clarify whether patients with TCA were included or not. Seven non-randomised studies evaluating ECPR included trauma patients with cardiac arrest, none of which was specified as TCA [22–28]. In total, nine studies [29–37] (retrospective study, n = 4; case series, n = 2; case report, n = 3) reporting a total of 84 patients, who underwent ECPR because of a TCA, were included into the analysis (Fig. 1). The risk of bias of these studies is specified in Electronic Supplementary Material Figure E1.

How feasible was ECPR in patients with TCA?

All ECPR procedures were performed in-hospital. Initial extracorporeal blood flow rates were specifically given for 26 patients (Table 1). In one study [32], the authors mentioned that adequate extracorporeal blood flow could be maintained in all patients (n = 5). Without detailing extracorporeal blood flow rates, one study specifically mentioned that it was not possible to maintain adequate extracorporeal blood flows in four of eleven (36%) TCA patients undergoing ECPR [29]. The authors reported that extracorporeal life support was stopped in these four patients after a mean duration of 3.1 ± 0.7 h. Compared to TCA patients, in whom an adequate extracorporeal blood flow could be established, patients failing to achieve adequate extracorporeal blood flows had a greater mean Injury Severity Score (65 ± 9.6 vs. 46.5 ± 16.3 points; p = 0.04), a longer active bleeding time (385 ± 103 vs. 201 ± 91 min; p = 0.003), a longer duration of cardiac arrest before ECPR (79 ± 9 vs. 56 ± 24 min; p < 0.001), and required higher vasopressor/inotropic doses (308 ± 31 vs. 192 ± 51 µg/kg/min; p < 0.001) [29]. Two studies reported on the amount of blood transfusions in TCA patients undergoing ECPR [29, 30]. In one retrospective study [29], TCA patients, in whom an adequate extracorporeal blood flow could be established, received 11.9 ± 5.3 units of blood, while TCA patients, in whom no adequate extracorporeal blood flow could be established, received 18.8 ± 3.3 units of blood (p = 0.015). In one case report, the patient received 22 units of packed red cells, two litres of salvaged blood, four units of plasma, and five units of platelet concentrates during ECPR and subsequent surgery [30].

What were the outcomes of patients who underwent ECPR because of TCA?

Thirty-three of 84 (39%) patients survived to hospital discharge. Information on functional outcome was available for eleven of the 33 survivors. Hospital discharge was the longest time point at which functional outcome was reported. Until hospital discharge, all of these eleven survivors either recovered completely or with minor to moderate neurological disability.

How did survivors differ from non-survivors?

Table 2 presents meta-analysed data of all patients, survivors and non-survivors. The rate of brain injuries was lower but the rates of chest injuries, respiratory failure as the cause of TCA, out-of-hospital TCA, and surgery following ECPR were higher in survivors compared with non-survivors. Survivors had a shorter duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation before ECPR than non-survivors.

What were complications associated with ECPR in patients with TCA?

Presence or absence of ECPR-related complications was reported in 32 patients. The pooled complication rate was 56%. One retrospective study found a complication rate of 66.7% in 24 TCA patients undergoing ECPR corresponding to a median number of 1 (0–2) complications per patient. The most frequent complications in that study were complications related to the extracorporeal circuit (n = 8, 33.3%), renal complications (n = 5, 25%), haemorrhagic complications (n = 5, 20.8%), cardiovascular complications (n = 5, 20.8%), and neurological complications (n = 5, 20.8%) [37]. Another study [32] specified cerebral infarctions as a complication of ECPR in two subjects.

What were practical considerations of ECPR in patients with TCA?

Details on cannulation and anticoagulation practices applied in the nine included studies are summarised in Table 3. The initial anticoagulation strategy was described in detail for 27 patients. While 21 patients (74%) received no initial anticoagulation, unfractionated heparin was administered as a bolus dose in the remaining six patients.

Discussion

This comprehensive systematic review of the literature used a multi-layered search strategy and identified 84 patients, who were treated with ECPR because of a TCA during the acute phase following injury. While 72 patients were included into four retrospective studies, the remaining patients were extracted from cases series or single case reports. Our results confirm the assumption that patients with TCA have so far not been included into randomised, controlled trials or non-randomised studies on ECPR in refractory cardiac arrest. In line with these findings, the majority of contemporary international guidelines and recommendations on TCA or ECPR does not consider ECPR as a treatment option for trauma patients, who sustain a cardiac arrest from traumatic aetiology (Table 4) [38–44].

Which patients with TCA underwent ECPR?

Comparable to other TCA populations [4], patients undergoing ECPR because of TCA were young and predominantly male. The age range was wide with the youngest patient being 15 years and the oldest 68 years. In comparison to other studies from Western countries including patients with TCA [45, 46], the rate of penetrating injuries among TCA patients in our meta-analysis was high (46%). With a median Injury Severity Score of 38 points, TCA patients, who were treated with ECPR, suffered from a significant injury severity. Almost half of them were polytraumatised having sustained life-threatening injuries of two or more body regions. A notable finding was that the chest was the most frequently (80%) injured body region. Haemorrhagic shock followed by respiratory failure and cardiac tamponade were the three main aetiologies of TCA in this ECPR population. The finding that almost 80% of patients, who were included in this review, developed TCA only after hospital admission is an important finding. It is in contrast to the fact that most TCAs occur at the scene of injury, while subjects experiencing TCA only during the first hours following injury and hospital admission make up a smaller proportion of all trauma deaths [45, 47, 48]. This observation could, however, also be explained by the fact that no ECPR was performed on the scene of injury or before hospital admission.

How feasible was ECPR in patients with TCA?

All ECPR procedures were performed in-hospital. We extracted data on cannulation, initial extracorporeal blood flows from identified records, and blood transfusion requirements in order to assess the feasibility of ECPR in TCA. Interestingly, no cannulation failures were reported in any of the included records. This is striking given common technical difficulties to percutaneously cannulate femoral vessels in bleeding patients [7] as well as reported cannulation failure rates of up to 14% in ECPR attempts in patients with refractory cardiac arrest from non-traumatic aetiology [17]. It is thus likely that only TCA patients, in whom cannulation for ECPR was successful, were published. Accordingly, the results of our review about feasibility of ECPR in TCA must be interpreted with caution. Similarly, only one study reported on the inability to maintain adequate extracorporeal blood flows in TCA patients undergoing ECPR. Bonacchi et al. found that despite successful cannulation extracorporeal blood flow remained inadequate and led to termination of resuscitation efforts in approximately one third of subjects. The authors suggested that a high injury severity and prolonged cardiac arrest due to protracted haemorrhagic shock were risk factors that no adequate extracorporeal blood flow could be established [29]. Massive transfusions were reported in both studies indicating the number of blood units these patients required during or immediately after ECPR [29, 30].

What were the outcomes of patients who underwent ECPR because of TCA?

The hospital survival rate among patients undergoing ECPR because of TCA in this meta-analysis was 39%, which is exceptionally high compared to previous survival rates in TCA populations [4]. In view of a highly likely publication bias, it is impossible to interpret these results as absolute figures. It is, however, noteworthy that the median duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation before ECPR was 43 (25–71) minutes even in TCA survivors. A recent retrospective observational study analysing 422 patients with TCA reported that resuscitation efforts beyond 38 min in total were universally futile [49]. Similarly, a retrospective cohort study including 601 TCA patients undergoing prehospital resuscitative thoracotomy reported no survivors beyond a 15 min duration of TCA in patients with cardiac tamponade and beyond a 5 min duration of TCA in patients with exsanguination [6]. In line with previous data [50, 51], the majority of TCA survivors receiving ECPR had a good functional outcome with most subjects recovering without neurological sequelae.

How did survivors differ from non-survivors?

Despite the overall low number of patients, in whom individual data could be extracted and who could be included in the statistical comparisons between survivors and non-survivors, certain differences were noted between patients discharged alive or dead following ECPR for TCA. It is, however, important to remember that these findings must be considered hypothesis-generating, as the number of available data was low for several comparisons between survivors and non-survivors. Interestingly, all survivors sustained chest injuries, while not a single patient with isolated or concomitant traumatic brain injuries survived. Even though haemorrhage played a contributory role to TCA in one third of survivors, respiratory failure and cardiac tamponade caused TCA in almost half of patients surviving TCA following ECPR. This could suggest a potential role of ECPR, particularly in the management of patients with TCA due to respiratory failure and/or cardiac tamponade. Similar to patients treated with ECPR for non-traumatic cardiac arrest [52, 53], the duration of cardiopulmonary resuscitation before ECPR was shorter in survivors than non-survivors underlining the time-sensitivity of this intervention. Another noteworthy finding of our meta-analysis was that out of those patients, in whom the surgical status following ECPR could be extracted, all survivors underwent surgery while none of the non-survivors did. This might be a signal that ECPR has a role in bridging TCA in subjects with injuries amenable to surgical repair (e.g., cardiac tamponade, haemorrhage). Although only a minority (23%) of patients undergoing ECPR experienced TCA out-of-hospital, the survival rate in these patients was higher than in those sustaining TCA only after hospital admission. Given the low number of patients meta-analysed, this result might well be a finding by chance, but could also be interpreted as an indicator not to exclude patients with out-of-hospital TCA from ECPR studies. What were complications associated with ECPR in patients with TCA?

Complications associated with ECPR were specifically evaluated in 32 of the 84 patients identified by this systematic review. Although complications related to the extracorporeal circuit were reported in one third of patients, haemorrhagic complications, the most feared adverse event of using extracorporeal life support in trauma patients, occurred in one fifth of trauma patients undergoing ECPR in one study [37]. Once again, the bias to publish only successful cases, who did not experience major complications probably explains this very low complication rate. The latter is in remarkable contrast to the substantial morbidity associated with ECPR in patients with non-traumatic cardiac arrest [54, 55]. Similarly, complication rates of 50–80% were reported in adult trauma patients undergoing extracorporeal life support because of other conditions than cardiac arrest [56, 57].

What were practical considerations of ECPR in patients with TCA?

Apart from patient-related characteristics, several ECPR-related differences were noted between the identified TCA population and patients with non-traumatic cardiac arrest [9, 58]. These TCA-specific aspects include rather low extracorporeal blood flows, delayed initiation or avoidance of anticoagulation and short durations of extracorporeal life support (Fig. 4). Future research is needed to evaluate whether extracorporeal circuits intended for ECPR in TCA patients require different or other components than in patients with refractory cardiac arrest from non-traumatic aetiology.

Limitations

Important limitations need to be mentioned when analysing the results of this systematic review and meta-analysis. First, although we applied an in-depth and comprehensive search strategy, it is possible that some records on ECPR in patients with TCA have still been missed. Second, as indicated by the risk of bias assessment, all reports included in our meta-analysis were subject to a relevant risk of bias. In addition, the high likelihood of a publication bias is particularly relevant to consider when interpreting indicators of patient outcomes such as survival rates, as mentioned before. Third, in view of the fact that only retrospective studies, case series and case reports were included into this meta-analysis, its results are supported by only a low level of evidence prone to multiple biases and must therefore be considered hypothesis-generating. Finally, the low number of patients together with the relatively high rate of missing variables further impairs the validity of the findings.

Conclusions

This comprehensive systematic review of the literature identified 84 patients, who were treated with ECPR because of a TCA during the acute phase following injury. The results of this meta-analysis are hypothesis-generating and suggest that ECPR may be feasible and potentially beneficial in selected patients with TCA. Future research on ECPR in TCA is warranted. Entry of respective cases into international registries appears essential.
