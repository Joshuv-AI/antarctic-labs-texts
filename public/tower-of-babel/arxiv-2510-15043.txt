Linearly Stable Generalizations of ESFR Schemes
Mathias Dufresne-Piché, Siva Nadarajah

Abstract
The energy stable flux reconstruction (ESFR) method encompasses an infinite family of high-order, linearly stable schemes and thus provides a flexible and efficient framework for achieving high levels of accuracy on unstructured grids. One remarkable property of ESFR schemes is their ability to be expressed equivalently as linearly filtered discontinuous Galerkin (FDG) schemes. In this study, we introduce Sobolev Stable discontinuous Galerkin (SSDG) schemes, a new conservative and linearly stable generalization of ESFR schemes via the FDG framework. Additionally, we review existing generalizations of the ESFR method and consider their relationship with the FDG framework. The linear properties of SSDG schemes are studied via Von Neumann analysis and compared to those of the existing extended ESFR (EESFR) method. It is found that while SSDG and EESFR schemes exhibit fundamentally different dispersive and dissipative behaviors, they can achieve a similar increase in CFL limit and exhibit a similar spectral order of accuracy. Moreover, it is seen that the range of scheme parameters over which SSDG schemes can be used to increase the explicit time-stepping limit is much larger than for EESFR schemes. Finally, it is observed that the order of accuracy of EESFR schemes under h h -refinement is generally p + 1 p+1 while that of SSDG schemes is p p .

Introduction

In the past decades, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has proven to be a valuable and cost-effective tool to solve complex fluid dynamics problems which arise in a wide range of engineering systems. High-order methods are particularly interesting in that regard as they have the ability to compute more accurate solutions on coarse meshes, thus improving overall computational efficiency.

As a result, in recent years, extensive work has been performed in the research community to characterize and develop high-order methods and improve their associated robustness. Flux reconstruction (FR) schemes are a class of high-order methods which have been introduced by
[
1
]
and further extended through the work of
[
2
,
3
]
. The FR approach is particularly appealing because it allows for the unification of various well-known schemes under one single elegant framework. In particular, collocation-based nodal discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods and some spectral difference methods can be understood as specific cases of FR
[
1
]
. The FR framework has moreover been used by
[
3
]
to identify an infinite family of linearly stable methods known as energy stable flux reconstruction (ESFR) schemes. The linear properties of ESFR schemes have been investigated by
[
4
,
5
]
, and the ESFR framework was extended to triangular and tetrahedral elements by
[
6
,
7
]
and to the advection-diffusion problem by
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Through the work of
[
11
,
12
]
, it was moreover shown that ESFR schemes could be expressed as filtered DG (FDG) schemes. More recently, this property has been used by
[
13
]
to construct non-linearly stable flux reconstruction (NSFR) schemes, an entropy stable extension of the classical ESFR framework.

ESFR schemes are particularly appealing as they are capable of achieving a good trade-off between accuracy and computational cost. More precisely, it is well-known that certain ESFR schemes are associated with larger Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) explicit time-step limit than standard nodal DG schemes while being still able to retain a similar order of accuracy
[
7
]
. This property is vital when considering realistic viscous CFD test cases, where explicit time-stepping restrictions can become a significant bottleneck.

In recent years, the desirable numerical properties of the schemes arising from the ESFR framework have provided both theoretical and practical motivations for the development of generalizations of the latter. Such generalizations have been proposed by
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
, who introduced the extended ESFR (EESFR) and the generalized Sobolev stable flux reconstruction (GSFR) approaches, respectively. Although EESFR and GSFR schemes share some numerical properties with ESFR schemes, neither is readily compatible with the NSFR framework introduced by
[
13
]
. Additionally, the non-trivial extension of these schemes to triangular and tetrahedral elements is yet to be proposed.

In this study, we introduce Sobolev stable DG (SSDG) schemes, a new FDG generalization of ESFR schemes. As a result of their FDG structure, SSDG schemes can easily be extended to both triangular and tetrahedral elements and to the NSFR framework. Additionally, we compare the linear properties of SSDG schemes to those of existing generalizations of ESFR schemes and investigate the relationship of the latter with the FDG framework.

This paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we first review filtered nodal DG and FR methods in the context on one-dimensional scalar conservation laws and discuss the relationship of the latter with the ESFR framework. In section
3
, we then introduce EESFR and SSDG schemes, two linearly stable and conservative generalizations of ESFR schemes. Section
4
reviews the application of Von Neumann analysis to these two families of numerical schemes. This is finally used in
5
, where we compare the linear properties of EESFR and SSDG schemes.

2
Preliminaries

Consider the general 1D scalar conservation law given by

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial f(u)}{\partial x}=0\quad;\quad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\quad;\quad x\in\Omega,

(1)

where
\Omega
is an interval in
\mathbb{R}
,
f(u)
is the flux of
u
in the
x
direction and
u(x,t)
is the conserved scalar. In this section, we start by describing two common high-order spatial discretizations for Eq.(
1
) and revisit how the energy stable flux reconstruction (ESFR) schemes developed by
[
3
]
can be expressed within these frameworks.

2.1
Filtered Nodal Discontinuous Galerkin

The DG spatial discretization focuses on finding an approximate weak solution to Eq.(
1
). To achieve this,
\Omega
is first discretized into
N
non-overlapping intervals
\Omega_{k}:=[x_{k},x_{k+1}]
such that

\Omega=\bigcup_{k=0}^{N-1}\Omega_{k}\quad;\quad\bigcap_{k=0}^{N-1}\Omega_{k}=\emptyset.

(2)

To simplify implementation, each element
\Omega_{k}
is usually transformed to a standard element
\bar{\Omega}:=[-1,1]
via the linear mappings
\Gamma_{k}:\Omega_{k}\to[-1,1]
defined as

\xi=\Gamma_{k}(x)=2\frac{x-x_{k}}{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}-1,

(3)

which have the inverse

x=\Gamma^{-1}_{k}(\xi)=\frac{1-\xi}{2}x_{k}+\frac{1+\xi}{2}x_{k+1}.

(4)

This allows rewriting Eq.(
1
) as

\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{k}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\hat{f}_{k}}{\partial\xi}=0

(5)

with local normalized quantities given by

\hat{u}_{k}(\xi,t):=u(\Gamma_{k}^{-1}(\xi),t)\quad;\quad\hat{f}_{k}(u)=\frac{2}{|\Omega_{k}|}f(u),

(6)

where
|\Omega_{k}|:=x_{k+1}-x_{k}
. In the DG framework, the local solutions
\hat{u}_{k}
are then approximated using a combination of
p+1
linearly independent polynomials
\chi_{j}\in\mathcal{P}^{p}(\bar{\Omega})
such that

\hat{u}_{k}(\xi,t)\approx\hat{u}_{k}^{h}(\xi,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\hat{u}^{h}_{jk}(t)\chi_{j}(\xi)

(7)

and the approximate global solution on
\Omega
is consequently given by

u(x,t)\approx u^{h}(x,t)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{N-1}\hat{u}_{k}^{h}(\Gamma_{k}(x),t).

(8)

A DG discretization is obtained by requiring the residual of Eq.(
7
) under Eq.(
5
) to be orthogonal to
\mathcal{P}^{p}(\bar{\Omega})
. Integrating by parts twice and introducing the numerical flux
f^{*}_{k}
yields the strong form of the DG method,

\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\chi_{i}\chi_{j}d\xi\frac{d\hat{u}^{h}_{jk}}{dt}+\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\frac{\partial\hat{f}_{k}}{\partial\xi}\chi_{i}d\xi=\left[(\hat{f}_{k}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\chi_{i}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}.

(9)

If the local flux is approximated in the polynomial basis via a collocation projection of
\hat{f}_{k}(\hat{u}^{h}_{k})

\hat{f}_{k}(\xi,t)\approx\hat{f}_{k}^{h}(\xi,t)=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\hat{f}_{jk}^{h}(t)\chi_{j}(\xi),

(10)

Eq.(
9
) can be rewritten in matrix form as

\mathbf{M}\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}}{dt}=-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k}^{h}+\left[(\hat{f}_{k}^{h}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\bm{\chi}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}},

(11)

where

(\mathbf{M})_{ij}:=\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\chi_{i}\chi_{j}d\xi\quad;\quad\bm{\chi}_{i}(\xi)=\chi_{i}(\xi)\quad;\quad(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h})_{i}:=\hat{u}_{ik}^{h}\quad;\quad(\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k}^{h})_{i}:=\hat{f}_{ik}^{h},

(12)

\mathbf{D}
is the matrix representation of the differentiation operator on
\mathcal{P}^{p}(\bar{\Omega})
expressed with respect to the basis
\{\chi_{0},\dots,\chi_{p}\}
and
\partial\bar{\Omega}:=\{-1,1\}
.

The nodal DG spatial discretization introduced in Eq.(
11
) can be generalized by applying a linear filter to the right-hand side of Eq.(
11
). The resulting family of schemes, which will henceforth be referred to as filtered DG (FDG) schemes, can be written as

(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}}{dt}=-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k}^{h}+\left[(\hat{f}_{k}^{h}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\bm{\chi}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
defines the filtering operator employed. When the flux function is linear,
ie.
,
f(u)=au
, Eq.(
13
) takes the form

(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}}{dt}=-\hat{a}_{k}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}+\left[(\hat{a}_{k}\hat{u}^{h}_{k}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\bm{\chi}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}},

(14)

where
\hat{a}_{k}:=2a/|\Omega_{k}|
is the normalized local advection velocity.

2.1.1
Linear Stability and Conservativeness

Simple sufficient conditions for linear stability and conservativeness of FDG schemes have been provided by
[
11
]
. They are summarized here for completeness.

Theorem 1
.

The spatial discretization given by Eq.(
14
) is stable provided that
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}\succ 0
and the numerical flux is given by

\hat{f}^{*}(u^{+},u^{-})=\hat{a}\frac{u^{+}+u^{-}}{2}-(1-\alpha)|\hat{a}|\frac{u^{+}-u^{-}}{2},

(15)

where
u^{+}
and
u^{-}
represent the solution values at the right and left of a given elemental interface and
\alpha\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}\succ 0
, then

||u^{h}||_{\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}}^{2}:=\sum_{k=0}^{N}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h})^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}

(16)

is a norm of the solution
u^{h}
. Pre-multiplying Eq.(
14
) by
(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h})^{T}
and summing over all elements yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}||u^{h}||_{\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}-\hat{a}_{k}(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h})^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}+(\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h})^{T}\left[(\hat{a}_{k}\hat{u}^{h}_{k}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\bm{\chi}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}.

(17)

It is well known from the derivation of the linear stability of the DG method that the right-hand side of Eq.(
17
) is non-positive for numerical fluxes given by Eq.(
15
) (see
[
16
,
11
]
for example). Hence,

\frac{d}{dt}||u^{h}||_{\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}}^{2}\leq 0

(18)

and boundedness of all other norms of the solution follows from the equivalence of norms on finite-dimensional vector spaces.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
(\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{n})_{i}:=\delta_{in}
, where
\delta_{in}
stands for the Kronecker delta. The spatial discretization given by Eq.(
14
) is conservative provided that
(\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{0})^{T}\mathbf{K}=0
when
\mathbf{K}
is expressed in the Legendre basis.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that Eq.(
13
) is written in the Legendre basis. Letting
\psi_{n}
denote the
n
th order Legendre polynomial, we first note that

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\hat{u}^{h}_{k}d\xi=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\psi_{0}\hat{u}^{h}_{k}d\xi=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{e}_{0}^{T}\mathbf{M}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{e}_{0}^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{h}_{k}.

(19)

Pre-multiplying Eq.(
13
) by
(\mathbf{e}^{0})^{T}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\hat{u}^{h}_{k}d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{e}^{0})^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{h}_{k}=-(\mathbf{e}^{0})^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k}^{h}+(\mathbf{e}^{0})^{T}\left[(\hat{f}_{k}^{h}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\bm{\chi}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\bar{\Omega}}\frac{\partial\hat{f}^{h}_{k}}{\partial\xi}\psi_{0}d\xi+\left[(\hat{f}_{k}^{h}-\hat{f}^{*}_{k})\psi_{0}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}=-\left[\hat{f}_{k}^{h}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}+\left[(\hat{f}_{k}^{h}-\hat{f}^{*}_{k})\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\hat{f}^{*}_{k}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}},

(20)

which completes the proof.
∎

2.2
Flux Reconstruction

The flux reconstruction (FR) framework was initially introduced by
[
1
]
as a means to develop a family of spatial discretizations for Eq.(
1
). FR schemes are usually derived by constructing piecewise high-order polynomials satisfying Eq.(
1
) in its differential form and introducing correction functions to ensure continuity of the flux function at the elemental interfaces.

In this paper, for notational convenience, we, however, somewhat depart from the usual approach and define FR schemes via

\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}}{dt}=-\mathbf{D}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{k}^{h}+(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\left[(\hat{f}_{k}^{h}-\hat{f}_{k}^{*})\bm{\chi}\right]_{\partial\bar{\Omega}}.

(21)

It can readily be seen that the schemes described by Eq.(
21
) are effectively obtained by applying a linear filter to the discontinuous flux terms in the DG formulation. Using the notation in Eq.(
21
), the derivatives of the correction functions
g_{R}
and
g_{L}
typically used in the FR framework are given by

\displaystyle g_{R}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\chi}(\xi))^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r},

(22)

\displaystyle g_{L}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-(\bm{\chi}(\xi))^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l},

(23)

where
\mathbf{r}:=\bm{\chi}(1)
and
\mathbf{l}:=\bm{\chi}(-1)
. To ensure Eq.(
21
) is strictly equivalent to the usual FR formulation, one must require
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
to be such that the conventional boundary conditions on
g_{R}
and
g_{L}
are satisfied,
ie.
,

\displaystyle g_{R}(1)

\displaystyle=1\quad;\quad g_{R}(-1)=0\implies\int_{-1}^{1}g_{R}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi=1

(24)

\displaystyle g_{L}(1)

\displaystyle=0\quad;\quad g_{L}(-1)=1\implies\int_{-1}^{1}g_{L}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi=-1.

(25)

This will be the case provided that
\mathbf{Q}
is such that the spatial discretization shown in Eq.(
21
) is conservative.

2.2.1
Conservativeness

Following the simple argument presented by
[
14
]
, a sufficient condition for conservativeness of the schemes given by Eq.(
21
) can be derived.

Theorem 3
.

The spatial discretization given by Eq.(
21
) is conservative provided that
(\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{0})^{T}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{0}=0
when
\mathbf{Q}
is expressed in the Legendre basis.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that Eq.(
13
) is written in the Legendre basis. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g_{R}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{-1}^{1}(\bm{\psi}(\xi))^{T}d\xi\right)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r},

(26)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g_{L}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=-\left(\int_{-1}^{1}(\bm{\psi}(\xi))^{T}d\xi\right)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l},

(27)

where
\bm{\psi}_{i}(\xi)=\psi_{i}(\xi)
. Leveraging the properties of
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{Q}
in the Legendre basis, one concludes

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g_{R}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=2(\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{0})^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}=2(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}=1,

(28)

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}g_{L}^{\prime}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=-2(\hat{\mathbf{e}}^{0})^{T}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}=-2(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}=-1,

(29)

which is compatible with the boundary conditions prescribed on
g_{R}
and
g_{L}
. Hence, provided that the sufficient condition is satisfied, Eq.(
21
) is equivalent to the standard FR formulation and yields a conservative family of spatial discretizations.
∎

By linear independence of
\bm{\psi}(1)
and
\bm{\psi}(-1)
, it follows that any pair of correction functions satisfying the required boundary conditions can be generated through the selection of a suitable matrix
\mathbf{Q}
of the form prescribed above. Moreover, if
g_{R}
and
g_{L}
are symmetric, such a symmetric
\mathbf{Q}
can always be found.

2.3
Energy Stable Flux Reconstruction Schemes

ESFR schemes are a family of linearly stable FR schemes developed by
[
3
]
. As shown by
[
11
]
and later generalized by
[
12
]
, ESFR schemes can be constructed via both the FDG and FR approaches and can hence be defined through

(\mathbf{K})_{ESFR}=\mathbf{Q}_{ESFR}=\frac{1}{2}c_{p}(\mathbf{D}^{p})^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}^{p},

(30)

where
\mathbf{D}^{p}
is used to denote the
p
th power of the differentiation matrix. The consistency of this statement relies on the fact
(\mathbf{K})_{ESFR}\mathbf{D}=0
, which ensures equivalence of the FDG and FR definitions. ESFR schemes are provably linearly stable (
\mathbf{M}+(\mathbf{K})_{ESFR}\succ 0
) whenever
c_{p}>c^{-}_{ESFR}
for

c_{ESFR}^{-}=\frac{-2}{(2p+1)(k_{p})^{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad k_{p}:=\frac{(2p)!}{2^{p}p!}.

(31)

Other important values of
c_{p}
have also been identified by
[
3
]
and are listed here for completeness

c_{SD}:=\frac{2p}{(2p+1)(p+1)(k_{p})^{2}}\quad;\quad c_{HU}:=\frac{2(p+1)}{(2p+1)p(k_{p})^{2}},

(32)

where using
c_{SD}
and
c_{HU}
results in a spectral difference scheme and Huynh’s
g_{2}
scheme respectively. Additionally, for notational convenience, in what follows, we will denote the ESFR scheme maximizing the CFL time-step limit as
c_{ESFR}^{\tau}
.

3
Generalizations of ESFR Schemes

We now present the generalizations of ESFR schemes studied in this paper.

3.1
Extended Energy Stable Flux Reconstruction Schemes

Extended ESFR (EESFR) schemes have been introduced by
[
14
]
as an extended family of linearly stable FR schemes which generalizes the ESFR framework. EESFR schemes are defined by requiring
\mathbf{Q}
to satisfy Theorem
3
along with

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}^{T},

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}{}\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{D}^{T}\mathbf{Q}^{T}=0,

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q}\succ 0,

(35)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{J},

(36)

where
\mathbf{J}_{ij}:=\delta_{ij}(-1)^{i+1}
. As shown by
[
14
]
, Eq.(
33
), Eq.(
34
) and Eq.(
35
) ensure stability of the FR spatial discretization while Eq.(
36
) guarantees symmetry of the correction functions. The general form of
\mathbf{Q}_{EESFR}
for
p\in\{3,4,5,6\}
has been derived by
[
14
]
. While the number of scalar parameters required to describe
\mathbf{Q}_{EESFR}
increases with
p
, in this paper, for simplicity, as done by
[
17
]
we will restrict ourselves to two-parameter EESFR schemes for which
\mathbf{Q}_{EESFR}
, in the Legendre basis, is given by

\mathbf{Q}_{EESFR}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\cdots&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&-\beta q_{1}\\
0&\cdots&0&q_{1}&0\\
0&\cdots&-\beta q_{1}&0&q_{0}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{with }\beta=\frac{2p-1}{2p-3}.

(37)

In this case, it can easily be checked that positive-definiteness of
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q}_{EESFR}
is guaranteed provided that

\displaystyle q_{1}>

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{2p-1},

(38)

\displaystyle q_{1}^{2}<

\displaystyle\frac{2(2p-3)}{(2p-1)^{2}}\left(\frac{2}{2p+1}+q_{0}\right),

(39)

which is consistent with the stability conditions presented by
[
14
]
and
[
17
]
although written in a slightly different form. In what follows, we will denote the stability upper and lower bounds for
q_{1}
by
q_{1}^{+}
and
q_{1}^{-}
. Both
q_{1}^{+}
and
q_{1}^{-}
are functions of
q_{0}
. When
q_{1}=0
, the one-parameter family of ESFR schemes is recovered. In this case, the ESFR and EESFR representations are connected via

q_{0}=k_{p}^{2}c_{p}.

(40)

Finally, as observed by
[
17
]
, it can readily be seen from Eq.(
37
) that in the limit
q_{0}\to\infty
, EESFR schemes of degree
p
collapse to ESFR schemes of degree
p-1
.

3.2
Sobolev Stable Discontinuous Galerkin Schemes

ESFR schemes are derived by requiring the boundedness of a broken Sobolev norm of the numerical solution. In this paper, we extend this idea by introducing a family of FDG discretizations which ensure the boundedness of more general Sobolev norms. These schemes, which will hereafter be referred to as Sobolev stable DG (SSDG), are defined via

(\mathbf{K})_{SSDG}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{p}c_{k}(\mathbf{D}^{k})^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}^{k}.

(41)

It is straightforward to verify that
(\mathbf{K})_{SSDG}
always satisfies the sufficient condition for conservativeness and ensures linear stability of SSDG schemes whenever
\forall_{k=1}^{p}c_{k}>0
. This follows directly from Theorems
1
and
2
presented in section
2.1.1
. Clearly, when
\forall_{k=1}^{p-1}c_{k}=0
, one recovers
(\mathbf{K})_{ESFR}
. In this paper, for simplicity, we will mainly restrict ourselves to two-parameter SSDG schemes, that is, SSDG schemes for which
\forall_{k=1}^{p-2}c_{k}=0
. In this case, since
(\mathbf{K})_{SSDG}
is always diagonal in the Legendre basis, it is found that

(\mathbf{K})_{SSDG}=\text{diag}\left(0,...,0,k_{p-1}^{2}c_{p-1},\frac{1}{3}k_{p}^{2}c_{p-1}+k_{p}^{2}c_{p}\right).

(42)

from which, by simple application of Theorem
1
, the following sharper stability requirement can be deduced

\displaystyle c_{p-1}

\displaystyle>\frac{-2}{k_{p-1}^{2}(2p-1)},

(43)

\displaystyle c_{p}

\displaystyle>-\frac{1}{3}c_{p-1}-\frac{2}{k_{p}^{2}(2p+1)}.

(44)

In what follows, the stability lower bound for
c_{p-1}
will be denoted by
c_{p-1}^{-}
. As can be seen from Eq.(
43
) and Eq.(
44
),
c_{p-1}^{-}
is a function of
c_{p}
.

From Eq.(
42
), it can be seen that in the limit
c_{p-1}\to\infty
, the
p
th and
(p-1)
th order Legendre modes are annihilated from the scheme and SSDG schemes of degree
p
collapse to a DG scheme of degree
p-2
. This behavior is analogous to that of ESFR schemes, which become a DG scheme of degree
p-1
in the limit
c_{p}\to\infty

[
3
]
. Finally, Eq.(
42
) also shows that as
c_{p}\to\infty
, SSDG schemes of degree
p
collapse to ESFR schemes of degree
p-1
.

3.3
Generalized Sobolev Stable Flux Reconstruction

Generalized Sobolev Stable FR (GSFR) schemes are another generalization of the ESFR framework proposed by
[
15
]
, which can be defined through

\mathbf{Q}_{GSFR}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}b_{k}(\mathbf{D}^{k})^{T}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}^{k}.

(45)

One should note that although the notation used in this definition somewhat differs from that used in the work of
[
15
]
, it is mathematically equivalent to the latter. While GSFR schemes may be regarded as locally stable in the sense that they satisfy the condition provided by
[
15
]
Eq.(37), some schemes in this family admit unbounded solutions, which implies they are not linearly stable in the usual sense. For instance, the
p=3
GSFR scheme with
b_{1}=0.03
,
b_{2}=0.03
,
b_{3}=0.0075
and an upwind numerical flux is not linearly stable, although it satisfies the local stability condition of
[
15
]
Eq.(37). Details are provided in
B
. Since GSFR schemes cannot be regarded as linearly stable in the usual sense, they will not be further studied as part of this work, and we will restrict ourselves to the characterization of EESFR and SSDG schemes.

3.4
Connections between Filtered DG and FR Schemes

As demonstrated in
A
, it is not difficult to show that for general numerical fluxes given by Eq.(
15
), ESFR schemes are the only FR schemes with symmetric correction functions which can be expressed in the FDG framework. Analogously, as shown in
A
, ESFR schemes are also the only FDG schemes with a symmetric
\mathbf{K}

4
Von Neumann Analysis

To investigate the properties of the generalizations of the ESFR framework presented in the previous section, we follow the approach described by
[
4
,
1
]
and use Von Neumann analysis.

Letting
f(u):=u
in Eq.(
1
) results in the classic linear advection problem

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=0,

(46)

which admits Bloch wave solutions of the form

u(x,t)=e^{I(\theta x-\omega t)},

(47)

provided that the temporal frequency
\omega(\theta)\in\mathbb{C}
is related to the wavenumber
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
via the dispersion relation

\text{Re}(\omega)=\theta

(48)

and the dissipation relation

\text{Im}(\omega)=0,

(49)

where
I:=\sqrt{-1}
. To compute the dispersion-dissipation relations of the schemes previously discussed, for simplicity, following the approach of
[
3
]
, we take
\forall_{k=0}^{N}|\Omega_{k}|=1
, which results in
\hat{f}_{k}^{h}=2\hat{u}^{h}_{k}
in Eq.(
13
) and Eq.(
21
). We then seek numerical Bloch wave solutions of the form

\hat{\mathbf{u}}^{h}_{k}=e^{I(k\theta^{h}-\omega^{h})}\hat{\mathbf{v}}.

(50)

Substituting Eq.(
50
) in Eq.(
13
) and Eq.(
21
) yields

I\omega^{h}\hat{\mathbf{v}}=2\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{v}},

(51)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{FDG}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^{T}+\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{l}^{T}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{l}^{T}e^{I\theta^{h}}-\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{r}^{T}e^{-I\theta^{h}},

(52)

for FDG schemes and

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{FR}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^{T}+\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{l}^{T}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{l}^{T}e^{I\theta^{h}}-\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{r}^{T}e^{-I\theta^{h}}

(53)

for FR schemes. Eq.(
51
) is a classical eigenvalue problem and admits
p+1
eigenpairs
(\omega^{h},\hat{\mathbf{v}})
for every input wavenumber
\theta^{h}
. To identify the physical eigenpair among the
p+1
admissible ones, the approaches described by
[
4
,
18
]
can be used. As shown by
[
1
]
, the spectrum of
\mathbf{H}
is independent of the polynomial basis selected for the implementation of the numerical scheme. Hence, results derived from Von Neumann analysis can effectively be regarded as basis-free.

4.1
Spectral Error

The combined spectral error
E_{T}
of a numerical scheme can be calculated from its dispersion-dissipation relations via

E_{T}(\theta^{h}):=|\omega^{h}(\theta^{h})-\omega(\theta^{h})|,

(54)

where
\omega
and
\omega^{h}
stand for the exact and numerical temporal frequencies, respectively. As done by
[
4
,
1
]
, this can be used to define a spectral order of accuracy
A_{T}
through

A_{T}:=\frac{\ln(E_{T}(\theta_{R}^{h}))-\ln(E_{T}(\theta^{h}_{R}/2))}{\ln(2)}-1,

(55)

where
\theta^{h}_{R}
is a sufficiently small wavenumber which ensures
E_{T}<<1
and is in the well-resolved range.

4.2
Stability

To determine the CFL time-step limit of a numerical scheme via Von Neumann analysis, we first recall that, by construction, its associated numerical Bloch wave solutions satisfy the ordinary differential equation

\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}}{dt}=-2\mathbf{H}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h}.

(56)

Applying an explicit Runge-Kutta (RK) temporal discretization with a time-step
\tau
to Eq.(
56
) leads to a fully discrete scheme of the form

\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h(n+1)}=\mathbf{R}\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h(n)},

(57)

where
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h(n+1)}
and
\hat{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{h(n)}
denote the numerical solution at time
t+\tau
and
t
respectively and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
depends on
\tau
and the type of time-stepping scheme selected. Namely, for a three-stage third-order RK scheme (RK33)
\mathbf{R}
can be written as
[
19
]

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}-2\tau\mathbf{H}+\frac{1}{2!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{2}-\frac{1}{3!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{3},

(58)

for a four-stage fourth-order RK scheme (RK44),
\mathbf{R}
has the form
[
19
]

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}-2\tau\mathbf{H}+\frac{1}{2!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{2}-\frac{1}{3!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{3}+\frac{1}{4!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{4},

(59)

and for the “optimal” five-stage fourth order low-storage RK scheme (RK45) identified by
[
19
]
,
\mathbf{R}
has the form

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{I}-2\tau\mathbf{H}+\frac{1}{2!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{2}-\frac{1}{3!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{3}+\frac{1}{4!}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{4}-\frac{1}{200}(2\tau\mathbf{H})^{5}.

(60)

The stability of the fully discrete scheme described by Eq.(
57
) is guaranteed if
\rho(\mathbf{R})
, the spectral radius of
\mathbf{R}
, is less than unity for all possible values of
\mathbf{H}
. Thus, we define
\tau_{CFL}
as the maximum value of
\tau
for which
\rho(\mathbf{H})<1
for all wavenumbers
-\pi\leq\theta^{h}\leq\pi
. Since it was assumed that
|\Omega_{k}|=1
and
a=1
when constructing
\mathbf{H}
,
\tau_{CFL}
can effectively be regarded as the maximum non-dimensional time-step for the scheme.

Results and Discussion

This section focuses on characterizing the linear properties of EESFR and SSDG schemes. Most results presented for EESFR schemes have already been discussed in depth by

and are hence presented for comparison purposes. For concision, the sections that follow mostly focus on the case

with an upwind numerical flux. Schemes of higher order are expected to behave in a qualitatively similar way.

From Figures

q_{1}
lead to an increase in dispersion at high wavenumbers and a comparatively small decrease in dispersion at low wavenumbers, leaving the range over which the dispersion profile is accurate mostly unchanged compared to the case
q_{1}=0

q_{1}
reduce dispersion at high wavenumbers and increase dispersion at lower wavenumbers, thereby decreasing the range over which the dispersion profile of the scheme is accurate when compared to the case
q_{1}=0

q_{1}

For SSDG schemes, the dispersive behavior observed appears to be much closer qualitatively to that of ESFR schemes as initially described in the work of
[
4

. Namely, increasing
c_{p-1}
decreases dispersion at high wavenumbers. As expected, when
c_{p-1}\to\infty
the dispersion vanishes over wavenumbers in the range
(p-1)\pi\leq\theta\leq(p+1)\pi

As can be observed in
3

5

7

q_{1}
increase dissipation at high wavenumbers while increasing the range over which the dissipation relation of the scheme is accurate. Negative values of
q_{1}
have the opposite effect. The qualitative similarity between SSDG and ESFR schemes is once again apparent when considering the dissipation profiles of the former. Namely, increasing
c_{p-1}
decreases dissipation at high wavenumbers and decreases the range over which the dissipation relation is accurate. In the limit
c_{p-1}\to\infty
, the dissipation vanishes over wavenumbers in the range
(p-1)\pi\leq\theta\leq(p+1)\pi
, as expected. Both EESFR and SSDG schemes are purely dissipative,
ie.
,
\text{Im}(\omega^{h})\leq 0
, which is consistent with the linear stability of both types of schemes.

All results presented for EESFR schemes are consistent with those reported by
[
17
]
. We refer the reader to this work for a more thorough discussion on the latter.

It is clear that the stability behavior of EESFR schemes differs significantly from that of SSDG schemes, although both frameworks can be employed to increase the CFL limit of ESFR schemes, no matter the type of time-stepping scheme used.

EESFR schemes feature a parabolic stability region with a narrow centered band of schemes associated with a high CFL limit. The CFL limit is maximized along that band for some positive and finite values of scheme parameters. As can be seen, the CFL limit varies non-monotonically with respect to both
q_{0}

[

\tau_{CFL}

and

Under the current normalization of scheme parameters, the extent of the latter significantly exceeds the stability region of EESFR schemes. 6
Conclusion

In this work, using the FDG framework, we have introduced SSDG schemes, a new conservative and linearly stable generalization of ESFR schemes wich is compatible with the NSFR approach developed by
[
13
]
. For general numerical fluxes, the schemes generated via the EESFR and SSDG frameworks are distinct and only overlap for classical ESFR schemes.

The linear properties of two-parameter EESFR and SSDG schemes were moreover studied. Using Von Neumann analysis, it was found that while SSDG and EESFR schemes exhibit fundamentally different dispersive and dissipative behaviors, they can achieve very similar increases in CFL limit for all the types of explicit time-stepping schemes considered. The range of scheme parameters over which the CFL limit could be improved was, however, much larger for SSDG schemes than for EESFR schemes. Additionally, when considering the spectral order of accuracy, EESFR and SSDG schemes were observed to behave in a qualitatively identical way in the vicinity of
c_{p-1}=q_{1}=0
.

Finally, for the 1D linear advection problem, it was found that SSDG schemes were only
p
th order accurate under
h
-refinement when
c_{p-1}\neq 0
while EESFR schemes could achieve
(p+1)
th order accuracy for
q_{1}\neq 0
.

Future studies should include the relatively trivial extension of SSDG schemes to the NSFR framework developed by
[
13
]
. The feasibility of extending EESFR schemes to triangular and tetrahedral elements and to the NSFR framework should moreover be examined. Applications of SSDG schemes for shock capturing purposes, as investigated by
[
20
]
for ESFR schemes, could also be considered.

Appendix A
Connection between FR and FDG Schemes

Lemma 1
.

A FDG scheme can be expressed in FR form if and only if its associated filtering matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
is such that
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}
is invertible and

\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}=0.

(62)

Additionally, a FR scheme can be expressed in FDG form if and only if its associated matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
is such that
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q}
is invertible and
\exists\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p+1)\times(p+1)}
with
\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K}
invertible satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=0,

(63)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r},

(64)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}.

(65)

Proof.

Suppose that a scheme can be expressed both in FR and FDG form. Then, the two representations for this numerical scheme must admit the same Bloch wave solutions. Consequently, one has

\forall\theta\in[-(p+1)\pi,(p+1)\pi]:\mathbf{H}_{FR}(\theta)=\mathbf{H}_{FDG}(\theta).

(66)

Using Eq.(
52
) and Eq.(
53
), this implies

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^{T}+\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{l}^{T}

(67)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{D}+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^{T}-\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{l}^{T}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{l}^{T}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}\mathbf{l}^{T}\right)e^{I\theta^{h}}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{r}^{T}-\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}\mathbf{r}^{T}\right)e^{-I\theta^{h}}

\displaystyle=0.

(68)

By linear independence of
\{1,e^{I\theta},e^{-I\theta}\}
, all terms in parentheses must vanish. Hence, if
\alpha\neq 0
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=0,

(69)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r},

(70)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}.

(71)

Eq.(
69
), Eq.(
70
) and Eq.(
71
) form a set of necessary conditions for equivalence between FR and FDG schemes. Sufficiency of the latter can easily be verified by direct substitution in the definition of the schemes.
∎

Theorem 4
.

If a given numerical scheme that can be expressed in FR and FDG form is such that
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{T}
or
\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{J}
, then it must be an ESFR scheme, ie., ESFR schemes are the only numerical schemes satisfying the conditions from Lemma
1
with symmetric filtering operators or symmetric correction functions.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, in the following, we assume that all computations are performed in the Legendre basis. In this case, recall that
\mathbf{J}_{ij}=(-1)^{i+1}\delta_{ij}
. We first note that the only possible form for a matrix
\mathbf{K}
satisfying Eq.(
69
) is

\forall_{i=0}^{p}\forall_{j=0}^{p-1}:(\mathbf{K})_{ij}=0.

(72)

Let
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{T}
. Then, the only non-zero entry in
\mathbf{K}
is
(\mathbf{K})_{pp}
and hence
\mathbf{K}
is the usual ESFR filtering matrix. This completes the first part of the proof.

Using Eq.(
72
), direct computations shows that
(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}
is given by

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}&0&&\cdots&-(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{00}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
0&(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}&0&\cdots&-(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{11}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\end{bmatrix}

(73)

Let us now assume that
\mathbf{J}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{J}
. Then, knowing that
\mathbf{l}=-\mathbf{J}\mathbf{r}
and that
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}^{-1}
, we must have

\displaystyle(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{J}

(74)

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}=(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{r}

(75)

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{r}=-(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{Q})^{-1}\mathbf{l}

(76)

Using Eq.(
70
) and Eq.(
71
), this implies that

\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{r}=-(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{K})^{-1}\mathbf{l}.

(77)

Substituting Eq.(
73
) in Eq.(
77
), one obtains

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(-1)^{1}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}+(-1)^{1}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{00}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
(-1)^{2}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}+(-1)^{3}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{11}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
(-1)^{3}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}+(-1)^{p}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{22}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{22}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
\vdots\\
(-1)^{p+1}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}(-1)^{1}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}+(-1)^{0}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{00}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{00}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
(-1)^{2}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}+(-1)^{1}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{11}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{11}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
(-1)^{3}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{22}+(-1)^{2}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{22}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{11}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\\
\vdots\\
(-1)^{p+1}(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}/(1+(\mathbf{M}^{-1})_{pp}(\mathbf{K})_{pp})\end{bmatrix}

(78)

Clearly, this constraint can only be satisfied if
(\mathbf{K})_{pp}
is the only non-zero entry of
\mathbf{K}
. Hence
\mathbf{K}
is the ESFR filetring matrix, which completes the second part of the proof.
∎

It should be noted that the results derived in this section, strictly speaking, hold for non-upwind numerical fluxes. If an upwind numerical flux is used (
\alpha=0
), then Eq.(
70
) or Eq.(
71
) must be removed from the statement of Lemma

. Since Eq.(

has
p+1
free parameters, the remaining constraint can always be satisfied, no matter the choice of
