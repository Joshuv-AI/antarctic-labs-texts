Evaluating the knowledge and usage of digital health tools in rural Oyo State, Nigeria: a cross-sectional study of healthcare professionals and residents
Authors: Emeka Benjamin Okeke, Dawn Forman, Elaina Taylor, Emma Hyde, Fabio Parente, Kehinde Kazeem Kanmodi, Stephen Wordsworth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDigital health technologies present a promising avenue for improving healthcare access in low-resource settings. In both rural and urban communities in Nigeria, challenges such as workforce shortages, limited infrastructure, and socioeconomic barriers continue to restrict healthcare delivery. This study aimed to evaluate knowledge and usage of digital health tools among healthcare professionals and residents in rural communities of Oyo State, Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from July to August 2024 in eight primary healthcare centres across two rural local government areas. A total of 393 participants, comprising 158 healthcare professionals and 235 residents, completed structured questionnaires assessing digital competency, tool usage, and willingness to adopt digital health solutions. Data were analysed using SPSS version 28. Chi-square and Kruskal–Wallis tests (non-parametric test) were used to assess group differences, while logistic regression identified predictors of digital health readiness.ResultsHealthcare professionals demonstrated high levels of digital tool usage, with 91.8% reporting daily use of these tools. Significant differences in digital knowledge were found across professional roles [p < 0.001], with nurses and doctors exhibiting greater competency than other allied healthcare workers. Among residents in the community, daily use was low [14.7%], though 78.3% reported prior experience with digital tools. Education level was significantly associated with both usage [p = 0.025] and willingness to adopt digital health tools [p < 0.001]. Logistic regression confirmed education as the strongest predictor of readiness, with residents possessing general education being significantly more likely to express willingness [OR = 8.178, p = 0.025] compared to those with postgraduate education.ConclusionsHealthcare professionals demonstrate high engagement with digital health tools, while residents face accessibility and literacy barriers. Interestingly, readiness among residents appears driven by necessity rather than digital confidence. Addressing infrastructural gaps and expanding inclusive training and outreach efforts are essential to foster equitable digital health adoption in rural Nigeria.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25600-0.

Introduction

Background

Digital health tools, including telemedicine platforms, mobile health (mHealth) applications, and electronic health records (EHRs), are increasingly recognised as essential instruments for improving healthcare delivery in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. These tools play a pivotal role in reducing healthcare disparities by enhancing access, improving continuity of care, and facilitating timely health interventions, particularly in underserved and remote regions [1, 2]. As LMICs struggle with fragile health systems, digital health tools offer a pathway toward scalable and sustainable solutions.

Nigeria, a prominent lower-middle-income country and the most populous in Africa, contributes significantly to sub-Saharan Africa’s overall disease burden. Yet, it accounts for only about 3% of the global health workforce [1, 2]. These disparities are particularly evident in rural areas of some Nigerian states, such as Oyo State, where communities face compounded challenges. Limited health infrastructure, frequent stock-outs of essential supplies, shortages of trained personnel, and geographic inaccessibility severely restrict access to care [3, 4]. These issues are further exacerbated by socio-economic factors such as poverty, low levels of formal education, poor transport systems, and weak health-seeking behaviours [3, 4].

Although mobile phone penetration has significantly increased across Nigeria, and national interest in digital health initiatives is growing, the adoption of digital health tools in rural areas remains suboptimal. Common barriers include unreliable electricity supply, unaffordable internet or mobile data, low levels of digital literacy, and scepticism towards the reliability and privacy of remote healthcare models [5, 6]. Many rural residents are either unaware of available digital health tools or remain reluctant to trust non-traditional methods of healthcare delivery. Similarly, healthcare professionals often struggle with inadequate training, inconsistent support policies, and infrastructural limitations that inhibit the integration of digital health tools into routine practice [3].

In this study, digital health tools broadly refer to technologies relevant to both residents and healthcare professionals, including smartphones, tablets, wearable devices, wellbeing applications, remote counselling channels (such as SMS, video, and phone), digital collaboration platforms (such as Microsoft Teams and Zoom), and electronic health record systems, as identified in the context of rural healthcare delivery.

Most existing studies have focused on digital health adoption among healthcare professionals in urban centres, or on patient perspectives alone, without concurrently analysing both provider and user experiences within the same rural context [7]. This has created a significant gap in understanding the interplay between healthcare supply and demand in rural environments, where digital health interventions could potentially have the greatest impact. Moreover, the bulk of available evidence is derived from qualitative research, which, while valuable for generating insights, lacks statistical generalisability and limits the formulation of scalable, evidence-based policies [8, 9].

To address these shortcomings, there is a need for empirical, population-based research that examines both the healthcare providers’ and the residents’ perspectives on digital health tool usage, acceptability, and knowledge within rural Nigerian settings. Such evidence can support more inclusive, equitable, and context-specific digital health strategies aimed at improving service delivery in areas where health systems are weakest.

Study rationale and objectives

Given the growing importance of digital health tools in resource-limited settings and the persistent barriers to their adoption in rural Nigeria, this study seeks to generate robust, quantitative evidence on their current usage, knowledge, and acceptability among both healthcare professionals and residents in rural communities of Oyo State.

The specific objectives of the study are to:

Assess current usage patterns of digital health tools among healthcare professionals and rural residents.Evaluate rural residents’ willingness to access healthcare services using digital health tools.Assess healthcare professionals’ knowledge and self-reported competency in the use of digital health tools for healthcare delivery.

This dual-perspective approach ensures that both supply-side (healthcare providers) and demand-side (community residents) factors are addressed within the same geographic context, facilitating more integrated and effective digital health policy planning.

Contribution and relevance

This study contributes to the literature by offering a comprehensive assessment of digital health tool adoption in rural Nigeria through a cross-sectional design that captures both user and provider perspectives. Unlike many previous studies that rely solely on qualitative insights or urban samples [8, 9], this research provides statistically generalisable data from a rural context, where healthcare access challenges are most severe [2, 3].

Unlike previous studies that focus predominantly on urban settings or only one side of the healthcare equation, either users or providers, this research adopts a dual-perspective, cross-sectional design. It captures both supply-side (healthcare professionals) and demand-side (community residents) factors within the same rural environment. This integrated approach offers a more comprehensive understanding of digital health adoption in resource-constrained settings.

The findings generate statistically generalisable insights that can support the development of equitable, context-sensitive digital health strategies. By identifying key barriers and facilitators on both ends of the healthcare delivery system [4, 5], this study provides valuable guidance for policy-makers, public health planners, and technology developers aiming to expand the reach and impact of digital health interventions in rural Nigeria and similar LMIC contexts [1, 7].

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to assess the usage, knowledge, and willingness to adopt digital health tools among healthcare professionals and residents in rural Oyo State, Nigeria. A cross-sectional approach is well-suited for capturing data at a single point in time and identifying associations between key variables [10]. This design is particularly valuable in low-resource environments where longitudinal studies are logistically challenging [11]. Cross-sectional surveys also serve as effective tools in formative research on digital health, enabling baseline assessments prior to intervention design [9].

Study setting

The research was conducted in Oyo State, located in southwestern Nigeria. The study focused on two rural Local Government Areas [LGAs]: Akinyele and Iddo. These LGAs were purposively selected due to their demographic diversity and accessibility, and because they reflect the infrastructure and service delivery conditions found in much of rural Nigeria [3, 12]. Within each LGA, four Primary Healthcare Centres [PHCs] were selected based on their geographic distribution and operational capacity. In Akinyele LGA, surveys were conducted at Ojoo PHC, Ajibode PHC, Olorisa Oko PHC, and Akinyele Proper PHC. In Iddo LGA, selected centres included Omi Adio PHC, Iddo Town PHC, Ile Titun Ward 10 Elebu PHC, and Apete Ward 4 PHC. These PHCs represent the frontlines of rural healthcare and are strategically positioned to support future digital health implementation [12].

Study population

The study population consisted of two primary groups. The first group included healthcare professionals, such as doctors, nurses, and allied health workers employed in the selected PHCs. The second group consisted of adult residents aged 18 years and above living in the surrounding communities. Participants in both groups were required to be proficient in English, as the questionnaire was designed in English without translation. This requirement reflected the status of English as the official language of instruction and professional communication in Nigeria. The mode of data collection followed a hybrid approach. Healthcare professionals generally completed the questionnaires independently, while trained community health workers provided assistance to community residents when necessary. This support included reading questions aloud, clarifying items, and guiding participants through the survey interface. Similar mixed methods of questionnaire administration have been employed in resource-limited settings to improve inclusivity and ensure data quality [3, 13]. Consequently, the procedure was partly self-administered and partly interviewer-assisted, depending on participants’ needs.

Inclusion criteria required participants to be adult residents of Oyo State who provided informed consent. Individuals who were under 18 years of age, cognitively impaired, unable to comprehend the questionnaire, were excluded from the study [13].

A convenience sampling strategy was employed for both healthcare professionals and rural residents. This approach was considered appropriate given the absence of a comprehensive sampling frame and the logistical challenges of accessing dispersed rural communities in Oyo State [10].

For healthcare professionals, participants were primarily recruited during scheduled site visits to eight primary healthcare centres. As not all eligible staff were present during those visits, additional recruitment was facilitated through facility heads who helped distribute the questionnaires to other staff on duty at different times.

Rural residents were recruited directly on-site at the same healthcare centres while they accessed services. Although this non-probabilistic method limits generalisability, it remains a widely accepted strategy in rural health research and has been effectively used in similar low- and middle-income country contexts [14, 15].

Data collection period

Data collection was conducted over a six-week period, from early July 2024 to mid-August 2024. This timeframe was selected to coincide with periods of steady clinical activity at the PHCs, maximizing access to eligible participants. Healthcare professionals were engaged during clinical hours, while community residents were reached through scheduled health education sessions and clinic days at the PHCs [9, 12].

Sample size determination

Sample size requirements were determined using G*Power version 3.1 [16], based on the specific statistical methods planned for each objective. For comparisons of knowledge among healthcare professionals, a one-way ANOVA was calculated with an assumed effect size of f = 0.25, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, resulting in a minimum required sample of 158. For the logistic regression assessing residents’ willingness to use digital tools, an odds ratio of 2.2, with five predictors and power set at 0.80, indicated a minimum required sample of 92. For usage pattern analysis using the Chi-square test [w = 0.3], a sample of at least 88 was required. The final sample comprised 393 valid responses, consisting of 158 healthcare professionals and 235 residents, which exceeded the minimum sample sizes needed for all analyses. This enhanced the statistical power of the study, reduced the risk of Type II errors, and allowed for more stable and reliable subgroup analyses, thereby increasing the robustness of the findings. Detailed parameters and calculation outputs are provided in Appendix S3.

Survey instrument and pilot testing

Two structured questionnaires were employed: one for healthcare professionals and another for rural residents. These instruments were adapted from previously validated tools, including the Digital Health Competency Questionnaire and the Digital Health Readiness Questionnaire, and contextualised to rural Nigeria through expert input and iterative review. The complete instruments are provided in Appendix S1.

For healthcare professionals, the questionnaire assessed the use and competency across a range of digital health tools. These included teleconsultation methods, that is, video and phone consultations; electronic patient record systems; wearable technologies for clinical monitoring such as glucometers, digital thermometers, and blood pressure devices; and communication platforms including Microsoft Teams, Zoom, and institutional intranet systems. Respondents rated the frequency of use and self-assessed their proficiency across these tools. Items also explored institutional and peer support for digital adoption.

The residents’ questionnaire focused on use and perceptions of mobile health services, SMS-based health communications, and smartphone-enabled health information seeking. It captured respondents’ access to internet-connected devices, digital literacy, trust in digital services, willingness to use such tools, and prior exposure to digital health training or services.

To ensure clarity and relevance, a pilot test was conducted with 15 participants, representing both target groups. Feedback from the pilot led to revisions in item wording, skip logic, and user interface design. This process aligns with global best practices on adapting digital health tools for LMIC contexts and mirrors observed challenges in recent implementation reviews [1]. A detailed report of the pilot findings and revisions made is included in Appendix S4.

Data collection procedures

All questionnaires were administered digitally via the Qualtrics platform, which allowed secure, real-time data capture using mobile devices. Healthcare professionals completed the survey independently on personal or facility devices. For community residents, surveys were administered on-site at the PHCs. Community health workers provided basic assistance in navigating the digital interface and interpreting survey items where needed, particularly for participants with low digital literacy or limited formal education. Significantly, these workers did not influence responses and were only present to support access to the tool. Surveys were completed privately or semi-privately within the PHC premises to ensure confidentiality. Average completion time was between 15 and 20 min. Informed digital consent was obtained before participation, as detailed in Appendix S7.

Data management and cleaning

Following the completion of data collection, all responses were exported from the Qualtrics platform and underwent a multi-step review and cleaning process to ensure accuracy and analytical readiness. The initial screening and preliminary cleaning were conducted by EBO, who removed duplicate entries, incomplete responses, and logically inconsistent data based on a predefined protocol. Subsequently, the dataset was independently reviewed by an external supervisor, ET, to validate the accuracy of the cleaning process and ensure compliance with data integrity standards. Final certification of the cleaned dataset was jointly confirmed by DF, EH, and ET, who cross-verified variable coding, consistency checks, and the application of inclusion criteria. The fully cleaned and anonymized dataset comprised 393 valid responses and was deemed fit for analysis. A comprehensive outline of cleaning procedures, variable labels, and coding logic is available in Appendix S5.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize socio-demographic characteristics and digital health metrics, including means, medians, standard deviations, interquartile ranges, and frequencies. These descriptive summaries provided an overview of participant characteristics and patterns of digital health engagement [6, 13].

Prior to conducting inferential analyses, the residuals of continuous outcome variables, specifically digital health knowledge, digital tool usage, and digital health readiness, were tested for normality. Both the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were applied, and p-values below 0.05 indicated statistically significant deviations from normality. Visual inspection of histogram plots confirmed right-skewed distributions with substantial dispersion in certain variables. These findings justified the use of non-parametric methods in subsequent analyses, which is consistent with best practices for analysing non-normally distributed health data in similar settings [11, 17]. Full outputs from the normality tests are presented in Appendix S6.

Inferential analyses were then performed to examine associations and group differences. Chi-square tests were used to assess relationships between categorical variables, while Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied to evaluate differences in continuous variables across subgroups. Where the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated statistically significant variation, Dunn’s post hoc tests were conducted to identify specific intergroup contrasts. Binary logistic regression was used to model predictors of digital health readiness among rural residents. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with non-significant p-values [>0.05] indicating good fit [10].

This statistical framework ensured robust and valid interpretation of the data despite underlying distributional limitations.

Ethical considerations

The conduct of this study was by the Helsinki Declaration of 1964. The study received ethical approval from the University of Derby Research Ethics Committee [Reference: ETH2324-2537] and the Oyo State Ministry of Health [Reference: NHREC/OYOSHRIEC/10/11/22]. All participants provided written informed consent before completing the survey and were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. No identifying information was collected, and all data were encrypted and stored in secure, password-protected digital environments. Ethical procedures were followed by international standards for human research, including the principles outlined in the World Health Organization’s digital health ethics framework [1]. Copies of the ethical approval letters are included in Appendix S2.

Socio-Demographic characteristics of both healthcare professionals and residents

Comparison of demographic characteristics between professionals and residents

Among healthcare professionals, the majority were female (61.4%). The age distribution was almost evenly split between those aged ≤ 40 years (48.7%) and those aged > 40 years (51.3%). Most professionals identified as Yoruba (86.1%), and a significant proportion (92.7%) held at least a bachelor’s degree. Over half (53.2%) had more than 10 years of professional experience, and nearly all (96.2%) worked full-time, with frequent daily interaction with patients (89.9%).

In contrast, residents were generally younger, with 60.9% aged ≤ 40 years, and a similar gender profile, with 60.0% female. Most were Yoruba (83.0%), but educational attainment was notably lower, with only 32.8% holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. The majority had either completed general education or diploma-level qualifications.

Digital health knowledge among healthcare professionals

Digital health knowledge among healthcare professionals was generally high, with substantial variability across professional categories. As shown in Table 2, the mean knowledge score was 221.29 (SD = 20.83), indicating a strong average level of digital competence among respondents.

To examine whether professional roles influenced knowledge levels, a Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted. The test revealed a statistically significant difference in digital health knowledge across professional groups (H(2) = 16.667, p < 0.001). Median knowledge scores were 228 for both doctors and nurses, whereas allied health professionals had a slightly lower median score of 225, suggesting variations in digital literacy among different health worker cadres.

Mean rank comparisons further confirmed these differences, with allied health professionals ranking substantially lower than doctors and nurses. Summary of key variability and patterns

Descriptive and inferential analyses revealed notable variability in digital health knowledge, tool usage, and willingness to adopt digital technologies across the two study groups. Discussion

This study examined the utilization, readiness, and knowledge of digital health tools among healthcare professionals and residents in rural Oyo State, Nigeria. The findings contribute to the growing body of evidence on digital health implementation in low-resource settings, providing practical insights for improving adoption and access. The discussion is structured around the study’s core objectives and is integrated with relevant global and local literature.

Digital health tool usage among healthcare professionals

Digital tool usage among healthcare professionals was substantial, with 91.8% reporting daily interaction. This reflects the increasing integration of digital technologies into clinical workflows in resource-constrained environments. Similar patterns have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa, where healthcare providers rely on telemedicine, mobile applications, and electronic health records for service delivery [2, 17]. In Tanzania, for instance [6], observed that over 85% of professionals routinely employed mobile apps for diagnostics and continuing education.

In Nigeria, the adoption of telemedicine among clinicians has shown gradual progress; however, challenges remain regarding the consistent implementation of policy and infrastructural frameworks to support its scalability [18]. Moreover, continuing professional development through distance learning has proven effective in enhancing digital competence, especially for rural allied health practitioners who often lack access to in-person training [19].

Notably, doctors and nurses showed significantly higher digital knowledge than allied health professionals. This finding is consistent with [12], who argued that nurses’ and physicians’ proximity to patient care and decision-making drives frequent interaction with digital tools. Conversely, allied professionals often lack access to these platforms and structured digital training opportunities [11, 19]. The results emphasize the importance of inclusive training across all cadres of healthcare workers to ensure equitable digital health capacity. Rural facilities should incorporate digital health modules into ongoing professional development programs to bridge these gaps [20, 21].

Despite high adoption levels, challenges persist. Infrastructural limitations, such as poor internet connectivity, aging equipment, and a lack of technical support, were frequently cited, echoing concerns raised in Nigerian contexts by [11] and [22]. Without addressing these systemic deficiencies, digital health adoption will remain uneven and unsustainable. Policymakers should consider providing infrastructure subsidies, expanding national broadband, and offering dedicated IT support to rural facilities.

Digital health tool usage among residents

In contrast, the use of digital health tools among residents was far less frequent. Although 78.3% had used digital health tools at least once, only 14.7% reported using them daily. This reflects global trends where rural and low-income populations face significant digital divides due to infrastructural, educational, and economic constraints [5, 17, 23].

This rural-urban gap becomes even more evident when compared to findings from urban Nigerian contexts, where adoption rates and daily usage of digital health tools tend to be significantly higher. Consistent daily engagement with mobile health platforms among urban clinicians has been reported, largely due to stronger institutional support, better infrastructure, and higher digital competence [18]. Similar patterns of frequent teleconsultation and mobile diagnostic tool usage in urban areas have been supported by stable connectivity and increased patient trust in virtual care [23]. In contrast, the present study found that only 14.7% of rural residents reported daily usage, despite high overall awareness. These differences highlight structural and infrastructural inequalities between rural and urban populations, suggesting that barriers such as affordability, internet coverage, and digital confidence are far more pronounced in underserved rural communities.

Interestingly, the study found that residents with lower formal education were more likely to express willingness to use digital tools, which contrasts with the common assumption that education correlates with adoption [24].

This inverse relationship is supported by findings in Ethiopia and India, where necessity, rather than technological proficiency, drives usage among the underserved [11, 25]. In rural Nigeria, where healthcare infrastructure is limited or absent, digital platforms may represent the only accessible health service for many. This reality underscores the importance of designing digital health interventions that are not only technically functional but also responsive to the lived experiences and constraints of target populations. Assuming readiness based solely on education may overlook more critical factors such as trust, necessity, and infrastructural challenges. A context-specific approach is essential to ensure that digital health solutions are equitable, usable, and effective in underserved settings [22, 24].

Barriers such as smartphone affordability, data costs, and irregular electricity were also significant, consistent with reports from Kenya and Nigeria [5, 11]. Multi-sectoral partnerships, such as subsidized health data packages or offline-compatible applications, are necessary to mitigate these constraints. Additionally, about 27.7% of residents cited lack of physical health access as a motivator for digital engagement, a trend also noted in Latin America and rural U.S. regions [2, 13, 24]. However, the true value of digital health will only be realised through concurrent investments in literacy and trust-building initiatives. Strengthening digital literacy in rural communities requires context-appropriate interventions such as adult education programs, digital training via community health workers, and integrating health education into existing outreach structures [26]. In Nigeria and similar settings, community involvement and language-adapted content delivery have shown promise in increasing digital health adoption, particularly when trust and usability are prioritised [5, 11, 25]. These approaches not only build technical skills but also enhance user confidence, which is essential for sustained digital health engagement.

Cultural preferences and trust were also influential. Many residents expressed scepticism toward virtual consultations, fearing misdiagnosis and depersonalized care. These findings align with global evidence showing that satisfaction with telehealth services depends not just on access but also on trust and user experience [23]. Similar patterns were reported in Ethiopia, where digital hesitancy stemmed from safety and reliability concerns [11]. To address this, future programs should incorporate culturally sensitive outreach, leverage trusted community voices, and implement hybrid models that blend digital and face-to-face care.

Digital health knowledge among healthcare professionals

Digital health knowledge varied significantly by profession, with doctors and nurses demonstrating higher scores than those in allied health professions. This pattern echoes findings from [12], who observed that clinical decision-makers interact more frequently with patient-facing systems. Allied professionals, in contrast, may lack exposure to or integration with digital platforms [7, 27]. Additionally, studies from Saudi Arabia and the United States indicate that willingness to adopt digital health tools is strongly linked to exposure, training, and workplace culture, emphasizing the importance of early professional engagement and digital readiness [27, 28].

The digital divide within the health workforce highlights the need for more inclusive and role-specific training [29]. advocate for capacity-building programs that engage all staff tiers, while [19] recommend blended, scalable training formats suited to rural environments. Furthermore, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration through shared digital systems can enhance efficiency and learning across health professions.

Efforts to close the knowledge gap will improve equity, consistency, and care quality, enabling all health personnel to confidently use technology and contribute to a digitally competent health system.

Residents’ willingness to use digital health tools

Just over half of the residents (54.9%) expressed willingness to adopt digital health tools. While this reflects a moderate level of openness, it also highlights a significant level of hesitancy among the remaining population. This reluctance may stem from a combination of socio-demographic, technological, and trust-related barriers that shape perceptions of digital healthcare. Factors such as age, education, previous digital exposure, infrastructural limitations, and fears surrounding confidentiality all appear to influence residents’ readiness. These findings mirror patterns observed in other underserved communities, where acceptance of digital health tools is mediated by perceived need, trust in digital systems, and lived experience with healthcare alternatives [2, 13, 24].

Age and readiness

Contrary to expectations, older residents were more likely to express willingness than their younger counterparts [p = 0.044] [2] and [24]. similarly found that older adults are more motivated by health needs and reduced mobility. Younger individuals may resist digital consultations due to privacy concerns and a preference for in-person care, as observed by [13]. This underscores the need for nuanced communication strategies tailored to different age groups’ motivations and expectations.

Education and readiness

Education level showed a statistically significant inverse relationship with willingness [p < 0.001]. Residents with general education were more open to using digital tools than those with university or postgraduate degrees. This could reflect limited healthcare alternatives among less-educated populations, as found by [5] in Kenya. Conversely, more educated individuals may be more critical or cautious due to privacy concerns and higher expectations [7]. Bridging this divide requires building trust across the spectrum through targeted education, transparency, and reliable service delivery.

Necessity as a driver of digital health adoption

Necessity emerged as a powerful motivator. In contexts where traditional services are scarce, digital tools function as vital alternatives rather than optional conveniences. This may help explain why older residents in this study were more willing to adopt digital health tools compared to their younger counterparts. Their higher willingness could reflect a greater dependence on available remote healthcare options due to mobility limitations and chronic health conditions. Similar patterns have been reported in underserved regions of Latin America and the United States, where digital platforms are viewed as essential in the absence of physical infrastructure [2, 13, 24].

Despite the influence of necessity, a significant proportion of residents remained hesitant. Nearly 45% expressed reluctance or uncertainty toward using digital platforms. This hesitancy may stem from concerns about reliability, risk of misdiagnosis, internet instability, and associated costs. Additionally, lack of trust in virtual consultations and unfamiliarity with digital interfaces have been reported as barriers in other rural settings, including Ethiopia and India [11, 26]. Bridging the gap between necessity and usability requires digital health strategies that are co-designed with communities, grounded in trust-building measures, and inclusive of offline-compatible and culturally relevant features.

Predictors of digital health readiness among residents

Logistic regression analysis identified education level as the strongest predictor of digital health readiness among rural residents. Those with general education were over eight times more likely to be willing to use digital tools than individuals with postgraduate degrees [p = 0.025]. This contrasts with findings from high-income countries, where higher education often correlates with greater technology adoption. In this context, however, residents with lower formal education may rely more heavily on digital health options due to limited alternatives, as observed in studies from Kenya and Ethiopia [5, 11].

Although age was statistically significant in earlier bivariate analysis [p = 0.044], it did not remain a significant predictor in the regression model [p = 0.084]. This suggests that while older residents expressed a greater willingness to adopt digital tools, other factors, such as accessibility, education, and need, were more influential in shaping their readiness.

Overall, these findings reinforce the importance of context-specific factors in digital health adoption. Education level and healthcare accessibility appear to have a greater influence on readiness than age or gender. This highlights the need for targeted interventions that consider socio-economic realities and structural barriers [26]. Policymakers must move beyond generalized assumptions and instead use evidence like this to design interventions that are locally tailored, socially inclusive, and economically feasible.

Research gaps and future directions

Despite the significant insights generated by this study, several important research gaps remain. Firstly, there is a need for longitudinal research to explore how digital health tool usage and willingness evolve in rural populations. Such data would help determine whether current levels of engagement translate into long-term behavioural change or improved health outcomes. Secondly, comparative analyses between rural and urban populations within Nigeria remain scarce. Existing findings suggest stark differences in infrastructure, literacy, and user experience [18, 23], but more systematic, within-country comparisons are needed to inform equitable digital health policies. Thirdly, although this study touched on willingness and usage, a deeper investigation is needed into how cultural beliefs, digital trust, and perceived service quality shape digital health adoption in rural areas, particularly among underserved or older populations [5, 24]. Future studies should employ mixed-methods approaches that integrate quantitative assessments with qualitative insights to better capture contextual barriers and co-design locally acceptable digital interventions.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths of the study

A major strength of this study is its dual-population approach, which explored digital health tool usage from both healthcare professionals and community residents in rural Nigeria. This comprehensive scope enabled a broader understanding of digital health adoption from both supply- and demand-side perspectives, providing insights that are more representative of real-world conditions in underserved settings [2, 28].

The study employed validated instruments, rigorous data collection, and robust statistical techniques, including descriptive and inferential analyses. Logistic regression analysis further strengthened the findings by identifying key socio-demographic predictors of digital health readiness [27, 28]. The inclusion of participants across multiple healthcare facilities and rural communities enhanced the diversity of the sample and the credibility of the findings within the specific context of Oyo State.

Limitations of the study

However, the study has limitations. The use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, as individuals with a greater interest in or access to digital tools may have been more likely to participate [26]. This non-probabilistic approach may also explain the skewed distribution and substantial dispersion observed in key outcome variables, which required the use of non-parametric statistical methods. The absence of probabilistic sampling further limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of healthcare professionals and residents in rural Oyo State. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to determine causality or observe behaviour changes over time. Longitudinal studies are recommended for future research to assess evolving trends in digital health adoption and use [30].

Reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of response bias, particularly when measuring digital competency or frequency of usage [21]. Furthermore, excluding non-English-speaking participants may have inadvertently excluded the most marginalized voices, limiting the representativeness of the findings [20]. Finally, the study focused on rural populations; the results may not be generalizable to urban areas or regions with more advanced digital infrastructures and different socio-economic contexts [4, 7].

Despite these limitations, the study offers foundational evidence for improving the design and implementation of digital health interventions. It supports the development of inclusive, data-driven strategies tailored to the unique realities of rural and resource-limited settings.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings reveal apparent disparities in digital health readiness and usage between healthcare professionals and rural residents, as well as among different professional and educational groups. To address these gaps, national and regional health policymakers should develop inclusive digital health training programs that cater to diverse populations. These programs must go beyond doctors and nurses to also support allied health professionals, who have demonstrated lower digital competency [7, 12].

The study found that residents with lower levels of formal education were more willing to adopt digital health tools. As a result, digital platforms should be designed to be user-friendly, with community-based education and sensitization campaigns that specifically target individuals with limited education. This challenges the assumption that higher education always correlates with higher digital readiness in underserved rural communities [5].

Infrastructural barriers, such as poor internet access, high mobile data costs, and electricity shortages, significantly limit digital tool usage. To overcome these obstacles, policymakers should encourage collaboration with telecommunications providers to reduce data costs and promote the development of applications that can function offline or with minimal connectivity [18, 30].

Since the study showed that many residents adopted digital tools out of necessity rather than preference, digital health interventions should prioritize accessibility and trust-building. Introducing hybrid service delivery models that combine digital and in-person consultations can improve trust and enhance adoption, particularly in culturally diverse and resource-limited settings [11, 31].

Conclusion

This study offers critical insights into the adoption, knowledge, and readiness of healthcare professionals and rural residents in Oyo State, Nigeria, for digital health tools. It reveals substantial disparities between these groups. Healthcare professionals, particularly nurses and doctors, demonstrated high levels of digital engagement and competency. In contrast, rural residents faced significant barriers, including limited digital literacy, economic constraints, and scepticism. Interestingly, those with less formal education were often more willing to adopt digital tools, not because of digital fluency, but due to limited access to healthcare services.

Education level emerged as the most significant predictor of digital health readiness. Residents with only general education were more inclined to use digital health platforms than their more formally educated counterparts. This challenges common assumptions in high-income settings and highlights the role of necessity and perceived utility in technology adoption. On the other hand, individuals with higher educational attainment expressed greater caution, likely influenced by concerns about data privacy and service quality.

Among healthcare workers, professional role and education level, rather than years of experience, were key determinants of digital health knowledge. Allied health professionals reported lower levels of competency, emphasizing the need for inclusive and targeted training programs that reach all healthcare roles.

Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of context-sensitive digital health strategies. Successful interventions should incorporate equitable training for all healthcare professionals, culturally appropriate education for communities, and infrastructural improvements that specifically address rural challenges.

With well-designed and inclusive efforts, digital health can serve as a powerful tool to bridge healthcare gaps and extend quality services to underserved populations across Nigeria and similar low- and middle-income countries.
