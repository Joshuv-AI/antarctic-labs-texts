Structural and Vascular Findings in Autologous Retinal Transplantation for Macular Hole
Authors: Jad Madani, Rami Madani, Timothy Mayotte, Ahmad Zeido, Tarek Alasil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To describe the fluorescein angiography (FA) and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) findings in autologous retinal transplantation for refractory full-thickness macular hole (FTMH).Methods:A single case was reviewed.Results:A 71-year-old Hispanic man presented with a refractory FTMH for which internal limiting membrane peel and gas tamponade had previously failed. The axial length was 23.17 mm, and the basal diameter measured 1500 µm (corrected to 1448.13 µm). The patient underwent pars plana vitrectomy and autologous retinal transplantation with short-term perfluoro-n-octane heavy liquid, followed by gas tamponade. The patient was followed for 4 years (48 months). Complete anatomic closure of the FTMH was seen on OCT. The preoperative best-corrected Snellen visual acuity was counting fingers, which improved to 20/100 at 13 months postoperatively. FA showed intragraft vascularization, and evidence of reperfusion at the superficial plexus level was seen on OCTA.Conclusions:Autologous retinal transplantation for this large, refractory FTMH provided anatomic closure with vascularization of the graft and improvement in visual acuity.

Introduction

With an estimated prevalence of up to 3.3 per 1000, idiopathic full-thickness macular holes (FTMH) predominantly occur in individuals over the age of 55. 1 These FTMH form due to significant vitreomacular traction, often associated with age-related foveal stretching. 2 Surgical management has traditionally involved pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) with internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling. By relieving anterior-posterior traction, removal of the ILM permits the centripetal movement of surrounding retinal tissue toward the center of the hole.3,4 However, this procedure has shown limited efficacy in managing large FTMH (>400 μm), prompting the subsequent development of the inverted ILM flap technique. 5 Yielding improved anatomic outcomes, this technique involves partially removing the ILM and using the remainder to create an inverted flap that bridges the defect.6–8 Yet this approach remains insufficient for managing challenging cases, especially very large, refractory FTMH where the ILM has already been removed or is too short to create a well-approximated flap. 9

To address this therapeutic gap, autologous retinal transplantation has recently emerged as a promising alternative, particularly for FTMH that have failed prior techniques.10,11 Autologous retinal transplantation involves harvesting a peripheral retinal graft and positioning it over the FTMH. The graft plugs the hole and presumably serves as a scaffold for the migration and proliferation of Müller cells, further potentiating integration into the surrounding retina. 12 Successful anatomic closure and corresponding improvements in visual acuity (VA) have been increasingly documented using this approach. For instance, Moysidis et al 13 observed that anatomic closure was achieved in 85.7% of primary and 88% of refractory cases when autologous retinal transplantation was performed. In addition, the management of FTMH with retinal detachment using this technique has been demonstrated in cases complicated by coexisting ocular pathologies, such as severe myopia and proliferative vitreoretinopathy.14–16

Despite growing evidence of long-term structural integration, the extent to which vascular function is restored within the autologous retinal graft remains poorly characterized. The present case report describes fluorescein angiography (FA) and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) findings in autologous retinal transplantation for refractory FTMH. By offering layer-specific insights into graft vascularization, a potentially limiting determinant of functional recovery, our findings seek to contribute to the growing literature on the prognostic implications of autologous retinal transplantation for FTMH.

Case Report

A 71-year-old Hispanic man presented with a refractory FTMH that had failed prior ILM peel and gas tamponade. The axial length was 23.17 mm (LenStar). Using the caliper tool function on the Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg Engineering), the basal diameter of the FTMH was measured at 1500 µm (corrected to 1448.1 µm), and the minimal diameter was 834 µm (corrected to 805.2 µm). 17

Surgical Technique

Three-port 23-gauge PPV (Stellaris Elite, Bausch + Lomb) was performed with retrobulbar anesthesia using monitored anesthesia care. Indocyanine green dye solution (0.06% concentration) was delivered to the retinal surface around the MH to confirm the status of the ILM. A neurosensory retinal graft was selected, choosing a donor site between the nasal arcades and the equator (posterior). The size of the graft was at the discretion of the surgeon and was estimated to be 1 disc diameter in this case. A barrier endolaser treatment was delivered in multiple rows around the graft harvest site, along with endodiathermy to cauterize blood vessels at the edges of the graft site. The graft itself was harvested from healthy retinal tissue within the barrier zone of the endolaser, ensuring that the edges of the graft were not affected by laser or diathermy. The graft was cut using vertical scissors (Pinnacle 360, Synergetics). Perfluoro-n-octane (PFO, Alcon) was injected up to the equator level to secure the transplant during the final stages of harvesting the graft and during the transfer of the graft to its intended site, at the FTMH, using a finesse flex loop (Alcon). End-grasping forceps (Alcon) were used to gently hold the edge of the graft. The finesse flex loop was used to gently tuck the graft into the MH space under PFO, which was left in the eye for 5 days as a short-term tamponade while the patient was positioned supine. This was followed by PFO–air exchange and expansile gas injection (sulfur hexafluoride [SF6] 18% as the tamponade agent), and the patient was positioned facedown for 3 days. Follow-Up

The Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg Engineering) was used to demonstrate the complete anatomic closure of the FTMH and to perform FA and OCTA. Restoration of the outer retinal bands, including the external limiting membrane (ELM) and ellipsoid zone (EZ), was observed with integration of the autologous retinal transplantation graft (Figure 1).

Sequentially captured optical coherence tomography images showing the status of macular hole closure (A) preoperatively and (B) 1 day postoperatively under perfluoro-n-octane, (C) 38 weeks, (D) 13 months, (E) 18 months, and (F) 32 months. (G) Ultra-widefield retinal imaging offers a holistic view of the macula at 32 months postoperatively.

Best-corrected Snellen VA was counting fingers (CF) preoperatively and 1 day postoperatively. When the patient returned to the clinic 38 weeks postoperatively, VA was CF, and visually significant cataracts had developed, a common complication of PPV. After cataract removal, at 13 and 18 months after autologous retinal transplantation, VA improved from CF to 20/100 (logMAR from 2.0 to 0.7). Thirty-two months after autologous retinal transplantation, VA dropped again to CF due to posterior capsule opacification, which was treated with YAG posterior capsulotomy. The patient’s VA improved to 20/200 and remained stable for the remainder of his follow-up. The retina remained attached, and development of a noncontractile epiretinal membrane on the graft was observed. The cystoid changes within the inner retina of the graft spontaneously improved, likely representing remodeling changes within the graft.

FA demonstrated vascularization within the graft. OCTA also showed evidence of vascularization within the graft at the superficial capillary plexus level (Figure 2). Microperimetry was not used because it was not available at our facility. However, the patient’s subjective visual outcomes were documented. The patient was followed for 4 years (48 months).

Conclusions

This case highlights the capacity of a transplanted peripheral retinal graft to structurally integrate and vascularize over a refractory FTMH, as demonstrated by FA and OCTA. Although graft reperfusion using autologous retinal transplantation has previously been reported, our study distinctively highlights the longitudinal progression of anatomic hole closure and demonstrates the layer-specific vascularization within the graft. 18 FA showed intragraft vascularization without leakage, and OCTA revealed that perfusion was confined to the superficial capillary plexus, with no detectable flow in the deep capillary plexus. This layer-specific perfusion pattern is consistent with early multimodal imaging evidence documented by Tabandeh showing superficial inner retinal perfusion by 6 weeks postoperatively. 18 Reperfusion was hypothesized to arise from localized upregulation of angiogenic pathways within an ischemic graft, promoting anastomosis between host and graft vasculature. However, even in these early reports, OCTA characterization remained limited to superficial layers, and deep plexus reperfusion status was not definitively demonstrated.

Selective vascular remodeling after transplantation likely reflects the preferential growth of superficial vessels, potentially supported by the dense and continuous Müller glial sheaths within the superficial capillary plexus. 19 Experimental studies have shown that prolonged hypoxia induces a proangiogenic Müller cell phenotype, characterized by basic fibroblast growth factor–mediated costimulation of neovascularization and hypoxia-inducible factor-1–dependent upregulation of Netrin-4 and vascular endothelial growth factor expression.20–22 Structurally, Müller cell endfeet at the ILM and their ensheathment of the superficial capillary plexus create both a physical scaffold and a localized signaling niche that may favor superficial vascular regeneration relative to deep capillary plexus reconstitution. 23

Although our patient’s graft remained viable, persistent ischemia within the deep capillary plexus can hinder the layer’s ability to metabolically support the inner nuclear layer, namely the rod bipolar cells. 24 Interestingly, however, the EZ and ELM were restored, suggesting that the outer retina photoreceptors may remain metabolically supported via the choriocapillaris.25,26 Such differential intragraft vascularization may indicate that outer retinal phototransduction remains intact despite impaired inner retinal synaptic transmission, a potential constraint on visual outcomes. This interpretation is further supported by clinical observations that functional recovery after autologous retinal transplantation is spatially heterogeneous, which may reflect incomplete or uneven vascular integration across retinal layers. 27

Postoperatively, the thin, noncontractile epiretinal membranes over the autologous retinal transplantation graft indicate gliosis. 28 In conjunction with ELM restoration, these findings suggest the re-establishment of structural connections between Müller cells and photoreceptors at the host–graft interface. Furthermore, neuronal integration with the surrounding retina may be achieved as the graft’s rod bipolar and horizontal cells interact with photoreceptors outside the FTMH lesion. 29 This mechanism has been explored preclinically when the transplantation of rod precursors in night-blind (Gnat1 -/-) mice led to the regeneration of classic triad synaptic connections with bipolar and horizontal cells, along with restored scotopic vision. 30

Moving forward, holistic graft vascularization may represent a key determinant of visual outcome, necessitating exploration of strategies that promote deep retinal revascularization, such as the modulation of angiogenic pathways. 31 Larger-scale longitudinal studies that use OCTA to examine layer-specific vascularization after autologous retinal transplantation–mediated FTMH repair will also be essential for understanding the long-term functional implications of this surgical technique. As autologous retinal transplantation shifts the trajectory for managing refractory FTMH, further research will be critical in paving the way for optimal physiologic graft incorporation and, ultimately, visual improvements.
