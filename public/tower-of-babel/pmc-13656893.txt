Cognitive Impairment, Depression and Cerebral Microbleeds in Immune Thrombocytopenia (ITP)
Authors: Camelia Vladescu, Rozanna Meijboom, Alice C J Hart, Deena Paul, Christine Ademokun, Ariam Teklemichael, Bridget Chivers, Anastasia Gontsarova, Richard Szydlo, Adam D Waldman, Nichola Cooper
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an autoimmune condition characterized by low platelet counts (< 100 × 109/L) and associated risks of bleeding [1]. Although life‐threatening bleeds such as intracranial hemorrhage remain rare [2], we have recently reported occult cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) in 43% of patients with ITP [3]. CMBs are also reported in older healthy individuals and in neurological diseases [4], and have been associated with cognitive impairment [5, 6].

In addition to bleeding symptoms, patients with ITP report poor health‐related quality of life, including fatigue, memory and concentration problems, anxiety and depression [7, 8] These symptoms often do not improve over time and are not typically targeted in clinical trials of new treatments [8]. Consistent with patient‐reported cognitive complaints, one study using an automated neuropsychological test battery (Cogstate) identified cognitive impairment in 59% of patients [9].

Here we measured cognitive impairment in a separate cohort of patients and examined the relationship with disease parameters and presence of CMBs in ITP.

Adults with primary ITP were prospectively recruited from the Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust Immune Hematology Clinic between 2014 and 2020 as part of an ongoing observational study; the current analysis is cross‐sectional. Patients were sequentially approached if they had a confirmed diagnosis of ITP, at least one platelet count ≤ 30 × 109/L and were ≥ 18 years old (full inclusion and exclusion criteria in Supporting Information, Section 1). Ethical approval was obtained from the London‐Surrey Borders Health Research Authority (13/LO/1365), the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants underwent a dedicated brain imaging protocol on a 3‐Tesla magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system (Siemens Verio, Erlangen, DE; VB19), lasting about 60 min, including susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) for CMB detection (Supporting Information, Section 2). SWI images were visually assessed by an experienced neuroradiologist blinded to clinical and cognitive data, and the number of CMBs per patient was recorded. CMB findings for the first 37 patients have been previously reported [3].

Cognitive testing was added to the protocol in 2018 and performed using CANTAB Insight, a Class II CE‐approved medical device [10, 11] validated for clinical and research assessment of cognitive impairment [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Tests were administered on an Apple iPad 2017 in a quiet, distraction‐free room and took approximately 20–25 min to complete. Five cognitive domains (processing speed, attention, working memory, executive function, and episodic memory) were assessed with CANTAB tasks (summary and task descriptions in Supporting Information, Section 3; domain characterization in Section 4). The software processed scores to generate age‐, sex‐, and education‐standardized Z‐scores, which represent the number of standard deviations a patient's score is from the mean score of a healthy population. A Z‐score of 0 indicates average performance, while scores above or below 0 reflect better or worse performance, respectively. Performance categories (superior, above average, average, low average, poor, very poor) were defined using these standardized Z‐scores. An example CANTAB report is provided in Supporting Information, Section 5. Patients were classified as having cognitive impairment if they had at least one impaired cognitive domain (low or very low Z‐score), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Mood at the time of testing was assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS‐15) embedded within the CANTAB platform. Scores were categorized as normal (< 5), suggestive of depression (5–9), or indicative of depression (10–15).

After cognitive testing was added to the protocol in 2018, newly enrolled patients underwent brain MRI and cognitive testing during the same study visit, and those who had undergone brain MRI before 2018 were invited to return for cognitive testing at a later clinic visit (median imaging‐to‐testing interval = 21 months; range 3–55 months).

Analyses were performed using RStudio 3.6.1. Descriptive statistics summarized demographics and clinical characteristics (medians and ranges for continuous variables; percentages for categorical data). Clinical characteristics were compared between patients with and without impairment in at least one cognitive domain using independent‐samples t‐tests (p < 0.05). Binomial logistic regression assessed associations between either (a) CMB group (0 = no CMBs; 1 = CMBs present) or (b) CMB count (independent variable) and cognitive status (0 = normal; 1 = impaired; dependent variable), adjusted for age and sex. A square root transformation was applied to age to meet the linearity assumption for logistic regression. To evaluate the effect of the imaging‐to‐testing interval, logistic regressions were repeated in two subgroups defined by the interval between brain MRI and cognitive testing (≤ 1 year vs. >1 year; Supporting Information, Section 6). Further exploratory binomial logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the effect of mood group (0 = normal, 1 = suggestive of depression, 2 = indicative of depression; independent variable) on cognitive functioning (dependent variable), with age and sex as covariates (p < 0.05).

Seventy patients were recruited; two were excluded because they did not complete the brain MRI. Sixty‐eight patients completed cognitive testing: 66 completed all tests; 2 patients did not complete the Paired Associates Learning task (episodic memory) due to time restrictions. Thirty patients completed cognitive and depression testing on the same day as the brain MRI, and 38 completed them a median of 21 months after their MRI (range, 3–55 months). On the brain MRI, 22/68 patients (32%) had CMBs; the median number of CMBs was 2 (range 1–10).

Thirty‐three patients (48.5%) (95% CI [36%, 60%]) had impairment in at least one cognitive domain (Z‐score < −1.5—poor or very poor). Thirteen (19%) had global impairment (impairment in more than one domain; Figure 1A).

The proportion of patients with impairment in each cognitive domain is shown in Figure 1B: Episodic memory was impaired in 23% of patients (95% CI [13%–34%]); executive function in 18% (95% CI [9%–28%]); processing speed in 16% (95% CI [8%–27%]); working memory in 12% (95% CI [5%–21%]); and attention in 3% (95% CI [0.3%–10%]).

Twenty‐five patients (37%) had GDS‐15 score ≥ 5, indicative of (n = 7) or suggestive of (n = 18) depression (S1, Section 7).

Presence of CMBs (Odds ratio [OR] = 0.70, p = 0.51) and CMB count (OR = 0.97, p = 0.83) were not associated with impaired cognition (Supporting Information, Sections 8 and 9). Covariates age and sex were not significant predictors in either model. In the CMB group model, older age (OR = 1.43, p = 0.14) and female sex (OR = 2.02, p = 0.21) showed nonsignificant trends towards increased risk of cognitive impairment. In the CMB count model, the odds of impairment increased numerically with age (OR = 1.40, p = 0.15) and female sex (OR = 1.89, p = 0.24) but did not reach statistical significance.

Compared with normal mood scores (mood group 0), scores suggestive of depression (mood group 1; OR = 1.91, p = 0.30) and indicative of depression (mood group 2; OR = 3.72, p = 0.15) were not significantly associated with impaired cognition (Figure 1C). Age (OR = 1.03, p = 0.10) and sex (OR = 1.69, p = 0.36) were also not significant (Supporting Information, Section 8).

In conclusion, this study confirms that cognitive impairment is a prevalent feature of ITP, with nearly half of patients showing impairment in at least one cognitive domain, a fifth experiencing global impairment, and over a third having GDS scores suggestive or indicative of depression. We found no significant associations between impaired cognition or depression and hematological disease severity features, including CMBs.

Our results broadly concur with the only other study to utilize automated tests to measure cognitive impairment objectively in patients with ITP [9]. Both studies found similar overall prevalence of cognitive impairment, 48.5% in the current study compared to 59% in the previous study [9]. Differences in the most affected cognitive domains are likely related to methodological differences.

The most commonly impaired cognitive domains were episodic memory [18], executive function [19] and processing speed [20], all of which could significantly impact activities of daily living. Notably, the prevalence of cognitive impairment across domains is comparable with that reported in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) [13].

Limitations include the cross‐sectional design with a single time‐point cognitive assessment; use of the GDS‐15 for mood assessment, which was originally developed for older adults; and uncertainty regarding the natural history of CMBs in ITP. CMBs observed here may reflect historical processes rather than current brain pathology. Longitudinal studies incorporating serial brain MRI and repeated cognitive assessments are needed to clarify temporal relationships and the potential contribution of cerebral microbleeds to cognitive impairment.

Future trials in ITP should incorporate cognitive testing and patient‐reported outcomes, particularly fatigue, to better align treatment goals with patients' lived experiences and to identify effective interventions for non‐hematological aspects of the disease.
