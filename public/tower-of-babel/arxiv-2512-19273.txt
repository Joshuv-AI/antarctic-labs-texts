Scale-Invariant Robust Estimation of High-Dimensional Kronecker-Structured Matrices
Xiaoyu Zhang, Zhiyun Fan, Wenyang Zhang, Di Wang † \dagger Tongji University, ‡ \ddagger Shanghai Jiao Tong University, § \lx@sectionsign University of Macau

Abstract
High-dimensional Kronecker-structured estimation faces a conflict between non-convex scaling ambiguities and statistical robustness. The arbitrary factor scaling distorts gradient magnitudes, rendering standard fixed-threshold robust methods ineffective. We resolve this via Scaled Robust Gradient Descent (SRGD), which stabilizes optimization by de-scaling gradients before truncation. To further enforce interpretability, we introduce Scaled Hard Thresholding (SHT) for invariant variable selection. A two-step estimation procedure, built upon robust initialization and SRGD–SHT iterative updates, is proposed for canonical matrix problems, such as trace regression, matrix GLMs, and bilinear models. The convergence rates are established for heavy-tailed predictors and noise, identifying a phase transition where optimal convergence rates recover under finite noise variance and degrade optimally for heavier tails. Experiments on simulated data and two real-world applications confirm superior robustness and efficiency of the proposed procedure.



Keywords
: Kronecker product, robustness, sparse matrix decomposition, heavy-tailed distributions, gradient descent

1
Introduction

1.1
Kronecker Structure and the Robustness-Scaling Tension

High-dimensional structured matrix estimation is a cornerstone of modern statistical methodology, offering parsimonious representations that effectively capture multi-modal interactions while substantially reducing dimensionality. Prominent examples include matrix regression
(
Negahban and Wainwright, 2011
)
, matrix completion
(
Negahban and Wainwright, 2012
)
, and spatio-temporal modeling in neuroimaging
(
Hung and Wang, 2013
)
and econometrics
(
Chen et al., 2021
)
. The Kronecker product structure, wherein a target matrix is decomposed into the product of smaller factors, is particularly effective in settings where interactions separate along distinct but interdependent modes.

Despite these modeling advantages, reliable estimation of Kronecker-structured matrices faces a unique intersection of challenges: non-convex optimization and heavy-tailed data. While these issues are individually well-studied, their interaction creates a specific failure mode for existing algorithms. Specifically, Kronecker factorizations are non-identifiable up to a rescaling of their factors. This invariance induces a scaling ambiguity where the norms of the latent factors can become arbitrarily unbalanced. In standard optimization, this leads to ill-conditioned Hessians. However, in robust statistical estimation, the consequences are far more severe.

Standard robust methods rely on fixed-threshold mechanisms, such as gradient truncation and Huber loss, to suppress outliers. In the Kronecker setting, however, the efficacy of these thresholds becomes arbitrarily coupled with the scaling ambiguity of the factorization. When a factor scale is large, the gradient is artificially amplified, causing valid signals to exceed the threshold and be indiscriminately clipped, thereby discarding valuable information. Conversely, when a factor scale is small, the gradient diminishes, allowing gross outliers to fall below the threshold and leak into the estimate, effectively bypassing the robustification mechanism. Consequently, standard robust algorithms fail to be scale-invariant, as their statistical performance fluctuates wildly based on the arbitrary parameterization of the factorization.

Furthermore, enforcing sparsity, often desirable for interpretability, introduces a structural contradiction. Standard row-wise hard thresholding is sensitive to basis transformations; rescaling the factors changes the relative row norms, leading to inconsistent support recovery. Achieving interpretable, robust estimation requires a methodology that decouples statistical robustification from the scale indeterminacy of the model.

1.2
Contributions

In this article, we propose a unified framework for the robust estimation of Kronecker-structured matrices that resolves the tension between scaling ambiguity, heavy-tailed noise, and sparsity. Our contributions are three-fold:

1.

Scale-Invariant Robust Optimization.
We introduce Scaled Robust Gradient Descent (SRGD), an algorithm that stabilizes updates via a three-step mechanism: (i)
de-scaling
to isolate the statistical error signal from the factor norms; (ii)
truncation
in the normalized domain to suppress heavy-tailed outliers; and (iii)
re-scaling
to respect the optimization geometry. This ensures that the robustification mechanism is invariant to the factorization scaling, preventing the failure modes of naive truncation.

2.

Scale-Invariant Sparsity Recovery.
To enforce structured sparsity, we develop Scaled Hard Thresholding (SHT). Unlike standard thresholding, SHT operates on
scaled factors
whose row norms are invariant to the factorization scaling. This ensures that the selected support set is intrinsic to the Kronecker product rather than an artifact of the initialization.

3.

Provable Convergence and Sharp Phase Transitions.
We establish the convergence of a two-stage estimation procedure, based on robust initialization and SRGD–SHT iterates, for three canonical models: matrix trace regression, matrix GLMs, and bilinear regression. Our theory accommodates heavy tails in both predictors (finite
(2+2\lambda)
-th moment) and noise (finite
(1+\epsilon)
-th moment). We derive non-asymptotic error bounds that reveal a smooth phase transition: for finite-variance noise (
\epsilon=1
), we recover the optimal parametric rate
O(\sqrt{s/n})
, showing that robustness incurs no efficiency loss in ideal settings; for heavy-tailed noise (
\epsilon<1
), the rate slows to
O(s^{1/2}n^{-\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)})
, matching information-theoretic lower bounds. Crucially, the error bounds are independent of the factorization condition number, confirming the stability of the approach.

2
Scaled Robust Gradient Descent

2.1
Problem Formulation and Scaling Ambiguity

We consider the estimation of a structured matrix
\mathbf{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
by minimizing the empirical risk
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta};\mathcal{D}_{n})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Theta};z_{i})
. We assume
\mathbf{\Theta}
admits a rank-
K
Kronecker product decomposition

\mathbf{\Theta}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbf{A}_{k}\otimes\mathbf{B}_{k},

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times q_{1}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times q_{2}}
.
To facilitate optimization, we utilize the permutation operator
\mathcal{P}:\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
with
d_{1}=p_{1}q_{1}
and
d_{2}=p_{2}q_{2}
, which maps the Kronecker product to a standard low-rank factorization
(
Cai et al., 2022
)
:

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{\Theta})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\text{vec}(\mathbf{A}_{k})\text{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{k})^{\top}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top},

(2)

where
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times K}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times K}
. The estimation task is thus transformed into optimizing the latent factors
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{R}
via the composite objective
f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top});z_{i})
, where
\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse operator of
\mathcal{P}
.

A fundamental challenge in optimizing
f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})
is the intrinsic non-identifiability of the factorization. For any nonsingular matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\text{GL}(K)
, the transformation
(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}^{-\top})
preserves the product
\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
. This invariance implies that the norms of the factors are arbitrary, yet the partial gradients depend explicitly on these scales. Let
\mathbf{G}_{i}=\mathcal{P}(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top});z_{i}))\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
denote the gradient of the loss with respect to the permuted parameter, capturing the prediction error signal. By the chain rule, the partial gradients are:

\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})=\mathbf{G}_{i}\mathbf{R}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})=\mathbf{G}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}.

(3)

Consequently, if
\mathbf{R}
has a large norm (and
\mathbf{L}
a correspondingly small norm), the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{L}
is artificially amplified by the factor
\mathbf{R}
, while the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{R}
is diminished by
\mathbf{L}
. This scaling ambiguity creates a critical vulnerability when introducing robust gradient techniques.

2.2
Motivation: The Failure of Fixed-Threshold Robustness

Standard methods for robust estimation typically rely on modifying the gradient via a threshold-based influence function. Before detailing our algorithm, we illustrate why a naive application of these techniques fails in the Kronecker setting. The core issue is that the activation of the robust mechanism becomes arbitrarily coupled with the scaling of the factors.

Consider a simplified rank-
1
scalar setting where the target is
\theta_{\ast}=l_{\ast}r_{\ast}
. Suppose we observe
y=\theta_{\ast}+\xi
, where
\xi
is a heavy-tailed noise term. Evaluated at the true parameter, the gradient of the quadratic loss with respect to
l
is
g_{l}=(l_{\ast}r_{\ast}-y)r_{\ast}=-\xi r_{\ast}
.
A standard robust update replaces the raw gradient
g_{l}
with a robustified version
\psi_{\tau}(g_{l})
. The most common approach, utilized in both truncated gradient descent and Huber regression, is to clip the gradient magnitude at a fixed threshold
\tau
:

\psi_{\tau}(u)=\text{sgn}(u)\min(|u|,\tau).

(4)

The efficacy of this robust estimator is critically compromised by the arbitrary scaling factor
\alpha
in the reparameterization
(l,r)\to(\alpha l,\alpha^{-1}r)
. When the factorization is scaled such that
|r|
is small, the gradient magnitude
|g_{l}|=|\xi r|
diminishes; consequently, even gross outliers may satisfy
|\xi r|<\tau
, rendering the truncation inactive and reverting the method to non-robust least squares. Conversely, when
|r|
is large, the gradient is artificially amplified, causing valid signals to exceed
\tau
and be indiscriminately clipped. This leads to a vanishing effective step size and arbitrarily slow convergence, demonstrating that a fixed threshold cannot simultaneously accommodate the conflicting scales induced by the factorization.

The fundamental flaw is that fixed-threshold robustifiers conflate the statistical noise
\xi
with the arbitrary scaling factor
r
. To resolve this, we must
de-scale
the gradient to isolate the statistical component
\xi
, apply the robust function to the noise, and then
re-scale
to match the current parameterization. This ensures that robustness is scale invariant, motivating the three-stage mechanism of our proposed Scaled Robust Gradient Descent (SRGD).

2.3
Algorithm: De-scaling, Truncation, and Re-scaling

We formally implement the scale-invariant strategy via a three-stage gradient modification process. The geometric intuition behind this procedure is visualized in Figure
1
.

Step 1: De-scaling.
We first rescale the gradients to remove the influence of the current factor magnitudes. The
de-scaled gradients
are obtained by post-multiplying the raw gradients with the inverse square root of the Gram matrices:

\begin{split}\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}&=\mathcal{P}(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top});z_{i}))(\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}),\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2}&=\mathcal{P}(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top});z_{i}))^{\top}(\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2}).\end{split}

(5)

This operation captures the descent
direction
invariant to the relative scaling of
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{R}
. As illustrated in Figure
1
(b), de-scaling effectively preconditions the gradient space, restoring an isotropic geometry where the statistical magnitude of the noise is decoupled from the parameter scale.

Step 2: Truncation.
To enforce robustness, we apply element-wise truncation to the de-scaled gradients. For a matrix
\mathbf{M}
and threshold
\tau>0
, let
\text{T}(\mathbf{M},\tau)_{j,k}=\text{sgn}(\mathbf{M}_{j,k})\cdot\min(|\mathbf{M}_{j,k}|,\tau)
. The averaged truncated gradients are:

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{L}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};\tau)&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\Big(\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2},\tau\Big),\\
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};\tau)&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\Big(\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2},\tau\Big).\end{split}

(6)

Crucially, because truncation is applied in the de-scaled domain, the threshold
\tau
acts uniformly on the gradient directions. Figure
1
(a) demonstrates the consequence of skipping the de-scaling step: if the factors are imbalanced (e.g.,
\|\mathbf{R}\|_{\text{F}}\gg\|\mathbf{L}\|_{\text{F}}
), the gradients are distorted. Valid signals along the
\mathbf{L}
-direction are artificially amplified and clipped by the fixed
\tau
-box, while outliers along the
\mathbf{R}
-direction are shrunk and thus omitted from the robustness control. Our de-scaled approach (Figure
1
(b)) avoids these failure modes.

Step 3: Re-scaling.
Finally, we map the robustified update directions back to the parameter space by post-multiplying with the scaling factors
(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}
and
(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2}
, respectively. This restores the correct scale for the parameter update.

The resulting Scaled Robust Gradient Descent (SRGD) procedure is summarized in Algorithm

. The algorithm requires the Gram matrices
\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j}
and
\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j}
to remain nonsingular. Theorem
1
guarantees that, given suitable initialization, this condition holds throughout the optimization trajectory.

2.4
Computational Convergence Analysis

We establish the local convergence of SRGD under mild regularity conditions. Let
\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}
denote the ground truth, with SVD
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast})=\mathbf{U}_{\ast}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\ast}\mathbf{V}_{\ast}^{\top}
. To resolve the factorization ambiguity in our analysis, we fix the target factors as
\mathbf{L}_{\ast}=\mathbf{U}_{\ast}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\ast}^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\ast}=\mathbf{V}_{\ast}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\ast}^{1/2}
. We adopt the factorization distance metric from
Tong et al., 2021a
:

Definition 1

(Factorization Distance)
.

Let
\mathbf{F}=(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})
and
\mathbf{F}_{\ast}=(\mathbf{L}_{\ast},\mathbf{R}_{\ast})
. The distance between
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{F}_{\ast}
is defined as

d(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}_{\ast})=\inf_{\mathbf{Q}\in\textup{GL}(K)}\sqrt{\left\|(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{L}_{\ast})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\ast}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\left\|(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{\ast})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{\ast}^{1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}}~.

(7)

We assume the loss function satisfies the Restricted Correlated Gradient (RCG) condition. This condition is imposed on the
expected
gradient to accommodate heavy-tailed distributions where the empirical loss may not concentrate uniformly.

Definition 2

(Restricted Correlated Gradient)
.

The loss function
\mathcal{L}
is said to satisfy the Restricted Correlated Gradient (RCG) condition if, for any
\mathbf{\Theta}
satisfying (
1
), we have

\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Theta};z_{i})],\mathbf{\Theta}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\rangle\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\mathbf{\Theta}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Theta};z_{i})]\|_{\textup{F}}^{2},

(8)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are RCG parameters satisfying
0<\alpha\leq\beta
.

To ensure statistical stability, we require the de-scaled robust gradients to be accurate estimators of the population gradients.

Definition 3

(De-scaled Robust Gradient Stability)
.

Given
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})
, the de-scaled robust gradient functions
\mathbf{G}_{L}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})
and
\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})
are said to be stable if there exist positive constants
\phi
,
\xi_{L}
, and
\xi_{R}
such that for all
(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})
,

\begin{split}\left\|\mathbf{G}_{L}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})\right](\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top})-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{L}^{2},\\
\left\|\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R})-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}f(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{R};z_{i})\right](\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top})-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2}.\end{split}

(9)

Here,
\xi_{L}
and
\xi_{R}
represent the statistical error floor induced by truncation bias and variance, while
\phi
controls the sensitivity to parameter estimation error.

Theorem 1

(Local Convergence of SRGD)
.

Suppose the loss function
\mathcal{L}
satisfies Definition
2
, and the stability condition of Definition
3
holds uniformly for all iterates with parameters
\phi\lesssim\alpha^{2}
and
\xi_{L}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{3}\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}
. Furthermore, assume the initial distance satisfies
d(\mathbf{F}_{0},\mathbf{F}_{\ast})^{2}\lesssim\alpha\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}
, and the step size is chosen as
\eta\asymp\beta^{-1}
. Then, for all
j=1,2,\dots,J
, the iterates produced by Algorithm
1
satisfy

d(\mathbf{F}_{j},\mathbf{F}_{\ast})^{2}\asymp\|\mathbf{\Theta}_{j}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\leq(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})^{j}d(\mathbf{F}_{0},\mathbf{F}_{\ast})^{2}+C\alpha^{-2}(\xi_{L}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2}),

(10)

where
\mathbf{\Theta}_{j}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top})
. Moreover,
\mathbf{L}_{j}
and
\mathbf{R}_{j}
are nonsingular for all
j=1,\dots,J
.

Theorem
1
underscores two fundamental advantages of the SRGD framework. First, the algorithm achieves local linear convergence at a rate determined solely by the condition number of the expected loss (
\beta/\alpha
). Crucially, this rate is independent of the condition number of the underlying matrix factorization (
\kappa
), indicating that the de-scaling step effectively preconditions the optimization landscape against geometric ill-conditioning. Second, the estimation error stabilizes at a floor determined by the statistical quality of the robust gradients (
\xi_{L},\xi_{R}
). This clean separation between optimization geometry and statistical robustness validates our design: the algorithm maintains efficient convergence and robust error control even when the target matrix is highly ill-conditioned or the factors are arbitrarily scaled.

3
Sparsity and Scaled Hard Thresholding

3.1
Sparse Kronecker Factors and Scaled Hard Thresholding

In high-dimensional regimes where the ambient dimensions
d_{1},d_{2}
exceed the sample size
n
, exploiting low-dimensional structure is essential for consistent estimation. While the Kronecker rank
K
captures global correlation, additional parsimony is often achieved by assuming the factor matrices
\{\mathbf{A}_{k},\mathbf{B}_{k}\}
are sparse.
In the permuted domain
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{\Theta})=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}
, this corresponds to row-wise sparsity in
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{R}
. Specifically, if the rows of
\mathbf{L}
are sparse, entire blocks of the Kronecker product are zeroed out.

However, enforcing this sparsity is non-trivial due to the factorization ambiguity. A standard approach would be to apply row-wise hard thresholding directly to the iterates
\mathbf{L}_{j}
. This approach is flawed by the scaling ambiguities.
Consider the transformation
(\mathbf{L}^{\prime},\mathbf{R}^{\prime})=(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}^{-\top})
. The row norms of the transformed factor
\mathbf{L}^{\prime}
are:

\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{\prime}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q}\|_{2},

(11)

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the coordinate vector whose
i
-th element is one and others zero.
Since
\mathbf{Q}
is arbitrary, it can arbitrarily inflate or deflate specific row norms, thereby changing which rows survive the thresholding operation. Consequently, the recovered support set depends on the arbitrary basis
\mathbf{Q}
rather than the intrinsic signal, leading to inconsistent variable selection.

To resolve this, we introduce Scaled Hard Thresholding (SHT). The core principle is to perform variable selection in a
scale-invariant domain
. We define the
scaled factors
as:

\overline{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{1/2}.

(12)

The row norms of these scaled factors are invariant to the parameterization. For any
\mathbf{Q}\in\text{GL}(K)
, the
i
-th row norm of the transformed scaled factor
\overline{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{L}}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{Q}^{-\top})^{1/2}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{i}=\|\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{L}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus, thresholding based on the row norms of
\overline{\mathbf{L}}
selects a support set that depends strictly on the underlying parameter
\mathbf{\Theta}
, respecting the geometry of the Kronecker manifold.

The SRGD–SHT algorithm (Algorithm
2
) integrates this operator into the iterative update. In each iteration
j
, we compute robust gradient updates to obtain intermediate iterates
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1},\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}
, apply hard thresholding to their scaled counterparts, and then restore the original scaling to maintain the factorization structure.

Algorithm 2

SRGD with Scaled Hard Thresholding (SRGD–SHT)

Input
: initial value
\mathbf{F}_{0}
, step size
\eta>0
, truncation parameter
\tau>0
, number of iterations
J
, and sparsity levels
s_{L},s_{R}

For
j=0,\dots,J-1

// 1. Scaled Robust Gradient Descent

\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}=\mathbf{L}_{j}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}_{L}(\mathbf{L}_{j},\mathbf{R}_{j};\tau)(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}

\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}=\mathbf{R}_{j}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}_{R}(\mathbf{L}_{j},\mathbf{R}_{j};\tau)(\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j})^{-1/2}

// 2. Scaled Hard Thresholding

\mathbf{L}_{j+1}=\text{HT}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1})^{1/2},s_{L})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1})^{-1/2}

\mathbf{R}_{j+1}=\text{HT}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1})^{1/2},s_{R})(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1})^{-1/2}

End for

Output

\widehat{\mathbf{F}}=(\mathbf{L}_{J},\mathbf{R}_{J})
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}_{J}\mathbf{R}_{J}^{\top})

3.2
Computational Convergence Analysis with Sparsity

We analyze the local convergence of SRGD–SHT assuming the ground truth factors
\mathbf{L}_{\ast}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\ast}
are row-sparse with supports of size
s_{L,\ast}
and
s_{R,\ast}
. Let
s_{L}\geq s_{L,\ast}
and
s_{R}\geq s_{R,\ast}
be the user-specified sparsity levels.
To handle high-dimensional constraints, we relax the gradient stability condition (Definition
3
) to hold only over sparse subsets.

Definition 4

(Stability on Sparse Sets)
.

The de-scaled robust gradient functions
\mathbf{G}_{L},\mathbf{G}_{R}
are stable on sparse sets if there exist constants
\phi
,
\xi_{L,s}
, and
\xi_{R,s}
such that for all row-subsets
S_{1},S_{2}
with
|S_{1}|\leq s_{L}+s_{L,\ast}
and
|S_{2}|\leq s_{R}+s_{R,\ast}
:

\begin{split}\|\{\mathbf{G}_{L}-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{L}}f](\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2}\}_{S_{1}}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top})-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{L,s}^{2},\\
\|\{\mathbf{G}_{R}-\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{R}}f](\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2}\}_{S_{2}}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top})-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}+\xi_{R,s}^{2},\end{split}

(13)

where
\mathbf{M}_{S}
denotes the submatrix of
\mathbf{M}
formed by rows indexed by
S
.

This condition implies that the truncated gradients concentrate around their population means when projected onto low-dimensional subspaces, even if the ambient dimension is large.

Theorem 2

(Local Convergence of SRGD–SHT)
.

Suppose the loss function
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the RCG condition (Definition
2
) and the sparse stability condition (Definition
4
) holds with
\phi\lesssim\alpha^{2}
and
\xi_{L,s}^{2}+\xi_{R,s}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{3}\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}
.
If the sparsity levels satisfy
s_{L}\gtrsim(\beta/\alpha)^{2}s_{L,\ast}
and
s_{R}\gtrsim(\beta/\alpha)^{2}s_{R,\ast}
, the initial distance satisfies
d(\mathbf{F}_{0},\mathbf{F}_{\ast})^{2}\lesssim\alpha\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}
, and
\eta\asymp\beta^{-1}
, then for all
j=1,2,\dots,J
:

\|\mathbf{\Theta}_{j}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\leq(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})^{j}d(\mathbf{F}_{0},\mathbf{F}_{\ast})^{2}+C\alpha^{-2}(\xi_{L,s}^{2}+\xi_{R,s}^{2}).

(14)

where
\mathbf{\Theta}_{j}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top})
. Moreover,
\mathbf{L}_{j}
and
\mathbf{R}_{j}
are nonsingular for all
j
.

Theorem
2
establishes that SRGD–SHT attains local linear convergence up to a statistical error floor. Crucially, because the gradient error is measured only on sparse sets (
\xi_{L,s},\xi_{R,s}
), the resulting statistical rate scales with the sparsity level
s
rather than the ambient dimensions
d_{1},d_{2}
.

4
Application to Matrix Models

In this section, we instantiate the proposed SRGD–SHT framework for three canonical matrix estimation problems: matrix trace regression, matrix generalized linear models (GLMs), and bilinear models. We specify the gradient oracles and derive explicit sample size and moment requirements for each case. We focus on Algorithm
2
, with Algorithm
1
recovered when the sparsity levels satisfy
s_{L}=d_{1}
and
s_{R}=d_{2}
. For convenience, we define
s=\max(\min(s_{L}+s_{L,\ast},d_{1}),\min(s_{R}+s_{R,\ast},d_{2}))
and
d=\max(d_{1},d_{2})
.

4.1
Matrix Trace Regression

We first consider the matrix trace regression model

Y_{i}=\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\rangle+E_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(15)

where
\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}
admits the Kronecker structure in (
1
). We minimize the quadratic loss function
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})=(2n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}\rangle)^{2}
.

To ensure consistent estimation, we employ a two-stage estimation procedure.

1.

Initialization (Robust Dantzig Selector):
We first compute a coarse initial estimate using a robustified Dantzig selector. Let
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}(\tau_{x})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}(\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top},\tau_{x})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}(\tau_{yx})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}(Y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i},\tau_{yx})
be truncated covariance estimators, where
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
. We solve:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{DS}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}p_{2}}}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{1}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}(\tau_{x})\boldsymbol{\theta}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}(\tau_{yx})\|_{\infty}\leq R.

(16)

We reshape
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{DS}}
into a matrix, apply the permutation
\mathcal{P}
, and perform SVD followed by Scaled Hard Thresholding (SHT) to obtain initial factors
\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{0}
.

2.

Refinement (SRGD–SHT):
Starting from
(\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{0})
, we run Algorithm
2
using the de-scaled robust gradients:

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{L}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\left(\left\{\langle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\rangle-Y_{i}\right\}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2},\tau\right),\\
\mathbf{G}_{R}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\left(\left\{\langle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\rangle-Y_{i}\right\}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2},\tau\right).\end{split}

(17)

To quantify robustness, we define the requisite moment conditions. For any
\eta>0
, define the global moments
M_{x,\eta}=\sup_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1}\mathbb{E}[|\textup{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{v}|^{\eta}]
and
M_{e,\eta}=\mathbb{E}[|E_{i}|^{\eta}|\mathbf{X}_{i}]
. For high-dimensional inference, we control the predictor moments on sparse subspaces:

M_{x,\eta,s}=\max\left(\max_{j}\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}(d_{2},s_{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\mathbf{v}|^{\eta}\right],\max_{k}\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}(d_{1},s_{2})}\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\mathbf{c}_{k}|^{\eta}\right]\right),

(18)

where
\mathbb{S}(d,s)=\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{0}=s,\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1\}
.

Assumption 1

(Moment Conditions)
.

The vectorized covariate
\textup{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{i})
has zero mean and positive-definite covariance
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}
with condition number
\kappa_{x}=\beta_{x}/\alpha_{x}
. There exist
\epsilon\in(0,1]
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
such that
M_{e,1+\epsilon}<\infty
and
M_{x,2+2\lambda}<\infty
.

We now state the main result establishing the convergence of the two-stage procedure.

Theorem 3

(Convergence of Matrix Trace Regression)
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. Let the tuning parameters for initialization
(\tau_{x},\tau_{yx},R)
be chosen as in Proposition
1
(see Appendix
B
), and the SRGD parameters satisfy
\tau\asymp[nM_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}/\log d]^{1/(1+\epsilon)}
and
\eta\asymp\beta_{x}^{-1}
.
If the sample size
n
satisfies:

n\gtrsim\mathfrak{C}_{1}\cdot\left(sK\right)^{\frac{1+\min(\epsilon,\lambda)}{2\min(\epsilon,\lambda)}}\log d,

(19)

where
\mathfrak{C}_{1}
is a polynomial function of the moment constants and condition numbers (see Appendix
B
), then with probability at least
1-C\exp(-c\log d)
, the final output
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}
satisfies:

\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}\lesssim\alpha_{x}^{-1}\sqrt{s}\left[\frac{M_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(20)

Theorem
3
guarantees that the proposed pipeline converges to the optimal error floor provided the sample size is sufficient. Importantly, the result elucidates the distinct roles played by the predictor tail index
\lambda
and the noise tail index
\epsilon
.

Remark 1

(Optimality and Phase Transitions)
.

The theoretical guarantees exhibit two distinct phase transitions governed by the tail behaviors of the data.

1.

Statistical Efficiency (
\epsilon
): The final estimation error in (
20
) depends solely on the noise moment
\epsilon
. In the finite variance regime (
\epsilon=1
), the estimator recovers the optimal parametric rate
O(\sqrt{s\log d/n})
characterizing Gaussian settings. As the noise tails become heavier (
\epsilon<1
), the rate naturally slows to
O(n^{-\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}})
, reflecting the information-theoretic limit of robust estimation. This phase transition phenomenon has also been observed in other regression literature and is proved to be minimax optimal
(
Sun et al., 2020
;
Tan et al., 2023
)
.

2.

Sample Complexity (
\lambda
): The sample size requirement (
19
) depends on
\min(\epsilon,\lambda)
. This implies that the bottleneck for estimation is determined by the heavier of the two tails (predictor or noise). Specifically, if the predictors are very heavy-tailed (
\lambda<\epsilon
), the sample complexity scales as
O(s^{\frac{1+\lambda}{2\lambda}}\log d)
, which is super-linear in the dimension. This increased requirement reflects the difficulty of ensuring uniform concentration of the robust gradient and covariance estimators when the predictors themselves are prone to large deviations.

4.2
Matrix Generalized Linear Models

We next consider Matrix Generalized Linear Models (Matrix GLMs), which relate a matrix predictor
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
to a scalar response
Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
via an exponential family distribution:

f_{Y_{i}}(y_{i}|\mathbf{X}_{i})\propto\exp\left\{\frac{y_{i}\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}\rangle-g(\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}\rangle)}{c(\sigma)}\right\},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(21)

The negative log-likelihood loss is
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[g(\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}\rangle)-Y_{i}\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}\rangle]
. We impose the following smoothness condition on the link function.

Assumption 2

(Lipschitz Smoothness)
.

The cumulant function
g(\cdot)
has an
L
-Lipschitz continuous derivative; i.e.,
|g^{\prime}(a)-g^{\prime}(b)|\leq L|a-b|
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

Analogous to the trace regression case, we employ a two-stage estimation procedure:

1.

Initialization (Robust Lasso):
We solve a robustified Lasso problem on the vectorized parameter. Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}(\tau_{x})=\text{T}(\mathbf{X}_{i},\tau_{x})
be the truncated predictors. We compute:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\textup{LA}}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[g(\langle\text{vec}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}(\tau_{x})),\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle)-Y_{i}\langle\text{vec}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i}(\tau_{x})),\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle\right]+R\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{1}\right\}.

(22)

The solution is matricized, permuted, and subjected to SVD-based SHT to yield
\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{0}
.

2.

Refinement (SRGD–SHT):
We refine the estimates using Algorithm
2
with gradients:

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{L}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\left(\left\{g^{\prime}(\langle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\rangle)-Y_{i}\right\}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i})\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2};\tau\right),\\
\mathbf{G}_{R}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\left(\left\{g^{\prime}(\langle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i}),\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\rangle)-Y_{i}\right\}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{X}_{i})^{\top}\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2};\tau\right).\end{split}

(23)

We adopt the same moment definitions as in Section
4.1
, denoting the effective conditional noise moment by
M_{e,\eta}=\mathbb{E}[|Y_{i}-g^{\prime}(\langle\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\rangle)|^{\eta}|\mathbf{X}_{i}]
.

Theorem 4

(Convergence of Matrix GLM)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Let the initialization parameters be tuned as in Proposition
4
(Appendix
C
), and SRGD parameters satisfy
\tau\asymp[nM_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}/\log d]^{1/(1+\epsilon)}
and
\eta\asymp\beta_{x}^{-1}
.
If the sample size satisfies

n\gtrsim\mathfrak{C}_{2}\cdot\left(sK\right)^{\frac{1+\lambda}{2\lambda}}\log d,

(24)

with a constant
\mathfrak{C}_{2}
adapted for GLM (see Appendix
C
), then with probability at least
1-C\exp(-c\log d)
, the two-stage estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}
satisfies:

\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}\lesssim\alpha_{x}^{-1}\sqrt{s}\left[\frac{M_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(25)

Theorem
4
extends our robust guarantees to the exponential family. A particularly important instance is
matrix logistic regression
, which is widely used in classification tasks.

Corollary 1

(Matrix Logistic Regression)
.

Consider the logistic regression model with
g(t)=\log(1+e^{t})
. Since the response
Y_{i}
is bounded, we have
M_{e,\eta}<\infty
for all
\eta
. Setting
\epsilon=1
, and assuming predictors have finite
(2+2\lambda)
-th moments, the two-stage estimator achieves the rate

\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}\lesssim\alpha_{x}^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{sM_{x,2,s}\log d}{n}}.

(26)

This result highlights a key advantage: for logistic regression, the statistical rate is not penalized by the heavy tails of the predictors; robustness only imposes a sample complexity cost via
\lambda
to ensure uniform convergence of the gradients. This relaxes the finite fourth-moment conditions commonly required in prior robust GLM literature
(
Prasad et al., 2020
;
Zhu and Zhou, 2021
)
.

4.3
Bilinear Regression Models

Finally, we consider the Bilinear Regression model, which captures the relationship between a matrix predictor
\mathbf{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{1}\times q_{2}}
and a matrix response
\mathbf{Y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
:

\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}^{\top}+\mathbf{E}_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

(27)

where
\mathbf{E}_{i}
is the noise matrix. Vectorizing this relationship yields the Kronecker structure:

\mathbf{y}_{i}=\text{vec}(\mathbf{Y}_{i}^{\top})=(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B})\text{vec}(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top})+\text{vec}(\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\top})=\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{i}.

(28)

Here, the target
\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}=\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}
has Kronecker rank
K=1
. This model is fundamental in spatio-temporal modeling and matrix autoregression
(
Chen et al., 2021
;
Fan et al., 2025
)
.

We estimate
\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}
by minimizing the least-squares loss
\mathcal{L}_{n}(\mathbf{\Theta})=(2n)^{-1}\sum_{i}\|\mathbf{y}_{i}-\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
. We employ the following two-stage estimation procedure:

1.

Initialization (Robust Dantzig Selector):

Recognizing that
\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{yx}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}
, we estimate the cross-covariance
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{yx}(\tau_{yx})=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\text{T}(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top},\tau_{yx})
and auto-covariance
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}(\tau_{x})
via truncation. We solve the robust Dantzig selector:

\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}_{\text{DS}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{\Theta}}\|\mathbf{\Theta}\|_{1}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{yx}(\tau_{yx})-\mathbf{\Theta}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}(\tau_{x})\|_{\infty}\leq R.

(29)

The solution is matricized and subjected to rank-1 SVD and SHT to yield initial factors
\mathbf{L}_{0},\mathbf{R}_{0}
.

2.

Refinement (SRGD–SHT):
We refine the factors using Algorithm
2
(
K=1
) with gradients:

\begin{split}\mathbf{G}_{L}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\Big(\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}^{\top}-\mathbf{Y}_{i})\right)\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1/2};\tau\Big),\\
\mathbf{G}_{R}&=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}\Big(\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\otimes(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}^{\top}-\mathbf{Y}_{i})^{\top}\right)\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1/2};\tau\Big).\end{split}

(30)

To rigorously characterize robustness, we must control the tail behavior of the gradient noise. Unlike trace regression, the gradient noise here involves the interaction term
\mathbf{J}_{i}=\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{E}_{i}
. We define the effective interaction moment on sparse sets as:

M_{\textup{eff},\eta,s}:=\max\left\{\max_{j}\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}(d_{2},s_{R})}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{i}\mathbf{v}\right|^{\eta}\right],\max_{k}\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{S}(d_{1},s_{L})}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{i}\mathbf{c}_{k}\right|^{\eta}\right]\right\}.

(31)

Assumption 3

(Moment Conditions)
.

The predictor
\mathbf{x}_{i}
satisfies the covariance and moment conditions of Assumption
1
with tail index
\lambda\in(0,1]
. Furthermore, there exists
\epsilon\in(0,1]
such that the interaction moment
M_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}<\infty
.

Theorem 5

(Convergence of Bilinear Estimation)
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds. Let initialization parameters be tuned as in Proposition
5
(Appendix
D
), and SRGD parameters satisfy
\tau\asymp[nM_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}/\log d]^{1/(1+\epsilon)}
and
\eta\asymp\beta_{x}^{-1}
.
If the sample size satisfies:

n\gtrsim\mathfrak{C}_{3}\cdot s^{\frac{1+\min(\epsilon,\lambda)}{2\min(\epsilon,\lambda)}}\log d,

(32)

where
\mathfrak{C}_{3}
is a constant defined in Appendix
D
, then with probability at least
1-C\exp(-c\log d)
, the estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}
satisfies:

\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\textup{F}}\lesssim\alpha_{x}^{-1}\sqrt{s}\left[\frac{M_{\textup{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(33)

Remark 2

(The Challenge of Multiplicative Noise)
.

The convergence rate is governed by
\epsilon
, which characterizes the tail of the interaction
\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{E}_{i}
. This highlights a unique challenge in bilinear models: even if the noise
\mathbf{E}_{i}
and predictors
\mathbf{X}_{i}
are independently moderately heavy-tailed, their product can exhibit extremely heavy tails. For instance, if both possess only finite second moments and they are independent, their product has only a finite second moment, making robustification essential. Our SRGD framework handles this naturally via the truncation of the interaction term
\mathbf{J}_{i}
.

5
Simulation Experiments

In this section, we conduct numerical experiments to validate our theoretical findings and evaluate the empirical performance of the proposed SRGD–SHT framework. The simulations are designed to achieve three main objectives: (1) verify the theoretical relationship between statistical convergence rates and the tail behavior of the noise (Theorems
3
and
5
); (2) demonstrate that our scale-invariant optimization scheme is computationally efficient and robust to the condition number
\kappa
, unlike standard gradient methods; and (3) highlight the superiority of the proposed two-stage estimation procedure over existing baselines in settings characterized by heavy-tailed predictors, heavy-tailed noise, and scaling ambiguity.

For all experiments, we initialize using the Robust Dantzig Selector as described in Section
4
. For the refinement stage, the step size is set to
\eta=0.01
. Regarding the truncation threshold
\tau
, while our theory suggests a scaling of
\tau\asymp n^{1/(1+\epsilon)}
, the exact moment
\epsilon
is often unknown. In practice, we select
\tau
via five-fold cross-validation on a logarithmic grid. Our experiments confirm that the performance is stable around the optimal
\tau
, suggesting the method is not overly sensitive to precise tuning.

Regarding the third objective, since the qualitative patterns are consistent across different model specifications, we report findings for matrix trace regression in the main text. Comprehensive results for bilinear models and matrix logistic regression are provided in Appendix
E
of the Supplementary Materials.

5.1
Experiment 1: Phase Transitions and Tail Behavior

We first validate the theoretical prediction that the statistical error rate is governed by the tail index
\epsilon
of the noise (and interaction terms). We focus on matrix trace regression and bilinear models.
According to Theorems
3
and
5
, the estimation error satisfies
\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}-\mathbf{\Theta}_{\ast}\|_{\text{F}}\propto n^{-\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)}
. Consequently, in a log-log plot of error vs. sample size, we expect a linear relationship with slope
-\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)
.

We adopt the following settings.

•

Matrix Trace Regression:

p_{1}=q_{1}=p_{2}=q_{2}=6
, rank
K=1
, with sparsity
s_{L,\ast}=s_{R,\ast}=5
. The true factors are set to
\mathbf{L}_{\ast}=\mathbf{R}_{\ast}=(1,\dots,1,0,\dots,0)^{\top}
.

•

Bilinear Model:

p_{1}=p_{2}=5
,
q_{1}=q_{2}=10
, rank
K=1
, sparsity
s_{L,\ast}=s_{R,\ast}=8
. Nonzero entries of
\mathbf{A}_{\ast}
and
\mathbf{B}_{\ast}
are set to
\sqrt{5/8}
.

•

Data Generation:
Predictors
\mathbf{X}_{i}
are generated with i.i.d.
t_{2.5}
entries. Noise terms (

7
Conclusion and Discussion

In this article, we have introduced a unified framework for high-dimensional structured matrix estimation that resolves the fundamental tension between
non-convex scaling ambiguity
and
statistical robustness
. Our work demonstrates that standard robustification techniques, such as gradient truncation or Huber loss, are geometrically fragile in factorized models. When the factorization is ill-scaled, these fixed-threshold mechanisms fail to distinguish between heavy-tailed outliers and valid signals along large-norm directions.

Our proposed methodology, integrating Scaled Robust Gradient Descent (SRGD) and Scaled Hard Thresholding (SHT), addresses this failure mode by operating in a transformation-invariant domain. By de-scaling the gradient before truncation and performing variable selection on normalized factors, we ensure that the estimation process is stable regardless of the arbitrary parameterization of the Kronecker product.

From a theoretical perspective, our analysis establishes the convergence for a two-stage estimation procedure across three canonical models: matrix trace regression, matrix GLMs, and bilinear regression. A key insight from our results is the characterization of a smooth phase transition governed by the tail index
\epsilon
of the noise. We prove that robustness does not incur an efficiency penalty in finite-variance regimes (
\epsilon=1
), while optimally degrading to
O(n^{-\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)})
in heavier-tailed settings. This provides a rigorous quantification of the cost of robustness in high-dimensional structured estimation.

The principles developed here extend beyond the specific case of Kronecker products. The “scale-then-robustify” philosophy is broadly applicable to other non-convex factorizations plagued by scaling ambiguities, such as tensor CP/Tucker decompositions and deep neural networks. In these settings, gradient magnitudes can vary exponentially across layers or modes; our results suggest that de-scaled, element-wise truncation may offer a generic path toward stabilizing robust optimization in deep or multi-modal architectures. Future work will explore these extensions, as well as the adaptation of our two-stage framework to online and streaming data environments.

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\

&+(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(j)}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+(1+\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(j)}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},\end{split}

(45)

where the first term can be further bounded by

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\cdot\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-2\eta\left\langle(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{L}_{*}^{\top},\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]^{\top}\right\rangle\\
&+2\eta\left\langle(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2},\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]^{\top}(\mathbf{U}_{*}-\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2})\right\rangle.\end{split}

(46)

Combining these two upper bounds in (
44
) and (
46
), we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+\eta^{2}\left(\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}+\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\right)\cdot\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&-2\eta\left\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}],(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}+\mathbf{L}_{*}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\right\rangle\\
&+2\eta\left\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}],(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}(\mathbf{V}_{*}-\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2})^{\top}\right.\\
&\left.\quad\quad\quad+(\mathbf{U}^{*}-\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\right\rangle\\
=:&\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\eta^{2}M_{1}-2\eta M_{2}+2\eta M_{3},\end{split}

(47)

where
M_{1}
,
M_{2}
, and
M_{3}
are defined as above.

By Lemma
1
and the bound

\max(\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}},\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}})\leq B,

(48)

we have the upper bound for
M_{1}
given as

M_{1}\leq\frac{2}{(1-B)^{2}}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.

By the permutation operator
\mathcal{P}
, it is clear that
\|\nabla f^{(j)}\|_{\text{F}}=\|\nabla\mathcal{L}^{(j)}\|_{\text{F}}
. In addition, by the RCG condition, Lemma
2
and mean inequality,

\begin{split}&M_{2}=\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}],\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}-(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\rangle\\
=&\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}],\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\rangle-\langle\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}],(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\rangle\\
\geq&\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{4\beta}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-\beta\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\frac{\alpha}{2}\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4\beta}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}-\beta\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},\end{split}

(49)

where the last term can be bounded by

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2}\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\cdot\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{B^{2}}{2}\left(\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(50)

Also, by the mean inequality and Lemma
1
, for any
\gamma>0
,

\begin{split}M_{3}\leq&\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}\cdot\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}\cdot\|\mathbf{V}_{*}-\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}\\
&+\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}\cdot\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}\cdot\|\mathbf{U}_{*}-\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}\\
\leq&\frac{\sqrt{2}B\gamma^{-1}}{1-B}\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\frac{\sqrt{2}\gamma}{4}\left(\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\right).\end{split}

(51)

Therefore, combining the results above, by Lemma
2
, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\left(1+B^{2}\beta\eta+\frac{\sqrt{2}\gamma B\eta}{2}\right)\left(\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\right)\\
&-\alpha\eta\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{(1-B)^{2}}+\frac{2\sqrt{2}B\gamma^{-1}\eta}{1-B}-\frac{\eta}{2\beta}\right)\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\left(1+B^{2}\beta\eta+\frac{\sqrt{2}\gamma B\eta}{2}-\frac{\alpha\eta}{2}\right)(\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2})\\
&+\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{(1-B)^{2}}+2\sqrt{2}B\gamma^{-1}\eta-\frac{\eta}{2\beta}\right)\|\mathbb{E}[\nabla f^{(j)}]\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(52)

Letting
\gamma=C_{1}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
,
\eta=C_{2}\beta^{-1}
, and
\zeta=C_{3}\alpha\beta^{-1}
, if
B\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\alpha/\beta}
, for some universal constants
C_{1},\dots,C_{4}>0
, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})(\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2})\\
&+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}^{(j)}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(j)}(\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(53)

Here,
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}^{(j)}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}^{(j)}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}
, and

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}=\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{\top}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2})\\
=&\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2})=\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(1-B)^{2}}\leq C.\end{split}

(54)

Similarly, we also have
\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\leq C
.

According to the stability of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}^{(j)}
and
\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(j)}
, we have

\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{L}^{(j)}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\phi\|\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{L}^{2}

(55)

and

\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{R}^{(j)}(\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim\phi\|\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2}.

(56)

Therefore, with
\phi\lesssim\alpha^{2}
, we have that

\begin{split}d(\mathbf{F}_{j+1},\mathbf{F}_{*})^{2}\leq&~(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})d(\mathbf{F}_{j},\mathbf{F}_{*})^{2}+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}(\xi_{L}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2})\\
\leq&~\cdots\\
\leq&~(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})^{t}d(\mathbf{F}_{0},\mathbf{F}_{*})^{2}+C\alpha^{-2}(\xi_{L}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2}).\end{split}

(57)

Finally, since
\xi_{L}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{3}\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}
, we have

\begin{split}d(\mathbf{F}_{j+1},\mathbf{F}_{*})^{2}&\leq(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})C\alpha\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}(\xi_{L}^{2}+\xi_{R}^{2})\\
&\leq C\alpha\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}(\xi^{2}_{L}+\xi^{2}_{R}-C\alpha^{3}\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2})\\
&\leq C\alpha\beta^{-1}\sigma_{K}^{2}.\end{split}

(58)

Then, the whole proof can be completed inductively. In addition, it naturally implies that
\mathbf{L}_{j+1}
and
\mathbf{R}_{j+1}
are not singular.
∎

A.2
Convergence of SRGD–SHT

Proof of Theorem
2
.

The proof is developed by induction. The proposed projected robust gradient descent algorithm is formulated
as

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}&=\mathbf{L}_{j}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}_{L}^{(j)}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2},\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}&=\mathbf{R}_{j}-\eta\cdot\mathbf{G}_{R}^{(j)}(\mathbf{L}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{L}_{j})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{L}_{j+1}&=\text{HT}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1})^{1/2},s_{L})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1})^{-1/2},\\
\mathbf{R}_{j+1}&=\text{HT}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1})^{1/2},s_{R})(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1})^{-1/2}.\end{split}

(59)

Denote the nonzero active sets of
\mathbf{L}_{j}
,
\mathbf{R}_{j}
,
\mathbf{L}_{*}
, and
\mathbf{R}_{*}
as
S_{L,j}
,
S_{R,j}
,
S_{L,*}
and
S_{R,*}
, respectively. Let
\bar{S}_{L,j}=S_{L,j}\cup S_{L,*}
and
\bar{S}_{R,j}=S_{R,j}\cup S_{R,*}
, for all
j=1,2,\dots,J
. Let
s_{1}=s_{L}+s_{L,*}
and
s_{2}=s_{R}+s_{R,*}
.

First, we analyze the scaled hard thresholding step. For any
j=1,2,\dots,J
,

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2},\end{split}

(60)

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{Q}}\{\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j}\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}\mathbf{Q}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\}

(61)

Then, as
\mathbf{L}_{j}=\text{HT}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2},s_{L})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}
, we have the upper bound

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|(\text{HT}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2},s_{L})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
=&\|(\text{HT}((\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2},s_{L})(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}-(\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\text{HT}((\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2},s_{L})-(\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+q_{1})\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2}-(\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}^{-1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+q_{1})\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\cdot\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\cdot\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2},\end{split}

(62)

where
q_{1}=2\sqrt{s_{L,*}/(s_{L}-s_{L,*})}
. Note that by Lemma
1
,

\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}^{-\top}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}=\|\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\leq(1+B)^{2},

(63)

and

\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j})^{-1/2}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}=\|\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{op}}^{2}\leq(1-B)^{-2}.

(64)

These imply the upper bound

\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq(1+q_{1})(1+B)^{2}(1-B)^{-2}\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.

(65)

Similarly, we have

\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\leq(1+q_{2})(1+B)^{2}(1-B)^{-2}\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}_{j}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.

(66)

Given
\max(q_{1},q_{2})\lesssim\alpha\beta^{-1}
and
B=C\alpha^{1/2}\beta^{-1/2}
, by Lemma
3
, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j+1}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j+1}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+C\alpha\beta^{-1})\left[\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\right].\end{split}

(67)

Second, we derive the upper bounds for the gradient descent step. By the optimality of
\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}
, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(68)

Here, for any
\zeta>0
,

\begin{split}&\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1+\zeta)\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*}-\eta\mathbb{E}[\nabla\mathcal{L}_{j}]\mathbf{R}_{j}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&+(1-\zeta^{-1})\eta^{2}\|(\mathbf{\Delta}_{L,j})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}(\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{R}_{j})^{-1/2}\mathbf{Q}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(69)

Following the proof of Theorem
1
, they can be further bounded by

\begin{split}&\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{j+1}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}-\mathbf{L}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j+1}\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{j+1}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})_{\bar{S}_{R,j+1}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})(\|\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2})\\
&+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}(\|(\mathbf{\Delta}_{L,j})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{\Delta}_{R,j})_{\bar{S}_{R,j+2}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}).\end{split}

(70)

Then, by the stability of the robust de-scaled gradient functions,

\begin{split}\|(\mathbf{\Delta}_{L,j})_{\bar{S}_{L,j+1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{L,s_{1}}^{2},\\
\text{and }\|(\mathbf{\Delta}_{R,j})_{\bar{S}_{R,j+1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}&\leq\phi\|\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{R}_{j}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\xi_{R,s_{2}}^{2}.\end{split}

(71)

Hence, given
\phi\lesssim\alpha^{2}
, we have the following upper bound

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{L}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{j}\mathbf{Q}_{j}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
\leq&(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})\left\{\|(\mathbf{L}_{t-1}\mathbf{Q}_{t-1}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{t-1}\mathbf{Q}_{t-1}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\right\}\\
&+C\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}(\xi_{L,s_{1}}^{2}+\xi_{R,s_{2}}^{2})\\
\leq&\cdots\\
\leq&(1-C\alpha\beta^{-1})^{t}\left\{\|(\mathbf{L}_{0}\mathbf{Q}_{0}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|(\mathbf{R}_{0}\mathbf{Q}_{0}^{-\top}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\right\}\\
&+C\alpha^{-2}(\xi_{L,s_{1}}^{2}+\xi_{R,s_{2}}^{2}).\end{split}

(72)

The remaining proof follows a similar argument to that in Appendix
A.1
.
∎

We state some auxiliary lemmas. The first lemma is Lemma 12 in

Lemma 1
.

For any
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
, suppose that

\max\left\{\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}},\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}\right\}<1,

(73)

then we have

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}&\leq 1+\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}},\\
\|\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}&\leq 1+\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}},\\
\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}&\leq\frac{1}{1-\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}},\\
\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}&\leq\frac{1}{1-\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}},\\
\|\mathbf{L}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}-\mathbf{U}_{*}\|_{\textup{op}}&\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}}{1-\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}},\\
\text{and }\|\mathbf{R}(\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R})^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{1/2}-\mathbf{V}_{*}\|_{\textup{op}}&\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}}{1-\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}},\end{split}

(74)

where
\mathbf{U}_{*}=\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}
and
\mathbf{V}_{*}=\mathbf{R}_{*}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}
.

The second lemma combines Lemmas 11 and 13 in
Tong et al., 2021a
, constructing an approximate equivalence between the distance
\text{dist}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}_{*})
and
\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times r}
,

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}\leq&\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\max\left\{\|(\mathbf{L}-\mathbf{L}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}},\|(\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}_{*})\mathbf{\Sigma}_{*}^{-1/2}\|_{\textup{op}}\right\}\right)\\

(75)

and

d(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}_{*})^{2}\leq 2\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}.

(76)

The third lemma develops an upper bound for hard thresholding operation, as in Lemma 3.3 of

Lemma 3
.

For

\textup{HT}(\cdot;k)

(77)

Proposition 1

(79)

where

(80)

Proof of Proposition
1
.

The proof consists of two steps. In the first step, we develop the theoretical results for the initialization via the robust Dantzig selector in (
16
). In the second step, we establish the estimation accuracy of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_{x}(\tau_{x})
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}(\tau_{yx})
. Then, we use these conclusions to verify the initialization condition.

Step 1.
(Error bounds of robust Dantzig Selector)

To begin with, we show that under certain accuracy conditions of
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}
, the estimation error of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{DS}}(R,\tau_{x},\tau_{yx})
is well-behaved with properly selected
R
. Specifically, when

\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}-\mathbf{\sigma}_{yx}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta_{1},

(81)

and
R\geq\zeta_{0}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}+\zeta_{1}
, then the error
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}
can be bounded in terms of multiple vector norms.

Note that

\begin{split}&\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}\|_{\infty}=\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}\|_{\infty}\\
=&\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}\|_{\infty}\\
\leq&\|(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x})\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{\infty}+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}\|_{\infty}\\
\leq&\|(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x})\|_{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}\|_{\infty}\leq\zeta_{0}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}+\zeta_{1}\leq R.\end{split}

(82)

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}
is feasible in the optimization constraint, and hence
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{1}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}
. Then, by triangle inequality, we have

\begin{split}&\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}\|_{\infty}=\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}]\|_{\infty}\\
=&\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}[\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}]\|_{\infty}\\
\leq&\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\cdot\|(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x})\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\cdot\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\widehat{\mathbf{\sigma}}_{yx}\|_{\infty}+\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\cdot\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{yx}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{yx}\|_{\infty}\\
\leq&\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\cdot(\zeta_{0}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{1}+R+\zeta_{1})\leq 2R\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}.\end{split}

(83)

As
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}
is a
s_{*}
-sparse vector with nonzero support
S
and
s_{*}=s_{1,*}s_{2,*}
, we have
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{1}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}=\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1}
. Moreover, we have

\begin{split}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|_{1}=&\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})\|_{1}=\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S^{\perp}}\|_{1}\\
\geq&\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S^{\perp}}\|_{1}-\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1}\\
\geq&\|(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1}+\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S^{\perp}}\|_{1}-\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1},\end{split}

(84)

where the first inequality follows from the triangle inequality and the second one follows from the decomposability of
\|\cdot\|_{1}
. Therefore, we have

\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S^{\perp}}\|_{1}\leq\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1}.

(85)

Hence, the upper bound in terms of
\|\cdot\|_{1}
can be bounded as

\begin{split}&\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}=\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1}+\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S^{\perp}}\|_{1}\\
\leq&2\|(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*})_{S}\|_{1}\leq 2s_{*}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 4s_{*}R\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}.\end{split}

(86)

Finally, by the duality between
\|\cdot\|_{1}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and
R\asymp\zeta_{0}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}+\zeta_{1}
, we have

\begin{split}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{2}\leq&\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{*}\|_{\infty}^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{8s_{*}}R\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\\
\lesssim&\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{-1}\|_{1,\infty}\sqrt{8s_{*}}(\zeta_{0}\|\boldsymbol{\theta_{*}}\|_{1}+\zeta_{1}).\end{split}

(87)

Step 2.
(Accuracy of robust covariance estimation)

Next, we establish the estimation accuracy
\zeta_{0}
and
\zeta_{1}
. Denote
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\text{Vec}(\mathbf{X}_{i})

M_{x,2+2\lambda,1}

j

(88)

where

represents the

\mathbf{x}_{i}

For

(1+\delta)

M_{e,1+\delta}M_{x,1+\delta}

3

(91)

Proof of Proposition

Step 1.
(Moment bounds)

Denote
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}=[\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{K}]
where each
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}

s_{R}
-sparse vector with unit Euclidean norm. Since

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{ijk}|^{1+\epsilon}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[|E_{i}\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}|^{1+\epsilon}|\mathbf{X}_{i}\right]\right]\\

\leq&M_{e,1+\epsilon}\cdot M_{x,1+\epsilon,s}=M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}.\end{split}

(100)

In addition, the
(j,k)
-th entry of
\mathbf{M}_{L,3}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\{\left\langle\mathbf{Z}_{i},\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}\right\rangle\right\}\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k}]

(101)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}[\text{T}\left(\left\{\left\langle\mathbf{Z}_{i},\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\right\rangle-E_{i}\right\}\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k};\tau\right)]-\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(-E_{i}\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}_{k};\tau)]

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[q_{ijk}]+\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(-q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)]-\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)].

Next, we develop an upper bound for the

-th moment for

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[|q_{ijk}|^{1+\lambda}\right]\\

\leq&M_{x,2+2\lambda}\cdot\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{1+\lambda}.\end{split}

(Bound

For any

Step 5.
(Bound

By Bernstein’s inequality, for any

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)\Big]\right|\geq t\right)\\

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\Bigg(&\max_{j\in S_{1},k\in[K]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)\Big]\right|\geq t\Bigg)\\

(119)

For the upper bound
t

and

\displaystyle=C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{V^{2}\log d}{n}}+C_{2}\tau\frac{\log d}{n}

\displaystyle\asymp M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/2}M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{(1-\lambda)/(2+2\epsilon)}\left(\frac{\log d}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon+\lambda}{2+2\epsilon}}\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{R}^{\top}-\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{\frac{1+\lambda}{2}}+M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}\left(\frac{\log d}{n}\right)^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

\displaystyle\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,4})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle+s_{1}KM_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{2}\left(\frac{\log d}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

In summary, based on the upper bounds in steps 2 to 5, we have

where

s=\max\left(\min\left(s_{1},d_{1}\right),\min\left(s_{2},d_{2}\right)\right)
.

In addition, the
j
-th entry of
\mathbf{M}_{L,3}
is

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\mathbf{A}_{*}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right]\\
&+\mathbb{E}\left[\text{T}\left(\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}^{\top}-\mathbf{Y}_{i})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\text{T}\left(\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\mathbf{A}_{*}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top}-\mathbf{Y}_{i})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right)\right]\\
=:&\mathbb{E}[q_{ij}]+\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij}-q_{ij};\tau)]-\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)].\end{split}

(183)

Let
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}_{*}-\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{B}_{*}-\mathbf{B}
. Therefore, for
\mathbb{E}[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\mathbf{A}_{*}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

(184)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\mathbf{A}_{*}\mathbf{X}_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}.

For the first term of (
184
), by Theorem 16.2.2 of
Harville, 1997
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\text{mat}(\mathbf{c}_{j})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top}\text{mat}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right)

(185)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{C}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right)/\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}},

where
\text{mat}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse of the
\text{vec}(\cdot)
operator with appropriate dimensions. The term
\mathbf{C}_{j}=\text{mat}(\mathbf{c}_{j})
represents a sparse indicator matrix, where the entry corresponding to the index
j
is 1 and all other entries are 0. Since
\mathbf{C}_{j}
is rank-1, we decmopose it as
\mathbf{C}_{j}=\mathbf{u}_{j}\mathbf{v}_{j}^{\top}
, where each of
\mathbf{u}_{j}
and
\mathbf{v}_{j}
contains a single 1 and 0s elsewhere. In addition, denote the SVD of
\mathbf{B}_{*}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
as
\mathbf{B}_{*}\mathbf{B}^{\top}=\sum_{k=1}^{r_{B}}\gamma_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\top}
, where
r_{B}
is its rank,
\{\gamma_{k}\}
are singular values and
\{\mathbf{w}_{k}\}
and
\{\mathbf{z}_{k}\}
are corresponding singular vectors. Then, for the first term of (
184
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

(186)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{v}_{j}\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{B}}\gamma_{k}\mathbf{w}_{k}\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\right)\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}^{-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{k=1}^{r_{B}}\gamma_{k}\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{k}\right)\left(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right)\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{B}}\gamma_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{k}\right)\left(\mathbf{z}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{j}\right)\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}^{-1}\|\mathbf{u}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{B}}\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\|_{\text{op}}\|\mathbf{B}_{*}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\|_{\text{nuc}}M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)},

where the first inequality is given by the Minkowski’s inequality, the second is given by an analogous argument to (
102
), and
\|\cdot\|_{\text{nuc}}
denotes the nuclear norm.

Next, we derive a upper bound for
\|\mathbf{B}_{*}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\|_{\text{nuc}}
. We have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}_{*}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\|_{\text{nuc}}\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\|_{\text{nuc}}+\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{nuc}}

(187)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\right)+\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{nuc}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\text{F}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}},

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
\|\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}\|_{\text{nuc}}\leq\|\mathbf{M}_{1}\|_{\text{F}}\|\mathbf{M}_{2}\|_{\text{F}}
for any conformable matrices
\mathbf{M}_{1}
and
\mathbf{M}_{2}
. Since in the bilinear model,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{*}
reduces to the scalar
\sigma_{1}=\|\mathbf{A}_{*}\otimes\mathbf{B}_{*}\|_{\text{F}}=\|\mathbf{B}_{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\|\mathbf{A}_{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
, by the initialization condition (
40
), we obtain that
\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\text{F}}\leq C\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}
. Moreover,
\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{B}}\|_{\text{F}}+\|\mathbf{B}_{*}\|_{\text{F}}\leq C\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}
. Therefore, we have that

\|\mathbf{B}_{*}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\|_{\text{nuc}}\leq C\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\|\mathbf{B}\|_{\text{F}}.

(188)

Finally, by Lemma
2
, the first term of (
184
) is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathbf{c}_{j}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{X}_{i}\otimes(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{B}_{*}^{\top})\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\right|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathbf{A}}\|_{\text{op}}M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)}

(189)

\displaystyle\leq Cd(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{F}_{*})M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\|_{\text{F}}M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)}.

The upper bound of the second term of (
184
) can be devived analogously. Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}]\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\|_{\text{F}}^{1+\lambda}M_{x,2+2\lambda}.

(190)

Step 2.
(Bound
\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,1})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
)

For
\mathbf{M}_{L,1}
and any
S_{1}

\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,1})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in S_{1}}\Big|\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)]-\mathbb{E}[v_{ij}]\Big|^{2}.

(191)

For any

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)]-\mathbb{E}[v_{ij}]\Big|\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[|v_{ij}|1\{|v_{ij}|\geq\tau\}\Big]\leq M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}\cdot\tau^{-\epsilon}\asymp\left[\frac{M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}},\end{split}

(192)

with the truncation parameter
\tau\asymp(nM_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}/\log d)^{1/(1+\epsilon)}
. Hence, for any
|S_{1}|\leq s_{1}
,

\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,1})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim s_{1}\left[\frac{M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(193)

Step 3.
(Bound
\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,2})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}
)

By definition, for any
S_{1}
,

\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,2})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in S_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)-\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)]\right|^{2}.

(194)

For each

,
|\text{T}(v_{ij},\tau)|\leq\tau
, and its variance is upper bounded by

\text{var}(\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau))\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)^{2}\right]\tau^{(1-\epsilon)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{ij}|^{1+\epsilon}\right]\leq\tau^{1-\epsilon}M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}.

(195)

By Bernstein’s inequality, for any
j\in S_{1}
, and

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)-\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)]\right|\geq t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{4\tau^{1-\epsilon}M_{\text{eff}}}\right).

(196)

Letting

, similar to (
110
), we have for

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in S_{1}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)-\mathbb{E}[\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)]\right|\gtrsim\left[\frac{M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}\right)\leq C\exp\left(-C\log d\right).

(197)

Hence, with probability at least
1-C\exp(-C\log d)
,

\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,2})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\lesssim s_{1}\left[\frac{M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{1/\epsilon}\log d}{n}\right]^{\frac{2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}}.

(198)

Step 4.
(Bound
\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,3})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}

By definition, for any
S_{1}

Similar to (

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}[q_{ij}]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{T}(v_{ij}-q_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)\Big]\Big|

(200)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[q_{ij}\cdot 1\{|q_{ij}\geq\tau/2|\}\Big]\Big|+\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[q_{ij}\cdot 1\{|v_{ij}\geq\tau/2|\}\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}\Big]}{\tau^{1+\lambda}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}+\mathbb{E}\left[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big[|v_{ij}|^{1+\epsilon}\Big]}{\tau^{1+\epsilon}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}\right]\cdot\tau^{-\lambda}~+~\mathbb{E}\left[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}\right]^{\frac{1}{1+\lambda}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|v_{ij}|^{1+\epsilon}\right]^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}\cdot\tau^{-\frac{\lambda(1+\epsilon)}{1+\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{x,2+2\lambda}\cdot\left(\frac{\log d}{nM_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\epsilon}}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{1+\lambda}

\displaystyle+M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)}\cdot M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{\lambda/(1+\lambda)}\cdot\left(\frac{\log d}{nM_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}}\right)^{\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{1}^{\lambda}\left(M_{x,2+2\lambda}M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{-\lambda/(1+\epsilon)}+M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{1/(1+\lambda)}\right)\left(\frac{\log d}{n}\right)^{\min\left(\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda},\frac{\lambda}{1+\epsilon}\right)}\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}},

where
\bar{\sigma}_{1}=\max(\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{L}_{*}\mathbf{R}_{*}^{\top}),1)
. Therefore, we have

\begin{split}&\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,3})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
&\lesssim\bar{\sigma}_{1}^{2\lambda}\left(M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{2}M_{\text{eff},1+\epsilon,s}^{-2\lambda/(1+\epsilon)}+M_{x,2+2\lambda}^{2/(1+\lambda)}\right)\left(\frac{\log d}{n}\right)^{\min\left(\frac{2\lambda}{1+\lambda},\frac{2\lambda}{1+\epsilon}\right)}\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(201)

Step 5.
(Bound
\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,4})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}

By definition, for any
S_{1}
,

\|(\mathbf{M}_{L,4})_{S_{1}}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in S_{1}}\Bigg|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ij}+v_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ij}+v_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)\Big]\Bigg|^{2}.

(202)

For each
i=1,\dots,n
, we have
|\text{T}(q_{ij}+v_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)|\leq 2\tau
, and hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\text{T}(q_{ij}+v_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)\Big)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(2\tau)^{1-\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|q_{ij}|^{1+\lambda}\right]

(203)

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{1-\lambda}M_{x,2+2\lambda}\left\|\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{1+\lambda}

\displaystyle=:V^{2}.

By Bernstein’s inequality, for any
j\in S_{1}
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ij}+v_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ij}+v_{ij};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ij};\tau)\Big]\right|\geq t\right)\\

(204)

Let

, where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are two positive constants. By a similar argument to that in Step 5 of Appendix
B.2
, if

Conversely, if

, the RHS of (

\displaystyle\text{RHS}

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{Cnt^{2}}{V^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{Cn(C_{1}V\sqrt{\log d/n}+C_{2}\tau\log d/n)^{2}}{V^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2CnC_{1}(V\sqrt{\log d/n})^{2}}{V^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-C\log d\right).

Then, with
K=1
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\Bigg(&\max_{j\in S_{1},k\in[K]}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\text{T}(q_{ijk}+v_{ijk};\tau)-\text{T}(v_{ijk};\tau)\Big]\right|\geq t\Bigg)\\

For the upper bound

and

Appendix E
Additional Simulation Results

To complement the findings in Section
5.3
, we report the performance of the proposed two-stage estimator for matrix logistic regression and bilinear models in the presence of heavy-tailed distributions.

E.1
Matrix Logistic Regression

We compare our method with unrobustified ScGD-SHT to highlight the benefit of truncation. The simulation setup is configured as follows:
p_{1}=q_{1}=p_{2}=q_{2}=6
,
K=1
,
s_{L,*}=s_{R,*}=5
,
s_{L}=s_{R}=7
,
n=0.5p_{1}q_{1}p_{2}q_{2}
, and
\sigma_{1}=2
. For the true values, we first generate
\mathbf{L}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{36}
and
\mathbf{R}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{36}
, where the first 5 entries are drawn i.i.d. from
N(0,1)
and the remaining entries are set to zero. Then, we let
\mathbf{L}_{*}=\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{L}_{1}/\|\mathbf{L}_{1}\|_{2}
and
\mathbf{R}_{*}=\sqrt{\sigma_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{1}/\|\mathbf{R}_{1}\|_{2}
. For
\mathbf{X}_{i}
, we introduce a heterogeneous distribution. The entries of
\mathbf{X}_{i}
corresponding to the nonzero entries of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{*}
, which effectively contribute to the response
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
, are sampled from
N(0,1)
. In contrast, entries in the inactive positions are drawn from a truncated
t_{1.1}
distribution, denoted by
\bar{t}_{1.1}
and
\bar{t}_{1.6}
, where values are clipped to
[-1000,1000]
to ensure the existence of the second moment. This configuration mimics a scenario where a clean signal is embedded in heavy-tailed ambient noise.
