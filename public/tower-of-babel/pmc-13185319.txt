Exploratory study of large language models in surgical decision-making for lumbar disc herniation: a multicenter analysis based on multisource clinical information
Authors: Zebing Ma, Liang Kong, Yang Shu, Lili Ni, Yongxin Ma, Rui Chen, Huayu Fan, Xiangyang Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the performance of large language models (LLMs) in surgical decision-making for lumbar disc herniation (LDH), and to evaluate the impact of radiology report text and manually summarized clinical information on model decision outputs.MethodsA total of 48 LDH cases from multiple centers were included. Four mainstream LLMs (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-4) were used to perform a binary classification task (surgical vs. conservative treatment). Two input scenarios were designed: Group A used radiology report text only, while Group B incorporated additional manually summarized clinical information based on the same reports. Primary performance metrics included sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1 score. Cohen’s kappa was reported as a supplementary measure of agreement. Decision confidence was further analyzed using stratified analysis.ResultsUsing radiology report text alone, GPT-5 demonstrated relatively strong diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 0.92, specificity of 0.58, and accuracy of 0.75. After incorporating clinical information, its accuracy increased to 0.85, with improvements observed in specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1 score. Gemini and Grok also showed performance improvement following the addition of clinical information, whereas DeepSeek-R1 exhibited minimal change across input scenarios. McNemar’s test indicated that only Gemini showed a statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.013). Confidence analysis showed that the inclusion of clinical information increased the coverage of high-confidence predictions in most models; however, the alignment between high-confidence outputs and actual clinical decisions varied across models.ConclusionThis exploratory study suggests that adding clinical information, such as symptoms, disease duration, and prior treatment, to radiology report text may help some LLMs produce outputs that are more consistent with actual clinical decisions in LDH. However, the findings are limited by the small sample size, the quality of the input data, and the complexity of real clinical decision-making. Further validation in larger studies with more complete information is still needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03478-1.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) is one of the most common spinal disorders, primarily manifesting as low back pain, sciatica, radiating pain in the lower limbs, and sensory or motor deficits [1]. Although clinical guidelines generally recommend prioritizing conservative treatment [2], surgical intervention remains a necessary option for patients with persistent worsening symptoms. Making precise and individualized decisions between surgical and conservative treatment [3], considering multiple factors such as symptom severity, imaging findings, nerve compression status, and patient preferences, continues to be a clinical challenge. This is particularly true in “borderline cases” where symptoms do not fully align with imaging findings, as different clinicians may reach different judgments based on clinical experience, risk preferences, and patient factors.

With the increasing integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in medicine, the application of AI in surgical decision support has expanded considerably [4]. In orthopedics, studies have used deep learning models to predict whether patients will require total knee replacement (TKR), or to estimate time to TKR, based on MRI and clinical data [5, 6]. AI has also been explored for preoperative planning, such as implant size prediction and surgical positioning optimization [7]. Compared with traditional machine learning or computer vision models, large language models (LLMs) have the ability to understand and generate natural language, allowing them to reason using medical texts, guideline knowledge, and case information. As a result, LLMs have demonstrated considerable potential in tasks such as medical education, case analysis, and preoperative strategy recommendations [8]. While preliminary exploration of LLMs in orthopedic education and cognitive tasks has been conducted, empirical research on their application in spinal surgery, particularly in surgical decision-making for LDH patients, is still lacking [9]. Current studies mainly focus on imaging segmentation, lesion identification, or intraoperative navigation, with limited research on the integration of clinical multisource information and treatment decision-making. Therefore, systematically evaluating the performance of LLMs in LDH surgical decision-making will not only help fill this gap in the field but also promote the development of AI-assisted spinal diagnosis and treatment systems toward greater precision, interpretability, and clinical applicability.

In this study, we conducted an exploratory pilot analysis based on multicenter case data to examine the preliminary performance of several LLMs in the surgical decision-making task for LDH. We evaluated the models’ recommendations regarding surgical decisions and their associated confidence levels using MRI report text alone and with additional clinical information, and assessed their consistency with real-world clinical decisions. The findings aim to provide preliminary evidence regarding the feasibility of LLM-assisted decision support in spine surgery and to inform future studies with larger samples and more rigorous designs.

Methods

Case selection

The cases included in this study were obtained from patients screened between June 2024 and June 2025 at three hospitals in Hunan and Henan Provinces, China. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee (Approval No: 2023KYKT0045-01). Inclusion criteria were: (1) primary diagnosis of LDH, confirmed by medical records and MRI reports; (2) complete medical records and MRI data; (3) patients aged between 18 and 75 years; (4) MRI examination performed during hospitalization. Exclusion criteria included: (1) primary diagnosis of other spinal disorders unrelated to LDH; (2) duplicate hospitalization records; (3) history of lumbar spine surgery; (4) presence of severe underlying conditions or complications; (5) poor-quality medical records (e.g., incomplete symptom descriptions or physical examination records). After case selection, all cases were reviewed by two independent researchers (ZM and LK) for consistency checks. In case of disagreement, final inclusion decisions were made through independent review and adjudication by a third-party expert (HF).

Definition of ground truth

In this study, the final treatment decision made during the index hospitalization was used as the ground truth. Cases were classified as the surgical group if patients underwent LDH-related surgery, including open discectomy, percutaneous endoscopic discectomy, or nerve decompression. Cases were classified as the non-surgical group if no surgery was performed and only conservative treatment was received. Patients who initially received short-term conservative treatment but ultimately underwent surgery during the same hospitalization were classified according to the final treatment and included in the surgical group. Conservative treatment included medication, rehabilitation, physical therapy, and traditional Chinese medicine. Invasive procedures such as needle knife therapy were still categorized as conservative treatment, as they are not considered surgical interventions in spine surgery.

Clinical information summarization and data standardization

Before model evaluation, terminology and record formats across hospitals were standardized. Following previous methods [10], two researchers independently extracted information relevant to clinical decision-making from the original medical records and MRI reports, and manually summarized it into a unified text format using a predefined template. The summarized clinical information included age, sex, major symptoms, specialty physical examination findings, prior treatment, disease duration, and social background.

Symptom descriptions were standardized according to the NIH HEAL Pain CDEs [11]. Physical examination findings were summarized with reference to North American Spine Society guidelines [12, 13]. Treatment response was standardized based on patient-reported outcomes [14]. Disease names, disease duration, and treatment history were recorded according to ICD-10. Economic status, family support, and occupational background were summarized with reference to the social determinants of health framework [15].

The MRI input used in this study consisted of radiology report text, rather than imaging pixel data. Key terms in the MRI reports were standardized with reference to the RSNA RadReport system and the RadLex and ASNR terminology frameworks [16, 17]. The summarized content included major sequence information, such as T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging, and STIR sequences, as well as structural findings including the affected disc level, nerve root compression, and spinal canal stenosis. Descriptions were further standardized according to established imaging grading systems, including the Pfirrmann classification for disc degeneration and the Modic classification for endplate changes [18, 19]. Each grade was mapped to a predefined dictionary based on the original definitions and interpretation criteria in the source literature. The clinical information template and MRI report standardization rules are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Clinical information summarization was performed independently by two researchers (YS and LN), followed by a consistency check. Any discrepancies were reviewed and resolved by a third senior investigator (XC). To reduce information bias, the researchers involved in this process were masked to the final treatment labels.

Introduction to LLMs

This study evaluated four mainstream LLMs: GPT-5.0 [20], Gemini 2.5 Pro [21], DeepSeek-R1 [22], and Grok-4 [23]. GPT-5.0 is the latest multimodal LLM released by OpenAI in 2025, known for its exceptional reasoning abilities and multitasking capabilities. Gemini 2.5 Pro is an advanced language model developed by Google DeepMind, excelling at handling multimodal data and complex reasoning tasks, particularly in clinical data analysis. DeepSeek-R1 is an open-source LLM developed by DeepSeek, employing an expert-mixing architecture with chain reasoning capabilities, especially suited for generating complex diagnostic hypotheses and recommending treatment plans. Grok-4 is a next-generation LLM released by xAI in July 2025, designed for medical data analysis.

Prompt engineering

The core principle of prompt design was to position the LLM as a physician making clinical decisions based on the provided information [24]. A unified prompt framework was used for all models. The task was defined as a binary classification of whether a patient with lumbar disc herniation should receive surgical treatment or conservative treatment, with the model also required to report its decision confidence. For result analysis, the four included LLMs were anonymized as GPT 5 (LLM-1), Gemini 2.5 Pro (LLM-2), DeepSeek R1 (LLM-3), and Grok 4 (LLM-4).

Two prompt scenarios were designed according to the input information. In Group A, only the standardized MRI radiology report text was provided. In Group B, the MRI radiology report text was supplemented with manually summarized clinical information, including demographic characteristics, major symptoms, specialty physical examination findings, prior treatment, and social background. Both groups used the same task instructions and output format. All inputs were organized into a unified text format using predefined templates before being entered into the models. Model outputs included the treatment decision and the corresponding confidence level.

Confidence was recorded as a continuous variable from 0% to 100% and further categorized into four predefined levels: very low (0% to 25%), low (25% to 50%), moderate (50% to 75%), and high (75% to 100%), with reference to evidence grading standards. The reported confidence reflected the model’s confidence in its output and was not probability calibrated [25]. The full prompt template is provided in Supplementary File S2. Example inputs under different scenarios and representative model outputs are provided in Supplementary File S3.

Analysis of output answers

The outputs of different LLMs were statistically analyzed. For each model under each input scenario, confusion matrices were constructed and sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and F1 score were calculated, with 95% confidence intervals reported for all metrics. Cohen’s kappa was included as a supplementary measure of agreement beyond chance between model predictions and actual clinical decisions [26]. McNemar’s test was used to compare classification results between Group A and Group B for the same model, and among different models within the same input scenario. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1). All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [27].

An analytic hierarchy process was used to assign weights to the evaluation metrics [28].

In this binary classification task, the primary clinical objective is to correctly identify patients who may require surgical intervention. Sensitivity was therefore assigned the highest weight (0.30) [29], as missing such patients (i.e., false negatives) may lead to delayed treatment and potential worsening of neurological function. This weighting reflects a safety-oriented clinical consideration, rather than an intention to encourage surgical treatment. Accuracy and PPV were assigned weights of 0.25 and 0.20, respectively. The F1 score was assigned a weight of 0.15, while specificity and Cohen’s kappa were each assigned a weight of 0.05. A composite score was then calculated and used for visualization.

Based on the confidence scores reported by the models, the coverage of high-confidence cases and of moderate plus high-confidence cases was calculated for each model. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were then calculated within these subsets. The association between model confidence and prediction correctness was assessed using Spearman rank correlation [30]. The reported confidence values were not probability calibrated and were used only for stratified and descriptive analyses.

Results

Basic characteristics of selected cases

A total of 240 clinical cases were collected from three hospitals. After preliminary screening based on the inclusion criteria, 230 cases were selected, and after further screening, 48 cases were finally included (in Fig. 1). The personal information of the patients was anonymized, and only the following basic data were retained: age, gender, disease duration, affected segment, and BMI. In the final inclusion results, the surgical and non-surgical groups each had 24 cases, with 26 male and 22 female patients. The average age of all included cases was 50.31 ± 13.59 years, the average disease duration was 3.37 ± 5.36 years, and the average BMI was 24.62 ± 2.67 (in Table 1).

Primary diagnostic performance

The primary diagnostic performance metrics under both input scenarios are presented in Table 2. In Group A, using MRI radiology report text alone, GPT-5.0 showed the best overall performance, with high sensitivity (0.92), moderate specificity (0.58), accuracy of 0.75, PPV of 0.69, and NPV of 0.88. Grok-4 also showed high sensitivity (0.79) but low specificity (0.33), indicating a tendency to classify more cases as surgical. Gemini-2.5 Pro and DeepSeek-R1 showed similar overall performance. In Group B, after adding manually summarized clinical information, overall performance improved across models. GPT-5.0 remained the best performing model, with accuracy increasing to 0.85 and improvements observed in specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1 score. Gemini-2.5 Pro showed a marked improvement in accuracy from 0.52 to 0.75, while Grok-4 improved from 0.56 to 0.67. DeepSeek-R1 showed minimal change, with accuracy remaining at 0.52. The distribution of prediction correctness is shown in Fig. 2.

McNemar’s test showed that in Group A, GPT-5.0 significantly outperformed Gemini-2.5 Pro (P = 0.007), DeepSeek-R1 (P = 0.007), and Grok-4 (P = 0.022), whereas no significant differences were found among the other models (P > 0.05). In Group B, GPT-5.0 remained superior to DeepSeek-R1 (P < 0.001) and Grok-4 (P = 0.022), but not to Gemini-2.5 Pro (P = 0.227). Gemini-2.5 Pro also outperformed DeepSeek-R1 (P = 0.013). Across the two input scenarios, only Gemini-2.5 Pro showed a significant difference between Group A and Group B (P = 0.013), whereas the other models did not (P > 0.05). Overall, GPT-5.0 showed the highest primary diagnostic performance under both scenarios. The addition of clinical information was associated with a more pronounced improvement in Gemini-2.5 Pro, whereas DeepSeek-R1 showed limited change. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary File S4.

Supplementary analysis and composite scores

The confusion matrix results and Cohen’s kappa values for all models under the two input scenarios are shown in Table 3. In Group A, using MRI radiology report text alone, GPT-5.0 showed higher agreement with the actual clinical decisions (Kappa = 0.50, P < 0.001). Agreement was low for the other models, suggesting limited consistency between their predictions and the real clinical decisions. In Group B, after adding manually summarized clinical information, agreement improved for GPT-5.0 (Kappa = 0.71, P < 0.001) and Gemini-2.5 Pro (Kappa = 0.50, P = 0.005). Grok-4 also showed improvement (Kappa = 0.33, P = 0.014), although its overall agreement remained lower than that of GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro. In contrast, DeepSeek-R1 showed consistently low kappa values under both scenarios (Kappa = 0.04, P > 0.05).

The addition of clinical information was associated with higher composite scores for most models. GPT-5.0 maintained the highest overall composite performance under both scenarios, whereas DeepSeek-R1 showed limited change. In Group A, the composite scores were highest for GPT-5.0 (0.74), followed by Grok-4 (0.56), Gemini 2.5 Pro (0.48), and DeepSeek-R1 (0.42). In Group B, the composite scores increased overall, with GPT-5.0 remaining highest (0.84), followed by Gemini 2.5 Pro (0.73), Grok-4 (0.65), and DeepSeek-R1 (0.48).

Decision confidence

The distribution of cases across confidence levels, together with coverage and the corresponding diagnostic performance, is shown in Table 4. Overall, after adding manually summarized clinical information, most models showed higher coverage in the high-confidence category. Among the four models, GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro showed relatively stable decision performance in high-confidence and moderate to high-confidence cases, whereas DeepSeek-R1 showed weaker agreement between high-confidence outputs and actual decision outcomes. The distribution of confidence scores is presented in Fig. 3.

In Group A, using MRI radiology report text only, coverage in the high-confidence category differed across models, with relatively higher coverage for GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro and lower coverage for DeepSeek-R1. In Group B, after adding manually summarized clinical information, high-confidence coverage increased for most models, and all cases assessed by Gemini 2.5 Pro fell into the high-confidence category. No cases were assigned to the very low-confidence category in either group, and no low-confidence cases were observed in Group B.

Within the high-confidence category, GPT-5.0 showed relatively strong diagnostic performance in both groups, with further improvement in specificity and PPV in Group B. Grok-4 also showed high sensitivity in high-confidence cases and maintained relatively favorable PPV and NPV in Group B. Gemini 2.5 Pro showed relatively stable performance across high-confidence cases in both groups. In contrast, DeepSeek-R1 showed relatively strong diagnostic performance among high-confidence cases in Group A, but coverage was low. Although high-confidence coverage increased in Group B, specificity and NPV declined markedly, suggesting inconsistency between high-confidence outputs and actual decision outcomes. Among moderate to high-confidence cases, GPT-5.0 reached 100.00% coverage in both groups and maintained stable decision performance. Coverage for Gemini 2.5 Pro increased from 75.00% in Group A to 100.00% in Group B, with similarly stable decision results. DeepSeek-R1 and Grok-4 both showed high coverage in this category, but the improvement remained limited for DeepSeek-R1. By contrast, Grok-4 maintained high sensitivity in Group B, with improved specificity and NPV.

As a supplementary analysis, the association between model confidence and prediction correctness was also evaluated. Significant positive correlations were observed for GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro in both groups. For DeepSeek-R1, the correlation was significantly positive in Group A but became significantly negative in Group B. Grok-4 showed no significant correlation in Group A, but a significant positive correlation in Group B. Overall, the relationship between confidence and actual decision correctness differed across models. Because confidence was not probability calibrated in this study, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive only.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of radiology report text and manually summarized clinical information on the performance of LLMs in surgical decision-making for LDH. To compare the effects of different types of clinical information on model outputs, two input scenarios were designed. In one scenario, decision-making was based on radiology report text alone, whereas in the other, manually summarized clinical information was added to the radiology report text. In clinical practice, surgical decision-making for LDH does not rely solely on imaging findings. Instead, it typically requires integration of multiple factors, including patient symptoms, physical examination findings, prior treatment response, and disease duration. Radiology report text primarily reflects imaging descriptions, which may not fully correspond to the patient’s clinical presentation. Therefore, decisions based on radiology report text alone may be insufficient to support comprehensive treatment decisions. Based on this rationale, additional clinical variables, including age, sex, disease duration, symptoms, and body mass index, were incorporated to examine how model decision performance changed after the inclusion of clinical context. All models were evaluated under standardized text-based input conditions, ensuring comparability between the two input scenarios.

GPT-5.0 is based on a deep Transformer architecture and is capable of handling complex relationships and semantics in textual and radiological information, with strong reasoning capabilities [31]. In this study sample, GPT-5.0 showed high primary diagnostic performance under both input scenarios, and its ability to distinguish between surgical and non-surgical cases further improved after the addition of manually summarized clinical information. Gemini 2.5 Pro is optimized for complex and multistep reasoning, and showed more marked improvement after clinical information was added, suggesting greater sensitivity to additional clinical context [32]. By contrast, DeepSeek-R1 changed little across the two input scenarios, whereas Grok-4 maintained relatively high sensitivity but still showed limitations in distinguishing non-surgical cases. The supplementary agreement analysis showed a pattern broadly consistent with these findings.

When comparing the same model across the two input scenarios, only Gemini 2.5 Pro showed a statistically significant difference after the addition of manually summarized clinical information, whereas GPT-5.0, DeepSeek-R1, and Grok-4 did not show significant changes. For GPT-5.0, this may be related to its relatively high prediction accuracy when based on radiology report text alone. Although specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy improved after clinical information was added, the overall difference did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, Gemini 2.5 Pro showed more pronounced improvement after the inclusion of clinical information, suggesting that its decision performance may be more sensitive to additional contextual factors, such as patient symptoms, disease duration, and prior treatment history. This pattern was also observed at the individual case level. For example, in case P41, a 75-year-old female patient, the radiology report described multilevel disc herniation, spinal canal stenosis, and ligamentum flavum thickening. When only radiology report text was provided, the model tended to favor surgical treatment based on these imaging descriptions. After manually summarized clinical information was added, the model incorporated additional factors, including symptom relief with rest and the absence of clear neurological deficits, as well as the patient’s advanced age and comorbidities, which are associated with higher surgical risk. The decision result was subsequently adjusted to non-surgical management. However, this change is more appropriately interpreted as a response of model outputs to differences in input conditions, rather than as a full representation of real clinical reasoning. The findings of this study suggest that incorporating clinical information may help some models generate outputs that are more consistent with actual treatment decisions. Nevertheless, this observation remains preliminary and requires further validation in larger cohorts.

In the within-scenario comparison, when based on radiology report text alone, GPT-5.0 showed the best performance among the four models and significantly outperformed the others, indicating that its decision results were more consistent with actual clinical decisions under this condition. According to reports from 2025, GPT-5.0 demonstrates lower bias and higher robustness when processing standardized medical terminology and social determinants of health [33], which may contribute to its advantage in medical text analysis and reasoning tasks. After the addition of manually summarized clinical information, GPT-5.0 maintained a high level of performance, but the difference between GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro was no longer statistically significant. This suggests that the decision performance of Gemini 2.5 Pro improved substantially after incorporating additional clinical context, approaching that of GPT-5.0. In contrast, DeepSeek-R1 showed relatively weak performance under both input scenarios and did not demonstrate clear improvement after clinical information was added, suggesting that additional clinical context did not substantially enhance its decision results. Grok-4, which is primarily designed for general conversational tasks, showed limitations in complex medical decision-making requiring specialized knowledge. Previous studies have reported that Grok-4 exhibits poor contextual adaptation and limited content quality when addressing esophageal cancer–related tasks, with overall performance inferior to models developed for specialized domains [34]. In this study, Grok-4 showed relatively low specificity under both scenarios, indicating a tendency to favor surgical decisions, and remained limited in distinguishing non-surgical cases. Although its overall performance improved after the addition of clinical information, it did not reach the level observed for GPT-5.0 or Gemini 2.5 Pro.

The results of the supplementary agreement analysis and composite scoring were broadly consistent with these trends. From a clinical perspective, these within scenario differences provide additional insight. For LDH cases that require integrated assessment of symptoms, physical findings, and radiology report text, differences between models may be more closely related to their ability to utilize additional information, rather than differences in overall accuracy alone. These findings support interpreting LLMs as decision support tools that are sensitive to the quality and completeness of input information, rather than as general systems that perform consistently across different input conditions. Accordingly, future research may benefit more from examining how information is structured and presented to the model, rather than focusing solely on performance differences between models.

After the addition of manually summarized clinical information, the confidence distributions of most models shifted toward higher levels, with broader coverage of high-confidence cases. This suggests that, in the LDH surgical decision-making task, adding background information such as symptoms, disease duration, and prior treatment tends to make the models produce more confident outputs. However, higher confidence did not necessarily correspond to better performance. The stratified analysis showed that performance within the high-confidence category differed across models. GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro maintained relatively stable decision performance in high-confidence and moderate to high-confidence cases, suggesting better alignment between their reported confidence and actual decision results. By contrast, although DeepSeek-R1 showed a marked increase in high-confidence coverage in Group B, its specificity and NPV declined substantially, indicating that in some settings it could produce highly confident but less stable decision results. Grok-4 showed a different pattern: after manually summarized clinical information was added, its coverage of high-confidence cases increased and sensitivity remained relatively high, but its ability to distinguish non-surgical cases remained limited. The correlation analysis further supported these differences. GPT-5.0 and Gemini 2.5 Pro both showed significant positive correlations between confidence and prediction correctness under both input scenarios, suggesting that, within this study sample, their reported confidence was directionally consistent with actual decision correctness. In contrast, DeepSeek-R1 showed a positive correlation in Group A but a negative correlation in Group B, suggesting that when the input became more complex, its reported confidence no longer stably reflected actual decision performance. Grok-4 showed no significant correlation in Group A, but a significant positive correlation in Group B, indicating that the relationship between confidence and decision results was likewise sensitive to input conditions. These findings suggest that confidence reported by LLMs is better understood as an expression of model certainty under specific input conditions, rather than a measure directly equivalent to true probability or clinical reliability. Although the addition of clinical information changed the distribution of confidence, the responses to this change were not consistent across models. Accordingly, when confidence is used as a supportive reference in future applications, it should not be interpreted on the basis of magnitude alone, but rather in combination with its coverage and the corresponding decision performance within different confidence strata.

This study also identified typical decision errors made by the models in the LDH surgical decision-making task. One illustrative example was patient P25, a 63-year-old woman with chronic low back pain and radiating pain in the right lower limb. Her radiology report described multiple disc herniations and spinal canal stenosis consistent with degenerative changes. Although her symptoms were intermittent and fluctuating, and the actual treatment choice was conservative management, all models tended to recommend surgery. This finding suggests that, when faced with more prominent radiological abnormalities, the models may place greater weight on structural findings described in the radiology report text, while giving relatively less weight to symptom persistence, indications for conservative treatment, and the actual treatment choice made in practice. Even after manually summarized clinical information was added for P25, the models remained limited in their ability to reflect the patient’s social background and real-world constraints. In this case, the final choice of conservative treatment was related not only to the clinical symptom profile, but also to practical factors such as age, farming-related work demands, and financial burden. These findings suggest that although LLMs can use some of the medical information contained in medical records and radiology report text to generate outputs, they still have difficulty integrating the social background, living context, and practical constraints that may influence treatment decisions in real clinical settings to the same extent as clinicians. Future studies may explore how to incorporate social background, treatment preferences, and related contextual factors more fully while maintaining data standardization, in order to improve the consistency between model outputs and actual clinical decisions.

This study has several limitations. First, although cases were collected from multiple centers, only 48 cases were ultimately included. Therefore, this study should be considered exploratory, and the generalizability of the findings remains limited. In addition, a number of cases with incomplete or lower-quality records were excluded during case selection. This process may have altered the final case mix and could affect the interpretation of metrics such as PPV and NPV. Second, the clinical information used in this study was manually extracted and summarized from the original medical records according to a predefined template. Although independent extraction, consistency checks, and third-party review were implemented, this process may still introduce bias related to subjective judgment, information simplification, and variation in expression. In particular, when medical records varied in detail, structure, or terminology, some original information may not have been fully preserved after summarization. Third, the model inputs consisted of radiology report text rather than raw imaging data. While these reports provide key information such as disc herniation level, nerve compression, and spinal canal stenosis, they represent summarized descriptions of imaging findings and may not capture subtle details present in the original images. Therefore, the findings of this study more accurately reflect the models’ ability to utilize radiology report text, rather than their ability to interpret raw MRI images. In addition, the ground truth in this study was defined as the final treatment decision made during the index hospitalization. Although this definition provides a consistent reference for comparison, real-world treatment decisions may still be influenced by factors such as symptom progression, patient preference, financial constraints, and access to care. As a result, discrepancies between model outputs and observed clinical decisions should not be interpreted solely as errors, as some differences may reflect divergence between medical judgment and real-world decision-making. Finally, the models in this study relied primarily on radiology report text and manually summarized clinical information. Their ability to capture social background, living conditions, treatment preferences, and real-world constraints remains limited. For conditions such as LDH, where treatment decisions are influenced by both clinical factors and contextual considerations, reliance on medical text alone may be insufficient to fully represent the basis of real clinical decision-making.

Future research may focus on developing a disease-specific knowledge base for lumbar disc herniation and further exploring targeted optimization based on models that demonstrated relatively stable performance. In addition, the integration of raw imaging data, together with radiology reports and clinical information, may help to further enhance the potential of these models in clinical decision support. These models may also have potential applications in clinical practice, such as preoperative communication and patient education, where they could help patients better understand treatment options, surgical risks, and expected outcomes.

Conclusion

This study provides an exploratory analysis of the performance of LLMs in surgical decision-making for LDH using multicenter clinical cases. After adding manually summarized clinical information to radiology report text, decision performance improved in several models. GPT-5.0 showed relatively stable overall performance in this study sample, while Gemini 2.5 Pro demonstrated more pronounced improvement. Beyond radiology report text, incorporating clinical context such as symptoms, disease duration, and prior treatment may help models generate outputs that are more consistent with actual clinical decisions. However, model performance remains influenced by factors such as sample size, the quality of input information, the process of manual summarization, and the complexity of real-world decision-making. At present, the findings should be considered preliminary and are better interpreted as initial evidence supporting the feasibility of LLM-assisted clinical decision support. Further validation in larger cohorts and under more comprehensive data conditions is required.
