Heavy metal contamination, source apportionment and health risk in soil-crop systems of high-geochemical-background Pb-Zn mining area
Authors: Zhengyang Duan, Junyu Chen, Yongtao Xu, Sheng Wang, Tianguo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMining and smelting, combined with high-geochemical-backgrounds, cause heavy metal accumulation in soil-crop systems, endangering food-chain health. Topsoil (0–20 cm) and three local crops were sampled from Yunnan’s Huize lead-zinc mining area. Pb, Zn, Cu, Cd contamination, spatial patterns, source contributions and risks were assessed via ArcGIS interpolation, PN, RI, APCS-MLR, PMF, and USEPA health risk models. All soils exceeded Yunnan background values (100% exceedance), presenting extreme ecological risks mainly driven by Cd (92.94%). Cd has the highest bioavailability, while Pb existed mostly potentially bioavailable. Leafy crops show 100% over-standard rates for Pb and Cd, maize exceeds Pb limits. Four pollution sources were identified. Ingestion was the primary exposure pathway. Children suffered prominent non-carcinogenic Pb risks, while all populations encountered unacceptable Cd carcinogenic risks. Mismatches between soil metal contents and crop safety reveals uniform soil-standard limitations, offering references for pollution control, crop-layout optimization and differentiated risk management in analogous mining areas.Keywords: mining area, soil-Crop System, heavy metals, bioavailability, risk assessment

Introduction

In 2014, the “National Soil Pollution Survey Report” disclosed that the overall condition of China’s soil environment was far from encouraging, with an overall exceedance rate of 16.1%.1,2,3 Consequently, research focusing on the pollution characteristics, spatiotemporal distribution patterns, and health risk assessments of heavy metals in mining area soils has emerged as a focal point for scholars globally.4,5,6,7,8,9,10 However, the current pollution assessment system exhibits notable limitations. Firstly, most existing environmental quality standards are formulated based solely on the total heavy metal content in a single medium, namely soil, making it challenging to fully capture the migration and transformation risks of heavy metals in areas with high geological backgrounds. Secondly, the bioavailability of heavy metals is intricately linked to their speciation. Nevertheless, current evaluation methods frequently overlook speciation analysis, resulting in inaccuracies in assessing risks within the soil-crop system. Furthermore, there is a prevalent lack of integrated assessment in current research concerning the soil-crop system. The relationship between the two is not merely linear, and assessments focusing solely on a single medium often lead to a substantial underestimation of actual pollution risks.

The Huize lead-zinc mining district, situated in northeastern Yunnan Province, is a pivotal non-ferrous metal mining and smelting hub with a long history of mining, ore dressing, and smelting operations. The region is characterized by a typical distribution of carbonate rocks with a high geological background, and the superimposition of anthropogenic pollution has resulted in the formation of composite pollution characteristics. Recent studies conducted by Chen11 have revealed that the concentrations of heavy metals in soils surrounding the mining area are significantly higher than the regional background levels in Yunnan Province, revealing a distinct and persistent pollution pattern that already poses a threat to the local ecological environment security. Consequently, this region serves as an exemplary location for investigating the migration, transformation, and risk mechanisms of heavy metals within soil-crop systems in mining areas characterized by high background values, and the research outcomes offer substantial reference significance for risk management in analogous mining regions. It is evident that this area serves as an ideal site for investigating the migration and transformation of heavy metals within the soil-crop system of mining regions with high background values, and the research findings hold substantial reference significance for risk management in analogous mining areas.

Given this context, to more precisely evaluate the actual risks posed by heavy metals through the “soil-crop-human” pathway, this study selected the Yunnan Huize lead-zinc mining area, characterized by a typical high geological background, as its research site. Soil samples (0–20 cm depth) and samples of the primary locally cultivated crops (Maize, Napa cabbage, and Bok Choy) were collected concurrently (Figure 1). By integrating total heavy metal concentrations, speciation analysis, ArcGIS spatial interpolation, the Nemerow Comprehensive Pollution Index (PN), the Potential Ecological Risk Index (RI), APCS-MLR and PMF source apportionment models, along with the USEPA health risk assessment model, the objectives are to: (1) clarify the concentration levels and spatial distribution patterns of heavy metals (Pb, Zn, Cu, and Cd) in mining area soils; (2) quantify the contributions of various pollution sources and elucidate the mechanisms underlying differences in crop accumulation; (3) specify which groups are regarded as key at-risk populations, and highlight the shortcomings of current assessment standards through soil-crop collaborative evaluation.

Results

Heavy metal content and spatial distribution characteristics in soil

As indicated, the pH values of the 78 soil sampling points in the study area range from 4.7 to 7.68, with a mean value of 6.4, indicating an overall acidic nature consistent with the typical soil acidity observed in southern China. Analysis of heavy metal content reveals that the average concentrations of Pb, Zn, Cu, and Cd are 778.30, 2129.60, 218.24, and 13.07 mg kg−1, respectively. The coefficient of variation for heavy metal concentrations ranges from 0.47 to 1.21, with Pb displaying the highest variability. This pronounced spatial heterogeneity suggests a strong influence of anthropogenic activities on heavy metal distribution. Given the region’s history of mining operations, it is reasonable to infer that past mining activities have been the primary contributor to the observed spatial variation in Pb concentrations.

To investigate the spatial distribution patterns of heavy metals in the study area, this study employed the Kriging interpolation method based on the ArcGIS 10.8 spatial analysis platform to conduct spatial analysis of heavy metal elements (Figure 2). The results reveal pronounced spatial aggregation characteristics for the four heavy metals. A multi-element composite enrichment zone was identified in the northwestern region, and field verification confirmed that this high-concentration area closely corresponds to the spatial distribution of surrounding smelting enterprises. Continuous inputs from industrial emissions are likely the primary driver of heavy metal accumulation in this zone. Zn exhibits a belt-like enrichment pattern extending along the east-west axis of the study area, with its distribution strongly associated with residential villages and concentrated farmland areas. It is inferred that long-term application of fertilizers and pesticides has contributed to elevated Zn levels through anthropogenic inputs. Cu displays a distinct high-value cluster in the southwestern region, where its spatial pattern shows significant positive correlation with major transportation routes and paddy field distributions. The enrichment of Cu is likely driven by combined pollution mechanisms, including dust deposition from traffic activities and heavy metals introduced via agricultural irrigation water.13,14 Cd demonstrates marked spatial heterogeneity across the study area, characterized by a “dual-core low-value zone (1.02–9.6 mg kg−1) in the northeastern and southeastern regions and a high-value zone (29.5–57.58 mg kg−1) in the central region”. The high-value zone corresponds closely with industrial areas, while the low-value zone is controlled by bedrock weathering parent materials, supporting a dual regulatory mechanism whereby anthropogenic inputs dominate local enrichment, and geological background maintains baseline level.15 Based on these findings, it is recommended that future research prioritize monitoring of Cd contamination, particularly in the central industrial zone, and continue to assess the migration and transformation dynamics of other heavy metals in areas characterized by intensive industrial and agricultural activities to safeguard soil stability and environmental quality.

Analysis of heavy metal speciation in soil

The speciation of heavy metals in soil serves as a critical indicator for assessing their bioavailability, as different metal fractions exhibit distinct behaviors during plant uptake and utilization. According to established chemical fractionation criteria,16,17 heavy metal species can be classified into bioavailable fractions (including water-soluble and mild acid-soluble fraction), potentially bioavailable fractions (oxidisable and reducible fraction), and stable residual fractions. The bioavailable fractions can be directly absorbed by crop root systems, while the potentially bioavailable fractions may transform into readily available forms under strongly acidic conditions. In contrast, the residual fraction, which is incorporated within mineral crystal lattices, exhibits minimal bioavailability.

Figure 3 illustrates the percentage distribution of chemical speciation of heavy metals in the soils of the study area. The results show that Cd has the highest proportion of bioavailable fractions (41.69%), significantly exceeding those of other heavy metal elements. This indicates a high potential for Cd to migrate and transform within the soil-crop system. This phenomenon is closely linked to the acidic soil environment, where low pH conditions promote the formation of exchangeable Cd species, thereby enhancing its bioavailability and increasing the risk of crop uptake and accumulation.18 These findings are consistent with previous studies by Zou19 and Yang20 on Cd bioavailability in acidic soils. Pb exhibits the highest proportion of potentially bioavailable fractions (82.92%), with the reducible fraction accounting for 78.98%. This is primarily attributable to the continuous input of chemical reagents from mineral processing and metallurgical activities, as well as the accumulation of organic matter (e.g., fertilizers and domestic waste) in residential and industrial areas. In contrast, Cu is predominantly present in the residual fraction (66.48%), suggesting that it likely occurs as Cu(II)-silicate complexes within the soil mineral lattice, representing a long-term reservoir of Cu.21 The residual fractions of the other heavy metals follow the order: Zn (31.78%) > Cd (13.40%) > Pb (13.15%), further confirming the relatively high geochemical stability of Cu in the soil.

Notably, the Pb concentration in maize, which serves as the primary cash crop in the study area, surpassed the standard limit by 100%, whereas the concentrations of Cu and Cd remained below the safety threshold. This finding indicates that heavy metal accumulation in crops is not solely determined by total soil metal concentrations but is also influenced by factors such as metal speciation.22,23 This outcome arises from the synergistic effects of multiple mechanisms: Firstly, the citric acid and oxalic acid secreted by roots preferentially activate and chelate Pb, whereas Cu and Cd exhibit lower chelation stability constants with these secretions, leading to reduced absorption. Secondly, pectin and hemicellulose within the root cell wall can bind substantial amounts of Pb, effectively immobilizing it within the roots, while Cu and Cd predominantly exist in a free state within the cytosol, facilitating their easier excretion from the organism. Thirdly, the expression levels of Pb transporters in roots are 3–6 times higher than those of Cu and Cd transporters, and the rate of transmembrane Pb absorption significantly surpasses that of Cu and Cd.24,25 This mechanism is consistent with the previously discussed speciation analysis, which revealed that the high proportions of bioavailable Cd (41.69%) and potentially bioavailable Pb (82.92%) are the key factors contributing to the exceedance of heavy metal limits in edible crops.

Contamination assessment of heavy metals

Figure 4 presents the calculated single pollution indices (Pi) and the Nemerow comprehensive pollution index (PN) for heavy metals in the soils of the study area. The pollution index ranges for each heavy metal are as follows: PPb (1.04–122.66), PZn (1.51–105.57), PCu (2.57–22.39), PCd (4.64–459.09), and PN (5.50–365.69), with mean values of 19.17 ± 23.37, 23.74 ± 25.85, 5.01 ± 2.39, 59.39 ± 60.80, and 49.82 ± 48.84, respectively. Based on the Pi values, the average contamination levels of four heavy metals in soil are ranked as Cu < Pb < Zn < Cd. Among these elements, Cu ranges from moderate pollution (2 < Pi ≤ 3) to severe pollution (Pi > 3). Pb and Zn exhibit broader pollution spectra, extending from light pollution (1 < Pi ≤ 2) to severe pollution (Pi > 3), with overall contamination levels substantially higher than those of Cu. Cd is the most severely contaminated element, consistently exceeding the threshold for severe pollution (Pi > 3). Based on the evaluation criteria of the Nemerow Comprehensive Pollution Index (PN), the overall soil pollution index in the study area (PN = 49.82 > 3) indicates that the soil in this region is under severe contamination.

Source apportionment of heavy metals

Pearson’s correlation analysis was conducted to examine the concentrations of Pb, Zn, Cu, and Cd in the soils of the study area, with results presented in Table 4. The analysis reveals statistically significant positive correlations between each pair of the four heavy metals, which is highly consistent with findings from Chen’s previous study on heavy metal correlations in a typical lead-zinc mining area.28 This pronounced correlation pattern may be attributed to two underlying mechanisms: First, it reflects the similar geochemical behaviors of the four heavy metals, which tend to form associated or symbiotic relationships during migration and transformation processes, resulting in a highly homologous spatial distribution pattern. Second, it corroborates the close association between the excessive Cd levels in the study area and lead-zinc smelting activities, indicating that industrial emissions serve as a major driving force for Cd contamination.

The present study extracted four principal components through PCA (Table 5), with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 94.84%, effectively explaining the majority of variation information regarding heavy metals in the soil of the study area. Component F1 exhibited an eigenvalue of 1.478, accounting for 36.955% of the total variance, which is significantly higher than the other principal components. Its primary loading elements are Cu and Cd. Existing research has confirmed that Cu and Cd released during industrial smelting processes can enter soils through atmospheric deposition. In regions characterized by intensive industrial activities, concentrations of these two heavy metals are frequently elevated well above natural background levels.29,30 Combined with spatial analysis, it is inferred that the primary component F1 originates from industrial sources. Component F2 exhibited an eigenvalue of 1.208, explaining 30.202% of the total variance, with Zn identified as the dominant element. Given the elevated Zn concentrations in farmland soils within the study area and their strong association with agricultural practices such as fertilizers and pesticides application, it can be inferred that agrochemical inputs (e.g., zinc fertilizers and zineb) constitute a primary pathway for Zn introduction into the soil system. Accordingly, F2 is classified as an agricultural source. These results are consistent with findings reported by Zhu31 and Angon.32 Principal component F3 has an eigenvalue of 1.107, explaining 27.685% of the total variance, with Pb being its primary loading element. As a historically industrialized zone with concentrated industrial and mining activities, the study area experiences frequent truck traffic associated with mining-related production operations. Relevant studies have shown that vehicle exhaust emissions, tire and brake wear, and ore spillage during transportation contribute significantly to Pb accumulation in soils.33,34 Therefore, F3 is attributed to transportation-related sources. The proportion associated with unidentified sources is 5.159%.

Multiple linear regression analysis of the four heavy metals based on the APCS-MLR model revealed a strong agreement between predicted and observed values. Specifically, the R2 values for Pb, Zn, Cu, and Cd were 0.99, 0.96, 0.96, and 0.88, respectively, all exceeding the threshold of 0.8. Moreover, the estimated-to-observed (E/O) ratios for each metal were close to unity, indicating excellent model goodness-of-fit and high reliability. Further quantification of pollution source contributions (Figure 6) reveals that the average contribution rates of the three major sources are as follows: industrial sources (37.38%), agricultural sources (33.16%), and transportation sources (29.46%). Industrial sources are primarily characterized by high contributions to Cu (75.67%) and Cd (45.59%), whereas agricultural sources show the greatest contribution to Zn, at 55.92%, and transportation sources account for 67.48% of Pb emissions.

PMF. To validate the source apportionment results derived from the APCS-MLR model, the PMF model was further applied for quantitative source analysis of four heavy metals (Pb, Zn, Cu, Cd). For each configuration involving 3 to 5 factors, a total of 100 iterative runs with random initial values were executed, ultimately determining that the optimal number of factors was 4. The iteration results revealed that the optimal solution was achieved in the 21st run, with its QRobust value closely approximating the Qtrue value, thereby demonstrating that the 4-factor model exhibited favorable fitting performance for the soil heavy metal dataset. Residual analysis indicated that the residuals for all samples fell within the range of −3 to 3, suggesting high credibility of the model apportionment results. The coefficients of determination (R2) for Pb, Zn, Cu, and Cd were 0.60, 0.89, 0.87, and 0.92, respectively. All R2 values for these four heavy metals exceeded 0.6, confirming the model’s high goodness of fit.

The PMF analysis results (Figure 7) indicate that Factor 1 is predominantly characterized by Pb (45.37%) and Cd (30.72%) as its major contributing elements, showing a high degree of consistency with the industrial source profile identified by the APCS-MLR model. Therefore, Factor 1 is attributed to an industrial source. Factor 2 is dominated by Cu (46.55%) as the principal loading element. Combined with documented evidence on the contribution of traffic emissions to non-natural sources of copper in soil and transport mechanisms of road dust,35,36 it is classified as a traffic-related source. Factor 3 is characterized by the dominant elements Cu (68.72%) and Zn (31.28%), with its spatial distribution showing a significant overlap with the western paddy field region. This spatial correlation suggests an association with long-term applications of chemical fertilizers and pesticides in agricultural activities, as well as the input of contaminants into farmland soils through irrigation water, leading to heavy metal enrichment. These findings align with the research outcomes reported by Guo37 and Fan,38 thereby supporting the classification of Factor 3 as an agricultural source. Factor 4 is predominantly characterized by Cd (45.13%). In conjunction with the high geochemical background characteristics of northeastern Yunnan, this factor is attributed to parent material sources. The average contribution rates from the four pollution sources are as follows: industrial sources (32.21%), transportation sources (20.03%), agricultural sources (14.29%) and parent material sources (33.47%).

A comprehensive comparative analysis demonstrates substantial agreement between the APCS-MLR and PMF methods in source apportionment results. Specifically, both models consistently identify industrial sources as the primary contributors, with Pb and Cd as dominant elements, while Cu serves as the characteristic tracer for traffic-related sources. Moreover, the ranking of source contribution rates is highly consistent, with industrial sources showing the highest contribution in both modeling approaches.

Nevertheless, the two models differ in the number of identified sources: APCS-MLR extracts 3 sources, while PMF identifies 4 sources including an additional natural parent material source. This discrepancy is determined by the algorithmic characteristics of the models. APCS-MLR extracts factors based on the PCA eigenvalue threshold, prioritizing high-variance anthropogenic sources, with natural background contribution implicit in the regression intercept. In addition, negative contributions are prone to occur and require manual correction, making it difficult to independently extract weak-signal natural sources. PMF adopts non-negative matrix factorization and avoids negative contributions via uncertainty weighting, with higher sensitivity to low-heterogeneity natural parent material sources for independent identification.39

Overall, this discrepancy reflects the methodological characteristics of the two models, and their combination can achieve complementary advantages: APCS-MLR can objectively determine the number of factors, while PMF is more accurate in identifying weak sources. Dual-model cross-validation provides a more reliable scientific basis for soil heavy metal source apportionment.

Results of human health risk assessment for heavy metals in soil

Table 6 presents the daily exposure doses and risk assessment results for the four heavy metals. Data analysis indicates that, for both adults and children, the average daily dose (ADD) of these metals through ingestion (ADDing) is substantially higher than through inhalation (ADDinh) and dermal contact (ADDder). This suggests that ingestion is the predominant exposure pathway for heavy metals in both population groups, whereas inhalation and dermal contact play relatively minor roles in overall exposure. Further analysis revealed that pediatric populations generally exhibit higher aggregate exposure to heavy metals compared to adults, a difference likely attributable to developmental behavioral patterns (e.g., soil-contact activities during play, digit sucking) and greater susceptibility to exposure due to immature immune system development. These findings align with the conclusions presented in the existing literature.40,41

In terms of non-carcinogenic health risk assessment, the non-carcinogenic values for ingestion (HQing) of the four heavy metals were consistently higher than those for inhalation (HQinh) and dermal contact (HQder), indicating that ingestion is also a primary exposure pathway for non-carcinogenic risks in both adults and children. The HI ranking for adults was Pb > Zn > Cd > Cu, whereas in children, the ranking was Pb > Zn > Cu > Cd. Although all HI values for adult subgroups were below the safety threshold (HI < 1), the HI value for Pb in children reached 2.73, exceeding the threshold and indicating a potential risk of non-carcinogenic health effects associated with Pb exposure among the pediatric population in the study area.

Furthermore, the TCR values for Cd in adults and children were 2.13 × 10−2 and 2.82 × 10−2, respectively, both of which substantially exceed the acceptable threshold of 1.0 × 10−4. These results indicate that both adults and children in the region are exposed to significant carcinogenic risks associated with Cd, with children demonstrating a higher risk level than adults. This observation aligns with findings reported in references,42,43 which suggest that physiological and behavioral characteristics unique to children, such as increased frequency of contact with soil and heightened susceptibility to environmental contaminants, may contribute to greater exposure to heavy metals. Based on these findings, it is recommended to strengthen health risk monitoring and implement targeted protective measures for children.

Results of human health risk assessment for heavy metals in crops

Figure 8 illustrates the HRI for four heavy metals in vegetables and maize, derived from dietary intake, for both adults and children. The findings reveal that the HRI values for all heavy metals in vegetables are notably higher than those observed in maize, with the HRI for children being approximately double that of adults. Specifically, for vegetables, the adult HRI values for Pb, Cd, Zn, and Cu are 66.56, 18.06, 9.41, and 2.21, respectively, whereas the corresponding values for children are 137.83, 37.40, 19.48, and 4.57, respectively. Given that the HRI values for all heavy metals substantially exceed 1, this indicates that the consumption of locally cultivated vegetables presents a considerable non-carcinogenic health hazard. For maize, only the adult HRI for Pb (1.49) exceeds the risk threshold, while Zn, Cu, and Cd remain within safe bounds. Nevertheless, when children consume maize, the HRI values for Pb, Zn, and Cu are recorded at 3.03, 1.86, and 1.51, respectively, suggesting significant health risks, with only Cd not posing a considerable threat. Overall, dietary intake of vegetables constitutes the primary pathway of heavy metal exposure for inhabitants in the area, with children being particularly susceptible to heavy metal contamination. Pb and Cd emerge as the principal pollutants contributing to health risks in these two crop types.

Soil-crop response relationship

To investigate the accumulation of heavy metals within the soil-crop system and the applicability of current regulatory standards, we constructed cross-validation scatterplots of paired soil-crop samples (comprising 26 samples each for Maize, Napa cabbage and Bok choy), based on the agricultural soil risk control threshold (GB 15618-2018) and food contaminant limits (GB 2762-2025). Results revealed distinct inconsistencies between soil contamination assessment and crop safety evaluation (Figure 9): the exceedance rates of Pb and Cd in leafy vegetables both reached 100%, while 100% of maize samples exceeded the Pb limit with no Cd exceedance. Specifically, Pb showed a “false positive” pattern: where certain soil samples surpassed the 500 mg kg−1 threshold, yet the Pb concentrations in the corresponding crops remained within safety limits. Conversely, Cd demonstrated a “false negative” pattern: with most soil samples did not exceed the 2 mg kg−1 threshold, while the Cd levels in the corresponding vegetables exceeded the established standard. Comparable patterns were also identified for Cu and Zn, thereby corroborating the widespread occurrence of such misjudgments. These discrepancies are primarily driven by variable heavy metal bioavailability in soil and regional high geochemical background. In areas of northeastern Yunnan with high geochemical backgrounds, soil heavy metals predominantly occur in stable residual and Fe-Mn oxide-bound forms with low bioavailability. This results in limited uptake and translocation to the edible parts of crops, thereby leading to an overestimation of soil risks, termed as “false positives”. Conversely, in soils with low pH or low organic matter, the bioavailability of heavy metals is enhanced. Coupled with the strong Cd accumulation capacity of leafy vegetables, crop Cd levels may exceed safety standards even when the total soil Cd concentration remains below the established limit, resulting in hidden “false negative” health risks.

This study demonstrates the limitations of current uniform total-quantity thresholds in high-background areas. The one-size-fits-all thresholds fail to consider the impacts of soil properties, metal speciation and crop types on bioavailability, which may lead to unnecessary remediation or neglected food safety risks. Future standard revision and regional management should shift from total quantity control to refined bioavailability-based regulation, integrating soil physicochemical properties, metal speciation and crop types to establish tiered risk thresholds, thus improving the scientific applicability of standards and balancing soil ecological protection and agricultural product safety.

Discussion

This study confirmed that Pb, Zn, Cu, and Cd concentrations in all soil samples from the Huize lead-zinc mining area far surpassed local Yunnan soil geochemical background values, with a 100% over-standard rate, reflecting severe pollution due to the area’s high carbonate geochemical background and prolonged mining-smelting. Heavy metal speciation distribution further unveiled key differences in element mobility in soil: total heavy metal content alone cannot precisely reflect actual agricultural contamination risk, as metal bioavailability, governed by speciation, is the primary driver of heavy metal accumulation in crops.

The dual receptor model (APCS-MLR+PMF) confirmed that soil heavy metal pollution in the study area stemmed from four sources: industrial smelting, agricultural chemical input, traffic emissions, and natural carbonate parent material weathering. Industrial emissions, the main anthropogenic pollution source in both models, showed high Cu and Cd loading, aligning with spatial high-value zones near smelting plants from Kriging interpolation. The consistent source contribution rankings from both models confirmed a composite pollution pattern of “natural high background + multi-path human input” in the mining area.

Ingestion via soil contact and crop consumption dominated human exposure, whereas inhalation and dermal pathways posed minimal risks. Notably, children suffered severe non-carcinogenic Pb risks, and Cd posed unacceptable carcinogenic threats to all residents. The Dietary Risk Index (HRI) results further showed leafy vegetables as the main exposure source, with Pb and Cd hazard indices far surpassing safety limits; staple maize also posed excessive Pb risks for adults and children, making Pb and Cd priority toxic pollutants.

A prevalent decoupling (“false positive/negative”) exists between soil total metal concentrations and crop safety, raising widespread concerns in carbonate-rich areas with high natural background levels in Southwest China and near Zambia’s Kabwe mine. This arises from applying uniform national standards for total metals, which ignore variations in metal bioavailability.

Combined with the pollution source apportionment, ecological risk, and soil-crop coupling analysis results of this study, targeted management strategies can be proposed for high-geochemical-background Pb-Zn mining areas. Firstly, optimize regional crop planting: restrict leafy vegetables with high Pb and Cd accumulation in heavily polluted areas near smelters and traffic trunk lines, prioritizing low-accumulation crops such as maize to block heavy metal exposure in the food chain. Secondly, standardize the use of zinc-containing pesticides and fertilizers in agricultural areas to reduce heavy metal sources; for traffic roads through farmlands, install dust control facilities and regularly clean road sediment to mitigate traffic-related Pb pollution. Thirdly, set up a differentiated soil risk assessment system based on heavy metal speciation and bioavailability, rather than solely on total soil heavy metal concentration, to prevent resource waste from “false positive” and hidden food safety risks from “false negative”. The interference of geological background must be fully considered when setting regional soil risk thresholds. Finally, establish special health intervention and protection programs for children, a sensitive risk group, including regular heavy metal testing and environmental health education, to reduce non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks of Pb and Cd to locals.

Limitations of the study

Although this study systematically revealed the pollution characteristics, source composition, and human-health risk responses of soil heavy metals in the area, several limitations remain. First, sampling was only conducted from June to October 2025, without seasonal repetition, failing to capture the temporal variations characteristics of heavy metal speciation, bioavailability, and crop accumulation driven by rainfall, temperature, and farming practices. Second, human-health risks were quantified by the total concentrations of toxic elements, not bioavailability. Third, the exposure parameters for health risk assessment mainly came from USEPA recommendations and related literature, without correction for local residents’ activity patterns or children’s behaviors. Fourth, the “false positive/negative” mismatch was only descriptively analyzed, without quantitative models linking soil properties, metal speciation, and crop enrichment, lacking tools for regional risk prediction.

Method details

Description of the study area

The study area is located in Huize County, Qujing City, Yunnan Province, China (26°29′43″-26°36′00″N, 103°32′41″-103°40′14″E), Covering 365 km2. It has a subtropical plateau monsoon climate with annual average temperature of 12.6°C and annual precipitation of 847.1 mm. The terrain is dominated by mountainous land, stepping down from northeast to southwest. The dominant soil type is red soil, primarily developed from Quaternary red clay and weathered limestone materials, with inherently high heavy metal geological background.48,49 It is highly representative for researching heavy metal contamination in soil-crop system of southwest China mining areas.

Sample collection and processing

The field investigation and sampling were carried out from June to October 2025. Based on full investigation of mining pollution distribution and crop planting layout, systematic grid sampling combined with serpentine/S-shaped composite sampling was adopted. Quadrat size was 10 m × 10 m; each sampling site adopted 5-point mixed sampling with 20% duplicate sampling for quality control. Sampling coverage included mining functional zones, river riparian buffer zones and roadside farmland belts. Longitude and latitude of each site were accurately recorded, and a total of 78 groups of soil-crop paired samples were collected, including 26 samples for maize, Napa cabbage and Bok choy respectively. All samples were sealed, labeled and preserved strictly in accordance with national technical specifications.

Sampling and preparation of soil and crops

Adhered rigorously to the “Technical Specifications for Soil Environmental Monitoring” (HJ/T 166-2004) and the “Soil Quality Technical Guidelines on Soil Sampling” (GB/T 36197-2018). Approximately 1 kg of topsoil (0–20 cm depth) was collected per composite sample. The samples were subsequently air-dried naturally, removed plant residues and gravel, ground and sieved through 100-mesh nylon sieve, then sealed in polyethylene bags for subsequent detection.

Paired crop samples were synchronously collected at each soil sampling location in accordance with “Geochemical Specifications for Land Quality” (DZ/T 0295-2016). The specific parts collected included the edible fresh leaves of Napa cabbage and Bok choy, as well as mature and well-developed maize grains. The crop samples were thoroughly cleaned with deionized water, dried at 65°C, subsequently ground and sieved, and then stored for the determination of heavy metal content.

Detection indicators and methods

Soil pH was determined in accordance with the “Determination of Soil pH” (NY/T 1377-2007). Heavy metal (Pb, Zn, Cu, Cd) concentrations in soil and crops were measured by ICP-MS (ICAP-RQ, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the procedures specified in the “Analytical Methods for Regional Geochemical Samples” (DZ/T 0279–2016). The speciation analysis of heavy metals was conducted in accordance with the “Soil and Sediment-Sequential Extraction Procedure for the Speciation of 13 Trace Elements” (GB/T 25282-2010).

Instrument method detection limits (MDL) of Pb, Zn, Cu, Cd were 0.02, 0.03, 0.05, 0.002 mg kg−1 respectively. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate (with a repetition rate of 20%), while a blank sample was analyzed to check reagent impurities and minimize measurement errors. Analytical performance and precision were checked by the Chinese standardized reference material (soil, GBW07408; maize, GBW10012; Napa cabbage and Bok choy, GBW10014). Spike recovery rates were controlled at 90%–110%, and the relative standard deviations (RSDs) of all indicators were below 10%, thereby complying with the laboratory quality control requirements stipulated in GB/T 27476-2010.

Soil heavy metal pollution and ecological risks were evaluated via the Nemerow Comprehensive Pollution Index (PN) and Potential Ecological Risk Index (RI). Two distinct evaluation benchmarks were applied to standardize calculations and prevent confusion from mixed criteria, ensuring comparability of results: single pollution indices and RI were calculated against Yunnan’s provincial soil element background values, and the exceedance rates of farmland risk thresholds were determined in strict accordance with GB 15618-2018.
