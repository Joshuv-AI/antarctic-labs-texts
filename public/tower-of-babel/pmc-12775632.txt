Intraoperative bradycardia leading to cardiac arrest during anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) in a morbidly obese patient—a rare complication and management strategy: a case report
Authors: Worawich Kraipindakul, Teerachat Tanasansomboon, Wicharn Yingsakmongkol, Weerasak Singhatanadgige, Vit Kotheeranurak, Worawat Limthongkul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntraoperative bradycardia leading to cardiac arrest is a rare and serious complication during anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) surgeries. This case report presents a unique and previously undocumented instance of bradycardia progressing to asystole during an ALIF, highlighting the importance of autonomic nervous system involvement and intraoperative vigilance.Case DescriptionA 64-year-old female with a history of hypertension and morbid obesity was scheduled for an ALIF at L5/S1 due to adjacent segment disease (ASD) and foraminal stenosis at that level. Intraoperatively, after manipulation of the peritoneum and blunt dissection around the aortic bifurcation, her heart rate (HR) suddenly dropped to 50 beats per minute (bpm), followed by a rapid decrease in blood pressure (BP) and cardiac arrest. Immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and administration of ephedrine successfully restored spontaneous circulation within 30 seconds. The surgical and anesthesia teams promptly decided to abort the operation. Further evaluation revealed no evidence of myocardial infarction, and the patient made a full recovery. This rare event suggests that hyper vagal stimulation from manipulation of the superior hypogastric plexus (SHP) and peritoneum may be a possible mechanism. It underscores the critical importance of vigilant monitoring, prompt intervention, and effective communication between surgical and anesthesia teams to manage acute hemodynamic instability during high-risk spinal surgeries.ConclusionsThis case illustrates a rare but critical intraoperative event likely caused by hyper vagal stimulation during anterior spinal exposure near autonomic plexuses. Surgeons and anesthesiologists must be aware of this potential complication. Prompt recognition and a coordinated team response are essential for favorable outcomes. Consideration of invasive hemodynamic monitoring and collaboration with access surgeons may improve safety in complex ALIF procedures, especially in high-risk patients such as those with morbid obesity.Keywords: Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), case report, cardiac arrest, obesity

Highlight box

Key findings

• Intraoperative bradycardia, leading to cardiac arrest, is an extremely rare but serious complication during anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) procedures.

• The most likely cause was hyper vagal stimulation from surgical manipulation near the superior hypogastric plexus (SHP) and forceful peritoneal retraction in a morbidly obese patient.

• It highlights the need for vigilance from both the surgical and anesthesia teams to stop procedures causing bradycardia. Anesthetic medications should be discontinued, rescue drugs like ephedrine or atropine should be administered, and the Advanced Cardiac Life Support protocol must be activated if bradycardia progresses to asystole.

What is known and what is new?

• Intraoperative bradycardia has been documented in various abdominal and posterior spinal surgeries, but there have been no documented cases of this adverse event during ALIF procedures.

• Bradycardia is one of the complications of ALIF procedures. The possible etiology is hyper vagal stimulation from surgical manipulation near the SHP.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Increased awareness of bradycardia during ALIF is crucial. The use of an access surgeon and invasive BP monitoring via an arterial line (A-line) should be considered, especially in high-risk patients such as those with morbid obesity.

Introduction

The anterior approach to the lumbosacral spine, including the L5/S1 level, is widely accepted for treating various spinal pathologies, such as spondylolisthesis and degenerative disc disease (1,2). This approach provides direct access to the intervertebral space. Compared to posterior spinal fusion procedures, anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) offers several advantages (3,4). First, it provides a direct midline view of the disc space and efficient disc space clearance, which allows for the use of larger implants, maximizing implant size and surface area and leading to higher fusion rates. Second, release of the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL) facilitates more efficient lumbar lordosis restoration. Third, ALIF spares the posterior spinal elements, reducing postoperative pain and disability.

Approach-related complications of ALIF differ from posterior lumbar spine surgeries and are primarily related to vascular and visceral injuries. The incidence of vascular injury during ALIF varies widely, ranging from 1.9% to 24%, with laceration of the left common iliac vein (LCIV) being the most commonly reported injury (5,6). Visceral complications may include ureteric injury, retrograde ejaculation, and erectile dysfunction (7).

Intraoperative bradycardia, defined as a heart rate (HR) below 50 beats per minute (bpm) (8), has been documented in various intra-abdominal procedures, including gynecological, laparotomy, and laparoscopic surgeries (9,10). It can result from factors like sympathetic blockade due to neuraxial anesthesia, vagotonic drugs, or physical stimulation, increasing vagal activity (10). To our knowledge, intraoperative bradycardia is an uncommon complication during ALIF, especially when it progresses to cardiac arrest (11,12). This case report describes a rare event, highlighting the need for increased awareness and management strategies for this complication during ALIF surgery. We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-56/rc).

Case presentation

A 64-year-old female presented with low back pain and left-sided S1 radiculopathy, lasting for a year. Neurological examination revealed normal motor power and reflexes but decreased pinprick sensation on the left-sided S1 dermatome. Radiographs showed posterior spinal fixation at the L3–5 with L3/4 and L4/5 interbody cage placement. (Figure 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed evidence of previous right-sided L4 laminotomy and oblique lumbar interbody cages at L3/4 and L4/5. At L5/S1, there was severe disc space narrowing, causing stenosis of bilateral L5/S1 neural foramina (Figure 2). She was diagnosed with adjacent segment disease (ASD) with bilateral foraminal stenosis at L5/S1. Conservative treatment failed to relieve her symptoms. So, ALIF at L5/S1 was planned as definitive treatment.

Preoperative review revealed morbid obesity [body mass index (BMI) was 41.7 kg/m2] and well-controlled hypertension treated with enalapril 5 milligrams (mg) twice daily. Her anesthetic history included (I) general anesthesia (GA) for undergoing oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) L3–5 with microscopic posterior decompression at L4/5 5 years ago and (II) epidural spinal anesthesia for right total hip arthroplasty 3 years ago. Her blood pressure (BP) was well-controlled, and the electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed a normal sinus rhythm with a rate of 75 bpm.

After admission, BP and HR were monitored following the hospital protocol and remained within her normal range. Preoperative laboratory tests were all within normal range. The last dose of medication was enalapril 5 mg, taken in the evening, the day before surgery. On arrival at the operating theatre, her BP and HR were 130/80 mmHg and 82 bpm, and her room-air peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) was 99%. Standard monitoring equipment for HR, BP, SpO2, and end-tidal capnography (ETCO2) was established. Invasive BP monitoring was not used, as it is not part of routine monitoring for this procedure. An intravenous infusion of 300 milliliters (mL) of Ringer’s acetate solution was administered. GA was induced with 150 mg of propofol and 50 micrograms (µg) of fentanyl. Muscle relaxation was achieved with 10 mg of IV cisatracurium. The patient was subsequently intubated and connected to mechanical ventilation. ETCO2 was maintained at about 35 mmHg. Anesthesia was maintained with 50% oxygen with air and sevoflurane. She was positioned supine (Figure 3), and the skin was scrubbed using sterile technique. The anterior approach for the ALIF procedure was performed by an experienced spine surgeon. A transverse mini-Pfannenstiel skin incision was made on the left side just lateral to the midline, followed by blunt dissection of subcutaneous layer until left-sided anterior rectus sheath was identified. The anterior rectus sheath was then opened longitudinally by sharp dissection using electrocautery. Then, the lateral border of the rectus muscle was identified and mobilized medially to expose the underlying arcuate line and transversalis fascia. While mobilizing the muscles and gaining access to retroperitoneal space through the transversalis fascia, her HR decreased to 50–55 bpm, followed by a decrease in BP to 78/40 mmHg shortly after the onset of bradycardia. The anesthetist administered 6 mg of ephedrine, which resulted in an increase in HR to 75–85 bpm and BP to 100/60 mmHg. The surgery proceeded, and the OLIF51™ triple arm retractors system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used to gently move aside peritoneal and abdominal contents to the midline. The surgeon carefully identified and mobilized the aortic bifurcation, which lies in front of the L5/S1 disc in this patient. In the meantime, the assistant surgeons had to use more force than usual to stabilize the retractors due to the patient’s morbid obesity and very large abdomen. While mobilizing the left common iliac artery (LCIA), the anesthesiologist quickly noted severe bradycardia, and the carotid pulse was not palpable (Figure 4). The procedure was halted, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) protocol was initiated immediately. Return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved within 30 seconds following the CPR, no inotropic drugs were administered. A 12-lead ECG revealed a normal sinus rhythm at 70 bpm with no sign of myocardial ischemia/infarction, such as ST-T changes. An arterial line (A-line) was subsequently placed under ultrasonographic guidance, The arterial blood gas (ABG) results showed a potential of hydrogen (pH) of 7.38, bicarbonate (HCO3) of 24.5, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2) of 41, partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) of 425.7, potassium (K) of 4.53, and lactate of 0.8. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs-cTnI) was below 1.6 nanograms per liter (ng/L). A bedside echocardiogram revealed no signs of myocardial infarction and a fair left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The surgeon and anesthesiologist discussed the situation and decided to defer the surgery. The wound was then closed layer by layer. A total of 1,200 mL of IV fluids was administered and urine output was 350 mL intraoperatively. Once the patient was stable, she was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU).

The anesthesia report showed that 20 minutes after the operation began, while mobilizing the LCIA for access to the L5/S1 spine, and with the retractor system already placed with more force than usual due to the large abdomen, severe bradycardia occurred (red arrow), followed by hypotension and subsequent cardiac arrest. LCIA, left common iliac artery.

At the ICU, the patient was fully conscious, her vital signs were stable, and no inotropic drugs were needed. Postoperative laboratory results were all normal, including hs-cTnI. The endotracheal tube (ETT) was removed the next morning. On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the patient transitioned from intubation to an oxygen cannula. She was monitored in the ICU for one more day before being transferred to standard surgical ward. On POD2, she was able to ambulate in bed, and by POD3, she could sit up and walk with a walker. The patient and her relatives were counseled about the intraoperative events by the surgical and anesthesia teams. It was jointly decided to treat the L5/S1 condition on the next admission. She was discharged on POD3. The treatment plan was discussed between the surgeon and the patient at a 2-week follow-up, and the minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) at L5/S1 was considered on the next admission.

Discussion

Previous evidence suggests that the incidence of cardiac complications is approximately 6.7 per 1,000 lumbar spine surgeries (13). Intraoperative bradycardia has been reported in a limited number of case reports involving posterior spinal procedures (14,15). Despite the increasing adoption of the ALIF procedure, particularly in minimally invasive spine surgery, there have been no documented cases of this adverse event during this procedure (11,12,16). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of intraoperative cardiac arrest due to bradycardia during ALIF, particularly in a morbidly obese patient. Several hypotheses have been evaluated to explain hemodynamic changes for decreased BP that lead to cardiac arrest during the ALIF procedure. One possible explanation is intraoperative compression of the great vessels, reducing venous return, lowering cardiac output, and potentially causing hypotension and leading to cardiac arrest. However, this mechanism typically causes compensatory tachycardia through the baroreceptor reflex. A decrease in venous return and hypotension activates the sympathetic nervous system, which then increases HR to maintain cardiac output (17). The reverse Bainbridge reflex, which causes a decrease in HR under conditions of reduced venous return (18), is a possible but atypical mechanism because bradycardia should occur after venous return and BP decreases, not before. In this case, bradycardia occurred prior to the BP drop. Additionally, this reflex is not generally associated with cardiac arrest.

The most likely pathomechanism is bradycardia caused by hyper vagal stimulation, which activated the parasympathetic autonomic pathway during the procedure, leading to bradycardia and cardiac arrest (19). Supporting evidence includes the occurrence of bradycardia during retraction of the peritoneum and dissection in front of the aortic bifurcation, where the parasympathetic pelvic splanchnic nerves are located (20) (Figure 5). Additionally, the bradycardia occurred prior to cardiac arrest, suggesting bradycardia was the primary cause, leading to a drop in BP and ultimately cardiac arrest.

It receives sympathetic input from the lower two lumbar splanchnic nerves (L3–L4) and parasympathetic input from the pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2–S4). SHP, superior hypogastric plexus.

The superior hypogastric plexus (SHP) is a retroperitoneal structure situated anterior and slightly inferior to the bifurcation of the abdominal aorta and LCIV (21) (Figure 5). It receives sympathetic input from the lower two lumbar splanchnic nerves (L3, L4) and parasympathetic input from the pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2–S4) (20). The visceral peritoneum (VP) is innervated by both the parasympathetic vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) and the pelvic splanchnic nerves (20). In open abdominal and gynecological surgeries, vagal responses can occur due to peritoneal stretching and stimulation of the vagovagal reflex (10). In laparoscopic surgeries, A high flow rate of CO2 causes rapid peritoneal stretching, triggering a strong vagal response (10). HR variability (HRV) analysis also suggests that both vagal and sympathetic tones can be activated during abdominal surgical manipulation. The clinical outcome may show vagal dominance, which is manifested as bradycardia (9). Regarding our case, we think that the mechanism of bradycardia is due to parasympathetic vagal stimulation, which might occur during dissection of the SHP, as it receives parasympathetic fibers from the pelvic splanchnic nerves. Additionally, forceful retraction of the peritoneum during the surgical approach may also stimulate the afferent vagus and pelvic splanchnic nerves, which innervate the peritoneum.

The anterior approach involves unfamiliar anatomy and a higher risk of injuring surrounding structures (11). To ensure safe exposure, many spine surgeons prefer assistance from an access surgeon, typically a vascular surgeon (22,23). However, access surgeons are not routinely involved in some institutions, and spine surgeons often perform the entire ALIF procedure independently (24), as in our hospital. Phan et al. reported that overall intraoperative complication rates were similar with or without an “access surgeon” (16). However, the rates of peritoneal injuries were slightly lower when an “access surgeon” was present (16). Regarding obese patients, the greater depth from skin to spine makes retroperitoneal access and peritoneal manipulation more difficult (25). Especially in our morbid obesity patient, the anterior approach can be challenging and may require forceful peritoneal retraction, potentially triggering parasympathetic vagal stimulation. In such cases, involving an access surgeon should be considered (6,11,16).

The effect of anesthetic agents should also be considered as a potential cause of intraoperative bradycardia, as bradycardia is frequently observed following the administration of propofol and cisatracurium (26,27). However, in our patient, the HR did not decrease after induction, suggesting anesthetic agents were not the cause of the events. Inhalational agents like nitrous oxide (N2O) can potentially increase the risk of intraoperative bradycardia (28). Sevoflurane-induced bradycardia has been reported in infants with trisomy 21, but it is not typically seen in adult patients (29). Other medications linked to reflex perioperative bradycardia include beta-blockers, digitalis, cardiac glycosides, lithium, calcium channel blockers, tricyclic antidepressants, and phenytoin (8). However, our patient was not taking these medications. Bradycardia can occur unexpectedly with spinal or epidural block (30), but these procedures were not performed in our patient. Therefore, based on our analysis, we believe the primary cause of intraoperative bradycardia was hyper vagal stimulation during the anterior spinal approach.

The management of intraoperative bradycardia involves effective communication between the surgeon, anesthesiologist, and the entire team. Halting the procedure causing bradycardia is crucial. The anesthesiologist should promptly inform the surgeon of any issues, who should then acknowledge the concern and communicate with the team to stop any contributing actions. If the stimulus is removed, the issue is typically resolved (14,15). The medications that can cause bradycardia, such as dexmedetomidine, sufentanil, and cisatracurium should be discontinued (26,27). Ephedrine is the most logical single-drug of choice due to its dual mechanism of action: (I) alpha-1 agonist, which causes vasoconstriction and raises the BP; and (II) beta-1 agonist, which increases HR and cardiac contractility (31). Anticholinergic drugs, such as atropine, are frequently used as alternative drugs for intraoperative bradycardia (32). When bradycardia progresses to asystole, the Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) protocol should be immediately activated (33). CPR should be initiated immediately if the pulse cannot be palpated, even if electrical activity is still detected on ECG. Invasive BP monitoring via an A-line should be considered for continuous BP monitoring, as it allows for quicker and more precise detection of abnormalities in HR and BP compared to non-invasive methods (34). This approach is crucial in morbidly obese patients, where non-invasive BP monitoring may be inaccurate.

After ROSC, the decision to continue or abort surgery depends on several factors (19), including patient factors (post-resuscitation hemodynamics, cardiac function, underlying diseases, and overall physical condition) and surgical factors, with the most important consideration being the etiology of the bradycardia. Identifying the cause of this adverse event helps prevent triggers. The surgeon’s experience and surgical stage are also factors to consider. If surgery is still in early stages and will take a long time to complete, it is advisable to cancel the surgery. A discussion between the surgical team and anesthesiologists is also essential (35). This collaboration helps determine the best approach and the need for further monitoring or investigations before proceeding with surgery. In our case, the etiology of the intraoperative bradycardia that led to cardiac arrest was inconclusive at the time. As the procedure was still at the exposure stage and required significant time to complete, we deferred surgery and monitored the patient in the postoperative ICU.

Conclusions

Intraoperative bradycardia during ALIF is an extremely rare but serious complication. This case highlights the value of multidisciplinary planning, real-time communication, and access-surgeon support in high-risk patients. The most probable cause was hyper vagal stimulation, triggered by surgical manipulation near the SHP and forceful peritoneal retraction. The management requires prompt communication between the surgical and anesthesia teams to stop the procedure that may induce bradycardia. Anesthetic medications that can cause bradycardia should be discontinued. Rescue drugs like ephedrine or atropine should be administered to correct the hemodynamic disturbances. If bradycardia progresses to asystole, the ACLS protocol must be immediately activated. After ROSC, the decision to proceed with surgery depends on the patient’s condition and surgical factors. If the cause of bradycardia cannot be identified at the time and the surgery is still in the early stage, the operation should be considered to abort and transfer the patient to the ICU for close monitoring and further investigations.
