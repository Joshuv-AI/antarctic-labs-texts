A study on physicians’ perceptions of privacy in the context of the e-Nabiz (e-Pulse) in the Turkish healthcare system
Authors: Cihan Unal, Hakan Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines physicians’ perceptions of privacy and data protection in Turkey’s national personal health record system, e-Nabız. While e-Nabız enhances continuity of care through centralized access to prescriptions, laboratory results, imaging, and clinical records, its centralized governance model raises concerns regarding unauthorized access, accountability, and alignment with professional privacy norms. To empirically investigate these concerns, we conducted a survey with 309 healthcare professionals. The results reveal a pronounced usage–trust paradox: although system usage is high (87%), only 56% of respondents consider existing data-protection mechanisms adequate, and a substantial proportion express concerns about potential misuse or leakage of health data. Importantly, physicians’ concerns are not primarily directed at the absence of role-based access control (RBAC), but at the lack of verifiable enforcement, transparent oversight, and tamper-evident auditability of access decisions. This indicates a perceived misalignment between expected information flows grounded in the physician–patient confidentiality context and the opaque governance of access practices within the system. Based on these findings, the study derives a governance-oriented design implication: a supplementary layer in which RBAC decisions and access events are recorded through blockchain-supported immutable logs and smart contracts to enhance accountability and auditability. The proposed approach does not store medical data on the blockchain; rather, it aims to make authorization and access histories verifiable and resistant to manipulation. Introduction

The rapid digitalization of healthcare has fundamentally transformed how medical data are collected, stored, and shared. Worldwide1, health authorities increasingly adopt nationwide electronic health record (EHR) platforms to improve cost efficiency, accessibility, and continuity of care. In Turkey, this transformation is exemplified by the e-Nabız system, a national digital health platform integrating prescriptions, laboratory results, imaging records, and physician notes into a centralized database managed by the Ministry of Health. As of 2025, e-Nabız has reached more than 60 million registered users, making it one of the most comprehensive digital health infrastructures in Europe2. This scale has significantly enhanced healthcare efficiency by enabling real-time, cross-institutional access to patients’ medical histories. According to the World Health Organization3, Turkey has rapidly expanded its national digital health infrastructure over the last decade, positioning centralized platforms such as e-Nabız as core components of healthcare delivery.

Despite these benefits, nationwide digital health systems face structural challenges that directly affect privacy, security, and governance. Centralized architectures store highly sensitive medical data and are therefore exposed to risks such as unauthorized access, large-scale breaches, and secondary use without meaningful consent. In addition, health data must circulate across heterogeneous actors-including family physicians, hospitals, laboratories, pharmacies, and public agencies-creating complex data flows that are difficult to control and audit.

At national scale, further challenges arise in terms of scalability, transparency, and accountability, particularly when access logs, consent records, and authorization mechanisms are maintained in mutable or opaque databases. For healthcare professionals, such opacity directly affects trust in whether access to sensitive patient data aligns with professional confidentiality norms. Health data constitute one of the most sensitive categories of personal information, and their misuse may lead to severe psychological, economic, or social harm.

Consequently, the sustainability of large-scale digital health platforms such as e-Nabız depends not only on technological performance but also on user trust, transparency, and effective governance. Although numerous national and international studies have examined the technical capabilities and functional success of e-Nabız, empirical research focusing on how healthcare professionals perceive its privacy and data-protection mechanisms remains limited.

Recent investigative reports and media coverage have confirmed serious incidents of health data leaks in Turkey’s national systems, particularly concerning the e-Nabız platform4,5. These incidents have triggered public concern and legal scrutiny, underscoring the urgent need to evaluate how such breaches affect professional trust in system governance. Unlike prior studies that emphasize system functionality, patient satisfaction, or legal compliance, this study empirically examines privacy governance from the perspective of physicians within a fully deployed national health platform. This gap is critical, as physicians interact with sensitive health data daily and directly shape how privacy principles are implemented in practice. Accordingly, this study addresses the following research questions. Addressing these challenges requires verifiable, traceable, and user-centric approaches to data governance that extend beyond conventional security models.

RQ1: How do healthcare professionals perceive the privacy and data-security mechanisms of the e-Nabız system?

RQ2: Which factors (e.g., role, specialty, or professional experience) influence their level of trust and perceived adequacy of protection?

RQ3: How do physicians evaluate the potential of emerging technologies, such as blockchain and smart contracts, to enhance health-data privacy?

The objective of this research is twofold: first, to empirically evaluate healthcare professionals’ perceptions, concerns, and expectations regarding the privacy of health data managed through e-Nabız; and second, to derive governance-oriented technological implications that may strengthen privacy protection in nationwide digital health systems. By focusing on physicians rather than patients or policymakers, the study offers an insider perspective on privacy practices within Turkey’s digital health ecosystem. Conceptually, the study is informed by Nissenbaum’s6 theory of Contextual Integrity, which conceptualizes privacy as the appropriate flow of information according to context-specific norms. In healthcare, these norms are rooted in the physician–patient relationship and its expectation of confidentiality. Contextual integrity has been widely applied to analyze digital privacy issues across healthcare, social media, and organizational settings7,8, and it provides a useful lens for assessing whether e-Nabız preserves or disrupts normative expectations surrounding data access, sharing, and governance. The analysis also draws on comparative insights from international digital health systems. Platforms such as Germany’s Elektronische Patientenakte (ePA) and Estonia’s e-Health Record demonstrate that technological sophistication must be accompanied by privacy-centered design, including role-based access control and consent-driven data sharing2. At the same time, recent studies highlight that large-scale digital health infrastructures remain exposed to cyber-physical risks, interoperability challenges, and evolving threat vectors. For instance, Gampel and Eveleigh9 show how model-driven risk assessment can reveal vulnerabilities in critical health infrastructures, while Laila10 emphasizes the role of interconnected medical devices as potential sources of privacy compromise. Together, these findings reinforce the argument that traditional centralized architectures alone are insufficient to guarantee traceability, enforceable consent, and sustained user trust.

This study makes three primary contributions to the literature on digital health privacy and national EHR platforms. First, it provides one of the few physician-centered empirical analyses of privacy perceptions within a fully deployed nationwide digital health system. Second, it applies the framework of contextual integrity to evaluate how existing access and governance mechanisms align with physicians’ normative privacy expectations and legal requirements under KVKK and GDPR. Third, it derives a conceptual design implication suggesting that blockchain-supported, tamper-evident logging and smart contract-based authorization mechanisms can enhance the auditability and accountability of role-based access control. Importantly, the study is informed by empirical findings rather than proposing a standalone technical solution.

Importantly, the proposed architecture does not store medical data on the blockchain; rather, it is derived directly from empirical findings as a governance-oriented implication aimed at strengthening transparency and trust in systems such as e-Nabız.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews the literature on digital health privacy, role-based access control, and blockchain-based systems. Section III describes the research methodology and survey design. Section IV presents the empirical findings and discusses them in light of international practices. Finally, Section V concludes with recommendations for policymakers and directions for future research.

Literature review and theoretical framework

Health data privacy and national digital health systems

The digital transformation of healthcare has significantly expanded the scale, scope, and sensitivity of medical data processing. International organizations and regulatory frameworks, including the World Health Organization and the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), classify health data as a special category of personal information requiring heightened protection due to its potential psychological, social, and economic consequences if misused. As a result, privacy and data governance have become central policy concerns rather than purely technical issues in the design of national electronic health record (EHR) systems.

National digital health platforms aim to improve efficiency, continuity of care, and accessibility by centralizing medical data across institutions. However, prior research consistently highlights that such centralization introduces structural privacy risks, particularly related to unauthorized access, secondary data use, and insufficient transparency in data governance11–13. Comparative studies demonstrate that while centralized systems facilitate interoperability and large-scale analytics, they also amplify the impact of security failures and complicate accountability mechanisms14. These challenges are especially pronounced in nationwide systems that involve multiple stakeholders, including hospitals, primary care providers, laboratories, pharmacies, and public authorities.

In the Turkish context, the e-Nabız system represents one of the most comprehensive national digital health initiatives, integrating a wide range of medical data into a single platform2. While the system has been widely recognized for its functional success and user adoption, existing studies primarily focus on technical capabilities, patient satisfaction, or legal compliance. Empirical research addressing privacy governance from the perspective of healthcare professionals remains limited, despite physicians’ central role as both data producers and custodians. This gap underscores the need for studies that examine how privacy is perceived and experienced by healthcare professionals within fully deployed national systems.

Role-based access control and its limitations in healthcare

Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) has long been considered a foundational mechanism for protecting sensitive information in healthcare information systems. By assigning access permissions based on professional roles, RBAC aims to reduce unauthorized access and ensure that users can only retrieve data necessary for their clinical responsibilities. Empirical evaluations indicate that RBAC implementations can significantly reduce accidental or inappropriate data exposure when properly configured15,16.

Despite these advantages, the literature also emphasizes several limitations of traditional RBAC models in large-scale healthcare environments. First, RBAC systems are typically implemented within centralized architectures, where authorization decisions and access logs are controlled by a single authority. This structure creates reliance on institutional trust and limits independent verification of access events. Second, RBAC alone does not address contextual or purpose-specific access requirements mandated by privacy regulations such as GDPR and Turkey’s Personal Data Protection Law (KVKK). Third, access logs in centralized systems are often mutable, making retrospective auditing and accountability difficult in cases of dispute or suspected misuse17.

Recent studies further suggest that RBAC becomes increasingly difficult to manage as system complexity grows. In nationwide platforms involving heterogeneous institutions and dynamic clinical contexts, static role definitions may fail to capture real-world access needs, leading either to excessive restrictions or overly broad permissions13. These findings indicate that while RBAC remains necessary, it is insufficient on its own to guarantee transparency, enforceable consent, and sustained trust in national digital health systems.

Emerging technologies for privacy and accountability in healthcare

Recent studies in telemedicine and interoperable health systems emphasize that privacy risks do not primarily stem from data exchange itself, but from insufficiently transparent access governance. The British Journal of Healthcare Management highlights that interoperability and data security should not be treated as competing objectives; rather, failures in accountability, auditability, and access traceability are the main sources of trust erosion in large-scale digital health systems18. This perspective aligns with findings that traditional role-based access control mechanisms, when implemented without verifiable and independent audit layers, are insufficient to sustain professional trust in interconnected healthcare environments.

To address the limitations of traditional access control and centralized governance, recent research has explored the potential of emerging technologies, particularly blockchain and smart contract-based systems, to enhance privacy and accountability in healthcare data management. Blockchain technology provides an immutable, tamper-evident ledger that records transactions in a distributed manner, thereby reducing reliance on a single trusted authority. However, its implementation in medical systems still faces notable challenges regarding scalability, integration, and regulatory compliance19. In healthcare contexts, blockchain has been proposed primarily as a mechanism for secure logging, traceability, and verification rather than as a storage medium for medical data12,14.

Several studies demonstrate that blockchain-supported audit trails can improve transparency by ensuring that every data access or modification event is permanently recorded and cannot be altered retroactively20. Smart contracts, operating as programmable logic layers on top of blockchain infrastructures, enable automated enforcement of access rules, consent conditions, and temporal limitations. Recent implementations demonstrate that smart contracts can align with GDPR by enabling dynamic, user-controlled consent mechanisms21. This combination allows for fine-grained, context-aware authorization that extends beyond static RBAC models22.

Importantly, the literature cautions against storing large volumes of sensitive health data directly on blockchain networks due to scalability, cost, and privacy concerns. Instead, a consensus has emerged around hybrid architectures in which medical data remain in off-chain repositories, while blockchain is used to record access decisions, consent states, and audit logs14,23. Such architectures aim to strengthen accountability and user trust without disrupting existing healthcare information systems.

Contextual integrity as a framework for health data privacy

This study adopts Nissenbaum’s theory of Contextual Integrity as its primary conceptual framework for analyzing privacy in digital health systems6. Unlike traditional approaches that define privacy in terms of secrecy or control alone, contextual integrity conceptualizes privacy as the appropriate flow of information according to context-specific norms. These norms specify who may access information, under what conditions, and for what purposes.

In healthcare, contextual norms are deeply rooted in the physician–patient relationship and the ethical principle of confidentiality. Empirical research applying contextual integrity to digital health, telemedicine, and electronic records demonstrates that privacy violations often arise not from data breaches per se, but from deviations in expected information flows-such as unclear consent, excessive access privileges, or opaque governance structures7,8. This perspective is particularly relevant for national digital health platforms, where data circulate across multiple institutional boundaries and technological layers.

By applying contextual integrity, researchers can evaluate whether existing access control mechanisms and governance practices align with professional and societal expectations of medical confidentiality. Prior studies suggest that systems lacking transparent, enforceable, and auditable access controls are more likely to erode trust among healthcare professionals, even when formal security measures are in place13.

One practical implementation of these principles can be found in Estonia’s national health system, where patients have the ability to monitor who accesses their medical data and to actively manage permissions. This patient-centered approach has been shown to increase both user trust and accountability in digital health environments24.

Research gap and contribution

The reviewed literature reveals three key gaps. First, while national digital health systems have been widely studied from technical, legal, and patient-centered perspectives, empirical research focusing on healthcare professionals’ privacy perceptions remains scarce. Second, existing studies on RBAC and blockchain-based healthcare systems often emphasize technical design or conceptual prototypes, with limited validation in fully deployed nationwide platforms. Third, few works integrate empirical findings with a normative privacy framework such as contextual integrity to derive governance-oriented design implications.

This study addresses these gaps by providing a physician-centered empirical analysis of privacy perceptions within Turkey’s e-Nabız system, one of the largest national digital health platforms in Europe. By combining survey-based evidence with the theoretical lens of contextual integrity, the study identifies trust deficits related to access transparency and accountability. Rather than proposing blockchain as a standalone technical solution, the paper derives a design implication in which blockchain-supported, tamper-evident logging and smart contract-based authorization strengthen the auditability of RBAC mechanisms. In doing so, the study contributes both to the theoretical understanding of privacy in national e-health infrastructures and to practical discussions on how trust and accountability can be enhanced in large-scale digital health systems.

Despite extensive research on access control and emerging technologies in digital health systems, empirical evidence on how physicians perceive privacy governance in fully deployed national platforms remains limited.

Methodology

Research design

This study adopts a quantitative, survey-based research design to examine healthcare professionals’ perceptions of privacy and data protection in the e-Nabız system. The methodological approach is descriptive and exploratory, aiming to empirically identify trust deficits, privacy concerns, and governance-related expectations within a fully deployed national digital health platform. Rather than testing a predefined technical model, the study uses empirical evidence to derive governance-oriented design implications related to access control and auditability.

This approach enables an evidence-based assessment of whether existing access and governance mechanisms align with healthcare professionals’ expectations and normative privacy standards.

Sampling and participants

The study employed a convenience sampling strategy to reach healthcare professionals actively working within the Turkish healthcare system. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Physicians from public hospitals, foundation universities, private healthcare institutions, and primary care settings were invited to participate through professional networks, email lists, and digital communication platforms.

A total of 309 physicians participated in the study. The sample includes participants from a wide range of medical specialties, professional titles, and years of experience, providing diversity in institutional background and clinical practice. This heterogeneity allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how privacy perceptions may vary across professional roles and experience levels within the national digital health ecosystem.

Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics of the 309 physicians who participated in the study, including professional titles, medical specialization, years of service, and institutional affiliation. The distribution indicates that the sample is dominated by specialist doctors (35.2%) and general practitioners (31.5%), with a substantial proportion having more than 20 years of professional experience (65.2%). The majority of respondents are employed in public healthcare institutions (76.3%), suggesting that the findings primarily reflect privacy perceptions within the state healthcare system.

Privacy and data protection perceptions

The second section included 12 closed-ended items designed to assess participants’ perceptions of system security, privacy protection, access control, and trust in the e-Nabız system. Responses were measured using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree.

Open-ended feedback

An optional open-ended question allowed participants to share additional observations, concerns, or suggestions regarding privacy and data governance in e-Nabız.

Survey items were developed based on prior studies on digital health privacy, access control, and user trust11–13. To ensure content validity and contextual relevance, the questionnaire was reviewed by two experts in medical informatics and one researcher specializing in ethics and data protection. A pilot test with ten physicians was conducted to assess clarity, readability, and completion time. Minor wording revisions were made based on pilot feedback.

Data collection procedure

The survey was administered digitally using the JotForm platform. The introduction page of the survey clearly explained the purpose of the study, emphasized the voluntary nature of participation, and informed respondents that no personally identifiable information would be collected. Participants were informed that their responses would be used solely for scientific research purposes.

Data collection was conducted over a defined period, during which the survey link remained publicly accessible to eligible participants. No incentives were offered for participation, and respondents were free to withdraw at any point prior to submission.

Reliability and validity

The internal consistency of the survey instrument was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a value of 0.87, indicating a high level of reliability. Sampling adequacy was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure, resulting in a value of 0.673, which is considered acceptable for exploratory analysis. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (χ² = 1254.41, p < .001), confirming the suitability of the data for further analysis.

To examine relationships between demographic variables and privacy perceptions, inferential statistical analyses were conducted. A Chi-square test revealed a significant association between frequency of e-Nabız usage and perceived system reliability (χ² = 18.72, p = .004, Cramér’s V = 0.24), indicating a small-to-moderate effect size. A one-way ANOVA indicated significant differences in privacy perceptions across medical specialties (F = 3.41, p = .018, η² = 0.04), suggesting a small effect. Additionally, an independent-samples t-test showed that physicians with more than ten years of professional experience reported significantly higher privacy awareness (t = 2.63, p = .009, Cohen’s d = 0.32), indicating a small effect.

When the ANOVA results indicated statistically significant differences, post-hoc comparisons were conducted using the Tukey HSD test to identify specific group differences.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including frequency and percentage distributions, to summarize participants’ responses and usage patterns. Inferential statistical tests were applied to explore associations between demographic variables and privacy perceptions, as described above.

Qualitative responses from the open-ended question were analyzed using thematic coding to identify recurring concerns related to unauthorized access, insufficient transparency, and governance shortcomings in the e-Nabız system. These qualitative insights were used to contextualize and support the quantitative findings but were not subjected to formal content analysis due to their limited volume.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Hacettepe University Ethics Committee (Decision No. 3953820, dated 26 December 2024). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained implicitly through survey participation.

No personal identifiers, IP addresses, or tracking metadata were collected. All data were stored in encrypted format and accessed only by the research team. The dataset is not publicly available due to ethical and privacy considerations but may be made available for verification purposes upon reasonable request and subject to ethical approval.

Methodological scope and limitations

This methodology is designed to identify privacy perceptions and trust-related concerns among physicians rather than to evaluate the technical performance of specific security technologies. As a cross-sectional, self-reported survey study, the findings reflect perceptions at a specific point in time and may be subject to response bias. Nevertheless, the approach is appropriate for uncovering governance-related trust deficits that inform the design implications discussed in later sections.

Scope and interpretation clarification

This study is designed as a cross-sectional, descriptive investigation focusing on physicians’ perceptions of privacy and data governance within a nationwide digital health system. It does not aim to establish causal relationships, evaluate the technical performance of security technologies, or test predictive or robustness models. Accordingly, all analytical results are interpreted as exploratory and perception-based findings that inform governance-oriented implications rather than validated technical solutions.

Survey results reveal that 87% of respondents actively use the e-Nabız system in their daily professional practice. This high usage rate confirms that e-Nabız has become an indispensable operational component of healthcare delivery rather than a voluntary or optional digital service. Physicians rely on the system for accessing patient histories, laboratory results, prescriptions, and diagnostic information, indicating strong functional integration into clinical workflows.

Despite this widespread use, perceptions of privacy and data protection remain ambivalent. Only 56% of participants reported that patient data are adequately protected within the e-Nabız system, while 32% expressed explicit concerns that health data could be accessed without authorization or misused. This coexistence of high usage and moderate trust suggests a structural trust deficit rather than a lack of system adoption. A similar pattern is observed in other studies, where users express privacy concerns based more on perception than on actual security performance25.

Regarding access control mechanisms, 52% of respondents agreed that role-based access control (RBAC) could enhance privacy protection, whereas 21% disagreed. Notably, qualitative comments revealed recurring concerns about excessive or disproportionate access rights in certain subsystems, particularly pharmacy-related applications. These concerns point to inconsistencies between professional expectations of confidentiality and actual access practices within the broader digital health ecosystem.

Attitudes toward emerging technologies were generally positive but cautious. 56% of participants believed that blockchain-based applications could contribute to improving privacy and security in digital health systems, while 45% reported limited knowledge about such technologies. This indicates openness toward technological innovation alongside a clear need for better understanding and transparency regarding implementation and implications.

Only 32% of physicians believed that patients are sufficiently aware of the privacy implications of digital health systems, reflecting broader public uncertainty and perceived lack of control over personal data26. This perception highlights a broader societal dimension of privacy governance, suggesting that trust in digital health platforms depends not only on technical safeguards but also on user awareness and institutional communication.

Interpretation of findings

The findings demonstrate a critical paradox: e-Nabız is widely used and functionally valued, yet it does not fully inspire trust regarding privacy and data protection. This paradox reflects the mandatory nature of the system rather than confidence in its governance mechanisms. Physicians use e-Nabız because clinical practice depends on it, not because they perceive it as transparently secure.

Figure 6 visualizes the discrepancy between the high level of system usage and the comparatively lower level of trust in privacy protection mechanisms. Despite near-universal reliance on e-Nabız in clinical practice, a substantial portion of physicians remain uncertain about data security, underscoring that system adoption is driven by necessity rather than confidence in governance.

Usage–trust paradox in the e-Nabız system.

Importantly, the data suggest that physicians’ concerns are not primarily directed at the absence of access control mechanisms. Rather, they stem from the lack of verifiable enforcement and transparent accountability. While RBAC exists in principle, respondents expressed uncertainty about who accesses data, under what conditions, and whether such access can be independently audited. This aligns with prior research emphasizing that access control without auditability fails to generate sustained trust in large-scale information systems.

The reported discrepancies in access privileges-particularly cases where non-physician personnel are perceived to have broader access than physicians-underscore an ethical as well as technical problem. Such inconsistencies challenge the normative expectations of medical confidentiality and professional responsibility, potentially exposing healthcare professionals to legal and ethical risks.

From the perspective of contextual integrity, these findings indicate a misalignment between expected and actual information flows. Physicians anticipate that access to sensitive health data will be purpose-limited, role-appropriate, and traceable. When these conditions are not visibly met, trust erodes even if no concrete data breach has been personally experienced. Thus, privacy concerns emerge not solely from technical vulnerabilities but from opaque governance structures.

Discussion

Building on the empirical patterns identified above, this section situates the findings within broader debates on privacy governance, professional trust, and accountability in national digital health systems. This study set out to examine physicians’ perceptions of privacy and data protection in a fully deployed national digital health system. Taken together, the findings reveal a clear usage–trust paradox: while e-Nabız is deeply embedded in daily clinical practice, a substantial proportion of physicians remain uncertain about the adequacy, transparency, and enforceability of its privacy governance. This tension suggests that widespread adoption does not necessarily translate into confidence in how access to sensitive health data is governed. In this section, the empirical findings are interpreted in relation to existing literature on digital health privacy, role-based access control, and professional confidentiality, and are discussed through the lens of contextual integrity to elucidate the governance-related sources of trust erosion observed among physicians.

Implications for access control and governance

The empirical evidence suggests that strengthening privacy in national digital health systems requires more than refining existing RBAC models. While RBAC remains necessary for defining baseline access permissions, it does not address critical questions of transparency, auditability, and enforceability at national scale. The design implication proposed in this study directly responds to the empirically observed accountability gap by making authorization and access histories verifiable and tamper-evident, rather than introducing an entirely new security layer.

The positive -but cautious- attitudes toward blockchain and smart contract-based systems should therefore be interpreted as a demand for verifiable governance mechanisms rather than for novel technology per se. Physicians appear to value the potential of immutable logging and programmable authorization insofar as these mechanisms can make access decisions traceable, contestable, and accountable. Desai27 emphasizes that blockchain’s decentralized architecture enhances transparency and trust. Similarly, Psarra et al.28 propose a permissioned blockchain framework for access control in electronic health records.

Crucially, the findings support an architectural implication in which medical data remain stored off-chain, while authorization decisions and access events are recorded in tamper-evident logs. Such an approach does not replace existing systems but complements them by addressing the trust gap identified through the survey. By rendering access histories immutable and auditable, this model directly responds to physicians’ concerns regarding invisible or unverifiable data access.

Integration with international and theoretical perspectives

The observed trust deficit in e-Nabız mirrors concerns reported in other national digital health systems, where adoption is hindered by limited transparency and inadequate communication strategies29. International experiences indicate that privacy-centered design requires not only formal consent mechanisms but also continuous, transparent oversight of data access practices.

Insights from critical infrastructure cybersecurity research further contextualize the trust deficits observed in the e-Nabız system. Gampel and Eveleigh9, in their study of industrial control systems, demonstrate that security failures in large-scale, interconnected infrastructures rarely originate from isolated technical vulnerabilities. Instead, they emerge from insufficient visibility into system-level interactions, access dependencies, and governance structures. By integrating the NIST Risk Management Framework with model-based systems engineering, the authors emphasize that risk becomes manageable only when authorization paths, control relationships, and accountability mechanisms are explicitly modeled and traceable. This perspective is highly relevant to national digital health platforms such as e-Nabız, where privacy concerns similarly stem from opaque access governance rather than from the absence of formal security controls. The parallel suggests that healthcare information systems should be approached as critical infrastructures, requiring system-level transparency and verifiable accountability to sustain professional trust.

When evaluated through the lens of contextual integrity, the findings highlight that privacy violations are perceived less as isolated technical failures and more as deviations from expected professional and ethical norms. Systems that fail to provide clear answers to “who accessed what, when, and why” undermine contextual expectations, even if formal security controls are present.

Therefore, the empirical findings reinforce the argument that transparent and verifiable access governance is a prerequisite for sustainable trust in national digital health platforms. Technologies such as blockchain and smart contracts are relevant insofar as they operationalize these principles, rather than as standalone security solutions.

An alternative explanation for the observed trust deficit may be related to limited transparency in institutional communication rather than deficiencies in technical security mechanisms themselves. Physicians’ concerns may partly reflect limited visibility into how access control policies are implemented and audited in practice. Future studies could therefore examine whether improved transparency and communication regarding existing security practices might mitigate perceived privacy risks without necessarily requiring major technological redesign. It is also important to note that blockchain is not the only technological approach proposed for strengthening privacy in digital health systems. Alternative approaches such as differential privacy and federated learning have been explored to enable secure data analysis while minimizing the exposure of sensitive health information. Differential privacy focuses on protecting individual-level data through controlled statistical noise, whereas federated learning enables distributed model training without transferring raw data to centralized repositories. Compared with these approaches, blockchain primarily contributes to transparency and auditability of access events rather than privacy-preserving computation itself. Therefore, the technologies should be viewed as complementary mechanisms that address different dimensions of privacy and governance in digital health infrastructures.

Conclusion and recommendations

Conclusion

The rapid expansion of national digital health systems has fundamentally reshaped how medical data are accessed and governed. In Turkey, the e-Nabız system represents one of the most comprehensive nationwide digital health platforms, delivering substantial gains in efficiency, continuity of care, and data integration. However, the findings of this study demonstrate that technical success and widespread adoption do not automatically translate into trust in privacy and data protection.

Based on a survey of 309 physicians, this study reveals a clear tension between mandatory system usage and moderate confidence in privacy safeguards. While e-Nabız is deeply embedded in everyday clinical practice, a significant proportion of healthcare professionals remain concerned about unauthorized access, insufficient transparency, and limited accountability in data governance. Importantly, these concerns are not directed at the absence of access control mechanisms per se, but at the inability to verify and audit how access decisions are enforced in practice.

By applying the framework of contextual integrity, the study shows that perceived privacy risks arise when information flows deviate from professional and ethical expectations embedded in the physician–patient relationship. When healthcare professionals cannot clearly determine who accessed patient data, under what conditions, and for what purpose, trust erodes even in the absence of confirmed data breaches. This finding underscores that privacy in national digital health systems is a governance problem as much as a technical one.

The primary contribution of this study is empirical. It provides one of the few physician-centered analyses of privacy perceptions within a fully deployed nationwide digital health system and identifies a trust deficit rooted in opaque access governance. The proposed blockchain- and smart contract-supported architecture is not presented as an independent technological innovation, but as a design implication derived directly from empirical findings. By recording authorization decisions and access events in tamper-evident, verifiable logs-while keeping medical data off-chain-such an approach addresses the core concern identified by physicians: the lack of transparent and enforceable accountability.

Overall, the study demonstrates that the long-term sustainability of national digital health platforms like e-Nabız depends not only on functional performance and integration, but also on the ability to provide verifiable, auditable, and ethically grounded privacy governance that aligns with professional expectations and legal requirements under KVKK and GDPR.

Limitations and future research

This study is subject to several limitations. The findings are based on self-reported perceptions from a cross-sectional survey and may be influenced by response bias. The sample, while diverse, may not fully represent all medical specialties or regional practices. Additionally, the study focuses exclusively on physicians; perspectives of patients, pharmacists, and system administrators were not included.

Future research should incorporate multi-stakeholder analyses and longitudinal designs to examine how privacy perceptions evolve over time. Pilot deployments of verifiable access logging mechanisms could also be empirically evaluated to assess their practical impact on trust, accountability, and system usability. Such research would further inform the design of privacy-centered governance models for national digital health platforms.
