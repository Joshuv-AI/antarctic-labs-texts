Bayesian Transfer Learning for High-Dimensional Linear Regression via Adaptive Shrinkage
Parsa Jamshidian

Abstract
We introduce BLAST, Bayesian Linear regression with Adaptive Shrinkage for Transfer, a Bayesian multi-source transfer learning framework for high-dimensional linear regression. The proposed analytical framework leverages global-local shrinkage priors together with Bayesian source selection to balance information sharing and regularization. We show how Bayesian source selection allows for the extraction of the most useful data sources, while discounting biasing information that may lead to negative transfer. In this framework, both source selection and sparse regression are jointly accounted for in prediction and inference via Bayesian model averaging. The structure of our model admits efficient posterior simulation via a Gibbs sampling algorithm allowing full posterior inference for the target regression coefficients, making BLAST both computationally practical and inferentially straightforward. Our method achieves more accurate posterior inference for the target than regularization approaches based on target data alone, while offering competitive predictive performance and superior uncertainty quantification compared to current state-of-the-art transfer learning methods. We validate its effectiveness through extensive simulation studies and illustrate its analytical properties when applied to a case study on the estimation of tumor mutational burden from gene expression, using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA).



Keywords:
High-dimensional Regression, Multi-source Data Integration, Regularization, Shrinkage Priors, Transfer Learning

Introduction

Modern regression techniques have undergone significant development to address the challenges posed by high-dimensional settings, characterized by a large number of predictors and potentially sparse signals. Methods such as the Lasso
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
, Elastic Net
(
Zou & Hastie 2005
)
, and various Bayesian shrinkage and selection approaches
(
Carvalho et al. 2009
,
Park & Casella 2008
,
Li & Lin 2010
,
Ishwaran & Rao 2005
,
Rossell & Telesca 2017
)
have proven effective in producing accurate and computationally efficient estimates by enforcing sparsity and regularizing model complexity. These tools have become indispensable in fields such as genomics, neuroimaging, and text analysis, where high-dimensional data is pervasive.

While these methods have demonstrated strong performance, their effectiveness can be compromised when sample sizes are limited, particularly in biomedical domains such as rare disease research or personalized medicine. In such low-sample-size scenarios, relying on a single target dataset alone can result in unstable estimates and poor generalization. A promising solution is to incorporate information from additional, related
source
datasets if available. When carefully leveraged, these datasets can help improve inference and predictive performance compared to using a target dataset alone. The statistical framework for this idea is known as
transfer learning
(TL), which aims to extract and transfer useful knowledge from source domains to enhance learning in a target domain
(
Suder et al. 2023
)
.

Transfer learning encompasses a broad range of methods designed to leverage information from one or more related domains to improve inference in a target domain. In its simplest form, single-source transfer learning borrows information from a single auxiliary dataset to enhance estimation or prediction in the target. Other transfer learning approaches include domain adaptation, where the feature distributions of the source and target may differ, and inductive transfer learning, where the source and target tasks differ
(
Pan & Yang 2010
)
.

This work investigates the multi-source transfer learning problem, where a target population or application is interrogated through a target data-set
\mathcal{D}_{0}=(\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{y}^{(0)})
with design matrix
\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}\times p}
and outcome vector
\mathbf{y}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
. Related studies ensue in multiple source data denoted as
\mathcal{D}_{k}=(\mathbf{X}^{(k)},\mathbf{y}^{(k)})
, where
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times p}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
for
k=0,\ldots,K
. Broadly, our aim is to transfer information from source datasets that are likely to improve inference and prediction for the target.

Let the
i
’th row of
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}
and the
i
’th element of
\mathbf{y}^{(k)}
be denoted as
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(k)}
and
y_{i}^{(k)}
, respectively. The target and the auxiliary data are assumed to arise from a Gaussian random quantity, with location indexed by a linear projection of the covariates such that:

{y}_{i}^{(k)}|\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(k)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left((\mathbf{x}_{i}^{(k)})^{T}\bm{w}^{(k)},\sigma^{2}_{k}\right),

(1)

for
i=1,\ldots,n_{k}
where
\bm{w}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the regression coefficient for the
k
th study. The main parameter of interest is
\bm{w}^{(0)}
, the regression coefficient for the target data. Intuitively, the source data can be leveraged for an improved estimation of the target parameter
\bm{w}^{(0)}
, when the coefficients
\bm{w}^{(k)}
are, in some sense, similar to the target parameter.

To highlight the focus on the target parameter, we will use the dedicated symbol
\bm{\beta}\equiv\bm{w}^{(0)}
moving forward. In general, we assume that
\bm{\beta}
is
\ell_{0}
-sparse such that
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}=s\ll p
. That is, only
s
of the
p
predictors are important in contributing to the target response. We further make the assumption that the source data coefficients are biased from the target such that
\bm{w}^{(k)}=\bm{\beta}+\bm{\delta}^{(k)}
for some
\bm{\delta}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Our primary goal is to transfer informative source datasets to make inference on the target regression coefficient,
\bm{\beta}
.

In order to leverage the source data effectively and avoid
negative transfer
, defined as a decline in target performance due to irrelevant source information, it is important that we make the distinction between informative and noninformative source data. We adopt the definition presented by
Li et al. 2022
who consider the global similarity measure which classifies informative sources as those whose contrast vectors
\bm{\delta}^{(k)}=\bm{\beta}-\bm{w}^{(k)}
are sufficiently sparse:

{\mathcal{A}_{q}}=\left\{1\leq k\leq K:\|\bm{\delta}^{(k)}\|_{q}\leq t\right\}

for a fixed
q\in[0,1]
. The threshold
t
is commonly referred to as the
transferring level
and
\mathcal{A}_{q}
is known as the
informative set

(
Li et al. 2022
,
Tian & Feng 2023
)
. From this point forward, we will refer to the informative set as
\mathcal{A}
which corresponds to the particular case when
q=1
. In general,
|\mathcal{A}|
(many informative source datasets) and small
t
(high global similarity between the source and target) are favored for transfer.

The informative set
\mathcal{A}
is typically unknown in practice and needs to be estimated from the data. Nevertheless, its definition serves as an important theoretical benchmark for characterizing and understanding “informativeness” in TL.

1.1
Our Contribution

Transfer learning in high-dimensional linear regression has attracted increasing attention in recent years, particularly within the frequentist paradigm.
Li et al. 2022
proposed a foundational framework for multi-source transfer learning, introducing a frequentist Lasso-based approach for estimating target study coefficients under continuous outcomes.
Tian & Feng 2023
later extended this work to high-dimensional generalized linear models and provided a procedure for constructing asymptotic confidence intervals for the target estimates.

More recently, interest has shifted toward Bayesian approaches which offer a natural framework for transfer learning by incorporating informative priors derived from source data to guide inference in the target domain. Works such as
Suder et al. 2023
have given a comprehensive overview of recent Bayesian approaches for transfer learning, highlighting how hierarchical modeling and prior specification can effectively leverage source information. In the specific case of high-dimensional regression,
Abba et al. 2024
used the horseshoe prior in a single-source setting to model the contrast between source and target means in the normal means problem.
Zhang et al. 2024
introduced extensions to high-dimensional linear regression in the multi-source case through covariate-specific spike-and-slab (SSL) priors to enable selective borrowing across sources.
Lai et al. 2024
proposed a method that centers the horseshoe prior at a weighted average of source-specific estimates, offering a principled mechanism for multi-source integration. While these methods provide valuable advances,
they rely on potentially prior-sensitive SSL strategies or pre-estimated source summaries. We build on these ideas by proposing a generalizable Bayesian framework that leverages shrinkage priors to incorporate source data information and enhance inference in the target domain in the high-dimensional multi-source transfer learning setting. We name this method BLAST, which stands for
B
ayesian
L
inear regression with
A
daptive
S
hrinkage for
T
ransfer.

To implement BLAST, we develop a Bayesian Gibbs sampling algorithm that jointly samples source coefficients
\bm{w}
and contrast parameters
\bm{\delta}
from their posterior distribution, enabling full Bayesian inference for the target regression coefficients,
\bm{\beta}
. When the informative source set
\mathcal{A}
is known, the model structure is fixed and the algorithm follows a standard Gibbs sampling routine without the need for study selection. However, in the more practical case where
\mathcal{A}
is unknown, we introduce a source study selection step based on posterior inclusion probabilities to estimate
\mathcal{A}
, with inference carried out through model averaging over probable configurations of
\mathcal{A}
.

Our proposed method contributes several advantages to existing approaches to transfer learning. First, the framework is compatible with a broad class of shrinkage priors, providing flexibility to tailor prior specifications to different problem contexts or domain knowledge. Second, by adopting a fully Bayesian approach, our method delivers robust uncertainty quantification for the target regression coefficients. Third, our approach is naturally scalable: as more informative source studies are incorporated, estimation accuracy and predictive performance improve.

The rest of our paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review the general Bayesian shrinkage model and present the methodology of BLAST in detail for the
\mathcal{A}
-known and
\mathcal{A}
-unknown cases. We further provide details on the model specification and implementation using the horseshoe shrinkage prior
(
Carvalho et al. 2009
)
. We show results of simulations using our method in Section 3, comparing the performance with other popular transfer learning algorithms in the literature. The efficacy of our method in a real-data application is demonstrated in Section 4 which involves the prediction of tumor mutational burden from gene expression profiles using publicly available data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Finally, in Section 5 we summarize our findings and provide concluding remarks.

2
Methodology

In this section, we first review Bayesian penalized regression with shrinkage priors, which forms the foundation of our approach. We then introduce BLAST, a Bayesian transfer learning method that integrates a hierarchical shrinkage model with an efficient MCMC algorithm for inference. We present BLAST in both the oracle setting, where the informative set
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to be known, and the more practical setting, where
\mathcal{A}
must be learned from the data. In each case, the corresponding sampling algorithm is designed to jointly update the source coefficient vector and the contrast vector, enabling coherent inference on the target regression coefficients. Finally, we show an example implementation of the BLAST framework using the horseshoe shrinkage prior.

2.1
Overview of the Bayesian Shrinkage Model

Our work is based on continuous shrinkage priors as a mechanism to introduce approximate sparsity through Gaussian scale mixtures
(
Polson & Scott 2011
,
Johndrow et al. 2020
)
. Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be the design matrix and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the observed outcome vector. Global-local shrinkage in the high-dimensional linear regression setting relies on Normal sampling, s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\bm{\theta},\sigma^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{X}\bm{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}),

(2)

where
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the regression coefficients and
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. A common shrinkage prior is represented as independent scale mixtures of normals, such that:

\theta_{j}\mid\sigma^{2},\nu_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\nu_{j}),\;\;\nu_{j}\sim f,\;\;\sigma\sim g,\;\;(\text{for }j=1,\ldots,p);

(3)

where
\nu_{j}
represents a local scale parameter, that governs the degree of shrinkage applied to
\theta_{j}
, and
f
and
g
are densities with support
(0,\infty)
. Table 1 summarizes several commonly used specifications of
\nu_{j}
giving rise to different shrinkage priors. As shown in the table, different choices of prior distributions for
\nu_{j}
lead to distinct shrinkage behaviors, ranging from global regularization to coefficient-specific shrinkage.

2.2
The
\mathcal{A}
-known Case

We first describe BLAST under the assumption that the informative set
\mathcal{A}
is known in advance. This setting, commonly referred to as the
oracle case
in the transfer learning literature, is admittedly rare in practice but serves as an important benchmark. By establishing performance under oracle knowledge, we can better assess the relative gains and limitations of BLAST when source study selection must instead be learned from the data.

2.2.1
The
\mathcal{A}
-known Model

Consider a target dataset
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\bigl(\mathbf{y}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\bigr)
, where
\mathbf{y}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
is the outcome vector and
\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}\times p}
is the corresponding design matrix.
In addition, we have a collection of
K
informative source datasets indexed by
\mathcal{A}=\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
, where each dataset is given by
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\bigl(\mathbf{y}^{(k)},\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\bigr)
with
\mathbf{y}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}}
and
\mathbf{X}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times p}
for
k=1,\ldots,K
. Further, let

\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}^{(1)}\\
\mathbf{X}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{X}^{(K)}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A})}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}^{(1)}\\
\mathbf{y}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}^{(K)}\end{bmatrix},

be the stacked design matrix and outcome vector of the informative source datasets, respectively. Here,
\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}\times p}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A})}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathcal{A}}}
, where
n_{\mathcal{A}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}n_{k}
.

A Gaussian linear model is used for the likelihood of the data:

\begin{array}[]{lclcl}\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A})}&\mid&\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A})}\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}\mathbf{I}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{(0)}&\mid&\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\;\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\bm{\delta},\;\sigma^{2}_{(0)}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\left(\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}+\bm{\delta}\right),\;\sigma^{2}_{(0)}\mathbf{I}\right),\end{array}

(4)

where we assume that all auxiliary data sets are anchored to the target through the coefficients
{\bm{w}}^{(\mathcal{A})}
, and the target is allowed to deviate from
{\bm{w}}^{(\mathcal{A})}
via a set of sparse contrasts
\bm{\delta}
. In other words, the target regression coefficient takes the form
\bm{\beta}=\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}+\bm{\delta}
where
\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}
and
\bm{\delta}
are aggregate regression parameters representing the source data coefficients and contrasts, respectively.

Shrinkage priors on the source coefficients are a natural choice given that in high-dimensional settings, only a small number of predictors are expected to carry signal. They are also appropriate for the contrast vector, since knowledge of the informative set
\mathcal{A}
implies that only a small number of coefficients differ between the target and source, resulting in an
\ell_{1}
-sparse contrast vector. Specifically, we propose using independent normal scale-mixture shrinkage priors that take the form in (
3
) for both aggregate parameters to effectively capture their underlying sparsity structure:

{\bm{w}}^{(\mathcal{A})}_{j}\mid\sigma^{2}_{{(\mathcal{A})}},\nu_{j}^{\bm{w}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}\nu_{j}^{\bm{w}}\right),\;\;\bm{\delta}_{j}\mid\sigma^{2}_{(0)},\nu_{j}^{\bm{\delta}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\;\sigma^{2}_{(0)}\nu_{j}^{\bm{\delta}}\right).

(5)

For instance, under the horseshoe prior, we assume

\nu_{j}^{\bm{w}}=\lambda_{j}^{2}\tau^{2},\quad\lambda_{j}\sim\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1),\quad\tau\sim\mathrm{C}^{+}(0,1),

and can do so similarly for
\nu_{j}^{\bm{\delta}}
. More considerations about this specific implementation are discussed in Section
2.3.3
. Finally, we specify

\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})},\sigma^{2}_{(0)}\sim\mathrm{IG}\left(a,b\right)
,
(a,b>0)
,
reflecting a weakly-informative, heavy-tailed prior on the residual scale.

2.3
The
\mathcal{A}
-unknown Case

In practice, assuming that the informative set
\mathcal{A}
is known is often unrealistic. Moreover, when some source studies are noninformative, failing to correctly identify
\mathcal{A}
can lead to negative transfer and degraded performance
(
Li et al. 2022
)
. To address this challenge, we extend the
\mathcal{A}
-known model by introducing a
K
-dimensional binary indicator vector
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K})
, where each
\gamma_{k}\in\{0,1\}
denotes whether the
k
-th source study is informative. Rather than fixing
\bm{\gamma}
, we learn it from the data and develop a Gibbs sampling algorithm that jointly estimates
\bm{\gamma}
along with model parameters to improve inference under uncertainty about source informativeness. We henceforth refer to this method as BLAST with source selection, or simply BLAST.

2.3.1
The
\mathcal{A}
-unknown Model

In this general case, we consider a library
\mathcal{S}:=\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
of
K
auxiliary data sets. We aim to identify the informative set
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
from these
K
available sources. We introduce a
K
-dimensional binary indicator vector
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K})\sim f_{\gamma}
, with
f_{\gamma}
denoting a probability mass function supported on
\Gamma:=\{0,1\}^{K}
. In this framework, we assume that any realization of
\bm{\gamma}\in\Gamma
partitions the
K
source datasets into:

Informative Sources

{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}}

=

\{k\in(1,2,\ldots,K):\gamma_{k}=1\}
,

Noninformative Sources

{\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}}}

=

\{k\in(1,2,\ldots,K):\gamma_{k}=0\}.

Henceforth, we denote noninformative data as
\mathcal{D}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}=(\mathbf{X}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})},\mathbf{y}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})})
. Informative sources are expected to contribute positively to inference as they are deemed compatible with a sparse contrast structure, whereas noninformative sources may degrade performance.

To accommodate uncertainty in the structure of the informative source set, we extend the Gaussian linear transfer learning model from (
4
) to incorporate dependence on
\bm{\gamma}
and introduce a separate likelihood component for the noninformative sources. The sampling model in the
\mathcal{A}
-unknown case is specified as:

\begin{array}[]{lclcl}\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}&\mid&\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})},\;\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}\mathbf{I}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{(0)}&\mid&\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\;\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\;\bm{\delta},\;\sigma^{2}_{(0)}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(0)}\left(\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}+\bm{\delta}\right),\;\sigma^{2}_{(0)}\mathbf{I}\right),\\
\mathbf{y}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}&\mid&\mathbf{X}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})},\;\bm{w}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}\bm{w}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}\mathbf{I}\right).\end{array}

(6)

In addition to the priors specified in (
5
), we further place a shrinkage prior on the noninformative source coefficients:

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A})}}_{j}

\displaystyle\mid\sigma^{2}_{{(\bar{\mathcal{A})}}},\bm{\nu}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A})}}\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A})}}\nu_{j}^{\bm{w}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}\right).

Although inference on
\bm{w}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}
is not the primary focus, inference on its variance and shrinkage parameters will inform inference on
\bm{\gamma}
, as we will demonstrate later.

Let
\mathcal{D}
denote the union of target and all auxiliary source data. The posterior distribution of the target parameter
\bm{\beta}
here is naturally represented as

p(\bm{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D})=\sum_{\bm{\gamma}\in\Gamma}p(\bm{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D},\bm{\gamma})p(\bm{\gamma}\mid\mathcal{D}).

Here, inferential uncertainty in the selection of the informative set
\mathcal{A}
is made explicit through model averaging over
\Gamma
. Crucially, the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\gamma}\mid\mathcal{D})
induces a posterior over all combinations of auxiliary studies in
\mathcal{S}
. This allows for formal posterior inference for the informative set
\mathcal{A}
and the estimation of study-specific posterior inclusion probabilities
p(\gamma_{k}=1\mid\mathcal{D})
.

2.3.2
BLAST Algorithm with Source Selection

Algorithm
2
presents our Gibbs sampling procedure for obtaining posterior inference on the target regression coefficients when
\mathcal{A}
is unknown. As with Algorithm
1
, the explicit parameter expressions for the posterior conditional distributions are provided in online Appendix A. Although most of their underlying machinery is similar, there are several key differences to note between Algorithm
2
and Algorithm
1
.

The first distinction is that in Algorithm
2
, regression parameters corresponding to noninformative sources are sampled. Sampling these parameters follows the same normal conjugacy, and adds minimal computational complexity.

The second, most critical difference is that each iteration of the Gibbs sampler in Algorithm
2
includes a “source study selection step” in which posterior inclusion probabilities are computed sequentially for each source study. This yields an updated
\bm{\gamma}
vector at every iteration, dynamically redefining the partition of source studies into informative and noninformative subsets. In the source study selection step, conditional posterior inclusion probabilities are computed to assess the compatibility of each source with the target. These probabilities are derived by evaluating the marginal likelihood, obtained by integrating out
\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}
,
\bm{w}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}
,
\bm{\delta}
, and the corresponding error variance parameters. This integration avoids the need to condition on high-dimensional parameters while accounting for their uncertainty.

\sigma^{2}_{(0)},\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})},\sigma^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}\leftarrow 1
;

\bm{\nu}_{\bm{w}^{\mathcal{A}}},\bm{\nu}_{\bm{w}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}},\bm{\nu}_{\bm{\delta}}\leftarrow\mathbf{1}_{d}
.

for

t=1

to

T

do

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}&\mid&\mathcal{D}_{0},\mathcal{D}_{(\mathcal{A})},\bm{\delta},\bm{\nu}_{\bm{w}^{\mathcal{A}}},\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{\mu}_{w^{\mathcal{A}}},\;\mathbf{\Lambda}_{w^{\mathcal{A}}}^{-1}\big)\\[6.0pt]
\bm{\delta}&\mid&\mathcal{D}_{0},\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\bm{\nu}_{\bm{\delta}},\sigma^{2}_{(0)}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\!\big(\bm{\mu}_{\delta},\;\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\delta}^{-1}\big)\\[6.0pt]

\sigma^{2}_{(0)}&\mid&\mathcal{D}_{0},\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})},\bm{\delta}&\sim&\mathrm{InvGamma}\!\big(a_{0}^{*},\;b_{0}^{*}\big)\\[6.0pt]
\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}&\mid&\mathcal{D}_{(\mathcal{A})},\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}&\sim&\mathrm{InvGamma}\!\big(a_{\mathcal{A}}^{*},\;b_{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\big)\\[6.0pt]

\bm{\nu}_{\bm{w}^{\mathcal{A}}},\bm{\nu}_{\bm{w}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}},\bm{\nu}_{\bm{\delta}}
via Gibbs, MH or AR step ;

Compute
\bm{\beta}=\bm{w}^{(\mathcal{A})}+\bm{\delta}
;

Algorithm 2

Gibbs sampler for BLAST with Source Selection

The conditional posterior inclusion probability for the
k
th source dataset can be written (up to a normalizing constant) as

\displaystyle p(\gamma_{k}=1\mid\bm{\gamma}_{(-k)},\bm{\nu},\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\propto p\!\left(\mathbf{y}^{(0)},\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})},\mathbf{y}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})},\mathbf{X}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})},\bm{\nu}\right)\;f_{\gamma}(\gamma_{k}=1\mid\bm{\gamma}_{(-k)}).

(7)

Here,
\bm{\gamma}_{(-k)}
denotes the vector
\bm{\gamma}
with its
k
th entry removed. The first factor on the right-hand side of (
7
) is the marginal likelihood obtained by integrating out the source coefficients, contrasts, and error variance parameters. The second factor,
f_{\gamma}(\gamma_{k}=1\mid\bm{\gamma}_{(-k)})
, is the prior inclusion probability for the
k
th source study, which reduces to a fixed Bernoulli prior if independence is assumed.

Conveniently, the marginal likelihood has a closed-form expression for shrinkage priors of the form in (
3
) when conditioning on the shrinkage parameters,
\bm{\nu}
. The complementary probability
p(\gamma_{k}=0|\bm{\gamma}_{(-k)},\bm{\nu},\mathcal{D})
is computed analogously by swapping the membership of the
k
’th study from informative to noninformative. The explicit calculation of this marginal likelihood is provided in online Appendix A.

It is worth noting here that when the number of source datasets
K
is large, exploring the combinatorial space of
\bm{\gamma}
becomes challenging, often resulting in poor mixing and sticky behavior of the Markov chain. These issues are further exacerbated in high-dimensional settings, where large
p
can induce extreme differences in log marginal likelihoods, leading to near-deterministic updates of
\bm{\gamma}
. To mitigate these issues, we recommend tempering the posterior distribution by introducing a scaling parameter
\kappa
, which rescales the log-marginal likelihoods to promote smoother transitions and more stable exploration of the inclusion space. While
\kappa
can be fixed to a small value, such as
\kappa=1/p
, one may also adaptively learn it during sampling using a Robbins-Monro stochastic approximation algorithm
(
Robbins & Monro 1951
)
. This approach adjusts
\kappa
iteratively to target a desired acceptance rate for
\bm{\gamma}
updates. Further details on this tempering strategy are provided in online Appendix B.

Finally, Algorithm
2
must accommodate cases when the current state of the inclusion vector in the chain leads to degenerate partitions of the source studies. Specifically, we must address iterations where
\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}}=\emptyset
(
\bm{\gamma}=\mathbf{0}_{K}
) or
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}}=\emptyset
(
\bm{\gamma}=\mathbf{1}_{K}
), as the corresponding data subsets
\mathcal{D}_{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}
and
\mathcal{D}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}
are then empty. This presents a challenge because evaluation of the posterior conditionals depend on these data being non-empty. A naive solution is to temporarily impute
\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}
(or
\mathbf{X}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}
) with zeros. Such zero-imputation is acceptable for marginal likelihood calculations, since empty data subsets contribute neutrally to the likelihood. However, when used within the sampling steps, zero-imputation can introduce artificial variability into the posterior, leading to unstable sampling and unreliable inference. Therefore, while we retain zero-imputation for evaluating marginal likelihoods, we recommend imputing pseudo-data when updating model parameters in the Gibbs sampler. For instance, when
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}=\emptyset
, one may construct pseudo-informative data
\mathcal{D}_{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}^{\text{pseudo}}=(\mathbf{X}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}_{\text{pseudo}},\mathbf{y}^{(\mathcal{A}_{\bm{\gamma}})}_{\text{pseudo}})
by sampling a small subset (e.g., 5%) of the target data. Similarly, for
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\gamma}=\emptyset
, pseudo-noninformative data
\mathcal{D}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\bm{\gamma}})}^{\text{pseudo}}
can be generated by randomly sampling a small subset from the source data and applying a random shuffle to the outcome vector. This strategy maintains well-defined sampling steps throughout the MCMC and allows for more robust and stable posterior inference. Alternatively, one may choose to set particular values for variance or shrinkage parameters based on empty data subsets (see online Appendix C).

2.3.3
BLAST Horseshoe Implementation

As previously noted, BLAST can be implemented with any prior from the class defined in (
3
). A prominent example is the horseshoe (HS) prior
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
)
, in which the global-local shrinkage for each coefficient is determined by
\nu_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{2}\tau^{2}
. The local shrinkage parameters,
\lambda_{j}
, govern the shrinkage of individual coefficients while the global shrinkage parameter,
\tau
, controls overall sparsity across all coefficients. The HS shrinkage prior has demonstrated robust theoretical guarantees in high-dimensional sparse settings
(
van der Pas et al. 2014
,
van der Pas et al. 2017
)
.

For our implementation, HS priors are placed on the source regression coefficients and contrasts, s.t.

\begin{array}[]{lclcl}{\bm{w}}^{(\mathcal{A})}&\mid&\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})},\,\tau_{(\mathcal{A})},\,c,\,\Lambda_{(\mathcal{A})}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}\Lambda_{(\mathcal{A})}\tau^{2}_{(\mathcal{A})}\right),\\
{\bm{w}}^{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}&\mid&\sigma^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})},\,\tau_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})},\,c,\,\Lambda_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\;\sigma^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}\Lambda_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}\tau^{2}_{(\bar{\mathcal{A}})}\right),\\
\bm{\delta}&\mid&\sigma^{2}_{(0)},\,\tau_{(0)},\,c,\,\Lambda_{(0)}&\sim&\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\;\sigma^{2}_{(0)}\Lambda_{(0)}\tau^{2}_{(0)}\right)\end{array}

where each matrix
\Lambda_{(\cdot)}=\mbox{diag}\left({\lambda}^{2}_{1(\cdot)},\cdots,{\lambda}^{2}_{p(\cdot)}\right)
, (
(\cdot)
indexing either
\mathcal{A}
or
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
).
Using this representation of the HS, we further assume:

\begin{array}[]{lclclcl}\lambda_{j(\cdot)}&\sim&\text{C}^{+}(0,1),&&\tau_{(\cdot)}&\sim&\text{C}^{+}(0,\psi^{2}).\\
\end{array}

In default analyses, the scale parameter
\psi
is often fixed at
\psi=1
. Alternatively, this parameter may be estimated through an empirical Bayes Gibbs sampling approach
(
Casella 2001
)
; see online Appendix C for further details on this procedure.
Finally, the residual variance and source indicator priors are defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lclclcl}\sigma^{2}_{(\cdot)}&\sim&\mathrm{IG}(\omega/2,\omega/2),&\bm{\gamma}&\sim&\mathcal{U}\{0,1\}^{K}.\\
\end{array}

For the horseshoe, there are many algorithms for efficient sampling of the regression parameters and their respective shrinkage parameters.
Makalic & Schmidt 2016
presented a simple horseshoe sampler that has Gibbs updates for all parameters.
Johndrow et al. 2020
introduce a more scalable sampling algorithm which is shown to have great performance in higher dimensional settings. We provide concrete details of the
Johndrow et al. 2020
sampling algorithm in online Appendix B.

3
Simulation Studies

In this section, we conduct a series of simulations to evaluate the empirical performance of BLAST and benchmark it against existing approaches in the high-dimensional linear regression transfer learning literature. Specifically, we compare the naive Lasso (which uses only the target data), the Oracle Trans-Lasso, Trans-Lasso,
\mathcal{A}_{h}
-Trans-GLM, Trans-GLM, Oracle BLAST (Algorithm
1
), and BLAST (Algorithm
2
). In our simulations, Oracle BLAST and BLAST consistently outperform methods relying solely on the target data, while matching and often surpassing the performance of Trans-Lasso and Trans-GLM.

The metrics used to assess performance included (1) Sum of Squared Estimation Errors (SSE): defined as SSE
=\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{j}-\bm{\beta}_{j})^{2}
where
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{j}
is the estimate of
\bm{\beta}_{j}
; (2) Mean Squared Prediction error (MSPE): defined as
\text{MSPE}=1/p\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}
where
\hat{y}
is the predicted value of
y_{i}
on a cross-validation holdout set; (3) Average width: the width of 95% credible/confidence interval averaged over all simulations; (4) Coverage: the proportion of the 95% credible/confidence intervals that correctly captured the true value in the simulations. Metrics (1) and (2) assess the estimation and prediction accuracy of each method, while metrics (3) and (4) evaluate the quality of uncertainty quantification provided by the corresponding intervals. We note that metrics (3) and (4) are only comparable between methods that provide interval estimates—namely,
\mathcal{A}_{h}
-Trans-GLM and our Bayesian methods. For completeness, we also include the target-only desparsified Lasso
(
van de Geer et al. 2014
)
as an additional point of comparison for interval estimation.

All experiments are done in R. The Oracle Trans-Lasso and Trans-Lasso functions are obtained from Sai Li’s public GitHub repository (
https://github.com/saili0103/TransLasso
), and the
\mathcal{A}
-Trans-GLM and Trans-GLM methods are available in the
glmtrans
package
(
Tian & Feng 2025
)
.

Simulation Setup

Our simulation setup is as follows. We set
p=200
, with
n_{0}=150
target samples and
n_{k}=150
auxiliary samples for each
k=1,\dots,K
with

. The covariates

For the target parameter, we define:

We construct the source regression coefficients by introducing a structured bias to random components of the target parameter. Specifically, for a given

we define:

\bm{w}_{j}^{(k)}=\bm{\beta}-0.3*\mathds{1}(j\in H_{k}),\quad\text{if }k\in\mathcal{A},

where

is a random subset of
[p]
with
|H_{k}|=h

Similarly, for

\bm{w}_{j}^{(k)}=\bm{\beta}-\mathds{1}(j\in H_{k}),\quad\text{if }k\notin\mathcal{A},

Prediction of Tumor Mutational Burden

4.1
Background and Motivation and Data

Tumor Mutational Burden (TMB) represents the total number of somatic coding mutations in a tumor and has emerged as a promising biomarker for predicting immunotherapy response in cancer patients. Clinical studies have shown that high TMB is associated with better responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors and greater survival benefits in certain cancers (e.g., lung cancer and melanoma)
(
Li et al. 2019
)
. While Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) provides a comprehensive and accurate measurement of TMB, it is often time-consuming and costly. As a result, several studies have explored whether sequencing specific gene panels through targeted enrichment can serve as a more practical and clinically predictive alternative
(
Fancello et al. 2019
,
Wu et al. 2019
)
.

Figure 5
:
(Top panel) Cross-validated relative prediction error for TMB predicted using 303 genes from the FoundationOne Gene Panel. Results are shown
for various cancer targets (LUAD, KIRC, LUSC) and TL methods. (Bottom panel) Heatmap of posterior inclusion probabilities from BLAST selection for different target cancers.

Although clinically attractive, panel-based estimates can be noisy or biased, especially since sample sizes are limited in cancer studies. In this context, transfer learning offers a powerful framework for improving TMB prediction by borrowing strength from similar cancer datasets with molecular data. By leveraging molecular profiles from multiple source studies, we can potentially enhance the predictive accuracy of a target cancer model, particularly when the sample size of the target is small or the signal is weak. Our goal is to demonstrate that incorporating information across cancers through Bayesian transfer learning leads to improved prediction of TMB.

We set to evaluate the performance of our proposed method
using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)
(
The Cancer Genome Atlas Research Network 2013
)
. Specifically, we use the FoundationOne mRNA expression panel in a pan-cancer setting to accurately predict TMB in a target cancer. The FoundationOne gene panel has demonstrated generally reliable TMB estimation with accuracy greater than 90% in some cancers
(
Wu et al. 2019
)
. In this analysis, we consider 16 different cancers with extensive representation in peer-reviewed TCGA-based studies, though not necessarily in the context of TMB estimation. These include—but are not limited to Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma, Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma, Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma, Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma, and others. Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD), Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma (LUSC), and Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC) were chosen as target cancers due to their well-documented response to immune checkpoint inhibitors
(
Antonia et al. 2017
,
Borghaei et al. 2015
,
Motzer et al. 2018
)
. The 15 TCGA cancer studies excluding the chosen target are used as source data.

All clinical and gene expression data were extracted using the
TCGAretriever
package in R
(
Fantini 2024
)
. To ensure consistency across datasets, we refined the gene panel from 324 to 303 genes by excluding those whose expression profiles were absent in at least one of the cancer studies of interest or whose expression levels were zero in more than 80% of samples.

5
Summary and Discussion

In this paper, we introduced BLAST, a novel Bayesian method for multi-source transfer learning in high-dimensional linear regression. Through the use of shrinkage priors, BLAST can robustly and adaptively learn the underlying sparsity structure and infer the regression coefficients for a target dataset via MCMC effectively by leveraging information from available source data. To avoid negative transfer in cases where the informative set of source studies is unknown, BLAST incorporated a source study selection mechanism. This mechanism distinguished informative from noninformative source datasets in a data-driven manner using posterior inclusion probabilities based on marginal likelihood evaluations.
Lastly, our empirical results for both simulated and real-world genomic datasets demonstrated that BLAST consistently outperforms regularization methods that use the target data alone and achieves comparable performance to existing transfer learning approaches. The
BLASTreg
R package implementing our methods, along with scripts to reproduce the TCGA analysis, is available at
https://github.com/TelescaLab/BLASTreg
.

Future directions include extending BLAST to non-Gaussian outcomes and incorporating non-linear effects via Bayesian Gaussian processes or neural networks. Furthermore, in this paper, we assumed that the source data were homogeneous, which may introduce bias in our posterior inference when heterogeneity across studies is present. In practice, cross-study heterogeneity may arise from covariate shifts, batch effects, different measurement platforms, or differences in study design. Developing BLAST variants that explicitly model this heterogeneity is a natural next step to improve robustness and generalizability.

Finally, our work did not dwell on the large sample properties of the proposed procedure. Some encouraging literature on large sample behavior of Bayesian horseshoe estimators has been reported in
van de Geer et al. 2014
,
van der Pas et al. 2017
, together with some recent interesting results related to transfer learning reported in
Lai et al. 2024
. Although empirical results in our simulations suggest well-behaved operating characteristics for both predictive accuracy and uncertainty quantification, we maintain that more work is needed to establish theoretical assurances for the proposed modeling framework.
