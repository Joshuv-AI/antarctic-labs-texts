Emerging strategies and innovations in circular RNA synthesis
Authors: Nitin D. Bansode, Kirankumar M. Phutane, Prashant R. Latthe, Obul Reddy Bandapalli, Ganesh Babu Karunakaran, Subhendu K. Mohanty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circular RNAs (circRNAs) have emerged as a promising class of therapeutic molecules distinguished by their covalently closed topology, which provides exceptional exonuclease resistance, prolonged intracellular stability, and sustained protein expression. These properties, combined with reduced innate immunogenicity and capacity for cap-independent translation, position circRNAs as attractive candidates for vaccines, oncology therapeutics, and treatments for genetic and neurological disorders. Parallel advances in chemical biology have significantly expanded the synthetic toolkit for generating circRNAs, complementing traditional enzymatic ligation and ribozyme-based approaches with efficient chemical, chemoenzymatic, and bioorthogonal circularization strategies that enable scalable production and incorporation of diverse chemical modifications. This review integrates the biological roles of circRNAs with a comparative analysis of emerging synthetic methods, highlighting their mechanistic principles, advantages, and limitations. Advances in delivery platforms and multi-omics validation further strengthen the translational potential of engineered circRNAs as next-generation RNA therapeutics.

Introduction

Messenger RNA (mRNA) therapeutics have rapidly risen to prominence owing to their ability to produce fast, potent, and transient protein expression, enabling effective interventions against infectious diseases, cancer, and other conditions. The remarkable success of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic further highlighted the platform’s speed, scalability, and strong safety profile, establishing mRNA as a powerful and adaptable tool in modern medicine.1,2,3 However, despite this progress, the development of mRNA therapeutics has progressed slowly due to several significant challenges that continue to constrain the broader therapeutic potential of mRNA technologies. These include the intrinsic instability and immunogenicity of mRNA molecules, the difficulty in achieving precise control over protein expression levels, and the persistent limitations of current delivery systems—particularly, their inefficiency in targeting extrahepatic tissues.3,4 To ensure sufficient biological activity of therapeutic mRNA, it is necessary to protect it from premature cleavage by numerous RNA-targeting enzymes, particularly exonucleases. Consequently, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have gained attention as candidates for next-generation mRNA therapeutics due to their exceptional stability and exhibited significantly longer protein expression profiles. Their covalently closed structure confers resistance to exonucleases, making circRNAs significantly more stable than linear mRNAs and positioning them as promising alternatives for therapeutic applications.4,5,6,7,8,9

Over the past decade, research into circRNAs has expanded rapidly, revealing their diverse biological functions. circRNAs act as microRNA (miRNA) sponges (e.g., ciRS-7/CDR1as), protein scaffolds that stabilize RNA-binding protein (RBP) complexes, translation templates via internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) or m6A-mediated initiation, and nuclear regulators of parental gene transcription. These multifunctional roles highlight circRNAs as both coding and noncoding regulators of cellular processes. In parallel, engineered circRNAs are emerging as promising RNA-based vaccine platforms, cancer therapeutics, and modulators of neurological pathways. Their durability, reduced immunogenicity, and versatility further strengthen their potential as next-generation RNA medicines.5,7,8,10

Beyond biological applications, synthetic circRNAs are essential for studying circRNA structure and function, and their therapeutic promise has created a growing need to clarify and optimize circRNA synthesis methodologies.11,12 Mechanistically, in vitro circRNA production can be categorized into three major classes: enzyme-catalyzed end-joining, ribozyme-driven self-circularization, and purely chemical coupling.8,12 Each approach is defined by its specific terminal end-requirements, scalability, and characteristic impurity profile, which may include concatemers, nicked circles, residual linear RNA, double-stranded RNA species, or splint-derived fragments.5

To address the specific limitations of prior literature, this review aims to bridge the gap between recent chemical innovations and their biological applications. We provide a comprehensive overview of cutting-edge synthetic strategies for circRNA construction, focusing on how these emerging approaches overcome current manufacturing bottlenecks. Furthermore, we integrate advances in circRNA chemistry with current knowledge of circRNA biology, therapeutic potential, delivery systems, and omics-based validation approaches. By doing so, we illustrate how engineered circRNAs are emerging as a transformative platform for next-generation RNA therapeutics, vaccine development, and precision medicine applications.

History of the circRNA

circRNAs were first recognized in 1976, when plant viroids were shown to be single-stranded covalently closed circRNAs. Their presence in mammals resurfaced in 1991, when “scrambled exon” transcripts from the DCC gene suggested back-splicing events capable of generating circular molecules.13,14 The field remained quiet until RNA-sequencing revealed in 2012 that circRNAs are abundant and conserved in human cells, overturning the assumption that they were rare splicing artifacts.15 In 2013, functional breakthroughs showed that circRNAs such as ciRS-7/CDR1as act as potent miRNA sponges, establishing them as regulatory molecules rather than transcriptional noise. Since then, circRNAs have been recognized as stable, diverse, and sometimes protein-coding RNAs with important roles in gene regulation, development, and disease.16,17,18

Circularity is a defining molecular feature that fundamentally influences the design and function of circRNAs. Their covalently closed structure—lacking both 5′ and 3′ ends—provides exceptional resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation, giving circRNAs significantly greater stability than their linear counterparts. This closed-loop topology also reshapes intramolecular base-pairing and imposes unique secondary and tertiary conformations that can modulate turnover, binding interactions, and cellular function. Importantly, circularity enables efficient cap-independent translation through mechanisms such as internal ribosome entry sites and m6A-mediated initiation, allowing circRNAs to support sustained protein production despite lacking a canonical 5′ cap. Engineering studies show that optimizing circularization efficiency directly enhances the stability and translational output of synthetic circRNAs, underscoring circularity as a central design principle for next-generation RNA therapeutics and vaccines.19,20

Biogenesis of circRNA

In cells, circRNA is produced through a circularization process involving a canonical spliceosome-mediated precursor mRNA (premRNA) known as back-splicing. Typically, RNA splicing operates much like film editing, with non-coding segments excised and the remaining coding portions joined together. But in some instances, the RNA takes an unexpected turn, folding back on itself, pinching off, and forming a standalone loop.21,22 There are three models of circRNA loop formation based on circRNA compositions: intron pairing-driven, RBP-driven, and lariat-driven circularization (Figure 1). According to their genomic origin and cycling mechanism, circRNAs are divided into exonic circRNAs (ecircRNAs), circular intronic RNAs (ciRNAs), and exon-intron circRNAs (EIciRNAs). The ecircRNAs are mainly derived from single or multiple exons of pre-mRNA. The ciRNAs are derived from introns by spliceosome-mediated splicing. EIciRNAs consist of both exonic and intronic sequences. RBPs play roles in circRNA biogenesis, while adenosine deaminase acting on RNA (ADARs) participates in the formation of circRNAs through A-to-I substitution.23,24,25Figure 1Different types of circRNA concerning the biogenesis model circRNA23

Back-splicing requires an intricate dance between various proteins, all of which are naturally present inside cells but not readily available at the laboratory bench.26 So, in the early 1990s, researchers came up with two possible workarounds for creating synthetic circRNAs.27 One uses DNA bridges to hold the ends of an RNA strand together while another enzyme seals it, a process known as ligation.28,29 The other harnesses the enzymatic properties of specialized RNA sequences themselves. When two such sequences pair in a hairpin formation, they can initiate cross-joining reactions, forming a ring (see “RNA round-up”).30,31

ecircRNAs

ecircRNAs are the most common type of circRNA molecules identified in eukaryotic cells.32,33 They form when the cell joins exons through a non-canonical splicing event known as back-splicing (Figures 1A and 1B). In this process, a downstream splice donor site attaches back to an upstream splice acceptor site. This generates a covalently closed circRNA molecule that lacks free ends.32,34,35,36 Because of this unique structure, ecircRNAs can resist exonuclease-mediated degradation, resulting in enhanced stability compared with linear RNAs. They are highly stable, conserved across species, and predominantly localized in the cytoplasm. Once there, they regulate genes by acting as miRNA sponges, interacting with RBPs, or even serving as templates for the translation of functional peptides.37,38,39,40

EIciRNAs

EIciRNAs are unique circular molecules that retain both exons and introns. They are generated during back-splicing when specific introns fail to be removed between the joined exons (Figure 1C). Unlike canonical ecircRNAs, EIciRNAs are predominantly retained within the nucleus.41,42,43 Once there, they play a major role in binding to the spliceosome component U1 snRNPs. Together, this complex interacts with RNA polymerase II at the gene’s promoter region. This action enhances the transcription of their parent genes.44,45,46

ciRNAs

ciRNAs are circRNA molecules composed exclusively of intron sequences. They form from the leftover loop pieces, called lariats, generated during pre-mRNA splicing (Figure 1D). To make a ciRNA, the lariat must escape the cell’s normal breakdown process and avoid debranching-mediated degradation.47,48,49,50 This escape depends on consensus sequence motifs located near the 5′ splice site and branchpoint that promote lariat stabilization and circularization. Like EIciRNAs, cIRNAs are predominantly retained within the nucleus. They interact with the transcriptional machinery, including RNA polymerase II. By doing this, they function as positive regulators of the transcription of their parental genes.51,52

Role of the different factors in the RNA biogenesis

circRNA biogenesis is a tightly regulated process governed by the interplay of complementary intronic elements, RBPs, and RNA-editing enzymes, including ADARs, which together influence the efficiency and specificity of back splicing.

circRNA biogenesis: Role of the complementary intronic sequences

ciRNAs are generated through a distinct mechanism that relies heavily on specific cis-acting sequence motifs and intronic regulatory elements, rather than on exon circularization events.38 During pre-mRNA splicing, ciRNAs are formed from intron lariats that escape debranching and degradation due to the presence of conserved sequence features-typically a GU-rich element near the 5′ splice site and a C-rich element near the branchpoint.52 These motifs stabilize the lariat structure and prevent debranching enzymes from processing and linearizing the intron, allowing it to persist as a circRNA.53,54 In addition, complementary intronic sequences can facilitate intronic interactions that enhance lariat stability thereby protecting it from degradation pathways.53 Unlike exon-derived circRNAs, ciRNA formation does not require back-splicing but instead depends on the retention and stabilization of intron lariats following splicing, enabling the intron to adopt a circular conformation.53,55

These ciRNAs are predominantly localized in the nucleus,39 where they have been implicated in regulating the transcriptional activity of their parental genes,44,52 thereby highlighting the functional importance of intronic sequence architecture in circRNA biogenesis and gene regulation.

ciRNA biogenesis: Role of the RBPs

RBPs such as Quaking (QKI), Fused in Sarcoma (FUS), and Muscleblind (MBL/MBNL1) play central roles in regulating circRNA biogenesis and represent a key milestone in establishing that circRNA formation is highly regulated process rather than merely a byproduct of canonical splicing events.56,57,58 These RBPs facilitate back-splicing by binding to sequence motifs within introns flanking circularized exons and promoting their physical juxtaposition, thereby enhancing circularization efficiency.58 QKI enhances circularization through a dimerization-dependent intron-bridging mechanism, binding to QKI response elements (QREs) and looping intervening exons into a configuration favorable for back-splicing, thereby increasing circularization efficiency in a cell type- and context-dependent manner.56 In contrast, FUS acts primarily as a splicing regulator, modulating splice-site selection and local RNA structure to favor non-canonical back-splicing. FUS may also recruit or stabilize spliceosomal components, thereby enhancing circRNA production in a transcript- and cell-specific manner.57 MBL provides a classic example of autoregulated circRNA formation, in which it binds to conserved intronic sites within its own pre-mRNA, promotes circMBL production, and simultaneously reduces linear mRNA output, establishing a feedback loop that links protein abundance to circRNA generation.59 Collectively, these RBP-mediated mechanisms demonstrate that circRNA biogenesis is tightly integrated into post-transcriptional gene regulation networks and dynamically controlled by cellular and developmental contexts.60

ciRNA biogenesis: Role of the RNA editing enzymes

ciRNAs are generated from intron lariat intermediates that escape debranching-mediated processing during pre-mRNA splicing, giving rise to stable, covalently closed circular molecules that predominantly localize in the nucleus and regulate the transcription of their parental genes.33 Their biogenesis critically depends on conserved intronic sequence features, including a GU-rich element near the 5′ splice site and a C-rich region close to the branchpoint, which together promote lariat stabilization and resistance to debranching by DBR1.53,61 RNA editing enzymes, particularly ADAR1 and ADAR2, play a multifaceted regulatory role in this process.62 ADARs catalyze adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) conversion within double-stranded RNA regions formed by intronic complementary sequences, leading to local destabilization of RNA duplexes and alteration of RNA secondary structures.63,64 This editing can disrupt or remodel intron-pairing interactions that are important for maintaining lariat integrity, thereby influencing whether introns are efficiently debranched and degraded or alternatively stabilized for ciRNA formation.65 In addition, ADAR-mediated editing near splice sites or splicing regulatory elements can modulate spliceosome assembly and splice-site selection, indirectly affecting intron retention and lariat formation.66,67 ADAR1, in particular, has been implicated in preventing excessive circRNA accumulation by unwinding double-stranded RNA structures, whereas reduced ADAR activity can favor circularization.68,69 Beyond its catalytic activity, ADAR proteins can exert editing-independent regulatory functions through RNA binding, influencing ribonucleoprotein complex formation and determining the fate of intronic RNAs.70 Collectively, these mechanisms position ADAR enzymes as key modulators of ciRNA biogenesis, acting through both structural remodeling of intronic RNA and regulation of splicing dynamics to fine-tune ciRNA production and downstream gene expression programs.71

circRNA degradation or turnover

Although circRNAs are structurally stable due to their covalently closed configuration, their abundance is actively regulated by multiple degradation and turnover mechanisms.72 One important pathway involves RNase L, an innate immune endoribonuclease activated through the 2′–5′ oligoadenylate (2-5A) pathway in response to double-stranded RNA.73 Once activated, RNase L cleaves RNA molecules at internal sites without requiring free ends, thereby enabling the degradation of otherwise exonuclease-resistant circRNAs, particularly during antiviral responses or cellular stress conditions.73,74 In contrast, circRNA turnover can also occur through highly sequence-specific AGO2-mediated slicing, a process guided by miRNAs.75 When a miRNA exhibits near-perfect complementarity to a circRNA target, it recruits the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), within which Argonaute-2 (AGO2) catalyzes endonucleolytic cleavage. The catalytic PIWI domain of AGO2 introduces a precise cut within the circRNA, typically between nucleotides paired with positions 10 and 11 of the miRNA guide strand.76,77 This internal cleavage linearizes the circRNA, rendering the resulting fragments susceptible to exonuclease-mediated degradation.72 Notably, this mechanism is distinct from miRNA sponging, which occurs under conditions of partial complementarity and does not lead to RNA degradation, thereby linking circRNA stability directly to cellular miRNA expression profiles.78

Beyond intracellular degradation pathways, circRNA levels are further regulated through extracellular vesicle (EV)-mediated export. In this process, circRNAs are selectively packaged into vesicles such as exosomes within multivesicular bodies, a process frequently facilitated by RBPs that recognize specific sequence motifs or structural features. These vesicles are subsequently released into the extracellular environment, facilitating both intercellular communication and the effective clearance of circRNAs from the originating cell. EV-mediated export is dynamically regulated and can be enhanced under conditions of cellular stress or immune activation, thereby reducing intracellular circRNA abundance while influencing systemic RNA distribution and signaling.79,80

Collectively, these mechanisms highlight that circRNA stability is not merely a consequence of its circular structure but rather a tightly regulated and context-dependent process involving immune-associated degradation pathways, miRNA-guided endonucleolytic cleavage, and extracellular vesicle-mediated clearance.72

The therapeutic potential of circRNAs

Circularization represents a unifying design principle across synthetic macrocycles and circRNAs, as both rely on covalently closed architectures to enhance stability, reduce degradation, and enable emergent functions not accessible to their linear counterparts. In chemical macrocyclization, ring closure rigidifies molecular conformation and typically improves proteolytic and metabolic stability, paralleling how the covalently closed loop of circRNAs confers exceptional resistance to exonucleases and markedly prolongs intracellular persistence. Recent reviews highlight that circRNAs closed topology not only stabilizes the transcript but also drives unique structural conformations that alter turnover, regulatory interactions, and translational potential, thereby enabling therapeutic applications such as vaccines and long-duration protein expression. Moreover, advances in engineered circRNA systems demonstrate that circularization can preorganize functional elements to improve expression efficiency and modulate biological activity—an effect conceptually analogous to the preorganized binding topologies and functional specificity observed in macrocyclic chemotypes.5,9,81

circRNAs exhibit substantially greater intracellular stability than linear RNAs owing to their covalently closed structure, a property demonstrated for both endogenous and exogenously delivered molecules. Notably, circRNA half-lives vary widely, with reported values ranging from ∼20 h to over 50 h, consistently exceeding those of corresponding mRNAs.5,11,82,83,84,85 For in vitro transcription (IVT) constructs, circRNAs display half-lives of 56–86 h, compared with 15–24 h for IVT mRNAs, contributing to prolonged protein expression and 3- to 5-fold higher yields.20,86,87 A deeper understanding of circRNA decay, its inherent robustness, gives circRNAs a substantially longer half-life compared with linear RNAs, making them highly attractive candidates for applications requiring sustained therapeutic activity.32,33,85,88 Endogenous circRNAs participate in diverse cellular functions, including encoding proteins, modulating protein and miRNA activity, scaffolding molecular complexes, and altering RNA structures (Figure 2).87 Their therapeutic appeal continues to grow, driven by advantages over mRNA platforms across both (IVT) and vector-based formats. Despite the significant clinical promise of synthetic circRNAs as durable expression platforms, advancing these molecules into therapeutic applications requires a careful evaluation of their potential adverse physiological consequences. A critical concern associated with excessive intracellular accumulation of circRNAs is the potential induction of genomic instability, alterations in chromatin architecture, and transcriptional perturbations through the formation of aberrant nucleic acid structures. Specifically, hyper-accumulation of both endogenous and exogenous circRNAs can trigger the formation of stable circular RNA-DNA hybrids, known as circR-loops (or ciR-loops), which have been proposed to exhibit increased stability and persistence relative to their cognate linear counterparts.92,93,94,95 Beyond these hybrids, the unique spatial constraints and sequence characteristics of accumulating circRNAs can promote the formation and stabilization of complex secondary and tertiary non-canonical structures, including intramolecular G-quadruplexes (G4-RNA) and stem-loop hairpins. These structures may act as physical barriers to transcriptional elongation, potentially resulting in transcriptional pausing and collisions between the transcription and replication machineries.96,97,98

Consequently, these structural aberrations have been associated with several downstream cellular consequences, including antisense transcription, premature transcriptional termination, and the accumulation of double-strand DNA breaks at susceptible genomic loci. Furthermore, overabundant circRNAs may promote the assembly of aberrant ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes or condensates, which can sequester essential splicing factors and RBPs away from their endogenous targets. Within this conceptual framework, the prolonged half-life of engineered circRNAs ordinarily regarded as a therapeutic advantage—may inadvertently exacerbate these genotoxic and proteotoxic risks if cellular RNA surveillance and turnover mechanisms become overwhelmed. Therefore, the future clinical translation of circRNA-based therapeutics will require a careful balance between sustained expression and safety, supported by optimized dosing regimens and rational design strategies aimed at minimizing potential adverse effects.99,100,101

While linear mRNA technologies remain the clinical benchmark for transient gene expression due to their established manufacturing infrastructure, emerging platforms are positioning circRNA as a long-acting alternative.102,103 Recent studies further strengthen the translational relevance of circRNA therapeutics by providing concrete in vivo evidence across multiple disease areas.102,104 For instance, a 2024 study demonstrated that lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-delivered circRNA encoding nerve growth factor (circNGF) enabled sustained protein expression and conferred significant neuroprotection in a mouse model of optic nerve injury, outperforming recombinant protein therapy and highlighting the durability advantage of circRNA constructs.105 In cancer immunotherapy, emerging work has shown that circRNA can be used to encode chimeric antigen receptors (CARs), where LNP-delivered circRNA generated functional immune cells capable of robust tumor suppression and modulation of the tumor microenvironment in mice.106 Complementary studies also indicate that circRNA-based cancer vaccines can induce potent and durable antigen-specific T cell responses, particularly when combined with immunotherapeutic strategies.107,108 Furthermore, advances in circRNA vaccine development suggest improved and sustained antigen expression compared with traditional mRNA approaches, along with the capacity to elicit strong humoral and cellular immune responses.102,109 Collectively, these recent reports provide concrete examples of circRNA applications progressing from proof-of-concept toward more advanced preclinical validation, while still underscoring that most approaches remain at the experimental stage and require further optimization and clinical evaluation.103,110

Enzyme-based methods for synthesis of circRNA

Enzyme-based ligation methods are widely employed for circRNA preparation. Typically, ATP-dependent ligases such as T4 DNA ligase, T4 RNA ligase 1, and T4 RNA ligase 2 catalyze the joining of RNA termini bearing a 5′ phosphate and a 3′ hydroxyl through a series of three nucleotidyl-transfer steps. Chemical-based methods for synthesis of circRNA

The optimal ring size of the cirRNA is generally dictated by the functional interaction domain required for the target. circRNA ring size plays a critical role in determining both circularization efficiency and the choice of synthetic strategy, as structural constraints influence how readily RNA ends can align for ligation or self-splicing. Smaller RNAs often exhibit poor circularization due to backbone rigidity, whereas larger constructs may form competing secondary structures. Although numerous chemical and enzymatic protocols are available, most are optimally suited for small to medium-sized RNAs, which can be chemically synthesized to provide highly homogeneous 5′- and 3′-terminal groups, thereby facilitating efficient circularization.

In vitro circRNA can be synthesized from linear RNA precursors through enzymatic ligation, chemical coupling, or ribozyme-mediated self-circularization.113,114 Conventional enzymatic approaches-including T4 RNA ligase-mediated ligation, ribozyme-driven circularization, and pre-mRNA back-splicing-remain widely used but are hindered by significant drawbacks. In contrast, emerging chemical circularization strategies provide a compelling alternative.63,64 Recent advances now enable efficient chemical synthesis of long RNA constructs ranging from 35 to 4,000 nucleotides, effectively overcoming earlier limitations that restricted chemical ligation to short oligoribonucleotides.114,115,116 Enzymatic strategies typically require 5′-phosphate and 3′-hydroxyl termini to support efficient end joining, whereas chemical ligation tolerates a broader range of terminal functionalities and can yield circles containing either natural 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkages or non-natural junctions. Importantly, a single modified linkage embedded within an otherwise native phosphodiester backbone is generally well-tolerated and rarely perturbs RNA structure or function.113,117

Circularization by an intramolecular reaction

Dolinnaya and colleagues demonstrated that, following chemical synthesis of a linear RNA precursor, an intramolecular reaction between the terminal nucleotides can drive its circularization. In purely chemical approaches, condensation reagents such as cyanogen bromide (BrCN) or 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) are commonly used to activate this circularization process (Figure 4). Additional chemical strategies involve replacing the native terminal phosphate and hydroxyl groups with alternative reactive functionalities. When exposed to suitable activating reagents, these modified groups can undergo bond formation with greater efficiency and specificity, resulting in higher yields and improved purity of the circRNA product.118Figure 4Chemical ligation of the distal ends of a linear RNA precursor can be achieved using BrCN or EDC

However, a key limitation is that the resulting circularizing linkage is not a natural phosphodiester bond and therefore does not replicate the back-splice junction found in vivo. Such non-natural linkages may influence the secondary structure or functional properties of the synthesized circRNA, necessitating careful evaluation of their suitability for experimental applications.119,120 Regardless of the chemical approach employed, intramolecular circularization inherently competes with intermolecular reactions that generate linear concatenated products.120,121 Therefore, additional strategies are needed to preferentially drive the formation of circRNA.121,122

Reductive amination-based circularization

Abe and colleagues successfully achieved the one-pot chemical synthesis of 126-mer circRNAs using an EDC/HOBt-based chemical ligation reaction applied to RNA fragments, demonstrating that long circRNAs can be generated purely through chemical means without enzymatic assistance (Figure 5).91 The resulting circRNAs contained unnatural phosphoramidite (P-N) linkages, yet remarkably, they were shown to function as templates for rolling circle translation, producing long, repeating polypeptides once ribosome-initiated translation.90,91Figure 5Two-step chemoenzymatic synthesis or one-pot chemical synthesis of circRNA91

This work represents the first report demonstrating that chemically synthesized RNA molecules, rather than enzymatically or ribozyme-generated circRNAs, can support rolling circle translation and generating extended, repetitive peptide products—significantly expanding the synthetic and functional scope of chemically engineered RNA systems.90,91

The application of EDC/HOBt chemistry offers an effective non-enzymatic strategy for generating synthetic circRNAs, locking the transcript ends into a non-native phosphoramidate framework.91 Deciphering the biological consequences of this abiotic junction is pivotal for transcript engineering. From a structural standpoint, the closed-loop format inherently shields the RNA from exonucleolytic degradation. However, the localized nitrogen substitution adds an extra layer of defense by protecting against site-specific endonuclease cleavage inside the ring.90,123 Beyond stability, subtle variations in surface charge and lipophilic density redirect interactions with endogenous trafficking machinery. Crucially, the biological compatibility of this modified junction is demonstrated by its performance during protein synthesis. Even without a native phosphodiester backbone, circRNAs containing phosphoramidate connections fully accommodate continuous ribosome elongation. The translation machinery effortlessly bypasses these synthetic junctions during rolling-circle translation, maintaining high-fidelity protein expression without inducing premature truncation or ribosomal dissociation.90,91,124,125

Periodate oxidation and reductive amination

Jemielity and colleagues recently demonstrated that RNA generated through standard in vitro transcription can be circularized when transcription is performed in the presence of primers bearing an ethylenediamine motif (Figure 6).119Figure 6Circularization by PORA

Circularization by PORA

Following transcription, the resulting RNA undergoes periodate oxidation and reductive amination (PORA), a one-pot, two-step chemical transformation that uses simple reagents, requires less than 2 h, and achieves conversion efficiencies between 35% and 63%.119 Notably, this chemically driven circularization strategy overcomes limitations of earlier methods by enabling the incorporation of previously inaccessible chemical modifications into circRNAs. This includes the successful installation of an endocyclic cap structure capable of supporting cap-dependent translation, as well as the incorporation of N1-methylpseudouridine (m¹ψ), thereby broadening the functional and therapeutic potential of chemically synthesized circRNAs.119,120

Implementing a one-pot periodate oxidation and reductive amination (PORA) protocol introduces a distinct, synthetic morpholine-derived inter-nucleotide linkage at the circularization boundary. This strategy relies on an IVT precursor equipped with a 5′-ethylenediamine (EDA) anchor, which directly pairs with a periodate-activated 3′-terminus. Architecturally, while loop formation completely protects the framework against exonucleolytic breakdown, the non-natural morpholine structure renders the ligation junction structurally unrecognizable to cellular endonucleases, conferring superior serum longevity. Regarding spatial dynamics, this neutral bridge introduces minimal changes to local charge density and hydrophobicity, ensuring excellent biocompatibility and clean cellular tolerance without the toxicity often associated with metal catalysts. However, because it adapts cleanly to long mRNA transcripts—incorporating functional features like endocyclic caps and modified bases—its impact on translation fidelity is remarkably positive. Unlike other sterically disruptive chemical joins, ribosomes successfully readthrough this morpholine junction. This allows translationally active, protein-coding circular constructs to maintain steady elongation without prompting ribosome disassembly or translation arrest.119,126

Methodological framework for circRNA synthesis selection

The existing literature describes diverse chemical and enzymatic methodologies for generating exogenous circRNA in vitro, typically involving the generation of a linear RNA precursor followed by end-to-end ligation. Because these methodologies feature distinct structural limits, sequence fidelity constraints, and purification profiles, published experimental designs generally select a generation pathway based on the target length and structural properties required for the construct.

Chemical synthesis and ligation (<80 nt)

Solid-phase phosphoramidite chemistry combined with chemical ligation (e.g., copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition or reductive amination) provides an effective route for generating short circRNAs, such as miRNA sponges and small aptamers of length <80 nt. These fully synthetic strategies enable precise, site-specific incorporation of non-natural and modified nucleosides. However, because phosphoramidite coupling efficiency scales exponentially with sequence length, raw product yields drop precipitously as the target chain approaches or exceeds 100 nucleotides (nt), restricting purely chemical synthesis to shorter oligomers.127,128

Enzymatic ligation (80–500 nt)

For short-to-intermediate transcripts from 80 to 500 nt, a hybrid platform integrates IVT with downstream enzymatic ligation via T4 RNA ligases. To mitigate the low yields, template dependency, and intermolecular concatemerization typical of splinted ligations, contemporary protocols utilize splint-free methods. These rely on RNA secondary-structure-guided folding to bring the 5′ and 3′ termini into proximity for seamless circularization. This approach is optimal when downstream applications demand a precise, “scarless” junction that preserves native structural and functional topology.129,130,131

Ribozyme-based self-splicing (>500 nt)

For long >500 nt, protein-coding constructs, ribozyme-mediated self-splicing—specifically the permuted intron-exon (PIE) system derived from group I introns—is the primary synthesis strategy. This autocatalytic mechanism drives circularization in vitro during or immediately following transcription using standard or modified nucleoside triphosphates (NTPs).27,132,133 A key limitation of the PIE mechanism is the retention of exogenous intronic “scar” sequences at the splice junction, which can disrupt native secondary structure or alter protein-coding frames. Consequently, this method requires rigorous downstream purification, such as RNase R counter-selection, to isolate the functional circRNA from highly immunogenic linear precursors and nicked byproducts.134,135

Alternative strategies for promoting efficient oligonucleotide circularization

As an alternative to chemical synthesis, RNA can be produced by in vitro transcription, which allows preparation of RNAs of virtually unlimited length; however, it requires post-synthetic functionalization strategies.113

Chemical ligation of a 3′-aldehyde with a 5′-oxoamine (oxime circularization)

Dumy and colleagues developed an elegant chemical strategy to achieve the cyclization of oligonucleotides through the formation of an intramolecular oxime linkage. In this approach, a reactive 5′-oxyamine moiety was introduced at one end of the oligonucleotide, while a complementary 3′-aldehyde functionality was incorporated at the opposite terminus (Figure 7A). When brought into proximity under mildly acidic conditions, these two groups underwent a highly chemoselective condensation reaction to form a stable oxime bond, effectively linking the 5′ and 3′ ends of the strand and generating a cyclic structure.119,136,137Figure 7Chemical-based methods for the RNA circularization

This head-to-tail oxime cyclization method enabled efficient synthesis of small to medium-sized cyclic DNA sequences, specifically ranging from 3- to 11-mers. To facilitate this process, the researchers employed a 3′-glyceryl solid support that allowed precise installation of the aldehydic group at the 3′ terminus. In parallel, a specially designed 5′-aminooxy phosphoramidite linker was used during solid-phase synthesis to introduce the oxyamine handle at the 5′ end.136

This methodology not only provided a robust and modular route to cyclic DNA structures but also demonstrated how the strategic incorporation of bioorthogonal reactive groups can streamline post-synthetic modifications of oligonucleotides. Importantly, the same chemical strategy—based on selective condensation between complementary functional handles—can, in principle, be adapted for the synthesis of circRNA. By introducing aldehyde and aminooxy (or other mutually reactive bioorthogonal groups) at the 3′ and 5′ termini of an RNA strand, a similar intramolecular ligation could be achieved, offering a non-enzymatic route to generate covalently closed circRNA molecules. This approach may provide an attractive alternative to enzyme-mediated circularization methods, especially for chemically modified RNAs or sequences that are difficult to process using ligases.119,136,137

Shifting toward chemical oxime ligation generates a distinct, abiotic (–C=N–O–) bridge at the circRNA junction, imposing unique functional constraints relative to native counterparts.136,138 While the covalently closed architecture naturally prevents exonucleolytic decay by removing open termini, the oxime linkage provides site-specific immunity against endonuclease attacks, significantly boosting overall biostability in serum. Regarding cellular trafficking, the unique spatial geometry and hydrophobic signature of the oxime group rearrange the local presentation of the loop. This configuration can shift passive transport kinetics and alter binding profiles with intracellular transport proteins.139 Finally, its impact on translation machinery is highly distinct from other chemical interventions. Because the oxime modification introduces greater steric bulk than a phosphoramidate bond, it acts as a physical roadblock to active scanning complexes. Consequently, while oxime-circularized transcripts exhibit exceptional longevity and structural fidelity when deployed as non-coding platforms—such as miRNA sponges—they prompt ribosomal stalling or frame-shifting if placed directly inside an active open reading frame.140,141

Chemical ligation of a 5′-or 3′-azide with a 3′-OR 5′-alkyne (click circularization)

Brown and coworkers demonstrated DNA cyclization using the copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) reaction. In their template-mediated approach, two complementary oligonucleotide strands-one functionalized with a 5′-alkyne and the other with a 3′-azide-were ligated through the formation of a triazole linkage, generating a DNA molecule containing an unnatural backbone at the ligation junction (Figure 7B).142,143

Beyond this templated ligation, they also established a template-free click-ligation strategy that enabled efficient intramolecular cyclization of a single-stranded oligonucleotide, yielding a circular DNA structure that subsequently served as a template for constructing a covalently closed DNA catenane. Notably, this CuAAC-based ligation platform can, in principle, be extended to the synthesis of circRNA by installing azide and alkyne functionalities at the 5′ and 3′ termini of an RNA strand, allowing non-enzymatic cyclization under mild reaction conditions. Click chemistry on nucleic acids is inherently clean, efficient, and highly tolerant of diverse functional groups, making it compatible with large-scale synthesis. Furthermore, careful optimization has produced a biocompatible triazole linkage that is faithfully recognized by DNA and RNA polymerases, and genes incorporating such triazole linkages have been shown to function in E. coli.119,137,142,143

Harnessing azide-alkyne cycloaddition for circRNA assembly inserts a non-native 1,2,3-triazole ring at the ligation site, introducing distinct biophysical differences from a standard phosphodiester backbone. From an architectural perspective, while the circular topology acts as a primary shield against exonucleases, the rigid triazole structure provides complete localized protection against endonuclease-mediated cutting, leading to outstanding serum longevity.144,145 Intracellularly, the uncharged and highly hydrophobic nature of the triazole bridge alters local spatial geometry and surface charge dynamics.111,146 These features can alter passive cellular entry and modify binding affinity for transport-mediating RBPs.147 Finally, the presence of this artificial ring strongly dictates translation behavior. Due to the planar rigidity and steric profile of the triazole modification, the junction functions as a stark physical barrier to moving ribosomes. As a result, while click-circularized RNAs serve as highly resilient non-coding tools (e.g., modular miRNA sponges), they trigger ribosomal stalling if placed directly inside a translationally active coding sequence.140,141

Template-directed photo ligation

Taylor and colleagues demonstrated that 4-thiothymidine (s4T) can be used to photochemically assemble a longer oligonucleotide from two shorter fragments with high efficiency, exhibiting remarkable sensitivity to single-base mismatches and operating at wavelengths that are minimally damaging to nucleic acids. They further showed that template-directed photoligation can be carried out on tethered oligonucleotides, enabling the simultaneous delivery of two strands for photoligation and allowing the “photo-padlocking” of circular DNA molecules (Figure 7C). These findings highlight the potential of s4T-mediated photoligation for developing photo-triggered antisense or antigene tools, diagnostic probes, and therapeutic agents—particularly in contexts where chemical or enzyme-mediated ligation is undesirable or impractical, such as within living cells.148

Importantly, the same photochemical ligation principles can, in principle, be extended to RNA circularization. By incorporating s4T or analogous photoreactive nucleotides at the ends of an RNA strand, light-induced intramolecular ligation could generate covalently closed circRNA under mild conditions. Such a strategy would provide a non-enzymatic, spatially and temporally controlled method for producing circRNA, which is increasingly valuable for applications in RNA therapeutics, structural RNA studies, and the design of stable RNA nanostructures. Thus, s4T-based photoligation may offer a powerful approach wherever precise, light-activated RNA circularization is desired.89,110,119,137,149

Deploying photochemical ligation routes integrates photo-reactive nucleobase analogs, such as 4-thiothymidine or carboxyvinyldeoxyuridine, which undergo an intramolecular (2 + 2) cycloaddition under 366 nm UV irradiation to yield a covalent photo-adduct junction.150,151 This reagent-free circularization method offers a highly unique functional footprint. Architecturally, the rigid, abiotic photo-adduct scar resists endonucleolytic ring-opening, working alongside the transcript’s natural exonuclease defenses to extend serum survival. In terms of intracellular dynamics, the complete absence of residual chemical catalysts or heavy metals ensures clean cellular tolerance, though the localized hydrophobic bulk of the cross-linked bases shifts standard interactions with transport-mediating RBPs.146,152 Finally, the structural distortion caused by the photo-adduct heavily disrupts translation fidelity. The rigidified junction acts as an absolute physical barrier to ribosomal translocation, causing elongation arrest. Consequently, while photo-ligated loops are unsuited for coding sequences, their precise spatial and temporal control makes them excellent candidates for light-activated, non-coding circRNA platforms, enabling users to actively switch regulatory networks using controlled light exposure.141,153

Circularization by metal chelation (M = Zn2+ or Fe2+, = terpyridine)

Moreau and coworkers developed a metal-mediated strategy for generating circular 2′-O-methyl RNA by attaching metal-binding ligands, such as terpyridine, to both the 5′-and 3′-termini of a linear oligonucleotide. Using standard phosphoramidite chemistry, terpyridine groups were incorporated at each end of a short 2′-O-Me RNA strand, creating a system in which the two termini possess complementary metal-chelating units. Upon the addition of divalent metal ions—specifically Zn2+ or Fe2+—the terpyridine moieties coordinate strongly with the metal center, effectively bringing the RNA ends into close spatial proximity. This coordination-driven preorganization facilitates intramolecular ring closure and results in efficient formation of a covalently circularized RNA molecule (Figure 7D).119,137,154

This metal-templated approach offers an elegant, nonenzymatic route to circRNA synthesis, taking advantage of predictable coordination chemistry to drive cyclization under mild conditions. Because no protein ligases or harsh chemical reagents are required, the method is particularly attractive for RNA constructs that are otherwise difficult to circularize by enzymatic or chemical means. Moreover, by simply varying the positioning of terpyridine groups or the identity of the coordinating metal ions, this strategy may be further adapted to larger or more structurally complex RNA targets, providing a versatile platform for producing stable, covalently closed circRNAs suitable for applications in RNA therapeutics.

Transitioning from covalent loops to supramolecular assemblies, metal-chelation-mediated circularization incorporates terminal chelating ligands—such as terpyridine or dipicolylamine—that self-assemble around transition metal ions (Zn2+, Fe2+) to form a non-covalent coordination complex at the circRNA junction.155,156 This supramolecular architecture dictates a distinct biological profile compared with native phosphodiester (P-O) backbones.157,158 Extracellularly, the chelated complex provides steric defense against exonucleolytic degradation while remaining immune to cellular endonucleases, granting exceptional serum stability.5,159 However, this non-covalent junction introduces a unique vulnerability to competitive cellular chelators or local pH fluxes, enabling a highly programmable, switchable ring-opening mechanism.160

In terms of cellular trafficking, the embedded metal-ligand core reduces local charge density and enhances lipophilicity, altering standard interactions with transport-mediating RBPs.161,162 Finally, the steric bulk and coordination geometries of the metal complex profoundly impair translation fidelity.162,163 The junction acts as an absolute physical barrier to the elongation machinery, prompting immediate ribosomal stalling.164,165 While this precludes their use as translatable templates, it renders metal-chelated circRNAs highly valuable as stimuli-responsive non-coding therapeutics, such as miRNA sponges, whose regulatory capacity can be actively toggled by cellular metal homeostasis.166

Disulfide bond formation

Chaudhuri and coworkers demonstrated that thiopropyne-modified deoxyuridine can be efficiently incorporated into oligonucleotides during automated DNA synthesis. A key advantage of this modification is that it base-pairs normally with adenine and does not disrupt duplex stability, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the DNA helix (Figure 7E).119,137,167

The introduced thiol functionality enables the formation of stable intramolecular disulfide cross-links within triple-helical DNA, offering a powerful alternative to traditional nucleotide loops because it stabilizes the helix with fewer additional nucleotides while maintaining high structural precision. This same chemical logic can be conceptually extended to the synthesis of circRNA. By installing thiol-reactive modifications analogous to thiopropyne-substituted nucleosides at the 5′ and 3′ termini of an RNA strand, the ends can be induced to form an intramolecular disulfide bond, thereby achieving nonenzymatic and covalent RNA cyclization. This approach offers a chemically controlled, mild, and structurally precise strategy for generating stable circRNA molecules.89,110,149,167

Adopting disulfide-mediated circularization introduces a redox-sensitive (–S–S–) bond at the loop junction, creating a reversible functional profile that stands in sharp contrast to permanent chemical linkages.168 Mechanistically, while the closed loop provides solid protection against extracellular exonucleases, the disulfide bond remains completely stable against endonucleases in non-reducing serum environments.169 However, upon crossing the cellular membrane, the high concentration of intracellular reducing agents (such as glutathione) drives site-specific cleavage of the disulfide bridge.170 This redox responsiveness dynamically controls cellular processing by actively converting the circRNA back into a linear layout within the cytoplasm.168,170 Regarding translation fidelity, this intracellular linearization dictates how ribosomes interact with the transcript.171 Because the loop opens inside the cell, the molecule evades the strict structural constraints typical of continuous rolling-circle translation on rigid, synthetic loops.171 Instead, it seamlessly enters standard linear translation pathways, preventing the ribosomal stalling or frame-shifting associated with permanently modified chemical junctions.140

Functional role of circRNAs in cells

circRNAs are increasingly recognized as multifunctional molecules with diverse biological roles. One of the most studied functions is miRNA sponging, where circRNAs act as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs). A prominent example is ciRS-7/CDR1as, which contains more than 70 binding sites for miR-7, thereby sequestering it and modulating downstream gene expression.172 Beyond miRNA regulation, circRNAs also serve as protein scaffolds, interacting with RBPs to influence transcription, splicing, and translation. These interactions allow circRNAs to stabilize protein complexes and modulate cellular processes.173 Another emerging role is their function as translation templates. Certain circRNAs harbor IRES or are modified with N6-methyladenosine (m6A), enabling cap-independent translation. This expands their role beyond noncoding functions to direct protein synthesis.174 circRNAs contribute to gene regulation. Nuclear circRNAs (EIciRNAs) interact with transcriptional machinery, such as RNA polymerase II, to regulate parental gene expression, adding another layer of transcriptional control.173

Delivery strategies

Efficient delivery is critical for circRNA therapeutics. LNPs, widely used in mRNA vaccines, are now adapted for circRNA delivery, ensuring efficient encapsulation and cellular uptake.175 Viral vectors such as AAV and lentivirus enable stable expression of circRNAs in vivo, particularly useful for long-term therapeutic applications.176 Additionally, exosomes and extracellular vesicles serve as natural carriers that can deliver circRNAs across tissues, offering biocompatibility and targeted delivery potential.177

Integration with omics

circRNAs are increasingly studied using multi-omics approaches. In transcriptomics, pipelines such as CIRI and circExplorer identify endogenous circRNAs and validate synthetic constructs.178 In proteomics, mass spectrometry confirms circRNA-encoded proteins, linking RNA sequence to functional protein output. A large-scale colorectal cancer study integrating short- and long-read RNA sequencing with MS identified 6,848 circRNA-derived peptides, including nearly 1,000 detectable only with long-read integration.179 Additional work has shown that circRNAs such as circPICALM undergo m6A-dependent translation, producing metastasis-promoting oncoproteins in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, again validated by proteomic approaches.180 In metabolomics, circRNA-driven pathways can be connected to metabolic changes, providing functional readouts in systems biology.181

Case studies

Recent studies highlight the translational potential of circRNAs. In vaccines, synthetic circRNAs encoding viral antigens such as the SARS-CoV-2 spike elicit prolonged immune responses compared with mRNA vaccines, showing promise for durable immunization.182 In cancer therapy, circRNAs engineered to sponge oncogenic miRNAs or encode tumor suppressor proteins are emerging as novel therapeutic strategies.183 In neurological disorders, circRNAs modulate synaptic plasticity and neurodegeneration pathways, suggesting potential in treating diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s.184

Conclusion

circRNAs represent a promising therapeutic modality owing to their enhanced stability and sustained protein expression; however, several key limitations and translational challenges must be addressed for clinical application. A major bottleneck is delivery efficiency, as current systems, primarily LNPs, often exhibit suboptimal tissue targeting, limited cellular uptake, and inefficient endosomal escape, particularly in extrahepatic tissues. In addition, manufacturing and molecular design issues, including variability in circularization efficiency and the presence of linear RNA or double-stranded RNA impurities, can compromise both efficacy and reproducibility. Importantly, immunogenicity associated with circRNA constructs, especially at chemical junctions or arising from synthesis-related impurities, remains a significant concern, as these features can activate innate immune pathways such as RNase L-mediated responses, thereby reducing RNA stability and translational output. Furthermore, the prolonged persistence of circRNAs-while advantageous for sustained expression-raises challenges in dose control, pharmacokinetics, and potential off-target effects, which are less predictable compared with well-established mRNA platforms. From a translational perspective, additional hurdles include scalability of production, purification standardization, regulatory framework development, and the need for robust head-to-head comparisons with existing RNA technologies. Collectively, these challenges underscore that while circRNAs hold considerable therapeutic potential, significant advances in delivery technologies, manufacturing consistency, and safety profiling are required before their widespread clinical adoption.
