EigenScore: OOD Detection using Posterior Covariance in Diffusion Models
Shirin Shoushtari, Yi Wang, Xiao Shi, Ulugbek S. Kamilov

Abstract
Out-of-distribution (OOD) detection is critical for the safe deployment of machine learning systems in safety-sensitive domains. Diffusion models have recently emerged as powerful generative models, capable of capturing complex data distributions through iterative denoising. Building on this progress, recent work has explored their potential for OOD detection. We propose EigenScore , a new OOD detection method that leverages the eigenvalue spectrum of the posterior covariance induced by a diffusion model. We argue that posterior covariance provides a consistent signal of distribution shift, leading to larger trace and leading eigenvalues on OOD inputs, yielding a clear spectral signature. We further provide analysis explicitly linking posterior covariance to distribution mismatch, establishing it as a reliable signal for OOD detection. To ensure tractability, we adopt a Jacobian-free subspace iteration method to estimate the leading eigenvalues using only forward evaluations of the denoiser. Empirically, EigenScore achieves SOTA performance, with up to 5 % 5\% AUROC improvement over the best baseline. Notably, it remains robust in near-OOD settings such as CIFAR-10 vs CIFAR-100, where existing diffusion-based methods often fail.

Background

Diffusion Models.
Diffusion models learn to generate samples by simulating a gradual denoising process. During training, a clean sample
{\bm{x}}\sim{p({\bm{x}})}
is perturbed by Gaussian noise across timesteps
t=1,\cdots,T
, producing noisy states
{\bm{x}}_{t}
through the forward Markov chain
p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})={\mathcal{N}}({\bm{x}},\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}})
, which allows for direct sampling via
{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}+{\bm{z}}
, where
{\bm{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}})
.

The reverse process is approximated by a denoising network
{\mathsf{D}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
, trained to predict either the clean signal or the injected noise. A standard training objective is mean squared error (MSE):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}}({\mathsf{D}}_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{t},t}\left[\Big\|{\bm{x}}-{\mathsf{D}}_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(1)

Once trained, the model generates new samples by iteratively denoising from Gaussian noise at
t=T
back to
t=0
. Importantly, Tweedie’s formula
[
38
,
39
]
connects Gaussian denoising with score estimation, linking the posterior mean to the gradient of the log-density as

{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]={\bm{x}}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(2)

where
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})
denotes an MMSE estimator trained on samples from distribution
p
.
Here, the gradient is with respect to
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, and
p({\bm{x}}_{t})
denotes the marginal distribution noisy image

p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}){p({\bm{x}})}{\differential{\bm{x}}}=\int G_{\sigma_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}})p({\bm{x}}){\differential{\bm{x}}},

(3)

where
G_{\sigma_{t}}
denotes the Gaussian density function with standard deviation
\sigma_{t}\geq 0

[
40
,
41
]
.
This relationship implies that denoising does more than produce samples—it provides access to score functions and posterior statistics of the underlying distribution. In the context of OOD detection, this observation motivates our use of the denoiser’s covariance structure as a principled signal of distribution shift. Diffusion models admit several formulations (e.g., variance-preserving, variance-exploding, and SDE-based), but all share the key property of learning the score function
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})
to guide denoising
[
21
,
22
,
42
,
43
]
.

Unsupervised OOD detection.

Unsupervised OOD detection aims to determine whether a given sample
{\bm{x}}
originates from the same distribution as the training data, using only unlabeled InD samples
{\bm{x}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{x}}_{n}\sim p({\bm{x}})
. The goal is to learn a detector that assigns an OOD score to each input, where higher scores indicate a greater likelihood that
{\bm{x}}
was drawn from a different distribution, such as the OOD density
q({\bm{x}})

[
3
,
35
]
.

Likelihood-based methods.

These methods use generative models including VAEs, flows, diffusion models to estimate sample likelihoods, under the assumption that OOD data should receive lower likelihoods
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
. However, it has been shown that generative models often assign high likelihoods to OOD inputs
[
5
,
14
,
48
]
. To mitigate this, refined scores have been proposed, including likelihood ratios
[
49
]
, compression corrections
[
50
]
, WAIC ensembles
[
5
]
, and typicality tests
[
51
]
. Diffusion-based variants further extend this idea by analyzing statistics across the denoising trajectory
[
35
,
52
]
.

Reconstruction-based methods.

Another line of work assumes that InD samples reconstruct well, whereas OOD samples do not. Early examples include autoencoders
[
53
]
and GANs
[
54
]
. More recently, diffusion models have been exploited for their strong reconstruction fidelity, leading to perceptual quality scores
[
3
]
, projection regret
[
37
]
, and masked inpainting methods like LMD
[
36
]
.

Feature-based methods.

These approaches distinguish InD from OOD by leveraging learned representations, such as Mahalanobis distance in latent space
[
55
]
, unsupervised contrastive features
[
17
,
56
,
18
]
, or encoder features from invertible models
[
57
]
. Pretrained feature extractors have also proven effective
[
58
]
.

Complementing these approaches, we introduce a new perspective based on posterior covariance in diffusion models, which provides a principled feature for quantifying distribution shift.

3
Diffusion Model for Out-of-Distribution Detection

EigenScore is a novel OOD detection method that exploits covariance structure of the denoising diffusion process. Our key insight is that when a diffusion model trained on InD data is applied to OOD inputs, the variance of its denoising predictions inflates, leaving a characteristic signature in the eigenvalue spectrum of the score Jacobian.

To motivate EigenScore, we first revisit why commonly used diffusion-based OOD metrics—likelihood, score norm, and score derivatives—are unreliable (Sec.
3.1
). We then show, both theoretically and empirically, that posterior covariance offers a consistent marker of distribution shift (Sec.
3.2
), before formalizing EigenScore and its efficient computation (Sec.
3.3
).

3.1
Why Likelihood and Score Dynamics Are Insufficient

Since diffusion models are trained via a variational lower bound (ELBO), likelihood-based scores such as negative log-likelihood (NLL) are natural candidates for OOD detection. However, likelihood does not necessarily align with semantic structure: diffusion models often emphasize low-level statistics while ignoring higher-level semantics, making NLL unreliable
[
51
,
50
]
. Empirically, NLL can even assign higher likelihoods to OOD samples than to InD ones
[
35
]
. As shown in Fig.
1
, NLL is not a reliable metric for separating InD from OOD samples.

Beyond likelihood, diffusion-based OOD metrics have also used the score function
\epsilon_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
and its temporal derivative
\partial_{t}\epsilon_{\theta}({\bm{x}}_{t},t)
as statistics
[
35
]
. Their norms provide some empirical separation, but they remain unstable. In near-OOD settings (C10 vs. C100), the distributions overlap substantially (Fig.
1
, top row). In some settings (C10 vs. SVHN), the ordering can invert, with OOD samples receiving lower scores than InD (Fig.
1
, bottom row). These limitations motivate shifting from scalarized scores toward a covariance-based perspective, where the structure of denoiser variability itself provides a more principled signal of distribution shift

3.2
Uncertainty as a Signal of Distribution Shift

We formalize why denoising uncertainty yields a principled OOD signal. Let
{p({\bm{x}})}
denote InD and
{q({\bm{x}})}
an OOD distribution. Under Gaussian corruption with variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
, the KL divergence admits the score-based representation
[
59
,
60
,
61
,
35
]

\mathrm{D_{KL}}(p\;\|\;q)=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{t}}\Big[\big\|\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})-\nabla\log q({\bm{x}}_{t})\big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]\sigma_{t}~\differential t.

(4)

where
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is generated by the forward diffusion applied to
{\bm{x}}
. Building on prior analyses of KL divergence in diffusion processes
[
61
,
35
]
, we restate the divergence in terms of denoising error (a derivation is given in App.
A.1
for completeness).

Proposition 1
.

Let
p_{t}
and
q_{t}
denote the noisy marginals of InD and OOD distributions generated by the forward diffusion process (Eq. (
3
)). For MMSE denoisers
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]
and
{\mathsf{D}}_{q}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]
,

\mathrm{D_{KL}}(p\;\|\;q)=\int_{0}^{T}\left[\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{q},t)-\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)\right]\sigma_{t}^{-3}\differential t

where
\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)=\mathbb{E}\big[\|{\bm{x}}-{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\big]
at noise level
t
.

This proposition, adapted from earlier derivations, shows that KL divergence—and thus distribution shift—can be viewed as the accumulation of
excess denoising error
incurred when contrasting the optimal MMSE denoiser under
q
with that under
p
. Thus, we have
\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p};q,t)\geq\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{q};q,t)
for each
t
and the denoising error of a single InD denoiser
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}
is
larger in expectation
on OOD inputs, yielding a practical detection signal without access to
q
.

For the MMSE denoiser
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}
, the mean-squared error admits a law-of-total-variance decomposition (proof in App.
A.3
):

\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|{\bm{x}}-{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\!\left[\tr\!\big(\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]\big)\right].

(5)

Thus, denoising error equals the
total posterior variance
—the trace of the conditional covariance—averaged over noisy observations at noise level
t
. Intuitively,
\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]
quantifies the spread of plausible clean signals consistent with
{\bm{x}}_{t}
. When
{\bm{x}}_{t}
is informative (e.g., InD or low noise), this spread is small and the MSE remains low; when
{\bm{x}}_{t}
lies off-manifold, the spread inflates and the MSE increases. In this sense, denoising error corresponds directly to the model’s predictive uncertainty, providing a principled basis for OOD detection and motivating our focus on the
spectral structure
of posterior covariance in Section
3.3
.

While Eq.
4
relates distribution shift to differences in score norms, such metrics do not preserve ordering: depending on the dataset and noise scale, OOD samples may appear either larger or smaller than InD ones. By contrast, the MSE-based formulation in Prop.
1
guarantees that OOD denoising error is systematically larger in expectation than InD error. This ensures a consistent separation with preserved ordering, whereas score-norm methods such as DiffPath
[
35
]
capture only relative differences without indicating direction.

3.3
Eigenvalue-Based Uncertainty estimation

Section
3.2
established that denoising error equals the total posterior variance and that this variance inflates under distribution shift. We now make this connection operational by expressing posterior covariance through the Jacobian of the MMSE denoiser and analyzing its eigen-spectrum.
For the MMSE denoiser
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]
, the posterior covariance admits the Miyasawa identity
[
39
]
:

\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\left({\bm{I}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)=\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(6)

so that

\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left[\tr\left(\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]\right)\right]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left[\mathrm{tr}\left(\nabla{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)\right],

(7)

Thus, the Jacobian trace measures how much posterior uncertainty remains after observing
{\bm{x}}_{t}
: small traces indicate concentrated beliefs (InD), while large traces signal inflated uncertainty (OOD). Derivations are provided in Appendix (
A.2
).

Writing
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\,\coloneqq\,\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})
, the covariance is symmetric positive semi-definite and admits the eigen-decomposition
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})={\bm{U}}_{t}\diag(\lambda^{t}_{1},\cdots,\lambda^{t}_{n}){\bm{U}}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}
, with nonnegative eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{t}
. Since
\tr(\Sigmabm_t)=\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}^{t}
, the MSE corresponds to the sum of eigenvalues—the total uncertainty across all principal directions at noise level
t
:

\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left[\tr\left(\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\left[\tr\left({\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda^{t}_{k}({\bm{x}}_{t})\Big].

(8)

For InD samples, the spectrum is compact with smaller leading eigenvalues, reflecting structured denoising aligned with the training data. For OOD samples, uncertainty spreads across multiple eigen-directions, inflating both the spectrum and the trace. This spectral inflation provides a quantitative signal of distribution shift. Figure
2
illustrates this effect: OOD samples exhibit consistently higher uncertainty, confirming the link between spectral inflation and distribution mismatch.

3.4
EigenScore as an OOD metric

While Prop.
1
links distribution shift to excess denoising error, it requires two denoisers, whereas in practice we have only a single InD model. Combining Prop.
1
with Eq.
7
implies that OOD inputs exhibit inflated posterior covariance, which we can read from its eigenvalues. For each input
{\bm{x}}
and diffusion step
t
, we compute

m_{t}({\bm{x}})\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda^{t}_{k}({\bm{x}}_{t}),\quad{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}+\sigma_{t}{\bm{z}},\;\;{\bm{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bm{I}}),

(9)

the sum of the top–
K
eigenvalues across
I
noise realizations. These are aggregated (e.g., by mean, median, or full set) into
\overline{m}_{t}({\bm{x}})
, yielding the EigenScore feature vector

\mathbf{M}({\bm{x}})\;=\;\big[\overline{m}_{1}({\bm{x}}),\ldots,\overline{m}_{T}({\bm{x}})\big]^{\mathsf{T}}.

(10)

We normalize each coordinate using Z-scores with statistics
(\mu_{t},\sigma_{t})
computed on training set
z_{t}({\bm{x}})\;=\;(\overline{m}_{t}({\bm{x}})-\mu_{t})/\sigma_{t}
. The final OOD score is the sum of normalized coordinates, with the number of timesteps
T
tuned for efficiency on validation data.

Direct Jacobian evaluation is costly. Following
[
62
]
, we estimate the top–
K
eigenvalues using a Jacobian-free subspace iteration. The method approximates Jacobian–vector products with finite differences of the denoiser
{\bm{v}}^{+}\approx({\mathsf{D}}({\bm{x}}_{t}+c{\bm{v}})-{\mathsf{D}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-c{\bm{v}}))/2c
, where
c\ll 1
is the linear approximation constant,
{\bm{v}}
is the current principle component, and
{\bm{v}}^{+}
in the next principle component. We then orthogonalizes directions via QR. After a few iterations, eigenvalues are obtained as

\lambda^{t}_{k}({\bm{x}}_{t})\approx\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2c}\,\big\|{\mathsf{D}}({\bm{x}}_{t}+c{\bm{v}}^{k})-{\mathsf{D}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-c{\bm{v}}^{k})\big\|_{2}.

(11)

Full pseudo-code is given in App.
B.1
.

3.5
EigenScore vs. MSE: Capturing Structure Instead of Collapse

Eq. (
4
) and Prop.
1
show that distribution shift manifests as excess denoising error, measurable through the posterior covariance. However, scalar metrics such as MSE or score norms collapse this uncertainty into a single number, discarding how variance is distributed across directions. At high noise levels, this collapse is particularly problematic: many small eigenvalues are dominated by isotropic noise, obscuring class-specific structure. The following lemma formalizes this effect.

Lemma 1
.

Let
p({\bm{x}}_{t})=p*\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}})
denotes the noisy marginal in Eq. (
3
) and let
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\;=\;\sigma_{t}^{2}\!\left({\bm{I}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})\right)
from Eq. (
6
). As
\sigma_{t}\to\infty
,
\|\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})\|\to 0
uniformly on compact sets. Hence

\Sigma_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}+o(\sigma_{t}^{2}),

so all eigenvalues satisfy
\lambda_{k}^{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}+o(\sigma_{t}^{2})
.

Lemma
1
implies that the spectrum flattens under heavy Gaussian smoothing: all directions approach the same variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
, so low-variance components lose discriminative information. Consequently, MSE and score norms aggregate mostly isotropic noise, rather than meaningful structure (proof in App.
A.4
). To retain the informative structure, we focus on the dominant modes. Ky Fan’s theorem guarantees that the top-
K
eigenvalues capture the maximal variance among all
K
-dimensional projections:

Proposition 2

(
Ky Fan’s theorem

[
63
]
)
.

Let
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\succeq 0
have eigenvalues
\lambda^{t}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{t}_{n}
with eigenvectors forming
{\bm{U}}_{t}=[{\bm{u}}^{t}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}^{t}_{K}]
. For any
K\!\in\!\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\max_{{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}:\ {\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}_{K}}\ \mathrm{tr}~\big({\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{V}}\big)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k},

and a maximizer is
{\bm{V}}^{\star}=[{\bm{u}}^{t}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}^{t}_{K}]
.

By retaining only the top-
K
eigenvalues, EigenScore preserves the most informative uncertainty directions while discarding noise-dominated components. This explains its consistent advantage over MSE and score-based metrics. The choice of
K
is not dictated by theory, but reflects a practical trade-off between capturing discriminative information and computational efficiency (see App.
A.5
for proof).

5
Conclusion

We introduced EigenScore, a principled OOD detection method for diffusion models that leverages the spectral structure of denoising uncertainty. By linking KL divergence to excess denoising error and showing that posterior covariance inflation consistently signals distribution shift, EigenScore offers both theoretical justification and strong empirical performance. Across diverse benchmarks, EigenScore consistently outperformed likelihood-based and score-norm methods, with particularly robust gains in near-OOD settings where traditional approaches fail. Ablation studies further confirmed that most discriminative information lies in the leading eigenvalues at moderate noise levels, validating our spectral perspective.

Limitations

Despite its strengths, our approach has several limitations. First, EigenScore only leverages a subset of the information available in diffusion models: we use the eigenvalues of the posterior covariance but discard eigenvector structure, which may contain additional discriminative cues. Moreover, we compute features at a limited set of timesteps rather than across the full diffusion trajectory, potentially overlooking temporal dynamics of uncertainty. Second, our framework focuses on the magnitude of eigenvalues, but does not explicitly exploit their rate of change across eigenvalues, which itself may differ systematically between InD and OOD inputs and could serve as a detection signal.

A.2
Connecting MSE of Denoising to Covariance

We assume that MMSE estimator
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}
is an optimal denoiser trained on images sampled from data distribution
p({\bm{x}})
. Consequently, the MSE for this operator is defined as

\displaystyle\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{t}}\Big[\|{\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]\|_{2}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\Big[\tr(\xbm- \E_p[\xbm|\xbm_t])({\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}])^{\mathsf{T}}\big|{\bm{x}}_{t}\Big]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}}\Bigg[\tr\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]\Bigg],

where the first equality follows from the definition of MSE using the MMSE estimator
{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]
, and the second equality applies the law of total expectation along with the identity
\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}=\tr(\vbm\vbm^\Tsf)
. In the last equality, we used the fact that the inner expectation corresponds to the conditional covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]
, whose trace gives the expected squared error. From Theorem 1 of
[
62
]
, we have

\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla{\mathsf{D}}_{p}({\bm{x}}_{t})\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}^{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{u}}_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}},

(14)

where
\nabla{\mathsf{D}}_{p}
is the Jacobian, assumed to be positive semi-definite and symmetric. We approximate the Jacobian matrix using the top
K
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{K}
, nd their corresponding eigenvectors
{\bm{u}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{u}}_{K}

which is a low-rank approximation of the full covariance matrix based on its spectral decomposition of symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. By retaining only the top
K<n
terms in this expansion, we obtain a rank-
K
approximation of the covariance, capturing most of its variance while reducing dimensionality and computation. The trace of this approximate covariance is then given by

\tr(\Cov_p[\xbm|\xbm_t])\approx\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}^{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(15)

since each term
{\bm{u}}_{k}{\bm{u}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}
is a rank-one projection matrix with unit trace. This expression allows us to estimate the total variance (or the MSE) using only the top eigenvalues, under the assumption that the remaining eigenvalues contribute negligibly. Consequently, we have:

\text{MSE}({\mathsf{D}}_{p},t)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}}\Bigg[\tr\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[{\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t}]\Bigg]=\sigma_{t}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}^{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}).

(16)

A.3
Miyasawa Relationship

The connection between MMSE estimation under Gaussian noise and the score function was first established in
[
39
]
and later generalized in
[
41
]
. For completeness, we provide a derivation here.

Let
{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}+{\bm{z}}
, denote a noisy observation of
{\bm{x}}\sim{p({\bm{x}})}
, where
{\bm{z}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}})
, and define
p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})
as the Gaussian likelihood. The marginal distribution of y is given by:

p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int{p({\bm{x}})}\,p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\,\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

To derive the score function
\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})
, we differentiate the log-marginal using the identity
\nabla h({\bm{x}}_{t})=h({\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla\log h({\bm{x}}_{t})
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\int p({\bm{x}})p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\bigg/p({\bm{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\int p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}\right],

which can be interpreted as a chain rule applied to score functions rather than densities.

Next, we compute the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})
. Differentiating again:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})\left(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})^{\mathsf{T}}+\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

Using Bayes’ rule:

\log p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})+\log p({\bm{x}})-\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}),

and differentiating with respect to y, we obtain:

\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})=\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})-\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}).

Substituting this into the previous expression yields:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\int p({\bm{x}}|{\bm{x}}_{t})\left((\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})-\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}))\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})^{\mathsf{T}}+\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\right)\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})-\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}))\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})^{\mathsf{T}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}\left[\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}\right].

Now, since
p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})
is Gaussian with variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}
, we have:

\displaystyle\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\|{\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}\|^{2}+\text{const},

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}}),

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\bm{I}}.

Plugging into the previous results gives the Miyasawa identities:

\displaystyle\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]-{\bm{x}}_{t}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\mathrm{Cov}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\bm{I}}.

(18)

Rearranging, we recover both the posterior mean and posterior covariance in terms of the score and its Hessian:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}),

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{2}\left({\bm{I}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})\right).

(20)

Finally, the optimal denoising error is given by the expected trace of the posterior covariance:

\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bm{x}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{tr}\,\mathrm{Cov}[{\bm{x}}\mid{\bm{x}}_{t}]\right].

Let
G_{\sigma_{t}}
denotes the Gaussian density function with standard deviation
\sigma_{t}\geq 0
. Then, we have

p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int p({\bm{x}}_{t}|{\bm{x}}){p({\bm{x}})}{\differential{\bm{x}}}=\int G_{\sigma_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}})p({\bm{x}}){\differential{\bm{x}}},

(21)

Differentiation under the integral yields

\nabla G_{\sigma}({\bm{z}})=-\frac{{\bm{z}}}{\sigma^{2}}G_{\sigma}({\bm{z}}),\qquad\nabla^{2}G_{\sigma}({\bm{z}})=\Big(\frac{{\bm{z}}{\bm{z}}^{\mathsf{T}}}{\sigma^{4}}-\frac{{\bm{I}}}{\sigma^{2}}\Big)\,G_{\sigma}({\bm{z}}).

(22)

Hence

\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int\nabla G_{\sigma_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}})\,p({\bm{x}})\,{\differential{\bm{x}}},\qquad\nabla_{{\bm{x}}_{t}}^{2}p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\int\nabla^{2}G_{\sigma_{t}}({\bm{x}}_{t}-{\bm{x}})\,p({\bm{x}})\,{\differential{\bm{x}}}.

(23)

Fix a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Using Cauchy–Schwarz and that
p
has finite second moment, one obtains bounds of the form

\frac{\|\nabla p({\bm{x}}_{t})\|}{p({\bm{x}}_{t})}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma_{t}},\qquad\frac{\|\nabla^{2}p({\bm{x}}_{t})\|}{p({\bm{x}}_{t})}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}},\qquad\forall{\bm{x}}\in K,

(24)

for constants
C_{1},C_{2}
independent of
\sigma_{t}
. Consequently,

\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})=\frac{\nabla^{2}p({\bm{x}}_{t})}{p({\bm{x}}_{t})}-\frac{\nabla p({\bm{x}}_{t})\,\nabla p({\bm{x}}_{t})^{\mathsf{T}}}{p({\bm{x}}_{t})^{2}}

(25)

satisfies

\|\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})\|\;\leq\;\frac{C_{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}+\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\;=\;O\!\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Big),

(26)

uniformly on
K
. Thus
\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t})\to 0
as
\sigma_{t}\to\infty
.

Plugging into

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}({\bm{I}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla^{2}\log p({\bm{x}}_{t}))

(27)

gives

{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bm{I}}+o(\sigma_{t}^{2}),

(28)

and hence
\lambda_{k}^{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})=\sigma_{t}^{2}+o(\sigma_{t}^{2})
for all
k
.
∎

This implies that at large noise, the posterior covariance becomes asymptotically isotropic; the spectrum
flattens
and “small” eigenvalues are lifted to
\approx\sigma_{t}^{2}
. Their contribution is therefore indistinguishable from isotropic noise, which explains why including late timesteps or many tail eigenvalues does not improve OOD discrimination.

A.5
Proof of Ky Fan’s Theorem

Prop.
2
.

Let
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})\succeq 0
have eigenvalues
\lambda^{t}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{t}_{n}
with eigenvectors forming
{\bm{U}}_{t}=[{\bm{u}}^{t}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}^{t}_{n}]
. For any
K\!\in\!\{1,\dots,n\}
,

\max_{{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}:\ {\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}_{K}}\ \mathrm{tr}~\big({\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{V}}\big)\;=\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k},

and a maximizer is
{\bm{V}}^{\star}=[{\bm{u}}^{t}_{1},\dots,{\bm{u}}^{t}_{K}]
.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}
with
{\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}_{K}
. Write
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t})={\bm{U}}_{t}\Lambda_{t}{\bm{U}}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}
with
{\bm{U}}^{t}
orthogonal.
Set
{\bm{Q}}\coloneqq{\bm{U}}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K}
. Since
{\bm{U}}_{t}
is orthogonal,
{\bm{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{Q}}={\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{U}}_{t}{\bm{U}}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}_{K}
,
so the columns of
{\bm{Q}}
are orthonormal.

We have

\mathrm{tr}({\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{t}){\bm{V}})=\mathrm{tr}\big({\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{U}}_{t}\Lambda_{t}{\bm{U}}_{t}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}\big)=\mathrm{tr}\big({\bm{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda_{t}{\bm{Q}}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda^{t}_{i}\,\|q_{i}\|_{2}^{2},

where
q_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
denotes the
i
-th row of
{\bm{Q}}
(so
\|q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0
). Because
{\bm{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{Q}}={\bm{I}}_{K}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathrm{tr}({\bm{Q}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{Q}})=K,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1\ \ \text{for all $i$}\ \ (\text{each row is a subvector of a unit vector}).

Thus the objective is a linear functional of the nonnegative weights
w_{i}\coloneqq\|q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
subject to

\sum_{i}w_{i}=K
and
0\leq w_{i}\leq 1
. Since the eigenvalues are ordered

\lambda^{t}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{t}_{n}
, the sum
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}w_{i}
is maximized by assigning

w_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,K
and
w_{i}=0
otherwise (rearrangement/greedy argument), which yields

\max_{{\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{V}}={\bm{I}}_{K}}\ \mathrm{tr}({\bm{V}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{V}})\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}\lambda^{t}_{i}.

This maximum is attained by taking
{\bm{Q}}=[\,{\bm{e}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{e}}_{K}\,]
, i.e.,
{\bm{V}}={\bm{U}}{\bm{Q}}=[\,{\bm{u}}^{t}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}^{t}_{K}\,]
,
the matrix of the top-
K
eigenvectors.
∎
