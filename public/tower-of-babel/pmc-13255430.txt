Viral suppression status and associated factors among key populations on ART in Western Oromia, Ethiopia: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Kenate Bekele, Endalu Tesfaye Guteta, Abdi Diriba, Gadisa Chewaka, Demeke Jabessa, Daniel Amsalu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundViral suppression, the key goal of antiretroviral therapy (ART) for improving health and preventing transmission, remains understudied among key populations (KPs) in Ethiopia. This study assessed viral suppression and associated factors among KPs on ART in Oromia, Ethiopia.MethodA retrospective study was conducted among KPs in the study area using patient charts (n = 360). Twelve-month post-ART viral load results were extracted. Data were entered into Epi Data 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS. Logistic regression identified associations, with significance set at p-value ≤ 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for adjusted odds ratios (AORs).ResultThe study included 360 participants, including female sex workers (FSWs) 347 (96.4%) and 13 (3.6%) prisoners. Most participants, 164 (45.6%) were aged 25–34, and 320 (88.9%) achieved viral suppression. Being in WHO clinical stage I (AOR = 3.34; 95% CI: 1.35–8.24), being on ART for < 3 years (AOR = 9.25; 95% CI: 3.71–23.03), and having good adherence (AOR = 13.62; 95% CI: 5.85–31.74) were significant determinants.Conclusion and recommendationThe rate of viral suppression among KPs on ART in Western Oromia, Ethiopia was below the UNAIDS 95% target. Early WHO clinical stage I, shorter ART duration, and good adherence were significant determinants of suppression.Keywords: Key population, viral suppression, adherence, HIV, Western Ethiopia

Introduction

HIV remains a major global burden, with an estimated 39.9 million people living with the virus, 1.3 million new infections, and 630,000 AIDS-related deaths in 2023 [1, 2]. Advances in HIV care have reshaped the global epidemic, with 30.7 million of 39.9 million people living with HIV receiving ART in 2023 [3]. Since the development of efficient therapies, especially ART, HIV/AIDS has changed from a deadly acute illness to a chronic illness that can be controlled in many areas [4]. This change highlights the significant influence of ART and represents a pivotal moment in the treatment and prognosis of HIV [5].

Stigmatized communities face unequal access to HIV services, making estimates of new infections among KPs and their partners essential to track program success [6]. Key Populations (KPs) refer to groups of people who are more likely to get HIV, viral hepatitis, or sexually transmitted infections because of certain higher-risk behaviors and other structural variables involved [7], and whose engagement is essential to an effective HIV response [8]. They encompass transgender women, injecting drug users, sex workers, men who have sex with men (MSM), people in prisons and other confined environments [9].

To achieve the global 2030 goals, nations are urged to meet the “95–95–95” targets: 95% of people with HIV knowing their status, 95% of those diagnosed on treatment, and 95% of those on treatment achieving viral suppression [10]. Monitoring viral suppression in patients on ART is essential for optimizing health and reducing transmission risk [11]. ART works by restoring immunity and suppressing viral replication to undetectable levels [12, 13]. While first-line ART achieves high rates of viral suppression, suggesting HIV can be managed with simplified care [14], but some patients experience viral rebound [15]. Rebound increases susceptibility to other illnesses, treatment failure, drug resistance [16], and potential for HIV transmission [17]. Patients who experienced virological failure faced a higher risk of clinical progression to AIDS and mortality compared to those with a full virological response [18].

To improve quality of life and attain a high treatment success rate, it is critical to identify and manage factors of virological failure [19]. According to earlier research, viral suppression status was linked to a number of factors. It was found that age, educational status [20], history of injecting drug use [21], substance abuse, intimate partner violence [22], duration on ART [23, 24], baseline CD4 level [25], WHO clinical stage [26, 27], and ART regimen [28] were reported to be associated with viral suppression status. Ethiopia has begun tracking specific viral load monitoring. However, viral suppression status and factors leading to virological failure are not well understood and studied especially among KPs. Thus, the aim of this study was to determine viral suppression status and identify the determinants of virological failure among KPs who were on ART in the study area.

Methods and materials

Study area, design and period

The study was conducted in KP Clinics in health facilities of Western Oromia, Ethiopia. Western Oromia is located in western part of Ethiopia with an estimated population of 13 million in 8 Zones & three administrative towns. With regard to ART health facility distribution there are 20 public Hospitals, and 52 Health centers. In three towns there were 6 special KP friendly service clinics in which KPs are being served. Key population-specific health services provided include targeted community outreach testing, health education, condoms and lubricants, ART and psychosocial counseling services. A retrospective institutional-based patient chart review was conducted from April to May 2025.

Study population, sample size determination and sampling techniques

All ART patients were used as a source population, whereas KPs (FSWs and prisoners) on ART in Western Oromia were used as a study population. All the included males were prisoners. The study included clients who were > 15 years of age, on ART follow-up care, and had a viral load test done in the past 12 months. Data collection points included six health facilities: Shanan Gibe General Hospital, Jimma Higher 2 Health Center, Jimma Health Center, Ambo Health Center, Nekemte Health Center, and Cheleleki Health Center. Charts of all eligible clients were reviewed: 117, 52, 62, 12, 100, and 17 clients from each facility, respectively. A retrospective cross-sectional study design that included all eligible clients did not require a sample size calculation.

Variables and measurements

Viral load suppression status was dependent variable. Viral load measurement of the KPs was obtained at 12th month after ART initiation. Sociodemographic characteristics are (Age, sex, residence, client’s education level, marital status), behavioral characteristics (smoking, chat chewing, alcohol consumption) and clinical characteristics (CD4 level, duration on ART, current ART regimen, WHO stage) were obtained from chart. Viral load non-suppression is regarded as > 1000 copies/mL of blood and is confirmed 12 months after initiation of ART. Viral load suppression is said to be attained when the count is ≤ 1000 copies/ml of blood.

Data collection tools and procedures

Socio-demographic and clinical information was extracted from patients’ registers and folders using a structured checklist. Data collected included: (1) socio-demographic information; (2) clinical information; and (3) treatment and behavioral information. Data collection was conducted by three health professionals at each facility.

The plasma specimens were shipped to Nekemte Public Health Research Referral Laboratory, Ambo General Hospital, and Jimma University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital to extract RNA and determine viral loads. HIV-1 RNA extraction and quantification were done using Abbott m2000sp and Roche cobas systems; Abbott m2000rt and cobas 4800/6800 were used to quantify HIV-1 RNA, respectively (range: 40–10 million copies/ml), following manufacturers’ instructions. CD4 cell count was done using a BD FACSPresto.

Quality assurance

The collected data were reviewed for completeness and consistency, and any incomplete information was verified and corrected. High, low positive and negative controls were checked before performing patient sample for viral load determination in the three testing sites. For CD4 determination, the BD FACSPresto instrument performs automatic self-checks each time it is powered on and during operation. The BD FACSPresto CD4/Hb cartridge has built-in system controls that verify cartridge integrity and functionality.

Data processing and analysis

Data was coded, cleaned, and then imported into EpiData 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS 24. The normality of the data on age, CD4 count, and viral load was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual plots, and categorical data was described using frequency and proportions. Descriptive statistics were also generated, and virological suppression was determined by calculating the proportion of patients who had suppressed viral loads. Candidate variables were determined by performing a binary logistic regression, and those with p < 0.25 were chosen for multiple regression analysis, where p < 0.05 was considered significant. The fitness of the model was checked by performing the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and results were expressed as AORs and CI. Results were expressed in figures, tables, and text format.

Operational definitions

Viral load suppression: Viral load < 1000 copies of RNA/ml of plasma after a first line ART [29].

Viral non-suppression: Elevated viral load Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) copies of ≥ 1000 copies/ml in plasma [30].

Results

Sociodemographic, clinical, treatment and behavioral characteristics of the study participants

A total of 360 participants were included in the study. The ages of the participants ranged from 15 to 56 years, with a mean age of 31.3 (± 9.2 SD) years. In this study, the mean body mass index (BMI) of the participants was 21.1 (± 2.3 SD) kg/m². The majority of participants, 330 (91.7%), were within the normal BMI range. Regarding clinical disease status, most participants were classified as WHO clinical stage I, accounting for 314 (87.2%) of the total sample, while the remaining 46 (12.8%) participants were categorized as WHO clinical stage II (Table 1).

Associated factors for viral suppression among the study participants

All variables had undergone binary logistic regression analysis. Variables with p-values < 0.25 were selected as candidate variables to be included in multivariate analysis. The fitness of the model was checked by performing the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Variables with p-value < 0.05 were significant determinants. Then, strength of association was determined by AOR.

In the bivariate analysis age, educational status, body mass index, WHO clinical stage, CD4 count, comorbidities, duration on ART, adherence level, treatment regimen, khat chewing, and smoking were significant variables. In multivariable analysis, WHO stage I, duration of ART of < 3 years, and good adherence were independently associated with viral suppression among KPs. Participants at WHO stage I had a higher probability of viral suppression than those at stage II+ (AOR 3.34; 95% CI 1.35–8.24). Similarly, participants receiving ART for < 3 years had a higher probability of viral suppression than those receiving it for 3 years or more (AOR 9.25; 95% CI 3.71–23.03). The strongest predictor of viral suppression was good adherence (AOR 13.62; 95% CI 5.85–31.74), emphasizing the importance of adherence in the prevention of viral replication (Table 2).

Discussion

Viral suppression is a key component in the care of persons living with HIV and is important in preventing the progress and transmission of the virus. Despite this, disparities still exist in key populations such as MSM, FSM, persons who inject drugs, transgender persons, and prisoners. The study aimed to determine the level of viral suppression and its factors in key populations on ART.

The current study demonstrated that 88.9% (95% CI: 85.3–91.8) of participants achieved viral suppression. This finding aligns closely with previous reports from Ethiopia (88.8%) [31], (87.2%) [30], and Uganda (85.7%) [20]. However, it is notably higher than findings from the United States (67.6%) [32] and Nigeria (64.3%) [33]. In contrast, the suppression rate observed in this study was lower than that reported in India (95.3%) [34] and Nepal (90.1%) [35]. Although relatively encouraging, the rate reported here falls short of the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) target of 95% by 2030 [36]. While variations may reflect socioeconomic factors, ART regimens, and population variations, these findings emphasize the need to improve adherence support, optimize ART, strengthen health systems, and address systemic barriers.

The WHO clinical stage was found to be associated with viral suppression, and patients at clinical stage I were 3.34 times more likely to have viral suppression than those at clinical stages II-IV (AOR 3.34, 95% CI: 1.35–8.24), as observed in studies from Ethiopia [26, 29, 37, 38]. Another study from Mozambique revealed advanced WHO clinical stage III/IV results in virological failure [20]. In the contrary, other findings showed that no significant association was observed between viral suppression and WHO clinical stages [19, 25]. Given that advanced WHO stages are associated with lower CD4 counts, higher viral loads, and more opportunistic infections, which impede immune recovery and lessen the efficacy of ART, this association is biologically plausible [39].

In the present study, among participants with duration on ART for less than three years, the likelihood of viral suppression is about 9.25 times when compared to those who are on ART for at least three years (AOR = 9.25; 95% CI: 3.71–23.03). This finding is consistent with studies from Gondar, Ethiopia [19], Uganda [24], Ghana [23], Gabon [40], Mozambique [20] and Swaziland [41]. The association between longer duration on ART and viral non-suppression has been observed in several studies and can be explained by both clinical and programmatic factors. Patients on ART for many years may experience treatment fatigue, reducing adherence over time. Poor adherence is the strongest predictor of viral non-suppression [42]. Prolonged ART use increases the likelihood of developing HIV drug resistance, especially if adherence is inconsistent. Resistance mutations compromise the effectiveness of first-line regimens, leading to non-suppression [43]. In many resource-limited settings, routine viral load monitoring is limited, and switching from first-line to second-line ART is often delayed. Patients remain on failing regimens for years, contributing to persistent viremia [44]. In light of programmatic and structural barriers, patients who have been on ART for longer durations may face changing life circumstances (mobility, stigma, and socioeconomic challenges), affecting adherence and clinical follow-up.

Adherence is a key predictor of HIV treatment success; in this study, good adherence made participants 13.62 times more likely to achieve viral suppression (AOR = 13.62; 95% CI: 5.85–31.74). This is consistent with studies in Ethiopia [26, 45, 46], and elsewhere in the world [47, 48]. Maintaining sufficient plasma drug concentrations to successfully inhibit viral replication requires high adherence rates, typically above 95% [49]. This indicates that poor adherence causes fluctuating drug levels, promoting viral replication and resistance, while sustained adherence ensures durable suppression, lowers transmission, and improves survival [50]. Poor adherence among KPs is associated with stigmatization, unstable living arrangements/mobility, mental health concerns, drug use, and confidentiality concerns. These are the same barriers to retention of patients in care and viral load monitoring [51, 52]. In comparison to the general population, KPs have significant and complex barriers to adherence that cause lower and irregular levels of viral suppression. In order to bridge the gap, it is vital to have regular KP-disaggregated viral load monitoring, the right regimen choice, and community-led integrated service delivery [53].

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations. First, it included only FSWs and prisoners with documented viral load test results. Second, due to its retrospective design, the study relied solely on information available in patients’ medical records, which restricted the ability to capture a comprehensive range of potential factors influencing viral suppression, such as behavioral, social, and structural determinants.

Conclusion

Viral suppression among key populations, especially FSWs and prisoners receiving ART in Western Ethiopia, is still low compared to the 95-95-95 targets of the WHO. Targeted interventions are recommended to be tailored to the needs of the populations. Adherence support, viral load monitoring, and the identification of treatment challenges among patients with advanced WHO stage, long duration of ART use (≥ 3 years), and poor adherence are recommended.
