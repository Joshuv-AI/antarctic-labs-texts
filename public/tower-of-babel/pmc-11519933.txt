Brain tumor classification utilizing pixel distribution and spatial dependencies higher-order statistical measurements through explainable ML models
Authors: Sharmin Akter, Md. Simul Hasan Talukder, Sohag Kumar Mondal, Mohammad Aljaidi, Rejwan Bin Sulaiman, Ahmad Abdullah Alshammari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Brain tumors are among the most fatal and devastating diseases, and they often result in a significant reduction in life expectancy. The devising of treatment plans that can extend the lives of affected individuals hinges on an accurate diagnosis of these tumors. Identifying and analyzing large volumes of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data manually proves to be both challenging and time-consuming. As a result, there exists a pressing need for a reliable machine-learning approach to accurately diagnose brain tumors, and numerous methods have already been proposed over the last decade. In this paper, a novel, comprehensive approach is proposed for identifying and classifying a given MR brain image as abnormal. Three common brain diseases, namely glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumor, are chosen as abnormal brains, and the Figshare MRI brain image dataset was collected from the Kaggle and IEEE websites. The proposed method is initiated by employing 1st-order statistics, 2nd-order statistics, and higher-order transformed (DWT) feature extraction to extract features from images. Then missing data is addressed and handled using KNNImputer, followed by the application of the ExtratreesClassifier and PCA feature selection methods to identify the most relevant features and reduce the dimensions of these features. Subsequently, the reduced features are submitted to seven machine learning models, namely RF, GB, CB, SVM, LGBM, DT, and LR. The strategy of k-fold cross-validation is utilized to enhance the performance of those models. Finally, the models are evaluated using XAI approaches, which ensure transparent decision-making processes and provide insights into the model’s predictions. Remarkably, our approach achieves the highest accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, MCC, Kappa, AUC-ROC, and R2, as well as the lowest loss, among the seven models evaluated, proving its effectiveness and applicability in multiple analytic applications relying on publicly available datasets.

Introduction

Brain is one of the most complex and vital organs in the human body, which regulates most of the human activities such as vision, emotion, cognitive, hearing, and response1. The major functional areas of the human brain in Fig. 1 are the frontal lobe represented by the number 1, the parietal lobe represented by the numbers 5 and 6, the occipital lobe represented by the number 7, and the temporal lobe marked by the number 82. The frontal lobe is responsible for speech and movement skills, as well as logic, emotions, and decision-making. Touch and spatial information are processed by the parietal lobe. The temporal lobe controls auditory information and semantic processing for speech and vision, whereas the occipital lobe is responsible for processing visual information3. Abnormalities of the brain can have significant consequences. Brain function can be disrupted by congenital abnormalities, traumatic brain traumas, and unregulated development of central brain cells4. Multiple physiological issues can arise from untreated brain disorders5. Uncontrolled cellular proliferation can lead to brain tumors and abnormalities, which is a worldwide epidemic6. Worldwide, more than 250,000 new cases of brain and nervous system cancer are reported each year. According to the WHO project, in the European region in 2030, , there will be more than 85,000 new cases, and nearly 70,000 deaths of brain and nervous system cancer were reported in 20157,8. Alarmingly, the death rate from brain tumors has risen by 300% in developing countries. In the United States alone, where more than 700,000 people are currently living with brain tumors, and cases have been increasing for the last three decades9. This highlights the urgent need for improved medical resources and support to effectively address this significant health challenge.

Brain tumors can be classified as primary brain tumors, which comprise 70% of cases and only spread within the brain, while secondary brain tumors begin in other organs such as the lung, breast, or kidney before spreading to the brain10. Primary brain tumors commonly include gliomas, meningiomas, and pituitary tumors. Gliomas originate from abnormal growth in glial cells, which comprise around 80% of the brain. Meningiomas form in the meninges of the spinal cord, the brain’s protective layer, whereas pituitary tumors evolved in the pituitary gland, which produces vital hormones. While pituitary tumors are usually benign, they are susceptible to disturbances in hormones and irreversible loss of vision11. Prompt and precise diagnosis of these specific forms of brain tumor Accurate and timely diagnosis of these specific forms of brain tumors is critical since it allows medical professionals to apply specialized treatment plans, improving patient outcomes and promoting improved disease management.

Generally, the internal structures of brain are visualized through a various kind of medical imaging technologies, including computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)12. Of all these imaging techniques, MRI is the most popular and efficient technique for diagnosing brain tumors non-invasively since it provides significant information regarding the type, size, shape, and location of the tumors in 2D and 3D forms13. However, manually analyzing these images is time-consuming, stressful, and error-prone due to the influx of patients. To deal with this issue, an automatic computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) platform could be developed in order to reduce the burden of brain tumor classification and diagnosis and to serve as a useful tool for radiologists and physicians14. In the multimodal domain of brain tumor detection and classification, automated approaches encounter several challenges that reduce their efficacy. The classification process of BT typically involves two key parts, such as feature extraction and classification. Several machine learning algorithms, such as Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Deep Neural Network (DNN), and Transfer Learning (TL), are used to brain tumor classification. Among them, CNNs, are considered one of the primary methods for identification and classification of images are widely used, such as object detection, face recognition, image classification, etc.15. Due to the limited availability of datasets in the medical domain, transfer learning has become a popular method to address the issue of small dataset sizes. Various hybrid approaches are also being employed to classify brain tumors efficiently, although these typically require significant computational resources. Another challenge is ensuring the transparency of model predictions. Our approach addresses these issues by incorporating Explainable AI (XAI).

In our proposal, we have proposed a robust Computer-Aided Diagnosis (CAD) AI-based machine learning approach that can automatically diagnose and classify brain tumors with impressive accuracy as well as reliability. The methodology we employ incorporates a wide range of techniques, such as statistical image feature analysis (first, second, and higher order), advanced missing data handling, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), feature selection using Extra Trees (ET), and the application of various machine learning algorithms, such as Decision Trees (DT), Gradient Boosting (GB), CatBoost (CB), Random Forests (RF), LightGBM (LGBM), and Logistic Regression (LR), which are further enhanced by explainable artificial intelligence techniques. The explainable AI approach ensured the fairness, trust, and transparency of the model outcomes. Our suggested method demonstrates better accuracy and performance compared to current state-of-the-art techniques.

Literature Survey

Numerous scholars have undertaken extensive research in medical diagnosis, approaching the topics from different perspectives16–18. One prominent field of focus has been computer-aided brain tumor diagnosis. Researchers are exploring plenty of methods to improve the accuracy and reliability of brain tumor diagnosis.

Binary classification of brain tumors, or no brain tumor, is one of them. Several techniques, including preprocessing and machine learning, are being employed in yes-no BT classification using the Br35h dataset19–22. Transfer learning, namely LeNet, AlexNet, VGG16, VGG19, and ResNet50, contrasting analyses of different optimizers, segmentation and classification using PG one-shot learning, and many other methods were used to classify whether BT exits or not. For example, Lamrani et al.23 introduced a straightforward approach to the automatic identification of brain tumors on the Kaggle dataset, which consists of 3000 images. The method utilized a CNN model to identify the presence or absence of the tumor (yes, tumor or not). The process involved three stages: pre-processing, feature extraction, and classification. During pre-processing, filters are applied to eliminate noise, and color moments are extracted as mean features from MRI images. The method achieved superior performance, having 96.33% accuracy, 97.93% precision, 95% sensitivity, 96.44% F1-score, and 75.72% specificity. M.S. Tahosin et al.24 developed an optimized approach using machine learning techniques to identify brain tumors on the Kaggle dataset, which consists of 3000 MRI images, categorized into two classes: “yes” and “no. This method involves preprocessing the images, extracting features, selecting the most significant ones, and tuning the model parameters. Seven traditional machine learning models, namely Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, Extreme Gradient Boosting, K Nearest Neighbors, Category Boosting, Extra Trees, and Naive Bayes, were employed in this study to detect BT. This approach achieved an accuracy of 98.0% after thorough hyperparameter optimization.

But the accurate identification and classification of brain tumor patterns and types is crucial for medical experts to determine the precise location and offer the right course of treatment. In an attempt to achieve this goal, ML engineers and researchers have initiated efforts to diagnose multiple types of brain tumors. This study has access to two publicly available datasets: Figshare and Kaggle. Figshare’s dataset has three classifications, while Kaggle’s has four. Consequently, some research concentrates on the three-class Figshare dataset, while others focus on the four-class Kaggle dataset. Following, Amaliah et al.25 analyzed a four-class Kaggle brain tumor dataset (3264 images) and employed Random Forest, SVM, and ANN classifiers to identify BT. The research approach utilized the Canny method for image segmentation and extracted features through the Humoment method. The RF Classifier achieved an accuracy of 0.7, a weighted precision of 0.55, a weighted recall of 0.7, and a weighted F1 score of 0.59. The SVM demonstrated an accuracy of 0.71, a weighted precision of 0.5, a weighted recall of 0.71, and a weighted F1 score of 0.59. Lastly, the ANN attained an accuracy of 0.62, a weighted precision of 0.6, a weighted recall of 0.62, and a weighted F1 score of 0.61.

Conventional classifiers have fallen short of expectations due to a shortcoming in feature extraction. CNN and DCNN surpass these constraints by being superior at extracting intricate features from images. In its continuation, Aloraini et al.26 proposed a hybrid model that extracts local as well as global information simultaneously for an effective BT classification on two publicly available datasets, namely the two-class BraTS 2018 dataset and the three-class Figshare dataset. They employed a hybrid transformer-enhanced convolutional neural network (TECNN)-based model for BT classification, where the CNN was applied for local feature extraction and the transformer was employed to extract global features. This study achieved an average accuracy of 96.75% and 99.10%, respectively. Tarun et al.27 developed a deep learning model (MultiFeNet) based on a convolutional neural network to classify brain tumors on the 3064 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced images with three types of brain tumors. Multi-scale feature scaling was employed to extract features from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images. The approach obtained 96.4% sensitivity, 96.4% F1-score, 96.4% precision, and 96.4% accuracy on the brain tumor Figshare dataset.

Many applications face challenges when employing CNN and DCNN due to their substantial computational resources and large dataset requirements. Transfer learning has emerged as a solution to these challenges, particularly in the context of CNN. Transfer learning is being employed exponentially in recent research to address these challenges, which is a significant change in this field. Following that, Mohammad et al.28 employed CNN and CNN-based inception (V3, EfficientNetB4, VGG19) transfer learning methods for brain tumor classification on three-class Figshare MRI images. VGG19 achieved the best performance, having 98% accuracy, 97% F1-score, 98% precision, 98% recall, and 99% AUC. Talukder et al.29 applied fine-tuned transfer learning algorithms, including Xception, ResNet50V2, InceptionResNetV2, and DenseNet201, to the three-class Figshare MRI Brain Tumor dataset and achieved accuracy scores of 99.40%, 99.68%, 99.36%, and 98.72% for those models, respectively. Shaimaa et al.30 conducted a comparative study of five fine-tuned pre-trained models, namely GoogleNet, AlexNet, ShuffleNet, SqueezeNet, and NASNet-Mobile, to classify BT on a three-class Figshare dataset and proposed a hybrid approach that incorporated majority voting techniques to combine the outputs of the five models. This approach achieved superior performance, having 99.31% accuracy, 99.92% precision, 98.30% recall, and 98.9% F1 score. Daniel et al.31 applied fine-tuned pre-trained models, namely Custom, VGG, ResNet, Xception, DenseNet, MobileNet, EfficientNet, and ConvNeXt, to identify and classify brain tumors using two different datasets, namely the three-class Figshare and four-class Kaggle datasets. Those models achieved accuracy scores of 96.7% of Custom, 97.4% of VGG, 98.4% of ResNet, 96.4% of Xception, 98% of DenseNet, 98% of MobileNet, 98.4% of EfficientNet, and 95.4% of ConvNeXt. But pruning techniques were employed in a few transfers learning studies. Cuneyt et al.32 added the technique and established a customized innovative CNN model to accurately diagnose brain tumors on the 3064 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced images with three kinds of brain tumors. Firstly, they applied a comprehensive preprocessing procedure to all images within the dataset. Secondly, they utilized the ADASYN method to address the class imbalance of the dataset and employed the network DenseConv4Block33 as a pruning technique to reduce unnecessary parameters, connections, or weights. Lastly, they embodied a pre-trained model such as DenseNet201 with an Avg-TopK pooling layer for enhanced feature extraction. This approach achieved 99.53% accuracy, 99.53% precision, 99.53% recall, and 99.53% f1-score.

Additionally, other hybrid approaches have acquired significant popularity for enhancing the performance of brain tumor classification. Moving forward, S. U. R. Khan et al.33 proposed a hybrid-net model to classify the BT of a three-class Figshare dataset. The DenseNet169 model was used for feature extraction of brain MRI images, and RF, SVM, and XGBoost classifiers were applied to classify brain tumors based on the extracted features. Overall accuracy was found to be 95.10% in this study. Haq et al.34 presented a CNN-LSTM model to classify BT using a three-class Figshare dataset. CNN was used for feature extraction, and LSTM was used to get the domain of the features. The approaches achieved 99.22%. The same author also proposed a ResNet-CNN model incorporating augmentation and some preprocessing methods on the same dataset in a different study35. The ResNet-CNN achieved 99.90% accuracy, 100% specificity, and 89.60% sensitivity. Usharani et al.36 developed an ELM approach that integrated image preprocessing, segmentation, feature extraction, and classification. Hybrid contrast enhancement-based pre-processing technique, fuzzy C-means-based segmentation, traditional feature extraction technique, and relevant feature (shapes, colors, textures, and so on) selection improved the performance of the ELM classifier. The approach attained accuracy, precision, and recall of 99.42%, 99.75%, and 99.28% on the Kaggle dataset and 98.47%, 98.59%, and 98.74% on the Figshare dataset, respectively.

In a very recent study, Vimala et al.37 employed five fine-tuned Efficient Net models (EfficientNetB0 to EfficientNetB4) to classify a three-class Figshare brain tumor dataset. The author added top layers and a fully connected layer with pre-trained models and evaluated integrating augmentation and gradient cam. EfficientNetB2 yielded test accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores of 99.06%, 98.73%, 99.13%, and 98.79%, respectively. Saurabh et al.38 proposed an explainable brain tumor identification and classification approach by utilizing pre-trained models. A conditional generative adversarial network was utilized to deal with the overfitting and data unbalancing. Later, MobileNet, InceptionResNet, EfficientNet, and VGGNet were employed to detect BT. Additionally, layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) is utilized to interpret the model outcome. InceptionResNetV2 achieved a superior outcome, having 99.6% training accuracy, 99.2% validation accuracy, and 99.0% testing accuracy. A summary of the literature review is shown in Table 1.

From our literature review, it’s found that conventional machine learning approaches have expressed average performance in brain tumor classification. Various sophisticated methods, like CNNs, TL, DCNNs, and hybrid models, have demonstrated potential; nevertheless, they frequently require significant processing power and sophisticated hardware. We provide a unique strategy that combines conventional machine learning with remarkable performance to address these issues.

Previous research has not considered important factors such as PCA, optimal feature selection, imputation, 1st, 2nd, and higher-order statistical feature extraction, and advanced classical machine learning models. Notable is also the lack of cross-validation and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques for evaluating model robustness. In our research, we have addressed these gaps comprehensively.

Dataset description

The brain MRI dataset, Figshare dataset, has been collected from a trustworthy IEEE repository that was developed in 2017 by Jun Cheng et al.39,40. The dataset consists of 3064 T1-weighted contrast-enhanced images of the brains of 233 patients who had one of three brain tumor types identified: pituitary tumor (930 slices), glioma (1426 slices), or meningioma (708 slices). The images are stored in. mat format. Each image file has a structure that contains the patient ID (PID), image data, tumor border, and relevant image information, such as the label (1 for glioma, 2 for meningioma, and 3 for pituitary tumor). Moreover, the dataset contains color images with a resolution of 512 by 512 pixels, where each pixel contains three values, one for each color channel (red, green, and blue). Figures 2 and 3 illustrate the distribution and visualization of the dataset, respectively. Figure 2 shows the unbalanced distributions of the images.

Proposed methodology

The architecture of our proposed approach is delineated in Fig. 4. Initially, the feature extraction process is initiated. Two distinct methodologies for feature extraction are employed on the MRI Figshare dataset: the statistical feature extraction method encompassing 1st and 2nd order statistics, and the higher-order transformed-based DWT feature extraction method. These methods yield disparate sets of features. In the first method, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is utilized on DWT features to prognosticate pertinent data and reduce dimensionality. Subsequently, seven prominent machine learning models, namely RF, GB, CB, SVM, LGBM, LR, and DT, are applied for the classification of BT. The performance of the models is evaluated through various metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Kappa, R2, confusion matrix, Area Under the Curve (AUC), and Roc curve. The models undergo validation through 5-fold cross-validation, and explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) techniques are employed to interpret the decision-making process of the models. However, the performance achieved with this approach falls short of expectations. Thus, the study proceeds to the second approach, where emphasis is placed on 1st and 2nd-order statistical features.

In the second approach, 34 significant features, combining 1st and 2nd-order statistics, are extracted. Subsequently, exploratory data analysis, correlation analysis, and handling of missing data utilizing KNNImputer, along with the Extra Trees feature selection method, are conducted as preprocessing stages. The Extra Trees feature selection technique identifies the 27 most relevant attributes. Following a similar procedure as before, the aforementioned seven models are employed for BT classification. The models are subjected to 5-fold cross-validation, model evaluation, and model explanation utilizing the LIME approach. Remarkably, the second approach surpasses the previous study in the classification of BT into three classes.

Features extraction

Feature extraction is a crucial part of brain tumor classification. The features basically capture the unique properties of BT images41. In our study, we extracted features using two different techniques. The primary is the combination of 1st and 2nd order 34 statistical features. The 1st and 2nd order features are Mean, Standard Deviation, Maximum, Minimum, Skewness, Kurtosis, Entropy, Median, Mode, Data Range, Interquartile Range, Variance, Coefficient of Variation, 25th Percentile, 50th Percentile (Median), 75th Percentile, Sum of Squares, Geometric Mean, Harmonic Mean, Standard Error of the Mean, Covariance, Correlation Chi-Square Statistic, P-value (Chi-Square Test), t-statistic, P-value (t-test), F-statistic, P-value (F-test), Cramer’s V, Effect, Size (Cohen\‘s d), Durbin-Watson Statistic, Jarque-Bera Statistic, P-value (Jarque-Bera Test). The second is the higher-order transformed based on 980,000,000 features. All the features were extracted using the NumPy, SciPy, Pandas, and Pywavelets libraries of Python.

Feature selection

Feature selection is a crucial step in machine learning to choose the most relevant features and reduce dimension48. In our study, the Extra Trees (ET) Classifier technique was utilized to select relevant features. The Extra Trees approach generates several decision trees by utilizing the entire training dataset. It selects split rules for nodes based on random feature subsets and cut points. This is a cyclical procedure that continues until leaf nodes are reached. The predictions made by each tree are combined by a majority vote. By calculating the Gini coefficient, users can select the top k features to be entered into the categorization process49. In the second approach, 27 features were selected using ET based on their importance in BT classification from 34 extracted features. Features that showed up more often in splits with good performance were considered more significant. Since the selected 27 features frequently lead to splits that significantly improve prediction accuracy, we chose them in our study. The importance of those selected features is shown in Fig. 6.

Principle component analysis

PCA is an unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique that constructs relevant features/variables through linear (linear PCA) or non-linear (kernel PCA) combinations of the original variables (features)50. The construction of relevant features is achieved by linearly transforming correlated variables into a smaller number of uncorrelated variables. This is done by projecting the original data into the reduced PCA space using the eigenvectors of the covariance/correlation matrix of the principal components (PCs)51. PCA minimizes the mean square error between the original and projected data by applying an orthogonal transformation that relates the principal coordinate system to the original one. The successive step of PCA is illustrated on Fig. 7. In our study, PCA is employed for dimensionality reduction, involving the projection of the data into a space where the first axis of the new coordinate system aligns with the direction exhibiting the largest variance in the data.

K-fold cross validation

K-fold cross validation is crucial for robustly assessing model performance in machine learning. For k-fold cross validation, the dataset is divided into k segments, with one-fold reserved for testing and the model trained on the remaining k-1 folds in each iteration. Consequently, the procedure is repeated until every dataset has been analyzed. The K-Fold CV results are typically repeated using the MLM’s mean score53. Figure 8. illustrates k-fold cross validation where k = 5 that are utilized in our work. The KFold() function from the Scikit-learn library was used to perform fivefold cross-validation for training and testing purposes. This approach splits the dataset into five subsets, where each subset is used as a test set once while the remaining four subsets are used for training.

Machine learning models

A machine learning model is an algorithm or statistical model that employs data to identify patterns and relationships in order to generate predictions54. In this work, seven separate models namely SVM, RF, LR, CB, GB, LGBM, DT were employed for training and predictions based on different k-fold cross validation.

SVM

The supervised machine learning algorithm SVM is applied to regression and classification problems. The primary goal of SVM is to find the hyperplane in the feature space that best divides the classes. This hyperplane is chosen to maximize the margin, which is the distance between the hyperplane and the closest data points from each class, also known as support vectors, as seen in Fig. 955. SVM was selected for its robustness against overfitting, and efficiency in high-dimensional spaces with a distinct margin of separation.

Fig. 9Support vector machine (SVM).

Support vector machine (SVM).

RF

The Random Forest Classifier stands as a stalwart in the realm of machine learning, boasting resilience and accuracy. Its essence lies in an ensemble of decision trees, each branching out with its own insights. Through collective wisdom, the forest harmonizes these individual projections, culminating in a final prediction. This democratic voting process, where the most frequent class reigns supreme, underpins the robustness of the model56. Since RF effectively manages huge datasets, minimizes overfitting by averaging, and offers feature significance, we included it.

LR

Logistic Regression emerges as a statistical powerhouse tailored for binary classification quandaries, where the outcome variable resides within a categorical realm of only two possibilities. With precision honed for this specific terrain, LR navigates the intricacies of dichotomous outcomes, offering a reliable compass for predictive modeling.

DT

The decision tree emerges as a visual marvel, akin to a flow-chart graced with the elegance of a tree’s branching limbs. Within its structure, each internal node serves as a pivotal “test” on an attribute, while every branch embodies the diverging outcomes of these tests. Like leaves adorning its boughs, each leaf node bears the weight of a distinct class label. Amongst the pantheon of categorization algorithms, the decision tree reigns supreme, celebrated for its simplicity in implementation and unparalleled ease of comprehension57.

GB

Gradient Boosting is an ensemble learning method that involves gradually adding weak learners (typically decision trees) to the ensemble to create a strong model. Each new model is trained to fix the errors made by the existing ensemble. By adding new models with the appropriate direction and degree of modifications, it minimizes a loss function58. The ensemble nature of the model is being used widely.

LGBM and CB

LightGBM (LGBM) and CatBoost (CB) are powerful gradient boosting frameworks. LGBM focuses on speed and scalability through the use of effective tree-based learning with histogram-based algorithms. It also facilitates GPU learning and parallel processing. Conversely, CB focuses on automatically managing categorical features with a particular technique that turns them into numerical values during training, improving accuracy without requiring a lot of preprocessing59. For those advantages, we employed the models.

Explainable AI

Explainable AI (XAI) is the development of artificial intelligence (AI) systems that provide a clear and human-understandable explanation for the decisions made by AI and machine learning models. XAI approaches enhance trust and confidence by transparently revealing AI model decisions and any biases. They employ model-agnostic approaches, visualization tools, and feature importance analysis, among other strategies, to provide accountability and transparency across applications60. In our work, we employed the Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) XAI technique to offer insights into our AI model’s decision-making. LIME supports understanding the model’s predictions by approximating the behavior of the model around specific instances.

In the process of comprehensive evaluation, the model’s overall performance is thoroughly assessed. The outcome analysis delves into various aspects, investigating accuracy and performance parameters for each model. The choice of the best model depends on a thorough performance study that takes recall, accuracy, precision, and F1-score, AUC- ROC, MCC, Kappa, Loss, R2 into account. Result and discussion

For the tasks of feature extraction, preprocessing, training, testing, and evaluation of brain tumor classification techniques, we employed the Google Colab environment in combination with the Python 3.8.10 version. Our approach focused on utilizing pixel distribution and spatial dependencies to compute higher-order statistical measurements through explainable machine learning models. In addition to additional Python libraries like Pandas, Scikit-Learn, Matplotlib, NumPy, and OpenCV, TensorFlow version 2.9.2 was used to complete the implementation. The high-performance NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB GPU that is accessible on the Google Colab platform was utilized for computational workloads.

The study adopted a comprehensive approach for brain tumor classification, integrating feature extraction, missing data handling, feature selection, and classifier evaluation within a machine learning framework. Initially, we applied Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) for feature extraction, focusing on higher-order statistical features from the Figshare brain tumor dataset. This yielded distinct feature sets, which were then subjected to Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for feature selection, aiming to retain the most relevant data and reduce dimensionality. We evaluated the performance of seven classifiers—Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), CatBoost (CB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), LightGBM (LGBM), Decision Tree (DT), and Logistic Regression (LR)—using k-fold cross-validation on the DWT features. The results for each model are detailed in Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10. To provide a comprehensive assessment of model performance, we included confusion matrices and ROC curves, which highlight the models’ success and shortcomings across different categories. Each cell in the confusion matrix reflects the combination of predicted and actual classes, facilitating a quick evaluation of model accuracy, while the ROC curve assesses the model’s ability to distinguish between classes. Figures 10 and 11 illustrate the confusion matrices and ROC curves for the selected models. Notably, the CatBoost model achieved the highest performance, with an average accuracy and recall of 86.68%±1.56%, precision of 86.71%±1.60%, F1-score of 86.67%±1.59%, AUC-ROC of 96.21%±0.74%, MCC of 79.14%±2.33%, Kappa of 79.11%±2.34%, R² of 62.27%±5.53%, and a loss of 28.00%±4.17%. The confusion matrix for CatBoost in Fig. 10c shows the highest classification accuracy, while the ROC curve in Fig. 11c aligns closest to the (0,1) coordinate, indicating superior performance.

Despite these results, the DWT-based approach with higher-order features did not show substantial performance improvements. Consequently, we employed a second approach utilizing first and second-order pixel and spatial dependency features, aiming to enhance classification outcomes.

Conclusion and future work

Our experiment was structured around a comprehensive framework aimed at extracting relevant insights from our dataset and developing robust predictive models. We initiated the process by employing two distinct feature extraction methods, including the DWT method and the statistical features method (1st order and 2nd order), each designed to extract different aspects of the underlying structure of the data. This step was crucial for converting the raw data into a suitable format for machine learning analysis, to increase the representativeness of our model. Subsequently, we carefully address and handle missing data using KNNImputer to ensure the integrity and reliability of our analysis. Following that, we applied two-type feature selection method that strikes a delicate balance between reducing dimensionality to increase model performance and collecting relevant information needed for accurate predictions. By using feature selection methods, we were able to identify and grade the elements that had the highest predictive value, which helped us to simplify our models and enhance their performances. Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation, providing robust estimates of predictive performance across different data samples. Evaluation metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, MCC, Kappa, R2, and AUC-ROC, were calculated to measure each model’s ability to detect the underlying data patterns. Our work not only achieved high accuracy but also provided valuable insight into predictive factors through explainable AI techniques, emphasizing the importance of individual characteristics in prediction. Our experiment reflects a holistic approach to data analysis and model development, integrating advanced methodology and automated validation methods to achieve outstanding performance. These results have significant implications for real-world applications, where accurate and interpretable predictive models are essential for informed decision-making and problem solving. The study has several limitations, for instance relying on publicly available datasets from kaggle dataset that could not capture real-world variability that may affect generalizability. Additionally, we have used KNNImputer for handling missing data, and this assumes accurate imputation, but it might not always be. Thirdly, we did not sufficiently test the extensive robustness and overfitting of the model in different environments and data sources. Future researchers may address those limitations.
