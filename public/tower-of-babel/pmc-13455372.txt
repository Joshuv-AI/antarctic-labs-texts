A nurse-led palliative care programme for women receiving palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer
Authors: Sulleh Gbande, Johanna Elizabeth Maree, Oluchukwu Loveth Obiora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, breast cancer is the most common cancer among women, with 2.3 million women newly diagnosed in 2020. In Africa, most women present with advanced disease necessitating nurse-led palliative care interventions. This study pilot tested a nurse-led palliative care programme for women receiving palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer at a teaching hospital in the Volta Region of Ghana.MethodsAn intervention design and pre-test post-test approach was used. The primary outcome focused on unmet palliative care needs and secondary outcomes included pain, quality of life, and spiritual needs, measured by means of the Symptoms Distress Scale (SDC), Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpNQ), and European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire – Breast Cancer Module 45 items (EORTC QLQ-BR45). Census sampling entered 31 women in the programme but only 24 completed it. Descriptive statistics were used to analyzed the data and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with significance set at p ≤ 0.05, compared the medians of the two samples. The study was registered with Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR202509735988120) on September 29th, 2025.ResultsResults showed positive outcomes post-intervention. Symptom distress decreased from x̄ 2.98 to x̄ 1.97, pain severity reduced from x̄ 9.34 to x̄ 2.75, and quality of life improved from x̄ 2.79 to x̄ 1.94. Spiritual needs increased from x̄ 1.04 to x̄ 3.00.ConclusionThe study provided evidence that a nurse-led palliative care programme, based on the care needs of women with advanced breast cancer receiving palliative chemotherapy in the Volta Region of Ghana, lessened symptom distress, pain and improved quality of life.Keywords: Breast Cancer, Palliative Chemotherapy, Palliative care, Nurse-led palliative care programme, Ghana

Background

Globally, breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women, with an estimated 2.3 million women newly diagnosed in 2020 [1]. In Africa, the breast cancer incidence is lower compared to high-income countries, but mortality rates are higher due to late-stage presentation and limited access to early detection and treatment services [2, 3]. Sub-Saharan Africa faces particular challenges with breast cancer. The region accounts for a significant proportion of the cancer burden in Africa, with late-stage diagnosis being prevalent [4]. In addition, palliative care services are insufficient, often provided by non-governmental organizations and community health workers rather than being fully integrated into national health systems [5]. This scarcity of services results in high symptom burden and limited access to pain management and other supportive care [6].

In Ghana, breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer deaths among women [7]. The incidence rate is increasing, with approximately 4,600 newly diagnosed women and 2,000 deaths reported in 2020 [1]. Palliative care services are in the early stages of development. The few available services are concentrated in urban areas, leaving many rural patients without access to necessary care [8]. Efforts are being made to integrate palliative care into the national health system, but challenges such as funding, education, and cultural acceptance remain significant barriers [9].

Providing palliative care is essential as it aims to enhance the quality of life for patients and their families facing life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering. It involves early identification, impeccable assessment, and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial, and spiritual [10]. Palliative chemotherapy, while often essential in managing symptoms and progression of disease, can lead to a range of unrelenting life experiences for patients such as fatigue, nausea and vomiting, pain, anxiety and depression, social isolation and low self-esteem [11]. However, poverty can hinder consenting to palliative chemotherapy and compliance with treatment as many households are not able to afford the costs involved [1].

Traditional models of palliative care may not always adequately address the specific needs of patients undergoing palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer [12] especially in sub-Saharan Africa where tradition, stigma and poverty are barriers to palliative care [1] and necessitate a tailored nurse-led palliative care programme.

In palliative care, several service delivery models exist, including physician-led, multidisciplinary team-led, community health worker-led, and nurse-led approaches [13]. The choice of model is typically influenced by workforce capacity, health system organization, and contextual care needs [14]. Physician-led models are commonly used in specialist oncology and tertiary settings, while multidisciplinary models integrate physicians, nurses, social workers, psychologists, and spiritual care providers to address the complex needs of patients with life-limiting illnesses [14, 15] .

However, in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Ghana, access to specialist palliative care remains constrained by shortages of trained palliative care physicians, limited oncology infrastructure, and inequitable distribution of healthcare professionals, particularly in regional and rural areas [16–18]. In the Volta Region of Ghana, where this study was conducted, palliative care services are still developing, and nurses often serve as the primary providers of supportive and palliative care due to these systemic workforce gaps. This situation makes nurse-led models a pragmatic and contextually appropriate strategy for improving access to palliative care for patients receiving chemotherapy for advanced breast cancer.

Globally, nurse-led palliative care programmes have gained increasing recognition as an effective and sustainable approach to addressing the multidimensional needs of patients with advanced cancer [19, 20]. Nurses are well positioned for this role due to their continuous patient contact, holistic training, and central involvement in symptom monitoring, patient advocacy, and care coordination [19]. Nurse-led programmes are typically delivered by oncology nurses, palliative care nurses, or advanced practice nurses and include symptom management, psychosocial and emotional support, patient and family education, and coordination of referrals and services [21, 22].

Evidence from high-income countries indicates that nurse-led palliative care interventions can improve symptom control, quality of life, patient satisfaction, communication, and continuity of care, while potentially reducing avoidable hospital admissions and healthcare utilization [20, 23]. Despite this growing evidence base, there remains limited research on nurse-led palliative care models in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in relation to women receiving palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer. Furthermore, little is known about how such interventions can be effectively adapted and implemented in resource-constrained oncology settings where nurses often assume expanded clinical roles due to workforce shortages.

In addition, evidence on the specific palliative care needs of women with advanced breast cancer in the Ghanaian context remains limited, and context-specific, nurse-led interventions tailored to these needs are scarce. It is against this background that the researchers sought to develop, validate, and pilot test a nurse-led palliative care programme for women receiving palliative chemotherapy in a teaching hospital in the Volta Region of Ghana. This study formed part of a broader programme of work and was guided by the following research question: Would a nurse-led palliative care programme, based on the care needs of women with advanced breast cancer receiving palliative chemotherapy in the Volta Region of Ghana, improve symptom distress, pain, quality of life, and spiritual well-being?

Methods

Study design

The study adopted an intervention design and a pretest-post-test method to assess the outcomes of the nurse-led palliative care programme. Intervention research is valuable in identifying effective strategies that work better improve health outcomes [24], particularly through the piloting of a newly developed programme [25]. By utilizing a pretest-post-test approach, the researchers were able to determine changes in the variables despite the absence of a control group [26].

Study setting

This study was conducted at Ho Teaching Hospital located in the Ho Municipality, the capital of the Volta Region of Ghana. The Volta Region is situated in the south-eastern corridor of Ghana and shares borders with the Republic of Togo to the east, the Eastern Region to the west, and the Northern and Oti Regions to the north. The region is characterised by a mix of rural and peri-urban settlements, with a predominantly agrarian population and limited access to specialised oncology and palliative care services outside the regional capital. As a result, most advanced healthcare services, including cancer care, are concentrated in Ho, making the study site a major referral hub for both urban and rural populations within and beyond the Volta Region. The hospital serves as the principal referral facility for the region and receives patients from district hospitals, health centres, and private facilities across the Volta Region and adjoining parts of the Oti Region. The facility was purposively selected because it is the main provider of palliative chemotherapy services in the Volta Region. The oncology unit receives inpatient and a dedicated day-care unit for outpatient chemotherapy administration. The unit is staffed with oncology nurse specialist and consultant surgeons. The hospital is also affiliated with the University of Health and Allied Sciences (UHAS), which strengthens its role as both a clinical and academic institution. This partnership supports clinical training, research, and capacity building in oncology and palliative care, thereby contributing to the development of specialised human resources in the region. In Ghana, palliative care services are still in a developmental phase and are largely concentrated in tertiary and teaching hospitals located in major urban centres such as Accra, Kumasi, Cape Coast and Ho [27].

Study Population

According to Berndt, study population refers to the entire group of individuals, objects, or events that a researcher is interested in studying and from which a sample may be drawn [28]. The population for this study consisted of all women aged 18 years and above undergoing palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer at the teaching hospital in the Volta Region of Ghana.

Sampling and sample size

Sampling involves selecting a subset of the population, or individuals chosen from the entire population, to represent the entire group for the purpose of making inferences and drawing conclusions [28]. However, because the size of the population for this study is relatively small, a census approach was used. Census approach is a data collection method where attempt is made to collect data from every member of a defined population. This approach is suitable mostly for obtaining highly accurate data from a relatively small population [29]. Data were collected from all women who met the inclusion criteria: diagnosed with breast cancer, receiving palliative chemotherapy, aged 18 years or older, with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of three or more, indicating significant functional limitation requiring palliative care, and who volunteered to participate in the study.

A total of 31 women (N = 31) were recruited and enrolled in the programme. This sample size was deemed adequate to assess the feasibility and potential effectiveness of the intervention [30].Twenty-four (N = 24) participants completed the post-test, resulting in an attrition rate 22.6%. The reasons for attrition included death and fatigue.

Procedure

Developing and implementing the palliative care programme

Based on the palliative care needs of the sample (reported elsewhere), the palliative care programme, developed by the first author, was primarily focused on providing informational support but also included emotional and spiritual support, as well as counseling when needed. After validation of the programme using a group of experts in palliative care, which included patients receiving palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer, the programme was implemented in the medical oncology department of the study setting between September and November 2023. The programme was facilitated by a trained palliative care nurse with over 15 years of bedside clinical nursing experience and 10 years of university teaching experience in palliative care. The trainer’s doctoral supervision was undertaken by a distinguished professor in palliative care, who also serve as co-author of the study. The programme was delivered over a three-month period consisting of five modules. Each module was delivered twice, with a one-week interval between repeated sessions. This resulted in a total of 10 sessions. Each session lasted between 45 and 90 min and was implemented over a three-month period. The average number of attendees per session was 20, with an overall attendance rate of 93.3%.

The first author facilitated the programme, summarizing the previous session before continuing with the scheduled content. Participants were given time for questions, comments, and experience sharing. The sessions covered the following topics:

Recruitment

The first author collaborated with the nurse in-charge of the oncology unit to identify women undergoing palliative treatment for breast cancer and invited them to join the study. All women meeting the inclusion criteria were invited to participate. A total of 31 women (N = 31) enrolled in the program. This sample size was deemed adequate to assess the outcomes of the intervention [30].

Data collection method and instruments

A researcher administered questionnaires consisting of a demographic data collection sheet developed by the first author and four validated questionnaires, the Symptoms Distress Scale (SDS), Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire – Breast Cancer Module 45 items (EORTC QLQ-BR45) and the Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpQN) were used with permission. The demographic data sheet asked questions about age, marital status, number of pregnancies, level of education, religion and ethnicity.

Symptom distress

The Symptom Distress Scale (SDS), developed by McCorkle and Young in 1978 and further refined by McCorkle colleagues in 1998 [31, 32], measures the level of discomfort patients experience due to various symptoms they perceive. The scale includes ten symptoms: nausea, mood, appetite, insomnia, pain, mobility, fatigue, bowel pattern, concentration, and appearance. It consists of 13 items scored using a 5-point Likert scale, where a score of 5 represents the highest level of distress. A score of 3 or higher indicates significant distress. A total score of 25 or more reflects moderate distress, while scores above 33 suggest severe distress that requires immediate intervention. Previous studies have established the scale’s reliability through test-retest, reproducibility, and internal consistency [33]. The SDS consists of a list of symptoms that individuals may experience, and participants are asked to rate the severity and distress associated with each symptom. The scoring process typically involves assigning numerical values to the responses. For instance, a Likert scale is used, where participants rate each symptom from 0 to 4 (0 = no distress, 4 = severe distress). The individual symptom scores are then summed to produce a total score. A higher total score reflects greater overall symptom distress. Researchers or clinicians may establish cutoff points or categories to interpret the severity of distress. These categories may include “mild,” “moderate,” and “severe,” determined by predefined score ranges.

Pain experience

The Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) is a self-administered questionnaire designed primarily to assess cancer-related pain. The BPI is available in two versions: the short form (comprising nine items) and the long form (comprising 17 items). The short form is most commonly used for measuring cancer pain and has demonstrated strong reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha for the pain severity scale ranging from 0.80 to 0.89, and for the pain interference scale, it ranges from 0.89 to 0.91[34]. The study utilized the short form of the BPI questionnaire, which is scored and analyzed as follows: Patients rate their pain on a numeric rating scale (NRS) from 0 to 10, where 0 represents “no pain” and 10 represents “pain as bad as you can imagine.” Pain severity is assessed in four categories: “worst,” “least,” “average,” and “right now” pain experienced over the past 24 h. Patients rate the degree to which pain has interfered with various aspects of their life using a 0 to 10 NRS, where 0 indicates “no interference” and 10 represents “complete interference.” Common interference items include general activity, mood, walking ability, normal work, relationships with others, sleep, enjoyment of life, and overall activity. Each item is scored separately based on the patient’s responses. The individual scores can be analyzed to assess specific aspects of pain severity and interference. The severity scores (worst, least, average, and right now) are often averaged to calculate an overall pain severity score. The interference scores for each item are typically averaged to provide an overall pain interference score. This reflects the overall impact of pain on various aspects of a patient’s daily life. Higher scores indicate greater pain severity or interference. Researchers and clinicians can use these scores to assess how pain affects a patient’s daily functioning and quality of life.

Quality of life

The EORTC QLQ-BR45 is a widely used tool for assessing the quality of life (QoL) in patients. This questionnaire module is specifically designed to evaluate various aspects related to breast cancer. It includes nine multi-item scales that assess body image, sexual functioning, breast satisfaction, side effects of systemic therapy, arm symptoms, breast symptoms, symptoms associated with endocrine therapy, skin and mucous membrane symptoms, and endocrine-related sexual symptoms. The questionnaire also contains individual items focused on sexual enjoyment, future outlook, and emotional distress due to hair loss. The scoring methodology for the QLQ-BR45 follows the same principles as those used for the functional and symptom scales and individual items in the QLQ-C30 [35]. The EORTC QLQ-BR45 can be analyzed as a or grouped into the following categories whole [36],1. Functional Scale, 2. Symptom Scales/Items - Systemic Therapy Side-Effects 3. Symptom Scales/Items - Arm Symptoms, 4. Symptom Scales/Items - Breast Symptoms 5. Target Therapy Scale - Endocrine Therapy Symptoms, 6. Target Therapy Scale - Skin Mucosis Symptoms, 7. Target Therapy Scale - Endocrine Sexual Symptoms, and 8. Satisfaction Scales. The study grouped the scale into 8 groups as proposed by [36, 37]. The EORTC QLQ-BR45 scale uses a 1–4 scoring system. Lower scores indicate better quality of life, where 1 = “no distress at all” and 4 = “severe distress.” However, the global health/quality of life item uses a revised 7-point linear analogue scale to measure QoL, ranging from “very poor” to “excellent.” The Cronbach’s alpha for the scale ranges from 0.68 to 0.95 [36].

Spiritual needs

The Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpNQ) for adults, developed by [38], is a self-report tool designed to assess the spiritual needs of individuals facing advanced or terminal cancer diagnoses. The questionnaire consists of a series of statements, each representing a specific spiritual need. Respondents are asked to evaluate both the presence and intensity of these needs in their lives. The internal consistency of the SpNQ ranges from 0.74 to 0.92. This standardized measure has been translated into several languages and is widely used as a valid and reliable instrument to assess various spiritual needs in patients with cancer, chronic illnesses, the elderly, adolescents, and even healthy individuals [39, 40]. The questionnaire serves dual purposes: as a diagnostic tool and a research instrument, with optional items. The SpNQ identifies four primary factors: Religious (alpha = 0.92): Includes activities such as praying with or for others, attending religious ceremonies, reading spiritual/religious texts, and seeking connection with a higher presence (e.g., God, angels). Inner Peace (alpha = 0.82): Involves desires for serenity and tranquility, appreciation of natural beauty, finding inner peace, discussing fears and concerns, and receiving support from others. Existential (Reflection/Meaning) (alpha = 0.82): Focuses on contemplating one’s life, discussing the meaning of life and suffering, addressing unresolved life issues, and exploring the concept of an afterlife. Actively Giving/Generativity (alpha = 0.74): Emphasizes the desire to provide comfort to others, share personal life experiences, and ensure that one’s life holds meaning and value [40]. Respondents indicate the presence of respective needs (yes/no) and rate their perceived strength using a 4-point scale: 0 = Not at all, 1 = Somewhat, 2 = Strong, 3 = Very strong. The SpNQ consists of 30 items, each assessed using a 4-point Likert scale. The overall analysis is conducted based on the aggregated responses [38].

To collect the pretest data, the first author, who is fluent in the languages spoken at the facility, administered all five questionnaires at baseline, prior to participants entering the nurse-led palliative care programme, with the assistance of a field worker. The post-test questionnaires (Symptoms Distress Scale (SDS), Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC QLQ-BR45) and the Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpQN)) were administered on the day participants completed the programme.

Data management and analysis

The completed questionnaires were collected and stored in an envelope before being assigned numerical identifiers. The data were then entered into an Excel spreadsheet, cleaned, and imported into the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the data, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test were conducted to determine significant differences between the variables [41]. To evaluate the overall effects of the nurse-led palliative care programme, a completer-only approach was employed, including only the 24 participants who completed all required study assessments (BPI, SD, EORTC QLQ-BR45, and Spiritual Needs) in the final data analysis. This approach was chosen to avoid partial data from participants who did not complete all assessments, as such data could introduce bias and compromise the validity of the findings, particularly given the relatively small sample size (n = 24). Furthermore, as a pilot study, the focus was on assessing the potential effectiveness of the intervention under optimal conditions, which further justified the use of the completer-only approach [42].

Validity and reliability

The Symptom Distress Scale (SDS), Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpNQ), and EORTC QLQ-BR45 were employed as internationally validated tools widely applied in oncology and palliative care research, with their construct and content validity well established in multiple settings [39, 43, 44]. Permission to use these instruments was obtained, and standardized administration protocols were followed throughout the study. To ensure contextual appropriateness, the second and third authors who are research experts reviewed the instruments and an oncology and palliative care experts in Ghana, and a pre-test with a subset of women [2] confirmed the comprehensibility and relevance of the items. Reliability was also addressed, as the tools have demonstrated strong internal consistency in previous studies, with Cronbach’s α values typically above 0.70; in this study, internal consistency was assessed, and Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.82 to 0.92, indicating acceptable reliability. Researcher administered data collection process was used to maintain uniformity, and the use of a pre-test/post-test design reduced individual bias by comparing participants against their own baseline. Finally, appropriate non-parametric statistical analysis (Wilcoxon signed-rank test) was applied, given the small sample size and non-normal distribution of the data, to ensure the robustness and validity of results.

Ethical considerations

The researchers took measures to minimize risks. These measures included obtaining informed consent from all participants, safeguarding confidentiality, ensuring data protection, respecting the right to withdraw, and informing participants about potential benefits and harms. Ethical clearance was granted by the University of the Witwatersrand’s Research Ethics Committee (M220540) and by the Research Ethics Committee of the Ho Teaching Hospital (HTH-REC (08) FC_2022). The study adhered to the ethical principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice as outlined in the Belmont Report, as well as the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [45]. It further complied with all relevant ethical guidelines and regulatory standards applicable in Ghana.

Discussion

The study investigated whether a nurse-led palliative care programme, based on the care needs of women with advanced breast cancer receiving palliative chemotherapy in the Volta Region of Ghana, can improve symptom distress, pain, quality of life, and spiritual well-being. Half (50%) of the participants (N = 24) were aged 51-60years. The mean age of participants was approximately x̄ = 52.2 years, with a standard deviation of (SD ± 11.7) years, indicating a diverse age distribution within the study population. This is similar to the findings reported by Mosalo and Maree [46], where 37.5% (n = 21) of participants were in a similar age range in a pilot intervention study supporting women undergoing radiotherapy for cervical cancer in South Africa. The study also aligns with findings from another pilot study which reported a median participant age of 64.5 years (range 49–72 years), with a substantial proportion (60%) falling within the 60–69 age bracket and some reaching ≥ 70 years in United States, [47].

Findings from the current study revealed a significant reduction in the overall symptom distress. These findings align with several previous studies that have reported improvements in symptoms after interventions or treatments for breast cancer [48, 49]. For example, a study by Özdelikara and Tan [48] involving breast cancer patients attending the ambulatory chemotherapy unit of Ondokuz Mayıs University medical faculty hospital in Türkiye on reflexology for 30 participants observed a significant reduction in nausea, vomiting, and fatigue among breast cancer patients post-chemotherapy treatment. Similarly, in a randomized, parallel-group clinical trial for 34 women diagnosed with breast cancer and received chemotherapy in Brazil, de Souza, da Silva [50] reported an improvement in fatigue and appetite among breast cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy treatment through a nutritional-based intervention.

Pain was one of the most distressing symptoms among participants before the intervention, with high mean scores for pain severity (m = 9.34; SD ± 1.04) and pain interference (m = 8.80; SD ± 0.53). However, after the intervention, there was a remarkable reduction in both pain severity (m = 2.75; SD ± 0.00) and pain interference (m = 2.57; SD ± 0.00), with both reductions being highly statistically significant (p-value ≤ 0.05). This is consistent with previous studies [51, 52]. For example, in a randomized control trial of the effect of spiritual care on pain among breast cancer patients in Isfahan, Iran, the mean scores of pain severity dimensions had a p-value of (P = 0.004) among the intervention group as compared to the control group. The study, therefore, concluded that spiritual care is effective in the reduction of pain severity and its adverse effects on the lives of breast cancer patients [53]. This also agrees with the nurse-led pain management programme by Germossa, Hellesø [54] conducted in Ethiopia, where they reported that there was a general decrease in the average pain interference with both physical and emotional functions. These reductions were found to be statistically significant, with a p-value (p < 0.01). This further strengthens the current study’s findings.

Unlike other symptoms that improved significantly after the intervention, concerns regarding appearance remained unchanged, maintaining the highest mean score of 4.29 (SD ± 0.46) pre- and post-intervention (p-value = 1.00). This finding suggests that while palliative care interventions were effective in addressing physical and psychological symptoms, they had limited impact on patients’ concerns about their physical appearance. This is consistent with the study of Zhu, Sun [55] In their meta-analysis study, which involved seven studies, including two RCTs and five quasi-experimental studies, from 1994 to 2022, they reported that there were no significant changes in appearance care on body image for women receiving chemotherapy for breast cancer. Inversely, the finding that appearance remained unchanged after the intervention contradicts some earlier research [56–58]. For instance, a systematic review by Sebri, Durosini [56] showed that implemented psychosocial interventions for breast cancer patients, including appearance-related components, reported a significant improvement in body image and appearance concerns. The three-level meta-analysis showed a statistically significant effect for Body Image [g = 0.50; 95% CI (0.08; 0.93); p < 0.05]. This inconsistency might be attributed to differences in the nature, focus and duration of the interventions between the current study and those reporting positive changes in appearance.

Similarly, pain in the buttocks increased after the support programme. This unexpected finding should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the small sample size, which limits the ability to determine the exact cause of the observed change. The increase in buttocks pain may reflect multiple interacting factors and warrants further investigation in larger studies. One possible explanation is prolonged sitting during participation in or after the support sessions, which may have resulted in sustained pressure on the sacral and gluteal regions. Cancer patients with reduced mobility are particularly vulnerable to pressure-related discomfort and tissue ischemia due to impaired tissue perfusion and prolonged compression of soft tissues [59]. However, it remains unclear whether sitting duration, seating conditions, or other contextual factors directly contributed to this outcome. Future studies should therefore assess environmental and ergonomic factors, including seating comfort, duration of sitting, mobility support, and repositioning practices during intervention sessions.

Additionally, the reduction in chest and generalized pain following the intervention may have increased participants’ awareness of previously underreported localized pain, such as discomfort in the buttocks, suggesting a possible redistribution or unmasking of pain rather than the emergence of a new symptom. Neuroinflammatory mechanisms associated with cancer progression and chemotherapy may also contribute to evolving and fluctuating pain patterns across body regions [60]. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that cancer pain is dynamic and may vary according to disease progression, treatment effects, physical functioning, and symptom perception, which may explain the variability observed in this study [61]. Therefore, the finding should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive, highlighting the need for further longitudinal and adequately powered studies to better understand localized pain responses during supportive care interventions.

Another significant finding was the improvement in psychological outlook among participants, with mean scores increasing from 3.71 (SD ± 0.46) pre-intervention to 4.33 (SD ± 0.48) post-intervention (p-value = 0.01). This suggests that the intervention positively influenced patients’ overall psychological well-being, fostering a more optimistic outlook. This finding is in line with previous research emphasizing the psychological benefits of palliative care interventions [62–64]. For example, Melnyk, Kelly [65] in their study demonstrated that targeted psychological interventions significantly contributed to the improved mental health and overall well-being of breast cancer patients. Furthermore, interventions incorporating mindfulness-based techniques have also been shown to enhance psychological resilience and outlook among cancer patients [66]. These findings highlight the importance of integrating psychological support components into palliative care programmes to improve patients’ coping mechanisms and mental health outcomes.

Finally, the results demonstrate a substantial increase in spiritual needs scores from pre-intervention (M = 1.04, SD = 0.00) to post-intervention (M = 3.00, SD = 0.00), with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.00), indicating heightened awareness needs following the support programme. This finding aligns with evidence that spiritual care interventions significantly enhance spiritual well-being, awareness needs and quality of life among palliative care patients [67]. Similarly, a randomized controlled trial reported significant awareness needs and improvements in spiritual quality of life following a brief mindfulness-based intervention in Malaya (p = 0.000) [68].

Limitation

The effectiveness of the programme notwithstanding, this study has several limitations. First, the small sample size, with only 31 participants recruited and 24 completing the study, limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. The attrition rate may also have introduced bias, as those who completed the programme might differ in important ways from those who did not. Additionally, the study was conducted in a single region of Ghana, which may restrict the applicability of the results to other settings and cultural contexts. As a pilot study without a control group, the observed improvements cannot be solely attributed to the nurse-led palliative care programme, and the findings should therefore be considered preliminary.

Furthermore, the study only assessed the immediate post-intervention effects without any long-term follow-up. Consequently, the sustainability of the observed benefits, including symptom relief and quality of life improvements, could not be determined. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal follow-up assessments to evaluate the durability of intervention effects, such as symptom recurrence and maintenance of quality of life over time.

In addition, the study did not account for potential confounding factors that may have influenced the outcomes. Such factors may have contributed to improvements in pain and symptom distress independently of the intervention. Future research should consider controlling for these variables using stratified analyses or multivariable regression techniques to strengthen causal inference.

Conclusion

This pilot study tested a nurse-led palliative care programme for women receiving palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer in the Volta Region of Ghana. Thirty-one women receiving palliative chemotherapy for breast cancer were recruited for the study but only 24 completed it. The primary outcome focused on unmet palliative care needs (symptoms distress) and secondary outcomes included pain, quality of life, and spiritual needs, measured by means of the Symptoms Distress Scale (SDS), Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), Spiritual Needs Questionnaire (SpNQ), and EORTC QLQ-BR45.The results showed reduced symptom distress, reduced pain, and improved quality of life. It is recommended that the programme be refined and pilot-tested in a larger sample to further evaluate unexpected symptom changes, including localized pain such as buttocks discomfort, while also addressing issues related to body image and femininity alongside symptom management. Future interventions should additionally incorporate attention to participant comfort and ergonomic support during programme sessions to further enhance overall quality of life.
