Antitumor properties of fungal-derived compounds: potential applications in skin cancer therapy
Authors: Magdalena Kloc, Laura Jonderko, Katarzyna Herbetko, Karolina Kublińska, Aleksandra Wojno, Oliwia Cichy, Agata Wojno, Anna Karwowska, Sara Suwała, Anna Choromańska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin cancers, including basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and melanoma, remain a major therapeutic challenge due to their prevalence, invasive potential, and the limited efficacy of conventional treatments. Fungal-derived secondary metabolites have emerged as promising sources of anticancer agents, acting through pro-apoptotic, anti-angiogenic, immunomodulatory, and anti-metastatic mechanisms. This review summarizes current evidence on representative fungal compounds – including cordycepin, ganoderic acid D, and immunomodulatory polysaccharides such as lentinan – with particular focus on their activity against skin cancer cells in vitro, in vivo, and in early clinical trials. We further discuss key translational challenges, including production, bioavailability, and toxicity, and highlight the potential of fungal metabolites in combination with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy. Collectively, the available data indicate that fungal-derived compounds represent promising, though still largely preclinical, candidates for the treatment of skin cancer, warranting further mechanistic and clinical investigation.Graphical abstract Keywords: Fungal-derived compounds, Secondary metabolites, Anticancer agents, Apoptosis, Anti-angiogenesis, Immunomodulation, Skin cancer

Introduction

Skin cancer is primarily classified into two types: melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC). The two major subtypes of NMSC are basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) [1]. BCC represents 75% of all keratinocyte cancers. The accurate incidence of BCC is undervalued as many cases aren’t registered, and the histopathological examination isn’t always performed. Moreover, often only the first confirmed case per patient is registered. The risk factors of BCC are ultraviolet light (UV), indoor tanning, ionising radiation, arsenic, light pigmentary features, age, immunodeficiency, melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1R) gene variants and Gorlin syndrome. BCC grows at a slow rate, and it rarely metastasizes [2]. However, in SCC a metastasis to proximal lymph nodes can occur. SCC can arise from actinic keratosis (AK) [3]. This skin cancer is formed from the malignant proliferation of keratinocytes from the epidermis, pilosebaceous units and eccrine glands [4]. Leading environmental factor, which takes part in SCC development, is UV exposure [5]. While melanoma makes up only around 1% of skin cancer cases, it is responsible for approximately 80% of skin cancer-related deaths [6]. Melanoma is among the most metastatic cancers [7]. Cutaneous melanoma, which represents over 90% of melanoma diagnoses, is mostly a result of the mutagenic effect of the UVR. The transformation to this cancer comes from a combined effect of oncogenic aberrations commenced by the UVR (for example BRAF, NRAS, KIT mutations), inherited germline genetic modifiers and risk factors related to the phenotype [8]. Cutaneous melanoma is a highly mutagenic tumor. It is among the most immunogenic tumors and possibly the only tumor which can regress spontaneously, in the primary tumor, as a result of an antitumor immune response [7].

Recently, the significance of bioactive compounds and natural products as sources of anticancer drugs has been emphasized through a collaborative, integrated, and multidisciplinary approach [9]. Natural compounds have anti-inflammatory effects, can stimulate cell apoptosis, prevent invasion, metastasis and boost immune response. They offer various benefits in drug development such as structural range, the potential for multi-target results and can surpass drug resistance [10]. By reducing cancer-related side effects like bone marrow suppression, nausea and anemia, mushrooms play a valuable role as an addition to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [11]. Fungi constitute a highly important source of bioactive compounds. Fungal-derived extracts and secondary metabolites exhibit immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer activities. These properties have been described, among others, for compounds derived from Ganoderma lucidum, Ophiocordyceps sinensis, Agaricus bisporus, Hericium erinaceus, Pleurotus ostreatus, and Tricholoma mongolicum, which may affect cancer cell proliferation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and the immune response [12–15]. Nevertheless, only a small proportion of fungal metabolites has been thoroughly characterized to date; therefore, they remain a potentially underexploited source of anticancer compounds (Fig. 1).

Antitumor mechanisms of fungal-derived compounds

Induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in cancer cells

Fungal-derived compounds have emerged as promising agents in cancer therapy due to their diverse bioactive properties and ability to target multiple cellular processes (Fig. 2). Their antitumor mechanisms primarily revolve around two key processes: induction of apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a critical process in cancer treatment as it helps eliminate cancerous cells without affecting surrounding healthy tissue. Fungal compounds have demonstrated the ability to induce apoptosis through various molecular pathways.

Antitumor mechanisms of fungal-derived compounds in skin cancer [19, 39, 41, 52]

Molecular cascades in melanoma

In melanoma, the most aggressive form of skin cancer, fungal compounds target both intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death receptor) apoptotic pathways. Cordycepin (3’-deoxyadenosine), derived from Cordyceps militaris, exemplifies this multi-targeted approach. Recent research (2022–2024) has elucidated that cordycepin triggers apoptosis in melanoma cells through the activation of initiator caspases (caspase-8 and caspase-9) and the executioner caspase-3 [16]. This process is further amplified by the cleavage of the pro-apoptotic protein Bid and the translocation of Bax to the mitochondria, facilitating the release of cytochrome c into the cytosol [16, 17]. Furthermore, cordycepin activates critical mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways, including c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38 MAPK, which are essential for mediating stress-induced apoptosis in melanoma [16].

Hispolon, a polyphenol from Phellinus linteus, has demonstrated a unique capacity to induce apoptosis in melanoma cells by downregulating the microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) and tyrosinase [18]. This downregulation not only inhibits melanogenesis but also triggers a pro-apoptotic cascade involving the modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins, effectively shifting the balance toward cell death [18]. Additionally, hispolon induces cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase by regulating cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), thereby preventing the progression of malignant melanocytes [18].

Mechanisms in Non-Melanoma Skin Cancer (NMSC)

For NMSC, including basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), fungal compounds target pathways frequently dysregulated by ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Ganoderic acid D (GAD), a triterpenoid from Ganoderma lucidum, has been shown to inhibit cell proliferation and induce apoptosis by suppressing the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [19]. Disruption of this pathway is particularly effective in SCC, where it leads to the simultaneous induction of apoptosis and autophagic cell death [19].

Ganoric acid from ganoderma lucidum

Previous studies [19] have demonstrated that EC9706 and Eca109 cell lines treated with ganoderic acid D (GAD) - a bioactive triterpenoid derived from Ganoderma lucidum at concentrations of 0, 10, 20, and 40 µM for 24 h exhibited significant inhibition of cell proliferation and induction of both apoptosis and autophagic cell death. Furthermore, GAD treatment was found to reduce the levels of phosphorylated PI3K, AKT, and mTOR proteins, which are key components of the mTOR signaling pathway. This disruption resulted in a synergistic enhancement of apoptosis and autophagic cell death in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cells.

Cordycepin from Cordyceps spp. (Peptides and Proteins)

Cordycepin, a bioactive compound derived from fungi of the genus Cordyceps, has demonstrated significant pro-apoptotic effects in various cancer cell lines. Research has shown that cordycepin effectively induces apoptosis in OEC-M1 cells through activation of caspase-dependent pathways. Specifically, cordycepin treatment resulted in increased release of cytokeratin 18 fragments, an effect reversed by caspase inhibition, confirming caspase involvement. Additionally, cordycepin activated initiator caspases (caspase-8 and caspase-9) and the executioner caspase-3, accompanied by cleavage of the pro-apoptotic protein Bid. These findings indicate that cordycepin triggers apoptosis via both extrinsic and intrinsic apoptotic pathways [16]. Moreover, cordycepin treatment activated key mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways, including c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and p38 MAPK. The activation of these pathways suggests their critical role in mediating cordycepin-induced apoptosis, highlighting potential therapeutic implications [16].

In another study [20] cordycepin was shown to induce apoptosis in the human colon cancer cell line HCT116 through a Bax-dependent mitochondrial pathway. Cordycepin promoted Bax translocation to mitochondria, facilitating cytochrome c release into the cytosol and subsequent cleavage of poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), a hallmark of apoptosis. These apoptotic effects were absent in Bax-deficient cells but restored upon reintroduction of Bax, underscoring the essential role of Bax in cordycepin-induced apoptosis.

Further research [21] on MA-10 mouse Leydig tumor cells demonstrated that cordycepin enhances radiosensitivity both in vitro and in vivo. Combined treatment with cordycepin and radiation significantly reduced tumor cell viability, induced cell cycle arrest at G2/M and sub-G1 phases, and activated caspase-dependent apoptotic pathways, including PARP cleavage and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. In vivo studies confirmed these findings, showing reduced tumor growth and angiogenesis, along with increased apoptosis markers, suggesting a synergistic therapeutic potential for cordycepin in combination with radiotherapy. Additionally, cordycepin has been reported to inhibit pancreatic cancer cell growth both in vitro and in vivo by targeting fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2) and blocking downstream ERK signaling [22].

Gliocladicillins A and B from cordyceps-colonizing fungi and bionectriaceae

Gliocladicillins A and B, bioactive alkaloids isolated from Cordyceps-colonizing fungi and Bionectriaceae strains (MSX 64546 and MSX 59553), have demonstrated significant anticancer activity against various cancer cell lines, including HeLa, HepG2, and MCF-7. Treatment with these compounds induced cell cycle arrest specifically at the G2/M phase in a concentration-dependent manner. Additionally, gliocladicillins triggered apoptosis through modulation of critical regulatory proteins involved in cell cycle control and apoptosis, such as p53, p21, and cyclin B. The apoptotic process was further characterized by activation of caspase-dependent pathways, including initiator caspases (caspase-8 and caspase-9) and the executioner caspase-3 [23].

Averufanin from aspergillus carneus

Averufanin, a bioactive secondary metabolite isolated from the fungus Aspergillus carneus, has demonstrated significant anticancer activity in various cancer cell lines. Treatment with averufanin induced marked cell cycle arrest, particularly at the G2/M phase, and promoted apoptosis through the induction of DNA damage. The apoptotic effects were associated with increased DNA fragmentation and elevated expression of DNA damage markers, such as γ-H2AX [24].

Ostreolysin from pleurotus ostreatus

Ostreolysin, a bioactive fungal protein derived from the mushroom Pleurotus ostreatus, has demonstrated significant anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects on colon cancer cells. Treatment with recombinant ostreolysin (rOly) markedly reduced cell viability and induced apoptosis through activation of caspase-dependent pathways and disruption of mitochondrial membrane integrity. Additionally, rOly influenced critical intracellular signaling pathways, including the induction of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of ostreolysin as an effective agent targeting apoptosis and key signaling pathways in colon cancer cells [25].

Ergosterol peroxide from ganoderma lucidum (Lipids)

Ergosterol peroxide, a bioactive lipid compound isolated from the medicinal mushroom Ganoderma lucidum, has demonstrated significant anticancer activity, particularly against triple-negative inflammatory breast cancer cells. Studies have shown that ergosterol peroxide and its chemically modified derivatives effectively inhibit cancer cell proliferation and induce apoptosis. Structural modifications, such as conjugation with thiazolidinedione, further enhanced these anticancer effects, leading to increased apoptosis induction and suppression of tumor cell growth. Mechanistically, ergosterol peroxide derivatives exert their anticancer activity by targeting critical signaling pathways, notably the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. By modulating this pathway, these compounds effectively disrupt cancer cell survival and proliferation signals, highlighting their therapeutic potential [26].

Apicidin from fusarium semitectum

Apicidin, a bioactive secondary metabolite isolated from the fungus Fusarium semitectum, has demonstrated significant anti-proliferative activity against various cancer cell lines, including leukemia, cervical carcinoma, gastric carcinoma, breast carcinoma, and ovarian cancer. Its anticancer effects are primarily attributed to its potent inhibitory activity against histone deacetylases (HDACs), enzymes critically involved in chromatin remodeling and gene expression regulation. Studies have shown that apicidin induces cell cycle arrest at the G1 phase, accompanied by selective modulation of key regulatory proteins such as p21^WAF1/Cip1 and gelsolin [27]. Additionally, apicidin treatment has been associated with increased histone acetylation, particularly hyperacetylation of histone H4, leading to altered gene expression profiles and subsequent inhibition of cancer cell proliferation.

In leukemia cells (HL-60), apicidin exhibited concentration-dependent effects, inducing apoptosis at higher concentrations and promoting histone hyperacetylation at lower concentrations. Furthermore, in ovarian cancer cells (SKOV-3), apicidin effectively reduced the expression of histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4), resulting in decreased migration and invasion capabilities. This effect was mediated by downregulation of matrix metalloproteinase-2 (MMP-2) and upregulation of the metastasis suppressor RECK [28].

Resorcylic acid lactones (RALs)

Rhinoclactones A-E from rhinocladiella similis

Rhinoclactones A-E, belonging to the class of resorcylic acid lactones (RALs), are bioactive secondary metabolites isolated from the endophytic fungus Rhinocladiella similis. These compounds have demonstrated significant anti-proliferative activities against various cancer cell lines. Their anticancer effects are primarily attributed to their ability to modulate key cellular processes, including cell cycle progression, apoptosis induction, and inhibition of critical signaling pathways. RALs, including rhinoclactones, typically exert their biological effects by targeting essential regulatory proteins and signaling cascades involved in cancer progression. Notably, resorcylic acid lactones have been reported to inhibit protein kinases and histone deacetylases, leading to cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and suppression of tumor cell proliferation [29].

β-resorcylic acid from penicillium antarcticum

The study [30] investigated the anticancer potential of five novel β-resorcylic acid derivatives isolated from the alga-associated fungus Penicillium antarcticum KMM 4685. These compounds, including 14-hydroxyasperentin B and β-resoantarctines A-C, were shown to exhibit cytotoxic effects against human cancer cell lines, specifically HeLa (cervical cancer), A549 (lung cancer), and T-47D (breast cancer). Among the tested compounds, 14-hydroxyasperentin B demonstrated the strongest anticancer activity, with IC50 values ranging from 2.5 to 8.0 µM, indicating significant inhibition of cancer cell proliferation. The other derivatives also displayed moderate cytotoxic effects, with IC50 values in the low micromolar range. A study has shown the anticancer properties of penicimenolides A–F, a group of resorcylic acid lactones isolated from Penicillium sp., found in the rhizosphere soil of Panax notoginseng [31]. These compounds were evaluated for their ability to inhibit the proliferation of human cancer cell lines, including HeLa (cervical cancer), MCF-7 (breast cancer), and A549 (lung cancer). Among the isolated compounds, penicimenolides A and B demonstrated the most potent anticancer activity, with IC50 values in the low micromolar range (approximately 5–10 µM), indicating significant inhibition of cancer cell growth. The remaining compounds exhibited moderate to weak cytotoxic effects.

Resorcylic acid lactones from curvularia lunata

The study investigated four novel resorcylic acid lactones (RALs), curvulomycins A-D, along with six known derivatives, were isolated from the coral-derived fungus Curvularia lunata GXIMD 02512 found in the Beibu Gulf. Among them, curvulomycin A (1) and the known compound LL-Z1640-2 (5) exhibited significant antiproliferative effects against two prostate cancer cell lines, PC-3 and 22Rv1, with IC50 values of 9.70 ± 0.77 µM and 7.64 ± 0.46 µM for PC-3, and 5.96 ± 0.43 µM and 3.15 ± 0.27 µM for 22Rv1, respectively. Moreover, curvulomycin A (1) demonstrated dose-dependent inhibition of clonal cell growth, disrupted the cell cycle by blocking the S and G2 phases in PC-3 cells and the G1 phase in 22Rv1 cells, and induced apoptosis in both cell lines [32].

Okaramines A from keratinophyton lemmensii

The filamentous fungus Keratinophyton lemmensii has been identified as a source of bioactive compounds with significant anticancer potential. Among these, a newly discovered okaramine derivative, named lemmokaramine, along with two known congeners, okaramine H and okaramine J, has shown promising activity against human cancer cell lines, including MCF-7 (breast cancer) and A549 (lung cancer). Lemmokaramine exhibited the strongest antiproliferative effects, inducing cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase and promoting apoptosis, as evidenced by the detection of apoptotic markers. These findings suggest that lemmokaramine disrupts critical cellular processes involved in cancer cell survival and division. In comparison, okaramine H and J displayed moderate anticancer activity, with less pronounced effects on cell proliferation [33].

γ-pyrone compounds from aspergillus (Secondary Metabolites)

The anticancer potential of naphtho-γ-pyrones (NGPs), secondary metabolites produced by the filamentous fungus Aspergillus tubingensis, has been extensively studied [34]. These compounds, produced under varying culture conditions, demonstrated significant antiproliferative activity against human cancer cell lines, including HeLa (cervical cancer) and A549 (lung cancer). Certain NGPs were found to effectively inhibit cancer cell growth, with IC50 values in the low micromolar range (approximately 5–10 µM). Their anticancer effects were attributed to their ability to induce cell cycle arrest and trigger apoptosis, disrupting key processes involved in cancer cell survival and proliferation [34].

Another study on NGPs produced by Aspergillus tubingensis G131 primarily focused on their antioxidant properties but also explored their potential anticancer effects. While these compounds exhibited varying levels of cytotoxicity, their antioxidant activity was highlighted as a mechanism that could indirectly influence cancer progression by reducing oxidative stress in healthy cells, thereby supporting overall cellular health [35].

In a separate study, naphtho-γ-pyrones isolated from the marine-derived fungus Aspergillus foetidus were evaluated for their anticancer activity. Among the nine compounds tested, including rubrofusarine B, TMC 256 A1, fonsecinones A and B, aurasperones A, B, and F, dianhydro-aurasperone C, and asperpyrone B, several exhibited strong antiproliferative effects. Notably, fonsecinone A and aurasperone A demonstrated the most potent activity, effectively inhibiting cancer cell proliferation. These compounds were shown to induce cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase and promote apoptosis, as evidenced by the presence of apoptotic markers in treated cells. These findings underscore the potential of naphtho-γ-pyrones as promising therapeutic agents in cancer treatment. Their ability to modulate key biological pathways, such as cell cycle regulation and apoptosis, highlights their relevance for further exploration in anticancer drug development [36].

Malformin A1 from several marine fungi, including Aspergillus niger

Malformin A1 (MA1), a cyclopentapeptide derived from fungi such as Aspergillus niger, has shown significant potential in overcoming chemoresistance in ovarian cancer. A study investigated its ability to enhance the sensitivity of cisplatin-resistant ovarian cancer cells to apoptosis. MA1 demonstrated strong cytotoxic effects against both cisplatin-sensitive (A2780S) and cisplatin-resistant (A2780CP) ovarian cancer cell lines, with IC50 values of 0.23 µM and 0.34 µM, respectively, highlighting its effectiveness even in resistant cells [37]. MA1 was found to amplify cisplatin-induced apoptosis in resistant cells. This effect was associated with increased DNA fragmentation and elevated levels of apoptotic markers, suggesting that MA1 promotes apoptosis through the activation of specific molecular pathways. Additionally, MA1 induced significant changes in cellular processes, including the disruption of mechanisms that contribute to chemoresistance.

Modulation of immune responses

Cancer cells modulate immune response in their environment to suppress immune response and support tumor growth. Countering these mechanisms is a promising strategy for cancer treatment which can allow our immune cells to eliminate cancer cells effectively [38].

Fungal-derived compounds, especially polysaccharides, have shown potential in modulating the immune system to fight or prevent cancer. They can activate various cells of immune system, including macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells and lymphocytes cytotoxic as well as Th lymphocytes [39–42].

The best example is one of beta-glucans, lentinan (LNT). LNT is one of the most studied fungal polysaccharides derived from Lentinula edodes (shiitake mushroom). Studies have shown its efficacy in increasing the number of active CD8 + lymphocytes and enhancing the presence of neutrophils within the tumor microenvironment, what results in reduce of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) which all play a critical role in tumor progression and immunity. LNT has been demonstrated in various cancers both in vitro and in vivo and is already used as an immunomodulatory agent in anti-cancer therapy [43–45].

Polysaccharide peptide (PSP) and polysaccharide-K (PSK), both derived from Trametes versicolor, are other important fungal polysaccharides with immunomodulatory properties. These compounds also enhance the activity of various immune cells, including T-cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages, and regulate cytokine production, creating a favorable immune environment for anti-tumor responses, which helps suppress tumor development and enhances the effectiveness of cancer treatments [39–42].

The fungal metabolites polysaccharides from Ganoderma lucidum have been also shown to influence the gut microbiota, promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria and reducing the number of harmful ones. This helps to reduce inflammation, an important factor in the development of cancer. By lowering chronic inflammation, the immune system becomes more balanced, allowing it to better combat cancer cells. For instance, GLP polysaccharides reduce pro-inflammatory substances and support immune function, which helps prevent cancer [42].

Although melanoma-specific studies remain limited for lentinan, PSP and PSK, evidence from other fungal β-glucans supports the relevance of immunomodulatory approaches in skin cancer. Recent studies have demonstrated the potent immunomodulatory effects of β-glucan derived from the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae (β-GESc) in a B16F10 melanoma model. Administration of β-GESc significantly reduced tumor growth and improved overall survival, which was associated with the expansion of key immune cell populations, including macrophages, dendritic cells (DCs), NK cells, and NKT cells. Furthermore, β-GESc enhanced systemic anti-tumor immunity by increasing circulating leukocyte numbers and promoting the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α by both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells. Beyond immune cell activation, this yeast-derived β-glucan preserved splenic architecture, prevented melanoma-cell infiltration into the spleen, and supported germinal center formation, thereby facilitating a sustained adaptive immune response. Collectively, these findings indicate that fungal β-glucans can remodel the tumor microenvironment and enhance both innate and adaptive anti-tumor immunity in melanoma. Nevertheless, these observations are currently limited to preclinical models, and further studies are required to determine their translational potential in patients with melanoma and other skin cancers [46].

Inhibition of Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is critical for tumor progression. It enables cancer cells to obtain nutrients and oxygen. Its multi-targeted effects and low toxicity make it an important therapeutic target, especially when treated with natural compounds such as fungal metabolites [41].

The inhibition of angiogenesis is particularly important in melanoma, where neovascularization plays a crucial role in tumor progression and metastatic dissemination. Angiogenesis facilitates the transition from the radial growth phase to the more invasive vertical growth phase of melanoma. Increased intratumoral microvessel density has been associated with greater Breslow thickness, higher metastatic potential, and poorer clinical outcomes. Among the numerous pro-angiogenic mediators involved, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and its receptor VEGFR-2 represent the principal signaling axis regulating endothelial cell proliferation and survival. In addition, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF/FGF-2) further promotes VEGF-dependent neovascularization, while matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), including MMP-1, MMP-2, and MMP-9, contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling, endothelial cell migration, and metastatic spread. Therefore, fungal-derived compounds capable of targeting VEGF, VEGFR-2, bFGF, and MMP-dependent pathways may represent promising therapeutic agents for melanoma treatment [47].

Polysaccharides PSP and PSK from Trametes versicolor exhibit anti-angiogenic properties by inhibiting VEGF (Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor) expression, suppressing endothelial cell proliferation, and disrupting signaling pathways such as MAPK kinase phosphorylation induced by basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF). PSP regulates tumor angiogenesis through its immunomodulatory effects. It increases pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which leads to activation of the TLR4 receptor, resulting in activation of the NF-κB and STAT3 pathways, which regulate both inflammation and tumor angiogenesis [40].

PSP also modulates tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) from a pro-angiogenic M2 to an anti-angiogenic M1 phenotype, which indirectly reduces VEGF and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) levels in the tumor microenvironment. MMPs are enzymes that are critical for tumor invasion and angiogenesis and can also be inhibited by PSK [40, 48].

Coumarin-derived metabolites are another fungal metabolite that regulates angiogenesis. They effectively inhibit VEGFR-2, disrupting a key angiogenic pathway in cancer. Novel derivatives such as thiazolylpyrazolyl have no significant cell toxicity, making them promising candidates for anti-angiogenic cancer therapy [49, 50].

Extracts from Ganoderma lucidum (EGL) have been also shown to have anti-angiogenic properties in addition to their other functions. EGL disrupts VEGF-mediated pathways and affects the tumor microenvironment, reducing blood vessel formation, which is essential for tumor growth [51].

Although fungal-derived metabolites exhibit promising anti-angiogenic activity through inhibition of VEGF signaling, modulation of TAM polarization, and suppression of MMP expression, most available evidence originates from in vitro studies or non-skin cancer models. Furthermore, the concentrations required to achieve anti-angiogenic effects may not always be clinically attainable. Therefore, additional studies using melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer models are necessary to validate these findings and assess their translational potential.

Prevention of metastasis

Metastases are the cause of most cancer-associated deaths and yet preventing and treating them remains of the major challenges in cancer treatment [52].

Specifically in melanoma, which remains the most lethal form of skin cancer, metastatic dissemination is a critical determinant of patient prognosis. Melanoma progression is associated with a transition from the radial growth phase to the more aggressive vertical growth phase, which markedly increases the metastatic potential of tumor cells. Several factors contribute to melanoma dissemination, including the acquisition of laminin receptor expression, which facilitates tumor cell adhesion to the vascular wall and promotes extravasation into distant tissues. Moreover, increased intratumoral microvessel density (MVD) and lymphatic vessel density (LVD) have been strongly associated with a higher risk of recurrence, sentinel lymph node metastasis, and poorer overall survival. The metastatic behavior of melanoma is further promoted by the acidic tumor microenvironment, which favors the selection of more invasive cellular phenotypes and enhances tumor cell motility. Therefore, targeting molecular pathways involved in cell migration, invasion, adhesion, and colonization of distant organs represents an important therapeutic strategy for limiting melanoma progression and metastatic spread [47].

Fungal-derived compounds, such as cordycepin which is a nucleoside from Cordyceps mushrooms, have shown potential in inhibiting metastasis. In vitro studies indicate that cordycepin exhibits its anti-cancerous properties through activating kinase GSK3β, which leads to phosphorylation and degradation of β-catenin. Reduction in β-catenin levels limits the transcription of oncogenes, such as cyclin D1, which slows down the proliferation, migration, and invasion of cancer cells. In animal models, it was observed to decrease the number of metastases in the lungs, highlighting its role in preventing the spread of cancer to distant organs [53, 54]. Another mechanism is reducing the expression of adhesion molecules, which are crucial for the migration of cancer cells from the primary tumor to the bloodstream and distant organs. Studies both in vitro and in vivo have shown that Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides (GLP) can decrease ICAM-1 expression which effectively disrupts metastasis formation [55, 56].

Ganoderma lucidum also produces triterpenoids (GLT) that inhibit metastasis through various mechanisms. They influence cytoskeleton reorganization through interactions with 14-3-3 proteins, which are responsible for actine polymerization. This change inhibits cancer cell migration and invasion, decreasing its ability to develop metastases [57, 58].

Melanoma-specific evidence further supports the anti-metastatic activity of Ganoderma lucidum. In a B16-F10 melanoma mouse model, Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharide peptide (GL-pp) significantly reduced pulmonary metastatic burden and preserved lung tissue architecture. Mechanistically, GL-pp interfered with focal adhesion signaling by modulating the expression of genes involved in cell adhesion and migration, including Ptk2 (encoding focal adhesion kinase, FAK) and Grb2, thereby impairing the invasive capacity of melanoma cells. In addition, GL-pp promoted the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages from the pro-tumorigenic M2 phenotype toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype, resulting in an increased M1/M2 ratio. The compound also reduced systemic TNF-α levels and modulated gut microbiota composition, suggesting that both local and systemic immune mechanisms contribute to its anti-metastatic activity. These findings indicate that Ganoderma-derived polysaccharides may suppress melanoma dissemination through the combined regulation of focal adhesion pathways, tumor-associated macrophages, and host immune responses [59].

Additional melanoma-specific evidence has recently been reported for Ganoderma tsugae (Songshan Lingzhi). In B16-F10 metastatic melanoma models, treatment with G. tsugae water extract (GTWE) significantly reduced tumor cell migration, metastatic colonization, and pulmonary metastatic burden. Mechanistically, GTWE decreased intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and suppressed the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which is frequently associated with melanoma progression and metastatic behavior. Inhibition of PI3K/Akt signaling promoted mitochondrial apoptosis through upregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-9, accompanied by downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2. Furthermore, oral administration of GTWE markedly reduced the number of metastatic foci and lung weight in melanoma-bearing mice without inducing detectable toxicity in major organs. These findings suggest that Ganoderma-derived compounds may inhibit melanoma metastasis through the combined modulation of oxidative stress, apoptotic pathways, and tumor cell invasiveness [60].

More over, melanoma-specific evidence further supports the anti-metastatic potential of cordycepin. In human melanoma cell lines (A375 and Lu1205), cordycepin suppressed migration and invasion through upregulation of the tumor-suppressive microRNA miR-33b. Increased miR-33b expression resulted in the downregulation of HMGA2, Twist1, and ZEB1, key regulators of metastatic progression and EMT. Consequently, cordycepin reduced MMP-2 and MMP-9 activity, inhibited cytoskeletal remodeling, and promoted MET phenotype characterized by decreased N-cadherin and vimentin expression and restoration of E-cadherin levels. In vivo melanoma models demonstrated significantly reduced metastasis to the lungs, liver, and bones, leading to prolonged survival without affecting primary tumor growth. These findings indicate that cordycepin may interfere with multiple stages of melanoma dissemination through modulation of the miR-33b/HMGA2/Twist1/ZEB1 signaling axis [61].

Although fungal-derived compounds exhibit promising anti-metastatic activity through modulation of β-catenin signaling, EMT regulators, adhesion molecules, and cytoskeletal dynamics, most evidence remains limited to preclinical studies. Furthermore, only a small number of investigations have evaluated these compounds in melanoma-specific models. Therefore, additional studies are required to validate their efficacy and determine their translational potential in patients with melanoma and other skin cancers.

Preclinical and clinical studies on skin cancer therapy

In vitro evaluation of fungal compounds in skin cancer cell models

Skin cancer, one of the most prevalent types of cancer, requires novel therapeutic approaches to overcome limitations associated with current treatments. Fungi, as prolific producers of bioactive secondary metabolites, represent a valuable resource for the discovery of novel anticancer agents. In vitro studies serve as a foundational step in evaluating the cytotoxicity, mechanisms of action, and therapeutic potential of these compounds against skin cancer cell lines. In vitro activity of fungal-derived compounds in melanoma-related experimental models

In vivo evaluation of fungal-derived compounds in animal models of skin cancer

While numerous fungal metabolites have demonstrated significant anticancer potential in vitro, their translation into effective clinical therapies requires extensive in vivo validation. Animal models play a crucial role in bridging the gap between laboratory findings and clinical applications, offering insights into the efficacy, safety, and mechanisms of action of these compounds in a complex biological context. In the field of skin cancer research, where aggressive growth and metastasis pose substantial treatment challenges, in vivo studies are particularly essential for evaluating the therapeutic potential of novel agents. Despite promising preliminary findings, further exploration is necessary to fully understand the multifaceted mechanisms underlying the anticancer activities of these metabolites, paving the way for their eventual clinical application. In research concerning pulmonary metastasis model, the mice were first injected with B16F10 melanoma cells. Secondly, they were intraperitoneally injected with either vehicle or hydroquinone (HQ) (10 and 50 mg/kg) twice a week for 18 days. After euthanasia at day 19., the total number of melanoma metastatic spots in lung tissue were counted. HQ did not exhibit toxicity in studied doses, and the number of metastatic spots was significantly reduced in lungs of HQ-injected mice in comparison to vehicle-injected ones. The results described prove the anticancer activity of HQ in vivo, and thus examined metabolite constitutes a promising potential agent in skin cancer therapy [67]. Antitumor activity of gliocladicillins A and B was examined in vivo using xenograft model of C57BL⁄6 J mice injected subcutaneously with B16 melanoma cells. After 24 h, the mice were injected with either gliocladicillins A (0.25 and 0.50 mg⁄kg) or B (0.10 and 0.40 mg ⁄ kg) once a day for 21 days with their body weight being noted each day. After that amount of time, the mice were killed, and the tumors were removed and weighted. Examined gliocladicillins did not exhibit toxicity in the studied doses. Obtained results indicated that treatment with gliocladicillins A and B both resulted in meaningful anti-tumor efficiency with inhibition levels of 69.8–87.2% for gliocladicillin A and 56.7–82.5% for gliocladicillin B [68]. Nevertheless, it should be discussed whether presented results constitute the evidence for only antiproliferative potential of gliocladicillins A and B. As growth and metastasis of neoplasms are dependent upon blood vessels supply, possible influence on angiogenesis remains probable mechanism that, alongside with antiproliferative properties, would explain described in vivo success. 11,11’-dideoxyverticillin, which, similarly, to examined gliocladicillins is classified as epipolythiodioxopiperazine, exhibits anti-angiogenic activity [3]. In the light of this structural correlation, further studies concerning potential anti-angiogenic activity of gliocladicillins A and B are needed in order to fully understand the mechanism of their anticancer properties [23, 69].

In conclusion, the promising results obtained from in vivo studies of hydroquinone and gliocladicillins A and B highlight the potential of fungal metabolites as effective agents in skin cancer therapy, while emphasizing the need for further in vivo research in to fully elucidate mechanisms of their action, and to ensure their safety and efficacy in clinical settings.

Clinical trials involving fungal-derived compounds in skin cancer therapy

Although fungal secondary metabolites have provided numerous lead structures for anticancer drug discovery, their clinical translation in skin cancer remains limited. Most human studies have been conducted in mixed populations of patients with advanced solid tumours, with melanoma included as a subgroup rather than as the sole indication. Therefore, only a small number of fungal-derived compounds or their synthetic analogues can currently be discussed in direct relation to melanoma or non-melanoma skin cancer.

One of the earliest clinical examples was rhizoxin, a 16-membered macrolide originally isolated from the pathogenic fungus Rhizopus chinensis. Rhizoxin inhibits microtubule function and showed broad preclinical antitumour activity, including activity against vinca alkaloid-resistant cells. In a phase II EORTC study, rhizoxin was evaluated in patients with advanced breast cancer and melanoma. Twenty-six patients were enrolled in the melanoma arm and treated with intravenous rhizoxin at 2.0 mg/m² every 21 days. The treatment was considered feasible, but no objective responses were observed in melanoma patients. The authors concluded that rhizoxin had little clinical activity in advanced melanoma despite acceptable administration at the tested dose [70]. Thus, rhizoxin represents an important negative clinical example: it demonstrates early translational interest in fungal metabolites for melanoma, but also illustrates the difficulty of converting preclinical cytotoxicity into meaningful patient benefit.

A more melanoma-relevant example is E6201, a synthetic analogue of fungal resorcylic acid lactones related to hypothemycin/LL-Z1640-2-type compounds. E6201 acts primarily as an intravenous MEK1 inhibitor and also affects other kinases involved in tumour progression. Because activation of the MAPK pathway, particularly through BRAF mutations, is central to melanoma biology, E6201 has a stronger mechanistic rationale in melanoma than many other fungal-derived anticancer candidates. In a first-in-human phase I study in advanced solid tumours, with expansion cohorts including advanced melanoma, an intermittent regimen of 320 mg/m² intravenously once weekly for the first three weeks of a 28-day cycle was considered feasible and reasonably well tolerated. Clinical activity was limited but notable: partial responses were reported in melanoma patients, including both BRAF-mutated and BRAF-wild-type cases [71]. A subsequent report described an exceptional and durable response in a patient with BRAF V600E-mutated metastatic melanoma with brain metastases, with overall survival exceeding eight years [72]. Although this is a single-patient observation and cannot establish general efficacy, it supports further exploration of E6201 or related fungal-derived MEK-pathway inhibitors in molecularly selected melanoma, particularly in the difficult setting of CNS metastases.

Another compound of interest is PX-866, an irreversible pan-PI3K inhibitor derived from wortmannin, a fungal metabolite originally isolated from Talaromyces wortmannii/Penicillium wortmannii. The PI3K/AKT pathway is relevant to melanoma, especially in tumours with PTEN loss or altered PI3K signalling. However, the available clinical evidence for PX-866 in melanoma is weak. Early phase I studies in advanced solid tumours established tolerability and pharmacodynamic activity [73], and a melanoma-specific trial combining PX-866 with vemurafenib in BRAF-mutant melanoma was initiated. Nevertheless, this programme did not generate convincing published efficacy data in melanoma and was reportedly limited by slow accrual. For this reason, PX-866 should be discussed only briefly in the context of melanoma as a mechanistically relevant but clinically unproven fungal-derived PI3K inhibitor, rather than as a successful skin cancer therapy.

NUC-7738 is a ProTide transformation of cordycepin, a nucleoside analogue originally derived from Cordyceps species. Cordycepin itself has limited clinical utility because it is rapidly degraded by adenosine deaminase and has inefficient intracellular activation. NUC-7738 was designed to overcome these resistance mechanisms and generate sustained intracellular levels of the active metabolite 3′-dATP. In preclinical and early clinical studies, NUC-7738 showed pro-apoptotic activity, modulation of RNA polyadenylation, and effects on cancer-associated signalling pathways, including NF-κB-related processes [74]. Importantly for this section, the NuTide:701 phase I/II study includes evaluation of NUC-7738 in combination with pembrolizumab in patients with PD-1 inhibitor-resistant cutaneous melanoma. Early data from this cohort suggest antitumour activity, including partial responses, but melanoma-specific results remain preliminary and should be interpreted cautiously until mature peer-reviewed phase II data are available [75]. Therefore, NUC-7738 should be presented as one of the most relevant ongoing fungal-derived clinical candidates for melanoma, but its therapeutic value remains investigational.

For non-melanoma skin cancer, particularly basal cell carcinoma (BCC), the best clinical evidence is associated with itraconazole, a synthetic triazole antifungal drug repurposed because of its ability to inhibit Hedgehog signalling, a key pathway in BCC pathogenesis. Although itraconazole is not a fungal secondary metabolite, it is clinically relevant in this section as an antifungal-drug repurposing example. In an open-label exploratory phase II trial, oral itraconazole reduced tumour cell proliferation, Hedgehog pathway activity, and tumour area in patients with BCC [76]. A related pilot study in patients with refractory metastatic BCC evaluated sequential intravenous arsenic trioxide and oral itraconazole. This combination was designed to inhibit Hedgehog signalling at sites distinct from Smoothened inhibitors. In five patients, treatment reduced GLI1 mRNA levels in tumour or skin biopsy samples, and stable disease was observed as the best response in three patients who completed treatment cycles [77]. These findings suggest that antifungal-drug repurposing may have therapeutic relevance in selected BCC settings, especially where resistance to standard Hedgehog pathway inhibitors is a concern. However, because itraconazole is a synthetic antifungal agent rather than a fungal natural product, it should be clearly separated from fungal-derived metabolites such as rhizoxin, wortmannin derivatives, E6201, and cordycepin-derived NUC-7738.

Overall, clinical evidence for fungal-derived compounds in skin cancer therapy remains preliminary. Among available melanoma-related examples, rhizoxin showed minimal activity, PX-866 has a plausible molecular rationale but lacks convincing melanoma efficacy data, and E6201 provides the strongest published melanoma signal, particularly because of reported responses in advanced melanoma and an exceptional response in a patient with BRAF-mutated melanoma and brain metastases. NUC-7738 is currently one of the most relevant ongoing candidates, especially in PD-1 inhibitor-resistant cutaneous melanoma, but mature peer-reviewed clinical efficacy data are still needed. In non-melanoma skin cancer, itraconazole-based studies in BCC provide clinically meaningful evidence for antifungal-drug repurposing, although they should not be overrepresented as examples of fungal natural products. Future studies should prioritise molecularly selected melanoma and BCC populations, rational combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies, and improved formulation strategies to overcome the pharmacokinetic limitations that have historically restricted the clinical development of fungal natural products.

Challenges in translating fungal compounds to clinical use

Notwithstanding that numerous fungal metabolites have been identified as potential anticancer agents, limitations of logistical, biotechnological and physiological origin hinder their entrance into clinical use. Firstly, procurement of fungal material is more difficult than in the case of bacterial and botanic material. Not only is growing fungi more demanding than growing bacteria and plants but also obtained amounts of desired metabolites are relatively little compared to the cost of their production. For instance, phenylahistin, a metabolite of Aspergillus ustus, was isolated in milligram quantities per liter of a liquid culture [78], similarly to aphidicolin produced by Nigrospora sphaerica [79] whereas other promising potential anticancer agents of fungal origin are acquired in lower quantities [80, 81]. Fungi are grown in purity on liquid substrates that have simple and mineral defined composition, which enables relatively simple isolation and identification of produced metabolites. The application of liquid shaken bioreactors is problematical in terms of formation of fungal mycelial clumps and pellets, which impede mass transfer and interfere with oxygen allocation in fermenters. However, the described inconvenience can be, at least partially, solved by either adsorbing cells to surface or causing self-immobilization via cell pellets formation. In this way, increase in cell density and productivity can be achieved [82–84]. While discussing effective production techniques, solid-state fermentation (SSF) cannot be omitted, as it offers higher fermentation productivity and thus higher concentration of obtained products, higher stability of these products, and lower requirement for sterility as in SSF low water activity is used [82–84]. The application of waste from food industries, which constitute ultimately low-cost substrates, provides fungi with a considerable variety of metabolic compounds. With well-preserved efficiency, the described approach allows to reduce demand for fossil-based resources, simultaneously promoting circular economy by transforming food waste into valuable chemicals [85].

In order to enhance metabolites production, it is possible to manipulate the composition of environmental factors, as even minor changes in nutritional or physical growth conditions may have significant influence on the fermentation products characteristics [86]. Manipulating light intensity, temperature, quantity and quality of accesible nutrients, and many other variables, leads to unpredictable changes in secondary metabolites production, which offers tremendous opportunities in terms of creating environment directed toward acquiring certain metabolite. Significantly, not only enumerated components forming growth conditions play roles in metabolic profile, but also surrounding microorganisms are able to alter fermentation outcomes. Mixed fermentation constitutes the process in which presence of specific microbes induces production of certain metabolites [87]. This method allows us to acquire a variety of metabolites, many of which were never before detected and analyzed, as a result of combining metabolic pathways of microorganisms. A more genetic-oriented approach involves triggering biosynthetic pathways that lead to desired secondary metabolites through epigenetic remodeling [88].

Apart from inconveniences related to production and optimization, physiological challenges further complicate the clinical application of fungal metabolites. Many of these compounds exhibit limited bioavailability, poor solubility, or instability under physiological conditions, which can hinder their effective delivery to target tissues. Additionally, the potential toxicity of fungal metabolites, even at therapeutic doses, necessitates extensive pharmacokinetic and toxicological studies to ensure their safety for human use [89]. Moreover, their mechanisms of action, including possible effects on angiogenesis or interactions with cellular signaling pathways, often remain incompletely understood, requiring detailed investigation. Addressing these physiological obstacles constitutes therefore the key to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of fungal metabolites and ensuring their successful transition from laboratory studies to clinical application.

In conclusion, while fungal metabolites hold immense promise as anticancer agents, their successful transition into clinical use application faces numerous challenges stemming from described limitations. Advances in fermentation techniques, environmental and genetic manipulation, and sustainable production methods offer potential solutions to logistical and biotechnological barriers. However, addressing physiological limitations such as bioavailability, stability, toxicity, and understanding their mechanisms of action is critical for unlocking their true therapeutic potential. Further research areas are essential to fully understand the clinical applicability of fungal metabolites.

Synergistic effects and combination therapies

Fungal-derived compounds have a distinctive significance in cancer therapy research, including the treatment of skin cancer. Their biological properties, such as the ability to induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis, and modify the tumor microenvironment, make them promising agents for supporting the fight against cancer. In particular, available studies focus on leveraging the synergistic effects that may occur when combined with other therapies. This approach not only enhances treatment efficacy but also offers a promising strategy for reducing the adverse effects often associated with aggressive cancer therapies [90, 91]. Due to the broad range of possible combination strategies, this section focuses primarily on those combinations for which a synergistic or additive effect has been supported by preclinical evidence, such as stronger inhibition of tumor growth, enhanced activation of the immune response, or reduced treatment-related toxicity.

In combination therapies involving compounds of fungal origin, the most convincing evidence currently concerns β-glucans and polysaccharides with immunomodulatory activity, particularly in combination with blockade of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. The significance of these compounds lies primarily in their ability to reprogram cells within the tumor microenvironment, enhance the activity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and partially reverse immunosuppressive mechanisms. In the context of skin cancers, data from the B16F10 melanoma model are particularly important, in which β-glucan produced by Aureobasidium pullulans (AP-BG) was combined with an anti-PD-L1 antibody. AP-BG was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 30 mg/kg on days 8, 11, and 14 after tumor cell implantation, whereas the anti-PD-L1 antibody was administered at a dose of 200 µg/mouse on days 8 and 12. AP-BG monotherapy resulted in 24.8% inhibition of tumor growth relative to the control group, while anti-PD-L1 monotherapy resulted in 19.3% inhibition. In this study, combination treatment led to 54.9% inhibition of tumor growth. The Q value for the AP-BG and anti-PD-L1 combination was 1.396, which the authors classified as a synergistic effect [92].

Another example of combination therapy in the B16F10 melanoma model involves a β-glucan-coated ferumoxytol nanocomposite (FMT-β-glucan). In the study by Liu et al. (2021), β-glucan derived from Saccharomyces cerevisiae was combined with ferumoxytol, an iron oxide nanoparticle formulation clinically used as an iron preparation and experimentally applied for macrophage modulation. A subcutaneous B16F10 melanoma model was established in male C57BL/6 mice by administering 5 × 10⁵ B16F10 cells, and treatment was initiated when tumours reached approximately 100 mm³. The animals received intraperitoneal PBS, FMT at a dose of 400 µg, β-glucan at a dose of 80 µg, a mixture of FMT + β-glucan, or the FMT-β-glucan nanocomposite containing 400 µg FMT and 80 µg β-glucan. FMT-β-glucan inhibited tumour growth more effectively than the simple FMT + β-glucan combination, reduced tumor mass and volume, and limited Ki67 expression in tumor tissue [93]. The antitumour effect resulted from the functional integration of a nanoparticulate carrier and an immunostimulatory fungal polysaccharide. FMT-β-glucan increased the accumulation of ferumoxytol in tumor tissue, intensified M1 macrophage infiltration, and reduced the proportion of M2 cells compared with the FMT + β-glucan mixture. However, no CI or Q values were reported for FMT-β-glucan. Therefore, this system should be regarded as a combined nanomedical system with a strong immunomodulatory effect rather than as formally confirmed pharmacological synergy.

A similar strategy is represented by carboxymethylated β-D-glucan from the sclerotium of Pleurotus tuber-regium combined with iron oxide nanoparticles (CMPTR/IONPs). The CMPTR/IONPs nanocomplex had a diameter of approximately 193 ± 7 nm, exhibited colloidal stability, low cytotoxicity toward RAW 264.7 and THP-1 cells while maintaining more than 80% cell viability, and showed greater uptake by macrophages than by B16F10 cells. In an in vitro model, CMPTR/IONPs reprogrammed M2-like BMDM macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype by increasing the expression of CD86, TNF-α, and iNOS and reducing the expression of M2 markers such as CD206 and Arg-1. iNOS expression after CMPTR/IONPs treatment was almost 20-fold higher than in M2-like macrophages and approximately threefold higher than after CMPTR alone. In co-culture with B16F10 cells, such macrophage reprogramming significantly reduced melanoma cell viability. The mechanism of CMPTR/IONPs action was associated with the parallel effects of both components of the system: CMPTR activated receptors recognizing β-D-glucan, including TLR2, TLR3, TLR4, and CD11b, as well as the NF-κB pathway, whereas the iron oxide component participated in IRF5 activation. CMPTR/IONPs also increased MyD88 expression, reduced IκB-α, and enhanced ROS generation in M2-like macrophages [94].

A related mechanism has been described for whole glucan particle β-glucan (WGP β-glucan) in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. In a preclinical and clinical study, the authors evaluated whether β-glucan could delay or partially reverse resistance to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy. In the murine model, combination treatment increased the recruitment of immune cells to the tumor microenvironment, regulated the balance between T-cell activation and immune tolerance, and delayed tumor progression [95]. The clinical part of the study included 13 patients with advanced cancers in whom anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy had previously been discontinued due to disease progression. The study showed that adding WGP β-glucan to renewed PD-1/PD-L1 blockade was associated with improved progression-free survival in this population [95]. Nevertheless, these findings cannot be treated as direct evidence of efficacy in skin cancer, because the study included a heterogeneous group of advanced malignancies rather than a distinct melanoma or keratinocyte carcinoma model.

A more targeted strategy for delivering β-glucan to the tumor microenvironment was developed in a study of an anti-PD-L1 antibody–β-glucan conjugate. The conjugate, obtained using click chemistry, was designed to combine immune checkpoint blockade with local delivery of an immunostimulatory polysaccharide. In the murine MC38 model, 86.7% inhibition of tumor growth was achieved, and the effect was accompanied by earlier activation of the immune response, increased dendritic cell infiltration, and activation of pre-existing T cells within the tumor microenvironment [32]. Moreover, T-cell proliferation occurred mainly locally within the tumor rather than because of cell migration from peripheral organs [32].

Another combination approach, directly oriented toward melanoma, uses a chitin and β-glucan hydrogel, composed of the main constituents of the yeast cell wall, as both a carrier and an immune adjuvant. Yang et al. (2023) developed a hybrid hydrogel containing a B16F10 melanoma cell membrane (CM) and indocyanine green (ICG), thereby combining photothermal therapy with immunotherapy based on cell membrane antigens and immunostimulatory fungal components. In the primary melanoma model, C57BL/6J mice received a subcutaneous injection of 100 µL of B16F10 cell suspension at a concentration of 8 × 10⁵ cells/mL, and after the tumor reached a volume of 80–100 mm³, 100 µL of the appropriate therapeutic system was injected intratumorally. In irradiated groups, an 808 nm laser was applied for 5 min on the first and third days after injection. The CM-ICG-hybrid hydrogel rapidly ablated the primary tumor, inhibited its further growth, and increased mouse survival, while also increasing IFN-γ and TNF-α concentrations [96]. The authors described the effect as a synergistic combination of photothermia, melanoma cell membrane antigens, and the immunostimulatory chitin/β-glucan hydrogel. It should be noted, however, that no formal CI or Q values were provided. Terefore, the evidence for synergy is functional in nature and is based on comparison of the multicomponent system with the relevant control groups.

The use of Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharide in combination with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy further indicates that a similar mechanism may also apply to other fungal polysaccharides. In a colorectal cancer model, supplementation with G. lucidum polysaccharide inhibited tumor growth, increased the proportion of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T lymphocytes and Th1 lymphocytes in the spleen and tumor tissue, and simultaneously reduced the percentage of regulatory T lymphocytes. These immunological changes were accompanied by mitigation of microbiota dysbiosis, increased production of short-chain fatty acids, and a reduced serum kynurenine-to-tryptophan ratio, which the authors associated with a favorable effect on the antitumor T-cell response. The combination of G. lucidum polysaccharide with an anti-PD-1 antibody further enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy [97]. In this respect, however, due to the tumor location, these data should be interpreted as mechanistic support for the concept of combination therapies rather than as evidence of efficacy in skin cancers.

In the context of melanoma, the study of G. lucidum polysaccharide peptide (GL-pp) in the B16-F10-luc-G5 model in mice subjected to sleep fragmentation is of substantial relevance. GL-pp was administered orally at a dose of 80 mg/kg for 15 days after melanoma cell inoculation. Sleep fragmentation enhanced melanoma metastasis, whereas GL-pp significantly reduced metastases in this model. Proteomic analyses and gut microbiota assessment indicated that this effect was accompanied by changes in regulatory pathways associated with the tumor microenvironment, immune response, and macrophage polarization [59].

The clinical application of fungal β-glucans as adjuvants in anticancer treatment has been documented more in relation to the tolerability of chemotherapy and radiotherapy than to the direct enhancement of cytotoxicity against cancer cells. A systematic analysis of clinical studies showed that β-glucans derived, among others, from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Lentinula edodes, and Schizophyllum commune were generally well tolerated and, in some studies, reduced treatment-induced immunosuppression or accelerated the recovery of hematological parameters [98]. In breast cancer, L. edodes mycelial extract used during FEC75 chemotherapy was associated with less deterioration of hematological parameters after treatment, whereas 1,3/1,6-D β-glucan administered between chemotherapy cycles was associated with increased IL-12 levels and a smaller decrease in leukocyte count than in the control group [98]. This indirectly supports the classification of β-glucans as immunoprotective adjuvants.

Thus, the most strongly documented combination directly related to skin cancer remains AP-BG with PD-L1 blockade in the melanoma model, because it includes defined dosing, comparison of monotherapy with combination treatment, a measurable tumor growth inhibition effect, and a Q parameter indicating synergism [92]. Additional studies on FMT-β-glucan and the CM-ICG-hybrid hydrogel demonstrate that, in the B16F10 model, multicomponent systems combining immunostimulatory fungal polysaccharides with iron oxide nanoparticles, melanoma cell membrane antigens, or photothermia may also be effective [93, 96]. However, the presented data requires cautious interpretation, as combination index values have not been reported for most systems involving fungal compounds, and direct data from skin cancer models remain limited. For this reason, fungal compounds should currently be regarded primarily as candidates for adjuvant, immunomodulatory, and carrier-based therapies, whose usefulness in the treatment of melanoma, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, and basal cell carcinoma requires further validation in models specific to these malignancies.

Conclusions

Fungal-derived compounds constitute a diverse and promising source of bioactive molecules with considerable potential for the prevention and treatment of skin cancer. Evidence demonstrates that these metabolites exert anticancer effects through multiple mechanisms, including induction of apoptosis and cell-cycle arrest, modulation of immune responses, inhibition of angiogenesis, and suppression of tumor invasion and metastasis. Their ability to target several molecular pathways simultaneously makes them strong candidates for overcoming therapeutic resistance and improving the efficacy of current anticancer strategies. Preclinical studies, particularly in melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer models, have provided encouraging evidence of the antitumor activity of compounds such as cordycepin, ganoderic acid D, ergosterol peroxide, gliocladicillins, and fungal polysaccharides. Moreover, recent findings suggest that fungal-derived β-glucans and other immunomodulatory metabolites may enhance the effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors and other combination therapies by remodeling the tumor microenvironment and promoting antitumor immunity. Despite these promising findings, the clinical translation of fungal-derived compounds remains limited. Challenges related to sustainable production, standardization, bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and safety continue to hinder their widespread therapeutic application. In addition, most available evidence originates from in vitro studies or animal models, while well-designed clinical trials in patients with skin cancer remain scarce. Future research should focus on optimizing the production and formulation of fungal metabolites, improving their pharmacological properties through advanced drug-delivery systems, and conducting robust clinical studies to establish their efficacy and safety in melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers. A better understanding of their molecular mechanisms and their integration into combination treatment strategies may ultimately facilitate the development of novel, more effective, and less toxic therapeutic approaches for patients with skin cancer.
