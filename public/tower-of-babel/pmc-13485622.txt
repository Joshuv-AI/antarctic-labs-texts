Ultrasound-Assisted enzymatic extraction of polysaccharides from agricultural By-Products: A comprehensive review☆
Authors: Aqsa Bibi, Ume Roobab, Umama Zulfiqar, Sadia Ansar, Shahzad Hussain, Rana Muhammad Aadil, Sajid Maqsood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polysaccharides are complex biopolymers of monosaccharide units linked through glycosidic bonds that serve roles in energy storage and diverse biological functions in living organisms. Agricultural by-products, including fruit peels, pomace, seeds, cereal brans, and plant residues, are abundant, renewable sources of bioactive polysaccharides with applications in the food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical sectors. These residual materials contain functional polysaccharides with antioxidant, immunomodulatory, prebiotic, and hypoglycemic properties. Conventional extraction methods have limitations, including prolonged processing times, excessive energy and solvent consumption, thermal degradation of heat-labile compounds, and scalability challenges. To address these constraints, ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE) has emerged as a green extraction technology combining ultrasonic cavitation-induced mechanical disruption of plant cell walls with enzymatic hydrolysis for selective polysaccharide liberation. This review explains how enzyme-assisted extraction improves release by hydrolyzing cell-wall barriers (cellulase, hemicellulase, pectinase, and protease), often requiring optimized multi-enzyme cocktails and multivariate mixture designs to avoid antagonism. UAEE enhances extraction efficiency, reduces processing time, and operates under mild conditions that preserve structural integrity and bioactivity. Extraction performance is governed by the interplay of enzyme loading, pH, temperature, ultrasonic power, treatment time, and solid–liquid ratio, each of which displays a substrate-dependent optimum beyond which yield or structural integrity declines. Because the reported optima differ markedly with the botanical source and the target polysaccharide, they are best regarded as matrix-specific windows that require re-optimization for each feedstock rather than as universal set points. It evaluates UAEE applications, analyzes effectiveness across agricultural substrates, examines optimization methodologies, and discusses extracted polysaccharides, providing an overall sustainable framework.

Introduction

Polysaccharides represent a diverse class of naturally occurring biopolymers composed of monosaccharide units interconnected by glycosidic bonds, functioning as fundamental structural and energy-storage components in plants and diverse organisms [1], [2]. The unique properties of polysaccharides, derived from plants, animals, marine organisms, fungi, and microorganisms, including their inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and non-toxicity, have established them as invaluable biomolecules across the food, pharmaceutical, cosmetic, and biomedical industries [3], [4], [5]. The functional properties of polysaccharides, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, hypoglycemic, and anticancer activities, are intricately linked to their molecular weight, monosaccharide composition, branching, and structural conformation [6], [7]. Despite extensive research, efficient extraction methods that preserve these critical structural features remain a significant challenge.

Agricultural by-products, generated in vast quantities globally, represent an abundant and underutilized source of polysaccharides and other bioactive compounds [8], [9], [10], [11]. For instance, Perilla frutescens (L.) Britt seed meal, a residue of oil processing, is a recognized source of recoverable water-soluble polysaccharides in addition to proteins and lipids; ultrasound-assisted enzymatic processing of this meal liberates polysaccharide fractions (yields of the order of 6–8%) composed of rhamnose, arabinose, xylose, mannose, glucose, and galactose that exhibit concentration-dependent antioxidant activity [12], [13]. These residues, including fruit peels, seed meals, and processing wastes, are rich in proteins, polysaccharides, dietary fibers, polyphenols, and other functional molecules [14], [15], [16]. However, their chemical composition varies considerably depending on the source, complicating standardized extraction and valorization strategies. The widespread uncontrolled disposal of agricultural wastes through decomposition and greenhouse gas emission, creating an urgent imperative for sustainable recovery and utilization approaches. The quantitative scope of this challenge is substantial: current data from the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) 2025 report documents that 13.2% (1.25 billion tones) of food is lost between harvest and retail stages, with an additional 19% (exceeding 1 billion tones) lost at household, food service, and retail levels [17]. Although prior studies have highlighted the potential of agricultural residues as reservoirs of functional biopolymers [18], [19], existing extraction techniques encounter persistent challenges, including operational inefficiencies, undesired structural degradation, and substantial environmental consequences.

Conventional extraction methods, such as hot water extraction, acid/base hydrolysis, ethanol precipitation, and enzymatic treatments, have well-documented limitations, including prolonged processing times, harsh conditions leading to polysaccharide degradation, impurity retention, and substantial resource consumption [1], [6], [20], [21]. These constraints hinder the full exploitation of polysaccharides’ functional potential and limit scalability for industrial applications. Recent advances have introduced modern extraction technologies, including ultrasound-assisted, microwave-assisted, enzyme-assisted, pressurized liquid, deep eutectic solvent, and supercritical solvent extractions, which aim to overcome these challenges [21]. Among these, ultrasound-assisted enzyme extraction (UAEE) uniquely combines mechanical disruption of plant cell walls via ultrasound-induced cavitation and shear forces with enzymatic specificity for targeted polysaccharide release [21], [22], [23]. This synergistic approach enhances extraction efficiency, reduces processing time, and preserves molecular integrity, presenting a green, scalable solution aligned with sustainability goals [24].

Nonetheless, despite promising developments, standardization and optimization of UAEE parameters remain underexplored, particularly in the context of diverse agricultural by-products with variable compositions [25]. There is a critical gap in the comprehensive understanding of how UAEE protocols can be tailored to maximize the yield and bioactivity of polysaccharides while minimizing environmental impact. Addressing this gap is essential for advancing the sustainable valorization of agro-industrial residues and fostering circular economy principles in food systems.

This comprehensive review systematically evaluates the current UAEE strategies for extracting polysaccharides from diverse agricultural by-products. The analysis focused on the critical interactions between ultrasound parameters (frequency, power, amplitude, and treatment time), enzyme selection and concentration, and substrate characteristics, examining how these variables synergistically enhance polysaccharide yield and preserve functional properties relative to conventional methodologies. By synthesizing mechanistic insights with empirical optimization data, this review aims to establish a comprehensive framework for UAEE application and identify future research priorities that are necessary for widespread industrial adoption.

Polysaccharides from agricultural by-products: Sources, composition, and characteristics

Agricultural by-products as sustainable polysaccharide sources

Plant-derived polysaccharides extracted from agricultural by-products represent a fundamentally sustainable resource available for isolation, valorization, and utilization across the food, pharmaceutical, and biomedical sectors [3]. These residual materials, generated during the commercial processing of fruits, vegetables, grains, and legumes, are biochemically enriched in both structural (such as pectin, cellulose, and hemicellulose) and storage polysaccharides (particularly starches). The compositional profile of agricultural by-product polysaccharides demonstrates substantial quantitative significance (Table 1). For instance, mango peels and seeds collectively constitute 35–60% of the fruit's total mass [26], lychee peel accounts for approximately 18% by weight [27], pineapple pomace comprises 25–35% of processed fruit weight [28], blackberry seeds represent 80% of processing residues [29], and sugarcane hulls constitute approximately 20% of total cane weight while containing valuable proteins and structural cellulose [30]. Diversity of polysaccharide types and their agricultural sources

The outer protective materials typically discarded during fruit and vegetable processing function as gelling, thickening, and prebiotic agents due to their polysaccharide composition, rendering them beneficial for manufacturing diverse food products [3], [21]. Specifically, residual materials generated from juice extraction, fermentation, or oil processing contain soluble fibers, starch fractions, and non-starch polysaccharide complexes that can be selectively isolated through advanced extraction methodologies including ultrasound-assisted, microwave-assisted, enzymatic, and high-pressure extraction technologies, subsequently enabling application in biodegradable films, encapsulation systems, and dietary enrichment formulations [31]. The outer bran layers of cereal grains, conventionally discarded as processing byproducts, are biochemically abundant in substantial quantities of hemicellulose, arabinoxylans, β-glucans, and cellulose, which collectively exhibit significant water-holding and gelling capacities [19]. The green foliar biomass accumulating from vegetable and herb crop processing contains substantial amounts of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic substances, rendering this material particularly valuable for the production of biodegradable packaging materials, pharmaceutical capsules, and nutritional health supplement formulations [18]. Seed coats and associated seed husks, recovered as residues from protein or fat extraction processes, serve as sources for obtaining soluble plant polysaccharides, including galactomannans, alongside insoluble starch fractions that function effectively as natural thickeners, texture modifiers, and carriers for bioactive ingredients in diverse functional food applications [31]. Floral and inflorescence materials, conventionally removed during spice processing or ornamental plant utilization, can be processed to yield mucilaginous polysaccharides possessing emulsifying and bioactive properties, rendering them suitable as stabilizing agents for emulsions and foam systems, and as health-promoting agents in nutraceutical formulations [7], [32]. Structural plant components, including stalks, stems, husks, and shell materials, consist primarily of cellulose alongside associated lignocellulose polymers that, following appropriate pretreatment, yield nanocrystalline cellulose, hydrogels, biofuels, and bio-based composite materials with substantial utility for food industry product development, particularly in packaging applications, biomedical device fabrication, and industrial biotechnology [14], [18]. By strategically targeting these underutilized waste fractions from fruits and vegetables, emerging opportunities can be systematically developed to advance industrial functionality and sustainability objectives.

Molecular structure and physicochemical characteristics

The functional and biological activities exhibited by plant-derived polysaccharides are fundamentally determined by their principal chemical composition, molecular weight distribution, and the specific nature of glycosidic linkages maintaining monosaccharide connectivity. In general terms, the molecular weight of plant polysaccharides exhibits considerable variation, typically ranging from several thousand Daltons to millions of Daltons, encompassing a spectrum from 10 to 1,000 kDa and potentially extending to multimillion Dalton polymers. These molecular weight variations depend on chain length characteristics and the extent and type of branching patterns present within the polymer structure [49], [50]. Mechanistically, polysaccharides with lower molecular weights characteristically carry more free reducing groups per unit mass, resulting in pronounced radical scavenging activity and enhanced antioxidant capacity. Conversely, higher molecular weight polysaccharides typically form complex three-dimensional networks and associations that generate substantial viscosity properties and facilitate gelation phenomena, rendering them suitable for texture modification applications [49], [51].

The nature of the glycosidic linkages interconnecting individual monosaccharides fundamentally determines the overall three-dimensional architectural arrangement and conformational properties of polysaccharides. These linkages display considerable structural diversity, occurring in both α- and β-configurations and involving connections between the first carbon position and the fourth and sixth carbon atoms, as well as more complex branching patterns [14]. Collectively, these intricate structural characteristics generate a spectrum of biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and anti-glycation effects, mediated through mechanisms involving the regulation of polysaccharide interactions with cellular membranes, enzymatic systems, and intracellular signaling pathways [49], [51]. Understanding these structure–function relationships is essential for predicting and optimizing the bioactivity of extracted polysaccharides.

Fundamentals of extraction technologies: Enzyme-assisted extraction

Mechanisms and principles of enzyme-assisted extraction (EAE)

Enzyme-assisted extraction (EAE) is a green extraction technology that selectively hydrolyzes structural barriers within biological matrices to facilitate the release of intracellular polysaccharides [52]. Hydrolytic enzymes catalyze the degradation of cell wall polymers, systematically loosening the rigid polysaccharide network structure and increasing cellular membrane permeability to facilitate compound release [53]. This enzymatic hydrolysis mechanistically reduces resistance to mass transfer, enabling polysaccharides to readily diffuse into the extraction solvent with markedly greater efficiency than non-enzymatic extraction methodologies. EAE demonstrates superior effectiveness relative to chemical extraction because enzymatic catalysts specifically target structural macromolecules with high biochemical selectivity, accomplishing this objective without the requirement for harsh chemical reagents or elevated temperatures that would compromise compound stability [52].

During extraction pretreatment, enzymes interact with substrate materials under carefully controlled pH and temperature conditions optimized to ensure maximal enzymatic activity. Enzymatic hydrolysis of structural polymers results in progressive cell wall erosion, systematic pore formation, cellular fragmentation, and enhanced solvent infiltration, collectively culminating in substantially improved polysaccharide release efficiency [52], [54]. The efficiency of EAE is substantially influenced by the compositional and structural characteristics of plant and algal cell walls. Specific enzyme classes demonstrate substrate specificity for particular polysaccharide components: cellulase catalyzes the depolymerization of cellulose microfibrils, hemicellulose targets branched hemicellulose polymers, pectinase degrades pectic substances, and proteases enzymatically cleave structural proteins that might otherwise sterically hinder polysaccharide release [55].

Multi-enzyme synergy and optimization

Due to the inherent structural complexity and compositional diversity of plant cell walls, single-enzyme systems frequently prove insufficient for comprehensive cell wall degradation [56]. Instead, multi-enzyme complexes displaying complementary specificity work synergistically, wherein sequential enzymatic reactions expose additional substrate sites accessible to subsequent enzymes, thereby progressively enhancing cell wall degradation efficiency and maximizing polysaccharide extraction [52], [57]. However, inappropriate enzyme proportions can paradoxically produce antagonistic interactions, thereby reducing overall extraction efficiency. Consequently, mixture design methodologies and multivariate regression analysis are essential analytical tools employed to systematically optimize enzyme proportions and achieve maximal extraction yields [55].

Operational parameters influencing EAE performance

The polysaccharide yield achieved through EAE is significantly influenced by operational parameters, which exert direct impacts on both enzymatic activity and the efficiency of mass transfer processes [58]. The quantity of enzyme used fundamentally dictates the extent of substrate hydrolysis. Within an optimal concentration range (approximately 400–600 U/g), increasing enzyme concentration progressively improves enzyme-substrate interactions, facilitates more efficient cell wall breakdown, and enhances polysaccharide release from the biological matrix [59]. However, excessive enzyme concentrations (e.g., 1400 U/g) can lead to substrate saturation or undesirable partial hydrolysis of released polysaccharides, thereby counterintuitively limiting further yield improvement [59].

Extraction pH has the most significant influence on the effectiveness of the extraction process, both in terms of the extraction yield and the yield of polysaccharides. Enzymes function optimally at their optimum pH, whereas extremes from this optimum will alter the structure of the enzymes [60]. As reported by Gao et al. [61], the highest polysaccharide yield from Erythronium sibiricum bulbs was at an optimum pH of 7.5. Temperature exerts dual effects on extraction efficiency, affecting both the reaction rate of enzymatic processes and the solubility characteristics of target polysaccharides. The yield of polysaccharide increases with the rise in temperature until the optimum temperature for polysaccharide extraction; thereafter, an increase in temperature causes a decrease in yield because the enzymes are denatured. However, for enzyme-assisted polysaccharide extraction from pumpkin seeds, the yield began to rise gradually with increasing reaction temperature up to 60 °C, which yielded a maximum polysaccharide (2.94 ± 0.13%), but decreased above 60 °C because of the denaturation of enzymes [62].

The extraction duration determines the total extent of enzymatic hydrolysis achievable. The polysaccharide yield generally increases with extended treatment time until substrate accessibility becomes limiting or equilibrium conditions are attained. For Acanthopanax senticosus polysaccharide, for example, extraction times ranging from 20–80 min produced progressive yield increases, reaching 10.40% at 80 min [59]. For pomegranate peel polysaccharides, the extraction yield increased as the extraction time was extended from 45 to 90 min, reaching its maximum at 90 min [63]. Extension of treatment to 100 min produced negligible additional yield improvement and resulted in slight decreases, likely attributable to complete enzymatic cleavage of available substrate within the 80-min interval. Extended treatment beyond the optimal duration may precipitate undesirable polysaccharide degradation without conferring significant recovery advantages [59].

The solid-to-liquid ratio fundamentally impacts solvent penetration and diffusion dynamics, with proper ratios enhancing enzyme-substrate contact and promoting mass transfer efficiency [59]. Expansion of the extraction volume from 15 mL to 40 mL resulted in continuous yield improvements, attributed to increased water-to-raw material ratios that enhance solvent diffusivity into cells and promote polysaccharide desorption from particle surfaces, achieving maximum yields (10.50%) at 40 mL extraction volume. Further volume increases to 50 mL did not improve extraction rates, likely because excessive solvent causes material over-swelling that paradoxically reduces available polysaccharide contact. Consequently, 40 mL was identified as the optimal extraction volume for such systems [59]. Similarly, in the EAE of polysaccharides from Potentilla anserina L., the extraction yield increased with the solid-to-liquid ratio from 1:10 to 1:15 (19.85 ± 0.02% at the highest ratio), but decreased at higher ratios (1:15–1:30) because the interaction between the solvent and plant matrix was reduced [64].

It should be emphasized that these numerical optima are specific to the polysaccharide and matrix under study and are not directly transferable to other systems. The reported optimal enzyme loadings, temperatures, and treatment times differ substantially between substrates, such as Lycium barbarum, Atractylodes macrocephala, and citrus peel, reflecting differences in cell wall architecture, target polysaccharide solubility, and enzyme accessibility [56], [59], [65]. Reviews of EAE and UAEE consistently conclude that each new feedstock requires its own optimization and that parameters determined for one polysaccharide type provide, at best, an initial range for another [53], [66].

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE): Mechanisms and optimization

Physical principles of ultrasonic technology

Ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) technology, which has substantially evolved over recent years for plant material polysaccharide extraction, converts electrical energy into mechanical acoustic waves, creating alternating compression and rarefaction cycles that generate microbubble formation within the extraction solvent [23]. The collapse of these microbubbles through acoustic cavitation generates shock waves, turbulent flow patterns, intense shear forces, and localized heating and pressure phenomena, collectively enhancing extraction efficiency [67]. Critically, acoustic cavitation mechanisms accomplish this enhancement while simultaneously preserving the structural and molecular integrity of target compounds [68]. This cavitation-mediated process substantially amplifies solvent-sample interactions, enhances penetration into plant tissues, and optimizes the diffusion of target compounds, thereby significantly improving overall extraction efficiency [68].

Operational modes and equipment configuration

Ultrasonic extraction can be conducted through two principal operational modes. The direct probe method involves positioning an ultrasonic transducer probe directly into the extraction solution, operating at high intensity to generate substantial cavitation forces and efficient extraction. However, this direct contact method can promote corrosion of equipment surfaces, necessitating the use of durable materials such as titanium for probe construction [69], [70]. Alternatively, the indirect bath method positions the sample container within an ultrasonic bath system, operating at a comparatively lower intensity with reduced cavitation forces and extraction efficiency but eliminating contamination risks associated with direct probe contact [69]. The selection between these operational modes depends on the specific experimental objectives and constraints.

Solvent and environmental parameters affecting UAE

The extraction efficiency of UAE is affected by numerous interrelated factors, including the solvent type and concentration, extraction duration, solvent-to-material ratio, and temperature [67], [71]. The selection of solvent type and concentration significantly influences extraction process efficiency, as commonly employed solvents such as water, ethanol, or their aqueous mixtures differentially affect the solubility of target compounds. Optimization of solvent concentration to maximal levels enhances extraction efficiency, whereas concentrations either higher or lower than the optimal value result in substantially reduced extraction performance [72]. An elevated solvent-to-material ratio generally improves extraction efficiency through mechanisms involving increased concentration gradients and enhanced mass transfer efficiency. However, beyond a critical threshold ratio, utilization of excessively large solvent volumes produces marginal or slight decreases in efficiency, indicating the existence of an optimal extraction ratio [73].

The treatment duration of ultrasonication significantly influenced the release kinetics of the target compounds. Extraction efficiency progressively increases with extension of ultrasonication time up to a definable threshold, beyond which further temporal increases result in decreased extraction efficiency, potentially attributable to degradation of thermally or acoustically sensitive compounds, or detrimental overexposure effects [73]. Temperature is a critical parameter that affects both the solubility and diffusion kinetics of compounds within the extraction solvent. Increasing the extraction temperature generally improves the yield up to an optimal level, beyond which elevated temperatures substantially reduce the stability of the extracted compounds [74]. Ultrasonic power intensity distinctly influences the degree of cavitation and the magnitude of sample matrix disruption. Moderate ultrasonic power levels enhance extraction efficiency by promoting solvent penetration and mass transfer, whereas excessively high power intensities may damage the target compounds, thereby paradoxically reducing the yield and extraction efficiency [73]. Studies have demonstrated that the extraction efficiency of antioxidants from mung bean coat, whose cell wall is composed of structural polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, increased marginally with ultrasonic power increases up to 500 W, whereas further power elevation from 500 W to 800 W resulted in declining extraction efficiency, establishing 500 W as the optimal power level for such applications [73].

Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE): Integration and synergistic mechanisms

Principles of synergistic integration

UAEE represents an integrated extraction technique that enhances bioactive compound recovery from biomass by synergistically combining the complementary effects of ultrasonic cavitation and enzymatic hydrolysis [75]. The mechanical forces generated through acoustic cavitation provide substantial mechanical disruption of cell wall structures while concurrently improving solvent penetration efficiency, whereas enzymes, including cellulase, hemicellulase, and pectinase, catalyze the targeted biochemical breakdown of structural polysaccharides within the plant matrix, facilitating enhanced compound diffusion through cellular tissues [75]. The interdependent synergy of these two complementary processes, mechanical and biochemical, operates synergistically to overcome structural barriers and enhance mass transfer efficiency, culminating in substantially more efficient extraction compared to either methodology applied independently [76]. UAEE can be systematically conducted utilizing ultrasonic devices equipped with either probe or bath transducers that effectively transform electrical energy into ultrasonic waves [77]. The extraction process involves combining biomass with a suitable solvent and selected enzymes, followed by sonication under carefully controlled conditions encompassing temperature, treatment time, and acoustic power intensity, administered either as continuous treatment or in periodic pulse cycles according to experimental requirements [77]. The resulting processed extract is subsequently separated from solid residue through centrifugation and filtration operations, with the clarified liquid extract subsequently preserved through appropriate storage methodologies for subsequent analytical characterization [77].

Critical parameters determining UAEE efficiency

The extraction efficiency achieved through UAEE depends fundamentally on the optimal synergy between the ultrasonic and enzymatic parameters [78]. Extraction time represents a critical variable in compound release kinetics, as acoustic sonication effectively enhances mass transfer up to an optimal duration threshold, beyond which accumulated bioactive compound degradation may commence [65]. Temperature exerts a significant influence on both enzyme activity and solute solubility; optimal temperature conditions ensure maximal extraction yield; empirical studies demonstrate yield increases from 30 °C to 60 °C, reaching peak values of 8.07% [65]. Similarly, UAEE of polysaccharides from Nostoc commune, increasing the enzymolysis temperature from 30 to 70 °C, improved the extraction yield by enhancing enzyme activity and catalytic efficiency. However, temperatures above 60 °C reduced the extraction yield due to thermal denaturation and irreversible enzyme inactivation [79]. Enzyme concentration substantially influences the extent of structural breakdown occurring within the biomass matrix; while higher enzyme levels generally improve accessibility to intracellular compounds, excessive enzyme amounts do not yield proportional additional benefits [80]. A study on polysaccharide extraction from Rhizoma atratylodes macrocephala indicated that the enzyme amount of 2.0% proved sufficient for achieving high polysaccharide yields without additional benefits from further concentration increases [65].

Ultrasonic power significantly affects cavitation intensity and mass transfer efficiency; moderate power levels facilitate efficient compound release, whereas excessive power can damage heat-sensitive molecular structures. Previous studies have demonstrated that polysaccharide yield increases progressively as ultrasonic power increases from 100 W to 250 W, with declining yields observed at 300 W power levels, establishing 250 W as the optimal power intensity for rhizoma atractylodes macrocephala polysaccharide extraction [65]. Similarly, during the extraction of polysaccharides from Tremella fuciformis, the extraction yield increased progressively as ultrasonic power increased from 0 to 600 W, whereas no further improvement was observed at 700 W, indicating 600 W as the optimum ultrasonic power [81]. The liquid-to-material ratio represents an additional critical parameter, with low-to-moderate ratios enhancing solvent penetration and mass transfer efficiency, whereas ratios exceeding optimal levels may decrease extraction efficiency due to excessive dilution and reduced concentration gradients [30]. These integrated parameters demonstrate the combined effects of physical ultrasonication and biochemical enzymatic hydrolysis, underscoring the absolute necessity of systematically optimizing both conditions to maximize overall extraction performance.

In practice, the term UAEE encompasses several process configurations that differ in how the acoustic and enzymatic steps are ordered, and this ordering materially affects both yield and product structure. Three arrangements are commonly reported. In ultrasound pretreatment schemes, cavitation is applied first to increase the surface area and porosity, so that the enzymes subsequently gain improved access to their substrates. In simultaneous (in situ) treatment, sonication and hydrolysis proceed concurrently, so that freshly exposed glycosidic bonds are continuously acted upon, although the mechanical and thermal stresses of sustained sonication can partially inactivate the enzyme. In the enzyme-then-ultrasound arrangement, pre-loosening of the cell wall enhances the mass-transfer benefit of the later acoustic step. Fig. 1 depicts this last sequential configuration, which is widely adopted because it shields the enzyme from prolonged cavitation. The optimal order is matrix-dependent, and recent work has shown that pulsed or alternating schemes, coupled with careful control of acoustic energy, can reconcile the competing demands of enzyme stability and cavitation-driven mass transfer [76]. Advantages of UAEE over traditional methodologies

UAEE demonstrates remarkable capability for optimizing both the yield and quality of bioactive compounds while ensuring an eco-friendly and sustainable extraction process [25]. The integration of ultrasonic waves' disruptive capabilities with the specific enzymatic action characteristic of UAEE synergistically enhances mass transfer, disrupts cellular structures, and facilitates access to intracellular materials, collectively accelerating the extraction process and promoting the solubilization of desired compounds while reducing processing time requirements [25]. The extraction of dietary fiber using the UAEE technique could be an economical and eco-friendly approach for the valorization of agro-industrial byproducts. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis indicated that the UAEE technique could positively influence the functional properties of dietary fiber fractions, highlighting its advantages over conventional extraction methods [78]. The process demonstrates substantial environmental advantages through reducing solvent consumption and energy requirements, supporting sustainable practice implementation by utilizing renewable enzymatic catalysts and enabling substantially solvent-reduced or solvent-free extraction paradigms [25]. Dietary fibers extracted using the UAEE technique exhibited higher crystallinity than those obtained by alkaline extraction (ALE), indicating that ultrasound treatment disrupted the amorphous regions of the fibers. Thermal analysis further showed that UAEE-extracted fibers possessed greater thermal stability than ALE-extracted fibers. These findings highlight the advantages of UAEE over conventional extraction methods and demonstrate its potential as a sustainable approach for the valorization of agro-industrial byproducts, contributing to improved resource efficiency and zero-waste generation within a circular economy framework [82]. These multifaceted benefits establish UAEE as an efficient, safe, and adaptable extraction methodology suitable for both laboratory-scale research applications and large-scale industrial production scenarios [25].

Ultrasound-assisted enzymatic extraction of polysaccharides from various agricultural by-products.

Crude polysaccharide extraction

Crude polysaccharides represent the initial extraction fraction, containing complex carbohydrate molecules and co-extracted non-carbohydrate materials, reflecting the inherent compositional complexity of plant cell wall matrices [105]. Different extraction techniques yield variable polysaccharide quantities and compositions. For example, crude polysaccharide extraction from blackberry seed residue demonstrated comparative yields: hot-water extraction produced the lowest yield (1.73%), whereas advanced methodologies, including ultrasound-assisted (2.78%), microwave-assisted (2.25%), enzyme-assisted (3.02%), ultrasound-microwave combined (3.26%), and ultrasound-enzyme combined (4.25%) extraction, increased yields through enhanced cell wall disruption, improved mass transfer, and synergistic effects [29]. Although all methodologies yielded similar monosaccharide compositions, notable variations were observed in the molar ratios and molecular weight distributions [29].

Song et al. [85] investigated lychee nuclear polysaccharide extraction employing pectinase as the enzymatic catalyst in UAEE protocols, isolating 4.86% polysaccharide characterized by high sugar content (85%) and elevated glucuronic acid levels at an ultrasonic power of 360 W, extraction time of 55 min, and temperature of 55 °C, substantially exceeding the yields from conventional hot water extraction (2%). Progressive increases in pectinase dosage from 1.5% to 2.5% enhanced both the yield and sugar content of the recovered polysaccharides due to increased pectin molecular breakdown, facilitating the active release of polysaccharides [85]. However, pectinase concentrations exceeding 2.5% resulted in decreased yields, attributable to excessive enzymatic hydrolysis and undesirable degradation of glycosidic bonds [85].

Compound enzyme cocktails employed for polysaccharide extraction from Shatian pomelo peels demonstrated enhanced total sugar content and elevated glucuronic acid levels compared to hot water extraction methodologies [83]. Wang et al. [41] documented extraction of crude polysaccharides from dandelion (Taraxacum officinale) leaves through UAEE, achieving maximum extraction efficiency at 240 W ultrasonic power, 30 min duration, 50 °C temperature, pH 5.0, and 1.5–2.0% enzyme concentration, with each parameter contributing to extraction yield improvements until threshold limits beyond which degradation and over-hydrolysis occurred. During UAEE of Perilla frutescens seed meal polysaccharides employing pectinase, papain, and cellulase at 210 W ultrasonic power and 40 kHz frequency, extraction time and temperature substantially influenced yield outcomes [90]. Lower levels of both factors resulted in reduced yields, whereas increasing both factors significantly enhanced recovery [90]. At 50 min extraction time, yield improvements with temperature increases peaked between 50 and 65 °C then slightly decreased; at 70 min, yield initially improved then decreased [90]. Additional research exploring ultrasound-assisted enzymatic hydrolysis for sugarcane leaf polysaccharide extraction revealed that UAEE achieved superior yields compared to conventional extraction methodologies, with ultrasound enhancing enzyme accessibility to plant matrices [104]. This extraction was substantially influenced by enzymatic extraction conditions (enzyme dosage, pH, and temperature) and ultrasound parameters (time and intensity), which improved yield and antioxidant activity while requiring careful control to prevent compound degradation from overuse [104].

Dietary fiber extraction

Dietary fiber, comprising compounds that cannot be digested or absorbed by the human gastrointestinal system, exists in both soluble and insoluble forms and represents a major target for UAEE applications [106]. Extraction of dietary fiber through conventional methods is challenging; however, UAEE enables recovery with minimal structural damage. The yields of insoluble dietary fiber (IDF: 41.06–56.16%) and soluble dietary fiber (SDF: 4.82–11.86%) from Citrus limetta peels increased with moderate parameter values, including temperature, ultrasonic amplitude, solid–liquid ratio, and extraction time, attributed to increased cavitation intensity, enhanced cell rupture, and improved mass transfer efficiency [82]. Optimal conditions identified through Box-Behnken design and response surface methodology were 40 °C, 30% amplitude, 30 w/v solid–liquid ratio, and 20-min duration, with yield decreases occurring beyond these parameters owing to cavitation instability and polysaccharide degradation [82].

The synergistic effect of combined ultrasound and enzymatic treatments produced markedly elevated soluble dietary fiber yields compared to ultrasound alone [94]. The SDF yield from glutinous rice bran progressively increased with ultrasonic treatment duration from 5 to 35 min, achieving a maximum yield (34.89%) at 35 min [97]. This pattern reflects the progressive enhancement of polysaccharide yield as the duration of ultrasonic wave interaction with bran material increases to a specific temporal threshold. Similar extraction trends have been documented for dietary fiber recovery from queen pineapple peel [107]. Kaur et al. [57] conducted dietary fiber extraction studies using Kinnow peel, demonstrating that UAE alone yielded 52% total dietary fiber (TDF), which increased significantly to 61% when the samples underwent subsequent enzymatic processing, substantiating the superiority of the extraction methodology. UAE of dietary fiber from mango peel demonstrated maximal dietary fiber yields compared to alternative extraction methodologies [78]. Optimization of conditions, including 40% amplitude, 25 °C temperature, 9-min treatment time, and 1:50 liquid-to-solid ratio, studied through response surface methodology, demonstrated that variables substantially affect extraction efficiency [78]. Likewise, excessive conditions may result in sample degradation.

Pectin extraction

Pectin is a complex heteropolysaccharide found predominantly within the cell walls and middle lamellae of plant cells [108], where it functions as a cellular adhesive by binding cells together and contributing to tissue structural integrity. The chemical composition and structural characteristics of pectin vary considerably depending on the botanical source, extraction methodology, and maturation stage of the plant material [109]. This compositional variability directly influences the functional properties and subsequent applications of extracted pectin in the food, pharmaceutical, and biomedical industries.

UAEE technology significantly enhances pectin extraction efficiency through synergistic mechanisms that combine acoustic cavitation with enzymatic degradation. Ultrasonic treatment induces intense cavitation phenomena, generating shock waves and microjets that disrupt cell wall structures and increase solvent penetration into plant tissues, thereby facilitating superior release of polysaccharides [38]. Concurrent enzymatic treatment breaks down cell wall polymers and middle lamellae components, further promoting cell wall disruption through targeted hydrolysis of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectic substances. This combination results in increased pectin production and controlled molecular weight reduction through partial hydrolysis, while maintaining bioactive compound integrity compared to conventional extraction methods [76], [89].

Controlled studies utilizing sugar beet pulp as a model substrate demonstrated substantial pectin yield improvements through ultrasonic pretreatment combined with enzymatic treatment [92]. When maintaining a xylanase-to-cellulase ratio of 1:1.5, pretreatment durations of 15–45 min increased pectin yield by 84–92% at 1-hour enzyme treatment and 67–95% at 2-hour enzyme treatment, with diminishing returns (2–16% improvement) observed at 4-hour treatment. This pattern reflects the balance between increased mass transfer rates caused by acoustic cavitation, enhanced enzyme penetration into cell wall matrices, and increased porosity of cellular structures, which collectively facilitate pectin depolymerization without excessive degradation. Comparative analysis between extraction modalities revealed that MAE achieved superior pectin yields (23.32%) compared to UAEE (7.95%) from apple pomace under optimized conditions [89]. The same results were also reported for pectin isolation from kiwi peel, as microwave treatment outperformed ultrasound [35].

The higher pectin yields typically reported for MAE are attributed to rapid volumetric dielectric heating, which accelerates the hydrolysis of protopectin and disrupts the cell-wall matrix more aggressively than acoustic cavitation [110]. However, this intensity is a double-edged property: the elevated bulk temperatures characteristic of MAE promotes depolymerization, de-esterification, and loss of the rhamnogalacturonan side chains that govern gelling behavior; therefore, a yield advantage is frequently accompanied by a decline in functional quality [111]. UAEE, by contrast, couples localized cavitation with substrate-specific enzymatic hydrolysis under milder thermal conditions, and its more modest yields are often offset by higher molecular weight, better preserved structural domains, and retained bioactivity. In practical terms, MAE is preferable when maximal yield and throughput are the priorities and moderate structural degradation is acceptable, whereas UAEE is the more appropriate choice when the functional and biological quality of the recovered pectin must be preserved [112]. However, recent research indicates that surfactant-ultrasonic-assisted acid extraction from citrus peel residues can achieve a maximum extraction efficiency of 15.77% (w/w) while preserving critical structural domains essential for functional properties [113]. Starch extraction

Starch is the predominant storage polysaccharide in plant tissues, consisting of two distinct glucose-based polymers, amylose and amylopectin, arranged within semicrystalline granular structures [121]. This biopolymer serves crucial roles as both a functional food ingredient and industrial raw material with applications spanning food processing, pharmaceutical formulations, and biodegradable packaging systems. The starch content and molecular composition exhibit substantial variation across botanical sources, with reported levels ranging from 88% in rice grains to 98% in pineapple stems [122]. Correspondingly, amylose content, a critical determinant of starch functionality, varies from 6% in rice to 34% in pineapple stems. These compositional differences directly influence the physicochemical properties, digestibility profiles, and functional applications of starches derived from different plant sources, necessitating source-specific extraction and modification strategies.

Green banana (Musa species) peels and pulp represent underexploited sources of resistant starch (RS), a non-digestible carbohydrate with demonstrated prebiotic and metabolic benefits [123]. The RS fraction extracted from both peeled and unpeeled green bananas exhibits superior physiochemical properties compared to that of mature banana starch, including high swelling power, water and oil retention capacities, and acceptable solubility suitable for beverage and health food formulation [124]. Enzyme-assisted extraction methods utilizing amyloglucosidase and pancreatin preparations effectively release bound RS from green banana peel matrices, with UAEE demonstrating particular efficacy in improving extraction efficiency and yield. The combination of acoustic sonication with enzymatic treatment synergistically enhances RS recovery from green banana peels, representing a more efficient alternative to conventional heat-based extraction methods.

Systematic optimization of UAEE parameters for resistant starch extraction from green banana peels revealed that moderate, controlled processing conditions substantially enhance yield while maintaining structural integrity [91]. Treatment at temperatures between 30 and 35 °C, acoustic application periods of 3–6 min, and liquid-to-solid ratios of 20:1–30:1 produce maximum resistant starch yields, attributable to synergistic enhancement of enzyme activity, improved substrate diffusion, and favorable molecular rearrangement of starch granules [91]. Conversely, processing conditions beyond these optimal parameters result in substantial yield reduction, explained by enzyme inactivation at elevated temperatures, structural damage from prolonged ultrasonic exposure, and diminished diffusion advantages at excessive solvent ratios. Recent advances in banana starch modification techniques, including preheating combined with branching enzyme approaches, have further expanded the potential for producing tailored starch ingredients with enhanced functional and nutritional properties [125].

Other polysaccharides

Beyond the major fractions discussed above, UAEE has been applied to structurally distinct polysaccharides that do not fall neatly into the crude-polysaccharide, dietary-fiber, pectin, or starch categories, two of which are listed in Table 2. β-Glucan, a linear (1 → 3)(1 → 4)-linked glucan valued for its cholesterol-lowering and immunomodulatory properties, has been recovered from oat bran using an ultrasound- and enzyme-assisted procedure in which α-amylase, protease, and endo-1,3(4)-β-glucanase remove co-extracted starch and protein, 25 °C sonication improves solvent penetration, yielding a purified β-glucan fraction under mild acoustic energy [96]. Corn silk polysaccharide, obtained from the maize by-product corn bean by cellulase-assisted sonication, was efficiently recovered by UAEE and is of interest because of its reported antioxidant activity [100]. These examples underline that the applicability of UAEE extends across a broad structural spectrum of by-product polysaccharides and that the enzyme system must be matched to the specific target rather than applied generically to achieve optimal results.

UAEE treated polysaccharides as functional and bioactive components

Polysaccharides recovered from plant products and agricultural by-products have attracted considerable scientific and commercial interest because of their remarkable diversity of bioactive constituents, which have applications in the food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical industries [3], [5]. UAEE represents an efficient, environmentally sustainable, and economically viable technology platform for extracting polysaccharides from agricultural residues, which addresses both resource utilization and waste minimization objectives inherent to circular economy principles [15]. The polysaccharide products generated through UAEE processing exhibit exceptional functional versatility, encompassing gelling, thickening, emulsifying, film-forming, and stabilizing properties, while simultaneously retaining or enhancing bioactive characteristics, including potent antioxidant activity, hypoglycemic effects, prebiotic functionality, and immunomodulatory effects [80], [102], [107]. This combination of technical functionality and biological activity significantly expands the potential applications of agricultural by-product-derived polysaccharides across diverse commercial sectors. Functional properties and food processing applications

Extensive biochemical characterization and functional testing have established that polysaccharides extracted from diverse agricultural residues, including fruit peels, pulp residues, leafy biomass, and seed coats, possess substantial availability and critical importance as bioactive agents, functional food ingredients, dietary supplements, and sustainable packaging materials [8], [9]. Resistant starch isolated from green banana peels demonstrates favorable physicochemical attributes, including high swelling power, exceptional water and oil retention capacities, and acceptable solubility profiles, rendering these materials suitable for incorporation into beverages and specialized health food formulations [91]. Pectin materials derived from jaboticaba fruit peels exhibit excellent gelling, emulsifying, and stabilizing capabilities during food processing applications while preserving endogenous phenolic compounds with documented health-promoting properties [95]. Apple pomace and orange peel pectin provide superior gelling, stabilizing, thickening, and emulsifying properties for the development of food texturizers and edible protective coatings [84], [89]. Sugar beet pulp pectin functions effectively as a versatile hydrocolloid in bakery, confectionery, and beverage formulations [92].

Prebiotic and disease prevention applications

Emerging evidence demonstrates that UAEE-derived polysaccharides from diverse botanical sources possess significant prebiotic and preventive therapeutic potential for human health applications. Barley-derived polysaccharides show considerable promise as functional food ingredients for the development of prebiotic-enriched products targeting the prevention and management of diverse metabolic and gastrointestinal disorders [127]. Agricultural waste streams, particularly mango processing residues, represent valuable feedstocks for the recovery of polysaccharides with elevated dietary fiber content and potent antioxidant properties, enabling the formulation of enhanced nutritional products that simultaneously improve consumer health and reduce agricultural waste accumulation [128]. The comprehensive characterization of bioactivity profiles and mechanistic understanding of structure–activity relationships for UAEE-extracted polysaccharides continues to expand the evidence base supporting their application as pharmaceutical adjuvants, functional food ingredients, and nutraceutical supplements. Contemporary research utilizing advanced analytical methodologies and cell-based bioassay systems has established that polysaccharide structural features, including monosaccharide composition, glycosidic linkage patterns, molecular weight distribution, and degree of substitution, directly determine their biological activity and therapeutic potential [49], [50], [51].

Much of the prebiotic and metabolic data concerning by-product polysaccharides is derived from in vitro fermentation and cell-based assays. In contrast, relatively few UAEE-specific extracts have been assessed in animal models [129], [130], [131], [132], [133], and even fewer in humans [102]. Consequently, the strength of the evidence varies significantly among claims and should not be generalized across sources. It is crucial not to attribute the intrinsic bioactivity of a polysaccharide directly to UAEE. The process can plausibly influence the structure–activity relationship: by integrating controlled cavitation with selective hydrolysis, UAEE tends to reduce molecular weight, increase the proportion of accessible reducing ends, and alter branching and uronic-acid content. These features are correlated with radical-scavenging and immunomodulatory potency [130], [134]. When such structural changes have been directly compared with those produced by conventional extraction, UAEE-derived fractions can exhibit enhanced activity. However, in the absence of matched, head-to-head comparisons, bioactivity should be cautiously regarded as a property of the polysaccharide that UAEE helps to preserve or fine-tune, rather than as a unique advantage conferred by the technique.

Challenges and future perspectives

Strategic optimization through advanced computational approaches

The UAEE technology platform demonstrates exceptional promise for recovering polysaccharides from agricultural by-products through the integration of green chemistry principles, high extraction efficiency, superior yields, preservation of thermolabile bioactive constituents, and scalability potential supporting long-term sustainability objectives [139], [140]. Systematic optimization of all critical extraction parameters, including ultrasonic frequency, acoustic power intensity, wave amplitude, treatment duration, reaction temperature, and enzymatic composition, is essential to unlock the full technical and economic potential of the UAEE process [57]. However, conventional univariate experimental methodologies typically require prohibitively lengthy optimization periods and demonstrate insufficient capacity to effectively characterize complex nonlinear interactions among multiple operational parameters. The strategic incorporation of computer-assisted analytical tools, including machine learning algorithms, artificial intelligence frameworks, and advanced predictive modeling systems, substantially facilitates accurate predictions of optimal extraction conditions for specific plant matrices, rational design of enzyme combinations, and selection of target polysaccharide fractions [141]. These computational approaches enable reduction of experimental burden, acceleration of process development timelines, improvement of extraction authenticity and benefit profiles, substantial cost minimization, and enhancement of overall productivity metrics.

A recent application illustrates this shift. Li et al. [142] trained a support-vector-regression model against response-surface data to optimize the ultrasound-synergistic enzymatic extraction of Saposhnikovia divaricata polysaccharides, achieving an 11.82% yield while identifying enzyme dosage and acoustic power as the dominant predictors and outperforming conventional regression in predictive accuracy [142]. Such data-driven models illustrate how machine learning can compress the experimental burden of multiparameter UAEE optimization and expose nonlinear interactions that univariate designs cannot capture.

Enzyme engineering and synergistic system design

Future technological advancements in enzyme engineering constitute a critical research and development priority, as coordinated combinations of enzymatic activities, including cellulases, hemicellulases, pectinases, and proteases, function synergistically to efficiently disrupt plant cell wall architecture and facilitate the release of target polysaccharide components [66]. Current knowledge gaps regarding optimal enzyme proportion ratios, precise enzyme quantities for specific biomass compositions, reaction condition requirements, and enzyme stability under intense ultrasonic cavitation conditions represent significant constraints limiting extraction efficiency and product composition consistency. Deployment of stabilized immobilized enzyme systems, recyclable enzyme formulations, or microorganism-derived enzyme preparations promises substantial enhancement of enzyme stability, preservation of enzymatic activity through multiple extraction cycles, significant simplification of overall extraction procedures, and substantial improvement of economic viability for large-scale industrial production operations [116].

Industrial scale-up and reactor design innovation

The technological transition of UAEE from laboratory-scale research protocols to commercially viable industrial-scale operations presents substantial engineering challenges that require the development of advanced continuous-flow ultrasonic reactor systems [143]. State-of-the-art reactor designs must incorporate uniform cavitation field generation, effective mixing mechanisms, precise real-time temperature control, and continuous monitoring of essential operational parameters, including fluid turbidity, viscosity, and bioactives concentration [114]. Integration of adaptive response management systems with these advanced reactors would guarantee consistent product quality, uniform productivity across extended production campaigns, meaningful energy conservation, and sustained operational durability. Strategic combination of UAEE with complementary green extraction modalities, including microwave-assisted extraction, pulsed electric field treatment, and deep eutectic solvent-assisted extraction, represents a viable approach to further enhance solvent penetration, optimize mass transfer rates, and enable targeted extraction of selective polysaccharide fractions for specialized food, nutraceutical, and pharmaceutical applications [55], [93], [95], [140].

A practical constraint that is frequently overlooked in scale-up is the progressive ageing of the ultrasonic hardware itself. Sustained cavitation erodes the emitting surface of probes and transducers, resulting in tip pitting, mass loss, and detachment of metal fragments, which gradually reduce the delivered acoustic intensity and can introduce metallic contamination into the extract [144]. Because the effective cavitation field drifts as the equipment ages, nominally identical settings no longer deliver identical energy, which undermines batch-to-batch reproducibility [145], an effect that is amplified in continuous industrial reactors operating over long periods. Mitigation strategies include the use of erosion-resistant titanium-alloy or ceramic emitters, scheduled acoustic-power calibration and probe replacement, real-time monitoring of delivered rather than nominal power, and a preference for indirect or push–pull reactor geometries that distribute cavitation more evenly and limit localized surface damage.

Circular economy integration and sustainability assessment

From sustainability and circular bioeconomy perspectives, UAEE technology represents an invaluable platform for comprehensive valorization of underexploited agricultural residues, including fruit peels, pomace materials, cereal brans, seed coats, and tuber residues [117]. These agricultural by-products are not merely waste streams but rather constitute valuable reservoirs of functional polysaccharides and other bioactive compounds. To ensure sustained environmental and economic viability of UAEE technologies, future research and development initiatives should prioritize comprehensive life cycle assessment studies, systematic energy consumption analysis, optimization of solvent utilization ratios, and detailed techno-economic feasibility [55], [146]. The strategic adoption of emerging smart manufacturing technologies utilizing real-time sensors for continuous monitoring of enzyme activity, cavitation intensity, and polysaccharide concentration, coupled with automated feedback control systems and seamless integration with downstream purification, drying, and product formulation processes, promises the achievement of fully automated production systems with reproducible high yields and consistent product quality [117], [147].

Conclusion

This review synthesizes current knowledge on UAEE as a green and potentially scalable route to structurally intact bioactive polysaccharides from agricultural by-products. The evidence assembled here indicates that coupling ultrasonic cavitation, which disrupts cell walls and enhances mass transfer, with selective enzymatic hydrolysis under mild conditions consistently improves polysaccharide recovery relative to single-method approaches, while helping to preserve molecular weight, structure, and associated bioactivity. Realizing this potential at an industrial scale nonetheless remains constrained by enzyme cost, the stability of both cavitation and enzyme activity, the need for matrix-specific optimization, and reactor design. Continued progress in enzyme engineering, reactor and process design, and data-driven optimization, together with life-cycle and techno-economic assessment, will be central to translating UAEE into a robust, resource-efficient technology for the valorization of agro-industrial residues within a circular economy framework.
