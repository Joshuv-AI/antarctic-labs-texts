The role of biophysical cues and their modulated exosomes in dental diseases: from mechanism to therapy
Authors: Bilun Jin, Yuxin Liao, Zhaojing Ding, Rui Zou, Feng Xu, Ye Li, Bo Cheng, Lin Niu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDental diseases such as caries and periodontitis have been common public health problems. Dental disease treatment can be achieved through stem cell-based dental regeneration. Biophysical cues determine the fate of stem cells and govern the success of dental regeneration. Some studies have manifested exosomes derived from stem cells could not only inherit biophysical signals in microenvironment but also evade some issues in the treatment with stem cells. Nowadays, biophysical cue-regulated exosomes become another promising therapy in dental regenerative medicine. However, methods to improve the efficacy of exosome therapy and the underlying mechanisms are still unresolved. In this review, the association between biophysical cues and dental diseases was summarized. We retrospected the role of exosomes regulated by biophysical cues in curing dental diseases and promoting dental regeneration. Our research also delved into the mechanisms by which biophysical cues control the biogenesis, release, and uptake of exosomes, as well as potential methods to enhance the effectiveness of exosomes. The aim of this review was to underscore the important place biophysical cue-regulated exosomes occupy in the realm of dentistry, and to explore novel targets for dental diseases.Keywords: Biophysical cues, Exosomes, Dental caries, Periodontitis, Regeneration

Introduction

Dental diseases, such as dental caries, periodontitis, dentin hypersensitivity and even inflammatory root resorption during orthodontic treatment, represent vexing problems that have challenged public health worldwide [1–3]. These dental diseases influence not only the oral functions of patients but also their aesthetics, and have been observed to be tightly associated with various systemic diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases [4], diabetes mellitus [5], thyroid diseases [6], carcinogenesis [7], gastrointestinal diseases [8] and reproductive diseases [9].

Biophysical cues are recognized as pivotal features of dental diseases. For instance, substrate stiffness is crucial in dental caries and periodontitis development. Caries would diminish the mineralization degree and the stiffness of dentin [10]. An abrupt stiffness gradient in the periodontal ligament entheses is a hallmark of periodontitis [11]. As for dentin hypersensitivity, the fluid shear stress is the most influential biophysical cue. When the patient is chewing food, brushing teeth and breathing, dentinal fluid would flow through the dentinal tubules rapidly [12]. The fluid shear stress produced by dentinal fluid flow could activate the mechanotransduction in odontoblasts and induce toothache [13]. Besides, the inappropriate orthodontic force is the primary cause of inflammatory root resorption during orthodontic treatment [14, 15]. On the other hand, some biophysical therapies are mainstream therapies for dental diseases, currently. However, these methods, encompassing root canal therapy, filling restoration, scaling and root planing, fail to reinstate the biology functions of dental tissues.

Dental tissue regeneration is a promising therapy to rebuild the biological functions in treating dental diseases [16]. Dental stem cells (DSCs) are stem cells extracted from dental tissue and are widely utilized in dental regeneration [17]. Ideally, these stem cells, such as dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), periodontal membrane stem cells (PDLSCs), have the potential to transform into various cell types [18]. Unfortunately, the efficacy of DSCs is unsatisfactory because of the diseased biophysical microenvironment [19]. Take periodontitis as an example. Low stiffness gradient strength in periodontitis would increase the expression of Toll-like receptor 3, a pro-inflammatory marker, in PDLSCs [20]. The osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs would also be inhibited by a lower matrix stiffness [21]. Considering the regulatory effect of biophysical cues on the biological functions of DSCs, there has been a growing emphasis on modulating DSCs by biophysical cues, aiming to augment the therapeutic efficacy. For example, mechanical stimulus such as cyclic tension, surface topographies and mechanical compression could modulate mechanosensors such as integrins, cytoskeleton and focal adhesion proteins, which then regulate downstream signaling pathways to guide the proliferation, migration and differentiation of DPSCs [22]. In addition, the electromagnetic and photothermal co-effect from laser would increase mitochondrial membrane potential to produce more adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and then expedite DPSC differentiation into odontoblasts [23].

With the development of stem cell therapies, some problems have thrown up. Immune rejection, tumorigenicity and ethical issues become inevitable difficulties during the application of stem cells in dental regeneration [24]. Exosomes refer to the nanoparticles secreted by cells and are identified as important carriers of intercellular communication [25]. They have lipid bilayer membranes with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nm [26]. Exosomes not only overcome the limitations of stem cell transplantation in terms of lower immunogenicity and few side effects, but also show the ability to treat dental diseases [27, 28]. For instance, exosomes from DPSCs have been an attractive approach for the treatment of endodontic diseases [27]. Exosomes from PDLSCs have been widely explored in treating periodontal diseases [29].

Interestingly, exosomes could inherit the response of donor cells to the biophysical cue [30]. For example, exosomes derived from breast cancer cells in stiffer substrate accelerated the migration and invasion of breast cancer cells [31]. Some research has also explored the relationship between biophysical cues and exosomes from DSCs. For example, 1.5 g/cm2 static compressive force for 24 h promoted exosomes secretion from PDLSCs to modulate bone metabolism [32]. Tension generated by 20% strain for 72 h increased miR-200b/c in PDLSC-derived exosomes, enhancing bone regeneration [33]. Dimensionality also affected exosomes from DPSCs and regulated the balance of Th17 cell/Treg in periodontium [34]. We generated a word cloud for keywords related to exosomes, dental diseases and biophysical cues from publications between 1960 and 2024. As shown in the results, microRNA was one of the most frequent keywords in the list. MicroRNAs such as miR-200b/c [33, 35], miR-181b-5p [36] miR-1246 [34] and miR-26a [37] have been proven to be features of exosomes regulated by biophysical cues. At the same time, stem cells from exfoliated deciduous teeth (SHEDs) and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are two of the most studied stem cells. SHEDs and MSCs from dental pulp or periodontal ligaments served as origin cells or recipient cells for exosomes regulated by biophysical cues and played a key role in treating dental diseases [32–37]. In summary, biophysical cues are key targets of dental diseases and important regulators of exosome therapy in dental regeneration. The potentials of biophysical cue-regulated exosomes in treating diseases have been gradually manifested. However, the underlying mechanisms and beneficial doses of exosomes in futural clinic trials are blurry [38]. In this review, we firstly reviewed the relationship between biophysical cues and dental disorders. Then we briefly retrospected the role of exosomes regulated by biophysical cues in curing dental diseases. Finally, research frontiers in the mechanisms by which physical cues regulated exosomes were delved. Some forms of application were also summarized. The aim of this review was to reveal the important place of biophysical cue-regulated exosomes in dental diseases, to inspire more research on related mechanisms and materials, and to explore promising novel targets for dental diseases.

Biophysical cues in development and therapy of dental diseases

Biophysical cues in development of dental diseases

In this section, we mainly reviewed some biophysical cues as pivotal manifestations and pathogenic factors of prevalent dental diseases. The stiffness of substrate is a vital factor in the development of dental caries and periodontitis. Caries would lead to the loss of dentin elastic modulus by reducing the mineralization degree [10]. The elastic modulus of normal peritubular dentin (33.9 to 43.5GPa) were significantly more than those of transparent dentin in carious teeth (33.0 to 39.2GPa) [39]. The minimum modulus of carious dentin was only 0.015GPa compared to the physiological range (7.64 to 20.15GPa) in primary teeth [40]. The lower stiffness could inhibit the proliferation [41], migration [42] and odontogenic differentiation [43] of carious DPSCs in dental pulp and affect the repair process. Gradual stiffness gradient is a mark for healthy periodontal ligament entheses. An abrupt stiffness gradient could present characteristically in the periodontal ligament entheses and prompt the development of periodontitis [11].

Mechanical forces are another common biophysical cue during orthodontic treatment. Appropriate mechanical forces exerted by orthodontic appliances could remodel the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone, resulting in tooth movement [44]. Inappropriate orthodontic forces have negative effects on dental pulp. After a 12-week treatment with a 300 g buccally orthodontic intrusive force, degeneration, vacuole formation, and adipose degeneration of odontoblast was observed in the coronal pulp of the maxillary first premolars [45]. Additionally, lack of occlusal stimuli during orthodontic treatment would contribute to atrophy of periodontal ligament and contraction of dental pulp arterioles [46, 47].

Moreover, fluid shear stress is an influential biophysical signal in dentin hypersensitivity [48]. The fluid shear stress with 10 dyn/cm2 for 60s activated PIEZO1 in odontoblasts differentiated from DPSCs. They released ATP to sensory pulp nerve fibers and induced tooth pain [12].

Biophysical cues as therapies for dental diseases

Some biophysical cues have been included in mainstream therapies for dental diseases. Treatment methods such as root canal therapy and scaling and root planning allow diseased tissues to be mechanically removed to prevent further development of dental diseases. Some biophysical cues have also served as new technologies in clinic practice. AcceleDent device is a popular appliance to accelerate orthodontic tooth movement. The device includes an activator and a mouthpiece part to generate a cyclic vibration of 25 g at 30 Hz [49]. A double-blind, randomized controlled trial has shown that using it for 20 min a day significantly accelerated the movement of maxillary canines in patients with the extraction of maxillary first premolars (maximum posterior anchorage and ≥ 3 mm of space after alignment) [50]. As for the biological mechanism, the vibratory stimulation exerts pressure on the human periodontal ligament and enhances the differentiation of osteoclasts and osteoblasts, thereby accelerating tooth movement [51]. Another example is the application of laser in treating caries. The erbium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Er: YAG) laser system consists of a fiber delivery and contact probe and a non-contact irradiation with cooling water spray [52]. The therapeutic mechanisms can be explained at both macroscopic and microscopic levels. Macroscopically, the Er: YAG laser operates at a wavelength of 2.94 μm, which is readily absorbed by water and hydroxyl radicals in hydroxyapatite [53]. The water content of sound dentin is about 10%, while that of carious dentin can be as high as 53% [54]. Leveraging the difference in water content, the laser could induce microbursts and prioritize the removal of carious dentin, thereby maximizing the protection of healthy dentin. Microscopically, a 100 mJ laser could significantly increase the odontogenic differentiation of DPSCs, thus promote the formation of reparative dentin [55].

As for severe pulp inflammation or injury, regeneration the pulp-dentin complex is a novel therapy and is expected to replace root canal treatment [56]. Since DPSCs could differentiate into different cells in response to biophysical cues, targeting biophysical cues to regenerate the pulp-dentin complex becomes an attractive area [22]. Stiffness was a target for pulp-dentin regeneration. DPSCs and a NF-gelatin scaffold with lower stiffness (0.89 ± 0.43 kPa) in the center and higher stiffness (6.10 ± 0.47 kPa) in the peripheral areas were implanted together into nude mice for four weeks. Finally, a pulp-dentin complex was successfully constructed in vivo. The regenerated dentin density was 633.6 ± 150 mg HA/cm3, which was about 60% of natural dentin in human. In the central area, adequate neovascularization without mineralized dentin was observed in the regenerated pulp [57]. Matrix topography, tubular size, and gravity have been proved to influence the polarization of DPSCs in a fabrication of the nanofibrous micropatterned tubular platform [58]. The polarization of DPSCs is a hallmark of successful differentiation into odontoblasts as cells can extend into dentin tubules and play the roles of immune defense neural sensation, which solves the dilemma of previous studies that could only form osteodentin but lack biological functions [59].

In conclusion, biophysical cues played pivotal roles in dental pathology and therapies (Fig. 2). Targeting biophysical cues to regenerate dental tissues has been a hot area of research. Nevertheless, stem cells have low survival and poor engraftment because of the diseased microenvironment [60]. Exosomes secreted by stem cells could partly replace stem cell therapies [61]. Meanwhile, exosomes have indispensable advantages. Firstly, they could be stored at -20℃ for 6 months with no significant biochemical activity changes and are safer for patients [62–64]. Secondly, exosomes can inherit biophysical features of the donor cell microenvironment [65]. Therefore, biophysical cue-regulated exosomes become an alternative in regenerative medicine.

The roles of biophysical cues in the development and treatment of dental diseases

Biophysical cues mediated biogenesis, secretion and uptake of exosomes

Biophysical cue-tuned exosomes have been burgeoning therapies for several diseases. Substrate stiffness promoted the cell invasion and migration of breast cancer cells via driving exosome secretion and the expression of thrombospondin-1 in exosomes [31]. Stiff substrate also deteriorated the tumor by increasing exosome secretion of human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line [66]. A thirty-minute heat stress at 43° induced heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) enrichment in supporting cell exosomes and protected hair cells from neomycin ototoxicity through the combination with toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) [67]. Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) was able to increase exosome secretion, and exosomes treated with LIPUS had a better anti-inflammatory effect compared to exosomes without LIPUS stimulation [68]. LIPUS could also promote the uptake of exosomes in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to accelerate diabetic wound healing [69]. Furthermore, other biophysical factors such as radiation, dimensionality and shock wave could also affect the functions of exosomes [30].

The potential of biophysical cue-regulated exosomes in treating dental diseases were also explored in some studies. Mechanical cues, including the tension, cyclic stretch, compressive force and even dimensionality, had specific efficacy in enhancing exosomes in the treatment of dental diseases. These exosomes regulated by biophysical cues had greater osteogenic induction [35], osteoclastogenic induction [32], anti-inflammatory capacity [34] and angiogenic function [37] on recipient cells compared to those not regulated by biophysical cues. Involvement of biophysical cue-regulated exosomes in treating dental diseases

Although exosomes are regarded as novel methods to improve dental health, how to obtain sufficient exosomes is a great challenge. Generally, no more than 1 µg exosomes could be extracted from 1 mL medium. But the beneficial dose is 10 to 100 µg exosomal proteins per mouse [70]. Effective uptake and adequate secretion of exosomes are equally necessary for clinical application [71]. Exosomes release their contents and perform functions after being uptake by recipient cells [72]. However, the clinical application of exosomes has been hindered by the low cellular uptake rate at about 1% within 1 h [73]. So far, limited research has elucidated the mechanisms by which biophysical cues affect exosomes. Therefore, possible regulatory mechanisms of biophysical cues on biogenesis, release and internalization of exosomes were focused in the next section.

The biogenesis, release of exosomes regulated by biophysical cues

Exosomes are extracellular vesicles produced by fusion of membrane and multivesicular bodies (MVBs). The cell membrane is firstly deformed, followed by endocytosis to produce MVBs. Then membrane budding in MVBs initiates the biogenesis of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) [74]. The pathway of ILV biogenesis can be categorized into endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT)-dependent pathway and ESCRT-independent pathway [75]. MVBs are transported through microtubules with the regulation of kinesins and RAS-related protein (RAB) GTPases [76–78]. Finally, the MVBs docked to the cell membrane and fused to release ILVs as exosomes. Some small GTPases are regulators for docking. Soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor-activating protein receptors (SNARE) play important roles in membrane fusion [79]. HSP90 could also regulate the fusion between MVBs and cell membranes [80]. When the HSP90 dimer is open, an amphipathic helix would expose to deform the membrane shape and increase exosome secretion [80].

Stiffness is the most commonly studied regulator for the biogenesis and release of exosomes. On the one hand, cancer cells in stiffer substrate would secrete more exosomes than cells in softer substrate. 10 kPa polyacrylamide (PA) gels promoted a variety of cancer cells (such as primary human hepatocytes, breast cancer cells and pancreatic cancer cells) to secrete more exosomes compared to 0.5 kPa PA gels [66]. Increasing substrate stiffness activated Akt and phosphorylated Rabin8 at Serine 149 (guanine nucleotide exchange factor of Rab8). Then Rabin8 activated Rab8 and promoted the guanosine triphosphate (GTP) loading on Rab8 to regulate the exosome secretion of cancer cells [66]. Breast cancer cells in 5 kPa PA gels secreted 2 to 2.5-fold more exosomes than cells in 0.5 kPa PA gels through the activation of YAP/TAZ, which is an important axis by which stiffness of extracellular matrix affects cells [31, 81]. On the other hand, a study based on hydrogels of alginate polymer linked with the cell adhesion peptide Arg-Gly-Asp showed opposite results. Mesenchymal stromal cells in 3 kPa hydrogels could secrete 10-time exosomes as much as those in 20 kPa hydrogels [82]. Actin-related protein 2/3 complex was found to limit the transport of MVBs and the release of exosomes in stiffer hydrogels [82]. Additionally, the CD63+ MVBs were found to have faster transfer speed in stem cells around softer hydrogels [82]. To sum up, the effect of stiffness on exosome secretion has not reached an agreement, and more studies are needed to probe the mechanisms.

Besides, there are some other biophysical factors affecting the release of exosomes. Dimensionality could regulate the secretion of exosomes. The release of exosomes in three-dimensional culture system was significantly increased than that in two-dimensional system through the dephosphorylation of YAP [83]. A higher temperature was able to promote the release of exosomes through the upregulated Rab3a [84].

Biophysical cues and the uptake of exosomes

Uptake of exosomes is often a selective progress in recipient cells. The progress could be mainly divided into three steps. Firstly, exosomes collide with cells and contact with cell membranes. Secondly, exosomes bind with cell membranes through membrane proteins. Thirdly, exosomes would be internalized through endocytosis and membrane fusion [85], or they could interact with membrane receptors situated on the recipient cells to initiate intercellular signaling pathways [76]. It is worth noting that inefficient uptake of exosomes has been a great challenge in the clinical application of exosomes. The exosomes which were not internalized by recipient cells would be quickly cleared through circulation [86]. Considering the plight, some biophysical therapies have been tried to enhance the efficacy of exosome uptake. Some examples were shown in the below section to discuss the potentials of increasing exosome uptake by biophysical cues.

Biophysical cues could increase the uptake of exosome by stimulating exosomal proteins in donor cells. For example, mechanical force is a factor in regulating the internalization of exosomes. Compressive force at 1.5 g/cm2 for 1 day would promote the expression of annexin A3 (ANXA3) in exosomes of PDLSCs. And ANXA3 could increase the exosome internalization in macrophages [32]. Mechanical force might be an optional method to raise the efficacy of exosomes.

Directly stimulation on recipient cells is another mechanism by which biophysical cues influence the uptake of exosomes. Here are some of the evidences. Stiffness of metrics was a factor that affected the rate of exosome uptake. Breast cancer cells on tissue culture polystyrene with a stiffness of 3 GPa had significant lower levels of extracellular vesicle uptake than cells on gelatin with a stiffness of 2 kPa to 24 kPa [87]. LIPUS was verified to accelerate the uptake of exosomes in HUVECs [69]. As for the mechanism, LIPUS induced a stable cavitation effect that caused the expansion and contraction of microbubbles in the liquid [88]. The stable cavitation effect resulted in a microstream, which facilitated the interaction between exosomes and HUVECs, thereby elevating the permeability of cell membranes and improving endocytosis significantly [89]. Methods to enhance the effectiveness of biophysical cue-tuned exosomes

Biophysical cue-regulated exosome-loaded hydrogels

Exosomes injected intravenously would be easily eliminated by macrophages [90]. Exosome-loaded hydrogels could provide a controlled release system and have been a promising method in regenerative medicine [91, 92]. The role of synergies between exosomes and hydrogels were widely explored in various fields, such as cartilage regeneration [93, 94], bone regeneration [95, 96], wound healing [97–101], endometrial regeneration [102], tympanic membrane perforation regeneration [103], spinal cord injury [104, 105], cardiac repair [106], liver regeneration [107] and corneal epithelial healing [108].

It is precisely because of hydrophilicity, water retention, biodegradability and biocompatibility of hydrogels that they have been widely used in tissue engineering [109]. Hydrogels have also been applied to deliver exosomes and treat dental diseases (Table 2). Specially, some properties have been changed to catered to the oral environment. For example, hydrogels were needed in exosome therapy of periodontitis. If exosomes were injected into periodontal pockets without hydrogels, the flow of saliva in the mouth, the exposed environment of periodontal pockets, and the rapid release of exosomes would reduce the efficiency of exosomes. In order to adapt to the oral environment, a semi-fluid-like hydrogel was injected to the periodontal pockets in liquid form to accommodate the irregular shape of periodontal pockets and release exosomes locally to promote periodontal regeneration [110]. For the propose of controlling the release rate of exosomes, gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel was grafted by NHS-PEG12-biotin onto the amino groups to persistently release biotin-modified exosomes and treat periodontitis. The hydrogel had good biocompatibility, even with biotin modification [111]. Additionally, hydrogels are also used in dental pulp regeneration. The small and irregular endodontic space necessitates the application of a hydrogel that exhibits adaptability. A thermosensitive hydrogel system with low viscosity was injected in root canal to adapt to the irregulate shape of endodontic space [112]. Finally, hydrogel served as buccal mucosal drug delivery system need to remain in intimate contact with the inner mucosal layer long enough to release exosomes in the moist conditions of the mouth [113]. In order to meet the requirements of buccal mucosal drug delivery systems and address the limitations of chitosan, a hydrogel renowned for its biocompatibility and biodegradability yet plagued by poor oral adhesion, chitosan was modified with catechol. The catechol-chitosan conjugates performed strong mucoadhesive bond with mucin chains on the mucosal surface, thus increasing adhesion of hydrogels [114].

In addition, only one research explored the features of exosomes and potential regulatory mechanisms. The study revealed that DPSC-Exo incorporated in chitosan hydrogel could convert the phenotype of macrophages and reduce periodontal inflammation in mice. As for mechanisms, the continual inflammation in the periodontium is partly attributed to the activation of NF-κB and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascades [115]. MiR-1246 in DPSC-Exo would downregulate the activation of NF-κB p65 and p38 MAPK signal pathways at the tissue level [116]. At the molecular level, DPSC-Exo and exosomal miR-1246 could decrease the mRNA and protein expression levels of the pro-inflammatory markers such as inducible nitric oxide synthase and CD68, and increase the mRNA and protein expression levels of the anti-inflammatory markers such as arginase-1 and CD206 in macrophages [116]. Exosomes in the above studies hardly involved biophysical cue-regulated exosomes. In view of the vital functions of biophysical cues, more studies are needed to develop a novel hydrogel delivery system to achieve the treatment with exosomes.

Surface engineering of exosomes

Surface engineering through genetic manipulation or chemical modification is another way to prompt the targeting specificity of exosomes and increase the efficiency of exosomes [117]. Genetic manipulation refers to tagging exosomes through fusing the gene sequence of a guide protein or polypeptide to the gene sequence of exosomal membrane proteins. For example, exosomes modified with rabies viral glycoprotein (RVG) had targeting to the brain and better efficiency in Alzheimer’s disease [118]. RVG specially recognized the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor on neuronal cells and increased the delivery efficiency of the intravenously injected exosomes [118, 119]. Chemical modification is another method in surface engineering of exosomes. It means tagging exosomes through conjugation reactions or lipid assembly. For instance, CP05-muscle-targeting peptide was loaded on exosomes with a phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer to enhance delivery and treat muscular dystrophy [120].

Some studies have also explored the role of engineering exosomes in treating dental diseases. C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) would target its ligand stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1) in inflammatory sites. Genetically engineered exosomes overexpressing CXCR4 were constructed to deliver miR-126 to M1 macrophages and inhibit inflammation of periodontitis in rats [121]. As for chemical modification, exosomes labeled with biotin by 1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoetha-nolamine-poly(ethylene glycol) (DSPE-PEG) could be anchored on the hydrogel through avidin-biotin interactions and provide sustained release to treat periodontitis [111]. Although limited research reported the surface engineering of biophysical cue-tuned exosomes in dental diseases, this approach is expected in the future.

Besides, exosome-containing microspheres have been applied in treating dental diseases with exosomes. A study encapsulated exosomes into polymeric microspheres based on poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid). Microspheres provided a sustainable release of exosome for ten weeks. During the first 72 h, 27% of exosomes were released and then at a rate of 0.8% per day. The amphiphilic synthetic polymeric vehicle from a triblock copolymer encapsulated with dentinogenic exosomes could significantly promote the formation of the reparative dentin bridge [122]. Concluding remarks

Dental diseases such as caries and periodontitis have been common public health problems. These diseases could influence oral functions of patients and promote the development of systemic diseases. Traditional clinic therapies have difficulty in restoring the biology functions of dental tissues. Regenerative dentistry brings a new light to the treatment of dental diseases. Comparing to stem cells, exosomes have some prominent advantages in dental regeneration, such as high biological security, easy in stocking and low immunogenicity. Biophysical cues played decisive roles in regulating the features of exosomes. As we reviewed, biophysical cues could influence the biogenesis, release and the uptake of exosomes. But the effects of biophysical cues on compositions of exosomes and on effectiveness of dental disorders are unknown. Advancing the research on the mechanisms by which biophysical cues affect exosome behaviors would help us to find new targets for dental diseases.

However, some limitations remain in laboratory studies. Although some studies explored the effects of one single cue, limited research clarified the effects when two or more biophysical cues appeared together. Present studies are limited to in vitro experiments and laboratory animal experiments. The culture environment of cells in vitro is usually Petri dishes or flasks, which is greatly different from dental tissues in vivo. Therefore, more bionic models should be developed to mimic in vivo environment.

There are also some barriers to the clinical application. The low extraction efficacy and non-specific cargo of exosomes hinder the development in this field [123]. Ultracentrifugation is the gold standard method for exosome separation, but it is not suitable for clinical application because of the low yield and purity. Tangential flow filtration (TFF) can significantly improve the yield and quality of exosomes. And TFF combined with size-exclusion chromatography enables further purification [71]. More research should focus on the methods for increasing the output of exosomes to achieve the large-scale application in clinical treatment. In addition, the doses of exosomes should be taken into account. Exosomes affect recipient cells in a dose-dependent and saturable manner [124]. Excessive exosomes are unnecessary, while insufficient exosomes cannot achieve therapeutic effect. More clinical trials should be conducted to ensure the beneficial dose of exosomes. Furthermore, exosome-loaded biomaterials are effective strategies to provide sustainable release and concentrated dose of exosomes [125]. Therefore, optimizing biomaterials should also be a focus of future research.
