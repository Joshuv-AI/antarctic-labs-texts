Likelihood-Based Root State Reconstruction on a Tree: Sensitivity to Parameters and Applications
David Clancy, Jr, Hanbaek Lyu, Sebastien Roch, Allan Sly

Abstract
. We consider a broadcasting problem on a tree where a binary digit (e.g., a spin or a nucleotide’s purine/pyrimidine type) is propagated from the root to the leaves through symmetric noisy channels on the edges that randomly flip the state with edge-dependent probabilities. The goal of the reconstruction problem is to infer the root state given the observations at the leaves only. Specifically, we study the sensitivity of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to uncertainty in the edge parameters under this model, which is also known as the Cavender-Farris-Neyman (CFN) model. Our main result shows that when the true flip probabilities are sufficiently small, the posterior root mean (or magnetization of the root) under estimated parameters (within a constant factor) agrees with the root spin with high probability and deviates significantly from it with negligible probability. This provides theoretical justification for the practical use of MLE in ancestral sequence reconstruction in phylogenetics, where branch lengths (i.e., the edge parameters) must be estimated. As a separate application, we derive an approximation for the gradient of the population log-likelihood of the leaf states under the CFN model, with implications for branch length estimation via coordinate maximization.



1.
Introduction

We consider the following broadcasting problem on a tree
T=(V,E)
. Information is transmitted from the root
\rho
to the leaves
L
through the edges, which function as noisy channels. More formally, information is propagated according to a stochastic process where the root
\rho
is initially assigned a spin
\sigma_{\rho}\in\{\pm 1\}
uniformly at random and the spins are transmitted down toward the leaves, where each edge
e=(u,v)
of
T
randomly flips the sign of the spin with probability
p_{e}^{*}
, or put differently according to the following transition matrix:

\begin{bmatrix}1-p^{*}_{e}&p^{*}_{e}\\
p^{*}_{e}&1-p^{*}_{e}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+\theta^{*}_{e}&1-\theta^{*}_{e}\\
1-\theta^{*}_{e}&1+\theta^{*}_{e}\end{bmatrix}.

where it can be checked that
\theta^{*}_{e}
, a useful alternative parameterization of the model, is equal to the second eigenvalue of the transition matrix.
The goal is to infer the root spin
\sigma_{\rho}
given the spins
\{\sigma_{x},x\in L\}
, at the leaves.
We assume that
p_{e}^{*}\in(0,1/2)
, or equivalently that
\theta^{*}_{e}\in(0,1)
, meaning that it is more likely that spins do not flip. We will refer to the collection
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=(\theta^{*}_{e}:e\in E)
as the “true parameter” of the model.

This so-called “reconstruction problem”
has been extensively studied.
Two competing forces impact the
reconstructibility of the root state, leading to a phase transition:
the noise on the edge channels produce a loss of information along the paths to the leaves;
on the other hand, the branching duplicates the signal and creates a large number of correlated noisy processes to the leaves. In the special case where
T
is a complete
b
-ary tree and
\theta_{e}^{*}=\theta
for all
e
, a classical result states that, as the number of levels goes to infinity, there exists a root state estimator with success probability bounded below from
1/2
if and only if
b\theta^{2}>1

[
2
,
13
,
15
]
. See, e.g.,
[
23
, Section 6.3.2]
for an account of the proof. This is known as the Kesten-Stigum (KS) bound and has been generalized in a number of ways (e.g.,
[
1
,
3
,
8
,
9
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
20
,
25
]
).
See also
[
19
]
for a recent survey and some applications.

The present work is motivated in part by applications in biology, where the model above is called the Cavender-Farris-Neyman (CFN) model
[
5
,
10
,
21
]
and is used to study molecular evolution on an evolutionary tree. Here the transformed parameters
l^{*}_{e}=-\ln\theta_{e}^{*}
are known as branch lengths.
The name “branch length” comes from the fact that one can view the flip probability
p^{*}_{e}
as resulting from a continuous-time constant rate of mutation along the edge
e
for an amount of time proportional to
l^{*}_{e}
. In that context the KS bound pertains to the reconstruction of ancestral sequences and has important implications for tree inference as well (e.g.,
[
6
,
22
,
18
,
24
]
).
Several different methods for ancestral reconstruction are used in the phylogenetics literature. Perhaps most common is the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE), which in this case boils down to estimating the root spin by the state with highest posterior probability given the observations at the leaves. Put differently, one infers a state according to the sign of the posterior root mean, also called magnetization of the root (i.e., the conditional bias under
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
given the leaf spins, see Def.
2.2
).

That root state estimator has a flaw – it depends on the branch lengths, which must be separately estimated. The central question we ask in this paper is the following:

How sensitive is the MLE to deviations in the branch lengths?
In a nutshell we show that, if the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
is sufficiently deep inside the KS reconstructibility regime and the estimated parameter
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
is within a constant factor, the magnetization of the root agrees with the root spin with high probability and deviates significantly from it with negligible probability.

Our results are not the first ones to show that a root state estimator is accurate without the precise knowledge of the true parameter. Indeed, for the CFN model on a binary tree, an estimator known as “recursive majority” does not use the branch lengths at all
[
18
]
; it also works all the way to the KS threshold
1/\sqrt{2}\approx 0.707
(i.e., whenever
\theta_{e}^{*}\leq 1/\sqrt{2}
for all
e
). But, unlike the MLE, it is
not
used in practice. Our current bound for the MLE holds whenever

\displaystyle{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\in[1-\delta,1-\delta/2]^{E(T)}\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\delta\leq\frac{1}{1190}.

See Remark
2.4
. This is quite far off from the KS bound. The extent to which this
can be improved is an interesting open problem.

Our results also have further implications in phylogenetics.
The related branch length estimation problem
is to recover the unknown true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
from repeated, independent observations of the states at the leaves of the tree under the true model
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
. It is of practical interest
whether standard optimization algorithms for the maximum likelihood approach, e.g., coordinate ascent
[
12
]
, can accurately estimate the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
.
Our new tail bounds on the magnetization provide new understanding of the likelihood landscape of the CFN model. This is because the gradient of the log-likelihood of the leaf states under the hypothetical model
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
admits a simple expression in terms of the magnetizations (see Lem.
2.5
).
As an application of our main result, we derive an approximation for the gradient of the population log-likelihood of the CFN model (Thm.
2.6
).
Deeper results on the Hessian of the population log-likelihood in the neighborhood of the true parameter and an analysis of coordinate maximization in a finite-sample setting will be detailed in forthcoming papers.

2.1.
Assumptions

We assume that the tree

We will denote the number of leaves in

Our analysis operates under the assumption that we are well within the reconstruction regime, that is, that mutation probabilities are sufficiently small that ancestral states can be reconstructed with better-than-random accuracy.
We introduce the following restricted parameter spaces that depend on

.

2.2.
Posterior-based ancestral state reconstruction: insensitivity to parameters

Fix two distinct nodes
u,v
in
T
. We call a node
w
a
descendant
of
u
with respect to node
v
if the shortest path between
w
and
v
contains
u
. The
descendant subtree at
u
with respect to
v
is the subtree
T_{u}
rooted at
u
consisting of all descendants of
u
with respect to
v
. A subtree of
T
rooted at
u
is a
descendant subtree of
u
if it is a descendant subtree of
u
with respect to some node
v
.

Our central quantity of interest is the posterior distribution of the state of an internal node given the leaf states in a descendant subtree. Because the states take only two values here, it is enough to consider the
posterior mean
, which is often referred to as “magnetization.”

Roughly speaking, the magnetization at a node
u
with respect to a descendant subtree
T_{u}
rooted at
u
is the “bias” on its spin after observing all spins at the leaves of the descendant subtree
T_{u}
. For instance, if all spins on the leaves of
T_{u}
are
+1
, then
u
will be quite likely to have a
+1
spin as well. The formal definition of the magnetization is given below and see Figure
1
for a
concrete example.

Definition 2.2

(Magnetization)
.

Let
T_{u}
be a descendant
subtree of
T
rooted at a node
u
. Let
L_{u}
denote the set of all leaves in
T_{u}
. For a generic parameter
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\in[-1,1]^{E(T_{u})}
and fixed spin configuration
\sigma_{L_{u}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L_{u}}

on the leaves of
T_{u}
, define the magnetization at the root
u
of
T_{u}
under
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
as

(3)

\displaystyle Z^{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}},T_{u}}_{u}(\sigma_{L_{u}}):=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\hat{\sigma}_{u}=+1\,|\,\hat{\sigma}_{L_{u}}=\sigma_{L_{u}})-\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\hat{\sigma}_{u}=-1\,|\,\hat{\sigma}_{L_{u}}=\sigma_{L_{u}}),

where
\hat{\sigma}
is a random spin configuration on
T
sampled from
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
.
Furthermore, if
\sigma
is a random spin configuration sampled from
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
, we consider the random variable

\displaystyle Z_{u}^{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}},T_{u}}:=Z_{u}^{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}},T_{u}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}).

We write this random variable simply as
Z_{u}
when
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}},T_{u}
are clear from the context.

Recall that our overarching goal is to infer the unobserved spin
\sigma_{u}
under
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
given the observed leaf spins
\sigma_{L_{u}}
on a descendant subtree
T_{u}
of a known tree
T
. The estimator we seek to analyze is simply the magnetization
Z_{u}^{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}},T_{u}}

using the estimated parameter
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
. We think of the latter as being deterministic and obtained externally—in particular, they do not depend on the observations. Fix
u
,
T_{u}
, and
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
. Roughly, we wish to establish conditions under which the following random approximation holds

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}\approx Z_{u}.

Since
\sigma_{u}\in\{\pm 1\}
and
Z_{u}\in[-1,1]
, the accuracy of the inference above can be quantified by the following quantity

(5)

\displaystyle\sigma_{u}Z_{u}\in[-1,1].

which we refer to as “unsigned magnetization.” If this is close to 1, then the estimation is accurate; if it is close to
-1
, then it gives almost the opposite answer.

Magnetizations are straightforward to compute efficiently even for a large tree by using a recursive formula (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
). The idea is to compute the magnetizations from the leaves of
T_{u}
and work towards
u
.

(1)

(
Base step
) For each leaf
\ell
in
T_{u}
, set
Z_{\ell}=\sigma_{\ell}
(since we get to observe the spin at
\ell
).

(2)

(
Induction step
) Suppose
T_{v}
is the descendant subtree of node
v
with respect
u
and let
w_{1},w_{2}
be its two children in
T_{v}
. There are corresponding descendant subtrees rooted at these nodes with respect to
u
, which defines the magnetization at these nodes,
Z_{w_{1}}
and
Z_{w_{2}}
. Then
[
3
, Lemma 4 and 5]
imply that

(6)

Z_{v}=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{w_{1}}Z_{w_{1}}+\hat{\theta}_{w_{2}}Z_{w_{2}}}{1+\hat{\theta}_{w_{1}}\hat{\theta}_{w_{2}}Z_{w_{1}}Z_{w_{2}}},

where
\hat{\theta}_{w_{1}}:=\hat{\theta}_{\{v,w_{1}\}}
for the edge
\{v,w_{1}\}
and similarly for
w_{2}
.

Now we state our main result in this work, Theorem
2.3
. Roughly speaking, it states that for every node
u
in
T

\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
, its spin
\sigma_{u}
is close to its magnetization

computed with respect to any descendant subtree
T_{u}
under estimated parameters satisfying certain conditions. More precisely,

is “close” to the spin

at

irrespective of the parameters used as long as they are in the “right range,” and it will be off “moderately” with probability

and “severely” with probability

. In the statement, recall that
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
indicates that the random spin configuration
\sigma_{L_{u}}
on the leaves in
L_{u}
is generated by the model with parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
.

2.3.
Application: the gradient of the population log-likelihood on a known phylogeny

To demonstrate the utility of Theorem
2.3
, we consider the branch length estimation problem using the maximum likelihood framework.
Fix an unrooted binary tree
T=(V,E)
—the known phylogeny.

The log-likelihood of
m
samples of the leaf observations
\sigma^{(1)},\dots,\sigma^{(m)}
under the model with parameter
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
over
T
is given by

(10)

\displaystyle\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}):=\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma^{(1)},\dotsm,\sigma^{(m)}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\hat{\sigma}_{v}=\sigma^{(j)}_{v},\,\,\forall v\in L),

where
\hat{\sigma}
is a random spin configuration on
T
sampled from
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
.
We then seek to find the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
as

(11)

\displaystyle{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{\textup{MLE}}\in\argmax_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\in[-1,1]^{E}}\,\,\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma^{(1)},\dotsm,\sigma^{(m)}),

where now the leaf observations
\sigma^{(1)},\dots,\sigma^{(m)}
are i.i.d. samples from
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
(on the known phylogeny
T
).

While the log-likelihood function
\ell
in (
10
) is in general non-concave, it has the nice property of
having at most one stationary point
when restricted to a single edge
e\in E
(unless it is constant)
[
11
,
7
]
. Thus, it is natural to cycle through the branch lengths and optimize one of them at a time
[
12
]
. This yields the following “cyclic coordinate maximization” algorithm for computing the MLE branch lengths
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
: given the current estimate
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{k}=(\hat{\theta}_{k;e};e\in E)
after
k
iterations, compute the new estimate
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{k+1}
by optimizing for one edge parameter
\theta_{k;e}
at a time by solving

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{k+1;e}=\argmax_{\hat{\theta}\in[-1,1]}\left[\overline{f}_{k;e}(\hat{\theta}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(\hat{\theta}_{k+1;1},\dots,\hat{\theta}_{k+1;e-1},\hat{\theta},\hat{\theta}_{k;e+1},\dots,\hat{\theta}_{k;|E|};\sigma^{(i)}),\right]

assuming that we label the edge set
E
as integers from 1 through
|E|
. This can be done by solving for

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\overline{f}_{k;e}(\hat{\theta})=0,

if such a point exists
(using, e.g.,
[
4
]
).

To analyze such an algorithm, one needs to consider the partial derivatives of the log-likelihood function in (
10
).

Lemma 2.5

(Likelihood and magnetization)
.

Let
\sigma
be a single sample from the true model
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
over
T
and let

be an edge in the tree
T

(resp.

) be the magnetization at the root

(resp.

) of the descendant subtree

(resp.
T_{y}
) with respect to

(resp.

Then we have

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{x}Z_{y}}{1+Z_{x}Z_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e}}.

Therefore, the function on the left-hand side of the fixed point equation (

,

(15)

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\left[\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma)\right]-\frac{\theta^{*}_{e}-\hat{\theta}_{e}}{1-\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}}\right|\leq C_{\ref{eqn:gradient}}\delta.

The proof of the result above is provided in Section
4
, where our main ancestral reconstruction result (Thm.
2.3
) is used crucially. We give a brief sketch of the main idea.
By Theorem
2.3
, we have
Z_{x}Z_{y}\approx\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}
with probability close to
1
, so essentially we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\left[\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma)\right]

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}\left[\frac{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}{1+\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\sigma_{x}=\sigma_{y})-\frac{1}{1-\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\sigma_{x}\neq\sigma_{y})

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1+\theta^{*}_{e}}{1+\hat{\theta}_{e}}-\frac{1-\theta^{*}_{e}}{1-\hat{\theta}_{e}}\right)

(17)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta^{*}_{e}-\hat{\theta}_{e}}{1-\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}},

where
\sigma
is a single sample from the true model
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
over
T
.
The main difficulty is to quantify the error of the approximation in the second step in the above display. The issue is that the (random) gradient of the log-likelihood
\frac{Z_{x}Z_{y}}{1+Z_{x}Z_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e}}
in (
14
) can blow up (i.e., is of order
\delta^{-1}
) with some small probability
when
Z_{x}Z_{y}\approx-1
. In order to control the expectation, we need to have tight control of the probabilities with which the denominator gets close to zero. To spell out further details, we note that the blow-up can happen in the following two ways:
(1)
\sigma_{x}Z_{x},\sigma_{y}Z_{y}\approx 1
but
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=-1
; or
(2)
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=1
, but either
\sigma_{x}Z_{x}\approx-1
,
\sigma_{y}Z_{y}\approx 1
or
\sigma_{x}Z_{x}\approx 1,\sigma_{y}Z_{y}\approx-1
.
The former is already accounted for in the above heuristic. The latter is rare; it has probability
O(\delta^{2})
by the reconstruction theorem. Therefore the expected contribution from the event
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=1
but
Z_{x}Z_{y}\approx-1
ends up being
O(\delta)
.

Theorem
2.6
gives some insights into why coordinate maximization may work for branch length estimation. For large enough sample size
m
, the partial derivative of the empirical log-likelihood with respect to each branch length parameter
\hat{\theta}_{e}
should be close to its population expectation, which almost vanishes uniquely at the true branch length
\theta_{e}^{*}
. More precisely, Theorem
2.6
shows that the derivative in the population limit will vanish at a branch length
\hat{\theta}_{e}
for which
\frac{\theta^{*}_{e}-\hat{\theta}_{e}}{1-\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}}
is of order
O(\delta)
. Note that the denominator is of order
O(\delta)
, so this requires the numerator to be at most of order
O(\delta^{2})
. Thus, solving the fixed point equation (
13
) in the population limit
only once
yields a
O(\delta^{2})
-accurate branch length estimate. This observation is stated in Corollary
2.7
below.

Corollary 2.7

(Initialization by coordinate maximization)
.

Suppose the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
and the initial estimate
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}
satisfies
A1
. Let
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{1}
be obtained by a single round of coordinate maximization. Then there exists a constant
C_{\ref{eqn:coord_ini_guarantee}}>0
such that, in the population limit
m\rightarrow\infty
,

(18)

\displaystyle\lVert{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}-{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{1}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C_{\ref{eqn:coord_ini_guarantee}}\delta^{2}.

While Corollary
2.7
shows why coordinate maximization in the empirical likelihood landscape may succeed in obtaining an accurate estimation of the true parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
, the result itself does not hold much of a practical significance because it only holds in the population limit.
In a companion paper, we establish that coordinate maximization is indeed guaranteed to return an accurate estimation of the true parameter with high probability in the finite sample regime, where the required sample size grows polynomially in the size of the tree (when sufficiently “balanced”). This requires a substantial analysis of the Hessian of the log-likelihood function—an analysis where our main result (Thm.
2.3
) plays a key role again.

3.1.
Conditional independence of magnetizations

We will need an important property of the magnetization at distinct nodes: they are conditionally independent given the spins at intermediate nodes. More precisely, suppose we have two node-disjoint descendant subtrees
T_{u}
and
T_{v}
. Then for any node
w
along the shortest path between
u
and
v
,

(20)

\displaystyle Z_{u}\Perp Z_{v}\,|\,\sigma_{w},

which follows from the Markov property of the CFN model and the fact that
Z_{u}
is determined by
\sigma_{L_{u}}
. In fact, the “unsigned magnetizations”
\sigma_{u}Z_{u}
are independent as long as the supporting descendant subtrees are node-disjoint, as stated in Claim
3.1
below. We state this in a slightly more general formulation than what we need.

Claim 3.1

(Independence of unsigned magnetizations)
.

The following hold:

(i)

Let
u
be a node in
T
with a descendant subtree
T_{u}
and corresponding magnetization
Z_{u}
. Then the unsigned magnetization
\sigma_{u}Z_{u}
is independent of
\sigma_{u}
under
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
.

(ii)

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
be nodes in
T
and suppose there are corresponding descendant subtrees
T_{v_{1}},\dots,T_{v_{k}}
that are node-disjoint. Let
Z_{v_{i}}
denote the corresponding magnetization at
v_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
.
Then the
\sigma_{v_{i}}Z_{v_{i}}
s,
i=1,\dots,k
, are independent under
\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}
.

Proof.

We will first show
(i)
. Denote
\zeta_{u}:=\sigma_{u}Z_{u}
.
It is enough to show that

(21)

\displaystyle\zeta_{u}\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=1\}\,\,\overset{d}{=}\,\,\zeta_{u}\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=-1\}\,\,\overset{d}{=}\,\,\zeta_{u}.

To establish this, note that, for any node set
V^{(i)}\subseteq V(T_{u})
, by symmetry of the model

(22)

\displaystyle\sigma_{V^{(i)}}\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=1\}\,\,\overset{d}{=}\,\,-\sigma_{V^{(i)}}\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=-1\}.

By applying (
22
) with
V^{(i)}=L_{u}
(the set of leaves in the descendant subtree
T_{u}
), we get

(23)

\displaystyle Z_{u}(\sigma_{L_{u}})\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=1\}\,\,\overset{d}{=}\,\,Z_{u}(-\sigma_{L_{u}})\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=-1\}=-Z_{u}(\sigma_{L_{u}})\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=-1\},

where the last equality follows from the definition of the magnetization. Recalling that
\zeta_{u}=\sigma_{u}Z_{u}(\sigma_{L_{u}})
, we deduce

(24)

\displaystyle\zeta_{u}\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=1\}\,\,\overset{d}{=}\,\,\zeta_{u}\,|\,\{\sigma_{u}=-1\}.

Then, for any measurable subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, using (
24
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{u}\in A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{u}\in A\,|\,\sigma_{u}=s)\,\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\sigma_{u}=s)=\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{u}\in A\,|\,\sigma_{u}=+1).

It follows that
\zeta_{u}
is independent of
\sigma_{u}
, which establishes (
21
). This shows
(i)
.

Next, we show
(ii)
. Denote
\zeta_{i}:=\sigma_{v_{i}}Z_{v_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,k
. Denote
R:=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
.
Note that by the conditional independence property of the spin configuration
\sigma
and magnetizations (see (
20
)), it follows that the unsigned magnetizations
\zeta_{i}
are conditionally independent given
\sigma_{R}
. Let
A_{1},\dots,A_{k}
be measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Then we can conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{1}\in A_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}\in A_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau_{R}:R\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{1}\in A_{1},\dots,\zeta_{k}\in A_{k}\,|\,\sigma_{R}=\tau_{R})\,\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\sigma_{L}=\tau_{L})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau_{R}:R\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{i}\in A_{i}\,|\,\sigma_{v_{i}}=\tau_{v_{i}})\,\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\sigma_{R}=\tau_{R})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{i}\in A_{i})\sum_{\tau_{R}:R\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\sigma_{R}=\tau_{R})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}}(\zeta_{i}\in A_{i}),

where the second equality is due to conditional independence and the following equality is from (
21
).
∎

3.2.
Bounds on
q

We first establish a few useful bounds on the function
q
.
The next claim bounds the output of
q
when both incoming inputs are “strong,” i.e., both are either close to
1
or
-1
, and agree.

Claim 3.2

(Magnetization: two strong agreeing inputs)
.

Fix any
\varepsilon\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
. If
s,t
are such that
1-\varepsilon\leq s,t\leq 1
then

q(s,t)\geq 1-\frac{4}{5}\varepsilon^{2}\qquad\textup{ and }\qquad q(-s,-t)\leq-1+\frac{4}{5}\varepsilon^{2}.

Proof.

Since
st\geq\frac{1}{4}
,

\displaystyle 1-q(s,t)=\frac{1+st-s-t}{1+st}=\frac{(1-s)(1-t)}{1+st}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+st}\leq\frac{4}{5}\varepsilon^{2}.

Note that
q(-s,-t)=-q(s,t)
gives the other claim.
∎

We will also need the following observation regarding the function
q
in the “opposite case,” i.e., when both inputs are “strong” but
in the opposite directions
.

Claim 3.3

(Magnetization: two strong disagreeing inputs)
.

Fix any
0<a<A
and let
\delta>0
with
A\delta<1
. For any
s,t\in[1-A\delta,1-a\delta]
then

-1+\frac{a}{A}\leq q(s,-t)=q(-s,t)\leq 1-\frac{a}{A}.

More generally, if
t\in[1-A\delta,1-a\delta]
and
|s|\leq 1-a\delta
then

q(s,t)\geq-1+\frac{a}{A}.

Proof.

By using the monotonicity of
q(\cdot,\cdot)
in both arguments,

\displaystyle q(s,-t)\geq q(1-A\delta,-1+a\delta)=\frac{(a-A)\delta}{(A+a)\delta-aA\delta^{2}}=-\frac{A-a}{A+a-aA\delta}\geq-\frac{A-a}{A}.

In the last inequality we use
A+a-aA\delta\geq A+a-a
since
A\delta<1
.
For the opposite bound, it is simply

q(s,-t)\leq q(1-a\delta,-1+A\delta)=-q(1-A\delta,-1+a\delta).

∎

3.3.
Effect of recursion

Our proof of Theorem
2.3

will be based on a case analysis for a three-level subtree rooted at the target vertex.
The next claim, which will be central to the analysis, bounds the effect
of a “corruption” (i.e., a flip or failure of reconstruction) followed by three recursions of

(with “strong signals”). Below,

corresponds to a possible corruption.

Claim 3.4

(Magnetization recursion: corruption at distance
3
)
.

Suppose that
0<a<A
,
0<B
and
\delta\in[0,1]
are such that
a<A/2
,
\delta<a/2
, and
\left(\frac{2A^{2}}{a}+B\right)\delta<1/2

If

and,
for each

and
i\in\{1,2\}

and

(25)

\displaystyle t_{2}q(t_{1}q(s_{1},s_{2}),s_{3})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\left(\frac{2A^{2}}{a}+B\right)\delta,

(26)

\displaystyle q\Bigg(t_{2}q\Big(t_{1}q(s_{1},s_{2}),s_{3}\Big),s_{4}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{4}{5}\left(\frac{2A^{2}}{a}+B\right)\delta^{2}.

Proof.

We bound the composition of functions from the inside one term at a time.

(1)

First, as
0<a\delta<A\delta<1
,
s_{1}\in[-1+a\delta,1]
and
s_{2}\in[1-A\delta,1]
,

q(s_{1},s_{2})\geq q(-1+a\delta,1-A\delta)=\frac{a-A}{a+A-aA\delta},

by the monotonicity of
q(\cdot,\cdot)
in both arguments.
Note that the rightmost expression is always negative (and
>-1
) by our assumptions. Since
0<t_{1}\leq 1
, it follows that

t_{1}\,q(s_{1},s_{2})\geq\frac{a-A}{a+A-aA\delta}.

(2)

By monotonicity again, we get using the previous bullet that

\displaystyle q(t_{1}q(s_{1},s_{2}),s_{3})

\displaystyle\geq q\left(\frac{a-A}{a+A-aA\delta},1-A\delta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{a-A}{a+A-aA\delta}+1-A\delta}{1+\frac{a-A}{a+A-aA\delta}(1-A\delta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2a+\left(-2a-A\right)A\delta+aA^{2}\delta^{2}}{2a+\left(-2a+A\right)A\delta}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2A^{2}\delta+aA^{2}\delta^{2}}{2a+\left(-2a+A\right)A\delta}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\geq}1-\frac{2A^{2}\delta+aA^{2}\delta^{2}}{2a}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{A^{2}}{a}\delta-\frac{A^{2}\delta^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\geq}1-\frac{2A^{2}}{a}\delta,

where (a) uses the hypothesis
a<A/2
and (b) uses
\delta<a/2
. Since
t_{2}\geq 1-B\delta
,
from the lower bound on
q(t_{1}q(s_{1},s_{2}),s_{3})
above, equation (
25
) follows.

(3)

Finally, using the last display and
s_{4}\geq 1-A\delta\geq 1-\left(\frac{2A^{2}}{a}+B\right)\delta
(since
0<a<A
and
B>0
), Claim
3.2
with
\varepsilon=\left(\frac{2A^{2}}{a}+B\right)\delta<1/2
(by the hypothesis) yields (
26
).

That concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

Let
T
be a binary tree and
{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=(\hat{\theta}_{e};e\in E(T))
denote a collection of edge parameters. The law of a random state vector
\sigma=(\sigma_{u};u\in T)
on
all
vertices of
T
satisfies that for each
\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{V(T)}
,

\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma=\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\prod_{e=\{u,v\}\in E(T)}\frac{1+\tau_{u}\tau_{v}\hat{\theta}_{e}}{2}.

Marginalizing over the leaves
and breaking things up according to the spins at nodes
u
and
v
, for a spin configuration
\tau\in\{\pm 1\}^{V(T)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(X_{L}=\tau_{L})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}},\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\tau^{\prime}_{u},\tau^{\prime}_{v}\in\{-,+\}}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}},\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}}|\sigma_{u}=\tau^{\prime}_{u},\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{u}=\tau^{\prime}_{u},\sigma_{v}=\tau^{\prime}_{v})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}_{u},\tau^{\prime}_{v}\in\{-,+\}}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}},\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}}|\sigma_{u}=\tau^{\prime}_{u},\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime})\frac{1+\tau_{u}^{\prime}\tau_{v}^{\prime}\hat{\theta}_{e}}{2}.

By the Markov property and using
Bayes’ rule to bring out the magnetization,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L}=\tau_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}_{u},\tau^{\prime}_{v}\in\{-,+\}}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}}|\sigma_{u}=\tau^{\prime}_{u})\,\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}}|\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime})\frac{1+\tau_{u}^{\prime}\tau_{v}^{\prime}\hat{\theta}_{e}}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\tau^{\prime}_{u},\tau^{\prime}_{v}\in\{-,+\}}\bigg\{\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{u}=\tau_{u}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})}{\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{u}=\tau_{u}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})}{\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime})}\frac{1+\tau_{u}^{\prime}\tau_{v}^{\prime}\hat{\theta}_{e}}{2}\bigg\}.

Since
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{x}=\tau_{x})=\frac{1}{2}
for all
x\in T
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}

\displaystyle(\sigma_{L}=\tau_{L})=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\,\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\sum_{\tau_{u}^{\prime},\tau_{v}^{\prime}\in\{-,+\}}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{u}=\tau_{u}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})(1+\tau_{u}^{\prime}\tau_{v}^{\prime}\hat{\theta}_{e})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})\Bigg(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}\sum_{\tau_{u}^{\prime},\tau_{v}^{\prime}\in\{-,+\}}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{u}=\tau_{u}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})\tau_{u}^{\prime}\tau_{v}^{\prime}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{u}Z_{v}).

In the last two lines we used the fact that
\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{u}=\tau_{u}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{v}=\tau_{v}^{\prime}|\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})
is a product probability measure on
\{(\tau_{u}^{\prime},\tau_{v}^{\prime}):\tau_{u}^{\prime},\tau_{v}^{\prime}\in\{-,+\}\}
and, under this measure,
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{u}^{\prime}]=Z_{u}
and
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{v}^{\prime}]=Z_{v}
.

It follows that

\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\tau_{L})=\log\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\sigma_{L}=\tau_{L})=\log(1+Z_{u}Z_{v}\hat{\theta}_{e})+\log\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})+\log\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}}).

Observing that neither
\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}=\tau_{L_{u}})

nor

\mathbb{P}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{L_{v}}=\tau_{L_{v}})
depend on

and hence have vanishing derivatives,
one finally obtains (

∎

Proof of Corollary

.

By Theorem
2.6
, there is some small signed error
\varepsilon\in[-C_{\ref{eqn:gradient}}\delta,C_{\ref{eqn:gradient}}\delta]
such that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma)\right]=\frac{\theta_{e}-\hat{\theta}_{e}}{(1-\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2})}+\varepsilon.

Hence solving
\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left[\ell({\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}};\sigma)\right]=0
amounts to solving the following equation in
\hat{\theta}_{e}

\varepsilon\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}+\hat{\theta}_{e}-(\theta_{e}+\varepsilon)=0.

The relevant root is

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{e}^{*}=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon(\theta_{e}+\varepsilon)}}{2\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(-1+1+\frac{4\varepsilon(\theta_{e}+\varepsilon)}{2}-\frac{16\varepsilon^{2}(\theta_{e}+\varepsilon)^{2}}{8}+\eta\right),

where
|\eta|\leq\frac{1}{16}|4\varepsilon(\theta_{e}+\varepsilon)|^{3}\leq 32\varepsilon^{3}
. The bound on
\eta
follows from a Taylor expansion and the fact that
\theta+\varepsilon\leq 2
. Recalling that
\theta_{e}=1-2p_{e}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{e}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(2\varepsilon(\theta_{e}+\varepsilon)-2\varepsilon^{2}(1-2p_{e}+\varepsilon)^{2}\right)+\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\theta_{e}+4p_{e}\varepsilon+4p_{e}\varepsilon^{2}-2\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{3}+4\varepsilon p_{e}^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}.

Therefore, using the bounds
p_{e}\leq C_{\ref{eqn:pBounds}}\delta
,
|\varepsilon|\leq C_{\ref{eqn:gradient}}\delta
, and
|\eta|\leq 32\varepsilon^{3}
, we deduce

\displaystyle|\hat{\theta}_{e}^{*}-\theta_{e}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|4p_{e}\varepsilon+(4p_{e}+2)\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{3}+4\varepsilon p_{e}^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\varepsilon}\right|\leq C_{\ref{eqn:coord_ini_guarantee}}\delta^{2}
