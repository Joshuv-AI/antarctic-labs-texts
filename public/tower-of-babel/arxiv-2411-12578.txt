Robust Inference for High-dimensional Linear Models with Heavy-tailed Errors via Partial Gini Covariance
Yilin Zhang

Abstract
This paper introduces the partial Gini covariance, a novel dependence measure that addresses the challenges of high-dimensional inference with heavy-tailed errors, often encountered in fields like finance, insurance, climate, and biology. Conventional high-dimensional regression inference methods suffer from inaccurate type I errors and reduced power in heavy-tailed contexts, limiting their effectiveness. Our proposed approach leverages the partial Gini covariance to construct a robust statistical inference framework that requires minimal tuning and does not impose restrictive moment conditions on error distributions. Unlike traditional methods, it circumvents the need for estimating the density of random errors, and enhances the computational feasibility and robustness. Extensive simulations demonstrate the proposed method’s superior power and robustness over standard high-dimensional inference approaches, such as those based on the debiased Lasso. The asymptotic relative efficiency analysis provides additional theoretical insight on the improved efficiency of the new approach in the heavy-tailed setting. Additionally, the partial Gini covariance extends to the multivariate setting, enabling chi-square testing for a group of coefficients. We illustrate the method’s practical application with a real-world data example.



Keywords:
Decorrelated score; Heavy-tailed data; High-dimensional data; Partial Gini covariance; Robust statistical inference.

1
Introduction

Modern real-world big data, prevalent in fields such as finance, insurance, climate, and biology, often exhibits
heavy-tailed behavior
(
Peinke et al., 2004
;
Zubair Ahmad & Kharazmi, 2020
;
Gupta & Chavan, 2022
)
,
characterized by the presence of outliers or extreme values.
When faced with heavy-tailed errors, conventional high-dimensional regression methods frequently yield biased estimates, imprecise inference, and untrustworthy risk assessment. For example,
Cavanaugh et al. (2015)
observed the heavy-tailed phenomenon while analyzing the daily precipitation data from more than 22,000 global stations.
Neglecting the contamination of heavy-tailed random errors in such data analysis could result in an underestimation of the likelihood
and severity of the events of interest, thereby significantly impacting disaster preparedness and response strategies.
While extensive research efforts have been dedicated
to statistical inference with heavy-tailed random errors for low-dimensional data

(
Kotchoni, 2012
;
Davidson, 2012
;
Cornea-Madeira & Davidson, 2015
)
,
studies on high-dimensional inference with heavy-tailed errors remain limited. This is a crucial gap in the literature, as many real-world applications, such as those in finance, insurance, climate, and biology, involve high-dimensional datasets with heavy-tailed behavior.

In this paper, we introduce the
partial Gini covariance
, a novel dependence measure
that serves as a foundation for robust statistical inference in high dimensions.
Standard high-dimensional inference procedures
based on the debiased Lasso
(
van de Geer et al., 2014
;
Javanmard & Montanari, 2014
;
Zhang & Zhang, 2014
)
are observed to suffer from inflated type I errors and substantial power loss with heavy-tailed random errors,
as demonstrated in the Monte Carlo studies in Section
5
.
Our new inference procedure addresses three
key challenges. First, it effectively handles heavy-tailed random errors, including Cauchy distribution errors, which are often overlooked in the existing
literature on high-dimensional robust regression.
Second, it is straightforward to implement with minimal tuning requirements, avoiding the complexities involved in selecting the regularization parameters in high-dimensional regression with heavy-tailed errors.
Lastly, it enables valid inference on low-dimensional target regression coefficients
without estimating the random errors’ density function.

Recent advances in robust high-dimensional estimation have primarily focused on Huber regression

(
Loh, 2021
;
Wang et al., 2021
;
Sun et al., 2020
)
. While these approaches relax the sub-Gaussian error assumption, they still require certain moment conditions.
For instance,
Sun et al. (2020)
assumes the existence of the
(1+\delta)
-th moment of the random error. The robust inference procedure developed in
Han et al. (2022)

requires the existence of the first moment of the random error. These moment conditions exclude many heavy-tailed distributions,
such as Cauchy or Laplace distributions, that are fundamental to traditional robust statistics.
Moreover, high-dimensional Huber-type regression requires tuning two parameters—one for regularization and one for robustification—making it computationally demanding.

Consider the high-dimensional linear
regression model

Y={\bf x}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}+\varepsilon,

(1)

where
{\bf x}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})^{\top}
is the
p
-dimensional vector of covariates,

{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}=(\beta_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\ast})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the
p
-dimensional coefficient,
\varepsilon
denotes the random error independent of
{\bf x}
.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\mbox{E}({\bf x})={\bf 0}_{p}
, where
{\bf 0}_{p}
denotes the
p
-dimensional zero vector and the intercept is absorbed into
\varepsilon
.
The number of covariates
p
may substantially exceed the sample size
n
,
and notably we do not impose any moment condition on
\varepsilon
.
For an arbitrary
1\leq k\leq p
,
we aim to test the significance of target parameter
\beta_{k}^{\ast}
in the presence of the high-dimensional nuisance parameter
{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}_{-k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\beta_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\beta_{k-1}^{\ast},\beta_{k+1}^{\ast},\ldots,\beta_{p}^{\ast})^{\top}
, and extend this
to simultaneous testing the significance of a group of coefficients.
Let
\{({\bf x}_{i},\varepsilon_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be independent copies of
({\bf x},\varepsilon)
.
The observed data consist of
\{(Y_{i},{\bf x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
Y_{i}={\bf x}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}+\varepsilon_{i}
.

The Gini covariance is a useful measure of the dependence between random variables from heavy-tailed distributions, and it is widely applied in areas such as economics (e.g., studying the relationship between income and expenditure) and social sciences
(
Yitzhaki & Schechtman, 2013
)
.
In this paper, we introduce the concept of partial Gini covariance, partly inspired by the Rank Lasso procedure
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
which incorporates the Gini covariance
(
Schezhtman & Yitzhaki, 1987
)
into its loss function for robust estimation.
We show that the partial Gini covariance, introduced in Section 2, acts as a robust decorrelated score function satisfying Neyman’s orthogonality condition, and serves as the foundation for robust statistical inference in high-dimensional regression.
We propose an estimator for the partial Gini covariance in the high-dimensional setting, and based on the estimation, we construct a hypothesis testing procedure and investigate the limiting distributions of the proposed test statistic under both the null hypothesis and the local alternatives.

Our approach differs from inference methods based on Huber regression in that it does not require moment conditions on random errors and it
circumvents the complexity of simultaneously
tuning two regularization parameters.
Unlike the debiased Rank Lasso method, which is informally discussed in
Fan, Ma & Wang (2020)
for the low-dimensional scenario, we address the more challenging high-dimensional inference problem. Our proposed statistic converges to a normal distribution, and its implementation does not need to estimate the density of the random error.
Furthermore, by exploring the double summation expression of the partial Gini covariance, we have considerably relaxed the constraints on the sample size
n
and the dimension
p
. Existing quantile-based debiased methods implicitly require
p=o\{\exp(n^{1/5})\}
when the random errors follow a sub-Gaussian distribution
(
Belloni et al., 2014
;
Zhao et al., 2014
;
Bradic & Kolar, 2017
)
, whereas our method only requires
p=o\{\exp(n^{1/3})\}
, despite the discontinuous nature of the sample loss function.
Our requirement is also less stringent than that for the debiased Huber regression, which requires
p=o\{\exp(n^{\alpha/3})\}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)

(
Han et al., 2022
)
.
In addition, we provide the asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) between the proposed method and the existing high-dimensional inference methods including
Zhang & Zhang (2014)
;
Ning & Liu (2017)
;
Ma et al. (2017)
.
Our ARE analysis provides additional theoretical insight on the improved efficiency of our approach in the heavy-tailed setting.
As illustrated in Figure
1
,
the efficiency improvement can be substantial, with power performance exceeding four times those of the existing methods when the random error follows a Cauchy distribution.

Additionally, we extend the concept of partial Gini covariance to the multivariate case and establish an asymptotic chi-square test to test the significance of a multivariate coefficient vector. We conduct extensive simulation studies and compare our method with three prominent existing methods, including
the debiased Lasso
(
van de Geer et al., 2014
;
Javanmard & Montanari, 2014
;
Zhang & Zhang, 2014
)
,
the partial Pearson covariance-based method
(
Ning & Liu, 2017
)
and partial quantile covariance-based methods
(
Li et al., 2015
;
Ma et al., 2017
)
. Our results demonstrate that our approach exhibits superior robustness and efficiency in handling high-dimensional data with heavy-tailed random errors.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the concept of partial Gini covariance and establish the corresponding estimation method. In Section
3
, we propose a statistical inference procedure for high-dimensional linear regression and establish asymptotic properties of the test statistic under both the null and alternative hypotheses. Furthermore, we extend the notion of partial Gini covariance to the multivariate scenario and construct a chi-square test.
In Section
4
, we compare our methods with standard
approaches for high-dimensional inference and analyze the asymptotic relative efficiency.
Section
5
provides comprehensive simulation studies
for testing the significance of both univariate and multivariate coefficients.
We illustrate the application of the new methods using a real-world data example in Section
6
. Technical proofs are deferred to the Supplementary Material.

2
Partial Gini Covariance

To construct a test statistic that effectively handles heavy-tailed errors in the high-dimensional setting, we introduce a novel measure called the partial Gini covariance in Section
2.1
. We then discuss how to estimate the partial Gini covariance in high dimensional settings in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Partial Gini Covariance and Neyman Orthogonality

To introduce the notion of partial Gini covariance, we first recall that
the Gini covariance between two random variables
Y
and
X
is defined as

\displaystyle{\rm{Gcov}}(Y,X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mbox{cov}\left\{F_{Y}(Y),X\right\},

where
F_{Y}(y)
is the cumulative distribution function of
Y
,
and
\mbox{cov}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the covariance function.
It is the basic building block for
Gini regression
(
Olkin & Yitzhaki, 1992
)
, the loss function of which can be written as

\displaystyle{\rm{Gcov}}(Y-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x},Y-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x})=\frac{1}{4}\mbox{E}\big\{|(Y_{i}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x}_{i})-(Y_{j}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x}_{j})|\big\},

where
1\leq i,j\leq n
;
Y_{i}
and
Y_{j}
are independent copies of
Y
;
{\bf x}_{i}
and
{\bf x}_{j}
are independent copies of
{\bf x}
(e.g.,
Lerman & Yitzhaki (1984)
, Section 2.1.3 of
Yitzhaki & Schechtman (2013)
). Gini regression is
known to be equivalent to the popular rank regression
loss function with the Wilcoxon score function

(
Jaeckel, 1972
;
Hettmansperger & McKean, 1998
)
,
which enjoys superior robustness and
efficiency properties.

For
1\leq k\leq p
, we denote
{\bf z}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}{\bf x}_{-k}
, where
{\bf x}_{-k}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1},X_{k+1},\ldots,X_{p})^{\top}
, for notational simplicity.
To measure the dependence between
Y
and
X_{k}
adjusting for the confounder
{\bf z}_{k}
in the heavy-tailed case, we introduce an intuitive new concept,
partial Gini covariance
.
We first regress
X_{k}
on
{\bf z}_{k}
and denote
{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\mbox{E}\left\{(X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})^{2}\right\}
.
Then, we regress
Y
on
{\bf z}_{k}
using Gini regression and denote
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mbox{E}\left\{(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}(Y\right.

\left.-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})\right\}
, where
F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}(\cdot)
represents the cumulative distribution function of the random variable
Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}
.
The partial Gini covariance between
Y
and
X_{k}
given
{\bf z}_{k}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=\mbox{cov}\left\{F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}),X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}\right\}.

(2)

The partial Gini covariance thus is the Gini covariance between
Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}
and
X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}
.
We observe that
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
satisfies an
important moment condition when
E({\bf z}_{k})={\bf 0}_{p-1}
and
\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
follows continuous distribution:

\displaystyle\mbox{E}\left\{{\bf z}_{k}F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})\right\}={\bf 0}_{p-1}.

(3)

The equation holds since the derivative of
\mbox{E}\left\{(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})\right\}
with respect to
{\bm{\theta}}
at
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
equals zero, which is proved in the Supplementary Materials.
As a result, the Gini covariance between
Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}
and covariate
{\bf z}_{k}
equals zero.

It is clear that
Y
has a bounded influence
for the partial Gini covariance as its effect is through
F(\cdot)
, which is bounded.
Meanwhile, Lemma
1
below
shows the partial Gini covariance in (
2
) enjoys a property called Neyman orthogonality, which
ensures
small errors in estimating the nuisance parameters do not significantly affect the estimation of the parameter of interest

(
Neyman, 1959
;
Neyman, 1979
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2015
)
.
The bounded influence and the Neyman orthogonality together provide the foundation for robust inference in high dimensions.

Define
M({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\gamma}})=\mbox{cov}\left\{F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}),X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}\right\}
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose Condition
(C1)
in Section
3.1
holds.
When
\beta_{k}^{\ast}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial M({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\gamma}})}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}}\Big\lvert_{({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\gamma}})=({\bm{\theta}}_{G,k},{\bm{\gamma}}_{k})}={\bf 0}_{p-1}~\textrm{ and }~\frac{\partial M({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\gamma}})}{\partial{\bm{\gamma}}}\Big\lvert_{({\bm{\theta}},{\bm{\gamma}})=({\bm{\theta}}_{G,k},{\bm{\gamma}}_{k})}={\bf 0}_{p-1}.

Lemma
1
indicates that the estimation of
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
is locally insensitive to small perturbations of the parameters
\bm{\theta}_{G,k}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}
.
As the result,
the estimate for the partial Gini covariance can still achieve root-
n
asymptotic normality even when the estimation bias
measured in the
L_{2}
norm of
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
and
{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}
is of order
O\{(n^{-1/2}\log p)^{1/2}\}
in high dimensions.

2.2

The Estimation of Partial Gini Covariance in High Dimensions

We are interested in the high-dimensional scenario where
p
is much larger than
n
(
p>\!\!>n
). To overcome the overfitting due to high dimensionality, we use regularized estimates with sparsity assumptions on both
\bm{\theta}_{G,k}
and
\bm{\gamma}_{k}
.

Estimation of
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
.

We adopt the regularized Gini regression (or equivalently rank regression) to estimate
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
in the high-dimensional setting. Specifically,
the estimate of
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
is defined
as

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(Y,{\bf z}_{k};{\bm{\theta}})
,
where

{\bm{\theta}}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{p-1})^{\top}
and

\mathcal{L}_{1}(Y,{\bf z}_{k};{\bm{\theta}})

is the regularized Gini loss function
given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}\left(Y,{\bf z}_{k};{\bm{\theta}}\right)=\{n(n-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}|(Y_{i}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k,i})-(Y_{j}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k,j})|+\lambda_{Y,G,k}\sum_{l=1}^{p-1}|\theta_{l}|.

(4)

The estimation error bound for
the high-dimensional regularized rank Lasso
was derived in
Wang et al. (2020)
.
Rank Lasso enjoys two particularly appealing properties.
First, it possesses desirable robustness
and efficiency in the presence of heavy-tailed error contamination.
Second, its implementation is free of tuning
parameter
in the sense that
\lambda_{Y,G,k}
could be simulated without knowledge of the random error distribution.
Specifically, let
{\bf s}_{n}=-2\{n(n-1)\}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf z}_{k,i}r_{i}
denote the subgradient, with
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\}
following the uniform distribution on the permutations of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
Wang et al. (2020)
showed that
for any given
0<\alpha_{0}<1
and
c>1
,
the choice of the tuning parameter

\lambda_{Y,G,k}=cF^{-1}_{\|{\bf s}_{n}\|_{\infty}}(1-\alpha_{0})
, where

F^{-1}_{\|{\bf s}_{n}\|_{\infty}}(1-\alpha_{0})
denotes
(1-\alpha_{0})
-quantile of the distribution of
\|{\bf s}_{n}\|_{\infty}
, ensures
a desirable estimation accuracy with high probability.
It is noted that the distribution of
{\bf s}_{n}
does not depend on the error distribution.

Estimation of
{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}
.

The estimate of
{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}
is defined as
\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}}\mathcal{L}_{2}\left(X_{k},{\bf z}_{k};{\bm{\gamma}}\right)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{2}\left(X_{k},{\bf z}_{k};{\bm{\gamma}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(2n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{k,i}-{\bm{\gamma}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k,i})^{2}+\lambda_{X,k}\sum_{l=1}^{p-1}|\gamma_{l}|.

with

\lambda_{X,k}
being the tuning parameter.

Estimation of the partial Gini covariance.
Denote
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}Y_{i}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k,i}
and let
R(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,i})
be the rank of
\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,i}
among
\{\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,i},i=1,\ldots,n\}
.
Our estimate for
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
is

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{k,i}-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k,i})\{R(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,i})/n-1/2\}

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(X_{k,i}-\widehat{{\bm{\gamma}}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k,i}\right)\left\{1\left(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,i}\geq\widehat{\varepsilon}_{k,j}\right)-1/2\right\}.

The penalized Gini regression method used to estimate
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}
and the indicator function in
\widehat{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
both contribute to the robustness of
the estimated partial Gini covariance.
Furthermore, the estimate is computationally efficient.

Denote
s_{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\|{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\|_{0}
and
s_{Y,G}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\|{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}\|_{0}
.
When the covariates follow the sub-Gaussian distribution,
\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\lambda_{Y,G,k}s_{Y,G}^{1/2})
and
\lambda_{Y,G,k}=O\{(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}\}
under the lower restricted eigenvalue condition and the random error condition
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
.
Under the lower restricted eigenvalue condition,
\|{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\|_{1}=O_{p}(\lambda_{X,k}s_{X})
, where
\lambda_{X,k}=O\{(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}\}

(
Fan, Li, Zhang & Zou, 2020
)
.
We relegate the lower restricted eigenvalue condition and the random error condition to the Supplementary Material.

3

The New Inference Procedures

3.1

Testing the Significance of a Target Covariate

We consider the target parameter of interest
\beta_{k}^{\ast}
, with
{\bf z}_{k}
representing the vector of high-dimensional confounders.
Our main objective is to test the following hypothesis:

H_{0,k}:\beta_{k}^{\ast}=0\quad\textrm{v.s}\quad H_{1,k}:\beta_{k}^{\ast}\neq 0.

(6)

We impose the following regularity conditions to facilitate our technical derivation.

(C1)

Let
F_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}(\cdot)
denote the distribution function of
\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
.
F_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}(\cdot)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
.

(C1
′
)

F_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
and
F_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}(\cdot)
are second order differentiable, with first and second order derivatives uniformly bounded by a constant
B_{\varepsilon}>0
.

(C2)

There exists some constant
c_{1}>0
such that
\mbox{E}\{\exp(t{\bf v}^{\top}{\bf x})\}\leq\exp(c_{1}t^{2}/2)
for each fixed
{\bf v}\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
where

\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
is the surface of
d
-dimensional unit ball.

(C3)

s_{X}n^{-1/2}\log p=o(1)
and
n^{-1/2}s_{Y,G}\log(p)\{\log(n\vee p)\}^{1/2}=o(1)
.

Condition
(C1)
and its strengthen version
Condition
(C1′)
allow the random error
\epsilon
to follow a heavy-tailed distribution such as the Cauchy
distribution, relaxing the standard normality or
sub-gaussianity assumption on
\epsilon
. Condition
(C2)
states that the covariates follow the sub-Gaussian distribution, which is common in high-dimensional inference literature
(
Javanmard & Montanari, 2014
;
Ning & Liu, 2017
)
.

The sparsity assumption in
Condition
(C3)
is typically imposed in the inference of high-dimensional quantile regression
(
Bradic & Kolar, 2017
)
or composite quantile regression
(
Zhao et al., 2014
)
for controlling the supremum of an empirical process.

Condition
(C3)
also provides relations among the sample size, the dimension of the data, and the sparsity levels of the regression coefficients. When
s_{X}
and
s_{G,Y}
are finite, this condition is equivalent to
\log(p)=o(n^{1/3})
. Compared with the conditions for the inference of high-dimensional quantile regression models
(
Zhao et al., 2014
;
Belloni et al., 2014
)
, which require
\log(p)=o(n^{1/5})
under the sub-Gaussian setting, the condition in our context is notably less restrictive. This relaxation is facilitated by the double summation form in (
5
), see the proof of Theorem
1
.

We first demonstrate that the testing problem in (
6
) is equivalent to the following hypothesis testing problem based on the partial Gini covariance:

\displaystyle H_{0,k}^{G}:{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=0\quad\textrm{v.s}\quad H_{1,k}^{G}:{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})\neq 0.

(7)

To see the above equivalence intuitively, it is known that
the population parameter

{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}
can be expressed as

\arg\min_{{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mbox{E}\{|(Y_{i}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x}_{i})-(Y_{j}-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x}_{j})|\}

(see, e.g.,
Hettmansperger & McKean (2010)
).
Hence, we also have

{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}=\arg\min_{{\bm{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\rm{Gcov}}(Y-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x},Y-{\bm{\beta}}^{\top}{\bf x})
.
If
\beta_{k}^{\ast}=0
, the derivative of
{\rm{Gcov}}(Y-\bm{\theta}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-bX_{k},Y-\bm{\theta}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-bX_{k})
with respect to
b
at
b=0
equals zero.
Following the similar derivation as that in Section S.1 of the Supplementary Material, we can show that

\displaystyle\mbox{E}\left\{\frac{\partial{\rm{Gcov}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-bX_{k},Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-bX_{k})}{\partial b}\Big\lvert_{({\bm{\theta}},b)=({\bm{\theta}}_{G,k},0)}\right\}=-4\cdot{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k}).

The equivalence of the two hypotheses thus follows by noting that
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=0
when
\beta_{k}^{\ast}=0
.
The the equivalence between (
6
) and (
7
)
is formally stated in Lemma
2
below.

Lemma 2
.

Under Condition
(C1)
,

\beta_{k}^{\ast}=0
if and only if
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=0
.

Let
f_{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}}(\cdot)
be the density function of
\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}
.
Define
\sigma_{G,k}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}12^{-1}\mbox{E}\left\{\left(X_{ki}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki}\right)^{2}\right\}
.
Theorem
1
below states the asymptotic normality of
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
under both the null and local alternative hypotheses.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Conditions

(C1′)
,
(C2)
and
(C3)
hold. We also assume that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}\|_{0}\leq c_{2}s_{Y,G}

(8)

and
\|{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\|_{2}
is bounded.

(i)

Under
H_{0,k}
in (
6
),

(ii)

Under

Remark.
Assumption (

For rank Lasso, we verify that (

It follows from Theorem

that the asymptotic variance of partial Gini covariance is

To conduct the hypothesis test (

We reject

\displaystyle n^{1/2}\left|\widehat{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k})/\widehat{\sigma}_{G,k}\right|\geq Z_{\alpha/2},

The proof of Theorem

3.2

Simultaneous Testing the Significance of a Group of Covariates

Let
\mathcal{S}=\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}\}
denote the index set of the parameters of interest, where
d\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}|\mathcal{S}|
denotes the cardinality of the set
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\bm{\beta}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast}=(\beta_{k_{1}}^{\ast},\ldots,\beta_{k_{d}}^{\ast})^{\top}

represent the corresponding sub-vector of
\bm{\beta}^{\ast}
. We now consider testing
if these coefficients are simultaneously zero:

\displaystyle H_{0,\mathcal{S}}:{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast}={\bf 0}_{d},\quad\textrm{v.s}\quad H_{1,\mathcal{S}}:{\bm{\beta}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast}\neq{\bf 0}_{d}.

(9)

To construct the test statistic, we define the multivariate partial Gini covariance by

\displaystyle{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mbox{cov}\left\{F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}),{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}\right\},

where
{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}=(X_{k_{1}},\ldots,X_{k_{d}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and
{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-d}
is the sub-vector removing
{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}
from
{\bf x}
.
Let
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min\limits_{{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-d}}\mbox{E}\left\{(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})F_{Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})\right\}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min\limits_{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-d)\times d}}\mbox{E}\big(\|{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})\|^{2}_{2}\big)
.
Lemma
3
below is an extension of Lemma
2
.

Lemma 3
.

Under Condition
(C1)
,

\beta_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast}={\bf 0}_{d}
if and only if
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})={\bf 0}_{d}
.

Based on Lemma
3
, testing (
9
) is equivalent to testing

\displaystyle H_{0,\mathcal{S}}^{G}:{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})={\bf 0}_{d},\quad\textrm{v.s}\quad H_{1,\mathcal{S}}^{G}:{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})\neq{\bf 0}_{d}.

To construct the test statistics,
we first apply Gini regression with Lasso penalty and obtain an estimate of
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}
as

\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}_{1}(Y,{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}};{\bm{\theta}})

with the tuning parameter
\lambda_{Y,G,\mathcal{S}}
.
To estimate
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}
, we use node-wise least square regression with Lasso penalty
(
van de Geer et al., 2014
;
Javanmard & Montanari, 2014
;
Zhang & Zhang, 2014
)
.
Let
\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}_{\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k_{d}})
be the estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}
. For
j=1,\ldots,d
, by using Lasso penalty with the tuning parameter
\lambda_{X,k_{j}}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k_{j}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{{\bm{\gamma}}}(2n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{k_{j},i}-{\bm{\gamma}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S},i}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{X,k_{j}}\|{\bm{\gamma}}\|_{1}.

Similarly to the univariate case, the error bounds for
{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}
have been derived in the previous work.
Based on
Wang et al. (2020)
, we have
\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}-{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\lambda_{Y,G,\mathcal{S}}s_{Y,G}^{1/2})
, where
\lambda_{Y,G,\mathcal{S}}=O\{(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}\}
.
According to
Fan, Li, Zhang & Zou (2020)
, we have
\|\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}_{\mathcal{S}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}\|_{1}=O_{p}(\lambda_{X,\mathcal{S}}s_{X})
, where
\lambda_{X,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\max\{\lambda_{X,k_{j}},j=1,\ldots,d\}=O\{(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}\}
.

Define
\varepsilon_{\mathcal{S},i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}Y_{i}-\widehat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S},i}
.
The estimate for
{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})
is

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}n^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left({\bf x}_{\mathcal{S},i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S},i}\right)\left\{1\left(\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{S},i}\geq\widehat{\varepsilon}_{\mathcal{S},j}\right)-1/2\right\}.

To test (
9
), we propose the following chi-square test statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\widehat{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}})^{\top}\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}^{-1}\widehat{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,{\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}\mid{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}),

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(12n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\bf x}_{\mathcal{S},i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S},i}\right)\left({\bf x}_{\mathcal{S},i}-\widehat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S},i}\right)^{\top}

is the estimate of the covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}12^{-1}\text{cov}\left({\bf x}_{\mathcal{S}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma$}_{\mathcal{S}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)
.
The asymptotic distributions for
\widehat{W}_{G,\mathcal{S}}
under both the null hypothesis and the local alternatives are given in Theorem

below.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose Conditions

(C1′)
,

(C2)
, and

(C3)
are valid,
\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\|_{0}\leq c_{2}s_{Y,G}

(i)

Under

,
n\widehat{W}_{G,\mathcal{S}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\chi_{d}^{2}

(ii)

Under

4

Power Advantages for Heavy-tailed Error Distributions: Comparison with Existing Methods

In this section, we conduct a detailed power comparison between our proposed testing method and those utilizing partial Pearson covariance and partial quantile covariance. Our analysis reveals that the proposed method can be substantially more efficient than the partial Pearson covariance approach, particularly in scenarios with heavy-tailed random errors. Furthermore, the analysis suggests greater power than the method based on partial quantile covariance under local alternatives.

4.1

Comparison with the Method Based on Partial Pearson Covariance

We derive the efficiency gain of the new test based on the partial Gini covariance versus the one based on partial Pearson covariance in the presence of heavy-tailed random errors. The partial Pearson covariance, proposed by
Yule (1897)
, is a classical tool for testing the significance of the coefficient in the linear model.
In the high-dimensional settings, the estimate for partial Pearson covariance is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm{pPcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{P,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki})(X_{ki}-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki}),

(10)

\lambda_{Y,P,k}

Under the null hypothesis, (

Zhang & Zhang (2014)
.
Therefore, the following comparison also includes some earlier testing methods including
Zhang & Zhang (2014)
;
van de Geer et al. (2014)
;
Javanmard & Montanari (2014)

Meanwhile, (

They further proved that

converges to the normal distribution with mean zero and variance

H_{0,k}

4.2

Comparison with the Method Based on Partial Quantile Covariance

In the context of robust regression, quantile-based methods are among the most common approaches. Several studies have investigated variable selection within these methods
(
Wu & Liu, 2009
;
Wang et al., 2012
;
Lee et al., 2014
)
. Notably, significant advancements have been made by
Li et al. (2015)
and
Ma et al. (2017)
, who introduced the concept of partial quantile covariance to quantify the interplay between variables
Y
and
X_{k}
, while taking into account the confounding influence of covariates
\mathbf{z}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
.
The partial quantile covariance is defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=\mbox{cov}\left\{\psi_{\tau}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-Q_{\tau,Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}),X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}\right\},

where

{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{({\bm{\theta}},a)}\mbox{E}\{\rho_{\tau}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-Q_{\tau,Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}})\}
.
The partial quantile covariance and partial Gini covariance are closely related. To bridge these two measures, we introduce an intermediate concept. Inspired by
Zou & Yuan (2008)
, who proposed composite quantile regression to simultaneously consider
T
levels of quantile
\tau_{t}\in[0,1]
for
t=1,\ldots,T
simultaneously,
we define the partial composite quantile covariance as

\displaystyle{\rm{pCQcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mbox{cov}\left\{T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\psi_{\tau_{t}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{CQ,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}-Q_{\tau_{t},Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{CQ,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}),X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}\right\},

where

{\bm{\theta}}_{CQ,k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mbox{E}\{T^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\rho_{\tau_{t}}(Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}-Q_{\tau_{t},Y-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}})\}
. In the case where
\tau_{t}=t/(T+1)
, we can establish Proposition
1
.

Proposition 1
.

{\rm{pCQcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})\to{\rm{pGcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
as
T\to\infty
.

In this study, we aim to formulate the asymptotic normality of the partial quantile covariance in the high dimensional case, extending the work of
Li et al. (2015)
which was limited to the case where
p
is finite.
Denote

\displaystyle(\widehat{Q}_{\tau,Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}},\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\arg\min_{\eta,{\bm{\theta}}}n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{\tau}(Y_{i}-{\bm{\theta}}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki}-\eta)+\lambda_{Y,Q,k}\sum_{l=1}^{p-1}|\theta_{l}|,

(12)

where
\lambda_{Y,Q,k}
is the tuning parameter.
Then we define the estimate for partial quantile covariance in high-dimensional settings is

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\tau}(Y_{i}-{\bf z}_{ki}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}-\widehat{Q}_{\tau,Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}})(X_{ki}-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki}).

Let
s_{Y,Q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\max_{1\leq k\leq p}\|{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}\|_{0}

be the sparsity level for quantile regression. We impose the following conditions to establish the asymptotic normality of
\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
:

(C3
enumi
)

\|\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}\|_{1}=O_{p}(\lambda_{X,k}s_{X})
,

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}\|_{2}+\|\widehat{Q}_{\tau,Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}-Q_{\tau,Y-{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k}}\|_{2}=O_{p}(\lambda_{Y,Q,k}s_{Y,Q}^{1/2})
,

\|\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}\|_{0}\leq c_{3}s_{Y,Q}
.

\lambda_{X,k}=O\{(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}\}
and
\lambda_{Y,Q,k}=O\{(n^{-1}\log p)^{1/2}\}
.

(C4
enumi
)

s_{X}n^{-1/2}\log p=o(1)
and

s_{Y,Q}^{3/4}\log(n\vee p)\{(\log p)/n\}^{1/4}=o(1)
.

Condition
(C3enumi)
includes the estimation error bound of
\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{Q,k}
derived in the previous literature
(
Belloni & Chernozhukov, 2011
)
. A similar condition is imposed in
Zhao et al. (2014)
for testing high dimensional composite quantile regression coefficients.
When
s_{X}
and
s_{Q,Y}
are finite, Condition
(C4enumi)
is equivalent to
\log p=o(n^{1/5})
. This condition has been imposed in
Belloni et al. (2014)
and
Zhao et al. (2014)
, which focus on the inference of the high-dimensional quantile regression model when
\epsilon
is sub-Gaussian.

Denote
\sigma^{2}_{Q,k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\tau-\tau^{2})\mbox{E}\{(X_{k}-{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{k})^{2}\}
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose Conditions
(C2)
,
(C1′)
,
(C3enumi)
,
and
(C4enumi)
are valid.

(i)

Under
H_{0,k}
,
n^{1/2}\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{Q,k}^{2})
as
n\to\infty
.

(ii)

Under
H_{1,k}
,
\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
as
n\to\infty
.

To conduct hypothesis test, we determine whether to reject
H_{0,k}
in (
6
) based on the value of
n^{1/2}\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})/\widehat{\sigma}_{Q,k}
, where
\widehat{\sigma}_{Q,k}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\tau-\tau^{2})\big\{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{ki}-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki})^{2}\big\}
.
This test, using
n^{1/2}\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})/\widehat{\sigma}_{Q,k}
for (
6
), is more restrictive than the proposed test based on partial Gini covariance, which requires
\log(p)=o(n^{1/3})
, as it necessitates
\log(p)=o(n^{1/5})
.

An issue arises when assessing the effectiveness of
\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
. Denote
\mu_{Q}(\beta_{k}^{\ast})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
.
\mu_{Q}^{\prime}(\beta_{k}^{\ast})
goes to
0
at
\beta_{k}^{\ast}=0
.
Consider a specific scenario where both
X_{k}
and
\varepsilon
follow the standard normal distributions, and
X_{k}
is independent with
{\bf x}_{-k}
.
Rather than calculating
\mu_{Q}^{\prime}(0)
, we investigate the limiting behavior of
\mu_{Q}^{\prime}(n^{-1/2}\beta_{k,0})
as
n\to\infty
. It is straightforward to derive

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{Q}^{\prime}(n^{-1/2}\beta_{k,0})\rightarrow 0.

(13)

This limit indicates that
\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
might be too small under local alternatives when covariates are mutually independent, leading to poor performance in testing (
6
).
This aspect has not been previously addressed in the literature. We illustrate the empirical power for
\widehat{\rm{pQcov}}_{\tau}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})
in Section
5.1
, which indeed confirms this observation. The derivation of Equation (

) is provided in the Supplementary Material.

5.1

Partial Gini Covariance Based Tests

In this section, we present the finite sample performance of the partial Gini covariance-based test.
We consider two studies for our analysis.
The first study aims to examine the empirical size of the partial Gini covariance-based test and partial quantile covariance-based test.
The second study focuses on comparing the power performance of other high-dimensional independence tests against others.
Throughout the simulations, we generate
{\bf x}_{i}=(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{ip})^{\top}
for

independently from multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and covariance
\Sigma
, where
(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})_{s,t}=0.5^{|s-t|}
. We consider the following linear model, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle Y_{i}={\bf x}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}+\varepsilon_{i}.

Here,
{\bm{\beta}}^{\ast}
here is set as
(\beta_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\beta_{10}^{\ast},{\bf 0}_{p-10})^{\top}
, and
\varepsilon_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
are generate independently from
{\bf x}_{i}
. We consider three different noise settings:
(i)
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
,
(ii)
\varepsilon\sim\textrm{T}_{2}(0,1)
,
(iii)
\varepsilon\sim\textrm{Cauchy}(0,1)
.
\textrm{T}_{2}(0,1)
represents standard
t
distribution with
2
degrees of freedom.
The latter two are both heavy-tailed distributions.
We fix
n=200
and consider
p=500
and
p=2000
under three different distributions of
\varepsilon_{i}
respectively.

We compare the empirical distributions of six test statistics in the following context, which can be divided into two types.
The first type is partial covariance-based tests, involving partial Gini covariance-based test statistic,
quantile partial covariance-based test statistic with
\tau=0.5
, and
partial Pearson covariance-based test statistic.
To ensure that linear coefficients
{\bm{\theta}}_{P,k}
are correctly specified in partial Pearson covariance under heavy-tailed cases, we also implement another modified estimate by applying rank Lasso
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
. The estimate is denoted as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rm{pPcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki})(X_{ki}-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki}),

The corresponding test statistic is
n^{1/2}\widetilde{\rm{pPcov}}(Y,X_{k}\mid{\bf z}_{k})/\widetilde{\sigma}_{P,k}
, where
\widetilde{\sigma}_{P,k}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\big\{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-\widehat{\bm{\theta}}_{G,k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki})^{2}\big\}\big\{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{ki}-\widehat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf z}_{ki})^{2}\big\}
.
The second group includes debiased Lasso
(
Zhang & Zhang, 2014
)
and its bootstrap version
Dezeure et al. (2017)
.
These two test statistics are implemented using R package
hdi
with
101
times of bootstrap.
