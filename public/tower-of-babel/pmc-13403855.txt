Photoacoustic imaging of oxygen saturation in the radial arterial wall and perivascular tissue in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Xiaofang Zhong, Yingying Pan, Lixiong Liu, Yingqi Zheng, Hui Wang, Xiaohua Liu, Xiaoxuan Lin, Guijuan Peng, Miaoru Yu, Yuanyuan Sheng, Jinfeng Xu, Shuyu Luo, Yingying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is associated with vasculitis of varying severity. Conventional ultrasonography faces challenges in accurately assessing this condition, whereas the development of photoacoustic (PA) imaging offers a promising approach. This study aims to evaluate the clinical utility of PA imaging in assessing vasculitis changes in patients with SLE.MethodsA total of 102 patients diagnosed with SLE were enrolled as the case group and subdivided into two subgroups: the non-active group and the active group, according to the systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index 2000 (SLEDAI-2000) scoring criteria. 35 healthy volunteers were recruited as the control group. PA was performed to measure the oxygen saturation of the bilateral radial arterial wall and perivascular tissues.Results(1) Active SLE patients showed significantly elevated oxygen saturation (SO2) levels of radial artery wall and perivascular tissues compared to nonactive patients and healthy controls (p < 0.05). Background

Vasculitis is a common pathological change in patients with SLE and serves as a primary component for assessing disease activity. Approximately 11% to 36% of SLE patients develop various types of vasculitis [1]. The underlying pathogenesis is complex, involving interactions among immune cell subsets and inflammatory mediators [2]. It is characterized by inflammation and injuries of the vascular wall, which affects arteries, veins, and capillaries across various organ systems. Clinical manifestations vary depending on the size, location, and inflammatory activity of the affected vessels and may present concurrently or sequentially, posing a diagnostic challenge due to their nonspecific nature.

Perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) is a distinct fat depot that surrounds most blood vessels except for the cerebral vasculature. In large vessels, PVAT tightly adheres to the adventitia of the vessel wall; in small and micro-vessels, it becomes an integral component of the vessel wall [3]. Current studies suggest that PVAT possesses unique endocrine and paracrine functions that regulate vascular homeostasis. Under healthy conditions, it protects vascular metabolism and function, similar to thermogenic brown or beige adipose tissue. However, under pathological conditions induced by high-fat diets and other cardiovascular risk factors, dysfunctional PVAT loses its thermogenic capacity and displays a white-like phenotype. This results in increased oxidative stress, enhanced immune cell infiltration, and upregulation of inflammatory cytokines/adipokines in the vascular wall, thereby contributing to endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis [4–8]. There is a complex bidirectional paracrine and vasocrine signaling pathway between PVAT and the vascular wall. The inflammatory mediators and oxidative products secreted by the vascular wall influence the PVAT phenotype. In turn, PVAT-secreted adipokines act synergistically with the inflammatory mediators to amplify the inflammatory response in the affected vessel segments, promoting vascular inflammation and endothelial dysfunction in a “from the outer to the inner” manner [9]. Compared with the healthy controls, computed tomography (CT) scans of SLE females showed higher volumes of PVAT around the thoracic aorta and elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers [10], which may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and intimal injuries. These findings suggest the presence of PVAT dysfunction in SLE patients, and the assessment of PVAT function may provide valuable information on vascular inflammation. Conventional ultrasonography provides real-time, high-resolution anatomical imaging of vascular structures and hemodynamic assessment through Doppler techniques, making it widely accessible for evaluating vasculitis in SLE patients. However, ultrasound alone lacks the capability to detect functional and molecular changes at the microvascular level, particularly in the early stages of vasculitis where structural alterations may be subtle or absent. PA imaging is an emerging non-invasive modality that addresses this limitation by providing functional information on tissue oxygenation and hemoglobin concentration with optical contrast and ultrasonic resolution. Photoacoustic/ultrasound (PA/US) imaging, which integrates both the high-contrast advantage of optical imaging and the high-spatial resolution of ultrasonography, provide a complementary approach to evaluate vascular health. Furthermore, through dual-wavelength excitation, PA/US imaging can quantify the relative concentrations of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin, calculating the total hemoglobin concentration and tissue oxygen saturation [11–14], which enables the early detection of functional changes in tissues. Previous studies have validated its effectiveness by employing intravascular PA/US imaging on the radial artery across various age groups and disease conditions [15, 16]. However, limited PA/US research has been conducted on the radial artery wall and its surrounding tissues. This study aims to investigate alterations in SO2 of the radial artery wall and perivascular tissues in patients with SLE using PA/US imaging, thereby contributing to the assessment of SLE-related vasculitis.

Methods

Participant recruitment and grouping

A total of 102 patients with SLE admitted to the Department of Rheumatology and Immunology between May 2024 and March 2025 were consecutively enrolled. All of them met the diagnostic criteria for SLE recommended by the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics in 2012 [17]. The SLE disease activity index was assessed using the SLEDAI-2000 scoring system [18], and the patients were further subdivided into two subgroups based on the score: inactive group (SLEDAI-2000:0–4, n = 52) and active group (SLEDAI-2000: ≥ 5, n = 50). Patients aged < 18 years, patients with non-SLE-related hypertension, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, arterio-venous fistula surgery, or unclear radial artery visualization were excluded. Concurrently, 35 healthy volunteers were recruited as the control group. This study was executed in strict adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Shenzhen People’s Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent.

Photoacoustic/ultrasound imaging acquisition protocol

All subjects received a PA/US multimodal examination on the day following admission using a Mindray Resona R7 system (Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with a PA/US L9-3 probe. The exams were conducted by a sonographer with 10 years of experience in ultrasonography who was blinded to the patients’ medical histories.

Before the examination, both the operator and the patient were required to wear laser safety glasses to ensure compliance with laser safety protocols. The patient was set in a supine position with arms extended and palms facing upward. First, the surface landmarks of the radial tuberosity on both forearms were identified and marked. An obvious radial pulse was readily detected proximal to the landmarks. Then, short- and long-axis images of the radial artery were acquired 1–2 cm proximal to the landmarks (Fig. 1). In this region, the radial artery follows a relatively straight and superficial course, allowing clear detection of the pulsation, and its few branches also reduce interference from vessel bifurcations [19]. During the acquisition, to ensure image stability, a gel pad was applied to the wrist area, and the probe was kept stable to avoid any compression or displacement.

ROI delineation and oxygen saturation quantification

Analysis was performed using Mindray Resona system ultrasonic diagnostic system with offline data analysis software (UtraOffice software). All the images were analyzed by another sonographer (Y. P.) with 5 years of experience, who was blinded to the patients’ medical histories.

Two regions of interest (ROIs) were defined: The first ROI was delineated by tracing the intima boundary of the radial artery on the grayscale short-axis image. The second ROI was established by radially expanding the first ROI by 0.5 mm radially based on the reference values for radial artery wall thickness derived from ultrasound and histological studies [20–22]. The outlined region is displayed simultaneously across all four-panel images, enabling concurrent acquisition of ultrasonic and photoacoustic variables within the ROI. To exclude the potential interference from intravascular SO2, the SO2 of the annular region formed between the two ROI–which included the vascular wall and perivascular tissues–was calculated (Fig. 3). Each radial artery was measured three times, and the average value was recorded.

Intraobserver and interobserver agreement

A total of twenty participants were randomly selected for the analysis. A sonographer (Y.P.) performed the measurements twice with a 2-month interval, blinded to the patients’ information and previous results. Another sonographer (H.W.) with more than 5-year experience in ultrasound diagnosis was asked to performed the SO2 measurement independently, blinded to the patients’ information and medical histories.

Statistical method and ROC analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc 23.0 software. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical data were expressed as the number of cases. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify the normality of the data distribution. For comparisons between two independent samples with normally distributed data, the t-test was applied; when the data did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were used. For categorical data, the chi-square test was employed. For comparisons of continuous variables among three groups, one-way analysis of variance was used for normally distributed data, while the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied for non-normally distributed data. When significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni method. The correlation between variables was assessed using Pearson correlation for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation for non-normally distributed data. Univariate and multivariate linear regression (stepwise) analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with radial artery SO2. The ROC curve was used to evaluate whether SO2 could reflect disease activity in SLE patients. Inter-rater correlation coefficients (ICCs) and Bland-Altman plots were used to examine the intraobserver and interobserver agreement for SO2 measurement.

Results

Comparisons of clinical characteristics

The diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of SLE patients was higher compared to the healthy controls, but there was no significant difference between the active group and the non-active group (p < 0.05). The active group had a shorter duration of illness and a higher SLEDAI-2000 (p < 0.05). There were no significant differences in the other indicators among the three groups. Compared with the non-active group, the active group demonstrated lower levels of red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB), C3, C4, and serum albumin (ALB), but higher levels of triglycerides (TC), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESC), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Correlations between average oxygen saturation and clinical characteristics

Correlation analysis and linear regression were conducted between the average oxygen saturation of bilateral radial arterial walls and perivascular tissues (average-SO2) and patients’ clinical characteristics. The results showed that average-SO2 was positively correlated with SLEDAI-2000 (r = 0.571, p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with the levels of C3 (r=-0.453, p < 0.001), C4 (r=-0.493, p < 0.001), and serum albumin (r=-0.313, p = 0.001) (Fig. 5). Given that the C4 and C3 levels are components of the SLEDAI-2000, only the SLEDAI-2000 and serum albumin level were selected for the multivariate regression analysis. The result (Table 3) demonstrated that SLEDAI-2000 (p < 0.001) was independently associate with average-SO2.

Association of oxygen saturation with disease activity

ROC curve analysis showed that the average-SO2 exhibited high accuracy in discriminating between active and inactive disease states, with an area under the curve of 79.6%. When the cut-off value was set 79.04%, the diagnostic sensitivity was 74%, and the specificity was 80.5%, indicating the potential clinical utility of average-SO2 (Fig. 6).

The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The average oxygen saturation exhibited high accuracy in predicting disease activity in systemic lupus erythematosus patients, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 79.6%

Interobserver and intraobserver agreement

Intra- and inter-observer agreement for R-SO₂ and L-SO₂ measurements demonstrated good repeatability, with ICCs of 0.962 (95%CI:0.907–0.985) and 0.971 (95%CI: 0.929–0.988), and inter-observer ICCs of 0.955 (95%CI:0.889–0.982), and 0.972 (95%CI:0.930–0.989), respectively (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussions

This study investigated the changes in SO2 of the radial artery wall and perivascular tissues in patients with SLE using photoacoustic imaging technology. The following findings were obtained: (1) Active SLE patients had a significantly elevated SO2 of the radial artery wall and perivascular tissues. (2) The radial artery wall and perivascular tissues of SO2 were independently and positively correlated with SLEDAI-2000. (3) The average SO2 of the bilateral radial artery wall and perivascular tissues demonstrated potential diagnostic value in identifying disease activity. The research results demonstrate that SO2 levels in the radial artery wall and perivascular tissues differ significantly according to disease activity status, suggesting an association with functional changes in early vasculitis.

Artery wall and perivascular tissues oxygen saturation in SLE patients

Vasculitis is one of the common manifestations of SLE, which results from. the autoimmune-mediated attack on the vessel wall, leading to vascular inflammation and injuries. PVAT plays a crucial role in this pathological process. It is recognized as a contributing factor in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis in SLE patients [23], as the pro-inflammatory properties of PVAT impair the vascular endothelial function. Small and medium-sized vessels are the most common targets for vascular involvement, posing challenges for detection by conventional ultrasound.

PA/US imaging obtains high-resolution, high-contrast, and multifunctional images by integrating the strengths of optical and ultrasound imaging, offering a novel tool and perspective for clinical diagnostics and research. Previous studies have primarily investigated the application of intravascular PA/US imaging in the assessment of radial artery luminal oxygen saturation. Bok et al. [15] demonstrated that luminal oxygen saturation in the radial artery tended to decrease with advancing age, a change that may be associated with the age-related degenerating vascular endothelial function, and the finding showed the implication of PA imaging in the diagnosis of age-related vascular diseases. Ganzleben et al. [16] also demonstrated the high sensitivity and specificity of PA imaging in diagnosing anemia. Our study applied this technique to the arterial wall, based on the principle that the close proximity of red blood cells within the lumen leads to mutual cancellation of their PA signals, whereas the signals of the vascular wall and its surrounding tissues are preserved and detectable [24]. The result of the study showed that SO2 levels in the active group were elevated compared to the non-active and control group. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis that phenotype changes in PVAT may be associated with altered tissue oxygenation. Animal studies on murine lupus demonstrated the downregulation of uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1), a marker of brown and beige adipocytes, and Increased Infiltration of CD45-positive white blood cells in SLE mice. Furthermore, the increased expression of pro-inflammatory adipokines and white blood cells markers was observed in PVAT, which further promotes the inflammation in vessels [25]. In response to tissue inflammation and damage, local vasodilation occurs, blood flow increases, and consequently, tissue oxygen saturation rises. The findings of this study demonstrate the value of PA imaging in non-invasively assessing SO2 in the radial arterial wall and its perivascular tissues, providing a possibility for the evaluation of functional changes in vasculitis.

Factors influencing radial vascular wall and perivascular oxygen saturation

The study demonstrated that the SO2 of the radial artery wall and the perivascular tissues was inversely correlated with the levels of C3 and C4 in patients with active SLE. SLE patients in the active phase presented with lower levels of complement C3, C4 compared to those in the non-active phase, which is consistent with findings in previous studies [26, 27]. Changes in complement levels reflect the activation of the immune system in SLE patients. Hypocomplementemia is considered a critical process in the pathogenesis of SLE-related vasculitis, which is associated with immune complex deposition in vessel walls [28]. In the active stage, complement‑mediated inflammatory responses mainly induce vasodilation and hyperperfusion, which increase oxygen delivery and elevate perivascular SO2. With persistent activation, progressive endothelial injury impairs oxygen diffusion, leading to subsequent reduction in SO2. Serum albumin is one of the biomarkers that reflect the nutritional status and inflammatory responses in the human body. In SLE patients, decreased serum albumin level results from several factors, including the inflammatory status of the disease, which suppresses albumin synthesis, and the presence of intestinal vasculitis, which leads to albumin leakage [29]. Previous studies validated that the albumin level is an independent predictor for the early detection of SLE [30], with a negative correlation to the disease activity [28, 31, 32]. Our results showed a similar trend that active SLE patients exhibited a significant decrease in serum albumin level, which was negatively correlated to the average-SO2.

Our study showed that the SLEDAI-2000 was independently associated with average-SO2, with higher scores corresponding to elevated SO2. The SLEDAI scoring system is a widely used tool for assessing disease activity in SLE patients, which integrates symptoms across multiple organ systems and laboratory indicators. Systemic vasculitis, a core pathological feature of SLE, contributes to multi-organ involvement, including the skin, kidneys, and nervous system, thereby leading to diverse clinical manifestations. These manifestations constitute the primary components assessed by the SLEDAI-2000 score. Thus, using the SLEDAI-2000 scoring system helps elucidate the pathological mechanisms of vasculitis and in identifying its manifestations across various target organs. Previous studies have also demonstrated that patients with vasculitis presented with higher SLEDAI-2000 scores [33]. Furthermore, vasculitis is a commonly included criterion in several SLE activity assessment tools including SLEDAI-2000 [18]. The correlation between SLEDAI-2000 score and SO2 suggested that the disease activity may influence local vascular oxygen metabolism. This could be attributed to inflammatory responses and immune dysregulation during the active phase, which impaired the endothelial function and causing hemodynamic disturbance. In this study, univariate linear regression was employed to identify potentially relevant variables of SO2. A multivariate linear regression was performed to assess the independent effect of SLEDAI score on SO2 and to explore potential interactions among variables. These findings provide a new perspective into the mechanism of vascular oxygen metabolism in patients with SLE, thereby highlighting the importance of monitoring vascular health in patients with high SLEDAI scores.

Potential value of photoacoustic imaging in SLE assessment

Currently, multiple systems are available for assessing SLE disease activity: The SLEDAI-2000 scoring system [18] is comprehensive, objective, and user-friendly; however, its indicators are cumbersome and rely on fixed weighting coefficients, which limit its flexibility in clinical scenarios. The British Isles Lupus Assessment Group index demonstrates strong specificity and is effective in evaluating disease severity, but the implementation is complex and with high subjectivity [34]. The Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Score exhibits high sensitivity and specificity; however, its clinical application remains limited due to insufficient validation and complex measurement requirements. Among laboratory indicators, antinuclear antibody demonstrates high sensitivity but limited specificity [35], while C3 and C4 are associated with disease activity, yet lack sufficient diagnostic specificity [36]. Given the distinct advantages and limitations of each method, a combination of multiple tools is often required to achieve a comprehensive and reliable evaluation of SLE activity in clinical practice, which in turn guides therapeutic decisions.

To validate the practicality of the novel variable, ROC analysis was used in the study to evaluate the performance of SO2 levels in predicting SLE disease activity. The results revealed an AUC of 79.6%, indicating that the SO2 level of the radial arterial wall and perivascular tissue has strong discriminative power in distinguishing between active and non-active SLE patients. When the cut-off value was set 79.04%, the diagnostic sensitivity was 74%, and the specificity was 80.5%. The potential of oxygen saturation as an indicator for assessing SLE activity may be associated with the perivascular inflammation during the active phase. Abnormal immune activation results in inflammatory infiltration of perivascular tissues, which impacts local oxygen metabolism and oxygen saturation. Therefore, SO2 has the potential to serve as a potentially valuable and accessible biomarker for assessing SLE disease activity. Complementary to the convention approaches such as visual inspection and serological markers, SO2 quantification via PA/US provided a non-invasive quantitative window into microvascular circulation and inflammatory status, thereby offering novel insights into the pathophysiology of SLE and enabling more precise, objective monitoring of disease activity.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, this is a single-center experience with a small sample size, and the number of patients with severe activity included is relatively limited. The generalizability and applicability of the results need to be further verified through multi-center and large-scale studies. Second, to mitigate the confounding effect caused by medications, we performed all the serum assays and PA/US exam on the day of admission before initiating therapies. However, some enrolled patients had started SLE medications prior to enrollment. These agents may modulate tissue oxygen saturation by altering microcirculation, hemodynamic or gas exchange, causing potential confounding effects. Third, the cross-sectional design of this study limits interpretation to associations rather than causal relationships. We cannot determine whether altered oxygen saturation contributes to disease activity, results from disease activity, or whether both are influenced by unmeasured confounding factors. Longitudinal designs are needed to clarify the temporal sequence of these relationships.

Conclusions

Photoacoustic imaging detects elevated SO₂ in the radial artery wall and perivascular tissues of active SLE patients, which positively correlates with SLEDAI-2000 scores. Bilateral average SO₂ shows potential in identifying disease activity. These findings suggest that tissue oxygenation changes may reflect functional alterations in early vasculitis, supporting photoacoustic imaging as a promising non-invasive biomarker for monitoring SLE activity and vascular involvement.
