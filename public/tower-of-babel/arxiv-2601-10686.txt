Vertex operator algebra bundles on modular curves and their associated modular forms
Daniel Barake, Owen Chuchman, Cameron Franc, Geoffrey Mason and Brett Nasserden

Abstract
. This paper describes the vector bundle on the elliptic modular curve that is associated to a vertex operator algebra V V (VOA) or more generally a quasi-vertex operator algebra (QVOA), with a view towards future applications aimed at studying the characters of VOAs. We explain how the modes of sections of V V give rise naturally to V V -valued quasi-modular forms. The space Q ⁡ ( V ) Q(V) of V V -valued quasi-modular forms is endowed with the structure of a doubled QVOA, and in particular the algebra Q Q of quasi-modular forms is itself a doubled QVOA. Q ⁡ ( V ) Q(V) also admits a natural derivative operator arising from the connection on the bundle defined by V V and the modular derivative, which we call the raising operator. We introduce an associated lowering operator Λ \Lambda on Q ⁡ ( V ) Q(V) having the property that the V V -valued modular forms M ⁡ ( V ) ⊆ Q ⁡ ( V ) M(V)\subseteq Q(V) are the kernel of Λ \Lambda . This extends the classical theory of scalar-valued quasi-modular forms. We exhibit an explicit isomorphism of M ⁡ ( V ) M(V) with M ⊗ V M\otimes V . Finally, the coordinate invariance of vertex operators implies that M ⁡ ( V ) M(V) has a natural Hecke theory, and we use this isomorphism to fully describe the Hecke eigensystems: they are the same as the systems of eigenvalues that arise from scalar-valued quasi-modular forms.



1.
Introduction

Vertex operators arose initially in physics as an algebraic device for aiding computations in conformal field theory and related fields such as statistical mechanics and string theory. See
[
4
]
for references to the physics literature. From the very start, surprising connections with modular forms were a part of these investigations. Later, mathematicians began to formalize and expand the nascent theory of vertex operator algebras or VOAs, again mainly from an algebraic viewpoint
[
12
]
. The main drivers of these investigations were to explain monstrous moonshine, and to explain a number of interesting phenomenon in the study of representations of some infinite dimensional Lie algebras. Inevitably, modular forms and quasi-modular forms often made contact with these investigations in surprising ways
[
16
]
,
[
13
]
.

Since these initial investigations, some of the modular mystery has been swept away, largely due to the geometric interpretation of VOAs and the theories that inspired their study. Indeed, in
[
11
]
, one finds a clear discussion of how the data of a VOA is the same as the data of a certain vector bundle — generally of infinite rank — on any smooth curve, or even better, on moduli spaces of curves. These bundles are endowed with connections that interact with the vertex operations in interesting ways. Since modular forms are sections of vector bundles on the moduli space of elliptic curves, this geometric viewpoint makes for a potent and convenient way to understand the appearance of modular forms in the theory of VOAs.

Inspired by earlier
p
-adic work on VOAs
[
10
]
, we began to investigate in what sense VOAs might also give rise to vector bundles on arithmetic curves such as, for example, the Igusa tower or other limits of Shimura curves. It became clear that it would be beneficial to have a detailed understanding of the geometric theory of VOAs on modular curves at finite level. The present paper constitutes our attempt to make this theory precise and accessible to readers already familiar with the theory of modular forms. More precisely, we give below a clear construction of the vector bundle
\mathcal{V}/X(1)
on the modular curve
X(1)
of elliptic curves of level one attached to a VOA or, more generally, a quasi-VOA (QVOA)
V
, using an approach based on uniformization as presented, say, in the lectures notes
[
14
]
. This approach allows us to describe the sections of
\mathcal{V}
in a clear way. These sections are holomorphic functions on the complex upper-half plane
\uhp

f\colon\uhp\to V

taking values in a finite-dimensional subspace of
V
and which satisfy a modular transformation law. Such functions, of varying weights, together describe a module
M(V)
of
V
-valued modular forms. See Section
3.4
for precise definitions of
V
-valued modular forms and the space
M(V)
.

Before turning to a discussion of our results, let us remark upon why we consider quasi-VOAs as a fundamental object of study below, as opposed to VOAs. The first basic point is that we are working on the moduli space of elliptic curves rather than the general genus case, so only the
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}_{2}
part of the Virasoro action on
V
really plays a rôle in our constructions. This observation has appeared in various guises in the literature, see for example
[
12
, Page 20]
. The second basic point is that the space
Q(V)
of
V
-valued quasimodular forms that we introduce below, only has the structure of a quasi-VOA, and not a full-blown VOA. Thus, in this subject quasi-VOAs are unavoidable and a natural object of study.

In this paper we give a rather complete description of
M(V)
and various related structures. To describe our results, it turns out to be natural and convenient to introduce a more general space of quasi-modular
V
-valued forms
Q(V)
, cf. Section
5.2
. The reason for introducing
V
-valued quasi-modular forms is simple: the space
M(V)
is not closed under the natural pointwise bilinear products that
M(V)
inherits from
V
. This assertion is precisely the VOA analog of the statement that
the algebra of modular forms
M
is not closed with respect to the operator

\theta=qd/dq
and that one should expand to the algebra of quasi-modular forms
Q

to get a good
\theta
-invariant space of functions. Although we introduce
Q(V)
as a space of functions satisfying certain quasi-modular transformation laws, we prove in Theorem
5.5
that there is a natural graded isomorphism:

(1)

Q(V)\cong Q\otimes_{\mathbf{C}}V^{(2)},

where
V^{(2)}
denotes the same underlying vector space as
V
, but with the grading doubled. This space
Q(V)
is then closed under the natural bilinear products that
Q(V)
inherits from
V
. Moreover, we explain in Section
6
how
Q(V)
inherits a natural structure of a (doubled) quasi-vertex operator algebra from the identification of equation (
1
). This quasi-VOA structure includes, in particular, raising and lowering operators defining a representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
.

It is an irony that here we have quasi-modular forms and vertex algebras yoked together in a setting in which Rankin-Cohen (RC) brackets appear to play no rôle, whereas it has long been anticipated
[
25
]
that RC brackets of (quasi)modular forms and vertex algebras should be connected. One might approach this by appropriately performing a deformation quantization of
Q
or
Q(V)
considered as a QVOA in order to realize such a connection. Such things have been well-studied in the literature on modular forms e.g.,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
nor are they entirely unknown in the VOA literature
[
9
]
,
[
20
]
, and we hope to return to this subject in the future.

Our first main result characterizes the containment of
V
-valued modular forms
M(V)\subseteq Q(V)
inside the space of
V
-valued quasi-modular forms using the apparatus described so far. Recall that every quasi-modular form can be realized as a polynomial in the Eisenstein series
E_{2},E_{4},E_{6}
, (see
[
24
]
) making
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial E_{2}}
a natural weight lowering linear operator on
Q
. On the other hand,
L(1)
is a natural weight lowering operator on
V
. Combining these lowering operators gives:

Theorem 1.1
.

The space of modular forms
M(V)
is the kernel of the lowering operator

\Lambda\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{12}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{2}}\otimes 1+1\otimes L(1)

acting on
Q(V)
.

Theorem
1.1
is proved as a consequence, cf. Corollary
7.2
, of Theorem
7.1
which describes the modular transformation behavior of elements of
Q(V)
. There are several perspectives that one can take with Theorem
1.1
. First, on the modular side, it is a natural analog of a result in the classical theory of modular forms, namely that the subspace
M=\mathbf{C}[E_{4},E_{6}]
of modular forms inside the space
Q=\mathbf{C}[E_{2},E_{4},E_{6}]
of quasi-modular forms is the kernel of the lowering operator
\partial/\partial E_{2}
. While this scalar-valued result is obvious once one knows the descriptions of
M
and
Q
in terms of Eisenstein series, Theorem
1.1
requires more work. On the other hand it is well-known that the
quasi-primary
states
QP(V)
of a VOA
V
are in some sense the most tractable (cf.
[
12
]
, Section 2.6), and for VOAs
V
we have (by definition)
QP(V)=\ker L(1)
. Thus a VOA-centric take on Theorem
1.1
is that
M(V)
may be viewed as the space of quasiprimary states in
Q(V)
.

It turns out that one can give a very concrete description of
M(V)
as a free-module of infinite rank over
M
. This is our second main result:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists an explicit
M
-linear isomorphism of graded spaces:

P\colon M\otimes_{\mathbf{C}}V^{(2)}\cong M(V)

A subtlety is that the isomorphism
P^{-1}
arising from Theorem
1.2
is
not
the restriction of the pointwise isomorphism of equation (
1
) to the subspace
M(V)\subseteq Q(V)
. See Section
7.3
for the definition of
P
and a proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Taken together, the isomorphism of equation (
1
), and Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
yield a detailed understanding of the space
M(V)
. Since the sections of a bundle characterize the bundle itself, this in turns provides a concrete, automorphic, description of the bundle
\mathcal{V}/X(1)
over the moduli space of elliptic curves that is associated to a QVOA or VOA
V
as in, say,
[
11
]
.

Let us now outline the structure of this paper. In Section
2
we recall the geometric definition of modular forms. Section
3.4
then describes the construction of the bundle
\mathcal{V}/X(1)
from
[
11
]
applied to a QVOA on the moduli space of elliptic curves following the orbifold approach of
[
14
]
. Section
4
follows by introducing the connection on the bundle
\mathcal{V}/X(1)
. This is a raising operator in the modular theory, and we provide detailed first-principle proofs to help ease the uninitiated into the subject. Then in Section
5
we introduce quasi-modular forms and give some motivation for their consideration. In the closely related Section
6
we discuss the general algebraic structure of
Q(V)
as a QVOA. Then in Section
7
we introduce the operators needed for proving our main Theorems, and we discuss some examples, dimension computations, and describe how the connection behaves when one applies the trivialization

to the space

. Finally, in Section
8

that arise from the coordinate-invariance of the QVOA-bundle construction. We use our isomorphism

from Theorem
1.2

and

are the same as those that arise from
M
and
Q

We also include two appendices. The first, Appendix
A

can be obtained easily from the Hecke eigensystems of modular forms of level one by differentiation. This result is known to experts, but we could not find a convenient reference and we include a proof for completeness. This facilitates the determination of all Hecke eigenforms in the spaces
M(V)
and
Q(V)
from the Hecke eigenforms in

B

Modular forms

2.1.
Geometric definition of modular forms

Let
Y(1)
be the (open) modular curve of level one associated to
\Gamma
, and let
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to Y(1)
be the universal elliptic curve over
Y(1)
. We recall how this is defined: define left and right actions of
\Gamma
and
\mathbf{Z}^{2}
on
\uhp\times\mathbf{C}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)(\tau,z)

\displaystyle=(\tfrac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},(c\tau+d)^{-1}z),

\displaystyle(\tau,z)\cdot(m,n)

\displaystyle=(\tau,z+m\tau+n).

The quotient
E\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\uhp\times\mathbf{C})/\mathbf{Z}^{2}
is a family of elliptic curves living over
\uhp
by projection onto the first factor. The fiber over
\tau\in\uhp
is
E_{\tau}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbf{C}/(\mathbf{Z}\tau\oplus\mathbf{Z})
. The double quotient

\mathcal{E}=\Gamma\backslash E

is then the universal elliptic curve over
Y(1)=\Gamma\obackslash\uhp
.

Definition 2.1
.

Set
\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}=p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}
, an invertible sheaf on
Y(1)
.

The importance of this definition is that, under a uniformization, global sections of
\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}
are holomorphic modular forms of weight one on the upper half plane, with no conditions imposed at cusps. To make this precise, note that the sheaf
\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}
can be described concretely by working on the cover
\uhp\times\mathbf{C}/\uhp
and describing
\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}/\uhp}
: sections of
\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}}
are nothing but differentials
\alpha d\tau+\beta dz
where
\alpha,\beta
are holomorphic functions on
\uhp\times\mathbf{C}
. Under this description, then,
\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}/\uhp}
is the line bundle on
\uhp\times\mathbf{C}
spanned by
dz
. That is,

\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}/\uhp}(\uhp\times\mathbf{C})=\{\alpha(\tau,z)dz\mid\alpha\textrm{ is holomorphic on }\uhp\times\mathbf{C}\}.

Then, since
E
is the quotient of
\uhp\times\mathbf{C}
by
\mathbf{Z}^{2}
, we can obtain a description of the global sections of
\Omega^{1}_{E/\uhp}
by taking invariants under this action:

\displaystyle\Omega^{1}_{E/\uhp}(E)

\displaystyle=\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}/\uhp}(\uhp\times\mathbf{C})^{\mathbf{Z}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\{\alpha(z,\tau)dz\mid\alpha(\tau,z+m\tau+n)=\alpha(\tau,z)\textrm{ for all }m,n\in\mathbf{Z}\}.

This is one of the axioms for a Jacobi form.

Remark 2.2
.

If we were working on
\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}}
, then
\mathbf{Z}^{2}
would not act trivially on
dz
: we would have
dz\cdot(m,n)=dz+md\tau
, but when working in the relative differentials
\Omega^{1}_{\uhp\times\mathbf{C}/\uhp}
, the
d\tau
term vanishes.

We can continue this analysis by taking the quotient on the left by the modular group
\Gamma
. Invariance under this action amounts to the functional transformation law:

\alpha\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},(c\tau+d)^{-1}z\right)\frac{dz}{c\tau+d}=\alpha(\tau,z)dz

That is,
\alpha(\tau,z)
is a weak Jacobi form of weight
1
and index
0
, where weak here means that we say nothing about its Fourier development:

\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}(\mathcal{E})=\{\alpha(\tau,z)dz\mid\alpha\textrm{ is a weak Jacobi form of weight }1\textrm{ and index }0.\}

Now, the projection map
p\colon\mathcal{E}\to Y(1)
is described concretely in terms of our uniformization as
p(\tau,z)=\tau
. It remains to identify the space

\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}(Y(1))=(p_{*}\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)})(Y(1))

with the space of weakly holomorphic modular forms of weight
1
. By definition, we have
f\in\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}(Y(1))
if and only if
f\circ p\in\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}(Y(1))
. Assume first that
f\circ p\in\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}(Y(1))
, so that

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)

\displaystyle=(f\circ p)\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d},(c\tau+d)^{-1}z\right)

\displaystyle=(c\tau+d)(f\circ p)\left(\tau,z\right)

\displaystyle=(c\tau+d)f(\tau),

where we have used the Jacobi form transformation law satisfied by
f\circ p
. Therefore every section
f\in\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}(Y(1))
can be interpreted as a modular form of weight one in this way.

Suppose conversely that
f
is a (weakly holomorphic) modular form of weight one. Then
f\circ p
is independent of
z
, and it is clear that we obtain a Jacobi form
f\circ p
of weight
1
and index
0
from this constancy in
z
and the transformation law for
f
. Thus, we have indeed verified that global sections of
\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}
can be identified with (weakly holomorphic) modular forms of weight one. Modular forms of weight
k
are then sections of
\omega_{\mathcal{E}/Y(1)}^{k}
.

2.2.
The compactified modular curve

Let now
X(1)
denote the compact modular curve of level one with the cusp added. Let
\mathbf{D}
denote the unit disc, and let
\mathbf{D}^{\times}
denote the punctured unit disc. Endow both of these spaces with the trivial action by the cyclic group

of order

. Then the compactified modular curve

is defined concretely, as an orbifold, by glueing

and

under the diagram

\textstyle{\langle-1,T\rangle\obackslash\uhp\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\tau\mapsto q}
\textstyle{C_{2}\obackslash\mathbf{D}^{\times}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\iota_{2}}
\textstyle{\Gamma\obackslash\uhp}
\textstyle{C_{2}\obackslash\mathbf{D}}

where the left diagonal map is the natural covering and the rightmost diagonal map is the natural inclusion.

In this way, a vector bundle
\mathcal{V}
on

can be described concretely as a triple
\mathcal{V}=(\mathcal{V}_{1},\mathcal{V}_{2},\phi)
where
\mathcal{V}_{1}
is a vector bundle on

, and
\mathcal{V}_{2}
is a vector bundle on
C_{2}\obackslash\mathbf{D}
, and

is a bundle isomorphism

\phi\colon\iota_{1}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{1}\to\iota_{2}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{2}.

See
[
14
]
for the case of line bundles, and Sections 2 and 3 of
[
1
]
for the higher rank case.

In our setting, we will have a QVOA

, or some finite-dimensional truncation of it, and bundles

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\obackslash(\uhp\times V),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{2}

\displaystyle=C_{2}\obackslash(\mathbf{D}\times V)

where this notation means that
\uhp\times V
and
\mathbf{D}\times V
are endowed with actions lifting the actions of
\Gamma(1)
and
C_{2}
on
\uhp
and
\mathbf{D}
, respectively. Then

\displaystyle\iota_{1}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle-1,T\rangle\obackslash(\uhp\times V),

\displaystyle\iota_{2}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{2}

\displaystyle=C_{2}\obackslash(\mathbf{D}^{\times}\times V)

Thus,
\iota_{1}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{1}
is nothing but the trivial bundle
\uhp\times V
endowed with the restriction of the action of
\Gamma(1)
to
\langle-1,T\rangle
, and
\iota_{2}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{2}
is nothing but
\mathbf{D}^{\times}\times V
with the restricted action of
C_{2}
. Then
\phi
is a map of the form

\phi(\tau,v)=(q,\phi_{0}(\tau,v))

that intertwines these two actions.

It turns out that the action of
\Gamma
on
V
that intervenes below leads to a trivial action of
\langle-1,T\rangle
, and similarly a trivial action of
C_{2}
on
V
. Therefore, there is a canonical way to glue these bundles, by taking
\phi_{0}(\tau,v)=v
. In this way one obtains a concrete description of QVOA bundles on compact modular curves. When one changes coordinates from
\uhp
to the coordinates provided by the
q
-disc

\mathbf{D}
, the square-bracket formalism of
[
27
]
(see also
[
6
,
21
]
) arises naturally via Huang’s change of coordinates formula, see Lemma 5.4.6 of
[
11
]
. Hence, in this sense, the bundle associated to
V
on the noncompact curve

is completed to a bundle on the compact curve
X(1)
by gluing in
V
, under the square-bracket formalism, at the cusp. This viewpoint will not play a strong rôle in what follows, as we will define modular forms using a more “trivial” notion of extension to the cusp, via
q
-expansions. We will point out how this trivial notion of holomorphy at the cusp differs from the natural one arising from coordinate- invariance of the VOA bundle construction.

3.
VOAs on modular curves

3.1.
The main construction

The goal here is to give a similarly concrete description of how a quasi-vertex operator algebra
V
is described geometrically as a family of vector bundles on all modular curves. For simplicity we shall restrict to level one. This will allow us to give a simplified presentation that involves, in particular, a unique cusp.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
denote the bundle associated to
V
on
Y(1)
as described in
[
11
]
. We wish to describe this as in the previous section, as a trivial bundle on
\uhp
endowed with an action of
\Gamma
such that

\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\Gamma\obackslash(\uhp\times V).

That is, to begin with, we consider the trivial bundle
\uhp\times V
, but we must factor out the action of the modular group that arises by change of coordinates under linear fractional transformations.

3.1.1.
The torsor of local coordinates

Following
[
11
]
, let
\mathcal{O}
denote a power series ring over
\mathbf{C}
in one variable, and let
\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}
denote its group of automorphisms. As a set we identify

\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\{\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}X^{n}\mid a_{1}\neq 0\right\},

and multiplication is defined as
(f*g)(X)=g(f(X))
.

We begin with the covering torsor

\Aut_{\uhp}\to\uhp

of local coordinates. Let
z
denote the usual variable on
\mathbf{C}
, which is a local coordinate at
z=0
in
\mathbf{C}
. Restrict this global variable to
\uhp
. Notice that
z
is not a local coordinate at any point of
\uhp
. However, at a point
\tau\in\uhp
, our global variable
z
on
\uhp
yields the local coordinate
X=z-\tau
at
\tau
. With this notation we trivialize the torsor of local coordinates via the identification

\Aut_{\uhp}=\uhp\times\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}.

We now describe an action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
on this bundle. Given

f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}(z-\tau)^{n}

a local coordinate at
\tau
in terms of the global variable
z
, then

f(\gamma^{-1}z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}(\gamma^{-1}z-\tau)^{n}

is a local coordinate at
\gamma\tau
. This endows
\Aut_{\uhp}
with a natural left action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
that lifts the action on
\uhp
:

\gamma(\tau,f(z))\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\gamma\tau,f(\gamma^{-1}z)).

This provides the extra data needed to descend to a bundle on
Y(1)
.

Remark 3.1
.

The added flexibility of being able to change coordinates also by the matrices in
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
that are not in the modular group will be used below to define Hecke operators.

To make this more concrete, we will identify all fibers of
\Aut_{\uhp}
with one another by setting
X=z-\tau
. Now set

\Aut_{Y(1)}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\uhp\times\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}.

This bundle is endowed with the following left action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
, which is the transport of structure of the action above under this reparameterization of the fibers:

\gamma(\tau,f(X))=(\gamma\tau,f(\gamma^{-1}(X+\gamma\tau)-\tau)).

We can write the new local coordinate intervening in the action above as a power series as follows: in the fiber above
\gamma\tau
, we have the relation
X=z-\gamma\tau
, and so if
c\neq 0
we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{-1}(X+\gamma\tau)-\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{d(X+\gamma\tau)-b}{-c(X+\gamma\tau)+a}-\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det\gamma}\frac{(c\tau+d)^{2}X}{1-\frac{c(c\tau+d)}{\det\gamma}X}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}c^{n-1}(c\tau+d)^{n+1}\det\gamma^{-n}X^{n}

Recalling
1
the
1
-cocycle
j(\alpha,\tau)
for
\alpha=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
, one finds that

(2)

\gamma^{-1}(X+\gamma\tau)-\tau=\sum_{n\geq 1}c^{n-1}j(\gamma,\tau)^{n+1}\det\gamma^{-n}X^{n}.

3.1.2.
The VOA bundle

Now consider the fiber product

\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}=(\uhp\times\Aut_{\mathcal{O}})\times_{\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}}V,

where
\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}
acts on
V
as in
[
11
]
. Concretely,

\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}=\left\{(\tau,f(X),v)\in\uhp\times\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}\times V\right\}/(\tau,f(X)*g(X),v)\sim(\tau,f(X),R(g)v).

In particular, in
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
we always have

(\tau,f(X),v)=(\tau,X,R(f)v)

where
R(f)
denotes the left action of
f\in\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}
on the quasi-vertex operator algebra
V
. That means that every fiber of
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
over
\tau\in\uhp
can be identified with
V
using the representatives
(\tau,X,v)
for
v\in V
. In this way we obtain a trivialization

\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}\cong\uhp\times V.

Remark 3.2
.

Recall from
[
11
]
that all that is needed to define
R(f)
are the Virasoro operators
L(n)
for
n\geq 0
acting on
V
. In fact, we will see below that in this simple case of changes of coordinates by linear fractional transformations, only
L(0)
and
L(1)
play a rôle, so that it is really an action on
V
arising from a subalgebra of the canonical
\sl 2
-action on
V
. Later, when we describe a connection on
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
, the operator
L(-1)
will also play a rôle, leading to the full action of
\sl 2
. This circumstance is the main reason that we take
V
to be a QVOA rather than a VOA.

Even though
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
can be trivialized as above, its more complicated expression as a fiber product is essential for endowing
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
with an action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
that lifts the fractional linear transformation on
\uhp
. Then, restricting this action to the modular group allows us to descend
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
to a bundle on
Y(1)
. In this way we arrive at the following definition:

Definition 3.3
.

The bundle
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
over the modular curve associated to the QVOA
V
is defined as the quotient

\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\Gamma\obackslash(\uhp\times V)

under the action

\gamma(\tau,v)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\gamma\tau,R(\gamma^{-1}(X+\gamma\tau)-\tau)v).

In order to find descriptions for various operators
R(\rho)
for
\rho(X)\in\Aut\mathcal{O}
that intervene below, we prepare with a basic well-known lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\rho(X)=\sum_{m\geq 1}\alpha^{m-1}X^{m}
, then

\rho(X)=\exp\left(\alpha X^{2}\partial_{X}\right)X.

Proof.

We verify this directly as follows: first observe by induction that

(X^{2}\partial_{X})^{m}X=m!X^{m+1}.

It then follows easily that

\exp\left(\alpha X^{2}\partial_{X}\right)X=\sum_{m\geq 0}\frac{\alpha^{m}}{m!}(X^{2}\partial_{X})^{m}X=\sum_{m\geq 0}\alpha^{m}X^{m+1}=\rho(X),

as desired.
∎

Our next goal is to understand the operator
R(\gamma^{-1}(X+\gamma\tau)-\tau)
on the QVOA
V
. We will do this for
\gamma\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
, not just for matrices in the modular group, with a view towards defining Hecke operators below. Given equation (
2
) above, following
[
11
]
, we must solve for scalars
v_{i}
so that

\sum_{n\geq 1}c^{n-1}j(\gamma,\tau)^{n+1}\det(\gamma)^{-n}X^{n}=\exp\left(\sum_{i\geq 1}v_{i}X^{i+1}\partial_{X}\right)v_{0}X.

Lemma
3.4
implies that

\sum_{n\geq 1}c^{n-1}j(\gamma,\tau)^{n+1}\det\gamma^{-n}X^{n}=\exp\left(\det\gamma^{-1}cj(\gamma,\tau)X^{2}\partial_{X}\right)j(\gamma,\tau)^{2}\det\gamma^{-1}X.

This yields the following concrete description of the action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
via linear fractional transformations on the bundle
\uhp\times V
:

(3)

\gamma(\tau,v)=(\gamma\tau,e^{-\det\gamma^{-1}cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}(\det\gamma)^{L(0)}v).

3.1.3.
An elementary treatment of (
3
)

Since we have followed the prescription of
[
11
]
, (
3
) necessarily yields a left group action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
. Here we will provide a separate and elementary verification of this fact for readers unfamiliar with the preceding material.

Elementary proof that (
3
) is a left group action.

Let

\displaystyle\alpha\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \beta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r&s\\
t&u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

be two matrices in
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
. Recalling the meaning of
X
in Section
1.1
, a brute-force calculation shows that

Lemma 3.5
.

We have

X(\alpha)|_{2}\beta=X(\alpha\beta)-X(\beta).

\hfill\Box

Turning to the proof of (
3
), let
v\in V_{k}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\beta(\tau,v)

\displaystyle=(\beta\tau,e^{-\det\beta^{-1}tj(\beta,\tau)L(1)}j(\beta,\tau)^{-2L(0)}(\det\beta)^{L(0)}v)

\displaystyle=(\beta\tau,e^{-\det\beta^{-1}tj(\beta,\tau)L(1)}j(\beta,\tau)^{-2k}\det\beta^{k}v)

\displaystyle=\left(\beta\tau,\det\beta^{k}j(\beta,\tau)^{-2k}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-t)^{n}j(\beta,\tau)^{n}}{\det\beta^{n}\cdot n!}L(1)^{n}v\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle\alpha(\beta(\tau,v))

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha(\beta\tau),e^{-c\det\alpha^{-1}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)L(1)}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)^{-2L(0)}(\det\alpha)^{L(0)}\det\beta^{k}j(\beta,\tau)^{-2k}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-t)^{n}j(\beta,\tau)^{n}}{\det\beta^{n}\cdot n!}L(1)^{n}v\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha\beta\tau,e^{-c\det\alpha^{-1}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)L(1)}\det(\alpha\beta)^{k}j(\beta,\tau)^{-2k}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)^{-2k}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-t)^{n}j(\beta,\tau)^{n}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)^{2n}}{\det(\alpha\beta)^{n}\cdot n!}L(1)^{n}v\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha\beta\tau,\det(\alpha\beta)^{k}j(\alpha\beta,\tau)^{-2k}e^{-c\det\alpha^{-1}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)L(1)}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-t)^{n}j(\alpha\beta,\tau)^{n}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)^{n}}{\det(\alpha\beta)^{n}\cdot n!}L(1)^{n}v\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha\beta\tau,\det(\alpha\beta)^{k}j(\alpha\beta,\tau)^{-2k}e^{-(c+tj(\alpha\beta,\tau)\det\beta^{-1})\det\alpha^{-1}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)L(1)}v\right)

On the other hand

\displaystyle(\alpha\beta)(\tau,v)

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha\beta\tau,e^{-(\det\alpha\beta)^{-1}(cr+dt)j(\alpha\beta,\tau)L(1)}j(\alpha\beta,\tau)^{-2L(0)}(\det\alpha\beta)^{L(0)}v\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\alpha\beta\tau,\det(\alpha\beta)^{k}j(\alpha\beta,\tau)^{-2k}e^{-\det(\alpha\beta)^{-1}(cr+dt)j(\alpha\beta,\tau)L(1)}v\right)

Thus, we see that (
3
) defines an action as long as the following identity holds for all
\alpha,\beta\in\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
:

(\det\alpha\beta)^{-1}(cr+dt)j(\alpha\beta,\tau)=(c+tj(\alpha\beta,\tau)\det\beta^{-1}j(\alpha,\beta\tau)\det\alpha^{-1},

and further manipulation shows that this is equivalent to the identity of Lemma

3.5
. Therefore, this gives an elementary verification that (
3
) indeed defines a left action of
\GL_{2}^{+}(\mathbf{R})
on
\uhp\times V
.
∎

3.2.
The one-cocycle
K(\gamma,\tau)

By definition, sections of the VOA-bundle
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
are
V
-valued modular forms that transform under

\Gamma
according to the action (
3
). That is, sections
f
are functions
f\colon\uhp\to V
satisfying

f(\gamma\tau)=e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}f(\tau)

for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Slightly more generally, we are led to introduce the following notation:

Definition 3.6
.

For
\tau\in\uhp
and
\gamma\in GL_{2}(\mathbf{R})^{+}
define the
\Endo(V)
-valued function

K(\gamma,\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=e^{-\det\gamma^{-1}cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}(\det\gamma)^{L(0)}.

Note that since
L(1)
and
L(0)
do not commute, the order of the factors in this expression is important.

Lemma 3.7
.

K
is a
1
-cocycle on
\GL_{2}(\mathbf{R})^{+}
. That is, for
\alpha,\beta\in\GL_{2}(\mathbf{R})^{+}
we have

K(\alpha\beta,\tau)=K(\alpha,\beta\tau)K(\beta,\tau)

Proof.

This is equivalent to the statement that (
3
) is a group action.
∎

K(\gamma,\tau)
takes a simpler form for certain choices of
\gamma
. For example if

\gamma=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
0&a\end{smallmatrix}\right)
then
K(\gamma,\tau)=\Id_{V}
. We shall be particularly concerned with the case
\gamma\in\Gamma
in which case, of course, Definition
3.6
reads

K(\gamma,\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}\quad\quad(\gamma\in\Gamma).

Thanks to Lemma
3.7
we get a right action
f||_{k}\gamma
of
\GL_{2}(\mathbf{R})^{+}
on sections
f
as in Section
1.1
. We repeat the definition here because of its centrality to our project:

(4)

\displaystyle f||_{k}\gamma(\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-k}(\det\gamma)^{k/2}K(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}f(\gamma\tau).

We call a
\Gamma
-invariant of this action a
weak
V
-valued modular form of weight
k
, where the adjective ”weak” means that we place no analytic conditions on
f(\tau)
. Here, we just point out, as a consequence of previous comments, that under suitable analytic conditions, such a
V
-valued form
f
will have a
q
-expansion because it is periodic:

(5)

\displaystyle f(\tau)=(f||_{k}\pm T)(\tau)=f(\tau+1).

3.3.
Extension to the compactified curve

In this subsection we extend the construction of
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
to the compact modular curve. Notice that the action defined in (
3
) satisfies
(\pm T)(\tau,v)=(\tau+1,v)
. Therefore, as discussed at the end of Section
2.2
, there is a canonical extension
\mathcal{V}_{X(1)}
of
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
to the compact modular curve
X(1)
.

Let
\iota_{1}
and
\iota_{2}
be the covering maps associated to
\mathcal{V}_{X(1)}
as in Section
2.2
. By abuse of notation we will write
\iota_{1}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{X(1)}
and
\iota_{2}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{X(1)}
for the pullbacks of the parts of
\mathcal{V}_{X(1)}
over
Y(1)
and
C_{2}\obackslash\mathbf{D}
, respectively. Given
v\in V
, the function
f(\tau)=(\tau,v)
is a map
\uhp\to\uhp\times V
, and it is equivariant for the action of
\pm T
:

f(\pm T\tau)=(\tau+1,v)=\pm T(\tau,v)=\pm Tf(\tau).

Therefore, every element
v\in V
defines a section
(\tau,v)
of
\iota_{1}^{*}\mathcal{V}_{X(1)}
.

Our goal now is to re-express sections of
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
in a neighborhood of the cusp, and thereby define
q
-expansions. To do this, let us first work on
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
, which recall is a quotient of
\uhp\times\Aut_{\mathcal{O}}\times V
. In the coordinates on
\mathbf{D}^{\times}
, the point
\tau
maps to
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
. Choose another global coordinate
q_{z}=e^{2\pi iz}
on
\mathbf{D}^{\times}
, so that a local coordinate at
q
is
q_{z}-q
. This local coordinate pulls back under the exponential map to a new coordinate

e^{2\pi iz}-e^{2\pi i\tau}=q^{-1}(e^{2\pi iX}-1)

on
\uhp
at
\tau
, since
X=z-\tau
on
\uhp
. Therefore, under the change of coordinates provided by the map
q
, we have

(\tau,q^{-1}(e^{2\pi iX}-1),v)\in\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}\mapsto(q,q_{z}-q,v)\in\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{D}^{\times}}.

In
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
we have the identification

(\tau,q^{-1}(e^{2\pi iX}-1),v)=(\tau,X,R(q^{-1}(e^{2\pi iX}-1))v).

Therefore, near the cusp, the section
(\tau,v)
of
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
looks like
(q,R(q^{-1}(e^{2\pi iX}-1))^{-1}v)
on the punctured
q
-disc
\mathbf{D}^{\times}
. Note that one has

R(q^{-1}(e^{2\pi iX}-1))^{-1}v=q^{-L(0)}R(e^{2\pi iX}-1)^{-1}v=q^{-L(0)}R\left(\tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\log(X+1)\right)v.

Thus, the section
(\tau,v)
near the cusp of
\uhp
corresponds to
(q,q^{-L(0)}R(\tfrac{1}{2\pi i}\log(X+1))v)
near
q=0
in the
q
-disc
\mathbf{D}^{\times}
. In particular, in the canonical coordinate invariant extension of
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
to the cusp, constant sections
(\tau,v)
of
\mathcal{V}_{\uhp}
on
\uhp
will acquire a finite order pole at
q=0
due to the factor of
q^{-L(0)}
. This is similar to the fact that Eisenstein series, in the classical scalar setting of modular forms, technically have poles at the cusp when interpreted geometrically as sections of the canonical bundle on the modular curve.

Thus, just as in the usual modular theory, we define
q
-expansions in usual the naive way.

Definition 3.8
.

A holomorphic section
f
of
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
is said to be
holomorphic at the cusp
if it admits a holomorphic
q
-expansion

f(q)\in V[\![q]\!].

Meromorphy at the cusp is defined similarly.

Remark 3.9
.

Note that since the bundle
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
is constructed as an inductive limit of finite rank bundles, the
q
-expansion will lie in some filtered subspace
F_{N}V[\![q]\!]
for some
N\geq 0
.

3.4.
VOA-valued modular forms

All of this prompts the following. Fix a quasi-vertex operator algebra

.

A weakly holomorphic

We now introduce spaces of

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}M_{k}(V).

4.
The connection

The bundle
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
is endowed with a connection

\nabla\colon\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}\to\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{Y(1)}

defined locally on sections by the formula
\nabla=d+L(-1)\otimes d\tau
. The text
[

]
defers the proof that this is a connection until quite late in the exposition, after the ground has been prepared to give a simpler conceptual argument. Therefore, we provide an elementary proof of this fact in Section

4.1.
Proof that
\nabla
is a connection

To verify this, let us first determine what transformation law is satisfied by
df
. In order to lay the groundwork for defining an analog of the modular derivative in higher weights, we allow
f
to be a
V
-valued modular form of possibly nonzero weight
k
. Therefore we assume that
f
satisfies the transformation law

(6)

f(\gamma\tau)=e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2L(0)}f(\tau).

Differentiating the left hand side of equation (
6
) yields:

d(f(\gamma\tau))=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2}df(\gamma\tau).

On the other hand, differentiating the right hand side of equation (
6

\displaystyle-c^{2}L(1)e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}f(\tau)+ce^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}(k-2L(0))j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2L(0)-1}f(\tau)+

\displaystyle e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}df(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}(-c^{2}L(1)j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2L(0)}f(\tau)+c(k-2L(0))j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2L(0)-1}f(\tau)+j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2L(0)}df(\tau))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{c(k-2L(0))}{j(\gamma,\tau)}-c^{2}L(1)\right)f(\gamma\tau)+e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2L(0)}df(\tau)

Thus, we find that

satisfies the transformation law

\displaystyle df(\gamma\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(cj(\gamma,\tau)(k-2L(0))-(cj(\gamma,\tau))^{2}L(1)\right)f(\gamma\tau)+e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k+2-2L(0)}df(\tau)

Therefore,

\displaystyle(\nabla f)(\gamma\tau)=

\displaystyle\left(cj(\gamma,\tau)(k-2L(0))-(cj(\gamma,\tau))^{2}L(1)+L(-1)\right)f(\gamma\tau)+

\displaystyle\quad e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k+2-2L(0)}df(\tau)

Recall that
E_{2}(\tau)

satisfies the transformation law

(7)

E_{2}(\gamma\tau)=j(\gamma,\tau)^{2}E_{2}(\tau)+\frac{12cj(\gamma,\tau)}{2\pi i}.

Definition 4.1
.

The
k
th modular derivative with respect to the QVOA
V
is the operator
\nabla_{k}
that acts on functions
f\colon\uhp\to V
via the formula

\nabla_{k}f=\nabla f-\frac{2\pi ik}{12}E_{2}f.

If
f
satisfies the transformation law of equation (
6
), then we find from the computations above, and the transformation law for
E_{2}
, that

\displaystyle(\nabla_{k}f)(\gamma\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(L(-1)-2cj(\gamma,\tau)L(0)-(cj(\gamma,\tau))^{2}L(1)\right)f(\gamma\tau)+

\displaystyle\quad e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k+2-2L(0)}df(\tau)-\frac{2\pi ik}{12}j(\gamma,\tau)^{2}E_{2}(\tau)f(\gamma\tau)

We want to show that this transforms according to the law

(8)

(\nabla_{k}f)(\gamma\tau)=e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k+2-2L(0)}(\nabla_{k}f)(\tau).

Our computations above have shown that by adding the factor of
E_{2}
in the definition of
\nabla_{k}
, this essentially reduces the proof of (
8
) to weight zero, in which case equation (
8
) simply amounts to the statement that
\nabla
is indeed a connection. Thus, equation (
8
) follows by the general theory outlined in
[
11
]
.

Here is a direct proof of (
8
): by the computations above, establishing (
8
) is tantamount to establishing the following identity:

\displaystyle L(-1)=

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\tau)^{2L(0)-2}e^{cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}L(-1)e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}

\displaystyle-c^{2}j(\gamma,\tau)^{2L(0)-2}L(1)j(\gamma,\tau)^{2-2L(0)}-2cj(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}L(0)

We will verify this one graded piece at a time.

Let
f_{N}\in V_{N}
, so that by linearity, to establish equation (
8
), it suffices to establish the following identity:

(9)

\displaystyle L(-1)f_{N}=

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\tau)^{2L(0)-2N-2}e^{cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}L(-1)e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}f_{N}-c^{2}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2}L(1)f_{N}

\displaystyle-2Ncj(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}f_{N}

The left hand side of equation (
9
) is equal to
L(-1)f_{N}
in degree
N+1
, and it is zero in all other degrees. The right hand side of equation (
9
) vanishes in degrees greater than
N+1
. The degree
N+1
part of the right hand side of equation (
9
) is

j(\gamma,\tau)^{2L(0)-2N-2}L(-1)f_{N},

and this equals
L(-1)f_{N}
since this is homogeneous of degree
N+1
. Therefore, equation (
9
) is true in homogeneous degrees
\geq N+1
.

It remains to prove that the degree
n
terms of the right hand side of equation (
9
) vanish when
n\leq N
. The degree
N
term of the right hand side of (
9
) is equal to

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\tau)^{2L(0)-2N-2}cj(\gamma,\tau)(L(1)L(-1)-L(-1)L(1))f_{N}-2Ncj(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}f_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}c((L(1)L(-1)-L(-1)L(1))-2N)f_{N}

This vanishes thanks to the commutator relation
[L(1),L(-1)]=2L(0)
in the Virasoro algebra, since
f_{N}
is homogeneous of degree
N
. Therefore, this confirms our identity in degree
N
.

Now compute the degree
N-1
term of the right hand side of (
9
). This time we obtain

c^{2}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2}(\tfrac{1}{2}L(-1)L(1)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}L(1)^{2}L(-1)-L(1)L(-1)L(1))f_{N}-c^{2}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2}L(1)f_{N}

The Virasoro relations imply that

\tfrac{1}{2}L(-1)L(1)^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}L(1)^{2}L(-1)-L(1)L(-1)L(1)=L_{1},

so that this does cancel to zero, and this confirms equation (
9
) in degree
N-1
.

Finally, in degree
N-n<n-1
(so
2\leq n\leq N
), the desired vanishing follows from the vanishing of the following operator in the Virasoro algebra:

\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-j}\frac{1}{j!(n-j)!}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\binom{n}{j}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}.

Again, we provide details here for completeness. When
n=2
we must show that the following expression vanishes:

\displaystyle L(-1)L(1)^{2}-2L(1)L(-1)L(1)+L(1)^{2}L(-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(L(1)L(-1)-2L(0))L(1)-2L(1)L(-1)L(1)+L(1)(L(-1)L(1)+2L(0))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

So our vanishing result holds when
n=2
. In general for
n\geq 3
we write:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\binom{n}{j}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}L(1)^{n}L(-1)+L(-1)L(1)^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{n}{j}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}L(1)^{n}L(-1)+L(-1)L(1)^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}\left(\binom{n-1}{j-1}+\binom{n-1}{j}\right)L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}L(1)^{n}L(-1)+L(-1)L(1)^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{n-1}{j-1}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{n-1}{j}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-L(1)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{j}\binom{n-1}{j-1}L(1)^{j-1}L(-1)L(1)^{n-j}\right)+

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{j}\binom{n-1}{j}L(1)^{j}L(-1)L(1)^{n-1-j}\right)L(1)

Each of the sums in the last line above vanish by induction, and this concludes the proof of equation (
9
). This yields both equation (
8
), and that
\nabla=d+L(-1)\otimes d\tau
defines a connection on
\mathcal{V}_{Y(1)}
.

5.
QVOA-valued quasi-modular forms

5.1.
Some motivation

Fix a QVOA
V=(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\rho)
. In this Subsection we consider some rudimentary aspects of the space
M(V)
of
V
-valued modular forms. These simple ideas inform later Sections and presage some of the main results. Recall the meaning of
X=X(\gamma,\tau)
from
Subsection
1
.

Example 5.1
.

Begin with a state
v\in V_{m}
of conformal degree
m
. We first ask if the constant map

f:\uhp\rightarrow V_{m},\quad\quad\tau\mapsto v

belongs to
M(V)
. Certainly
f
is holomorphic. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
we compute

\displaystyle f||_{2m}\gamma(\tau)=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2m}K(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}f(\tau)

\displaystyle=j(\gamma,\tau)^{2L(0)-2m}e^{cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}v

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{c^{n}j(\gamma,\tau)^{n-2m+2(m-n)}}{n!}L(1)^{n}v

(10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}X^{n}L(1)^{n}v.

For the last equality we used
L(1)^{n}v\in V_{m-n}
. From this we see that
f\in M_{2m}(V)

if, and only if,
L(1)v=0
, i.e.,
v
is a
quasi-primary state
. Note that (
5.1
) is redolent of the definition of a quasi-modular form of weight
2m
. Indeed, setting
Q_{n}(f):=\frac{1}{n!}L(1)^{n}f
yields
(at least formally) the definition of quasi-modular form, albeit one taking values in
V
rather than
\mathbf{C}
.

Before turning to our second
illustrative example, consider an arbitrary pair
of elements
f,g\in M(V)
. For an integer
n
we can define the
n^{th}
product
f(n)g
in a pointwise manner:

(11)

(f(n)g)(\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=f(\tau)(n)g(\tau).

In this way we have equipped
M(V)

with
n^{th}
products for all
n
so it is natural to ask if
M(V)
is itself some sort of vertex algebra. The answer is “no” for the simple reason that
M(V)
is
not closed
with respect to these products.

Example 5.2
.

Consider a pair of constant maps

f:\uhp\rightarrow u,\quad\quad g:\uhp\rightarrow v

and assume that
u
and
v
are both quasiprimary states in
V
, whereas
u(n)v
is
not
. This is a commonplace occurrence: for example in the rank
1
Heisenberg VOA the canonical weight
1
state
h
is quasi-primary whereas
h(-2)h
is not. Then we just saw in the previous example that
f
and
g
both belong to
M(V)
. On the other hand since
u(n)v
is assumed to be not quasi-primary then the same example shows that
f(n)g
does
not
belong to
M(V)
. Thus, in general
M(V)
is not closed under the natural pointwise modes of equation (
11
).

These examples motivate us to introduce quasi-modular forms.

5.2.
Definition and first properties of
V
-valued quasi-modular forms

Apropos of the preceding discussion we begin with the following basic definition.

Definition 5.3
.

A
V
-valued meromorphic
quasi-modular form
of weight
k
and depth at most
s
is a holomorphic map

f\colon\uhp\to F_{N}V

for some
N
, such that

f||_{k}\gamma(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{s}X^{n}Q_{n}(f)

for some holomorphic maps
Q_{n}(f)\colon\uhp\to F_{N}V
, and such that
f
is meromorphic at the cusp. As usual, we say that
f
is holomorphic if its
q
-expansion at the cusp has no pole.

We make some standard observations in this set-up. By considering the cases
\gamma=\pm T
(when
K(\gamma,\tau)=\Id
) we find that
f(\tau+1)=f(\tau)
so that
f
has a
q
-expansion and the Definition has meaning. Furthermore
Q_{0}(f)=f
and

f=0
if
k
is odd.

Example 5.4
.

Let
v\in V_{m}
and let
f:\uhp\rightarrow v
be the constant function as in the Example
5.1
. Then our computations (
5.1
) show that

f||_{2m}\gamma(\tau)=e^{XL(1)}v.

Thus,
f
is a holomorphic
V
-valued quasi-modular form of weight
2m
and its depth is the largest integer
s
such that
L(1)^{s}v\neq 0
. Note that
s
exists because
L(1)
is a lowering operator and
V_{n}=0
for
n\ll 0
.

Let
Q_{k}(V)
be the space of holomorphic
V
-valued quasi-modular forms of weight
k
and set
Q(V)=\bigoplus_{k\geq 0}Q_{2k}(V)
. Then
Q(V)
possesses an increasing filtration by depth.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
V
be any QVOA and regard
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
as a
2\mathbf{Z}
-graded linear space with the tensor product grading. Then there is a canonical identification of
2\mathbf{Z}
-graded
Q
-modules

\displaystyle\iota:Q\otimes V^{(2)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}Q(V),

\displaystyle f(\tau)\otimes v

\displaystyle\mapsto f(\tau)v.

Here,
V^{(2)}
is the doubled VOA, cf. Subsection
1.1
and Appendix
B
.

Proof.

Choose any
f(\tau)\in Q_{2k}
and
v\in V_{2m}^{(2)}=V_{m}
. First we claim that

f(\tau)v\in Q_{2(k+m)}(V)
. The proof is an elaboration of Example
5.1
. Thus there are holomorphic functions
Q_{n}(f)
such that

f|_{2k}\gamma(\tau)=\sum_{n=0}^{s}X^{n}Q_{n}(f),

and we compute

\displaystyle(f(\tau)v)||_{2k+2m}\gamma(\tau)

\displaystyle=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2k-2m+2L(0)}e^{cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}f(\gamma\tau)v

\displaystyle=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2k-2m+2L(0)}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{c^{r}j(\gamma,\tau)^{r}}{r!}L(1)^{r}f(\gamma\tau)v

\displaystyle=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2k-2r}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{c^{r}j(\gamma,\tau)^{r}}{r!}L(1)^{r}f(\gamma\tau)v

\displaystyle=j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2k}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{X^{r}}{r!}L(1)^{r}f(\gamma\tau)v

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{X^{r}}{r!}L(1)^{r}f|_{2k}(\tau)v

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{n=0}^{s}\frac{X^{r+n}}{r!}Q_{n}(f)L(1)^{r}v,

and this proves the claim. The map
\iota
is clearly injective, and our calculation shows that it is a graded map of
Q
-modules. Thus it remains to establish surjectivity.

With this in mind, fix
g\in Q_{2k}(V)
. Thus there are holomorphic functions

Q_{d}(g):\uhp\rightarrow F_{N}V
for some
N
satisfying

g||_{2k}\gamma(\tau)=\sum_{d=0}^{s}X^{d}Q_{d}(g).

Let
(v_{ij})_{j=1}^{n_{i}}
be a basis for
V_{i}
for each
i
, so that
g=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}v_{ij}
for
scalar-valued functions
g_{ij}
, and write

Q_{d}(g)=\sum_{i\leq N}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}Q_{d}(g)_{ij}v_{ij}.

for scalar-valued functions
Q_{d}(g)_{ij}
. We must show that each
g_{ij}
is quasi-modular. Hence, let us compute:

\displaystyle g(\gamma\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{s}X^{d}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}Q_{d}(g)_{ij}(\tau)j(\gamma,\tau)^{k}e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}v_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{s}X^{d}\sum_{i=0}^{N}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}Q_{d}(g)_{ij}(\tau)e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)L(1)}v_{ij}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{d=0}^{s}X^{d}j(\gamma,\tau)^{k-2N}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{N}}Q_{d}(g)_{Nj}(\tau)v_{Nj}\pmod{F_{N-1}V}

It follows that for each
j
we have

g_{Nj}|_{2N-k}\gamma(\tau)=\sum_{d=0}^{s}X^{d}Q_{d}(g)_{Nj}(\tau).

That is, the highest conformal degree coordinates
g_{Nj}
of
g
are quasi-modular forms of weight
2N-k
. Hence if we set
g=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n}}f_{Nj}v_{Nj}
, we then have
g\in Q\otimes V
, and
f-g\in Q(V)
has conformal degree
\leq N-1
. Now we may proceed inductively to complete the proof of the Theorem.
∎

5.3.
Modes of modular forms

Suppose that
f
and
g
are two
V
-valued quasimodular forms for some VOA
V
, say of weights
k
and
\ell
respectively. It follows by Proposition
5.5
that
f
and
g
are both elements of
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
, and hence the pointwise mode

(f(m)g)(\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=f(\tau)(m)g(\tau)

gives a well-defined element of
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
, of weight
k+\ell-2m-2
. We have already explained that if one assumes further that
f
and
g
are both modular, it is not in general true that
f(m)g
is modular, only quasi-modular.

When
m\geq-1
, one can use a transformation formula of Huang
[
15
,
11
]
to add more precision to the quasi-modular transformation properties of
f(m)g
. We first consider a two-variable mode

f(\tau_{1})(m)g(\tau_{2})

for two independent and distinct points
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\uhp
. We restrict to modes
m\geq-1
so that we can give a uniform proof of the next result without cases.

Proposition 5.6
.

If
f
and
g
are VOA-valued modular forms, of weights
k
and
\ell
respectively, then for
m\geq-1
, the
m
th mode
f(\tau_{1})(m)g(\tau_{2})
satisfies the transformation law

(12)

\displaystyle f(\gamma\tau_{1})(m)g(\gamma\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\tau_{1})^{k}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{\ell-2m-2}K(\gamma,\tau_{2})\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{n+m+1}{m+1}(-X(\gamma,\tau_{2}))^{2}f(\tau_{1})(n+m)g(\tau_{2})\right).

where
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\uhp
are distinct points and
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

Proof.

First let
A\in V
be any state. If we view
\tau_{2}
as being fixed and
\tau_{1}
as varying, then we have a local coordinate
\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}
at
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
. For
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we then have two coordinates at
\tau_{1}=\gamma\tau_{2}
: one is
\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2}
, and the other is
\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}
. These coordinates differ by a change of variable, and we shall prove our theorem by comparing Huang’s transformation formula with respect to these two local coordinates. Recall that his formula says the following: if
z=\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2}
then

Y(A,z)=R(\rho)Y(R(\rho_{z})^{-1}A,\rho(z))R(\rho)^{-1}

where
\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}=\rho(z)
. Recall that we saw earlier that

\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}c^{n-1}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{n+1}(\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2})^{n}.

and hence

R(\rho)=e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau_{2})L(1)}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-2L(0)}.

We also need to determine
R(\rho_{z})
where
z=\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2}
. Since we can write

\rho(X)=\gamma^{-1}(X+\gamma\tau_{2})-\tau_{2},

we find that

\displaystyle\rho_{z}(X)

\displaystyle=\rho(X+z)-\rho(z)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1}(X+z+\gamma\tau_{2})-\tau_{2}-(\gamma^{-1}(z+\gamma\tau_{2})-\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1}(X+\tau_{1})-\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}

Therefore, by a previous computation setting
\tau=\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}
, we have

\rho_{z}(X)=\sum_{n\geq 1}c^{n-1}j(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{n+1}X^{n}.

It follows that

R(\rho_{z})=e^{-cj(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})}j(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{-2L(0)}.

Thus, in terms of our notation
K(\gamma,\tau)=e^{-cj(\gamma,\tau)}j(\gamma,\tau)^{-2L(0)}
, we have

\displaystyle R(\rho)

\displaystyle=K(\gamma,\tau_{2}),

\displaystyle R(\rho_{z})

\displaystyle=K(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}).

Putting all this together, Huang’s formula is the identity:

(13)

Y(A,\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2})=K(\gamma,\tau_{2})Y(K(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{-1}A,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})K(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-1}.

This holds for
\tau_{1}\neq\gamma\tau_{2}
.

Recall that in terms of our new notation, VOA valued modular forms satisfy the transformation law

f(\gamma\tau)=j(\gamma,\tau)^{k}K(\gamma,\tau)f(\tau).

In particular, taking
\tau=\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}
, this says

f(\tau_{1})=j(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{k}K(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})f(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}).

Thus, if we set
A=f(\tau_{1})
in Huang’s formula (
13
), where
f
is a VOA-valued modular form, then we deduce that

(14)

Y(f(\tau_{1}),\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2})=j(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{k}K(\gamma,\tau_{2})Y(f(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}),\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})K(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-1}.

Now, if
g(\tau_{2})
is another VOA-valued modular form, we can multiply on the right by
g(\gamma\tau_{2})
to deduce that

(15)

Y(f(\tau_{1}),\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2})g(\gamma\tau_{2})=j(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{k}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{\ell}K(\gamma,\tau_{2})Y(f(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}),\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})g(\tau_{2}).

We now complete the proof by comparing the principal parts of both sides of equation (
15
). The extra weight-increasing automorphy factors arise by expressing
\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}
on the right hand side of equation (
15
) in terms of
\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2}
.

Indeed, to simplify notation, if we set
B=f(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})
,
E=\Endo(V)
, and
T=\tau_{1}-\gamma\tau_{2}
, then we find that

\displaystyle Y(B,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq-1}B(n)(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1}-\tau_{2})^{-n-1}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq-1}B(n)\left(\sum_{m\geq 1}c^{m-1}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{m+1}T^{m}\right)^{-n-1}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq-1}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-2n-2}B(n)T^{-n-1}\left(\sum_{m\geq 1}\left(cj(\gamma,\tau_{2})T\right)^{m-1}\right)^{-n-1}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq-1}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-2n-2}B(n)T^{-n-1}\left(1-cj(\gamma,\tau_{2})T\right)^{n+1}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq-1}\sum_{m=0}^{n+1}(-1)^{n+1-m}\binom{n+1}{m}c^{n+1-m}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-m-n-1}B(n)T^{-m}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(-c)^{-m}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-m}\left(\sum_{n=m-1}^{\infty}\binom{n+1}{m}(-c)^{n+1}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-n-1}B(n)\right)T^{-m}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{m=-1}^{\infty}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-2m-2}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{n+m+1}{m+1}(-c)^{n}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-n}B(n+m)\right)T^{-m-1}\pmod{TE[\![T]\!]}

Hence, combining this with equation (
15
) we obtain for
m\geq-1
:

(16)

\displaystyle f(\tau_{1})(m)g(\gamma\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})^{k}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{\ell-2m-2}K(\gamma,\tau_{2})\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{n+m+1}{m+1}(-c)^{n}j(\gamma,\tau_{2})^{-n}f(\gamma^{-1}\tau_{1})(n+m)g(\tau_{2})\right).

Finally, if we replace
\tau_{1}
by
\gamma\tau_{1}
, this proves the proposition.
∎

Our next goal is to explain why, for
m\geq-1
, the transformation law above extends to the diagonal
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}
.

Theorem 5.7
.

If
f
and
g
are
V
-valued modular forms of weights
k
and
\ell
, respectively, then for
m\geq-1
, the
m
th mode
f(m)g
defined by

(f(m)g)(\tau)=f(\tau)(m)g(\tau)

is analytic on
\uhp
and satisfies

(17)

\displaystyle(f(m)g)(\gamma\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle j(\gamma,\tau)^{k+\ell-2m-2}K(\gamma,\tau)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+n+1}{m+1}(-X)^{n}f(\tau)(m+n)g(\tau)\right).

for all
\tau\in\uhp
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

In particular, if
f
and
g
have conformal degrees
M
and
N
, respectively, then
f(m)g
satisfies the transformation law of a
V
-valued quasi-modular function of weight
k+\ell-2m-2
, depth at most
M+N-m-1
, and conformal degree at most
M+N-m-1

Proof.

Since
f
and
g
are of finite conformal degree
N
, say, we can regard the
m
th mode intervening in their product as a bilinear map

(m)\colon F_{N}V\times F_{N}V\to F_{N-m}V.

This is a bilinear map between finite dimensional complex vector spaces, and so it is analytic for the canonical complex structure on these spaces. Since
f
and
g
are analytic functions
\uhp\to F_{N}V
, their product likewise defines an analytic map

f\times g\colon\uhp\times\uhp\to F_{N}V^{2},

and the composed map:

f(m)g\colon\uhp\times\uhp\to F_{N-m}V

is therefore complex analytic on all of
\uhp\times\uhp
. The transformation law of Proposition
5.6
, which was only proved there for
\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{2}
, then extends to the diagonal of
\uhp^{2}
by analytic continuation. This proves the first part of the result.

For the statements about depth and conformal degree, observe that the highest degree piece of
f(\tau)(n+m)g(\tau)
lives in conformal degree at most
M+N-m-n-1
. When
n=0
this yields the bound on the conformal degree of
f(m)g
. Then, since
V_{s}=0
for
s<0
, it follows that
f(m+n)g=0
for
n\geq M+N-m
. Hence
f(m+n)g
has depth at most
M+N-m-1
.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

We emphasize that modes
f(m)g
are defined for
all

m\in\mathbf{Z}
if
f,g\in Q(V)
, by Proposition
5.5
, and the import of Theorem
5.7
is the equation (
17
) explaining how the modes
f(m+n)g
intervene in the quasi-modular transformation law of
f(m)g
when
m\geq-1
. We do not require Theorem
5.7
in what follows, which is why we have not given a definitive statement for all integers
m
.

6.
Q(V)
qua QVOA

The purpose of this Section is to describe the algebraic structure of the space
Q(V)
of quasi-modular forms with values in a QVOA
V
. The reader is referred to Appendix
B
for further details concerning QVOAs and related structures.

We have already considered
n^{th}
pointwise products of
V
-valued modular forms (
11
) and
V
-valued quasi-modular forms. In this way
Q(V)
is equipped with
n^{th}
products for all integers
n
, and it is immediate that all of these products are
Q
-linear. Furthermore any identity satisfied by the products in
V
carries over to
Q(V)
. Therefore the Jacobi identity is satisfied in
Q(V)
, and of course the vacuum vector is
1
. Finally, for similar reasons the creation property holds in
Q(V)
and therefore
Q(V)
is a vertex algebra over
Q
. Indeed it is straightforward to see that the map
\iota
of Theorem
5.5

is an isomorphism of vertex algebras over
Q
where here we regard the commutative algebra
Q
as a vertex algebra with vertex operators satisfying

Y(f,z)g\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=fg

and
Q\otimes V
is a tensor product of vertex algebras.

Despite the naturalness of this construction it is
not
so useful for us. The reason is that it ignores all considerations of the gradations on
Q
,
V
and
Q(V)
, and these are fundamental aspects of these structures that we want to keep track of. Put another way, if we reintroduce the same map with a different name:

(18)

\iota^{\prime}:Q\otimes V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}Q(V),\quad\quad f(\tau)\otimes v\mapsto f(\tau)v

then we here want to regard
Q\otimes V
as a tensor product of graded vertex algebras and
\iota^{\prime}
as a morphism of such objects. The difference between
\iota
and
\iota^{\prime}
is that they are morphisms in different categories.

6.1.
Q^{(1/2)}
qua QVOA

We are going to develop the algebraic structure on
Q(V)\cong Q\otimes V^{(2)}
that incorporates both the vertex algebra structure and the grading. It is convenient to begin with
Q
, or rather
Q^{(1/2)}
(cf. Appendix
B.0.9
). Here is the main result of this Subsection.

Theorem 6.1
.

Q^{(1/2)}
is the underlying Fock space for a quasi-vertex operator algebra

(Q^{(1/2)},Y,1,\rho).

(
Y
and
\rho
will be described in the course of the proof.)

Proof.

Recall that
Q^{(1/2)}
is nothing but a regrading of
Q
:

Q^{(1/2)}=\oplus_{k\geq 0}Q^{(1/2)}_{k},\quad\quad Q^{(1/2)}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=Q_{2k},

in particular
Q^{(1/2)}
is a commutative, associative algebra and it admits a graded
derivation

\theta\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=q\frac{d}{dq}
which is a raising operator of weight
1
. Then there is a vertex algebra
(Q^{(1/2)},Y,1,\theta)
for which

(19)

Y(f,z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=e^{z\theta}f=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\theta^{n}f\quad\quad(f\in Q^{(1/2)}).

(Cf. Example 1 of Appendix
B.0.4
.) Thus
n^{th}
products in
Q^{(1/2)}
are such that for all
n\geq 0
we have
f(n)g=0
and

f(-n-1)g\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{1}{n!}(\theta^{n}f)g.

Note that from the last display we see that
f(-2)1=\theta f
, so that
\theta
is the canonical derivation of the vertex algebra.

Next we show that this vertex algebra is
\mathbf{Z}
-graded. The grading is already on display and we have to show that if
f,g
have conformal degrees
k
and
\ell
respectively then
f(n)g
has conformal degree
k+\ell-n-1
. This is obvious if
n\geq 0
. Now take
n\geq 0
and consider

f(-n-1)g
as given above. Since
\theta
raises weights by
1
then

f(-n-1)g
has conformal degree
k+\ell+n=k+\ell-(-n-1)-1
as needed.

Now we come to the main point: the Lie algebra representation

\rho:\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\rightarrow\Endo(Q^{(1/2)}).

This is well-known in the theory of quasi-modular forms where the action of

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
on
Q
is described, for example, in
[
26
]
. In the notation of (loc. cit.),
Q=\oplus_{k}Q_{2k}
admits
a weight
2
lowering operator
\delta=\partial/\partial E_{2}
, a weight
2
raising operator
D=q\frac{d}{dq}
and the Euler operator
E
. The following relations hold:

[D,\delta]=E,\quad[E,D]=2D,\quad[E,\delta]=-2\delta.

If we transfer this action to
Q^{(1/2)}
, replace
D
with
\theta
, retain the notations
\delta,E
for the lowering and Euler operators respectively on
Q^{(1/2)}
(hopefully causing no confusion) we arrive at the definition of
\rho
, viz.,

(20)

\rho:L(1)\mapsto-\delta,\quad L(0)\mapsto E,\quad L(-1)\mapsto\theta

as prescribed in our definition of quasi-vertex operator algebra.

Finally we have to verify the following two axioms for
a\in Q_{k}^{(1/2)}
:

(i)

(Translation covariance).
(L(-1)a)(n)=-na(n-1)=[L(-1),a(n)]
,

(ii)

[L(1),a(n)]=(L(1)a)(n)+(2k-n-2)a(n+1)
,

where we are using the notation of (
20
)
We start with (i). If
n\geq 0
this follows because
a(n)=0
whenever
n\geq 0
. Suppose that
n<0
. Then

(L(-1)a)(n)=(\theta a)(n)=\tfrac{1}{(-n-1)!}\theta^{-n-1}\theta a=-\tfrac{n}{(-n)!}\theta^{n}a=-na(n-1),

which is the first equality in (i). As for the second,

[L(-1),a(n)]b=\theta(a(n)b)-a(n)\theta b=(\theta a(n))b=(L(-1)a)(n)b,

where to get the second equality we used the fact that
\theta
, being the canonical derivation of the vertex algebra
Q^{(1/2)}
, is a derivation of for all products
a(n)b
. This completes the proof of translation covariance.

It remains to establish (ii). As usual the result is obvious if
n\geq 0
, so assume that

n=-m<0
. We have

(21)

\displaystyle[L(1),a(n)]b=\tfrac{-1}{(m-1)!}[\delta,\theta^{m-1}a]b=\tfrac{-1}{(m-1)!}\delta(\theta^{m-1}a)b

where to get the second equality we use the fact that
\delta
is a derivation of
Q
. Next we need the following result which may be proved by an induction:

Lemma 6.2
.

If
a\in Q_{2k}
then

\delta\theta^{m}a=-m(2k+m-1)\theta^{m-1}a+\theta^{m}\delta a.

\hfill\Box

With this Lemma we see from (
21
) that

\displaystyle[L(1),a(n)]

\displaystyle=\tfrac{-1}{(m-1)!}\delta(\theta^{m-1}a)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{-1}{(m-1)!}\left\{((-(m-1)(2k+m-2)\theta^{m-2}a+\theta^{m-1}\delta a\right\}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2k+m-2}{(m-2)!}\theta^{m-2}a-\tfrac{1}{(m-1)!}\theta^{m-1}\delta a

\displaystyle=(2k-n-2)a(n+1)+(L(1)a)(n).

This shows that condition (ii) is indeed satisfied and the proof of the Theorem is complete.
∎

6.2.
Passage to
Q(V)

In this Subsection
Q^{(1/2)}
denotes the QVOA
described in Theorem
6.1
. We can form tensor products of QVOAs, cf. Appendix
B.0.8
. In particular for a QVOA
V
we have the QVOA

\displaystyle Q^{(1/2)}\otimes V

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(Q^{(1/2)},Y,1,\rho)\otimes(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\rho^{\prime})

(22)

\displaystyle=(Q^{(1/2)}\otimes V,Y\otimes Y,1\otimes\mathbf{1},\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}).

If we let (hopefully without introducing further confusion)
L(0)
and
L(\pm 1)
be the usual Virasoro operators on
V
then the corresponding Euler, raising and lowering operators on
Q^{(1/2)}\otimes V
are respectively

\displaystyle E\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes L(0),

(23)

\displaystyle\theta\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes L(-1),

\displaystyle\delta\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes L(1).

One easily sees that
1\otimes V
and
Q^{(1/2)}\otimes\mathbf{1}
are subQVOAs of the tensor product, that the latter is a commutative algebra isomorphic to
Q^{(1/2)}
with respect to the
-1
product, and that the latter is a module over the former again with respect to the
-1
product. Thus the perspective of (
18
) is not lost; it can be recovered from the structure of
Q^{(1/2)}\otimes V
as a QVOA.

Finally we simply note that by doubling the grading of
Q^{(1/2)}\otimes V
we get
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
which we identify with
Q(V)
by way of Theorem
5.5
. Thus we have

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
V
be a QVOA. Then the space
of
V
-valued quasi-modular forms
Q(V)

has the structure of a doubled quasi-vertex operator algebra.
\hfill\Box

7.
Some additional operators on
Q(V)

The tensor product
Q^{(1/2)}\otimes V
, or
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
, admits more than just the ‘diagonal’
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-action used to define the QVOA structure in the previous Section. It admits an action of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. We shall make use of some of these additional operators in the following Sections. They will turn out to be crucial to our cause, which is now to understand the embedding

M(V)\subseteq Q(V).

We have already seen that
Q(V)
is closed under the following derivative operators:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=qd/dq,

\displaystyle D_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta-\tfrac{k}{12}E_{2},

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}

\displaystyle=2\pi iD_{k}+L(-1),

though only the last of these leaves
M(V)
invariant, cf. Subsection
4.1
. All of these operators raise conformal degrees by
2
.

7.1.
The lowering operator and the main Theorem

We define a lowering operator
\Lambda
on
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
as follows:

\Lambda=\frac{12}{2\pi i}\delta\otimes\Id+\Id\otimes L(1).

In this Section we will mainly be viewing
Q(V)
as functions on the upper half-plane. Then
\Lambda
can be expressed more simply as:

(24)

\Lambda=\frac{12}{2\pi i}\delta+L(1)=\frac{12}{2\pi i}\frac{\partial}{\partial E_{2}}+L(1).

Among our main results are the next Theorem and its Corollary, which provides a very useful characterization of
M(V)
as the kernel of
\Lambda
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Suppose that
f(\tau)\in Q_{k}(V)
. Then

(25)

\displaystyle f||_{k}\gamma(\tau)=\exp(X\Lambda)f(\tau).

Before proving this theorem, let us first record some important corollaries:

Corollary 7.2
.

We have

M(V)=\ker\Lambda.

Proof.

By definition,
f\in M(V)
if, and only if,
f||_{k}\gamma(\tau)=f
. So by (
25
)
f\in M(V)
if, and only if,
\exp(X\Lambda)f(\tau)=f(\tau)
. Since
\Lambda
lowers weights by
2
this last equality holds if, and only if,
\Lambda f=0
and the Corollary is proved.
∎

Corollary 7.3
.

Suppose that

f||_{k}\gamma=\sum_{n\geq 0}X^{n}Q_{n}(f)

for
V
-valued holomorphic functions
Q_{n}(f)
. Then

(26)

\displaystyle Q_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n!}\Lambda^{n}(f).

Remark 7.4
.

Equation (
26
) is the VOA analog of the well-known result for scalar-valued quasi-modular forms that if
g\in Q_{k}
satisfies

g|_{k}\gamma=\sum_{n\geq 0}X^{n}Q_{n}(f)
then we have

Q_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n!}\partial^{n}f/\partial E_{2}^{n}.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
:

Since
f\in Q_{k}(V)
with
k=2n
then there is an equality of the form

(27)

\displaystyle f=\sum_{m=0}^{n}E_{2}^{m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-m}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell},

where
f_{m,\ell}\in M_{2\ell}
and
v_{m,\ell}\in V_{n-m-\ell}
. Let us write
j
in place of
j(\gamma,\tau)
. Then

\displaystyle f||_{k}\gamma(\tau)

\displaystyle=j^{-k}K(\gamma,\tau)^{-1}f(\gamma\tau)

\displaystyle=j^{-k+2L(0)}e^{cjL(1)}f(\gamma\tau)

\displaystyle=j^{-k+2L(0)}e^{cjL(1)}\sum_{m,\ell}E_{2}^{m}(\gamma\tau)f_{m,\ell}(\gamma\tau)v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=j^{-k+2L(0)}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{c^{r}j^{r}}{r!}L(1)^{r}\sum_{m,\ell}E_{2}^{m}(\gamma\tau)f_{m,\ell}(\gamma\tau)v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=j^{-k+2L(0)}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{c^{r}j^{r}}{r!}\sum_{m,\ell}E_{2}^{m}(\gamma\tau)f_{m,\ell}(\gamma\tau)L(1)^{r}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\sum_{m,\ell}j^{-k+2(n-m-\ell-r)+r}\frac{c^{r}}{r!}E_{2}^{m}(\gamma\tau)f_{m,\ell}(\gamma\tau)L(1)^{r}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,\ell}j^{-2m-2\ell-r}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{c^{r}}{r!}E_{2}^{m}(\gamma\tau)f_{m,\ell}(\gamma\tau)L(1)^{r}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}j^{-r}\frac{c^{r}}{r!}\sum_{m,\ell}(j^{-2}E_{2}(\gamma\tau))^{m}(j^{-2\ell}f_{m,\ell}(\gamma\tau))L(1)^{r}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(XL(1))^{r}}{r!}\sum_{m}\left(E_{2}(\tau)+\frac{12X}{2\pi i}\right)^{m}\sum_{\ell}f_{m,\ell}(\tau)v_{m,\ell}.

On the other hand we have

\displaystyle\exp(X\Lambda)f(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{X^{s}\Lambda^{s}}{s!}\sum_{m,\ell}E_{2}^{m}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{1}{s!}\left(\frac{12X}{2\pi i}\partial/\partial E_{2}+XL(1)\right)^{s}\sum_{m,\ell}E_{2}^{m}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{t=0}^{s}\binom{s}{t}\left(\frac{12X}{2\pi i}\partial/\partial E_{2}\right)^{s-t}(XL(1))^{t}\sum_{m,\ell}E_{2}^{m}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\frac{1}{s!}\sum_{t=0}^{s}\binom{s}{t}\sum_{m,\ell}\frac{m!}{(m-s+t)!}\left(\frac{12X}{2\pi i}\right)^{s-t}(XL(1))^{t}E_{2}^{m-s+t}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\geq 0}\sum_{m,\ell,t}\frac{1}{t!}\binom{m}{s-t}\left(\frac{12X}{2\pi i}\right)^{s-t}(XL(1))^{t}E_{2}^{m-s+t}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\geq 0}\sum_{m,\ell}\frac{(XL(1))^{t}}{t!}\left(E_{2}+\frac{12X}{2\pi i}\right)^{m}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}.

A comparison of these expressions completes the proof of the
Theorem.
∎

7.2.
Surjectivity of the lowering operator

We address the question of whether
\Lambda:Q(V)\rightarrow Q(V)
is a surjective map. This is the analog of asking whether
L(1)
is a surjective map on
V
. The answer for
V
, described in
[
7
]
and recalled below in Lemma
7.6
, depends on technical properties of
V
.

We start with a simple observation.

Lemma 7.5
.

For any QVOA
V
we have

\dim Q_{2k+2}(V)-\dim Q_{2k}(V)\leq\dim M_{2k+2}(V).

Equality holds if, and only if,

\Lambda:Q_{2k+2}(V)\rightarrow Q_{2k}(V)

is a surjection.

Proof.

Since
\Lambda
lowers weights by
2
then there is a linear isomorphism

\Lambda(Q_{2k+2}(V))\cong Q_{2k+2}(V)/M_{2k+2}(V).

This holds because, by Corollary
7.2
,
M_{2k+2}(V)=Q_{2k+2}(V)\cap\ker\Lambda.
Thus

\displaystyle\dim Q_{2k}(V)\geq\dim\Lambda(Q_{2k+2}(V))=\dim Q_{2k+2}(V)-\dim M_{2k+2}(V),

and both parts of the Lemma now follow.
∎

As preparation for the next result we state the Proposition from Section 2.4 of
[
7
]
mentioned above. Although the result likely holds for any QVOA, we state it only when
V
is a VOA because that is the assumption of
loc. cit.

Lemma 7.6
.

For any VOA
V
, the map

L(1):V_{k+1}\rightarrow V_{k}

is a surjection unless perhaps
k=0
.

\hfill\Box

The first main result of this Subsection is the
Q(V)
-analog of this Lemma.

Theorem 7.7
.

For any VOA
V
, the map

\Lambda:Q_{2k+2}(V)\rightarrow Q_{2k}(V)

is a surjection unless perhaps
k=0
.

Proof.

We have

Q_{2k}(V)=\bigoplus_{r=0}^{k}Q_{2r}\otimes V_{k-r}.

Fix any
k\neq 0
. We show by induction on
r
that
\im\Lambda\supseteq Q_{2r}\otimes V_{k-r}
. The first case is when
r=0
, when we have to show that
V_{k}\subseteq\im\Lambda
. We have

\displaystyle\Lambda(V_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{12}{2\pi i}\partial/\partial E_{2}\otimes L(1)\right)(V_{k+1})

\displaystyle=L(1)V_{k+1}

\displaystyle=V_{k}

where the last equality follows from Lemma
7.6
. Thus indeed

V_{k}\subseteq\im\Lambda
.

Proceeding by induction, choose
f\in Q_{2r}
and
v\in V_{k-r}
. Let
w\in V_{k-r+1}
satisfy
L(1)w=v
. Such a
w
exists by Lemma
7.6
as long as
k\neq r
. Let us assume this for now. Then

\displaystyle\Lambda(f\otimes w)=\frac{12}{2\pi i}\partial f/\partial E_{2}\otimes w+f\otimes v

By induction the first term on the right side lies in
\im\Lambda
. Therefore
f\otimes v\in\im\Lambda
whence

Q_{2r}\otimes V_{k-r}\subseteq\im\Lambda
.

It remains to consider the case
k=r
, when we have to show that
Q_{2k}\subseteq\im\Lambda
. But it is clear that
\Lambda(Q_{2k+2})=Q_{2k}
and this completes the induction, and with it the proof of the Theorem.
∎

Corollary 7.8
.

Suppose that
V
is of CFT-type (i.e.,
V_{0}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}
). Then
\Lambda:Q(V)\rightarrow Q(V)
is a surjection.

Proof.

Consider the map
\Lambda:Q_{2}(V)\rightarrow Q_{0}(V)
. We have

\displaystyle\Lambda(Q_{2}(V))\supseteq\left(\frac{12}{2\pi i}\partial/\partial E_{2}\otimes 1+1\otimes L(1)\right)(\langle E_{2}\otimes\mathbf{1}\rangle)=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}.

Because
V_{0}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}
by assumption it follows that

Q_{0}(V)=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}
and so
\Lambda
surjects
Q_{2}(V)
onto
Q_{0}(V)
. Therefore the statement of the Corollary follows by Theorem
7.7
.
∎

Corollary 7.9
.

For any VOA
V
we have

\dim Q_{2k+2}(V)-\dim Q_{2k}(V)=\dim M_{2k+2}

unless perhaps
k=0
. If
V
is of CFT-type then the equality holds for all
k
.

\hfill\Box

Remark 7.10
.

It is well-known, cf. Lemma 5.2 of
[
8
]
, that if
V
is of CFT-type i.e.,
V_{0}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}
, then
V_{k}=0
for
k<0
.

7.3.
The weight-preserving operator
P

It is defined as follows: as an endomorphism of
Q\otimes V^{(2)}
, it is described as

(28)

\displaystyle P\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi i}{12}E_{2}\otimes L(1)\right).

That is, on a pure tensor
f\otimes v
we have

P(f\otimes v)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{1}{n!}\left(\left(-\frac{2\pi i}{12}E_{2}\right)^{n}f\right)\otimes\left(L(1)^{n}v\right).

Similarly to
\Lambda
,
P
belongs to the natural
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
acting on
Q(V)
but
not
the diagonal
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
. We shall also use
P
to denote the composition of (
28
) with
\iota
. This should cause no confusion.
It is clear that
P
is an invertible weight-preserving map

P\colon Q\otimes V^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}Q(V).

Now we come to the main result of this Subsection. We state it only for QVOAs of CFT-type, however the interested reader may readily formulate the general case.

Theorem 7.11
.

Suppose that
V
is QVOA of CFT-type. Then the following diagram commutes

\textstyle{Q\otimes V^{(2)}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{P}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{Q(V)}
\textstyle{M\otimes V^{(2)}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{P|_{M\otimes V^{(2)}}}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{M(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

where the vertical maps are the natural containments.

Proof.

The main point of the Theorem is that restricting
P
to
M\otimes V^{(2)}
induces a surjection onto
M(V)
. Since
P
preserves weights it suffices to show that
P|_{M\otimes V^{(2)}}
induces an isomorphism at weight
k=2n
.

Let
f\in Q_{k}(V)
be as in (
27
) and assume that in fact
f\in M_{k}(V)
. Then
\Lambda f=0
by Corollary
7.2
. Writing this out explicitly shows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{12}{2\pi i}\partial/\partial E_{2}\otimes 1+1\otimes L(1)\right).\sum_{m=0}^{n}E_{2}^{m}\sum_{\ell}^{n-m}f_{m,\ell}v_{m,\ell}

Theorem

7.5.
The connection revisited

Recall that
\nabla_{k}
is a graded operator given by the formula

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}=2\pi iD_{k}+L(-1)=2\pi i\theta-\tfrac{2\pi ik}{12}E_{2}+L(-1).

It was also shown in section
4
that
\nabla_{k}
maps modular forms to modular forms, increasing weights by
2
. The goal of this subsection is to show that the operators
\nabla_{k}
can be used to describe a decomposition of the space
M_{k}(V)
. We require the following result:

Theorem 7.19
.

Let
V
be a QVOA of CFT-type. Fix a weight
k\geq 0
and write
f\in Q_{k}(V)
in the form

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i+j=k}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}

where
\alpha_{i}
has modular weight
i
and
\beta_{j}\in V
has
L(0)
-weight
(k-i)/2
in
V
.
Suppose that
\alpha_{i}\in M_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq k
even. Then
f\in M_{k}(V)
if and only if
f
is quasi-primary.

Proof.

A simple computation first shows that

\displaystyle\tau^{k-2L(0)}f=\sum_{i+j=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}=f(-1/\tau).

Suppose first that
f
is quasi-primary, that is,
L(1)f=0
. Using this, we get

\displaystyle e^{-\tau L(1)}\tau^{k-2L(0)}f(\tau)=e^{-\tau L(1)}\left(\sum_{i+j=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\right)=\sum_{i+j=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}

which is indeed equal to
f(-1/\tau)
from the previous display, and hence
f\in M_{k}(V)
. Now if
f\in M_{k}(V)
, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i+j=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}=\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\tau^{\ell}}{\ell!}L(1)^{\ell}\right)\cdot\sum_{i+k=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}.

Expanding this expression in
\ell
, we see that we must have

\displaystyle L(1)\sum_{i+j=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}=\left(\sum_{\ell\geq 2}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\tau^{\ell}}{\ell!}L(1)^{\ell}\right)\cdot\sum_{i+j=k}\tau^{i}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}

Notice that since applying
L(1)
to an element of
Q_{k}(V)
never introduces factors of
\tau
, the above expression holds only when
L(1)f=0
, which establishes the theorem.
∎

In what follows, we denote by

which lie in the kernel of

, and by

. We also realize

8.
Hecke operators

We introduce Hecke operators for
M(V)
in analogy with the classical Hecke operators. We explain that the systems of Hecke eigenvalues that arise are precisely the same systems as for classical (quasi)-modular forms of level one. The only thing that depends on the QVOA
V
are the multiplicities of these eigensystems, which are given by the graded dimensions of
V
.

8.1.
Classical Hecke operators

First we review the classical Hecke operators
T_{m}
. The standard action of
T_{m}
on
f\in M_{2k}
is as follows:

T_{m}f\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=m^{k-1}\sum_{[\alpha]}f|_{2k}\alpha.

Here,
[\alpha]
ranges over the orbits in
\Gamma\setminus{D_{m}}
where
D_{m}
is the set of
2\times 2
integral matrices of determinant
m
. We may without loss choose the representatives to be the set of matrices

\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
0&d\end{matrix}\right),\quad\quad ad=m,\quad 0\leq b<d.

Then we compute

(T_{m}f)(\tau)=m^{2k-1}\sum_{d}d^{-2k}f(\alpha\tau).

The induced action of
T_{m}
on
q
-expansions is well-known. If

f=\sum_{n}b_{n}q^{n}
is a modular form of weight
2k
then

(30)

(T_{m}f)(q)=\sum_{n}\sum_{a\mid(m,n)}a^{2k-1}b_{mn/a^{2}}q^{n}.

Following
[
23
]
, we extend the meaning of
T_{m}
using equation (
30
) so that it is an operator on the
q
-expansions of quasi-modular forms of weight
2k
, not just modular forms.

Remark 8.1
.

Equation (
30
) applies in the case of a constant
q
-expansion of weight
0
, when it says that

T_{m}(1)=m^{-1}\sigma_{1}(m).

8.2.
The operators
T^{\prime}_{m}

Now we turn to the action of the
m^{th}
Hecke operator acting on
M(V)
. We denote this by
T_{m}^{\prime}
to distinguish it from
T_{m}
. If

then we define

T^{\prime}_{m}f(\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=m^{k-1}\sum_{[\alpha]}f||_{2k}\alpha(\tau).

Because the double slash operator
f||_{2k}\alpha
defines a right group action of
\GL_{2}(\mathbf{R})^{+}
, this is well-defined and defines an operator on
M_{2k}(V)
.

On the other hand we may extend
T_{m}
to
Q\otimes V^{(2)}=Q(V)
componentwise, i.e.,

(31)

T_{m}\left(\sum g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}\right)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum T_{m}g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}\quad\quad(g_{2r}\in Q_{2r},v_{\ell}\in V_{\ell}),

where, as indicated, we continue to use the symbol
T_{m}
and
T_{m}g_{2r}
is the action (
30
) on
q
-expansions.

Theorem 8.2
.

Both of the operators
T_{m}
and
T^{\prime}_{m}
preserve the subspace
M(V)\subseteq Q(V)
, and we have the identity

T_{m}^{\prime}=m^{L(0)}T_{m}.

Especially,

T_{m}g_{2r}\in Q_{2r}

Proof.

We have already explained that
T^{\prime}_{m}
acts on
M(V)
. Our proof of the displayed formula will then imply that
T_{m}
also acts. Let
f\in M_{2k}(V)
. Using the definitions we have

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{m}f(\tau)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=m^{k-1}\sum_{[\alpha]}f||_{2k}\alpha(\tau)

\displaystyle=m^{k-1}\sum_{[\alpha]}j(\alpha,\tau)^{-2k}K(\alpha,\tau)^{-1}(\det\alpha)^{k}f(\alpha\tau)

\displaystyle=m^{2k-1}\sum_{[\alpha]}d^{-2k}K(\alpha,\tau)^{-1}f(\alpha\tau).

Because
c=0

appearing in the formula above, then

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{m}f=m^{2k-1}\sum_{[\alpha]}d^{-2k}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)^{L(0)}f(\alpha\tau)

\displaystyle=m^{2k-1}\sum_{a,d}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)^{L(0)}d^{-2k}\sum_{b}f((a\tau+b)/d).

Each form

is a sum of terms

where

and

and

f((a\tau+b)/d)=\sum_{*}g_{2r}((a\tau+b)/d)\otimes v_{\ell}

and we can apply standard arguments
using the
q
-expansion of
g_{2r}(\tau)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sum_{n}a^{g}_{n}q^{n}
. Thus

\sum_{b}g_{2r}((a\tau+b)/d)=\sum a^{g}_{n}e^{2\pi ian\tau/d}\sum_{b=0}^{d-1}e^{2\pi ibn/d}.

The innermost sum vanishes except when

\sum_{b}g_{2r}((a\tau+b)/d)=\sum_{d|n}a^{g}_{n}dq^{an/d}.

Now we have

\displaystyle T^{\prime}_{m}f

\displaystyle=m^{2k-1}\sum_{g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}}\sum_{a,d}\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)^{L(0)}d^{1-2k}\sum_{d|n}a^{g_{2r}}_{n}q^{an/d}\otimes v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}}\sum_{a\mid m}\left(\frac{m}{a^{2}}\right)^{L(0)}\left\{a^{2k-1}\sum_{t}a_{mt/a}^{g}q^{at}\right\}\otimes v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}}\sum_{n}\sum_{a\mid(m,n)}\left(\frac{m}{a^{2}}\right)^{L(0)}\left\{a^{2k-1}a_{mn/a^{2}}^{g}q^{n}\right\}\otimes v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}}\sum_{n}\sum_{a\mid(m,n)}\left(\frac{m}{a^{2}}\right)^{k-r}\left\{a^{2k-1}a_{mn/a^{2}}^{g}q^{n}\right\}\otimes v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}}\sum_{n}\sum_{a\mid(m,n)}m^{L(0)}\left\{a^{2r-1}a_{mn/a^{2}}^{g}q^{n}\right\}\otimes v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=m^{L(0)}\sum_{g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}}T_{m}g_{2r}\otimes v_{\ell}

\displaystyle=m^{L(0)}T_{m}f.

This completes the proof of the Theorem.
∎

8.4.
Hecke eigenstates

If
\{f\}
is a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates for the actions
of

on

then by Theorem

\{Pf\}
is a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates for the actions of

on

. We refer to such gadgets as

Based on what we have proved it is easy to write down such a set
\{f\}
. The most straightforward case is

1\otimes V_{k}.

m^{L(0)}T_{m}
acts as a scalar matrix
with eigenvalue
m^{k-1}\sigma_{1}(m)

(cf. Remark
8.1
). The corresponding Hecke eigenstates in

are, for

The generic case

is similar but requires the classical theory of Hecke operators for

.
Recall that for
2r\geq 4
,

contains a unique basis of simultaneous normalized eigenforms for the classical Hecke operators
T_{m}
. Let this basis be denoted by

be

”Normalized” means that

. Such a choice is always possible, and we have

Choose any basis

is a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates

(

We turn to a brief discussion of a quasimodular form, cf.

q
-expansion. As usual, we call

Q_{2k}

\displaystyle\theta(E_{4})=(E_{2}E_{4}-E_{6})/3,

\displaystyle\theta(E_{6})=(E_{2}E_{6}-E_{4}^{2})/2.

Appendix B
Vertex algebras

There are many variants of the notion of
vertex algebra
. Here we will describe just those that we need in this paper. They are mainly vertex algebras over
\mathbf{C}
. For a general presentation of vertex algebras over any commutative base ring, see
[
22
]
.

B.0.1.
Fields on a linear space

Fix a
\mathbf{C}
-linear space
V
, often referred to as (the)
Fock space
. Vectors in
V
are frequently called
states
. We consider the linear space
End(V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
consisting of (doubly infinite)
power series

a(z)=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}a(n)z^{-n-1}

where
a(n)
are arbitrary endomorphisms of
V
. The space of fields on
V
is the linear subspace of
End(V)[[z,z^{-1}]]
defined by

\mathcal{F}(V):=\{a(z)\mid\forall b\in V\ \mbox{we have}\ a(n)b=0\ \mbox{for}\ n\gg 0\}.

Thus for a field
a(z)\in\mathcal{F}(V)
we have

a(z)b:=\sum_{n}a(n)bz^{-n-1}\in\mathbf{C}[z^{-1}][[z]].

B.0.2.
Creative fields

We fix once and for all a nonzero state
\mathbf{1}\in V
called the
vacuum
. A field
a(z)\in\mathcal{F}(V)
is called
creative
if it satisfies

a(n)\mathbf{1}=0\ \mbox{for all}\ n\geq 0.

Put another way,

a(z).\mathbf{1}=a(-1)\mathbf{1}+a(-2)\mathbf{1}z+\ldots\in V[[z]].

In this situation we also say that
a(z)

creates
the state
a(-1)\mathbf{1}
, that is, the constant term of
a(z).\mathbf{1}
.

B.0.3.
Vertex algebras

A vertex algebra over
\mathbf{C}
is a triple
(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
consisting of the following ingredients:

1.
(V,\mathbf{1})
is a linear space with vacuum state
\mathbf{1}
.

2.
Y:V\rightarrow\mathcal{F}(V),\ a\mapsto Y(a,z)=\sum_{n}a(n)z^{-n-1}
is a linear map such that

3. Each field
Y(a,z)
is creative and creates
a\in V
, i.e.,
a(-1)\mathbf{1}=a
.

4. The following structural (Jacobi) identity holds for all states
a,b,c\in V
and all integers
r,s,t
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}\binom{r}{i}((a(t+i)b)(r+s-i)c=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{i}\binom{t}{i}\left\{a(r+t-i)b(s+i)c-(-1)^{t}b(s+t-i)a(r+i)c\right\}.

It follows from these axioms (loc. cit.) that
Y(\mathbf{1},z)=Id_{V}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{1}(n)=\delta_{n,-1}Id_{V}
.

B.0.4.
The canonical derivation

Let
V=(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
be a vertex algebra over
\mathbf{C}
. The creativity axiom permits us to define a sequence
(D_{0},D_{1},D_{2},\ldots)
of endomorphisms
D_{n}
of
V
:

\sum_{n\geq 0}D_{n}(a)z^{n}:=Y(a,z)\mathbf{1}

so that

D_{n}(a)=a(-n-1)\mathbf{1}\ (a\in V).

Thus
D_{0}=Id_{V}
. We usually write
D=D_{1}
, that is
Da:=a(-2)\mathbf{1}
. It can be shown (loc. cit.) that
D
is a
derivation
of
V
in the sense that
for every
n
we have

(36)

\displaystyle D(a(n)b)=(Da)(n)b+a(n)Db.

Then
D_{n}\mathbf{1}=\delta_{n,1}\mathbf{1}
and
D_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}D^{n}
so that

Y(a,z)\mathbf{1}=e^{zD}a.

D
is called the
canonical derivation
of
V
. If we want to identify the derivation then we may also refer to the quadruple
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},D)
as a vertex algebra, it being understood that
D
is the canonical derivation of
V
.

Example
1
. The following example is well-known, (
loc. cit.
, Section 5.2). Let
V
be a commutative, associative
\mathbf{C}
-algebra with identity
1
with
D

any
derivation of
V
. Identify each
a\in V
with the endomorphism defined by multiplication by
a
. Define the vertex operator by

Y(a,z)=e^{zD}a,\ \ \mbox{i.e.,}\ a(n)=0\ (n\geq 0),a_{n}=\tfrac{1}{(-n-1)!}D^{-n-1}a\ (n<0).

Then
(V,Y,1,D)
is a vertex algebra.

Example
2
. We may take
D=0
in the above construction. What obtains is the easiest example of a vertex algebra, where the vertex operator
Y(a,z)=a

is independent of
z
and
(V,Y,1,0)
is nothing but the
\mathbf{C}
-algebra
V
.

B.0.5.
\mathbf{Z}
-graded vertex algebras

A vertex algebra
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},D)
over
\mathbf{C}
is called
\mathbf{Z}
-graded if the Fock space carries a
\mathbf{Z}
-grading

V=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}V_{k}

into subspaces that satisfy the following conditions

1).
a\in V_{k},b\in V_{\ell}\Rightarrow a(n)b\in V_{k+\ell-n-1}
,

2).
\dim V_{k}<\infty
and
V_{k}=0
for
k\ll 0
.

We say that a state
a\in V_{k}
has
conformal degree

k
. (Weight
k
is more traditional but we reserve this name for modular and quasi-modular forms.)

In a
\mathbf{Z}
-graded vertex algebra
V
we have
\mathbf{1}\in V_{0}
, furthermore
D
is a raising operator of weight
1
:

\displaystyle D:V_{k}\rightarrow V_{k+1}.

V
also admits an
Euler operator

E
defined by

E.a=ka\ (a\in V_{k}).

E
is only a derivation with respect to the
-1
product.

Let
End(V)^{-}
be the Lie algebra consisting of
End(V)
equipped with the usual bracket of operators.
E
and
D
span a
2
-dimensional subalgebra of
End(V)^{-}
satisfying

[E,D]=D.

Remark B.1
.

There are significant vertex algebras which do
not
satisfy condition 2). For example lattice vertex algebras
V_{L}
for an
indefinite
even lattice
L

[
18
]
.

B.0.6.
QVOAs

A quasi-vertex operator algebra (QVOA) over
\mathbf{C}
is a quadruple
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\rho)
with the following ingredients:

1.
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},D)
is a
\mathbf{Z}
-graded vertex algebra with canonical derivation
D
and Euler operator
E
.

2.
\rho:\mathfrak{sl}_{2}\rightarrow End(V)^{-}
is a representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}

such that

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1/2&0\\
0&-1/2\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapsto E,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapsto D,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\mapsto\delta.

and the following structural identities hold for
a\in V_{k}
:

3.(Translation covariance).
(Da)(n)=-na(n-1)=[D,a(n)],

4.
[\delta,a(n)]=(\delta a)(n)+(2k-n-2)a(n+1)
.

One checks that
\delta:V_{k}\rightarrow V_{k-1}
is a lowering operator of weight
1
. Our axioms for a QVOA are essentially those of
[
12
]
, Section 2.8. In VOA theory it is traditional to replace

E,D,\delta
by
L(0),L(-1),L(1)
respectively, so that the bracket relations are
[L(m),L(n)]=(m-n)L(m+n)
for
m,n\in\{0,\pm 1\}
. A state
a\in V
is called
quasiprimary
if it satisfies
L(1)a=0

A QVOA
V
is of
CFT-type
if it satisfies
V_{0}=\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}
. This is often a very convenient assumption, for example it implies
[
8
]
that
V_{n}=0
for all
n<0
.

B.0.7.
VOAs

A vertex operator algebra (VOA) over
\mathbf{C}

is a special type of QVOA
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\rho)
with the following additional ingredient:

1. A distinguished state
\omega\in V
with vertex operator

\displaystyle Y(\omega,z)=:\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}L(n)z^{-n-2}

such that the modes of
\omega
satisfy the Virasoro relations

(37)

\displaystyle[L(m,L(n)]=(m-n)L(m+n)+\frac{m^{3}-m}{12}cId_{V}

for some
c\in\mathbf{C}
(the
central charge
of
V
).

2. The operators
L(n)
and
Id_{V}
span a Lie subalgebra
Vir\subseteq End(V)^{-}
(a Virasoro algebra of central charge
c
; this follows from (
37
)) and
\rho
is the restriction of
Vir
to the

\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-subalgebra spanned by
L(0),L(\pm 1)
.

This VOA is usually denoted
(V,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
and
\omega
is often called the
conformal
or
Virasoro
vector. We have
L(-2)\mathbf{1}=\omega,L(0)\omega=2\omega,L(1)\omega=0
.

B.0.8.
Some categories

It is evident that these vertex-type objects define concrete categories, in particular we have the category
\mathbf{QV}
whose objects are
QVOAs and morphisms
f:(U,Y,\mathbf{1},\rho)\rightarrow(V,Y^{\prime},\mathbf{1},\rho^{\prime})
are vacuum-preserving linear maps
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle f}}{{\longrightarrow}}V
that preserve
all products (
f(a(n)b)=f(a)(n)f(b)
) and
intertwine the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-representations (
f\rho(g)=\rho^{\prime}(g)f,\ g\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
).

Similarly, the category
\mathbf{V}
of VOAs has VOAs as objects with morphisms
f:(U,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)\rightarrow(V,Y^{\prime},\mathbf{1},\omega^{\prime})
being linear maps that preserve vacuum states and conformal vectors and preserve all products as in
\mathbf{QV}
.

Every VOA is a QVOA and there is a forgetful functor

\mathbf{V}\longrightarrow\mathbf{QV}

where we ignore the presence of the conformal vector.

We can define tensor products of QVOAs (coproduct in
\mathbf{QV}
)

(U,Y,\mathbf{1},\rho)\otimes(V,Y^{\prime},\mathbf{1}^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}):=(U\otimes V,Y\otimes Y^{\prime},\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{1}^{\prime},\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}).

The tensor product vertex operator is

(Y\otimes Y^{\prime})(a\otimes b,z)=Y(a,z)\otimes Y^{\prime}(b,z),

which means that

(a\otimes b)(n)=\sum_{i+j+1=n}a(i)\otimes b(j)

(cf.
[
22
]
, Subsection 6.5.) and

\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}
is the tensor product representation of
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
on
U\otimes V
. Thus

g\mapsto\rho(g)\otimes Id+Id\otimes\rho^{\prime}(g).

In particular
(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})(L(0))=L(0\otimes Id+Id\otimes L(0)
is the Euler operator for
U\otimes V
and

U\otimes V
gets the tensor product grading:

(U\otimes V)_{n}=\sum_{k+\ell=n}U_{k}\otimes V_{\ell},

One checks that the canonical derivation of
U\otimes V

coincides with the action of
L(-1)
on
U\otimes V
.

B.0.9.
Doubling

Suppose that

(V,Y,\mathbf{1})
is a
\mathbf{Z}
-graded vertex algebra with
V=\oplus_{k}V_{k}
. The
doubled
space is

(V^{(2)},Y,\mathbf{1})
where

V^{(2)}=\oplus_{k}V^{(2)}_{2k},\ V_{2k}^{(2)}:=V_{k}.

(V^{(2)},Y,\mathbf{1})
is a vertex algebra, however it may no longer be

\mathbf{Z}
-graded because axiom 1) in Subsection
B.0.5
may not be satisfied.

B.0.10.
Rank
1
Heisenberg VOA

(cf.
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
). The complex Heisenberg Lie algebra has a basis

\{h(n),0\neq n\in\mathbf{Z}\}\cup{\{k\}}
satisfying the canonical commutator relations

(38)

\displaystyle[h(m),h(n)]=m\delta_{m,-n}k,\ [h(n),k]=0.

There is a VOA
(S,Y,\mathbf{1},\omega)
for which the Fock space
S
is a
Verma module
induced from the
1
-dimensional space
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{1}
satisfying
h(n).\mathbf{1}=0(n>0),k.\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
.
S
has a basis consisting of states

h(-n_{1})h(-n_{2})\ldots h(-n_{k}).\mathbf{1}\ (n_{1}\geq\ldots\geq n_{k}\geq 1).

V_{m}
is spanned by those states for which

\{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\}\vdash m
. In particular

S
is of CFT-type,
S_{1}
is spanned by
h:=h(-1)\mathbf{1}
(the canonical weight
1
state) and its vertex operator is

Y(h,z):=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}h(n)z^{-n-1}

where the action of the mode
h(n)
on
S
is determined by (
38
). The Virasoro vector is

\omega:=\tfrac{1}{2}h(-1)^{2}.\mathbf{1}
and the central charge (or
rank
) is
c=1
;
h
is a quasiprimary state.

A common approach to this VOA is to set
x_{-n}=h(-n).\mathbf{1}\ (n>0)
and identify
V
with the symmetric algebra
\mathbf{C}[x_{-1},x_{-2},\ldots]
(whence the appellation
S
). Then for

n>0
the operator
h(-n)
is multiplication by
x_{-n}
and
h(n)=n\partial/\partial x_{-n}
.
