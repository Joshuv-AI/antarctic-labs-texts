Job satisfaction among postgraduate dental students and its contributing factors: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Fahimeh Farzanegan, Erfan Shadi, Habibollah Esmaily, Fateme Hashemi, Arsalan Shahri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis analytical cross-sectional study assessed job satisfaction, stress, and perceived social support among postgraduate dental students and identified contributing factors.MethodsSixty-four postgraduate dental students across 10 specialties at Mashhad Dental School participated between January and February 2024. A structured questionnaire included the Brayfield–Rothe Job Satisfaction Scale, a validated Persian Job Satisfaction Scale, the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support, and the Ryff Psychological Well-Being Scale. Demographic data were also collected. Statistical analyses examined relationships between variables, with a significance level of 0.05.ResultsThe Brayfield–Rothe Scale indicated above-average job satisfaction (p < 0.05), while the Persian Job Satisfaction Scale showed below-average scores (p < 0.05). Perceived social support (p < 0.05) and psychological well-being (p < 0.05) were above average. Married students reported significantly higher satisfaction and social support (p = 0.002). Significant correlations were found between job satisfaction, perceived social support, and psychological well-being.ConclusionPostgraduate dental students reported high perceived social support and psychological well-being but elevated stress levels, with varying job satisfaction across scales. Married students demonstrated higher satisfaction and support. Enhancing support systems and stress-reduction strategies in dental education is recommended.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12960-025-01039-7.

Background

Job satisfaction describes individuals’ feelings and contentment with their work [1]. Locke defines job satisfaction as “the affective orientation of the individual toward the work role which he is presently occupying” [2]. It significantly influences turnover intentions, performance, and, consequently, the quality of care patients receive [3]. As employees are the most valuable resource in any organization, their job satisfaction plays a key role in the overall efficiency and success of the organization.

Dentistry is widely regarded as a prestigious and financially rewarding profession, leading to the assumption that dental practitioners are inherently satisfied with their careers [4]. However, the dental profession also carries substantial health risks and stress factors that negatively impact dentists’ well-being [5]. This career is highly stressful, exposing dentists to hazards that impact their quality of life [6–8], with postgraduate dental students at greater risk of stress, anxiety, and disability [9].

Furthermore, misconceptions about the dental profession, fueled by public perception, may contribute to discouragement and negative feelings among dental practitioners. These challenges influence the quality of dental practice and the delivery of healthcare in dental institutions. Consequently, many health organizations conduct job satisfaction surveys to identify areas for improvement and to enhance the work environment [10].

During residency, trainees often experience a transitional phase in which their theoretical knowledge and practical expertise are still developing. Managing real patients while still acquiring advanced clinical competence can create psychological tension and contribute to mental exhaustion, even in otherwise capable individuals [11]. Studies have concentrated on dental job satisfaction among undergraduate students [12, 13], general dentists [14–22], and also specialists [7, 23].

Only a few studies have evaluated job satisfaction among postgraduate dental students. Prior studies across multiple settings and professional groups generally report moderate to high job satisfaction among dental students, general dentists, and specialists [24–26]. Although a few studies observed lower-than-average satisfaction [27]. Research into job satisfaction among postgraduate students is especially critical, as their educational environment adds additional stress to the already demanding clinical setting [28–30]. Given the limited research available in this field, this study aimed to evaluate job satisfaction among postgraduate dental students and identify the contributing factors.

Methods

The Research and Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUMS.DENTISTRY.REC.1402.030) granted approval for this analytical cross-sectional study on July 10, 2023. The research was carried out between January and February 2024. A census-based sampling approach was employed, including all postgraduate students enrolled at Mashhad Dental School, Mashhad, Iran, during the study period, across ten specialty fields of dentistry from 2018 to 2023. In total, 78 individuals were invited to participate in the survey, and 64 completed and returned the questionnaire, corresponding to a response rate of 82%. Nonresponse was primarily due to availability issues (e.g., absence during the survey windows). Students who were licensed but not practicing were excluded from the study. All included participants had a minimum of one year of clinical work experience in clinics outside the dental school.

Participants were informed that their responses would remain confidential. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Each participant completed a structured questionnaire during their weekly seminar session in their respective departments. A designated staff member from each department instructed them to fill out the forms and collected the completed questionnaires afterward.

The structured questionnaire consisted of two parts: (1) demographics—age, sex, marital status, number of children, place of residence, year of residency, entry quota (a nationally administered admission category that allocates seats by region or special status), and specialty field; and (2) four standard instruments assessing job satisfaction.

The Brayfield–Rothe Job Satisfaction Scale [31] (BRJSS; 19 items including 8 reversed score questions [5, 7, 9, 11, 12, 15, 17, 19]; 5-point scale; score range: 19–95) evaluates job satisfaction based on individuals’ feelings toward their work. A validated Persian job satisfaction scale authenticated by Hessari [19] (HJSS; 33 items, 5-point scale; score range: 33–165) covers 11 domains, including income, empathy, work autonomy, relationships, skills, workload, satisfaction, and occupational stress. The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support [32] (MSPSS; 12 items, 7-point scale; score range: 12–84) gauges support from family, community, and friends. The Ryff Psychological Well-Being Scale [33] (RPWBS; 18 items, 6-point scale; score range: 18–108) measures autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relationships, life purpose, and self-acceptance. Reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) exceeded 0.7 for all scales: BRJSS 0.873, HJSS 0.866, MSPSS 0.921, and RPWBS 0.766. Full instruments are provided in Supplementary File S1.

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26, SPSS Inc., IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, interquartile range, median, and 95% confidence intervals, were computed for all variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To assess relationships between the variables, independent-samples t tests or one-way ANOVA were used for normal data; otherwise, Mann–Whitney U or Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied for non-normal data. Pearson (parametric) or Spearman (non-parametric) correlation coefficients were used to evaluate associations between the different scales. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study included 64 postgraduate dental students in 10 different specialties, aged 24 to 48 years, with a mean age of 29.08 ± 4.78 years. Most participants (96.8%) were under 28 years of age. Among them, 51.6% were female, 60.9% were single, and 85.9% had no children. Residential distribution revealed that 39.1% lived with their families, while 31.3% lived in rental properties. Only 23.4% of the students had a quota to enter the university. A significant difference in age distribution was observed among students based on the year of residency (p = 0.03). Discussion

The above-average job satisfaction levels observed among dental residents in this study (as assessed by the BRJSS, MSPSS, and RPWBS) are consistent with multiple previous investigations across various dental populations. Bates et al. [34] and Alqahtani et al. [24] similarly reported high satisfaction among pediatric dental specialists and orthodontists, respectively, indicating that structured specialty training and growing clinical competence may enhance professional fulfillment. Kobza et al. [26] and Molina-Hernández et al. [20] found comparable results among general dentists, emphasizing autonomy and supportive work environments as key contributors. In contrast, studies among undergraduate dental students [12, 13, 35, 36] showed only moderate satisfaction, likely reflecting limited clinical exposure and evolving professional identity. Overall, these findings suggest that advancing specialization and greater responsibility are generally associated with higher job satisfaction among dental professionals.

In terms of stress, elevated levels reported in this study correspond with prior evidence from multiple populations, including dental students [37, 38], general dentists [15, 39, 40], and specialists [18, 21]. These studies collectively underscore that persistent stressors, such as workload, patient demands, and fear of litigation, remain pervasive across professional levels and can inversely affect job satisfaction, as also highlighted by Pontes et al.

Research focused specifically on dental residents [4, 9, 29, 30, 41, 42] consistently shows that postgraduate trainees experience higher stress compared with students or practicing dentists. The main reported causes include long working hours, sleep deprivation, academic pressure, and financial strain, while access to supportive mentorship and healthy coping mechanisms, such as physical activity, mitigate these effects. Consistent with these findings, the present study observed differences in well-being across specialties; pediatric dentistry residents showing the highest overall well-being, while radiology, surgery, and periodontics reported lower scores in different subscales, though these differences were not statistically significant.

However, in the current study, job satisfaction measured by the HJSS was below the median, differing from other scales. This discrepancy may stem from the broader psychosocial and economic domains covered by the HJSS. Similarly, Hamasha et al. [27] found below-average satisfaction among Saudi dentists using Jeong’s Dental Satisfaction Scale, which places stronger emphasis on income and job security—factors that might vary regionally and institutionally. This study found that job satisfaction, as measured by the BRJSS, differed significantly among dental postgraduate students based on their residency-year cohorts, while no significant differences were observed across other scales, such as the HJSS or RPWBS. The residency-year differences in satisfaction may be attributed to first-year students entering their preferred specialty programs with enthusiasm and not yet experiencing the pressures of residency, such as patient management, work demands, or exams like board certifications and thesis defenses. The MSPSS revealed that married students reported significantly higher satisfaction, consistent with findings from Abraham et al. [43], who noted that married dentists experience greater social and emotional support, financial stability, and professional networking opportunities, which contribute to their job satisfaction.

Positive and significant correlations were found between most of the scales, indicating that higher scores in one were associated with increases in the other and also suggesting that psychological well-being contributes to job satisfaction.

Job satisfaction plays a vital role in the performance of healthcare professionals. Higher job satisfaction not only improves individual and system efficiency but also enhances organizational productivity, particularly by optimizing human capital. Assessing job satisfaction levels among dental residents and identifying the factors that influence it can inform the development of effective policies, ultimately improving the overall efficiency of the healthcare system.

This study included a diverse group of dental residents across various specialties, ensuring broad participation. However, as a single-center study, its findings have limited generalizability. The lack of participation from pathology and oral disease residents also narrows specialty-specific insights. Additionally, because the outcomes were self-reported, estimates may be affected by recall and social-desirability bias despite the use of validated instruments. Furthermore, environmental factors such as relocating from smaller cities or universities to larger ones may influence satisfaction levels, as previously described by Fu et al. [44]. They found that US-trained specialists reported higher life satisfaction than Taiwan-trained specialists or general dentists, primarily due to better self-rated health and family relationships, while professional achievements had less impact. Future studies are encouraged to analyze additional influencing factors, employ multi-center, longitudinal designs with larger, specialty-balanced samples to enhance generalizability.

Conclusions

The findings of this study suggest that first- and second-year dental specialty residents reported higher levels of job satisfaction than third-year residents. Additionally, married residents perceived higher levels of social support compared to their unmarried counterparts. Understanding the factors influencing job satisfaction among dental specialty residents is key to informing policies that can improve productivity within the healthcare system. Emphasizing proactive stress management will help protect the welfare of dental residents, their teams, and patients, ultimately supporting a sustainable and rewarding career in dentistry. However, as a single-center, cross-sectional survey based on self-reported data, the generalizability of these findings is limited.
