Zearalenone causes female reproductive lipotoxicity through the ERα-CD36/TLR4 signaling pathway
Authors: Chao Dai, Wei Fu, Yuanmeng Huang, Tiantian Li, Zifeng Ma, Xiaojiao Chang, Changpo Sun, Shujin Wang, Xin Wu, Yang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exposure to zearalenone (ZEN), an estrogen-like mycotoxin produced by Fusarium fungi, impairs reproductive performance and farm-animal welfare; however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Using female pigs as the large animal model, we demonstrate that ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity is associated with alterations in estrogen receptor-alpha (ERα) expression, diacylglycerol levels, and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Mechanistically, upregulated ERα directly binds to the promoter regions of Cd36 and Tlr4 in the nucleus, leading to increased expression of the lipid transporter CD36 and the inflammatory receptor TLR4, as well as enhanced CD36-TLR4 interaction. This subsequently results in diacylglycerol accumulation and inflammation. Consistently, studies using Cd36-knockout mice and the TLR4-specific inhibitor TAK-242 reveal that ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity is dependent on the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with the ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 in female pigs also counteracts ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity through the inhibition of the ERα-CD36/TLR4 signaling pathway. Together, these findings reveal a novel mechanism underlying ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity, and highlight the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

Recent comprehensive evaluations reveal that up to 60–80% of global food crops are contaminated with detectable levels of mycotoxins, a prevalence far exceeding the historically cited estimate of 25%1. Zearalenone (ZEN), an estrogen-like mycotoxin produced by Fusarium fungi, is widely found in grain crops such as maize, wheat, and other cereals2,3. In Europe, the residues of ZEN were detected in more than 15% of 9877 unprocessed grain samples and 13,000 food samples, with concentrations ranging from 823 to 3000 ppb. Additionally, serious ZEN contamination has also been reported recently in food samples from North America, Asia, and Africa, with levels up to 1500 ppb4. European legislation sets maximum ZEN levels at 500 ppb for animal feed and 100 ppb for human consumption5,6. According to a risk assessment performed by the European Food Safety Authority, human consuming above-average amounts of cereals may exceed the tolerable daily intake (TDI, 0.25 µg/kg b.w) of ZEN by 2.2 fold change7. Notably, chronic exposure to excessive ZEN poses public health risks to both farm animals and humans, including reproductive toxicity, developmental toxicity, lipotoxicity, hepatotoxic, carcinogenic, and immunotoxicity8,9. The widespread threat of ZEN-contaminated food has drawn increasing attention to understanding the mechanisms of ZEN-induced toxicity and developing detoxification strategies.

In both farm animals and humans, ZEN is rapidly absorbed and then metabolized into α-zearalenol (α-ZOL) and β-zearalenol (β-ZOL) in the liver and small intestine6,10. Due to their structural similarity to 17β-estradiol (E2, the predominant estrogen), ZEN and its metabolites can competitively bind to estrogen receptors (ERs), leading to hormonal imbalances, reduced fertility, and reproductive system dysfunction11,12. 17β-estradiol exerts its effects mainly through binding to three specific ERs, including GPER (a G-protein coupled receptor associated to the cell membrane), ERα (a nuclear receptor encoded by ESR1), ERβ (a nuclear receptor encoded by ESR2). Consequently, these specific ERs can regulate transcriptional processes and intracellular signaling cascades13,14. Different types of ERs have specific biological effects by various response mechanisms. In terms of the direct genomic pathway, cytoplasmic ERα/ERβ can be activated upon ligands binding, dimerize, and translocate to the nucleus, subsequently bind to the DNA of its target-genes on estrogen responsive elements13. Additionally, this direct genomic mechanism requires protein-protein interaction between ligand-activated ERs (ERα and ERβ) and other transcription factors involved in DNA binding13. Notably, the two above estrogen signalings can be disrupted by ZEN, a non-steroidal estrogenic mycotoxin, which causes developmental/reproductive disorders in both females15 and males16. Indeed, ZEN exposure contributes to increases in infertility rates, and declines in litter size, and also leads to a hyperestrogenic syndrome10. These adverse effects are closely associated with its structural similarity to estrogen and its ability to bind ERα17. Therefore, ZEN directly activates estrogen signaling and is commonly recognized as an endocrine disruptor18. To date, growing evidence has explored the molecular mechanisms of ZEN-induced reproductive toxicity, such as significant alterations in the transport of fatty acids, the biosynthesis of lipids, the increases of reactive oxygen species (ROS)/lipid peroxides, the induction of DNA damage, and the promotions of pro-inflammatory responses. Of note is that these above changes are mainly due to increased level of ERα19,20. Considering ZEN-induced alterations in lipid metabolism/inflammatory responses, its underlying mechanisms on reproductive organs are yet to be investigated.

In this study, we aimed to investigate underlying mechanisms involved in ZEN-induced female reproductive lipotoxicity. Accordingly, the following experimental designs were explained in more detail: (i) Female pigs during the phase I were used as a large-animal model to examine whether ZEN exposure directly led to the accumulation of ZEN/its major metabolites in the reproductive system, and whether this accumulation resulted in reproductive toxicity. Furthermore, we assessed whether this ZEN-induced reproductive toxicity was accompanied by significant alterations in ERα expression, the lipid transporter CD36-induced lipid accumulation, the inflammatory receptor TLR4-mediated inflammation. (ii) And, we then revealed that ZEN-induced upregulated of ERα led to its directly binding to the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4 in the nucleus, indicating that reproductive lipotoxicity is mediated through the ERα-CD36/TLR4 signaling pathway. To confirm this, (iii) The deletion of Cd36 (Cd36 knockout mice) and (iv) The inhibition of TLR4 (a specific TLR4 inhibitor, TAK-242) could protect against ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity. (v) Finally, for female pigs during the phase II, by using ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 as the tool to assess the feasibility of enzymatic removal of ZEN from mycotoxin-contaminated feed, we tested whether supplements of ZLHY-6 enzyme counteracted ZEN-induced female reproductive lipotoxicity through the ERα-CD36/TLR4 signaling pathway. These findings contribute to our understanding of ZEN’s impact on reproductive health and offer potential therapeutic strategies.

Results

ZEN exposure leads to reproductive lipotoxicity in female pigs during the phase I

By the analysis of the assay of HPLC-MS/MS, higher residues of ZEN and/or its major metabolites, including α-ZOL and β-ZOL, were observed in uterine tissues from ZEN-treated female pigs during the phase I than those in the Ctrl group (Fig. 1A, B). It suggests that reproductive organs are highly sensitivity to ZEN exposure. Subsequently, obvious increases in uterine dimensions (uterine horn’s width, uterine length, and uterine width) (Fig. 1C), inflammatory lesions (Fig. 1D), and swelling nucleus/mitochondria (Fig. 1E) were showed in the reproductive system from ZEN-treated pigs. Intriguingly, these ZEN-induced reproductive abnormalities were accompanied by significant increases in plasma levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate a link between ZEN-induced reproductive toxicity and disrupted plasma lipid profile. Using lipidomics for uterine tissues, we assessed the effects of ZEN on lipid accumulation. In this study, more than 1600 lipid molecules were identified in uterine tissues, which were mainly distributed among 27 lipid classes. Accordingly, we firstly categorized the most abundant ones, and then calculated log2 fold changes with the Z-score normalization (Fig. 1G and Supplementary Fig. 1). Next, the lipid classes were mainly focused, and it illustrated significant increases in most lipid classes in response to ZEN exposure. The most striking change upon ZEN exposure concerned the glycerophospholipids (GPs, such as PA, PC, PE, PG, and PS) and the diacylglycerols (DAG) (Fig. 1G). Given that the DAG might be one of main mediators involved in ZEN-induced lipotoxicity, the present study aimed to reveal the potential effects of ZEN exposure on mRNA/protein levels of key enzymes involved in stepwise lipid biosynthesis from free fatty acids (FFAs) (Fig. 1K–M). As indicated the result in Fig. 1L, mRNA expressions in most of key enzymes involved in fatty acid uptake, fatty acid β-oxidation and lipid biosynthesis were remarkably increased upon ZEN exposure. Furthermore, we confirmed that ZEN exposure led to significant increases in protein expressions of some DAG biosynthesis enzymes Lpin1/2 isoforms and adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL), but dramatic decline in a key TAG synthesis enzyme, such as diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT) (Fig. 1M). Taken together, the findings above suggested that ZEN exposure contributed to lipid accumulation, thereby causing female reproductive lipotoxicity.

The mechanism of ZEN-induced female reproductive lipotoxicity: upregulated ERα activates the CD36-TLR4 signaling axis

To disclose the underlying mechanism of ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity, we reanalyzed the RNA-seq data for reproductive organs in female pigs, including the uterus (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Figs. 2, 4) and the ovary (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). Accordingly, we analyzed the top 10 pathways enriched in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), most pathways were related to lipid metabolism and inflammation (TLR4 signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway). Consistent with this, by using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) analysis for the RNA-seq data, we also observed that the ZEN regime resulted in a full-scale activation of fatty acid metabolism and of TLR4 signaling and tolerance (Fig. 2B). Among these, the lipid transporter CD36 and the intrinsic immune receptor TLR4 were chosen to verify the reproducibility of the RNA-seq analysis. Given that CD36 not only is a key facilitator of fatty acid transport but also initiates fatty acid signaling by binding to TLR421, we subsequently assessed the complex formation of TLR4 with CD36 (Fig. 2D–F). Using co-immunoprecipitations (Co-IPs) (Fig. 2D), immunofluorescence imaging (IFM, Fig. 2E) and proximity ligation assay (PLA, Fig. 2F), increases in the CD36-TLR4 association were observed in ZEN-treated pigs.

As mentioned earlier, the CD36-TLR4 association is at the start of pro-inflammatory responses to excessive fatty acids, which we started to investigate further downstream. Regarding this, we consistently found the subsequent activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways involved in phosphorylated p42/44-ERK1/2 and p38-MAPK (Fig. 2G), followed by activation of TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 2H).

Here, we ask how ZEN exposure leads to increases in CD36-TLR4 association. Given that ZEN as an endocrine disruptor due to its structural similarity to endogenous estrogens, it can bind to estrogen receptors (ERs), including ERα and ERβ. Our current observations showed that ZEN exposure significantly upregulated protein levels of ERαin uterine tissues (Fig. 2I), suggesting that ZEN acts as a partial antagonist for ERβ and a full agonist for ERα. Using Ishikawa cells for cell models, the assay of ChIP-qPCR was further employed to verify potential binding sites/its relative binding levels between ERα and the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4 (Fig. 2J, K). Initially, by accessing the UCSC public gene database (University of California, Santa Cruz, https://genome.ucsc.edu), we identified the potential interactions between ERα and the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4. According to this, we predicted two potential binding sites between ERα and the promoter regions genes of Cd36/Tlr4, respectively, and then designed the ChIP-qPCR primers for ERα. Indeed, the current results of ChIP-qPCR demonstrated that when compared with the Ctrl condition, 90 µm of ZEN significantly enhanced the interactions between ER-α and the promoter region genes, including Cd36 (Fig. 2J) and Tlr4 (Fig. 2K). Using IFM (Fig. 2L)/PLA (Fig. 2M) for the interaction between ERα and CD36, we consistently observed that ZEN exposure significantly increased ERα-CD36 interaction in the nucleus. Combining with these above results (Fig. 2J–M), we concluded that ZEN exposure significantly increased the bindings of ERα on the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4. It suggests that both the lipid transporter CD36 and the intrinsic immune receptor TLR4 are potential targets for ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity.

Cd36 deficiency protects against ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity

Using mouse endometrial stromal cells (mEC, primary culture cells) as cell models, we evaluated in vitro effects of CD36 deficiency on ZEN-induced lipotoxicity. As indicated in the results of this study, ZEN exposure resulted in impairments of cell viability/mitochondrial function, an increase of MitoROS, upregulated mRNA levels of key genes (Cd36, Ppar-γ, Cpt1a, and Fasn) involved in lipid metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, ZEN-induced abnormalities in mEC cells could be prevented by ShRNA-Cd36 (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Subsequently, Cd36-knockout (Cd36-KO) female mice were used for assessing in vivo effects of Cd36 deficiency on ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity (see experimental design in Fig. 3A). Consistent with the findings from female pigs during the phase I (Fig. 2C–E), we also confirmed that ZEN exposure contributed to significant increases in vulvar area, uterine size/weight, inflammatory lesions, and mitochondrial swelling in reproductive organs from wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 3B–D). Furthermore, ZEN-induced female reproductive toxicity was linked with increases in plasma concentrations of TG, TC and LDL (Fig. 3E). Using uterine lipidomics for female mice, we further revealed that ZEN exposure resulted in the most prominent increase in lipid species, such as DAG and GPs (Fig. 3F, G), all of which were consistent with the findings from female pigs (Fig. 2G, H). For the quantitative analysis of other major lipid classes, please see Supplementary Fig. 6. Again, when zooming in on the DAG, it showed that DAG 16:0-16:1, DAG 18:0-18:1, and DAG 18:0-20:4 also belonged to the most three striking species, thus indicating a similar pattern of changes in content/concentration as the total DAG class upon ZEN exposure (Fig. 3G, I). Strikingly, ZEN-induced significant changes in lipid species could be partly prevented in Cd36-KO mice (Fig. 3F–I, Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, we observed that ZEN exposure significantly upregulated mRNA/protein expressions of most key enzymes (LPIN1 and ATGL) involved in lipid metabolism. And these ZEN-induced changes could be preserved in Cd36-KO mice (Fig. 3J, K). Taken together, the above findings suggested that Cd36 deficiency ameliorated ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity.

Next, by using Cd36-knockout female mice as animal models, we consistently assessed expression levels of ERα/CD36/TLR4, the ERα-CD36 interaction, the CD36-TLR4 interaction and pro-inflammatory signaling actions (Fig. 4). Consistent with the findings from female pigs (Fig. 2), higher expressions of ERα/CD36/TLR4 were observed in WT mice upon ZEN exposure when compared with those in the Ctrl group (Fig. 4A, B for Western blots, Fig. 4C, D for IFM). Additionally, increased ERα-CD36 interactions in the nucleus, as indicated by IFM (Fig. 4C)/PLA (Fig. 4E), were observed in ZEN-treated mice than those in the Ctrl group. Furthermore, a higher interaction between CD36 and TLR4, as evidenced by IFM (Fig. 4D)/PLA (Fig. 4F), was also indicated in ZEN-treated mice than those in the Ctrl group. Given that an increase of the CD36-TLR4 association is at the start of the pro-inflammatory response, we subsequently investigated that ZEN-treated mice showed significant increases in phosphorylated p42/44-ERK1/2 and p38-MAPK (Fig. 4G). Discussion

In this study, the obtained results led us to formulate the following major findings: (i) For female pigs during the phase I, ZEN exposure resulted in high residues of ZEN and its major metabolites (α-ZOL and β-ZOL) in reproductive organs, subsequently increased the binding of ERα on the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4 in the nucleus, thus contributing to significant increases in expressions of CD36/TLR4 as well as their interactions; (ii) This increased lipid transporter CD36 not only enhanced DAG accumulation but also interacted with inflammatory receptor TLR4. This resulted in DAG-induced lipotoxicity and subsequent activation of MAPK signaling pathway, followed by increases in productions of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β), eventually leading to reproductive toxicity; (iii) Furthermore, by using Cd36 knockout mice and TLR4 specific inhibitor TAK-242, we consistently identified that ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity was dependent of the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis. (iv) Finally, for female pigs during the phase II, supplements of ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 successfully lowered residues of ZEN and its major metabolites in reproductive organs. And, it then inhibited expression of ERα/CD36/TLR4, as well as their interactions, thereby protecting against ZEN-induced lipotoxicity. Taken together, these observations above illustrated that ZEN-induced female reproductive lipotoxicity, including disturbed lipid metabolism and aggravated inflammation, was in a ERα-CD36/TLR4 dependent manner. In the following, the below issues will be discussed in greater detail.

Using the HPLC-MS/MS assay, we firstly observed that higher residues of ZEN and its major metabolites, including α-ZOL and β-ZOL, were highly accumulated in reproductive organs from ZEN-treated female pigs during the phase I/II (Figs. 1B, 7B). Consistent with previous findings from pig models6,11, the present study further confirms that the reproductive system is a key target organ for ZEN to exert its toxic effects, such as significant increases in uterine dimensions and inflammatory lesions (Fig. 1C–E, Fig. 7C–E). As an exogenous estrogen, ZEN contributes to the imbalance of serum concentrations of sex hormones, including luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone and E2, all of which directly interfere with target genes through competitively binding with ERs, eventually resulting in reproductive disorders in both farm animals and humans2,20,23. There is considerable selectivity for the ERs affinity to bind with various estrogenic ligands, such as estrogen-like chemicals and phytoestrogens24. Furthermore, ZEN’s high affinity for ERs is considered as a major factor contributing to its adverse effects, yet the precise mechanisms by which ZEN influences ER expression, particularly ERα and ERβ, are not fully understood. Considering the effects of agonists and/or antagonists on the transcription of ERs, the ERs subtypes recruit various ligands/coactivators to exert their functional properties25. In this study, we surprisingly observed that ZEN exposure led to significant increases in ERα expression but not ERβ (Figs. 2I, 4A, 6A and 8A), indicating that ZEN was a complete agonist for ERα. ZEN has commonly been believed to bind to two estrogen receptors, including ERα and ERβ, with a high affinity for ERα, thereby leading to estrogen-like effects by stimulating gene transcription26. Nevertheless, further research is needed to elucidate the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying impacts of ZEN on ERs in the reproductive system.

These ZEN-induced reproductive abnormalities in pigs were closely linked with increases in plasma levels of lipids, the content of DAG, and the activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and subsequent increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines, all of which could be referred to as lipotoxicity (Figs. 1, 2, 7 and 8). Our investigation into the mechanisms behind impacts of ZEN on lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses revealed that ZEN exposure activates CD36-mediated lipid metabolism and TLR4 signaling pathways in reproductive organs (Fig. 2A, B). Furthermore, the present finding also confirmed that expressions of CD36/TLR4 as well as their interactions were significantly upregulated unpon ZEN exposure (Fig. 2C–F), indicating the lipid transporter CD36 and the intrinsic immune receptor TLR4 might be key targets in ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity. As described earlier, ZEN exposure significantly upregulated the protein level of ERα in reproductive organs (Fig. 2I), and we then investigated the potential interactions between the transcription factor ERα and CD36/TLR4. Intriguingly, our current findings illustrated that ZEN exposure led to increases in the bindings of ERα on the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4 in the nucleus, demonstrating that both Cd36 and Tlr4 genes were potential targets for ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity (Fig. 2J–M). To support this, studies with Cd36 knockout mice and/or TLR4 specific inhibitor TAK-242 consistently observed that ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity, such as increases in lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses, was indeed dependent on the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6).

Next, we examined how the upregulation of CD36/TLR4 upon ZEN exposure led to lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses. As described earlier, ZEN exposure resulted in significant increases in plasma concentrations of lipids (TG, TC, and LDL), lipopolysaccharides, and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, and IL-1β). And, they then attracted CD36/TLR4 to induce DAG accumulation and inflammatory responses in reproductive organs. Mechanistically, CD36 is not only a facilitator of fatty acid transport across the sarcolemma but also involves in other roles, including initiation of fatty acid signaling via binding with the inflammatory receptor TLR427,28. For the inflammatory receptor TLR4, it by itself recognizes lipids/lipopolysaccharides/pro-inflammatory cytokines as key ligands, while needs CD36 as a co-receptor for the activation in response to plasma fatty acid oversupply28. Subsequently, the TLR4 activation contributes to the induction of cellular inflammation through the pathway of stress-related kinases (p38-MAPK and P42/44-ERK), resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β)29. This CD36/TLR4-induced pro-inflammatory signaling action can aggravate the maladaptive effect of CD36-induced lipid accumulation on the functioning of ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity. Taken together, our findings suggested that targeting the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis could protect against ZEN-induced female reproductive lipotoxicity.

Microbial ZEN-degrading enzymes are regarded as the good and/or feasible tool for ZEN-detoxifying, as they can be genetic engineered to enhance its enzymatic activity and stability30. Regarding the recombinant ZEN-degrading microorganisms, in addition to its key roles in food industrial production, it is also employed to degrade ZEN itself and/or its metabolites in the gastrointestinal tract so as to protect against reproductive disorders6. Recently, a probiotic strain of Lactobacillus reuteri Pg4-expressing ZHD101, which was isolated from the intestinal tract of broilers, surprisingly decreased the concentration of ZEN from 4.5 mg/L to 0.03 mg/L in the MRS broth after 14 h incubation31. Also, Takahashi-Ando et al., a lactone hydrolase ZHD101, which was extracted from Clonostachys rosea, successfully hydrolyzed the lactone bond of ZEN to generate non-toxic chemicals32. Moreover, the lactonase encoding the Zenc gene, which was overexpressed in Pichia pastoris, was used for maize byproducts, distiller’s dried grains with soluble and corn bran, and the concentration of ZEN dropped to 11%, 29%, and 95%, respectively33. However, the degradation mechanism of recombinant microorganisms still remains unclear, and the in vivo toxicity of the degradation byproducts generated by microbial ZEN-degrading enzymes is also unknown, all of which limit its further application in farm animals. Recently, our laboratory successfully generated recombinant B. subtilis 168-expressing ZEN degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 (the Bs-Z6 strain). After 12 h of incubation at 37 °C, the degradation effects of and the relative enzyme activity of the recombinant Bs-Z6 strain on ZEN-contaminated diet showed 92%, 219.02 U mL−1, respectively6. And, we then found that supplements of recombinant Bs-Z6 strain to pigs counteracted ZEN-induced toxicity on the immune defense in the reproductive-immune axis, which was highly linked with altered intestinal microbial-derived metabolites, including decreased butyrate production and increased lipopolysaccharides production6. Following this, the present study further found that supplements of ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 to female pigs during the phase II successfully lowered residues of ZEN and/or its major metabolites and ERα expression in reproductive organs, and then decreased the content of DAG and deactivated productions of pro-inflammatory cytokines through inhibiting the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis (Figs. 7 and 8). Altogether, our findings revealed detoxification mechanisms of ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 on female reproductive lipotoxicity, and then assessed in vivo the feasibility of enzymatic removal of ZEN from ZEN-contaminated diet.

The present study has some limitations, as follows: (i) The research focused on reproductive organs, potentially overlooking abnormalities in other critical target organs such as the kidneys and liver, which were not extensively examined in this study; (ii) Although our studies with CD36 whole knockout mice and/or TLR4 specific inhibitor consistently identified that ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity was dependent of the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis, and CD36 and/or TLR4 conditional knockout in reproductive organs should be considered in future study; (iii) Our untargeted lipidomics analysis of uterine tissues from female pigs and mice identified DAG as a potentially harmful lipid species involved in ZEN-induced lipotoxicity. However, additional research is necessary to fully understand the underlying mechanisms of DAG’s role in this process.

In conclusion, employing female pigs and mice, this study reveals that ZEN exposure contributes to increases in CD36-mediated lipid accumulation and TLR4-induced inflammation in reproductive organs, all of which are referred to as reproductive lipotoxicity. Mechanistically, ZEN-upregulated ERα directly binds the promoter region genes of Cd36/Tlr4 in the nucleus, and then results in increases in expressions of CD36/TLR4 and their interactions, thereby leading to lipid accumulation and subsequent activation of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In this following, studies with CD36 deficiency and/or TLR4 inhibition for mice, and supplements of ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 to pigs consistently identified that ZEN-induced reproductive lipotoxicity is dependent on the ERα-CD36/TLR4 axis. These findings not only clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying ZEN-induced reproductive toxicity but also provide practical strategies for mitigating mycotoxin-related hazards in animal production, thereby contributing to the improvement of farm animal health and food safety.

Materials and methods

Reagents, antibodies and primers

Detailed information on chemical reagents, primary/secondary antibodies and primers was indicated in (Supplementary Tables 1–3, Supplementary Data 1).

Animal experiment design

Animal experiment I for female pigs during the phase I: feeding and management of female pigs were indicated in a recent publications6. In this study, ZEN (the purity over 98.5% by the method of HPLC) was provided by Prof. Changpo Sun (Standards and Quality Center of National Food and Strategic Reserves Administration, Beijing, China). The corn husk hydrolysate with and without ZEN was provided by Neimenggu Fufeng Biotechnologies Co., Ltd (Neimenggu, China). A total of forty-eight Landrace-Duroc female pigs (the body weight= 19.97 ± 0.73 kg), which were provided by the Shenzhen PremixINVE Nutrition Co., LTD (Shenzhen, China), were randomly designed into the following groups (n = 6 each pen, 4 pens per group) that were provided: (i) the basal diet with 5% corn husk hydrolysate that was not contaminated with ZEN and other mycotoxins (the Ctrl group; the concentrations of ZEN and other mycotoxins, including deoxynivalenol and aflatoxin B1 were not detected). (ii) the basal diet with 5% corn husk hydrolysate that was contaminated with ZEN (the ZEN group; the concentration of ZEN in per kg diet was 3,684.83 ± 40.52 μg, and the consumed amount of ZEN that each pig was approximately 4613.96 μg per day. However, the concentrations of other mycotoxins, including deoxynivalenol and aflatoxin B1 were not detected). The entire experiment was consistently conducted for two weeks. In the end, two female pigs each pen (four pens per group) were randomly chosen for sacrificing and for sample collection, and the power of each group was therefore eight.

Animal experiment II for Cd36-knockout mice: In this study, Cd36-knockout mice were kindly provided by Prof. Lizhong Liu (Department of Physiology, Health Science Center, School of Medicine, Shenzhen University, Shenzhen, China). Female wide-type mice (WT) at the age of eight weeks were intraperitoneally injected with: (i) 0.5% DMSO in PBS (the Ctrl group, n = 10), (ii) 3 mg/kg.bw/d ZEN (the ZEN group, n = 10), or (iii) female Cd36 knockout mice were intraperitoneally injected with 3 mg/kg.bw/d ZEN (the CD36-KO group, n = 10) for two weeks. After the two-week trial, the blood samples and reproductive organs from all animals were collected for further experiment.

Animal experiment III for TLR4-specific inhibitor TAK-242: female C57BL/6 J mice at the age of eight weeks were intraperitoneally injected with: (i) 0.5% DMSO in PBS (the Ctrl group, n = 10), (ii) 3 mg/kg.bw/d ZEN (the ZEN group, n = 10), or (iii) 3 mg/kg.bw/d ZEN + 0.3 mg/kg.bw/d TLR4-specific inhibitor TAK-242 (the ZEN + TAK-242 group, n = 10) for two weeks. After the two-weeks trial, the blood sample and reproductive organs from all animals were collected for further experiment.

Animal experiment IV for female pigs during the phase II: In this study, ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 (purity >98.5% by HPLC method), which was expressed in Pichia pastoris by generating the plasmid including three optimized expression cassettes of zlhy-6 codon and signal peptides, was previously provided by Prof. Changpo Sun (Standards and Quality Center of National Food and Strategic Reserves Administration, Beijing, China). In total, seventy-two Landrace-Duroc female pigs during the phase II (body weight: 8.87 ± 0.43 kg), which were provided by the Shenzhen PremixINVE Nutrition Co., LTD (Shenzhen, China), were randomly designed into the following groups (n = 6 each pen, 4 pens per group) that were provided: (i) the basal diet with 8% corn husk hydrolysate (the Ctrl group; the concentrations of ZEN and other mycotoxins including deoxynivalenol and aflatoxin B1 were not detected), (ii) the basal diet with 8% ZEN-contaminated corn husk hydrolysate (the ZEN group; the concentration of ZEN in per kg diet is 1687.42 ± 58.93 μg, and the consumed amount of ZEN that each pig was approximately 899.17 μg), (iii) the ZEN group supplemented with 300 mg/kg of ZEN-degrading enzyme ZLHY-6 that was expressed in Pichia pastoris Strain GS11 (the ZEN + ZLHY6 group). The entire experiment was consistently carried out for two weeks. In the end, two female pigs each pen (four pens per group) were randomly chosen for sacrificing and sample collection, and the power of each group was therefore eight.

We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All experimental animals were euthanized in accordance with established humane guidelines. Pigs were humanely slaughtered following the Chinese National Standard GB/T 22569-2008 (Technical Specification for the Humane Slaughter of Pigs), which involved immediate electrical stunning to induce unconsciousness, followed by exsanguination via sticking within 15 s. For rodent subjects, mice were euthanized by an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg) for anesthesia, followed by cervical dislocation.

Isolation and culture of mouse endometrial stromal cells (mEC)

Mouse endometrial stromal cells (mEC, primary culture cell) were cultured in cell medium containing DMEM/F12 medium (Biosharp, Hefei, China), 10% fetal bovine serum (Newzerum, Christchurch, New Zealand) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, Waltham, USA), and then incubated in 5% CO2 humidifier at 37 °C. The mEC were separated from mice’s uterus according to a modified protocol34. Briefly, mice were estrus synchronization by i.p. injection of 5 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotrophin (PMSG, Ningbo Hormone Product Co., Ltd, Ningbo, China) at day 1, and a further i.p. injection of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG, Ningbo Hormone Product Co., Ltd) at day 3 following copulation. At day 7, successful mating mice were sacrificed and uterus were dissected to divide mEC in biosafety cabinet. Uterus were washed twice by Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS, Biosharp) and then cut into pieces in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. Meat pastes were delivered into 5 mL tubes and a mixture of tryptase (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co.,Ltd., Beijing, China) and collagenase (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co.,Ltd.) with 1:5 ratio was also added to disperse tissues. Afterwards, samples were incubated in humidifier for 60 min, and centrifuged at 4000 × g for 3 min. Cells in deposits were suspended by cell medium, and then cultivated into 10 cm dishes. Medium was changed daily and cells were used to do further experiments when cell confluency achieved to 80%.

Culture and passage of Ishikawa cells

Ishikawa cells, a human endometrial adenocarcinoma cell line of epithelial origin, were purchased from Cell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China), and were also cultured according to a modified protocol34. Ishikawa cells were cultured in phenol red-free minimum essential medium, supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/ml streptomycin. Ishikawa cells were used within 10 passages according to the provider’s recommendation to avoid changes in cell characteristics, including down-regulation of estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor expression. Medium was changed daily and cells were used to do further experiments when cell confluency achieved to 80%.

Gene interference with Cd36 RNAi

Cd36-knockdown (Cd36 RNAi) cell line was established by transfecting Cd36 shRNA lentiviral construct and targeting sequence Cd36-Top strand 5′-CACCGCAAATGCAAAGAAGGAAAGCCGAAGCTTTCCTTCTTTGCATTTGC-3′, Cd36-Bottom strand 5′-AAAAGCAAATGCAAAGAAGGAAAGCTTCGGCTTTCCTTCTTTGCATTTGC-3′ or scrambled shRNA lentivirus as control (NC) to mEC. All Cd36 RNAi and scrambled shRNA lentivirus were purchased from Vigene Biosciences Co., Ltd (Shandong, China).

Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS) for mEC

Once cell confluence reached above 70%, different concentrations of chemicals were supplemented into each condition. After 24 h incubation, the medium containing DCFH-DA (Beyotime Biotech. Inc., Shanghai, China) was added into each well, and subsequently was incubated in the humidifier for 20 min. Before cell images were captured using immnuo-fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), it would be washed three times using the basic medium without fetal bovine serum. Immnuo-fluorescence intensity of cells per condition were analyzed and quantified by ImageJ Fiji software.

Detection of mitochondrial membrane potential for mEC

Mitochondrial membrane potential for cells was measured by the JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (JC-1) (US Everbright Inc, J6004s, Suzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the medium was removed, and JC-1 working solution was added to cover the cell surface and placed in a 37 °C cell incubator and incubated for 20 min, the staining solution was removed and the cells were washed once with 1 × PBS buffer or medium. The culture medium was added again. The live cells were observed and analyzed with fluorescence microscope.

Detection of zearalenone/its metabolites residues by UPLC-MS/MS

The residues of ZEN itself and its major metabolites in uterine tissues from female pigs during the two phases were detected by Pribolab Pte. Ltd (Qingdao, China) in accordance with a modified protocol6. Briefly, 1.0 g of uterine sample was put into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, and then 5000 U β-Glucuronidase buffer solution and 10 ml Acetic Acid-Sodium Acetate buffer solution (0.05 mol/L, pH = 5.0) were also added into each tube. After mixing, this was hatched in an incubator at 37 °C for 12 h. In this following, 200 μL of 1.0 mol/L NaOH solution and 10 mL of acetonitrile were also added into the buffer, and shaken for 30 min, and centrifuged at above 5000 g/min at 20 °C) for 5 min. Subsequently, 10 mL of supernatant was transferred into a 50 mL centrifuge tube, and then diluted using 30 mL of 1 × PBS buffer. Eventually, adjusting pH to 7.40 in the buffer with 1.0 mol/L of NaOH solution, and was then filtered with a 0.22 μm pore-size membrane filter (Jinlong Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China).

For the procedure of HPLC-MS/MS, the separation was conducted in the Hypersil Gold AQ C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 3 μm particle size; Thermo Scientific, Waltham MA, USA) with a TSQ QuantisTM triple-stage quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the column temperature was reset as 30 °C. The chromatographic elution of ZEN and its major metabolites (zearalanone, α-ZOL, β-ZOL, α-ZAL, β-ZAL) was detected using the mobile phase, which consisted of part A-acetonitrile and part B-ultrapure water. In more detail, for the separation of both ZEN and zearalanone, an isocratic elution at a 0.4 mL/min flow rate was started at 25% of part B and was then kept for 1 min. Subsequently, 75% of part A at a 0.4 mL/min flow rate was conducted at 1 min, and then kept for 6 min. Afterwards, 40% of part B at a 0.4 mL/min flow rate was started at 6 min, and then kept for 9 min. The injection volume was 5 μL. The mass spectrometer was performed using a turbo ion-spray ionization source configured for electrospray ionization in the positive/negative switching ion modes, and the acquisition was then conducted by multiple reaction monitoring with a dwell time of 100 ms. The ion voltages for the positive/ negative ion modes were 3500 V. Resolutions at Q1 and Q3 were set to units. Argon gas (99% purity) was used in the electrospray ionization source and the collision cell. Optimal collision energies (and cone voltages were chosen for each transition and retention time.

Untargeted lipidomics analysis for uterine tissues

Detections of untargeted-uterine lipidomics from both female pigs and mice were performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) in accordance with a modified protocol27. Briefly, 0.75 mL of methanol was added to a 10 mg sample, which was placed into a glass tube with a Teflon lined cap, and the tube was then vortexed. Next, 2.5 mL of methyl tert-butyl ether was added, and the mixture was incubated for 1 h at room temperature in a shaker. Afterwards, phase separation was induced by adding 0.625 ml of MS-grade water. Upon 10 min of incubation at room temperature, the sample was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min. The upper (organic) phase was collected, and the lower phase was re-extracted with 1 mL of the solvent mixture (methyl tert-butyl ether/methanol/water (10: 3: 2.5, v/v/v)), and then collected the upper phase. Finally, combined organic phases were dried and dissolved in 100 μL of isopropanol for storage, and further analyzed by UHPLC-MS/MS. The UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher, Germany) coupled with an Orbitrap Q ExactiveTM HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, Germany) in Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Samples were injected onto a Thermo Accucore C30 column (150 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) using a 20 min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The column temperature was set at 40 °C. Mobile phase buffer A was acetonitrile/water (6/4) with 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid, whereas buffer B was acetonitrile/isopropanol (1/9) with 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid. The solvent gradient was set as follows: 30% B, initial; 30% B, 2 min; 43% B, 5 min; 55% B, 5.1 min; 70% B, 11 min; 99% B, 16 min; 30% B, 18 min. The Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer was operated in positive [negative] polarity mode with sheath ga: 40 psi, sweep gas: 0 L/min, auxiliary gas rate: 10 L/min [7 L/min], spray voltage: 3.5 kV, capillary temperature: 320 °C, heater temperature: 350 °C, S-Lens RF level: 50, scan range: 114-1700 m/z, automatic gain control target: 3e6, normalized collision energy: 22 eV; 24 eV; 28 eV, Injection time: 100 ms, Isolation window: 1 m/z, automatic gaincontrol target (MS2): 2e5, dynamic exclusion: 6 s.

The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS/MS were processed using the Compound Discoverer 3.01 (CD3.1, Thermo Fisher) to perform peak alignment, peak picking, and quantitation for each metabolite. The main parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; actual mass tolerance, 5 ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal/noise ratio, 3; and minimum intensity, 100,000. Afterwards, peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity. The normalized data were used to predict the molecular formula based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. And then peaks were matched with the Lipidmaps and Lipidblast databases to obtain accurate qualitative and relative quantitative results. Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software R (version R-3.4.3), Python (Python 2.7.6) and CentOS (CentOS release 6.6). For clustering heat maps, the data were normalized using Z-scores of the intensity areas of differential metabolites, and were then plotted by Pheatmap package in R language. Statistically significant of correlation between differential metabolites were calculated by cor.mtest(mat) value in R language. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant and correlation plots were plotted by corrplot package in R language.

Assessments of plasma lipid profiles

The concentrations of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) in the plasma from both female pigs and mice were detected with the automatic biochemical analyzer (7170, Hitachi Corp., Tokyo, Japan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guides from corresponding commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Assessment of uterus morphology

Uterine slides from both female pigs and mice were sampled based on our earlier studies6. Briefly, the well fixing uterine samples were embedded into paraffin, and then sliced and deparaffinized. The uterine slides were rehydrated and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Eventually, BA210 Digital microcamera system (Motic, Xiamen, China) was used for imaging and quantifying.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) for the uterus

Using the TEM assay, uterine tissues from both female pigs and mice were sampled for comparing its subcellular structure. Briefly, uterine slides were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde solution, and subsequently re-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. Afterwards, uterine slides were dehydrated and permeated prior to embedding in Epon. In this following, uterine slides were stained using uranyl acetate for 15 min, followed by lead citrate for 2 min at room temperature. The uterine slides were analyzed by JEM-1400FLASH TEM (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Analysis of RNA-seq data for reproductive organs

The RNA-seq data was analyzed for disclosing whether ZEN-induced toxicity in reproductive organs from female pigs during the phase I were also characterized by changes in lipid metabolism and inflammation. Here, different expression genes (DEGs) in both the uterus and the ovary were firstly screened, and subsequently the enrichment analysis of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathways were investigated. Furthermore, by using the software of Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), the genes were ranked based on the degree of differential expression in the two types of samples. Finally, it was tested whether the predefined gene set was enriched at the top or bottom of the ranking list.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP experiments were conducted using the ChromaFlash™ High-Sensitivity ChIP Kit (Epigentek, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instruction. Initially, when we collected samples from cell plate, we ensured that each group had more than 2 × 106 cells (Ishikawa cells). The cells were fixed with complete medium containing 1% PFA for 10 min and then washed once with 1× PBS. Cells were then lysed for 10 min using the Lysis Buffer with occasional vortex, followed by an additional 10 min of lysis with ChIP Buffer. Next, the lysed chromatin was fragmented using a sonicator (SONICS&MATERIALS, US), with a program set at 180 WATTS, 20 kHz, 10 s on and 40 s off, for a total of 10 cycles, to ensure the chromatin length was dispersed between 200 bp and 1000 bp. To prepare input samples, 1 μg chromatin from each group was mixed with an equal volume of DNA Release Buffer (DRB) and 2 μL Proteinase K, and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min, following 95 °C for 10 min. These samples were diluted 10 folds to serve as the input template for qPCR. To obtain DNA-protein complex, 1 μg of ChIP-specific antibodies including anti-RNA Polymerase II (positive control), Non-Immune IgG (negative control) and ERα (Santa, Bolivia, United States) were added into the microtiter plate, and the mixture was incubated on a shaker at 100 rpm for 2 h. Afterwards, 5 μg of chromatin was correspondingly added to each well and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The reaction wells were washed, and 40 μL of DRB-RNase A was then added to per well and incubated at 42 °C for 30 min. To release DNA fragments, 2 μL of proteinase K was added to each well and incubated at 60 °C for 45 min. The samples were then heated to 95 °C for 15 min in a PCR amplifier, followed by DNA purification using DNA Elution Buffer (DEB) for subsequent ChIP-qPCR experiments. With respect to this, candidate binding sites of ERα on the promoter region of Cd36/Tlr4 genes were selected and specific primers were designed (Supplementary Table 3). The assay of RT-qPCR was used to amplify and quantify the immunoprecipitated DNA in each group according to the reaction condition.

Co-immunoprecipitations (Co-IPs) for CD36-TLR4 interaction

The protocol of Co-IPs for uterine tissues was performed with a modified protocol27. Briefly, 500 µg samples were hatched overnight at 4 °C with either control IgG or specific primary antibodies recognizing CD36 (rabbit anti CD36, Abcam, Cat#ab133625)/TLR4 (mouse anti TLR4, Santa Cruz, Cat#SC293072). Afterwards, the target protein-antibody complex was coupled to Sepharose-4 beads for 4 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed using lysis buffe three time, boiled in 1.5 × sample buffer, centrifuged 8000 × g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was used for Western blotting. Both CD36 and TLR4 proteins were detected by Western blotting.

Immunofluorescence microscopy (IFM) for ERα and CD36/TLR4

For determining co-localizations of CD36-TLR4 and of ERα-CD36 in uterine slides from both female pigs and mice, the direct method of IFM was employed in accordance with our earlier descriptions27. Briefly, 4 µm thicknesses of uterine slides were dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, rehydrated, dewaxed and antigen repaired. After draining off 1× PBS, uterine slides were circled by a PAP pen (Cat#AR1181, Boster Biological technology, Wuhan, China). Afterwards, the sections were incubated in Tissue AutoFluo Quencher A solution (Servicebio, G1221) at room temperature for 30 min, followed by washing with 1 × PBS three times, and then incubated in normal goat serum solution (Cat#ZLI-9056, Zhong Shan -Golden Bridge Biological Technology CO., LTD, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the coverslips were incubated with primary antibodies rabbit anti-CD36 (1: 200, Cat#ab133625, Abcam) and mouse anti-TLR4 (1: 200, Cat#ab22048, Abcam) or mouse anti-ERα (1: 200, Cat#SC8002, Santa Cruz) for overnight at 4 °C. After washing with 1× PBS twice, the coverslips were incubated with Alexa Fluor 555 mouse anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1: 1000, Cat#4409, Cell signaling technology, USA) and Alexa Fluor rabbit anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1: 1000, Cat#4412, Cell signaling technology, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Additionally, for determining co-localizations of CD36-TLR4 on cell-surface, the coverslips were further incubated with Caveolin-1 antibody (1: 200, Cat#31411, Cell signaling technology, USA) for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing with 1 × PBS twice, the coverslips were incubated with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Cat#D9542, Sigma Alrich, USA) for 20 min at room temperature. Afterwards, the sections were incubated in Tissue AutoFluo Quencher B solution (Servicebio, G1221) at room temperature for 5 min, followed by washing with 1× PBS three times. Finally, the coverslips were sealed with nail polish, and then detected under a Nikon A1R confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The co-localizations of CD36 and TLR4 on cell surface, and of CD36 and ERα in the nucleus were quantified using ImageJ Fiji software. As for negative controls, only Alexa Fluor rabbit anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody was incubated for investigating IFM for CD36 and TLR4/ ERα in vivo.

Duolink® Proximity-ligation assay (PLA™) for interactions of CD36-TLR4 and of ERα-CD36

The PLA™ was conducted by Duolink®In Situ Orange Starter Kit Mouse/Rabbit (DUO92102-1KT) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The primary antibodies couples involving the PLA protocol were used for assessing, as follows: (i) the CD36-TLR4 interaction, including rabbit anti-CD36 (1: 200, Cat#ab133625, Abcam) and mouse anti-TLR4 (1: 200, Cat#ab22048, Abcam). (ii) the ERα-CD36 interaction, including mouse anti-ERα (1: 200, Cat#SC8002, Santa Cruz) and rabbit anti CD36; (iii) As for negative controls of Duolink® PLA staining above, only primary antibody of CD36 and of ERα was used for assessing the interactions of CD36-TLR4 and of ERα-CD36, respectively. In this study, the detailed protocols for Duolink® PLA™ for interactions of CD36-TLR4 and of ERα-CD36 were performed with a modified protocol27.

Quantitative real time-PCR (qRT-PCR) for gene expression analyses

The protocol of RT-qPCR was performed with a modified protocol35. In more detail, cell lysates/heart lysates were incubated in 1 ml of TRIzol regent, followed by three rounds of freeze-thaw cycles, and then homogenized. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and 900 µl of clear supernatant (not the lipid layer on the top) was transferred to an RNase-free tube. And, the supernatant was then added with 200 µl of chloroform, followed by vigorous vortexing for 15 s. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, 450 µl of the upper aqueous layer was transferred to an RNase-free tube. Afterwards, the RNA was precipitated by adding 450 µl of isopropanol, followed with centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol in DEPC-treated water, and once with 100% ethanol, and then dissolved with 20 µl of DEPC-treated water. The concentration of total RNA was determined by a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). A total of 1 µg of RNA was added with 1 µl Random Primer, diluted with DEPC-treated water to a final volume of 12 µl, and then heated at 65 °C for 5 min, chilled on ice immediately. Next, the RNA was added with the 5 × Reaction Buffer, Ribolock RNase inhibitor, 10 mM dNTP mix and Revertacid M-MuLVRT (all form the RNeasy Plus Universal Kit), and was then incubated at 25 °C for 5 min, 42 °C for 60 min, 70 °C for 5 min on a thermocycler. The reverse-transcribed cDNA was quantified with SYBR Green quantitative PCR 10 Master 9Mix on a 77900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with the following programs: incubating at 50 °C for 2 min; pre-denaturing at 95 °C for 10 min; denaturing at 95 °C for 15 s; annealing and extending at 60 °C for 1 min in each cycle for a total of 40 cycles. All primer sequences were found in Supplementary Table 2. The mRNA levels of target genes were calculated using the comparative ΔΔct method using the QuantStudio Design & Analysis Software.

Western blots

The protocol of Western blots was performed with a modified protocol36. In more detail, samples were prepared in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 500 mM sodium chloride, 1% NP40, 20% glycerol, 5 mM EDTA and 1 mM PMSF), supplemented with a cocktail of Roche protease inhibitors. The homogenates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were then used for subsequent analyses. Protein concentration was determined by the BCA assay. Protein lysates were denatured in Laemmli buffer (60 mM Tris, pH 6.8, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.05% bromophenol blue, 0.7 M β-mercaptoethanol), resolved by 10% Tris/Glycine SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. Primary antibodies were diluted in TBS containing 0.05% Tween, 5% BSA and 0.02% sodium azide. Membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. Primary and secondary antibody dilutions can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Results were visualized with enhanced chemiluminescence Western blotting substrates (Bio-Rad). In this study, the actual size of the molecular weight (MW) standards resolved with experimental protein lysates was displayed.

Statistics and reproducibility

The statistical analysis of the current study was performed using GraphPad 8.0 PRISM® and IBM SPSS Statistics 23 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). For group samples with n ≥ 6, the Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used. Data with a n ≥ 6 that passed the normality test were then analyzed using the parametric t-test with Welch’s correction (unpaired and two-tailed) for two groups, or by parametric one-way ANOVA for more than two groups with the adjustment for multiple comparisons with Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data (n ≥ 6) or when n < 6, they were analyzed using non-parametric tests, either by Kolmogorov–Smirnov t-test (unpaired and two-tailed) for two groups, by one-way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis test for more than two groups, with the adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Dunn’s post hoc test. All values are expressed as mean ± SEM, with at least three independent experiments, as provided in figure legends. Significance is indicated in the figures as P < 0.05.
