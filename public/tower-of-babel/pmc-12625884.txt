Phenotypically driven subgroups of primary antiphospholipid syndrome-associated thrombocytopenia display distinct outcomes: a prospective cohort study with cluster analysis
Authors: Jun Li, Ruijie Mo, Can Huang, Yangzhong Zhou, Liying Peng, Hui Jiang, Yu Shi, Yufang Ding, Yan Zhao, Xiaofeng Zeng, Mengtao Li, Jiuliang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify antiphospholipid antibody-associated thrombocytopenia (aPLs-TP) phenotypes and assess their clinical outcomes.MethodsThis single-centre, prospective cohort study (January 2012 to April 2024) consecutively enrolled patients with aPLs-TP from Peking Union Medical College Hospital. Inclusion required persistent aPL positivity (≥12 weeks apart) and platelet (PLT) count <100×109/L twice, excluding secondary causes. Demographic aPL profiles and clinical outcomes (thrombosis, pregnancy morbidity, microangiopathy and valve disease) were analysed. Hierarchical clustering and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis were performed.ResultsA total of 123 patients (65.9% female, mean age 36.0 years) were consecutively enrolled in the study. Median PLT count was 50.0×109/L. Three clusters were identified: cluster 1 (n=35, all male, median PLT 67.0×109/L) consisted of men with smoking history, hyperhomocysteinaemia and diabetes, demonstrating the highest rate of atherothrombotic and valvular events; cluster 2 (n=51, all female, median PLT 60.0×109/L) included females with recurrent pregnancy morbidity and mild anaemia; and cluster 3 (n=37, 81.1% female, median PLT 27.0×109/L) comprised patients with isolated severe thrombocytopenia with the lowest rate of complete remission. Analysis of event-free survival for key clinical outcomes differed significantly among clusters at 5 years (p=0.026): cluster 1 at 66.9% (95% CI 52.50 to 85.24), cluster 2 at 45.85% (95% CI 32.41 to 64.86) and cluster 3 at 88.68% (95% CI 78.80 to 99.80).ConclusionsSignificant heterogeneity exists in patients with aPLs-TP, thus making PLT count alone an inadequate predictor of clinical phenotypes and prognosis. Subgroup analysis leveraging distinct clinical features is essential to develop individualised treatment strategies and improve outcomes.

Introduction

Antiphospholipid antibody-associated thrombocytopenia (aPLs-TP), a common yet under-recognised clinical manifestation in patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), exhibits significant clinical and immunological heterogeneity.1 As a multimechanism-driven haematological disorder, aPLs-TP may involve immune-mediated platelet (PLT) destruction, hypercoagulable state-induced thrombosis consumption and even life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy.2 3 Epidemiological studies reveal that 20–46% of patients with primary APS experience TP, defined as a PLT count of less than 100×109/L.47 Notably, TP in patients with aPLs may be associated with increased risks of vascular thrombosis, neurological manifestations, musculoskeletal manifestations, pregnancy morbidity and mortality,810 while a subset of patients exhibit persistently low PLT count without overt thrombosis or bleeding tendencies,11 demonstrating marked clinical variability. This heterogeneity suggests that patients with aPLs-TP may encompass multiple distinct disease subtypes.

The precise and effective management of patients with aPLs-TP remains extremely challenging. Prior guidelines recognised TP as a ‘non-criteria’ manifestation of APS.12 Although the 2023 APS diagnostic criteria included TP as a score component,13 none have clearly defined its risk stratification. This absence of standardised guidance, coupled with the inherent clinical heterogeneity of this patient cohort, substantially complicates the implementation of personalised therapeutic strategies.

Current research on aPLs-TP predominantly focuses on single-dimensional metrics, such as PLT count or antibody titres, with a lack of systematic integration of risk factors, clinical manifestations and laboratory parameters. In contrast, recent advances in machine learning-driven clinical subgrouping offer a paradigm shift for deciphering disease heterogeneity in complex immune-mediated disorders. For instance, clustering analyses applied to SLE cohorts have successfully identified distinct subphenotypes, such as serologically active clinically quiescent (SACQ) cluster14 and APS-associated pregnancy subgroups.15 These findings suggest that unsupervised clustering methods integrating clinical phenotypes, laboratory indices and multi-omics data may provide a critical breakthrough for precision subclassification in aPLs-TP.

Responding to this need, our study pioneered the application of unsupervised clustering algorithms in a prospective Chinese cohort to categorise aPLs-TP subphenotypes through systematic multidimensional data acquisition, while evaluating their prognostic heterogeneity. Such stratification holds promise for advancing tailored treatment strategies for patients with aPLs-TP.

Methods

Patients

This was a single-centre, prospective cohort study conducted at Peking Union Medical College Hospital from January 2012 to April 2024 using the RheumCloud App for data collection and patient management.16 TP was defined as a PLT count <100×109/L, confirmed on peripheral blood smear and by repeat testing. The study enrolled consecutive patients with persistent aPL positivity (at least 12 weeks apart) and antibody-mediated TP. Other secondary causes, such as other autoimmune diseases, haematological disorders, medications and viral infections, were excluded and ultimately determined by clinical specialists to be associated with aPLs. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Clinical data collection

Comprehensive baseline data were systematically collected, including demographics, comorbidities, clinical manifestations and laboratory profiles. Demographic parameters encompassed gender, age, disease duration, smoking status and body mass index. Comorbidity comprised hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia and hyperhomocysteinaemia. Clinical manifestations were categorised as arterial thrombosis (AT), venous thrombosis (VT), obstetric morbidity, cardiac valve disease (valve thickening and vegetation) and microangiopathy (eg, aPL-nephropathy, non-stroke central nervous system manifestations, livedo racemosa, pulmonary haemorrhage and myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries). Isolated immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is defined as TP without any of the aforementioned manifestations. Laboratory features include anticardiolipin antibodies (aCL), anti-beta-2-glycoprotein I antibodies (aβ2GPI), lupus anticoagulant (LA), PLT count, haemoglobin (Hb) levels and C3/C4 levels.

Antibody detection

Serum immunoglobulin G (IgG)/IgM aCL and IgG/IgM aβ2GPI were detected by ELISA (QUANTA Lite ELISAs, INOVA Diagnostics, San Diego, California, USA). The cut-off values for positivity were set as 40 IgG phospholipid units or 40 IgM phospholipid units. LA was detected by a traditional three-step procedure based on the guidelines of the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis.17 LA test positivity was defined as a prolonged diluted Russell viper venom time in the screening step, which was not reversed by mixing with normal plasma but reversed by the addition of excess phospholipids in the confirmation step.18 A high-risk aPL profile consists of positivity for LA (in ≥2 occasions at least 12 weeks apart), or of double (any combination of LA, aCL or aβ2GPI antibodies) or triple aPL positivity, or the presence of persistently high aPL titres.13

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients were followed at outpatient clinics every 3–6 months, with data collected on incident events including thrombosis events, obstetric morbidity, isolated ITP, microangiopathy, cardiac valve disease, bleeding events, laboratory parameters and treatment strategies (eg, glucocorticoids, hydroxychloroquine, immunosuppressant (tacrolimus, sirolimus, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine, mycophenolate, mofetil, methotrexate, leflunomide and thalidomide), anti-PLT agents and anticoagulants). Response criteria for TP were defined as follows: complete response (CR) required PLT recovery to ≥100×10⁹/L; response (R) was achieved with PLT count between 30 and 100×10⁹/L and a twofold or greater increase from baseline; and non-response indicated PLTs <30×10⁹/L or failure to double from baseline. Loss of CR occurred when PLTs fell below 100×10⁹/L, while loss of R was defined as PLTs dropping below 30×10⁹/L or failing to maintain a twofold or greater increase from baseline. Key clinical outcomes during follow-up were determined as a combination of recurrence or new-onset thrombotic events (venous thromboembolism and AT), obstetric morbidity (prefetal death, fetal death, pre-eclampsia with several features and placental insufficiency with severe features), microangiopathy (livedo racemosa, aPL-nephropathy, pulmonary haemorrhage and myocardial disease) or cardiac valve disease (valve thickening and valve vegetation).13 Follow-up continued until patient death or the last recorded follow-up date.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution are expressed as mean±SD, and non-normally distributed variables as medians and 25th and 75th percentiles (quantile 1 (Q1) and quantile 3 (Q3), respectively). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables with normal distributions were compared with the Student’s t-test. Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare data with non-normal distribution. Χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare categorical variables. We assessed the baseline data of 123 enrolled patients, systemically conducting cluster analysis based on demographic characteristics, key clinical presentations and laboratory investigations. A TwoStep Cluster Analysis procedure was performed to identify natural groupings within the dataset. The selection of 12 clustering variables—sex (female), smoking history, AT, VT, obstetric morbidity, microangiopathy, PLT count, cardiac valve disease, isolated ITP and positivity for aCL, aβ2GPI and LA—was based on clinical relevance and correlation analysis (online supplemental figure 1). This analytical approach combines preclustering with hierarchical methods, automatically determining optimal cluster numbers through Bayesian information criterion with cluster quality metrics validating the solution (online supplemental figure 2). Survival curves were determined by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. A p value ≤0.05 (two sided) was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software V.26 (IBM SPSS Statistics) and R statistical software V.4.3.1.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 312 patients were screened based on the single-centre Chinese Antiphospholipid Syndrome Cohort Collaborative Networks prospective cohort. After excluding cases with TP secondary to SLE and other aetiologies, 123 patients with TP associated with aPLs were finally enrolled in this study, including 77 diagnosed with APS (figure 1). There were 81 females (65.9%) with a median age of 36.0 years (IQR 29.8–51.2 years), and the median follow-up was 5.0 years (IQR 3.2–7.4 years). At the time of baseline TP, 43 patients (35.0%) presented with arterial thrombotic events, 37 patients (30.1%) presented with venous thrombotic events and 26 female patients (32.1% of all females) reported pregnancy morbidity. Cardiac valve diseases were observed in 10 patients (8.1%), while microangiopathy was diagnosed in 24 cases (19.5%). The median baseline PLT count was 50.0×109/L (IQR 25.0–73.0×109/L). Seropositivity rates for aCL, aβ2GPI and LA were 79.7%, 78.0% and 94.3%, respectively.

Clinical subgroups by cluster analysis

The Hopkins statistic for our dataset was 0.3841, indicating the presence of a cluster structure. The silhouette method suggested an optimal number of three clusters, corroborated by the NbClust function. Three distinct subgroups were identified with unique phenotypic features through the cluster analysis (table 1; figure 2).

Cluster 1 (n=35, 28.5%) consisted predominantly of men with a high prevalence of smoking history (57.1%), hyperhomocysteinaemia (74.3%) and diabetes mellitus (11.4%). These patients exhibited a relatively higher rate of atherothrombotic events (62.9% vs 41.2% in cluster 2 vs 0% in cluster 3, p<0.001), microangiopathy (25.7% vs 29.4% vs 0%, p=0.001) and cardiac valve disease (14.3% vs 9.8% vs 0%, p=0.044). Notably, this subgroup demonstrated the highest aCL positivity rate (94.3% vs 80.4% vs 64.9%, p=0.008).

Cluster 2 (n=51, 41.5%) comprised exclusively female patients and was characterised by the highest rate of obstetric morbidity (51.0% vs 0% in other clusters, p<0.001) and microangiopathy (29.4% vs 25.7% in cluster 1 vs 0% in cluster 3, p=0.001). Notable features included significantly lower Hb levels compared with other clusters (median Hb 96.0 g/L (IQR 73.0–115.0) vs 122.0 g/L (IQR 97.0–145.0) vs 129.0 g/L (IQR 98.5–142.0), p<0.001) and a higher incidence of C3/C4 deficiency (52.9% vs 34.4% vs 24.3%, p=0.020).

Cluster 3 (n=37, 30.0%) represented an isolated TP phenotype with the lowest PLT count (median 27.0×109/L (IQR 9.0–50.0) vs 67.0×109/L (IQR 40.0–90.0) in cluster 1 vs 60.0×109/L (IQR 41.0–77.0) in cluster 2, p<0.001). Despite severe TP (100.0% vs 14.3% in cluster 1 vs 0% in cluster 2, p<0.001), this subgroup showed minimal systemic manifestations.

Key clinical outcomes across clusters

The three clusters demonstrated distinct profiles of aPL-related events and PLT dynamics during follow-up (tables2 3). Cluster 1 was notable for the highest prevalence of cardiac valve disease (22.9% vs 13.7% in cluster 2 vs 2.7% in cluster 3, p=0.032), while obstetric morbidity was common in cluster 2 (19.6% vs 0% in other clusters, p<0.001). Cluster 3 exhibited a high risk of isolated ITP (83.8% vs 11.4% in cluster 1 vs 0% in cluster 2, p<0.001), with the lowest rate of complete remission (37.8% vs 68.6% vs 64.7%, p=0.006), but other aPL-related manifestations such as arteriovenous events and bleeding events were not significant. Treatment patterns also diverged significantly: immunosuppressant use was most common in cluster 3 (91.9% vs 62.9% in cluster 1 and 66.7% in cluster 2, p=0.008), while anticoagulant use was markedly reduced in this cluster (10.8% vs 71.4% vs 84.3%, p<0.001). As indicated by the Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis (figure 3), the event-free survival (EFS) rates at 5 years were 66.9% (95% CI 52.50% to 85.24%) for cluster 1, 45.85% (95% CI 32.41% to 64.86%) for cluster 2 and 88.68% (95% CI 78.80% to 99.80%) for cluster 3. Cluster 3 demonstrated prolonged EFS time (log-rank p=0.026).

Discussion

aPLs-TP exhibits remarkable heterogeneity, involving immune-mediated processes, vascular endothelial function impairment, hypercoagulable tendency, etc. In our study, we aimed to identify the different phenotypic subgroups of patients with aPLs-TP and evaluate the prognosis of these subgroups (online supplemental figure 3). Through unsupervised cluster analysis, we identified three distinct clusters: AT subtype (cluster 1), pregnancy morbidity subtype (cluster 2) and isolated TP subtype (cluster 3). This stratification allowed us to elucidate the distinct patterns of clinical features in patients with aPLs-TP, informing personalised treatment strategies.

Cluster 1 was characterised by the highest burden of arterial thrombotic events, male predominance and multifactorial cardiovascular risk aggregation. 57.1% of these patients reported a smoking history, a strong independent predictor for AT19 and TP20 in thrombotic APS. Concurrently, hyperhomocysteinaemia was observed in 74.3% of cluster 1 patients, in accordance with prior evidence21 that identifies homocysteine as an independent risk factor for atherosclerotic and thrombotic diseases.22 Consistent with our findings, previous research also demonstrated that male patients with APS and TP were more frequently associated with AT.23 Mechanistically, the high rate of atherosclerotic events in cluster 1 suggests that aPLs may promote inflammation via proinflammatory cytokines (tumour necrosis factor alpha, interleukin 6),24 which synergise with traditional cardiovascular risk factors like smoking and hyperhomocysteinaemia to amplify thrombin generation and PLT aggregation, thereby precipitating atherothrombotic events.25 Additionally, this cluster exhibited a higher risk of heart valve disease, aligning with previous evidence linking AT to aPL-related valvopathy.26 27 These findings underscore the critical need for secondary cardiovascular prevention in this high-risk population. Lifestyle modifications such as smoking cessation programmes and dietary folate supplementation should be prioritised, as long-term smoking cessation has been shown to normalise thrombotic biomarkers compared with non-smokers.28 As for pharmacological strategies, while anticoagulation remains the cornerstone of thrombotic APS management, the synergistic risks of AT and bleeding in cluster 1 patients necessitate balanced antithrombotic regimens. Furthermore, prospective studies focusing on these risk mitigation strategies are urgently needed to guide clinical practice. Collectively, male patients with aPLs-TP require prioritised arterial event prevention and enhanced cardiovascular disease risk factor control.

In cluster 2, composed entirely of females, a high risk of recurrent pregnancy morbidity was observed, with mild anaemia and complement deficiency being particularly prevalent. This aligns with evidence linking hypocomplementaemia in patients who are aPL positive and have APS to a worse pregnancy outcome,29 30 likely mediated by aβ2GPI-β2GPI complex-driven complement cascade activation.31 Subsequent generation of C5b-9 membrane attack complex induces trophoblast cell injury, monocytes and neutrophil recruitment and activation.32 Meanwhile, hypocomplementaemia itself correlates with TP in SLE and APS,33 while TP during the first trimester independently increased the risk of premature birth <37 weeks.34 While low-dose aspirin (LDA, 75–100 mg/day) and low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) remain the first-line treatments for obstetric APS,35 their potential to induce or exacerbate TP increases bleeding risk and complicates anticoagulation therapy. Concerns regarding bleeding complications may deter physicians from prescribing these agents despite their established efficacy. Recent evidence supported the safety of LDA and LMWH in pregnancy prophylaxis for patients with TP and APS, as they neither exacerbate adverse gestational outcomes nor heighten antepartum haemorrhage risk.34 For individuals with recurrent miscarriage but without thrombotic history, prophylactic-dose LMWH and LDA during pregnancy and for 6 weeks post partum are recommended.36 Additionally, the therapeutic window for aPL-associated obstetric morbidity may extend beyond conventional timelines, emphasising the rationale of preconceptional intervention.

Cluster 3 showed isolated TP with the lowest baseline PLT count and the lowest rate of complete remission in PLT levels. Notably, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated markedly prolonged EFS in cluster 3 compared with other subtypes. A retrospective analysis of 305 patients with primary APS also revealed no proven impact of PLT count on major bleeding risk.37 However, patients in this subgroup received more intensive therapeutic regimens due to markedly reduced PLT count, which may contribute to an increased incidence of drug-related adverse events and impose additional economic burdens. This observation highlights the inadequacy of isolated TP as a surrogate marker for disease severity, particularly given that PLT count restoration posed substantial clinical challenges in our cohort and should not be prioritised in patient management, a conclusion aligning with prior studies.38 Furthermore, while the proportion of patients with isolated TP is not negligible, it is noteworthy that the 2023 American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology APS classification criteria have incorporated TP as a contributing clinical feature, marking a significant shift from the 2006 Sydney criteria. This update aligns with our findings and supports the broader recognition of more moderate TP phenotypes within the APS spectrum. The underlying mechanisms may involve the following pathways: (1) aPLs (particularly aCL and aβ2GPI) may enhance macrophage phagocytosis of PLTs directly or via FcγR-mediated mechanisms39; (2) antibodies targeting PLT membrane glycoproteins (GPIIb/IIIa, GPIb/IX, GPIa/IIa and GPIV), detected in 40–70% of patients with TP and APS,40 induce complement activation and coagulation cascade while directly disrupting PLT function through GPIIb/IIIa interactions.41 42 Furthermore, the relatively low thrombotic risk in this subgroup may be attributed to critically low PLT count, which critically impairs thrombotic capacity.

In summary, our study identified three distinct phenotypes in patients with aPLs-TP through unsupervised cluster analysis, revealing profoundly prognostic differences. Male patients with cardiovascular risk factors had higher AT risk, supporting secondary prevention strategies. Female patients with pregnancy complications showed a high recurrent rate, emphasising preconceptional intervention. Meanwhile, patients with isolated TP demonstrated prolonged EFS compared with other clusters. This may suggest a distinct clinical phenotype, although treatment differences and other confounders could have contributed to the observed outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of phenotypic stratification of patients with aPLs-TP, as this enables personalised management strategies and improves clinical outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Our study explored the diversity of clinical manifestations and outcomes within a single-centre inception cohort of well-characterised patients. Key limitations include its single-centre design, which may restrict the generalisability of our findings due to potential referral bias and population-specific characteristics. Second, while the three clusters identified were based on clinical and laboratory features, the association between prognosis and dynamic changes of these factors still needs further investigation. Furthermore, due to sample size constraints, cluster stability could not be assessed via bootstrap resampling or subset validation. External validation in an independent multicentre cohort will be essential to confirm the reproducibility, robustness and clinical applicability of the identified clusters. Future large-scale cohort studies or multicentre replication is required to establish statistical robustness.
