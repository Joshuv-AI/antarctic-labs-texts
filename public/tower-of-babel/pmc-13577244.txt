Measles as tracer and tugboat: Why eradication can strengthen immunization systems
Authors: David N. Durrheim, Jon K. Andrus, Mimi Mynak, David Githanga, Mira Kojouharova, Nadia Teleb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAs measles resurges globally, we face an important strategic choice. Large outbreaks reflect declining vaccination coverage, inequities in health services access, conflict, population displacement, vaccine hesitancy, and inadequate investment in immunization programs. However, measles is more than a vaccine-preventable disease. Because of its extraordinary transmissibility, it functions both as a tracer of weaknesses in immunization systems and public health infrastructure, and as a potential tugboat for strengthening them.The Immunization Agenda 2030 highlights strong immunization systems, equitable service delivery, and effective surveillance as foundations for controlling vaccine-preventable diseases. Bill Foege, a leader of smallpox eradication, argued that measles eradication could reduce health inequalities while acting as a “tugboat” that pulls stronger immunization systems into place. This perspective is increasingly relevant. The capacities required for measles eradication, including high routine immunization coverage, sensitive surveillance, quality laboratory networks, rapid outbreak response, community engagement, and political accountability, are also fundamental to resilient health systems and preparedness for future pandemics.Rather than competing with health-system strengthening, measles eradication can accelerate it. The current resurgence should be viewed not only as a warning signal of system fragility but also as an opportunity to strengthen immunization systems, advance health equity, and renew commitment to global eradication.

Introduction

The past three years have seen measles resurging globally.1 Large outbreaks are occurring across all WHO regions, affecting countries at every income level. Between February 2025 and January 2026 alone, at least 51 countries experienced large or disruptive outbreaks, defined as 20 or more measles cases per million population.2 The virus is exploiting immunity gaps created by declining vaccination coverage, disruptions to health services resulting from conflict and population displacement, vaccine misinformation, and inadequate political and financial commitment to immunization programs.3 The consequences are increasingly evident in rising case numbers, preventable deaths, pressure on health services, and reversal of hard-won progress toward elimination.

Measles is one of the most contagious pathogens known to affect humans. With a basic reproductive number commonly estimated between 12 and 18, even small declines in population immunity can result in substantial outbreaks.4 Importations repeatedly demonstrate that transmission anywhere threatens populations everywhere. National achievements in measles elimination remain vulnerable in the absence of sustained global progress.5 Maintaining the status quo is increasingly costly epidemiologically, economically, and socially.

The resurgence of measles is often described as a failure of vaccination programs.6 While accurate, this interpretation is incomplete. Measles outbreaks provide important diagnostic information about the health of immunization programs and broader public health systems. Because exceptionally high levels of population immunity are required to interrupt transmission and there are regular international movements of travelers to and from countries where measles is still endemic or experiencing large outbreaks, measles rapidly exposes weaknesses in vaccine delivery systems, surveillance, program management, donor funding and community confidence. In this respect, measles functions as a highly sensitive “tracer” of the performance of health systems delivering childhood services.

This perspective aligns closely with the vision of Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030), which places equity, strong immunization programs, integrated primary healthcare, and data-driven decision-making at the center of the global immunization agenda.2 IA2030 seeks to ensure that everyone, everywhere benefits from vaccines and explicitly recognizes the importance of reaching populations that are consistently missed by health services. The World Health Organization (WHO) measles programmatic risk assessment tool was developed to help national programs to identify areas not meeting measles programmatic targets, and based on the findings, guide and strengthen measles elimination program activities and reduce the risk of outbreaks.7

At the Global Technical Consultation to Assess the Feasibility of Measles Eradication, held in Washington DC in July 2010, the late William “Bill” H. Foege articulated two compelling arguments for measles eradication.8 First, eradication would help reduce health inequalities because the burden of disease falls disproportionately on disadvantaged populations. Second, measles eradication could serve as a “tugboat” that leads to stronger immunization systems. More than a decade later, these observations are even more relevant in a world confronting declining vaccine confidence, widening inequalities, and recurrent global health emergencies. Together, they suggest that measles eradication should not be regarded as a narrow disease-specific objective, but as a strategic pathway toward stronger, more equitable, and more resilient health systems.

Measles as a tracer of immunization system performance

Few health indicators reveal weaknesses in immunization systems as quickly and reliably as measles. Because of its extraordinary transmissibility, sustained interruption of transmission requires consistently high coverage with two doses of measles-containing vaccine across all communities. Even modest immunity gaps can sustain outbreaks.

Consequently, measles functions as a real-time stress test of immunization program performance. When routine immunization systems weaken because of workforce shortages, inadequate financing, conflict, service disruptions, poor program management, or declining vaccine confidence, measles is often among the first diseases to reemerge. Conversely, where immunization systems are equitable, accessible, and trusted, measles transmission struggles to become established.

This tracer function extends beyond vaccination coverage alone. Measles outbreaks frequently identify deficiencies in disease surveillance, laboratory networks, outbreak preparedness, risk communication, and community engagement. As a result, measles incidence may provide a more informative indicator of immunization system performance than administrative vaccination coverage estimates alone, particularly where data quality is uncertain.

Importantly, outbreaks seldom occur randomly. They typically arise in communities that are underserved, socioeconomically disadvantaged, geographically isolated, displaced by conflict, or otherwise marginalized. Measles therefore functions not only as a tracer of program performance but also as a tracer of health inequity.9 The populations affected by measles outbreaks are often the same populations that experience barriers to healthcare more broadly.

Experience from measles elimination programs consistently demonstrates that national coverage averages can conceal substantial subnational inequities. It is these pockets of under-immunized populations, rather than national averages, that sustain transmission and threaten elimination gains.

From the perspective of IA2030, this characteristic is particularly valuable. Measles identifies precisely where immunization systems are failing to leave no one behind. Consequently, actions taken to address measles susceptibility frequently address broader deficiencies in primary healthcare delivery.

The work of the WHO Regional Verification Commissions for Measles and Rubella Elimination illustrates this tracer function in practice. Country reviews examine not only case counts but also the underlying strength of surveillance systems, laboratory performance, and program data, and routinely uncover weaknesses that would otherwise go unaddressed. Verification is therefore not simply an accounting exercise; it is itself a mechanism through which the tracer becomes a tugboat.10

Reducing health inequalities

One of Bill Foege’s most important observations was that disease eradication can be a powerful instrument for reducing health inequalities. Measles disproportionately affects populations that have historically benefited least from health and development advances. Children living in poverty, remote communities, conflict zones, informal settlements, and areas with weak health systems remain at greatest risk of infection, severe disease, and death.

Unlike many public health interventions, eradication cannot succeed by reaching only the easiest populations to access. Success depends on identifying and protecting every population that remains vulnerable. The final stages of eradication require deliberate attention to those communities that conventional health services have struggled to reach.

This principle distinguishes eradication from disease control. Control programs can coexist with persistent pockets of vulnerability. Eradication cannot. The pursuit of eradication therefore creates strong incentives to strengthen health information systems, improve population mapping, support community engagement, and ensure accountability for reaching underserved populations.

Equity is therefore not simply a consequence of measles eradication. It is a prerequisite for success.11 Every remaining susceptible population represents both a risk for continued transmission and an opportunity to address underlying health inequalities.

In this sense, measles eradication and IA2030 are mutually reinforcing goals. Both require a sustained commitment to identifying and reaching populations that have been consistently left behind by health systems.

In many low- and middle-income settings, the final unreached child is rarely separated from services by distance alone, but by a prior loss of trust in the health system. Community health workers, who live within the communities they serve, are often better positioned than time-limited outbreak response teams to close this gap. Reaching the last mile therefore requires investment in community health worker platforms that is every bit as deliberate as investment in cold-chain infrastructure, surveillance, and vaccine supply systems.12

Measles eradication as a tugboat for stronger immunization systems

Foege’s second observation was that measles eradication could act as a “tugboat” that leads to stronger immunization systems. The metaphor is particularly apt. A tugboat is relatively small, yet it possesses the power to move vessels many times its size. Similarly, measles eradication is a disease-specific objective capable of driving improvements throughout immunization programs and public health systems.

Critics of eradication initiatives have occasionally argued that vertical disease programs can divert resources from broader health priorities. However, measles differs from many disease-specific programs because the very conditions required for successful eradication, including high routine coverage, strong surveillance, trusted community engagement, reliable supply chains, and accountable governance, are themselves core attributes of effective immunization systems. In this respect, measles eradication does not compete with health-system strengthening; it depends upon and accelerates it.

The 2010 Global Technical Consultation concluded that measles eradication is biologically and technically feasible and that operational models already existed in many countries that had successfully interrupted endemic transmission.8 However, the consultation also identified weaknesses in immunization systems, insufficient political commitment, and inadequate financial investment as major barriers to success.

These observations remain highly relevant. To eradicate measles, countries must achieve and sustain high coverage with two doses of vaccine, maintain sensitive surveillance systems, support accredited laboratory networks, rapidly investigate cases, determine the source of outbreaks, and sustain public confidence in vaccination. These capacities are the essential building blocks of effective immunization systems.

Building readiness for future epidemics and pandemics

The benefits of strengthening systems for measles eradication extend beyond immunization programs. Recent public health emergencies, including Ebola virus disease, Zika virus disease, COVID-19, mpox, and other emerging threats, have demonstrated the critical importance of surveillance, laboratory capability, rapid response, and trusted communication systems.

Many of these same capacities are required for measles eradication. Measles surveillance systems are among the most sensitive disease detection platforms in many countries. Every suspected case should trigger case investigation, specimen collection, laboratory testing, and epidemiological assessment. Maintaining this capability enhances broader epidemic intelligence.

Similarly, laboratory networks established for measles and rubella surveillance support diagnostic services that can be leveraged during emerging health emergencies. Trained epidemiologists, outbreak response teams, and risk communication specialists contribute to preparedness for a wide range of infectious threats.

The COVID-19 pandemic also demonstrated both the fragility and the importance of public trust in vaccination programs. Community engagement structures developed for measles vaccination and outbreak response provide an important foundation for crisis communication during future public health emergencies.

As a result, investments in measles eradication generate substantial collateral benefits for health security and pandemic preparedness. Strengthening systems to identify and stop measles transmission also strengthens the capacity to identify and respond to future threats whose nature cannot yet be predicted.

This is especially true in conflict-affected and fragile settings, where fixed health facilities cannot always follow displaced populations. Measles outbreak response, built for rapid and mobile deployment, is frequently the first public health capacity able to reach these communities, and the same teams and cold-chain systems mobilized for measles are often repurposed for cholera, polio, and mpox responses. In such settings, eradication efforts do not wait for stability; they help build the capacity that stability will eventually require.13

The economic case

The economic rationale for measles eradication is equally compelling. Childhood immunization remains among the most cost-effective public health investments available. Economic analyses have estimated that measles vaccination generates societal returns of approximately US$74 for every US$1 invested, making it one of the highest-value interventions in public health.14 Vaccination programs generate substantial returns through reduced healthcare expenditure, increased productivity, prevention of disability, and avoidance of premature mortality.

Importantly, these estimates do not fully capture the secondary benefits of strengthened surveillance systems, improved workforce capacity, enhanced outbreak preparedness, and greater public confidence generated through measles elimination and eradication efforts.

The benefits become even greater when eradication is considered.15 While disease control requires indefinite expenditure to contain transmission, eradication offers the prospect of permanently ending circulation of the measles virus. This would eliminate recurrent outbreaks and substantially reduce future costs associated with emergency response activities.

Integration with rubella elimination further strengthens the economic argument. Prevention of congenital rubella syndrome provides additional benefits through avoidance of lifelong disability and associated healthcare and societal costs.

The relevant question is therefore not whether the world can afford measles eradication. Rather, it is whether the world can afford the continuing consequences of repeated outbreaks, avoidable deaths, persistent health inequities, and preventable economic losses.

Conclusions

The recent global measles resurgence should be interpreted as both a warning and an opportunity. It warns of declining vaccination coverage, widening immunity gaps, persistent health inequalities, and weaknesses in immunization systems. At the same time, it presents an opportunity to strengthen those systems by pursuing a shared and ambitious global objective.

Bill Foege’s argument that measles eradication could reduce health inequalities and serve as a “tugboat” that leads to stronger immunization systems has become even more relevant in the era of IA2030. The communities most affected by measles outbreaks are often those left behind by health services, while the capacities required to eradicate measles are precisely those required for resilient and equitable immunization programs.

Measles is therefore simultaneously a tracer and a tugboat.

Measles is a tracer because outbreaks reveal where health systems are failing, where immunity gaps persist, and where health inequalities remain entrenched. It exposes weaknesses that must be addressed if IA2030’s vision of leaving no one behind is to be realized.

Measles is also a tugboat because the pursuit of eradication drives the investments, accountability, partnerships, and system improvements needed to strengthen routine immunization services. The surveillance systems, laboratory networks, workforce capabilities, community engagement strategies, and program management structures required for eradication generate benefits that extend well beyond a single disease.

The world already possesses the tools needed to prevent every measles death. The challenge is no longer technical feasibility but collective commitment. Whether measles remains a recurring and preventable scourge, or becomes the catalyst for stronger immunization systems, greater equity, and enhanced global health security, will depend on decisions made today.
