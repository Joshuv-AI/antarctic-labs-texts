Natural history of Chiari I malformation-syringomyelia: longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Chenghua Yuan, Yueqi Du, Qingyu Yao, Can Zhang, Lei Zhang, Zhenlei Liu, Kai Wang, Wanru Duan, Zuowei Wang, Xingwen Wang, Gao Zeng, Hao Wu, Zan Chen, John D Heiss, Jian Guan, Feng-Zeng Jian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe natural history of clinically stable patients with Chiari I malformation (CM-I)-syringomyelia is uncertain. To understand their outcomes, we examined conservatively managed CM-I-syringomyelia patients’ long-term clinical and radiological courses.MethodsWe enrolled 156 mild CM-I-syringomyelia cases (Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score ≥13) managed non-surgically between 1994 and 2014 and followed them periodically until December 2024 for significant progressive myelopathy that we termed ‘obvious deterioration’. Obvious deterioration was defined as a ≥2-point decline in JOA score to less than 13. Spontaneous syrinx resolution was radiologically defined as >50% reduction in syrinx length or maximal axial diameter on T1-weighted MRI.ResultsThe entire cohort had over 1401 patient-years of follow-up. 55 patients exhibited clinical deterioration, yielding an annual progression rate of 3.9%. Obstructive sleep apnoea-hypopnoea syndrome (OSAHS) (HR=1.841, 95% CI 0.999 to 3.392; p=0.049), positive Babinski sign (HR=2.252, 95% CI 1.229 to 4.125; p=0.009) and without spontaneous resolution (HR=20.308, 95% CI 4.804 to 85.849; p<0.001) independently predicted later clinical obvious deterioration. Spontaneous resolution of CM-I-syringomyelia was more frequent with cervical syringes (HR=2.12, 95% CI 1.224 to 3.674; p=0.007) and absence of OSAHS (HR=3.83, 95% CI 1.376 to 10.640; p=0.01).ConclusionThis study showed that the natural course of myelopathy in CM-I-syringomyelia varies according to the OASHS status, Babinski sign and spontaneous syrinx resolution. Additionally, baseline characteristics, including the spinal region of the syrinx and the absence of OSAHS, correlated with spontaneous syrinx resolution.Keywords: SYRINGOMYELIA, CSF, CSF DYNAMICS

Introduction

Syringomyelia represents a clinically heterogeneous disorder pathologically defined by aberrant cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) accumulation within the central canal, exhibiting diverse neurological manifestations and prognostic trajectories.1 Syringomyelia most frequently occurs secondary to distinct pathological processes, with Chiari I malformation (CM-I) representing the predominant aetiology. Other recognised causes include spinal cord injury (SCI), inflammatory arachnoiditis and tumour.1 Neurosurgeons or neurologists usually recommend operative treatment if a syrinx expands or myelopathy develops.2 The surgical treatment of CM-I-syringomyelia usually reduces syrinx size and arrests myelopathy. Non-operative therapy has uncertain outcomes. Few patients are available for studies of the natural history of CM-I-syringomyelia because most patients undergo operative care.3,8 Short-term case series8 typically report stable syrinx dimensions but fail to capture the longitudinal clinical and radiological progression that may occur over decades in conservatively managed CM-I-syringomyelia patients.

The rare phenomenon of spontaneous resolution of syrinx, documented in longitudinal case studies with extended follow-up,9 challenges prevailing CSF dynamic models of syrinx pathophysiology. While current hydrodynamic models, primarily derived from cerebral ventricular studies, describe the direction of ciliary beating aligning with CSF flow,10 these models fail to adequately explain the unique fluid dynamics observed within the central canal.11 12 Notably, ventricular ciliary beating demonstrates unidirectional CSF flow alignment, whereas central canal flow dynamics lack equivalent empirical verification.11 Thouvenin O et al11 recently revealed exceptional bidirectional CSF flow within zebrafish embryonic central canals (24–30 hpf)—a physiologically unique finding given the rarity of symmetrical bidirectional flow in biological conduits. However, whether bidirectional fluid flow occurs within the human central canal remains unknown, as noted in a recent NEJM review, due to ethical constraints and limitations in visualization techniques.13 Conditions like syringomyelia provide clinicians with a unique opportunity to observe fluid movement within the central canal. 14 15

In this article, we studied a cohort of patients with CM-I-syringomyelia undergoing conservative management. Patients who experienced neurological deterioration during conservative management were directed to surgical treatment. Surgical treatment was posterior fossa craniectomy and C1 laminectomy (extradural craniocervical decompression) or posterior fossa craniectomy and C1 laminectomy with intradural exploration (intradural craniocervical decompression). We describe the natural history of a conservatively managed cohort with CM-I-syringomyelia, including the rate over time of neurological deterioration and the factors predicting a decline in neurological function or spontaneous syrinx resolution.

Methods

Patients

The patients were initially diagnosed with CM-I-syringomyelia using MRI. Objective clinical findings, such as the status of deep tendon reflexes, pathological reflexes, sensory disturbance and/or Babinski sign, were considered in assigning a JOA scale score (Japanese Orthopaedic Association cervical myelopathy scores). The severity of myelopathy was classified according to our grading system: severe (0–9 points), moderate (10–12 points) or mild (13–17 points), based on JOA scale scores. Neurologists and neurosurgeons may elect to observe syringomyelia patients with stable or slowly progressive neurological clinical courses. Conservative management was sometimes recommended to patients in our institute between 1994 and 2014 with mild signs and symptoms and JOA above 13 (the highest JOA score is 17, signifying normal function).9 We recommend surgical treatment to conservatively managed patients whose syrinx has significantly enlarged or whose JOA scores decreased to <13 with a decrease of at least 2 points.16 Patients were followed up periodically every 2 years, but if any obvious deterioration in symptoms occurred, they were examined sooner. Patients were given ongoing details of their pathological condition and precautions to avoid the aggravation of their myelopathy, but no surgical intervention was performed. The study endpoint for each patient was December 2024, or earlier if deterioration of myelopathy resulted in a JOA score of less than 13 with a decrease of at least 2 points. The time from the first visit to the endpoint was considered the tolerance period of mild CM-I-syringomyelia and was used for the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The patients who had severe cervical spondylotic myelopathy, ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, tumours or possible other central nervous system (CNS) diseases that may affect myelopathy were excluded. This study used a methodology like that used in a study of conservatively managed Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in our hospital.17 Patients were enrolled prospectively with the use of an electronic data-capture system. Data were recorded periodically after enrolment.

Data collection

The JOA was used to assess neurological status quantitatively by two independent physicians who were blinded to the study. The first JOA grading was made at the baseline, initial visit. Study subjects whose JOA decreased after the baseline visit to less than 13 with a decrease of at least 2 points had an outcome of ‘obvious deterioration’. Study subjects developing spontaneous resolution related to syringomyelia had a ‘spontaneous resolution’ outcome. The change in JOA score between the examinations before and after spontaneous resolution developed was recorded. Clinical status was compared at onset, admission and last follow-up to discover how symptoms changed. An apnoea-hypopnoea index of five events per hour in adults and one event per hour in children18 was the threshold used to diagnose obstructive sleep apnoea-hypopnoea syndrome (OSAHS) in patients with CM-I-syringomyelia.

Chiari-I malformation was defined as cerebellar tonsillar ectopia of more than 3–5 mm inferior to the foramen magnum. A syrinx was defined as a central canal fluid collection 3 mm or more in axial width. Patients meeting these CM-I-syringomyelia criteria during the conservative imaging period were included in the present study. Spontaneous resolution of syrinx was defined as >50% improvement in length, maximal cross-sectional diameter or both, consistent with the criteria used to define successful foramen magnum decompression (FMD) of CM-I.19,20 Patients were entered in the cervical or cervicothoracic syrinx group depending on the spinal level of their syrinx.

Measurements of CSF spaces, bony structures and angles at the craniovertebral junction (CVJ) abnormalities were performed according to previous recommendations and are listed in tables13.19 MRI studies were reviewed to evaluate changes in syrinx size over time. The following MRI findings were analysed: (1) the broadest axial diameter of the syrinx and spinal canal,7 (2) the extent of cerebellar tonsillar ectopia inferior to the foramen magnum,7 (3) axial syrinx patterns21 and (4) the broadest anteroposterior diameter of the spinal cord at the C4 and C7 cervical levels.19

Statistical analysis

We used Fisher’s exact test or the Pearson χ2 test for categorical variables and the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test and Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables. To determine HRs for clinical deterioration or spontaneous resolution, we employed a Cox proportional-hazards model. The multivariate Cox regression analysis included covariates such as place of residence (with urban participants being more likely to adhere to the study) and neuropathic pain (as central canal dilation typically involves the spinothalamic tract first, often presenting as neuropathic pain initially), along with variables that showed statistical significance (p<0.05) in the univariate analysis and were associated with significant deterioration. Variables linked to spontaneous resolution in univariate analyses p<0.05 were incorporated into a backward stepwise multivariate analysis. Cumulative deterioration rates were graphed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. We used survival analysis to estimate the risk of neurological decline attributable to syringomyelia. Analyses were performed using SPSS (V.25, IBM Corp) and Prism (V.9.0, LLC) software. All p values were two-sided. Statistical significance was considered as p<0.05.

Results

A total of 1194 patients with newly diagnosed CM-I-syringomyelia were enrolled from January 1994 through December 2014. When extraction of data from the registry was performed in 2024, a total of 156 consecutive, conservatively managed patients with a 10-year imaging follow-up of CM-I-syringomyelia met the eligibility criteria (figure 4, table 1, onlinesupplemental tables 1 2).

Baseline characteristics

Out of the 156 cases of CM-I-syringomyelia studied, 52 patients had spontaneous syrinx resolution. Of these, the syrinx was found in the cervical spinal level in 23 (44.2%) and in the cervicothoracic spinal level in 29 (55.8%). The mean age at the first evaluation was 37.8±13.8 years (range: 1–63). The deteriorating and stable groups did not differ significantly in their gender composition, onset age and body mass index (BMI) (p=0.190, 0.713, 0.495, respectively). The clinically deteriorating group more often had OSAHS on enrolment (p=0.012). The most frequent first symptom in both groups was dysaesthesia. The second and third most common admission symptoms were pain and motor dysfunction. The stable group had a higher incidence of neuropathic pain than the deteriorating group (p=0.024). The deteriorating group had a higher incidence of pyramidal tract signs than the stable group (p<0.001). The finding of ‘initial cerebellar cisterns reduced’ was more common in the deteriorating group (p<0.0001). Most patients in the deteriorating group had cervicothoracic syringes, syrinx/spinal canal diameter ratio >50% and were ‘without spontaneous resolution’ (p<0.001, <0.001, <0.001, respectively).

The groups with spontaneous resolution and without spontaneous resolution did not differ significantly in gender, age and BMI (p=0.198, 0.971 and 0.104, respectively). The duration of symptoms in the two groups did not differ significantly (p=0.129). The two groups did not differ significantly in dysaesthesia incidence, neuropathic pain and impaired motor function (p=0.906, 0.146, 0.146, respectively). There was a significant difference between the groups in ‘with OSAHS’ or ‘without OSAHS’ (p=0.001). The group with spontaneous resolution differed significantly from the group without spontaneous resolution in the spinal level of the syrinx and the initial syrinx/spinal canal width ratio (p=0.015, 0.031).

Clinical deterioration before surgical treatment

During a follow-up period encompassing 1401 CM-I-syringomyelia-years in the 156 conservatively managed patients, obvious deterioration was observed in 55 patients, a deterioration rate of 3.9% of patients per year. The Kaplan-Meier method estimated the annual rate of patients developing obvious deterioration before surgical intervention in the ‘with OSAHS’ group at 6.50% per year and in the ‘without OSAHS’ group at 3.25% per year (p=0.003). The Kaplan-Meier method estimated the annual rate of patients developing obvious deterioration before surgical intervention in the Babinski sign group as 8.28% per year and in the absent Babinski sign group as 2.60% per year (p<0.001). The Kaplan-Meier method estimated the annual rate of patients developing obvious deterioration before surgical intervention in the without spontaneous resolution group at 5.97% per year and in the spontaneous resolution group at 0.39% per year (p<0.001) (figure 5).

Discussion

Small studies with short follow-up intervals provide a limited description of the natural history of patients with untreated CM-I-syringomyelia.3,9 Most of the earlier natural history studies lacked ongoing MRI evidence, which hindered a comprehensive observation of syrinx dynamic changes.3,6 In recent years, however, the widespread use of MR has enabled periodic longitudinal imaging. Despite this, the advancement of minimally invasive surgical techniques and improvements in the efficacy of surgical interventions13 have skewed treatment choices toward surgery and limited the participants in natural history studies of syringomyelia. Recent research on the natural history of syringomyelia has predominantly been limited to case reports or case series.7,9 In our longitudinal study, we describe the largest conservatively managed CM-I-syringomyelia cohort reported to date. A considerable proportion of these patients experienced a relatively benign, non-progressive course. CM-I-syringomyelia patients with milder myelopathy had greater spinal cord parenchyma thickness, reflecting preserved spinal cord substance.

The severity of imaging findings is often inconsistent with the clinical symptoms of patients with syringomyelia. Greenberg et al proposed the CM-I-syringomyelia severity grading system,22 which demonstrates some practical reliability in predicting treatment outcomes preoperatively. They concluded that a syrinx greater than 6 mm is an independent risk factor for poor prognosis. However, this grading, particularly the cut-off of 6 mm, appears somewhat subjective. Another quantitative imaging assessment of syringomyelia that other authors have proposed is the Syringomyelia Tension Index (STI).23 For cases with a low STI, the proportion of symptom relief after surgical decompression is low, which may indicate either the syringomyelia is in a relatively low-pressure stage, and the preoperative symptoms are mild, or syringomyelia is in a late stage, irreversible SCI has occurred, and the syrinx already has undergone spontaneous resolution. However, prognosticating based solely on a single grading system numerical value can be subjective, such as using a 75% cut-off. According to previous international guidelines of CM-I-syringomyelia,2 surgery is indicated for a Vaquero Index (greatest syrinx width divided by the spinal canal width at the same level) greater than 0.5. Conversely, for a Vaquero Index less than 0.5, there seems to be no consensus on surgical indications. Unfortunately, our statistical analysis did not reveal significant associations of Vaquero Index values greater than 0.5 and those less than 0.5 with the natural progression of syrinx. We speculated that several factors could contribute to this: (1) the selection of cut-off values is relatively arbitrary, (2) the definition of clinical deterioration may vary16 and (3) the imaging parameters of the syrinx may not significantly correlate with clinical outcomes. Interestingly, the Babinski sign demonstrated good predictive power for clinical deterioration of syrinx patients. Patients with syringomyelia typically present with pain or dysaesthesia at relatively low-syrinx pressure levels that do not produce dysfunction of the pyramidal system. However, once dysfunction of the pyramidal system develops, it is typically associated with severe myelopathy, suggesting higher pressure in the syrinx, analogous to ‘hypertensive hydrocephalus’23 24 in the brain. Higher syrinx pressure can cause ongoing damage to the pyramidal tracts, which lie in the lateral part of the spinal cord, ultimately manifesting as evident clinical deterioration.

Although the pathogenesis of CM-I-syringomyelia is controversial, the formation and progression of syringomyelia appear to be associated with CM-I obstructing CSF flow through the CVJ and the upper cervical spinal canal.25 26 Most patients in the spontaneous resolution group had syringes located in the cervical segment. Similarly, this group also exhibited less caudal tonsillar descent compared with those without spontaneous resolution.27 The size of the syrinx may expand or shrink depending on changes in CSF flow at the foramen magnum, altered CSF pressure gradients and extracellular fluid dynamics.9 28 Interestingly, the serial syrinx imaging provides evidence of bidirectional fluid flow in the human central canal,11 which a recent NEJM review considered a mystery because of MRI having limited central canal visualisation capabilities. Furthermore, we recently introduced, for the first time, an original and novel surgical technique for the treatment of post-traumatic syringomyelia progressing to the medulla oblongata, which also provided evidence of fluid drainage from the central canal toward the cranial side.14 15

The exact prevalence of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) in individuals with CM-I remains unknown, largely due to the small sample sizes in many studies, which report prevalence rates ranging from 1% to 60%.18 The precise cause of OSA in the context of CM-I is not well understood. The prevailing theory suggests that the compression or stretching of cranial nerves IX and X, or their corresponding pontomedullary nuclei, may impair the function of the pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles. This functional impairment could allow the negative thoracic pressure that naturally occurs with inhalation to collapse the upper airway.29 The causal relationship between OSA and CM-I remains unclear. Some speculate that the onset or exacerbation of OSA could indicate the development of increased intracranial pressure in patients with CM-I.29 Conversely, there is a theory that OSA may contribute to the progression of CM-I.30 Losurdo et al31 found a higher prevalence of syringomyelia in the CM-I population affected by OSA. It is established that OSA can elevate intracranial pressure, and untreated OSA may lead to increased intracranial pressure, potentially worsening cerebellar tonsil herniation and possibly resulting in the development of a syrinx, or progression of a cervical syrinx to a cervicothoracic syrinx.32 Interestingly, Liu S et al recently33 highlighted that changes in intrathoracic pressure significantly influence tracer influx into the spinal cord, a finding consistent with our clinical evidence demonstrating that untreated OSA, which increases intracranial and intraspinal pressure, was a risk factor for obvious deterioration during conservative observation of syringomyelia. This suggests that CM-I-syringomyelia patients with OSAHS may benefit from earlier surgical decompression rather than opting for conservative follow-up.

Disturbances in CSF drainage can lead to hydrocephalus and syringomyelia, while the failure of interstitial fluid drainage appears to play a role in the aetiology of neurodegenerative diseases, particularly AD.34 Previous reports have indicated that CNS atrophy can result from various neurodegenerative diseases, including AD and SCI.35 Currently, there is significant controversy in international guidelines regarding surgical interventions for CM-syringomyelia with a Vaquero Index <50%, highlighting the need for further exploration.2 Spontaneous resolution of a syrinx may be related to increased CSF flow at the foramen magnum, flow from the syrinx into the spinal subarachnoid space, reduced CSF pressure gradients9 24 and other mechanisms involving the glymphatic system,36 ciliary actions12 37 and aquaporin channels37 that direct syrinx fluid to the spinal subarachnoid space. Longer intervals of syringomyelia before spontaneous resolution of syrinx seem to be associated with a loss of spinal cord substance, leading to progressive spinal cord atrophy. Spontaneous syrinx resolution reduces syrinx pressure and size(Vaquero Index <50%) and prevents further damage to the spinal cord, similar to spontaneous hydrocephalus resolution reducing ventricular pressure, size and further brain injury. Early surgical intervention is preferred in cases of syringomyelia with progressive myelopathy to prevent the syrinx from causing irreversible injury to the central spinal cord structures and impairing ependymal ciliary function.12 However, it remains unclear whether impaired ependymal ciliary function would result in clinical effects following syrinx collapse and resolution.12 38 Surgical intervention following spontaneous resolution of the syrinx typically results in minimal reversal of syrinx-related SCI, thereby limiting symptomatic improvement and recovery of clinical function or ciliary function post-surgery. This outcome is analogous to the incomplete recovery of cerebral function often observed after treating chronic hydrocephalus.39 It remains to be clarified if the spontaneous resolution of CM-I-syringomyelia restores the spinal cord microenvironment, BSCB and local osmotic milieu to the same extent as surgically induced syrinx resolution. Currently, there is significant controversy in international guidelines regarding surgical interventions for CM-syringomyelia with a Vaquero Index <50%, highlighting the need for further exploration.

Limitations

Describing a disease’s natural history requires a clinical study of a group of patients treated conservatively and without disease-modifying interventions for many years. Most patients with active syringomyelia associated with CM-I undergo FMD, which reliably reduces syrinx size and arrests neurological deterioration. Patients with stable syringes and neurological symptoms sometimes opt for conservative treatment, as neurological dysfunction has not significantly affected their quality of life or employment. Long-term prospective data about the natural history of patients with CM-I-syringomyelia and stable symptoms can be obtained by following these patients with periodic clinical evaluations and MRIs. To better understand the natural history of CM-I-syringomyelia, we included patients with an imaging history of at least 10 years. Although patients with worsening symptoms who are conservatively managed are generally recommended for timely surgical intervention, some patients continue to decline surgery due to ongoing controversies surrounding FMD treatment outcomes (NCT02669836, NCT06079125).13

Treatment bias toward conservative management of patients with stable neurological signs and symptoms may have been influenced by factors such as gender (41 males vs 115 females) and residence (urban patients may be more inclined to undergo periodic imaging follow-ups). Although our study represents the largest cohort to date, the sample size remains relatively small, limiting further subgroup analyses of BMI, detailed spinal level or OSAHS severity. Additionally, the small sample size of the group with obvious deterioration may introduce bias. While the spontaneous resolution rate in our cohort (30%) compares favourably with real-world studies, we recognise that spontaneous resolution may be overrepresented in our CM-I-syringomyelia cohort. Furthermore, the true symptom progression rate of CM-I-syringomyelia may be higher in non-research-related, real-world studies. The primary outcome, obvious deterioration, holds significant clinical relevance. CM-I-syringomyelia is a unique imaging-defined condition, and assessing myelopathy severity remains highly controversial. For instance, the Klekamp-Samii score lacks robust cumulative statistical power, while the Chicago Chiari Outcome Scale scores complications separately. This ongoing controversy about Chiari-related myelopathy scoring underscores the lack of effective, simple and specific disease severity scores.23 Many retrospective studies on CM-I-syringomyelia have used specific measures, such as a one-point improvement in the JOA score,23 40 while referencing the natural history of cervical myelopathy16 and CM-I-syringomyelia.7 8 For this reason, we cautiously employed the JOA score as an indicator of myelopathy severity.

Finally, although our serial imaging data suggest that CM-I-syringomyelia may exhibit a waxing-and-waning pattern over time, indicative of dynamic fluid exchange along the central canal, direct human evidence remains limited. Recently, we proposed a minimally invasive subarachnoid-subarachnoid bypass14 for post-traumatic syringomyelia and are working on documenting intrathecal pressure measurements and direct morphological changes in that type of syringomyelia, which may further elucidate the physiology of the human central canal.

Conclusions

Our findings have important implications for clinical practice. CM-I-syringomyelia patients without concurrent OSAHS or a Babinski sign, and with spontaneous resolution of syrinx had a reduced risk of significant deterioration. Moreover, CM-I-syringomyelia patients without concurrent OSAHS and with a cervical syrinx were more likely to have spontaneous resolution of CM-I-syringomyelia than patients with concurrent OSAHS and a cervicothoracic syrinx. Patients with CM-I-syringomyelia, OSAHS and a positive Babinski sign were at higher risk of developing progressive myelopathy. Such patients should be encouraged to undergo surgical decompression to prevent progressive myelopathy. Other patients with CM-I-syringomyelia without OSAHS, the Babinski sign or progressive myelopathy and stable symptoms can consider conservative follow-up with serial clinical and MRI monitoring.
