Machine learning reveals structural determinants of odor detection thresholds and identifies high-potency food odorants
Authors: Xin Hou, Zhilei Zhou, Peiqin Shi, Yan Wang, Shuangping Liu, Zhongwei Ji, Dachuan Zhang, Jian Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Odor detection threshold (ODT) quantifies the perceptual potency of volatile compounds and is a key parameter in flavor chemistry. It is widely used to identify key food odorants, calculate odor activity values, and evaluate the contribution of volatiles to overall aroma perception. However, reliable ODT data remain sparse and inconsistent, and are unavailable for many volatiles detected in metabolomics analyses, complicating the identification of key food odorants and the quantitative interpretation of aroma contributions. Here we developed a machine learning framework to predict aqueous ODTs using a curated dataset of 1003 compounds, achieving a test-set R² of 0.83. External validation with 177 independently measured compounds supported the model’s predictive utility, with 87.0% of predictions falling within a 10-fold deviation and 97.7% within a 100-fold deviation. Structural analysis suggested that aliphatic acids and nitrogen-containing compounds exhibit relatively high ODTs, whereas acyclic sulfur compounds, methoxypyrazines, haloanisoles, and exocyclic esters display low ODTs. Model-guided screening prioritized candidate high-potency odorants, and human sensory evaluation of 12 representative compounds provided preliminary prospective support for the prioritization workflow. These results provide a computational framework for prioritizing potent food odorants and offer data-driven insights into molecular features associated with odor potency in food aroma chemistry.

Introduction

In food flavor science, odor detection threshold (ODT) is a key parameter for identifying key food odorants and evaluating their sensory impact1. Defined as the minimum concentration required to elicit olfactory perception2, ODT provides a quantitative measure of odor potency: compounds with lower ODT values can influence food aroma even at trace concentrations3. However, determining ODTs experimentally remains challenging. Conventional sensory methods rely on geometric dilution series and panel evaluation4, which are labor-intensive and sensitive to variations in panelist sensitivity, experimental design, and environmental conditions. Consequently, reported ODT values for the same compound often vary substantially across studies, in some cases spanning more than three orders of magnitude3,5,6.

Interpretation of odor threshold data is further complicated by inconsistencies in endpoint definition and measurement matrix. A strict distinction exists between ODT, the lowest concentration at which the presence of an odor is perceived, and odor recognition threshold (ORT), the concentration at which the odor quality can be identified. ORTs are typically substantially higher than ODTs, with differences reaching up to two orders of magnitude for certain compounds7–9. In addition, reported odor threshold values depend strongly on the measurement medium, such as water, oil, or air, because of differences in solubility, gas–matrix partitioning behavior10, and interactions between odorants and food matrix components, including lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates11. Therefore, mixing different endpoint definitions and matrices can introduce substantial numerical inconsistency, making standardization of threshold type and experimental matrix essential for quantitative modeling.

Despite the importance of ODTs in flavor chemistry, available experimental data remain limited. Although more than 500,000 odor-active molecules have been reported12, only around 1800 compounds have experimentally determined odor threshold values6. This large data gap makes systematic experimental determination impractical and motivates predictive approaches. Because ODT values are influenced by molecular properties involved in odorant release, transport, and receptor interaction, including hydrophobicity, molecular size, polarity, and stereoelectronic characteristics13,14, they are expected to be associated with molecular structure. However, it remains unclear which structural classes tend to exhibit high ODT values and which are associated with high odor potency.

Quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) modeling provides a promising strategy for estimating odor threshold values from molecular structure. Early studies used linear regression with simple physicochemical descriptors such as van der Waals volume and dipole moment15,16, whereas later nonlinear approaches, including radial basis function neural networks, captured more complex structure–threshold relationships17,18. More recently, larger datasets have enabled broader predictive models for gaseous and aqueous odor thresholds, including studies by Toropov et al.19 and Huang et al.20. However, many existing models relied on heterogeneous datasets in which ODT and ORT values, or thresholds measured in different matrices, were combined to increase data coverage. Such heterogeneity can limit model interpretability and reduce the reliability of structure–ODT relationships.

In addition, most previous threshold-prediction studies have focused on retrospective model evaluation rather than model-guided prioritization followed by experimental sensory validation. As a result, the practical value of structure–ODT modeling for identifying high-potency odorant candidates remains insufficiently explored, leaving a gap between computational prediction and flavor discovery21,22.

To address these challenges, the present study integrates data curation, machine-learning modeling, and experimental sensory validation to investigate structure–ODT associations for aqueous odor thresholds. First, a rigorously curated aqueous ODT dataset was established through standardization of endpoint definitions and measurement matrices, enabling analysis of ODT distributions across major chemical classes. Second, machine-learning models based on molecular descriptors were developed to predict ODT values and identify molecular features associated with odor potency. Third, model performance was evaluated using an external validation set with laboratory-measured ODTs, and model-guided screening was applied to prioritize high-potency food odorant candidates for sensory verification. By combining curated data, interpretable machine learning, and sensory experiments, this study provides a practical and interpretable framework for aqueous ODT prediction and candidate odorant prioritization in food aroma research.

Results

Dataset characteristics and chemical space of aqueous odor thresholds

A standardized aqueous ODT dataset containing 1003 unique compounds was curated through literature collection, medium restriction, structural standardization, and data cleaning (see “Methods”). For modeling, ODTs (T, mg L⁻¹) were transformed into pT values using the negative logarithmic transformation shown in Eq. 1, where higher pT values correspond to lower odor thresholds and therefore greater odor potency. This transformation reduced the large dynamic range of threshold values and enabled model development on a logarithmic odor-potency scale.

The resulting dataset showed a roughly unimodal distribution of pT values (Fig. 1A). Most compounds were concentrated between pT 1 and 5, corresponding approximately to aqueous ODTs of 0.01–100 mg L⁻¹, with the highest density around pT ≈ 3, corresponding to approximately 1 mg L⁻¹. To examine the structural coverage of the dataset, Morgan fingerprints were projected into two dimensions using UMAP. The resulting map showed that the 1003 compounds occupied multiple regions of chemical space rather than forming a single compact cluster (Fig. 1B). When pT values were overlaid onto the UMAP projection, compounds with different odor potencies were broadly distributed across the chemical space, with some local enrichment of high- or low-pT compounds. This pattern suggests that aqueous ODT variation is associated with molecular structure, while also indicating that odor potency is not determined by a single structural class alone.

Odor detection threshold distributions across chemical classes

To examine whether ODT variation followed interpretable structural patterns, the dataset was grouped into aliphatic and cyclic systems and further subdivided by major functional classes (Fig. 1C, D and Table S1). Among aliphatic compounds, acids and nitrogen-containing compounds were concentrated in the low-pT region and showed relatively narrow dispersion, indicating generally high thresholds. In contrast, hydrocarbons, aldehydes, ketones, and esters exhibited much broader pT distributions, suggesting substantial within-class heterogeneity and indicating that certain members of these classes can display markedly lower ODTs. Aliphatic alcohols and sulfur-containing compounds also displayed wide threshold ranges, with several sulfur compounds extending into the high-pT region.

Distinct patterns were also observed among cyclic compounds (Fig. 1D). Cyclic alcohols tended to have a lower pT range, whereas cyclic ketones and heterocyclic classes such as thiazoles and furans showed broader distributions. Within the cyclic ester category, lactones were mainly located in the lower pT region, while compounds bearing exocyclic ester groups were enriched in the higher pT region. Prominent high-pT outliers were also evident in the pyrazine and benzene-derivative classes. These potent compounds were often characterized by recurring substitution patterns: methoxy-substituted pyrazines were frequent among the high-pT pyrazines, whereas highly potent benzene derivatives commonly carried both methoxy and halogen substituents. Together, these results show that odor thresholds are not randomly distributed across chemical classes but are associated with scaffold type and substitution pattern.

To further assess generalization stability, five-fold cross-validation was performed. The XGBoost model achieved a cross-validated R² of 0.7980 ± 0.0394, RMSE of 0.8268 ± 0.0753, and MAE of 0.6045 ± 0.0448. These cross-validation results were consistent with the independent test-set performance, indicating that the model performance was not driven by a single favorable train–test split.

The contrast between models also revealed differences in generalization behavior. K-nearest neighbors, for example, produced an almost perfect fit on the training set (R² = 0.9987) but a substantially lower test-set R² of 0.7677, indicating strong overfitting. In comparison, XGBoost maintained a more balanced performance between the training and test sets, with a smaller reduction from training to test performance than KNN and the lowest test-set RMSE among the tested models.

Model reliability was further examined using Y-randomization. When pT values were randomly reassigned to molecular structures, the resulting models showed markedly reduced R² values, approaching or falling below zero, together with substantially increased RMSE and MAE compared with the original XGBoost model (Fig. 2B). This pronounced deterioration indicates that the predictive signal captured by the XGBoost model reflects non-random structure–threshold associations rather than chance correlations.

External validation on commercially available odorants

To assess whether the model retained predictive performance beyond the internal test set, an external validation set comprising 177 commercially available odorants was established, and their aqueous ODTs were independently determined by human sensory evaluation in our laboratory (Fig. 3A, B; see Methods). None of the validation compounds were included in model training. Nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity analysis showed that the external validation compounds had an average maximum Tanimoto coefficient of 0.5771 relative to the training set, indicating moderate structural similarity rather than complete structural independence. Using nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity thresholds, compounds with maximum similarity ≥ 0.6 were classified as in-domain, those with 0.4 ≤ similarity < 0.6 as borderline, and those with similarity < 0.4 as out-of-domain. Accordingly, 58, 85, and 34 external validation compounds were assigned to the in-domain, borderline, and out-of-domain categories, respectively (Table S5). External validation supported the predictive utility of the model for independent commercially available odorants (Fig. 3B). For the 177 independently measured compounds, 87.0% of predictions were classified as satisfactory, corresponding to predictions within a 10-fold deviation from the experimental ODT values, while 10.7% were classified as acceptable, corresponding to predictions within a 100-fold deviation. Only 2.3% of the external validation compounds fell outside the 100-fold deviation range. Considering the well-known variability of odor threshold measurements, where reported ODT values for the same compound may vary across orders of magnitude owing to differences in experimental protocols, panel composition, and data-processing methods4,5,8, predictions within one order of magnitude can be considered useful for odorant prioritization. Therefore, these results indicate that the model provides practical predictive support for estimating aqueous odor potency, particularly for compounds that fall within or near the chemical domain represented by the training data.

Compared with previous QSPR models for odor thresholds, which typically reported moderate predictive power (e.g., test R² around 0.6–0.7)19, these results support the practical applicability of the present framework for estimating odor potency and prioritizing candidate odorants.

Interpretation of the XGBoost model

To identify the molecular features most strongly associated with the XGBoost predictions of aqueous odor detection thresholds, the optimized XGBoost model was interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis (Fig. 4). The SHAP dependence plots suggested model-derived directional associations between descriptor values and predicted pT values. Higher values of PEOE_VSA1, NHOHCount, and TPSA were generally associated with negative SHAP contributions, indicating that molecules with larger electrostatic surface contributions and greater hydrogen-bonding or polar surface characteristics tended to have lower predicted pT values, corresponding to higher predicted ODTs. This trend is consistent with the expectation that highly polar or strongly hydrogen-bonding molecules may be less efficiently transferred from the aqueous phase to the headspace, thereby reducing their apparent odor potency in water-based threshold measurements23,24.

In contrast, descriptors related to molecular size, refractivity, and topological/electronic structure, including MolWt, MolMR, Chi1v, and BCUT descriptors, showed positive SHAP contributions within specific descriptor ranges. These results suggest that larger or more polarizable molecules may be associated with lower predicted ODTs in the model, possibly because such descriptors capture structural features related to volatility, hydrophobic partitioning, and molecular interaction potential25,26. However, these associations should be interpreted as model-derived physicochemical trends rather than direct evidence of receptor-level binding mechanisms.

Notably, several electronic, topological, and surface-area descriptors ranked above the classical hydrophobicity descriptor MolLogP. This finding suggests that aqueous odor thresholds are not captured solely by overall hydrophobicity, but are also associated with more specific molecular features, including electrostatic distribution, hydrogen-bonding capacity, molecular size, polarizability, and structural connectivity. Because the present model was based on two-dimensional molecular descriptors and did not include receptor-specific binding, docking, or three-dimensional conformational analysis, the SHAP results should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating, rather than as direct mechanistic evidence.

Model-guided prioritization of high-potency food odorants

To explore whether the model could be used not only for retrospective prediction but also for prospective odorant prioritization, a model-guided screening workflow was applied to a food-related candidate set. Candidate molecules were first assembled from the FEMA flavor ingredient list27. After removing compounds without retrievable SMILES structures, 1679 molecules remained. Further removal of compounds already present in the modeling dataset, including the training and external validation sets, yielded 1041 unique candidate molecules for prospective screening.

The XGBoost model was then used to predict aqueous ODTs, expressed as pT values, for these candidates. Compounds predicted to exhibit very low thresholds, corresponding to high predicted pT values, were retained as prioritized candidates. This procedure yielded 102 molecules predicted to possess low aqueous ODTs and high odor potency (Table S3). Human sensory evaluation provided preliminary prospective support for the model-guided prioritization workflow. Among the 12 screened odorants, 8 compounds were classified as satisfactory, with predicted thresholds within a 10-fold deviation from the experimentally measured values, whereas the remaining 4 compounds were classified as acceptable, with predicted thresholds within a 100-fold deviation (Fig. 3B and Table 2). These results indicate that the model was useful for prioritizing high-potency candidate odorants, although the 12-compound verification set should be interpreted as preliminary prospective evidence rather than exhaustive validation.

Several prioritized compounds, including esters, sulfur-containing aliphatics, aldehydes, ketones, and methoxypyrazines, were experimentally found to exhibit very low aqueous ODTs. In particular, sulfur-containing compounds and methoxypyrazines showed low experimental thresholds, consistent with the class-level threshold patterns observed earlier (Fig. 1C, D), where sulfur-containing compounds and pyrazine derivatives frequently appeared in the high-pT region. These findings support the use of the model as a practical tool for candidate ranking and targeted experimental verification of high-potency food odorants.

Discussion

ODT determines which volatile compounds contribute to perceived food aroma, yet systematic modeling of these thresholds has been limited by heterogeneous data and sparse experimental measurements. In this study, a carefully curated dataset of aqueous ODTs of 1003 unique compounds enabled the development of a machine-learning framework capable of predicting odor potency and supporting model-guided prioritization of high-potency food odorants.

The roughly unimodal distribution of pT values and the stable predictive performance observed in the test set and cross-validation together indicate that careful endpoint definition and data standardization substantially improved the reliability of the curated aqueous ODT dataset. Odor threshold data reported in the literature are inherently heterogeneous because reported values vary with endpoint definition, panel design, and experimental conditions3,5,6. In particular, ODTs and ORTs differ conceptually and numerically, and reported ODT values also vary strongly across matrices because volatility and partitioning depend on the surrounding medium7,8,10. Restricting the present dataset to aqueous ODTs, merging duplicate compounds by standardized molecular structures, and aggregating repeated aqueous ODT records in log space using geometric means therefore likely reduced major sources of noise and helped reveal more reliable structure–ODT associations. This result reinforces a broader point increasingly emphasized in food AI research: predictive reliability depends not only on the choice of algorithm but also on careful definition, standardization, and uncertainty-aware curation of the experimental endpoint20,21,28.

Analysis of chemical classes further showed that aqueous ODTs follow recognizable structural trends rather than being randomly distributed across chemical space. Aliphatic acids and nitrogen-containing compounds tended to show lower pT values, corresponding to higher aqueous ODTs, which is consistent with their strong polarity and hydration that may hinder transfer from water into the headspace and reduce apparent odor potency23,29. In contrast, hydrocarbons, aldehydes, ketones, esters, and alcohols displayed much broader pT distributions, indicating that when dominant functional groups are less polar, factors such as scaffold size, branching, and substitution pattern may contribute more strongly to variation in aqueous ODTs30,31. Cyclic classes further emphasize the importance of scaffold architecture: differences between cyclic alcohols, cyclic ketones, heterocycles, and ester structures suggest that ring size, conformational flexibility, and substituent position are associated with class-dependent ODT variation32–34. Particularly notable are methoxypyrazines and haloanisole-like benzene derivatives, which repeatedly appeared among the high-pT, low-ODT odorants, consistent with previous observations linking methoxy substitution patterns to high odor potency20,35,36.

The SHAP interpretation of the XGBoost model provided a model-derived physicochemical interpretation for these trends. Descriptors related to electrostatic surface area, polarity, and hydrogen-bonding capacity showed negative SHAP contributions to predicted pT values, suggesting that highly polar molecules may tend to remain more strongly solvated in water and therefore transfer less efficiently into the headspace23,37. Conversely, descriptors associated with molecular size and polarizability contributed positively in part of their ranges, suggesting that larger and more polarizable molecules may be associated with lower predicted aqueous ODTs, possibly through effects related to volatility, hydrophobic partitioning, and molecular interaction potential25,26. Importantly, several electronic and topological descriptors ranked above the global hydrophobicity parameter MolLogP, indicating that aqueous ODTs are not captured solely by bulk hydrophobicity but are also associated with more specific electrostatic, hydrogen-bonding, topological, and structural features. Thus, SHAP-derived patterns should be interpreted as associative physicochemical trends rather than direct evidence of receptor binding or molecular recognition mechanisms.

External validation against 177 commercially available odorants with independently measured aqueous ODTs supported the model’s practical predictive utility despite the well-known variability of sensory threshold measurements across laboratories4,5,8. The model also provided preliminary support for prospective odorant prioritization: among the 12 model-prioritized candidates selected for sensory verification, 8 showed satisfactory agreement with experimental aqueous ODTs and the remaining 4 fell within the acceptable range. This result is particularly relevant for food aroma research because trace-active odorants—such as methoxypyrazines, sulfur compounds, haloanisoles, and certain esters—can shape aroma perception at extremely low concentrations. Notably, several of the compounds prioritized by the model belong to chemical families that are well recognized as highly potent odorants in food systems. Methoxypyrazines, for example, are widely known contributors to green and vegetable-like aromas in vegetables and certain wines38, whereas sulfur-containing aliphatics frequently contribute to onion-, garlic-, or cooked-meat-like odor notes39. By prioritizing candidate molecules from large chemical spaces before sensory testing, the present framework provides a rational strategy for ranking potent odorant candidates that may influence food flavor.

In practical aroma research, flavor scientists frequently encounter situations where ODT values are unavailable for newly identified volatile compounds. For example, non-targeted GC–MS or GC×GC metabolomics analyses of foods often reveal hundreds of volatile molecules, many of which lack reported ODT values. In such cases, researchers must decide which compounds are likely to contribute to aroma and therefore merit sensory evaluation or quantitative analysis. The present framework can help address this data gap by providing rapid estimates of odor potency directly from molecular structure, enabling prioritization of candidate key odorants for further evaluation when experimental ODT data are unavailable. This capability may be particularly useful during early stages of aroma characterization, when researchers seek to narrow large lists of detected volatiles to a smaller set of compounds that are most likely to influence perceived flavor.

Several limitations should nevertheless be acknowledged. The present model relies on two-dimensional molecular descriptors and therefore does not explicitly capture stereochemistry, conformational flexibility, or receptor-specific interactions, all of which can influence odor potency. Although applicability-domain and bootstrap uncertainty analyses were introduced to improve prediction interpretation, predictions for out-of-domain compounds should still be treated with caution. Predictions may also be less reliable for chemically unstable or strongly adsorptive compounds, such as certain polysulfides and terpene esters, whose ODT values are difficult to measure accurately in aqueous systems40–42. In addition, the model is calibrated for aqueous matrices and may not directly extend to lipid- or ethanol-rich food systems where partitioning behavior differs substantially.

Despite these limitations, the present work provides interpretable structure–ODT associations and a practical tool for prioritizing candidate key odorants. By combining curated aqueous ODT data, machine-learning prediction, applicability-domain assessment, and experimental sensory verification, this framework may help flavor researchers address ODT data gaps and prioritize key aroma candidates in complex food systems.

Methods

Data curation and standardization

This study used the monograph Odor Thresholds (2011 edition) edited by Van Gemert as the primary data source, supplemented by peer-reviewed literature published after 201143,44,. To ensure a clearly defined modeling endpoint, only ODTs measured in water or aqueous solution were retained. Odor recognition thresholds, thresholds measured in air, oil, or other non-aqueous matrices, entries with ambiguous endpoint definitions, mixtures, metal-containing salts, and compounds without defined molecular structures were not considered for model development.

Canonical SMILES for each compound were retrieved from the PubChem database and standardized using RDKit. Duplicate compounds were merged according to canonical SMILES. For compounds with only one reported aqueous ODT value, the reported value was directly retained. For compounds with multiple reported aqueous ODT values, all values were first converted to mg L⁻¹ and examined on a logarithmic order-of-magnitude scale. A frequency histogram was generated according to the log₁₀ magnitude of the reported values. The most frequent order-of-magnitude bin and its two neighboring bins were retained as the representative value cluster, whereas values far outside this range were regarded as inconsistent reports and were not used for consensus calculation. The final consensus ODT was calculated as the geometric mean of the retained values, which is equivalent to averaging threshold values in log space.

The number of original ODT records, minimum and maximum reported ODT values, geometric mean ODT, and the standard deviation of log₁₀-transformed ODT values were recorded where applicable to describe literature-level variability. The final curated dataset contained 1003 discrete odorants and is provided in Table S1. For model development, the consensus ODT value (T, mg L⁻¹) was transformed into pT according to Eq. 1:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{pT}=-{\mathrm{log}}_{10}(T\times {10}^{-3})$$\end{document}pT=−log10(T×10−3)where higher pT values correspond to lower ODTs and therefore greater odor potency.

Chemical classification and structure–threshold analysis

To examine how molecular structure influences odor detection thresholds, the curated dataset was classified according to molecular scaffolds and functional groups. Molecular structures were visualized from canonical SMILES using RDKit (version 2024.3.5), and compounds were manually categorized based on their structural characteristics. Specifically, molecules were first divided into two major structural systems, aliphatic and cyclic, depending on whether ring structures were present. Each group was then further subdivided according to dominant functional groups and key structural features.

The distributions of pT values within each structural class were then analyzed to identify systematic structure–threshold patterns across different chemical families.

Molecular representation and feature engineering

Molecular descriptors were calculated from canonical SMILES using RDKit (version 2024.3.5). The calculated descriptors covered multiple physicochemical and topological properties, including molecular size, electronic characteristics, hydrogen-bonding capacity, molecular connectivity, and surface-related features. Prior to model development, descriptors were curated to remove uninformative or unstable variables. Descriptors with zero variance across the dataset were removed, and descriptors containing excessive missing values were excluded. Missing values in the retained descriptor matrix were imputed using the mean value calculated from the training set. To avoid information leakage, preprocessing parameters were fitted using the training set and then applied to the test set and external prediction datasets.

In addition, Morgan fingerprints were generated to represent molecular structures in high-dimensional binary space. For visualization of chemical space and structure–threshold relationships, dimensionality reduction was performed using the unsupervised UMAP algorithm with cosine distance.

After descriptor filtering, 121 molecular descriptors were retained for model development. The final descriptor matrix used for model training is provided in Table S4. These descriptors were used as input variables for machine-learning model construction and comparison.

Morgan fingerprints were generated to represent molecular structures in high-dimensional binary space. For chemical-space visualization, canonical SMILES were converted into Morgan fingerprints using RDKit with a radius of 2 and a bit length of 2048. Dimensionality reduction was then performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). UMAP was conducted with n_neighbors = 150, min_dist = 0.75, spread = 1.5, metric = cosine, and random_state = 42. The relatively large neighborhood size and minimum distance were selected to emphasize the global distribution of the chemical space rather than highly compact local clustering.

Model interpretability

Nine machine-learning algorithms were evaluated, including ensemble models (XGBoost, Random Forest, GBDT, AdaBoost, CatBoost, LightGBM), a neural network model (multi-layer perceptron, MLP), and classical statistical learning approaches (support vector machine, SVM; k-nearest neighbors, KNN). The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%) using random_state = 42. Hyperparameters were optimized using GridSearchCV combined with five-fold cross-validation on the training set. The coefficient of determination (R²) was used as the scoring metric. The hyperparameter search space and optimized parameters of the final XGBoost model are provided in Table S6.

Model performance was evaluated using mean squared error, root mean squared error, mean absolute error, and the coefficient of determination. To further assess the robustness of the optimized model, five-fold cross-validation performance was reported as mean ± standard deviation. To verify that the learned structure–threshold relationships were not due to chance correlations, Y-randomization tests were performed by randomly shuffling pT values in the training set while keeping the descriptor matrix unchanged (n = 100). The five-fold cross-validation and Y-randomization results are provided in Table S7.

To interpret the contribution of molecular features to odor thresholds, the best-performing model was analyzed using SHAP45. SHAP values quantify the marginal contribution of each descriptor to model predictions and enable identification of structural features associated with odor potency.

External validation

To evaluate predictive robustness using independent experimental measurements, an external validation set consisting of 177 commercially available odorants was established. These compounds were selected according to the following criteria: they were not included in the model-training dataset, had defined molecular structures and retrievable SMILES, were commercially available as analytical standards, covered diverse chemical classes and structural features, and included food-related aroma compounds relevant to flavor research. Among them, 104 compounds were listed in the FEMA flavor ingredient database. Their aqueous ODTs were determined experimentally in our laboratory through human sensory evaluation (see “Computational environment and software”).

To characterize the structural relationship between the external validation compounds and the training set, nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity was calculated using Morgan fingerprints with a radius of 2 and 2048 bits. Applicability-domain status was assigned according to the maximum Tanimoto similarity to the training set, and the corresponding results are provided in Table S5. Bootstrap-based prediction uncertainty was also estimated for the external validation compounds and is reported in Table S5.

Prediction accuracy was quantified using the absolute difference between predicted and experimental pT values (|ΔpT|). Because pT is a base-10 logarithmic transformation of ODT, a difference of 1 pT unit corresponds to an approximately 10-fold difference in ODT, whereas a difference of 2 pT units corresponds to an approximately 100-fold difference. Screening of high-potency food odorants

To evaluate whether the developed model could be applied for model-guided prioritization of potent food odorants, a screening workflow was implemented. Candidate molecules were first assembled from the FEMA flavor ingredient list. After removing compounds without retrievable SMILES structures, 1679 molecules remained.

To ensure independence from the modeling and external validation datasets, compounds already present in the curated dataset used for model development and external validation were removed. This step yielded a final screening set of 1041 candidate molecules. Canonical SMILES for these compounds were used to calculate molecular descriptors following the same procedures applied during model construction. The optimized XGBoost model was then applied to predict aqueous ODTs for all candidate molecules, expressed as pT values.

Compounds predicted to exhibit very low aqueous ODTs (pT ≥ 5, corresponding to predicted ODT ≤ 0.01 mg L⁻¹) were prioritized as potential high-potency odorants. This screening step yielded 102 candidate molecules. Applicability-domain status and bootstrap-based prediction uncertainty for these FEMA candidate compounds were evaluated and are provided in Table S5. The full list of candidate compounds predicted to exhibit very low aqueous ODTs is provided in Table S3. From these candidates, 12 commercially available compounds were selected for preliminary prospective sensory verification.

Sensory evaluation

For the 189 odorants evaluated in this study, including 177 compounds for external validation and 12 compounds for preliminary prospective sensory verification, analytical standards were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd., Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., and Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Except for two compounds (purity 85%), all standards had a gas chromatographic purity of ≥90% (Table S2).

Sensory evaluation experiments were conducted in an ISO 8589:2007–compliant sensory laboratory with the ambient temperature controlled at 25 ± 1 °C. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The experimental protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University (Approval No. JNU202509RB028). Oral informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangnan University because the sensory evaluation involved no more than minimal risk to participants.

Panel selection and training followed ISO 8586:2023. Candidate assessors were first screened for normal olfactory function using an n-butanol threshold test to exclude anosmia. Thirty trained panelists (age 25 ± 3 years; gender ratio 1:1) were then recruited and trained to perform the three-alternative forced-choice (3-AFC) procedure and odor intensity calibration. Only panelists demonstrating consistent performance during training were included in the final panel.

ODTs were determined according to ISO 13301:2018 using the 3-AFC method combined with the best-estimate threshold calculation. Test solutions were prepared in ultrapure water using geometric dilution series with a dilution factor of 3. The starting concentration for each compound was selected based on its expected odor potency and preliminary range-finding tests, so that the highest concentration was detectable by trained panelists. Samples (20 mL) were transferred into 60 mL amber sniffing bottles and equilibrated for at least 1 h prior to evaluation to ensure headspace stabilization.

During each trial, panelists were presented with three samples (two blanks and one odorant sample) and asked to identify the sample containing the odorant. Samples were coded and presented in randomized order, and panelists were blinded to compound identity and sample position during the 3-AFC test. Each concentration level was tested in triplicate. Individual thresholds were calculated using the BET method, and the geometric mean across panelists was reported as the group ODT. Variability among panelists was summarized using the standard deviation of log₁₀-transformed individual thresholds, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the group ODT where applicable. The corresponding sensory results are provided in Table S2.

Computational environment and software

All data preprocessing, modeling, and visualization were performed using Python 3.7 within the Anaconda distribution. Machine-learning analyses were implemented using scikit-learn (1.3.2), XGBoost (2.1.4), LightGBM (4.5.0), CatBoost (1.2.8), and Keras (2.13.1). Data processing and visualization were performed using pandas (2.0.3), NumPy (1.24.4), seaborn (0.13.2), and matplotlib (3.7.3).

Safety

No unexpected or unusually high safety hazards were encountered during this study. All odorants were handled in a well-ventilated laboratory in accordance with their Safety Data Sheets. Sensory evaluation was performed using food-grade standards or high-purity chemicals diluted to safe inhalation concentrations.
