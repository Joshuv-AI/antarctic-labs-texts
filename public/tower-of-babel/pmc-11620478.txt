Quantitative measures of recent and lifetime agricultural pesticide use are associated with increased pesticide concentrations in house dust
Authors: Shuai Xie, Jonathan N. Hofmann, Joshua N. Sampson, Pabitra R. Josse, Jessica M. Madrigal, Vicky C. Chang, Nicole C. Deziel, Gabriella Andreotti, Alexander P. Keil, Mary H. Ward, Laura E. Beane Freeman, Melissa C. Friesen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Objective:Elevated pesticide concentrations have been found in dust from homes with residents who use agricultural pesticides, but few studies have compared these concentrations to quantitative measures of their use. We evaluated household pesticide dust concentrations in relation to quantitative, active ingredient-specific metrics of agricultural pesticide use in the Biomarkers of Exposure and Effect in Agriculture Study.Methods:Participants provided vacuum dust samples (2013–2018) and information regarding recent (last 12 months) and lifetime pesticide use. Thirty-two pesticide analytes were measured in 295 dust samples from 213 participants; 54 had repeated measurements (median = 96 days between visits). We used mixed-effects quantile regression models to estimate relative differences in pesticide concentrations for recent and lifetime agricultural use (number of days, intensity-weighted days), recent home/garden use (yes/no), and household characteristics. Only household characteristics were examined for dacthal because of no use information. We calculated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to evaluate temporal variability. We report only descriptive statistics for pesticides with detection rates <25 %.Results:For currently used pesticides, quantitative measures of recent agricultural use were associated with significantly increased household pesticide dust concentrations for malathion, metolachlor, acetochlor, cyfluthrin, and atrazine (p-trends < 0.001), but not permethrin. Similarly, quantitative measures of lifetime use were associated with increased concentrations of malathion, metolachlor, carbaryl, diazinon, and atrazine (p-trends < 0.001), but not permethrin, chlorpyrifos, or chlorothalonil. For banned pesticides, ever agricultural use was associated with elevated chlordane and heptachlor concentrations and non-significantly elevated dieldrin concentrations, but not lindane, p,p-DDD, p,p-DDE, or p,p-DDT. Recent home/garden use predicted increased malathion, carbaryl, and cyfluthrin concentrations. ICCs (range = 0.57–0.90) suggested moderate to high correlation over 3–6 months. Detection rates were <25 % for alachlor, butylate, EPTC, metribuzin, simazine, carbofuran, coumaphos, as well as for three banned pesticides (cyanazine, aldrin, endosulfan).Conclusions:Household pesticide dust concentrations were strongly associated with the frequency of agricultural pesticide use.

Introduction

Pesticide residues have been frequently detected in house dust samples (Deziel et al., 2017; Teysseire et al., 2021). House dust is an indoor reservoir that protects pesticide residues from weathering processing, microbial decay, and UV degradation (Anthopolos et al., 2012). Studies consistently reported higher pesticide dust concentrations in farm homes compared to non-farm homes (Bennett et al., 2019; Coronado et al., 2011; Curwin et al., 2005; Gunier et al., 2011; McCauley et al., 2003), during planting season (Tamaro et al., 2018), and in homes where farmers personally mix and apply pesticides (Golla et al., 2012). Similarly, higher concentrations have been observed in homes living in proximity to pesticide-treated fields (Madrigal et al., 2023). Other factors that can influence pesticide dust concentrations include the number of family members working in agriculture (Gunier et al., 2011; McCauley et al., 2001; Plascak et al., 2019; Quandt et al., 2004), crop types (Coronado et al., 2006), and different job tasks/work types (Coronado et al., 2004; Fenske et al., 2013; 2002; Lu et al., 2000; Simcox et al., 1995; Smith et al., 2017; Tamaro et al., 2018).

Agricultural pesticide use in these studies was primarily evaluated using proxy measures (e.g., job tasks, farm homes vs. non-farm homes). Only a few studies have utilized quantitative or timing-based measures to investigate how agricultural pesticide use patterns affect pesticide dust concentrations (Curwin et al., 2005; Golla et al., 2012; Lozier et al., 2012). For instance, a study conducted in Iowa found that concentrations of atrazine and metolachlor were significantly higher in vacuum dust collected from farm homes within seven days of use compared to both farm homes without use and non-farm homes (Curwin et al., 2005). Likewise, household atrazine dust concentrations were higher with increased hours of mixing and applying soybean pesticides (Golla et al., 2012), and with increased number of pounds, days, and acres of atrazine handled and applied (Lozier et al., 2012).

In addition, elevated pesticide dust concentrations have been associated with use of pesticides, in and around the home (hereafter, home/garden use) to treat insects (e.g., ants/flies/cockroaches, fleas/ticks, and bees/wasps/hornets) and lawn and garden weeds. For example, analyses conducted in the California Childhood Leukemia Study, a population-based case-control study, observed that treatments for fleas/ticks were associated with higher concentrations of chlorpyrifos (Gunier et al., 2011), permethrin (Deziel et al., 2015a), and cypermethrin (Madrigal et al., 2023). Similarly, treatments for lawn/garden weeds were associated with higher concentrations of glyphosate (Ward et al., 2023), 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), mecoprop, dicamba (Deziel et al., 2015a), and carbaryl (Madrigal et al., 2023). In a meta-regression analysis, pesticide dust concentrations were on average 1.3 to 1.5 times higher in homes with home/garden pesticide use versus no use, with concentrations varying based on the likelihood an active ingredient was used for a particular home/garden treatment (Deziel et al., 2017).

Indoor household dust may be a significant indoor health hazard, particularly for children (Lewis et al., 1994; Roberts et al., 2009). For example, in a region with high agricultural pesticide use, dacthal dust concentrations in homes were associated with an increased risk of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Metayer et al., 2013). Among Latino children living in an orchard community in Oregon, significant associations were observed between organophosphate pesticide concentrations in house dust and emotional and behavioral deficits (somatic problems, p = 0.02; thought problems, p = 0.05) (Khan et al., 2024). Additionally, in adults, increasing levels of organochlorine insecticides or metabolites in household dust have been linked to non-Hodgkin lymphoma (Colt et al., 2006; Colt et al., 2005). Thus, it is important to fill the research gap to identify potential determinants of pesticide dust concentrations.

To gain insights into potential contributors to indoor pesticide concentrations in homes in agricultural settings, with a focus on agricultural pesticide use by a household resident, we evaluated associations of pesticide concentrations in house dust samples from participants in the Biomarkers of Exposure and Effect in Agriculture (BEEA) Study, a sub-study of the Agricultural Health Study (AHS). This population provided a unique opportunity to evaluate quantitative, active ingredient-specific metrics of recent and lifetime agricultural pesticide use, and provided repeated measurements with which to evaluate temporal variability. In addition, we investigated associations with recent home/garden use (yes/no), and household characteristics. We focused on 32 pesticide analytes that could be quantified via same extraction and analytical method, prioritizing pesticides known to be used by BEEA participants. Characterizing pesticide concentrations and identifying sources of indoor pesticide contamination is a first step in identifying exposure mitigation strategies. It also provides insights into improving future exposure assessment for epidemiologic studies.

Methods

Study population and dust sample collection

The BEEA Study included 1681 male private pesticide applicators with current and/or former agricultural pesticide use (hereafter, farmers) (Hofmann et al., 2015) and 211 males who had not lived or worked on a farm or held a job that involved mixing, loading, or applying pesticides within the last 10 years or for more than 12 months since the age of 18 (hereafter, non-farmers) (Chang et al., 2023). All BEEA farmers were 50 years of age or older, living in Iowa or North Carolina, who had completed three prior AHS questionnaires eliciting information on agricultural pesticide use. These farmers are predominantly non-Hispanic White and had never been diagnosed with cancer other than non-melanoma skin cancer at the time of BEEA recruitment (2010–2018). Similarly, the non-farmers were cancer-free, and had similar age, geographic (state and county of residence), and race and ethnicity distributions as the farmers. During each home visit, farmers and non-farmers completed a BEEA questionnaire and provided biospecimens; 63 farmers and no non-farmers had repeated visits. The farmers with repeated visits were all located in Iowa and had been selected for investigating the applicators’ exposure to permethrin, with visits timed for the off-season, on the day after permethrin application and approximately three weeks following permethrin application (Shearer et al., 2019), or to investigate their exposure to bioaerosols, with visits timed in the spring and fall (Sauvé et al., 2020).

From November 2013 through January 2018, a dust sample was collected from the canister or bag of each participant’s most frequently used vacuum during home visits. In total, 873 dust samples were collected, representing 532 active and former farmers with a single visit, 56 active farmers with 2–3 visits (140 samples), and 201 non-farmers with a single visit. These participants completed a dust collection questionnaire, which queried information about their household (as described below). The dust samples were shipped to the Southwest Research Institute (San Antonio, TX), where they were aliquoted, sieved at 150 μM, and stored in a −20 °C freezer. All participants provided written informed consent, and study procedures were approved by Institutional Review Boards at the National Cancer Institute and other relevant organizations.

Dust selection and laboratory analysis

For this study, we selected 295 of the 873 dust samples to be analyzed. We first selected samples that allowed us to examine temporal variability and to ensure good coverage of recent agricultural pesticide usage. As a result, we selected all available samples from active farmers with multiple home visits. This comprised 136 samples with sufficient dust mass from 54 farmers (median (interquartile range [IQR]): 96 (22–188) days between visits). For the remaining 159 samples, using a random number generator in Stata, we randomly selected 138 of the 532 samples from active and non-active farmers with a single visit (26 % of samples) and we randomly selected 21 of the 201 samples from non-farmers (10 % of samples). The dust samples were extracted and analyzed by Southwest Research Institute for 32 pesticide analytes, with detectable concentrations reported in units of ng/g. The extraction, analysis, and quality control methods are described in Supplemental Appendix A and Supplemental Table S1.

Exposure determinants from questionnaires

Recent and lifetime pesticide use

From the BEEA and AHS questionnaires (available at https://aghealth.nih.gov/collaboration/questionnaires.html), we extracted active ingredient-specific responses of agricultural pesticide use and home/garden pesticide use, as described previously (Hofmann et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2023). In the BEEA questionnaire, farmers provided detailed information about agricultural pesticide use in the last 12 months (hereafter, recent agricultural use), including trade names, number of days used, dates of the three most recent uses, application method, and personal protective equipment (PPE) use. Farmers and non-farmers also reported the trade names of pesticide products used in their home/garden in the last 12 months (hereafter, recent home/garden use). We derived variables for each active ingredient, including recent agricultural use (yes/no, number of days used, intensity-weighted days used, and days since last use) and recent home/garden use (yes/no). The intensity-weighted days metric was calculated by multiplying the number of days by an intensity-weighting factor derived from an algorithm that considers frequency of mixing or loading of pesticides (no, <50 % of the time, >50 % of the time), application method (e.g., hand spray, boom spray, and in-furrow), repairing equipment (yes/no), as well as the use of PPE as a protection factor (e.g., chemically resistant gloves, face shield, googles, and boots) (Coble et al., 2011).

We obtained information on farmers’ lifetime agricultural pesticide use from AHS questionnaires. At AHS enrollment (1993–1997), farmers were asked whether they have ever personally mixed or applied 50 specific pesticides, the number of days of its use in an average year, and the number of years of its use. These questions were not specifically limited to occupational use; although we assumed the reported use by farmers were predominantly occupational, their use may also include home/garden use. In the AHS Phase 2 (1999–2003) and Phase 3 (2005–2010) questionnaires, farmers provided updated information on use of specific pesticides since the previous questionnaire. From the AHS and BEEA questionnaires we derived three variables describing the farmers’ agricultural use of each pesticide during their working lifetime up to the day of dust sample collection (hereafter, lifetime agricultural use): ever/never use, number of days, and intensity-weighted days (Hofmann et al., 2021).

Household characteristics

From the BEEA dust collection questionnaire, we extracted household characteristics, as previously described (Xie et al., 2023). Several vacuum-related characteristics were obtained, including vacuum type, number of days since the vacuum was last used or emptied, whether the vacuum bag or canister was changed or emptied since the last use, number of days between the vacuum’s last emptying and use, and locations where the vacuum was used other than inside the home. Additional characteristics included carpet age (>5 years, 1–5 years, 6 months-1 year, and <6 months), year house was built (before 1940, 1940–1969, 1970–1989, 1990–2009, and after 2009), number of rooms vacuumed, number of rooms with carpets or rugs vacuumed, the proportion of uncarpeted rooms vacuumed, presence of air conditioning, as well as whether shoes (all shoes and, separately, work shoes) were removed before entering the house (always, sometimes, never). From the questionnaire, we also extracted information on cigarette smoking status (never, former, current) and whether participants owned cats (yes/no) or dogs (yes/no). We also categorized participants by the season of dust sample collection (September–November, December–February, March–May, and June–August).

Information on the distance between farmer’s residence and the nearest pesticide-treated fields (hereafter, distance of home to field) was obtained from the question “How far is your home from the nearest field or orchard where pesticides are applied” in the AHS take-home questionnaire at enrollment. We identified farmers who had moved by comparing the addresses reported in the BEEA and the AHS enrollment questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

For each pesticide, we calculated the detection rate (percentage of measurements with a laboratory-reported concentration), as well as the median and interquartile range (IQR) among detected concentrations. We also stratified these statistics by pesticide usage (used recently, used in the past but not recently, and never used) and by farming activity subgroup (active farmer, non-active farmer, non-farmer). Non-active farmers were former farmers who answered “no” to the question “In the last 12 months, have you personally performed farm work or farming activities?” We examined the prevalence of recent and lifetime agricultural use and recent home/garden use of each pesticide. These statistics were also stratified based on when the pesticide was used: used recently, used in the past but not recently, and never used. We conducted all descriptive statistics in STATA version 17 (StataCorp LLC; College Station, TX).

Data imputation

The laboratory reported a quantitative pesticide concentration wherever possible, which included concentrations below their technical limit of quantification. Thus, we derived an empirical limit of detection (LOD) based on the 5th percentile of the detected measurements (reported in Supplemental Table S2). For measurements without a laboratory-reported concentration (non-detects), we imputed the concentration of analytes with a detection rate ≥25 % using multiple imputation via chained equations. Following the approach of Lubin et al. (2004), we modeled concentrations under a log-normal model, where detected and undetected concentrations are assumed to come from the same distribution. We assessed appropriateness of this distribution using histograms of the detected measurements. We observed that detection rates and median concentrations among detected values varied depending on whether the pesticide was recently used, used in the past but not recently, or never used. Thus, we included this variable as a predictor in the imputation procedure to improve accuracy of the imputation (Austin et al., 2021). We accounted for repeated samples via a cluster-robust variance estimator in the imputation model. We set the 5th percentile of the detected measurements as the upper limit for imputation (Supplemental Table S2). We repeated the imputation ten times, resulting in ten data sets. The imputation model was implemented using the mice.impute.leftcenslognorm function in the R package qgcomp (Keil et al., 2023). For p,p-DDT, p,p-DDE, and p,p-DDD, we imputed their non-detects simultaneously, which leveraged their high correlation in detected concentrations. For permethrin, we replaced the single non-detectable measurement with a value of one-half of the 5th percentile of detected permethrin concentration.

Temporal variability

We estimated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to examine the temporal variability of the repeated dust measurements for analytes with a detection rate ≥25 %. The ICCs were calculated as the ratio of between-home variance (σBW2) to the sum of between-home variance σBW2 and within-home variance (σWI2). The two variance components were obtained from mixed-effects linear regression models, which included natural-log transformed dust concentrations as the dependent variable and participant ID as a random effect. We fit each model ten times and combined the results using Rubin’s rules with the testEstimates function in the R package mitml (Barnard and Miscellanea, 1999; Rubin, 1987). These calculations were conducted only for pesticide analytes with detection rates ≥40 %. To provide estimates for pesticides with lower detection rates and examine how robust the temporal variability described by ICC, we also calculated Spearman correlation coefficients (ρs) between the first and last visits for all analytes with detection rates ≥25 % for the 54 pairs (n = 108) of repeated dust samples. In the Spearman correlation analyses, non-detects were replaced with the value 0.

Quantile regression models

Due to the large variability in detection rates, relationships between pesticide dust concentrations and potential predictors of exposure were examined for analytes with a detection rate ≥25 % using mixed-effects quantile regression models using the R package lqmm (Geraci and Bottai, 2014). Quantile regression models do not make assumptions about the distribution of the response variable, and are less sensitive to skewness and potential outliers (Chen et al., 2021). In a simulation study with similar exposure distributions to the measurements in this study, quantile regression performed better than mean regression models, providing consistent parameter estimates with lower mean squared errors (Chen et al., 2021). Quantile regression models can estimate any user-specified percentile. In this study, to reduce the impact of imputed values on the model results, we estimated a specific percentile of a pesticide (50th, 75th, or 90th percentile) that was higher than the proportion of samples that required imputation (100 – detection rate). For example, a pesticide with a detection rate of 40 % required imputation for 60 % of the measurements; as a result, we estimated the 75th percentile. Thus, we estimated the 50th percentile for pesticides with a ≥65 % detection rate, the 75th percentile for those with a 40 % – 64 % detection rate, and the 90th percentile for those with a 25 % – 39 % detection rate.

In all quantile regression models, the dependent variable was the natural log-transformed dust concentrations, the above-mentioned questionnaire variables were incorporated as fixed effects and participant ID was included as a random effect. We exponentiated the regression coefficients (β) of the fixed-effects to estimate relative differences in dust concentrations, RDx, where x denotes the specified percentile, between categories defined by the questionnaire variables for the specified percentile (e.g., exp(β) = RD50 = 2.0 equates to a doubling of the median dust concentrations between two categories of a predictor). We only reported models for the cis isomers of permethrin and chlordane because their models were essentially identical to models for the trans isomers due to highly correlated isomer concentrations.

Our modeling process evaluated the available fixed effects in three stages due to the large number of characteristics and to avoid overfitting because of relatively small sample size: 1) agricultural and home/garden pesticide use metrics; 2) household characteristics; and 3) agricultural use metrics and household characteristics together.

In the first set of models, we analyzed each agricultural use variable in separate models, with each model also including recent home/garden use (yes/no). The agricultural use variables examined were recent agricultural use (yes/no), number of days of recent use, intensity-weighted days of use, lifetime agricultural use (yes/no), number of days of lifetime use, and intensity-weighted days of use. The quantitative metrics were treated as categorical variables, with values greater than zero categorized into tertiles and compared to the “no use” category, which included both farmers and non-farmers with no agricultural use of that pesticide. We assessed trends across tertiles of number of days and intensity-weighted days of agricultural use using within-category median values as a continuous linear variable, where “no use” category was coded as 0. We categorized the number of days since the last recent agricultural use as no use, ≥5 times the number of days of its estimated soil half-life, or <5 times soil half-life. Trends for days since last agricultural use were assessed by treating the categories as an ordinal variable (0, 1, 2). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a cut-off of 3 times the soil half-life. We fit each model ten times, and pooled the results using Rubin’s rules (1987). We also examined the association between pesticide dust concentrations and self-reported distance of home to field [<100 vs. ≥100 yards (91 m)] among farmers who had not moved since enrollment. We then further examined whether the associations with the agricultural metrics with the lowest p-trend were modified by either distance of home to field (<100 vs. ≥100 yards) or timing of last agricultural use (<5 vs. ≥5 times soil half-life). When there was low prevalence of recent home/garden use (n <5), we excluded dust samples from participants with recent home/garden use to avoid model instability and only evaluated the agricultural use variable.

In the second set of models, we assessed associations with household characteristics individually for each pesticide. We tested trends by modeling within-category median values as continuous variables for most characteristics; the exceptions were carpet age, year house was built, and whether shoes (any, and work shoes) were removed before entering the house, which were treated as ordinal variables (0, 1, 2, etc. …).

In the third set of models, we evaluated the best fitting agricultural use metric and the best fitting household characteristic [based on statistical significance and the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC)], along with home/garden use, together in a multi-variable model. For dacthal, for which we had no agricultural use information, we included the two household characteristics with the lowest AIC into a multivariable model.

Results

Descriptive statistics and temporal variability

The detection rates were highly variable, with the lowest rate observed for EPTC at 0.7 % and the highest rate observed for permethrin at 99.7 % (Table 1). Of the 32 analytes, six had detection rates ≥90 %, eight had detection rates between 65 % and 89 %, five had detection rates between 40 % and 64 %, one (atrazine) had a detection rate of 29 %, and twelve had detection rates <25 %.

Recent and lifetime agricultural use was reported for 13 and 21 pesticides, respectively (Table 1). The pesticides with the most frequent agricultural use were atrazine (n = 77 of 295 samples, 26 % with recent use; n = 241, 82 % with lifetime use), malathion (n = 50, 17 % with recent use; n = 224, 76 % with lifetime use), and permethrin (n = 105, 36 % with recent use; n = 169, 57 % with lifetime use). Chlorpyrifos had frequent lifetime use (n = 169, 57 %) but low recent use (n = 10, 3 %). Lifetime agricultural use information was unavailable for 5 pesticides because they were not asked about at enrollment. Recent home/garden use was reported for 9 pesticides; the most frequently used were carbaryl (n = 52, 18 %), malathion (n = 14, 5 %), and cyfluthrin (n = 11, 4 %).

Permethrin had the highest median detected concentrations among all pesticide analytes (Table 1). Detection rates and median detected concentrations varied between strata based on pesticide use (Supplemental Table S2) and farming activity level (Supplemental Table S3). Although house dust samples from participants with recent or lifetime use of specific pesticides generally had higher detection rates and median detected concentrations of those pesticides than samples from participants who never used them, there were several exceptions to these patterns. For example, samples from participants who never used diazinon, permethrin, or chlorothalonil had similar or higher median detected concentrations of these pesticides than those with recent use (Supplemental Table S2).

The distribution of household characteristics are shown in Supplemental Table S4. These distributions were generally similar among active farmers, non-active farmers, and non-farmers, with two exceptions. Active farmers had a higher proportion of owning cats and dogs (≥60 %) compared to non-active farmers and non-farmers (≤39 % and ≤33 %, respectively). Dust samples from non-farmers were collected more frequently in summer/fall (76 %) than active farmers (40 %) or non-active farmers (43 %). The variance components, ICCs, and Spearman correlations are also shown in Table 1. The ICCs ranged from 0.57 for acetochlor and diazinon to 0.90 for permethrin, with a median of 0.79. Spearman correlations between the first and last visits were >0.5 for all analytes and >0.80 for 11 of the 18 analytes. Overall, the Spearman correlations were similar to the ICCs, except for acetochlor and diazinon, which both had higher Spearman correlations.

Quantile regression models for currently used pesticides

Associations with recent and lifetime agricultural and home/garden pesticide use for the nine pesticides in current use in our study are shown in Table 2. We estimated the RD50 for chlorpyrifos, malathion, metolachlor, and permethrin, the RD75 for acetochlor, carbaryl, cyfluthrin, and diazinon, and the RD90 for atrazine.

Recent agricultural use (yes/no) was statistically significantly associated with higher household dust concentrations for malathion, metolachlor, acetochlor, carbaryl, cyfluthrin, diazinon, and atrazine, but not chlorpyrifos or permethrin (Table 2). Significant trends of increasing concentrations with both the number of days and intensity-weighted days metrics were observed for malathion, metolachlor, acetochlor, and atrazine. In addition, cyfluthrin had significantly increasing trends with intensity-weighted days, but not number of days. Trends based on timing of last use were also observed, with higher concentrations of malathion, acetochlor, and atrazine observed when the respective pesticide was used more recently (number of days since used <5 times its soil half-life). For example, compared to homes with no recent use, the median malathion level was over 9 times higher in homes with days since last use ≥5 times the soil half-life (RD50 = 9.3; 95 % confidence interval (CI): 3.4, 25), and 16 times higher in homes with days since last use <5 times the soil half-life (RD50 = 16; 95 % CI: 1.5, 180; p-trend = 0.003). The results were similar using 3 times soil half-life as the cut-point (data not shown).

Lifetime agricultural use (ever/never use) was statistically significantly associated with higher concentrations of malathion, metolachlor, carbaryl, and atrazine, with significantly increasing concentrations observed for both the number of days and intensity-weighted days metrics (Table 2). No associations with lifetime agricultural use metrics were observed for chlorpyrifos or permethrin. However, the highest tertile of both the number of days and intensity-weighted days for chlorpyrifos were suggestively higher than never use. Chlorothalonil, which is not included in Table 2 because of its limited prevalence of recent and lifetime use, was not associated with ever lifetime use (RD50 = 0.64, 95 % CI: 0.28,1.5; 12 samples with lifetime use).

Recent home/garden use was associated with statistically significantly higher concentrations of malathion (RD50 = 11; 95 % CI: 2.0, 61), carbaryl (RD75 = 4.5; 95 % CI: 1.9, 11), and cyfluthrin (RD75 = 7.6; 95 % CI: 1.1, 54) compared to no use (Table 2). No associations with home/garden use were observed for permethrin, diazinon, or atrazine.

Distance to field was not an independent predictor of concentrations of any pesticides listed in Table 2, or for chlorothalonil and dacthal (data not shown). Only malathion, metolachlor, carbaryl, and atrazine had sufficient numbers to examine whether the associations with lifetime agricultural days of use were modified by either distance of home to field (Fig. 1). For malathion and metolachlor, RD50′s were higher when living <100 vs. ≥100 yards from fields for both tertiles 1 and 2 (but not tertile 3) of days of lifetime agricultural use. For carbaryl, RD75′s were higher when living <100 vs. ≥100 yards from fields for tertiles 1 and 3 (but not tertile 2) of days of lifetime agricultural use. For atrazine, RD90′s were higher when living <100 vs. ≥100 yards from fields for all tertiles of days of lifetime agricultural use. We did not test whether the differences within a tertile were statistically significant because of limited statistical power.

We also examined whether the timing of agricultural use influenced the above-mentioned associations with intensity-weighted days of recent agricultural use of malathion and atrazine, as well as days of recent agricultural use of acetochlor (Fig. 2). For malathion, in tertile 3 of intensity-weighted days of recent agricultural use, we found that the RD50 was 25 (95 % CI: 6.3, 95) if the number of days since malathion was last used was ≥5 times the soil half-life and 30 (95 % CI: 2.9, 320) if number of days since its last use was <5 times the soil half-life compared to no use. We observed similar patterns for acetochlor in tertile 2 of days of recent agricultural use and for atrazine in tertile 1 (but not tertile 2) of intensity-weighted days of recent agricultural use.

The influence of household characteristics on household pesticide dust concentrations for currently used pesticides are shown for individual characteristic analysis in Supplemental Tables S5 and S6 and in multi-variable analyses in Supplemental Table S7. In multi-variable models, chlorothalonil, acetochlor, and dacthal concentrations were significantly lower in homes located in North Carolina than Iowa, whereas chlorpyrifos and permethrin concentrations were significantly higher in North Carolina. Diazinon concentrations were significantly higher in dust samples collected in the winter than in the fall. For dacthal, a significant trend with the year the house was built was observed (p-trend < 0.001), with concentrations highest for homes built before 1940 and lowest in homes built after 2009, in a model also adjusted for state. For permethrin, including state resulted in a suggestive association with recent home/garden use (RD50 = 3.7, 95 % CI: 0.95, 14; p-value = 0.06) that was not previously observed (Table 2). The selected household characteristic was not associated with concentrations of malathion, metolachlor, carbaryl, cyfluthrin, or atrazine, and these characteristics had negligible impact on the model parameters for both agricultural and home/garden pesticide use.

Quantile regression models for banned pesticides

Evaluations of banned pesticides and their degradation by-products with lifetime use metrics are shown in Table 3. Ever agricultural use was associated with significantly elevated chlordane concentrations (RD50 = 5.2; 95 % CI: 2.4, 11) and non-significantly elevated dieldrin concentrations (RD50 = 1.8; 95 % CI: 0.90, 3.7), but not with lindane, p, p-DDD, p,p-DDT, p,p-DDE, or heptachlor concentrations. For chlordane, we observed a significant, but not monotonic, trend for the intensity-weighted days of lifetime use metric (p-trend = 0.01). No statistically significant trends were observed for the other banned pesticides. None of these analytes were associated with the distance of home to field and there were insufficient numbers for stratification to test whether the associations with quantitative lifetime agricultural use were influenced by distance of home to field.

The influence of household characteristics on household dust concentrations for these banned pesticides in individual characteristic analysis is shown in Supplemental Table S8 and in multi-variable models in Supplemental Table S9. In multi-variable models, chlordane and heptachlor concentrations were significantly higher in homes located in North Carolina compared to Iowa. For dieldrin, lindane, p,p-DDD, p,p-DDE, and p,p-DDT, concentrations decreased with decreasing house age (p-trends ≤ 0.001), with homes built before 1940 usually having the highest concentrations. These characteristics had generally negligible impact on the model parameters for the agricultural pesticide use metrics. For heptachlor, including state resulted in significantly higher concentrations in homes with lifetime agricultural use vs. never use (RD75 = 2.7, 95 % CI: 1.0, 7.3) that was not previously observed (Table 3).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that increasing pesticide concentrations in house dust were associated with quantitative measures of recent and lifetime agricultural use and with a dichotomous measure for recent home/garden use for several currently used pesticides. We also observed some evidence that these associations were stronger when the pesticide was used more recently and when the home was closer to pesticide-treated fields (e.g., malathion). We found limited evidence that prior use of banned pesticides was associated with current indoor pesticide concentrations, except for chlordane and heptachlor (after adjusting for state of residence); however, we observed some differences in their concentrations based on state and house age. Over a 3–6 month time period, pesticide concentrations in repeated house dust samples exhibited moderate to very high correlation.

The detection rates of pesticides varied by active ingredient. The high detection rates (>95 %) for permethrin (LOD = 29 ng/g) and chlorpyrifos (LOD = 2.7 ng/g) are consistent with previous studies (detection rate ≥96 %, LODs ≤5 ng/g) (Deziel et al., 2015a, 2013; Gunier et al., 2016; 2011; Harnly et al., 2009; Madrigal et al., 2021; Morgan et al., 2014; Quirós-Alcalá et al., 2011). The low detection rates of alachlor (<7%,LOD = 3 ng/g) due to its lack of recent use in our study and its short half-lives are also consistent with a 7 % detection rate (LOD = 1.5 ng/g) in a previous study conducted in Iowa (Curwin et al., 2005). For some pesticides, the detection rates in our study differed from previous reports, which may be attributed to the focus of this study on a predominantly farming population (vs. general population), geographic differences in agricultural pesticide use patterns, meteorological differences in study areas, and sample selection criteria. For example, atrazine had a detection rate of 28.5 % (LOD = 54 ng/g) in this predominantly farming population and only an 8 % detection rate (LOD = 59 ng/g) in a population-based case-control study of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (Ward et al., 2006).

Our study also found similar or higher detection rates of banned pesticides with long half-lives, such as lindane, p,p-DDT, and dieldrin (range: 89 % to 98 %, LODs ≤4 ng/g), than in previous studies (range: 14 % to 81 %, LODs ≤2 ng/g) (Aslam et al., 2021; Morgan et al., 2014; Gunier et al., 2016). Chlordane’s >80 % detection rate (LOD = 0.83 ng/g) is lower than two studies conducted in California with LOD = 2 ng/g (Deziel et al., 2013) and 5 ng/g (Gunier et al., 2016), and one study where about 96 % of the sampled homes and 100 % of the sampled daycares reported applying insecticides within a year in North Carolina (LOD = 2 ng/g) (Morgan et al., 2014). Aldrin’s low detection rate was unsurprising since it is readily degraded to dieldrin. Lifetime use of the analyzed banned pesticides was moderately prevalent in our study population, but this metric has not been evaluated in other studies.

Quantitative metrics of recent agricultural use were significantly associated with increasing concentrations of five pesticides in current use. This supports that farmers are likely to bring pesticide residues into the home (Deziel et al., 2015b; Khan et al., 2024), and suggests farmers likely bring more pesticide residues if they applied more pesticides. Associations were sometimes slightly stronger with recent intensity-weighted days, which suggest that application method and PPE use, which were incorporated into the intensity weights, may influence the pesticide levels that enter the home. In our previous study of 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), we also observed similar pattern, where monotonic trends (p-trends = 0.06–0.07) were observed with number of days of use and intensity-weighted days of use (Xie et al., 2023). Our findings are consistent with previous studies that observed trends with metrics based on crop acreage sprayed or mass of pesticide applied. For example, in a study conducted in Iowa, compared to farm homes that sprayed ≤165 acres of crops, atrazine dust concentrations were 1.1 times higher when farm homes sprayed 166–700 acres and 6.1 times higher when farm homes sprayed >700 acres (p-value = 0.08) (Golla et al., 2012).

Quantitative measures of lifetime agricultural use were also significantly associated with increasing concentrations of five pesticides in current use. The trends were generally similar across the quantitative recent and lifetime agricultural use metrics, which were only low to moderately correlated (Supplemental Table S10). The magnitude of the RD in the highest tertile was usually higher with the recent use metrics than with the lifetime metrics. This provides some support for a decay effect based on timing of use, which may be lower in the protected environment of the homes than outdoors in soil (Butte and Heinzow, 2002). For banned pesticides, there were no clear trends with the quantitative metrics, although we observed some differences based on state and house age. This is unsurprising as some of these pesticides had not been used in over four decades. It also suggests that other factors not captured in our study, such as nearby pesticide soil contamination, should be investigated as potential contributing factors.

Pesticide concentrations in homes with the highest tertile of agricultural use were often more than five times higher than the comparison group of participants with no recent home/garden and no agricultural use (recent or lifetime, depending on metric). Comparisons of these magnitudes to other studies must be made cautiously because we estimated percentiles compared to the more typically reported geometric means (GMs). However, our findings were generally consistent with other studies that found higher concentrations of these pesticides in homes with agricultural use. For example, for atrazine, the RD90 of 32 for the highest tertile of intensity-weighted recent use (vs. no recent use) supports previous findings that found a >70 times higher GM observed in Iowa farm homes with atrazine used within 7 days (170 ng/g) vs. non-farm homes (2.3 ng/g) (Curwin et al., 2005). For malathion, the RD50 of 24 times for our highest tertile of recent use is consistent with the 10 times higher GM observed between farm homes and non-farm homes during the thinning season (Smith et al., 2017). For metolachlor, the RD50 of 12 times for the highest tertile of recent use is consistent with the 54 times higher GM observed in farm homes with metolachlor use within 7 days (310 ng/g) vs. non-farm homes (5.7 ng/g) (Curwin et al., 2005).

Distance to field appeared to modify the influence of the agricultural use metrics for several pesticides in our study, but it was not an independent predictor for any pesticide. This may be because our participant may have been the most likely person to apply pesticide treatments to the nearby fields. The joint effect of distance to field and agricultural use observed here supports infiltration from drift as an additional potential source for indoor pesticide contamination (Deziel et al., 2015b; Figueiredo et al., 2022; Madrigal et al., 2023; Madrigal et al., 2021). In a meta-analysis of published data, a non-linear decrease in pesticide concentrations in dust from agricultural homes with increasing distance from treated fields was reported (Deziel et al., 2017). A recent study supported these findings, indicating homes located within 25 m (27.3 yards) of an agricultural field or orchard had 3.57 (95 %CI: 1.27, 9.99) times higher chance of having higher phosmet concentrations (highest tertile vs. lowest tertile), after adjusting for the number of agricultural persons in home (Butler-Dawson et al., 2018). However, there is potential correlation between distance of home to field and agricultural pesticide use by a member of the household since farm families are likely live closer to the fields than non-farm families (Curl et al., 2023; McCauley et al., 2001). Even though most studies adjusted for agricultural pesticide use or number of farmers living in the home (Gunier et al., 2011; Madrigal et al., 2023; Madrigal et al., 2021), it is still challenging to exclude all the potential confounding factors and distinguish the impact of distance of home to field accurately (Dereumeaux et al., 2020; Teysseire et al., 2021).

The timing of when a pesticide was last used also influenced concentrations within homes. The concentrations of malathion and acetochlor were higher when the pesticide was used more recently, which is expected given their short soil half-life (17 days for malathion and 20 days for acetochlor) (EPA, 2006; Gervais et al., 2009). We observed inconsistent effects for atrazine, which is likely due to the inverse correlation between frequency and timing of use, as well as the limited statistical power in certain strata of our analysis. Previous studies also found a decay effect for atrazine and metolachlor. Compared to non-farm homes, concentrations were 39 and 41 times higher among Iowa farm homes that sprayed atrazine within seven days, and 11 and 2 times higher among farm homes that did not spray atrazine within seven days, respectively (Curwin et al., 2005). Similarly, a study of commercial pesticide applicators in Iowa found an inverse correlation between atrazine dust concentrations and the number of days since atrazine was last used (p-value = 0.09) during the atrazine application season (Lozier et al., 2012).

Recent home/garden pesticide use also increased household pesticide dust concentrations for several pesticides. This association was based on dichotomous active ingredient-specific metrics rather than relying on the types of pest or weed being treated, which was commonly used in previous studies (Deziel et al., 2017; Madrigal et al., 2023; Ward et al., 2023). One of the pesticides showing a significant association was cyfluthrin, a pyrethroid insecticide commonly applied to control a range of pests, including ants, termites, and mosquitoes (Butler et al., 2007; Gajendiran and Abraham, 2018). Our findings are consistent with studies in the California Childhood Leukemia Study, which found that cyfluthrin dust concentrations were significantly associated with lawn/garden insect treatment by a family member, as well as with professional outdoor and indoor treatments (Deziel et al., 2015a, 2013) and home/garden use of flea or tick products (Madrigal et al., 2023). Concentrations of carbaryl, another insecticide used for ants, fleas/ticks, and other outdoor pest control (Jampawal et al., 2022), were significantly associated with home/garden use. This is consistent with the previously observed suggestive associations with carpenter ant/termite treatment, flea/tick, indoor treatment, and professional indoor treatment (Colt et al., 2004; Deziel et al., 2015a; Madrigal et al., 2023). The magnitude of the RD for recent home/garden pesticide use was generally lower or similar to the RD for the highest tertile of recent agricultural use (e.g., malathion, cyfluthrin, and atrazine). For example, for the weed control herbicide atrazine, a RD90 of 32 was observed for the highest tertile of recent atrazine agricultural use, whereas a RD90 of 3.4 was observed for recent home/garden use. The similarity and differences with agricultural use may relate to where and how much pesticides were used in the home and garden (e.g., indoors vs. outdoors; on clothing, skin, pets, or surface).

Notably, permethrin dust concentrations were not significantly associated with any agricultural use metric, with distance to the field, or with home/garden use, even though it had the highest median concentrations among samples with detectable concentrations among all pesticide analytes studied. The high median concentrations among detectable samples is consistent with previous studies (Deziel et al., 2015a; Harnly et al., 2009; Madrigal et al., 2023), and the null associations with agricultural use were also consistent with a combined analyses from the California Childhood Leukemia Study and the Fresno Agricultural Pesticide Study, where both having a pesticide-exposed worker in the household and proximity-based agricultural pesticide use density metrics using the California Pesticide Use Reporting database were not predictive of permethrin concentrations (Deziel et al., 2015a; Madrigal et al., 2023). These findings are also consistent with a prospective cohort study conducted in the Salinas Valley of California, which found no association between agricultural applications of permethrin within 3 miles of the home and permethrin dust concentrations (Harnly et al., 2009). In the multi-variable model, we found some evidence that permethrin concentrations were higher in the homes in North Carolina compared to Iowa, and suggestively higher in homes with recent home/garden use. Associations may have been masked by other sources of permethrin that were not captured by the questionnaire. This includes the use of permethrin-treated clothing, which can be used as a prevention measure against ticks and mosquitoes. Additionally, carpets, particularly wool carpets, could be treated with permethrin during the manufacturing process (Berger-Preiß et al., 2002) and could serve asa possible source of permethrin (Schenk et al., 1997). We did not see clear patterns with age of carpet in this study and we did not have information on carpet material.

State and house age were associated with several pesticides in multivariable models that adjusted for agricultural and home/garden pesticide use. Homes in North Carolina had significantly higher concentrations than Iowa for chlorpyrifos, permethrin, chlordane, and heptachlor, but significantly lower concentrations for chlorothalonil, dacthal, and acetochlor. This is unsurprising as the two states differ in primary crop types and pesticide usage patterns. In our study, a significantly decreasing trend in concentrations were observed with building age for dacthal, dieldrin, lindane, p,p-DDD, p,p-DDE, and p,p-DDT, with the highest concentrations usually observed in homes built before 1940. This pattern with building age for banned pesticides was also observed in the California Childhood Leukemia Study (Gunier et al., 2016; Ward et al., 2006; Whitehead et al., 2014), in the non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma Study in Detroit, Iowa, Los Angeles, and Seattle (Colt et al., 2004), as well as a survey of attic dust concentrations in southern Nevada and Utah (Cizdziel and Hodge, 2000).

There was moderate to very high correlation in the dust concentrations in this study, indicating that a single pesticide measurement may reasonably represent the pesticide concentrations over 3–6 months for many but not all pesticides. Higher correlation was generally found for banned pesticides, due to their known persistence in the environment and lack of current use. Some variability was observed for pesticides in current use, which may reflect both recent use patterns and their varying half-lives indoors. ICC calculations based on models that accounted for recent use resulted in nearly identical ICCs (data not shown). The observed very high correlations for chlordane (ICC = 0.85) and permethrin (ICC = 0.90) is consistent with previous studies reporting ICC values of 0.95 and ≥ 0.75, respectively (Deziel et al., 2013; Starr et al., 2018). Similarly, we found high correlations for cyfluthrin (ICC = 0.70), which is comparable with the stability calculated in the Fresno Agricultural Pesticide Study with a median time frame of five months between visits (ICC = 0.65) (Deziel et al., 2013). However, cyfluthrin’s ICC here was lower than that calculated from repeated hard surface wipe samples collected in a pilot study in North Carolina on pyrethroid exposure conducted over a shorter time frame (ICC = 0.96), which included samples from the 4th day of weeks 1, 2, and 6 (Starr et al., 2018). We found higher correlations for carbaryl, dacthal, and chlorpyrifos (ICC ranged from 0.66 to 0.85) than those reported in the Fresno Agricultural Pesticide Study (ICC ranged from 0.45 to 0.69). The high correlation for chlorpyrifos (ICC = 0.79) was consistent with the findings of two studies with short duration between visits (ICC(~2 months) = 0.9 (Pang et al., 2002) and ρs(5–8 days) = 0.83 (Quirós-Alcalá et al., 2011)). We also observed moderate correlation for atrazine (ρs = 0.53), which aligned with a study conducted in Iowa with a one-month time difference between visits (ICC = 0.55) (Curwin et al., 2005). We observed higher correlation for metolachlor and chlorpyrifos (ICCs ≥0.79) compared to the findings reported in the Iowa study (ICC = 0.19 and 0.66, respectively); both studies had high detection rates but low prevalence of use for these pesticides. These differences may also be attributed to geographic differences such as weather conditions, which may directly impact decay rates or indirectly impact decay rates based on differences in housing characteristics (e.g., use of carpets, air conditioning). The temporal variability evaluated in this study was based on the priorities of other exposure sub-studies (permethrin, bioaerosol) within this population; however, it includes samples collected within- and between-seasons in farmers located in Iowa conducting a variety of pesticide and animal-related agricultural activities. Evaluations of this variability over longer time periods remains understudied, with only two studies evaluating time periods beyond one year (Butler-Dawson et al., 2018; Deziel et al., 2013).

Strengths and limitations

This study had many strengths. First, compared to previous studies, our sample size was relatively large and comprised both active and non-active farmers, as well as non-farmers, with detailed information on pesticide use and household characteristics. Second, we developed quantitative agricultural recent use metrics based on detailed questionnaire responses, including days of use and intensity-weighted days. We also had information on timing of agricultural use and proximity to fields, which allowed us to examine whether these factors modified associations with the quantitative metrics. Third, these metrics were active ingredient-specific, determined based on the provided product trade names. This approach could be more effective than previous studies that only relied on tasks, such as lawn/garden treatment. Fourth, we developed lifetime agricultural use metrics, leveraging information collected during BEEA and the three prior phases of AHS, allowing us to evaluate the impact of lifetime agricultural use for pesticides that are in current use and banned, which are relatively understudied. Lastly, because of the large proportion of non-detects for some pesticides, we developed our models using mixed-effects quantile regression to reduce the assumptions regarding the distribution of pesticide dust concentrations. Our conclusions were consistent when we deployed the more commonly used mixed-effect linear regression models (data not shown).

This study also had a few limitations. First, the low prevalence of agricultural use for certain pesticides limited our ability to assess quantitative agricultural pesticide use metrics or evaluate the interaction of metrics for some pesticides. Chlorpyrifos, for example, had recent agricultural use for only 10 of 295 dust samples, and thus only recent agricultural use (yes/no) could be evaluated. Similarly, few pesticides had sufficient numbers to evaluate whether days since last use or distance to field may modify associations observed with quantitative agricultural use metrics. We also note that information on the frequency of lifetime use for some pesticides was missing for 40 % of BEEA farmers that had completed the AHS enrollment questionnaire but not the take-home questionnaire, which queried the frequency of use for many of the pesticides. Second, quantitative measurements of recent home/garden use, such as amount or frequency or timing of last use, were not collected, which limited our evaluations of this source to a dichotomous measure (yes/no); however, this metric allowed us to show the independent contribution of agricultural use to indoor pesticide concentrations. Third, our analyses based on timing of use was categorized based on reported soil half-life. Pesticides may persist longer in the indoor environment than in soil due to reduced sunlight (Weschler and Nazaroff, 2008). Fourth, the distance from home to field was self-reported and not pesticide-specific. We did not incorporate any geographic information system (GIS)-based measures (e.g., crop density near homes) or factors affecting pesticide transportation (e.g., wind direction), which may be stronger predictors than self-reported distance of home to treated fields (Gunier et al., 2011; Harnly et al., 2009; Ward et al., 2006). Fifth, the variance components obtained using mixed-effects models with which to evaluate temporal variability are dependent on an assumption that the non-detects followed a log-normal distribution assumption, which could introduce bias, particularly for pesticides with a lower detection rate. However, our estimated ICCs were generally consistent with the Spearman correlation between the first and last visits, as well as previously reported ICCs based on similar assumptions for non-detects. Sixth, pesticide concentrations could be underestimated in the dust lab analysis, as exhibited by sometimes poor recovery rates, which may occur due to the complex and heterogeneous nature of dust samples. Lastly, our dust samples were collected from homes in a predominantly non-Hispanic White farming population in Iowa and North Carolina. Future studies of pesticide dusts concentrations would benefit from involving farmers of various racial and ethnic backgrounds, different geographical areas, and farming practices.

Our study focused on determinants of indoor pesticide concentrations; however, it is unclear the extent to which pesticide concentrations in house dust may impact health. A few studies have linked elevated indoor dust concentrations of pesticides to various health outcomes, but null associations have been reported as well. For example, farmworkers exposed to detectable concentrations of organophosphates in house dust reported a higher likelihood of experiencing symptoms such as muscle and joint pain (Strong et al., 2004). In the case of children, an increased risk of acute lymphoblastic leukemia was associated with pesticide dust concentrations of dacthal (Metayer et al., 2013), but not permethrin, carbaryl, chlorpyrifos (Madrigal et al., 2021) or glyphosate (Ward et al., 2023). Notably, house dust residues have the potential to contribute to overall exposure from both ingestion and inhalation sources (Obendorf et al., 2006) and the ingestion of carpet dust has been identified as a significant indoor health hazard for children (Lewis et al., 1994). Future studies are needed to investigate whether indoor dust concentrations primarily represent a proxy for pesticide exposure from a variety of sources or if it is an independent source of pesticide exposure to household members, especially for household members who are only indirectly exposed to pesticides (Deziel et al., 2015b).

Conclusion

In a predominantly farming population, we found increasing pesticide concentrations in the dust of homes with an occupant that had higher reported recent and lifetime agricultural use of currently used pesticides. We also observed some evidence that the associations with quantitative agricultural pesticide use metrics may be influenced by factors such as self-reported distance of home to the field and timing of agricultural pesticide use, as well as state of residence and house age. These findings help identify characteristics to consider in characterizing exposure for epidemiologic studies. Future studies characterizing pesticide concentrations in homes should consider incorporating comprehensive and quantitative information on the frequency and timing of agricultural and home/garden pesticide use by household occupants, as well as pesticide-specific proximity to treated fields using objective GIS-based measures. Future studies are also needed to investigate whether the indoor concentrations of pesticides represent a proxy that is correlated with pesticide exposure through other routes from personally using pesticides or living near where these activities occur, or whether it may contribute independently to exposure. Moreover, strategies to reduce indoor pesticide contamination from agricultural pesticide use need to be identified and implemented.
