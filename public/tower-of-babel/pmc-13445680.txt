Migraine phases and treatment response: a post hoc analysis of participants with migraine with aura from the TEAM study
Authors: Michael A. L. Johnson, Gregory Panza, Deena E. Kuruvilla
Published: 2026

Background

Migraine is the most prevalent chronic neurological disease worldwide and the second leading cause of years lived with disability (YLD) globally [1]. Among women aged 15–49, it is the leading cause of YLD [2]. Beyond its burden, migraine is increasingly understood as a phased neurological event, not simply a headache. The clinical course of a migraine attack typically progresses through four recognizable phases: prodrome, aura (when present), headache, and postdrome. Each phase reflects distinct neurobiological processes and presents different opportunities for therapeutic intervention.

The prodromal phase, driven by hypothalamic and brainstem activation, may begin hours to days before headache onset and represents the earliest identifiable window for intervention, with evidence suggesting that early treatment can improve 24-h pain freedom rates and reduce long-term migraine progression [3, 4]. The aura phase, experienced by approximately one-third of migraine patients, is characterized by transient, fully reversible visual, sensory, or other central nervous system symptoms developing over 5–60 min [5–7], and is the clinical manifestation of cortical spreading depolarization (CSD), which activates trigeminovascular nociceptive pathways linking aura to subsequent headache pain [8–13].

The headache phase involves activation of the trigeminovascular system, meningeal nociception, and central sensitization, producing the characteristic moderate-to-severe throbbing pain accompanied by photophobia, phonophobia, and nausea. Most acute treatments, including both pharmacological and neuromodulation therapies, are directed at this phase. The postdromal phase follows headache resolution and, though often underrecognized, can substantially affect patient functioning through fatigue, cognitive difficulty, and residual sensory sensitivity.

Migraine with aura (MA) and migraine without aura (MO) are classified as distinct subtypes by the International Headache Society [5, 6]. Beyond their clinical differences, these subtypes may differ in their response to acute therapies. Prior work has shown that triptans and other acute medications may be less effective when administered during the aura phase [14–18], raising important questions about whether neuromodulation shares this limitation.

External trigeminal nerve stimulation (eTNS), manufactured by CEFALY Technology, is a noninvasive neuromodulation device cleared by the FDA for both acute and preventive treatment of migraine [19]. It stimulates the supraorbital and supratrochlear branches of the trigeminal nerve—pathways central to migraine pathogenesis [20]. The Trial of eTNS for the Acute Treatment of Migraine (TEAM) study demonstrated that 2-h verum eTNS produced significantly higher rates of pain freedom, most bothersome symptom (MBS) resolution, pain relief, and sustained outcomes at 24 h compared with sham [21].

In this post hoc analysis, we examine whether the presence of aura modifies the treatment effect of eTNS. Given the phased nature of migraine and the mechanistic link between CSD and trigeminal activation, we hypothesized that eTNS would provide consistent benefit across MA and MO populations—and that the aura phase would not attenuate treatment response.

Methods

Ethics and trial design

The TEAM study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The TEAM study also adheres to CONSORT (Consolidated Standards or Reporting Trials) guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent.

Full methods have been reported previously [22]. Briefly, the TEAM study was a prospective, multicenter, randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial enrolling participants from April 2018 to January 2019 across 10 U.S. academic and research sites. Participants (N = 538) meeting ICHD-3 criteria for MA or MO with 2–8 migraine days per month were randomized 1:1 to verum (N = 259) or sham (N = 279) eTNS.

Intervention

Both verum and sham devices were identical in appearance and delivered transcutaneous neurostimulation via a forehead electrode covering the supratrochlear and supraorbital nerves. Stimulation consisted of rectangular biphasic symmetrical impulses (pulse width 250 µs) that increased linearly to 16 mA over 14 min and then remained constant for the 2-h treatment. The critical difference was frequency: verum delivered 100 Hz (high-frequency) and sham delivered 3 Hz (low-frequency). Participants self-applied the device at home during a qualifying migraine (moderate or severe headache with at least one associated symptom). Outcomes were recorded at 2 h and 24 h via paper diary.

Statistical approach

This post hoc, non-pre-specified analysis compared treatment outcomes between MA (N = 224) and MO (N = 314) participants. Data from all TEAM study participants were included and no data were missing. During the TEAM study, aura status was defined by the presence or absence of aura during the specific migraine attack treated in the TEAM study, as recorded by the participant in the treatment diary. Participants were therefore categorized according to whether aura was reported for the treated attack, rather than solely by historical ICHD-3 migraine subtype or diagnosis as an individual participant may experience both migraine with and without aura.

The central question was straightforward: does migraine aura change how well eTNS works? To answer this, we used logistic regression with an interaction term (aura subgroup × treatment group) for each endpoint. A significant interaction would indicate that eTNS works differently in MA versus MO patients. We also ran adjusted models accounting for age, sex, and baseline headache severity (moderate vs. severe), as these differed between subgroups. Baseline characteristics were compared using t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. All data were checked for normality of distribution and that assumptions for tests performed were met. All assumptions were met and verified with both visual inspections using histograms and Q-Q plots to assess practical deviations and statistically examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To ensure assumptions were met for logistic modeling, data were examined for extreme outliers, and independent variables were inspected for multicollinearity using variance inflation factor. Statistical significance was adjusted via Bonferroni correction and set at P < 0.025 (two-tailed) for the two primary endpoints, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for secondary, exploratory endpoints. Analysis was performed using SPSS Version 29.0.

As a post hoc analysis, the sample size was determined by the original TEAM study, which was powered (90%) to detect a 13.4% difference in the primary endpoint of pain freedom at 2 h. The current analysis was likely underpowered to detect modest interaction effects, and findings should be interpreted accordingly.

For the primary endpoints of 2-h pain freedom and MBS resolution, the treatment effect of verum over sham was consistent regardless of aura status (Fig. 1). Among secondary endpoints, the pattern was the same for 2-h pain relief, resolution of all associated symptoms, and sustained pain freedom and relief at 24 h (Fig. 2).

The only notable finding was that the interaction term for rescue medication use between 2 and 24 h approached significance in the adjusted model (P = 0.054, 95% confidence interval = 0.99–5.12), suggesting a possible trend toward increased use of rescue medication in patients with aura using eTNS (Table 2; Fig. 2b). Although not statistically significant, this finding warrants further investigation. All results remained non-significant after adjusting for age, sex, and baseline headache severity.

Discussion

This post hoc analysis of the TEAM study demonstrates that external trigeminal nerve stimulation provides consistent acute treatment benefit for migraine regardless of aura status. To our knowledge, this is the first stratified analysis of eTNS response by migraine aura subtype, and the findings have practical implications for how clinicians approach neuromodulation within the context of migraine’s phased biology.

Migraine phases as a framework for understanding treatment response

The phased nature of migraine reflects sequential activation of distinct neural circuits that create potentially distinct therapeutic windows (Fig. 3) — a framework that, while not directly testable in the present analysis, provides important context for interpreting these findings and motivating future research.Fig. 3Migraine phases and therapeutic windows. The prodromal phase, driven largely by hypothalamic and brainstem activation, offers the earliest opportunity for intervention. Recent evidence demonstrates that treating during the prodrome—for example, with ubrogepant—significantly improves 24-h pain freedom rates [3]. Moreover, optimizing early acute treatment may reduce the long-term risk of migraine chronification [4]. The TEAM study did not capture data on treatment timing relative to migraine onset, which limits our ability to determine whether patients who treated during prodrome or early aura fared differently from those who treated during established headache. This represents an important avenue for future investigation.

The aura phase is of particular mechanistic interest. Cortical spreading depolarization (CSD), the electrophysiological substrate of aura, triggers inflammatory and nociceptive activation of central trigeminovascular neurons [8–13]. This link between CSD and trigeminal pathway activation provides a plausible neurobiological rationale for why eTNS might be effective in MA patients: by modulating the trigeminal system, eTNS may counteract the downstream consequences of CSD (Fig. 4). In animal models, percutaneous trigeminal nerve stimulation has been shown to enhance cerebral blood flow and oxygenation [22–24], and in a subarachnoid hemorrhage model, trigeminal stimulation reduced CSD-induced cerebral ischemia [24]. These preclinical findings are hypothesis-generating and suggest that trigeminal stimulation may influence cortical physiology beyond its peripheral neuromodulatory effects, though this has not been demonstrated in humans or in the context of migraine specifically.Fig. 4Trigeminovascular pathway and proposed eTNS mechanisms

The headache phase is the period when most acute treatments are applied, and it is the phase most directly assessed in this analysis. Our finding that eTNS produces equivalent benefit in MA and MO populations during this phase is clinically reassuring and distinguishes eTNS from some pharmacological treatments. Prior studies have demonstrated that triptans may be less effective when administered during the aura phase [14–18], for reasons that remain incompletely understood. The mechanism of eTNS—direct neuromodulation of trigeminal afferents—may bypass these pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic limitations.

The postdromal phase remains the least studied period of migraine yet contributes substantially to disability, and whether eTNS treatment during the headache phase modifies postdromal symptoms is an important gap for future trials to address.

Demographic patterns

The demographic differences observed between subgroups align with existing epidemiological data. Although migraine is more common in women overall, MA was proportionally more prevalent in male participants in this cohort, consistent with prior reports [25]. The proportion of severe headache intensity was higher in the MA relative to the MO subgroup which is consistent with prior findings suggesting that aura – particularly visual aura – may be associated with more debilitating migraine attacks [26]. We adjusted for baseline headache pain difference in the interaction tests and found no notable change in results, indicating its minimal explanation of the variances for each outcome.

While adjusted analyses controlling for baseline headache severity did not demonstrate statistical significance, the baseline migraine severity in the MA subgroup may have important clinical relevance as migraine severity is a predictor of migraine response to acute therapy and may, at least partially, contribute to the trend of higher acute medication use observed at 2 and 24 h within the MA subgroup [27]. Nevertheless, these differences underline the importance of investigating sex-specific and severity-related factors in migraine treatment response [28, 29].

Clinical implications

For the practicing neurologist, these findings reinforce the utility of eTNS as a versatile non-pharmacological treatment option. The consistent treatment effect across MA and MO populations means that aura status need not be a factor in deciding whether to recommend eTNS. This is particularly relevant for patients in whom pharmacological options are limited or contraindicated.

The sequential phases of a migraine attack offer a practical framework for optimizing acute treatment decisions. Whether its onset could serve as a reliable cue to initiate treatment early — potentially before full central sensitization develops — remains an important question for future studies capturing phase-timing data.

While speculative in the absence of phase-timing data, the potential link between CSD and trigeminal pathway activation provides a plausible neurobiological rationale for why eTNS might be effective in MA patients: by modulating the trigeminal system, eTNS may counteract the downstream consequences of CSD. The direct neuromodulatory mechanism of eTNS—acting on trigeminal afferents rather than relying on systemic drug delivery—may allow it to remain effective across phases, making it a particularly valuable option for patients in whom pharmacological treatments are limited or contraindicated.

Whether aura could serve as a reliable cue to initiate eTNS early — potentially before full central sensitization develops — is an important question for future prospective studies with phase-timing data. Further study is needed to elucidate the role of eTNS within a stratified, phase-aware treatment approach alongside novel anti-migraine therapies such as gepants, CGRP-targeted therapies, and behavioral interventions. In addition, a future direction of study would be to examine the potential acute therapeutic effects of eTNS for both MA and MO in patients experiencing high-frequency episodic or chronic migraine.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. Most importantly, the absence of data on treatment timing relative to migraine phase onset prevents assessment of whether early intervention during prodrome or aura modifies outcomes. Placebo and expectancy effects are important considerations in noninvasive neuromodulation trials, as both active and sham stimulation produce perceptible sensations that support blinding but may also shape participants' expectations of benefit based on device sensation, treatment novelty, or prior beliefs about neuromodulation. The sham condition (3 Hz stimulation) may produce mild neuromodulatory effects, potentially narrowing the observed verum-sham difference—a recognized challenge in non-invasive neuromodulation trial design [30].

During the TEAM study participants selected only one qualifying migraine during the 2-month treatment window, which limits evaluation of intra-individual variability. Allowing treatment of multiple qualifying migraines in future acute studies would reduce the influence of any single atypical attack and help clarify whether regression toward the mean obscures therapeutic differences between verum and sham groups—particularly in patients who may have enrolled during an unusually severe or mild attack, and especially for patients who alternate between MA and MO attacks.

This post hoc analysis was not specifically powered to detect aura-by-treatment interactions, and the near-significant finding for rescue medication (P = 0.054) highlights the possibility that adequately powered prospective studies might reveal differences not detectable here. Finally, migraine treatment has advanced since the TEAM study enrollment period (2018–2019), and the role of eTNS should be evaluated alongside current and emerging therapies.

Conclusions

No evidence of an interaction was found for the therapeutic effect of eTNS for acute migraine across patients with and without aura. The aura phase does not appear to diminish treatment efficacy, distinguishing eTNS from certain pharmacological options whose effectiveness may be reduced when administered during aura. These findings support the use of eTNS as a reliable component of a stratified, patient-centered migraine treatment approach.

Future research should address three key questions: (1) whether initiating eTNS during specific migraine phases—prodrome or early aura—can optimize outcomes, (2) whether trigeminal nerve stimulation directly modulates cortical spreading depolarization, and (3) how eTNS integrates with current pharmacological therapies, including CGRP-targeted agents, in real-world practice. Answers to these questions will refine the role of neuromodulation within the evolving landscape of migraine treatment.
