Shortest Geodesic Loops, Sectional Curvature, and Injectivity Radius of the Stiefel Manifold
Jakob Stoye, Simon Mataigne, P.-A. Absil, Ralf Zimmermann

Abstract
We determine the length of the shortest nontrivial geodesic loops on the Stiefel manifold endowed with any member of the one-parameter family of Riemannian metrics introduced by Hüper et al. (2021). This family includes, in particular, the canonical and Euclidean metrics. By combining existing and new bounds on the sectional curvature, we determine the exact value of the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold under a wide range of members of the metric family.

Introduction

The Stiefel manifold is one of the most classical matrix manifolds
Edelman et al. 1998
;
Boumal 2023
;
Absil et al. 2008
. It appears in numerous applications ranging from optimization
Chen et al. 2024
;
Sato 2021
over numerical methods for differential equations
Benner et al. 2015
;
Celledoni et al. 2020
;
Hüper et al. 2008
;
Zimmermann 2021
to applications in statistics and data science
Chakraborty and Vemuri 2019
;
Pennec et al. 2020
;
Turaga et al. 2008
.
In particular, the geodesic endpoint problem on the Stiefel manifold has attracted a growing interest; see, e.g.,
Sutti 2024
;
Mataigne et al. 2025
.

Geodesics are manifold curves with no covariant acceleration generalizing straight lines in Euclidean spaces. The notion of
covariant acceleration
depends on the selected Riemannian metric and therefore, so do the geodesics. The geodesic endpoint problem is the problem of finding a geodesic that connects two given points. On complete Riemannian manifolds, such a geodesic always exists but is not necessarily unique. Among all solutions to the geodesic endpoint problem, a
minimal geodesic
, i.e., of shortest length, is especially desired since the Riemannian distance between the two points is defined by its length. Ensuring that a geodesic is minimal is therefore a crucial problem of Riemannian geometry for optimization on manifolds
Absil et al. 2007
;
Bonnabel 2013
;
Hosseini and Uschmajew 2017
, statistics on manifolds
Afsari 2011
;
Arnaudon and Nielsen 2013
;
Pennec 2006
;
Guigui et al. 2023
, and data fitting
Absil et al. 2016
;
Gousenbourger et al. 2019
.

The (
global
) injectivity radius of a complete Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
is the largest
r
such that every geodesic of length less than or equal to
r
is a shortest curve between its endpoints. The injectivity radius provides thus a sufficient condition on the length of a geodesic for its minimality to hold. Equivalent definitions can be found in Riemannian geometry textbooks such as
do Carmo 1992
;
Petersen 2016
. The injectivity radius is known in a closed form for very few manifolds, such as the
n
-sphere or the Grassmann manifold of
p
-dimensional subspaces
Wong 1967
;
Wong 1968
. Lately, the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold under the Euclidean metric was added to that list
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
.

A main tool in determining a manifold’s injectivity radius is due to Klingenberg
Klingenberg 1982
. If
K>0
is a global upper bound on the sectional curvatures of the compact Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{M}
, then either the injectivity radius is bounded from below by
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K}}
or its exact value is given by half the length of a shortest nontrivial geodesic loop on the manifold. Klingenberg’s statement requires thus the knowledge of i) a global upper bound on the sectional curvature of the manifold and ii) the length of the shortest nontrivial geodesic loops.

Contributions.

In this work, we give the length
of the shortest nontrivial geodesic loops of the Stiefel manifold under every member of the one-parameter family of metrics that was introduced by Hüper et al.
Hüper et al. 2021
.
This family of metrics is parameterized by the parameter
\beta>0
and the most prominent metrics on the Stiefel manifold are members of this metric family. Indeed,
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
gives the canonical metric and
\beta=1
gives Euclidean metric. For both metrics, the length of the shortest geodesic loops of the Stiefel manifold have been determined; see
Absil and Mataigne 2025
;
Rentmeesters 2013
for the canonical metric and
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
for the Euclidean metric. In both cases, the shortest geodesic loops have a length of
2\pi
.
For
\beta\in(0,2]
, we use the essential observation of
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
that the geodesics have constant-norm time derivatives, which makes them curves of constant Frenet curvatures, when considered as curves in the ambient Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We first derive bounds on the Euclidean length of the curves and translate them in a second step to the
\beta
-metrics. For
\beta>2
, we focus on the property that geodesic loops start and end at the same point to provide a matrix-analytic proof of the length of the shortest geodesic loops. In total, we prove that the length
\ell_{\beta}
of the shortest geodesic loops on the Stiefel manifold under the family of
\beta
-metrics is given by
\ell_{\beta}=\min\{\sqrt{2\beta},1\}2\pi
.

A second contribution is that we provide the sharp upper bound
K_{\beta}
on the sectional curvatures of the Stiefel manifold under the
\beta
-metrics for
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
. This closes a gap in the existing sharp upper bounds on the sectional curvature (see
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
), as there are now bounds on the sectional curvatures for
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Finally, with the upper bound
K_{\beta}
and the length
\ell_{\beta}
of the shortest geodesic loops, we utilize Klingenberg’s theorem to determine the exact value of the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold under the
\beta
-metrics for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]\cup[\frac{2}{3},1]
. For the
\beta
-intervals
(0,\frac{1}{3}]
and
[\frac{2}{3},1]
, the injectivity radius is given by
\sqrt{2\beta}\pi
and
\pi
, respectively. For
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
, the injectivity radius is bounded from above and below such that its value can differ at most
3\%
from the conjectured injectivity radius of
Absil and Mataigne 2025
.

\beta\leq 2
and

2
Preliminaries on the Stiefel manifold

2.1
The Stiefel manifold

We begin by introducing the Stiefel manifold and outline basic concepts of differentiable manifolds such as geodesics, curvature, and the injectivity radius. Related references are
Absil et al. 2008
;
Gallier 2011
;
Zimmermann and Hüper 2022
. The Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}(n,p)
is the compact matrix manifold of orthogonal
p
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{St}(n,p)\coloneq\{U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\ |\ U^{\top}U=I_{p}\}.

It is an
np-\frac{p(p+1)}{2}
-dimensional embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{np}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. The Stiefel manifold is extensively studied in the literature; see, e.g.
Edelman et al. 1998
;
Absil et al. 2008
;
Zimmermann 2021
. A special point of the Stiefel manifold that will be used a lot is
I_{n\times p}\coloneq[I_{p}\ 0_{p\times(n-p)}]^{\top}
. For any point
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, the tangent space to
\mathrm{St}(n,p)
at the point
U
is given by

T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)=\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\ |\ U^{\top}\Delta\in\mathrm{Skew}(p)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}.

Every tangent vector
\Delta\in T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
may be written as

\Delta=UA+U_{\perp}B,\text{ with }A\in\mathrm{Skew}(p),\ B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}\text{ and }[U\ U_{\perp}]\in\mathrm{O}(n).

This decomposition is obtained by taking
A=U^{\top}\Delta
and
U_{\perp}B=(I-UU^{\top})\Delta
. Letting
Q\coloneq[U\ U_{\perp}]
, we can write
\Delta=Q\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Notice that if
(I-UU^{\top})\Delta
is not of rank
n-p
, then
B
has not a full row-rank.

On the Stiefel manifold, there exists a one-parameter family of metrics that was introduced in

Hüper et al. 2021
and studied in
Nguyen 2022a
;
Nguyen 2022b
;
Zimmermann and Hüper 2022
;
Absil and Mataigne 2025
;
Nguyen and Sommer 2025
. Given the metric parameter
\beta\in(0,\infty)
, and any two vectors
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\in T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
,
\Delta_{i}=Q\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A_{i}\\
B_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, the
\beta
-metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\beta,U}:T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and its induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{\beta,U}:T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are defined by

\langle\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\rangle_{\beta,U}=\beta\mathrm{tr}(A_{1}^{\top}A_{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}),\quad\text{and}\quad\|\Delta_{1}\|_{\beta,U}=\sqrt{\langle\Delta_{1},\Delta_{1}\rangle_{\beta,U}}.

For brevity, we drop the
U
-subscript and write
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\beta}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\beta}
. The Stiefel manifold endowed with the
\beta
-metric is denoted by
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
.
To highlight the Euclidean case, we write occasionally

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathrm{E}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{E}}
instead of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{1}
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.
The
\beta
-length
of a continuously differentiable curve
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is:

L_{\beta}(\gamma)\coloneq\int_{0}^{1}\|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|_{\beta}\mathrm{d}t,

where
\dot{\gamma}
denotes the time derivative of
\gamma
. W.l.o.g., in this paper, we always consider curves to be defined on the time interval
[0,1]
, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

2.2
Geodesics and the injectivity radius

Geodesics are the generalization of straight lines in flat spaces to smooth manifolds, and candidates for length-minimizing curves, see, e.g.,
(
Kühnel 2015
, 5.19 Definition)
.
Geodesics are uniquely defined by a starting point and a starting velocity; on the Stiefel manifold by
U\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
,
\Delta=Q\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, where
Q\coloneq[U\ U_{\perp}]\in\mathrm{O}(n)
.
By
(
Zimmermann and Hüper 2022
, eq. (11))
, the associated geodesic is

\gamma_{\beta}(t)=Q\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\right)I_{n\times p}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)A).

The Riemannian exponential is
\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,U}:T_{U}\mathrm{St}(n,p)\rightarrow\mathrm{St}(n,p),\,\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,U}(\Delta)=\gamma_{\beta}(1)
, and is well-defined, since
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is complete. An important theoretical tool for studying the Riemannian exponential and its invertibility is the
injectivity radius
, see, e.g.,
(
Sakai 1996
, Chap. III, Def. 4.12)
.

Definition 2.1

(Injectivity Radius)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a complete Riemannian manifold,
q\in\mathcal{M}
be a point on the manifold and
\mathrm{Exp}_{q}
denote the Riemannian exponential at
q
.
We define the injectivity radius
\mathrm{inj}_{q}(\mathcal{M})
at
q
as

\displaystyle\sup\{r>0;\;\mathrm{Exp}_{q}|_{B_{r}(q)}\text{ is a diffeomorphism}\}.

The infimum of
\mathrm{inj}_{q}(\mathcal{M})
over all
q\in\mathcal{M}
is called the injectivity radius of
\mathcal{M}
and is denoted by
\mathrm{inj}(\mathcal{M})=\inf_{q\in\mathcal{M}}\mathrm{inj}_{q}(\mathcal{M})
.

The Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is a homogeneous space for all
\beta>0

(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Sect. 3.1 and 3.2)
. Therefore, the geodesic starting at
QI_{n\times p}\in\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with initial velocity
Q\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is the geodesic starting
I_{n\times p}
with initial velocity
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]
, left multiplied by
Q
.
In consequence, the injectivity radius is independent of the starting point and, in particular, it follows that
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))=\mathrm{inj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))
.

A result due to Klingenberg
Klingenberg 1982
(or see
(
Sakai 1996
, Chap. III, Prop. 4.13)
) states that

\displaystyle\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))=\min\left\{\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2},\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\right\},

(2.1)

where
\ell_{\beta}
is the length of a shortest nontrivial
geodesic loop
starting at
I_{n\times p}
and
\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))
is the so-called conjugate radius (see
(
Sakai 1996
, Chap. III, Prop. 4.13)
). A
geodesic loop
is a geodesic that begins and ends at the same point. Moreover, if the sectional curvature of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is globally bounded by
K_{\beta}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\geq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}},

(2.2)

see, e.g.,
(
Sakai 1996
, Chap. III, Prop. 3.6)
.

Upper bounds on the conjugate radius are given in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Theorem 5.1)
by finding explicit conjugate points, while lower bounds are given in
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
by determining the maximum sectional curvature
K_{\beta}
.
The shortest geodesic loops under the canonical and under the Euclidean metric have a length of
2\pi
, see

(
Rentmeesters 2013
, Section 5)
,
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Theorem 6.1)
(canonical case), and
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
(Euclidean case).
However, for
\beta\notin\{\frac{1}{2},1\}
, the length of the shortest geodesic loops is unknown but bounded from above by
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Eq. (6.1))

\ell_{\beta}\leq\min\{\sqrt{2\beta},1\}2\pi.

(2.3)

In this paper, we show that the upper bound from (
2.3
) is an equality for the complete range
\beta\in(0,\infty)
. Together with the lower bounds on the conjugate radius (from
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
and
section

6
), this finding allows us to determine the exact value of the injectivity radius of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]\cup[\frac{2}{3},1]
as well as defining bounds for the injectivity radius for all other values of
\beta
.
This is the content of
theorem

7.3
.

3
The
\beta
-length of geodesic loops (
\beta\in(0,2]
)

When
p=1
, the Stiefel manifold reduces to the unit sphere as a Riemannian submanifold of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
p=n\geq 2
, the Stiefel manifold reduces to the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
endowed with the Frobenius metric scaled by
\beta
. In both cases, the length of the shortest geodesic loops is well known, see, e.g.,
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Chap. 6)
.

Let
2\leq p\leq n-1
.
In this section, we consider
\beta\in(0,2]

and show that the
\beta
-length of shortest geodesic loops on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is
\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
for
\beta<\frac{1}{2}
, and
2\pi
for
\beta\geq\frac{1}{2}
(see
theorem

3.4
).
The essential observation is that the geodesics are curves of constant Frenet curvatures, when considered as curves in the ambient Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\cong\mathbb{R}^{np}
, see
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
.
As such a Euclidean transformation sends them to the form

\gamma(t)=\left(a_{1}\cos(b_{1}t),a_{1}\sin(b_{1}t),\ldots,a_{m}\cos(b_{m}t),a_{m}\sin(b_{m}t)\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{np},

(3.1)

if
np=2m
is even and to

\gamma(t)=\left(a_{1}\cos(b_{1}t),a_{1}\sin(b_{1}t),\ldots,a_{m}\cos(b_{m}t),a_{m}\sin(b_{m}t),a_{m+1}t\right)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{np},

(3.2)

if
np=2m+1
is odd. For loops, necessarily
a_{m+1}=0
, so that we are left with the form (
3.1
). When viewing the geodesics in this Frenet form, we see that all geodesic loops on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
are closed.
This means that the geodesic not only begins and ends at the same point, but also that the geodesic’s tangent at the end point matches the tangent at the starting point.
For background on Frenet curves, consult
(
Klingenberg 1978
, Sections 1.2, 1.3)
or
(
Kühnel 2015
, Chapter 2)
.

For a geodesic loop
\gamma
, we let
t_{L}
be the duration of the loop, i.e.,
\gamma(t_{L})=\gamma(0)
and
\gamma(t)\neq\gamma(0)
for all
t\in(0,t_{L})
. Since the length of a loop does not depend on its parameterization, it follows that the unit-speed assumptions in the following results causes no loss of generality and ensures that
t_{L}
is equal to the Euclidean length of the loop.

3.1
The Euclidean length of geodesic loops

We derive a lower bound on the Euclidean length of nontrivial geodesic loops on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
. This bound is valid for all
\beta>0
, but it is only useful for deriving a bound on
\ell_{\beta}
for
\beta\in(0,2]
(see
section

3.2
).
We start with a general length bound.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\gamma
be a unit-speed loop with constant Frenet curvatures in the form of (
3.1
).
Let
b_{\min}=\min\{|b_{i}|\mid b_{i}\neq 0\}
, i.e., the smallest non-zero
b
-coefficient in (
3.1
).
The Euclidean length of
\gamma
satisfies

L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma)=t_{L}\geq\frac{2\pi}{b_{\min}}\geq\frac{2\pi}{\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{E}}}.

Proof.

Since the loop is of unit speed, we have
1=\|\dot{\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{2}
. From the periodicity of sines and cosines in (
3.1
), it is clear that the curve
\gamma
closes if and only if there exist
t_{L}>0
and
l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
t_{L}=\frac{2\pi l_{i}}{b_{i}}
for all
i\text{ with }b_{i}\neq 0
. The smallest such
t_{L}
corresponds to the Euclidean length of
\gamma
:
L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma)=t_{L}
.

Let
b_{\min}:=\min\{|b_{i}|\mid b_{i}\neq 0\}>0
with associated
l_{\min}\in\mathbb{N}
, so that

t_{L}=\frac{2\pi l_{\min}}{b_{\min}}\geq\frac{2\pi}{b_{\min}}.

Let
\kappa=\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{E}}
be the Euclidean curvature of
\gamma
.
Then

\kappa^{2}=\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}=\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{4}\geq b_{\min}^{2}\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}^{2}=b_{\min}^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\frac{1}{b_{\min}}\geq\frac{1}{\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{E}}}.

As a consequence,
L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma)=t_{L}\geq\frac{2\pi}{b_{\min}}\geq\frac{2\pi}{\|\ddot{\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{E}}}
.
∎

Applying this result to the Stiefel geodesics yields the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Recall that
2\leq p\leq n-1
. Let
\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{n\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)
be a geodesic loop in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
of unit Euclidean speed, i.e.,
\sqrt{\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=1
, then the norm squared of the second derivative is bounded by

\displaystyle\|\ddot{\gamma}_{\beta}\|_{E}^{2}\leq 1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}.

As a direct consequence, the Euclidean(!) length of
\gamma_{\beta}
satisfies

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}}}.

Proof.

The first and second derivatives of the geodesic are given by

\displaystyle\dot{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)=

\displaystyle=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(t(1-2\beta)A),\quad\text{and},

\displaystyle\ddot{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\top}A-B^{\top}B\\
(2-2\beta)BA\end{bmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(t(1-2\beta)A).

Mind that
A
is skew-symmetric. For the norm of the second derivative, it holds

\displaystyle\|\ddot{\gamma}_{\beta}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{bmatrix}-A^{\top}A-B^{\top}B\\
(2-2\beta)BA\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(A^{4})+\mathrm{tr}((B^{\top}B)^{2})+2\underbrace{\mathrm{tr}(A^{\top}AB^{\top}B)}_{=\mathrm{tr}(A^{\top}B^{\top}BA)}+(2-2\beta)^{2}\mathrm{tr}(A^{\top}B^{\top}BA)

\displaystyle=\|A^{\top}A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\mathrm{tr}((B^{\top}B)^{2})+(6-8\beta+4\beta^{2})\|BA\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Next, we determine an upper bound on the norm of the second derivative that is only dependent on
\beta
,
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}
.
By the skew-symmetry of
A
, it holds
\|A^{\top}A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}
and
\|BA\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
, see
(
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, Lemma 3)
.
The
\beta
-parabola
p(\beta)=(6-8\beta+4\beta^{2})
is always positive, so that we obtain

\displaystyle\|\ddot{\gamma}_{\beta}\|_{\mathrm{E}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}+(6-8\beta+4\beta^{2})\frac{1}{2}\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}+\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}+2\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}.

Applying
lemma

3.1
concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

The
\beta
-parabola
p(\beta)=(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})
is non-positive in the range
\beta\in[1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]
. Hence, in this range, we have
\|\ddot{\gamma}_{\beta}\|_{\mathrm{E}}\leq 1
and
L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq 2\pi
by
lemma

3.2
.
Therefore, for
\beta\in[1,1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]
, the
\beta
-length of
\gamma_{\beta}
satisfies

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=t_{L}\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\geq t_{L}\sqrt{\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq 2\pi,

and the bound is sharp by the examples of
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Section 6)
.

3.2
The
\beta
-length of shortest geodesic loops (
\beta\in(0,2]
)

In this section, we translate the Euclidean length bounds from
lemma

3.2
to the
\beta
-metric.
Consider the geodesic loop
\gamma_{\beta}\colon[0,t_{L}]\to\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with
\gamma_{\beta}(t)=\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{n\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)
of unit Euclidean speed. We have,

\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=1\Longleftrightarrow\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=1-\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Recall that
t_{L}
is assumed to be the duration of the loop and that the Euclidean length of the loop is given by
t_{L}
.
When measured according to the
\beta
-metric, we have

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})^{2}=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t_{L}}\|\dot{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)\|_{\beta}\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=t_{L}^{2}(\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma_{\beta})^{2}(\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+1-\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})

\displaystyle=L_{\mathrm{E}}(\gamma_{\beta})^{2}(1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}).

Lemma

3.2
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})^{2}&\geq(2\pi)^{2}\frac{(1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})}{1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}}\\
&=(2\pi)^{2}\frac{(1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})}{1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+(-\frac{3}{2}+4\beta-2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}}.\end{split}

(3.3)

Theorem 3.4
.

Under the standing assumption that
2\leq p\leq n-1
and
\beta\in(0,2]
, the
\beta
-length
\ell_{\beta}
of a shortest geodesic loop on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is given by

\displaystyle\ell_{\beta}=\min\{\sqrt{2\beta},1\}2\pi.

Proof.

There are geodesic loops on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
that have the length
\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
or
2\pi
, see
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Section 6)
. Hence, we are left with proving that this upper bound
\ell_{\beta}\leq\min\{\sqrt{2\beta},1\}2\pi
on the length of a shortest geodesic loop is also a lower bound.
For
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, the upper bound is given by
2\pi
and, for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, the upper bound is given by
\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
.
It is sufficient to show that the parametric fraction in (
3.3
) is greater than or equal to
1
for all
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
and that it is greater than or equal to
2\beta
for all
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.

We start by showing that for all
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{(1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})}{1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+(-\frac{3}{2}+4\beta-2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq 1.

This sought conclusion is successively equivalent to

\displaystyle 1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+(-\frac{3}{2}+4\beta-2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}

\displaystyle\leq 1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\beta-\frac{1}{2}\right)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\left(2(\beta-2)+(3-2\beta)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 0.

(3.4)

The inequality is certainly fulfilled for
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
or
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0
. Assume
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}>0
and
\beta\in(\frac{1}{2},2]
. Then, the factor
(\beta-\frac{1}{2})
is positive and we have to show that the factor
(2(\beta-2)+(3-2\beta)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})
is smaller than or equal to zero. Define
c_{1}(\beta)\coloneq 2(\beta-2)
and
c_{2}(\beta)\coloneq 3-2\beta
. For
\beta\in[\frac{3}{2},2]
, it holds
c_{1}(\beta)\leq 0
and
c_{2}(\beta)\leq 0
and, therefore, the inequality in (
3.4
) is fulfilled. For
\beta\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2})
, it holds
c_{1}(\beta)<0
and
c_{2}(\beta)>0
. Hence, the factor
(2(\beta-2)+(3-2\beta)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2})
attains its largest possible value for
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
which is
-1
. In total, the inequality in (
3.4
) is fulfilled for all
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
.
This shows that for every geodesic loop
\gamma_{\beta}
in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
, its length is bounded below by
2\pi
, i.e.,

L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq 2\pi.

Therefore, we have
\ell_{\beta}=2\pi
, for
\beta\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
.

It remains to show that for all
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\left(1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right)}{1+(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+(-\frac{3}{2}+4\beta-2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}}\geq 2\beta.

(3.5)

This inequality is successively equivalent to

\displaystyle 1+(\beta-1)\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2\beta+2\beta(1-4\beta+2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+2\beta(-\frac{3}{2}+4\beta-2\beta^{2})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq 2\beta-1+(1+\beta-8\beta^{2}+4\beta^{3})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+(-3\beta+8\beta^{2}-4\beta^{3})\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{4}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq(\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-1)(2\beta-1)(\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\beta(2\beta-3)+1).

The inequality is certainly fulfilled for
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
. For
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1)
and
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, the first two factors are smaller than zero, i.e.,
(\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-1)<0
and
(2\beta-1)<0
. Hence, the third factor
(\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\beta(2\beta-3)+1)
has to be nonnegative. The term
(2\beta-3)
is negative for
0<\beta<\frac{1}{2}
. Hence, the factor
(\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\beta(2\beta-3)+1)
is larger than its attained value for
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
which is given by
2\beta^{2}-3\beta+1
. It is easy to verify that this
\beta
-parabola
p(\beta)=2\beta^{2}-3\beta+1
is positive for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. In total, the inequality in (
3.5
) is fulfilled for all
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
and
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. This shows that for a geodesic loop
\gamma_{\beta}
in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, it holds

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi.

Consequently, we have
\ell_{\beta}=\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
, for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
∎

Note that this result includes the earlier ones for the canonical and Euclidean metric of
Rentmeesters 2013
;
Absil and Mataigne 2025
and
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, respectively.

4
The exponential inverse and invariance groups

The proof technique of
Theorem

3.4
cannot be extended to the case
\beta>2
. Indeed, the left-hand side of (
3.4
) can become positive for some values of
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\in[0,1]
.
In order to prove the length of the shortest geodesic loops on the Stiefel manifold for
\beta>2
, we need to manipulate matrix exponential inverses. To this end, we present five short lemmata.

Let us define the inverse of the matrix exponential
\exp_{\mathrm{m}}:\mathrm{Skew}(p)\rightarrow\mathrm{SO}(p)
as the set-valued function

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(Q)\coloneq\{\Omega\in\mathrm{Skew}(p)\ |\ \exp_{\mathrm{m}}(\Omega)=Q\}.

A very important set of orthogonal matrices are the real Schur forms of
\mathrm{SO}(p)
. Let
k\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor
,
J_{2}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and
J_{2p}=J_{2}\otimes I_{p}
. For all diagonal matrices
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, if
p
is even, then

G_{p}(\Phi)\coloneq\cos(\Phi)\otimes I_{2}+\sin(\Phi)\otimes J_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\Phi_{11}&-\sin\Phi_{11}&&&\\
\sin\Phi_{11}&\cos\Phi_{11}&&&\\
&&\ddots&&\\
&&&\cos\Phi_{kk}&-\sin\Phi_{kk}\\
&&&\sin\Phi_{kk}&\cos\Phi_{kk}\end{bmatrix}.

Otherwise, if
p
is odd we have

G_{p}(\Phi)\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\Phi)\otimes I_{2}+\sin(\Phi)\otimes J_{2}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(p).

Similarly, we define the real Schur forms of
\mathrm{Skew}(p)
. For all diagonal matrices
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
, if
p
is even, then
\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)\coloneq(\Phi+2\pi K)\otimes J_{2}
. Otherwise, if
p
is odd we have

\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}(\Phi+2\pi K)\otimes J_{2}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{Skew}(p).

lemmas

4.1
and
4.2
describe the structure of the invariance groups of matrices with disjoint spectra. The
invariance group
of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is

\mathrm{ig}(A)\coloneq\{M\in\mathrm{O}(k)\ |\ A=MAM^{\top}\}.

The
complex invariance group
of
A\in\mathbb{C}^{k\times k}
is
\mathrm{cig}(A)\coloneq\{M\in\mathrm{U}(k)\ |\ A=MAM^{*}\}
. The invariance groups are fundamental to analyze the matrix exponential inverse. In particular, a matrix
M
is termed
J_{2n}
-orthosymplectic
if
M\in\mathrm{ig}(J_{2n})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given two diagonal matrices
\Lambda_{A},\Lambda_{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
with disjoint spectra, it holds that

\mathrm{cig}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{A}&0\\
0&\Lambda_{B}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{cig}(\Lambda_{A})&0\\
0&\mathrm{cig}(\Lambda_{B})\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Letting
M\coloneq\left[\begin{smallmatrix}M_{11}&M_{12}\\
M_{21}&M_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathrm{cig}\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\Lambda_{A}&0\\
0&\Lambda_{B}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
, it must hold by definition of invariance groups that

M_{12}\Lambda_{B}=\Lambda_{A}M_{12}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{21}\Lambda_{A}=\Lambda_{B}M_{21}.

(4.1)

Since
\Lambda_{A}
and
\Lambda_{B}
have disjoint spectra, (
4.1
) is true if and only if
M_{12}=M_{21}=0
. Therefore,
M
is block diagonal and this implies
M_{11}\in\mathrm{cig}(A)
and
M_{22}\in\mathrm{cig}(B)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

The result about diagonal matrices in
lemma

4.1
can be generalized to normal matrices.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given two normal matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with disjoint spectra, it holds that

\mathrm{ig}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{ig}(A)&0\\
0&\mathrm{ig}(B)\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

Let
A=V_{A}\Lambda_{A}V_{A}^{*}
and
B=V_{B}\Lambda_{B}V_{B}^{*}
be eigenvalue decompositions.
V_{A}
and
V_{B}
are unitary since
A
and
B
are normal.
For all
M\in\mathrm{ig}\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
, we have

M\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}M^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{bmatrix}\iff\widetilde{M}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{A}&0\\
0&\Lambda_{B}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{M}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{A}&0\\
0&\Lambda_{B}\end{bmatrix},

(4.2)

where
\widetilde{M}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}V_{A}^{*}&0\\
0&V_{B}^{*}\end{smallmatrix}\right]M\left[\begin{smallmatrix}V_{A}&0\\
0&V_{B}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Therefore, by
lemma

4.1
,
\widetilde{M}
has a block structure
\widetilde{M}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widetilde{M}_{11}&0\\
0&\widetilde{M}_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
and so does
M=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}V_{A}^{*}\widetilde{M}_{11}V_{A}&0\\
0&V_{B}^{*}\widetilde{M}_{22}V_{B}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. By (
4.2
), we have
M_{11}\in\mathrm{ig}(A)
and
M_{22}\in\mathrm{ig}(B)
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Next, we show how the invariance groups affect the computation of the matrix exponential inverse.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every positive integer
p
,
k\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor
and all diagonal matrices
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
, we have

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))=\left\{M\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)M^{\top}\ |\ K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}\text{ is diagonal},\ M\in\mathrm{ig}(G_{p}(\Phi))\right\}.

Proof.

For all diagonal
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
, since
\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K))=G_{p}(\Phi)
, we have
\{\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)\ |\ K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}\text{ is diagonal}\}\subseteq\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))
. Moreover, the spectrum of
G_{p}(\Phi)
imposes that every matrix in
\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))
is an orthogonal similarity transformation of
\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)
for some
K
, therefore,
\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))\subseteq\{M\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)M^{\top}\ |\ K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}\text{ is diagonal},M\in\mathrm{O}(2k)\}
. Finally,
M\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)M^{\top}\in\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))
if and only if

G_{p}(\Phi)=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(M\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)M^{\top})=M\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K))M^{\top}=MG_{p}(\Phi)M^{\top}.

Therefore, it must hold that
M\in\mathrm{ig}(G_{p}(\Phi))
.
∎

The next lemma is a corollary of
lemma

4.3
for the particular case where the diagonal matrix
\Phi
is the identity
I_{k}
multiplied by
\varphi\in(-\pi,\pi)\setminus\{0\}
. In this case,
\mathrm{ig}(G_{2k}(\varphi I_{k}))
is the very special set of
J_{2k}
-orthosymplectic matrices.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every
\varphi\in(-\pi,\pi)\setminus\{0\}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{2k}(\varphi I_{k}))=

\displaystyle\Big\{M\Omega_{2k}(\varphi I_{k},K)M^{\top}\ |\ K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k},

\displaystyle K\text{ is diagonal and }M\text{ is $J_{2k}$-orthosymplectic}\Big\}.

Proof.

Notice that
\mathrm{ig}(G_{2k}(\varphi I_{k}))=\{M\text{ is $J_{2n}$-orthosymplectic}\}
. Since
\mathrm{ig}(G_{2k}(\varphi I_{k}))\not\subset\mathrm{ig}\left(\Omega_{2k}(\varphi I_{k},K)\right)
, by
lemma

4.3
in general, this concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we relate the matrix exponential inverse of a block diagonal matrix to the block diagonal matrix of the matrix exponential inverses.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
k\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor
. For all positive integers
k_{1},p
, for all
\varphi\in(0,\pi]
and all nonnegative diagonal matrices
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
,
\|\Phi\|_{2}\leq\pi
, such that
\varphi
is not a diagonal entry of
\Phi
, we have

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}}))&0\\
0&\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

For every diagonal matrix
K_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k_{1}\times k_{1}}
and
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{k\times k}
, it holds that

\begin{bmatrix}\Omega_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}},K_{1})&0\\
0&\Omega_{p}(\Phi,K)\end{bmatrix}\in\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi)\end{bmatrix}\cap\begin{bmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}}))&0\\
0&\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{p}(\Phi))\end{bmatrix}.

By
lemma

4.3
, it is thus enough to prove that
\mathrm{ig}\left(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}G_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi)\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\mathrm{ig}(G_{2k_{1}}(\varphi I_{k_{1}}))&0\\
0&\mathrm{ig}(G_{p}(\Phi))\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. The later holds by
lemma

4.2
. This concludes the proof.
∎

5
The
\beta
-length of geodesic loops (
\beta\in(2,\infty)
)

Similar to
section

3
, we restrict to
2\leq p\leq n-1
. In this section, we show that the length of a shortest geodesic loop on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
, for
\beta\in(2,\infty)
, is
2\pi
, see
theorem

5.8
. We proceed in two phases. In the first phase, we study the structure of shortest geodesic loops to simplify the problem as much as possible. This is
section

5.1
. In the second phase, we analyze the simplified problem to prove that shortest geodesic loops have length
2\pi
. This is
section

5.2
.

5.1
The structure of shortest geodesic loops

We first prove that every lower bound on the length of the shortest geodesic loops on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2p,p)
is a lower bound on the length of shortest geodesic loops on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
. We can thus concentrate on geodesic loops on the Stiefel manifold with
n=2p
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Recall that
2\leq p\leq n-1
and let
\ell_{\beta}
be the length of the shortest nontrivial geodesic loop starting at
I_{n\times p}
in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
and
\widehat{\ell}_{\beta}
the length of the shortest nontrivial geodesic loop starting at
I_{2p\times p}
in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2p,p)
. Then,
\ell_{\beta}\geq\widehat{\ell}_{\beta}
.

Proof.

Assume first
n\geq 2p
. For every tangent vector
\Delta=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I_{p}\\
0_{n-p\times p}\end{smallmatrix}\right]A+\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0_{p\times n-p}\\
I_{n-p}\end{smallmatrix}\right]B\in T_{I_{n\times p}}\mathrm{St}(n,p)
, with
B\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-p)\times p}
, a thin QR decomposition yields
B=QR
with
Q\in\mathrm{St}(n-p,p)
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. Therefore

\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{n\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&0\\
0&Q\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
R\end{bmatrix}\right)\eqcolon\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}&0\\
0&Q\end{bmatrix}\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}(t).

Moreover, since
\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|R\|_{\mathrm{F}}
, we have
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=L_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta})
for all
\Delta\in\mathrm{St}(n,p)
and it holds that
\ell_{\beta}\geq\widehat{\ell}_{\beta}
if
n\geq 2p
.

Assume now that
n<2p
. Notice that for every geodesic
\gamma_{\beta}
of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
, the padded curve

\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{\beta}(t)\\
0_{(2p-n)\times p}\end{bmatrix},

is a geodesic of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2p,p)
. Finally, since
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=L_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta})
, it is clear that
\ell_{\beta}\geq\widehat{\ell}_{\beta}
.
∎

By
lemma

5.1
, a lower bound on the length
\widehat{\ell}_{\beta}
of shortest geodesic loops in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2p,p)
gives a lower bound on the length
\ell_{\beta}
of shortest geodesic loops in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
, for
2\leq p\leq n-1
. Therefore, if a geodesic loop in
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
has a length equal to
\widehat{\ell}_{\beta}
, then its length is also equal to
\ell_{\beta}
. It is thus sufficient to consider geodesic loops on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2p,p)
emanating from
I_{2p\times p}
of the form

\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\right)I_{2p\times p}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(t(1-2\beta)A),

(5.1)

with
A\in\mathrm{Skew}(p)
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. The definition of a geodesic loop is that it starts and ends in the same point. When parameterized on the unit interval,
\gamma_{\beta}(1)=\gamma_{\beta}(0)=I_{2p\times p}
. This yields the following equation

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}I_{2p\times p}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)A)=I_{2p\times p}.

(5.2)

A shortest (nontrivial) geodesic loop features
L(\gamma_{\beta})=\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}
as small as possible.
Define

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\coloneq\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}E_{11}&E_{12}\\
E_{21}&E_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(2p),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{E}

\displaystyle\coloneq\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)A)\in\mathrm{SO}(p).

Now, equation (
5.2
) holds if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}E_{11}&E_{12}\\
E_{21}&E_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{E}\\
0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I_{p}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

which implies
E_{11}\widetilde{E}=I_{p}
and
E_{21}\widetilde{E}=0
. Therefore, it holds
E_{11}=\widetilde{E}^{\top}\in\mathrm{SO}(p)
and thus
E_{12}=E_{21}^{\top}=0
. Finally, we have

\displaystyle E=\begin{bmatrix}E_{11}&0\\
0&E_{22}\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{E}=E_{11}^{\top},

with
E_{11}\in\mathrm{SO}(p)
,
E_{22}\in\mathrm{SO}(p)
.
Both
E_{11}
and
E_{22}
admit a real Schur decomposition such that

E_{11}=V_{1}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})V_{1}^{\top},\quad\text{and}\quad E_{22}=V_{2}G_{p}(\Phi_{2})V_{2}^{\top},

(5.3)

where
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathrm{SO}(p)
and
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
are nonnegative diagonal matrices,
k\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor
and
\|\Phi_{1}\|_{2},\|\Phi_{2}\|_{2}\leq\pi
. We now state a lemma on the isometry of curves.

Property 5.2
.

For all
\Delta=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in T_{I_{2p\times p}}\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2p,p)
, all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and all
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathrm{SO}(p)
, we have

\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&0\\
0&V_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\top}AV_{1}\\
V_{2}^{\top}BV_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right)V_{1}^{\top}\eqcolon\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&0\\
0&V_{2}\end{bmatrix}\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)V_{1}^{\top}.

(5.4)

Moreover,
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=L_{\beta}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta})
and if
\gamma_{\beta}
is a geodesic loop, so is
\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta}
.

Proof.

First, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\top}AV_{1}\\
V_{2}^{\top}BV_{1}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta V_{1}^{\top}AV_{1}&-(V_{2}^{\top}BV_{1})^{\top}\\
V_{2}^{\top}BV_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}I_{2p\times p}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)V_{1}^{\top}AV_{1})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\top}&0\\
0&V_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}I_{2p\times p}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)A)V_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}^{\top}&0\\
0&V_{2}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)V_{1}.

Moreover,

L_{\beta}(\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta})^{2}=\beta\|V_{1}^{\top}AV_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|V_{2}^{\top}BV_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})^{2}.

Finally, if
\gamma_{\beta}
is a geodesic loop such that
\gamma_{\beta}(0)=\gamma_{\beta}(1)=I_{2p\times p}
, by (
5.4
), we also have
\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta}(0)=\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta}(1)=I_{2p\times p}
.
∎

In view of
5.2
, for every geodesic loop
\gamma_{\beta}
, considering
V_{1},V_{2}
from (
5.3
), there is another geodesic loop
\widetilde{\gamma}_{\beta}
, with initial velocity
\widetilde{\Delta}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widetilde{A}\\
\widetilde{B}\end{smallmatrix}\right]
that has the same length as
\gamma_{\beta}
and such that

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widetilde{A}&-\widetilde{B}^{\top}\\
\widetilde{B}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi_{2})\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\exp((1-2\beta)\widetilde{A})=G_{p}(-\Phi_{1}).

(5.5)

For the rest of this section, it is thus sufficient to consider geodesic loops that satisfy the structure of (
5.5
). Moreover, by means of
lemma

5.3
, we prove that to study shortest geodesic loops, we can consider even more structure. More precisely, it is enough to consider that
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
in (
5.5
) are multiples of the identity matrix.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
p\leq n-1
and
k\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p}{2}\rfloor
,
A\in\mathrm{Skew}(p)
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, such that

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi_{2})\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)A)=G_{p}(-\Phi_{1}),

(5.6)

where
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
are (w.l.o.g., nonnegative) diagonal matrices such that
\|\Phi_{1}\|_{2},\|\Phi_{2}\|_{2}\leq\pi
.
Then, for every
\varphi\neq 0
that has a multiplicity
s_{1}\geq 1
in the vector
\mathrm{diag}(\Phi_{1})
and a multiplicity
s_{2}\geq 0
in
\mathrm{diag}(\Phi_{2})
, there is a
2s_{1}\times 2s_{1}

\widehat{A}
of
A
and a
2s_{2}\times 2s_{1}

\widehat{B}
of

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{2s_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)\widehat{A})=G_{2s_{1}}(-\varphi I_{s_{1}}).

(5.7)

Proof.

Assume
\varphi\neq 0
has a multiplicity
s_{1}
in the vector
\Phi_{1}
and a multiplicity
s_{2}
in
\Phi_{2}
, and let
m\coloneq s_{1}+s_{2}
. Then, there is a permutation
P_{\varphi}
such that for some
\widetilde{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p-m)\times(p-m)}

P_{\varphi}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi_{2})\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0&0\\
0&G_{2s_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})&0\\
0&0&G_{2(p-m)}(\widetilde{\Phi})\end{bmatrix}.

(5.8)

Notice that if
p
is odd,
\widetilde{\Phi}
has the diagonal entry zero at least twice. Moreover, it is understood that if
s_{2}=0
or
p-m=0
, then the associated matrix blocks vanish.

By hypothesis (
5.6
), it follows that

\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\in\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi_{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(5.9)

By combining (
5.8
) and (
5.9
), we obtain

X_{\varphi}\coloneq P_{\varphi}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}\in\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0&0\\
0&G_{2s_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})&0\\
0&0&G_{2(p-m)}(\widetilde{\Phi})\end{bmatrix}.

(5.10)

By
lemma

4.5
, it follows that the
2m\times 2m
upper left block of
X_{\varphi}
belongs to the set
\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{2s_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Moreover, if we require
P_{\varphi}
to satisfy that
G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})
is only obtained from matrix elements of

, this upper left block of

conserves a structure of the form
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]

\widehat{A}\in\mathrm{Skew}(2s_{1})
and
\widehat{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{2s_{2}\times 2s_{1}}
are, respectively, sub-blocks of

and
B
. We have thus shown that

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{2s_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})\end{bmatrix}.

By definition of a geodesic loop, we can write

\left(P_{\varphi}^{\top}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}\right)\left(P_{\varphi}^{\top}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}(1-2\beta)A&0\\
0&0_{p}\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}\right)P_{\varphi}^{\top}I_{2p\times p}=P_{\varphi}^{\top}I_{2p\times p}.

Notice that by definition of
P_{\varphi}
and by the hypothesis (
5.6
), we have

P_{\varphi}^{\top}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}(1-2\beta)A&0\\
0&0_{p}\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}=P_{\varphi}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(-\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&I_{p}\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(-\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0&0\\
0&I_{2s_{2}}&0\\
0&0&*\end{bmatrix}.

(5.11)

Moreover, the
2m\times 2m
upper-left block of
P_{\varphi}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}(1-2\beta)A&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}P_{\varphi}
has also the block structure
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}(1-2\beta)\widehat{A}&0_{s_{1}\times s_{2}}\\
0_{s_{2}\times s_{1}}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
where
\widehat{A}
is the same matrix as previously. By (
5.11
) and
lemma

4.5
, this yields
\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)\widehat{A})=G_{2s_{1}}(-\varphi I_{s_{1}})
.

Finally, it follows that

\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{2s_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{2s_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)\widehat{A})=G_{2s_{1}}(-\varphi I_{s_{1}}).

∎

Since
\widehat{A}
and
\widehat{B}
are submatrices of
A
and
B
, respectively, the length of the geodesic loop defined by
\widehat{A}
and
\widehat{B}
is smaller than the length of the geodesic loop defined by
A
and
B
,

\displaystyle\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\geq\beta\|\widehat{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|\widehat{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.

Note that by design the matrix
\widehat{A}
is nonzero.

Remark 5.4
.

From equation (
5.9
), we can deduce that if a diagonal entry
\breve{\varphi}
of
\Phi_{2}
is not in
\Phi_{1}
, then
\breve{\varphi}=0
. Equivalently, all nonzero diagonal entries of
\Phi_{2}
are in
\Phi_{1}
.
Indeed, assume that

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\Phi}_{2}&0\\
0&\breve{\Phi}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

and assume that the spectrum of
\breve{\Phi}_{2}
does not contain zero and is disjoint from the spectra of
\widehat{\Phi}_{2}
and
\Phi_{1}
. Now, there are suitable dimensions
q_{1},q_{2}\geq 0
with
p=q_{1}+q_{2}
such that the matrices
\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{q_{1}}(\widehat{\Phi}_{2})\end{bmatrix}
and
G_{q_{2}}(\breve{\Phi}_{2})
have disjoint spectra. Note that
q_{1}
is odd, if
p
is odd.
By equation (
5.9
) and a similar argument as in the proof of
lemma

4.5
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\in\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\Phi_{2})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\Phi_{1})&0\\
0&G_{q_{1}}(\widehat{\Phi}_{2})\end{bmatrix}&0\\

So, by identification of the matrix blocks,
0_{q_{2}\times q_{2}}\in\exp_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}(G_{q_{2}}(\breve{\Phi}_{2}))
and thus
\breve{\Phi}_{2}=0
. This contradicts the assumption that zero is not in the spectrum of

. Hence,
\breve{\Phi}_{2}
can not exist.

5.2
The
\beta
-length of shortest geodesic loops (
\beta\in(2,\infty)
)

First, we tackle the cases where one of the matrices
A
or
B
is zero.

Lemma 5.5
.

Recall that
\beta>2
and
2\leq p\leq n-1
. Let
\gamma_{\beta}
be a geodesic loop on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with initial velocity
\Delta=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in T_{I_{n\times p}}\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
.
If
B=0
and
A\neq 0
, then
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq\sqrt{2\beta}{2\pi}\,(>2\pi)
. Moreover, if
A=0
and
B\neq 0
, then
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq 2\pi
. These bounds are sharp.

Proof.

If
A=0
(and
B\neq 0
), then
\gamma_{\beta}
is also a geodesic loop on
\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(n,p)
(canonical metric). Therefore, the length is immediately bounded from below by
2\pi
, cf.
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Thm. 6.1)
. An example of a geodesic loop of length
2\pi
is given at the end of this section.

If
B=0
and
A\neq 0
, then we have
\gamma_{\beta}(1)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(A)\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}I_{p}\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
. Therefore, the singular values of
A
are nonnegative multiples of
2\pi
, and, since
A\neq 0
, at least two singular values of
A
are greater or equal to
2\pi
. Hence,
\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\sqrt{2(2\pi^{2})}\geq 2\pi\sqrt{2}
. Finally,
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}\geq\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
.
∎

We now proceed to show, without restriction on
A
and
B
, that the
\beta
-length of the shortest geodesic loops is bounded below by
2\pi
.

Section

5.1
exposed the structure of the shortest geodesic loops. In particular, let
\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{2p\times p}}\left(t\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
be a geodesic loop which satisfies the structure of (
5.5
). If
\Phi_{1}\neq 0
, by

5.3
, we can extract submatrices
\widehat{A}\in\mathrm{Skew}(p_{1})
and
\widehat{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{1}}
from

and
B
such that the geodesic loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,U}\left(t\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widehat{A}\\
\widehat{B}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
, with
U\coloneq I_{p_{1}+p_{2}\times p_{1}}
, satisfies the structure from (
5.7
). Here,
p_{1}=2s_{1}
,
p_{2}=2s_{2}
for suitable
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
\Phi_{1}=0
is zero, it also holds that the corresponding
\Phi_{2}=0
is zero (see

5.4
). Hence, we are able to write the geodesic loop
\gamma_{\beta}
in a similar structure to (

),

\displaystyle\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{p}(\varphi I_{k})&0\\
0&G_{p}(\varphi I_{k})\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)\widehat{A})=G_{p}(-\varphi I_{k}),

with
\varphi=0
,

and
\widehat{A}\coloneq A\in\mathrm{Skew}(p_{1})
,
\widehat{B}\coloneq B\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{1}}
, with

.

We show that
L_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta})\geq 2\pi
and, hence,
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})\geq 2\pi
. The cases in which either
\widehat{A}
or
\widehat{B}
is zero are treated in
lemma

5.5
.
If
\widehat{B}
is the empty matrix (
s_{2}=0
in

5.3
), we can argue analogously to the proof of

5.5
to show that the length of the geodesic loop is bounded below by
\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
. In

5.7
, we show that

if
\widehat{A}\neq 0
and
\widehat{B}\neq 0
. To prove this, we first need to introduce a result that is a direct consequence of the interlacing property of singular values.

Lemma 5.6
.

Recall that
\beta>2
. Let
p_{1},p_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
be a positive integers and let
A\in\mathrm{Skew}(p_{1})
be a skew-symmetric matrix and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{1}}
be arbitrary. Define the matrix
X\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}
and
\breve{A}\coloneq(1-2\beta)A
. If we assume that the singular values of
X
are given by
\sigma_{1}(X)\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{p_{1}+p_{2}}(X)\geq 0
and the singular values of
\breve{A}
are given by
\sigma_{1}(\breve{A})\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{p_{1}}(\breve{A})\geq 0
, then it holds

\displaystyle\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}\sigma_{j}(\breve{A})\leq\sigma_{j}(X),\quad\text{for }j=1,\dots,p_{1}

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the interlacing property of singular values
(
Horn and Johnson 2012
, Corollary 7.3.6)

∎

Next, we show that the length of the loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}
is also bounded below by
2\pi
if
\widehat{A}\neq 0
and
\widehat{B}\neq 0
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Recall that
\beta>2
. Let
\widehat{A}\in\mathrm{Skew}(p_{1})
and
\widehat{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{1}}
be the two matrices from above. So, they define the geodesic loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,U}\left(t\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\widehat{A}\\
\widehat{B}\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
, with
U\coloneq I_{p_{1}+p_{2}\times p_{1}}
, that satisfies the structure

\displaystyle\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{p_{1}}(\varphi I_{s_{1}})&0\\
0&G_{p_{2}}(\varphi I_{s_{2}})\end{bmatrix},\quad\text{and}\quad\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)\widehat{A})=G_{p_{1}}(-\varphi I_{s_{1}}),

(5.12)

where
\varphi\in[0,\pi]
and
s_{1}\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p_{1}}{2}\rfloor
,
s_{2}\coloneq\lfloor\frac{p_{2}}{2}\rfloor
.
If we assume that
\widehat{A}
and
\widehat{B}
are nonzero, then the length of the geodesic loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}
is greater than
2\pi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta})>2\pi.

Proof.

Recall that
p_{1}=p_{2}=p
, if
\varphi=0
, and
p_{1}=2s_{1}
,
p_{2}=2s_{2}
, if
\varphi\in(0,\pi]
.

With
X\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}2\beta\widehat{A}&-\widehat{B}^{\top}\\
\widehat{B}&0\end{bmatrix}
and
\breve{A}\coloneq(1-2\beta)\widehat{A}
,
we have

\|\widehat{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-2\beta^{2}\|\widehat{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
and thus

L_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta})\coloneq\sqrt{\beta\|\widehat{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|\widehat{B}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\beta(2\beta-1)\|\widehat{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{2\beta-1}\|\breve{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}.

(5.13)

Equation (
5.13
) is a function of the singular values of
X
and
\breve{A}
.
Since
X
and
\breve{A}
are skew-symmetric and their matrix exponential satisfies (
5.12
), the singular values of
X
come in pairs

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|,|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|,\text{ for }k_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }j\in\{1,\dots,p_{1}+p_{2}\},

(5.14)

and so do the singular value of
\widetilde{A}
,

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|,|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|,\text{ for }\ell_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }j\in\{1,\dots,p_{1}\}.

(5.15)

If
p_{1}
is odd, then
\breve{A}
additionally has the singular value zero.
We assume, w.l.o.g., that the singular value (pairs) are ordered by magnitude.
By
lemma

5.6
, we know that if we compare the
s_{1}
largest singular value pairs of
X
with those of
\breve{A}
in descending order, the singular value pair of
X
is greater in magnitude than the singular value pair of
\breve{A}
in each pairing. We show that there are pairings in which the difference between the values of the singular value pairs of
X
and
\breve{A}
is greater than
2\pi
.
We start by showing this property for
\varphi=\pi
. Let
j_{a}\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
be arbitrary. By
lemma

5.6
,

\displaystyle 0<|\pi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|<\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\pi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\leq|\pi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|.

For the integers
\ell_{j_{a}}
,
k_{j_{a}}
, we therefore have
|2\ell_{j_{a}}+1|<|2k_{j_{a}}+1|
and thus

\displaystyle|\pi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|-|\pi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\geq 2\pi.

Next, let
\varphi=0
. Since
\widehat{A}\neq 0
is nonzero, there is a
j_{a}\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
such that the corresponding singular value pair of
\breve{A}
is nonzero,
|2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|>0
. By
lemma

5.6
,

\displaystyle 0<|2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|<\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\leq|2\pi k_{j_{a}}|.

For the integers
\ell_{j_{a}}
and
k_{j_{a}}
, we therefore have
|\ell_{j_{a}}|<|k_{j_{a}}|
, and thus

\displaystyle|2\pi k_{j_{a}}|-|2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\geq 2\pi.

For
\varphi\in(0,\pi)
, there is also a
j_{a}\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
such that

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|-|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\geq 2\pi.

(5.16)

To see this, we refer the reader to
lemma

8.1
in Appendix
8
.
With this property of the singular values of
X
and
\breve{A}
, we show that the length of the geodesic loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}
is greater than
2\pi
.
For any
\varphi\in[0,\pi]
, there exists a

corresponding to the

-th largest singular value pair of

and
\breve{A}
such that

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|-|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\geq 2\pi.

(5.17)

By

5.6
,

\displaystyle\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\leq|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|.

So, by squaring and rearranging equation (
5.17
), we can make use of the interlacing property to obtain

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi^{2}+2|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}||\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|-|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi^{2}+\left(2\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}-1\right)|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|^{2}.

Furthermore, by using the interlacing property
lemma

5.6
again, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|^{2}\leq\left(\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|\right)^{2}\leq|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|^{2},

for all
j\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
. Hence, it follows by (
5.13
) that

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|X\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{2\beta-1}\|\widetilde{A}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{1}}\underbrace{\left(|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|^{2}-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|^{2}\right)}_{\geq 0}+\sum_{j=s_{1}+1}^{\frac{p_{1}+p_{2}}{2}}\underbrace{|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|^{2}}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\geq|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|^{2}-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 4\pi^{2}+\left(\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}-1\right)\underbrace{|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|^{2}}_{>0}

\displaystyle>4\pi^{2}.

The length of the geodesic loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}
is thus greater than
2\pi
. This concludes the proof.
∎

In total, we proved the following:

Theorem 5.8
.

Under the standing assumptions that
\beta>2
and
2\leq p\leq n-1
, the length
\ell_{\beta}
of a shortest geodesic loop on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is
\ell_{\beta}=2\pi
.

Proof.

Above, we prove that the length of a geodesic loop on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is at least
2\pi
. From
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Section 6)
, we obtain that there is a geodesic loop on
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with length
2\pi
.
∎

An example of a geodesic loop of length
2\pi
is
\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{3\times 2}}\left(t\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
with
A=0\in\mathrm{Skew}(2)
and
B=\begin{bmatrix}2\pi&0\end{bmatrix}
. It holds

\displaystyle\gamma_{\beta}(1)

\displaystyle=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\begin{bmatrix}2\beta A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}I_{3\times 2}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}((1-2\beta)A)

\displaystyle=I_{3}I_{3\times 2}I_{2}=I_{3\times 2}=\gamma_{\beta}(0)

and
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=\|B\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=2\pi
. This geodesic loop can be embedded in any Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with
2\leq p\leq n-1
by padding the matrices
A
and
B
with zeros.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
\gamma_{\beta}(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{n\times p}}\left(t\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\\
B\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right)
be a geodesic loop on the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
with length
2\pi
, i.e.,
L_{\beta}(\gamma_{\beta})=2\pi
. Then, it holds that
A=0
is zero.

6
Bounds for the sectional curvature

As outlined in equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) from
section

2.2
, the injectivity radius is given by
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))=\min\left\{\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2},\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\right\}
and the conjugate radius is bounded by
\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\geq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}}
. We already calculated the length
\ell_{\beta}
of a shortest nontrivial geodesic loop for all values of
\beta>0
. Next, we provide a positive constant
K_{\beta}
that globally bounds the sectional curvature of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
from above in order to obtain a bound for the conjugate radius. This allows us to make a statement about the injectivity radius.
We exclude the cases
n=2,p=2
and
n=2,p=1
, since here the Stiefel manifold is one-dimensional, so that the concept of sectional curvature does not apply. From
(
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, Theorem 12, Theorem 13, Theorem 14)
,

\displaystyle K_{\beta}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4\beta}&0<\beta\leq\frac{1}{6}(4-\sqrt{10})\approx 0.14...,\\
\frac{4-3\beta}{2}&\frac{1}{6}(4-\sqrt{10})<\beta\leq\frac{2}{3},\\
1&0.71...\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\leq\beta\leq 1.\end{cases}

There is a gap for
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
which is not covered in
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
. In this section, we close this gap.

A formula for the sectional curvature is derived in
(
Nguyen 2022a
, eq. (34))
.
For a
\beta
-orthonormal basis
\{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\}\in T_{I_{n\times p}}\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
of a two-dimensional tangent space section, it reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=&\frac{1}{2}\|B_{1}B_{2}^{\top}-B_{2}B_{1}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{(1-2\beta)^{3}}{2}\|B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\\
&+\beta^{2}\|B_{1}A_{2}-B_{2}A_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\\
&+\frac{\beta}{4}\|[A_{1},A_{2}]-(3-4\beta)(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}.\end{split}

(6.1)

The matrix blocks that form
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
are related by

\|\Delta_{i}\|_{\beta}^{2}=\beta\|A_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=1
for
i=1,2
and

\langle\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\rangle_{\beta}=\beta\mathrm{tr}(A_{1}^{\top}A_{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2})=0
.

Similar to
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, we have the following term-by-term estimates, where
\alpha_{i}\coloneq\|A_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
,
\eta_{i}\coloneq\|B_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\omega\coloneq\|B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
:

•

\frac{1}{2}\|B_{1}B_{2}^{\top}-B_{2}B_{1}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}
,

•

\beta^{2}\|B_{1}A_{2}-B_{2}A_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\eta_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\eta_{2}^{2}\alpha_{1}^{2}+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
,

•

\|[A_{1},A_{2}]\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}
,

•

\|[A_{1},A_{2}]-(3-4\beta)(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1})\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+2(3-4\beta)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\omega+(3-4\beta)^{2}\omega^{2}
.

We start by using the term-by-term estimates and the relation
\eta_{i}^{2}=1-\beta\alpha_{i}^{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}+\frac{(1-2\beta)^{3}}{2}\omega^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(-\beta^{3}\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}\right)+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta}{4}\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}(3-4\beta)^{2}\omega^{2}+\frac{\beta}{4}2(3-4\beta)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\omega,

\displaystyle\begin{split}=&\frac{2-3\beta}{4}\omega^{2}+\frac{2\beta(3-4\beta)}{4}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\omega+\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}\\
&+\left(\frac{\beta}{4}-\beta^{3}\right)\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}\right)+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}.\end{split}

(6.2)

This is a parabola in
\omega
with a downward opening for any value of
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
and, by the matrix inequality of Wu and Chen
Wu and Chen 1988
, we have

\displaystyle\omega=\|B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{2}\|B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\|B_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{2}.

We show that the maximal value of the parabola is bounded above by one and, hence, the sectional curvature is bounded by one, thereby closing the aforementioned gap.
The maximum value of the parabola is attained at
\omega_{\max}\coloneq\frac{\beta(3-4\beta)}{(3\beta-2)}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
, if
\omega_{\max}<\sqrt{2}
, or at
\omega=\sqrt{2}
, if
\omega_{\max}\geq\sqrt{2}
.
Assume that
\omega=\sqrt{2}
. Then, we necessarily have
\|B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|B_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1
and therefore
\|A_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\|A_{2}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0
. In this case, the expression for the upper bound (
6.2
) becomes

\displaystyle~\eqref{eq:upperbound}|_{\omega=\sqrt{2}}=\frac{2-3\beta}{4}\sqrt{2}^{2}+1=\frac{4-3\beta}{2}(<1).

Now, assume that
\omega_{\max}<\sqrt{2}
. Plugging in
\omega_{\max}
into the expression for the upper bound (
6.2
) gives

\displaystyle~\eqref{eq:upperbound}|_{\omega=\omega_{\max}}=\underbrace{\left(\frac{\beta^{2}(3-4\beta)^{2}}{4(3\beta-2)}-\beta^{3}+\frac{\beta}{4}\right)}_{\eqcolon T_{\beta}}\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}+\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}.

(6.3)

Next, we show that (
6.3
) is bounded above by one. Recall that
t_{1}t_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2})
for any
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. By this fact and the relation
\eta_{i}^{2}=1-\beta\alpha_{i}^{2}
, we have:

•

\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2})=1-\frac{\beta}{2}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})\leq 1-\beta\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
,

•

\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}-\beta\right)(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})\leq(\beta^{2}-2\beta)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
, with
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
.

So,

(
6.3
)

\displaystyle\leq T_{\beta}\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}(1-\beta\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})+(1-\beta\alpha_{1}^{2})(1-\beta\alpha_{2}^{2})

\displaystyle=(T_{\beta}+\beta^{2}-\beta^{3})\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}-\beta\right)(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+1

\displaystyle\leq(T_{\beta}+\beta^{2}-\beta^{3})\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+2(\beta^{2}-\beta)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}+1.

This is a parabola in
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
with an upward opening, since
(T_{\beta}+\beta^{2}-\beta^{3})\geq 0
holds for
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
. Therefore, the parabola becomes maximal for
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}
as large or as small as possible. In our case, we have
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\geq 0
and, since
\omega_{\max}<\sqrt{2}
, we have
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}<\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\beta}\frac{3\beta-2}{3-4\beta}
. The value of the parabola at
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}=0
is one. For
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\beta}\frac{3\beta-2}{3-4\beta}
, we show that the value of the parabola is smaller than one. With
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
, it can be shown that this is equivalent to

\displaystyle 0<(4+8\sqrt{2})\beta^{3}-2(1+7\sqrt{2})\beta^{2}+(6\sqrt{2}-2)\beta+1.

It is easy to verify that the cubic polynomial on the right-hand side is positive for any
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
.
Hence, the gap in
(
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, Theorem 12, Theorem 13, Theorem 14)
is filled and combined, we have

Theorem 6.1
.

The sectional curvature of the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
is bounded above by
K_{\beta}>0
with

\displaystyle K_{\beta}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4\beta}&0<\beta\leq\frac{1}{6}(4-\sqrt{10}),\\
\frac{4-3\beta}{2}&\frac{1}{6}(4-\sqrt{10})<\beta\leq\frac{2}{3},\\
1&\frac{2}{3}\leq\beta\leq 1.\end{cases}

For a graphical illustration, see
Figure

1
.

Figure 1:
The value of
K_{\beta}
for
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Remark 6.2
.

For
2\leq p\leq n-2
, the bound on the sectional curvature of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
for
\beta\in(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}})
is sharp and is only attained for tangent sections with zero skew-symmetric
A
-blocks and
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
such that
\|B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0
and
\|B_{1}B_{2}^{\top}-B_{2}B_{1}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{2}
. e.g.,
A_{1}=A_{2}=0
and

\displaystyle B_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\,B_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Of course, the curvature is the same for all matrices of higher dimensions that feature the above examples as subblocks and are otherwise filled up with zeros.

7
Implications for the injectivity radius

Recall the results due to Klingenberg
Klingenberg 1982
, displayed in equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) in
section

2.2
: The injectivity radius is given by

\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))=\min\left\{\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2},\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\right\}

and the conjugate radius is bounded from below by

\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\geq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}}.

We can now exploit the knowledge on the length
\ell_{\beta}
of the shortest geodesic loops and the bound
K_{\beta}
on the sectional curvature gathered in the previous sections in order to determine the exact value for or an upper bound on the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
.

7.1
Case
p=1
or
p\geq n-1

As previously noted, when
p=1
, the Stiefel manifold reduces to the unit sphere as a Riemannian submanifold of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, independently of the parameter
\beta
. The injectivity radius is
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,1))=\pi
; see, e.g.,
(
Cheeger and Ebin 1975
, §1.6)
. In this case, half the length of the shortest geodesic loops coincides with the conjugate radius. Since the geodesics on the sphere are great circles, the shortest geodesic loops have length
2\pi
, and thus
\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2}=\pi
. Moreover, the unit sphere has constant sectional curvature equal to one, implying that the conjugate radius is bounded by
\pi
. By
(
do Carmo 1992
, Chapter 5 Example 3.3)
this bound is attained, so the conjugate radius is exactly
\pi
.

When
p=n\geq 2
, the Stiefel manifold reduces to the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
endowed with the Frobenius metric scaled by
\beta
. From
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Section 6)
, we obtain
\ell_{\beta}=\sqrt{2\beta}2\pi
. This result can alternatively be derived using an argument analogous to that in the proof of
lemma

5.5
.
In the next theorem, we determine the exact value of the conjugate radius of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n)
, for
n>2
.
When
n=2
, the manifold is one-dimensional, and therefore no conjugate points exist and the injectivity radius is immediately given by
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(2,2))=\sqrt{2\beta}\pi
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
n>2
. The conjugate radius of the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(n)
endowed with the Frobenius metric scaled by
\beta
is given by

\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n))=\begin{cases}\sqrt{8\beta}\pi&n=3,\\
\sqrt{4\beta}\pi&n>3.\end{cases}

Therefore, the injectivity radius is given by
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n))=\sqrt{2\beta}\pi
.

Proof.

We begin by establishing a lower bound on the conjugate radius using equation (
2.2
).
For
p=n>2
, the sectional curvature formula (
6.1
) simplifies to
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=\frac{\beta}{4}\|[A_{1},A_{2}]\|_{F}^{2}
, where
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathrm{Skew}(n)
satisfy
\beta\|A_{i}\|_{F}^{2}\leq 1
for
i=1,2
. Invoking the inequality
\|[A_{1},A_{2}]\|_{F}^{2}\leq\|A_{1}\|_{F}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{F}^{2}
from
(
Ge 2014
, Lemma 2.5)
, we obtain
\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})\leq\frac{1}{4\beta}\eqcolon K_{\beta}
.
Consequently, the conjugate radius is bounded below by

\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n))\geq\sqrt{4\beta}\pi.

Next, we describe the relationship between conjugate points and critical points of the matrix exponential. The tangent space at
I_{n}
is given by the set of skew-symmetric matrices
T_{I_{n}}\mathrm{St}(n,n)=\mathrm{Skew}(n)
, and a geodesic starting in
I_{n}
with initial velocity
\widetilde{A}\in\mathrm{Skew}(n)
is given by
\gamma(t)=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(t\widetilde{A})
. Hence, the Riemannian exponential at
I_{n}
takes the form

\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{n}}\colon\mathrm{Skew}(n)\to\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n),\quad\mathrm{Exp}_{\beta,I_{n}}(\widetilde{A})=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(\widetilde{A}).

According to
(
do Carmo 1992
, Chapter 5 Proposition 3.5)
, a point
\gamma(t_{0})
is conjugate to
\gamma(0)=I_{n}
along
\gamma
if and only if the differential
\mathrm{D}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(A)
is rank-deficient, where
A\coloneq t_{0}\widetilde{A}
. The corresponding geodesic distance is
\|A\|_{\beta}=\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}
.
In
(
Deng et al. 2025
, Definition 4.1)
the tangent conjugate locus of
\mathrm{O}(n)
is described. We obtain a specific condition on the eigenvalues of
A
for
\mathrm{D}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(A)
to be rank-deficient.
Let the eigenvalues of
A
be given by
i\theta_{1},-i\theta_{1},\dots,i\theta_{m},-i\theta_{m}
, for
n=2m
; when
n=2m+1
, there is an additional zero eigenvalue.
For
n
even,
\mathrm{D}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(A)
is rank-deficient if and only if there exist
i\neq j
and a nonzero integer
\ell
such that
\theta_{i}\pm\theta_{j}=2\pi\ell
. When
n
is odd, the same condition applies, or alternatively,
\theta_{i}=2\pi\ell
for some
i
and
0\neq\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For
n>3
, consider the skew-symmetric matrix
A
whose upper-left
4\times 4
block is
I_{2}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}0&-\pi\\
\pi&0\end{bmatrix}
with all other entries zeros. This matrix satisfies the above criterion, since
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}=\pi
and
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=2\pi
. Therefore,
A
corresponds to a conjugate point at a geodesic distance of
\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=\sqrt{4\beta}\pi
. Hence, the lower bound on the conjugate radius is sharp and
\mathrm{conj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n))=\sqrt{4\beta}\pi
, for
n>3
.
For
n=3
, the eigenvalues of
A
are
0
and
\pm i\theta_{1}
. For
A
to define a conjugate point, it has to hold
\theta_{1}=2\pi\ell
with
0\neq\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. The corresponding geodesic distance of the conjugate point is
\sqrt{\beta\|A\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}=\sqrt{2\beta\theta_{1}^{2}}=\sqrt{8\beta}\pi|\ell|
. The minimum occurs for
\ell=1
, for instance with
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-2\pi&0\\
2\pi&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}
. Hence, the conjugate radius is given by
\mathrm{conj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(3,3))=\sqrt{8\beta}\pi
.

Since half the length of the shortest geodesic loops is smaller than the conjugate radius in either case, we obtain by equation (
2.1
) that the injectivity radius is given by
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n))=\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2}=\sqrt{2\beta}\pi
.
∎

It is worth noting that the choice
\beta=1
yields the Frobenius inner product, see, e.g.,
(
Guigui et al. 2023
, Example 3.5)
. For
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
, the shortest geodesic loops have the nice-looking length
2\pi
; a choice made, e.g., in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, (3.12))
.

When
p=n-1
, we are able to determine the exact value of the injectivity radius up to
\beta=1
.

Theorem 7.2
.

For the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1)
, it holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1))\begin{cases}=\sqrt{2\beta}\pi&0<\beta<\frac{1}{2},\\
=\pi&\frac{1}{2}\leq\beta\leq 1,\\
\leq\pi&1<\beta.\end{cases}

Proof.

For
\beta>1
, the length of the shortest geodesic loops is given by
\ell_{\beta}=2\pi
in
theorems

3.4
and
5.8
. Hence, by equation (
2.1
), we immediately obtain that the injectivity radius is bounded by
\pi
, i.e.,
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1))\leq\pi
.

For
\beta\leq 1
, in addition to the bounds on the length of the shortest geodesic loops (
theorem

3.4
), we also have bounds on the sectional curvature (
theorem

6.1
), which in turn provide bounds on the conjugate radius. From equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), it follows that the injectivity radius of
\mathrm{St}\beta(n,n-1)
equals half the length of a shortest geodesic loop whenever

\displaystyle\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}}.

(7.1)

It is straightforward to show that this inequality holds for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]\cup[\frac{2}{3},1]
. In Appendix
9
, we derive a sharper bound on the sectional curvature of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1)
for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
, given by
K_{\beta}=\frac{1}{2\beta}
. With this refined bound, inequality (
7.1
) is also satisfied for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
. Consequently, the injectivity radius of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1)
is given by half the length of a shortest geodesic loop,
\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2}
, for all
\beta\in[0,1]
. ∎

7.2
Case
2\leq p\leq n-2

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
2\leq p\leq n-2
and consider the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
. Let
K_{\beta}
be the upper bound on the sectional curvature from
Theorem

6.1
and
\ell_{\beta}
be the length of the shortest geodesic loops from
Theorems

3.4
and
5.8
. Define

\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\begin{cases}=\sqrt{2\beta}\pi&0<\beta\leq\frac{1}{3},\\
\in[\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}},\min\{\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2},\sqrt{2}t_{\beta}^{\mathrm{r}}\}]&\frac{1}{3}<\beta<\frac{2}{3},\\
=\pi&\frac{2}{3}\leq\beta\leq 1,\\
\leq\pi&1<\beta.\end{cases}

Proof.

Analogously to the proof of
Theorem

, we obtain that the injectivity radius is bounded by
\pi
for
\beta>1
and that the injectivity radius is given by half the length of the shortest geodesic loops,
\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2}
, for
\beta\in(0,\frac{1}{3}]\cup[\frac{2}{3},1]
.

For
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
, the bound on the sectional curvature in
Theorem

6.1
is sharp and cannot be improved, unlike in the case
p=n-1
. In this range, the lower bound on the conjugate radius,
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}}
, is smaller than half the length of the shortest geodesic loops,
\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2}
. Hence, we cannot derive the exact value of the injectivity radius from equations (

) and (

) without knowing the exact value of the conjugate radius. From
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Theorem 5.1)
, it follows that
\sqrt{2}t_{\beta}^{r}
is an upper bound on the conjugate radius. Therefore,

\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}}\leq\mathrm{conj}_{I_{n\times p}}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\leq\sqrt{2}t_{\beta}^{r}.

By substituting into equation (

), we obtain
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))\in[\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{K_{\beta}}},\min\{\frac{\ell_{\beta}}{2},\sqrt{2}t_{\beta}^{r}\}]
.
For a visual illustration of the argument, see

For
\beta=1

\pi

Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
.
For
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
, our result provides only an interval estimate for the injectivity radius, while for
\beta>1
we obtain merely an upper bound. This is due to the lack of an explicit expression for the conjugate radius. It is conjectured in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Conj. 8.1)
that the upper bound on the injectivity radius is actually sharp, namely that
\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))=\min\{\sqrt{2\beta}\pi,\pi,\sqrt{2}t_{\beta}^{r}\}
, for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})

\mathrm{inj}(\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p))=\pi

. This conjecture is supported by numerical experiments in

. The experiments employ an algorithm that investigates various geodesics of a given length. For each such geodesic, the algorithm searches for a shorter geodesic connecting the same endpoints. If a shorter geodesic is found, the original geodesic cannot be length-minimizing, implying that the injectivity radius is smaller than its length. By repeating this process for a range of lengths, the injectivity radius is effectively approached from above.
It should be noted that the evidence is more conclusive for
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}
, since in case the algorithm is guaranteed to converge whenever the investigated length exceeds the injectivity radius. For
\beta>\frac{1}{2}
, convergence is not formally ensured, although it is strongly expected in practice.

Note that the Stiefel manifold equipped with the canonical metric (
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
) falls in this case where there is only a conjecture on the injectivity radius. We strengthen the conjecture for the canonical metric—namely, that the upper bound for the injectivity radius is sharp—by providing explicit expressions for all Jacobi fields along a specific geodesic of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta=\frac{1}{2}}(4,2)
in Appendix
10

(n,p)=(4,2)
and
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
, the conjugate points identified in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Theorem 5.1)
are indeed first conjugate points.

In total, the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold under the
\beta
-metrics is known—or conjectured—to coincide with half the length of the shortest geodesic loops for all
\beta
outside the interval
(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
. Resolving the case
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
calls for a refined study of the conjugate radius.

is at least
2\pi
., i.e., equation (
5.16
) holds,

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi k_{j_{a}}|-|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j_{a}}|\geq 2\pi.

First, by
lemma

8.4
, the existence of the geodesic loop
\widehat{\gamma}_{\beta}
leads to the equation (
8.4
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta-1}\frac{\varphi}{2\pi}+\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}\ell_{1}\in[\min_{j}k_{j},\max_{j}k_{j}].

To prove the existence of a
j_{a}\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
such that equation (
5.16
) holds, we assume the contrary and lead this to a contradiction.
So, we assume that

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|-|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|<2\pi

(8.13)

holds for all
j\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
. By the interlacing property of singular values (
lemma

5.6
), we obtain that

\displaystyle|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|<\frac{2\beta}{2\beta-1}|\varphi+2\pi\ell_{j}|\leq|\varphi+2\pi k_{j}|

holds for all
j\in\{1,\dots,s_{1}\}
. Therefore, we are able to apply
lemma

8.5
(for
j=1
) to obtain that
k_{1}=-\ell_{1}-1
, if
\ell_{1}\geq 0
, and
k_{1}=-\ell_{1}
, if
\ell_{1}<0
. Hence, the requirements of
lemma

8.6
are fulfilled and we obtain that the contrary to equation (
8.4
) holds. This gives the desired contradiction and concludes the proof.
∎

9
Bounds on the sectional curvature of the Stiefel manifold under the family of
\beta
-metrics for
p=n-1
and
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})

From
Theorem

6.1
, we obtain bounds on the sectional curvature of the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
. When
p=n-1
, these bounds are not sharp and can be improved. In view of the proof of
Theorem

7.2
, we need sharper bounds for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
in order to determine the injectivity radius for all values of
\beta\in(0,1]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
. The sectional curvature of the Stiefel manifold
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1)
is bounded above by
K_{\beta}\coloneq\frac{1}{2\beta}
.

Proof.

For a
\beta
-orthonormal basis
\{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\}\in T_{I_{n\times(n-1)}}\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,n-1)
of a two-dimensional tangent space section, the matrix blocks are related by

\|\Delta_{i}\|_{\beta}^{2}=\beta\|A_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\|B_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=1
for
i=1,2
and

\langle\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}\rangle_{\beta}=\beta\mathrm{tr}(A_{1}^{\top}A_{2})+\mathrm{tr}(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2})=0
. Furthermore,
B_{1},B_{2}
are
1
-by-
(n-1)
dimensional matrices and therefore
\|B_{1}B_{2}^{\top}-B_{2}B_{1}^{\top}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0
. A reformulation of the formula for the sectional curvature (
6.1
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})=

\displaystyle\frac{2-3\beta}{4}\|B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\beta^{2}\|B_{1}A_{2}-B_{2}A_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta}{4}\|[A_{1},A_{2}]\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{2}(3-4\beta)\mathrm{tr}\left([A_{1},A_{2}]^{\top}(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1})\right).

As explained in
Zimmermann and Stoye 2025
, we have the following term-by-term estimates,
where
\alpha_{i}=\|A_{i}\|_{F}
,
\eta_{i}=\|B_{i}\|_{F}
:

•

\frac{1}{2}\|B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}
,

•

\beta^{2}\|B_{1}A_{2}-B_{2}A_{1}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\eta_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\eta_{2}^{2}\alpha_{1}^{2}+\beta^{2}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
,

•

\|[A_{1},A_{2}]\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leq\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}
,

•

|\frac{\beta}{2}(3-4\beta)\mathrm{tr}\left([A_{1},A_{2}]^{\top}(B_{1}^{\top}B_{2}-B_{2}^{\top}B_{1})\right)|\leq\frac{\beta(3-4\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
.

We start by using the term-by-term estimates and the relation
\alpha_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta}(1-\eta_{i}^{2})
to obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\beta}(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2-3\beta}{2}\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}(\eta_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}\alpha_{1}^{2})

\displaystyle+\frac{\beta}{4}\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}^{2}+\left(\beta^{2}+\frac{\beta(3-4\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}=&\left(\frac{2-3\beta}{2}-\beta+\frac{1}{4\beta}\right)\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{2\beta^{2}-1}{4\beta}\right)(\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2})\\
&+\left(\beta^{2}+\frac{\beta(3-4\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}+\frac{1}{4\beta}.\end{split}

(9.1)

The factor in front of the
\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
-term is positive and the factor in front of the
(\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2})
-term is negative (for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
). Therefore, we may use the estimate
t_{1}t_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2})
and
\alpha_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta}(1-\eta_{i}^{2})
to obtain:

•

\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})\eta_{1}\eta_{2}=\frac{1}{2\beta}(2-(\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2}))\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}(1-\eta_{1}\eta_{2})\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
,

•

\left(\frac{2\beta^{2}-1}{4\beta}\right)(\eta_{1}^{2}+\eta_{2}^{2})\leq\left(\frac{2\beta^{2}-1}{2\beta}\right)\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
.

Hence, we obtain an upper bound on the sectional curvature given by

\displaystyle~\eqref{eq:intermediate_upperbound}\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2-3\beta}{2}-\beta+\frac{1}{4\beta}\right)\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2}+\left(\frac{2\beta^{2}-1}{2\beta}\right)\eta_{1}\eta_{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\beta+\frac{(3-4\beta)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)(\eta_{1}\eta_{2}-\eta_{1}^{2}\eta_{2}^{2})+\frac{1}{4\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\underbrace{\frac{1}{4\beta}\left(2\beta^{2}(7-4\sqrt{2})+2\beta(3\sqrt{2}-2)-1\right)}_{=:a_{\beta}}x^{2}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\beta}\left(2\beta^{2}(2-2\sqrt{2})+3\sqrt{2}\beta-1\right)}_{=:b_{\beta}}x+\frac{1}{4\beta},

for
x\coloneq\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\in[0,1]
. It holds
a_{\beta}>0
for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{3}{2})
. So, this is a parabola
p_{\beta}(x)
in
x
with a downward opening. The parabola attains its maximum value at
x_{\max}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{b_{\beta}}{a_{\beta}}
.
In order to show that the sectional curvatures of
\mathrm{St}_{\beta}(n,p)
are bounded by
\frac{1}{2\beta}
, for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
, it is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle p_{\beta}(x_{\max})=\frac{1}{4\beta}+\frac{1}{4}\frac{b_{\beta}^{2}}{a_{\beta}}\leq\frac{1}{2\beta}.

With
a_{\beta}>0
, we obtain equivalently
b_{\beta}^{2}-\frac{a_{\beta}}{\beta}\leq 0
and it can be shown that this is equivalent a problem of showing that a polynomial of degree
4
is non-positive for
\beta\in(\frac{1}{3},\frac{2}{3})
,

\displaystyle\beta^{4}(48-32\sqrt{2})+\beta^{3}(24\sqrt{2}-48)+\beta^{2}(16\sqrt{2}-4)+\beta(4-12\sqrt{2})+2\leq 0.

One can prove that the polynomial is monotonically decreasing in the
\beta
-interval of interest. Therefore, the quartic polynomial is smaller than its value at the left boundary (
\beta=\frac{1}{3}
), which is
\frac{2}{81}(69-70\sqrt{2})\approx-0.74
. Hence, the quartic polynomial is negative in the
\beta
-interval of interest and the inequality holds true. In total, we obtain that the sectional curvature is bounded above by
K_{\beta}\coloneq\frac{1}{2\beta}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

It is worth noting that this bound on the sectional curvature is not sharp.

10
A first conjugate point on
\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2)

The conjugate radius is given by the minimum of the geodesic distances between the starting point and the first conjugate point of geodesics on the manifold. The Theorem 5.1 in
Absil and Mataigne 2025
gives conjugate points at a geodesic distance of
\sqrt{2}t_{\beta}^{\mathrm{r}}
with
t_{\beta}^{\mathrm{r}}
being the first positive root of
\frac{\sin(t)}{t}+\frac{1-\beta}{\beta}\cos(t)
. This provides an upper bound on the conjugate radius. In this section, we show that the conjugate point found in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Theorem 5.1)
describes a first conjugate point on
\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2)
. This strengthens the conjecture made in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Conj. 8.1)
that the upper bound on the conjugate radius is sharp.
Since the Stiefel manifold is a homogeneous space, we choose the starting point of the geodesic to be at
U\coloneq I_{4\times 2}
. The initial velocity of the geodesic considered in
(
Absil and Mataigne 2025
, Theorem 5.1)
is given by
\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\in T_{I_{4\times 2}}\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2)
, with
A=0
and
B=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}I_{2}
when parameterized by its arc-length.
So, we consider the geodesic
\gamma(t)\coloneq\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4\times 2}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}\right)
.

Conjugate points are characterized as zeros of special vector fields along a geodesic, the so-called Jacobi fields. Jacobi fields, in turn, are determined by an ordinary second-order differential equation. For a geodesic
\gamma
and a tangent vector
W\in T_{\gamma^{\prime}(0)}(T_{U}\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2))
, the associated Jacobi field takes the explicit form of

\displaystyle J(t)\coloneq(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},U})_{t\gamma^{\prime}(0)}(tW),

(10.1)

cf.
(
do Carmo 1992
, Chap. 5, Cor. 2.5)
. By definition
(
do Carmo 1992
, Chap. 5, Def. 3.1)
, a point
\gamma(t_{1})
is conjugate to the starting point
\gamma(0)=U
along the geodesic if there exists a non-zero Jacobi field along
\gamma
such that
J(0)=J(t_{1})=0
.

To determine conjugate points along the geodesic
\gamma
, we need to calculate the directional derivative of the Riemannian exponential on
T_{I_{4\times 2}}\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2)
with base at
t\gamma^{\prime}(0)
in the direction
tW
. We follow the approach of
(
Zimmermann 2020
, Section 4.2)
.
The tangent
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}
is given by the matrices
A
and
B
. Since a tangent space to a vector space can be identified with the vector space itself (see e.g.
(
Bendokat et al. 2020
, p.13)
),
T_{\gamma^{\prime}(0)}(T_{I_{4\times 2}}\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2))\cong T_{I_{4\times 2}}\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2)
, the direction of variation
W\in T_{\gamma^{\prime}(0)}(T_{I_{4\times 2}}\mathrm{St}_{\frac{1}{2}}(4,2))
can be parameterized analogously by some
A_{w}\in\mathrm{Skew}(2)
and
B_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
.
According to the formula for the Riemannian exponential of the Stiefel manifold with respect to the canonical metric

\displaystyle\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4,2}}(t\gamma^{\prime}(0))=\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\left(t\begin{bmatrix}A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}\right)I_{4\times 2},

the calculation of its directional derivative boils down to extracting the first two columns from the directional derivative of the matrix exponential at

t\begin{pmatrix}A&-B^{\top}\\
B&0\end{pmatrix}

in the direction

t\begin{pmatrix}A_{w}&-B_{w}^{\top}\\
B_{w}&0\end{pmatrix}

. Najfeld and Havel
Najfeld and Havel 1995
provide a formula for calculating the directional derivative of the matrix exponential. The directional derivative
D\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(X)[Y]
can be calculated via

\displaystyle\exp_{\mathrm{m}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}X&Y\\
0&X\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(X)&\mathrm{D}\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(X)[Y]\\
0&\exp_{\mathrm{m}}(X)\end{pmatrix}.

(10.2)

This goes by the name of Mathias’ Theorem in
(
Higham 2008
, Theorem 3.6)
.
Next, we explicitly write down a basis of the vector space of Jacobi fields along the geodesic
\gamma
. Those basis elements involve trigonometric functions. The problem of finding the first conjugate point along the geodesic is then reduced to the problem of finding the smallest positive zero of those trigonometric functions.
By a standard result on Jacobi fields, see e.g.
do Carmo 1992
, there are
5=\text{dim}(St(4,2))
linearly independent Jacobi fields along
\gamma
when the starting point
J(0)=0
is fixed. They can be obtained from (
10.1
) by choosing linearly independent directions
W
, see
(
do Carmo 1992
, Chap. 5, Remark 3.2)
. Five linearly independent directions
W_{i}\in T_{U_{0}}St(n,p)
are

\displaystyle W_{1}

\displaystyle=U\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{2}=U^{\perp}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{3}=U^{\perp}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle W_{4}

\displaystyle=U^{\perp}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{5}=U^{\perp}\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Calculating the Jacobi fields via the formula for the directional derivative of the matrix exponential (
10.2
) can be done with the Jordan canonical form. We obtain

\displaystyle J_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4\times 2}})_{t\gamma^{\prime}(0)}[tW_{1}]=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\frac{t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}{2}\\
\frac{t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)}{2}&0\\
0&-\frac{t}{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
\frac{t}{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle J_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4\times 2}})_{t\gamma^{\prime}(0)}[tW_{2}]=\begin{pmatrix}-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&0\\
0&-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&-\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle J_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4\times 2}})_{t\gamma^{\prime}(0)}[tW_{3}]=\begin{pmatrix}-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&0\\
0&-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
-\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle J_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4\times 2}})_{t\gamma^{\prime}(0)}[tW_{4}]=\begin{pmatrix}-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&-t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle J_{5}(t)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{d}\mathrm{Exp}_{\frac{1}{2},I_{4\times 2}})_{t\gamma^{\prime}(0)}[tW_{5}]=\begin{pmatrix}t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&-t\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
-t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)&t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

These Jacobi fields all vanish at
t=0
and are linearly independent. But, there is no conjugate point that can be read of directly. So, we investigate whether there are linear combinations of Jacobi fields that define conjugate points. First, we observe that also the matrices defined by the Jacobi fields at some time
t
are linearly independent as long as all
t
-dependent entries are non-zero. Hence, we are looking for linear combinations of Jacobi fields that vanish at some time
t
where a
t
-dependent entry becomes zero.
All the Jacobi fields
J_{k}
feature the terms
\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
or
\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

in some of their entry-functions.
The term
\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
becomes zero for multiples of
\sqrt{2}\pi
. The term
\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)
becomes zero for
\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi\right)
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. The smallest positive root of the term
\frac{1}{2}\left(t\cos\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\sqrt{2}\sin\left(\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)
that features in
J_{1}
is
t_{1}
, where
t_{1}=\sqrt{2}t_{\frac{1}{2}}^{r}\approx 2.8690968494
. Recall that the geodesic length between the starting point of a geodesic and its first conjugate point is bounded below by the injectivity radius. Since, the injectivity radius of the Stiefel manifold equipped with the canonical metric is bounded below by
\sqrt{\frac{4}{5}}\pi\approx 2.8099258924
, the smallest candidate to look for a linear combination of Jacobi fields vanishing at
t\geq\sqrt{\frac{4}{5}}\pi
is
t_{1}
. Indeed,
J(t)\coloneq J_{1}(t)+\frac{t_{1}}{4\sqrt{2}}(J_{3}(t)-J_{2}(t))
is a Jacobi field along
\gamma
that vanishes at
t_{1}
.
This results in
\gamma
having its first conjugate point at
t_{1}=\sqrt{2}t_{\frac{1}{2}}^{r}
.
