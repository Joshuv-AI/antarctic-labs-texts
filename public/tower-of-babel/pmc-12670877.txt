Spontaneous superior mesenteric vein tear
Authors: Alexandra McMillan, Dustin Williamson, Stefan Iverson, Burt Katubig

Background

Non-traumatic injury of the superior mesenteric vein (SMV) is an extremely rare but potentially fatal injury. Hemoperitoneum from blunt abdominal trauma has been well documented in the literature; however, hemorrhage from a ruptured SMV has been rarely reported. There are case reports describing non-traumatic rupture of the SMV are secondary to patients with underlying conditions such as portal hypertension and hepatic cirrhosis or acute pancreatitis [[1], [2], [3]]. However, our patient did not express any of these findings. It has been documented that intra-abdominal hemorrhage may be due to other rare vascular lesions, such as a spontaneous rupture of mesenteric varices [1]. In this case report, we highlight a rare case of a spontaneous SMV tear in a patient without any underlying comorbidities.

Case presentation

The patient is a 46-year-old female with a past medical history of ADHD and anxiety who presented to the emergency department for worsening abdominal pain. On initial evaluation, the patient's labs were unremarkable and CT of the abdomen and pelvis did not show any acute abnormalities (Fig. 1). The patient's symptoms were attributed to gastritis. She was given a GI cocktail and discharged home. Upon leaving the emergency department, she had several episodes of emesis and became lethargic; therefore, she represented six hours after discharge to the emergency department. On re-presentation, she was hypotensive with pressure of 91/56, tachycardic to 108, hypothermic at 33.4. EKG showed sinus tachycardia and non-specific ST abnormality. Venous blood gas showed significant acidosis with pH 6.79, CO2 56, O2 40, HCO3 8. She subsequently received 2 ampoules of bicarbonate in the emergency department and was actively warmed with a Bair-Hugger. The patient had a significant drop in hemoglobin from 14.7 on initial presentation to 9.7. Two units of pRBCs and TXA were administered in the emergency department. The patient was immediately taken to the CT scanner for a PAN scan to look for sources of bleeding. Repeat CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed a hematoma measuring 9.2 × 7.4 × 12.6 cm with presence of contrast blush along the medial portion of hematoma, possibly communicating with a branch of the SMV (Fig. 2). General surgery was consulted after the CT scan results were obtained. A majority of the patient's past medical history was gathered from a family member as the patient had altered mental status secondary to her severe abdominal pain. She had a history of intermittent abdominal cramping, but the patient's family denied history of any previous abdominal surgeries or blunt trauma between the period of overnight discharge and re-presentation. On the physical exam, her abdomen was moderately distended with a peritoneal sign of involuntary guarding. There were no abrasions, ecchymosis, palpable masses or other signs of trauma. Her GCS was 13 (E3, V4, M6). MTP was initiated due to hypotension. The patient was consented via spouse for an emergent exploratory laparotomy. While preparing for transport to the operating room, she had an episode of bradycardia and apnea with impalpable carotid or femoral pulses. One round of CPR was administered along with IV calcium. Return of spontaneous circulation was obtained. Her GCS had significantly declined to 7 (E2, V1, M4) and the patient was emergently intubated for airway protection. MTP was continued in the operating room, with a total of 11-units pRBCs, 8-units FFP, 1-unit cryoprecipitates, 1-unit platelets transfused.

CT abdomen and pelvis on initial presentation. No acute abnormality was identified given the history of abdominal pain.

CT abdomen and pelvis shows a right anterior pararenal space hematoma measuring 9.2 × 7.4 × 12.6 cm (blue arrow) with presence of contrast blush along the mesial portion of hematoma (red arrow), possibly communicating with a branch of the superior mesenteric vein.

The patient underwent an exploratory laparotomy, transverse colon resection, left in discontinuity, suture ligation/repair of mesenteric vessels and temporary abdominal closure with an Abthera. In the OR, there was a large volume hemoperitoneum immediately evacuated. All four quadrants were packed. There was active bleeding at the root of the small bowel mesentery and a large right abdominal retroperitoneal hematoma. The mesentery of the hepatic flexure was extremely edematous and ecchymotic. This portion did not appear viable and therefore, the hepatic flexure was resected and left in discontinuity, which indirectly improved the exposure to the root of the small bowel mesentery. The hemorrhage appeared to stem just right of midline, which was concerning for an IVC tear. It was difficult to gain exposure due to brisk bleeding; therefore, pressure was applied, and vascular surgery was consulted intra-operatively. The vascular surgeon was able to isolate the bleed, which appeared to originate from a proximal branch of the SMV. The tear was roughly 1 cm and primarily repaired with 5–0 prolene suture. The colon was left in discontinuity and an Abthera device was placed. After the case, the patient was hemodynamically stable off pressors and not requiring any additional blood products. She remained intubated and transferred to the ICU in stable condition.

The following day, the patient underwent re-exploration to re-establish continuity with abdominal closure. Upon abdominal entry, there was no evidence of continued hemorrhage from previous primary repair of the SMV or the mesenteric root at the previous operation. The small bowel and large bowel were inspected without any evidence of bowel necrosis. Due to the minimum length of ascending colon, an ileocecectomy was performed and the abdomen was closed. The patient was extubated in the operating room and transferred back to the ICU for further monitoring. The patient had an uncomplicated postoperative course and continued to progress with pain control, tolerating a diet and ambulation. The patient was discharged on postoperative day six from her initial procedure.

Discussion

SMV rupture is rare and can be associated with cirrhosis, pancreatitis and trauma. In a case report published in 1992, non-traumatic rupture of the SMV with hemoretroperitoneum was discovered on autopsy along with acute pancreatitis and thrombosis of the superior mesenteric artery [2]. From the histological findings, the acute pancreatitis appeared to damage the SMV wall externally, while the superior mesenteric artery thrombosis and the anomalous termination of the inferior mesenteric vein contributed to portal venous system overload and eventual rupture [2]. Another case of massive and fatal intraperitoneal hemorrhage occurred from ruptured superior mesenteric varices secondary to portal hypertension and hepatic cirrhosis, confirmed by postmortem angioradiography [3]. Published in 1985, this was the second case to be reported in literature to highlight the possibility of hemorrhagic complications of portal hypertension [3]. Compared to the case reports above, our case is unique as there are no underlying risk factors present in this patient for a SMV rupture that have been currently identified in the literature. An intraoperative cause of a SMV tear could be due to retraction, but this is unlikely in this case as there was already an identified hemorrhage stemming from the mesenteric root seen on imaging. In our case, the patient's intractable emesis was the only differentiating variable between her initial presentation and representation. Further research on risk factors for non-traumatic spontaneous SMV rupture might shed light on the etiology of this case.
