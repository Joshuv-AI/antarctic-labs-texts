Random vectors in the presence of a single big jump
Dimitrios G. Konstantinides, Charalampos D. Passalidis

Abstract
. Multidimensional distributions with heavy tails have recently attracted the attention of several papers on Applied Probability. About multivariate subexponentiality we can find several approximations, but none of them have been widely established. Having in mind the single big jump principle, and further the multivariate subexponentiality suggested by Samorodnitsky and Sun (2016), we introduce the multivariate long, dominatedly and consistently varying distribution classes. We examine the closure properties of these classes with respect to the product convolution, the scale mixture and the convolution of multivariate distributions. Additionally, for two distributions from the class of multivariate long tailed distributions, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions in order their convolution to belong to the class of multivariate subexponential distributions. Furthermore, we study the multivariate, linear, single big jump principle in finite and in random sums of random vectors, permitting some dependence structures, which contain the independence as a special case. Finally, we present an application on the asymptotic behavior of the entrance probability of the discounted aggregate claims into some rare sets, in a risk model, with a common Poisson counting process, financial factors and independent, identically distributed claims, with common multivariate subexponential distribution.



Keywords: multivariate heavy-tailed distributions; closure properties; sums of random vectors; multivariate linear single big jump; risk model

: Primary 62P05 ; Secondary 60G70.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation.

On the one hand side, it is known that the distributions with heavy tails play a crucial role in applied probability; see for example
[
24
]
,
[
41
]
,
[
3
]
among others. On the other hand side, the dependence modeling does not play a secondary role in the practical applications. Thus, in several branches of applied probability, as in risk theory and in risk management, emerges intensive study of multidimensional models through random vectors; see for example
[
59
]
,
[
55
]
,
[
58
]
, among others. In these papers we find some multidimensional distribution classes with heavy tails, which represent the multivariate regular variation, symbolically
MRV
, and some of these papers study both
MRV
and some type of multivariate Gumbel distributions.

However, although the multivariate regular variation class is well established; see
[
31
]
and
[
67
]
for introduction and significant treatments, it is somehow restrictive with respect to its distribution range. Indeed, an
MRV
vector does not contain some moderately heavy-tailed marginal distributions, because of the regular variation of the marginals. Furthermore, in the case of multivariate Gumbel distributions, the restriction comes from the common normalization function, which divides each component and also excludes the dominatedly varying marginal distributions. Similar problems in the
MRV
are handled through non-standard
MRV
.

Therefore, these multivariate classes fit better to extreme value theory, but are not sufficient alone for applications on insurance, finance and queuing theory. Thus, recently, several papers have appeared focused on multidimensional distribution classes with heavy tails, with the ultimate aim of the multivariate subexponentiality.

We find at least six different definitions of the multivariate subexponentiality, where we can distinguish two major directions:

(1)

multivariate, non-linear, single, big jump principle: In
[
46
]
were introduced some classes of distributions with heavy tails, in two-dimensional set up, imposing that their subexponentiality satisfies some kind of max-sum equivalence in the two dimensions. The way of definition provides some kind of relation to the dimension of random vectors, as also to the flexibility with respect to the convergence speed of the marginal tails. However, the properties of such distributions are not connected immediately with the corresponding one-dimensional properties. A similar approach can be found in
[
76
]
, where some smaller classes of this kind were introduced.

(2)

multivariate, linear, single, big jump principle: The papers
[
18
]
,
[
65
]
and
[
70
]
are focused on a kind of single big jump through five different definitions of subexponentiality. These ways of definition contain some kind of ’insensitivity with respect to dimension’ in relation to the single big jump principle, while the same time it seems to keep the corresponding one-dimensional properties, in some cases, that is not the case for direction (1).

In the current paper we follow the direction (2). In first look of these three papers, we find that all of these approaches have a variety of applications. With the first one,
[
36
]
was considered the compound Poison process, where the subexponential and convolution equivalent densities are suggested. The second one, examines mostly the asymptotic behavior of randomly stopped sums of random vectors and consequently of also Lévy processes; see for example
[
20
]
,
[
38
]
. However, these two approaches present a handicap against the third one, where found some good properties, like the preservation of the closure properties of the classes as we step from the one-dimensional to multidimensional set up, or the property for any linear combination of the components of the multivariate subexponential random vector, to have one-dimensional subexponential distribution. In spite of these good properties from
[
70
]
, it did not attract the attention it deserved. Hence, having in mind some applications on risk theory, we suggest some other distribution classes with heavy tails, through a similar way, in order to study the closure properties, the behavior of the sums of random vectors (with distributions on these classes), and some applications on risk theory.

The paper is organized as follows. In subsection
1.2
we give some basic concepts of the one-dimensional distributions with heavy tails. In section
2
after reminding the known multidimensional classes of heavy-tailed distributions, and we introduce three new classes in a multidimensional context, together with some preliminary results. In section 3, we study these new classes, and also the multivariate subexponential class, with respect to some closure properties, as the convolution, the product convolution, the scale mixture and the finite mixture. In section 4, we consider the asymptotic behavior of the sums of random vectors, with the summands belonging to these classes, and with random vectors that are not necessarily independent. We also consider the asymptotic behavior of randomly stopped sums of random vectors. These results highlights the multivariate linear single big jump principle on, the finite, and on the randomly stopped, sums, respectively while such results were available only in
MRV
case. In section 5, we take into account the asymptotic behavior of the entrance probability of the discounted aggregate claims, into some rare sets, in a multidimensional risk model with a common stochastic discount factor and a Poisson counting process, when the claim distributions belong to the class of multivariate subexponential distributions.

1.2.
Heavy-tailed distributions

In this section we present some basic definitions of distribution classes with heavy tails, after a short survey of the notations, used further in the whole paper.

For any two positive functions
f,\,g
, we denote by
f(x)\sim g(x)
,
f(x)=o[g(x)]
and
f(x)=O[g(x)]
, as
x\to\infty
, if

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=1\,,\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0\,,\quad\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}<\infty\,,

hold respectively, while we write
f(x)\asymp g(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, if both
f(x)=O[g(x)]
and
g(x)=O[f(x)]
, as
x\to\infty
hold. If the
d
-dimensional, positive functions
{\bf f},\,{\bf g}
are of the form
{\bf f}(x\,A),\,{\bf g}(x\,A)
, for some
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with
{\bf 0}\notin\overline{A}
, where
\overline{A}
represents the closed case of
A
, then the previous asymptotic relations in one dimension can be generalized in
d
dimensions, for example
{\bf f}(x\,A)\sim{\bf g}(x\,A)
, as
x\to\infty
, implies
\lim_{x\to\infty}{\bf f}(x\,A)/{\bf g}(x\,A)=1
. For two
d
-dimensional, with
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, vectors
{\bf X},\,{\bf Y}
, with
{\bf X}=(X_{1},\,\ldots,\,X_{d})^{\top}
,
{\bf Y}=(Y_{1},\,\ldots,\,Y_{d})^{\top}
(where
{\bf X}^{\top}
denotes the transpose of
{\bf X}
) the operations hold by components, hence they take the form
{\bf X}+{\bf Y}=(X_{1}+Y_{1},\,\ldots,\,X_{d}+Y_{d})^{\top}
and
{\bf X}\,{\bf Y}=(X_{1}\,Y_{1},\,\ldots,\,X_{d}\,Y_{d})^{\top}
. Further the scalar product is defined for some
\lambda>0
as
\lambda\,{\bf X}=(\lambda\,X_{1},\,\ldots,\,\lambda\,X_{d})^{\top}
, with
{\bf 0}
the zero vector. For two real numbers
x
,
y
, we denote
x\vee y:=\max\{x,\,y\}
,
x\wedge y:=\min\{x,\,y\}
, and
\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor
is the integer part of
x
.

For any one-dimensional distribution
V
, we denote by
\overline{V}(x)=1-V(x)
its tail. In the case that the random variable (or, vector)
X
follows distribution
V
, we denote
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}V
. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two random variables with distributions
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, and denote the tail of their sum by
\overline{V}_{X_{1}+X_{2}}(x)=\mathbf{P}[X_{1}+X_{2}>x]
. When
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent, this tail can be expressed as convolution tail
\overline{V_{1}*V_{2}}(x)
. Further, we denote by
V_{X_{1}\vee X_{2}}(x)
the distribution of the maximum, namely
V_{X_{1}\vee X_{2}}(x)=\mathbf{P}[X_{1}\vee X_{2}\leq x]
. We say that two distributions
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
are strongly tail equivalent, if holds
\overline{V}_{1}(x)\sim c\,\overline{V}_{2}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, for some
c\in(0,\,\infty)
, while we say that they are weakly tail equivalent, if
\overline{V}_{1}(x)\asymp\overline{V}_{2}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
.

For reasons of consistency, we give the following definitions, only in the case where
V
have support on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}=[0,\,\infty)
, although most of these classes can be extended immediately with support on
{\mathbb{R}}=(-\infty,\infty)
. For all distributions with heavy tails we keep in mind that the condition
\overline{V}(x)>0
, for any
x\geq 0
, is valid.

We say that the distribution
V
has heavy tail, symbolically
V\in\mathcal{K}
, if it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\varepsilon\,x}\,V(dx)=\infty\,,

for any
\varepsilon>0
. A big subclass of the class
\mathcal{K}
is the class
\mathcal{L}
, of long-tailed distributions. We say that the distribution
V
has long tail, symbolically
V\in\mathcal{L}
, if it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{V}(x-y)}{\overline{V}(x)}=1\,,

for any (or, equivalently, for some) constant
y>0
. It is well-known that if
V\in\mathcal{L}
, then there exists a function
a\;:\;[0,\,\infty)\to(0,\,\infty)
, named insensitivity function of
V
, such that
a(x)\to\infty
,
a(x)=o(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, and

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\sup_{|y|\leq a(x)}\left|\dfrac{\overline{V}(x-y)}{\overline{V}(x)}-1\right|=0\,,

(1.1)

see for example
[
26
]
,
[
41
]
. The most popular class of heavy-tailed distributions is the class of subexponential distributions. We say that the distribution
V
is subexponential, symbolically
V\in\mathcal{S}
, if it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{V^{n*}}(x)}{\overline{V}(x)}=n\,,

for any (or, equivalently, for some) integer
n\geq 2
, where the
V^{n*}
represents the
n
-th fold convolution of distribution
V
with itself.

These three distribution classes were introduced in
[
16
]
, where it was established that
\mathcal{S}\subsetneq\mathcal{L}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}
; see
[
16
, Th. 2]
. For applications of subexponentiality in actuarial science and finance we refer to

[
24
]
,
[
2
]
and
[
41
]
.

The distribution class of dominatedly varying distributions was introduced in
[
25
]
. We say that the distribution
V
is dominatedly varying, symbolically
V\in\mathcal{D}
, if it holds

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{V}(b\,x)}{\overline{V}(x)}<\infty\,,

for any (or, equivalently, for some)
b\in(0,\,1)
. It is well-known that
\mathcal{D}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}
,
\mathcal{D}\not\subseteq\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}\not\subseteq\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
; see for example in
[
30
]
. Another even smaller than
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}
is class
\mathcal{C}
of the consistently varying distribution, introduced by
[
17
]
and
[
19
]
. We say that the distribution
V
is consistently varying, symbolically
V\in C
, if

\displaystyle\lim_{b\uparrow 1}\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{V}(b\,x)}{\overline{V}(x)}=1\,.

One of the smallest, but important, heavy-tailed distribution classes is that of regularly varying distributions, introduced by
[
37
]
. We say that the distribution
V
is regularly varying with index
\alpha\in(0,\,\infty)
, symbolically
V\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
, if it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{V}(t\,x)}{\overline{V}(x)}=t^{-\alpha}\,,

for any
t>0
. For more properties in this class see in
[
6
]
. It is well-known that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\alpha>0}\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}\subsetneq\mathcal{C}\subsetneq\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}\subsetneq\mathcal{S}\subsetneq\mathcal{L}\subsetneq\mathcal{K}\,.

(1.2)

Let close this section with the upper Matuszewska index of a distribution
V
, defined as follows

\displaystyle J_{V}^{+}=-\lim_{v\to\infty}\dfrac{\log\overline{V}_{*}(v)}{\log v}\,,

where
\overline{V}_{*}(v)=\liminf_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{V}(v\,x)}{\overline{V}(x)}
. It is well known that
V\in\mathcal{D}
if and only if
J_{V}^{+}<\infty
, and if
V\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
then
J_{V}^{+}=\alpha
. Further, an important inequality related with the upper Matuszewska index, is the Potter inequality. Namely, if
V\in\mathcal{D}
, then for any
p>J_{V}^{+}
there exist two positive constants
C_{1}
and
x_{0}
, such that for any
x\geq y\geq x_{0}
it holds

\displaystyle\dfrac{\overline{V}(y)}{\overline{V}(x)}\leq C_{1}\,\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)^{p}\,.

(1.3)

We refer the reader to
[
6
, Sec. 2.1]
,
[
51
, Sec. 2.4]
, for further details about
J_{V}^{+}
, and related indexes.

2.
Multivariate heavy-tailed distributions

In this section, we remind some definitions of multivariate distributions with heavy tails and precisely the standard
MRV
and the class
\mathcal{S_{R}}
of multivariate subexponential distributions. Next, we introduce three new distribution classes with heavy tails in multidimensional set up, namely the
\mathcal{D_{R}}
of multivariate dominatedly varying distributions, the
\mathcal{L_{R}}
of multivariate long-tailed distributions and the
\mathcal{C_{R}}
of multivariate consistently varying distributions. Additionally, we provide some initial results for these classes, related with their ordering and some closure properties with respect to their ’tail equivalence’. All the random vectors have distribution support on the
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}:=[0,\,\infty)^{d}
.

Definition of
MRV
was given in
[
31
]
. We say that a random vector
{\bf X}
follows standard
MRV
, if there exist a Radon measure
\mu
, non-degenerate to zero, and a distribution
V\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
, with
\alpha\in(0,\,\infty)
, such that it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{\overline{V}(x)}\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,{\mathbb{B}}\right]=\mu({\mathbb{B}})\,,

(2.1)

for any Borel set
{\mathbb{B}}\subseteq[0,\,\infty]^{d}
, with
{\bf 0}\notin\overline{{\mathbb{B}}}
, which is
\mu
-continuous. The measure
\mu
, has the property of homogeneity, that means it holds

\displaystyle\mu\left(\lambda^{1/\alpha}\,{\mathbb{B}}\right)=\lambda^{-1}\,\mu({\mathbb{B}})\,,

for
\lambda>0
and any
\mu
-continuous Borel set
{\mathbb{B}}\subseteq[0,\,\infty]^{d}
, with
{\bf 0}\notin\overline{{\mathbb{B}}}
. If
{\bf X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}F
belongs to
MRV
distributions, then we write
F\in MRV(\alpha,\,V,\,\mu)
.

This class of distributions has been used in the modeling of several problems in applied probability and mathematical statistics; see for example
[
5
]
,
[
69
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
63
]
for the importance of this class in heavy-tailed Time Series, Risk theory and Risk management.

Let us consider a family of sets, represented as

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}:=\left\{A\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\;:\;A\;\text{open,\;increasing},\,A^{c}\;\text{convex},\,{\bf 0}\notin\overline{A}\right\}\,,

where by
A^{c}
we denote the complementary set of
A
, and a set
A
is called ’increasing’ if for
{\bf x}\in A
and any
{\bf a}\in[0,\,\infty)^{d}
, then it holds
{\bf x}+{\bf a}\in A
. We should remark, that the set
\mathscr{R}
represents a cone, with respect to positive scalar multiplication; namely, if
A\in\mathscr{R}
, then for any
\lambda>0
, we have
\lambda\,A\in\mathscr{R}
.

From
[
70
, Lem. 4.3]
we find that for fixed
A\in\mathscr{R}
, there exists some set
I_{A}\subsetneq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle A=\left\{{\bf x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\;:\;{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf x}=p_{1}\,x_{1}+\cdots+p_{d}\,x_{d}>1\,,\;\exists\;{\bf p}\in I_{A}\right\}\,,

and further for some fixed
A\in\mathscr{R}
and
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}=(-\infty,\,\infty)^{d}
there exists some
u_{1}>0
, such that it holds

\displaystyle(u+u_{1})\,A\subsetneq u\,A+{\bf a}\subsetneq(u-u_{1})\,A\,,

(2.2)

for all
u>u_{1}
.

Furthermore, from
[
70
, Lem. 4.5]
, we have that for some random vector
{\bf X}
with distribution
F
, we can define the random variable
Y_{A}:=\sup\{u\;:\;{\bf X}\in u\,A\}
, following the proper distribution
F_{A}
, whose tail has the following representation

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{A}(x)=\mathbf{P}[{\bf X}\in x\,A]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf X}>x\right]\,,\qquad\forall\;x>0\,.

(2.3)

In such a case, we say that the random vector
{\bf X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}F
has a multivariate subexponential distribution on
A\in\mathscr{R}
, symbolically
F\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
, if
F_{A}\in\mathcal{S}
. Next, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{S_{R}}:=\bigcap_{A\in\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{S}_{A}\,.

The class
\mathcal{S_{R}}
of multivariate subexponential distributions, introduced in
[
70
]
.

Let us notice, that the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}:=\left\{{\bf x}\;:\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}l_{i}\,x_{i}>c\right\}\,,

(2.4)

for some
c>0
,
l_{1},\,\ldots,\,l_{d}\geq 0
, with
l_{1}+\cdots+l_{d}=1
, belongs to
\mathscr{R}
. Hence, if
F\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathscr{R}}
through relation (
2.3
), it follows that all the non-negative, non-degenerate to zero linear combinations of the components of
{\bf X}
have one-dimensional subexponential distribution.

Following this definition of subexponentiality, we introduce four distribution classes with heavy tails, in multidimensional set up.

Definition 2.1
.

Let us consider some fixed
A\in\mathscr{R}
and some random vector
{\bf X}
following distribution
F
. We say that
{\bf X}
has

(1)

multivariate consistently varying distribution on
A
, symbolically
F\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
, if
F_{A}\in\mathcal{C}
.

(2)

multivariate dominatedly varying distribution on
A
, symbolically
F\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
, if
F_{A}\in\mathcal{D}
.

(3)

multivariate long tail on
A
, symbolically
F\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, if
F_{A}\in\mathcal{L}
.

(4)

multivariate dominatedly varying, long tail on
A
, symbolically
F\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
, if
F_{A}\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}
.

For these last classes we introduce the notations

\displaystyle\mathcal{C_{\mathscr{R}}}:=\bigcap_{A\in\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{C}_{A}\,,\qquad\mathcal{D_{\mathscr{R}}}:=\bigcap_{A\in\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{D}_{A}\,,\qquad\mathcal{L_{\mathscr{R}}}:=\bigcap_{A\in\mathscr{R}}\mathcal{L}_{A}\,,\quad\mathcal{(D\cap L)_{\mathscr{R}}}:=\bigcap_{A\in\mathscr{R}}(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}\,,

as the multivariate
\mathcal{C},\,\mathcal{D},\,\mathcal{L},\,\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}
over the whole
\mathscr{R}
, respectively.

Remark 2.1
.

It follows directly from the comments in Subsection
1.2
and by the definitions of these concrete multivariate classes, that, for any fixed
A\in\mathscr{R}
,
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}=(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{S})_{A}
(in the sense
F\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{S})_{A}
if
F_{A}\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{S})
) and further we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{A}\subsetneq(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}\subsetneq\mathcal{S}_{A}\subsetneq\mathcal{L}_{A}\,.

(2.5)

The same inclusions hold for the corresponding classes over
\mathscr{R}
. Now, from relations (
2.3
) and (

) we find that if
F\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathscr{R}}
, with
\mathcal{B}\in\{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}\}
, then the non-negative and non-degenerate to zero linear combinations of the components of

, let say the

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}l_{i}\,X_{i}\,,

have distribution which belongs to one-dimensional

[

[

Remark 2.2
.

Later, in remark
5.1
, we present some possible forms of set
A
, related with ruin probabilities. The following form
A=\{{\bf x}\;:\;x_{i}>b_{i},\;\forall\;i=1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
depicts, in some sense, the ruin set, where the
d
-lines of business fail all together. However, we observe that
A\notin\mathscr{R}
, when
d\geq 2
. Indeed, for the membership to
\mathscr{R}
, the complement set
A^{c}
should be convex. Let assume that
{\bf x},\,{\bf y}\in A^{c}
, namely they have the form

\displaystyle\{{\bf x}\;:\;x_{i}\leq b_{i},\;\exists\;i=1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,\qquad\{{\bf y}\;:\;y_{i}\leq b_{i},\;\exists\;i=1,\,\ldots,\,d\}\,,

with some integer
d\geq 2
, then their convex combination
\lambda\,x_{i}+(1-\lambda)y_{i}
, is not necessarily smaller or equal to
b_{i}
for some
i=1,...,d
, and any
\lambda\in[0,\,1]
. Hence, set
A^{c}
is not convex. For this ruin probability of all the lines of business, the distribution classes from
[
46
]
and
[
76
]
, fit better, as they are defined according the common tail behavior.

The next result provides insight with respect to ordering of the multivariate classes.

Proposition 2.1
.

MRV(\alpha,\,V,\,\mu)\subsetneq\mathcal{C_{\mathscr{R}}}
, for
\alpha\in(0,\,\infty)
.

Proof.

Let
F\in MRV(\alpha,\,V,\,\mu)
, for some
\alpha\in(0,\,\infty)
. For arbitrarily chosen
A\in\mathscr{R}
, taking into account
[
70
, proof of Prop. 4.14]
, we obtain
\mu(\partial A)=0
, and
\mu(A)\in(0,\,\infty)
. Hence, from relation (
2.1
) we get

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{\overline{V}(x)}\,\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}>x\right]=\mu(A)\,.

From the fact that
V\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}
, for some
\alpha\in(0,\,\infty)
and by the closure property of regular variation with respect to strong tail equivalence; see
[
51
, Prop. 3.3(i)]
, it is implied that
F_{A}\in\mathcal{R}_{-\alpha}\subsetneq\mathcal{C}
, as follows from (
1.2
). Therefore,
F\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
, and by the arbitrary choice of
A\in\mathscr{R}
, we conclude that
F\in\mathcal{C_{\mathscr{R}}}
.

and
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
.

Next, we assume, that there are two arbitrarily dependent random vectors
{\bf X}
,
{\bf Y}
with distributions
F
,
G
respectively, namely
F(x\,A)=\mathbf{P}[{\bf X}\in x\,A]
,
G(x\,A)=\mathbf{P}[{\bf Y}\in x\,A]
, for
x\geq 0
, and we explore some closure properties with respect to the asymptotic equivalence of these distributions, in the frame of the newly introduced distribution classes. The following properties comply with the corresponding one-dimensional properties.

Proposition 2.2
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
, and consider two arbitrarily dependent random vectors
{\bf X}
,
{\bf Y}
with distributions
F
,
G
respectively.

(i)

If
F\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F(x\,A)}{G(x\,A)}=c\,,

(2.7)

for some
c\in(0,\,\infty)
, then
G\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

(ii)

If
F\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
and

\displaystyle 0<\liminf_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F(x\,A)}{G(x\,A)}\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F(x\,A)}{G(x\,A)}<\infty\,,

(2.8)

then
G\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

(iii)

If
F\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
and
(
2.7
) is valid for some
c\in(0,\,\infty)
, then
G\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
.

(iv)

If
F\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
and
(
2.7
) is valid for some
c\in(0,\,\infty)
, then
G\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
.

Proof.

Let us consider the random variable
Y_{A}^{\prime}:=\sup\{u\;:\;{\bf Y}\in u\,A\}
, with distribution
F_{A}^{\prime}
.

(i)

By relation (
2.7
), since
\overline{F}_{A}(x)\sim c\,\overline{F}_{A}^{\prime}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, and by the closure property of class
\mathcal{C}
with respect to strong tail equivalence; see for example
[
51
, Prop. 3.5(i)]
, we find that
F_{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
and therefore
G\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

(ii)

By relation (
2.8
), with the help of relation (
2.3
), we obtain
\overline{F}_{A}(x)\asymp\overline{F}_{A}^{\prime}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, and by closure property of class
\mathcal{D}
with respect to weak tail equivalence; see for example
[
51
, Prop. 3.7(i)]
, we find that
F_{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
and therefore
G\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

(iii)

By relation (
2.7
), we obtain
\overline{F}_{A}(x)\sim c\,\overline{F}_{A}^{\prime}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, and by the closure property of class
\mathcal{L}
with respect to strong tail equivalence; see for example
[
51
, Prop. 3.9(i)]
, we find that
F_{A}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
and therefore
G\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
.

(iv)

It follows immediately by the two previous parts and the fact that (
2.7
) is included in (
2.8
). ∎

The way we introduced class
\mathcal{L}_{A}
, allows us to find an insensitivity function for the asymptotic behavior of random vectors belonging to this class. Namely, if

, then

and hence there exists some insensitivity function

, such that (

) holds, and hence by (

) we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\sup_{|y|\leq a(x)}\left|\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in(x-y)\,A\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]}-1\right|=0\,.

In next result we find another property of distribution class
\mathcal{L}_{A}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

If we assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set and
F\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, then it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F(x\,A+{\bf a})}{F(x\,A)}=1\,,

(2.9)

for any
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

Let us consider initially
{\bf a}={\bf 0}
, then relation (
2.9
) follows immediately. Now, we consider that
{\bf a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
. Then by relation (
2.2
), there exists some
u_{1}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F(x\,A+{\bf a})}{F(x\,A)}\leq\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F[(x-u_{1})\,A]}{F(x\,A)}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}>x-u_{1}\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}>x\right]}=1\,,

(2.10)

where obviously the inequality
x>u_{1}
holds, since
x\to\infty
, and in the last step we used the property of class
\mathcal{L}
. From the other side, using the first inclusion of (
2.2
) and the property of class
\mathcal{L}
we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F(x\,A+{\bf a})}{F(x\,A)}\geq\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{F[(x+u_{1})\,A]}{F(x\,A)}=\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}>x+u_{1}\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}>x\right]}=1\,,

(2.11)

and so through relations (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) we conclude (
2.9
).
∎

Note that (
2.9
) does not imply necessarily membership in
\mathcal{L}_{A}
.
Next, we generalize the
[
70
, Lem. 4.9]
, and the proof is very similar. Let us adopt the following notation

\displaystyle{\bf X}^{(i)}=\left(X_{1}^{(i)},\,\ldots,\,X_{d}^{(i)}\right)^{\top},\,{\bf S}_{n}={\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+{\bf X}^{(n)},\,Y_{A}^{(i)}:=\sup\left\{u\;:\;{\bf X}^{(i)}\in u\,A\right\}\,,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{Y_{A}^{(1)}+\cdots+Y_{A}^{(n)}}(x)=\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}+\cdots+Y_{A}^{(n)}>x\right],\;F_{{\bf S}_{n}}(xA):=\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+{\bf X}^{(n)}\in xA\right]\,,

for
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
, with
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where the random vectors
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
are arbitrarily dependent and not necessarily identically distributed.

Proposition 2.4
.

For any fixed set
A\in\mathscr{R}
, it holds

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{Y_{A}^{(1)}+\cdots+Y_{A}^{(n)}}(x)\geq F_{{\bf S}_{n}}(x\,A)\,,

(2.13)

for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
x\geq 0
.

Proof.

From relation (
2.3
) for
x\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle F_{{\bf S}_{n}}(x\,A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+{\bf X}^{(n)}\in xA\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\left({\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+{\bf X}^{(n)}\right)>x\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf X}^{(n)}>x\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}+\cdots+Y_{A}^{(n)}>x\right]\,,

and now from (

Closure properties

It is well known that the closure properties of heavy-tailed distributions can be useful in applications; see for example
[
51
]
for the one-dimensional distributions and
[
34
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
21
]
for the closure properties of
MRV
. In this section, we provide some closure properties of the multidimensional distribution classes, previously introduced, as also for multivariate subexponential distributions.

3.1.
Product convolution and scale mixture.

Here, we study the closure properties of class
\mathcal{D}_{A}
with respect to product convolution of random vectors, and the closure properties of classes
\mathcal{L}_{A}
,
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
,
\mathcal{C}_{A}
,
\mathcal{S}_{A}
with respect to scale mixture.

In case of two random vectors
{\bf\Theta}=(\Theta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\Theta_{d})^{\top}
and
{\bf X}=(X_{1},\,\ldots,\,X_{d})^{\top}
, the Hadamard product of random vectors is defined component-wise, as
{\bf\Theta}\,{\bf X}=(\Theta_{1}\,X_{1},\,\ldots,\,\Theta_{d}\,X_{d})^{\top}
. To avoid the trivial cases, we assume that the
\Theta_{1},\,\ldots,\,\Theta_{d}
are non-degenerate to zero. For the previous product we define the random variable

\displaystyle Z_{A}:=\sup\{u\;:\;{\bf\Theta}\,{\bf X}\in u\,A\}\,,

with distribution
H_{A}
. In the special case of
{\bf\Theta}=\Theta\,{\bf 1}
, we find the scale mixture
\Theta\,{\bf X}=(\Theta\,X_{1},\,\ldots,\,\Theta\,X_{d})^{\top}
, for which we define the following random variable

\displaystyle M_{A}:=\sup\{u\;:\;\Theta\,{\bf X}\in u\,A\}\,,

(3.1)

with distribution
G_{A}
. Let us remind that all the random vectors have non-negative components. The next result shows a stability property of class
\mathcal{D}_{A}
, with respect to product convolution.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{R}
be a fixed set, and
{\bf X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}F\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
. If
{\bf\Theta}
and
{\bf X}
are independent, and
\mathbf{E}[\Theta_{i}^{p}]<\infty
, for some
p>J_{F_{A}}^{+}
, and any
i=1,\,\ldots,\,d
, then for the product convolution
{\bf\Theta\,\bf X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}H
, it holds
H\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
, and further we obtain
H(x\,A)\asymp F(x\,A)
, as
x\to\infty
.

Proof.

We shall show that
\overline{H}_{A}(x)\asymp\overline{F}_{A}(x)
. On the one hand, for
\check{\Theta}:=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{d}\Theta_{i}
, we find for
x>0

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Z_{A}>x\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf\Theta}\,{\bf X}>x\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\check{\Theta}\,{\bf X}>x\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\check{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]\,.

On the other hand, following similar arguments for
\widehat{\Theta}:=\bigvee_{i=1}^{d}\Theta_{i}
, for any
x>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{H}_{A}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf\Theta}\,{\bf X}>x\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\widehat{\Theta}\,{\bf X}>x\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\widehat{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]\,.

Therefore, for any
x>0
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\check{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]\leq\overline{H}_{A}(x)\leq\mathbf{P}\left[\widehat{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]\,.

(3.2)

Further, by
[
19
, Th. 3.3(iv)]
, because of the moment condition on the component of
{\bf\Theta}
and the independence of
{\bf\Theta}
and
{\bf X}
, we obtain as
x\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\check{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]\asymp\overline{F}_{A}(x)\,,\qquad\mathbf{P}\left[\widehat{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]\asymp\overline{F}_{A}(x)\,.

(3.3)

From (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) we find

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{H}_{A}(x)}{\overline{F}_{A}(x)}\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[\widehat{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]}{\overline{F}_{A}(x)}<\infty\,,

and

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{H}_{A}(x)}{\overline{F}_{A}(x)}\geq\liminf_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[\check{\Theta}\,Y_{A}>x\right]}{\overline{F}_{A}(x)}>0\,.

From these two last relations we obtain
\overline{H}_{A}(x)\asymp\overline{F}_{A}(x)
, and consequently
H(x\,A)\asymp F(x\,A)
, which, since
F\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
, from Proposition
2.2
(ii) follows the inclusion
H\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
.
∎

Now we restrict ourselves to the case of scale mixture. The following assumption is an extension of a popular assumption, in closure with respect to product in one-dimensional heavy-tailed distributions; see for example
[
72
]
,
[
42
]
,
[
45
]
.

Assumption 3.1
.

It holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\Theta>c\,x]=o\left(\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}>x\right]\right)\,,

(3.4)

as
x\to\infty
, for each
c>0
.

By relations (
3.1
) and (
3.4
), we obtain that this is equivalent to the asymptotic relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[\Theta>c\,x]=o\left(\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta\,{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\right)=o\left(\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\Theta\,{\bf X}>x\right]\right)=o\left(\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta\,Y_{A}>x\right]\right)\,,

as
x\to\infty
. Furthermore, if the random variable
\Theta
has a support with a finite right endpoint, and
Y_{A}
has a support with infinite right endpoint, then Assumption
3.1
is true immediately. The scale mixture is omnipresent in several applications, although it seems quite restrictive in relation to product convolution, for example see
[
56
]
,
[
81
]
.

Before giving the main theorem for the scale mixtures, we note that the distribution of
\Theta
is denoted by
Q
, while the set of all positive discontinuity points of
Q
is denoted by
D(Q)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{R}
be a fixed set, and we assume that the distribution of
{\bf X}
is
F
.

(i)

If
\Theta
and
{\bf X}
are independent, and they satisfy Assumption
3.1
with
F\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, then for the scale mixture
\Theta\,{\bf X}
with distribution
G
, it holds
G\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
.

(ii)

If the assumptions of the previous part hold, with the restriction
F\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
, then for the scale mixture
\Theta\,{\bf X}
with distribution
G
, it holds
G\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
.

(iii)

If the assumptions of the previous part hold, with the restriction
F\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
, then for the scale mixture
\Theta\,{\bf X}
with distribution
G
, it holds
G\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

(iv)

Let
\Theta
be independent of
{\bf X}
, with
F\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Then for the scale mixture
\Theta\,{\bf X}
with distribution
G
, it holds
G\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
, if and only if either
D(Q)=\emptyset
or
D(Q)\neq\emptyset
and it holds

\displaystyle\overline{Q}\left(\dfrac{x}{d}\right)-\overline{Q}\left(\dfrac{x+1}{d}\right)=o(\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}>x\right])\,,

as
x\to\infty
, for all
d\in D(Q)
.

Proof.

Let denote by
\mathcal{B}
a general distribution class from the set
\{\mathcal{C},\,(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}),\,\mathcal{S},\,\mathcal{L}\}
. The inclusion
G\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
is equivalent to
M_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}G_{A}\in\mathcal{B}
. However, from relation (
2.3
) and (
3.1
) we obtain
\mathbf{P}[M_{A}>x]=\mathbf{P}[\Theta\,Y_{A}>x]
, for
x>0
. Hence, it is enough to show that the distribution of
\Theta\,Y_{A}
belongs to class
\mathcal{B}
. Since
F\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
, thus we obtain
Y_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\sim}}F_{A}\in\mathcal{B}
and from the fact that the
\Theta
,
Y_{A}
are independent, non-negative random variables, because of the assumptions in points
(i)-(iv)
, the result follows directly from the following collective statement

(i)

For the class
\mathcal{L}
, the result is implies by
[
19
, Th. 2.2(iii)]
.

(ii)

For the class
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})
, the result is implied by
[
51
, Cor. 5.2(b)]
.

(iii)

For the class
\mathcal{C}
, the result is implied by
[
19
, Th. 3.4(ii)]
.

(iv)

For the class
\mathcal{S}
, the result is implied by
[
79
, Th. 1.3]
.
∎

3.2.
Convolution and finite mixture.

In this subsection we study the closure of some distribution classes with respect to convolution and finite mixture. The motivation to study these two properties together stems from
[
23
]
,
[
50
]
where the closure of one-dimensional subexponential distributions with respect to convolution was examined. In the case of independence, we simplify the notation from (

) as follows: let
{\bf X}^{(1)}:=\left(X_{1}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,X_{d}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}
and
{\bf X}^{(2)}:=\left(X_{1}^{(2)},\,\ldots,\,X_{d}^{(2)}\right)^{\top}
be independent random vectors, with distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
respectively, then we define their convolutions as follows

, and we put
Y_{A}^{(1)}
and
Y_{A}^{(2)}
, as in comments before Proposition
2.4
. We have to note that for a multivariate distribution class
\mathcal{B}_{A}
, with

, we say that the convolution
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
, if for the random variable

\displaystyle Y_{A}^{*}:=\sup\left\{u\;:\;{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in u\,A\right\}\,,

(3.5)

with distribution

For the closure with respect to finite mixture it is enough to show

\displaystyle p\,F_{1}(x\,A)+(1-p)\,F_{2}(x\,A)\in\mathcal{B}_{A}\,,

(3.6)

for any

, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle p\,\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+(1-p)\,\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\in\mathcal{B}\,,

(3.7)

where by

is denoted the distribution tail of the

for

Proposition 3.1
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set,
F_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
, and

a)
F_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{A}
, then (
3.6
) holds, for any
p\in(0,\,1)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{A}\in\left\{\mathcal{C}_{A},\mathcal{D}_{A},\mathcal{L}_{A},(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}\right\}
.

b)
F_{2}(x\,A)=o\left[F_{1}(x\,A)\right]
, as
x\to\infty
.
Then relation (
3.6
) holds, for any
p\in(0,\,1)
, where
\mathcal{B}_{A}\in\{\mathcal{C}_{A},\,\mathcal{L}_{A}\}
.

Proof.

In both cases, it follows by the equivalence of (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), in combination with
[
51
, Prop. 3.5(iii)]
for
\mathcal{C}
, with
[
51
, Prop. 3.7(iv)]
for
\mathcal{D}
, with
[
51
, Prop. 3.9(iii)]
for
\mathcal{L}
and with
[
51
, Prop. 3.10(iii)]
for
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Next statement establishes the closure property of distribution classes
\mathcal{D}_{A}
,
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
and
\mathcal{C}_{A}
, together with some kind of weak or strong max-sum equivalence, in multidimensional set up.

Theorem 3.3
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set and
{\bf X}^{(1)}
,
{\bf X}^{(2)}
are independent random vectors with distributions
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
respectively.

(i)

If
F_{1},\,F_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
, then

\displaystyle F_{1}*F_{2}(x\,A)\asymp F_{1}(x\,A)+F_{2}(x\,A)\,,

(3.8)

as
x\to\infty
, and further it holds
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

(ii)

If
F_{1},\,F_{2}\in\mathcal{(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})}_{A}
, then

\displaystyle F_{1}*F_{2}(x\,A)\sim F_{1}(x\,A)+F_{2}(x\,A)\,,

(3.9)

as
x\to\infty
, and further it holds
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})}_{A}
.

(iii)

If
F_{1},\,F_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
, then it holds (
3.9
) and further it holds
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
.

Proof.

(i)

By
[
7
, Prop. 2.1]
, see also in
[
51
, Prop. 3.7 (ii)]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\asymp\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\,,

(3.10)

as
x\to\infty
, since
F_{A}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{D}
for
i=1,\,2
. Further this provides that
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{D}
, which is implied
by relation (
3.10
), from the closure of class
\mathcal{D}
with respect to weak tail equivalence and by the inclusion
\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}\in\mathcal{D}
. Indeed, this last comes from the definition of class
\mathcal{D}
, since it holds

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(b\,x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(b\,x)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)}\leq\max\left\{\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(b\,x)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)}\,,\;\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(b\,x)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)}\right\}<\infty\,,

for any
b\in(0,\,1)
.

Now, by relation (
3.10
) and Proposition
2.4
we find the upper bound

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}\in x\,A\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]}\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)}<\infty\,.

(3.11)

For the lower bound, because we have non-negative random vectors and the set
A
is by definition increasing, through Bonferroni’s inequality we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{2}\left\{{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right\}\right]

(3.12)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}\in x\,A\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]-\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}\in x\,A\,,\;{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]-\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]\,\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)-o\left[\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)\right]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}\in x\,A\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

as
x\to\infty
. Hence,

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}\in x\,A\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]}\geq 1>0\,.

(3.13)

Thus, by relations (
3.11
) and (
3.13
) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]\asymp\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}\in x\,A\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

as
x\to\infty
, which leads to relation (
3.8
). Via (
3.5
) and (
3.8
) we find

\displaystyle\overline{F_{A}^{*}}(x)\asymp\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\,,

as
x\to\infty
. Therefore, by
\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}\in\mathcal{D}
, we find
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{D}
from where we conclude the
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{A}
.

(ii)

We already have known that distribution class
\mathcal{D}_{A}
is closed with respect to convolution, from the previous part. By
[
7
, Th. 2.1]
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}+Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]\sim\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\,.

(3.14)

as
x\to\infty
, when
F_{A}^{(1)},\,F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}
. Due to Proposition
2.4
and relation (
3.14
) we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]

(3.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}+Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]\sim\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\sim F_{1}(x\,A)+F_{2}(x\,A)\,,

as
x\to\infty
. From relation (
3.12
) and (
3.15
) we obtain (
3.9
). Next, for the closure property of class
\mathcal{L}_{A}
, we observe that for any
y>0

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x-y\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x-y)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x-y)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x-y)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)}\,,\;\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x-y)}{\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)}\right\}=1\,,

where in the second step we used relation (
3.9
). Hence, we have
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}
, so we find
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, from where, in combination with statement
(i)
, we conclude
F_{1}*F_{2}\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
.

(iii)

At first, from the second part of this theorem and the fact that
\mathcal{C}_{A}\subsetneq(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
, it follows that (
3.9
) is true. To show that
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
, it is enough to obtain
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, we calculate

\displaystyle 1\leq\lim_{b\uparrow 1}\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>b\,x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{b\uparrow 1}\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>b\,x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>b\,x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{i\in\{1,\,2\}}\left\{\lim_{b\uparrow 1}\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>b\,x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]}\right\}=1\,,

where in the last step we took into account that
F_{A}^{(1)},\,F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{C}
, while in the second step we used relation (
3.9
). Therefore we conclude
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
. ∎

Remark 3.1
.

The condition
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
is not negligible. In
[
52
]
we find a counterexample, based on
[
23
]
, where it was shown that class
\mathcal{S}
is not closed with respect to convolution. Recently in
[
50
]
were established necessary and sufficient conditions for the closure of subexponentiality with respect to convolution of two distributions from the class
\mathcal{L}(\gamma)
, for some
\gamma\geq 0
. Especially,
[
50
, Th. 1.1]
for the case
\gamma=0
, states that if
F_{A}^{(1)},\,F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{L}
, then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
.

(2)

F_{Y_{A}^{(1)}\vee Y_{A}^{(2)}}\in\mathcal{S}
.

(3)

p\,F_{A}^{(1)}+(1-p)\,F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
for any (or, equivalently, for some)
p\in(0,\,1)
.

Moreover, each of these statements implies the asymptotic equivalence

\displaystyle\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\sim\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\,,

(3.16)

as
x\to\infty
, where by
F_{Y_{A}^{(1)}\vee Y_{A}^{(2)}}
is denoted the distribution of the maximum. We should mention that if
F_{A}^{(1)},\,F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
, then the points (1) - (3) and (
3.16
) are equivalent, see
[
50
, Cor. 1.1]
.

Inspired by this result, we examine the closure property of the multivariate subexponentiality with respect to convolution. The following statement, provides necessary and sufficient conditions for the closure property of multivariate subexponentiality, that are finally reduced to corresponding one-dimensional ones.

Theorem 3.4
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set, and the claims
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)}
are independent random vectors, with distributions
F_{1},\,F_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
respectively. Then it holds
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
if and only if
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
). Let
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
. In order to establish that
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
we should show that
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
.

From relation (
3.12
) and recalling (
3.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x\right]\gtrsim\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]\,,

as
x\to\infty
. Thus, let
{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
Y_{A}^{*}
. Further, for
i=1,\,2
, let
{Y_{A}^{(i)}}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
Y_{A}^{(i)}
, (with
{Y_{A}^{(1)}}^{\prime}
independent of
Y_{A}^{(2)^{\prime}}
). Then we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}+{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}>x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x\right]}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}+{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}>x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]}

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[\left(Y_{A}^{(1)}+Y_{A}^{(2)}\right)+\left({Y_{A}^{(1)}}^{\prime}+{Y_{A}^{(2)}}^{\prime}\right)>x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{2\,\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}+Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{2\,\left(\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]\right)}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]}=2\,,

where in the second step we applied an argument similar to the proof of Proposition
2.4
. The third step uses the assumption
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
, which, by Remark
3.1
, implies the max-sum equivalence of
F_{A}^{(1)}
and
F_{A}^{(2)}
.

From the other side, for non-negative and independent random variables
Y_{A}^{*}
,
{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}
, we find

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}+{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}>x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}>x\right]}\geq 2\,.

(3.18)

Hence, from relations (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) we have
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
.

(
\Leftarrow
). Let assume now
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
, that implies
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
. From the fact that
F_{1},\,F_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, by
[
23
, Th. 3(b)]
we obtain
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{L}
.

Furthermore, by Proposition
2.4
we find the lower bound

\displaystyle\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\geq\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(1)}+{\bf X}^{(2)}\in x\,A\right]=\overline{F}_{A}^{*}(x)\,,

and from the other side, since
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
, we can write

\displaystyle\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)=\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(1)}+Y_{A}^{(2)}>x\right]\leq\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{*}+{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}>x\right]\sim 2\,\overline{F}_{A}^{*}(x)\,,

as
x\to\infty
, where in the second step we employ the definitions of
Y_{A}^{(1)},\,Y_{A}^{(2)},\,Y_{A}^{*},\,{Y_{A}^{*}}^{\prime}
and the fact that the random vectors
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)}
are non-negative.

Therefore, by the last two inequalities, follows that
\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\asymp\overline{F}_{A}^{*}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, but since
F_{A}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}
and
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{L}
, from
[
39
, Th. 2.1(a)]
; see also in
[
51
, Prop. 3.13(ii)]
, we get
F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}\in\mathcal{S}
.
∎

From Theorem
3.4
, since class
\mathcal{S}
is not closed, with respect to convolution; see
[
52
]
, is implied the following result.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{R}
be a fixed set. Class
\mathcal{S}_{A}
is not closed, with respect to convolution, namely we can find two independent random vectors with distribution from class
\mathcal{S}_{A}
, whose convolution does NOT belong to class
\mathcal{S}_{A}
.

By Theorem
3.4
, we also get easily the following corollary, that shows an easy way to control the closure property of
\mathcal{S}_{A}
with respect to convolution.

Corollary 3.1
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set, and
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)}
are independent random vectors, with distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
respectively. If
F_{1},\,F_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{A}
, then the statements (1) - (3) of Remark (
3.1
) are equivalent to

F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Furthermore, in such a case (namely,
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
) (
3.9
) holds.

Proof.

The parts (1) - (3) of Remark
3.1
are equivalent, see
[
50
, Th. 1.1]
, and from Theorem
3.4
the part (1) is also equivalent
to
F_{1}*F_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Further, from part (1) of Remark
3.1
we obtain the asymptotic equivalence
\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}*F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)\sim\overline{F_{A}^{(1)}}(x)+\overline{F_{A}^{(2)}}(x)
, as
x\to\infty
, from where through relations (
3.12
) and (
3.15
) we conclude (
3.9
).
∎

4.
Sums of random vectors

4.1.
Finite sums.

Now, we study the asymptotic behavior of the entrance probability of finite sums of random vectors in the ’rare set’
x\,A
. In one-dimensional case a desired property for the subexponential distributions takes the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}>x\right]\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[X_{i}>x\right]\,,

(4.1)

as
x\to\infty
, with fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It is clear that if the random variables
X_{1},\,\ldots,\,X_{n}
are independent and identically distributed with common subexponential distribution, then relation (
4.1
) holds. There exists a series of papers, where relation (
4.1
) is studied under various conditions for the distributions of
X_{i}
,
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
, as well as for the dependence among these random variables; see
[
64
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
29
]
among others. It is worth to notice that in these papers there exists some ’insensitivity’ with respect to dependence structure, in the sense that the heavy tails show to asymptotically override the dependence. However, there are some cases in which this ’insensitivity’ with respect to dependence does not appear; see
[
61
]
,
[
62
]
,
[
48
]
,
[
27
]
. We keep in mind the single big jump principle, thence the multivariate form of (
4.1
) can be given as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

(4.2)

as
x\to\infty
, with fixed
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
{\bf S}_{n}:={\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+{\bf X}^{(n)}
. Relation (
4.2
) represents the multivariate linear single big jump principle, where the word ’linear’ stems from the insensitivity of this property with respect to dimension. See also
[
47
, Sec. 5]
for more discussions in relation to the ’non-linear’ case.

Usually, the study of asymptotic behavior of
{\bf S}_{n}
, for heavy-tailed summands, is restricted in
MRV
class; see for example
[
34
]
,
[
67
]
,
[
21
]
. In somehow bigger classes, we find in
[
32
]
and
[
33
]
the study of the asymptotic
behavior of the random walk and large deviations of independent and identically distributed random vectors. In
[
70
, Cor. 4.10]
it was shown that for independent and identically distributed random vectors whose distribution belongs to
\mathcal{S}_{A}
, relation (
4.2
) holds. We extend this result, by relaxing the conditions of independence and identical distribution.

Let us present three dependence structures among the random vectors, inspired by
[
49
]
,
[
29
]
and
[
12
]
respectively.

Assumption 4.1

(Lehmann)
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{R}
be a fixed set. We say that the
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
, with distributions
F_{1},\,\ldots,\,F_{n}
respectively, are regression dependent on
A
, symbolically we write
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}\in RD_{A}
, if
Y_{A}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,Y_{A}^{(n)}
are regression dependent (say
RD
), that means there exist constants
x_{0}
and
C
, such that it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x_{i}\;\Big|\;Y_{A}^{(j)}=x_{j}\,,\;\text{with}\;j\in J_{i}\right]\leq C\,\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x_{i}\right]\,,

(4.3)

for
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
,
j\in J_{i}
, with
\emptyset\neq J_{i}\subseteq\{1,\,\ldots,\,n\}\setminus\{i\}
, for
x_{i}\wedge x_{j}>x_{0}
, for all
j\in J_{i}
.

Assumption 4.2

(Geluk - Tang)
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{R}
be a fixed set. We say, that the random vectors
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
, with distributions
F_{1},\,\ldots,\,F_{n}
, are tail asymptotically independent on
A
, symbolically
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}\in TAI_{A}
, if
Y_{A}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,Y_{A}^{(n)}
are
TAI
, that means it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x_{i}\wedge x_{j}\to\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x_{i}\;\Big|\;Y_{A}^{(j)}>x_{j}\right]=0\,,

(4.4)

for all
i\neq j
with
i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,n\}
.

Assumption 4.3

(Chen - Yuen)
.

Let
A\in\mathscr{R}
be a fixed set. We say, that the random vectors
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
, with distributions
F_{1},\,\ldots,\,F_{n}
, are quasi asymptotically independent on
A
, symbolically
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}\in QAI_{A}
, if the
Y_{A}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,Y_{A}^{(n)}
are
QAI
, namely it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\,,\;Y_{A}^{(j)}>x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(j)}>x\right]}=0\,,

(4.5)

for all
i\neq j
with
i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,n\}
.

Let us remind, that each random vector has arbitrarily dependent components.

Remark 4.1
.

Let us notice, that from relations (
4.3
), (
4.4
), (
4.5
), we can easily verify that
RD_{A}\subsetneq TAI_{A}\subsetneq QAI_{A}
, for any
A\in\mathscr{R}
, and furthermore if the
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
are independent, that means the
Y_{A}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,Y_{A}^{(n)}
are also independent, then they satisfy the dependence
RD_{A}
. Dependencies
RD
,
TAI
and
QAI
have been used in several applications of risk theory and risk management; see for example
[
75
]
,
[
53
]
,
[
9
]
.

Now, we provide the main result.

Theorem 4.1
.

(i)

Under Assumption
4.3
and if holds
F_{1},\,\ldots,\,F_{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{A}
, then the asymptotic relation (
4.2
) is valid.

(ii)

Under Assumption
4.2
and if holds
F_{1},\,\ldots,\,F_{n}\in(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
, then the asymptotic relation (
4.2
) is valid.

(iii)

Under Assumption
4.1
and if holds
F_{1},\,\ldots,\,F_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
and
F_{i}*F_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
for all
i,\,j\in\{1,\,\ldots,\,n\}
with
i\neq j
, then the asymptotic relation (
4.2
) is valid.

Proof.

(i)

By Proposition
2.4
and
[
12
, Th. 3.1]
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]\leq\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

(4.6)

as
x\to\infty
, which provides the upper bound for (
4.2
). From the other side, for the lower bound, since the
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
are non-negative random vectors and
A
is an increasing set, using Bonferroni’s inequality we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\{{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right\}\right]

(4.7)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\,,\;{\bf X}^{(j)}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\,,\;Y_{A}^{(j)}>x\right]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

as
x\to\infty
, where in the pre-last step due to Assumption
4.3
, for term of the second sum we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\,,\;Y_{A}^{(j)}>x\right]=o\left(\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]+\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(j)}>x\right]\right)\,,

as
x\to\infty
, for
i,\,j=1,\,\ldots,\,n
and
i\neq j
. From relations (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) we find (
4.2
).

(ii)

By Proposition
2.4
and
[
29
, Th. 3.1]
we obtain (
4.6
). For the lower bound, because of the inclusion
TAI_{A}\subsetneq QAI_{A}
, the relation (
4.7
) remains true. From relations (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) we find (
4.2
).

(iii)

Because of the inclusion
RD_{A}\subsetneq QAI_{A}
, the relation (
4.7
) remains true in this case. The upper bound of relation (
4.2
) follows similarly by (
4.6
), where we used Proposition
2.4
and
[
29
, Th. 3.2]
. ∎

4.2.
Randomly stopped sums.

In the one-dimensional case, several papers study the asymptotic behavior of the distribution tail of the randomly stopped sum

\displaystyle S_{N}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\,,

where
N
represents a discrete, non-negative, non-degenerate to zero random variable. We note that the summation over an empty index set is equal to zero by convention (and to
{\bf 0}
in
d
-dimensions). For the case with heavy-tailed
X_{i}
, see
[
66
]
,
[
77
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
78
]
, among many others. In multidimensional set up, we are considering the probability
\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]
, where

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{N}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,,

where
N
is a discrete, non-negative, non-degenerate to zero random variable, that is independent of
\left\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\right\}
. The asymptotic behavior of
{\bf S}_{N}
can play crucial role in insurance industry. For example, we consider an insurer with
d
-lines of business, then over a concrete time interval the number of claims is unknown. For some relatively commonly usefull forms which can take the set
A
; see below in Remark
5.1
. Next, we extend
[
26
, Th. 3.37]
in multivariate set up.

Theorem 4.2
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set, and
\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
, are non-negative, independent random vectors with common distribution
F\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Let
N
be a discrete, non-negative, non-degenerate to zero random variable, such that
\mathbf{E}[N]<\infty
, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[(1+\varepsilon)^{N}\right]<\infty\,,

for some
\varepsilon>0
. If further
N
is independent of
\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
, then it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]\sim\mathbf{E}[N]\,F(x\,A)\,,

(4.8)

as
x\to\infty
. Furthermore, the distribution of
{\bf S}_{N}
belongs to class
\mathcal{S}_{A}
.

Proof.

Let assume that the right endpoint of
N
is infinite. For the upper bound we obtain for
x>0

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\left({\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+{\bf X}^{(n)}\right)>x\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,{\bf X}^{(n)}>x\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\sim\mathbf{E}[N]\,\overline{F}_{A}(x)=\mathbf{E}[N]\,F(x\,A)\,,

as
x\to\infty
, where in the first step we exclude the term for
n=0
, since
{\bf 0}\notin\overline{A}
. In the sixth step we can use
[
26
, Th. 3.37]
because of condition on
N
and the fact that
\{Y_{A}^{(i)}\}
is sequence of independent, identically distributed random variables, following subexponential distribution.

From the other side, for the lower bound for (
4.8
), from
[
70
, Cor. 4.10]
; or by Theorem
4.1
(iii), via Fatou’s lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]

(4.10)

\displaystyle\gtrsim

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]=\mathbf{E}[N]\,F(x\,A)\,,

as
x\to\infty
. From relations (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) we get (
4.8
).

If the support of
N
is bounded from above, with right endpoint
M
, the relations (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) hold similarly with summation from one to

.

The membership of the distribution of
{\bf S}_{N}
to class
\mathcal{S}_{A}
follows by

, relation (
4.8
), and
[
70
, Prop. 4.12(a)]
.
∎

In the next theorem is established relation (
4.8
) in the case of not necessarily independent
\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
, but they follow a common distribution from some classes smaller than
\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Additionally, we should require an alternative moment condition on the discrete random variable

Theorem 4.3
.

Let

be a fixed set, and
\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
be a sequence of non-negative random vectors, with common distribution
F
. Let

be a non-negative, non-degenerate to zero, discrete random variable, independent of
\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
.

(i)

If the
\{{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
are
TAI_{A}
, with

and it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[N^{p+1}\right]<\infty\,,

(4.11)

for some

, then relation (
4.8
) is true, and further the distribution of
{\bf S}_{N}
belongs to class
(\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L})_{A}
.

(ii)

If the

are

, with

and (

) holds, for some

(iii)

Under the conditions of part

,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]\sim\mathbf{E}[N]\,\mu(A)\,\overline{V}(x)\,,

(4.12)

as

.

Proof.

(i)

Let consider

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]=\left(\sum_{n=1}^{M}+\sum_{n=M+1}^{\infty}\right)\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]\mathbf{P}[N=n]=:I_{1}(x,\,M)+I_{2}(x,\,M)\,.

(4.13)

\displaystyle I_{1}(x,\,M)\sim\sum_{n=1}^{M}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]=\mathbf{E}\left[N\,{\bf 1}_{\{N\leq M\}}\right]\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\,.

(4.14)

For the second term

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}>x\right]\,\sum_{n=M+1}^{\left\lfloor x/{K}\right\rfloor}n^{p+1}\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]+\left\lfloor\dfrac{x}{K}\right\rfloor^{-(p+1)}\,\mathbf{E}\left[N^{p+1}\,{\bf 1}_{\{N>\left\lfloor x/{K}\right\rfloor\}}\right]

\displaystyle=C_{1}\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\,\sum_{n=M+1}^{\left\lfloor x/{K}\right\rfloor}n^{p+1}\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]+\left\lfloor\dfrac{x}{K}\right\rfloor^{-(p+1)}\,\mathbf{E}\left[N^{p+1}\,{\bf 1}_{\{N>\left\lfloor x/{K}\right\rfloor\}}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{1}\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{E}\left[N^{p+1}\,{\bf 1}_{\{N>M\}}\right]\,,

as

, where at the first step we used Proposition

, at the third step the constant

as

.

Hence, from (

\displaystyle I_{2}(x,\,M)\lesssim\delta\,\mathbf{E}\left[N\right]\,F(x\,A)\,,

as

For the lower bound in (

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{N}\in x\,A\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]

\displaystyle\gtrsim\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N=n]\sim\mathbf{E}[N]\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\in x\,A\right]\,.

where at the second step we took into consideration that the

,
{\bf X}^{(j)}
are

. Hence, (

) is true.

Further, since

(ii)

5.
Asymptotic behavior of aggregate claims.

Now, we give an application in risk theory. More precisely, we want to find an asymptotic expression for the probability of the entrance of discounted aggregate claims into some rare set
x\,A
, for a multivariate Poisson risk model, with claim distributions from the class
\mathcal{S}_{A}
and permitting for the insurer, risk-free and risky investments simultaneously, through a general stochastic process.

In risk theory there exist several papers considering the asymptotic behavior of discounted aggregate claims; or even the ruin probability, with claims from the class of subexponential distributions in one-dimensional or two-dimensional risk models; see for example
[
1
]
,
[
57
]
,
[
35
]
,
[
80
]
. However in most multivariate models we meet as basic assumption the
MRV
class for the distribution of claims, and additionally the asymptotic dependence among the components of each claim vector; see for example
[
43
]
for risk model with constant interest force and
[
54
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
for risk models with both risk-free and risky investments. In the case of larger claim distribution classes, we refer
[
70
]
, where a renewal risk model without interest force and with multivariate subexponential claims is examined.

Motivated by the modern insurance industry, having in mind that most of the insurance companies invest their surpluses, we consider a multivariate risk model, which covers a general price process of the financial portfolio. The following concept is inspired by
[
10
]
, and generalizes in some sense their result in multidimensional set up.

We consider an insurer with
d
lines of business, with claims
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots
, that are independent, identically distributed, non-negative random vectors, that arrive at moments
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots
, which constitute a Poisson counting process
N_{t}:=\sup\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\;:\;\tau_{n}\leq t\}
,
\sup\emptyset=0
, with intensity
\lambda>0
. The price process of the investment portfolio has the form
\{e^{R_{t}}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
, where by
\{R_{t}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
we denote a general real-valued stochastic process, which represents the logarithmic return of the prices of the investment portfolio. Hence, the insurer’s discounted aggregate claims, up to moment
t>0
, are provided by the relation

\displaystyle{\bf D}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}{\bf X}^{(i)}e^{-R_{\tau_{i}}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}X_{1}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{\tau_{i}}}\,,\;\cdots\,,\;\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}X_{d}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{\tau_{i}}}\right)^{\top}\,.

(5.1)

Before reaching to the main result we present some preliminary lemmas. The first two seem to have value on their own merit. In the first we verify that the presence of the multivariate linear single big jump remains invariable under some scale mixtures, that are upper bounded and non-negative. Let us denote the scale mixture sum as

\displaystyle{\bf S}_{n}^{\Theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\,.

(5.2)

Recall that, as analogue to (
3.1
), we define

\displaystyle M_{A}^{(i)}:=\sup\left\{u\;:\;\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in u\,A\right\}\,,

for
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
.

Lemma 5.1
.

We assume that
A\in\mathscr{R}
is a fixed set, and the claims
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
are independent, identically distributed random vectors, with common distribution
F\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Let
\Theta^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,\Theta^{(n)}
be arbitrarily dependent, non-negative and non-degenerate to zero random variables, which are uniformly upper bounded by
0<b<\infty
. If
\Theta^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,\Theta^{(n)}
are independent of
{\bf X}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,{\bf X}^{(n)}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}^{\Theta}\in x\,A\right]\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

(5.3)

as
x\to\infty
.

Proof.

On the one hand, for the upper bound of (
5.3
) we obtain the following

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}^{\Theta}\in x\,A\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\right)>x\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\Theta^{(1)}\,{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\Theta^{(n)}\,{\bf X}^{(n)}>x\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Theta^{(i)}\,Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

as
x\to\infty
, where in the fifth step we used
[
74
, Th. 1]
, while in the pre-last step we took into consideration the relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\left(\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\right)>x\right]

(5.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}{\bf X}^{(i)}>x\right]=\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\,,

for
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
.

On the other hand, for the lower bound of (
5.3
), since
A
is an increasing set, and
\Theta^{(i)}
,
{\bf X}^{(i)}
are non-negative, through Bonferroni’s inequality we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}^{\Theta}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\,,\;\Theta^{(j)}\,{\bf X}^{(j)}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]-\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\,\mathbf{P}\left[Y_{A}^{(j)}>\dfrac{x}{b}\right]

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\left[1-o(1)\right]\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

as
x\to\infty
.
∎

The next lemma establishes a Kesten-type bound for the scale mixture sum of (
5.2
), thereby extending
[
70
, Prop. 4.12 (c)]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
5.1
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists constant
K=K(\varepsilon)>0
, such that it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}^{\Theta}\in x\,A\right]\leq K\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all
x\geq 0
.

Proof.

For any
x>0
, we obtain the following inequalities

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf S}_{n}^{\Theta}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\left(\Theta^{(1)}\,{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+\Theta^{(n)}\,{\bf X}^{(n)}\right)>x\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\Theta^{(1)}\,{\bf X}^{(1)}+\cdots+\sup_{{\bf p}\in I_{A}}{\bf p}^{\top}\,\Theta^{(n)}\,{\bf X}^{(n)}>x\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Theta^{(i)}\,Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]\leq K\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,Y_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[M_{A}^{(i)}>x\right]=K\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[\Theta^{(i)}\,{\bf X}^{(i)}\in x\,A\right]\,,

where in the forth step we have applied
[
8
, Th. 1.2]
and in the penultimate step we have used relation (
5.4
).
∎

Now, we remind a classical result on Poisson process; see for example
[
68
, Th. 5.2]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

We assume that
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\ldots
are jump epochs of a Poisson process
\{N_{t}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
. Let
U_{1},\,U_{2},\,\ldots
be a sequence of independent, identically distributed random variables, with uniform distribution over the interval
(0,\,1)
, that are independent of
\{N_{t}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
. For any arbitrarily constant
T>0
and any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
it holds
\left\{(\tau_{1},\,\ldots,\,\tau_{n})\;|\;N_{T}=n\right\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left(T\,U_{(1,n)},\,\ldots,\,T\,U_{(n,n)}\right)
, where the symbol
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
means equality in distribution and the sequence
U_{(1,n)},\,\ldots,\,U_{(n,n)}
represents the order statistics of
U_{1},\,\ldots,\,U_{n}
.

Let us formulate the assumptions for the risk model (
5.1
).

Assumption 5.1
.

For some fixed set
A\in\mathscr{R}
, let the claims
\{{\bf X},\,{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
, be a sequence of independent, identically distributed, non-negative random vectors, with common distributions
F\in\mathcal{S}_{A}
. Further we assume that
\{{\bf X},\,{\bf X}^{(1)},\,{\bf X}^{(2)},\,\ldots\}
,
\{N_{t}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
and
\left\{e^{R_{t}}\,,\;t\geq 0\right\}
are mutually independent.

Let us observe that the
d
lines of business, at each jump epoch
\tau_{i}
, are probably dependent, that means the vector
{\bf X}^{(i)}=\left(X_{1}^{(1)},\,\ldots,\,X_{d}^{(1)}\right)^{\top}
may has dependent components through
\mathcal{S}_{A}
, which contains cases of asymptotic independence and asymptotic dependence. In opposite to the most of multidimensional risk models with
MRV
claims, where the requirement of asymptotic dependence very often is needed. Some of the components of
{\bf X}^{(i)}
can be zero, but not all of them (in order to have renewal epoch at
\tau_{i}
). Finally, from the fact that the model has common counting process at the
d
lines of business we obtain also another source of dependence between the lines.

Assumption 5.2
.

Let
T>0
be finite. The logarithmic price process of an investment portfolio
\left\{R_{t}\,,\;0<t\leq T\right\}
, is bounded away from
-\infty
, namely there exists a constant
C=C(T)<\infty
, such that satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\inf_{0\leq t\leq T}R_{t}>-C\right]=1\,.

Assumption
5.2
is general enough to permit to the insurer to have both risk-free and risky investments simultaneously; see in
[
10
, Exam. 1]
. The Assumption
5.2
is clearly dependent of the choice of
T>0
in several cases.

Theorem 5.1
.

We assume Assumption
5.1
and Assumption
5.2
are valid in risk model (
5.1
). Then it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf D}(T)\in x\,A\right]\sim\lambda\,\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{t}}\in x\,A\right]\,dt\,,

(5.5)

as
x\to\infty
.

Remark 5.1
.

In the one-dimensional case, relation (
5.5
) is interesting for
A=(1,\,\infty)
, since then it represents the tail of discounted aggregate claims. However, in the multivariate risk models there are several interesting cases. For example if the set
A
is of the form (
2.4
), then the (
5.5
) express the probability that the sum of the discounted aggregate claims of the
d
-lines of business, exceeds the initial capital. As we see in
[
70
]
the set
A
can take the form
\{{\bf x}\;:\;x_{i}>b_{i}\,,\;\text{for some}\;i=1,\,\ldots,\,d\}
, where
b_{1},\,\ldots,\,b_{d}>0
, in which we are interested in the probability that one business line to exceed its initial capital, accordingly. For a depiction of several ruin probabilities in multivariate risk models see in
[
13
, Sec. 1]

Proof.

By the law of total probability, and the independence of the counting process
\{N_{t}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
from the other sources of randomness we obtain for any
x>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf D}(T)\in x\,A\right]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{\tau_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}\left[N_{T}=n\right]\,.

We now define the
U_{1},\,U_{2},\,\ldots
as a sequence of independent identically uniformly distributed over the interval
(0,\,1)
random variables, which are independent of all the other sources of randomness. Then by Lemma
5.3
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[{\bf D}(T)\in x\,A]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{(i,n)}}}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N_{T}=n]

(5.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]\,\mathbf{P}[N_{T}=n]\,,

where in the last step we used Assumption
5.1
. Further the

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\,,

is a scale mixture sum, as in relation (
5.2
), with
\Theta^{(i)}=e^{R_{TU_{i}}}
, for
i=1,\,\ldots,\,n
. From Assumption
5.2
we have that the
\Theta^{(i)}
are non-negative and upper bounded by
e^{C}
. Hence all the assumptions of Lemma
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
are satisfied, namely by the first one we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]\sim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]=n\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{TU}}\in x\,A\right]\,,

(5.7)

as
x\to\infty
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
U
is a copy of
U_{1},\,\ldots,\,U_{n}
.

Furthermore, by Lemma
5.2
, and for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
K=K(\varepsilon)>0
such that it holds

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]

(5.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,n\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{TU}}\in x\,A\right]\,,

for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x\geq 0
and additionally, since the process
\{N_{t}\,,\;t\geq 0\}
is Poisson, we can find some
\varepsilon>0
, such that it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\,(1+\varepsilon)^{n}\,\mathbf{P}\left[N_{T}=n\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[N_{T}\,(1+\varepsilon)^{N_{T}}\right]<\infty\,,

for
T\in(0,\,\infty)
(see for example
[
40
, Th. 1]
). From this, together with relation (
5.8
) we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf D}(T)\in x\,A\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{TU}}\in x\,A\right]}\leq K\,\mathbf{E}\left[N_{T}\,(1+\varepsilon)^{N_{T}}\right]<\infty

Therefore, we can apply the dominated convergence theorem on (
5.6
) and through (
5.7
) we have

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf D}(T)\in x\,A\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{TU}}\in x\,A\right]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lim_{x\to\infty}\dfrac{\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf X}^{(i)}\,e^{-R_{TU_{i}}}\in x\,A\right]}{\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{TU}}\in x\,A\right]}\,\mathbf{P}\left[N_{T}=n\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\,\mathbf{P}[N_{T}=n]=\mathbf{E}\left[N_{T}\right]=\lambda\,T\,,

from where follows, through integration with respect to
U
, the asymptotic relation

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[{\bf D}(T)\in x\,A]\sim\lambda\,T\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{TU}}\in x\,A\right]\sim\lambda\,\int_{0}^{T}\,\mathbf{P}\left[{\bf X}\,e^{-R_{t}}\in x\,A\right]\,dt\,,

as
x\to\infty
, which is the desired result.
∎
