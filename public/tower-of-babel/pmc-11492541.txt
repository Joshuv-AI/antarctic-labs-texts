Epidemiology and characteristics of unintentional self-inflicted penetrating ocular injuries in children less than 6 years-old
Authors: Shahram Bamdad, Mohammad Shirvani, Sepehr Shahriarirad, Mohammadsaber Tamaddon, Alireza Attar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPediatric ocular injuries, notably unintentional self-inflicted penetrating ocular injuries in children under 6, pose a substantial public health concern, leading to visual impairment and psychological distress. With global prevalence indicating severe visual impairment or blindness in 2–14% of affected children, these injuries necessitate multiple surgeries and extended monitoring. Our study aims to analyze the epidemiology and characteristics of such injuries, emphasizing preventive measures, parental supervision, and timely medical intervention to mitigate their impact on young lives.MethodConducted at Khalili Hospital in Shiraz, Iran, our retrospective study included 141 cases admitted between April 2018 and April 2023. Ethical principles guided data collection from medical records, encompassing demographic details, causative objects, ocular laceration specifics, surgical procedures, complications, and visual acuity assessments.ResultsThe mean age of the participants in this study was 47.18 ± 16.48 months (ranging from 10 to 71 months), and the male to female ratio was 1.76:1 (90 vs. 51). Objects of injury mostly included knives (34.8%), wood (12.1%), and glass (10.6%), and the most common initial manifestation of injury was iris prolapse (56.7%), followed by traumatic cataract (46.8%) and limbus laceration (34.0%). Fifteen (10.6%) patients had a visual acuity better than LogMAR 0.5, 24 (17.1%) had finger count vision, 37 (26.2%) had hand motion, 7 (0.5%) had light perception, and 58 (41.1%) were uncooperative. Type of laceration and season of injury were associated with retinal detachment, and a longer time-to-visit was correlated with a higher risk of developing retinal detachment or endophthalmitis.ConclusionOur study highlights the significant burden of unintentional self-inflicted penetrating ocular injuries in children under 6. Emphasizing preventive measures, parental supervision, and timely medical intervention, our findings underscore the potential psychological and visual consequences. This research advocates for parental education and safety measures to reduce the occurrence and severity of such injuries in this vulnerable population, promoting a healthier and safer environment for children.Keywords: Pediatric Ocular injuries, Unintentional penetrating trauma, Visual impairment

Introduction

Perforating ocular injuries are a significant concern, accounting for 20–50% of all reported ocular injury cases. These injuries can result in monocular visual impairment and non-congenital unilateral blindness [1, 2].

Moreover, such injuries globally result in severe visual impairment or blindness in 2–14% of affected children [3, 4]. These injuries often necessitate multiple surgeries and extended monitoring, and they can also give rise to psychological distress and adjustment difficulties [5]. Assessing and treating these injuries in children pose challenges due to their limited cooperation [1].

Common pediatric ocular injuries include corneal or scleral lacerations, traumatic cataracts, intraocular foreign bodies, vitreous hemorrhages, and intraocular tissue prolapses [6]. Notably, corneal opacity, often accompanied by traumatic cataracts, is a frequent occurrence in pediatric cases, significantly impacting visual acuity [7]. The severity of these injuries can lead to additional complications such as lens dislocation, iridodialysis, hyphema, and posterior segment issues like vitreous hemorrhage or retinal detachment [8]. Comprehensive knowledge of the epidemiological aspects of open globe injury is essential for preventing severe harm to children’s well-being. Recognizing childhood blindness as a preventable leading cause, the World Health Organization emphasizes understanding epidemiologic risk factors and prognosis for healthier outcomes and reduced ocular injury prevalence [9]. Hence, we conducted a retrospective study to assess the clinical course and outcomes of unintentional self-inflicted penetrating ocular injuries in children under 6 years old. This study included patients admitted to a tertiary referral university-affiliated ophthalmology center in southern Iran due to open globe injury.

Material and method

In this retrospective analysis, we included pediatric patients aged 6 years and younger who were admitted to Khalili Hospital in Shiraz, Iran, due to self-inflicted ocular injuries (OGIs) between April 2018 and April 2023. Informed consent for participation was obtained from the parents or legal guardians, as all patients were below the age of six. The exclusion criteria were patients with full-thickness ocular injuries repaired at other centers, those older than six years, as well as those with non-self-inflicted or partial-thickness lacerations. Khalili Hospital serves as the sole tertiary referral center for pediatric eye trauma in Shiraz, covering most regions in southern Iran. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Iran.

We collected data from various sources, including admission records, surgical documentation, and outpatient clinic files. The dataset encompassed demographic details, initial and final visual acuity after all suture removal measurements. However, it is noteworthy that many children did not cooperate for visual acuity assessments due to pain and their age. The collected information included the causative object of injury, the type and location of ocular lacerations, the shape of the laceration, the number of times they underwent surgery, the condition of the crystalline lens, as well as other anterior and posterior segment findings observed during the initial examination. Additionally, we documented the primary and additional surgical procedures performed and any ensuing complications such as secondary IOL insertion after traumatic cataract. Furthermore, we recorded the presence or absence of intraocular foreign bodies and additional observations made through ocular or other imaging techniques. To explore potential seasonal patterns, we tabulated the month in which the injuries occurred.

Results

Out of the 141 patients enrolled in this study, 90 (63.8%) males and 51 (31.2%) females were included. The age of the patients ranged from 10 to 71 months, averaging 47.18 ± 16.489 months (Fig. 1).

The initial manifestation of injury consisted of iris prolapse in 80 (56.7%) eyes, uveal prolapse in 12 (8.5%), vitreous prolapse in 15 (10.6%), gross hyphema in 31 (22.0%), microhyphema in 30 (21.3%), lens rupture in 2 (1.4%), intracorneal foreign body in 5 (3.5%), and intracameral foreign body in 2 (1.4%). The objects of injury mostly included knives (34.8% of cases), wood (12.1%), and glass (10.6%), respectively (Table 1).

The cornea and sclera were both injured in 33 (23.4%) patients, while in 101 (71.6%) patients only the cornea was damaged and in another 7 (5.0%) patients the sclera was injured alone. Wound size ranged from 1 to 15 mm (mean 5.65 ± 3.005 mm) with the injuries consisting of 105 (74.5%) linear, 14 (9.9%) angular, 18 (12.8%) gaping, and 4 (2.8%) stellate formations. Also, limbus laceration was seen in 48 (34.0%) patients and 66 (46.8%) had a traumatic cataract.

All patients arrived at the ER within 1 week of injury (median: 2.00[IQR: 1.00–2.00] days). During the follow-up time of 3 to 12 months (4.00[3.00–5.00]), the number of operations performed ranged from 2 to 10 (3.00[2.00–4.00]). Operations performed included primary repair (100%), lensectomy (36.9%), 23-gauge pars plana vitrectomy (14.9%), Suture removal (100%) which ranged from 1 to 4 times, 53.2%, 36.2%, 8.5%, and 2.1%, respectively), silicone oil emulsification and subsequent removal (9.9% once and 1.4% twice), anterior vitrectomy (31.2%), posterior-chamber intraocular lens implantation (24.8%), intraocular lens placement in the sulcus (4.3%), and penetrating keratoplasty (0.7%). Also, intravitreal injection of ceftazidime, vancomycin, and dexamethasone was administered in 7.1% of the patients. In pediatric patients, low scleral rigidity, a pliable cornea, and higher posterior pressure cause rapid suture loosening, increasing the risk of infection [10]. Therefore, all loose sutures must be promptly removed. Due to poor cooperation, suture removal under general anesthesia is necessary, continuing until three months postoperatively when complete removal is safe.

Upon arrival, 15 (10.6%) patients had VA better than LogMAR 0.5, 24 (17.1%) had CF, 37 (26.2%) had HM, 7 (0.5%) had LP, and 58 (41.1%) couldn’t be evaluated due to being uncooperative. Figure 2 displays the initial and improved VA of the patients in this study.

Considering the complications in our study, nearly half of the patients had a traumatic cataract (n = 66, 46.8%), 8 (5.7%) experienced endophthalmitis, and 18 (12.8%) suffered from retinal detachment. Also, the duration between primary repair and IOL placement ranged from 1 to 8 weeks (2.00[2.00–4.00]).

Complications of patients with corneal laceration based on age and gender

The patient’s initial and final VA were significantly correlated (P < 0.001) and according to WHO’s definition of visual impairment [11], the percentage of blindness, moderate impairment, mild impairment, and normal visions improved from 48.2 to 35.5%, 0.0–0.7%, 2.8–13.5%, and 7.8–16.3%, respectively, showing an overall shift from visual impairment to sight restoration. Moreover, evaluating the correlation between VA and complications, initial VA was found to contain a statistically significant correlation with traumatic cataract (P = 0.002) and final VA was strongly associated with traumatic cataract (P < 0.001) and retinal detachment (P = 0.004).

Time-to-visit was also found to be positively correlated with patients developing retinal detachment (P = 0.024, 2[2–3] in those who developed the complication vs. 2[1–2] in those who didn’t) or endophthalmitis (P = 0.007, 3[2–3] vs. 2[1–2]) in our study. Shape of laceration, wound size, and surgery count were not associated with the development of these complications. Moreover, traumatic cataract was found to contain a positive correlation with the development of endophthalmitis or retinal detachment (OR and 95% CI: 1.137, 1.040―1.245 and 7.059, 1.942―25.655, respectively).

Discussion

Eye injuries, a major cause of disabling ocular morbidity, especially impact the young, with undeniable public health significance [12]. These injuries generate unnecessary tolls, but 90% can be prevented [13]. The public health importance of ocular trauma is evident, causing a significant and often avoidable toll in medical care, human suffering, long-term disability, productivity loss, rehabilitation services, and socioeconomic cost [14]. Through parental supervision and education on protective eyewear during high-risk activities, visual loss can be significantly mitigated or entirely averted [15].

Ocular trauma is a major cause of noncongenital monocular blindness in children globally [3, 16]. In the U.S., studies show an annual incidence of 15.2 cases per 100,000 children, underscoring the significant impact on young individuals [17]. Moreover, accurately gauging the complete financial toll of ocular injuries remains challenging. The amalgamation of direct and indirect costs reaches hundreds of millions of dollars annually. The greatest burden falls upon developing nations, where the financial strain is exacerbated, as they are the least equipped to bear such expenses [18].

Children who experience trauma often grapple with feelings of helplessness, confusion, shame, and a reluctance to trust others or their surroundings [19]. Childhood trauma can leave an enduring imprint on an individual’s development, potentially paving the way for future disorders. The impact varies based on the nature and pattern of traumatic events, with some individuals developing PTSD, particularly in the aftermath of sudden and unexpected incidents [20]. Others may exhibit a spectrum of PTSD symptoms, behavioral disorders, anxiety, phobias, and depressive disorders [20].

We observed that the number of male patients hospitalized for open injuries was twice that of females. This discovery aligns with prior research, consistently indicating a significantly higher frequency of pediatric eye injuries in males [18, 21, 22]. The observed disparity in injury rates between sexes appears to be linked to more aggressive behavior in males and a greater participation in high-risk activities compared to females [23].

However, it’s noteworthy that other studies, similar to our own, have identified an increase in ocular injuries with age [24, 25]. This escalation in the gender disparity may be attributed to the natural progression of childhood development. As children grow older, they commence walking, gradually advance to running, and their innate curiosity leads to an increased tendency to touch things. Consequently, this developmental phase correlates with a rise in self-limited eye damage.

The time of presentation for pediatric self-inflicted eye damage to surgical interventions in our center was approximately 2 days, significantly shorter than the duration reported in the study conducted in Nigeria [26]. This difference may be attributed to the improved facilities and preparation in our center.

In our study, we documented that objects leading to accidental open globe injuries were primarily sharp objects, such as knives (34.8% of cases), wood (12.1%), and glass (10.6%). This finding aligns with the observations of Azimi et al. [27]. However, it contrasts with the results of Omobolanle et al. [26]., where stone was reported as a common cause. This highlights the importance of keeping sharp tools away from children.

Our study reveals that the risk of pediatric lacerations increases in spring, a phenomenon associated with the Norouz holidays in Iranian culture. During this period, the weather becomes warmer, leading children to engage in outdoor group games without parental supervision. This risk can be mitigated through parental supervision, keeping dangerous devices out of children’s reach, and educating children about proper safety considerations.

In our investigation, we observed a heightened incidence of endophthalmitis (5.7%) among children with penetrating ocular injuries with traumatic ocular injuries. Notably, all instances of endophthalmitis manifested within the initial week, underscoring the elevated virulence and severity associated with infectious endophthalmitis following penetrating ocular trauma. This trend aligns with findings reported by Nakayama et al., who documented a comparable rate of 6.62% [28].

In our investigation, we observed that patients with traumatic cataract exhibiting self-injurious behavior are at an elevated risk for concurrent retinal detachment. Additionally, among children who experienced traumatic cataract and concurrent retinal detachment, a lower final visual outcome was noted. This finding aligns with the study conducted by Qiu BS et al., where they similarly reported a poor prognosis for visual recovery in cases of traumatic cataract associated with retinal detachment [29]. Furthermore, in conjunction with traumatic cataract and retinal detachment, our study revealed a statistically significant association between the final visual outcome and the initial visual acuity.

At our center, we perform secondary cataract extraction due to its several advantages, including the potential for more accurate intraocular lens calculation, improved intraoperative visualization, and the benefit of operating in a “quiet” eye, as demonstrated in the study by Memon et al. [30].

Parents must exercise vigilant care and provide proper education to prevent self-injuries in young children. Since children under six are naturally curious [31], it’s crucial to keep sharp objects like scissors, pencils, and knives out of reach. Encourage children to ask for adult help when using sharp items, and teach them not to run or play with these objects [32]. During crowded events like holidays, extra precautions should be taken to ensure sharp objects are inaccessible to children, protecting their eyes and keeping them safe.

Conclusion

This study highlights the high prevalence of self-inflicted penetrating ocular injuries in children under six, with traumatic cataracts occurring in 46.8% of cases. Prompt treatment within 2 days and awareness of sharp objects, particularly knives, are crucial for better outcomes. Male children are more affected, and injury rates peak during spring. Preventive measures, timely intervention, and targeted education are essential to reduce severe ocular injuries in this age group.
