Gut microbiota contributes to heat tolerance in chickens through metabolic remodeling and suppression of thermogenesis
Authors: Sheng Li, Yuyan Wang, Xi Yang, Xiaoqing Li, Qingjie Hu, Yansen Li, Chunmei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a key regulator of host metabolic adaptation to environmental challenges. However, whether breed-specific gut microbiota contributes to variation in heat tolerance among poultry breeds remains unclear. This study used Cyan-shank partridge chickens, a native Chinese breed with superior environmental adaptability, to investigate the role of the gut microbiota in heat tolerance and its underlying mechanisms. Cyan-shank partridge chickens exhibited a higher inflection point temperature of rectal temperature and greater tolerance to heat stress than Arbor Acres (AA) broilers. Under cyclic heat stress, Cyan-shank partridge chickens maintained lower body temperatures, superior growth performance, and reduced expression of thermogenesis-related genes in skeletal muscle and liver. Concurrently, Cyan-shank partridge chickens exhibited lower cecal microbial α-diversity and a distinct microbial community structure compared with AA broilers. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) of Cyan-shank partridge chickens derived microbiota into AA broilers improved growth performance and reduced the expression of the thermogenesis-related genes under heat stress. FMT also substantially remodeled the recipient gut microbiota, decreasing the abundance of Bacteroides while enriching several potentially beneficial taxa, including Limosilactobacillus and Phascolarctobacterium. Furthermore, untargeted metabolomics revealed profound alterations in microbial metabolic functions, particularly in pathways related to the tricarboxylic acid cycle, amino acid metabolism, and neurotransmitter metabolism. These metabolic functional changes support the potential mechanisms by which the altered microbiota may contribute to the observed improvements. These findings demonstrate that the gut microbiota contributes to the superior heat tolerance of Cyan-shank partridge chickens and that this phenotype can be partially transferred through FMT, accompanied by reduced thermogenic activity in recipient broilers. This study provides a potential microbiota-based strategy for improving the heat tolerance of commercial broilers.

Introduction

With rising global temperatures, heat stress has become a major challenge to poultry production. As homeothermic animals, poultry maintain body temperature within a thermoneutral zone, where metabolic heat production and heat dissipation remain in balance with minimal thermoregulatory effort (Meltzer, 1983; Yehia et al., 2025). The upper critical temperature of this zone can be estimated using the inflection point temperature (IPT), which reflects the onset of physiological responses such as increased evaporative heat loss, elevated metabolic activity, or rising body temperature (Chang et al., 2018; Peng et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2025a). When ambient temperature exceeds this threshold, the balance between heat production and heat dissipation is disrupted, resulting in progressive increases in body temperature (Huynh et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2025a). Sustained hyperthermia subsequently triggers a series of physiological and metabolic disturbances that ultimately lead to heat stress.

Modern commercial broiler strains have been intensively selected for rapid growth and improved feed efficiency, resulting in elevated metabolic activity and endogenous heat production, which increase their susceptibility to heat stress (Buzała et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2015). Numerous studies have shown that heat stress impairs intestinal integrity (Nanto-Hara et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023), disrupts hepatic metabolism (Ma et al., 2021, 2022), and promotes skeletal muscle atrophy (Li et al., 2024b), ultimately reducing production performance and increasing mortality (Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). In contrast, indigenous chicken breeds generally exhibit greater tolerance to high-temperature environments (Adu-Asiamah et al., 2021). Cyan-shank partridge chickens, a native breed widely distributed in southern China, are recognized for their strong environmental adaptability. Previous studies have shown that cyclic heat stress affects intestinal health and redox homeostasis in Cyan-shank partridge chickens, although their growth performance remains largely unaffected, indicating superior heat tolerance (Wang et al., 2025a, 2026). Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying this breed-specific heat tolerance may provide valuable insights for developing strategies to enhance thermal resilience in commercial broiler production.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the gut microbiota plays a critical role in host environmental adaptation (Gabanyi et al., 2022; Khakisahneh et al., 2020). Long-term natural selection and artificial breeding can shape distinct microbial communities that influence host metabolism and adaptive capacity (Bo et al., 2019). Previous studies have shown that cold acclimation remodels the gut microbiota of mice, and transplantation of this cold-adapted microbiota enhances thermogenesis and cold tolerance in recipient animals (Bo et al., 2019; Chevalier et al., 2015). Conversely, microbiota depletion impairs thermogenic capacity and lowers body temperature, whereas supplementation with specific microbial strains or metabolites can restore these adaptive responses (Li et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2021). Breed-specific differences in gut microbial composition may likewise contribute to variation in environmental adaptability. Indeed, high-altitude animals, including Tibetan chickens and Tibetan pigs, harbor gut microbiota enriched in butyrate-producing bacteria compared with commercial breeds, a feature thought to facilitate energy metabolism under the combined challenges of cold and hypoxia (Du et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2024). Although the role of the gut microbiota in metabolic adaptation has been extensively studied in mammals, its contribution to the development of heat tolerance in Cyan-shank partridge chickens remains largely unknown.

In the present study, we compared heat tolerance and gut microbiota composition between Cyan-shank partridge chickens and AA broilers. We further employed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) to determine whether the Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota contributes to thermogenic regulation and heat tolerance in recipient broilers and to identify the metabolic pathways potentially involved in this process. This study provides new insights into the interactions between the gut microbial community and the host and may facilitate the development of microbiota-based strategies for improving thermal resilience in commercial poultry.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male Arbor Acres (AA) broiler chickens were obtained from a commercial farm in Jiangsu Province, China, whereas male Cyan-shank partridge chickens (QJ) were sourced from a commercial farm in Guangdong Province, China. Following health status verification, birds were housed in environmentally controlled chambers. These chambers were equipped with temperature and humidity sensors (ZL-TH10TP, Beijing CIMC Technology Co., Ltd., China) to continuously monitor environmental parameters. An intelligent environmental control system (iRVC-045, Beijing Keland Co., Ltd., China) was employed to integrate these data and automatically regulate heating, cooling, and humidification in real time, thereby maintaining environmental conditions within predefined ranges. The system provided a temperature control accuracy of ±1°C and a relative humidity control accuracy of ±7%. Ambient temperature, relative humidity, and lighting schedules were managed in accordance with standard broiler husbandry guidelines. Both AA and QJ broilers were fed the same commercial white-feathered broiler grower diet (19.0% CP and 3030 kcal/kg of ME) until the end of the experiment. Throughout the experimental period, birds had ad libitum access to feed and water, and the diet was formulated to meet the nutritional requirements recommended by the National Research Council (NRC, 2012). The diet formula is shown in Table S1 (Additional file 1).

Inflection point temperature (IPT) assessment

Twenty-four healthy 35-day-old Cyan-shank partridge chickens (QJ) and twenty-four 21-day-old Arbor Acres (AA) broilers with comparable body weights (950 ± 30 g) were selected, with eight replicates per breed and three birds per replicate, and housed in an environmentally controlled chamber for a 7-day acclimation period. During acclimation, ambient temperature was maintained at 24 ± 1°C, and relative humidity was maintained at 60%. Following acclimation, ambient temperature was increased from 20°C to 36°C at a rate of 1°C every 30 min and maintained at 36°C for 30 min, while relative humidity was kept constant at 60% throughout the experiment. From the start of the temperature increase, the rectal and body surface temperatures of the chickens were measured hourly, and respiratory rate was recorded via video. The data were used for subsequent analysis to determine the inflection point temperature. Physiological measurements were obtained from individual birds, and the replicate mean was used as the experimental unit for statistical analysis.

In the fecal microbiota transplantation experiment, twenty-four healthy 21-day-old recipient broilers with similar body weights were selected from the HAF and HQF groups, with eight replicates per group and three birds per replicate. The temperature protocol was initiated at 18°C and increased to 36°C at a rate of 1°C every 30 min, followed by maintenance at 36°C for 30 min, with relative humidity maintained at 60% throughout. Measurement time points and physiological parameters were consistent with those described above.

Experiment design

A total of forty-eight 35-day-old Cyan-shank partridge chickens (QJ) and forty-eight 21-day-old Arbor Acres (AA) broilers with similar body weights (950 ± 30 g) were selected and acclimated at room temperature for 7 days. Following acclimation, birds were randomly assigned to four treatment groups, with eight replicates per group and three birds per replicate. The AA-RT and QJ-RT groups were maintained under thermoneutral conditions (24 ± 1°C, 60% RH), whereas the AA-HT and QJ-HT groups were subjected to cyclic heat stress (34 ± 1°C, 60% RH, 8 h/day). For the remaining time each day, HT groups were returned to thermoneutral conditions (24 ± 1°C, 60% RH) for a total experimental period of 2 weeks. Each thermal condition was conducted in a separate environmental chamber, with multiple replicate cages housed within each chamber. Throughout the experiment, birds had ad libitum access to feed and water. On the final day, rectal temperature was measured during the heat exposure period under fasting conditions.

Preparation of fecal microbial suspension

Donor birds from both QJ-RT and AA-RT groups were selected using the same physiological screening criteria. Healthy individuals showing stable rectal temperature responses during thermal assessment and no abnormal physiological responses were chosen as representative donors of each breed. Donor birds were individually housed in sterilized cages equipped with stainless steel trays for fecal collection. Fresh fecal samples were collected between 06:00 and 08:00 immediately after defecation by continuous observation and were transferred into sterile, sealed containers. An anaerobic environment was established using an anaerobic gas-generating bag during sample handling. All samples were immediately placed at 4°C and processed as soon as possible for fecal suspension preparation.

Fecal microbiota suspensions were prepared according to a previously described method (Wu et al., 2025). Briefly, fecal samples were diluted in sterile 0.75% saline solution and homogenized thoroughly. The homogenate was filtered through double-layer sterile gauze to remove undigested material and particulate impurities. The filtrate was then centrifuged at 3,000 × g at 4°C, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in sterile glycerol-saline solution and stored at -80°C until use. Prior to gavage, viable bacterial counts of each fecal suspension were determined using plate-counting methods. Based on these results, suspensions were serially diluted with sterile saline to obtain a final concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL for both donor groups before subsequent experiments.

Fecal microbiota transplantation

Ninety-six 1-day-old AA broiler chicks were randomly assigned to two groups, with eight replicates per group and six birds per replicate. Chicks in the HAF group received 1 mL of fecal microbiota suspension derived from AA donors, whereas chicks in the HQF group received 1 mL of fecal microbiota suspension derived from Cyan-shank partridge chickens (QJ) donors, administered once daily by oral gavage until 21 days of age. Following the FMT period, both groups were subjected to cyclic heat stress in an environmentally controlled chamber (34 ± 1°C, 60% RH, 8 h/day) for 7 consecutive days, with the remaining time maintained under thermoneutral conditions (24 ± 1°C, 60% RH). Throughout the experiment, birds had ad libitum access to feed and water. On the final day, rectal temperature was measured during the heat exposure period under fasting conditions.

Sample collection

Feed intake and body weight gain per cage were recorded throughout the experimental period to calculate the feed conversion ratio (FCR). One bird was randomly selected from each replicate within each treatment group for sample collection. Blood samples were collected from the wing vein and allowed to clot at room temperature. Samples were then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting serum was collected and stored at -20°C until further analysis. Birds were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation followed by exsanguination. Pectoral muscle and liver tissues, as well as cecal contents, were collected and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored for subsequent analyses.

Rectal temperature measurement

Rectal temperature was measured individually under fasting conditions using a digital rectal thermometer (TH-5, Physitemp, Clifton, NJ, USA). The thermistor probe was inserted approximately 2–3 cm into the rectum, and temperature readings were recorded after stabilization. The measurement accuracy of the device was ± 0.1°C.

Surface temperature measurement

Broilers were placed on a temperature-controlled platform, and thermal images were captured using an infrared thermographic camera (TiS55+, Fluke, USA; accuracy ± 0.1 °C). Thermographic images were analyzed using SmartView Classic software (Fluke, USA), and head skin temperature was quantified and used as an indicator of body surface temperature.

Respiratory behavior monitoring

Respiratory activity was continuously recorded using a video camera throughout the experimental period. Respiratory rate was determined based on the rhythmic movements of the chest and abdomen. Each bird was observed for 1 min per measurement, and the average of three independent measurements was used to calculate the respiratory rate.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from cecal content samples. Six samples meeting the predefined DNA quality requirements were selected from each group for 16S rRNA sequencing. The V3-V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR using universal primers 338F and 806R. Sequencing libraries were constructed and quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw sequencing data were processed using DADA2 (v1.24.0) for quality filtering, denoising, and chimera removal, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were subsequently inferred. Alpha diversity indices, including Sobs, Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou indices, were calculated to evaluate microbial richness and diversity within samples. Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances. Differentially abundant taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a threshold of LDA score > 2.0. DNA extraction, PCR amplification, sequencing, and bioinformatic processing were conducted by Majorbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Metabolomics

Cecal contents were homogenized and transferred into centrifuge tubes, and a mixture of methanol containing formic acid was added for metabolite extraction, followed by vortex mixing. Samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. Metabolite profiling was performed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 0.04% acetic acid in water (phase A) and 0.04% acetic acid in acetonitrile (phase B). Raw data were normalized to the total ion intensity, and metabolites were annotated based on accurate mass, adduct ions, and characteristic fragment ions. Metabolite identification and validation were performed using the mzCloud high-resolution mass spectrometry database and the ChemSpider chemical structure database. Differential metabolites were identified based on partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), using fold change (FC), Student’s t-test P-values, and variable importance in projection (VIP) scores, with thresholds set at |FC| > 1.0, P < 0.05, and VIP > 1. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the hypergeometric test implemented in the ClusterProfiler package. Quality control for sample preparation, instrumental analysis, and data processing was performed by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from pectoral muscle and liver tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed prior to reverse transcription, and cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ABclonal, Wuhan, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a QuantStudio 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with SYBR Green Master Mix (ABclonal, China), using cDNA as the template. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference gene. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2 (Additional file 1).

Serum hormone analysis

Serum corticosterone concentrations were determined using a commercial ELISA kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Serum thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) concentrations were measured by radioimmunoassay using 125I-thyroxine and 125I-3,3′,5-triiodothyronine radioimmunoassay kits (Beijing North Biotechnology Research Institute Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analysis

Based on previous studies (Huynh et al., 2005; Wang et al., 2025a), a broken-line model (BLM) was applied using piecewise regression analysis. Nonlinear piecewise regression was performed using SPSS 18.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to analyze body surface temperature, rectal temperature, and respiratory rate under progressively increasing ambient temperature conditions. The BLM equations were defined as follows:

When AT ≥ IPT: Y = C + Z × (AT − IPT)

When AT < IPT: Y = C where Y represents body temperature or respiratory rate, C denotes the baseline value when thermoregulatory parameters remain stable, Z represents the slope after the inflection point, AT is the ambient temperature, and IPT is the inflection point temperature at which thermophysiological responses begin to change.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 8e; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the main effects of temperature and breed, as well as their interaction effects. When significant main or interaction effects were detected, one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test was performed for post hoc comparisons. Differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, and differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Cyan-shank partridge chickens exhibit stronger heat resistance under high-temperature conditions

As ambient temperature increased, rectal temperature rose in both breeds (Fig. 1A). At 28°C, rectal temperature was significantly higher in AA broilers than in Cyan-shank partridge chickens (QJ) (P < 0.05). A broken-line model was applied to estimate the IPT. When ambient temperature was below the IPT, rectal temperature remained stable, with baseline values of 40.80°C in AA broilers and 40.98°C in QJ chickens, respectively (Fig. 1B and C). Once ambient temperature exceeded 24.2°C, rectal temperature in AA broilers increased linearly, rising by 0.17°C for every 1°C increase in temperature, whereas rectal temperature in QJ chickens did not begin to increase until ambient temperature reached 27.25°C. Body surface temperature exhibited a similar temperature-dependent pattern, increasing with rising ambient temperature (Fig. 1D). During the initial warming phase, body surface temperature was significantly higher in AA broilers than in QJ chickens (P < 0.05). At 28°C, body surface temperature in AA broilers transiently decreased and became lower than that of QJ chickens (P < 0.05). However, with further temperature elevation, it increased again and was significantly higher than that of QJ chickens at 36°C (P < 0.05). The IPT values for body surface temperature were 23.77°C in AA broilers and 23.29°C in QJ chickens, respectively (Fig. 1E and F). Respiratory rate increased progressively with increasing ambient temperature (Fig. 1G). Below 28°C, respiratory rate in QJ chickens was significantly lower than that in AA broilers (P < 0.05). However, once ambient temperature exceeded 28°C, respiratory rate in QJ chickens increased sharply and remained higher than that of AA broilers (P < 0.05). Significant differences were observed in cecal microbiota composition between Cyan-shank partridge chickens and AA broilers

We further analyzed the cecal microbiota composition of the two broiler breeds under RT and HT conditions (Fig. 4). Under RT conditions, the Shannon and Pielou indices of the cecal microbiota were significantly lower in QJ chickens than in AA broilers (P < 0.05), indicating reduced α-diversity (Fig. 4A). This difference was not observed under HT conditions (P > 0.05). Neither ambient temperature nor breed significantly affected the Sobs, ACE, or Chao1 indices (P > 0.05). PCoA revealed that ambient temperature induced only minor separation in β-diversity, whereas a clear and significant segregation between QJ and AA chickens was observed regardless of temperature, indicating distinct cecal microbial community structures between the two breeds (Fig. 4B). At the phylum level, the dominant phyla were generally similar between QJ and AA chickens (Fig. 4C). Compared with the AA-RT group, the QJ-RT group exhibited an increased relative abundance of Bacteroidetes and a decreased abundance of Firmicutes. At the genus level, Bacteroides and Phascolarctobacterium were the predominant taxa (Fig. 4D). Relative to the AA-RT group, QJ-RT chickens showed an increase in the abundances of Bacteroides and Phascolarctobacterium. Discussion

Compared with commercial broiler breeds, indigenous chicken breeds generally exhibit superior environmental adaptability and greater resistance to environmental stressors. Although accumulating evidence suggests that the gut microbiota plays a critical role in regulating host metabolism and environmental adaptation, its contribution to the formation of adaptive traits in indigenous chickens remains poorly understood. In the present study, we demonstrated that Cyan-shank partridge chickens exhibited enhanced heat tolerance and a distinct gut microbial composition compared with commercial broilers. Importantly, transplantation of the Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota partially transferred the heat-tolerant phenotype to recipient broilers, accompanied by extensive remodeling of microbial and metabolic profiles. These findings provide functional evidence for the role of gut microbiota in the development of heat tolerance in indigenous chickens.

Sensible heat loss through radiation and conduction represents an important avenue of heat dissipation under moderate thermal conditions. As ambient temperature continues to rise, the thermal gradient between the body surface and the surrounding environment progressively decreases, reducing the efficiency of radiative and conductive heat transfer (Adomako and Asamoah, 2025; Tickle and Codd, 2019). Under these conditions, evaporative heat loss through respiratory cooling becomes the predominant mechanism for heat dissipation (Nascimento et al., 2017). When heat dissipation can no longer offset heat production, core body temperature begins to increase. The inflection point temperature, estimated using the broken-line model, is widely used to define this critical thermal threshold. Previous studies have reported an IPT range of 26.52–27.02°C for Cyan-shank partridge chickens, which is higher than the upper critical temperature reported for commercial laying hens and broiler chickens (Chang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2025a). Consistent with these observations, the present study showed that Cyan-shank partridge chickens exhibited a higher IPT for rectal temperature but lower IPTs for body surface temperature and respiratory rate than AA broilers. These findings indicate that Cyan-shank partridge chickens initiate thermoregulatory responses, particularly respiratory heat dissipation, at lower ambient temperatures, thereby delaying the rise in core body temperature under heat stress conditions.

Sustained heat exposure disrupts the balance between metabolic heat production and heat dissipation, triggering a range of physiological and metabolic responses collectively referred to as heat stress. Numerous studies have demonstrated that heat stress impairs growth performance by reducing feed intake and body weight gain (Al Amaz et al., 2024; Goel, 2021). However, breed-dependent variation in heat tolerance has been widely reported, with native tropical chicken breeds generally exhibiting greater resilience than commercial strains in terms of thermoregulation, physiological stability, and production performance (Feng et al., 2024; Nawaz et al., 2023; Renaudeau et al., 2012). A previous study has shown that cyclic heat stress (32°C for 4 h/day) does not significantly affect the growth performance of Cyan-shank partridge chickens (Wang et al., 2026). Similarly, in the present study, Cyan-shank partridge chickens maintained lower body temperatures and superior growth performance compared with AA broilers under heat stress conditions. Moreover, significant breed and temperature interactions were observed for these traits, further supporting the greater heat tolerance of this indigenous breed. These differences may be partly explained by variation in metabolic heat production. Intensive selection for rapid growth and feed efficiency has increased metabolic activity and endogenous thermogenesis in commercial broilers, making them more susceptible to thermal challenge (Tickle and Codd, 2019). In contrast, indigenous breeds generally exhibit lower metabolic rates and reduced heat production (Kong et al., 2024). Supporting this notion, Cyan-shank partridge chickens displayed lower expression of thermogenesis-related genes under heat stress. Because pathways involved in uncoupled respiration and calcium cycling contribute to energy dissipation and thermogenesis, their downregulation may reduce endogenous heat production while preserving energy for growth (Chouchani et al., 2016; Maurya et al., 2018; Periasamy et al., 2017). Collectively, these findings suggest that Cyan-shank partridge chickens achieve superior heat tolerance through the combined effects of enhanced heat dissipation and reduced metabolic thermogenesis.

Gut microbiota composition is shaped by multiple factors, including host genetics, diet, and environmental conditions, and plays a critical role in regulating host metabolism and physiological adaptation. Long-term genetic selection and environmental adaptation can drive the formation of breed-specific microbial communities. For example, animals adapted to high-altitude environments, such as Tibetan chickens and Tibetan pigs, harbor distinct gut microbiota enriched in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, which may support energy metabolism under hypoxic and cold conditions (Wu et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2024). Similarly, previous studies have reported that Cyan-shank partridge chickens are enriched in SCFA-producing genera, including Bacteroides and Prevotella, potentially reflecting their unique metabolic characteristics (Liu et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023). Consistent with these observations, we identified marked differences in cecal microbiota composition between Cyan-shank partridge chickens and AA broilers, characterized by lower microbial diversity and distinct community structures in Cyan-shank partridge chickens. Previous studies of chicken gut microbiota development have shown that, although microbial colonization is highly dynamic during early life, the community becomes relatively stable after approximately 21 days of age (Feng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Therefore, although the influence of the age difference in the present study may have been limited, age-related differences in microbiota maturation cannot be completely excluded because the two breeds were matched for body weight rather than chronological age. Importantly, because both breeds were maintained under the same experimental conditions and received the same diet, the observed microbial differences likely reflect intrinsic breed-associated factors. Furthermore, although previous studies have shown that heat stress can alter microbial diversity and community structure (Ncho, 2025; Wang et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022), the effects observed in our study were relatively modest. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in heat-stress protocols, including temperature intensity, exposure duration, and experimental design.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in host environmental adaptation. In rodents, depletion of the gut microbiota impairs thermogenic capacity and cold adaptation, whereas microbial recolonization or supplementation with microbiota-derived metabolites restores these functions (Li et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2021). Furthermore, studies have shown that FMT can transfer nutritional intervention- induced heat-resistant phenotypes in mice, further highlighting the importance of the gut microbiota in host phenotype transfer (Wang et al., 2024b, 2025b). Consistent with these findings, transplantation of Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota improved growth performance, delayed the increase in body temperature, and reduced the expression of thermogenesis-related genes in recipient broilers under heat stress. This suggests that the gut microbiota is not a bystander but a significant driver in shaping heat tolerance. FMT using Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota markedly altered the microbial composition of recipient broilers, characterized by reduced abundance of Bacteroides and enrichment of Phascolarctobacterium, Barnesiella, and Limosilactobacillus. These taxa have been linked to short-chain fatty acid production, energy metabolism, and host metabolic regulation (Qiu et al., 2025). Specifically, SCFAs such as butyrate produced by Bacteroides can upregulate UCP1 expression through the “SCFAs-FFAR2-UCP1” pathway, promoting adaptive thermogenesis to maintain body temperature (Bo et al., 2023; Li et al., 2019). Therefore, the reduced abundance of Bacteroides may have contributed to the decreased expression of thermogenic genes, including avUCP, observed in this study. Phascolarctobacterium is a major producer of propionate, which serves not only as an energy substrate but also as a regulator of hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism (Lan et al., 2025). Similarly, members of the genus Limosilactobacillus have been implicated in lipid metabolism, maintenance of intestinal homeostasis, and resilience to environmental stressors (Lee et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024a). Consistent with these findings, correlation analysis in the present study revealed that Bacteroides was positively associated with thermogenesis-related gene expression, whereas Limosilactobacillus and Phascolarctobacterium were negatively associated with these traits. Therefore, this structural remodeling of the gut microbiota may promote heat tolerance by suppressing thermogenesis and optimizing metabolic homeostasis, thereby reducing endogenous heat production under thermal challenge. It should be noted that broilers receiving Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota also exhibited greater feed intake during heat stress, which may have contributed to the observed microbial and metabolomic differences. Although this increase may represent part of the physiological response to FMT (Yu et al., 2024), the absence of a pair-fed control prevents us from distinguishing direct microbiota-mediated effects from those secondary to altered nutrient intake.

Recent studies have demonstrated that gut microbiota can influence adaptive thermogenesis through microbial metabolites and host metabolic pathways (Cheng et al., 2025; Li et al., 2017). Consistent with this concept, we observed marked differences in cecal metabolite profiles between the HAF and HQF groups, primarily involving metabolites related to the TCA cycle, amino acid metabolism, and neurotransmitter signaling. The TCA cycle, as a core hub of cellular energy metabolism, drives ATP synthesis by oxidizing carbon sources to generate NADH and FADH2(Arnold and Finley, 2023). Simultaneously, its intermediates provide the carbon skeleton for amino acid and fatty acid synthesis, coupling energy generation with biosynthesis and providing substrates for UCP-mediated uncoupling respiration (Arnold and Finley, 2023). Notably, several TCA cycle intermediates, including citrate, isocitrate, and NAD⁺, were enriched in the HQF group. Given the concurrent reduction in avUCP expression, these changes may reflect a shift in mitochondrial metabolism toward energy utilization rather than thermogenesis. Furthermore, creatine levels were reduced in the cecum of HQF broilers. Although direct microbial metabolism of creatine has not been demonstrated, this change may reflect altered availability of creatine precursors and subsequent modulation of host creatine metabolism (Wang et al., 2024a). Because creatine-dependent substrate cycling contributes to non-shivering thermogenesis, the decrease in creatine may further suppress heat production through a UCP-independent mechanism (Bunk et al., 2025; Kazak et al., 2015). Collectively, these findings suggest that microbiota-driven metabolic remodeling contributes to heat tolerance by reprogramming host energy metabolism and reducing endogenous heat production, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

Body temperature homeostasis and energy metabolism in homeothermic animals are tightly regulated by the central nervous system. Increasing evidence indicates that the gut microbiota influences host metabolism, appetite, behavior, and thermoregulation through the gut-brain axis, partly via microbial metabolites and neurotransmitter signaling (Bo et al., 2019; Gabanyi et al., 2022). Consistent with this concept, our previous study showed that antibiotic-induced disruption of the gut microbiota altered hypothalamic neurotransmitter profiles and was accompanied by changes in thermogenic metabolism (Li et al., 2023). Notably, FMT using Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota significantly altered neurotransmitter-related metabolites in the cecal contents of recipient broilers, characterized by reduced dopamine and GABA levels and increased acetylcholine levels. Dopamine and GABA have been implicated in the regulation of energy expenditure and thermogenesis (Folgueira et al., 2019; Geisler et al., 2021), whereas acetylcholine promotes heat dissipation through both peripheral and central thermoregulatory pathways (Beckman and Carlisle, 1969; Hu et al., 2018). Specifically, reduced dopamine signaling has been associated with decreased sympathetic activation of thermogenic tissues (Folgueira et al., 2019), while GABA can modulate energy expenditure by influencing peripheral thermogenic activity (Geisler et al., 2021). In contrast, acetylcholine has been shown to facilitate heat loss by promoting vasodilation and activating thermoregulatory circuits in the hypothalamus (Beckman and Carlisle, 1969). Correlation analysis further linked these neurotransmitter-related metabolites to specific bacterial taxa and thermoregulatory phenotypes in this study. Therefore, these neurotransmitter changes may contribute to the reduced thermogenesis and improved heat tolerance observed in HQF broilers. However, because central nervous system responses were not directly assessed, the potential involvement of gut-brain axis signaling remains speculative.

In summary, Cyan-shank partridge chickens exhibited greater heat tolerance than AA broilers, characterized by lower thermogenic activity and improved thermal homeostasis under heat stress. Furthermore, transplantation of Cyan-shank partridge chicken-derived microbiota reshaped the cecal microbial and metabolic profiles of recipient broilers, reduced metabolic thermogenesis, and improved heat tolerance under thermal challenge. Collectively, these findings demonstrate an important role for the gut microbiota in regulating metabolic thermogenesis and heat tolerance in chickens. This study deepens our understanding of host–microbiota interactions and offers a potential microbiota-based strategy for improving heat tolerance in commercial broiler production.
