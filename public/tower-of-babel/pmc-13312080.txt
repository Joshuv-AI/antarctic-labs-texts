POCUS in pediatric arrest (Res-US pulse check): development and evaluation of a simulation-based curriculum
Authors: Tasuku Takadera, Elana Thau, Lianne McLean, Laura Simone, Deborah Schonfeld, Jabeen Fayyaz, Noa Kvatinsky, Sasha Litwin, Maya Harel-Sterling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A simulation-based curriculum was developed to integrate point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) into a pediatric cardiac arrest protocol.•The Res-US Pulse Check protocol provides a structured approach to the POCUS pulse check during CPR.•The curriculum improved pulse detection accuracy and efficiency in simulated pediatric resuscitation.•POCUS pulse checks did not prolong CPR pauses.

Introduction

During pediatric cardiac arrest, rapid and accurate pulse assessment is critical to minimize delays in chest compressions. Current Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) guidelines emphasize limiting pulse assessments to no more than 10-s.1, 2 However, manual pulse palpation in pediatric cardiac arrest is frequently inaccurate and unreliable, with both false-positive and false-negative assessments reported.3 Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has emerged as a valuable adjunct during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). When performed by experienced personnel, it can improve the accuracy of detecting arterial pulsatility and identification of cardiac activity, and is an additional diagnostic tool looking for specific reversible causes of arrest.4, 5, 6, 7 However, cardiac POCUS has important limitations to consider, including substantial interobserver variability when assessing cardiac standstill,8, 9 and concerns that ultrasound use may inadvertently prolong interruptions in chest compressions if performed without appropriate training or structure.10, 11 Therefore, its use requires careful training, interpretation, and integration within the clinical context.

While the use of POCUS during CPR has expanded rapidly in adult resuscitation, pediatric-specific protocols remain limited.12, 13 Compared with adult resuscitation, pediatric cardiac arrest is a relatively rare event but is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.14 Given the high-acuity, low-frequency nature of pediatric cardiac arrest, simulation-based training plays a critical role in maintaining resuscitation readiness.15 Simulation provides an optimal modality for teaching and assessing high-acuity, low-occurrence (HALO) skills in a psychologically and physically safe environment that supports deliberate practice, feedback, and competency assessment.16 Implementing a structured, simulation-based curriculum prior to clinical application enables safer skill acquisition and reduces the risk of CPR interruptions due to operator inexperience.17, 18

Competency-based medical education (CBME) provides an outcomes-based approach to developing and assessing specific clinical skills through deliberate practice.19 The Hospital for Sick Children emergency department has a well-established CBME program, originally launched in 2016 for faculty and later adapted for trainees, the program provides structured pathways for evaluating core pediatric emergency medicine (PEM) competencies.14, 20 However, formal instruction on integrating POCUS into pediatric cardiac arrest management was absent prior to the introduction of this curriculum. We addressed this gap through the introduction of a simulation-based curriculum, which aimed to develop, implement, and evaluate a structured protocol for integration of POCUS into cardiac arrest management. We hypothesized that the introduction of a structured protocol and multimodal learning curriculum would improve pulse assessment accuracy and learner confidence in POCUS integration into pediatric cardiac arrest, without prolonging interruptions in chest compressions.

Methods

Study setting and population

This study was conducted at the Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Canada. Participants included PEM staff physicians and subspecialty fellows. The curriculum was developed and delivered as part of the institution’s competency-based medical education (CBME) program for both groups. This work was approved as part of a Quality Improvement project through the Hospital for Sick Children Quality Management Department and did not require formal Research Ethics Board review. Participant consent was obtained for collection of performance and survey data, as well as video recording of simulations.

Curriculum development

This simulation-based educational curriculum employed a structured training approach, grounded in rapid cycle deliberate practice, with immediate and targeted feedback to optimize skill acquisition.17, 18 A multimodal, flipped-classroom design was used, combining an online learning module with in-person simulation-based training.

The curriculum consisted of three phases:

Phase 1: POCUS-integrated resuscitation protocol development

A group of Pediatric Emergency Medicine physicians with expertise in POCUS and resuscitation developed a standardized protocol for integrating POCUS into pediatric cardiac arrest management within existing resuscitation workflows (see Fig. 1 – Res-US Pulse Check Algorithm). The algorithm was informed by current best available evidence in pediatric POCUS and resuscitation literature and was based on the most up-to-date PALS guidelines for high-quality CPR.2 Consensus was achieved through iterative discussion and review within the development group, with refinement of workflow steps based on feasibility testing in simulation and collective agreement on usability and clarity. The resulting protocol was then tested by the study team in a simulation environment and further refined for clarity based on iterative feedback.Fig. 1Res-US pulse check algorithm.

Res-US pulse check algorithm.

The Res-US Pulse Check Algorithm (Fig. 1) focuses on pulseless electrical activity (PEA) and asystole. It incorporates early epinephrine administration and high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) during a 2-min cycle, followed by preparation for a POCUS-assisted pulse check with predefined probe selection and imaging views. To minimize interruptions to chest compressions, the protocol places an emphasis on completing the POCUS pulse check within 10 s. The femoral artery was designated as the first-line site for pulse assessment, with the carotid artery permitted when clinically appropriate, based on patient age and access constraints during resuscitation. Following the initial POCUS pulse check, a subxiphoid cardiac view was incorporated to assess for potential reversible causes of cardiac arrest, including pericardial effusion and right heart strain. Within the algorithm, absence of cardiac activity or effective contractility on cardiac POCUS was defined as pulselessness and triggered resumption of CPR. To reduce the risk of prolonged CPR pauses related to defibrillator pad interference, the subxiphoid view was recommended with immediate gel removal. Parasternal long-axis views were permitted if a subxiphoid view could not be obtained. After return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), cardiac POCUS reassessment was integrated into the algorithm to guide post-resuscitation management, including evaluation for further interventions such as fluid resuscitation, vasopressor support, or inotropic therapy.

Phase 2: Online pre-learning

Pre-learning was delivered using a customized online module (Affinity Learning) with 20 image interpretation cases of POCUS pulse checks (10 pulseless, 10 with a pulse), each presented with a 10-s countdown timer. The module covered the POCUS-integrated resuscitation protocol, relevant anatomy and sonoanatomy, scanning techniques with instructional videos, common pitfalls, and video examples of POCUS-integrated resuscitation performed by expert faculty (Appendix 1).

Phase 3: Simulation-based training

Participants engaged in simulation training using an ultrasound-compatible task trainer and a pediatric cardiac arrest scenario delivered using a Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RCDP) approach.13, 14 A low-cost, high-fidelity ultrasound simulator was incorporated for image interpretation during resuscitation scenarios (Appendix 1).21

Simulation scenario and environment

Three in-person simulation sessions were conducted: two for PEM staff physicians and one for PEM fellows. Each 45-min session included hands-on practice scanning a pulse on an ultrasound-compatible task trainer (CentraLineMan System, Simulab) supplemented, as needed, by carotid artery scanning on a volunteer using a Venue GO ultrasound system (GE Healthcare). Sessions also incorporated a review of the ultrasound machine, including knobology, and a structured pre-brief with instruction on the use of the ultrasound simulator.

The simulation case involved a 4-year-old child with a complex medical history who presented in pulseless electrical activity (PEA) arrest following respiratory failure (Appendix 2). Participants were required to integrate POCUS into the standard Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) PEA algorithm, identify the presence or absence of a femoral pulse using POCUS, assess for cardiac standstill and reversible causes of arrest, while maintaining the standards of the high-quality CPR throughout the scenario.

During the CBME session, participants were divided into groups of three to four. The scenario was delivered using a Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RCDP) approach.17, 18 The simulation scenario was paused at the end of each cardiac resuscitation cycle, during which participants received targeted debriefing focused on POCUS settings, probe selection and application, resuscitation workflow including duration of interruption in chest compressions, image interpretation and management. Roles were rotated after each pause and debriefing session. Each group completed a final uninterrupted run-through at the conclusion of the scenario. This structure ensured that each participant had the opportunity to serve as both team leader and POCUS operator at least once. The same ultrasound used in the clinical resuscitation environment was used for all simulations (Venue Go, GE Healthcare). During the resuscitation scenario, image acquisition and interpretation was performed using a low-cost, high-fidelity POCUS simulator (EDUS2J, Java version).21 However, participants were required to select the appropriate probe and preset on the Venue Go system prior to applying the simulator probe, thereby replicating the clinical workflow.

Data collection

Participant demographics and baseline characteristics, including prior POCUS experience were collected, along with frequency of clinical POCUS use and POCUS credentialing status. Data from the online pre-learning module, including module completion and performance on a 20-case image interpretation assessment was collected. The pre-learning module consisted of femoral, carotid, and simulator-based ultrasound clips depicting both pulse and pulseless conditions, randomly distributed across 20 cases (10 of each). Each case was presented with a 10-s countdown timer, and the time from start of video to participant response was recorded. Interpretation accuracy was defined as correct identification of pulse presence or absence. The assessment was time-limited and configured to prevent revision of prior responses.

Simulation sessions were video recorded using two cameras positioned at different angles. Skill assessment using a checklist and global rating scale was performed for both staff and fellow sessions (Appendix 3). However, detailed time-based analysis of resuscitation performance and pulse-check interpretation during simulation was conducted exclusively for the PEM fellow sessions. The staff physician group included individuals with variable years of independent clinical practice and differing levels of POCUS experience and credentialing, including POCUS faculty, resulting in substantial heterogeneity in baseline characteristics. To allow for a more homogeneous analytic cohort and more interpretable time-based performance metrics, detailed video analysis was therefore limited to the fellow cohort. Video recordings from the PEM fellow simulation session were analyzed using an event logging and annotation software (ELAN version 7.0; The Language Archive, Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics, Nijmegen, The Netherlands). Videos were reviewed in slow motion with audio enabled. Time stamps were recorded for predefined events, including cessation of chest compressions, verbal call-out of pulse or pulselessness, instruction to resume chest compressions, and actual resumption of chest compressions. Pause duration was defined as the interval from cessation of chest compressions to resumption of chest compressions.

Participants completed pre- and post-curriculum surveys assessing their confidence with POCUS use in the context of pediatric cardiac arrest. Confidence was rated on a 5-point Likert scale across multiple domains of resuscitation, leading a team, and POCUS use during cardiac arrest.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was pulse detection accuracy, defined as correct identification of pulse presence or absence. Secondary outcomes for the online module included completion of responses within the 10-s time limit and interpretation time, reported as median and mean response times. Secondary outcomes for the in-person simulation sessions included overall performance, assessed using a Global Rating Scale (GRS). For the fellow cohort only, additional secondary outcomes included decision time and chest compression pause duration. Educational outcomes for all simulation participants included changes in self-reported confidence and perceived skill improvement, satisfaction with the curriculum, and perceived barriers to POCUS use during cardiac arrest.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as means ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze performance metrics and survey items measured on a 5-point Likert scale. Effect sizes for pre- and post-curriculum comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d, (small effect (d = 0.2), moderate effect (d = 0.5), and large effect (d ≥ 0.8)).22, 23 All statistical analyses were performed using Stata MP version 19.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

In-person simulation training

Ten PEM fellows participated in the in-person simulation sessions, all of whom completed the pre-course online module (Table 1). Across the analyzed simulation sessions, fellows performed a total of 23 pulse-check interpretations: 11 during Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RCDP) and 12 during full run-through simulations. Of the 23 pulse checks, 18 were pulseless and 5 had a pulse. Pulse identification accuracy was 100% (5/5), while accuracy for identifying pulselessness was 94% (17/18). After excluding one instructor-interrupted scenario, 82% (14/17) of pulseless cases reached a pulse/no-pulse decision verbalized within the recommended 10-s threshold. Chest compressions were also resumed within 10 s in 82% of cases. The median time from stopping compressions to making a decision to resume compressions or to hold compressions was 4.6 s (IQR 3.8–8.1 s), with a mean of 6.5 s. The median chest compression pause was 6.8 s (IQR 5.6–9.0 s), and the longest pause recorded was 12.5 s. The longest interpretation time (27.1 s), occurred in a scenario with a pulse, and therefore did not lead to a delay in performing chest compressions. Overall performance during the simulation session was assessed for all simulation participants (N = 29) using a checklist and global rating scale (GRS) (scale from 1 to 5, where 1 is ‘not yet competent’, 3 is ‘competent’ and 5 is ‘superior’) (Appendix 3). 17.2% scored 3 out of 5 (competent), 41.3% scored 4 out of 5, and 41.3% scored 5 out of 5 (superior).

Satisfaction and confidence

Participants’ confidence in overall use of POCUS in cardiac arrest increased from a mean of 2.5/5 pre-training to 3.9/5 post-training, with Cohen’s d = 1.47; large effect size (see Table 4). Participants reported that the simulation training most improved their understanding of clinical integration of POCUS (84.6%), resuscitation choreography (80.8%), and image interpretation of POCUS pulse checks (69.2%) (Fig. 3). Following completion of the simulation curriculum, 96% of participants agreed or strongly agreed that integrating POCUS into cardiac arrest simulation training was a valuable component of their education and that the session enhanced their understanding of how to use POCUS clinically in cardiac arrest (mean 4.7/5 for both measures). Barriers identified

Several barriers to POCUS use during cardiac arrest were identified in the post-curriculum survey (Fig. 4). The most frequently reported barriers included limited availability of qualified personnel to perform POCUS (57.7%), limited expertise or comfort with image acquisition (53.8%), limited expertise or comfort with image interpretation (46.2%), and environmental or access constraints during resuscitation, including space and time limitations (46.2%).Fig. 4Perceived barriers to POCUS use in cardiac arrest.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effectiveness of a structured educational curriculum designed to integrate POCUS into pediatric cardiac arrest using a combination of asynchronous online pre-learning and in-person simulation training. We found that when explicit emphasis was placed on minimizing interruptions, incorporation of POCUS did not prolong cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) pauses beyond 10 s in the majority of simulated cases, suggesting that structured choreography training using Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RCDP) may help limit CPR pause duration when incorporating POCUS. Importantly, this curriculum was successfully embedded within an existing competency-based medical education (CBME) framework, demonstrating the feasibility of training PEM physicians to adopt new technologies to enhance resuscitation practice.14, 20 Learner confidence improved across most domains related to POCUS use during resuscitation, further supporting the educational value of this approach.

Manual pulse palpation during cardiac arrest has been shown to be unreliable in prior studies,6, 7 with particularly low accuracy reported in pediatric populations, where smaller vessel size and patient characteristics further complicate pulse detection.3 POCUS has been increasingly incorporated into adult cardiac arrest workflows to support pulse assessment and identification of reversible causes.24, 25 However, concerns persist that POCUS use may prolong CPR pauses in the absence of adequate training.10, 11 Our findings suggest that CPR pause duration can be minimized through accurate pulse interpretation supported by structured training. The online pre-learning module, which incorporated timed image interpretation assessments, proved to be an effective adjunct for evaluating POCUS-based pulse detection accuracy. Prior studies have demonstrated that randomly assigned ultrasound image sets can be used to assess interpretation skills for identifying pulse presence or pulselessness.26 In this context, our findings also support the use of online image interpretation training as a complementary modality when combined with in-person simulation-based education.

Recent adult literature suggests that carotid POCUS pulse checks may be a faster alternative to subxiphoid cardiac assessments.27 In our simulation exercises, participants were generally able to promptly resume CPR upon identification of pulselessness, with only one instance in which the decision to resume compressions exceeded 10 s (14.7 s). This outlier occurred using a subxiphoid cardiac assessment, and highlights potential challenges associated with probe positioning for subxiphoid views and interpretation of cardiac contractility, both of which may contribute to prolonged CPR pauses if not adequately addressed.8, 9, 13, 25 We therefore prioritized femoral POCUS pulse checks over subxiphoid cardiac views in our protocol due known limitations of cardiac POCUS during arrest, including variable definitions and low interobserver agreement reported in multiple studies.8, 9

Building on these findings, the introduction of POCUS into pediatric cardiac arrest requires careful consideration of the balance between improved pulse detection and identification of reversible causes, with the risk of prolonging interruptions in chest compressions, as well as logistical challenges in obtaining optimal views.10, 11, 24, 28 Several strategies may mitigate these challenges. First, incorporating a standardized verbal countdown during pulse checks can reinforce time awareness and limit scanning duration.24 Second, targeted training to improve proficiency in acquiring and interpreting subxiphoid cardiac views may enhance both image acquisition efficiency and diagnostic confidence.13 Finally, regular reinforcement of POCUS-assisted resuscitation skills within a competency-based medical education (CBME) framework may support skill retention.29

Post-curriculum survey results also identified several barriers to POCUS use during cardiac arrest, including limited availability of trained personnel and discomfort with both image acquisition and interpretation. These findings highlight the need for broader faculty development and support the importance of training a larger proportion of PEM physicians to competently perform and interpret POCUS during resuscitation.30 Additional barriers, such as time constraints, physical space limitations, and interference between ultrasound gel and defibrillator pads, reflect practical challenges inherent to high-acuity resuscitation environments.30 Repeated, structured training that emphasizes resuscitation choreography, time awareness, and equipment management, including through in-situ simulation, may help address these challenges and facilitate sustained clinical integration.14, 21 Notably, participants’ ability to identify these real-world barriers following brief simulation exposure further underscores the educational value of this curriculum.

Our study has several important strengths to highlight. The curriculum was intentionally structured around Rapid Cycle Deliberate Practice (RCDP), as described by Hunt and colleagues, which is particularly well suited to teaching time-sensitive and team-based resuscitation skills.17, 31 Rather than focusing only on technical image acquisition, we used RCDP to rehearse the timing, coordination, and role clarity required to integrate POCUS into the workflow of cardiac arrest management. Although decision time did not differ significantly between interrupted RCDP segments and the final uninterrupted run-through, decision times tended to be shorter during the final run-through segment. This pattern may reflect a learning effect resulting from repetitive, feedback-driven practice during RCDP segments. Additionally, our curriculum addresses an emerging gap in pediatric emergency medicine, related to the defining an optimal protocol and the training required for effective POCUS integration in cardiac arrest, without causing harm or delays in chest compressions.

Our study has some important limitations to consider. This was a single center study, and the simulation-based design cannot fully replicate real-world resuscitation dynamics. However, the controlled simulation environment allowed for standardized measurement of pulse-check timing and interpretation accuracy. Additionally, due to the inherent limitations of the simulation environment, this study did not compare pulse check duration or accuracy between POCUS and manual palpation. Due to variability in baseline POCUS experience among PEM staff physicians, analysis of simulation session outcomes was limited to the fellow cohort only. While this highly motivated learner group was well suited for an initial feasibility study, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to staff physicians and to providers with varying levels of resuscitation and ultrasound experience.

Future work should focus on the translation of these findings to the clinical environment, including evaluation of POCUS-assisted pulse checks and comparison to manual pulse checks during real pediatric cardiac arrests using video review, and assessment of downstream resuscitation and patient-centered outcomes. Expansion of this curriculum across institutions and integration into routine CBME activities may further support safe and effective adoption of POCUS in pediatric cardiac arrest management.

Conclusion

This novel educational curriculum for teaching POCUS-assisted pulse checks during pediatric cardiac arrest was associated with improved pulse check accuracy, efficiency, and clinician confidence in a simulation setting without prolonging CPR pauses. These findings suggest that a structured, multimodal training approach may support the integration of POCUS into pediatric resuscitation, and further study in clinical settings is warranted.
