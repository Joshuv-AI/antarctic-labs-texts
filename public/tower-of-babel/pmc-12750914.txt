Evaluation of non-vaccination brucellosis control strategies in class II regions using a transmission dynamics model: a case study from Hubei Province
Authors: Buasiyamu Abudunaibi, Qi Chen, Jiangshaya Bahati, Xiaoying Zhao, Yao Wang, Jia Rui, Tianmu Chen, Xuhua Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a transmission dynamics model for brucellosis in Hubei Province and evaluate non-vaccination control strategies.MethodsWe constructed a SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh(Susceptible sheep - Infected sheep - Environmental bacterial load - Susceptible humans - Exposed humans - Acute infected humans - Chronic infected humans) transmission dynamics model and assessed disease transmissibility through R0 calculation. Four intervention measures (environmental disinfection, improved diagnosis, personal protection, and infected sheep management) were evaluated independently and in combination.ResultsModel fitting showed high consistency with reported cases (R²=0.884, p > 0.999). Disease transmission exhibited three phases: high incidence (2014–2016, R0 = 1.86), decline (2020, R0 = 0.69), and rebound (2023 R0 = 1.76). Direct transmission (sheep-to-human) was the primary driver (78.6%-91.5% of R0). Among single interventions, personal protection yielded the greatest case reduction (93.51% case reduction), followed by infected sheep management (91.02%). The combination of these two measures achieved optimal effectiveness (95.98% case reduction).ConclusionBrucellosis transmission in Hubei Province is predominantly driven by direct transmission. Comprehensive control strategies should prioritize personal protection and infected sheep management, supplemented by environmental disinfection and improved diagnosis.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12106-8.

Introduction

Brucellosis, a significant zoonotic disease caused by Brucella bacteria, poses a serious threat to global public health and livestock industry development. Human infection occurs through direct contact with infected animals, consumption of unpasteurized dairy products, and exposure to contaminated environments [1, 2]. The pathogen’s ability to survive for extended periods in favorable environmental conditions significantly increases transmission risks [2, 3].

Brucellosis transmission dynamics show marked geographical heterogeneity [4, 5]. In regions with intensive animal husbandry, basic reproduction numbers (R0) reach 1.9-2.0 [6], significantly higher than traditional pastoral areas (R0 = 0.8–1.2) [7]. In northern China’s pastoral regions, high livestock density and weak preventive measures result in R0 values around 1.9-2.0 [8], while southern agricultural regions generally show R0 values below 1.5 [9, 10].

In China, brucellosis is classified as a Class B infectious disease, with differentiated management strategies. Class I regions, characterized by higher disease burden and transmission risk, implement comprehensive control measures including mandatory animal vaccination, intensive surveillance, and strict quarantine protocols [5]. These regions are predominantly located in northern China. Class II regions, on the other hand, rely on surveillance systems and animal management without vaccination, implementing measures such as environmental disinfection, improved diagnostics, and enhanced personal protection [11]. These regions primarily comprise southern China [12].

Hubei Province, a typical Class II region, demonstrates the challenges of brucellosis control. The epidemic spread southward after 2014 due to intensive farming development and increased livestock transportation. Despite initial success in reducing incidence rates from 0.593/100,000 (2015) to 0.154/100,000 (2019), control measures were interrupted due to the COVID-19 pandemic [4], resulting in a rebound with incidence reaching 0.451/100,000 by 2023.

Significant progress has been made in brucellosis transmission dynamics research globally. The SIC (Susceptible-Infected-Contaminated environment) model revealed the crucial role of environmental transmission, while the SEIV-ShIhaIhc model analyzed seasonal impacts on epidemic patterns. Chinese researchers have extensively studied transmission patterns in regions such as Inner Mongolia and Jilin [6, 7, 13–18]. Regarding control measure evaluation, researchers have examined three core interventions using transmission dynamics models: reducing contact between susceptible and infected sheep [11, 16, 19], strengthening culling of infected animals, and minimizing environmental contamination, optimizing control strategies to minimize farmers’ losses. Additionally, time-delay dynamic models have enhanced understanding of detection impacts, emphasizing the importance of test-and-cull measures [20]. However, existing studies have primarily focused on Class I regions. Understanding transmission dynamics in Class II regions is critical for effective control because these regions face unique challenges: limited surveillance resources, delayed case detection, and fewer intervention tools compared to Class I regions. In the absence of comprehensive vaccination programs, Class II regions must rely exclusively on non-vaccination control strategies—such as individual protection, disinfection, and infected animal management—making it essential to quantitatively assess the effectiveness of these measures through modeling approaches. This distinction is fundamental because the optimal intervention strategies may differ substantially between regions with different institutional frameworks and resource constraints.

This study develops a transmission dynamics model (SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh: Susceptible sheep - Infected sheep - Environment - Susceptible humans - Exposed humans - Acute infected humans - Chronic infected humans) for Class II regions using Hubei Province as a case study. Based on monthly data from 2014 to 2023, we evaluate the effectiveness of various control measures, including environmental disinfection, improved diagnostics, personal protection, and infected sheep management. Through systematic assessment of different intervention strategies, we aim to provide theoretical support for developing effective brucellosis control strategies in Class II regions.

Methods

Study design

This research focuses on brucellosis transmission in Class II regions, comprising two main components: model development and intervention assessment. We first established an SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh(Susceptible sheep - Infected sheep - Environment - Susceptible humans - Exposed humans - Acute infected humans - Chronic infected humans) transmission dynamics model, deriving the basic reproduction number (R0) using the next-generation matrix method. Subsequently, we fitted the model to monthly human brucellosis case data from Hubei Province (2014–2023) to evaluate different transmission pathways and quantify the effectiveness of interventions, including environmental disinfection, improved diagnostics, personal protection, and infected sheep management.

Data sources

Case data were obtained from the Hubei Center for Disease Control and Prevention, including demographic information, onset dates, and diagnosis dates of human brucellosis cases from 2010 to 2023. Demographic statistics and livestock data (sheep stock and slaughter numbers) were collected from the Hubei Provincial Statistical Yearbook. This study was approved by our institutional ethics committee, and anonymized surveillance data were obtained from Hubei CDC through a formal data-sharing agreement.

Model development

Natural history of brucellosis transmission in class II regions

The transmission dynamics model, also known as the ordinary differential equation (ODE) model or compartment model, incorporates disease transmission mechanisms and individual progression patterns after infection. This mathematical model is constructed based on the occurrence, transmission, and development patterns of infectious diseases within populations (human or animal) [10]. Through qualitative and quantitative analysis and numerical simulation of the model’s dynamic characteristics, the model reveals disease progression patterns, elucidates epidemic patterns, predicts development trends, analyzes key factors in disease transmission, and identifies optimal prevention and control strategies. This approach provides theoretical foundation and quantitative evidence for control decisions.

B. melitensis and B. abortus are the primary pathogens causing human brucellosis, with both species prevalent in Class II regions. B. melitensis primarily infects sheep, while B. abortus mainly affects cattle (Supplementary Material, 1.1 Natural History of Brucellosis Transmission in Class II Regions). According to the “National Brucellosis Prevention and Control Plan (2016–2020),” Class II regions are required to maintain self-breeding practices and disease-free status. The policy emphasizes establishing comprehensive surveillance systems, eliminating positive animals, and implementing combined testing and culling strategies for either all animals or randomly selected representative samples.

Transmission dynamics model

Given that sheep are the primary transmission source in Hubei Province, we developed a transmission dynamics model based on available brucellosis transmission patterns. The model, extending from the basic SI framework, incorporates sheep-environment-human transmission chains: SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh (Susceptible sheep - Infected sheep - Environmental bacterial load - Susceptible humans - Exposed humans - Acute infected humans - Chronic infected humans) (Fig. 1). Here, Sh represents susceptible humans, Eh exposed humans, Ih acute infections, Ch chronic infections, Ss susceptible sheep populations, Is symptomatic and infectious sheep, and W the bacterial load in the environment from infected sheep. Both humans and sheep can become infected through contact with infected animals or exposure to environments contaminated by B. melitensis (W). Acute infection cases (Ih) can be reinfected after recovery, with a recovery rate of m.

To evaluate the relative importance of different transmission pathways in the SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model, we employed “knock-out” simulation, a method adapted from genetics where normal genes are replaced by defective genes at identical chromosomal locations [120]. In modeling studies, this approach involves setting specific parameters to zero and calculating the resulting reduction in case numbers or overall incidence to estimate parameter contributions. In this model, we separately set the four transmission rates (βs, βsw, βh, βhw) to zero and compared five scenarios: control (no change), βs = 0, βsw = 0, βh = 0, and βhw = 0, to assess the relative contribution of different transmission pathways to human brucellosis in Class II regions.

Intervention measures evaluation

Based on the natural history of brucellosis transmission in Class II regions, we developed a Susceptible sheep - Infected sheep - Environment-Susceptible humans - Exposed humans - Acute infected humans - Chronic infected humans (SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh) model. This model primarily considers two transmission pathways: sheep-to-human transmission and environment-to-human transmission, and their impact on human brucellosis spread.

In Class II regions, where sheep vaccination is not permitted, brucellosis control measures primarily focus on protecting high-risk populations, while sheep management is limited to quarantine, isolation, and culling of infected animals. Therefore, the main intervention measures in Class II regions include:

Environmental disinfection

We evaluated six disinfection frequency schemes, including fixed-cycle disinfection and seasonally differentiated strategies. In the SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model, environmental disinfection affects the clearance rate parameter δ of B. melitensis in the environment, which is related to disinfection efficiency and frequency (f). Previous research indicates that current disinfection measures have an efficiency of 0.5, and it makes δ = f×0.5.

Diagnostic capacity enhancement

Based on analysis of the onset-to-diagnosis interval, we simulated the effects of reducing diagnostic delays to 7, 5, and 3 days. Reducing the onset-to-diagnosis interval increases the recovery rate (m) in the model. This study simulated the intervention effects of shortening diagnostic time to 7, 5, and 3 days. In the baseline SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model, m = 1/30 (month⁻¹) represents the cure rate under the current 10-day diagnostic delay.

Personal protection measures

We designed a four-level protection scheme (personal hygiene, gloves, masks, protective aprons) by referring to Chinese national control technical guidelines and assessed the impact of varying compliance rates (10%-100%). The intervention model incorporates both specific protection measures (n) and their implementation rates (l), which jointly affect the relative transmission rates from sheep-to-human (βh) and environment-to-human (βhw).

Infected sheep management

We extended the original model to include a quarantine compartment (Qs) and evaluated combinations of detection rates (10%-100%) and culling implementation rates (10%-100%).

Statistical analysis

This study employed Jupyter Notebook 6.5.4 with Python for model implementation and analysis, utilizing NumPy (1.24.3) for numerical calculations and array operations, and SciPy (1.10.1) with solve_ivp function for solving ordinary differential equations. The fourth-order Runge-Kutta method (RK45) was applied to solve the ODE, with both relative and absolute error tolerances set to 1e-8. Model convergence was determined based on the least root mean square (LRMS). Parameter fitting was performed using least-squares optimization via the scipy.optimize module in Python. Excel 2023 was used for data organization, while NumPy statistical functions were employed to calculate monthly case averages, medians, and standard deviations. For visualization, Matplotlib (3.7.1) was used to generate time series plots, box plots, and bar charts, with GridSpec implemented for multi-subplot layouts. Model validation was performed using the coefficient of determination (R²) to assess overall goodness-of-fit. The chi-square test was used to compare simulated cases with fitted cases, validating model fit quality (p > 0.999, indicating strong agreement between observed and predicted values).

Results

Performance of the SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model for human brucellosis transmission dynamics in Hubei Province

Due to the sporadic nature of human brucellosis cases in Hubei Province during 2010–2013 with low annual incidence, the model fitting focused on monthly case data from 2014 to 2023. The SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model demonstrated high consistency between simulated values and actual reported cases (R²=0.884, p > 0.999), with RMSE = 15.2 and MAE = 10.8 (Fig. 2).

Analysis of temporal trends revealed distinct phases in R0 dynamics, with sustained high levels (R0 > 1) during 2014–2017 peaking at 1.86 in 2016, followed by a low period (R0 < 1) during 2018–2020 reaching a minimum of 0.59 in 2020, and a subsequent resurgence culminating in a new peak of 1.86 in 2023. Direct transmission (Rsh) emerged as the dominant pathway, contributing 78.6%-91.5% of R0, with its variations closely mirroring overall R0 trends and highlighting the crucial role of human-animal contact. Although environmental transmission (Rwh) played a secondary role, it demonstrated notable influence in specific years, particularly in 2015 (Rwh =0.41), suggesting persistent environmental effects on disease spread. These findings underscore the significant temporal heterogeneity in brucellosis transmission intensity across Hubei Province, characterized by predominant direct transmission complemented by environmental pathways.

Effectiveness of non-vaccination control measures for brucellosis prevention

We evaluated the effectiveness of four major non-vaccine control measures: environmental disinfection, diagnostic efficiency improvement, personal protection, and infected sheep management, both individually and in various combinations. All simulations were compared against a baseline scenario with an average of 23.93 monthly cases (detailed in Supplementary Material).

Single intervention measures

Individual intervention measures showed varying degrees of effectiveness (Table 3). Personal protection measures demonstrated the highest efficacy, achieving a 93.51% case reduction (95% CI: 91.2–95.8%) with comprehensive protection at 100% compliance. Infected sheep management with 80% detection and isolation rates achieved a 68.25% reduction (95% CI: 64.1–72.4%). Environmental disinfection, even at weekly intervals, showed limited effectiveness (28.33% reduction, 95% CI: 24.8–31.9%). Notably, improving diagnostic efficiency from 10 to 3 days showed minimal impact on case reduction.

Recommended intervention strategies

Dual intervention combinations

Combined interventions generally showed enhanced effectiveness (Table 3). The most effective combination was personal protection with infected sheep management (93.33% reduction, 95% CI: 90.8–95.7% with 100% implementation rates). Environmental disinfection combined with comprehensive personal protection achieved 91.00% reduction (95% CI: 88.2–93.8%). Seasonal differentiated disinfection strategies demonstrated optimal resource utilization while maintaining effectiveness.

Triple intervention combinations

Three-measure combinations further improved control effectiveness (Table 3). The optimal strategy combining weekly disinfection, comprehensive personal protection, and complete sheep management achieved a 91.97% case reduction (95% CI: 89.3–94.6%). Moderate-level combinations (80% compliance) maintained substantial effectiveness (78.22% reduction, 95% CI: 74.8–81.6%), while basic-level interventions (60% compliance) showed acceptable results (67.77% reduction, 95% CI: 63.9–71.5%). Timely cleaning in these strategies involved immediate decontamination procedures following potential exposure events.

Quadruple intervention combination

The integration of all four measures demonstrated the highest overall effectiveness (Table 3). The optimal combination (weekly disinfection, 3-day diagnosis interval, comprehensive protection, and 100% sheep management compliance) achieved a 97.46% case reduction (95% CI: 95.8–98.9%). Combination of the measures contributed an additional 15–20% reduction (95% CI: 12.3–17.8%) compared to individual implementations.

Discussion

The application of mathematical models in infectious disease prediction and early warning has become increasingly prominent during the COVID-19 pandemic [24, 25]. Since 2020, human brucellosis in China has shown a rebound trend, particularly in Class II control regions [11]. Based on this context, we constructed an SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh transmission dynamics model and calculated the basic reproduction number (R0) using the next-generation matrix method to systematically evaluate the transmission dynamics and effectiveness of various intervention measures in Hubei Province during 2014–2023.

Our findings revealed significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in brucellosis transmission in Hubei Province. During 2014–2023, disease transmission exhibited distinct phases: from 2014 to 2016, R0 increased from 1.03 to 1.86, primarily associated with the intensification of livestock farming and frequent cross-provincial animal transportation [26, 27]. Following the implementation of national prevention policies in 2016, R0 gradually decreased to its lowest point in 2020 (R0 = 0.69). However, due to the combined impact of COVID-19 disruptions to animal quarantine systems and livestock industry recovery, R0 rose again to 1.76 in 2023. These cyclical patterns align with both domestic and international research findings, supported by SEIV-ShIhaIhc model studies [26] and empirical evidence from Inner Mongolia and Jilin provinces [13, 19].

Through “knock-out” simulation analysis, direct transmission (Rsh) was identified as the primary driver of brucellosis transmission in Hubei Province, accounting for 78.6–91.5% of R0. This highlights the dominant role of human-animal contact in disease spread and emphasizes the importance of strengthening farm management, standardizing livestock transportation procedures, and enhancing occupational protection awareness. For high-incidence areas in northern and eastern Hubei, priority should be given to health monitoring and protection training for farm workers. While environmental transmission (Rwh) contributed less overall, it showed notable impact in certain years (e.g., Rw=0.41 in 2015) [12]. Environmental factors, including aborted animal fetuses, placentas, and contaminated water and soil, can serve as long-term pathogen reservoirs [17, 28].

Regarding intervention measures, personal protection proved most effective, with comprehensive measures (cleaning, gloves, masks, and aprons) achieving a 93.51% case reduction rate at 100% compliance, consistent with WHO recommendations. The effectiveness of infected sheep management strongly correlated with implementation rates, achieving 91.02% case reduction at 100% detection and isolation rates but declining significantly below 80% compliance [29]. Notably, false-negative rates (5–10%) in actual testing may affect real-world outcomes [30]. Furthermore, diagnostic delays present significant real-world challenges, as brucellosis symptoms are often non-specific and can be misdiagnosed as other febrile illnesses, leading to delayed treatment and continued transmission. Reducing diagnostic delays requires improved clinical awareness, enhanced laboratory capacity in rural areas, and standardized diagnostic protocols, though these improvements face constraints from healthcare infrastructure limitations and training requirements. Environmental disinfection showed limited independent effect (28.33%), contrasting with findings from Inner Mongolia [31]. In Class II regions without immunization programs, disinfection effectiveness is constrained by frequent animal movement and Brucella’s resistance to disinfectants [12, 32, 33], though it retains supplementary value in comprehensive control strategies.

Combined interventions, particularly personal protection with infected sheep management, demonstrated the highest effectiveness (95.98% case reduction), exemplifying the complementarity of “source control + human protection” approaches aligned with the One Health framework [34]. Based on these findings, we recommend prioritizing: mandatory protective equipment distribution and behavioral education, referencing the “hat-mask-gloves” program from northern regions, though implementation faces significant barriers including equipment costs, farmer compliance challenges, and the need for sustained behavioral change; optimizing infected sheep management procedures with rapid testing and compensation mechanisms, drawing from Australia’s foot-and-mouth disease control experience [35, 36], while acknowledging constraints such as testing accuracy limitations (false-negative rates of 5–10%), inadequate compensation funding, and farmer resistance to culling practices; and implementing seasonally-adjusted disinfection strategies, including intensified protocols during lambing season when bacterial shedding peaks and increased frequency during identified high-transmission months, recognizing that effectiveness is limited by disinfectant costs, proper application training requirements, and Brucella’s environmental resistance. Additionally, region-specific measures should be adopted, strengthening occupational protection in northern and eastern high-incidence areas and enhancing dairy and meat product surveillance in southern low-incidence regions [10, 12, 14].

Limitation

While this study evaluated brucellosis transmission dynamics and intervention effectiveness using the SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, regarding model construction, we identified three main constraints: (1) insufficient quantification of environmental contamination, (2) assumption of uniform exposure risk across populations, and (3) incomplete consideration of climate-pathogen interactions. Importantly, our model assumes constant sheep-to-human contact rates, which may vary significantly across subregions due to differences in farming practices, livestock density, seasonal patterns, and socioeconomic factors that influence human-animal interaction frequencies. Second, our model excludes human-to-human transmission, as this is negligible in normal community settings. However, rare cases of congenital brucellosis and laboratory-acquired infections have been documented in the literature, which our model does not capture. Third, the study faces data and validation challenges. These include potential inconsistencies in case reporting due to evolving diagnostic techniques and lack of field validation for intervention strategies. Additionally, the model’s effectiveness assessment is limited to the current policy context where animal vaccination is prohibited in Class II regions, necessitating potential reassessment if policies change. Due to practical constraints, we were unable to obtain real-world parameters for direct application in the model, which introduces additional limitations to our findings. We plan to address these limitations in future research to improve the model’s accuracy and applicability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our SsIs-W-ShEhIhCh model analysis revealed significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in human brucellosis transmission in Hubei Province during 2014–2023, with R0 values ranging from 0.69 to 1.86. Direct transmission was identified as the primary driver (78.6–91.5% of R0), while environmental transmission played a supplementary role. Among intervention measures, comprehensive personal protection achieved the highest independent effectiveness (93.51% case reduction), and its combination with infected sheep management demonstrated optimal results (95.98% reduction). Based on these findings, we recommend implementing region-specific strategies in Class II regions that prioritize personal protection, optimize infected sheep management, and adopt seasonally-adjusted disinfection protocols. While our study provides a quantitative foundation for evidence-based control strategies, future research should focus on validating these interventions through field studies.
