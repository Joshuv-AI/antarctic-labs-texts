Enhancing graph transformer encoding with graph heterogeneous memory for improved recommendation performance
Authors: Haiying Yu, Tianhe Hou, Yuxi Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recommender systems are crucial for mitigating information overload, yet their effectiveness is often constrained by data sparsity. While graph neural networks (GNNs) have shown promise, existing models face a critical limitation: they typically handle either complex heterogeneous relationships or capture historical user dependencies, but struggle to address both simultaneously. To bridge this gap, we propose the Heterogeneous Graph Memory Transformer (HMT), a novel architecture that synergistically integrates a heterogeneous graph transformer with dedicated graph memory modules. HMT is designed to concurrently learn rich semantic representations from heterogeneous graphs and preserve long-range contextual information from user interaction histories, thereby creating more robust and accurate user and item embeddings. Extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets—Amazon, iFashion, and Yelp2018—demonstrate HMT’s superior performance. Notably, our model achieves state-of-the-art N@5 scores of 0.3295, 0.4273, and 0.2748, respectively, significantly outperforming strong baselines. Further analyses confirm the model’s robustness against data imbalance and noise. The HMT framework offers a promising direction for developing next-generation recommender systems capable of contending with the complex and dynamic nature of real-world user-item ecosystems.

Introduction

Recommender algorithms provide personalized content recommendations based on users’ interests, behaviors, and preferences, helping users quickly find content they are interested in amidst the overwhelming amount of information, thus reducing the time and effort required for decision-making1. By accurately recommending content based on users’ historical behavior and preferences, recommender systems can not only increase user engagement but also enhance user retention and platform stickiness.

In the past, Collaborative Filtering (CF) was the widely used traditional algorithm in these systems2,3, utilizing similarities between users or items to make recommendations through matrix factorization and latent factor modeling. However, it struggles when faced with sparse user-item interaction data4. As a result, traditional machine learning algorithms, such as logistic regression3,5 and decision trees6,7, have been widely applied to handle complex recommendation scenarios. These methods primarily focus on low-order feature interactions in historical data, partially meeting the growing demands. However, they fall short in considering the complex connections between users and items, thus lacking in deep recommendation capabilities. Advanced deep learning recommendation algorithms effectively capture non-linear relationships in user-item data, enhanced by various contextual domain information, thereby establishing their utility in modern recommender systems8. The primary aim of deep learning-based sequential recommendations9–11 is to extract as much high-order and temporal data as possible from user-item interactions, learning representations of both short-term and long-term interests, which then inform personalized recommendations. This requires ongoing and sustained data collection.

Due to its ability to naturally represent user-item interactions as nodes and graphs, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have gained increasing attention in recommender systems by facilitating the exploration of complex relationships and the extraction of user intents12,13. Innovations such as LightGCN12,13 and GraphSAIL14 have shown significant advancements in handling the dynamics of data structures. New models integrate multiple entities and relationships into a single graph, effectively enhancing the depth of recommendations9,10,15,16. However, a core challenge is that most GNNs are designed for homogeneous graphs, making them unable to represent the heterogeneous structures common in the real world. To address this, heterogeneous information networks (HINs) have offered new possibilities for optimizing recommender systems16–18. By constructing co-occurrence networks and integrating the feature representations of multiple heterogeneous networks, some models have improved recommendation performance19,20.

Despite this progress, we identify a critical research gap: previous heterogeneous recommender models, while capable of handling multiple data types, often neglect the effective utilization of historical learning16,17. On the other hand, some methods that considered memory units have not effectively addressed the challenges of learning in heterogeneous graph contexts, where historical information is crucial. This separation has led to a superficial understanding of user-item dynamics and the underutilization of deep learning models capable of capturing complex heterogeneous relational patterns21,22. To fill this gap, the core contribution of this paper is the proposal of the Heterogeneous Graph Memory Transformer (HMT) architecture. Our solution aims to enhance the heterogeneous graph Transformer19 module with specially designed heterogeneous memory units. Its novelty lies in simultaneously considering both the historical learning information of heterogeneous nodes and the immediate encoded representations of heterogeneous graphs within a unified framework. The method uses a concatenated residual approach to help reduce information loss during transmission and improve the stability and efficiency of the gradient descent process. The design of the memory network module is to remember and store historical information to support subsequent processing, while the heterogeneous Transformer provides it with rich context, helping the memory module find a generalized approach to effectively preserve original features and meanings. This design achieves more precise recommendations by better utilizing the characteristics of heterogeneous information and optimizing the connections and interactions between modules.

In summary, while existing research has made significant progress, clear limitations remain. Models such as LightGCN13 and SGL19–23 effectively capture collaborative filtering signals in homogeneous graphs, but they simplify the types of nodes and relations, making it difficult to handle the data heterogeneity present in real-world recommendation scenarios. To address this issue, heterogeneous graph network models like HGT17 and DGNN19 were proposed, which can distinguish between different types of entities. However, these models primarily focus on instantaneous graph structural information and insufficiently utilize the historical information embedded in user interactions, which reflects the dynamic evolution of user preferences. This reveals a clear research gap in the current literature: the lack of a framework that can synergistically and uniformly handle both graph structure heterogeneity and the historical dependency of user behavior. The HMT model proposed in this study is designed precisely to fill this gap, aiming to provide deeper and more accurate user and item representations for recommender systems by fusing these two sources of information.

Related works

In this section, we briefly overview the latest relevant studies, focusing on their methodologies and contributions to enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of recommendation systems. By analyzing these works, we aim to identify potential spaces and opportunities for further development in this field. We also discuss the strengths and weaknesses of these approaches.

In terms of robust and invariant feature learning, Lin et al.24, based on the SSL recommendation model, proposed an Invariant Feature Learning (IFL) framework that utilizes a feature mask mechanism to eliminate unstable features, thereby preventing the transmission of negative effects and enhancing model robustness. However, the mask mechanism may inadvertently filter out valuable signals that could help understand user preferences. Regarding the application of graph convolutional networks in recommendation systems, Zheng et al.25 introduced GCNs to enhance the candidate generation stage, optimizing complex collaborative filtering mechanisms to improve recommendation accuracy, though diversity has not yet been fully explored. Xie et al.26 proposed GraphDR, a novel heterogeneous graph neural network framework, which significantly enhances the accuracy and diversity of recommendations by constructing a heterogeneous preference network and field-level heterogeneous graph attention network. However, these methods have not effectively considered the related information of heterogeneous graphs.

From the perspective of heterogeneous graphs and social network models, Salamat et al.10 modeled social networks as heterogeneous graphs using the HeteroGraphRec model. This approach uses graph neural networks to aggregate multi-source information, simulating complex social relationships and enhancing the efficiency of social recommendations. Yu et al.28,28 explored how to utilize implicit social relationships in recommendation systems without explicit trust, by building a heterogeneous network with five subnets and an improved SimRank algorithm. They proposed INMF-RRM, which effectively reconstructs the user-item rating network. In the realm of context-aware recommendation systems, Li et al.29 in the SanMove model, utilized a self-attention network to capture users’ long-term and short-term mobility patterns, significantly improving next location prediction accuracy through spatial-temporal guided non-invasive self-attention mechanisms. Wei and Chow30 introduced FGCR, a fused graph context-aware recommendation system that enhances the information aggregation process in the user-item-context interaction graph by combining GCN and item correlation matrices, significantly improving performance on several real-world datasets.

Considering novel loss functions and contrastive learning, Yu et al.31 showed that contrastive learning (CL) effectively mitigates popularity bias in recommendation systems. They proposed a simplified CL method that incorporates uniform noise in the embedding space instead of traditional graphical enhancements, addressing data sparsity more efficiently. Tang et al.32 introduced a ranking-based contrastive loss (RCL) that focuses on difficult and potential positive samples, enhancing training efficiency and applicability. Additionally, Tang et al.33 addressed data sparsity by proposing a multisample-based contrastive loss (MSCL), which balances the importance of positive and negative samples by using multiple positives simultaneously, thus enhancing data and improving recommendation accuracy.

In terms of personalized and attribute-aware models, Rashed et al.34 in the CARCA model, significantly enhanced recommendation personalization and accuracy by employing multi-head self-attention and cross-attention techniques to extract and score user features. Cui et al.27’s ASKAT model leveraged a sentiment knowledge graph and graph attention network to extract sentiment features from user reviews, thereby improving personalized effects and accuracy.

Additionally, research in federated recommender systems has addressed challenges of model heterogeneity35 and privacy protection, such as membership inference attacks36 and parameter transmission-free security37. Other studies have focused on the robustness of models against adversarial attacks38 and the unique challenges of deploying recommender systems on-device39. While these directions are critically important, our work focuses on the core challenge of improving recommendation accuracy and addressing data sparsity in a centralized environment by enhancing the representation capability of heterogeneous information.

Regarding integrated models and multifunctional strategies, Marwan et al.40 combined BILSTM and GNN to analyze social network and consumer behavior data, proposing a neuroevolution optimized recommendation system that outperforms on multiple evaluation metrics. Sang et al.41’s PA-GNN model optimized session-based recommendations by capturing item positions and applying attention mechanisms, enhancing the learning efficiency of short-term preferences.

In terms of price awareness and recommendation strategies, Zheng et al.42 used a GCN model to integrate price influences, capturing the interactions among users, items, and prices, thus enhancing the accuracy of preference predictions for price-sensitive users, particularly in handling unexplored item categories, highlighting its significance.

Methods

The overall architecture of our proposed Heterogeneous Memory Transformer (HMT) is illustrated in Fig. 1. The model is designed to process user-item interactions through a multi-stage pipeline. The process begins with the input user-item interaction graph, which is transformed into an initial embedding matrix representing users and items. These initial embeddings are then fed into a stack of L HMT layers, which form the core of our model. As detailed in the expanded view of a single layer (HMT Li) in Fig. 1, each layer consists of two parallel and crucial computing units: (1) a Heterogeneous Transformer branch that utilizes a heterogeneous attention mechanism to capture the complex, context-aware relationships between different types of nodes, and (2) a Memory Unit branch designed to preserve and recall historical information from the node embeddings. The outputs from these two parallel branches are then fused through an Aggregation mechanism. This aggregated vector is subsequently passed through two residual connections and a ReLU activation function to produce the updated node representations for that layer. Finally, the output embeddings from all L layers are concatenated to form comprehensive and rich final representations for users and items. These final embeddings are used to Predict Scores for recommendation. In the following sections, we will describe the detailed formulation of each component.

The heterogeneous graph transformer model incorporates the power of the transformer architecture into the graph domain to capture the rich semantic information encoded in heterogeneous graphs. It uses attention mechanisms to dynamically assign importance scores to different nodes and edges based on their context and connectivity patterns and get their learning representations.

Heterogeneous graph memory network

Drawing inspiration from the robust capabilities of memory neural networks in disentangled representation learning, our model aims to enhance graph neural networks with differentiable memory components operating within multiple latent representation spaces. This approach allows DGNN to adaptively learn the intricate relationships between node/edge types and latent factors, ultimately ensuring superior preservation of disentangled heterogeneous semantics.

Evaluation Metrics and Parameter Settings.

We split the interactions into training and testing set with a ratio of 8:2. Hit Rate (HR)@K and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)@K are two widely used evaluation metrics in the field of recommendation systems. H@K measures the proportion of test instances where the desired item is present in the top-K recommendations. N@K considers both the rank and the relevance of the recommended items. It assigns higher weights to higher-ranked relevant items. We evaluate the top-K recommendation performance and report the average H@K and N@K where K is set to 5, 10 and 20 respectively for the testing set. We focus on top-K item recommendation and combine H@ K and N@ K together in experiments to offer a comprehensive assessment on recommendation accuracy of different methods. Formally, the metrics are calculated as follows:20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H@K=\frac{\sum\:_{i=1}^{M}\sum\:_{j=1}^{K}{r}_{i,j}}{M},$$\end{document}21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N@K=\sum\:_{\boldsymbol{i}=1}^{M}\frac{\sum\:_{j=1}^{K}\frac{{r}_{i,j}}{{log}_{2}\left(j+1\right)}}{M\bullet\:{IDCG}_{\boldsymbol{i}}}.$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document} represents the count of tested users. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{i,j}$$\end{document} is assigned to 1 if the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:j$$\end{document}-th item in the ranked list of the i-th user is the positive item, otherwise \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{i,j}$$\end{document} is set to 0. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{IDCG}_{i}\:$$\end{document}denotes the possible maximum DCG@K value for the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document}-th tested user.

We implement our proposed method and the compared models based on Pytorch and Adam optimizer for experiments. The learning rate is set to 0.01. The total number of epochs is 200. The dropout rate is set at 0.2 to prevent the model from overfitting.

Baseline comparison models

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method, we conduct comparison experiments with several state-of-art models as follows: GCN (Graph Convolutional Network) is one of the most classic graph neural network algorithms. It applies convolution operations to graph-structured data and captures structural information by aggregating node features from neighboring nodes25. GAT (Graph Attention Network) addresses the limitations of GCN by introducing an attention mechanism for modeling complex relationships, capturing personalized preferences of users, and the similarity between items43. HGT (Heterogeneous Graph Transformer) is a Transformer-based model that implements a graph Transformer architecture for heterogeneous graph representation learning, calculating attention for specific edges to perform graph information propagation with heterogeneity encoding17. GraphSAGE is a graph neural network model that optimizes sampling strategies. It generates node embeddings by sampling and aggregating features from a node’s local neighborhood to improve performance44. LightGCN simplifies the model by removing feature transformation and nonlinear activation functions, focusing solely on neighbor aggregation. This simplification makes the model lighter and more efficient while maintaining competitive performance. LightGCN excels at capturing user-item interactions and generating accurate recommendations13. SGL (Simplified Graph Learning) aims to simplify graph learning by removing redundant components and focusing on the core elements of graph neural networks to optimize computational performance. By introducing sparse regularization terms, it achieves competitive performance with fewer parameters and less computation. Its simplicity and efficiency make it suitable for real-time recommendation scenarios23. CMM (Collaborative Memory Model) integrates collaborative memory components and neural attention mechanisms into neighborhood components, encoding complex user-item relationships through associative addressing schemes, to learn user-item specific neighborhoods45. DGNN (Decoupled Graph Neural Network) decouples node information and semantic perception to maintain factored representations for the diverse connections between heterogeneous types of users and items46.

A deeper analysis of these results reveals the advantages of the HMT architecture. Firstly, compared to models like LightGCN and SGL that excel on homogeneous graphs, HMT’s significant superiority demonstrates the necessity of modeling different node and relation types (i.e., handling heterogeneity) in recommendation scenarios. Secondly, the performance improvement over HGT, another advanced model for heterogeneous graphs, highlights the key role of the memory module we introduced; this indicates that merely capturing the instantaneous graph structure is insufficient to fully understand user preferences, and that integrating historical information reflecting users’ long-term interests is crucial. Different types of nodes and edges can maintain their unique representation spaces, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of the model’s decision-making process.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

To investigate the impact of different hyperparameters on the HMT model, we conducted a parameter sensitivity analysis. The parameter settings of our model are listed in Table 3. We implement experiments to reveal the impact of hyperparameters settings on the overall performance. It examines the effects of varying the transformer layers, the size of latent memory units, the HMT layers, and the dimensionality of embedding. The performance evaluation results with N@5 and H@5 are shown in Fig. 2. The number of HMT layers determines the depth of the model’s neighborhood aggregation, as deeper layers can capture more complex graph structural relationships. The number of Transformer heads controls the width of the multi-head attention mechanism, allowing the model to learn node relationships in different subspaces in parallel. The Memory size sets the capacity of the model’s memory network, directly affecting its ability to store and recall historical user behavior patterns. Finally, the Embedding length defines the dimensionality of the user and item representation vectors, determining the richness of the model’s feature expression. Together, these parameters regulate the model’s complexity and expressive power, and are crucial for the final recommendation performance.

The experimental results show that increasing the number of transformer layers typically enhances the model’s ability to capture complex patterns and relationships in the data. In our method, for the three datasets, the performance of N@5 and H@5 reaches its optimal level when the number of transformer heads and transformer layers reaches 6. The purpose of changing the memory size is to evaluate the model’s performance on tasks that require long-term dependencies or complex reasoning. However, in some cases, even if the number of memory layers is increased, the model’s recommendation performance may not continue to improve and may even degrade. The reason is that the model has reached its performance bottleneck, and too many memory layers lead to information redundancy and noise interference. As shown in Fig. 2, six memory layers achieve the best performance. The embedding length affects the model’s representational capacity. A longer embedding length allows for a richer and more detailed representation of the input data.

Imbalance experiment

In recommendation systems, data imbalance is a common issue. Some popular products or items may have a large amount of user interaction data, while most products or topics are relatively unpopular and have sparse interaction data. This imbalance may lead to recommendation algorithms favoring popular products or topics, while neglecting the personalized needs of users. To verify the recommendation effect of the HMT method under data imbalance, we conducted an imbalance experiment. In the experiment, the known relationships were set as positive samples, and negative samples were randomly selected. We set the ratio of positive to negative samples as 1:5 and 1:10, respectively. The experimental results are shown in Fig. 4. It can be seen that when the number of negative samples is greatly increased, the system’s performance does not deteriorate. The stability demonstrated by the HMT model in this scenario can be attributed to its unique memory module. This module helps the model anchor a user’s core preferences based on their historical positive interactions, forming a relatively stable user profile. Therefore, even when faced with a large number of random negative samples during training, the model can rely on the stable preferences learned from historical information without being easily skewed by noisy data, thus maintaining robust recommendation performance.

Noise experiment

To verify the robustness of the HMT method, we conducted noise experiments to test its robustness and adaptability. To evaluate the performance of the method in noisy environments, we simulated different levels of data missing by randomly replacing a portion of positive samples with negative samples, while simultaneously replacing an equal amount of negative samples with positive samples, setting different noise rates (1%, 5%, 10%). The experimental results are shown in Fig. 5. When 10% noise is added to the data, the N@5 on the three datasets decreases by 5.21%, 1.96%, and 3.37%, and the H@5 decreases by 1.67%, 1.22%, and 2.57%, respectively. The experimental results indicate that our model demonstrates good robustness and adaptability under noisy circumstances. The reason our model exhibits good noise resistance is due to its dual synergistic design. On one hand, the heterogeneous attention mechanism can dynamically assign weights to neighboring nodes based on context, thereby enabling it to mitigate the negative impact of noisy interactions (i.e., erroneous edges). On the other hand, the memory unit stores long-term user behavior patterns, forming a relatively stable preference baseline. This allows the model to effectively filter out short-term interference introduced by a small number of noisy samples, thus ensuring the robustness of the recommendation results.

Discussion

The Heterogeneous Graph Memory Transformer (HMT) proposed in this study offers significant advantages over existing technologies. Firstly, by integrating heterogeneous graph transformers with memory modules, HMT can more accurately capture the complex relationships and dependencies among heterogeneous nodes and edges. This integration not only enhances the precision of recommendation systems but also improves the system’s robustness against data sparsity. Compared to traditional methods, HMT excels in handling complex network relationships and diverse data types, enabling recommendation systems to better understand user-item dynamics and potential heterogeneous associative memory. Furthermore, through experimental validation, HMT demonstrated excellent performance and noise resistance across multiple benchmark datasets. Specifically, its N@5 scores on the Amazon, iFashion, and Yelp2018 datasets reached 0.3295, 0.4273, and 0.2748, respectively, with other metrics also significantly outperforming existing methods. Despite the strong performance improvements shown by HMT in recommendation systems, there are still some limitations and challenges. Firstly, HMT’s reliance on memory modules and complex graph neural networks results in high computational complexity, which may pose efficiency issues when processing large-scale datasets. Secondly, when faced with extremely sparse data, the memory modules might generate information redundancy and noise interference, leading to a decline in performance. Additionally, the HMT model may require extensive parameter tuning in practical applications to ensure optimal performance across different datasets. Finally, despite the model’s strong performance, its higher computational complexity, the potential challenges it faces with extremely sparse data, and the need for meticulous parameter tuning are limitations that warrant attention in future work. Future research could address these challenges by introducing more efficient resource management strategies and adaptive parameter optimization techniques.

Conclusion

In this paper, we addressed a critical challenge in recommendation systems by proposing the Heterogeneous Memory Transformer (HMT), an architecture designed to concurrently model complex heterogeneous relationships and preserve historical user information. Conventional recommendation algorithms often fail to effectively integrate these two aspects, limiting their performance on sparse and diverse datasets. Our proposed HMT architecture remedies this by synergistically combining a heterogeneous Transformer with dedicated memory modules, significantly improving the accuracy of user preference prediction. Through extensive experiments, we validated the effectiveness of HMT; its predictive results consistently outperform many state-of-the-art methods, achieving N@5 scores of 0.3224 on Amazon, 0.4535 on iFashion, and 0.2787 on Yelp2018. Despite these promising results, we acknowledge our model’s limitations, including its higher computational complexity and the potential for memory modules to introduce noise in extremely sparse scenarios. Looking ahead, a potential research direction is to further refine the memory components to improve their efficiency and scalability. Additionally, integrating real-time data processing capabilities could enable instant updates to recommendations47,48, reflecting real-time changes in user behavior and item attributes.
