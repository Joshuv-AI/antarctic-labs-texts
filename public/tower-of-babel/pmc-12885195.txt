Elective HFOV vs CMV as Early Intervention: A Clinical Trial in Neonates with Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome
Authors: Zohreh Badiee, Samira Saraeian, Alireza Sadeghnia, Maryam Yazdi, Samaneh Saraeian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:While Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation with Volume Guarantee (SIMV + VG) is commonly used for mechanical ventilation in preterm infants with respiratory failure, High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation (HFOV) is often reserved for cases unresponsive to conventional modes. Given the inconclusive findings on the early HFOV, this trial compares SIMV + VG and HFOV as initial ventilation strategies in preterm infants with respiratory distress syndrome (RDS).Materials and Methods:This randomized controlled trial included 86 neonates (25–35 weeks’ gestation) with RDS, enrolled from 2020 to 2023 at two tertiary referral hospitals affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran. Using stratified block randomization by birth weight, neonates were assigned to either the HFOV or SIMV + VG group (n = 43 each). Primary outcomes included total intubation duration, Non-Invasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV) duration, and total oxygen therapy duration. Secondary outcomes included hospitalization duration and the incidence of complications such as Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD), Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH), and Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP).Results:The HFOV group showed significantly shorter intubation duration (3.80 vs. 8.62 days, P = 0.02) and NIPPV duration (5.63 vs. 7.86 days, P = 0.017), and lower BPD incidence (21.2% vs. 46.7%, P = 0.032). Differences in oxygen therapy, hospitalization duration, and other complications were not statistically significant.Conclusion:Early HFOV application in preterm neonates with RDS significantly reduces intubation duration, NIPPV duration, and BPD incidence. These findings suggest potential benefits of HFOV in improving neonatal outcomes. Further multi-center studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to validate these results and explore broader clinical implications.

Introduction

Respiratory issues are the leading cause of Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admissions and intubation in preterm infants. Among these, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) is a major contributor to respiratory failure, with an incidence of 25% to 80%, depending on gestational age and other factors.[1]

Despite advances such as Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP) and surfactant administration, some infants still require Mechanical Ventilation (MV).[2] The development of specialized ventilators, have significantly reduced infant mortality.[3] However, even these specialized ventilators can lead to complications, including volutrauma, barotrauma, atelectrauma, oxygen toxicity, and biotrauma.[4]

Currently, in NICUs, synchronized respiratory modes such as Assist-Control (AC) or Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV), with or without Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV), are widely adopted as first-line interventions for infants requiring MV. High-Frequency Oscillatory Ventilation (HFOV) is typically not the initial choice among specialists and is reserved for cases of respiratory failure that do not respond to conventional ventilation modes.[3]

HFOV, characterized by extremely low tidal volumes and rapid ventilatory rates, offers potential advantages such as moderated peak airway pressures, effective oxygenation at minimal volumes, and preservation and recovery of normal lung architecture, thereby, mitigating risks of volutrauma, barotrauma, and atelectrauma. Additionally, HFOV facilitates more uniform pulmonary drug distribution. However, studies have reported inconsistent or inconclusive results, failing to demonstrate significant differences.[567]

Given HFOV’s potential benefits, we conducted a randomized clinical trial comparing SIMV + VG and HFOV in neonates with RDS who failed CPAP and required MV.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This randomized controlled trial included 86 neonates born between 25 and 35 weeks’gestation, diagnosed with RDS by a neonatologist based on clinical symptoms and chest radiography. Infants were admitted to the NICU at Shahid Beheshti or Alzahra University Hospitals from August 2020 to February 2023. Both centers are governmental, tertiary referral hospitals affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences. They manage high-risk pregnancies and neonatal referrals from surrounding regions, representing advanced-level NICU care.

Newborns requiring MV were randomly assigned to two groups of 43: one receiving SIMV + VG and the other HFOV, with HFOV as the primary mode of respiratory support.

We used stratified block randomization based on patient weight categories (<1200 g, 1200–1499 g, and ≥1500 g), with variable block sizes applied within each stratum. The random sequence was generated using the “blockrand” package in R (version 3.5.2).

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, was registered in the Iranian Registry for Clinical Trials, and received ethical approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (code: IRCT20130329012869N3). Written informed consent was obtained from parents before enrollment, and all collected data remained confidential.

Participants

The sample size was calculated to compare the mean total intubation duration between the SIMV + VG and HFOV groups, with a Type I error probability (α) of 0.05, a power (1−β) of 0.8, and a moderate effect size of 0.65. Allowing for a 15% dropout rate, the final required sample size was 43 per group.

Eighty-six neonates born in our hospital were included based on the following criteria: gestational age of 25 to 35 weeks, RDS diagnosis confirmed by a neonatologist via clinical symptoms and chest radiography, CPAP failure, administration of at least one surfactant dose while on CPAP, and undergoing intubation for the first time within 2 days of life.

Exclusion criteria included birth weight under 600 g, congenital anomalies, upper respiratory tract abnormalities, congenital diaphragmatic hernia, cyanotic congenital heart disease, evidence of asphyxia, structural lung diseases (pulmonary adenomatous malformation, sequestration, pulmonary hypoplasia), MV duration under 24 hours regardless of cause and maternal suspected respiratory symptoms with a confirmed positive COVID-19 test [Figure 1].

Study flowchart

Given the nature of the ventilation modes (HFOV vs. SIMV + VG), complete blinding of caregivers was not feasible. However, outcome assessors, data collectors, and statisticians were fully blinded to treatment assignments throughout data collection and analysis to minimize assessment bias. Detailed information for each infant was recorded and reviewed over a 4–8-week follow-up by an independent, blinded researcher. All analyses adhered to the intention-to-treat principle.

Interventions

MV was initiated after surfactant administration on NCPAP, following European Consensus Guidelines.[2] However, infants requiring intubation during initial resuscitation received MV directly, with surfactant administered during ventilation. MV was indicated for respiratory failure, defined by severe respiratory distress, including severe retractions or persistent tachypnea (>100/min), severe apnea with intractable or recurrent (>6 episodes/hour) events, gasping, or poor respiratory effort. Additional criteria included respiratory acidosis (arterial blood gas [ABG] pH <7.2, PaCO₂ >65 mmHg), and hypoxemia (PaO₂ <50 mmHg despite noninvasive ventilation). CPAP failure was defined as the need for FiO₂ >40% at CPAP 7 cmH₂O to maintain oxygen saturation (O₂Sat) >88%.[3]

Both groups were managed using Fabian ventilators, with settings adjusted at the clinician’s discretion to maintain O₂ saturation (88%–96%), PaO₂ (50–80 mmHg), PaCO₂ (45–55 mmHg), and arterial pH (7.25–7.45). If FiO₂ exceeded 40%, additional surfactant administration was considered.[3] All ventilators were calibrated regularly according to the manufacturer recommendations and hospital protocol to minimize instrumental bias.

ABG monitoring involved performing the first ABG upon admission, followed by subsequent monitoring 0.5–1 hour after intubation, 1–2 hours after any significant ventilator setting adjustment, and periodically until extubating, based on the clinical status.

The volume guarantee (VG) strategy was applied in both groups, with the control group managed on SIMV + VG, as evidence suggests a lower mortality risk with this mode compared to other conventional ventilation strategies.[8]

In the SIMV + VG group, ventilation was time-cycled and pressure-limited, with a tidal volume (TV) of 5 mL/kg. Peak inspiratory pressure (PIP) was adjusted based on chest movement and breathing effort, typically ranging from 15–20 cm H₂O. The initial inspiratory time (Ti) was set at 0.3–0.4 sec, modified using graphical data. For infants <1000 g, the respiratory rate was set to 30–60 breaths/min with PEEP of 5–7 cmH₂O; for 1000–1500 g, 30–60 breaths/min with PEEP of 5–8 cmH₂O; and for >2500 g, 20–40 breaths/min with PEEP of 6–9 cmH₂O.[3]

In the HFOV group, ventilation was initiated with a MAP of 10–16 cm H₂O and a frequency of 8–10 Hz, adjusted by weight. Amplitude (∆P) was set to be 2–3 times the initial MAP, with MAP adjustments based on FiO₂ requirements, pulmonary inflation on Chest X-rays (CXR), and surfactant administration. ∆P was modified based on PaCO₂ from ABG analysis, and an inspiratory-to expiratory ratio (I/E ratio) of 1:2 was applied.[2]

Ventilator settings were dynamically adjusted based on PaCO₂ and PaO₂ levels and progressively weaned to meet extubating criteria. Infants in the HFOV group were extubated directly without transitioning to CMV.

Following extubating, infants in both groups received Non-Invasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV) for a minimum of 12 to 24 hours to ensure stable respiratory support. Although extubating failure typically occurs within 24–48 hours, it was considered up to 1 week post-extubating. Infants <1200 g in both groups received caffeine 12 hours before extubating to prevent failure, with a 20 mg/kg loading dose, followed by 10 mg/kg daily for 48 hours.[3]

CXR were performed upon admission, post-intubation, 2 hours post extubating, and in cases of clinical deterioration, such as a sudden decrease in O₂ Sat or hemodynamic instability, to assess for pulmonary complications, including pneumothorax, pulmonary hemorrhage, and pneumonia.

Echocardiography was conducted within the first 1–3 days for all patients, with additional scans performed as needed. Specifically, in cases of a bounding pulse or murmur, echocardiography was repeated to evaluate Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA). A pediatric cardiologist performed all echocardiograms.

IVH screening via cranial ultrasound was performed on days 3–7 in all infants born before 30 weeks of gestation. Additionally, in infants with gestational age >30 weeks or <3 days old, an expert blinded neonatologist promptly conducted a brain ultrasound if IVH was suspected based on hypotension, sudden clinical deterioration, acute anemia, thrombocytopenia, or clinical signs of coagulopathy (e.g., ecchymosis or gastrointestinal bleeding [GIB]). IVH was classified as mild (grades 1–2) or severe (grades 3–4).

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) was defined as requiring oxygen for a total duration of 28 days post-birth.[3]

A retinal examination was performed by an ophthalmologist between 4 to 6 weeks following American Academy of Pediatrics,[9] to assess for retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), considered positive at stage two or higher. Subsequent follow-ups were conducted per national guidelines.

Sepsis was strictly diagnosed based on positive blood cultures.

Efficacy measurement

The primary outcomes were total intubation duration, total NIPPV duration, and total oxygen therapy duration, comparing the HFOV and SIMV + VG groups. Secondary outcomes included hospitalization duration and the incidence of complications such as BPD, pneumothorax, IVH, and ROP.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized by frequency (%), and continuous variables by mean ± standard deviation (SD). Categorical outcomes were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Normally distributed continuous outcomes were compared using the unpaired Student’s t-test, while nonparametric continuous outcomes were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Means of hospitalization, intubation duration, and oxygen therapy were compared using the log-rank test, accounting for death outcomes. All statistical analyses were two-tailed, adhered to the intention-to-treat principle, and were conducted using SPSS (version 27). A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Eighty-six neonates were randomized into two groups (SIMV + VG and HFOV, n = 43 each). After exclusions, 38 in the HFOV group and 39 in the SIMV + VG group completed the study. Data were reviewed over a 4–88-week follow-up. Randomization ensured balanced distribution across weight categories (<1200 g, 1200–1499 g, and ≥1500 g). Patient characteristics, including gestational age, Apgar scores, and delivery events, were comparable between groups [Table 1].

Primary outcome

The HFOV group had a significantly shorter total intubation duration than the SIMV + VG group. NIPPV duration was also reduced in the HFOV group. Although total oxygen therapy duration was lower in the HFOV group, the difference was not statistically significant [Refer to Table 1 for detailed data and P values].

Secondary outcomes

The incidence of BPD was significantly lower in the HFOV group (21.2% vs. 46.7% in the SIMV + VG group). Although hospitalization duration was reduced in the HFOV group, the difference was not statistically significant. The incidence of extubating failure was 18.4% (7 out of 38 patients) in the HFOV group and 25.6% (10 out of 39 patients) in the SIMV + VG group, with no significant difference between the groups. The incidence of ROP stage 2 or higher was lower in the HFOV group (6.1% vs. 13.3% in the SIMV + VG group), though the difference was not statistically significant.

In terms of IVH, the HFOV group had a higher overall incidence (36.8% vs. 26.8% in the SIMV + VG group), while severe IVH rates were similar (10.5% vs. 10.3%), with no statistically significant differences.

No significant differences were observed between the groups in mortality, pneumothorax, sepsis, PDA, Necrotizing Enterocolitis (NEC), or Urinary Tract Infection (UTI), as well as in the need for repeat surfactant administration or the frequency of blood product administration [Refer to Table 2 for detailed data and P values].

Discussion

Our findings indicate that HFOV as the initial ventilation mode may optimize respiratory outcomes in preterm neonates with RDS requiring respiratory support. A longstanding preference for CMV persists among neonatologists, with HFOV often considered a rescue therapy for critically ill patients already on CMV. This perception has contributed to resistance toward the early adoption of HFOV. Among studies conducted before the 2015 Cochrane review, only five (Gerstmann,[10] Plavka,[11] Durand,[12] Van Reempts,[6] and Salvo[13]) specifically investigated early HFOV.

Before Salvo’s study, several investigations, including those by Ogawa,[5] Plavka,[11] Moriette,[14] Van Reempts,[6] and Vento,[15] reported no significant difference in MV duration between the two groups.

Plavka’s study reported only improved oxygenation and reduced surfactant requirements in the HFOV group. However, the trial had a small sample size, with only 37 out of 43 enrolled patients, and focused on very low birth weight (VLBW) infants (<1500 g, <31 weeks gestation). The CMV strategy used was the Gerscher Limit Time Cycle, with or without synchronization, reflecting the ventilation practices of that era.[11]

Among these studies, Van Reempts investigated early HFOV and found no significant differences in intubation duration, BPD incidence, or other outcomes. Their study included neonates <32 weeks gestation and specific CMV settings were not detailed. They acknowledged as a limitation that some patients were transferred from other centers to their tertiary facility, potentially sustaining lung injury during resuscitation or transport while receiving CMV.[6] Similarly, our hospitals serve as referral centers; however, we excluded transferred cases to minimize this potential confounding factor.

Nevertheless, early studies should be interpreted within the context of evolving neonatal care practices, as they predate the routine use of prenatal corticosteroids and surfactant, and their treatment protocols did not consistently incorporate these interventions. Notably, Salvo’s 2012 study exclusively enrolled neonates who had not received prenatal corticosteroid.[13]

In agreement with our findings, Salvo[13] and Sun[16] reported a significant reduction in MV duration in the HFOV cohort. With a sample size of 44 per group, comparable to ours, Salvo’s study was among the key investigations on early HFOV, identified significantly shorter MV and CPAP durations in the HFOV group, along with lower extubation failure rates, reduced surfactant administration frequency, and shorter hospitalization. Unlike our approach, which used NIPPV post-extubation, their management included CPAP, supplemental oxygen, or room air. Additionally, VG was not used in the CMV group, which may have influenced outcomes. The HFOV group had a higher incidence of low Apgar scores and greater resuscitation needs at birth, serving as potential confounding factors. These findings suggest HFOV may have a more pronounced effect in neonates without antenatal corticosteroids, warranting further research to assess its benefits across different neonatal populations.

Gerstmann’s multicenter trial, conducted across three centers with 125 neonates, employed an open lung strategy in the HFOV group, and reported reduced surfactant requirements, MV duration, oxygen dependence, reintubation rates, BPD, NEC, and overall hospital costs. However, mortality, ROP incidence, and hospitalization duration showed no significant differences between groups.[10] In contrast, our study found no significant difference in surfactant administration between groups, which may be attributed to the use of the VG strategy in the SIMV group.

Courtney’s 2002 study[17] found reduced MV duration and oxygen requirements in the HFOV group, primarily assessing mortality and BPD in neonates weighing 600–1200 g. Unlike our study, infants initially received conventional ventilation before randomization, and caffeine or theophylline was administered before weaning, whereas we used a standardized caffeine dose. Post-extubation, they were placed on CPAP, while we used NIPPV for its greater effectiveness. The study’s multicenter design, with varying ventilator types, may have introduced bias. Our study demonstrates a significantly shorter NIPPV duration in the HFOV group. While the total duration of oxygen therapy was also shorter, the difference was not statistically significant. Variability in oxygen therapy duration across studies underscores the multifactorial nature of oxygen requirements in preterm infants. Larger studies with broader sample sizes may yield more definitive conclusions.

However, the 2015 Cochrane systematic review, which analyzed twelve trials on MV duration, found no significant difference between CMV and HFOV in ventilation duration or oxygen consumption.[18] While a trend toward shorter MV duration in the HFOV group was noted, only two trials (Salvo[13] and Sun[16]) reported statistically significant differences.[19] This meta-analysis highlighted the need for further research on early HFOV, a direction our study has pursued to better define its efficacy and clinical outcomes.

More recently, a 2021 Chinese study found no statistically significant reduction in MV duration between groups.[20] However, Liu’s study was retrospective and included conditions beyond RDS, such as meconium aspiration and asphyxia. It reported a lower incidence of IVH, with no significant difference in BPD rates and MV duration in the HFOV group. The type of conventional ventilation was not specified beyond being described as time-cycled, pressure-limited ventilation.

Our study found a significant reduction in BPD incidence in the HFOV group, consistent with findings by Durand[12] and Sun.[16] Similarly, the 2015 Cochrane systematic review reported lower BPD rates in neonates treated with HFOV, despite notable heterogeneity across studies.[19] A major challenge in comparing these findings is the variability in BPD definitions. However, the overall trend suggests that HFOV may contribute to a reduced incidence of BPD.

Zivanovic reported improved long-term respiratory outcomes in the HFOV group at 11–14 years of age, despite no significant difference in BPD rates at 36 weeks, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up.[21]

The 2015 Cochrane Review update[19] analyzed 17 studies and identified a significant reduction in BPD rates in HFOV groups under specific conditions. These included the absence of surfactant use, the application of piston-type oscillators, the lack of lung-protective strategies in the CMV group, early randomization within 2–6 hours of life, and the use of an I: E ratio of 1:2 in HFOV. These findings underscore the substantial heterogeneity among previous HFOV studies. In our study, neonates were initiated on piston-type HFOV with an I: E ratio of 1:2 as early as possible, while both groups received comprehensive measures to minimize BPD risk.

Other key strengths of this study include the administration of surfactant compared to older studies, the integration of VG strategies, and the use of HFOV as the primary ventilation modality. The significantly shorter NIPPV duration in the HFOV group, a factor not extensively evaluated in previous studies, may have important implications for reducing hospital costs and improving neonatal outcomes.

Despite these promising findings, the study’s limited sample size and the overlap with the COVID-19 pandemic may have influenced our findings. Conducting the study in specialized referral hospitals, where many mothers and infants were transferred shortly before birth, further restricted cohort diversity. Budgetary constraints also precluded long-term follow-up, limiting our analysis to short-term outcomes and preventing an assessment of potential long-term effects. Additionally, the inability to fully blind the study may have introduced bias in the administration of additional treatments and the assessment of caregiver-dependent outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that early application of HFOV in preterm neonates with RDS significantly reduces MV duration and NIPPV compared to SIMV + VG, while also lowering BPD incidence. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups in total oxygen therapy duration, hospitalization duration, or other complications, including ROP, IVH, pneumothorax, and culture-positive sepsis. These findings support the potential advantages of HFOV as an initial ventilation strategy in preterm neonates. Further multi-center trials with long-term follow-up are needed to validate these benefits and assess their clinical impact on neonatal respiratory management and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes, and determine whether its benefits extend beyond the neonatal period.
