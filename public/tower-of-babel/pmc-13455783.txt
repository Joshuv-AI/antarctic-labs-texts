Methodological and Translational Considerations for Blood-Based Machine-Learning Prescreening of Esophageal Cancer [Letter]
Authors: Zhipeng Fan, Lei Li
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the study by Wang et al, which developed an interpretable random forest model for esophageal cancer prescreening from routine peripheral blood indices.1 The use of low-cost laboratory data, comparison of seven algorithms, and SHAP-based interpretation address a clinically relevant problem, particularly where endoscopic capacity is limited. The reported validation AUC of 0.973 and Brier score of 0.059 are encouraging. While acknowledging these strengths, we believe four issues merit consideration before the model is advanced toward clinical implementation.

First, the validation architecture requires additional clarification regarding the machine-learning development process. The authors reported that LASSO regression was performed within the training set before 10-fold cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning. However, if feature selection was conducted only once before cross-validation rather than repeated within each resampling fold, the internal performance estimation may remain optimistic. In addition, the holdout set appears to have been used both for comparing seven algorithms and for identifying the final model. The AUC differences from XGBoost and LightGBM were only 0.013 and 0.018, respectively, without paired uncertainty estimates. A nested pipeline with selection and tuning confined to inner folds, followed by one locked evaluation in an untouched dataset, would provide a less biased comparison.2

Second, interpretation of predictive performance requires careful consideration of the study design. The reported case fraction of esophageal cancer was 43.6% in this case-control cohort, which is determined by sampling rather than reflecting population prevalence. Therefore, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, calibration performance, Brier score, and decision-curve estimates from this dataset cannot be directly extrapolated to screening populations without appropriate prevalence adjustment, weighting, or recalibration.3 The fixed 0.50 threshold also missed 7 of 59 cancers in the validation cohort. That operating point may be too restrictive for a prescreening tool when the consequence of a missed early cancer is weighed against additional endoscopies. Future evaluations should report calibration intercept and slope, recalibrate predictions to the intended population, present precision-recall performance after appropriate prevalence adjustment, and select thresholds using explicit clinical harms, endoscopic capacity, and cost-effectiveness assumptions.

Third, the biological interpretation of model predictors deserves further refinement. Although the authors appropriately discussed the limitations of case-control design and spectrum bias, additional attention should be given to whether laboratory abnormalities represent tumor-specific signals or systemic consequences of disease. Samples were obtained before treatment, but the study does not establish that they preceded symptoms or the diagnostic workup. The algorithm therefore distinguishes prevalent cancer from controls rather than predicting future cancer risk. Creatinine, the dominant feature, was lower in cases than controls, with medians of 51.64 and 61.13 µmol/L, and lower values increased predicted risk. Because serum creatinine is strongly influenced by muscle mass, this signal may partly capture dysphagia-related nutritional decline or cancer-associated body-composition change rather than tumor-specific susceptibility.4,5 The very high AUC reported for stages I and II disease against healthy controls does not resolve this temporal problem. Prospective studies should use prediagnostic specimens, prespecified lead-time windows, and concurrent measures of weight change, muscle mass, nutritional status, and renal function.

Finally, the proposed feature set may be less deployable than its “routine laboratory” label implies. Eligibility required complete data for all key indicators, yet the manuscript does not describe the extent or pattern of missing tests in the source population. It also remains uncertain whether adenosine deaminase, prothrombin time, and thrombin time would already be available at the intended primary-care encounter or would require additional ordering. Complete-case development can select a more intensively investigated population and obscure workflow costs. The pronounced U-shaped SHAP effects for potassium and coagulation times also occur near distribution tails despite narrow interquartile ranges, so their stability should be demonstrated with bootstrap confidence bands and density displays. A tiered comparison of a universally available core model versus the full panel, accompanied by prospective missingness, turnaround-time, budget-impact, and referral-yield analyses, would better define real-world value.6

In summary, Wang et al provide an encouraging proof of concept for low-cost blood-based triage. Clarifying the resampling pipeline, recalibrating to screening prevalence, establishing prediagnostic temporality, and testing a pragmatic feature set would materially improve clinical credibility. We congratulate the authors on this contribution and look forward to prospective multicenter studies evaluating whether the model can reduce unnecessary endoscopy without delaying cancer diagnosis.
