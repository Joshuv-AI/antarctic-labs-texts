Bandwidth Selection of Density Estimators over Treespaces
Ruriko Yoshida, ZhiwenWang

Abstract
A kernel density estimator (KDE) is one of the most popular non-parametric density estimators. In this paper we focus on a best bandwidth selection method for use in an analogue of a classical KDE using the tropical symmetric distance, known as a tropical KDE, for use over the space of phylogenetic trees. We propose the likelihood cross validation (LCV) for selecting the bandwidth parameter for the KDE over the space of phylogenetic trees. In this paper, first, we show the explicit optimal solution of the best-fit bandwidth parameter via the LCV for tropical KDE over the space of phylogenetic trees. Then, computational experiments with simulated datasets generated under the multi-species coalescent (MSC) model show that a tropical KDE with the best-fit bandwidth parameter via the LCV perform better than a tropical KDE with an estimated best-fit bandwidth parameter via nearest neighbors in terms of accuracy and computational time. Lastly, we apply our method an empirical data from the Apicomplexa genome.



keywords
Gene Trees, Kernel Density Estimators, Likelihood Cross Validation, Max-Plus Algebra

1
Introduction

Phylogenomics is a relatively new field in evolutionary biology. It applies tools in phylogenetics to genome data,with the goal of identifying genes whose evolutionary histories differ from the evolutionary history of the given set of species.
Such evolutionary processes include negative or balancing selection on a locus, which might increase the chance for ancestral gene copies to maintain through speciation events
15
, and horizontal gene transfer, which shuffles divergent genes among different species
9
.

To find evolutionary history of each gene in a genome data, we often infer a
gene tree
which is a
phylogenetic tree
reconstructed from an alignment of the gene. A phylogenetic tree is a weighted (rooted or unrooted) tree representation of an evolutionary history of the given set of species whose leaves represent species and internal nodes are unlabeled. Each branch in a phylogenetic tree has a non-negative weight. In this paper we focus on rooted gene trees. More specifically, we focus on an
equidistant tree
, a rooted phylogenetic tree whose total branch lengths from its root to each leaf is same.

A
k
ernel density estimator (KDE) is one of the most popular non-parametric density estimator. In 2024, Yoshida et al. in
18
introduced a
tropical KDE
to estimate a distribution of gene trees over the
space of phylogenetic trees
with a fixed number of leaves
m

using
the
tropical symmetric distance

d_{\rm tr}
. The space of phylogenetic trees
with
m
leaves
is the set of all possible phylogenetic trees with the leaf label set
[m]:=\{1,\ldots,m\}
. It is well-known
that the space of phylogenetic trees is the union of
m-2
dimensional polyhedral cones over
\mathbb{R}^{\binom{m}{2}}
(for example see
3
)
, and thus, is not convex.
Therefore we cannot directly apply classical density estimators using traditional kernels, such as the
Gaussian or Laplacian
kernel functions. Weyenberg et al. in
17
used the Billera-Holmes-Vogtmann (BHV) metric developed by Billera, Holmes, and Vogtmann in
3
to define a kernel density estimator over the space. However they also showed that the normalized constant for the kernel function with the BHV metric over the space of phylogenetic tree is not constant, i.e., it varies
depending
on the location of the function. In 2016, Weynberg et al. in
16

developed
an algorithm to approximate the normalized constant of the kernel function with the BHV metric using the holonomic gradient methods.
However,the resulting normalized constant approximation came at significant computational cost.
In 2024, Yoshida et al. used the fact that the space of all equidistant trees is
tropically convex
shown by Ardila and Develin in
1
and used the tropical symmetric distance
d_{\rm tr}
to define a kernel function. Yoshida et al. in
18
showed that the normalized constant of their kernel function is constant by computational experiments.
To
compute a bandwidth of each kernel function, they used simply a nearest neighbor to estimate the best-fit bandwidth. R code is available at
https://polytopes.net/tropical_KDE_new.zip
.

In this paper we first modify a tropical KDE proposed by Yoshida et al. in
18
. Then, we propose an analogue of the likelihood cross validation (LCV) defined by Habbema et al.
5
in terms of
d_{\rm tr}
and we show the
explicit
global optima in terms of the LCV with
d_{\rm tr}
. For computational experiments,
first,

we apply
tropical
KDEs
with the best-fit bandwidth parameter via the LCV
to simulated datasets generated by the multi-species coalescent (MSC) model
and our computational experiments show that our KDEs with the best-fit bandwidth parameters via the LCV perform better than KDEs proposed by Yoshida et al. in
18
in terms of accuracy and computational time. Lastly, we apply our method to
Apicomplexa genome dataset generated by Kuo et al.
7
.

Basics

Let
e:=\binom{m}{2}
. Throughout this paper, we consider the
tropical projective torus

\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
, which is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{e-1}
.
For more details, see
6
;
10
.

2.1
Tropical Basics

Definition 1

(Tropical Arithmetic Operations)
.

Under the tropical semiring
(\,\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\},\boxplus,\odot)\,
, we consider the max-plus arithmetic, that is, the tropical arithmetic operations of addition and multiplication defined as:

a\boxplus b:=\max\{a,b\},~~~~a\odot b:=a+b,

where
a,\,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}.

Notice that over the tropical semiring with the max-plus arithmetic, the identity element under the tropical addition
\boxplus
is
-\infty
and the identity element under tropical multiplication
\odot
is
0
.

Definition 2

(Tropical Scalar Multiplication and Vector Addition)
.

For any scalars
a,\,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
and for any vectors
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{e}),w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{e})
over the tropical projective space
\left((\mathbb{R}\cup-\{\infty\})^{e}-\{(-\infty,\ldots,-\infty)\}\right)\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
, we have tropical scalar multiplication and tropical vector addition as:

a\odot v\boxplus b\odot w:=(\max\{a+v_{1},b+w_{1}\},\ldots,\max\{a+v_{e},b+w_{e}\}).

Note that the tropical projective space
\left((\mathbb{R}\cup-\{\infty\})^{e}-\{(-\infty,\ldots,-\infty)\}\right)\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
contains the tropical projective torus
\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
and
throughout
this paper we consider the following equivalence relations: for any vector
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{e})
over
\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
and for a fixed
height

h>0
, we have

(v_{1},\ldots,v_{e})=(v_{1}-h_{0},\ldots,v_{e}-h_{0})

where
h_{0}=\max_{i\in[e]}v_{i}-h
so that we
always have

\max_{i\in[e]}v_{i}=h
.

Definition 3
.

Suppose we have a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
.
S
is tropically convex if

c_{1}\odot v\boxplus c_{2}\odot w\in S

for any
c_{1},c_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and for any points
v,w\in S
. Suppose
V=\{v^{1},\ldots,v^{s}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
. The smallest tropically-convex subset containing
V
is called the tropical convex hull or tropical polytope of
V
which can be written as the set of all tropical linear combinations of
V
as:

\mathrm{tconv}(V)=\{a_{1}\odot v^{1}\oplus a_{2}\odot v^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{s}\odot v^{s}\mid a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}\}.

A tropical line segment between two points
v^{1},\,v^{2}
is a tropical polytope of a set of two points
\{v^{1},\,v^{2}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
.

Definition 4

(Generalized Hilbert Projective Metric)
.

For any vectors
v:=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{e}),\,w:=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{e})\in\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
, the tropical symmetric metric
d_{\rm tr}
between
v
and
w
is defined as:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d_{\rm tr}(v,w):=\max_{i\in[e]}\bigl\{v_{i}-w_{i}\bigr\}-\min_{i\in[e]}\bigl\{v_{i}-w_{i}\bigr\}.}

d_{\rm tr}
is a well-defined metric over the tropical projective torus
\mathbb{R}^{e}\!/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}

8
.

2.2
Space of Ultrametrics

Suppose we have a map
d:[m]\times[m]\to\mathbb{R}
such that

d(i,j)=\begin{cases}d(j,i)&\mbox{for all }i,j\in[m]\\
0&\mbox{if and only if }i=j\end{cases}

and

d(i,j)\leq d(i,k)+d(j,k)\mbox{ for all }i,j,k\in[m].

Then we say that the map
d
is metric.
Suppose
d
is a metric on
[m]
. Then if
d
satisfies the following stronger triangle inequalities such that

\displaystyle\max\{d(i,j),d(i,k),d(j,k)\}

(1)

is attained at least twice for any

i,j,k\in[m]
then, a metric
d
is called an
ultrametric
.

Example 1
.

Suppose
m=3
. Let
d
be a metric on
[m]:=\{1,2,3\}
such that

d(1,2)=2,\,d(1,3)=2,\,d(2,3)=1.

Since the maximum is achieved twice,
d
is an ultrametric.

A phylogenetic tree is a non-negatively weighted (rooted or unrooted) tree such that their internal nodes do not have labels, their external leaves have labels
[m]
, and each edge in the phylogenetic tree has non-negative weight. Recall that, in this paper, we consider rooted phylogenetic trees with a leaf label set
[m]
.

Definition 5
.

Suppose we have a rooted phylogenetic tree
T
with a leaf label set
[m]
. If the total branch weight on the unique path from its root to each leaf
i\in[m]

is the same
for all
i\in[m]
, then we call
T
an equidistant tree.

The MSC model is the most widely used model for statistical analysis of gene trees. A key assumption of the MSC is that all gene trees are equidistant. Accordingly, we focus on equidistant trees in this paper.

Note that a phylogenetic tree is a discrete object so in order to conduct a statistical analysis, typically we convert a phylogenetic tree to a vector representation. Here we convert a phylogenetic tree to a
dissimilarity map
. Dissimilarity maps are maps
d:[m]\times[m]\to\mathbb{R}
such that
d(i,i)=0
and
d(i,j)=d(j,i)
. In phylogenetics, we convert a phylogenetic tree to a dissmilarity map by setting
d(i,j)
equal to the pairwise distance, the total weight on the unique path, between a leaf
i\in[m]
and a leaf
j\in[m]
. Then we have the following theorem:

Theorem 1

(
4
)
.

Suppose we have an equidistant tree
T
with a leaf label set
[m]
and suppose
d(i,j)
for all
i,j\in[m]
is the distance from a leaf
i
to a leaf
j
. Then,
d
is an ultrametric if and only if
T
is an equidistant tree.

Using Theorem
1
, we consider the space of ultrametrics as the space of phylogenetic trees on
[m]
. Here we define
\mathcal{U}_{m}
as the space of ultrametrics with a set of leaf labels
[m]
. Ardila and Klivans characterized
\mathcal{U}_{m}
as the
tropical linear space
which is tropically convex.

Theorem 2

(
2
;
12
)
.

Suppose we have a classical linear subspace
L_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}
defined by the linear
equations
x_{ij}-x_{ik}+x_{jk}=0
for
1\leq i<j<k\leq m
. Let
\mbox{Trop}(L_{m})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{e}/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
be the tropicalization of the linear space
L_{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}
, that is, we replace the classical addition by the tropical addition
\boxplus
and we replace the classical multiplication by the tropical multiplication
\odot
in the equations defining the linear subspace
L_{m}
, so that all points
(x_{12},x_{13},\ldots,x_{m-1,m})
in
\text{Trop}(L_{m})
satisfy the condition:

\max_{i,j,k\in[m]}\{v_{ij},v_{ik},v_{jk}\}

is achieved at least twice.
Then the image of
\mathcal{U}_{m}
inside of the tropical projective torus
\mathbb{R}^{e}/\mathbb{R}{\bf 1}
is equal to
\text{Trop}(L_{m})
.

Since
\mathcal{U}_{m}
is tropically convex by Theorem
2
, Yoshida et al. in
18
introduced the tropical KDE an analogue of a classical KDE with the tropical symmetric metric
d_{\rm tr}
defined in
Section
3
.

3
Tropical Kernel Density Estimation

Let
\mathcal{S}:=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{U}_{m}
be a random sample from the space of ultrametrics
\mathcal{U}_{m}
. In 2024, Yoshida et al.
18
proposed a
tropical kernel density estimator (KDE)
with the
tropical metric over the space of ultrametrics
\mathcal{U}_{m}
as an analogue of a classical KDE defined as:

\hat{f}(T)\propto\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{\sigma_{i}}(T,T_{i})

(2)

where
k
is a non-negative function defined over
\mathcal{U}_{m}
such that

k_{\sigma_{i}}(T,T_{i})=\exp\left({-{\left({\frac{d_{\rm tr}(T,T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}}\right)}}\right),

(3)

with a “bandwidth” parameter controls the local smoothness of a given kernel function
k(T,T_{i})
at any observation
T_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
with respect to
d_{\rm tr}
.
Yoshida et al. showed by computational experiments that the normalized constant

C(T_{i})=\int_{\mathcal{U}_{m}}{k_{\sigma_{i}}(T,T_{i})}dT

does not depend on
T_{i}\in\mathcal{U}_{m}

18
.
In this paper we make the same assumption.
Let
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{N})
. Then, Yoshida et al. in
18
considered the estimation

\hat{g}_{\sigma}^{\prime}(T_{j})=\frac{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(N-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}k_{\sigma_{i}}(T_{j},T_{i})

(4)

for
T_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
.
In this paper
, as Yoshida et al. in
18
,
we consider
the following:

\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}k_{\sigma_{i}}(T_{j},T_{i})

(5)

for
T_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
.

3.1
Likelihood Cross Validation for Kernel Density Estimation

In
17
;
16
;
18
, the default set up of this user-defined parameter
\sigma>0
is determined by the nearest neighbor of each
T_{i}\in\mathcal{S}
. However, this might not be an
optimal selection of the parameter
\sigma
. In this paper, we focus on one of the most popular bandwidth
selection methods
, the Likelihood Cross Validation (LCV) defined by Habbema et al.
5
:

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma>0}M(\sigma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma>0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln\left(\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})\right)

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma>0}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ln\left(\frac{1}{(N-1)}\sum_{i\not=j}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\exp\left(\frac{-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)\right).

Now we take a partial derivative on
M(\sigma)
in terms of
\sigma
using the
product law
:

\frac{\partial M(\sigma)}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=\frac{\partial M(\sigma)}{\partial\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})}{\partial\sigma_{i}},

for each
i=1,\ldots,N
and
where

\frac{\partial M(\sigma)}{\partial\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})}=\frac{(N-1)}{{\sum_{i\not=j}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\exp\left(\frac{-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)}}

and

\frac{\partial\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=\frac{1}{(N-1)}\frac{\left(\sigma_{i}-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})\right)\exp\left(\frac{-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

Therefore we have

\frac{\partial M(\sigma)}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=\frac{\partial M(\sigma)}{\partial\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})}\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{g}_{\sigma}(T_{j})}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=\frac{\left(\sigma_{i}-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})\right)}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\exp\left(\frac{-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)}{{\sum_{i\not=j}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}}\exp\left(\frac{-d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}\right)}}

for each
i=1,\ldots,N
. Thus, when

\sigma_{i}=d_{\rm tr}(T_{j},T_{i})

for each
i=1,\ldots,N
, we have

\frac{\partial M(\sigma)}{\partial\sigma_{i}}=0.

Therefore,
the equation in (
6
)
achieves optimal value
.

Discussion

In this paper we propose LCV to estimate the optimal bandwidth for tropical KDEs for estimating the distribution of gene trees over the space of ultrametrics. It is interesting to note that from the computational results, the NN for estimating the optimal bandwidth proposed by Yoshida et al.
18
seems to achieve almost the optimal in terms of LCV.

When we have the bandwidth

\sigma_{i}=d_{\rm tr}(T,T_{i}),

one notices that we have

k_{\sigma_{i}}(T,T_{i})=\exp\left({-{\left({\frac{d_{\rm tr}(T,T_{i})}{\sigma_{i}}}\right)}}\right)=\exp(-1).

This simplifies the kernel function and computation. However, this is only the optimal bandwidth in terms of the LCV.
Another interesting approach is the Least Squares Cross Validation (LSCV) proposed by Rudemo
14
.
