Polymer-tethered pyridine tunes activities of the two distinct CO2 electroreduction sites on Cu
Authors: Fan Zeng, Xuan Wang, Tianxiu Yin, Jinglei Shen, Binbin Pan, Wenzhi Teng, Tao Cheng, Yuhang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
CO2 electroreduction (CO2RR) to multi-carbon products on Cu was found to occur via a two-site mode: one site is more active for CO2-to-CO conversion, and the other is primarily responsible for CO reduction. However, it remains unclear whether the activities of these two sites can be modulated independently. Here, we show that polyvinylpyridine, a pyridine-functionalized polymer, preferentially enhances the activity toward multi-carbon products at the CO2-to-CO site by a factor of 3 while reducing that of the CO reduction site. Poly-4-vinylpyridine-(P4VP)-modified Cu2O-derived Cu (OD-Cu) increases the multi-carbon product Faradaic efficiency by a factor of 1.3 to 3 across a current density range of 60 to 260 mA cm-2. Moreover, the CO reduction reaction (CORR) activity decreases by up to 40% in the same range when P4VP is introduced. Isotopic labeling, in-situ Raman spectroscopy, contact angle tests, and theoretical calculations reveal that this phenomenon arises from CO2 enrichment, increased hydrophilicity, and Cu surface charge redistribution in the presence of polyvinylpyridine. These findings demonstrate the potential to independently control CO2 reduction at different active sites.

Introduction

Cu-catalyzed CO2 and CO electroreduction (CO(2)RR) offer a renewable path to producing hydrocarbon and alcohol fuels with net zero emissions1. These two processes share the same mechanism for forming multi-carbon (C2+) products2,3. CO2 is first converted to CO, and the adsorbed CO (*CO) is then reduced to C2+ molecules. However, the same Cu catalysts often exhibit distinct activities for the CO2RR and CORR4, suggesting that the sites responsible for these two reactions could differ. For example, Wang and co-workers reported that co-electrolysis of CO2 and CO results in higher ethylene production rates than the CO2RR and CORR, achieved through the dimerization of *CO at different sites5. Xu et al. recently reported that at least two types of active sites co-exist on Cu surfaces: one converts CO2 to CO (denoted as CuCO2), and the other is responsible for CO reduction (denoted as CuCO)6. They also suggested that the activity toward the C2+ product on CuCO sites is more than 6 times greater than that on CuCO2.

The consequence of the two-site mode, one can expect, is the probability of independently tuning CO2-to-C2+ and CO-to-C2+ activities on the same catalysts. However, this remains challenging, as these two sites are enriched on different Cu facets6, the ratio of which cannot be readily changed in steady-state electrolysis. In addition to catalyst structures, the reaction microenvironment, including gas mass transport7,8, local pH9–12, and cation identities and concentrations13–16, was found to play essential roles in the CO(2)RR. These factors can be effectively modulated by incorporating small molecules17–19, polymers20,21, and covalent organic frameworks22 into Cu catalyst layers (CLs) as additives.

Therefore, we posit that if one can design different microenvironments for CuCO2 and CuCO sites, C2+ product formation would be independently regulated on the same catalysts. This concept could be implemented using additives that interact distinctly with reactants (CO2 and CO) or with the sites responsible for these two reactions. If the activity at the CuCO2 sites increases, C2+ product formation from the CO2RR would not be limited to the migration of *CO from CuCO2 to CuCO via desorption and readsorption6, and higher CO2-to-C2+ product Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) at both low and high overpotentials would be expected.

In this study, we demonstrate that polyvinylpyridine, an ion-conductive polymer, preferentially favors C2+ product formation on CuCO2 sites while reducing the activity on CuCO. Using P4VP as an example, we show that, compared to bare OD-Cu, OD-Cu with the P4VP additive has increased CO2-to-C2+ activity by a factor of 1.3–3 and suppressed CORR Faradaic efficiencies by up to 40% at the same current densities (Fig. 1a). Co-electrolysis, 13C isotopic labeling, and theoretical calculations revealed that the enrichment of CO2, the reduction in surface hydrophobicity, and the charge rearrangement on Cu surfaces induced by P4VP are the underlying reasons why P4VP prefers the CO2RR. Results and discussion

Material characterizations

P4VP was introduced into the CL by adding it to the catalyst ink for spray coating. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images confirmed the presence of P4VP on the Cu2O precatalysts without changing their morphology (Fig. 1b, c). In addition to Cu2O, poly-2-vinylpyridine (P2VP), an isomer of P4VP, was used as a control sample (Supplementary Fig. 1). The transparent film connecting different Cu2O particles is the polyvinylpyridine additive (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2). X-ray diffraction (XRD) studies suggest the absence of the impact of polyvinylpyridine on the Cu2O crystal structure (Fig. 1d). The (111), (200), (211), (220), and (311) diffraction peaks of Cu2O located at 36.2°, 42.0°, 54.2°, 61.2°, and 73.4°, respectively, were observed. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was then employed to determine the chemical states of the precatalyst surfaces. For Cu2O-P4VP, pristine Cu2O, and Cu2O-P2VP, Cu+ at ~932.7 eV, characteristic satellite peaks were observed (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). This suggests that the Cu valence state was Cu+ in all three catalysts, together with the Cu Auger spectra (Supplementary Fig. 3d). The deconvoluted N 1s spectra of Cu2O-P4VP and Cu2O-P2VP suggest the presence of two chemical environments for N atoms (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3c). The peak at 399.3 eV corresponds to a single nitrogen atom bonded to a carbon atom within the pyridine ring (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 3c)23,24. These material characterization results indicate the successful incorporation of polyvinylpyridine into the Cu CL.

One outcome of introducing P4VP into the CL is increased CO2 solubility due to the acid-base reaction between CO2 and the pyridine ring25–27. This was verified by comparing the CO2 solubilities in 0.1 M KHCO3 solutions with and without the addition of P4VP (Fig. 1g). The results revealed a more-than-doubling CO2 solubility in the presence of P4VP (33.4 mM vs 77.6 mM) (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 4). Thus, the CO2 enrichment effect of P4VP was ascertained, which is consistent with the literature27. Similarly, CO2 solubility in 0.1 M KHCO3 increases by a factor of approximately 1.5 after adding P2VP (Supplementary Fig. 5).

CO(2)RR performance

Next, we compared the performance of the CO(2)RR on Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu and bare OD-Cu. The electrochemical measurements were conducted via membrane electrode assemblies in zero-gap electrolyzers (Supplementary Fig. 6). This reactor is equipped with gas diffusion electrodes that can improve CO(2) mass transfer and avoid the impact of the large difference between the solubilities of CO2 and CO (>33 mM vs ~1 mM)28. Across the current density range from 60 to 260 mA cm−2, we observed increased C2+ product FEs by a factor of 1.3–3 at the same applied current densities arising from the incorporation of P4VP (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 7). For example, at 60 mA cm−2, the C2+ product FE obtained using the Cu2O-P4VP precatalyst is 47.1 ± 3.9%, with an ethylene FE of 31.5 ± 3.4%, which is more than 3 times greater than that of the bare OD-Cu. The bare OD-Cu is a highly CO-selective catalyst offering an FE of 56.4 ± 0.3%. At 220 mA cm−2, the C2+ product FEs are 71.8 ± 4.3% for Cu2O-P4VP and 54.9 ± 3.1% for bare OD-Cu, a 1.3× increase. We performed 30-h electrolysis using P4VP-modified OD-Cu at 120 mA cm−2, during which constant FEs of ~30% and 65% for C2H4 and C2+ products, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8). The enhanced CO2RR to C2+ products was also observed in the case of Cu2O-P2VP (Supplementary Fig. 7c). P4VP promotes the CO2RR not only under a pure CO2 atmosphere but also in the case of diluted CO2 feedstocks. At a constant current density of 60 mA cm−2, reducing the CO2 volume fraction from 100% to 3.3% turns OD-Cu from a CO2RR catalyst to a hydrogen evolution reduction (HER) catalyst with more than 88% FE for H2 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). In stark contrast, Cu2O-P4VP has an overall CO2RR FE of more than 50% and a C2+ product FE of ~30% in the 3.3% CO2 and Ar mixture (Supplementary Fig. 9b). These observations can be attributed to the CO2 enrichment caused by P4VP. In the CORR scenario, however, we observed the opposite trend of activity change. Specifically, the presence of P4VP reduces C2+ product FEs at the same current density (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 10). At 60 mA cm−2, Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu and bare OD-Cu behave similarly, exhibiting C2+ products and C2H4 FEs of ~70% and ~40%, respectively. With increasing current density, the two electrodes show distinct activities for the same products. On bare OD-Cu, the total CORR FE increases to 87.9 ± 4.1% at 260 mA cm−2, and that for ethylene is nearly constant, varying between 40% and 45%. In contrast, the activities for both the overall CORR and ethylene production of the P4VP-modified OD-Cu monotonically decrease, leading to FEs of 51.5 ± 7.9% for C2+ molecules and 28.1 ± 6.5% for ethylene at 260 mA cm−2. This accounts for a reduction in CO-to-C2+ activity of up to 40%. Like P4VP, markedly decreased CORR activities were also seen in the case of Cu2O-P2VP-derived Cu (Supplementary Fig. 11). In addition, on commercial Cu nanoparticles with and without P4VP additives, phenomena similar to what we observed in the case of OD-Cu were also seen (Supplementary Fig. 12).

The opposing roles of polyvinylpyridine in the CO2RR and CORR prompted us to determine whether the CuCO2 and CuCO sites were impacted differently by this additive. To do this, we performed co-electrolysis of CO2 and CO mixtures with varying ratios at current densities of 60 and 220 mA cm−2. We compared the production rates of ethylene, C2+ liquids, and total C2+ molecules to those obtained during the electroreduction of pure CO2 and CO (Fig. 2c–f). For bare OD-Cu, the production rates of all C2+ products correlated positively with the CO fraction at the two investigated current densities (Fig. 2c, d). Specifically, the rate of C2+ product generation at 60 mA cm−2 increased from 7.8 ± 2.5 nmol s−1 cm−2 under pure CO2 to 53.4 ± 1.2 nmol s−1 cm−2 under 100% CO (Fig. 2c). Both the ethylene and liquid-phase production rates exhibited the same trend, increasing by 25.9 nmol s−1 cm−2 and 19.6 nmol s−1 cm−2, respectively. Similarly, at 220 mA cm−2, the C2+ product formation rate is improved by a factor of 1.4 because the CO fraction at the inlet increases to 100% (Fig. 2d). For the Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu, the rate of C2+ product generation at 60 mA cm−2 was 56.6 ± 1.8 nmol s−1 cm−2 and peaked at a CO2:CO ratio of 1:4 (Fig. 2e). Further increasing the CO fraction to 100% does not lead to noticeable product rate variation. The breakdown analysis of ethylene and C2+ liquids gives constant results. At 220 mA cm−2, a volcano-like relationship between the production rates and CO fractions emerges, with a peak position at a 1:1 CO2:CO ratio (Fig. 2f). The maximum production rates for ethylene, C2+ liquids, and total C2+ molecules are 107.3 ± 5.3, 83.1 ± 0.8, and 178.9 ± 13.6 nmol s−1 cm−2, respectively. These observations suggest that CuCO sites predominate on the surface of OD-Cu and that P4VP additives likely favor C2+ product formation on CuCO2 while suppressing it on CuCO. This could explain the transition from a monotonic increase to a volcano-like relationship.

We ruled out the impact of P4VP on rate-determining steps (RDSs) (Supplementary Fig. 13). As corroborated by the Tafel analysis, the slopes for the CO2RR to C2+ products are 299 mV dec−1 on OD-Cu and 266 mV dec−1 on the Cu2O-P4VP-derived catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 13a). For the CORR, both electrodes have Tafel slopes greater than 120 mV dec−1 (463 mV dec−1 for OD-Cu and 362 mV dec−1 for the Cu2O-P4VP-derived electrode) (Supplementary Fig. 13b). These results indicate the presence of complications such as the change in coverages and non-kinetic factors for both OD-Cu and P4VP-modified OD-Cu, although P4VP modification does make the process slightly more kinetically efficient. In addition, in-situ Raman spectra reveal the complete reduction of Cu2O to metallic Cu under operating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 14), meaning that the oxidation states of the Cu surfaces are similar with and without P4VP modification.

Post-measurement SEM images of electrodes after CO(2)RR confirm well-retained catalyst morphology under CO2 but distinct degrees of reconstruction under CO (Supplementary Fig. 15). XPS spectra ascertain the presence of P4VP in the catalyst layer after the reaction (Supplementary Fig. 16). Therefore, these different catalyst reconstruction behaviors could arise from the specific adsorption of P4VP on Cu surfaces, which was verified by the reduction in the intensities of these OH- adsorption peaks (Supplementary Fig. 17)29. Note that, to ensure that this reduction is exclusively caused by P4VP adsorption, the membrane was carefully peeled off from the catalyst layer, allowing for complete avoidance of catalyst adhesion on the membrane (Supplementary Fig. 18). Also, the adsorption of P4VP does not change the facet distribution. The anodic peaks at 0.33, 0.37, and 0.42 V vs. RHE correspond to OH- adsorption on the Cu(100), Cu(110), and Cu(111) surfaces, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 17a–c)30. The fractions of the charge pertinent to OH- adsorption on these facets on OD-Cu with and without P4VP coatings are ~45% for Cu(100), ~35% for Cu(110), and ~21% for Cu(111) (Supplementary Fig. 17d).

Isotopic labeling of C2+ production

We then sought to probe the activities of the C2+ products on the CuCO2 and CuCO sites by co-electrolysis of 13CO2 and CO. The activities of the two sites can be assessed by the ratio of unlabeled and fully labeled products with the same molecular structures6. In this case, we utilized ethylene as the probing molecule. Analysis of ethylene via mass spectrometry (MS) revealed ratios of 1/0.084/0.006 for 12CH212CH2/12CH213CH2/13CH213CH2 for bare OD-Cu (Fig. 3a). The distributions of 12C and 13C deviate from the binomial distribution because of the different reactivities of CO2 and CO for ethylene formation at the two sites and the slow migration of *CO adsorbed on them6. This was also observed for the Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu (Fig. 3b). The low fractions of 12CH213CH2 and 13CH213CH2 confirmed the dominance of CuCO sites on the OD-Cu surfaces. For Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu, the ratio of 12CH212CH2/12CH213CH2/13CH213CH2 is 1/0.078/0.017 (Fig. 3b). Notably, the intensity of 13CH213CH2 increases by a factor of ~3 with P4VP incorporation, indicating increased ethylene formation at the CuCO2 sites. The isotopic compositions of acetate and ethanol were also analyzed via 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR). 12CH312COO- shows a single peak at 1.69 ppm, corresponding to the hydrogen on the methyl group (Fig. 3c, d). 13C-labeled acetate (12CH313COO-, 13CH312COO-, and 13CH313COO-) shows peak splitting due to the presence of 13C. Similarly, the 13C-labeled ethanol exhibited further peak splitting than did 12CH312CH2OH (Fig. 3e, f). The markedly higher peak areas of 13CH313COO- and 13CH313CH2OH for the Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu than for the bare OD-Cu also indicate enhanced C2+ product formation at the CuCO2 sites. Mechanistic studies

To gain insight into the role of P4VP additives, we carried out in-situ Raman spectroscopy studies under 13CO2/CO co-electrolysis conditions (Supplementary Fig. 19), which confirms the presence of P4VP under operating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 20). At an applied potential of −1.0 V vs. the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE), two surface species can be deconvoluted from the spectra. The band centered at 2080 cm−1 corresponds to the linearly adsorbed 12CO on CuCO, and that at 2010 cm−1 corresponds to surface 13CO on CuCO2 (Fig. 4a, b)31–33, as verified by the *CO positions under pure 13CO2 reduction and 12CO reduction (Supplementary Fig. 21). The fraction of 13CO is 43% on bare OD-Cu and increases to 61% on the Cu2O-P4VP-derived sample, suggesting enhanced CO coverage at the CuCO2 sites (Fig. 4a, b). This can be partially explained by the role of P4VP in enriching CO2 on catalyst surfaces. However, the disagreement between the magnitude of the increase in CO2 solubility (2.3 times, from 33.4 mM to 77.6 mM) and the CuCO2 site activity (3 times) may imply the presence of other factors. To test this hypothesis, we used density functional theory (DFT) calculations that account for the solvation effect and the applied voltage (for more computational details, see Methods) to investigate the additional role of polyvinylpyridine. Cu(111) was used for calculations, as it consists mainly of CuCO2 sites6. Three simulation models were constructed, i.e., pristine Cu(111), P2VP-modified Cu(111) (labeled Cu(111)-P2VP), and P4VP-modified Cu(111) (labeled Cu(111)-P4VP). The introduction of polyvinylpyridine alters the charge density, inducing charge redistribution in the Cu(111) system (Supplementary Fig. 26). Such a phenomenon is attributed to the electron donation of specifically polyvinylpyridine on the Cu(111) surface, as implied by the OH- electro-adsorption tests (Supplementary Fig. 17). This not only modifies the local electronic structure but also creates an asymmetric electronic environment conducive to the stabilization of catalytic intermediates. Figure 4c shows the free energy profiles for CO2RR on Cu(111), Cu(111)-P2VP, and Cu(111)-P4VP. Compared with Cu(111) (0.76 eV) and Cu(111)-P2VP (0.69 eV), Cu(111)-P4VP accelerates CO2 adsorption to form *CO2 by lowering the adsorption energy to 0.61 eV. Charge redistribution also promotes charge transfer between the catalyst and CO2. Thus, more electrons can be withdrawn from the Cu surface to the C=O π* anti-bonding orbitals of the CO2 molecule. Our calculations revealed that 0.012e (Cu(111)), 0.017e (Cu(111)-P2VP), and 0.022e (Cu(111)-P4VP) of electrons were transferred from the Cu surface to CO2 in these models (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 1), which led to the gradual elongation of the C=O bond and thus favored CO2 activation. Moreover, the formation of *COOH on Cu(111)-P4VP and Cu(111)-P2VP is also more favorable than that on Cu(111) by 0.35 eV and 0.02 eV, respectively, which explains the increased *CO formation at the CuCO2 sites.

Further simulations of *CO adsorption revealed that the binding energy increased by 0.1 eV after P4VP was introduced on Cu(111) (Fig. 4e), resulting in a *CO residence that was about two orders of magnitude longer than that on bare Cu(111). A similar result was obtained for Cu(111)-P2VP. The charge transfer between *CO and the catalyst was further analyzed by charge density distribution and Bader charge analysis (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 1). A total of 0.376e and 0.371e electrons were transferred from the Cu(111) surface to *CO in the presence of P4VP and P2VP (Supplementary Table 1), respectively, which was greater than that of bare Cu(111). This, similar to the calculations for CO2 activation, is beneficial for the further reduction of *CO to C2+ products, as corroborated by the energy profile in Fig. 4c. The potential determining step (PDS) for the CO2RR, i.e., the free energy difference (ΔG) for C-C coupling on Cu(111)-P4VP, is 0.87 eV under −1.7 V vs. RHE, and that for Cu(111)-P2VP is 1.09 eV (Fig. 4c). Both are lower than that of the bare Cu(111) surface (1.25 eV). The outcome is an improved probability of C-C coupling, offering higher FEs for C2+ products on polyvinylpyridine-modified Cu. This is in accordance with the experimental observations (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Figs. 7, 10, and 11).

The last question is, why does polyvinylpyridine reduce the activity of the CORR? This is explained by the reduction in the contact angle between the electrode and a water drop from 120° to 58° after increasing the fraction of P4VP from 0% to 67% (Supplementary Fig. 27). The reduction in surface hydrophobicity was also confirmed after P2VP coating (Supplementary Fig. 28). The CO2-to-C2+ FE increases with the addition of more P4VP at both 60 and 200 mA cm−2 until the ratio reaches 1:1; this trend is more substantial at 60 mA cm−2. On the contrary, the CO-to-C2+ FE declines along with increased P4VP fractions, particularly at 220 mA cm−2. The loss of surface hydrophobicity is detrimental to the CO(2)RR to multi-carbon products because of the reduction in the interfacial gas/water ratio20,21, the shortening of *CO residence28,34, and the formation of more inactive bridge-bound *CO35–37. These adverse effects are overcome in the case of the CO2RR on Cu2O-P4VP-derived Cu by CO2 enrichment and charge redistribution. However, for the CORR, the availability of CO decreases in the presence of hydrophilic P4VP, which can offset the benefit of the charge distribution. Also, our DFT calculations suggest that P2VP and P4VP additives appear to suppress C2+ product formation at CuCO sites on defective Cu(100) surfaces that are composed of CuCO sites (Supplementary Fig. 29)6. Consequently, the CORR on CuCO sites is preferentially suppressed compared to that on bare OD-Cu.

In summary, we demonstrated the independent tuning of C2+ product formation at two distinct Cu sites by modifying OD-Cu via P4VP. The working principle of the approach is the difference in microenvironments created by P4VP near the two sites. With P4VP modification, CO2-to-C2+ activity increases by a factor of 1.3 to 3, and CORR FEs decrease by up to 40% at the same current densities compared with those of bare OD-Cu. Using CO2/CO co-electrolysis, 13C isotopic labeling tests, and DFT calculations, we detailed the underlying reasons, including CO2 enrichment, charge redistribution, and the loss of hydrophobicity on the Cu surfaces. These factors preferentially improve the C2+ product formation activity at the CuCO2 sites by a factor of 3.

Methods

Materials

Unless otherwise mentioned, all reagents and solvents were purchased from commercial sources and used as received without further purification. Poly-2-vinylpyridine (P2VP, MW 37500), poly-4-vinylpyridine (P4VP, MW 60000), copper sulfate (CuSO4, ≥99%), trisodium citrate (Na3C6H5O7, ≥99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥98%), ascorbic acid (C6H8O6, ≥98%), potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3, ≥99%), and NafionTM 1100 W solution (5 wt%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Methanol (CH3OH, ≥99.5%) and acetone (C3H6O, ≥99.5%) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl, 38%) and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%) were purchased from Qiangsheng Functional Chemical (Changshu) Co., Ltd. Iridium trichloride (IrCl3 xH2O, 50-58%) was purchased from J & K Reagent Co., Ltd. Titanium mesh (20 cm × 20 cm, thickness: 0.3 mm) was purchased from Suzhou Suke Lean Instrument Co., Ltd. Millipore water (18.2 MΩ) was used throughout all the experiments.

Computational methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) version 5.4.4, with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method and a plane wave basis set38–40. The exchange function was described by the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional parameterization of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)41. The DFT-D3 method with Becke-Johnson damping was included in the calculations to account for dispersion corrections42. The energy cutoff was set to 400 eV. The Brillouin zone was sampled with a grid of 3 × 3 × 1 via the Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack scheme43. Spin polarization was not included in the calculations because it had no appreciable effect on the overall energies, thereby reducing computational cost. A conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm was used to relax the ions during energy minimization. The minimization was considered converged when all atomic forces were smaller than 0.01 eV/Å, where convergence to an energy minimum was confirmed in all cases by frequency calculations to verify that no imaginary frequencies remained. The difference in energy between the two steps was less than 1 × 10−6 eV during the electron self-consistency process. Constant potential calculations were performed via the open-source software JDFTx on the basis of the first principles of plane-wave expansions and the GGA-PBE generalized function to describe the exchange-correlation energy with an energy cutoff of 20 Hartrees44. The solvation calculations were performed via the CANDLE implicit solvent model45, which includes an implicit ion model (0.1 M K+ and 0.1 M F- ions)46. In the CANDLE model, the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) potential was set to −1.7 V.

In the frequency calculations, only surface adsorbates were evaluated, while the slab atoms were kept fixed. Vibrational frequencies and normal modes were derived from the Hessian matrix. Finite differences were used to calculate the Hessian matrix. Each ion was displaced by ±0.04 Å along each Cartesian coordinate, and the Hessian matrix was determined from the forces. From the calculated vibrational frequencies, we obtained the zero-point energy (EZPE), which is also present at 0 K, and corrected the entropy (S), which contributes significantly to the free energy corrections of gas molecules and is the main source of the temperature effect47.

The single-point solvation and applied-voltage corrections were applied using the open-source joint density functional theory (JDFTx) package, based on optimized geometries obtained from VASP45. In JDFTx, the exchange-correlation energy utilized GGA-PBE with a 20 Hartree cutoff. The implicit CANDLE solvation model (with 0.1 M K+/F– ions) accounts for solvent effects, and the applied voltage was set at −1.7 V RHE. Simulation models

On the basis of the experimental XRD characterization report, the (111) facet of the face-centered cubic (fcc) Cu crystal structure was chosen as our simulation model in this study. Moreover, three simulation models, i.e., Cu(111), Cu(111)-P2VP, and Cu(111)-P4VP, were constructed to study the mechanisms of the CO2RR. Briefly, the Cu(111) surface was modeled as a 6 × 4 × 3 supercell. To reduce the computational cost and time while ensuring accurate calculation, representative units of the P2VP and P4VP polymers were selected for modeling and placed on Cu(111) surfaces. All systems were set up with a sufficiently large vacuum space (~15 Å) to prevent interactions between periodic images along the z direction. The simulation models are shown in Supplementary Fig. 22. During the optimization of the intermediates in the CO2RR process, the atoms in the bottom two layers are fixed, whereas the remaining atoms are relaxed.

Experimental section

Preparation of Cu2O nanocrystals

A 1.2 M Cu2+ stock solution was first prepared by dissolving 0.47 g CuSO4 in 2.5 mL deionized water. In a typical synthesis, a 100 mL solution containing 66 mg of trisodium citrate was poured into a round-bottom flask and stirred at 600 rpm at 20 °C. Then, 0.25 mL of 1.2 M CuSO4 solution was quickly injected, followed by the addition of 0.25 mL of 4.8 M NaOH after 5 min, and the clear solution turned turbid blue. A total of 0.25 mL of 1.2 M ascorbic acid was injected, and the resulting suspension was stirred for 30 min until it turned orange. The resulting Cu2O nanocrystals were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm, washed three times with deionized water, and dried under vacuum for further use.

Preparation of the anode

The anodes were prepared by depositing IrO2 on a titanium support (0.002 mm thickness, Fuel Cell Store) via dip-coating and thermal calcination. Briefly, the titanium mesh was first degreased with acetone and deionized water and then etched in a 6 M HCl solution at ~90 °C for 45 min prior to dip-coating. The dip-coating solution was prepared by dissolving 30 mg of IrCl3•xH2O in 10 mL of an isopropanol solution containing 10 vol% concentrated HCl. The etched titanium mesh was subsequently dipped into the IrCl3 solution and dried at 100 °C, then calcined at 500 °C for 10 min. The dipping and calcination process was repeated until a mass loading of ~2 mg cm−2 was achieved.

Preparation of the cathode

Preparation of P4VP solution

First, 106 mg of P4VP powder was dissolved in 2 g of isopropanol. The mixture was then placed in a sealed vial and sonicated for 2 h to achieve complete dissolution of P4VP and to form a homogeneous P4VP solution at 5% mass concentration, which was used for subsequent gas diffusion electrode preparation.

Preparation of the Cu2O electrode

First, 20 mg of Cu2O nanocrystals were weighed and mixed with 20 μL of NafionTM perfluorinated resin solution (Sigma Aldrich, 5 wt%) in 4 mL of anhydrous methanol. Sonication was carried out for 1 h to ensure the formation of a uniform catalyst ink. The prepared catalyst ink was subsequently spray-coated onto 3 × 3 cm2 hydrophobic carbon paper (Sigracet MB30). The catalyst loading was ~2 mg cm−2.

Cu2O-P4VP electrode

Initially, 20 mg of Cu2O nanocrystals were weighed, and 20 μL of P4VP solution and 20 μL of NafionTM (Sigma Aldrich, 5 wt%) were added. The mixture was dissolved in 4 mL of anhydrous methanol and sonicated for 1 h to achieve homogeneous mixing. The prepared catalyst ink was then spray-coated onto 3 × 3 cm2 hydrophobic carbon paper (Sigracet MB30) at ~2 mg cm−2.

The preparation methods for the P2VP solution and the Cu2O-P2VP electrode were the same as those described above.

Material characterization

XRD patterns were collected on a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation. SEM imaging was performed on a HITACHI SU8230 field-emission scanning electron microscope. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed with an ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer equipped with an Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV). In-situ Raman spectroscopy was performed on an inViaTM Qontor confocal Raman spectrometer (Renishaw) using a laser wavelength of 633 nm and an Autolab PASTAT302N workstation (Metrohm). In-situ Raman spectroscopy was conducted using a three-electrode structure design. A Pt wire and an Ag/AgCl electrode served as the working and reference electrodes, respectively. During testing, the immersion objective lens was immersed in the electrolyte at a distance of 1 mm from the electrode surface. A 633 nm laser was used for Raman spectroscopy in the range of 1850–2250 cm−1. The exposure time is 20 s, the laser power is 10%, integration is performed 4 times, and the voltage range is from −0.8 to −1.8 V vs. Ag/AgCl (non-iR corrected).

Electrocatalytic measurement of CO(2) reduction

Electrochemical experiments were performed in a 5 cm2 membrane electrode assembly (MEA) electrolyzer (Dioxide Materials Company). The anode and cathode were assembled with an anion-exchange membrane (PiperION A40, 40 μm thickness, 3 × 3 cm2, Versogen). Before tests, the membrane was activated in 1 M KOH at room temperature (25 °C) for one day. A 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte was used as the anolyte and circulated at a flow rate of 10 mL min–1 via a peristaltic pump (F01A-STP, Kameor). The CO2 flow rate to the cathode was maintained at 30 sccm using a digital mass flow controller (CS200A, Seven Star Fluid (Beijing) Co., Ltd.). CO2 or CO was bubbled through a humidifier before it entered the cathode flow field. The cathode outlet gas flow rate was measured using a soap-film flowmeter during electrolysis. Chronopotentiometry (V-t) within the current density range of 60–260 mA cm−2 was performed on a DongHua Analytical instrument (DH7001) (from Jiangsu Donghua Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd.). All experiments were performed with fresh materials and repeated at least three times to obtain error bars. All potentials reported are non-iR corrected. The gaseous products produced at the cathode outlet were analyzed via an online gas chromatograph (GC2060, Wuhao Information Technology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a flame ionization detector (FID). The liquid sample was collected from the electrolyte under each current condition and injected into a high-performance liquid chromatograph (Vanquish Core, ThermoFisher) to detect the liquid product.

For the tests using diluted CO2, CO2 was mixed with Ar at different ratios and injected into the electrolyzers using two mass flow controllers. The current density was fixed at 60 mA cm−2, and the measurement was performed using a 5-cm2 zero-gap CO2 electrolyzer fed with 0.1 M KHCO3 anolyte.

CO2 solubility measurements

For the CO2 solubility measurement, solutions of 0.1 M KHCO3 and 0.1 M KHCO3 with 0.06 g mL−1 of P4VP or P2VP were prepared. The pH of these solutions was then measured with a pH meter to determine the dissolved CO2 concentrations. The solutions were then purged with CO2 for 2 h to reach saturation, and their pH was measured again using a pH meter to determine the CO2 solubility.
