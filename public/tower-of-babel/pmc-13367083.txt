How I treat soft tissue sarcomas
Authors: Javier Pozas, Niveditha Vasagiri, Ioanna Nixon, Robin L Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSoft tissue sarcomas are mesenchymal malignancies with heterogeneous biological behavior and response to therapy. Surgery remains the gold standard approach for localized disease, with perioperative radiotherapy recommended for high-risk extremity tumors. The benefit of perioperative chemotherapy remains controversial. In the metastatic setting, anthracycline-based chemotherapy has been the first-line treatment for decades. Chemotherapy regimens upon progression to anthracyclines offer very limited disease control. Immune checkpoint inhibitors and T-cell receptor therapies have shown promising clinical activity in some histology subtypes. Advances in molecular profiling have revealed novel actionable targets, and several tailored therapies have proven to be highly effective in tumors harboring specific mutations. This review provides a comprehensive overview of current treatment strategies for soft tissue sarcomas, with emphasis on multidisciplinary management and the importance of developing histology-specific clinical trials to improve outcomes in these rare malignancies.

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) encompass over 70 histological subtypes of mesenchymal tumors, typically arising from smooth muscle, adipose tissue or blood vessels [1]. In Europe, the incidence of STS is estimated at 2–4 cases per 100,000 person-years [2]. Many of these entities are classified as ultra-rare sarcomas, for which there is little or no prospective evidence to guide treatment. Current management often relies on small single-arm phase II trials, retrospective series, case reports and expert consensus [3,4].

STS are generally classified according to site of origin into extremity STS (E-STS), trunk STS and intraabdominal and retroperitoneal STS (IaR-STS). More rarely, they can originate in the head and neck region [5].

Surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment in localized disease. Radiotherapy (RT) and chemotherapy (ChT) and can be offered in the perioperative setting for E-STS, depending on risk stratification, while there is little evidence to support perioperative treatment in IaR-STS. Management decisions should always be made by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) that include surgeons, medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, radiologists and pathologists, among others.

In patients with metastatic disease, systemic ChT remains the preferred frontline treatment modality in most circumstances and for most histological subtypes. However, responses to ChT are often short-lived. Surgical resection, local ablative therapies, or even active surveillance can be considered in certain situations. A better understanding of the molecular landscape of STS has allowed the development of new treatment strategies. As such, hormone agents, targeted therapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors can be used in some STS subtypes, even in the first line setting.

In this review, we aim to provide a comprehensive state-of-the-art summary of the multidisciplinary management of STS. Gastrointestinal stromal tumors and rhabdomyosarcoma have not been included as they have specific management.

Localized STS

For patients with localized disease, surgery remains the only curative treatment. Oncologic resection should aim for surgical margins of at least 20 mm, particularly in high-grade STS [6]. Achieving adequate surgical margins can be significantly morbid and technically challenging in certain anatomical locations. For instance, head and neck STS often require surgeries with complex reconstructions, limb salvage may not be possible in extensive E-STS, and large IaR-STS often require multivisceral resection to achieve clear margins. Even with optimal surgical management, a significant proportion of patients will experience local and/or distant recurrence [7–9], which tend to occur within the first couple of years after surgery, although late relapses are also well documented [10]. Tumor size, histological grade and surgical margins are established risk factors for local and distant recurrence, as well as predictors of survival [11–13].

Perioperative treatment has been explored in localized STS to reduce the risk of disease recurrence. Post-operative RT was prospectively evaluated in patients with E-STS, showing a reduction of local recurrence rates but no survival benefit [14]. Some years later, a randomized clinical trial compared pre-operative with post-operative RT in E-STS, showing greater risk of wound complications but improved survival for patients receiving pre-operative RT [15]. Neoadjuvant RT allows for a lower total dose and more precise target delineation, which translates into fewer long-term complications [16]. In retroperitoneal STS, the STRASS trial failed to demonstrate increased local control with post-operative RT [17]. In summary, perioperative RT is not recommended in retroperitoneal STS, whereas in E-STS, pre-operative RT is preferred over post-operative RT, except for rapidly growing or very symptomatic tumors, where upfront surgical resection is favored.

The role of perioperative ChT remains controversial. A large randomized trial of 351 patients with grade 2–3 STS found no improvement in relapse-free survival (RFS) or overall survival (OS) with post-operative ChT [18,19]. A subsequent meta-analysis, which did not include the aforementioned trial, reported a statistically significant but marginal benefit of adjuvant ChT [20]. A smaller study by the Italian Sarcoma Group in high-risk E-STS demonstrated a survival benefit with five cycles of adjuvant epirubicin and ifosfamide compared to observation [21], and subsequent evidence suggested that three cycles were not inferior to five [22]. There was an interest to determine whether histology-tailored ChT regimens, using agents considered more active in specific STS subtypes, could improve outcomes. This was explored in a randomized clinical trial, but histology-specific systemic therapy underperformed compared with standard anthracyclines and ifosfamide [23]. In high-risk retroperitoneal STS, the STRASS-2 phase III clinical trial is evaluating the efficacy of neoadjuvant ChT, and results are awaited [24]. Given the conflictive evidence, the indication for perioperative ChT should be discussed in a sarcoma MDT. Sarculator is a multivariable prognostic model that identifies high-risk patients who may benefit from perioperative ChT, and can help guide management in daily clinical practice [25].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are increasingly being explored in STS and, as in other tumor types, the interest has expanded from advanced disease to earlier, localized stages [26]. The SARC-032 study was a randomized phase III trial evaluating the combination of pre-operative anti-PD-1 pembrolizumab and RT versus pre-operative RT alone in patients with high-risk undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS) and dedifferentiated or pleomorphic liposarcoma (DD-LPS, P-LPS) of the extremity. The 2-year disease-free survival (DFS) was significantly higher in the experimental arm (67%, 90% CI 58 to 78) compared to the control arm (52%, 90% CI 42 to 64) [27]. The impact on OS has not yet been reported and will be important to establish the role of ICIs in the peri-operative setting, although patient selection will be essential. The SARC-032 trial did not report radiological response rates, but if we extrapolate the results from the metastatic setting, the phase II SARC-028 clinical trial exploring single agent pembrolizumab in patients with advanced STS, found an objective response rate (ORR) of 23% in the UPS cohort and 10% in the LPS cohort [28]. This is similar to the historically reported performance of doxorubicin-based ChT in advanced UPS and DD-LPS [29–31].

Unresectable and metastatic STS

Active surveillance and local therapies

Management of unresectable, locally advanced disease generally mirrors that of metastatic disease. However, definitive RT may be considered in the absence of metastatic spread or in patients with a low burden of distant disease. In a retrospective study, 59 patients with unresectable non-E-STS were treated with moderately hypofractionated definitive RT, achieving a local control rate at 1 year of 90.8% [32]. This is not a standard of care approach at present, and prospective evidence is needed to further interrogate the value of RT in this setting.

In patients with indolent oligometastatic disease, local therapies and even active surveillance can be appropriate. In a single center retrospective analysis of 223 patients with new metastatic disease, 36% had oligometastatic disease, defined as 1–3 distant lesions. One third of these patients had a period of active surveillance of at least 6 months before receiving any therapy. This period of watchful waiting is informative of the tempo of disease progression and may allow better planning of subsequent treatments. In this study, 74.1% of patients with oligometastatic disease were offered local therapy, including surgery (48%), RT (17%) and radiofrequency ablation (11%). Time to progression at any site was 7.9 months after surgery, 4.6 months after RT and 3.3 months after radiofrequency ablation [33]. Other studies focusing on the value of stereotactic body radiation therapy for lung metastases showed excellent local control in selected patients [34,35]. In addition to upfront treatment, local therapies may also be considered between courses of ChT, with some studies suggesting a potential survival benefit compared to patients receiving ChT alone [36].

First line chemotherapy

For most patients with widespread metastatic disease, ChT is the preferred choice of treatment. Single agent doxorubicin remains the standard of care approach in most patients [37]. The addition of ifosfamide was explored in the EORTC 62,012 phase III clinical trial. The combination of doxorubicin and ifosfamide provided higher ORR compared with single agent doxorubicin (26% vs 14%, respectively, p < 0.0006). However, there was no improvement in OS; 14.3 months for the combination and 12.8 months for doxorubicin alone (HR 0.83, 95.5% CI 0.67–1.03) [38]. Single agent doxorubicin is therefore sufficient for most patients. The combination should be reserved for situations in which rapid clinical response is needed, such as very symptomatic patients or tumors in critical anatomical locations where further growth could lead to serious complications.

Until very recently, no alternative ChT regimen had improved the outcomes of doxorubicin. A large meta-analysis including 6156 patients from 27 randomized clinical trials concluded that no experimental arm regimen offered improved efficacy compared with single-agent doxorubicin [39]. Over the past decades, clinical trials in STS have largely been conducted using a “one-size-fits-all” approach, grouping together multiple histological subtypes despite their significant biological heterogeneity. It is now well established that STS comprise a diverse family of diseases with distinct molecular drivers, clinical behavior, patterns of recurrence, and sensitivity to systemic therapies. In recent years, there has been a considerable effort within the community to conduct histology-specific clinical trials. The LMS04 phase III trial compared single agent doxorubicin with doxorubicin and trabectedin induction, followed by trabectedin maintenance in patients with advanced leiomyosarcoma (LMS). The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 12 months for the combination and 6 months for the doxorubicin group, and the OS was 33 months for the doxorubicin-trabectedin cohort and 24 months for the control arm [40]. When this combination is not available, a propensity score-matching analysis from a large retrospective European cohort of patients with advanced LMS suggested that the combination of doxorubicin and dacarbazine outperformed single agent doxorubicin and doxorubicin-ifosfamide in terms of ORR and PFS, representing an alternative treatment for these patients [41]. In DD-LPS, the eagerly anticipated phase III BRIGHTLINE-1 trial comparing the MDM2 inhibitor brigimadlin with doxorubicin in treatment-naïve patients, failed to meet its primary endpoint. Median PFS was 8.38 months for brigimadlin and 7.16 months for doxorubicin (HR 0.79; 95% CI 0.60–1.06) [42].

Second line treatment and beyond

After progression on doxorubicin, second line ChT may offer a survival advantage [43]. There is no standard-of-care treatment, but gemcitabine-based regimens, trabectedin, eribulin, ifosfamide and pazopanib are often used. Two randomized phase II trials showed that combination regimens are superior to gemcitabine or dacarbazine (DTIC) monotherapy. A trial from the Spanish Group for Research on Sarcomas randomized 113 patients with previously treated advanced STS to receive gemcitabine-DTIC or DTIC alone. PFS was higher in the gemcitabine-DTIC group (4.2 vs 2 months, HR 0.58; 95% CI 0.39–0.86) and so was OS (16.8 vs 8.2 months, HR 0.56; 95% CI 0.36–0.9) [44]. The SARC002 study randomized 122 patients to gemcitabine-docetaxel or gemcitabine monotherapy. Again, there was an improvement in both PFS (6.2 vs 3 months) and OS (17.9 vs 11.5 months) for the combination cohort [45]. A large, randomized phase III trial compared trabectedin and DTIC in patients with advanced LPS and LMS. Treatment with trabectedin resulted in improved PFS but no OS benefit in either the overall population or the subgroup of patients with uterine LMS [46–48]. Following satisfactory results in a non-randomized phase II study [49], eribulin was further evaluated in a randomized phase III trial against DTIC. This trial included patients with advanced LPS and LMS [50]. There was a benefit in OS for the eribulin cohort in the overall population (13.5 vs 11.5 months, HR 0.77; 95% CI 0.62–0.95). However, subgroup analyses showed that this benefit was likely driven by the LPS cohort [51], where eribulin improved both PFS and OS, but there was no significant difference between the two agents in patients with LMS [52]. Pazopanib is an oral multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) that was initially evaluated in STS in a non-randomized phase II clinical trial [53]. The promising results led to the pivotal phase III clinical trial PALETTE, which randomized patients to receive pazopanib or placebo. PFS was longer with pazopanib (4.6 vs 1.6 months, HR 0.31; 95% CI 0.25–0.40), with no significant difference in OS (12.5 vs 10.7 months, HR 0.86; 0.67–1.11) [54]. Adipocytic tumors were excluded from the PALETTE trial following discouraging results in the preceding phase II study. Subsequent data from two phase II trials is conflicting: one showed favorable activity in DD-LPS and myxoid LPS [55], whereas another trial had to close the myxoid LPS arm due to futility, while showing good activity in patients with WD/DD-LPS [56]. As such, the role of pazopanib in patients with advanced LPS remains controversial [57,58]. Cabozantinib, another multi-TKI, was evaluated in a single arm phase II trial in 54 patients with advanced STS. The ORR was 11.1%, and 49.3% of participants were progression-free at 6 months [59]. More recently, catequentinib improved median PFS compared with placebo (3.42 vs 1.41 months, respectively) in a phase III trial in patients with advanced LMS [60]. Single agent ifosfamide has also been used in refractory advanced STS for decades, supported by historical phase II trials demonstrating antitumour activity [61–64]. A more recent single-center retrospective analysis showed efficacy across STS subtypes, particularly in synovial sarcoma (SS) [65]. However, ifosfamide has a non-negligible toxicity profile, so careful treatment selection is imperative. Metronomic oral cyclophosphamide plus prednisolone has shown some activity in elderly patients, but there is no prospective evidence to support the role of this regimen in the management of STS [66]. Other ChT regimens such as oral etoposide have very limited efficacy [67,68].

Targetable opportunities in STS.

Other relevant histological subtypes

Uterine sarcomas

Approximately, 50% of uterine sarcomas overexpress estrogen (ER) and/or progesterone receptors (PR). This is more frequent in low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma (LG-ESS) and uterine LMS (uLMS), and represents an opportunity for hormone-based therapies [87]. A meta-analysis including 10 retrospective studies concluded that adjuvant hormone therapy can reduce the risk of recurrence but does not improve survival [88]. In the metastatic setting, Zang and colleagues recently compiled available retrospective series and case reports of patients with LG-ESS treated with aromatase inhibitors (AI), finding a clinical benefit rate of 91.7% in the first line setting and 89.7% in the second line setting [89]. Rarely, LG-ESS can dedifferentiate and transform to high grade uterine sarcomas [90]. This is usually associated with loss of estrogen/progesterone expression, and should be treated as high-grade STS [91]. uLMS, particularly low-grade uLMS, can also overexpress hormone receptors. A single arm phase II trial evaluated the AI letrozole in 27 patients with refractory metastatic uLMS expressing ER and/or PR. The 12-week PFS rate was 50% and patients with a higher level of hormone receptor expression had longer responses [83].

Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma

The SARC028 was one of the first clinical trials to evaluate the value of ICIs in STS. This trial enrolled patients with pretreated STS to receive a flat dose of three-weekly pembrolizumab. The results in the overall cohort were underwhelming, but there were some clear positive signals in some specific histotypes such as undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma (UPS), where the ORR was 40% [92]. UPS has higher levels of PD-1 and PD-L1 expression as well as high T-cell infiltration [93]. Consistent with this immunologically active tumor microenvironment, correlative analysis from the SARC028 trial showed that responses to pembrolizumab were more frequently observed in tumors with increased levels of T-cell activation and higher expression of PD-L1 on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) [94]. In the first line setting, cohort 7a of the master ImmunoSarc2 trial evaluated the combination of epirubicin, ifosfamide and nivolumab in patients with untreated advanced UPS. The ORR was 68.8%, significantly higher than the historical benchmark for standard ChT, and the median PFS was 9.9 months [95]. A phase III trial comparing frontline pembrolizumab and doxorubicin with single-agent doxorubicin in patients with UPS and DD-LPS is currently recruiting patients, and the results are eagerly awaited as they could be practice-changing [96].

Alveolar soft part sarcoma

Alveolar soft part sarcoma (ASPS) is an ultra-rare sarcoma that harbors the characteristic ASPSCR1:TFE3 fusion [97]. ASPS is intrinsically resistant to ChT; a series from the MD Anderson Cancer Centre reported no responses to ChT among 26 patients with metastatic ASPS [98]. The results from initial phase II trials evaluating ICIs in various sarcoma subtypes suggested that ASPS could be particularly sensitive to checkpoint inhibition [99,100]. In 2020, Shi and colleagues published the results of a single-arm phase II trial with anti-PD1 geptanolimab exclusively in patients with ASPS, showing an ORR of 37.8% and median PFS of 6.9 months. More recently, a multicentre phase II trial evaluated anti-PD-L1 atezolizumab in 52 patients with advanced ASPS in the US. The ORR was 37% and the median PFS was 20.8 months [101]. Following these results, atezolizumab was approved by the FDA and represents a new treatment opportunity for these patients.

TKIs have also been explored and represented the only reasonable therapy option before the arrival of ICIs. The CASPS phase II trial of cediranib versus placebo showed a −8.3% change in the sum of target lesion diameters compared with 13.4% with placebo [102]. A single-arm phase II trial reported an ORR of 35% and a disease control rate of 84% at 24 weeks [103]. Other TKIs such as pazopanib, crizotinib, sunitinib or cabozantinib have also been explored in phase II trials or retrospective studies [104]. As with other tumor types, the observed efficacy of TKIs and ICIs as monotherapies led to investigation of combination strategies. A phase II trial of axitinib and pembrolizumab in STS, enriched with ASPS patients (36%), showed an ORR of 54.5% (6/11) among patients with ASPS [100]. Similarly, in the ASPS cohort of the ImmunoSarc II study evaluating nivolumab and sunitinib, the ORR was 53% (9/17) and the median PFS was 13.1 months [105]. Despite the small numbers, the activity signal is encouraging, and these combinations should be explored in randomized studies.

Synovial sarcoma and myxoid round cell liposarcoma

SS and myxoid/round cell liposarcoma (MRCLS) are rare, aggressive STS that disproportionately affect young adults and are refractory to standard ChT, leaving a high unmet need for new systemic therapies. Both tumors frequently express cancer-testis antigens such as MAGE-A4 and NY-ESO-1, which are presented on tumor cells in the context of specific HLA class I alleles and can be targeted by engineered T-cell receptor therapy (TCR-T) [106–108]. This consists of autologous T cells that are genetically modified to express high-affinity receptors recognizing these intracellular antigen peptides, allowing antigen-specific tumor cell killing that is not possible with conventional CAR-T.

Afamitresgene autoleucel (afami-cel), targeting MAGE-A4 in HLA-A *02-positive patients, recently marked a milestone as the first FDA-approved TCR-T therapy for any solid tumor, based on the phase 2 SPEARHEAD-1 results in advanced SS and MRCLS. In this heavily pretreated population, afami-cel achieved an ORR of 39% in SS and 25% in MRCLS, with durable responses and an expected safety profile dominated by lymphodepletion-related cytopenias and predominantly low-grade cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [109]. Letetresgene autoleucel (lete-cel), which targets NY-ESO-1 in patients with HLA-A *02:01/02:05/02:06 alleles, has shown similarly compelling activity in the IGNYTE-ESO trial across SS and MRCLS, with an ORR of 39% and 41%, respectively, including complete responses, and a median duration of response of 10.6 months [110]. Despite these promising efficacy signals, TCR-T is not suitable for all patients: eligibility is restricted by HLA and tumor expression of the relevant antigen (MAGE-A4 for afami-cel, NY-ESO-1 for lete-cel), the manufacturing is complex and lengthy, and lymphodepletion ChT is associated with significant toxicities.

Beyond TCR-T, SS is particularly sensitive to ifosfamide, which can provide prolonged disease control in some patients [65,111]. The phase III APROMISS trial compared catequentinib with dacarbazine in patients with advanced SS. The trial met its primary endpoint, with a median PFS of 2.89 months with catequentinib and 1.64 months with dacarbazine, although the clinical relevance is limited [112].

PEComa

Perivascular epithelioid cell tumor (PEComa) is an ultra-rare mesenchymal vascular neoplasm, often characterized by inactivating alterations in the TSC1 or TSC2 tumor suppressor genes that lead to pathologic activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway. Early clinical experience with oral mTOR inhibitor sirolimus demonstrated some level of response in all treated patients [113]. The most significant prospective evidence comes from the AMPECT trial, which evaluated albumin‑bound sirolimus (nab‑sirolimus) in patients with advanced malignant PEComa. This phase II registration study reported an ORR of 39%, with durable disease control and a median PFS of 10.6 months and median OS beyond 40 months. Responses were more frequent in patients whose tumors harbored TSC2 mutations. In light of these results, nab‑sirolimus received FDA approval for advanced malignant PEComa, representing the first approved systemic therapy for this disease [70].

Conventional cytotoxic ChT has shown limited efficacy in advanced PEComa, with low ORR and short median PFS in retrospective series. In a multicenter retrospective analysis, mTOR inhibitors demonstrated the highest activity among systemic therapies, with an ORR of 41% and median PFS of 9 months, while ChT and antiangiogenic agents showed significantly lower efficacy [114]. Beyond mTOR inhibition, small case reports and case series have explored combinations such as antioestrogen therapy with mTOR inhibition in selected patients and immunotherapy with anti‑VEGFR after mTOR inhibitor progression, though data is retrospective and anecdotal [115,116]. A substudy of the DART phase II trial evaluating ipilimumab and nivolumab in malignant PEComa showed an ORR of 18.8%, including some durable responses, representing a potential treatment alternative for these patients [117].

Novel combinations and agents to overcome mTOR inhibitor resistance, as well as further evaluation of ICIs are part of the current clinical research strategies in advanced PEComas. There is a multicohort phase II trial evaluating ivonescimab, a PD-1/VEGF bispecific, including a malignant PEComa cohort [118]. This is a bispecific antibody that simultaneously targets PD-1 and VEGFA and has shown promising results in non-small cell lung cancer [119,120].

Epithelioid haemangioendothelioma

Metastatic epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (EHE) is an ultra-rare vascular sarcoma characterized by a heterogeneous clinical behavior. Because in many patients the disease remains stable for prolonged periods of time, active surveillance is a reasonable initial strategy for asymptomatic patients with low tumor burden and slowly progressive metastatic disease [121,122].

When systemic treatment is required, evidence derives from retrospective series and small prospective studies. Cytotoxic ChT has historically shown limited efficacy, with low response rates [123]. A recent single-arm phase II trial explored eribulin in patients with advanced angiosarcoma and EHE. The ORR was 33% (2/6) in the EHE cohort and both patients remained on treatment for over 12 months [124]. mTOR inhibitor sirolimus is one of the most active treatments in EHE. In a large retrospective series from the Italian Rare Cancer Network, sirolimus achieved a clinical benefit rate of 56% and a median PFS of 12 months in patients with advanced, progressive EHE, particularly in the absence of serosal effusions, which is a poor prognosis factor [125]. These findings were further confirmed in a subsequent expanded analysis with 57 patients, reinforcing sirolimus as the preferred choice in patients with progressive metastatic EHE [71]. Given the dependence of EHE carcinogenesis on MAPK signaling, TKIs have also been explored in this disease. A single-arm phase II trial of MEK inhibitor trametinib in patients with advanced EHE showed improved symptom control, but did not meet the primary endpoint of ORR, which was 3.7% [126]. The activity of multi-TKI pazopanib in treatment-naïve patients was explored in a retrospective study from the German group, achieving a median PFS and OS of 35.1 and 53.8 months, respectively [127]. Locoregional treatments such as surgery, radiotherapy, or ablation may play a role in selected patients with oligometastatic disease or for symptom control and should be considered within a multidisciplinary framework. As EHE is also driven by YAP/TAZ-TEAD signaling in the context of inactivation of the Hippo pathway, a phase 1/2 first-in-human clinical study (TEADES) is underway to assess the safety and preliminary efficacy of TEAD inhibitor ODM-212 in subjects with selected advanced solid tumors, including EHE [128].

Angiosarcoma

Angiosarcoma (AS) is a rare and aggressive vascular malignancy arising from endothelial cells. Primary AS can be classified into cutaneous angiosarcoma, often arising from the scalp, and visceral AS, which originates in deep organs, such as the liver or the heart. Secondary or radiation-induced AS appears years after exposure to RT. Despite adequate surgical resection, these tumors have a tendency to recur, particularly with distant metastases [129]. For this reason, peri-operative ChT can be considered, although there is a lack of consensus and practice varies across different countries and institutions [130]. The absence of prospective data evaluating the impact of perioperative treatment on survival limits the generalization of this approach, but it can be discussed in a case-by-case fashion.

Systemic therapy remains the mainstay of treatment for metastatic or unresectable disease. Historically, anthracycline-based ChT has been used by analogy with other STS, but there are no prospective clinical trials in AS [131]. Paclitaxel is a widely used alternative of anthracycline-based ChT. In the ANGIOTAX phase II trial the median PFS and OS were 4 and 8 months, respectively [132]. A subsequent phase II trial explored paclitaxel with or without bevacizumab. The combination was more toxic and did not improve survival. In the paclitaxel cohort, the median PFS and OS were 6.6 and 19.5 months, respectively [133]. In a large retrospective series, the performance of paclitaxel and doxorubicin in the front line setting was comparable [134]. Other cytotoxic agents, such as gemcitabine, are sometimes used in the management of these patients but the evidence is limited to retrospective studies [135,136]. TKIs, such as pazopanib and sorafenib have also been explored, leading to ORR of 12–23% and median PFS of 2–4 months [137–139]. A phase III trial of pazopanib with anti-endoglin monoclonal antibody carotuximab showed no improved survival compared with single agent pazopanib [140]. ICIs have emerged as a promising treatment option for patients with advanced AS, although efficacy varies across different trials [141]. Single-agent anti-PD-1 and combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies have reached response rates of approximately 20–30%, appearing to be more effective in patients with cutaneous AS [142,143]. This is probably due to a higher tumor mutational burden (TMB) associated with an UV signature. The novel Fc-enhanced anti-CTLA-4 botensilimab, in combination with anti-PD-1 balstilimab, has shown good preliminary results in a large phase I trial including multiple STS histologies, with an ORR of 33.3% in the visceral AS cohort and 22.2% in the cutaneous AS cohort [144]. Aiming to improve the outcome of ICIs, the addition of TKIs has also been explored in several phase II trials. The combination of cabozantinib and nivolumab reached an ORR of 62% and median PFS of 9.6 months in a taxane-pretreated population, suggesting some potential synergistic effect [145]. The Alliance A091902 first-line trial compared single-agent paclitaxel with paclitaxel and nivolumab, with no difference in PFS but good performance in the cutaneous cohort [146].

Although little is known about the molecular landscape of AS, The Angiosarcoma Project showed that patient-partnered research, using remote enrollment and direct patient engagement, can produce clinically meaningful genomic data. The study identified different molecular subsets of AS, including cutaneous AS enriched for a UV damage signature, and some breast AS harboring PIK3CA mutations, which represents a potential therapeutic target [147].

Solitary fibrous tumor

Metastatic solitary fibrous tumor (SFT) is another highly heterogeneous ultra-rare sarcoma, genetically driven by the NAB2-STAT6 fusion. Conventional ChT has shown very limited efficacy [148]. A phase II trial evaluating trabectedin as well as doxorubicin plus dacarbazine in patients with advanced STF had to close recruitment of the typical SFT (T-SFT) and malignant SFT (M-SFT) cohorts as no responses were observed [149]. The results of the dedifferentiated SFT (DD-SFT) cohort are not yet available. Anti-angiogenic agents seem to be more active in SFT, particularly in non-dedifferentiated variants and should therefore be considered. A phase II trial of pazopanib in patients with advanced T-SFT and M-SFT showed an ORR of 58% [150]. Another phase II trial with axitinib showed a similar Choi-ORR of 54%, with a median Choi-PFS of 5.1 months and median OS of 25.3 months [151].

Conclusion

In summary, surgery remains the cornerstone of management in localized STS. Perioperative RT is recommended in selected patients with E-STS, given when possible in the neoadjuvant setting. The use of (neo)adjuvant ChT in E-STS remains controversial and should be discussed case-by-case in an expert MDT. There is no evidence to support perioperative ChT or RT in retroperitoneal STS. In the metastatic setting, chemotherapy remains the backbone of treatment, although local therapies (including ablative treatments, surgery and RT), or even active surveillance can be considered in carefully selected patients. Immunotherapy continues to expand in the world of STS, with the first ICI approved for ASPS and promising results of TCR therapy in SS and MRCLPS. When available, enrollment in clinical trials should be considered given the paucity of useful treatment options in advanced STS. It is essential that patients with STS are managed in certified sarcoma centers to guarantee multidisciplinary expertise and access to clinical trials.

The sarcoma community faces significant challenges to advance the field. There is extensive molecular and clinical evidence showing that different histological entities have radically different clinical behaviors and sensitivity to treatments. For this reason, one-size-fits-all trials do not provide meaningful answers. However, conducting prospective trials in rare and ultra-rare tumors is exceedingly difficult due to low priority and slow recruitment. It is important to unite forces but also engage with the regulatory agencies to explore alternative pathways to facilitate drug approval in these diseases. Finally, more initiatives to bring together the sarcoma community and collaborate between centers are crucial to improve the outcomes of our patients.
