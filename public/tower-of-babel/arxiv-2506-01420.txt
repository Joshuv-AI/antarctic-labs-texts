Self-Refining Language Model Anonymizers via Adversarial Distillation
Kyuyoung Kim, Hyunjun Jeon, Jinwoo Shin

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in sensitive domains, where their ability to infer personal data from seemingly benign text poses emerging privacy risks. While recent LLM-based anonymization methods help mitigate such risks, they often rely on proprietary models (e.g., GPT-4), raising concerns about cost and the potential exposure of sensitive data to untrusted external systems. To address this, we introduce SElf-refining Anonymization with Language model (SEAL), a novel distillation framework for training small language models (SLMs) to perform effective anonymization without relying on external costly models at inference time. We leverage adversarial interactions between an LLM anonymizer and an inference model to collect trajectories of anonymized texts and inferred attributes, which are used to distill anonymization, adversarial inference, and utility evaluation capabilities into SLMs via supervised fine-tuning and preference learning. The resulting models learn to both anonymize text and critique their outputs, enabling iterative improvement of anonymization quality via self-refinement. Experiments on SynthPAI, a dataset of synthetic personal profiles and text comments, demonstrate that SLMs trained with SEAL achieve substantial improvements in anonymization capabilities. Notably, 8B models attain a privacy-utility trade-off comparable to that of the GPT-4 anonymizer and, with self-refinement, even surpass it in terms of privacy. These results show the effectiveness of our adversarial distillation framework in training SLMs as efficient anonymizers. To facilitate further research, we release the full dataset used in our experiments.

Introduction

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have significantly enhanced their capabilities
[
1
,
2
]
, particularly in reasoning
[
3
]
, leading to widespread adoption across domains such as conversational agents, healthcare, and finance
[
1
,
4
,
5
]
, where models often process sensitive personal information.
While early privacy concerns centered on memorization and data leakage
[
6
]
, recent work shows that LLMs can infer private attributes like location, identity, or demographics from subtle semantic cues in seemingly innocuous text
[
7
]
.
With sufficiently capable LLMs, such inferences can be surprisingly accurate and often go undetected by users, who continue to share content unaware of the privacy risks.
Traditional anonymization methods, such as those based on named entity recognition or pattern matching, focus on surface-level identifiers and often miss the context-dependent semantics that LLMs can exploit
[
8
]
.
This gap underscores the need for more robust anonymization techniques that account for semantic context to defend against inference-based privacy threats.

A growing body of research is addressing the limitations of traditional methods with LLM-based anonymization frameworks to protect semantic-level private information from model inference
[
9
,
10
]
.
In feedback-guided adversarial anonymization
[
9
]
, an LLM anonymizer processes an input text, while a second LLM, acting as an adversary, attempts to infer private attributes from the anonymized text.
Based on the adversarial feedback, the anonymizer refines the text to prevent re-identification of previously inferred attributes.
Using GPT-4 as both the anonymizer and inference model, this framework has been shown to achieve a significantly better privacy-utility trade-off compared to traditional industry-grade anonymizers.
While this demonstrates the potential of LLM anonymizers, relying on proprietary models is costly and poses privacy risks by sharing sensitive text to potentially untrusted cloud-based models
[
11
]
.
Efforts to distill GPT-4’s anonymization capabilities into smaller models have been explored to a limited extent
[
10
]
, but prior approaches continue to rely on GPT-4 for adversarial feedback, incurring similar costs and privacy risks.

To address these limitations, we propose
SElf-refining Anonymization with Language models
(SEAL), a novel framework for distilling the anonymization capabilities of LLMs into small language models (SLMs).
Our approach leverages adversarial interactions between an LLM anonymizer and an inference model to collect anonymized texts with inferred private attributes.
Using this data, we train an SLM anonymizer via a two-stage distillation: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) for task adaptation, followed by direct preference optimization (DPO)
[
12
]
for refinement.
In SFT, the model learns from trajectory pairs by taking each anonymized text as input and predicting a subsequent version with improved privacy and utility.
This encourages the model to generate diverse anonymizations with better privacy-utility trade-offs.
The model is also trained to infer private attributes and evaluate utility, enabling it to assess anonymization quality.
In DPO, we further refine the model using preference pairs, treating anonymizations that are more robust to attribute inference and exhibit better utility as preferred generations.
This trains the model to more reliably generate higher-quality anonymizations.
At inference time, the model performs iterative self-refinement, alternating between anonymization and evaluation to enhance its own outputs, without relying on external model feedback.

Experimental results on SynthPAI
[
13
]
, a diverse dataset of synthetic personal profiles and text comments, show that SLMs trained with SEAL achieve substantial improvements in anonymization performance.
Notably, 8B models attain a privacy-utility trade-off comparable to that of the GPT-4 anonymizer, which relies on the GPT-4 inference model, on unseen profile data, and, with sufficient self-refinement, even surpass it.
These results demonstrate SEAL’s effectiveness in training SLM anonymizers that defend against inference attacks without external models, enhancing privacy and efficiency through local processing of data.
Moreover, the model’s ability to perform attribute inference and utility evaluation allows users to control anonymization level based on their preferences.
To support further research, we release the full dataset used in our experiments.

Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We propose a novel framework for distilling the anonymization and adversarial inference capabilities of LLMs into SLMs for effective anonymization via iterative self-refinement.

•

We release a high-quality anonymization dataset with carefully crafted samples to train and evaluate anonymizers against LLM-based inference threats to facilitate further research.

•

Empirical results show that our framework trains SLMs that match or outperform the GPT-4 anonymizer, offering greater efficiency and enhanced privacy via local data processing.

Preliminaries

Text anonymization.

Text anonymization aims to process textual data to remove or obscure personally identifiable information (PII) while preserving the overall semantics and utility
[
14
]
.
Traditional methods primarily use rule-based de-identification
[
14
]
, such as removing explicit identifiers (e.g., names, SSNs, locations), through named entity recognition or pattern matching
[
15
]
.
While effective for surface-level PII, these methods fail to address contextually embedded or inferable private information—signals that sufficiently capable LLMs can recover with high accuracy
[
9
]
.
Recent work has explored LLM-based anonymization techniques to identify and redact such contextual cues
[
16
,
9
]
.
Our approach builds on this by distilling these capabilities into compact, locally deployable models, enabling efficient anonymization.

PII inference by LLMs.

The growing capabilities of LLMs pose emerging privacy risks, particularly when users interact with external services, as recent models can effectively infer sensitive information from seemingly innocuous user-provided data
[
7
]
.
Such inferences are often subtle and context-dependent such that users may continue to share content without fully understanding the privacy implications.
Additionally, user data are vulnerable to interception during transmission
[
17
]
or retention by service providers for purposes such as model training, increasing the risk of misuse or data breaches.
To address these concerns, we propose a framework for developing locally deployable anonymization models, enabling users to retain full control over their data while maintaining privacy protections without compromising utility.

3
Adversarial data synthesis for distillation

To enable small language models (SLMs) to serve as effective anonymizers, we employ a distillation-based approach using training data generated through adversarial anonymization
[
9
]
, where an LLM anonymizer and adversary alternate between anonymization and attribute inference to iteratively refine the input text.
We utilize this framework with sufficiently capable LLMs to collect trajectories of anonymizations and inferred attributes for distillation purposes.

Given a set of texts
\{x_{0}\}
and private attributes
\mathcal{P}
(e.g., age, gender, and location) to be anonymized, we simulate adversarial anonymization using an LLM anonymizer
\mathcal{M}_{\text{anon}}
, an inference model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{priv}}
, and a utility evaluator
\mathcal{M}_{\text{util}}
.
Specifically, we iterate the following procedure for a fixed number of steps or until no additional attributes are inferred, collecting all intermediate and final anonymizations, the inferred attributes, and the corresponding utility assessments.

1.

Attribute inference.
At each step
t
, we apply the inference model
\mathcal{M}_{\text{priv}}
to the current text
x_{t}
to identify any attributes in
\mathcal{P}
that can still be inferred:

\mathcal{P}_{t}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{priv}}(x_{t},\mathcal{P}).

For each correctly inferred attribute
p\in\mathcal{P}_{t}
, we also collect a rationale and a confidence score
\text{conf}(p)
from the model.

2.

Anonymization refinement.
If any attributes remain inferable, we use the anonymizer
\mathcal{M}_{\text{anon}}
to refine the text, providing
\mathcal{P}_{t}
as feedback:

x_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{anon}}(x_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t}).

3.

Utility evaluation.
To assess utility, we use
\mathcal{M}_{\text{util}}
to compare
x_{t+1}
to the original
x_{0}
, evaluating readability, semantic preservation, and whether any new information is introduced:

\mathcal{U}_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\text{util}}(x_{0},x_{t+1}).

For each measure, we collect a rationale and a numerical score.

At the end of the procedure, we obtain a data trajectory
\tau=(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{T})
, where each
s_{i}=(x_{i},\mathcal{P}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{i})
is a tuple consisting of a text, inferred attributes, and utility measurements.
By applying this to each text to be anonymized, we obtain a set of trajectories, which we use for distillation.

4
Self-refining language model anonymizers

In this section, we present SEAL, a novel distillation framework for training language models to perform effective anonymization via self-refinement without relying on external model feedback.

Figure 2:
Sample instructions for supervised fine-tuning.
Self-refining anonymizers are trained on anonymization (left), attribute inference (middle), and utility evaluation (right). By learning to both anonymize and assess their outputs, the models iteratively improve anonymization quality.

4.1
Learning to anonymize and critique

To enable language models to both anonymize text and evaluate their outputs, we propose a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) framework that jointly trains the model on three objectives.
Specifically, the model learns to perform the following tasks: (1)
anonymization
, rewriting input text to reduce the risk of attribute inference; (2)
adversarial inference
, predicting private attributes from a text; and (3)
utility evaluation
, assessing the semantic preservation and readability of a rewritten text.

Given a trajectory of anonymizations
\tau=(s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{T})
, where each
s_{i}=(x_{i},\mathcal{P}_{i},\mathcal{U}_{i})
consists of a text, a set of inferred private attributes with confidence scores, and a set of utility measures, we construct task-specific datasets for SFT for the three tasks.
First, to assess the relative quality of anonymizations, we define privacy and utility score functions as:

p(s_{i})=(-|\mathcal{P}_{i}|,\ -\sum_{m\in\mathcal{P}_{i}}\text{conf}(m)/|\mathcal{P}_{i}|),\quad u(s_{i})=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{U}_{i}}m/|\mathcal{U}_{i}|,

where
p(s_{i})
returns a tuple of the number of inferred attributes and the average confidence, and
u(s_{i})
returns the average utility.
Privacy scores are compared such that anonymizations for which the adversary infers fewer attributes or has lower confidence in its inferences are considered better.
Utility scores are compared based on the average of individual measures, such as those for semantic preservation and readability.
Using the score functions, we construct the dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{anon}}
by identifying all pairs in a trajectory where a later anonymization achieves better privacy and utility:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{anon}}=\left\{(x_{i},x_{j})\,\mid\,0\leq i<j\leq T,\ p(s_{j})>p(s_{i}),\ u(s_{j})\geq u(s_{i})\right\}.

Conceptually, the model learns to generate for a given text multiple anonymizations with improved privacy and utility.
For adversarial inference, the model is trained to infer private attributes from a given text, while for utility evaluation, it is trained to assess the utility of an anonymized text according to a set of criteria.
We construct the corresponding datasets as follows:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{priv}}=\left\{(x_{i},\mathcal{P}_{i})\,\mid\,s_{i}\in\tau\right\},\quad\mathcal{D}_{\text{util}}=\left\{(x_{i},\mathcal{U}_{i})\,\mid\,s_{i}\in\tau\right\}.

The model is then trained to minimize losses over the three datasets:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}=\lambda_{\text{anon}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{anon}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{anon}})+\lambda_{\text{priv}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{priv}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{priv}})+\lambda_{\text{util}}\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{util}}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{util}}),

where
\lambda
’s are weights that can be tuned based on the relative importance or size of each dataset.
Each task loss is a standard language modeling loss (i.e., next-token prediction), computed over input–output pairs derived from the corresponding dataset.
This allows the model to learn to generate anonymized text and evaluate its quality, enabling iterative self-improvement during inference.

4.2
Preference learning for anonymization

During SFT, the model is trained to generate multiple anonymizations for a given input text, without necessarily learning to prefer one over another.
We apply direct preference optimization (DPO)
[
12
]
to further enhance the model by training it to assign higher likelihoods to generations with better privacy–utility trade-offs. Preference data is constructed by treating each text in a trajectory as input and forming preference pairs from the subsequent anonymizations.
For each pair, the anonymization with better privacy and utility scores is labeled as the preferred output:

\mathcal{D}_{\text{pref}}=\left\{(x_{i},x_{w},x_{l})\,\mid\,0\leq i<w,l\leq T,\ p(s_{w})>p(s_{l}),\ u(s_{w})\geq u(s_{l})\right\}.

We then train the model on the preference dataset to minimize the DPO loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb{E}_{(x_{i},x_{w},x_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{pref}}}\biggr[\log\sigma\Bigr(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(x_{w}\mid x_{i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_{w}\mid x_{i})}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(x_{l}\mid x_{i})}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(x_{l}\mid x_{i})}\Bigr)\biggr],

where
\pi_{\theta}
denotes the model being trained, and
\pi_{\text{ref}}
is the SFT reference model.
Intuitively, the loss guides the model toward generating anonymizations with stronger privacy–utility trade-offs.
With more granular preference labels, e.g., those based on model confidence, margin-based preference learning
[
18
]
can also be applied.

4.3
Iterative self-refinement

Once trained, the anonymizer performs iterative self-refinement to improve its outputs without external model feedback.
At each step
t
, the model infers private attributes and evaluates utility for the current anonymization
x_{t}
, producing inferred attributes
\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\pi}
and utility measures
\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\pi}
.
The model then generates a refined anonymization
x_{t+1}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x_{t},\mathcal{P}_{t}^{\pi},\mathcal{U}_{t}^{\pi})
, conditioned on the privacy and utility evaluations.
This process continues until a desired privacy-utility trade-off is achieved or a fixed iterations limit is reached.
In contrast to prior work that depends on external, proprietary LLMs, our self-refining anonymizer enables fully local, privacy-preserving inference with a single model that performs both generation and critique.
This also allows interactive control over the anonymization process based on user-defined privacy–utility preferences.

5
Experimental results

5.1
Setup

Datasets.

For our experiments, we use SynthPAI
[
13
]
, a collection of synthetic personal profiles and text comments generated based on the profiles.
From this dataset, we select 3,456 text instances with high-quality human labels covering eight personal attributes: age, education level, gender, income level, location, marital status, occupation, and place of birth.
The selected comments serve as the initial texts for anonymization.
To generate anonymization trajectories for distillation, we simulate adversarial anonymization
[
9
]
for up to three steps, using GPT-4o for anonymization, attribute inference, and utility assessment.
We use 275 of the 300 synthetic profiles for training data collection and hold out the remaining 25 for evaluation, which we refer to as the
main
eval dataset.
This setup demonstrates a practical benefit of our framework: external models generate trajectories on synthetic data, while the distilled models handle sensitive data locally without external exposure.

Our analysis of SynthPAI shows that texts with contextually embedded personal information, as opposed to explicit keywords or phrases, are significantly harder to anonymize.
To evaluate on these more challenging cases, we construct 500 newly synthesized texts containing such embedded personal information using the 25 held-out profiles, forming the
hard
eval dataset.
Note that for SEAL, we do not train on any of the new data in order to evaluate how it generalizes to these more difficult cases.

Baselines.

We evaluate SEAL with several anonymization baselines.
Following prior work
[
9
]
, we include Azure’s PII detection tool
[
19
]
as a traditional anonymization baseline, which redacts sensitive information from text using named entity recognition.
To compare against pure semantic rewriting, we include Dipper
[
20
]
, a state-of-the-art 11B model trained to paraphrase but not explicitly designed for anonymization.
As a recent LLM-based anonymization approach, we evaluate the adversarial anonymization framework
[
9
]
, which iteratively refines outputs through interaction between an LLM anonymizer and an adversarial inference model.
This framework represents the current state-of-the-art for defending against inference-based privacy threats.
We evaluate using several proprietary models, including GPT-4o
[
1
]
, GPT-4o-mini, and Gemini-2.5-Flash
[
21
]
.
These baselines allow us to compare an SLM trained with SEAL against frontier models on previously unseen user profiles.

Evaluating privacy and utility.

To evaluate anonymization quality, we use GPT-4 as the inference model, given its demonstrated effectiveness at inferring personal attributes
[
7
]
.
Specifically, we use GPT-4 to infer the eight attribute types using zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting.
We report the average number of attributes it successfully infers, with a lower number indicating better privacy preservation.
For utility evaluation, we follow recent work
[
9
]
and use a GPT-4 utility judge to assess each anonymized text in terms of readability, semantic similarity to the original input, and whether it introduces new information.
The average of the three metrics is used as the final utility score.
We also report an overall score, which is defined as the difference between relative privacy improvement and relative utility loss, as a measure of the privacy-utility trade-off.

6
Discussion

In this section, we outline the broader impact, limitations, and future directions of our work.

Broader impacts.

With the growing use of LLMs in sensitive domains and their rapidly increasing capabilities, the risk of LLM-based personal attribute inference is becoming an emerging privacy concern.
While prior work demonstrates that LLMs can be leveraged to defend against such inference attacks
[
9
]
, most existing approaches rely on strong proprietary models.
SEAL presents a novel framework for training SLMs as effective anonymizers that, once trained, can perform anonymization without relying on external, potentially untrusted systems.
While further improvements are needed as LLM adversaries become more capable, we believe SEAL enables practical, privacy-preserving anonymization suitable for deployment in settings with strict data confidentiality requirements.

Limitations and future work.

While our empirical results show that SLMs trained with SEAL can be competitive with strong proprietary models, we observe that for more challenging anonymization tasks—where private information is contextually embedded rather than explicitly stated—the anonymizers require more self-refinement iterations to achieve strong privacy protection.
Future work may focus on improving the efficiency of the framework.
One potential direction is to leverage the anonymizer’s ability to act as a type of generative reward model
[
24
]
, enabling training-based self-improvement using its own feedback.

7
Conclusion

We introduce SEAL, a novel distillation framework for training language models to both anonymize text and evaluate the resulting privacy and utility, enabling effective anonymization via self-refinement.
By learning to critique their own outputs, models trained with SEAL achieve strong privacy-utility trade-offs even at moderate scale, without relying on external feedback.
Empirical results show that SEAL outperforms adversarial anonymization with frontier proprietary models in privacy, while maintaining competitive utility.
We further observe that SEAL generalizes beyond its training horizon, yielding continued gains across multiple refinement steps.
These results demonstrate the effectiveness of SEAL in training language model anonymizers, which is particularly important in privacy-sensitive settings where data must be processed locally under compute constraints.

Appendix A
Dataset Details

A.1
Personal attributes

We provide further details on the types of personal attributes we consider and how we validate the attribute values inferred by a model.

Taxonomy.

We use human-inferred personal attributes from the SynthPAI dataset as ground truth.
To ensure consistency and usability across samples, we apply the following post-processing for each of the eight attribute types:

•

Age
: Age ranges originally specified by pairs of numbers are converted to their respective midpoints.

•

Education level
: Each attribute is assigned one of the following values: ‘No High School’, ‘In High School’, ‘HS Diploma’, ‘In College’, ‘College Degree’, or ‘PhD’. Labels containing more specific details (e.g., fields of study) are mapped to these broader categories. For instance, “studying towards a Bachelor’s in International Relations” is mapped to ‘In College’.

•

Gender
: Following the original data, gender labels are categorized as ‘male’ or ‘female’, with labels marked as ‘single’ discarded.

•

Income level
: Labels are classified into one of five categories: ‘no income’, ‘low’, ‘medium’, ‘high’, or ‘very high’. Ambiguous entries such as ‘low/mid’ are standardized to the closest predefined category, in this case, ‘medium’.

•

Marital status
: Labels are categorized into four groups: ‘No Relation’, ‘In Relation’, ‘Married’, or ‘Divorced’. Other values are mapped to the closest fitting category; for instance, ‘engaged’ was recategorized as ‘In Relation’.

•

Occupation
: We retain the original values without modification.

•

Place of birth
: The values generally follow the ‘<city>, <country>’ format, with minor corrections applied, e.g., converting ‘Barcelona, Spain, USA’ to ‘Barcelona, Spain’.

•

Location
: As with place of birth, the labels follow the ‘<city>, <country>’ format and are used without additional post-processing.

Criteria for inferred attribute validation.

To evaluate the accuracy of model-inferred attributes, we assign scores on a 0–1 scale, with 1 indicating a correct inference and 0 indicating an incorrect one. Scoring criteria vary by attribute type:

•

Age
: A prediction is scored as 1 if the absolute difference from the ground truth is less than or equal to 5 years; otherwise, it receives a score of 0. For predicted age ranges, the midpoint is used for comparison.

•

Education level, gender, income level, marital status
: For these attributes, a case-insensitive string comparison is performed. A score of 1 is assigned if the predicted value matches the ground truth; otherwise, a score of 0 is assigned.

•

Occupation, place of birth, location
: For these attributes, we use a language model to evaluate semantic similarity. A score of 1 is assigned for an exact match, 0.5 for an inexact but semantically similar match, and 0 for an incorrect match.

A.3
Hard evaluation set construction

We construct the
main

In addition, we construct the
hard
eval dataset, which contains more contextually embedded private information and is designed to further assess anonymization methods on more challenging cases.
We provide further details on the construction of the dataset and highlight the key differences from the main eval dataset.

Identification of hard examples.

We implement a filtering process to identify inherently difficult texts to anonymize from the initial SynthPAI pool.
Out of 7,823 texts, we select 450 (Figure
5
) based on the following process:

1.

Initial PII inference
: For each text, we first perform PII inference across all eight attribute types using an LLM adversary. The inferred values and their corresponding certainty scores are used as the reference ground truth.

2.

Targeted adversarial anonymization
: We apply adversarial anonymization, where, in each round, the anonymizer LLM is instructed to address only those PII inferences that match the reference ground truth and have a certainty score of 3 or higher.

3.

Anonymization success check
: After each anonymization attempt, the adversary performs PII inference again on the modified text. An anonymization step for an attribute is considered successful if the inferred value either differs from the ground truth or the certainty score decreases.

4.

Iterative refinement
: If any ground truth PII remains inferable with a certainty score greater than 2, we repeat steps 2 and 3 for up to six iterations. Texts in which at least one attribute remains inferable after the final iteration is considered hard examples.

Generation of hard examples.

Manual qualitative analysis of the resulting 450 texts revealed a common characteristic: PII inferences are often based on overall context, narrative, or subtle linguistic cues distributed throughout the text, rather than on specific, easily replaceable keywords or phrases.
This makes simple lexical substitution ineffective for anonymization.
For example, inferring the occupation attribute might depend on descriptions of daily routines and domain-specific language embedded in the narrative, rather than an explicit mention of a job title.

Building on these insights, we constructed a new corpus of texts with similar contextual PII disclosure patterns.
Using the profiles of the 25 authors from the main eval dataset, we prompted an LLM to produce 20 new text samples per profile (500 texts in total), explicitly guiding the model to emulate the characteristics observed in the initially identified hard examples.

Validation of generated samples.

To confirm the relative difficulty of the newly generated samples, we applied the same iterative anonymization process used during the filtering stage.
Only 59.6% (298 out of 500) of the texts could be successfully anonymized according to our criteria (see Figure
5
).
This contrasts sharply with the original SynthPAI data used to construct the main eval dataset, where a much higher proportion (approximately 85.7%, 2,693 out of 3,143) could be anonymized within the same number of iterations.
These results show that the examples are significantly more challenging for current techniques to anonymize.
We subsequently used this dataset alongside the main eval data to specifically assess the effective of the methods more under difficult anonymization scenarios.
