Understanding NTK Variance in Implicit Neural Representations
Chengguang Ou, Yixin Zhuang

Abstract
Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) often converge slowly and struggle to recover high-frequency details due to spectral bias. While prior work links this behavior to the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK), how specific architectural choices affect NTK conditioning remains unclear. We show that many INR mechanisms can be understood through their impact on a small set of pairwise similarity factors and scaling terms that jointly determine NTK eigenvalue variance. For standard coordinate MLPs, limited input–feature interactions induce large eigenvalue dispersion and poor conditioning. We derive closed-form variance decompositions for common INR components and show that positional encoding reshapes input similarity, spherical normalization reduces variance via layerwise scaling, and Hadamard modulation introduces additional similarity factors strictly below one, yielding multiplicative variance reduction. This unified view explains how diverse INR architectures mitigate spectral bias by improving NTK conditioning. Experiments across multiple tasks confirm the predicted variance reductions and demonstrate faster, more stable convergence with improved reconstruction quality.

While empirical studies show that INR architectures differ substantially in NTK conditioning, a clear architectural explanation remains missing. In particular, how design choices such as positional encoding, normalization, or modulation modify the NTK to improve convergence is not well understood. Existing approaches—including SIREN and Fourier feature networks—improve high-frequency learning but are typically motivated by bandwidth or initialization heuristics rather than a unified kernel-level view.

We provide such a view by showing that many INR operations act through a small set of pairwise similarity factors and scaling coefficients that jointly determine NTK eigenvalue variance. In standard coordinate MLPs, similarities between encoded inputs and correlations among hidden features induce large eigenvalue dispersion and poor conditioning.

Our analysis reveals that architectural components modify these variance-controlling factors in complementary ways. Positional encoding reshapes input similarity geometry, normalization introduces contractive layerwise scaling without altering similarity patterns, and coordinate-dependent Hadamard modulation adds new similarity terms strictly bounded in
(0,1)
, yielding multiplicative variance reduction and a flatter NTK spectrum. This perspective explains why diverse INR architectures mitigate spectral bias and suggests a simple design principle: reshaping similarity geometry or injecting bounded modulation systematically improves NTK conditioning.

We validate our theory on CT reconstruction

ref63

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We derive an NTK variance decomposition for INRs in terms of pairwise similarities and scaling factors.

•

We prove that normalization contracts NTK variance and that Hadamard modulation introduces bounded similarity terms yielding multiplicative variance reduction.

•

We empirically demonstrate more stable optimization, improved reconstruction fidelity, and reduced spectral bias across multiple INR tasks.

4
Architectural Effects on NTK Variance

We analyze how common INR components reshape the NTK, focusing on their effect on eigenvalue variance. We first unify positional encoding, spherical normalization, and Hadamard modulation in a multi-layer architecture. To obtain analytic insight, we then extract a two-layer normalized Hadamard model with a closed-form NTK. This enables explicit spectral statistics that isolate how input reshaping, normalization, and multiplicative gating improve conditioning and mitigate spectral bias.

4.1
Architecture Components

We extend the baseline two-layer model (
1
) to a practical
multi-layer INR by integrating three common components: positional encoding,
spherical normalization, and Hadamard modulation.
First, inputs are mapped through random Fourier feature positional encoding
ref4
:

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\gamma(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{d}}\big[\,\cos(2\pi\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})\ ;\ \sin(2\pi\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x})\,\big],

where
\mathbf{B}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(0,\varsigma^{2})^{(d/2)\times d_{0}}
.
Each hidden layer applies a linear map, ReLU activation, spherical normalization,
and Hadamard modulation:

\mathbf{y}_{\ell}=\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{y}_{\ell-1})}{\|\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\ell}\mathbf{y}_{\ell-1})\|_{2}}\odot\mathbf{p}_{\ell},\qquad\ell=1,\dots,L,

with
\mathbf{y}_{0}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
. A final linear readout produces

f(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{L}
.

This architecture enforces controlled similarity and stabilized energy across
layers. Since a closed-form NTK for the full depth-
L
model is intractable, we
extract an analytically tractable two-layer variant that isolates these effects.

Two-layer normalized Hadamard model.

We define

f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{c};\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}c_{r}\,\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}{\sqrt{S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}},\qquad S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2},

(6)

where
\mathbf{w}_{r}
denote the first-layer weights and
\mathbf{c}
are fixed modulation coefficients, with
c_{r}=a_{r}p_{r}
and
a_{r}\in{\pm a}
drawn at initialization.

Top-
K
spherical normalization (TopK-SP).

As a practical refinement, we consider an energy-aware variant that normalizes
only the top-
K
hidden coordinates. Let
\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}
mask the
K
largest
entries of
\mathbf{y}_{i}
in magnitude. We define

\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{tk}}=\frac{\mathbf{y}_{i}\odot\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}\odot\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}\|_{2}}.

(7)

TopK-SP preserves dominant similarity structure while further reducing the

serves as the theoretical model, with TopK-SP viewed as a practical enhancement.

4.2
NTK Training Dynamics

We analyze optimization in the two-layer normalized Hadamard model using the NTK framework from the Preliminaries. The encoded input
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}=\gamma(\mathbf{x})
and the modulation vector
\mathbf{c}
are fixed, and only the first-layer weights
\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{w}_{r})_{r=1}^{m}
are trained.

Differentiating (
6
) with respect to
\mathbf{w}_{r}

gives

\frac{\partial f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{c_{r}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r}\,\beta_{r}}{\sqrt{S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}},

(8)

where
\mathbb{I}_{r}=\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\geq 0\}
and

\beta_{r}=1-\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2}/S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})

captures the normalization correction.

Substituting into the NTK definition yields

H_{ij}=\frac{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}c_{r}^{2}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\beta_{r,j}}{\sqrt{S_{j}}},

(9)

which explicitly reveals the roles of ReLU gating, normalization, and Hadamard
modulation in shaping pairwise similarity.

A1

\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{2}\leq R_{x}

Theorem 4.1
(Exponential decay under gradient flow)

Under
A1
–
A3

\|\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0))-\mathbf{H}^{\infty}\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}

\|\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}\leq e^{-\lambda_{0}t}\,\|\mathbf{u}(0)-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad t\geq 0.

Proof

Finite-width concentration and Weyl’s inequality give

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0)))\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}

\mathbf{e}(t)=\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{y}

\dot{\mathbf{e}}(t)=-\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0))\mathbf{e}(t)

Theorem 4.2
(Linear-rate convergence of gradient descent)

Under
A1
–
A3

\|\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0))-\mathbf{H}^{\infty}\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}

where

Proof

Freezing the NTK gives the linear recursion

\mathbf{e}(k+1)=(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0)))\mathbf{e}(k)

giving
\epsilon(k)=O(m^{-1/2})

\mathbf{u}(0)\approx\mathbf{0}

\lambda_{\min}
accelerates the slowest mode, while
reducing eigenvalue spread mitigates spectral bias. We next analyze the
eigenspectrum of the normalized Hadamard NTK and derive a variance decomposition
that isolates how similarity and normalization control conditioning.

4.3
NTK Spectral Statistics

Having established that the two-layer normalized Hadamard model remains in the
standard NTK setting and inherits the same convergence guaranties as the
baseline ReLU-MLP, we now turn to the structure of its NTK at initialization.
Our goal is to characterize how the architectural components introduced earlier
(PE, spherical/TopK normalization, and Hadamard modulation) shape the
eigenvalue distribution of the NTK matrix and thereby influence the spectral bias. We now derive a compact variance decomposition tailored to the normalized Hadamard
architecture. Full derivations appear in the appendix.

NTK variance decomposition.

From the NTK expression (
9
), define

\mathbf{s}_{i}=\Big(\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\Big)_{r=1}^{m},\quad\mathbf{p}_{i}=(p_{r})_{r=1}^{m},\quad\mathbf{t}_{i}=\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{i},

so that
H_{ij}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\rho_{ij}\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle
, and

\tau_{x,ij}=\frac{\rho_{ij}^{2}}{\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}},\quad\tau_{s,ij}=\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\tau_{p,ij}=\frac{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}},\quad\tau_{q,ij}=(\cos\angle(\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j},\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}))^{2}.

These quantities describe (i) the input similarity
(\tau_{x,ij})
, (ii) the hidden
similarity shaped by normalization
(\tau_{s,ij})
, (iii) the modulation similarity

(\tau_{p,ij})
, and (iv) the alignment between the two similarity structures

(\tau_{q,ij})
.Although
\tau_{x,ij},\tau_{s,ij},\tau_{p,ij},\tau_{q,ij}
arise from different normalizations (squared-cosine terms or normalized energy-overlap terms), we uniformly refer to them as similarities.

Corollary 2
(Effect of SP / TopK normalization on the energy factor)

In the baseline ReLU-MLP without normalization, the variance formula (

Corollary 3
(Hadamard modulation reduces modulation similarities)

In the baseline model,
\tau_{p,ij}=\tau_{q,ij}\equiv 1
.
A nontrivial modulation pattern (e.g., sinusoidal or structured masks) typically

Although the normalized Hadamard architecture obeys the same NTK training dynamics and convergence guaranties as the baseline, the structure of its NTK spectrum is qualitatively different.
The four-factor decomposition (

PE reshapes input similarities, normalization contracts the energy-weighted hidden similarity, and Hadamard modulation introduces two additional similarity factors that are identically

6
Conclusion

In this work, we analyzed the variance of neural tangent kernel eigenvalues in implicit neural representations and studied how architectural components—including positional encoding, spherical normalization, and multiplicative modulation—shape spectral bias and optimization dynamics. Through this lens, we examined the normalized Hadamard architecture and showed that it effectively reduces NTK variance while preserving expressive capacity. Experiments on CT reconstruction and image super-resolution tasks confirm that the variance-reduction effects predicted by our theory translate into improved reconstruction quality. Overall, our study provides a principled framework for understanding and designing implicit neural networks with controlled NTK spectra, enabling more stable and accurate high-fidelity function representation.

A
Baseline ReLU-MLP: NTK Dynamics and Spectral Statistics

This appendix makes the spectral-statistics analysis of Section
3
fully explicit for the baseline two-layer ReLU-MLP. We first derive the standard NTK gradient-flow dynamics for quadratic loss in a model-agnostic way
ref16
;
ref32
;
ref35
, then specialize to the baseline architecture (
1
) to obtain the exact NTK matrix at initialization. Building on this kernel expression, we compute the mean and variance.

A.1
Baseline Model, Loss, and Standing Assumptions

Baseline predictor and loss.

We consider the two-layer over-parameterized ReLU network of Section
3
. For an input
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, the predictor is

f(\mathbf{W};\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a\,\phi_{r}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\phi_{r}(\mathbf{x})=\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}),

(A.1)

where
m
is the hidden width,
a>0
is a fixed readout scale, and

\mathbf{w}_{r}(0)\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\kappa^{2}\mathbf{I}_{d_{0}})
are initialized i.i.d. Gaussian.

Given training data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq R_{x}
and
|y_{i}|\leq C
, we define the predictions and labels

u_{i}\triangleq f(\mathbf{W};\mathbf{x}_{i}),\qquad\mathbf{u}\triangleq(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})^{\top},\qquad\mathbf{y}\triangleq(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top},

and train using the quadratic loss

\Phi(\mathbf{W})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2}.

(A.2)

Standing assumptions.

Throughout this appendix we work under the standard NTK conditions of Section
3
: bounded inputs and outputs, i.i.d. Gaussian initialization, and a non-degenerate infinite-width kernel
\mathbf{H}^{\infty}
that is positive definite on the training set. In the over-parameterized regime and for sufficiently small learning rate, the NTK at initialization governs early-time training.

A.2
NTK Gradient-Flow Dynamics

We first derive the NTK dynamics for quadratic loss in a model-agnostic way.

Gradient flow in parameter space.

The continuous-time gradient flow associated with
(
A.2
) is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{W}}\Phi(\mathbf{W}(t)).

(A.3)

Write
u_{i}(t)=f(\mathbf{W}(t);\mathbf{x}_{i})
and

\mathbf{u}(t)=(u_{1}(t),\ldots,u_{n}(t))^{\top}
. For each neuron
r
, define the per-sample gradient

\mathbf{g}_{r,i}(t)\triangleq\frac{\partial u_{i}(t)}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}.

By the chain rule and the definition of
\Phi
,

\frac{\partial\Phi(\mathbf{W})}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial u_{i}}\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_{i}-y_{i})\,\mathbf{g}_{r,i}.

(A.4)

Substituting (
A.4
) into (
A.3
) and
using
\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{r}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}
gives

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{r}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{j}(t)-y_{j})\,\mathbf{g}_{r,j}(t).

(A.5)

NTK as a Gram matrix in parameter space.

Differentiating
u_{i}(t)
with respect to time and applying the chain rule,

\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u_{i}(t)}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}(t)},\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{r}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big\rangle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\big\langle\mathbf{g}_{r,i}(t),\tfrac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}_{r}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\big\rangle.

(A.6)

Substituting (
A.5
) into (
A.6
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}u_{i}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(u_{j}(t)-y_{j})\big\langle\mathbf{g}_{r,i}(t),\mathbf{g}_{r,j}(t)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_{ij}(t)\,(u_{j}(t)-y_{j}),

(A.7)

where the time-dependent neural tangent kernel (NTK) is

H_{ij}(t)\triangleq\sum_{r=1}^{m}\big\langle\mathbf{g}_{r,i}(t),\mathbf{g}_{r,j}(t)\big\rangle.

(A.8)

In vector form,

\dot{\mathbf{u}}(t)=-\mathbf{H}(t)\big(\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{y}\big).

(A.9)

Under the NTK assumptions and for large hidden width
m
, the kernel remains close to its initialization along the training trajectory,

\mathbf{H}(t)\approx\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0))\triangleq\mathbf{H}
. Replacing

\mathbf{H}(t)
by
\mathbf{H}
in (
A.9
) gives the
linear ODE

\dot{\mathbf{u}}(t)=-\mathbf{H}\big(\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{y}\big),

(A.10)

which is the standard NTK regime used in the main text.

Let
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top}
be the eigendecomposition with

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and
\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}]
orthonormal. Then

\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{y}=\exp(-\mathbf{H}t)\big(\mathbf{u}(0)-\mathbf{y}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}t}\,\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{u}(0)-\mathbf{y})\big)\mathbf{v}_{i},

and the training error decomposes as

\|\mathbf{u}(t)-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda_{i}t}\big(\mathbf{v}_{i}^{\top}(\mathbf{u}(0)-\mathbf{y})\big)^{2}.

(A.11)

Modes corresponding to larger eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
decay faster. A highly dispersed spectrum (large gaps between the
\lambda_{i}
) implies that some modes converge much more slowly than others, which is the spectral-bias pattern described in Section
3
.

A.3
Baseline NTK: Exact Expression

We now specialize the general definition (
A.8
) to the
baseline network (
A.1
) and make explicit how input geometry and hidden gate overlap enter the NTK.

For
i,j\in[n]
we write

\rho_{ij}\triangleq\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{j}.

(A.12)

For each hidden neuron
r
and sample
i
, the ReLU gate is

\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\triangleq\mathbb{I}\{\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}\geq 0\}.

(A.13)

Collecting these gates across channels yields the baseline hidden feature vector

\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\triangleq(\mathbb{I}_{r,i})_{r=1}^{m}\in\{0,1\}^{m}.

(A.14)

Its squared
\ell_{2}
norm counts active channels:

\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}_{r,i}.

(A.15)

We also define the average hidden energy scale

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(A.16)

Under i.i.d. Gaussian initialization, each gate is active with probability
1/2
at a random input, so typically

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}\approx m/2
.

For sample
i
,

u_{i}=f(\mathbf{W};\mathbf{x}_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a\,\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i}).

Differentiating with respect to
\mathbf{w}_{r}
and applying the chain rule gives

\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\,a\,\sigma^{\prime}(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{x}_{i}.

(A.17)

For ReLU,
\sigma(z)=\max\{z,0\}
and

\sigma^{\prime}(z)=\mathbb{I}\{z\geq 0\}
, so by (
A.13
)

\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{a}{\sqrt{m}}\,\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\mathbf{x}_{i}.

(A.18)

By definition,

H_{ij}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}},\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}\Big\rangle.

Substituting (
A.18
) yields

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\Big\langle\frac{a}{\sqrt{m}}\,\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\mathbf{x}_{i},\,\frac{a}{\sqrt{m}}\,\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\,\mathbf{x}_{j}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\,\langle\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\mathbb{I}_{r,j}.

(A.19)

The sum
\sum_{r}\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\mathbb{I}_{r,j}
counts the number of hidden channels that are simultaneously active on samples
i
and
j
. Using the gate vector
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}
from (
A.14
), we may write

\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\mathbb{I}_{r,j}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}(\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\mathbb{I}_{r,j})^{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\odot
denotes the entrywise product. Thus

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\mathbb{I}_{r,j}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\sum_{r=1}^{m}s_{i,r}^{\mathrm{bl}}s_{j,r}^{\mathrm{bl}}

(A.20)

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\,\langle\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}},\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\rangle=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2},

This expression makes explicit how input geometry and gate overlap jointly determine the baseline NTK.

A.4
Spectral Statistics and Variance Proxy

We now derive the spectral statistics used in Section
3
.
Recall that
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the (finite-width) NTK Gram
matrix with entries
H_{ij}
given in (
A.20
). As a Gram
matrix,
\mathbf{H}
is real symmetric and positive semidefinite, and therefore
admits an eigendecomposition

\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\top},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}),

where
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
, counted with
multiplicity, and
\mathbf{V}=[\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{n}]
is an orthonormal
basis of eigenvectors. In particular,
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}
and

\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}
.
Following Eq. (
3
) in the main text, we define

\mu_{\lambda}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i},

(A.21)

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_{ij}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2},

(A.22)

v_{\lambda}\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}.

(A.23)

To separate geometry and hidden overlap, we introduce the input squared-cosine similarity

\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\triangleq\frac{\rho_{ij}^{2}}{\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}}\in[0,1],

(A.24)

and the baseline hidden feature similarity

\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\triangleq\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}}\in[0,1].

(A.25)

For the binary gate vectors
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
,

\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}
counts jointly active channels, while
\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}
counts active channels on sample
i
. Combining (
A.14
) and (
A.25
),

\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}=\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(A.26)

Substituting (
A.26
) into
(
A.20
) gives the factorized NTK

H_{ij}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\,\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2},

(A.27)

which separates input similarity, hidden similarity, and hidden energy.

Next, we first prove Proposition
1
in the main text.For the baseline NTK (
A.27
),

\mu_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{ii}=\frac{a^{2}}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(A.28)

Proof

Setting
i=j
in (
A.20
) yields

H_{ii}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, one has

\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\odot\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}=\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}
, so

H_{ii}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Averaging over
i
and using (
A.21
) gives
(
A.28
).

We now turn to
\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top})
.
Squaring (
A.27
) gives

H_{ij}^{2}=\Big(\frac{a^{2}}{m}\Big)^{2}\rho_{ij}^{2}\,\big(\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\big)^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{4}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{4}.

(A.29)

Using the input similarity (
A.24
),

\rho_{ij}^{2}=\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\,\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}.

(A.30)

Combining (
A.29
) and (
A.30
), summing over
(i,j)
and dividing by
n
yields the exact identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\,\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\big)\,\big(\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\big)^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{4}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{4}.

(A.31)

We now impose a mild regularity assumption on the per-sample energy terms in order to factor out their contribution and isolate the dependence on
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}
and
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
. Specifically, we assume that the input energies and gate energies are approximately constant across samples:

\rho_{ii}\approx R_{x}^{2},\qquad\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}\approx\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}\quad\text{for all }i\in[n].

(A.32)

Under (
A.32
),

\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\approx R_{x}^{4},\qquad\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{4}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{4}\approx\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,4},

and the exact second moment (
A.31
) simplifies to

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top})\approx\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,4}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\,\big(\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\big)^{2}.

(A.33)

Similarly, combining (
A.28
) with (
A.32
) yields

\mu_{\lambda}\approx\frac{a^{2}R_{x}^{2}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}}{m},\qquad\mu_{\lambda}^{2}\approx\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}}{m^{2}}.

(A.34)

Substituting (
A.33
) and (
A.34
) into the variance definition (
A.23
) then gives

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{H}^{\top})-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,4}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\,\big(\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\big)^{2}\;-\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}}{m^{2}}.

(A.35)

The first term in (
A.35
) aggregates both diagonal
(i=j)
and off-diagonal
(i\neq j)
contributions. Using
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ii}=\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ii}=1
and the approximation (
A.32
), the purely diagonal part is of order

\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,4}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}1\;\asymp\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,4}}{m^{2}},

which is the same order as
\mu_{\lambda}^{2}
in (
A.34
). Hence the difference between the diagonal contribution and
\mu_{\lambda}^{2}
can be absorbed into an
O(1)
multiplicative constant in front of the variance.

For the remaining off-diagonal term, we further treat the squared hidden similarity as

\big(\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\big)^{2}=\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\cdot\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\approx\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\,\overline{\tau}_{s},

where
\overline{\tau}_{s}\in[0,1]
denotes a dataset-dependent typical value of
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
, which we again fold into the overall scalar prefactor. This yields the compact variance proxy highlighted in Section
3
:

v_{\lambda}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\,\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij},

(A.36)

up to dataset-dependent constants of order one. This expression matches the scaling form stated in Proposition
1
and is used as the baseline reference in the main text.

The expression (
A.36
) should be read as a caling form for the eigenvalue variance of the baseline NTK. Its role is to expose the dependence of
v_{\lambda}
on the input similarities
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}
and hidden similarities
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
, rather than to keep track of all dataset-dependent constants. Two approximations are implicit. First, regarding diagonal versus off-diagonal contributions: in the over-parameterized regime (
m\gg n
), the baseline NTK entries satisfy
H_{ii}
and
H_{ij}
(for
i\neq j
) having different typical magnitudes once we plug in
\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{bl}}\|_{2}^{2}\propto m
and use that only a fraction of channels are jointly active for
i\neq j
. When forming

\tfrac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})
, both the diagonal terms
H_{ii}^{2}
and the off-diagonal terms
H_{ij}^{2}
contribute at the same
m
–dependent scale after we factor out the common energy prefactor in (
A.31
). However, there are only
n
diagonal terms versus
n(n-1)
off-diagonal ones. It is therefore natural to treat the diagonal part together with
\mu_{\lambda}^{2}
as a dataset-dependent constant and to single out the off-diagonal mass
\sum_{i\neq j}\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\,\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
as the dominant structured contribution that controls how dispersed the eigenvalues are. This is precisely the quantity that appears in the compressed form (
A.36
). Second, in going from (
A.31
) to(
A.36
) we replace
\big(\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}\big)^{2}
by
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
times a typical factor
\overline{\tau}_{s}
, which is then absorbed into the overall constant. This mean-field linearization does not change the qualitative dependence of
v_{\lambda}
on
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
: pairs with larger hidden overlap still increase the variance more than pairs with smaller overlap.

The expression (
A.36
) shows that, once the overall scale

\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}

is fixed by initialization, the dominant contribution to the eigenvalue variance comes from the off-diagonal similarity mass

\sum_{i\neq j}\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}\,\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}.

Large input similarities
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{x,ij}
, large hidden similarities
\tau^{\mathrm{bl}}_{s,ij}
, or a large hidden energy scale
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}
all increase
v_{\lambda}
, leading to a more dispersed NTK spectrum and hence more imbalanced decay rates
\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{i}t}
in (
A.11
). This baseline analysis underpins the design objective in Section
4
, where positional encoding, normalization, and Hadamard modulation are introduced precisely to reduce
v_{\lambda}
while keeping
\mu_{\lambda}
sufficiently large.

B
Positional Encoding Shrinks Off-Diagonal Input Similarities

In this appendix we provide a detailed proof of Lemma
1
.Using the same notation for input squared-cosine similarities as in Section
3
, we analyze random Fourier feature (RFF) positional encoding on a uniform grid in
[0,1]^{2}
and show that, for sufficiently large feature dimension and bandwidth, the average off-diagonal squared-cosine similarity of the encoded inputs is strictly smaller than its raw-coordinate counterpart. We also show that this average is strictly decreasing in the bandwidth
\varsigma
.

B.1
Random Fourier Features and Second Moment

We adopt the RFF positional encoding

\begin{gathered}\gamma(\mathbf{x})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{d}}\;\Big[\cos(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}),\ \sin(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}),\ \ldots,\\
\cos(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{d/2}^{\top}\mathbf{x}),\ \sin(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{d/2}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\Big]^{\top},\end{gathered}

(B.1)

where the frequencies

\mathbf{b}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\varsigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{2})
are i.i.d.,

\varsigma>0
is the bandwidth, and
d
is the encoded feature dimension.
For a pair of inputs
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\in[0,1]^{2}
, we write

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\triangleq\gamma(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\qquad\Delta_{ij}\triangleq\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}.

To match the main text, we recall the squared-cosine similarities. For raw coordinates,

\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}\triangleq\frac{\big(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{j}\big)^{2}}{\|\mathbf{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}},

(B.2)

while for encoded inputs
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\gamma(\mathbf{x}_{i})
we
define

\tau_{x,ij}\triangleq\frac{\big(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\big)^{2}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(B.3)

These are exactly the quantities appearing in Lemma
1
.

For a single frequency
\mathbf{b}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\varsigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{2})

and a fixed displacement
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, set

Z\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta.

Since
\mathbf{b}
is Gaussian and the map
\mathbf{b}\mapsto\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta
is linear,
Z
is one-dimensional Gaussian with

Z\sim\mathcal{N}\big(0,\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big).

We first compute the first trigonometric moment

\kappa(\Delta)\;\triangleq\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{b}}\big[\cos(2\pi\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\big[\cos(2\pi Z)\big].

For a zero-mean Gaussian
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,s^{2})
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the characteristic function gives

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\mathrm{i}tZ}\big]=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}s^{2}\Big).

Taking real parts and using
\cos(tZ)=\Re(e^{\mathrm{i}tZ})
yields

\mathbb{E}[\cos(tZ)]=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2}t^{2}s^{2}\Big).

Here
t=2\pi
and
s^{2}=\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}
, hence

\kappa(\Delta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{b}}\big[\cos(2\pi\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta)\big]=\exp\!\big(-2\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big).

(B.4)

For the second trigonometric moment, we use the identity

\cos^{2}x=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos 2x)
and obtain

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{b}}\big[\cos^{2}(2\pi\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta)\big]=\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(1+\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\cos(4\pi Z)]\Big).

Applying the same Gaussian formula with
t=4\pi
and

s^{2}=\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}
gives

\mathbb{E}_{Z}[\cos(4\pi Z)]=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{1}{2}(4\pi)^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\Big)=\exp\!\big(-8\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big),

so that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{b}}\big[\cos^{2}(2\pi\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta)\big]=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\exp\!\big(-8\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta\|_{2}^{2}\big)\Big).

(B.5)

The next proposition computes the second moment of the encoded inner product.

Proposition 3
(Second moment of the RFF inner product)

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}=\gamma(\mathbf{x}_{i})
be as in
(
B.1
). Then, for any
i,j
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\Big[(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}\Big]=\frac{2}{d}\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{b}}\big[\cos^{2}(2\pi\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta_{ij})\big]+\Big(1-\frac{2}{d}\Big)\,\kappa(\Delta_{ij})^{2},

(B.6)

where
\kappa(\cdot)
is given by (
B.4
). Using
(
B.5
), this simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\Big[(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\Big(1+e^{-8\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)

(B.7)

\displaystyle+\Big(1-\frac{2}{d}\Big)\,e^{-4\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Proof

From (
B.1
),

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}=\frac{2}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d/2}\cos\!\big(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\top}\Delta_{ij}\big).

Let
c_{k}=\cos(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\top}\Delta_{ij})
. Then

(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}=\Big(\frac{2}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d/2}c_{k}\Big)^{2}=\frac{4}{d^{2}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{d/2}c_{k}^{2}+2\!\!\sum_{1\leq k<\ell\leq d/2}\!c_{k}c_{\ell}\Big).

Taking expectation and using i.i.d. of
\{c_{k}\}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}\big]=\frac{4}{d^{2}}\Big(\frac{d}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[c_{1}^{2}]+\frac{d}{2}\Big(\frac{d}{2}-1\Big)\,\big(\mathbb{E}[c_{1}]\big)^{2}\Big).

By definition,

\mathbb{E}[c_{1}]=\kappa(\Delta_{ij})=\exp(-2\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2})
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[c_{1}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\frac{1+\cos\!\big(4\pi\mathbf{b}^{\top}\Delta_{ij}\big)}{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(1+\exp(-8\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2})\Big),

which gives (
B.6
) and (
B.7
) after simplification.

B.2
Relating Inner Products to Encoded Similarities

We now relate the second moment

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}[(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}]

to the encoded squared-cosine similarity

\tau_{x,ij}
.

A key property of the RFF encoding (
B.1
) is that the squared norm of each encoded vector is deterministic and equal to 1. Indeed, for any input
\mathbf{x}_{i}
,

\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{2}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d/2}\Big[\cos^{2}(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i})+\sin^{2}(2\pi\mathbf{b}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{i})\Big]=\frac{2}{d}\cdot\frac{d}{2}\cdot 1=1,

(B.8)

where we used the trigonometric identity
\cos^{2}\theta+\sin^{2}\theta=1
. Consequently, the squared-cosine similarity (
B.3
) simplifies exactly to the square of the encoded inner product:

\tau_{x,ij}=(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}.

(B.9)

Taking expectations, we obtain the exact relation

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\tau_{x,ij}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}\big].

B.3
Grid Average and Proof of Lemma
1

Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset[0,1]^{2}
be a uniform grid (for example, an
m\times m
grid with
n=m^{2}
points, enumerated by
i
). We define the off-diagonal averages

\overline{\tau}_{x}\triangleq\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij},

(B.10)

\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}}\triangleq\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}.

(B.11)

On any non-degenerate grid in
[0,1]^{2}
we have

\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}}>0
.

Averaging (
B.9
) over all
i\neq j
and using Proposition
3
yields the following.

Lemma 2
(Averaged off-diagonal expectation)

For all
d\geq 2
(even) and any uniform grid
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset[0,1]^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\tau_{x,ij}\big]

(B.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\left[\frac{1}{d}\Big(1+e^{-8\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)+\Big(1-\frac{2}{d}\Big)e^{-4\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}\right].

For each fixed
d
and grid, the right-hand side is a continuous and strictly decreasing function of the bandwidth
\varsigma>0
.

Proof

By definition of
\overline{\tau}_{x}
(
B.10
) and the exact relation (
B.9
),

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big]=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j})^{2}\big].

Substituting the closed-form expression (
B.7
) from Proposition
3
yields (
B.12
).
For a fixed grid,
\Delta_{ij}\neq 0
for all
i\neq j
. The map

\varsigma\ \mapsto\ \frac{1}{d}\Big(1+e^{-8\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big)+\Big(1-\frac{2}{d}\Big)e^{-4\pi^{2}\varsigma^{2}\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}

is continuous and strictly decreasing in
\varsigma>0
(since exponential terms decay with
\varsigma
). A finite average of such maps preserves continuity and strict monotonicity.

Since
\|\Delta_{ij}\|_{2}\geq\delta>0
for all
i\neq j
on a fixed grid, the exponential terms in (
B.12
) converge to zero as
\varsigma\to\infty
, and we obtain

\lim_{\varsigma\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big]=\frac{1}{d}.

(B.13)

Thus, for fixed
d
, the average encoded off-diagonal similarity can be made of order
O(1/d)
by choosing
\varsigma
sufficiently large.

We are now ready to prove Lemma
1
.

Proof
(Proof of Lemma
1
)

By definition,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\!\left[\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big].

The target inequality in Lemma
1
can therefore be written as

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big]\;\leq\;\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}},

(B.14)

where
\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}}
is given by
(
B.11
) and satisfies

\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}}>0
for any non-degenerate grid.

Fix

\epsilon\in\Big(0,\frac{1}{2}\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}}\Big).

By Lemma
2
and the limit
(
B.13
), for any fixed
d
there exists

\varsigma_{0}(d)>0
such that for all
\varsigma\geq\varsigma_{0}(d)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big]\leq\frac{1}{d}+\epsilon.

Next, choose
d_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
large enough so that for all
d\geq d_{0}

we have

\frac{1}{d}\leq\epsilon.

For any
d\geq d_{0}
, take
\varsigma_{0}\triangleq\varsigma_{0}(d)
as
above; then for all
\varsigma\geq\varsigma_{0}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}\big[\overline{\tau}_{x}\big]\leq\frac{1}{d}+\epsilon\leq 2\epsilon<\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}},

where the last strict inequality follows from the choice of

\epsilon<\overline{\tau}_{x}^{\mathrm{bl}}/2
.
This establishes (
B.14
) for all

d\geq d_{0}
and
\varsigma\geq\varsigma_{0}
, proving
Lemma
1
.
Finally, Lemma
2
shows that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{B}}[\overline{\tau}_{x}]
is strictly decreasing in

\varsigma
for fixed
d
, so the reduction effect strengthens as

\varsigma
grows.

B.4
Interpretation for Spectral Statistics

In the main text and Appendix A, the spectral bias of the baseline NTK is quantified by the variance of eigenvalues
v_{\lambda}
, which depends on the off-diagonal input similarities
\tau_{x,ij}
. Lemma
1
shows that, on a uniform grid, random Fourier feature encoding with sufficiently large feature dimension and bandwidth strictly reduces the average off-diagonal input similarity mass in expectation. Therefore, in any spectral-variance expression where the input factor appears multiplicatively with hidden similarity terms, replacing raw coordinates by
\gamma(\mathbf{x})
directly lowers the contribution of the input similarity factor and thus helps mitigate spectral bias, provided that the hidden similarities remain of comparable order.

C
Hadamard-ReLU: NTK Dynamics and Spectral Statistics

This appendix provides the detailed NTK analysis for the two-layer normalized Hadamard model introduced in Section
4
. We start from the precise model and training setup, derive the gradient and NTK
entries step by step, and then obtain the exact eigenvalue statistics and the four-factor variance formula used to quantify spectral bias in the main text.

C.1
Setup, Model, and NTK Representation

We first fix notation for the training data, define the two-layer normalized
Hadamard model, and compute its NTK matrix at initialization.

Let
\{(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the original training pairs and

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\;=\;\gamma(\mathbf{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

be the encoded inputs obtained by the positional encoding

\gamma(\cdot)
.
We denote the encoded inner products by

\rho_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j},\qquad i,j\in[n].

(C.1)

Given first-layer weights
\mathbf{W}=(\mathbf{w}_{r})_{r=1}^{m}
and fixed
second-layer coefficients
\mathbf{c}=(c_{r})_{r=1}^{m}
, the prediction on

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
is written as

u_{i}\;\triangleq\;f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{c};\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}),\qquad\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We train
\mathbf{W}
by minimizing the squared loss

\Phi(\mathbf{W})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{u}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\top}.

(C.2)

Throughout, the encoded inputs
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})
and the modulation
coefficients
\mathbf{c}
are treated as fixed, while only
\mathbf{W}
is
updated.

Two-layer normalized Hadamard model.

The two-layer normalized Hadamard model extends the baseline two-layer ReLU network by (i) replacing the raw input by the encoded input
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
, and (ii) applying a global spherical normalization to the hidden activations before the Hadamard-modulated readout. For a generic encoded input
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the predictor is

\begin{gathered}f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{c};\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}c_{r}\,\frac{\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}{\sqrt{S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}},\\
S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{2},\end{gathered}

(C.3)

where:

•

\sigma(\cdot)
is the ReLU activation,

•

S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
is the pre-normalization hidden energy,

•

\mathbf{c}=(c_{r})_{r=1}^{m}
encodes the Hadamard modulation, with

c_{r}=a_{r}p_{r}
where
a_{r}\in\{\pm a\}
is a fixed readout scale and

p_{r}
is the
r
-th modulation coefficient of
\mathbf{p}
.

For the
i
-th training example, we abbreviate

S_{i}\;\triangleq\;S(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})^{2}.

(C.4)

The definition (
C.3
) is exactly the two-layer normalized
Hadamard model in (
6
) of the main text.

To make the gradient computation explicit, we introduce the pre-activation

g_{r,i}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\qquad r\in[m],\ i\in[n],

and the corresponding ReLU gate

\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\;\triangleq\;\mathbb{I}\{g_{r,i}\geq 0\},

so that
\sigma(g_{r,i})=\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,g_{r,i}
and

\partial\sigma(g_{r,i})/\partial g_{r,i}=\mathbb{I}_{r,i}
.
In addition, we define the normalization-induced correction factor

\beta_{r,i}\;\triangleq\;1-\frac{\sigma(g_{r,i})^{2}}{S_{i}},

(C.5)

which will appear naturally when differentiating through the spherical
normalization.

Gradient flow in parameter space.

Fix a neuron index
r\in[m]
and a sample index
i\in[n]
.
By definition (
C.3
) and (
C.4
), the prediction
on
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
is

u_{i}=f(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{c};\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}c_{\ell}\,\frac{\sigma(g_{\ell,i})}{\sqrt{S_{i}}},

(C.6)

where
S_{i}
depends on all pre-activations
(g_{q,i})_{q=1}^{m}
.
Only the term with
\ell=r
depends on
\mathbf{w}_{r}
, hence

\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{c_{r}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}\bigg(\frac{\sigma(g_{r,i})}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\bigg).

(C.7)

To differentiate the scalar quantity

\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{w}_{r})\;\triangleq\;\frac{\sigma(g_{r,i})}{\sqrt{S_{i}}},

we view
\varphi_{i}
as a function of the two scalar arguments

(g_{r,i},S_{i})
:

\varphi_{i}(g_{r,i},S_{i})=\sigma(g_{r,i})\,S_{i}^{-1/2}.

By the multivariate chain rule,

\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial g_{r,i}}\,\frac{\partial g_{r,i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}+\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial S_{i}}\,\frac{\partial S_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}.

(C.8)

We now compute the four factors in (
C.8
) one by one.

(1) Derivative with respect to
g_{r,i}
.

Treating
S_{i}
as a constant,

\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial g_{r,i}}=\sigma^{\prime}(g_{r,i})\,S_{i}^{-1/2}=\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,S_{i}^{-1/2}.

(2) Derivative of
g_{r,i}
with respect to
\mathbf{w}_{r}
.

By definition
g_{r,i}=\mathbf{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
, so

\frac{\partial g_{r,i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

(3) Derivative with respect to
S_{i}
.

For fixed
g_{r,i}
,

\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial S_{i}}=\sigma(g_{r,i})\,\frac{\partial}{\partial S_{i}}(S_{i}^{-1/2})=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma(g_{r,i})\,S_{i}^{-3/2}.

(4) Derivative of
S_{i}
with respect to
\mathbf{w}_{r}
.

Recall that

S_{i}=\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sigma(g_{q,i})^{2}.

Only the term with
q=r
depends on
\mathbf{w}_{r}
, so

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}\big(\sigma(g_{r,i})^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=2\,\sigma(g_{r,i})\,\sigma^{\prime}(g_{r,i})\,\frac{\partial g_{r,i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}

\displaystyle=2\,\sigma(g_{r,i})\,\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

(5) Combine all pieces.

Substituting the four derivatives into (
C.8
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,S_{i}^{-1/2}\big)\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}+\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(g_{r,i})\,S_{i}^{-3/2}\big)\,\big(2\,\sigma(g_{r,i})\,\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,S_{i}^{-1/2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\sigma(g_{r,i})^{2}\,S_{i}^{-3/2}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\Big(S_{i}^{-1/2}-\sigma(g_{r,i})^{2}S_{i}^{-3/2}\Big)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

Factor out
S_{i}^{-1/2}
and use the definition (
C.5
):

S_{i}^{-1/2}-\sigma(g_{r,i})^{2}S_{i}^{-3/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\Big(1-\frac{\sigma(g_{r,i})^{2}}{S_{i}}\Big)=\frac{\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}.

Therefore

\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

(C.9)

Substituting (
C.9
) into (
C.7
), we obtain
the per-neuron gradient

\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}=\frac{c_{r}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}.

(C.10)

This matches the gradient formula stated in (
8
).

NTK as a Gram matrix in parameter space.

By definition, the NTK matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
at
initialization is the Gram matrix of the gradients

\{\partial u_{i}/\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}\}
:

H_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\sum_{r=1}^{m}\Big\langle\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}},\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{w}_{r}}\Big\rangle.

(C.11)

Substituting (
C.10
),

\displaystyle H_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\Big\langle\frac{c_{r}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\,\frac{c_{r}}{\sqrt{m}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\,\beta_{r,j}}{\sqrt{S_{j}}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}c_{r}^{2}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\,\beta_{r,j}}{\sqrt{S_{j}}}\,\langle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{ij}}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}c_{r}^{2}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\,\beta_{r,j}}{\sqrt{S_{j}}}.

(C.12)

In the normalized Hadamard model, we write
c_{r}=a_{r}p_{r}
with

a_{r}\in\{\pm a\}
and modulation pattern
(P_{r})_{r=1}^{m}
.
Then
c_{r}^{2}=a^{2}p_{r}^{2}
and the
r
-th term in the sum can be grouped as a
product of a “hidden factor” and a “modulation factor”.
For later use, we introduce the hidden vectors, modulation vectors, and their
Hadamard product

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{s}_{i}\;\triangleq\;\Big(\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\Big)_{r=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\\
\mathbf{p}_{i}\;\triangleq\;(p_{r})_{r=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\\
\mathbf{t}_{i}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{i},\end{gathered}

(C.13)

so that

\sum_{r=1}^{m}c_{r}^{2}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,i}\,\beta_{r,i}}{\sqrt{S_{i}}}\,\frac{\mathbb{I}_{r,j}\,\beta_{r,j}}{\sqrt{S_{j}}}=a^{2}\sum_{r=1}^{m}s_{i,r}P_{r}\,s_{j,r}P_{r}=a^{2}\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle.

Substituting into (
C.12
) yields the compact NTK representation

H_{ij}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ij}\,\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle,

(13)

which is exactly the form used in Section
4.3
.
In the remainder of this appendix, we analyze the eigenvalue statistics of

\mathbf{H}
in (
13
) and show how its variance

v_{\lambda}
decomposes into four interpretable similarity factors that
reflect the roles of input reshaping, normalization, and Hadamard modulation.

C.2
Exact Eigenvalue Statistics in Similarity Form

We now express the eigenvalue statistics of
\mathbf{H}
in terms of
normalized similarity quantities.
Let
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}
.
Following (
3
), we define

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}\;\triangleq\;\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2},

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}\;\triangleq\;\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}.

The variance
v_{\lambda}
quantifies the spread of the NTK spectrum and
thus the degree of spectral bias, since the training error decomposes
into modes that decay at rates governed by
\{\lambda_{i}\}
.

Normalized similarity factors.

We introduce four families of normalized similarities.
For inputs, define

\tau_{x,ij}\;\triangleq\;\frac{\rho_{ij}^{2}}{\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}}\in[0,1],

(C.14)

which measures the squared cosine between
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
and

\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{j}
.

For the normalized hidden vectors
\mathbf{s}_{i}
and modulation vectors

\mathbf{p}_{i}
, define

\displaystyle\tau_{s,ij}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\in[0,1],

(15)

\displaystyle\tau_{p,ij}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\in[0,1].

(16)

Finally, to capture the alignment between the hidden and modulation similarities, define

\displaystyle\kappa_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\cos\angle\!\big(\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j},\ \mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}\big)\in[-1,1],

(C.15)

\displaystyle\tau_{q,ij}\;\triangleq\;\kappa_{ij}^{2}\in[0,1].

Under Assumptions
A1
–
A3
, these quantities are almost surely well defined at initialization.

Mean eigenvalue.

By definition of the trace,

\mu_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}H_{ii}.

(C.16)

Using (
13
) with
i=j
,

H_{ii}=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ii}\,\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{i}\rangle=\frac{a^{2}}{m}\,\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{t}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus

\mu_{\lambda}=\frac{a^{2}}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{t}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(C.17)

To connect
\|\mathbf{t}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}
with the similarity factors, introduce

\mathbf{z}_{i}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{i},\qquad\mathbf{v}_{i}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{i}.

Then

\langle\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}s_{i,r}^{2}p_{i,r}^{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbf{t}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}.

(C.18)

By the cosine representation,

\langle\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle=\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}\,\kappa_{ii},

(C.19)

where
\kappa_{ii}=\cos\angle(\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i})
.

On the other hand, from (
15
) and (
16
),

\tau_{s,ii}=\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{4}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{4}},\qquad\tau_{p,ii}=\frac{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{4}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{4}}.

Taking square roots yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}_{i}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{s,ii}},

(C.20)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{i}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{p,ii}}.

Combining (
C.18
), (
C.19
), and
(
C.20
),

\|\mathbf{t}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=\langle\mathbf{z}_{i},\mathbf{v}_{i}\rangle=\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{s,ii}\tau_{p,ii}}\,\kappa_{ii}.

Substituting into (
C.17
) gives

\mu_{\lambda}=\frac{a^{2}}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{s,ii}\tau_{p,ii}}\,\kappa_{ii},

(C.21)

which is precisely the mean-eigenvalue expression
(
10
) stated in the main text.

Second spectral moment.

The second spectral moment is

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}H_{ij}^{2}.

(C.22)

Using (
13
),

H_{ij}^{2}=\Big(\frac{a^{2}}{m}\Big)^{2}\rho_{ij}^{2}\,\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle^{2}.

(C.23)

First, the input term
\rho_{ij}^{2}
can be written using

\tau_{x,ij}
:

\rho_{ij}^{2}=\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\,\tau_{x,ij},

(C.24)

which follows directly from (
C.14
).

Next, we factorize
\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle^{2}
.
Define

\mathbf{z}_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j},\qquad\mathbf{v}_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}.

Then

\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle=\sum_{r=1}^{m}s_{i,r}p_{r}\,s_{j,r}p_{r}=\langle\mathbf{z}_{ij},\mathbf{v}_{ij}\rangle.

By the cosine representation in (
C.15
),

\langle\mathbf{z}_{ij},\mathbf{v}_{ij}\rangle=\|\mathbf{z}_{ij}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{ij}\|_{2}\,\kappa_{ij},\qquad\tau_{q,ij}=\kappa_{ij}^{2}.

(C.25)

From (
15
) and (
16
),

\tau_{s,ij}=\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{z}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}},

\tau_{p,ij}=\frac{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}=\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}.

Taking square roots yields

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{z}_{ij}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{s,ij}},

(C.26)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{v}_{ij}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{p,ij}}.

Combining (
C.25
) and (
C.26
),

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{t}_{i},\mathbf{t}_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{z}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{v}_{ij}\|_{2}^{2}\,\kappa_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big(\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{s,ij}\big)\,\big(\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{p,ij}\big)\,\tau_{q,ij}.

Substituting this and (
C.24
) into (
C.23
) gives

\displaystyle H_{ij}^{2}=\frac{a^{4}}{m^{2}}\,\big(\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\tau_{x,ij}\big)\,\big(\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{s,ij}\big)\,

\displaystyle\big(\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{p,ij}\big)\,\tau_{q,ij}.

Summing over
i,j
and dividing by
n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\tau_{x,ij}\big)

(C.27)

\displaystyle\cdot\big(\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{s,ij}\big)\,\big(\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{p,ij}\big)\,\tau_{q,ij},

which is exactly the second-moment expression
(
11
) in the main text.

Combining the exact formulas (
C.21
),(
C.27
) with (
14
) yields the exact variance identity for the Hadamard NTK and thus completes the derivation of
2
and
Theorem 4.3
. Since
v_{\lambda}
measures the dispersion of the eigenvalue sequence
\{\lambda_{i}\}
, these formulas provide a direct link between the similarity structure induced by the architecture and the strength of spectral bias.

C.3
Variance Formula under Diagonal Regularity

We now derive the compact four-factor variance expression used in Section
4.3
by imposing mild regularity assumptions on
diagonal quantities. The goal is to expose more clearly how the off-diagonal similarity mass controls
v_{\lambda}
.

Diagonal regularity assumptions.

Assume that, across the training set,

(i)

Encoded input norms are approximately constant:

\rho_{ii}=\|\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\approx R_{x}^{2},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

(ii)

Hidden and modulation energies are approximately constant:

\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\approx\overline{S},\qquad\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\approx\overline{P},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

(iii)

Self-similarities and self-couplings satisfy

\sqrt{\tau_{s,ii}\tau_{p,ii}}\,\kappa_{ii}\approx 1,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

These conditions capture the empirical effect of spherical normalization and well-conditioned modulation: diagonal energy scales and self-alignment are approximately uniform.

Approximate mean.

Substituting (i)–(iii) into (
C.21
),

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{ii}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\sqrt{\tau_{s,ii}\tau_{p,ii}}\,\kappa_{ii}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{a^{2}}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{x}^{2}\,\overline{S}\,\overline{P}\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{2}R_{x}^{2}\,\overline{S}\,\overline{P}}{m},

so that

\mu_{\lambda}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{2}R_{x}^{2}\,\overline{S}\,\overline{P}}{m}.

(C.28)

Approximate second moment.

Applying the same approximations to (
C.27
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\big(\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\tau_{x,ij}\big)\,\big(\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{s,ij}\big)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\big(\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\tau_{p,ij}\big)\,\tau_{q,ij}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\big(R_{x}^{2}R_{x}^{2}\tau_{x,ij}\big)\,\big(\overline{S}\,\overline{S}\,\tau_{s,ij}\big)\,\big(\overline{P}\,\overline{P}\,\tau_{p,ij}\big)\,\tau_{q,ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij}.

Thus

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij}.

(C.29)

Approximate variance and off-diagonal mass.

From (
C.28
),

\mu_{\lambda}^{2}\approx\left(\frac{a^{2}R_{x}^{2}\,\overline{S}\,\overline{P}}{m}\right)^{2}=\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{m^{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle v_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}-\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{m^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\bigg(\sum_{i,j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}-n\bigg),

(C.30)

where we used

\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{m^{2}}=\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\cdot n.

For each
i
, by definitions (
C.14
)–(
C.15
),

\tau_{x,ii}=\tau_{s,ii}=\tau_{p,ii}=\tau_{q,ii}=1,

so the diagonal contributions add up to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{x,ii}\tau_{s,ii}\tau_{p,ii}\tau_{q,ii}=n.

Therefore

\sum_{i,j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}-n=\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij},

and (
C.30
) becomes

v_{\lambda}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}.

(C.31)

This is exactly the four-factor variance formula (
12
) used in the main text. It shows that, up to a global prefactor depending on the diagonal energies, the eigenvalue variance
v_{\lambda}
is governed by the off-diagonal similarity mass

\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij},

which aggregates the effects of input geometry, normalized hidden similarity, modulation similarity, and their mutual alignment.

C.4
Monotonicity in Similarity Factors and Architectural Corollaries

We conclude by formalizing how the eigenvalue variance
v_{\lambda}
depends on
the similarity factors
(\tau_{x,ij},\tau_{s,ij},\tau_{p,ij},\tau_{q,ij})
and
by deriving the architectural corollaries invoked in Section
4.3
. Throughout this subsection we work with the exact NTK representation (
13
) and the spectral statistics (
C.21
)–(
C.27
), together with the four-factor approximation (
C.31
).

Proposition 4
(Monotonicity of
v_{\lambda}
in similarity factors)

Fix the diagonal quantities
\{\rho_{ii}\}
,
\{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}\}
, and
\{\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}\}
. For off-diagonal pairs
i\neq j
, treat
\tau_{x,ij}
,
\tau_{s,ij}
,
\tau_{p,ij}
, and
\tau_{q,ij}
as variables in
[0,1]
. If all but one of these families are held fixed, then the eigenvalue variance
v_{\lambda}
is (weakly) increasing in the remaining family.

Proof

We start from the exact second-moment expression (
C.27
).
Define the nonnegative coefficients

\alpha_{ij}\;\triangleq\;\rho_{ii}\rho_{jj}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mathbf{p}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\;\geq\;0.

(C.32)

Then (
C.27
) can be written compactly as

\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})=\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}\,\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}.

Fix an off-diagonal pair
(i,j)
and regard, for instance,
\tau_{x,ij}
as the
only variable, with all other factors held fixed. Denote

G\;\triangleq\;\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2}).

The dependence of
G
on
\tau_{x,ij}
is linear and takes the form

\displaystyle G(\tau_{x,ij})=\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\,\alpha_{ij}\,\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}

\displaystyle+\text{(terms independent of $\tau_{x,ij}$)}.

Differentiating with respect to
\tau_{x,ij}
yields

\frac{\partial G}{\partial\tau_{x,ij}}=\frac{a^{4}}{nm^{2}}\,\alpha_{ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}\;\geq\;0,

(C.33)

since
\alpha_{ij}\geq 0
and

\tau_{s,ij},\tau_{p,ij},\tau_{q,ij}\in[0,1]
. The same calculation applies
when varying
\tau_{s,ij}
,
\tau_{p,ij}
, or
\tau_{q,ij}
while holding the remaining factors fixed, because
G
is always linear and the corresponding
partial derivatives are nonnegative.

Next, the mean eigenvalue
\mu_{\lambda}
in (
C.21
) depends only
on the diagonal entries
H_{ii}
and therefore does not depend on any off-diagonal similarities:

\frac{\partial\mu_{\lambda}}{\partial\tau_{\bullet,ij}}=0,\qquad i\neq j,

(C.34)

for any
\tau_{\bullet,ij}\in\{\tau_{x,ij},\tau_{s,ij},\tau_{p,ij},\tau_{q,ij}\}
.
Since
v_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{H}^{2})-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}=G-\mu_{\lambda}^{2}
, combining (
C.33
) and
(
C.34
) gives

\frac{\partial v_{\lambda}}{\partial\tau_{\bullet,ij}}=\frac{\partial G}{\partial\tau_{\bullet,ij}}\;\geq\;0,

which proves the claimed monotonicity.

This monotonicity has direct implications for architectural design, because changes in positional encoding, modulation, or normalization primarily act by shrinking one or more of the similarity factors
\tau_{x,ij}
,
\tau_{s,ij}
,
\tau_{p,ij}
, or
\tau_{q,ij}
on off-diagonal pairs.

Positional encoding.

Corollary 4
(Positional encoding reduces NTK variance)

Consider two models that differ only in their input representation: a raw-coordinate model and a model with positional encoding.
Let
v_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{bl}}
and
v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{PE})}
be the
corresponding eigenvalue variances, and let

\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}
and

\tau_{x,ij}^{(\mathrm{PE})}
denote the input similarities appearing in
(
C.31
).
Assume that

\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}^{(\mathrm{PE})}<\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}},

while the hidden and modulation statistics are comparable in the sense that
for
i\neq j

\tau_{s,ij}^{(\mathrm{PE})}\approx\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}},\quad\tau_{p,ij}^{(\mathrm{PE})}\approx\tau_{p,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}},\quad\tau_{q,ij}^{(\mathrm{PE})}\approx\tau_{q,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}},

and the prefactor in (
C.31
) is the same up to constants.
Then

v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{PE})}<v_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{bl}}.

Proof

From the four-factor formula (
C.31
),

v_{\lambda}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij}.

By assumption, the scalar prefactor and the product
\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}
are (up to constants) the same for both
models on each off-diagonal pair. Thus the only systematic change in

v_{\lambda}
comes from replacing
\{\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}\}_{i\neq j}
by

\{\tau_{x,ij}^{(\mathrm{PE})}\}_{i\neq j}
in a sum of nonnegative terms.The strict decrease in the off-diagonal mass of
\tau_{x,ij}
therefore implies
v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{PE})}<v_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{bl}}
.

Interpretation.

Positional encoding contracts off-diagonal input similarities

\tau_{x,ij}
; via (
C.31
) this directly shrinks

v_{\lambda}
and hence reduces spectral dispersion and spectral bias.

Spherical and TopK-SP normalization.

Corollary 5
(Spherical and TopK-SP normalization shrink the energy factor)

Let
v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})}
be the variance of the unnormalized baseline model, and
v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{SP})}
,
v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{TopK})}
the variances under spherical normalization and TopK-SP, respectively. Assume that the baseline scaling is given by

v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}^{(\mathrm{bl})};

and spherical normalization enforces
\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\equiv 1
,so that the corresponding energy factor satisfies
\overline{S}\approx 1\ll\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}
when many neurons are active;,and in the TopK-SP variant, Appendix D shows that the effective energy-weighted hidden similarity
\tau_{s,ij}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}
is further reduced in expectation for
i\neq j
.
Then, whenever
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}\gg 1
at initialization,

\mathbb{E}\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{TopK})}\big]<\mathbb{E}\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{SP})}\big]\ll\mathbb{E}\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})}\big].

Proof

In the baseline,
v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})}
scales with
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}
as in Proposition
1
.Under spherical normalization, (
C.31
) shows that the variance scales with an energy factor of order
\overline{S}^{\,2}\approx 1
,while the off-diagonal similarity sum remains of comparable order.
Thus, for
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}\gg 1
,

\mathbb{E}\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{SP})}\big]\ll\mathbb{E}\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})}\big].

In the TopK-SP case, the same formula applies with the TopK-induced energy and similarities. The assumption that
\tau_{s,ij}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}
is strictly smaller in expectation for each
i\neq j
implies that every off-diagonal summand in the variance sum is reduced in expectation, while all other factors are nonnegative.
Summing over
i\neq j
yields
\mathbb{E}[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{TopK})}]<\mathbb{E}[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{SP})}]
. Combining the two inequalities gives the claim.

Interpretation.

The unnormalized baseline carries a large factor
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{\,2}
in
v_{\lambda}
.Spherical normalization removes this amplification by enforcing unit-norm hidden vectors, and TopK-SP further shrinks the energy-weighted hidden similarity. This progressively tightens the NTK spectrum and attenuates spectral bias.

Hadamard modulation.

Corollary 6
(Hadamard modulation reduces eigenvalue variance)

Consider two models with the same encoded inputs and normalized hidden
vectors, but different modulation patterns.In the baseline model, the modulation is trivial so that
\tau_{p,ij}^{(\mathrm{bl})}=\tau_{q,ij}^{(\mathrm{bl})}=1

for all
i,j
. In the Hadamard-enhanced model, the same
\tau_{x,ij}
and
\tau_{s,ij}
are used, but the modulation induces

\tau_{p,ij}
and

\tau_{q,ij}
for
i\neq j
.
Assume that there exists a constant
C_{P}\in(0,1)
such that

\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij}\leq C_{P}\qquad\text{for all }i\neq j,

and that the prefactor in (
C.31
) is comparable in both
models. Then

v_{\lambda}\;\leq\;C_{P}\,v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})},

with strict inequality if the above bound is strict on a nonempty set of off-diagonal pairs.

Proof

For the baseline model, (
C.31
) becomes

v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{bl})}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij},

since
\tau_{p,ij}=\tau_{q,ij}=1
.
For the Hadamard-enhanced model,

v_{\lambda}\;\approx\;\frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij},

where we used
\tau_{x,ij}
and
\tau_{s,ij}
being shared. Each off-diagonal summand in the Hadamard case is bounded by
C_{P}
times the corresponding baseline summand, and all factors are nonnegative, so

\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}\leq C_{P}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\tau_{s,ij}.

Multiplying by the common prefactor yields the claim.

Interpretation.

The Hadamard branch injects an additional similarity structure over hidden channels. When its off-diagonal alignment is weaker than that of the trivial modulation, the factors
\tau_{p,ij}
and
\tau_{q,ij}
shrink each off-diagonal contribution in (
C.31
), thereby clustering the NTK eigenvalues and weakening spectral bias.Corollary
6
is stated at the level of the compact four-factor proxy(
C.31
), whose baseline reference term is linear in the hidden similarity
\tau_{s,ij}
.By contrast, in the unnormalized baseline derivation of Appendix A the off-diagonal contribution to the second spectral moment naturally carries the squared overlap factor
(\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}})^{2}
(cf. (
A.29
)–(
A.31
)).The commonly used replacement
(\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}})^{2}\approx\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}\cdot\overline{\tau}_{s}
is therefore best viewed as a mean-field simplification: it sharpens the interpretation of which similarities drive the variance, but it is not an identity and should not be taken as a pointwise comparison against the squared-baseline term.

To articulate the stricter comparison, we keep track of the corresponding off-diagonal similarity masses. For the Hadamard model, (
C.31
) identifies

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ha}}\;\triangleq\;\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij},

while Appendix A suggests the unlinearized baseline reference

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{(2)}\;\triangleq\;\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}\,(\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}})^{2}.

Under the shared-input setting (so that
\tau_{x,ij}\approx\tau_{x,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}
) and comparable diagonal prefactors in the two variance proxies, the implication is direct: any uniform domination of each Hadamard off-diagonal summand by its squared-baseline counterpart yields a reduction of the dominant off-diagonal variance contribution, and hence a tighter spectrum in the sense of the proxy
v_{\lambda}
.

A sufficient condition that makes this domination explicit is the following pairwise bound:
there exists
C_{\Pi}\in(0,1)
such that for all
i\neq j
,

\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij}\;\leq\;C_{\Pi}\,(\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}})^{2}.

(C.32)

Multiplying (
C.32
) by
\tau_{x,ij}\geq 0
and summing over
i\neq j
gives
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ha}}\leq C_{\Pi}\,\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{bl}}^{(2)}
, with strict inequality whenever (
C.32
) is strict on a nonempty set of off-diagonal pairs.
With comparable prefactors, this yields a strict reduction of the off-diagonal variance mass,and therefore a strict improvement of the spectral-dispersion proxy
v_{\lambda}
.

It remains to justify why (
C.32
) is reasonable in the regimes considered in this work. First, at random ReLU initialization the baseline gate-overlap similarity typically satisfies
\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}=\Theta(m^{-1})
for
i\neq j
, hence
(\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}})^{2}=\Theta(m^{-2})
. Consequently, achieving (
C.32
) reduces to ensuring that the Hadamard product
\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}
is also of order
m^{-2}
(or smaller) on off-diagonal pairs. Second, the normalization mechanisms studied in the paper (spherical normalization and its TopK refinement) are introduced precisely to suppress off-diagonal overlap, and empirically yield
\tau_{s,ij}\lesssim\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}}
. Third, once the modulation pattern is phase-mixed (or otherwise channel-incoherent) relative to the hidden pair-structure, the alignment term
\kappa_{ij}^{2}=\tau_{q,ij}
becomes small for
i\neq j
: under mild mixing/independence across channels, standard concentration bounds imply
\tau_{q,ij}=O(m^{-1})
uniformly over off-diagonal pairs (up to logarithmic factors in
n
). When, in addition, the modulation overlap itself satisfies
\tau_{p,ij}=O(m^{-1})
for
i\neq j
(as is typical when
\mathbf{p}_{i}\odot\mathbf{p}_{j}
oscillates across channels), their product obeys
\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}=O(m^{-2})
, and thus
\tau_{s,ij}\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}\lesssim(\tau_{s,ij}^{\mathrm{bl}})^{2}
for sufficiently large width.

Taken together, these observations explain how the informal statement
\tau_{p,ij}\tau_{q,ij}
is small off-diagonal can be upgraded into a domination at the correct squared-baseline scale (
C.32
). In turn, this closes the logical loop behind Corollary
6
: beyond the interpretability of the linearized proxy, one can ensure a reduction of the dominant off-diagonal variance contribution at the unlinearized baseline level, and therefore justify that Hadamard modulation tightens the NTK spectrum and weakens spectral bias in the sense captured by
v_{\lambda}
.

D
Top-K Sparse Spherical Normalization and Energy-Weighted Hidden Similarity

This section makes precise how Top-
K
sparse spherical normalization (TopK-SP) modifies the hidden similarity factor that appears in our NTK variance proxy. We first formalize the notion of energy-weighted hidden similarity, then compare three normalization schemes: full spherical normalization (SP), a uniform-
k
sparsification baseline, and deterministic TopK-SP. The main message is that, under mild sub-Gaussian assumptions, TopK-SP preserves the
1/m
scaling of hidden similarity but induces a strict shrinkage in the energy-weighted overlap, which in turn suppresses NTK spectral variance.

D.1
Setup and Notation

For sample
i\in[n]
, let the pre-normalization hidden vector be

\mathbf{y}_{i}=(y_{i,1},\ldots,y_{i,m})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

For any (possibly normalized) features
\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we define the hidden similarity as

\tau_{s,ij}\ \triangleq\ \frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\odot\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}\frac{s_{i,r}^{2}\,s_{j,r}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{s}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,\|\mathbf{s}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}\in[0,1].

(D.1)

When
\mathbf{s}_{i}
and
\mathbf{s}_{j}
are individually
\ell_{2}
-normalized, the denominator equals
1
, and
\tau_{s,ij}
reduces to a simple overlap of squared coordinates.

We compare two concrete normalization schemes, both applied to the same pre-normalization vectors
\mathbf{y}_{i}
.

Spherical normalization (SP):

\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{sp}}=\frac{\mathbf{y}_{i}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}\|_{2}},\qquad\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}=\frac{\sum_{r=1}^{m}y_{i,r}^{2}y_{j,r}^{2}}{\big(\sum_{r=1}^{m}y_{i,r}^{2}\big)\big(\sum_{r=1}^{m}y_{j,r}^{2}\big)}.

(D.2)

Here
\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{sp}}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{sp}}\|_{2}=1
, so (
D.1
) indeed reduces to the ratio in (
D.2
).

Top-
K
sparse spherical normalization (TopK-SP):

Let
K_{i}=\operatorname{TopK}(\{|y_{i,r}|\}_{r=1}^{m})
with
|K_{i}|=k\ll m
and indicator vector
(\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}})_{r}=\mathbf{1}_{\{r\in K_{i}\}}
. Define

\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{tk}}=\frac{\mathbf{y}_{i}\odot\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}}{\|\mathbf{y}_{i}\odot\mathbf{1}_{K_{i}}\|_{2}},\qquad\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}(s^{\mathrm{tk}}_{i,r})^{2}(s^{\mathrm{tk}}_{j,r})^{2},

(D.3)

where we have used that
\|\mathbf{s}_{i}^{\mathrm{tk}}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{s}_{j}^{\mathrm{tk}}\|_{2}=1
so that the denominator in (
D.1
) is again equal to
1
.

We work under the following mild conditions, used only to justify law-of-large-numbers and concentration steps.

(A1)

(Channel i.i.d. and integrability)
For fixed
i
, the coordinates
\{y_{i,r}\}_{r=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. with

\mathbb{E}[y_{i,r}]=\mu_{i},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(y_{i,r})=\sigma_{i}^{2}\in(0,\infty),\qquad\mathbb{E}[y_{i,r}^{4}]<\infty,

and absolutely continuous. Moreover
y_{i,r}-\mu_{i}
is sub-Gaussian with parameter
K_{\mathrm{sg}}
.

(A2)

(Same-channel cross-sample correlation)
For
i\neq j
,

\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i,r},y_{j,r})=c_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\quad\text{(independent of $r$)},\qquad\mathrm{Cov}(y_{i,r},y_{j,s})=0\ \text{for }r\neq s,

where
c_{ij}\in[-1,1]
is a correlation coefficient.

(A3)

(Moderate SNR)
The signal-to-noise ratio is uniformly bounded:
\mu_{i}^{2}/\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq\kappa
for all
i
and
m
.

(A4)

(Large width)
The hidden width satisfies
m\to\infty
.

(A5)

(Weak rank correlation under small
c_{ij}
)
As
c_{ij}\to 0
, the (Spearman) rank correlation between the sequences
\{|y_{i,r}|\}_{r=1}^{m}
and
\{|y_{j,r}|\}_{r=1}^{m}
vanishes (e.g., joint Gaussian or Gaussian-copula constructions). Intuitively, when samples
i
and
j
are weakly correlated, their Top-
K
index sets behave as if they were almost independently drawn from the same marginal distribution.

To connect normalization to the NTK variance proxy, we consider an energy-weighted version of the hidden similarity. Let a mask
\mathsf{M}_{i,r}\equiv 1
for SP and
\mathsf{M}_{i,r}=\mathbf{1}_{\{r\in K_{i}\}}
for TopK-SP. Define

S_{i}\ \triangleq\ \frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}y_{i,r}^{2}\,\mathsf{M}_{i,r},\qquad\overline{S}\ \triangleq\ \mathbb{E}[S_{i}],\qquad\mathcal{M}_{ij}\ \triangleq\ \tau_{s,ij}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}.

(D.4)

The quantity
\mathcal{M}_{ij}
matches, up to architecture-specific constants, the hidden “energy
\times
similarity factor that appears in the NTK variance proxy in Appendix A and Appendix C.

D.2
SP Baseline: Mean Scale and Energy

We first characterize the baseline SP similarity and energy. Let

X_{r}=y_{i,r},\quad Y_{r}=y_{j,r},\quad Z_{r}=X_{r}^{2}Y_{r}^{2},

and define

S_{i}^{\Sigma}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}X_{r}^{2},\qquad S_{j}^{\Sigma}=\sum_{r=1}^{m}Y_{r}^{2},\qquad N=\sum_{r=1}^{m}Z_{r}.

(D.5)

Then (
D.2
) can be written as

\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}=\frac{N}{S_{i}^{\Sigma}S_{j}^{\Sigma}}.

Lemma 3

Under
(A1)–(A4)
,

\begin{gathered}\frac{S_{i}^{\Sigma}}{m}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\mu_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2},\\[2.0pt]
\frac{S_{j}^{\Sigma}}{m}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]=\mu_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{j}^{2},\\[2.0pt]
\frac{N}{m}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[Z]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}].\end{gathered}

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}]\ =\ \frac{A_{\mathrm{sp}}}{m}+o\Big(\frac{1}{m}\Big),\qquad A_{\mathrm{sp}}:=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}.

(D.6)

Proof

By (A1),
\{X_{r}\}_{r=1}^{m}
and
\{Y_{r}\}_{r=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. with finite fourth moment, and the pairs
(X_{r},Y_{r})
are independent across
r
by (A2). The strong law of large numbers gives

\frac{S_{i}^{\Sigma}}{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}X_{r}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}],\quad\frac{S_{j}^{\Sigma}}{m}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}],\quad\frac{N}{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}X_{r}^{2}Y_{r}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}].

Consider the smooth map

g(a,b,c)=\frac{c}{ab},\qquad a,b>0.

By (A1) we have
\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]>0
and
\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]>0
, and the SNR bound (A3) prevents degeneracy. Thus
(S_{i}^{\Sigma}/m,S_{j}^{\Sigma}/m,N/m)
converges a.s. to
(\mathbb{E}[X^{2}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}],\mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}])
in the domain of
g
, and the continuous mapping theorem yields

\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}=g\Big(\frac{S_{i}^{\Sigma}}{m},\frac{S_{j}^{\Sigma}}{m},\frac{N}{m}\Big)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}g\big(\mathbb{E}[X^{2}],\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}],\mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}]\big)=\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}Y^{2}]}{\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]\mathbb{E}[Y^{2}]}\cdot\frac{1}{m}

after factoring out the common
1/m
scale. A standard Delta-method expansion, together with uniform integrability guaranteed by
\mathbb{E}[X^{4}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[Y^{4}]<\infty
, then gives the first-order expansion in (
D.6
).

For the energy factor, SP corresponds to
\mathsf{M}_{i,r}\equiv 1
in (
D.4
), and hence

Lemma 4

For SP,

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}y_{i,r}^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=\mu_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2}\ \eqqcolon\ \mu_{2,i}.

(D.7)

Thus, under SP, both the similarity term
\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}]
and the energy factor
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}
admit clean closed forms in terms of the second and mixed fourth moments of
(X,Y)
.

D.3
Uniform-
k
Sparsification as a Reference Baseline

Before turning to deterministic TopK-SP, it is helpful to introduce a simplified baseline in which sparsification is random and independent of the hidden values. This allows us to isolate the effect of reducing the support size from
m
to
k
without the order-statistic bias of TopK.

Independently of
(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
, draw
K_{i},K_{j}\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}
uniformly at random without replacement with
|K_{i}|=|K_{j}|=k
, and define

\begin{gathered}D_{i}=\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2},\qquad D_{j}=\sum_{v\in K_{j}}y_{j,v}^{2},\\[2.0pt]
\tau^{\mathrm{uk}}_{s,ij}=\frac{\sum_{r\in K_{i}\cap K_{j}}y_{i,r}^{2}y_{j,r}^{2}}{D_{i}D_{j}}.\end{gathered}

(D.8)

Lemma 5

Conditioned on
(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
,

\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_{r\in K_{i}\cap K_{j}}y_{i,r}^{2}y_{j,r}^{2}\ \Big|\ \mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j}\Big]=\Big(\frac{k}{m}\Big)^{2}\sum_{r=1}^{m}X_{r}^{2}Y_{r}^{2},

and Serfling’s finite-population concentration inequality yields

\begin{gathered}D_{i}=\frac{k}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}X_{r}^{2}\big(1+O_{\mathbb{P}}(\varepsilon_{m,k})\big),\\[2.0pt]
D_{j}=\frac{k}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}Y_{r}^{2}\big(1+O_{\mathbb{P}}(\varepsilon_{m,k})\big),\end{gathered}

with
\varepsilon_{m,k}=C\sqrt{\frac{1-k/m}{k}}
for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Proposition 5

If
k\to\infty
and
k=o(m)
, then

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{uk}}_{s,ij}]=\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}]\,\big(1+O(\varepsilon_{m,k})\big),\qquad\varepsilon_{m,k}=C\sqrt{\frac{1-k/m}{k}}.

(D.9)

Proof

Condition on
(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
. By Lemma
5
, the numerator is equal in expectation to
(k/m)^{2}N
, while
D_{i}D_{j}
concentrates around
(k/m)^{2}S_{i}^{\Sigma}S_{j}^{\Sigma}
with relative error
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\varepsilon_{m,k})
. Thus

\tau^{\mathrm{uk}}_{s,ij}=\frac{N}{S_{i}^{\Sigma}S_{j}^{\Sigma}}\,\big(1+O_{\mathbb{P}}(\varepsilon_{m,k})\big)=\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}\,\big(1+O_{\mathbb{P}}(\varepsilon_{m,k})\big).

Taking expectations over
(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})
and using bounded fourth moments to control tails yields the stated expansion.

Uniform-
k
sparsification is therefore essentially neutral with respect to the mean scale of
\tau_{s}
: it preserves the SP behavior up to a vanishing
O(\varepsilon_{m,k})
error, so any substantial change in hidden similarity must come from the deterministic, value-dependent nature of TopK.

D.4
Deterministic Top-
K
: Mean Behavior of
\tau_{s}

We now return to TopK-SP, where the sparsity pattern is strongly coupled to the hidden values through order statistics. Our goal is to show that, despite this coupling, the mean similarity scale remains
\Theta(1/m)
, while the energy factor shrinks.

Let
t>0
be such that

\mathbb{P}(|y_{i,r}|\geq t)=\bar{p}=\frac{k}{m},

which is uniquely defined by absolute continuity in (A1). We introduce the threshold sets

K_{i}=\{r:\ |y_{i,r}|\geq t\},\qquad K_{j}=\{r:\ |y_{j,r}|\geq t\},

and note that
|K_{i}|\approx k
and
|K_{j}|\approx k
with high probability by Hoeffding-type concentration for Bernoulli indicators.

To understand the intersection size
|K_{i}\cap K_{j}|
, consider the standardized pair

(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big(0,\begin{bmatrix}1&c_{ij}\\
c_{ij}&1\end{bmatrix}\Big),

(y_{i,r},y_{j,r})

A standard bivariate normal expansion around

\begin{gathered}p_{c_{ij}}(t)=p_{0}(t)+4\phi(t)^{2}\,c_{ij}+O(c_{ij}^{2}),\\

(D.10)

with
\phi,\Phi
the standard normal pdf/cdf and
\bar{\Phi}(t)=1-\Phi(t)

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}[|K_{i}\cap K_{j}|]=m\,p_{c_{ij}}(t)=\frac{k^{2}}{m}\Big(1+\alpha(t)c_{ij}+O(c_{ij}^{2})\Big),\\[2.0pt]
\alpha(t)=\frac{4\phi(t)^{2}}{(2\bar{\Phi}(t))^{2}}\geq 0.\end{gathered}

(D.11)

Assumption (A5) ensures that, as

, the rank correlation between
\{|y_{i,r}|\}_{r}
and
\{|y_{j,r}|\}_{r}
vanishes , so that the threshold approximation (and hence (

onto a single coordinate. This guaranties that the normalization denominators remain well-behaved and that the per-coordinate weights are of order

The

1

gate/activation is bounded (e.g.,

Let

Lemma 6
(Non-concentration of Top-

If

\max_{r\in K_{i}}\frac{y_{i,r}^{2}}{\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2}}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\!\Big(\frac{\log k}{k}\Big),\qquad\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(k).

(D.12)

Proof

Let

\mathbb{P}(|y_{i,1}|\geq t)=\bar{p}

By Hoeffding’s inequality,

(D.13)

By boundedness,
|y_{i,r}|\leq B
almost surely and the CDF of
|y_{i,1}|
is continuous on
[0,B]
, strictly increasing near its upper tail. When
\bar{p}=k/m\to 0
slowly (e.g.,
k\geq c_{0}\log m
), the
(1-\bar{p})
-quantile
t
remains bounded away from zero: there exists
t_{\star}>0
(independent of
m,k
) such that
t\geq t_{\star}
for all sufficiently large
m
. Combining this with (
D.13
) yields

S_{k}=\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2}\ \geq\ k\,t_{\star}^{2}\qquad\text{with probability }1-o(1).

(D.14)

On the other hand,
y_{i,u}^{2}\leq B^{2}
for all
u
, so

S_{k}=\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2}\ \leq\ k\,B^{2}\qquad\text{always}.

(D.15)

Thus
S_{k}\in[k\,t_{\star}^{2},k\,B^{2}]
with probability
1-o(1)
, i.e.,
S_{k}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(k)
.

Finally,
M_{k}=\max_{r\in K_{i}}y_{i,r}^{2}\leq B^{2}
almost surely. Using (
D.14
),

\frac{M_{k}}{S_{k}}\ \leq\ \frac{B^{2}}{k\,t_{\star}^{2}}\ =\ \frac{C}{k},

for
C=B^{2}/t_{\star}^{2}
. Since
k\geq c_{0}\log m\to\infty
, we have
C/k=O((\log k)/k)
(trivially, as
\log k\leq k
for large
k
), which gives (
D.12
).

In words, Lemma
6
states that TopK-SP spreads the retained energy across
\Theta(k)
channels, with each coordinate contributing at most
O((\log k)/k)
of the total energy. This will directly control the weights in
\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}
.

Proposition 6
(TopK-SP preserves the
1/m
similarity scale)

Under
(A1)–(A5)
and
k\geq c_{0}\log m
, there exists
\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\geq 0
, with

\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})=O\!\Big(\frac{(\log k)^{2}}{k}\,(1+\alpha(t)c_{ij})\Big)\xrightarrow[k\to\infty,\ c_{ij}\to 0]{}0,

such that

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}]=\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}]\,\big(1+\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\big).

(D.16)

Proof

By definition of TopK-SP,

\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}=\sum_{r\in K_{i}\cap K_{j}}\frac{y_{i,r}^{2}}{\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2}}\cdot\frac{y_{j,r}^{2}}{\sum_{v\in K_{j}}y_{j,v}^{2}}.

Each factor

w_{i,r}=\frac{y_{i,r}^{2}}{\sum_{u\in K_{i}}y_{i,u}^{2}},\qquad w_{j,r}=\frac{y_{j,r}^{2}}{\sum_{v\in K_{j}}y_{j,v}^{2}}

is bounded by
O_{\mathbb{P}}((\log k)/k)
by Lemma
6
. Hence
w_{i,r}w_{j,r}=O_{\mathbb{P}}((\log k)^{2}/k^{2})
uniformly over
r\in K_{i}\cap K_{j}
. Summing over
|K_{i}\cap K_{j}|
terms and taking expectations,

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}]=\mathbb{E}\!\Big[\sum_{r\in K_{i}\cap K_{j}}w_{i,r}w_{j,r}\Big]=\mathbb{E}[|K_{i}\cap K_{j}|]\,\mathbb{E}[w_{i,r}w_{j,r}]+\text{(higher-order terms)}.

Under (A5), the rank correlation between
\{|y_{i,r}|\}_{r}
and
\{|y_{j,r}|\}_{r}
vanishes as
c_{ij}\to 0
, so the dependence between the event
\{r\in K_{i}\cap K_{j}\}
and the pair
(y_{i,r},y_{j,r})
becomes negligible in the weak-correlation regime. Using (
D.11
) and Lemma
6
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}]=\Theta\Big(\frac{k^{2}}{m}\Big)\cdot\Theta\Big(\frac{1}{k^{2}}\Big)\cdot\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{(\log k)^{2}}{k}\,(1+\alpha(t)c_{ij})\Big)\Big)=\Theta\Big(\frac{1}{m}\Big)\cdot\big(1+\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\big),

with
\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})
as stated. Comparing with Lemma
3
, which shows
\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}]=\Theta(1/m)
, yields (
D.16
).

Proposition
6
formalizes the intuition that TopK-SP preserves the coarse spectral scale of SP: both yield
\mathbb{E}[\tau_{s,ij}]=\Theta(1/m)
, and the multiplicative distortion
\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})
can be made arbitrarily small by taking
k
slightly super-logarithmic and
c_{ij}
small.

D.5
Incorporating the Energy Factor
\overline{S}^{\,2}

We now quantify how TopK-SP modifies the energy factor in (
D.4
).

SP baseline.

From Lemma
4
,

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}=\mu_{2,i}=\mu_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2}.

TopK-SP energy bound.

For TopK-SP, the mask selects the upper-
\bar{p}
tail in magnitude, and the corresponding energy is

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{tk}}=\mathbb{E}\big[X^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\geq t\}}\big],\qquad\bar{p}=\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq t)=\frac{k}{m}.

Using a standard sub-Gaussian tail integral argument (see, e.g., generic bounds for
\psi_{2}
-random variables), one obtains

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{tk}}\ \leq\ C\,(\mu_{i}^{2}+\sigma_{i}^{2})\,\bar{p}\Big(1+\log\tfrac{1}{\bar{p}}\Big),

(D.17)

for a constant
C
depending only on the sub-Gaussian proxy
K_{\mathrm{sg}}
in (A1). Squaring and comparing with
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}
,

\overline{S}_{\mathrm{tk}}^{\,2}\ \leq\ C_{\star}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\,2}\,\Big[\bar{p}\Big(1+\log\tfrac{1}{\bar{p}}\Big)\Big]^{2},\qquad\bar{p}=\frac{k}{m},

(D.18)

for some absolute constant
C_{\star}>0
.

Theorem D.1
(Energy-weighted similarity under TopK-SP)

Let
\mathcal{M}_{ij}=\tau_{s,ij}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}
be the energy-weighted similarity in (
D.4
). Under
(A1)–(A5)
and
k\geq c_{0}\log m
,

\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{tk}}_{ij}]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{ij}]\cdot\Big(1+\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\Big)\cdot C_{\star}\,\Big[\bar{p}\Big(1+\log\tfrac{1}{\bar{p}}\Big)\Big]^{2},\\[2.0pt]
\bar{p}=\frac{k}{m},\end{gathered}

(D.19)

with
\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})
from Proposition
6
. In particular, when
c_{ij}\approx 0
and
\bar{p}\ll 1
(e.g.,
\bar{p}\leq e^{-2}
and
k
modest relative to
m
), the right-hand factor can be made strictly smaller than
1
, and hence

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{tk}}_{ij}]<\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{ij}].

Proof

By construction,

\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{tk}}_{ij}=\tau^{\mathrm{tk}}_{s,ij}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{tk}}^{\,2},\qquad\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{sp}}_{ij}=\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\,2}.

Taking expectations and combining (
D.16
) with (
D.18
) yields

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{tk}}_{ij}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\mathrm{sp}}_{s,ij}]\,\big(1+\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\big)\cdot C_{\star}\,\overline{S}_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\,2}\Big[\bar{p}\Big(1+\log\tfrac{1}{\bar{p}}\Big)\Big]^{2},

which is exactly (
D.19
). The strict inequality for small enough
\bar{p}
and
|c_{ij}|
follows from the fact that
\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
,
c_{ij}\to 0
, while the function
\bar{p}\mapsto\bar{p}(1+\log(1/\bar{p}))
vanishes at
\bar{p}=0
.

Even though TopK-SP selects the largest-magnitude channels, Proposition
6
shows that it does not inflate the hidden similarity scale beyond the baseline SP order. The dominant effect is instead the energy sparsification captured by
\overline{S}_{\mathrm{tk}}^{\,2}
, which decays roughly quadratically in
\bar{p}=k/m
(up to a mild logarithmic correction). Theorem
D.1
therefore formalizes the intuitive picture that TopK-SP reduces the energy-weighted overlap
\mathcal{M}_{ij}
by a factor that is almost quadratic in the sparsity level.

D.6
Implications for NTK Spectral Statistics and Choice of
k

Recall the NTK variance proxy from Appendix A and Appendix C:

v_{\lambda}\ \approx\ \frac{a^{4}R_{x}^{4}\,\overline{S}^{\,2}\,\overline{P}^{\,2}}{n\,m^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tau_{x,ij}\,\tau_{s,ij}\,\tau_{p,ij}\,\tau_{q,ij},

(D.20)

where
\tau_{x,ij}
,
\tau_{p,ij}
, and
\tau_{q,ij}
denote the input, PE, and modulation similarity factors, respectively. Holding
\tau_{x,ij}
,
\tau_{p,ij}
,
\tau_{q,ij}
and
\overline{P}
comparable across normalizations, Theorem
D.1
implies

\mathbb{E}\!\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{TopK})}\big]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\!\big[v_{\lambda}^{(\mathrm{SP})}\big]\cdot\Big(1+\xi_{k,m}(c_{ij})\Big)\cdot C_{\star}\,\Big[\bar{p}\Big(1+\log\tfrac{1}{\bar{p}}\Big)\Big]^{2}.

(D.21)

Thus, at initialization or early training, in regimes where cross-sample correlations
c_{ij}
are weak and
\bar{p}=k/m\ll 1
, TopK-SP produces at least a near-quadratic suppression of the NTK spectral variance proxy relative to SP.

The theory above highlights two competing requirements for the choice of
k
:

1.

A small sparsity ratio
\bar{p}=k/m
strengthens the reduction factor in (
D.21
), as
\bar{p}(1+\log(1/\bar{p}))\to 0
when
\bar{p}\to 0
.

2.

Stability demands that TopK energy does not concentrate on a single channel, which in our analysis is encoded by the condition
k\geq c_{0}\log m
in Lemma
6
.

A practical rule that meets both constraints and matches our empirical choices is

k\ =\ \max\Big\{\ \lfloor\eta m\rfloor,\ \ \lceil c\log m\rceil\ \Big\},\qquad\eta\in[1/6,1),\ \ c\in[2,4].

(D.22)

Here
\eta
sets the effective sparsity level
\bar{p}=\eta
in the large-
m
limit, while the
c\log m
floor guarantees non-concentration and well-behaved denominators in (
D.16
) even for moderate widths. When significant positive correlations
c_{ij}
are observed, increasing
k
(larger
\bar{p}
) or introducing a soft stochastic threshold (soft-TopK) mitigates the amplification of intersection size due to the
\alpha(t)c_{ij}
term in (
D.11
).

Under standard sub-Gaussian assumptions and weak cross-sample correlation, TopK-SP preserves the
1/m
scaling of hidden similarities while strictly reducing the energy-weighted overlap
\mathcal{M}_{ij}
by a nearly quadratic factor in the sparsity ratio. Through the NTK variance identity of Appendix A and Appendix C, this leads to a principled reduction in NTK spectral dispersion and, consequently, a mitigation of spectral bias at initialization.

E
NTK Convergence and Stability

In this appendix we provide detailed proofs of the convergence results stated as Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
in the main text. Throughout, we work under the architectural setup and notation of Appendix
A
(baseline NTK dynamics) and assume
A1
–
A3
.

Our analysis follows the now-standard NTK framework introduced by Jacot et al.
ref15
and further developed in the over-parameterized optimization literature
ref35
;
ref32
;
ref67
;
ref16
. Both the gradient-flow result (Theorem
4.1
) and the discrete-time GD result (Theorem
4.2
) rely on the same linear-dynamics viewpoint: in the NTK regime, the network behaves like a kernel model with a (frozen) NTK Gram matrix. Our contribution here is to make explicit how the spherical normalization and Hadamard modulation of Appendix
A
enter these dynamics through the spectrum of the NTK.

Recall that we denote

\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=\gamma(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad\rho_{ij}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j},

and that the NTK Gram matrix on the training set is
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as in Eq. (
A.8
). The spherical normalization and Hadamard modulation only affect the entries of
\mathbf{H}
, not the abstract convergence arguments that follow.

E.1
From
\textbf{H}^{\infty}
to the Finite-Width NTK

We begin by isolating the finite-width NTK at initialization:

\mathbf{H}_{0}\;\triangleq\;\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0))\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

(E.1)

Assumption
A3
asserts that the infinite-width NTK

\mathbf{H}^{\infty}\;\triangleq\;\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(0))\big]

exists and is positive definite on the training set, with

\lambda_{0}\;\triangleq\;\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}^{\infty})>0.

In addition, as in the main text, we impose a finite-width concentration
condition at initialization:

\big\|\mathbf{H}_{0}-\mathbf{H}^{\infty}\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}.

(E.2)

This condition can be guaranteed with high probability once
m
is
polynomially large in
n
; see, e.g., the concentration results for
two-layer NTKs in
ref67
. Here we treat (
E.2
) as
a deterministic assumption and focus on its consequences for the
spectrum.

Lemma 7
(Finite-width NTK inherits the spectral gap)

Under Assumption
A3
and (
E.2
), we have

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\;\geq\;\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}.

Proof

Since
\mathbf{H}^{\infty}
is symmetric positive definite,

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}^{\infty})=\lambda_{0}>0
. For any two symmetric
matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
, Weyl’s inequality gives

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{A})\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{B})-\|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\|_{2}.

Taking
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{H}_{0}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{H}^{\infty}
yields

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}^{\infty})-\big\|\mathbf{H}_{0}-\mathbf{H}^{\infty}\big\|_{2}.

Substituting
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}^{\infty})=\lambda_{0}
and
(
E.2
) gives

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\;\geq\;\lambda_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0},

as claimed.

In the remainder of this appendix we work on the event where
Lemma
7
holds, and thus freely use the lower bound

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\;\geq\;\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}.

E.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1
(Gradient Flow)

We now turn to the continuous-time setting. In the NTK regime, the
parameter dynamics induce a kernel gradient flow on the training
predictions; freezing the NTK at its initialization value

\mathbf{H}_{0}
leads to a linear ODE that we can solve explicitly.

Let

\boldsymbol{u}(t)=\big(f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1};t),\dots,f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n};t)\big)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

denote the vector of predictions at time
t
. In the frozen-kernel model,
the NTK dynamics (
A.10
) specialize to

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)=-\,\mathbf{H}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}\big),\qquad t\geq 0.

(E.3)

We define the error vector

\boldsymbol{e}(t)\;\triangleq\;\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}.

(E.4)

Subtracting
\dot{\boldsymbol{y}}(t)\equiv\boldsymbol{0}
from both sides of
(
E.3
) gives the linear ODE

\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}(t)=-\,\mathbf{H}_{0}\,\boldsymbol{e}(t),\qquad t\geq 0.

(E.5)

To track the decay of the error, we consider the squared
\ell_{2}
norm

\phi(t)\;\triangleq\;\|\boldsymbol{e}(t)\|_{2}^{2}=\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}(t).

(E.6)

Differentiating (
E.6
) and using the product rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\big(\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}(t)^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}(t)+\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\dot{\boldsymbol{e}}(t),

(E.7)

where the last equality uses symmetry of the inner product.
Substituting (
E.5
) into (
E.7
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\big(-\mathbf{H}_{0}\boldsymbol{e}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=-2\,\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}\boldsymbol{e}(t).

(E.8)

Because
\mathbf{H}_{0}
is symmetric positive semidefinite, the Rayleigh
quotient inequality gives, for any nonzero
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}\boldsymbol{v}\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}^{2}.

(E.9)

Applying (
E.9
) with
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{e}(t)
,

\boldsymbol{e}(t)^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}\boldsymbol{e}(t)\;\geq\;\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\,\phi(t).

(E.10)

Combining (
E.8
) and (
E.10
), we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)\;\leq\;-2\,\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\,\phi(t).

(E.11)

By Lemma
7
,

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\geq\lambda_{0}/2
, hence

\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)\;\leq\;-\lambda_{0}\,\phi(t).

(E.12)

At this point we can invoke a standard Grönwall argument. Define

\psi(t)\;\triangleq\;e^{\lambda_{0}t}\,\phi(t).

Differentiating and applying (
E.12
),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\psi(t)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda_{0}t}\frac{d}{dt}\phi(t)+\lambda_{0}e^{\lambda_{0}t}\phi(t)

\displaystyle\leq e^{\lambda_{0}t}\big(-\lambda_{0}\phi(t)\big)+\lambda_{0}e^{\lambda_{0}t}\phi(t)

\displaystyle=0.

(E.13)

Thus
\psi(t)
is nonincreasing in
t
, i.e.,

e^{\lambda_{0}t}\phi(t)\leq\phi(0),\qquad t\geq 0,

which is equivalent to

\phi(t)\leq e^{-\lambda_{0}t}\phi(0),\qquad t\geq 0.

Using the definition (
E.6
) and

\phi(0)=\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}
, we arrive at

\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}\;\leq\;e^{-\lambda_{0}t}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad t\geq 0.

(E.14)

This is precisely the exponential convergence statement of
Theorem
4.1
for the gradient-flow dynamics in the NTK regime.

E.3
Proof of Theorem
4.2
(Gradient Descent)

We next turn to discrete-time gradient descent. In the NTK regime with
frozen kernel
\mathbf{H}_{0}
, GD on the training set obeys

\boldsymbol{u}(k+1)=\boldsymbol{u}(k)-\eta\,\mathbf{H}_{0}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}\big),\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots

(E.15)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate. As before, define the error

\boldsymbol{e}(k)\;\triangleq\;\boldsymbol{u}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}.

(E.16)

Then (
E.15
) is equivalent to the linear recursion

\boldsymbol{e}(k+1)=\big(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)\boldsymbol{e}(k),\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots.

(E.17)

Closed-form solution and spectral decomposition.

Iterating (
E.17
) gives the explicit solution

\boldsymbol{e}(k)=\big(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)^{k}\,\boldsymbol{e}(0),\qquad k\geq 0.

(E.18)

Since
\mathbf{H}_{0}
is symmetric positive semidefinite, it admits an
eigendecomposition

\mathbf{H}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top},

(E.19)

where
\{\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal basis of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda_{i}\geq 0
are the eigenvalues.

Using (
E.19
), we can rewrite

\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top},

(E.20)

and therefore

\big(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}.

(E.21)

We expand the initial error in this eigenbasis:

\boldsymbol{e}(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i},\qquad\alpha_{i}\;\triangleq\;\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e(0)}=\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}).

(E.22)

Plugging (
E.21
) and (
E.22
) into
(
E.18
), we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}\alpha_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}\alpha_{i}\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i},

(E.23)

where we used orthonormality
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
.

Consequently, the squared error norm has the exact spectral representation

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{e}(k)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}(k)^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}(k)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\Big)^{\top}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{j})^{k}\alpha_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}(1-\eta\lambda_{j})^{k}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}\alpha_{i}^{2}.

(E.24)

Restating this in terms of
\boldsymbol{u}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{y}
gives

\|\boldsymbol{u}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y})\big)^{2}},

(E.25)

which is exactly the frozen-kernel part of Theorem
4.2
. In the small-initialization regime where
\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)\|_{2}
is negligible compared to
\|\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}
, we have

\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y})\approx-\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{y}
, so the formula reduces, up to this sign convention, to the familiar label-projection view of Arora et al. (2019)
ref16
..

Stepsize condition and linear rate.

To convert (
E.25
) into a clean linear rate, we
impose the stepsize condition from Theorem
4.2
:

0<\eta\leq L^{-1},\qquad L\;\triangleq\;\|\mathbf{H}_{0}\|_{2}=\max_{i}\lambda_{i}.

(E.26)

Then
0\leq\eta\lambda_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
, so

0\leq 1-\eta\lambda_{i}\leq 1,\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

(E.27)

On the other hand, by Lemma
7
,

\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\geq\lambda_{0}/2
, hence

1-\eta\lambda_{i}\leq 1-\eta\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\;\triangleq\;\rho,\qquad i=1,\dots,n,

(E.28)

with
0\leq\rho<1
.

Using (
E.28
), we obtain the uniform bound

(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}\leq\rho^{2k},\qquad i=1,\dots,n.

(E.29)

Substituting (
E.29
) into (
E.25
),

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{2k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle=\rho^{2k}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

(E.30)

Since
0\leq\rho<1
implies
\rho^{2k}\leq\rho^{k}
for all
k\geq 0
, we can
further simplify to

\|\boldsymbol{u}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\rho^{k}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}=\Big(1-\eta\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}\Big)^{k}\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2},

(E.31)

which is the linear convergence rate claimed in Theorem
4.2

for the frozen-NTK model.

Kernel model vs. finite-width network.

The expression (
E.25
) describes the idealized

kernel model
obtained by freezing the NTK at
\mathbf{H}_{0}
.
For a true finite-width network, the NTK

\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(k))
evolves with
k
.
In the main text we denote by
\epsilon(k)
the discrepancy between the
finite-width model and its frozen-kernel counterpart. The next subsection
makes this precise, via a standard NTK stability argument.

E.4
NTK Stability and the Residual Term
\epsilon(k)

We now quantify how much the NTK can drift during training, and how this
drift translates into a discrepancy between the network trajectory and
the frozen-kernel dynamics. The following lemma is a standard NTK
stability result, adapted to our architecture and notation.

Lemma 8
(NTK stability in the over-parameterized regime)

Assume Assumptions
A1
–
A3
hold, with ReLU
activation and random initialization as in Assumption
A2
.
Let
n
be the number of training samples and let
\boldsymbol{u}(0)
be the
initial prediction vector. This is a specialization of the stability
results proved in

ref67
;
ref35
;
ref32
;
ref16

to our architecture with spherical normalization and Hadamard modulation.

Then there exist a polynomial
p(\cdot)
and constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(depending
only on
R_{x}
,
C
,
\kappa
,
a
, and the normalization scale defined in
Appendix A) such that for any
0<\delta<1
and any polynomially bounded time
horizon
T
, if the width satisfies

m\;\geq\;p\bigl(n,1/\lambda_{0},1/\delta,T\bigr),

then with probability at least
1-\delta
(over the random initialization)
we have, simultaneously for all
k=0,1,\dots,T
,

\displaystyle\max_{r\in[m]}\big\|\boldsymbol{w}_{r}(k)-\boldsymbol{w}_{r}(0)\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{m}},

(E.32)

\displaystyle\big\|\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(k))-\mathbf{H}_{0}\big\|_{2}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}.

(E.33)

Proof
(Proof sketch)

The argument follows the NTK stability analyses for two-layer ReLU networks
in

ref67
;
ref35
;
ref32
;
ref16
. We outline the main steps and highlight
where our architecture enters.

(1) Bounded gradients at initialization.

Using the boundedness of
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i},y_{i})
and Gaussian
initialization (Assumption
A2
), one shows that the initial
loss and gradient norms are polynomially bounded in
n
; see, e.g., Lemma 3.1
of
ref67
.

(2) Monotonic loss decrease.

Under the stepsize condition in Theorem
4.2
, the empirical loss
is nonincreasing along GD. Hence the gradient norms remain uniformly
bounded over
k\leq T
.

(3) Small weight drift.

Summing the GD updates for each neuron
\boldsymbol{w}_{r}
over

k=0,\dots,T-1
and using the gradient bounds, we obtain

\|\boldsymbol{w}_{r}(k)-\boldsymbol{w}_{r}(0)\|_{2}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad r\in[m],\ 0\leq k\leq T,

for a polynomially bounded constant
C_{1}
.

(4) Kernel Lipschitzness and activation stability.

On any region where the activation patterns

\mathbb{I}_{r,i}=\mathbb{I}\{\boldsymbol{w}_{r}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\geq 0\}

are fixed,
\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W})
is a smooth (indeed, polynomial) function
of the weights and hence Lipschitz. Gaussian concentration and a union bound
over
(i,r)
imply that, with high probability, only a small fraction of
neurons ever cross an activation boundary during training, so their
contribution to the kernel drift is negligible. Combining these facts with
the small weight drift in (3) yields the kernel stability bound
(
E.33
). The spherical normalization and Hadamard modulation
affect only the Lipschitz constants and enter through
C_{1},C_{2}
.

We now use Lemma
8
to control the discrepancy between
the finite-width network and the frozen-NTK model, thereby justifying the
residual term
\epsilon(k)
in Theorem
4.2
.

Lemma 9
(Kernel drift and the residual term)

Under the conditions of Lemma
8
, let

\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)
denote the prediction vector of the
finite-width network at step
k
, and

\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)
denote the prediction of the frozen-NTK
model (i.e., gradient descent with fixed kernel
\mathbf{H}_{0}
).
Define

\epsilon(k)\;\triangleq\;\big\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)-\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)\big\|_{2}.

Then for any polynomially bounded horizon
T
, there exists a constant

C_{3}>0
such that if

m\geq p(n,1/\lambda_{0},1/\delta,T)
, then with probability at least

1-\delta
,

\sup_{0\leq k\leq T}\,\epsilon(k)\;\leq\;\frac{C_{3}}{\sqrt{m}}.

Proof

Let

\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}

and

\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)-\boldsymbol{y}
.
We consider their difference

\Delta(k)\;\triangleq\;\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)-\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k),\qquad\epsilon(k)=\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}.

(E.34)

The finite-width network obeys

\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k+1)=\big(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(k))\big)\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k),

while the frozen-NTK model satisfies

\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k+1)=\big(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k).

Subtracting, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta(k+1)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k+1)-\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k+1)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)\Delta(k)-\eta\big(\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(k))-\mathbf{H}_{0}\big)\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k).

(E.35)

Taking the 2-norm and using the submultiplicativity of the operator norm,

\displaystyle\|\Delta(k+1)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\|_{2}\,\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\eta\big\|\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(k))-\mathbf{H}_{0}\big\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)\|_{2}.

(E.36)

As in the previous subsection,

\|\mathbf{I}-\eta\mathbf{H}_{0}\|_{2}=\max_{i}|1-\eta\lambda_{i}|\leq 1-\eta\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}=:\rho<1.

By Lemma
8
, for all
k\leq T
we have

\|\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{W}(k))-\mathbf{H}_{0}\|_{2}\leq C_{2}/\sqrt{m}
.
Furthermore,

\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{net}}(k)\|_{2}\leq\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)\|_{2}+\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}.

The frozen-NTK error satisfies the linear rate from
(
E.31
):

\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)\|_{2}\leq\rho^{k}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}.

Substituting these bounds into (
E.36
), and writing

\varepsilon_{m}\triangleq C_{2}/\sqrt{m}
, yields

\displaystyle\|\Delta(k+1)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\rho\,\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}+\eta\,\varepsilon_{m}\big(\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\mathrm{ker}}(k)\|_{2}+\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq(\rho+\eta\varepsilon_{m})\,\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}+\eta\varepsilon_{m}\,\rho^{k}\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}.

(E.37)

For sufficiently large
m
, we can ensure

\eta\varepsilon_{m}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho)
. Then

\rho+\eta\varepsilon_{m}\leq\rho+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\rho)=\frac{1+\rho}{2}=:\tilde{\rho}<1.

Thus (
E.37
) simplifies to

\|\Delta(k+1)\|_{2}\leq\tilde{\rho}\,\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}+\eta\varepsilon_{m}\,\rho^{k}\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}.

(E.38)

Since the initialization is shared between the network and the frozen-NTK
model, we have
\Delta(0)=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Iterating (
E.38
) and using a discrete Grönwall argument,
we obtain

\displaystyle\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\varepsilon_{m}\,\|\boldsymbol{u}(0)-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{k-1}\tilde{\rho}^{\,k-1-\tau}\rho^{\tau}.

(E.39)

The finite sum

\sum_{\tau=0}^{k-1}\tilde{\rho}^{k-1-\tau}\rho^{\tau}
is uniformly bounded
in
k
by a constant depending only on
(\rho,\tilde{\rho})
, so there
exists
C_{3}>0
(independent of
m
and
k
) such that

\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}\leq C_{3}\,\varepsilon_{m}=\frac{C_{3}C_{2}}{\sqrt{m}},\qquad 0\leq k\leq T.

Recalling
\epsilon(k)=\|\Delta(k)\|_{2}
and absorbing
C_{2}
into
C_{3}

completes the proof.

Putting Lemma
8
and Lemma
9

together, we obtain the following picture in the over-parameterized NTK
regime:

•

The frozen-NTK model enjoys a convergence rate fully controlled by
the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{H}_{0}
, with an exact spectral
decomposition given in (
E.25
).

•

For polynomially many iterations, the finite-width network stays

O(m^{-1/2})
-close (in prediction space) to this kernel model, as
quantified by the residual term
\epsilon(k)
.

This justifies approximating the training dynamics in the main text by a
nearly frozen NTK and explains the appearance of
\epsilon(k)
in
Theorem
4.2
. In particular, as
m\to\infty
, the residual
vanishes and the network converges at essentially the same rate as its
limiting kernel model.
