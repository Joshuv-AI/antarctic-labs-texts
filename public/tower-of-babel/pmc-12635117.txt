ZIC5, a survival biomarker, facilitates tumor progression and stemness in colon cancer via the activation of the Wnt signaling pathway
Authors: Xiaopeng Li, Chenye Zhao, Mingchao Mu, Hang Yuan, Gang Chen, Shihui Chen, Xiongwei Huo, Xuejun Sun, Junhui Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer (CRC), a highly heterogeneous disease, is the second leading cause of cancer related deaths. Mounting evidence has indicated that the zinc finger of the cerebellum 5 (ZIC5) is involved in the pathological processes of cancer. At present, there are few and conflicting studies on ZIC5 in CRC. The aim of this study was to elucidate the clinical value, function and molecular mechanism of ZIC5 in colon cancer. ZIC5 expression analysis in pan-cancer was performed using the TCGA database. The correlation of ZIC5 expression with clinicopathologic parameters of colon cancer was assessed in public and external datasets, respectively. The influence of ZIC5 expression on survival was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve and Cox regression models. In addition, a series of in vitro experiments on cell proliferation, invasion, migration and stemness were performed to further explore the function and potential mechanisms of ZIC5 in colon cancer. ZIC5 mRNA expression is significantly elevated in the vast majority of cancers. We found that ZIC5 is elevated in colon cancer tissues, and high ZIC5 expression was associated with poorer survival of colon cancer patients. ZIC5 expression was positively correlated with the malignant pathological phenotype, and it’s an independent risk marker for the overall survival (OS) of colon cancer patients. We demonstrated that ZIC5 promoted proliferation, invasion, migration and EMT of colon cancer cells. Additionally, ZIC5 enhances colon cancer stem cell (CSC) properties. Mechanistically, ZIC5 functions in colon cancer cells by upregulating Wnt signaling. ZIC5 could enhance Wnt/β-catenin signaling by interacting with β-catenin. Interestingly, in HCT116 cells, ZIC5 doesn’t affect the protein level of β-catenin and its nuclear translocation likely due to the deletion mutation of β-catenin at Ser45. We identified ZIC5 as a survival marker for colon cancer patients. Our study shows that inhibition of ZIC5 might be a potential therapeutic strategy for colon cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24809-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide based on the latest global cancer statistics1. Although the overall survival of CRC has improved remarkably with the popularization of screening and advances in surgical techniques and novel systemic therapies, the 5-year survival rate of patients with stage IV CRC is only 15%2,3. Metastasis is the leading cause of CRC-associated mortality. Therefore, it is urgent to identify survival markers of CRC and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms of its progression and metastasis.

The zinc finger of the cerebellum (ZIC) gene family discovered in the Drosophila (odd-paired) is highly conserved in sequence4. The human ZIC gene family consists of five members (ZIC1 to ZIC5), and they play pleiotropic roles in developmental and disease processes, including cell fate specification, cell proliferation, and cell differentiation4–6. ZIC family members which contain five Cys2His2 zinc-finger (C2H2-ZF) domains directly or indirectly regulate gene expression as a transcription factor or co-factor4,7. Accumulating evidence has indicated that ZIC5 is involved in multiple pathological processes of cancer8. CRC is a highly heterogeneous disease9, making the study of the role and mechanism of ZIC5 complicated and elusive.

Approximately 90% of CRC is caused by aberrant activation of the Wnt pathway, which plays a crucial role in the maintenance of the stem state of cancer stem cells (CSCs), carcinogenesis and metastasis in CRC10–12. The canonical Wnt pathway, known as the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, is activated dependent on the nuclear translocation of β-catenin13. In the absence of a Wnt stimulus, β-catenin is degraded by a multiprotein “destruction complex” consisting of Axin, APC, GSK3 and CK114,15. It is universally accepted that when β-catenin enters the complex by binding Axin and APC, CK1, which binds to Axin, initially phosphorylates β-catenin at Ser45, subsequently triggering the phosphorylation of β-catenin by GSK3 at more N-terminal residues (Thr41, Ser37, and Ser33), and phosphorylated β-catenin is eventually degraded by the ubiquitin-proteasome system15–17. The non-phosphorylated β-catenin accumulates in the cytoplasm, translocated into the nucleus and generated the complex with the TCF/LEF family members to regulate downstream target genes18, such as Cyclin D1. Approximately 80% of CRCs contain mutations in APC, and half of the remaining harbor mutations in β-catenin that stabilize β-catenin and activate Wnt signaling13,19. Within colon cancer cell lines, SW480 harbor APC mutations, and HCT116 contains a three-base deletion in the CTNNB1 gene, causing Ser45 deletion of β-catenin20.

In our study, we first analyzed the expression pattern of ZIC5 in pan-cancer using the TCGA database. Then we explored the clinical value of ZIC5 expression in colon cancer using multiple datasets. Further, the role of ZIC5 in colon cancer and how ZIC5 is connected to Wnt signaling were shared.

Materials and methods

Data obtained and analysis

The gene expression data (RNA-Seq, FPKM) and corresponding clinical information of colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) samples were obtained from the TCGA official website (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). A total of 514 samples included 473 colon cancer tissues and 41 normal colon tissues. Cases with incomplete clinicopathologic data and lack of follow-up time were excluded. Gene expression profile data for GSE4407621 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), including 98 tumor tissue samples and paired normal colon mucosa samples. 338 cases whose data were completed in terms of ZIC5 expression profiles, clinicopathologic indicators and prognostic information were obtained from the TCGA-COAD project (Supplementary Table 1). Clinicopathologic indicators included age, gender, T stage, Lymph node metastasis, M stage, and TNM stage. Cases with COAD were divided into two groups based on the median of ZIC5 mRNA expression. Those not below the median value are the high-expression group, otherwise they are the low-expression group.

Patients and tissue samples

Clinicopathologic information of 45 colon cancer patients was collected from the Department of General Surgery of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Clinicopathologic factors included age, gender, tumor size, differentiation, T stage, Lymph node metastasis, M stage, and TNM stage. More details of the clinicopathologic information of these patients are displayed in Supplementary Table 2. Tumor tissue and adjacent normal tissue samples were obtained from postoperative pathology specimens. Written informed consent forms were signed by all patients. The study involving human tissue samples strictly adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki. The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. All methods involved in the study were performed in accordance with our hospital’s ethics and guidelines.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

For IHC, the staining procedure followed a standard immunoperoxidase staining procedure. The anti-ZIC5 (1:100, ab115566, Abcam) was used for IHC following the instructions. The experimental result was evaluated according to the staining intensity and extent. The staining intensity was scored as negative (0), weak (1), moderate (2) or strong (3). The extent of positively stained cells was scored as 0 (≤ 5%), 1 (6–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), or 4 (> 75%). The immunoreactivity score (IRS) was calculated by multiplying the score of the staining intensity with that of the staining extent. An IRS of < 3 was defined as negative, and a score of ≥ 3 was defined as positive. All the specimens were evaluated by two pathologists in a blinded manner.

Cell cultures

SW480 and HCT116 cells were obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were routinely maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Lonsera, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C at 5% CO2.

Lentiviral vectors and transfection

The lentiviral vector pGC-FU-ZIC5 was used to overexpress ZIC5. ZIC5-overexpressing lentivirus and their control lentivirus fused with 3×Flag tag were generated from GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All transfections were conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection

The ZIC5 and negative control siRNAs were synthesized by Genecarer Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Xi’an, China). siRNA targeting ZIC5 (siZIC5: 5′-CGCCCAAUACAUACAUGUUUU-3′) and a nonspecific negative control siRNA (siNC: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′) were used in this study. A suitable amount of cells was seeded into a 6-well plate and transfected using Lipo3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the instructions.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK8) assay

The proliferation of colon cancer cell lines was evaluated using CCK8 Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). SW480 and HCT116 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2000 cells per well and incubated at 5% CO2 and 37 °C. Then at the indicated time points, 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent was added to 100 µl of fresh DMEM medium per well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The OD450 value of each well was measured with a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

For colony formation assay, 1000 HCT116 cells or 1500 SW480 cells were seeded in each well of a 6-well plate and cultured at 5% CO2 and 37 °C for 2 weeks. The cells were subsequently fixed in methanol for 20 min and stained with crystal violet for 30 min. Colonies with more than 50 cells were counted. Colonies were counted using ImageJ software under the same parameters. Each experiment was triplicated.

Transwell assays

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using the transwell chamber (8 μm pore size; Corning) in 24-well plates. In both assays, the lower chamber was filled with 500 µl of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. For migration assay, serum-free starved 50,000 cells were seeded in the upper chamber. For invasion assay, serum-free starved 80,000 cells were seeded in the upper chamber with pre-coated Matrigel (BD Biosciences). Cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h in migration assay and 72 h in invasion assay. After incubation, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the chamber were removed with cotton-tipped swabs and cells under the membrane were fixed with methanol for 20 min, stained with crystal violet for 30 min, and dried. The number of cells was counted in 3 randomly selected fields (20x magnification). Cells were counted using ImageJ software under the same parameters. Each experiment was triplicated.

Sphere formation assay

Cells were seeded in ultra-low attachment 24-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 1000 cells per well. Cells were in DMEM/F12 medium added with 20 ng/ml bFGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 20 ng/ml EGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% B27 supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific)22, and cultured at 37 °C at 5% CO2 for 2 weeks.

Fresh medium was added every two days. The number of spheres was counted in 3 randomly selected fields (20x magnification). The diameter of the sphere refers to the maximum diameter, measured using the ruler tool in ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted using the RNA extraction kit from Fastagen (RNAfast200, Shanghai, China) following the producer’s instructions. Total RNA was quantified using Nano-300. Subsequently, 1 µg of total RNA was used to synthesize cDNA by reverse transcription using ABScript III RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover (RK20429, ABclonal). RT-qPCR was conducted with a Fast SYBR qPCR Mix kit (GenStar, Cat# A301) on a CFX96 instrument (Bio-Rad, USA). Primer sequences for specific genes are shown in Supplementary Table 3. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt) method.

Protein extraction and western blotting

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cell lysates were sonicated, centrifuged and the supernatant was harvested as total protein. Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein Assay Kit (CWBIO, China). Proteins denatured by boiling after adding 5x loading buffer (P1040, Solarbio, China) further experimented. Proteins were then subjected to SDS-PAGE, wet transferred to PVDF membranes (Immobilon-P, Millipore, 0.2 μm), and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Then, membranes were respectively incubated with anti-ZIC5 (1:400, orb158782, biorbyt), anti-GAPDH (1:50000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), anti-E-cadherin (1:5000, 20874-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-N-cadherin (1:1000, R380671, Zen-bioscience), anti-Flag (1:40000, 20543-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-β-Catenin (1:1000, #8480T, CST), anti-CyclinD1 (1:1000, #2978T, CST), or anti-H3 (1:1000, AF0009, Beyotime) at 4 °C overnight. The membrane was then washed three times with TBST buffer for 10 min each time, and anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:5000, abs20040 or abs20039, Absin, Shanghai, China) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The ECL reagents (Mishu, Xi’an, China) were used to visualize the specific protein bands.

Extraction and isolation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins

The extraction and isolation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins was conducted using Beyotime’s Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027, Shanghai, China) according to the product’s instructions.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays

Cells were lysed by IP lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Cell lysates were not sonicated and the supernatant was collected by high-speed centrifugation for further experiments. 500 µl of protein sample was incubated with 5 µg of primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by the addition of 25 µl of Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 4 h. The agarose beads were washed four times using IP lysis buffer, and the complex precipitate was resuspended by the loading buffer, boiled to denaturation, and used for further western blot analysis.

Luciferase reporter assay

TOPFlash, FOPFlash and pRL-TK luciferase reporter plasmids were purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). 5000 cells per well were seeded in a 96-well plate. TOPFlash or FOPFlash and pRL-TK plasmids were co-transfected into cells using lipo3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the producer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, cells were harvested and detected by a Dual Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (RG027, Beyotime, China) and a multi-functional microplate reader (Cytation5M, BioTek, USA). The firefly luciferase intensity was normalized to renilla luciferase activity.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-β-Catenin (1:100, #8480T, CST) diluted in 1% BSA, then washed and exposed to Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:50, Cat no: SA00009-2, Proteintech) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and imaged under identical conditions using a laser confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) (continuous variables) or counts and percentages (categorical variables). Student’s t test, Mann-Whitney U test, Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the differences between the two groups. OS and DSS were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. The risk factors for survival were identified using Cox regression analyses. Spearman correlation was used to test the co-expression correlation between gene expression levels. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.1.3) or GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.0). A two-tailed test with P < 0.05 was significant.

Results

ZIC5 expression analysis in pan-cancer

We first analyzed the differences in ZIC5 expression in normal and tumor tissues of 33 cancers respectively using the TCGA database. The results indicated that ZIC5 mRNA expression is significantly elevated in the vast majority of cancers, including BLCA (bladder urothelial carcinoma), BRCA (Breast invasive carcinoma), CESC (Cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma), CHOL (cholangiocarcinoma), COAD (colon adenocarcinoma), ESCA (esophageal carcinoma), HNSC (head and neck squamous cell carcinoma), KICH (kidney chromophobe), KIRC (kidney renal clear cell carcinoma), LIHC (liver hepatocellular carcinoma), LUAD (lung adenocarcinoma), LUSC (Lung squamous cell carcinoma), PAAD (pancreatic adenocarcinoma), PRAD (prostate adenocarcinoma), READ (rectum adenocarcinoma), STAD (Stomach adenocarcinoma) and UCEC (uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma) (Fig. 1A). Additionally, to eliminate small sample sizes and large variability among samples within groups, a paired differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues in pan-cancer was performed. The results showed that compared to its paired normal tissues, ZIC5 mRNA expression was remarkably increased in BLCA, CHOL, COAD, ESCA, HNSC, KIRC, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, PRAD, READ, STAD, and UCEC (Fig. 1B). Based on the results of these analyses, we hypothesized that ZIC5 may play an important role in the above 13 cancers. Combined with the experimental direction and conditions of our research group, we determined colon cancer for follow-up studies.

ZIC5 is elevated in colon cancer tissues, and high ZIC5 expression associates with a worse survival of colon cancer patients

To investigate the role of ZIC5 in colon cancer, we analyzed the expression levels of ZIC5 in colon cancer and normal colon tissues using RNA-seq data from the TCGA-COAD project. The results showed that ZIC5 mRNA expression was upregulated in colon cancer tissues compared to unpaired or paired normal colon tissues (Fig. 2A, B). In addition, the same result was obtained from the expression analysis of ZIC5 on the tissue microarray GSE 44,076 (Fig. 2C). To confirm the protein expression level of ZIC5, immunohistochemical staining of colon cancer (n = 45) and normal colon tissues (n = 45) was executed. IHC analysis revealed that ZIC5 protein expression was significantly increased in colon cancer compared to normal colon tissues, and ZIC5 protein is mainly localized in the nucleus of cells of colon cancer tissues (Fig. 2D).

To determine whether ZIC5 expression levels are associated with prognosis in colon cancer patients, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis based on ZIC5 mRNA expression was performed utilizing survival data from the TCGA-COAD project. Below the median value of ZCI5 expression is the ZIC5 low expression group, otherwise it is the ZIC5 high expression group. Surprisingly, we found that colon cancer patients with high ZIC5 expression had more unfavorable OS and DSS compared to patients with low ZIC5 expression (Fig. 2E, F). Taken together, ZIC5 is elevated in colon cancer tissues, and high ZIC5 expression is correlated with poorer survival of colon cancer patients.

ZIC5 expression correlates with clinicopathologic parameters and is an independent prognostic indicator for colon cancer

We also explored the correlation between ZIC5 mRNA expression and clinicopathologic indicators using the TCGA-COAD dataset. The results suggested that high ZIC5 mRNA expression was dramatically associated with higher rates of lymph node metastasis (LNM), stage M1 and stage III + IV (Supplementary Table 4). We further explored the association between ZIC5 protein expression and clinicopathologic indicators using our own dataset. Similarly, ZIC5 protein expression was positively correlated with LNM, stage M1 and stage III + IV, but not with other indicators (Table 1). In addition, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to determine whether ZIC5 expression could serve as an independent predictor of OS for colon cancer patients. T stage, LNM, M stage, TNM stage and ZIC5 expression were statistically significant in the univariate Cox analysis, and M stage and ZIC5 expression were markedly different in the multivariate Cox analysis (Table 2). Thus, high ZIC5 expression is an independent risk factor for OS of colon cancer patients.

ZIC5 promotes colon cancer cell malignancy

To explore the function of ZIC5 in colon cancer cells, we constructed stably transfected cell lines overexpressing ZIC5 using lentiviral transfection. We then confirmed the transfection efficiency using RT-qPCR and western blotting at the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3A–D). To determine whether ZIC5 affects the proliferation of colon cancer cells, CCK8 and colony formation assays were performed. CCK8 and colony formation assays indicated overexpression of ZIC5 greatly increased the proliferation of SW480 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 3E–J). We also investigated the effects of ZIC5 on the invasion and migration of colon cancer cells using the Transwell assay. As shown in Fig. K–N, the invasive and migratory abilities of SW480 and HCT116 cells overexpressing ZIC5 were remarkably enhanced compared to those of control cells.

We also used siRNA to examine the functional effects of ZIC5 knockdown on colon cancer cell lines. First, we validated the knockdown efficiency of ZIC5 through western blotting (Fig. 4A, B). Then, CCK8 and colony formation assays were employed to study the proliferative capacity of cells. The results indicated that ZIC5 knockdown dramatically suppressed the proliferation of SW480 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 4C–F). Furthermore, Transwell assays demonstrated that ZIC5 knockdown significantly reduced the invasive and migratory capabilities of SW480 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 4G, H).

ZIC5 correlates with colon CSC properties and epithelial-mesenchymal transition

ZIC2 and ZIC5 are members of the same family of proteins, so we hypothesized that they might have similar properties. We first analyzed the correlation between ZIC2 and ZIC5 expression in pan-cancer and COAD at the transcriptional level, respectively. Surprisingly, there is a strong co-expression relationship between ZIC2 and ZIC5 both in pan-cancer (Supplementary Fig. 1A) and in COAD (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Previous studies23–25 have reported that ZIC2 is strongly associated with cancer stem cell properties. It is reasonable to speculate that ZIC5 is likely to be involved in cancer stem cell properties. We then respectively investigated the correlation between ZIC5 expression and the expression of several common colon cancer stem cell markers (such as CD44, CD26 and LGR426 in COAD. The results showed that ZIC5 expression was positively correlated with the expression of CD44, CD26 and LGR4, respectively (Fig. 5A, B, C). Further, a sphere formation assay of colon cancer cell lines was performed. We found that the number and size of spheroids in colon cancer cells overexpressing ZIC5 were significantly increased compared to those in control cells (Fig. 4D-G). Knockdown of ZIC5 significantly suppressed the ability of SW480 and HCT116 cells to form spheroids (Fig. 5H, I, J).

In addition, to determine the effect of ZIC5 on epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), several classical EMT markers were detected using western blotting. The immunoblot results showed that E-cadherin expression was significantly decreased and N-cadherin expression was upregulated in SW480 and HCT116 cells overexpressing ZIC5 compared to control cells (Fig. 5K–N). Collectively, ZIC5 facilitates malignant progression by maintaining colon CSC properties and promoting EMT.

ZIC5 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by interaction with β-catenin in colon cancer cells

It is well known that Wnt signaling is tightly associated with tumor proliferation, metastasis, and maintenance of cancer stem cells10,11,27–29. Therefore, we hypothesized that Wnt signaling is most likely involved in ZIC5-mediated malignant phenotypes and CSC properties of colon cancer cells. The TOP/FOP flash reporter assay of colon cancer cells further confirmed our hypothesis. Top/Fop reporter activity of SW480 and HCT116 cells with overexpression of ZIC5 was remarkably enhanced compared to their controls (Fig. 6A, B). Cyclin D1 acts as a downstream target gene of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway. We found that ZIC5 overexpression increased the protein expression of Cyclin D1 in SW480 (Fig. 6C, D) or HCT116 cells (Fig. 6E, F). We also found that ZIC5 overexpression markedly increased the protein expression of β-Catenin in SW480 cells, however, it did not affect the protein expression of β-Catenin in HCT116 cells (Fig. 5C–F). We also examined the effect of ZIC5 overexpression on β-Catenin mRNA expression by RT-qPCR in SW480 and HCT116 cells. The results showed that ZIC5 overexpression upregulated β-Catenin mRNA expression in SW480 cells, yet did not alter β-Catenin mRNA expression in HCT116 cells (Fig. 5G, H). Interestingly, the nuclear translocation of β-Catenin was remarkably increased in SW480 cells overexpressing ZIC5, but it was unchanged in HCT116 cells with ZIC5 overexpression (Fig. 5I–N). Thus, we hypothesized that activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by ZIC5 is not necessarily dependent on nuclear translocation of β-catenin in colon cancer cells. Surprisingly, our CO-IP assays found that ZIC5 interacts with β-catenin both in SW480 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 5O, P). Taken together, we demonstrated that ZIC5 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by interacting with β-catenin.

ZIC5 promotes colon cancer cell malignancy via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway

To determine whether ZIC5 promotes the malignant progression of colon cancer cells via the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we conducted phenotypic and functional experiments using IWR-I-endo (180 nM), a classic Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor. Since ZIC5 does not affect the nuclear translocation of β-catenin of classical Wnt signaling in HCT116, we selected SW480 cell line for subsequent functional and phenotypic experiments. As shown in Fig. 7A, IWR-I-endo reversed the effect of ZIC5 overexpression on elevated levels of β-catenin and CD44 proteins. CCK8 and colony formation assays demonstrated that the addition of IWR-I-endo similarly reversed the promotion of SW480 cell proliferation caused by ZIC5 overexpression (Fig. 7B, C, D). Transwell and spheroid formation assays also showed that IWR-I-endo significantly attenuated the enhancing effects of ZIC5 overexpression on SW480 cell migration, invasion and spheroid formation capacity (Fig. 7E, F, G, H). Considered together, the above results indicate that ZIC5 promotes malignant progression in colorectal cancer through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway.

Discussion

In the study, we first analyzed the expression pattern of ZIC5 in pan-cancer using the TCGA database. So far, this is the first ZIC5 expression analysis in pan-cancer. We found that ZIC5 mRNA expression was elevated in the majority of cancer tissues, while it was almost absent in normal tissues. We then confirmed that ZIC5 expression in colon cancer is upregulated in multiple independent datasets, and that high ZIC5 expression is remarkably associated with unfavorable survival of colon cancer patients. We also demonstrated that ZIC5 expression correlates with clinicopathologic indicators and may serve as an independent predictor of prognosis in colon cancer. Additionally, ZIC5 promoted proliferation, invasion, migration and EMT of colon cancer cells in vitro experiments, and it enhanced colon CSC properties. Mechanistically, ZIC5 drives tumor aggressiveness and stemness via activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by interaction with β-catenin. We provide experimental evidence for ZIC5 as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target in colon cancer.

The aberrant expression of ZIC5 has been reported in a variety of cancers, including prostate cancer30, cervical cancer31, lung cancer32,33, hepatocellular carcinomas34, melanoma35, and colorectal cancer36,37. We found that ZIC5 expression was elevated in colon cancer tissues, and that ZIC5 expression was significantly positively correlated with LNM rate, stage M1 and stage III + IV. These results are consistent with a previous study36 in colorectal cancer. Similar to what was reported by Satow et al.37, we identified ZIC5 as a biomarker for the survival of colon cancer patients.

At present, the vast majority of studies have suggested that ZIC5 plays a pro-oncogenic role in tumorigenesis and progression. We demonstrated that ZIC5 facilitates the proliferation, invasion, migration, and EMT of colon cancer cell lines. During tumor development, the cellular biological process known as EMT is often involved in invasiveness and metastasis38. The loss of E-cadherin promotes metastasis by initiating EMT and increasing invasiveness. In our study, overexpression of ZIC5 in colon cancer cells significantly decreased E-cadherin expression. A study in melanoma pointed out that ZIC5, as a transcription factor, directly binds to the promoter region of CDH1 to repress the expression of E-cadherin35. However, whether ZIC5 downregulates E-cadherin expression in colon cancer cells in the same way remains to be further determined.

Wnt signaling plays an important role in the maintenance of cancer stem cells and malignant progression of cancer11,12. We found for the first time that ZIC5 enhances the maintenance of colon cancer stem cells. In our study, we also found that the mechanism by which ZIC5 activates Wnt signaling may not be exactly the same in colon cancer cell lines. In SW480, on the one hand, ZIC5 upregulates the expression of β-catenin and promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin. On the other hand, ZIC5 acts as a transcriptional cofactor and interacts with β-catenin to activate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. However, in HCT116, ZIC5 did not affect the mRNA and protein expression of β-catenin as well as the nuclear translocation of β-catenin, which might be due to the in-frame deletion mutation (Ser45) in the gene encoding β-catenin20. ZIC5 may enhance Wnt signaling only by interacting with β-catenin in HCT116 cells.

So far, this study is a more comprehensive report on ZIC5 in colon cancer, but there were still some flaws. First, we only conducted in vitro experiments for verification and lacked in vivo animal experiments. Second, whether ZIC5 directly binds to β-catenin and where it binds need to be further determined. In addition, animal rescue experiments regarding phenotype and mechanism will also be performed in the next step.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our results show that ZIC5 is elevated in colon cancer tissues, and high ZIC5 expression associates with a worse survival of colon cancer patients. We identified ZIC5 as an independent prognostic indicator for OS of colon cancer patients. We demonstrated that ZIC5 promoted proliferation, invasion, migration, CSC properties and EMT of colon cancer cells. ZIC5 functions in colon cancer cells by interacting with β-catenin to enhance Wnt/β-catenin signaling. The above results indicate that inhibition of ZIC5 might be a potential therapeutic approach for colon cancer patients.
