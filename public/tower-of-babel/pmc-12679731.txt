Tissue regenerative potential of human dental pulp mesenchymal stem cell conditioned medium in wound healing of the medicinal leech
Authors: Gaia Marcolli, Ludovica Barone, Nicolò Baranzini, Federica Rossi, Andrea Massimiliano Nebuloni, Laura Pulze, Sobia Sadia, Piero Antonio Zecca, Christina Pagiatakis, Roberto Papait, Annalisa Grimaldi, Rosalba Gornati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs life expectancy increases, so does the incidence of age-related disorders, including vascular diseases and chronic wounds. These conditions are often associated with impaired angiogenesis and tissue repair, posing significant clinical challenges due to prolonged healing times. Recent studies have highlighted the tissue regenerative potential of the human Mesenchymal Stem Cell-Conditioned Medium (MSC-CM). Building on this, we investigated the ability of CM derived from human Dental Pulp Stem Cells (hDPSC-CM) to support angiogenesis and tissue repair in a surgically injured medicinal leech model. The medicinal leech Hirudo verbana offers a valuable in vivo tool for studying wound healing and regeneration, due to its well-characterized anatomy, regenerative capabilities, and the evolutionary conservation of key immune and vascular pathways.MethodsAdult leeches were surgically injured and treated with either FBS-free medium (CTRL) or hDPSC-CM. Animals were sacrificed at 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week post-treatment to evaluate tissue regeneration across experimental groups. Morphological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses were conducted.ResultshDPSC-CM significantly accelerated wound healing, promoting extracellular matrix remodelling and muscle regeneration. The expression of key markers acid phosphatase, CD31, VEGF, Desmin, AIF-1, and COL Iα1, along with vessel counts, confirmed the immunomodulatory activity (38.5% overall decrease in macrophage number), enhanced angiogenesis (64.2% overall increase in CD31 expression), and improved muscle regeneration (222.5% overall increase in Desmin expression) induced by hDPSC-CM in the injured area.ConclusionsOur findings support the therapeutic potential of hDPSC-CM as a novel, cell-free strategy for enhancing wound healing and managing chronic conditions related to impaired tissue repair.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04683-x.

Background

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a highly dynamic three-dimensional network, mainly consisting of proteins secreted by resident cells, that plays a fundamental role in regulating essential biological processes including angiogenesis, tissue repair, and cellular differentiation [1, 2]. Upon injury, stromal cells can establish a unique microenvironment that remodels the surrounding ECM, thus facilitating the processes essential to support healing and regeneration [3]. ECM remodelling is central for successful tissue repair, as it provides the appropriate 3D structure and biochemical cues that guide cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation.

Macrophages, as first responders to injury, play a key role in tissue repair by releasing a diverse array of chemokines, cytokines, proteins, and growth factors that orchestrate inflammation and promote its resolution, thereby facilitating the transition from the inflammatory to the regenerative phase [4–6]. Moreover, macrophages contribute to tissue regeneration by clearing cellular debris, stimulating both fibroblast and endothelial cell proliferation, thus facilitating new tissue formation. Lastly, they also play a crucial role in ECM remodelling and in the behaviour of Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs), enhancing their regenerative potential [7, 8].

MSCs, a specialized subset of stromal cells found in tissues like bone marrow, adipose tissue, dental pulp, and umbilical cord, have an essential role in tissue regeneration due to their ability to differentiate into various cell types [9]. These cells are the central players of homeostasis maintenance and tissue repair process producing ECM components and secreting cytokines, growth factors, and proteases that help ECM arrangement, facilitating the transition from the inflammatory to the regenerative phase during wound healing.

The regenerative potential of MSCs is largely attributed to their paracrine signalling mediated by a variety of bioactive molecules that not only participate in ECM remodelling, crucial for tissue regeneration [10–12], but also promote angiogenesis, reduce inflammation, and support cellular differentiation. However, the clinical application of MSCs is often limited by numerous challenges, including ethical concerns and practical issues. To overcome these barriers, the MSC secretome, herein referred to as conditioned medium (CM), has emerged as a promising alternative. Indeed, MSC-CM contains many of the same bioactive molecules involved in ECM remodelling and regeneration, thus providing an effective cell-free approach that can promote tissue repair without the complications associated with cell transplantation.

Among the numerous MSC populations, human Dental Pulp Stem Cells (hDPSCs) have garnered significant interest due to their high proliferative capacity and stemness properties. Additionally, they can be easily sourced from the removal of impacted wisdom teeth, which are typically discarded as surgical waste. These characteristics, alongside their ability to influence ECM remodelling and promote tissue regeneration, make hDPSCs an attractive source for regenerative therapies [13, 14].

In the same way as MSCs, even hDPSC-CM has been shown to enhance ECM remodelling, accelerate angiogenesis, and support muscle regeneration, which are all essential processes for effective tissue repair [15, 16].

Starting from these premises, this study aims to explore the impact of hDPSC-CM on ECM remodelling that in turn guides angiogenesis, inflammation, and muscle regeneration, by using the medicinal leech Hirudo verbana as injury model, thereby offering a promising therapeutic strategy in regenerative medicine. The medicinal leech is indeed a valuable and well-consolidated model for studying wound healing and immune responses, as its small size and distinct anatomy make it easy to monitor reactions to injuries or infections and to identify the cell types involved. In addition, unlike murine models, it allows in vivo observation of regenerative and immune processes with reduced biological complexity, lower maintenance costs, and without interference from adaptive immunity [17–20]. Following damage, connective tissue reorganization is regulated by diffusible factors like Endothelial Growth Factor (EGF), basic Fibroblast Growth Factor (bFGF), and cathepsin B, produced by fibroblasts and immune cells. These molecules stimulate fibroblast activity and collagen production. In addition, the leech’s immune and regenerative responses closely resemble those of vertebrates, relying on similar molecular pathways and cellular effectors [8, 21, 22].

However, the mechanisms by which hDPSC-CM promotes tissue repair in vivo remains unclear, particularly in non-mammalian models that offer simplified yet informative biological systems. To address this, we evaluated the pro-regenerative effects of hDPSC-CM in a surgically injured medicinal leech model, with a focus on its role in angiogenesis and tissue repair.

This study contributes to establishing the potential of hDPSC-CM as a cell-free therapeutic strategy for tissue regeneration in clinical contexts.

Materials and methods

Ethics and consent

hDPSCs were isolated from dental pulp of 4 healthy subjects (two males and two females, 12–15 years old), undergoing third molar extraction. The subjects provided their informed consent, to be included in the study and were non-smokers, had no history of metabolic disorders, and were not taking medications at the time of the surgical procedure. The study was approved by the institutional review board ethics committees. Subjects were recruited within a clinical protocol by “Ospedale di Circolo Fondazione Macchi” and approved by the institutional Ethical Committee (Multicenter studies on the survival and prosthetic success of dental implants: influence of the implant-abutment connection. Protocol number: 0034066, approval date: 3-10-2013) according to the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013.

In vivo studies have been conducted using medicinal leeches of the species H. verbana, which are not protected under Directive 2010/63/EU governing the use of animals in scientific research, nor are they listed in Legislative Decree no. 26, of 4 March 2014, “Implementing Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes”, published in the Italian Official Journal on 14 March 2014. However, their use has been authorized by the Animal Welfare Body (OPBA), University of Insubria.

Preparation of conditioned media and protein amount evaluation

hDPSC-CM was prepared slightly modifying the protocol previously described [23]. Briefly, the cells were cultured until the 10th passage, once they reached 80% confluence, media were removed, and cells were washed twice with Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) solution before adding foetal bovine serum (FBS)-free Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium F12 (DMEM/DMEM F12). After 72 h of starvation, the media were collected and centrifuged at 1000xg for 10 min, to remove cell debris. The collected CM was then concentrated using the Amicon Ultra 15 mL Centrifugal filter device (Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) with a 3 kDa cut-off, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentrated CM was collected and quantified in term of total protein content using NanoOrange® Protein Quantitation Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, Milano, Italy). Aliquots of 50 µg of total proteins were then stored at -80 °C until use.

Experimental design

In vivo experiments were conducted in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0

Adult leeches of the species H. verbana (Annelida, Hirudinea), kindly donated by ILFARM S.r.l. (Varese, Italy), were maintained in oxygenated tanks filled with lightly salted water (NaCl 1.5 g/L), at 20 °C. In the experiments, three animals were randomly assigned to each of the five groups using a computer-generated randomization list (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA). Each animal was assigned a unique identifier, and allocation to groups was performed by matching these identifiers to the randomization list to ensure unbiased and equal distribution across groups.

Before injury induction, leeches were anesthetized by immersion in a 10% ethanol solution in freshwater until complete cessation of movement and absence of response to tactile stimuli. Three longitudinal wounds, measuring 5 mm in length and affecting the entire thickness of the body wall (cuticle, epithelium, and the three muscle layers), were performed on the dorsal portion of all leeches using a razor blade. Either FBS-free DMEM/DMEM F12 or hDPSC-CM were inoculated laterally to the site of injury, using an insulin micro syringe. To assess the differences in tissue regeneration among the experimental groups, animals were sacrificed 48 h, 72 h, and 1 week after the treatment. Prior to tissue dissection, euthanasia was performed by prolonged immersion in a 10% ethanol solution in freshwater to ensure complete and irreversible cessation of vital functions.

Light and transmission electron microscopy

Leech tissues were dissected from the injection sites and fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde, diluted in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), overnight at 4 °C. Samples were then rinsed in the same buffer and post-fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) solution for 1 h. After a rapid dehydration with a serial ethanol scale (50%, 70%, 90% and uranyl acetate, 100%), the specimens were immersed in a solution of propylene oxide for 1 h, then embedded in an Epon-Araldite 812 mixture epoxy resin (Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy). Semithin Sect. (700 nm thick) for light microscopy were obtained using a Reichert Ultracut S ultratome (Leica, Wein, Austria), collected on a slide, and subsequently stained with crystal violet (1 g in 100 mL of distilled water) and basic fuchsine (0.13 g in 100 mL of distilled water). Samples were finally examined under a light microscope, and images captured using a DS-5 M-L1 digital camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

From the same samples, ultrathin sections (70 nm thick) were collected on copper grids (300 mesh, Sigma-Aldrich, Milan, Italy), counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed using a JEOL1400Plus transmission electron microscope (Centro di Ricerca e Trasferimento Tecnologico – CRIETT, University of Insubria). Data were recorded with a MORADA digital camera system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Masson trichrome staining

Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, then rinsed in PBS (138 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 4.3 mM Na2HPO4 – pH 7.4). Tissues were dehydrated with increasing ethanol concentrations (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 96%, 100%), immersed in pure xylol for 30 min, and paraffin embedded. 7 μm-thick sections, obtained with a rotary microtome (Jung multicut 2045, Leica, Wein, Austria), were processed for Masson Trichrome staining (Masson Trichrome kit, Bio Optica, Milan, Italy) as suggested by the datasheet. This technique highlights collagen and reticular fibres in blue, while the cytoplasm of cells in red. Images were observed under a light microscope (Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and captured with a DS-5 M-L1 digital camera (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Acid phosphatase (APC) assay

Wounded portions of both CTRL and hDPSC-CM-treated leeches were dissected and instantly included in OCT (Tissue-Tek OCT compound, Sakura Finetek, CA, USA), then gradually frozen in dry ice and liquid nitrogen, and finally stored at − 80 °C. Cryosections (7 μm), obtained with a cryostat (CM1850, Leica, Wein, Austria), were collected on gelatinised slides, and stored at − 20 °C.

As previously reported [6], cryosections were rehydrated in PBS, then incubated with 0.1 M acetic acid-sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.2) for 5 min. Subsequently, samples were immersed in the reaction mixture (sodium acetate-acetic acid buffer 0.1 M, 0.01% naphthol phosphate, 2% N, N-dimethylformamide, 0.06% Fast Red, and MnCl2 0.5 mM) for 90 min at 37 °C. Slides were washed twice with PBS, mounted with Citifluor (Citifluor Ltd., London, UK), and finally observed under a light microscope (Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence assays

All steps were performed at room temperature. Cryosections, obtained as described above, were rehydrated in PBS and pre-incubated in Bovine Serum Albumin blocking solution (2% BSA, 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS), which was also used to dilute both primary and secondary antibodies. Samples were then incubated for 90 min with the following primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal α-human Cluster of Differentiation 31 – CD31 (dilution 1:200, Novocastra – Nussloch, Germany), known to react with leech endothelial cells [5, 24, 25], mouse monoclonal α-human Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor –-VEGF (dilution 1:100, ThermoFisher Scientific – Waltham, MA, USA), recognizing leech VEGF [26], mouse monoclonal α-human Desmin (dilution 1:100, Abcam – Cambridge, UK), which highlights leech striated muscle fibres [17], and rabbit polyclonal α-Hirudo medicinalis Allograft Inflammatory Factor – HmAIF1 (dilution 1:150, kindly donated by Professor Vizioli – University of Lille, France), recognizing leech macrophage-like cells [27]. After several washes in PBS, cryosections were incubated for 60 min with suitable secondary antibodies, conjugated with either fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA – dilution 1:200) or Cyanine3 (CY3, ThermoFisher Scientific – dilution 1:500). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylinedole (DAPI, 0.1 mg/mL in PBS), and the slides mounted with Citifluor (Citifluor Ltd. Hatfield, PA, USA). Experimental negative controls were performed by omitting primary antibodies, and incubating sections with secondary antibodies only.

All the samples were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Eclipse, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), equipped with the emission filters 490/525 nm for FITC, 550/580 nm for CY3, and 340/488 nm for DAPI signals. Images were recorded with a DS-5 M-L1 digital camera (Nikon) and combined using Adobe Photoshop (Adobe System, San Jose, CA, USA). Immunofluorescence results were analysed using the ImageJ software package (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html).

Western blot analyses

Leech tissues were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and powdered with a mortar. Homogenates (10 µL per milligram of tissue) were resuspended in RIPA buffer (50 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM of Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.1% protease/phosphatase inhibitors cocktail). Cellular lysates were centrifuged at 13,200 xg for 20 min at 4 °C, and protein concentration quantified with the Bradford assay (Serva, Heidelberg, Germany). A total of 50 µg of protein extracts were loaded on either 8% or 12% SDS-PAGE gel, and the separated proteins then transferred onto 0.45 μm pore size nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham Protran Premium, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat dried milk in Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS), then incubated for 2 h with rabbit polyclonal α-human collagen – COL I-α1 [20, 28] (dilution 1:500, Sigma-Aldrich – Milan, Italy) and rabbit polyclonal α-HmAIF1 (dilution 1:150) primary antibodies, both diluted in TBS/5% milk. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, diluted 1:5,000) was used as housekeeping protein to normalize protein expression levels.

Following washes in TBS-T (Tris-Buffered Saline containing 0.1% Tween-20), the membranes were incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase conjugated anti-rabbit (dilution 1:7,000, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA) secondary antibody. As final step, the membranes were developed with the enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (LiteAblot PLUS, EuroClone, Milan, Italy) and signals captured with the Odyssey Fc (LI-COR).

Densitometric analyses of the resulting images was performed using ImageJ software. Data are reported as the relative optical density of the bands, with values normalized to GAPDH (see Supplementary Data, Fig. 2S).

Statistical analyses

Experiments were performed in triplicate and data were reported as mean values and statistical evaluations were performed using Graph Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com/features). Both the normality and the homoscedasticity of the samples were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and the Levene tests. Significant differences were then determined by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, in which a p-values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and bars represent mean ± standard deviations (SD).

Results

Morphological analysis of the injured leech treated or not with hDPSC-CM

The effects of hDPSC-CM on healing times and tissue regeneration in wounded leeches were assessed using crystal violet and basic fuchsine staining via light microscopy (Fig. 1A-F). At 48 h post-injury (Fig. 1A, B), a compact pseudoblastema was visible at the wound edges, with an abundant loose connective tissue (ECM) filling the injured area beneath it. It consisted of a thick layer of vasocentral cells detaching from the vasofibrous tissue. This tissue, migrating toward the injured area and crossing the muscle layers surrounding the injury, as well as the ECM, was observed in both experimental groups. In addition to vasofibrous tissue, botryoidal tissue was also visible, embedded within the abundant ECM. As previously shown, botryoidal tissue is a hemopoietic tissue that not only generates new vessels but also recruits immune and myoendothelial cells involved in muscle regeneration. Notably, in leeches treated with hDPSC-CM, the botryoidal tissue shifted from cluster-like or cord-like formations into a hollow, tubular structure, characteristic of pre-vascular tissues, leading to the formation of new capillary vessels. Indeed, compared to the CTRL group, which displayed only few muscle fibres within the loose ECM, the hDPSC-CM-treated leeches exhibited a faster healing process, with already differentiated muscle fibres populating the healing region beneath the pseudoblastema (Fig. 1B). By 72 h and 1 week, the differences between the experimental groups became even more pronounced. The hDPSC-CM-treated animals exhibited significantly improved wound closure and tissue regeneration, characterized by organized muscle groups integrated into a well-structured ECM (Fig. 1D, F). In contrast, CTRL samples showed delayed muscle regeneration, with a more disorganized ECM and poorly coordinated muscle fibre rearrangement (Fig. 1C, E).

Evaluation of connective tissue remodelling and muscle regeneration

Masson Trichrome staining was used to better evaluate both ECM remodelling and muscle regeneration stimulated by hDPSC-CM (Fig. 2A-F). In CTRL groups, the ECM appeared loose and disorganized, associated to a prominent pseudoblastema and only a few, poorly developed muscle fibres, suggesting a limited regenerative activity (Fig. 2A, C, E). Conversely, hDPSC-CM-treated leeches exhibited a remarkable rearrangement of the collagen-based connective tissue, resulting in a more compact and structured scaffold (Fig. 2B, D, F). Notably, already as early as 72 h post-injury, hDPSC-CM-treated animals displayed multiple well-formed muscle groups in the proximity of the wound site (Fig. 2D). After 1 week, the regeneration process in hDPSC-CM-treated samples was almost complete, with numerous organized and well-defined bundles of muscle fibres present throughout the wound area (Fig. 2F). This was in stark contrast to what was observed in the CTRL groups, which still exhibited a disorganized ECM and sparse muscle fibres (Fig. 2C, E).

Western blot analyses of type I collagen (COL I1) further supported these findings (Fig. 2G). CTRL samples showed higher COL I1 protein expression levels at all timeframes compared to hDPSC-CM-treated leeches. Both experimental groups exhibited a similar trend, with protein levels peaking at 48 h and gradually decreasing over time, reaching minimal levels at 1 week post-injury.

Characterization of innate immune cells recruited in the wounded area and involved in tissue regeneration

The effects of hDPSC-CM in modulating macrophage-like activity were first assessed using the histoenzymatic ACP assay, which specifically highlights lysosomal enzymes (Fig. 3A-F). Compared to hDPSC-CM-injected leeches (Fig. 3B, D, F), in CTRL samples (Fig. 3A, C, E) ACP staining revealed a significant macrophage-like cell infiltration into damaged tissues, particularly around the pseudoblastema and beneath the epithelium, at all considered timeframes.

Quantitative analyses of ACP+ cells (Fig. 3G) showed a significantly lower number of macrophage-like cells in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches at both 48 h and 72 h post-injection compared to CTRL group, highlighting a more controlled and attenuated inflammatory response. Notably, at 1 week post-injection, the number of ACP+ cells in hDPSC-CM-treated groups were reduced in the CTRL group but not in the hDPSC-CM-treated leeches.

To further validate these results, immunofluorescence assays were performed using a specific -HmAIF-1 antibody. Only a few HmAIF-1+ macrophage-like cells were detectable in injured hDPSC-CM-injected leeches (Fig. 3B, D, F, H), when compared to CTRL animals (Fig. 3A, C, E, G). In particular, a marked reduction in the number of HmAIF-1+ cells was observed at 48 h and 72 h post-injury in hDPSC-CM-treated samples compared to CTRL. A slight re-increase in HmAIF‑1⁺ cells was detected after 1week in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches. This rise mirrored the trends observed in the enzymatic histochemistry analysis.

Western blot analyses confirmed the trend observed in immunofluorescence assays. Data demonstrate a statistically significant difference between the two experimental groups, with hDPSC-CM-treated animals exhibiting a profound reduction of HmAIF-1 protein expression at each timeframe, as indicated by optical density measurements (Fig. 3J).

Evaluation of hDPSC-CM pro-angiogenic effects and its role in the muscle regeneration process

To investigate the pro-angiogenic properties of hDPSC-CM, immunofluorescence assays were performed using a specific -CD31 antibody, a well-known leech endothelial cell marker. The results revealed a progressive increase in signal starting at 48 h in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches (Fig. 4B), with a pronounced peak at 72 h (Fig. 4D). Overall, animals injected with hDPSC-CM exhibited significantly higher levels of CD31 expression (Fig. 4B, D, F) compared to CTRL samples (Fig. 4A, C, E), at all timeframes. As expected, CD31 expression declined in both experimental groups after 1 week. This trend was further confirmed by the fluorescence intensity graph, which showed a peak at 72 h post-injection in the hDPSC-CM-treated group, followed by a reduction in signal after 1 week (Fig. 4G).

Discussion

Recent advancements in regenerative medicine have highlighted the potential of stem cell-derived CM as a powerful tool for promoting tissue repair. This study focuses on evaluating the regenerative properties of hDPSC-CM in a muscle injury model using leeches as an alternative in vivo system. Due to their remarkable regenerative abilities, leeches serve as a valuable model for studying complex tissue repair mechanisms and testing novel therapeutic approaches. Based on our experience, hDPSCs demonstrated higher proliferation rate compared to human Adipose-derived Stem Cells (hASCs), indicating a stronger capacity for in vitro expansion. Additionally, hDPSC-CM contains higher levels of cytokines such as VEGFA, TGF-β, and HIF-1α involved in endothelial (VEGFA) and fibroblast activation (TGF-β) [15, 16, 29]. Moreover, some studies have reported that human bone marrow MSC-CM is comparable to that of hDPSC in supporting angiogenesis in vitro. However, its use is limited by the low frequency of stem cells in bone marrow (0.001% to 0.01%), its difficult collection, and the complexity of processing [30].

Light microscopy analyses revealed that CTRL leeches showed only a few muscle fibres embedded in a poorly organized ECM, which is consistent with previous reports highlighting slow and inefficient regenerative responses in the absence of exogenous factors [31, 32]. In both CTRL and hDPSC-CM-treated groups, crystal violet and basic fuchsine staining demonstrated the formation of a pseudoblastema, an essential structure for wound contraction and repair [33–35] within 72 h post-injury.

However, as shown by Masson Trichrome staining, muscle fibre organization, located beneath the pseudoblastema, progressed more rapidly in hDPSC-CM-treated animals, suggesting that hDPSC-CM accelerates the transition from the inflammatory to the regenerative phase, which is crucial for optimal tissue regeneration [36–38]. After 1 week, hDPSC-CM-treated leeches exhibited well-organized muscle fibres integrated into a more structured ECM, supporting the idea that the soluble factors within the CM promote a more coordinated tissue repair process.

Ultrastructural investigations further demonstrated that hDPSC-CM-treated animals exhibited a more compact and well-structured ECM, with multiple well-formed muscle groups near the wound site. In contrast, the CTRL group displayed a loose and disorganized ECM with only a small number of developing muscle fibres. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the importance of ECM reorganization in tissue regeneration, as it provides structural support for cell migration and differentiation [22, 39, 40]. Western blot analyses of COL Iα1 confirmed that expression was reduced in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches, suggesting that soluble factors within the CM contribute to more efficient ECM remodelling by promoting organized collagen deposition [41].

Another crucial aspect of the healing process is the regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly those secreted by macrophage-like cells. The presence of key factors in the CM may accelerate wound healing and reduce excessive scar tissue formation [42]. The role of macrophages in tissue repair is well-documented, with strong evidence supporting their involvement in the early stages of regeneration. Macrophages are essential for clearing cellular debris, promoting ECM remodelling angiogenesis, and secreting cytokines and growth factors that orchestrate the repair process [6, 27, 43–48].

Therefore, in this study, we also explored the impact of hDPSC-CM on the inflammatory response, particularly focusing on macrophage-like cell infiltration within the injured tissue. Histoenzymatic ACP assay revealed significant differences between the experimental groups, with hDPSC-CM-treated leeches displaying a more controlled inflammatory response at 48 h and 72 h post-injury, characterized by a significantly reduced presence of macrophage-like cells compared to the CTRL group. This finding suggests that hDPSC-CM exerts an immunomodulatory effect, likely facilitating faster inflammation resolution, which is crucial for tissue repair; this observation aligns with previous studies [36, 37, 49]. At 1 week post-injury, the number of macrophage-like cells was similar in both groups, indicating that inflammation had been largely resolved, and both groups had entered the tissue regeneration phase. Like what has been observed in vertebrates [48, 50, 51], macrophage-like cells in leeches are involved both in the initial immune defence, by clearing pathogens and cellular debris, and in later stages of tissue repair, through the release of cytokines and enzymes such as HmAIF-1, a key macrophage-like cell-derived cytokine involved in the coordination of inflammation and tissue regeneration [43]. Although distinct pro-inflammatory (M1-like) and anti-inflammatory/pro-regenerative (M2-like) macrophage phenotypes have not yet been clearly defined in leeches, such polarization states could exist, as suggested by evidence in other invertebrates [52], including molluscs. This potential functional shift might underlie the dual role of macrophage-like cells in both the inflammatory and reparative phases of regeneration. Notably, we hypothesize that the increased number of macrophage-like cells observed at 1 week post-injury in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches may be due to the recruitment of anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative macrophages, contributing to an optimal tissue repair environment [53].

In addition to modulating the inflammatory response, hDPSC-CM also promotes angiogenesis, a vital process for ensuring adequate nutrient and oxygen supply to regenerating tissues [15, 16]. Immunofluorescence analyses of CD31, a marker for endothelial cells, revealed significant vessel formation in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches compared to CTRL ones. CD31 expression peaked at 72 h post-injury, indicating active angiogenesis, and this finding was further supported by immunofluorescence analyses of VEGF [26]. Indeed, VEGF is a key angiogenic signalling molecule, that plays a role in recruiting myoendothelial cells, vessel-associated precursor cells responsible for leech muscle regeneration during wound healing. The following significant decline at 1 week of both factors clearly suggested that hDPSC-CM promotes the early stages of angiogenesis and contributes to the transition towards vessel stabilization, where newly formed vessels mature and integrate into the tissue [54]. These findings align with previous studies showing that stem cell-derived factors enhance vascularization and support tissue repair [11, 15, 55].

Finally, the potential of hDPSC-CM to enhance muscle tissue regeneration was reinforced by the immunofluorescence analyses of Desmin, a key protein involved in maintaining and stabilizing the structural integrity of muscle fibres, and one of the earliest myogenic markers expressed during muscle development. As expected, results showed a significantly higher level of Desmin in hDPSC-CM-treated leeches at 1 week post-injury compared to the CTRL, indicating that hDPSC-CM, besides promoting muscle fibre regeneration and differentiation, facilitates their alignment for contractile function [56].

In summary, our results demonstrate that hDPSC-CM enhances ECM remodelling and promotes angiogenesis, thereby accelerating muscle restoration in surgically injured leeches. Although leeches offer a cost-effective and ethically favourable in vivo platform, thanks to their conserved tissue repair mechanisms such as macrophage-like cells activation, ECM remodelling, and angiogenesis, they differ substantially from mammals in immune complexity and tissue architecture, which limits the direct translatability of our findings. To bridge the translational gap, future investigations should aim to assess some specific bioactive components of CM in rodent models.

Our findings also suggest that hDPSC-CM may represent a promising therapeutic approach to support wound healing and promote tissue regeneration in specific clinical contexts, including skin wounds caused by burns, frostbite, surgical procedures, radiation, and chronic skin ulcers. Furthermore, being a cell-free treatment, the use of hDPSC-CM is suitable for both autologous and allogeneic applications, even though, further studies exploring the translation of into clinical therapies in regenerative medicine will be crucial.

Conclusions

The leech, with its simple body organization and remarkable regenerative abilities, represents a promising experimental model for studying wound healing and tissue regeneration. The results presented here suggest that hDPSC-CM plays a multifaceted role in enhancing ECM remodelling, modulating inflammation, promoting angiogenesis, and accelerating muscle regeneration, supporting its superior therapeutic potential over hASC-CM in vascular and tissue repair applications.

Although further investigations are needed to confirm the efficacy of specific bioactive components of the conditioned medium in rodent models, these findings highlight the clinical potential of hDPSC-CM as an effective cell-free therapeutic strategy for enhancing tissue regeneration and wound healing across a broad range of clinical applications.
