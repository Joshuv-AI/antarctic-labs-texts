Free mutual information and higher-point OTOCs
Shreya Vardhan, Jinzhao Wang

Abstract
We introduce a quantity called the free mutual information (FMI), adapted from concepts in free probability theory, as a new physical measure of quantum chaos. This quantity captures the spreading of a time-evolved operator in the space of all possible operators on the Hilbert space, which is doubly exponential in the number of degrees of freedom. It thus provides a finer notion of operator spreading than the well-understood phenomenon of operator growth in physical space. We derive two central results which apply in any physical system: first, an explicit “Coulomb gas” formula for the FMI of two observables A ⁡ ( t ) A(t) and B B in terms of the eigenvalues of the product operator A ⁡ ( t ) ​ B A(t)B ; and second, a general relation expressing the FMI as a weighted sum of all higher-point out-of-time-ordered correlators (OTOCs). This second result provides a precise information-theoretic interpretation for the higher-point OTOCs as collectively quantifying operator ergodicity and the approach to freeness. This physical interpretation is particularly useful in light of recent progress in experimentally measuring higher-point OTOCs. We identify universal behaviours of the FMI and higher-point OTOCs across a variety of chaotic systems, including random unitary circuits and chaotic spin chains, which indicate that spreading in the doubly exponential operator space is a generic feature of quantum many-body chaos. At the same time, the non-generic behavior of the FMI in various non-chaotic systems, including certain unitary designs, shows that there are cases where an operator spreads in physical space but remains localized in operator space. The FMI is thus a sharper diagnostic of chaos than the standard 4-point OTOC.

Introduction and summary of results

Ergodicity is a universal feature of classically chaotic systems. Consider an initially localized distribution in the classical phase space of a particle. If the particle is governed by a chaotic evolution, this localized distribution spreads out over a large portion of the phase space at late times. More precisely, while the exact phase space volume of the distribution remains constant, a coarse-grained version of the volume grows with time. In contrast, in integrable systems, the coarse-grained distribution continues to be localized even at late times. As a concrete example, one could have in mind the standard map
[
1
,
2
]
in its chaotic and integrable regimes.

The idea of spreading of phase space distributions from classical chaos does not generalize in a straightforward way to quantum many-body chaos. In particular, there is no canonical and universal way to define the analog of phase space distributions in general quantum many-body systems. A more useful way of characterizing quantum many-body chaos, which has developed over the last decade, is in terms of universal properties of the Heisenberg evolution of operators.

operator size

Free mutual information

In this section, we will present a more formal version of the definition of the free mutual information which was summarized in the introduction. We will start by explicitly defining the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
, and volumes of subsets of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
.

Consider an initial Hermitian operator
A
in a quantum many-body system of Hilbert space dimension
d
, and its time-evolution
A(t)=U(t)^{\dagger}AU(t)
under the evolution operator
U(t)
of the system. For example, for an energy-conserving system,
U(t)=e^{-iHt}
for some Hamiltonian
H
. We are interested in characterizing the spreading of
A(t)
with time in the set
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
of all possible operators that can be obtained from
A
by any unitary evolution:

{\mathcal{S}}_{A}=\{UAU^{\dagger}~|~U\in\mathbf{U}(d)\}

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{U}(d)
is the group of
d
-dimensional unitary matrices.
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
can be viewed as a manifold which is diffeomorphic to
\mathbf{U}(d)/{\rm Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)
, where
\text{Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)
is the stabilizer of
A
, or the subgroup of
\mathbf{U}(d)
which commutes with
A
. Explicitly, we have the following bijection between the two manifolds:

\displaystyle\phi_{A}:\mathbf{U}(d)/{\rm Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)

\displaystyle\to{\mathcal{S}}_{A}

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\mapsto UAU^{\dagger}\ \,.

(2.2)

Let us first consider the dimension
\gamma_{A}
of the manifold
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
. The manifold
\mathbf{U}(d)
has dimension
d^{2}
. The subgroup
\text{Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)
depends on the degeneracies in the spectrum of
A
. For example, if the spectrum is non-degenerate, then the stabilizer group consists of diagonal unitary matrices in the eigenbasis of
A
, i.e.
\text{Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)=\mathbf{U}(1)^{d}
. Hence, for a non-degenerate
A
,
\gamma_{A}
is
d^{2}-d
. More generally, if

\text{Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)=\mathbf{U}(g_{1})\times\mathbf{U}(g_{2})\times\cdots\times\mathbf{U}(g_{k})

{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
, it will be useful to introduce a volume measure on
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
. The measure we will use throughout this paper is the one induced by the Haar measure
{\rm d}\nu_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(U)
on
\mathbf{U}(d)

{\rm d}U

\mathbf{U}(d)
which has the property of left- and right- unitary invariance:
{\rm d}U={\rm d}(UV)={\rm d}(VU)
for any
V\in\mathbf{U}(d)
. Since we will mostly be interested in ratios of volumes below, the normalization of

can be chosen arbitrarily. To use the Haar measure to define volumes in
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
, we will use the quotient map from
\mathbf{U}(d)
onto the coset
\mathbf{U}(d)/\text{Stab}_{\mathbf{U}(d)}(A)
, denoted by
q
.
q^{-1}(U)
denotes the preimage of

under this map. Then the induced volume measure
\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}_{A}}
is the pushforward of the Haar measure through

\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}_{A}}\equiv\nu_{\mathbf{U}(d)}\circ q^{-1}\circ\phi_{A}^{-1}

(2.3)

The integral of a function

on

is thus given by

\int_{{\mathcal{S}}_{A}}f(X){\rm d}\nu_{{\mathcal{S}}_{A}}(X)=\int_{\mathbf{U}(d)}f(UAU^{\dagger})\,{\rm d}U\ .

(2.4)

The total volume
\text{Vol}({\mathcal{S}}_{A})
is given by the above integral for

, and the volume of some subset
{\mathcal{S}}
of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
is given by taking

to be the indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{{\mathcal{S}}}(X)
, which evaluates to 1 for

and zero otherwise.

A more explicit way to visualize the size of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
is to discretize it, by constructing a finite “epsilon-net” of unitaries

such that any element of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
is at most

{\mathcal{S}}_{A,\epsilon}
according to some chosen metric. The number of elements in

(2.5)

for some

. Recall that

is
O(d^{2})
– hence,

is

We would now like to formalize questions such as: what fraction of the volume in
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
does
A(t)
occupy, or what fraction of the elements of the
\epsilon

{\mathcal{S}}_{A,\epsilon}

close to? As stated, the answer to the question is trivial: since
A(t)
is a single fixed operator, it occupies zero volume in
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
, and is
\epsilon

O(1)
number of elements of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A,\epsilon}
. To come up with a meaningful version of the question, we need to provide a prescription for replacing
A(t)
with some larger set of operators
{\mathcal{S}}_{A(t),{\rm coarse}}\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
, whose volume can grow with time.

As discussed in the introduction, we consider a coarse-graining procedure in terms of correlation functions of
A(t)
with some simple reference operator
B
. We define the coarse-grained set of
A(t)
conditioned on
\rho
and

S_{A(t)|B,\rho,\delta,N}

\tilde{A}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
such that

|\text{Tr}[\rho{\tilde{A}}^{m_{1}}B^{n_{1}}{\tilde{A}}^{m_{2}}B^{n_{2}}...{\tilde{A}}^{m_{r}}B^{n_{r}}]-\text{Tr}[\rho{A(t)}^{m_{1}}B^{n_{1}}A(t)^{m_{2}}B^{n_{2}}...A(t)^{m_{r}}B^{n_{r}}]|<\delta

(2.6)

for all possible sequences of positive integers
m_{i}
,
n_{i}
for all lengths

for some maximum length
N
.
In the rest of this paper, we will set

to be the maximally mixed state or infinite temperature density matrix,
\rho=\frac{\mathbf{1}}{d}
, and drop the
\rho
label, referring to the above set simply as

. We restrict to this choice of

4

4

In more general physical setups, especially in continuum systems such as quantum field theories, considering other choices of

We can also define a discretization
{\mathcal{S}}_{A(t)|B,\delta,N,\epsilon}
of

, which is a subset of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A,\epsilon}
constrained by the same condition as in (

With the above definition of

, we can now characterize the spreading of

in the space of all operators by the ratio

f_{A(t)|B,\delta,N}\equiv\frac{\text{Vol}({\mathcal{S}}_{A(t)|B,\delta,N})}{\text{Vol}({\mathcal{S}}_{A})}\,.

Equivalently, we can consider the following quantity, which we will refer to as the

of

and

\displaystyle I_{{\rm free}}(A(t):B)_{\delta,N}

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{4}{d^{2}}\log f_{A(t)|B,\delta,N}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{d^{2}}\left(\log\text{Vol}({\mathcal{S}}_{A})-\log\text{Vol}({\mathcal{S}}_{A(t)|B,\delta,N})\right)

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{d^{2}}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}(\chi(A(t);\epsilon)-\chi(A(t)|B;\delta,N,\epsilon))

(2.10)

In the final version of the above expression, we have written

in terms of

\chi(A(t);\epsilon)\equiv\log n_{S_{A(t),\epsilon}},\quad\chi(A(t)|B;\delta,N,\epsilon)\equiv\log n_{S_{A(t)|B,\delta,N,\epsilon}}

(2.11)

can be intuitively interpreted as the amount of information needed to specify

up to
\epsilon
precision with no knowledge except that it is obtained from

by unitary evolution, and can be seen as a physical version of the “free entropy” of
[

,

,
17
,

,
19
,

,

]
. Similarly,

is a version of the conditional free entropy of

conditioned on

,
and captures the remaining uncertainty in our knowledge of

if we know its correlation functions with

Note that each free entropy term in (

and

Hence, a natural way of coarse-graining

2.

The quantity

can be viewed as a new attempt to characterize “mutual information across time,” which has been approached from a variety of perspectives in
[

,

.

See also time-like entanglement entropies

Like in these previous approaches, one of our goals is to find an information-theoretic quantity that captures the fact that chaotic dynamics lead to a loss of correlations over time. One intrinsic difficulty in defining a mutual information of this kind is that one cannot define a joint density matrix for multiple times and its reduced density matrices for each time

For two non-commuting variables, the joint distribution is defined by the set of joint moments appearing in (

An alternative way to view

and

within the smaller set of

Explicit formulas for the free mutual information

In this section, we first derive an explicit formula for the free mutual information in terms of the eigenvalues of the operator

in Sec.

3.1
A Coulomb gas formula

In this section, we will derive a closed expression for the free mutual information defined in (

d/2

In local systems made up of

and

for any Pauli strings

Due to the involution property of

Note that for any pair of operators

. We will use a special notation

for the case

\vec{x}\equiv\vec{y}\left(A(t)B\right)

(3.3)

Our goal is to obtain an explicit formula for

defined in (

) that does not depend strongly on the parameters
\delta
and
N
, and is instead determined by intrinsic properties of
A(t)
and

)

\vec{y}(\tilde{A}B)=\vec{x}\,.

(3.4)

Using the definitions (
2.8
) and (
2.4

6
6

6

The limits of

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0,N\to\infty}I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)_{\delta,N}

\displaystyle=-\frac{4}{d^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\text{Vol}(\{\tilde{A}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{A}\text{ such that }\vec{y}(\tilde{A}B)=\vec{x}\})}{\text{Vol}\left({\mathcal{S}}_{A}\right)}\right)

Lemma 3.1

The probability under Haar-random

Proof.

We provide the proof in Appendix

3.2
An integral formula for ensembles of unitary evolutions

In some of the examples we will consider later in the paper, the time-evolution operator

While it may be possible to numerically probe single realizations from the random ensemble in such cases, for analytic calculations it is often convenient to average the quantity of interest over the ensemble. In this section, we will present a useful formula for the ensemble average of the free mutual information in such cases, and clarify the assumptions and approximations under which it is valid.

Let the real parts of the eigenvalues of

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d/2})

is

8

8

4.1
Derivation of the OTOC sum formula

Let us first write

\displaystyle{{\mathcal{C}}}(A(t):B)=\mathcal{X}+\mathcal{Y}-\log 2\ ,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}=-\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\log|{\rm Re}(z_{i})-{\rm Re}(z_{j})|\ ,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=\frac{2}{d^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq d}\log|{\rm Re}(z_{i})-{\rm Re}(z_{i})|\ .

(4.8)

Let us first focus on the term

\displaystyle\mu(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\lim_{\zeta\to z^{+}}R(\zeta)-\lim_{\zeta\to z^{-}}R(\zeta)\right),

\displaystyle\quad{\mathcal{R}}(\zeta)\equiv\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\zeta-z_{i}}=\frac{1}{d}\text{Tr}\left[\frac{1}{\zeta-A(t)B}\right]\,.

term also gives rise to two additional contributions. We expect that that the series in the last term in (
4.23
) is likely to be convergent quite generally as it involves both positive and negative terms with an overall suppression in magnitude of
O(1/n)
.

Suppose we are in a regime where
{{\mathcal{C}}}(A(t):B)

is not proportional to some power of
1/d
, so that it can be identified with
I_{\rm free}
according to Theorem
3.1
. The
O(1/d)
contributions to
{{\mathcal{C}}}
are not relevant for
I_{\rm free}
, so we can ignore the second and third terms of (
4.23
). In principle, we should keep the
1/d
term in the parenthesis first term to control a potential divergence in the infinite sum:

I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n}\left({\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\right)

(4.24)

up to finite corrections of
O(1/d)
. In cases where
I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)
has a finite value, the sum must converge and we should get a good approximation to
I_{\rm free}
by keeping some
O(1)
number of terms
n_{\rm max}
,

\displaystyle I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{n=1}^{n_{\rm max}}\frac{2}{n}\left({\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)^{2}-\frac{1}{d}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{n=1}^{n_{\rm max}}\frac{2}{n}{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)^{2}\,.

(4.25)

In the last line we have assumed that we are considering systems at times such that
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)
is
O(1)
for
n
of
O(1)
. If this were not the case, then the value of
I_{\rm free}
itself would be
O(1/d)
, and the Coulomb gas formula would not be valid.

We compare the partial sums of both (
4.23
) and (
4.25
) with the Coulomb gas formula in one physical model in Appendix
E
, and confirm that the difference between (
4.23
) and (
4.25
) is only important at late times when
{{\mathcal{C}}}
is an inverse power of
d
.

4.2
OTOC sum formula for ensemble averages

Let us now consider the setup from Sec.
3.2
, where the time evolution operator
U(t)
is drawn from some ensemble
\nu
. Recall that if we can approximate
\overline{\rho(x)\rho(y)}\approx\bar{\rho}(x)\bar{\rho}(y)
and the measure
\nu
obeys certain smoothness conditions, the ensemble average of
I_{\rm free}
can be approximated as
{{\mathcal{I}}}(A(t):B)
in (
3.40
). Equivalently in terms of
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{T}
,

{{\mathcal{I}}}(A(t):B)=-\oint dz_{1}\oint dz_{2}\bar{\mu}(z_{1})\bar{\mu}(z_{2})\log|\mathop{\rm Re}(z_{1})-\mathop{\rm Re}(z_{2})|

(4.26)

where
\bar{\mu}
is the ensemble average of
\mu
defined in (
4.10
). The analysis of
{{\mathcal{I}}}(A(t):B)
is exactly parallel to that of
{\mathcal{X}}
in the previous section, with the replacement

\mu(z)\to\bar{\mu}(z),\quad\frac{1}{d}\text{Tr}[(A(t)B)^{n}]\to\oint_{\mathbb{T}}{\rm d}z\,\bar{\mu}(z)z^{n}\,,

(4.27)

so that we find

{{\mathcal{I}}}(A(t):B)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{n}\,\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)}^{2},

(4.28)

where
\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}
is the ensemble-averaged OTOC:

\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)}\equiv\oint_{\mathbb{T}}dz\bar{\mu}(z)z^{n}=\frac{1}{d}\overline{\text{Tr}[(A(t)B)^{n}]}\,.

(4.29)

There is no analog of the
{\mathcal{Y}}
term of the previous section in this case. This concludes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.

4.3
Physical implications

Let us now note two physical implications of (
4.4
) and (
4.5
):

1.

These results immediately imply the following inequality for any (up to the approximation in (
4.4
), which requires neglecting
O(1/d)
contributions):

I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)\geq\frac{2}{n}{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)^{2}\ .

(4.30)

We can also put any partial sum over some subset of OTOCs as a stronger lower bound. The equality (
4.4
) is clearly a much stronger statement, but the bounds can be useful if we do not have access to higher-point OTOCs beyond some
n
. Moreover, the bound has a similar structure to
the following well-known bound for the quantum mutual information
I(X:Y)_{\rho}
between two regions
X
and
Y

[
68
]
in some state
\rho
,

I(X:Y)_{\rho}\geq\frac{1}{2}\langle O_{X}O_{Y}\rangle_{\rho,c}^{2}\ .

(4.31)

where
O_{X}
and
O_{Y}
are any operators in
X
and
Y
with unit operator norm, and
\langle O_{X}O_{Y}\rangle_{\rho,c}
is their connected two-point function in
\rho
. The
n=1
case of (
4.30
) is most directly analogous to (
4.31
) as it involves the two-point function
\frac{1}{d}\text{Tr}[AB(t)]
.
This analogy suggests that
I_{\rm free}
is a natural candidate for an information-theoretic measure of correlations over time. For various timelike mutual information measures proposed in the literature from other perspectives, see
[
54
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
58
,
59
]
.

2.

Recall from Sec.
3.2
that under a condition of sufficient smoothness of the ensemble that is satisfied by many examples of interest (including the GUE and random unitary circuit ensembles discussed later in this paper),
\bar{\rho}(x)
contains no delta functions after some characteristic time scale, and as a result
{{\mathcal{I}}}
is finite. In such cases, the infinite sum over
\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}^{2}
in (
4.5
) must converge.

Note that since the series (
4.5
) already involves a harmonic weight, it is right on the border of divergence. This implies that even a mild decay of the
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
with
n
, such as
\sim 1/n^{\delta}
for small
\delta>0
, may be sufficient for a finite FMI. This leaves room for a variety of different decay behaviours of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
with
n
. We shall examine the form of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
decay and its associated timescales in a variety of physical models in the next two sections.

5
Free mutual information in chaotic systems

Now that we have derived general formulas to study the free mutual information in physical systems, let us turn to some concrete examples. In this section, we will consider three increasingly realistic models of chaotic time-evolution. In each of these models, our main goal is to evaluate
I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)
and understand its time-dependence. Another important goal is to understand the role played by different
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
in the partial sums of (
4.4
) or (
4.5
). In the course of trying to understand these partial sums, we will also be able to extract certain universal features of the
n
-dependence of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
across these different models. The techniques used and the results we are able to derive in each model are somewhat different, so we summarize the main results below. In each case, we take
A
and
B
to be initially commuting, so that
I_{\rm free}(A(t):B)=\infty
at
t=0
.

1.

Random Hamiltonians from the GUE ensemble.
In Sec.
5.1
, we use the non-local GUE model as a first example. In this model, we numerically compute both
I_{\rm free}
and
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
for single realizations of the ensemble, and derive analytic formulas for the ensemble average of
\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}
. This allows for detailed comparison between ensemble averages and individual realizations, and between the the two sides of (
4.4
) for various
n_{\rm max}
. We find numerically that
I_{\rm free}(t)
becomes finite for any
t>0
and decays monotonically with time. All
{\rm OTOC}_{n}

start to decay for
t>0
, and higher
n
show faster initial decay with
t
. Due to this behavior of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
, at sufficiently late times we get a good approximation to
I_{\rm free}(t)
even with
n_{\rm max}=2
in (
4.4
).

At intermediate times,
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)
show oscillations as functions of both
t
and
n
, which do not lead to oscillations in
I_{\rm free}(t)
or the partial sums of (
4.4
), and do not prevent the sums from converging.

2.

Random unitary circuits.
In Sec.
5.2
, we consider brickwork Haar-random unitary circuits, which were introduced as a model for chaotic dynamics in
[
69
,
70
]
. This model is closer to realistic chaotic systems than the GUE model due to its local structure, although it lacks energy conservation. Due to the local structure, the decay of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
in this model for two operators initially separated by distance
x
begins at a time
t^{\ast}
proportional to
x
. We analytically study the ensemble average of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
in this model, and conjecture a result for
t>t^{\ast}
that is similar in structure to
\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}(t)
in the random GUE model. In particular, all
\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}(t)
start to decay simultaneously at
t^{\ast}
, and the result is consistent with a faster initial decay for higher
n
.

Based on this GUE-like structure, it is likely that the OTOC partial sums of (
4.4
) and
\overline{I_{\rm free}}
for
t>t^{\ast}
show a similar behavior to the GUE case, although we do not have direct access to these quantities from our analytic calculation.

3.

Chaotic spin chain.
To take into account the effects of energy conservation as well as locality, we next consider the Ising model with both transverse and longitudinal fields. This model is widely studied in the literature as a representative example of chaotic spin chains. We numerically evaluate
I_{\rm free}
and
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
in this model. We find that the qualitative features of both
I_{\rm free}(t)
and
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)
at times
t>t^{\ast}=x/v^{\ast}
(for some characteristic
v^{\ast}
for this model) are again similar to those of the random GUE model.

5.1
Random GUE Hamiltonian

As our first model for a highly chaotic time-evolution operator, let us consider
U_{\rm GUE}(t)=e^{-iHt}
, where
H
is a random
d\times d
Hermitian matrix from the probability distribution
p_{\rm GUE}(H)
of GUE Hermitian matrices. More explicitly,

p_{\rm GUE}(H)=\frac{1}{{{\mathcal{N}}}}e^{-\frac{d}{2}\text{Tr}[H^{2}]}\,.

(5.1)

The prefactor

correspons to the choice that the off-diagonal elements of

are independent complex random variables with zero mean and variance
\overline{|H_{ij}|^{2}}=\frac{1}{d}

lies between

and
2
independent of

This unitary invariance implies in particular that the eigenvectors of

and
{\rm OTOC}_{n}

We will first discuss numerical observations from single realizations of this ensemble in Sec.

We would now like to understand our more fine-grained notion of spreading in operator space in this model, quantified by
I_{\rm free}
and
{\rm OTOC}_{n}

\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}

\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}

As explained in Appendix
G

\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}(t,v)=0\,,\quad n\text{ odd}\,

For even

[

\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}

G

\overline{{\rm OTOC}_{n}}(t,v)=\begin{cases}1&v>v_{B}\\
\sum_{k=n/2}^{n-1}c^{(n)}_{k}q^{-k({{\mathcal{E}}}_{k+1}(v)-v)t}&v<v_{B}\end{cases},\quad n\text{ even }\,.

(5.15)

Here

As explained in more detail in the appendix around (

Considering the implications of the arguments of Appendix

6
Free mutual information in non-chaotic systems

In this section, we discuss the behaviour of the Coulomb gas formula and the free mutual information in a few examples which cannot be seen as generic chaotic models. In three of these cases – Clifford unitaries, PFC unitaries, and free fermions– we find degeneracies in the spectrum of
A(t)B
even at late times, so that the FMI is infinite. In each of these models, the behavior of
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
reflects delocalization in physical space, even though the divergence of the FMI indicates a lack of spreading in operator space. The precise mechanism underlying the degeneracy of the spectrum of
A(t)B
is different in each case. In the interacting integrable Heisenberg model, for operators that do not commute with the symmetries of the system, we find behavior similar to the generic chaotic spin chains of Sec.
5.3
.

6.1
Unitary designs

Recall from the discussion of Sec.
3.3
that under a single step of evolution by a Haar-random unitary, the free mutual information becomes zero in the limit of large Hilbert space dimension. It is then natural to ask whether ensembles known as unitary designs, which replicate certain features of Haar-random unitaries, also cause the free mutual information to vanish or become small after a single step. See for instance
[
47
]
for the precise definition of unitary
n
-designs.

We consider two examples of unitary designs below:

1.

Random Clifford unitaries
, which form exact 3-designs
[
75
]
. This implies in particular that the value of
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
agrees on average between random Cliffords and Haar-random unitaries. Random Cliffords thus lead to delocalization of operators in physical space, and the related information-theoretic phenomenon of decoupling
[
76
,
77
,
78
,
79
,
80
,
81
,
6
]
. This ensemble fails to form an exact or approximate 4-design
[
75
,
82
]
.

2.

Random PFC ensembles
, which were recently introduced in
[
47
]
and shown to form approximate unitary
n
-designs for any
n
that does not grow with the Hilbert space dimension
d
. As one component of a PFC unitary is a random Clifford, this ensemble also forms an exact 3-design.

From our OTOC sum formula for the free mutual information, it is clear that if all
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
are equal to the Haar-random values for some unitary dynamics, then the free mutual information must be small. However, under both random Clifford unitaries and random PFC ensembles, for all
n
that are multiples of 4,
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(UAU^{\dagger}:B)=1
. For random Cliffords, this is true for any Pauli operators
A
and
B
.
14
14

14

Note that we refer to any tensor product operator involving one of the four Pauli matrices
I
,
X
,
Y
,
Z
at each site as a Pauli.
For the random PFC ensemble, it holds for the case where
A
is any Pauli and
B
consists only of Pauli
Z
and
I
at each site. This is true for each individual realization from both ensembles, and hence also for the ensemble average. For Cliffords, this behavior is consistent with the fact that they do not form exact 4-designs; for the PFC ensemble, it is consistent because the
approximate

n
-design property, unlike the
exact

n
-design property, does not imply that
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
are close to the Haar values (see
[
48
,
28
]
).

For both random Cliffords and PFC unitaries, the fact that
{\rm OTOC}_{n}=1
for all
n
that are multiples of 4 comes from the fact that each of the eigenvalues of
A(t)B
takes only one out of four possible values: +1,
-1
,
i
, or
-i
. This also immediately implies that the spectrum has a large amount of degeneracy, and the Coulomb gas formula diverges. Let us see how this feature of the spectrum comes about in both cases.

6.1.1
Cliffords

The result in the Clifford case immediately follows from the fact that
UAU^{\dagger}
is a Pauli matrix if
A
is a Pauli matrix. Then since
B
is also a Pauli, there are three cases for the spectrum of
UAU^{\dagger}B
: (i) If
UAU^{\dagger}=B
, then
UAU^{\dagger}B=I
and all eigenvalues of
UAU^{\dagger}B
are 1. (ii) If
UAU^{\dagger}\neq B
but commutes with
B
, then
UAU^{\dagger}B
is
\pm P
for some nontrivial Pauli
P
, so that
d/2
eigenvalues are +1 and
d/2
are
-1
. (iii) If
UAU^{\dagger}
anticommutes with
B
, then
UAU^{\dagger}B
is
\pm iP
for some Pauli
P
. Half of the eigenvalues are
+i
, and half are
-i
.

Since random Clifford unitaries send Pauli matrices to other Paulis, the lack of chaotic behavior in this ensemble can be detected by a number of other measures, including operator entanglement
[
83
,
74
]
and the stabilizer Rényi entropy
[
84
]
. It is not too surprising, then, that the free mutual information shows non-generic behavior in this case. Next, let us turn to the more non-trivial example of the PFC ensemble
[
47
]
, which does not send a Pauli matrix to another Pauli matrix.

6.1.2
PFC unitaries

A PFC unitary augments a random Clifford (C) by further multiplying each computational state by a random binary phase (F) and applying a random permutation (P) to the computational basis. More precisely, a unitary in the PFC ensemble is given by the product

U=P\,F\,C\,,\quad\quad C\in\mathrm{Clifford},\quad F:\ket{x}\mapsto(-1)^{f(x)}\ket{x},\quad P:\ket{x}\mapsto\ket{\pi(x)}

(6.1)

where
f(x)
is a binary function and
\pi\in S_{d}
is a permutation among computational basis states. The probability measure on this ensemble is induced from a uniformly random Clifford
C
, a uniformly random binary function
f(x)
, and a uniformly random permutation
\pi
.

Consider some initial Pauli
A
, and its “time evolution” under a single step of the PFC unitary,

A^{\prime}\equiv PFCAC^{\dagger}F^{\dagger}P^{\dagger}.

(6.2)

We first argue that
A^{\prime}
always maps a computational basis state to another computational basis state (up to a phase), i.e., it never maps
\ket{x}
to a superposition of computational basis states. This is because each factor in
A^{\prime}=P\cdot F\cdot(CAC^{\dagger})\cdot F^{\dagger}\cdot P^{\dagger}
does so. This is obvious for
P
and
F
and their Hermitian conjugates. The middle factor
CAC^{\dagger}
is some Pauli operator, and a Pauli acting on a computational basis yields a phase flip, a bit flip, or both, so that again
CAC^{\dagger}\ket{x}
is a computational basis state.

Given this fact, let us denote the permutation action of
A^{\prime}
on the computational basis states by
\tau
. Since
A^{\prime 2}=I
,
A^{\prime}
decomposes the computational basis into disjoint orbits that are either 1-cycles (fixed points)
\tau(x)=x
, or 2-cycles of form
\{x,\tau(x)\}
with
\tau(\tau(x))=x
. On the subspace spanned by the states that belong to the 1-cycles,
A^{\prime}
acts as an identity. For a 2-cycle, we have

A^{\prime}\ket{x}=a_{x}\ket{\tau(x)},\quad A^{\prime}\ket{\tau(x)}=a_{\tau(x)}\ket{x},\quad a_{x}a_{\tau(x)}=1\ .

(6.3)

Since
B
only consists of
Z
’s, we have

B\ket{x}=b_{x}\ket{x},\,b_{x}\in\{\pm 1\}\,.

(6.4)

Hence, the restriction of
A^{\prime}B
to the span of an arbitrary 2-cycle
\{\ket{x},\ket{\tau(x)}\}
has the matrix form

A^{\prime}B\Big|_{\rm{span}\{\ket{x},\ket{\tau(x)}\}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{x}b_{\tau(x)}\\
a_{\tau(x)}b_{x}&0\end{pmatrix}\ .

(6.5)

Its eigenvalues satisfy

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=0,\,\quad\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}=-a_{\tau(x)}b_{x}a_{x}b_{\tau(x)}=-b_{x}b_{\tau(x)}\in\{\pm 1\}\,.

(6.6)

Hence, the only possible eigenvalues of each such
2\times 2
block, and hence of of
A^{\prime}B
, are
\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
.

We therefore see that even with the addition of the phase flips and permutations, the PFC ensemble does not lead to spreading in phase space and decay of higher-point OTOCs.
It is nevertheless possible that other variants of the PFC ensemble, such as CPFC
[
85
]
, PFCFP, or stacking up multiple layers of PFCs
[
86
]
could show behaviors more similar to the generic chaotic systems of Sec.
5
.

7
Discussion and outlook

In this paper, we identified a new universal property of the Heisenberg evolution of operators in a chaotic quantum many-body system. We found that a certain coarse-grained volume of the time-evolved operator generically spreads out to fill the abstract space of all operators, which is doubly exponential in the number of degrees of freedom. This notion of ergodicity in operator space goes beyond the more familiar notion of operator growth in the space of degrees of freedom. We introduced a quantitative measure of spreading in operator space called the free mutual information (FMI). We derived an explicit formula for the FMI for a broad class of operators, and showed a precise and general relation between the FMI and a sum over all higher-point out-of-time-ordered correlators (
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
). This relation provides an understanding of the precise role played by higher-point OTOCs in the approach to maximal scrambling and asymptotic freeness, which had previously been lacking.

We then studied the behaviour of the FMI and the higher-point OTOCs in a variety of representative examples of chaotic as well as integrable systems. In all examples of chaotic systems, we found that while higher-point OTOCs show large oscillations with time, the FMI decays monotonically, showing that it captures a more physically meaningful measure of chaos than individual
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
. Although the FMI starts to decay at a time scale close to that associated with decay of
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
in
chaotic
systems, we confirmed by looking at a variety of integrable models that it is a much finer probe of chaos than
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
. In each of these integrable models, we found cases where
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
decays, indicating spreading in physical space, while
I_{\rm free}
remains infinite, indicating localization in operator space.

While our study of various examples of chaotic quantum many-body systems has allowed us to extract a number of general patterns, various results in these models should be better understood in future work. In particular, it is important to address the challenge of performing the OTOC sum in (
4.5
) or directly evaluating the Coulomb gas formula analytically in some concrete physical example, to obtain an analytic form of
I_{\rm free}
as a function of time. While we proposed a toy model for this functional form in local chaotic models in (
5.18
), the true result should be of the form

I_{\rm free}(t)=-c\log(1-f(t))

(7.1)

where
f(t)
is a more non-trivial function which is equal to 1 for
t<t^{\ast}
and decays for
t>t^{\ast}
. We leave a study of its precise functional form to more detailed studies in future work. It is also important to better understand the general physical reasons why
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
show faster initial decay for higher
n
, and why later oscillations in
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)
do not lead to corresponding oscillations in
I_{\rm free}(t)
. Further, it would be interesting to find an example of a system which one ordinarily thinks of as chaotic from the perspective of
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
, which turns out not to be chaotic when probed through
I_{\rm free}
, or where
I_{\rm free}
starts to decay at a later time scale than
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
.

We comment on a number of broader generalizations and future directions below:

•

FMI with more reference observables.
In this paper, we have discussed the simplest way to coarse-grain
A(t)
with a single reference observable
B
. In principle, we could generalize the definition to a collection of multiple reference observables
\{B_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, which would give a more refined FMI
I_{\rm free}(A(t):B_{1}\ldots B_{k})
. It is clear that adding more reference observables imposes more constraints, and hence leads to a smaller volume fraction of the coarse-grained set of
\tilde{A}
and a larger mutual information:
I_{\rm free}(A(t):B_{1}\ldots B_{k})\geq I_{\rm free}(A(t):B_{1})
. This is the monotonicity of free mutual information, which can also be seen as the strong subadditivity of free entropy. It would be interesting to study when this inequality is saturated in physical contexts, and to understand the relation of
I_{\rm free}(A(t):B_{1}\ldots B_{k})
to more general higher-point OTOCs.

•

FMI at finite temperature.
In this paper, we have always considered the case where the reference state
\rho
in (
2.6
) is the maximally mixed state. In continuum systems such as quantum field theories, infinite temperature correlation functions are not well-defined, and it is necessary to consider a finite temperature state, i.e. take
\rho
to be
e^{-\beta H}
for some
\beta>0
. This choice of
\rho
is also more natural in the presence of energy constraints; indeed, the most interesting behaviour of the OTOC in models like SYK is seen at low temperature
[
10
,
12
]
, and interesting physical constraints on OTOCs such as the chaos bound apply only for finite temperature
[
11
]
.

Mathematically, the generalization to finite temperature poses the challenge that we are no longer dealing with tracial moments, so that our techniques in Sec.
3
need a revision. Nevertheless, we believe that it may still be possible to find an explicit formula in this case. If we take
A
and
B
to be traceless involutions (Paulis or rotated Paulis), the set of all joint moments in this case can be organized as
m_{n}\equiv d^{-1}\text{Tr}\rho[A(t)B]^{n}
,
m_{n}^{*}\equiv d^{-1}\text{Tr}\rho[BA(t)]^{n}
,
l_{n}\equiv d^{-1}\text{Tr}\rho B[A(t)B]^{n}
and
l_{n}^{*}\equiv d^{-1}\text{Tr}\rho A(t)[BA(t)]^{n}
. The resulting free mutual information might not admit a simple Coulomb gas formula that depends on a single eigenvalue density
\rho(x)
determined by one set of moments, but should somehow combine two densities determined by
(m_{n},m_{n}^{*})
and
(l_{n},l_{n}^{*})
respectively.

•

Symmetry-constrained free mutual information.

Our example in Sec.
6.3
indicates the need to adapt the FMI for systems with symmetries. In the case where
A
and
B
both commute with some symmetry
Q
of the time-evolution, the divergence in the FMI can be avoided by restricting the unitaries appearing in the definition (
2.1
) of
{\mathcal{S}}_{A}
to ones that also commute with the symmetry
Q
. We leave a systematic study of the FMI in systems with symmetries to future works.

Better understanding the OTOC sum formula.
There are a few subtle features of our main formula (
1.6
) that intrigue us. If we were to guess an explicit formula for the FMI, which is implicitly defined in terms of the OTOCs, a sum-of-squares form is the most natural candidate given that the FMI is nonnegative. It is striking that the actual answer turns out to be precisely a sum of squares up to the harmonic weight. As we have remarked, the harmonic weight is subtle because it allows any power law decay of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
with

to sum to a finite FMI, while not allowing
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
that do not decay with

to sum to a finite FMI. It would be interesting to better understand the physical interpretation of this weight. We expect that this harmonic weight is likely the underlying reason why

decays monotonically despite the oscillations at intermediate times in individual
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t)
in the spin chain and random GUE models.

The harmonic weight is likely due to the fact that the FMI is an entropic quantity whose definition comes with a logarithm, and its Taylor expansion is exactly the RHS of (

). Explicitly, we could rewrite the formula as

•

Relation to other measures of chaos and complexity.
Recall that we started with the motivation of coming up with a quantum many-body analog of ergodicity in classical phase space. In classical chaos, the natural entropy associated with this ergodicity is
the Kolmogorov-Sinai (KS) entropy, which measures the rate at which one needs to refine the phase space resolution to keep track of the trajectory of the system. A quantum version of KS entropy has previously been defined in
[
93
,
94
]
. This entropy quantifies how the information gained from a measurement on the system evolves over time, and is not obviously related to our discussion in this paper of the quantum trajectory of an initial operator in operator space. However, in the mathematical literature there is some discussion of the relation between the quantum KS entropy and the free entropy
[
95
,
96
]
. It is worth studying the potential connection between these quantities in the physical context of quantum chaos.

Another natural question is about how the free mutual information may be related to various notions of the complexity of the time-evolved operator
A(t)=U(t)^{\dagger}AU(t)
, or the complexity of the time-evolution operator
U(t)
. One relation to complexity comes from the fact that the free entropy captures a version of quantum Kolmogorov (descriptive) complexity of
A(t)

[
66
]
. There may also be a relation or bound between some variant of the FMI and circuit complexity, as estimates of circuit complexity often make use of the volume occupied by an ensemble of time-evolution operators such as a random unitary circuit in the space of all unitaries (see for instance
[
97
,
98
,
99
]
). The FMI studied in this paper, which involves two operators, saturates at much earlier time scales than the ones associated with the circuit complexity, but it is possible that a less coarse-grained, multi-operator version could saturate at much later time scales.

Free mutual information in mathematics.
The definition of free mutual information we have used is adapted from the orbital free entropy
[
23
]
in the mathematics literature. As mentioned in Footnote.

[

instead of on the counting of matrices
[

21
]
. These two definitions are expected to coincide

24

, but it is not known in full generality whether they coincide. In the companion paper

•

Free Rényi mutual information.
The partial sum expression in (
4.4
), truncated at
n=k
,
\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{2}{k}{\rm OTOC}_{k}^{2}
, can be thought of as defining a “Rényi free mutual information” of order
k
. These Rényi’s are practically useful, as in practical settings such as experiments only a finite number of OTOCs may be available. It is obvious from the sum-of-squares formula that they are ordered with respect to the Rényi index, similar to the classical and quantum Rényi entropies (except in a reverse order). A principled approach would be to define Rényi free mutual information by modifying the definition (
2.6
) so that it only depends on the first
k
moments. In the companion paper
[
67
]
, we shall prove that this proper definition exactly matches the expected formula
\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{2}{k}{\rm OTOC}_{k}^{2}
.

Higher-point OTOCs in random unitary circuits

{\rm OTOC}_{n}
in random unitary circuits, and provide an argument for the conjectured result in (
5.15
). We use the partition function method developed for studying the
n
-th Rényi entropy
S_{n}
in random unitary circuits in
[
72
]
. For readers familiar with the methods used in
[
72
]
, we note that the partition function for the calculation of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
has the same “bulk” rules as the partition function for
S_{n}
, but the boundary conditions are different.
This allows us to make use of the “membrane picture” developed for
S_{n}
while calculating
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
. The difference in the boundary conditions makes the calculation much more non-trivial than that of
S_{n}
, or that of
{\rm OTOC}_{2}
in
[
69
,
70
]
, and we need to take into account certain effects that are not captured by the membrane picture.

We will present the partition function calculation for
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
and review the relevant assumptions of the membrane picture in a mostly self-contained way in this appendix for clarity. The basic setup follows directly from that in
[
72
]
. Our main innovation is the argument in sections
G.5
and
G.6
showing that the membrane picture does not apply in certain cases due to subtle cancellations among various configurations. This argument is the underlying reason why only
k\geq n/2
appear in the sum in (
5.15
). Recall that the appearance of only
k\geq n/2
in this sum makes it plausible that
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
decay with
n
for
t\geq v_{B}
, and hence that
I_{\rm free}
becomes finite for
t\geq v_{B}
.

G.1
Definitions and properties of permutation states

To set up the partition function calculation, it will be useful to first introduce certain states on
2n
copies of the system associated with permutations in
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
. In this subsection, we define these states and discuss their relevant properties. We also summarize certain lemmas about the permutation group which will later be useful.

For
\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
and any operator
O
acting on one site, define the states
\ket{O,\sigma}
on
2n
copies of one site:

\braket{i_{1}i_{1}^{\prime}...i_{n}i_{n}^{\prime}|O,\sigma}=\frac{1}{q^{n/2}}O_{i_{1}i^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}...O_{i_{n}i^{\prime}_{\sigma(n)}}

(G.1)

Recall that
q
is the Hilbert space dimension of one site. In particular, we can label the states corresponding to
O=\mathbf{1}
as follows:

\braket{i_{1}i_{1}^{\prime}...i_{n}i_{n}^{\prime}|\sigma}=\frac{1}{q^{n/2}}\delta_{i_{1}i^{\prime}_{\sigma(1)}}...\delta_{i_{n}i^{\prime}_{\sigma(n)}}\,.

(G.2)

Two permutations which we will denote with a special notation are the identity permutation, denoted by
e
, and the cyclic permutation
(n\,n-1\,n-2\,...\,1)
, denoted by
\eta
.

The overlaps among the permutation states are given by

\displaystyle\braket{\sigma|\tau}=q^{-d(\sigma,\tau)}

(G.3)

where
d(\sigma,\tau)
is the Cayley distance between the permutations
\sigma
and
\tau
, defined as the minimum number of transpositions with which one needs to multiply
\sigma
to obtain
\tau
(or vice versa). More explicitly,

d(\sigma,\tau)=n-k(\sigma\tau^{-1})

(G.4)

where
k(\sigma)
denotes the total number of cycles in the permutation
\sigma
. Another useful overlap which will play a role in the discussion below is

\braket{\sigma|O,\tau}=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{k(\sigma\tau^{-1})}\text{Tr}[O^{|c_{i}|}]

(G.5)

where
c_{i}\in\sigma\tau^{-1}
refers to the cycles in
\sigma\tau^{-1}
, and
|c_{i}|
is the number of elements in the
i
-th cycle. In the discussion below,
O
will be either
A
or
B
, both of which have the properties that

O^{2}=\mathbf{1},\quad\text{Tr}[O]=0\,.

(G.6)

For such
O
, we always have
\braket{\sigma|O,\tau}=0
for odd
n
, as at least one of the
|c_{i}|
in (
G.5
) is odd. For even
n
,

\braket{\sigma|O,\tau}=\begin{cases}q^{-d(\sigma,\tau)}&\sigma\tau^{-1}\in P_{\rm even}\\
0&\sigma\tau^{-1}\notin P_{\rm even}\end{cases}

(G.7)

where we have defined a subset
P_{\rm even}\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
as follows:

P_{\rm even}\equiv\{\tau\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}|~|c_{i}|\text{ is even for each }c_{i}\in\tau\}\,.

(G.8)

For example, for
{\mathcal{S}}_{6}
,
(12)(36)(45)\in P_{\rm even}
while
{\mathcal{S}}_{6}
,
(12)(36)(4)(5)\notin P_{\rm even}
.

Another subset of permutations that will play an important role below is

P_{\rm NC}\equiv\{\tau\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}|\,k(\tau)+k(\eta\tau^{-1})=n+1\,\}.

(G.9)

Such
\tau
are in one-to-one correspondence with the non-crossing partitions of
n
elements, and we will sometimes refer to them as “non-crossing permutations.” Note that the following inequality is obeyed by any permutation in
\tau\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
:

k(\tau)+k(\eta\tau^{-1})\leq n+1\,.

(G.10)

This is equivalent to the triangle inequality

d(\tau,e)+d(\tau,\eta)\geq d(e,\eta)\,.

(G.11)

The non-crossing permutations saturate this inequality.

Let us now state two lemmas about permutations in these subsets of
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
which we will use in the later discussion. These lemmas can be referred to when they appear in the later subsections. The proofs of both lemmas are provided in Appendix
H
. To state Lemma
G.1
, in addition to the sets
P_{\rm even}
and
P_{\rm NC}
defined above, let us define a third set

Q_{\rm singleton}\equiv\{\tau\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}|~|c_{i}|=1\text{ for at least one }c_{i}\in\eta\tau^{-1}\}\,.

(G.12)

By definition, its complement is

\bar{Q}_{\rm singleton}\equiv\{\tau\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}|~|c_{i}|>1\text{ for all }c_{i}\in\eta\tau^{-1}\}\,.

(G.13)

Lemma G.1
.

Let
\tau
be an NC permutation of
n
elements where
n
is even.
(a) For any
\tau\in P_{\rm even}
, we have
k(\eta\tau^{-1})\geq\frac{n}{2}+1
, and
\eta\tau^{-1}
has at least one cycle of length 1. (b) For any
\tau\in\bar{Q}_{\rm singleton}
, we have
k(\tau)\geq\frac{n}{2}+1
and
\tau\notin P_{\rm even}
.

Part (a) of Lemma
G.1
implies in particular that

P_{\rm even}\cap P_{\rm NC}\subseteq Q_{\rm singleton}\cap P_{\rm NC}\,.

(G.14)

Another useful statement about
Q_{\rm singleton}
is the following lemma:

Lemma G.2
.

For
\tau\in Q_{\rm singleton}\cap P_{\rm NC}
, any
\sigma
that satisfies

d(\eta,\tau)=d(\eta,\sigma)+d(\sigma,\tau)

(G.15)

(i.e., any
\sigma
that lies on the geodesic between
\eta
and
\tau
)
is also in
Q_{\rm singleton}\cap P_{\rm NC}
.

G.2
Partition function for
{\rm OTOC}_{n}

We want to calculate
{\rm OTOC}_{n}(A(t):B)
for the setup shown in Fig.
6
. Recall that each two-site unitary
U^{(t)}_{x,x+1}
in the circuit is drawn independently from the ensemble of Haar-random unitaries from
\mathbf{U}(q^{2})
. The full time-evolution operator
U(t)
up to time
t
is constructed by taking products of these two-site unitaries. Below,
L
denotes the total number of sites. We will assume that
L
is large enough that we can ignore all edge effects.
We will always assume that
A
lies at location
x^{\prime}=0
and
B
at
x^{\prime}=x
, and we will often write
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
as a function of
t
and the velocity
v=x/t
.

In terms of the permutation states introduced in the previous subsection, we can rewrite
{\rm OTOC}_{n}=\frac{1}{q^{L}}\text{Tr}[\left(U(t)AU(t)^{\dagger}B\right)^{n}]
as a transition amplitude on
2n
copies of the system as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm OTOC}_{n}\left(t,v\right)=

\displaystyle\bra{\eta}\bra{\eta}...\bra{\eta}\bra{B,\eta}_{x^{\prime}=vt}\bra{\eta}...\bra{\eta}

\displaystyle(U(t)\otimes U(t)^{\ast})^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\ket{e}\ket{e}...\ket{e}\ket{A,e}_{x^{\prime}=0}\ket{e}...\ket{e}\times q^{(n-1)L}\,.

(G.16)

The above expression is simply a rewriting of
{\rm OTOC}_{n}
by manipulating various indices, and is true for any
U(t)
. This rewriting proves useful in random unitary circuits because the average of
(U\otimes U)^{\otimes 2n}
for each
U=U_{x,x+1}^{(t)}
can also be expressed in terms of permutation states:

\overline{\left(U^{(t)}_{x,x+1}\otimes{U^{(t)}_{x,x+1}}^{\ast}\right)^{\otimes n}}=\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}g^{\sigma\tau}\ket{\sigma}_{x}\ket{\sigma}_{x+1}\bra{\tau}_{x}\bra{\tau}_{x+1}

(G.17)

where
g^{\sigma\tau}
is the inverse of the matrix

g_{\sigma\tau}=(q^{2})^{-d(\sigma,\tau)}

(G.18)

(In terms of the more standard notation of the Weingarten function,
g^{\sigma\tau}=(q^{2})^{n}\,\text{Wg}(\sigma\tau^{-1},q^{2})
.)

By putting (
G.17
) into (
G.16
) for each two-site random unitary appearing in
U(t)
, we get a partition function on a two-dimensional lattice, with the top and bottom boundary conditions given by the top and bottom lines of (
G.16
), and the bulk interactions determined by (
G.17
). In figure Fig.
15
, we further explain the bulk interactions, and how some of the sites can be integrated out to get a triangular lattice. Each
s_{i}
corresponds to some permutation in
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
, so there are
n!
states on each site of the lattice.

Figure 15
:
We show a segment in space-time of the average of
(U(t)\otimes U(t)^{*})^{\otimes n}
over random unitaries, obtained using (
G.17
) and the structure of the circuit in Fig.

. The vertical lines come from (
G.17
) and the diagonal lines correspond to taking inner products between permutation states according to the rule (

). By integrating out each of top states such as

, we get a triangular lattice as shown on the right.

Explicitly, the interactions
J(s_{1},s_{2};s_{3})
are given by

J(s_{1},s_{2};s_{3})=\sum_{\mu}g^{\mu\,s_{3}}\braket{s_{1}|\mu}\braket{s_{2}|\mu}

(G.19)

These interactions can be either positive or negative. They take a simple form for general
n
only in two special cases:

(i)

If

s_{1}=s_{2}
, we have
J(s_{1},s_{1};s_{3})=\delta_{s_{1}s_{3}}
.

(ii)

If
d(s_{1},s_{2})=1
, then

J(s_{1},s_{2};s_{3})=\frac{q}{q^{2}+1}(\delta_{s_{1}s_{3}}+\delta_{s_{2}s_{3}})\,.

(G.20)

We expect in general that

|J(s_{1},s_{2};s_{3})|\leq 1

(G.21)

for all
q
and
n
and all
s_{i}
. We will prove this statement for the case
q=2
as Lemma
H.1
in Appendix
H
. The inequality is saturated only in case (i) listed above, i.e., when
s_{1}=s_{2}=s_{3}
.

By further taking into account the contributions from the top and bottom boundary conditions,
the full lattice and interaction rules are summarized in Fig.

K(s_{0})=\sum_{\tau}g^{\tau s_{0}}\braket{B,\eta|\tau}\braket{\eta|\tau}\,.

is generally non-zero for all

We consider all possible configurations of
\vec{s}

\vec{b}

Let now note two simplifications that follow immediately from rules (a)-(d):

1.

Rule (a) forces all spins in the shaded red region of the lattice to be
\eta

2.

{\rm OTOC}_{n}=0
for all times when

\braket{b_{i}^{\ast}|A,e}=0
for any choice of

, as discussed above (

).

We are then left with the task of summing over all possible configurations of
\vec{s}
,
\vec{b}
for even
n
. Below, we will refer to a triangle with
s_{1}\neq s_{2}
(

t^{\prime}=t
has two adjacent domain walls, between
\eta
and
s_{0}
and between
s_{0}
and
\eta

n
,
s_{0}
can now be any of the
n!
permutations in
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
.

If
d(s_{0},\eta)>1
, then unlike in the simple case (
G.20
),
J(s_{0},\eta;s_{3})

is generally non-zero for all
s_{3}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
(in particular,
s_{3}
is not forced to be either
s_{1}
or
s_{2}
). This implies that the two domain walls starting at the top boundary can in principle change into any number of further domain walls of various types as we go towards the bottom boundary. In fact, configurations where all three
s_{i}
on each triangle are distinct from each other (i.e., where the number of triangles with domain walls is extensive in the volume of the unshaded region) will generically give a non-zero contribution. However, note that the general inequality (
G.21
) makes it intuitive that each triangle with a domain wall comes with some cost, so that cases with an extensive number of domain walls are unlikely to dominate. We will state this intuition more formally in the next subsection.

Let us introduce some notation to better organize the sum over
\vec{s}
,
\vec{b}
in
{\rm OTOC}_{n}

{\rm OTOC}_{n}=\sum_{\vec{s},\vec{b}}{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\text{top, bulk}}(\vec{s},\vec{b})~{{\mathcal{B}}}(\vec{b})

(G.23)

where

{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\text{top, bulk}}(\vec{s},\vec{b})=K(s_{0})\prod_{\text{bulk triangles}}J(s_{i},s_{j};s_{k})

(G.24)

and

{{\mathcal{B}}}(\vec{b})=\begin{cases}q^{(n-1)2t}\braket{b_{i_{\ast}}|A,e}\prod_{i\neq i^{\ast}}\braket{b_{i}|e}&\text{if }v<1\\
q^{(n-1)2t}\prod_{i}\braket{b_{i}|e}&\text{if }v>1\end{cases}

(G.25)

Note that
{{\mathcal{B}}}
comes from a combination of the overall factor of
q^{(n-1)L}
in (
G.16
) with the factors coming from overlaps on the lower boundary. (For sites in the shaded red region, the overlap
\braket{\eta|e}=q^{-(n-1)}
cancels with
q^{(n-1)}
from this factor.) It is useful to further organize the contributions according to
\vec{b}
:

{\rm OTOC}_{n}=\sum_{\vec{b}}{{\mathcal{C}}}(\vec{b})\,{{\mathcal{B}}}(\vec{b}),\quad{{\mathcal{C}}}(\vec{b})=\sum_{\vec{s}}{{\mathcal{C}}}_{\text{top, bulk}}(\vec{s},\vec{b})\,.

(G.26)

Let us classify the configurations
\vec{b}
near the lower boundary by the number and type of domain walls in them. Say there are
m

\vec{b}
, whose locations are labelled
x_{1},...,x_{m}
from left to right. Due to rule (a), we must have
x_{1}\geq x-t
and
x_{m}\leq x+t
, with the first domain wall between
\eta
and some
\tau_{1}
, and the last domain wall between some
\tau_{m-1}
and
\eta
. It is convenient to replace the
x_{i}
labels with the “velocities”
v_{i}\equiv\frac{x_{i}-x}{t}

Before analyzing the terms in (

and the regime of interest. For

For

{\rm OTOC}_{n}
below: terms proportional to

, where

is some non-trivial non-negative function of

, and terms proportional to

, where

is independent of

. The functions

are such that

, and these functions grow as

is decreased from 1 to 0. Hence, for any fixed large

dominate over terms of the form

. It is possible that deep within the lightcone (i.e. for

saturates when seen as a function of

. So we have the following structure (see Fig.

):

{\rm OTOC}_{n}(t,v)=\begin{cases}1&|v|>1\\
F(t,v)&v^{\ast}<|v|<1\\
e^{-\Delta t}&|v|<v^{\ast}\end{cases}

(G.29)

Here
F(t,v)
is a function with a non-trivial dependence on both
t
and
v
that comes from terms of the form
e^{-f(v)t}
.
In our calculation below, we will focus on the
e^{-f(v)t}
terms, which corresponds to being in the regime
v^{\ast}<|v|<1
. Analyzing terms of the form
e^{-\Delta t}
(and in particular, seeing whether
v^{\ast}>0
, or whether terms of the form

dominate for all
v
) will be beyond the scope of our calculation. In all cases where we write

below, it is implicit that we mean
\Delta
is independent of
v
.

Appendix I
Singular spectral density of
A(t)B
in Transverse Field Ising Model

The physics of the transverse field Ising model (TFIM) is most transparent in terms of fermionic variables defined by the Jordan-Wigner transformation of (
6.7
). We define the Majorana fermion operators as follows:

\gamma_{2j-1}\equiv\left(\prod_{i<j}Z_{i}\right)X_{j},\quad\gamma_{2j}\equiv\left(\prod_{i<j}Z_{i}\right)Y_{j}\ ,\quad j\in[L]

(I.1)

The Majorana fermion operators are Hermitian involutions
\gamma_{i}^{\dagger}=\gamma_{i},\ \gamma_{i}^{2}=I
. The Majoranas satisfy the canonical anticommutation relation (CAR)
\{\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}
.

The TFIM Hamiltonian can be written as a free Majorana fermion Hamiltonian,

H=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2L}\gamma_{i}K_{ij}\gamma_{j}

(I.2)

where
K
is a real antisymmetric matrix. With open boundary condition,
K_{2j-1,2j}=2h
,
K_{2j,2j+1}=2J
. Heisenberg evolution is represented as a linear transformation,

\gamma_{i}(t)=U(t)\gamma_{i}U(t)^{\dagger}=\sum_{j}O_{H}(t)_{ij}\gamma_{j},\quad O_{H}(t):=e^{Kt}\in SO(2L)

(I.3)

The evolved Majoranas
\gamma_{i}(t)
still satisfy the CAR.

The Majoranas
\{\gamma_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2L}
, subject to the CAR, generate the Clifford algebra Cl
{}_{2L}(\mathbb{C})
. It is a mathematical fact that Cl
{}_{2L}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes L})
, which refers to the algebra of bounded operators on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes L}
, the physical Hilbert space of the spin chain. This is a formal statement of the fact that two Majorana fermions can be associated with the Hilbert space of one qubit.

We want to calculate the spectrum of
A(t)B
to calculate the FMI and OTOCs. Let us focus on the case of
A
and
B
being
single-site
Paulis. Let’s first study the case of
A=Z_{i}
and
B=Z_{j}
. We can write
Z
in terms of the Majoranas

A=Z_{i}=-i\gamma_{2i-1}\gamma_{2i},\quad B=Z_{j}=-i\gamma_{2j-1}\gamma_{2j}\ .

(I.4)

We have

\displaystyle A(t)B

\displaystyle=-U(t)\gamma_{2i-1}\gamma_{2i}U(t)^{\dagger}\gamma_{2j-1}\gamma_{2j}=-U(t)\gamma_{2i-1}U(t)^{\dagger}U(t)\gamma_{2i}U(t)^{\dagger}\gamma_{2j-1}\gamma_{2j}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{2i-1}(t)\gamma_{2i}(t)\gamma_{2j-1}\gamma_{2j}\ .

(I.5)

Therefore,
A(t)B
belongs to the Clifford subalgebra Cl
{}_{4}(\mathbb{C})
generated by four Majoranas
\{\gamma_{2i-1}(t),\gamma_{2i}(t),\gamma_{2j-1},\gamma_{2j}\}
.
Then we use the mathematical fact that Cl
{}_{4}(\mathbb{C})\simeq\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes 2})
, and thus
A(t)B
can be irreducibly represented as acting on a four-dimensional subspace. We know that generally the eigenvalues of

\{e^{i\pm\theta_{i}}\}_{i}
. It follows that there exist two angles
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
and the four eigenvalues are

\{e^{i\pm\theta_{1}},e^{i\pm\theta_{2}}\}\ .

(I.6)

Hence, the spectrum of
A(t)B
represented on the full physical Hilbert space is largely degenerate. It follows that the FMI of
Z_{i}(t)
and

remains divergent throughout the TFIM evolution.

The same analysis holds for the

and

\tilde{\gamma}_{2j-1}:=\left(\prod_{i<j}X_{i}\right)Z_{j},\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{2j}=\left(\prod_{i<j}X_{i}\right)Y_{j}\ ,\quad j\in[L]
