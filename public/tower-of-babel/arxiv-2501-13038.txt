Abstract
. This paper studies the effective convergence of iterative methods for solving convex minimization problems using block Gauss–Seidel algorithms. It investigates whether it is always possible to algorithmically terminate the iteration in such a way that the outcome of the iterative algorithm satisfies any predefined error bound. It is shown that the answer is generally negative. Specifically, it is shown that even if a computable continuous function which is convex in each variable possesses computable minimizers, a block Gauss–Seidel iterative method might not be able to effectively compute any of these minimizers. This means that it is impossible to algorithmically terminate the iteration such that a given performance guarantee is satisfied. The paper discusses two reasons for this behavior. First, it might happen that certain steps in the Gauss–Seidel iteration cannot be effectively implemented on a digital computer. Second, all computable minimizers of the problem may not be reachable by the Gauss–Seidel method. Simple and concrete examples for both behaviors are provided.

Introduction

Many problems in physics and engineering can be formulated as optimization problems.
The most important example may be the second law of thermodynamics which may be restated as the principle of minimum energy.
But also problems of finding the optimal allocation of limited resources,
signal processing and signal recovery problems
[
22
,
20
]
,
solutions for compressed sampling problems
[
11
,
10
,
14
]
,
or problems in financial mathematics
[
5
]
and operations research can be formulated as optimization problems.
In particular, a large number of problems and challenges in artificial intelligence and data science are formulated as optimization problems
[
19
]
.
Modern computer technology makes these theoretical optimization problems extremely powerful engineering tools because
fast digital hardware allows one to solve even huge optimization problems in high dimensions very fast based on advanced algorithms developed over the past few decades for the different optimization problems.

Nevertheless, the question of whether a particular value ”solves” a certain problem depends on the actual requirements on the ”solution” by the user.
Already immediately after the introduction of the mathematical notion of
computing
, due to Turing
[
27
,
28
]
, it became clear that few physical problems can be solved exactly by digital computations.
For most problems, the exact solution can only be approximated (arbitrarily well) by the outcome of computations on a digital computer.
For this reason, Turing required that the result of the computing process satisfy a predefined bound on the approximation error.
We will discuss this requirement later in more detail, because the underlying question of this paper is whether it is always possible to control this approximation error for solutions obtained by iterative optimization methods.
At this point, we only mention that the algorithms from
[
22
,
20
,
11
,
10
,
5
]
as many other algorithms in applications, do not satisfy this condition, i.e. in all these algorithms it is not possible to control the difference between the computed ”solution” and the true value of the problem.
The mentioned limitation of digital computation mainly originates from the fact that a digital computer can compute exactly only with rational numbers.
Real numbers can generally not be represented exactly on a digital computer, but rather can only be approximated by rational numbers.
If a real number can effectively (i.e. by controlling the approximation error) be approximated by rational numbers, it is said to be
(Turing-) computable
(cf.
Section

2
for details). Otherwise it is said to be
non-computable
.

This paper studies some consequences of this limitation of digital computers on the ability to solve optimization problems on digital hardware.
We basically consider the following simple optimization problem in the
m
-dimensional real Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
:

(1)

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}}f(\mathbf{x})

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function and
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
It is well known
[
23
]
that the minimum value
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}}f(\mathbf{x})
of this optimization problem is always Turing computable.
Moreover, if the problem has a unique minimizer, i.e. if the set

(2)

\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)=\big\{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}:f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})=\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)\big\}

contains exactly one vector, then also this minimizer is Turing computable.
If the minimizer is not unique, then some or all of the minimizers might not be Turing computable
[
23
]
which means that these optimizers cannot effectively be computed on any digital computer.

Still, as long as the optimization problem contains at least one computable minimizer, this minimizer can, in principle, algorithmically be computed on a digital computer.
One only needs to construct a sequence
\left\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of computable vectors
\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that effectively converges to the computable minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
.
This is always possible, since the minimizer is computable, although finding such a sequence might be a fairly complicated and creative problem.
The question is now whether this process, of finding a computable sequence that effectively converges to a computable minimizer, can (efficiently) be automated on a digital computer
[
12
]
.

Practical algorithms usually apply a certain (suboptimal) strategy to find such a sequence
\big\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\big\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges to a minimizer of the given function.
A very popular and successful strategy is to minimize successively only over one (or several) coordinates of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
while keeping the other dimensions fixed.
This approach appears in the literature in many different variants and under various names such as block Gauss-Seidel methods, block coordinate decent method, or block coordinate update method, to mention only some of many variants of the general idea
[
3
,
30
,
14
,
16
,
25
,
31
]
.
Thus, if

(3)

\mathcal{R}=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]\times\dots\times[a_{m},b_{m}]

and if
\mathbf{x}^{(0)}=(x^{(0)}_{1},x^{(0)}_{2},\dots,x^{(0)}_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is an arbitrary initialization vector then one successively solves, for
\ell=1,2,\dots,m
, the one dimensional optimization problems

(4)

x_{\ell}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{y\in[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]}f(x_{1}^{(k+1)},\dots,x_{\ell-1}^{(k+1)},y,x_{\ell+1}^{(k)},\dots,x_{m}^{(k)})

and iterates then over
k=0,1,2,\dots
.
This strategy produces a sequence
\left\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that converges to a global minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
of
f
under fairly weak (convexity) conditions on
f
.
However, the so determined sequence
\left\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
may not
effectively
converge to the global minimizer.
Instead, it may happen that:

•

Some of the computational steps of the algorithm are not Turing computable, i.e. they may not be realizable on a digital computer with an effective control of the approximation error.

•

The sequence
\big\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\big\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
may converge to a non-computable minimizer of
f
.

Thus even through there exists a global computable optimizer of
f
, the automated procedure might not be able to find a corresponding approximation sequence
\left\{\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of computable vectors that effectively converges to this minimizer.
So the automated procedure, i.e. the algorithm, might not be able to compute a global minimizer of
f
.

In this paper, we study the described iterative optimization strategy for finding the global minimizer of a function
f
.
We will show that there exists a very simple (piecewise linear) function
f
that is convex in each coordinate (but not jointly convex),
that has infinitely many computable minimizers but such that the iterative optimization method is not able to find a computable approximation sequence that converges effectively to any of the computable minimizers.
In fact, we will give an example of a function
f
for which some of the computational steps of the iterative algorithm are not Turing computable,
and we will provide an example of a function
f
, for which the iterative algorithm will always converge to non-computable minimizers, even though
f
possesses also computable minimizers.

Section

3

Sections

4
and
5
will then provide examples that illustrate that the convergence of the iterative optimization algorithm is generally not effective.
The paper closes with a short discussion on possible extensions in
Section

and with summary in
Section

.

2.1.
Computability analysis

This section briefly reviews the main concepts an notion of computability analysis as far as they are needed in this paper.
We refer to standard textbooks (e.g.,
[
27
,
28
,
23
,
29
,
15
,
17
]
) for more detailed expositions.

The central concept of computability analysis is the notion of effective convergence.

Definition 1

(Effective convergence)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of real numbers that converges to
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
We say that
\boldsymbol{x}
converge effectively to
x
if

\left|x-x_{n}\right|\leq 2^{-n}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

So for a sequence that effectively converges, it is possible to control the approximation error
\left|x-x_{n}\right|
,
in the sense that for any arbitrary small approximation error
\epsilon=2^{-n}

it is possible to determine algorithmically an index
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that the approximation error is guaranteed to be less than
\epsilon
.

Every
x\in\mathbb{R}
is the limit of a sequence of rational numbers, but only if there exists a rational sequence that effectively converges to
x
, it is said to be computable.

Definition 2

(Computable number and vector)
.

An
x\in\mathbb{R}
is said to be
computable
if there exists a sequence
\left\{r_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers that converges effectively to
x
.
In this case, the sequence
\left\{r_{k}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be a
representation
of
x
.

A vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is said to be computable if each of its components is a computable number.

Subsequently, we write
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}
for the proper subfield of all computable real numbers and
\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}^{m}
for the set of all computable vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Definition 3

(Computable sequence)
.

A sequence
\boldsymbol{x}=\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of real numbers is said to be
computable
if there exists a double index sequence
\left\{r_{n,k}\right\}_{n,k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{Q}
of rational numbers such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

\left|x_{n}-r_{n,k}\right|<2^{-k}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N}\,.

A sequence
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is said to be computable if every component is a computable sequence.

Besides computable numbers and sequences,
computable functions
will play an important role in this paper.
There are different notions of computable functions and we refer to
[
2
]
for an overview of these different notions.
Here, we need in particular the following two:

Definition 4

(Computable function)
.

Let
m,M\in\mathbb{N}
. A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
is said to be

•

Borel–Turing computable
,
if there exists a Turing machine that transforms every representation
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbf{x}
to a representation of
f(\mathbf{x})
.

•

Banach–Mazur computable
,
if for every computable sequences
\left\{\mathbf{x}_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
the sequence
\left\{f(\mathbf{x}_{n})\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
is computable.

We note that the notion of being Banach–Mazur computable is more general than being Borel–Turing computable.
Thus every Borel–Turing computable function is also Banach–Mazur computable but there exist Banach–Mazur computable functions that are not Borel–Turing computable.

A well known example of a function that is not Banach–Mazur computable is the sign function.
This example will be of some importance in this paper.
Therefore we state this result here in the form that is needed later.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
,
a>0
, let
\alpha\in[-1,1]
be arbitrary, and let
G_{\alpha}:[-a,a]\to\mathbb{R}
be the function defined by

G_{\alpha}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}1&:&x<0\\
\alpha&:&x=0\\
-1&:&x>0\end{array}\right..

Then
G_{\alpha}
is not Banach–Mazur computable and therefore not Borel–Turing computable.

Sketch of proof.

Assume
\alpha\in[-1,1]
is not a computable number. Then
G_{\alpha}(0)\notin\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
and so
G_{\alpha}
cannot be Banach–Mazur computable.
If
\alpha\in[-1,1]
is a computable number, then we can find a computable sequence of computable numbers
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\left\{G_{\alpha}(x_{n})\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not a computable sequence of computable numbers.
To construct such a sequence
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
, we can use a technique from the proof of
[
8
, Theorem
2
]
.
∎

Definition 5

(Computable continuous function)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a computable rectangle.
A function
f:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
effectively uniformly continuous
if there exits a recursive function
\mathrm{d}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
n=\mathrm{d}(k)

\left\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\|\leq 2^{-n}\quad\text{implies}\quad\left|f(\mathbf{x}_{1})-f(\mathbf{x}_{2})\right|\leq 2^{-k}\,.

A function that is Banach–Mazur computable and effectively uniformly continuous is called a
computable continuous function
and
we write
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathcal{R})
for the set of all computable continuous functions on the rectangle
\mathcal{R}
.

Similarly,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{c}}^{k}(\mathcal{R})
stands for the set of all
k
-times continuously differentiable computable functions whose partial derivatives up to order
k
are all computable continuous functions.

3.
Optimization of smooth functions

This section explains in greater detail the iterative optimization algorithm that will be studied in this paper and introduces some more notation needed to formulate and to prove our main results.

3.1.
Minimum value and minimizer

We consider the following general minimization problem with a so-called box constraint
[
9
]
:
Let

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\mathcal{R}_{2}\times\dots\times\mathcal{R}_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}

be a computable rectangle with
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
and
\sum^{r}_{\ell=1}n_{\ell}=m
,
and let
f:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function on
\mathcal{R}
.
Then we may ask for the
minimum value
of
f
on the rectangle
\mathcal{R}
, i.e. for the value

(6)

\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}}f(\mathbf{x})=\!\!\!\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}_{\ell}\in\mathcal{R}_{l},1\leq\ell\leq r\end{subarray}}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{r}).

Apart from the problem of finding the minimum value
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
of
f
on
\mathcal{R}
,
we may ask for a corresponding
minimizer
, i.e. for a vector

(7)

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}\quad\text{such that}\quad f\left(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\right)=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}}f(\mathbf{x})\,.

The minimizer is generally not unique but there may be a whole set (
2
) of
global minimizers
in
\mathcal{R}
, i.e. a set of vectors that satisfy (
7
).

It depends on the actual application whether one needs to find the minimum value or the minimizer.
However, from the previous definition, it is clear that if one knows a minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
then one also knows the minimum value
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)=f(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})
.
Conversely, knowing
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
may not help in finding a corresponding minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
.
This observation indicates that the problem of finding the minimizer is usually harder than just determining the minimum value.
Unfortunately, in practical application the minimizer is often much more important than the minimum value.
In fact, the minimum value often has no particular meaning or significance, rather the point where this minimum value is attained is of importance.

Remark 1
.

Instead of the minimization problem, one may consider a corresponding maximization problem,
i.e. the problem finding the maximum value
\mathrm{Max}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
of
f
or the maximizer of
f
on
\mathcal{R}
.
Such a maximization problem can always be transformed into a minimization problem by considering the function
-f
on
\mathcal{R}
.
Then the minimizer of
-f
is the maximizer of
f
.
So without loss of generality, this paper only discusses the minimization problem (
6
).

3.2.
Computability of the minimum value and the minimizer

Apart from very special cases, there exists no closed-form solution for the minimum value
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
or for the minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
of a minimization problem.
Therefore, these values are usually approximated using numerical algorithms that determine a sequence that converge to the optimal value.
For a wide variety of optimization problems, algorithms are known that converge to the minimum value or minimizer, respectively.
Most notable are certainly the many different algorithms developed for convex optimization problems
[
9
,
6
,
4
]
.
Nevertheless, from a practical point of view, the question is not only whether the algorithm converges to the optimum but whether this convergence is
effective
,
i.e. whether it is possible to control the approximation error and to stop algorithmically the computation if a desired error bound is achieved.
This problem of effective convergence is equivalent to the question of whether the minimum value or the minimizer are computable.

With respect to the computation of the minimum value
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
, the following result concerning its computability is well known
(cf., e.g.,
[
23
, Chapter
6
]
).

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,r
, be arbitrary computable rectangles, and let
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{R}_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
There exists a Turing machine
\mathrm{TM}_{\mathrm{Min}}
that computes for every computable continuous function
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathcal{R})
the value
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
.

Proposition

3.1
shows that the minimum value (
6
) is always algorithmically computable on a digital computer provided
f
is a computable continuous function.
Note in particular that the Turing machine
\mathrm{TM}_{\mathrm{Min}}
in
Proposition

3.1
is
universal
in the sense that it only depends on the rectangle
\mathcal{R}
.
So for a fixed
\mathcal{R}
, the corresponding
\mathrm{TM}_{\mathrm{Min}}
can compute
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
for all
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathcal{R})
as input.
Thus, if
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathrm{TM}_{\mathrm{Min}}
are fixed and if
f\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{c}}(\mathcal{R})
is arbitrary,
then for every description of
f
the Turing machine
\mathrm{TM}_{\mathrm{Min}}
effectively computes a description of the real number
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)

[
19
]
.

With respect to the computation of the minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
, it is known that if the minimization problem has a unique (global) minimizer, i.e. if the set (
2
) contains only one vector, then this minimizer is always computable (cf., e.g.,
[
23
, Chapter
I.0.6
]
).
If the minimizer is not unique then some (or all) minimizers might not be computable and there exist several examples of computable continuous functions
f
that attain their minimum only at non-computable points (see, e.g.,
[
26
]
and references in
[
23
]
).

3.3.
Iterative optimization methods

The global optimization problem (
6
) that minimizes jointly over all
m
components of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is often considered as being too complex.
Therefore, one applies block coordinate optimization methods of the Gauss–Seidel type that iteratively optimize over sub-rectangles
\mathcal{R}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,r
while keeping the other variables fixed
[
3
,
24
,
30
,
21
,
25
,
16
,
31
,
32
]
.
Starting with an initial guess for the minimizer
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(0)},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{2}^{(0)},\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(0)})\in\mathcal{R}
, one solves for
k=0
the optimization problems

(8)

\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell}^{(k+1)}=\arg\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{R}_{\ell}}f\big(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{1}^{(k+1)},\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell-1}^{(k+1)},\mathbf{y},\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\ell+1}^{(k)},\dots,\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}_{r}^{(k)}\big)

successively for
\ell=1,2,\dots,r
and iterates over
k=1,2,\dots
.
In particular, if
n_{1}=n_{2}=...=n_{\ell}=1
then each step optimizes only over one coordinate of the vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
while leaving all other coordinates fixed, (cf. (
4
)).
This procedure yields a sequence
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of approximations of a minimizer of the optimization problem (
6
).
The components of each vector
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}
are minimizers of a local optimization problem according to (
8
). Therefore we say that
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
sequence of local minimizers
.

One can show that under some mild conditions on
f
, this sequence of local minimizers converges to a global minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
of the optimization problem (
6
).
In fact, there exist many studies that investigate the convergence behavior of iterative algorithms for solving optimization problems of the form (
1
) (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
16
,
25
,
32
]
).
All these investigations consider the convergence of sequences
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
that were obtained by an iterative optimization algorithm.
The strongest convergence statements of these investigations have typically the following form:
Let
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of local minimizers determined by an iterative optimization algorithm.
Then, under some conditions on
f
, this sequence has at least one limit point which implies (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
) that all limit points of this sequence are critical points of (
1
).
Apart from results regarding the convergence of iterative optimization algorithms there seems to exist no estimates on the convergence speed of these algorithms.
However, such results are highly desirable from a practical point of view because the convergence results alone imply, in principle, that the iterative algorithm has to compute
ad infinitum
to reach the optimal value.
In practice, however, one needs a criterion to stop the iteration if a desired error bound is achieved,
i.e. one needs the possibility to pass an integer
M\in\mathbb{N}
to the algorithm such that the algorithm is able to stop the iteration at
K\in\mathbb{N}
if
\left|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(K)}\right|<2^{-M}

Up to now, no such algorithm is known and the following results will show that generally no such algorithm can exist, even in the simple case

Remark 2
.

The non-existence of such an algorithmic stopping criterion was recently observed in several central and concrete problems in information theory.
One example is the celebrated
Blahut–Arimoto algorithm

[
7
,
1
]
. It computes an infinite sequence of input distributions of a channel that converges to the capacity achieving input distribution
[
13
]
.
Since its invention, researchers tried to find a computable stopping algorithm that is able to stop the iteration based on a required approximation error.
To date, no such algorithm was found and
[
8
]
showed that no such computable stopping exists for the Blahut–Arimoto algorithm.
We refer to
[
18
]
for more information theoretic questions that show a similar behavior.

Presupposing a sequence of local minimizers

converges to a global minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
of (

), this paper asks whether this convergence is always effective, i.e. whether we are able to algorithmically stop the iteration if a predefined approximation error is achieved.

Question 1

Given a sequence
\big\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\big\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of local minimizers that converges to a global minimizer

of (

Is this convergence always effective?

As a second problem, we note that the

4.3.
Approximation of the assignment function

The main problem in solving the minimization problem (
12
) for the function
f_{1}
given in
Theorem

4.1
using the iterative coordinate-wise Algorithm
1
arises from the non-computability of the assignment functions
G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f_{1})
.
This raises the question of whether it is possible to replace the non-computable function by an appropriate computable function
\widetilde{G}_{1}
that allows one to compute an approximation
\widetilde{x}^{(k+1)}_{1}=\widetilde{G}_{1}(x^{(k)}_{2})
of
x^{(k+1)}_{1}=G_{1}(x^{(k)}_{2})
.

We will show that this is generally impossible.
In fact, even if we merely require that the error
\big|\widetilde{x}^{(k+1)}_{1}-x^{(k+1)}_{1}\big|
is upper bounded by a fixed constant
\epsilon\leq\max(1,a)
there exists no computable function
\widetilde{G}_{1}
of
G_{1}
that guarantees

\left|\widetilde{x}^{(k+1)}_{1}-x^{(k+1)}_{1}\right|=\left|\widetilde{G}_{1}(x^{(k)}_{2})-G_{1}(x^{(k)}_{2})\right|<\epsilon

at every iteration step
k
.
Of course, if it is not possible to guarantee a fixed error bound then it is
a fortiori
not possible to satisfy Turing’s requirement of an effective approximation that satisfies any arbitrary small approximation error.

The following theorem proves that the described approximation of the assignment function is not possible.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
,
a,b>0
and consider the minimization problem on the rectangle
\mathcal{R}=[-a,a]\times[-b,b]
for the function
f_{1}
, constructed in
Theorem

4.1
.
Let
G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f_{1})
be an arbitrary assignment function (
14
) and let
G:[-b,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a function that satisfies

\sup_{x_{2}\in[-b,b]}\left|G_{1}(x_{2})-G(x_{2})\right|<\min(1,a)\,.

Then
G
is not Turing computable.

This theorem shows that it is not possible to approximate a non-computable assignment function by a computable function such that the maximum error is less than
a
(if
a\leq 1
) or less than
1
(if
a>1
).

Proof.

Let
G_{1}:[-b,b]\to[-a,a]
and

be assignment functions of

as derived in the proof of

4.1

More precisely,

is given by (

is simply the zero function:

for all

First we consider the case
a\geq 1
.
Assume that there exists an assignment function
G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f_{1})
such that there is a Turing computable function

that satisfies

\sup_{x_{2}\in[-b,b]}\big|G_{1}(x_{2})-G_{*}(x_{2})\big|=\gamma<1\,.

Then for all

\left|1-G_{*}(x_{2})\right|\leq\gamma

G_{*}(x_{2})\geq 1-\gamma=:\delta>0

which implies
G_{*}(x_{2})\leq\gamma-1=-\delta<0

So

is discontinuous at

Therefore, one can show (in the same way as in the proof of

Second we consider the case

Indeed, for all

f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\geq f_{1}(a,x_{2})
for all
x_{1}\in[-a,a]
and

\min_{x_{1}\in[-a,a]}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=f_{1}(a,x_{2})\,.

Similarly, for all
x_{2}>0
, one has
f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\geq f_{1}(-a,x_{2})
for all
x_{1}\in[-a,a]
and

\min_{x_{1}\in[-a,a]}f_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=f_{1}(-a,x_{2})\,.

Moreover, every
x_{1}\in[-a,a]
is a minimizer of the function
f_{1}(\cdot,0)
.
Consequently, the assignment function
G_{1}
becomes

G_{1}(x_{2})=G_{1,\alpha}(x_{2})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}a&:&x_{2}\in[-b,0)\\
\alpha&:&x_{2}=0\\
-a&:&x_{2}\in(0,b]\end{array}\right.

for some arbitrary
\alpha\in[-a,a]
.
Now, we can apply the same arguments as for
a<0
to show that
G_{1}
cannot be approximated by a computable function.
∎

5.
Reachability of global minimizers

We still consider the optimization problem (
6
)
on a computable rectangle
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\mathcal{R}_{2}
with
\mathcal{R}_{1}=[-a,a]
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}=[-b,b]
for some positive
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
and with a given function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
This section studies the behavior of the assignment functions
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
associated with the iterative optimization problem in more detail.
In particular, we consider the point sets

\left\{(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\{(x_{1},G_{2}(x_{1}))\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}\right\}\,.

By the
Definition

6
of the assignment functions, we certainly have

\displaystyle G_{1}(x_{2})\in\mathcal{MIN}_{1}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}f(x_{1},x_{2})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle G_{2}(x_{1})\in\mathcal{MIN}_{2}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}f(x_{1},x_{2})\,.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=(\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2})\in\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
be an arbitrary global minimizer of
f
.
We want to study the behavior of the points
(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
x_{2}
approaches
\widehat{x}_{2}

and the behavior of the points
(x_{1},G_{2}(x_{1}))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
x_{1}
approaches
\widehat{x}_{1}
.
To this end, we define the sets

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{1}(\widehat{x}_{2})=\left\{\mathbf{x}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2}\to\widehat{x}_{2}\\
x_{2}>\widehat{x}_{2}\end{subarray}}\big(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2}\big)\ :\ G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f)\right\}

and

\mathcal{G}^{-}_{1}(\widehat{x}_{2})=\left\{\mathbf{x}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2}\to\widehat{x}_{2}\\
x_{2}<\widehat{x}_{2}\end{subarray}}\big(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2}\big)\ :\ G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f)\right\}\,,

i.e. the set of all limits of points
\left(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
x_{2}
converges to
\widehat{x}_{2}
from above and below, respectively.
Similarly, we define the sets

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{2}(\widehat{x}_{1})=\left\{\mathbf{x}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}\to\widehat{x}_{1}\\
x_{1}>\widehat{x}_{1}\end{subarray}}\big(x_{1},G_{2}(x_{1})\big)\ :\ G_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}(f)\right\}

and

\mathcal{G}^{-}_{2}(\widehat{x}_{1})=\left\{\mathbf{x}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1}\to\widehat{x}_{1}\\
x_{1}<\widehat{x}_{1}\end{subarray}}\big(x_{1},G_{2}(x_{1})\big)\ :\ G_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{2}(f)\right\}\,.

By these definitions, we have

\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{1}(\widehat{x}_{2})\subset\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{2}(\widehat{x}_{1})\subset\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)

and we notice that these inclusions are generally strict.
This motivates the following definition.

Definition 7

(Reachability along coordinates)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=(\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2})\in\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
be an arbitrary global minimizer of
f
.
We say that
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
is
reachable along the coordinate

x_{2}
if

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{G}^{+}_{1}(\widehat{x}_{2})\cup\mathcal{G}^{-}_{1}(\widehat{x}_{2})\,,

and
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
is
reachable along the coordinate

x_{1}
if

\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{G}^{+}_{2}(\widehat{x}_{1})\cup\mathcal{G}^{-}_{2}(\widehat{x}_{1})\,.

Remark 9
.

In other words
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in(\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2})
is reachable along the coordinate
x_{2}
if there is a
G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f)
such that

\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2}\to\widehat{x}_{2}\\
x_{2}\neq\widehat{x}_{2}\end{subarray}}\big(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2}\big)\in\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)\,.

Example 1
.

We consider the function
f_{1}
, defined in
Theorem

4.1
(cf. also
Figure

1
).
The set of all global minimizers of
f_{1}
is given by
\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f_{1})=\left\{(x_{1},0):x_{1}\in[-1,1]\right\}
.

For all
x_{2}>0
, we have
(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2})=(-1,x_{2})
and for all
x_{2}<0
, we have
(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2})=(1,x_{2})
, so that

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{1}(0)=(-1,0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{G}^{-}_{1}(0)=(1,0)\,.

Similarly, since
(x_{1},G_{2}(x_{1}))=(x_{1},0)
for all
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
we have
\mathcal{G}^{+}_{2}(x_{1})=\mathcal{G}^{-}_{2}(x_{1})=(x_{1},0)

for every
x_{1}\in[-1,1]
.

Thus all points in
\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f_{1})
are reachable along the coordinate
x_{1}

but only the points
(-1,0)
and
(1,0)
in
\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f_{1})
are reachable along the coordinate
x_{2}
.

The importance of
Definition

7
stems from the observation that if a minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
is not reachable along a certain coordinate, then the iterative coordinate-wise algorithm will not be able to compute this minimizer.
In such a case it might happen that even though the function
f
has global minimizers that are Turing computable, the iterative coordinate-wise algorithm may not be able to compute them because they are not reachable along a certain coordinate.

The following theorem provides such an example, namely it gives a function
f_{2}
such that all global optimizers of
f_{2}
that are reachable along the coordinate
x_{2}
are not Turing computable points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
with
a,b>0
be arbitrary and let
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{1}\times\mathcal{R}_{2}
with
\mathcal{R}_{1}=[-a,a]
,
\mathcal{R}_{2}=[-b,b]
.
There exists a computable continuous function
f_{2}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{C}
with the following properties

(1)

f_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

(2)

The function
f_{2}(\cdot,x_{2})
is strictly convex for every fixed
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,
x_{2}\neq 0
, and
f_{2}(\cdot,x_{2})
is it a computable continuous function for every
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
.

(3)

The function
f_{2}(x_{1},\cdot)
is strictly convex for every fixed
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f_{2}(x_{1},\cdot)
is a computable continuous function for every
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
.

(4)

f_{2}
has only global optimizers and the set of all global optimizers is a closed interval on the
x_{1}
-axis.

(5)

Every
(\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2})\in\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f_{2})
that can be reached along the coordinate
x_{2}
is not Turing computable in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

As an immediate consequence of the last statement of this theorem we obtain the following negative answer to
Question

1
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
f_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function defined in (the proof of)

and let

be an arbitrary sequence that converges to a global minimum
(\widehat{x}_{1},\widehat{x}_{2})\in\mathcal{MIN}_{\mathcal{R}}(f_{2})
of
f_{2}
, then this convergence cannot be effective.

.

Next we investigate the behavior of
G_{1}(x_{2})
as

converges to zero from above and from below, respectively.
First, we consider the case
x_{2}>0
and note again that
G_{1}(x_{2})
is the minimizer of
f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
with respect to
x_{1}

0=\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2})=g_{*}^{\prime}(x_{1})+\alpha\,x^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha x_{1}}=:F(x_{1},x_{2})\,.

Now we choose two arbitrary points
\widetilde{x}_{2}>x_{2}>0
with the corresponding values
x_{1}=G_{1}(x_{2})
and
\widetilde{x}_{1}=G_{1}(\widetilde{x}_{2})

\displaystyle F(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}_{*}(x_{1})+\alpha x^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha x_{1}}=0

\displaystyle F(\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2})

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}_{*}(\widetilde{x}_{1})+\alpha\widetilde{x}^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\widetilde{x}_{1}}=0\,.

Setting the difference of these equations to zero, i.e.
F(\widetilde{x}_{1},\widetilde{x}_{2})-F(x_{1},x_{2})=0
, yields

\frac{1}{\alpha}\big[g^{\prime}_{*}(x_{1})-g^{\prime}_{*}(\widetilde{x}_{1})\big]=\widetilde{x}^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\widetilde{x}_{1}}-x^{2}_{2}\,\mathrm{e}^{\alpha x_{1}}\,.

Next we use a first order Taylor expansion to get a lower bound for the value

\mathrm{e}^{\alpha\widetilde{x}_{1}}\geq\mathrm{e}^{\alpha x_{1}}+\alpha\mathrm{e}^{\alpha x_{1}}(\widetilde{x}_{1}-x_{1})

\widetilde{x}^{2}_{2}-x^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\big[g^{\prime}_{*}(x_{1})-g^{\prime}_{*}(\widetilde{x}_{1})\big]\,\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha x_{1}}+\alpha\,\widetilde{x}^{2}_{2}(x_{1}-\widetilde{x}_{1})\leq\big[\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha x_{1}}+\alpha\widetilde{x}^{2}_{2}\big]\left(x_{1}-\widetilde{x}_{1}\right)\,.

\widetilde{x}_{2}>x_{2}\quad\text{implies}\quad\widetilde{x}_{1}=G_{1}(\widetilde{x}_{2})<G_{1}(x_{2})=x_{1}\,,

i.e. for any monotonically decreasing positive sequence

0<x^{(k+1)}_{2}<x^{(k)}_{2}\,,\ \text{for all}\ k\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{(k)}_{2}=0\,,

-\xi_{*}

F_{1}(x_{2})=\min_{x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}}f_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
,
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By the definition of
f_{2}
, it follows that
F_{1}
is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
F_{1}(x_{2})=f_{2}(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2})
and with

(21)

\lim_{x_{2}\to 0}F_{1}(x_{2})=0\,.

Assume now that
\widehat{x}_{1}(+0)<-\xi_{*}
. Then the continuity of
F_{1}
implies

\lim_{x_{2}\to+0}F_{1}(x_{2})=\!\!\lim_{x_{2}\to+0}f_{2}\big(G_{1}(x_{2}),x_{2}\big)=f_{2}\big(\widehat{x}_{1}(+0),x_{2}\big)>0

which contradicts (
21

\widehat{x}_{1}(+0)=-\xi_{*}
.
In the same way, one considers the case
x_{2}<0

\lim_{x_{2}\to+0}G_{1}(x_{2})=-\xi_{*}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{x_{2}\to-0}G_{1}(x_{2})=\xi_{*}\,.

It is important to note that this holds for all (uncountably many) assignment functions
G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{1}(f_{2})

This shows that

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{1}(0)=\left\{(-\xi_{*},0)\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{G}^{1}_{1}(0)=\left\{(\xi_{*},0)\right\}\,.

i.e. each of these two sets contain only one point. So only these two points are reachable along the coordinate
x_{2}
. Since
\xi_{*}\notin\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{c}}
this finishes the proof. ∎

6.
Extensions

The functions
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
constructed in
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
are fairly simply. Since these functions are defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the corresponding block coordinate optimization method (
8
) is automatically a coordinate-wise iterative optimization as given in Algorithm
1
.
However, the derived results hold also for general block coordinate optimization methods as discussed at the beginning of
Section

3.3
with at least two blocks.

The main results of this paper (
Theorems

4.1
and
5.1
) are formulated for constraint minimization problems of the form (
1
).
However, it becomes clear from the constructions of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
in the proofs of these two theorems, that the statements hold also for unconstrained optimization problems.
Indeed, for the proofs of these statements, it was merely important that the functions only have global minima (i.e. the functions have no local minima that are not also global minima) and that all these global minima lie in a bounded region of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

7.
Summary and discussion

To solve the optimization problem (
1
) on a digital computer, one aims for an algorithm with two inputs: the function
f
and an integer
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
Based on these two inputs, the algorithm should compute
\mathrm{Min}_{\mathcal{R}}(f)
and a corresponding minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
with a guaranteed error of at most
2^{-M}
.
A common way to derive such an algorithm is based on the application of an iterative optimization strategy that optimizes successively over the single coordinates (cf. Algorithm
1
).

We have shown in this paper that even for a fixed given function
f_{*}
, it might be impossible to find an effective implementation of such an iterative optimization algorithm.
Since it is impossible to construct a specific algorithm for the fixed function
f_{*}
, it is
a fortiori
impossible to construct a general algorithm who takes
f
as an input and which is able solve the optimization problem for a larger set (including
f_{*}
) of functions.
The paper discussed two reasons why such an effective implementation of the iterative optimization procedure may fail.

1)

The
\arg\min
-operation (
8
) inside the iteration might not be Turing computable for the given function
f_{*}
.

2)

The iterative algorithm converges for any arbitrary initialization vector to a non-computable minimizer of
f_{*}
.

Sections

4
and
5
provided two concrete functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
such that a behavior according to 1) and 2) occur, respectively.

Behavior 1) was illustrated by an example in
Theorem

4.1
.
It was shown that the iterative algorithm converges (after at most two steps) to one of the computable global optimizers
(1,0)
or
(-1,0)
of the function
f_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.
However, for any arbitrary initialization vector, already the first
\arg\min
-operation is not Turing computable which means that this step cannot be computed effectively on a Turing machine.
Even more, not only can the
\arg\min
-operation generally not be solved algorithmically, it is even not possible to have a non-trivial approximation of this iterative optimization step (cf.
Section

4.3
).

Behavior 2) was illustrated by a function
f_{2}
in
Theorem

5.1
.
For this function, the iterative algorithm generates a sequence
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of local minimizers that always converges either to the global minimizer
(-\xi_{*},0)
or to the global minimizer
(\xi_{*},0)
, even thought there exist uncountably many other global minimizers.
Then, if
\xi_{*}
is not a computable number, the convergence towards these two minimizers cannot be effective.

We would like to emphasize, that the shown negative property is a consequence of the local (coordinate-wise) optimization strategy.
Indeed, the two functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
constructed in
Sections

4
and
5
, respectively, both have at least one global minimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
that is computable.
Therefore it is always possible to find a computable sequence
\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
that effectively converges to
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
.
Indeed, since
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
is computable, each component
\widehat{x}_{n}
,
n=1,2,\dots,m
of
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
is a computable number and so there exists a Turing machine
\mathrm{TM}_{n}
that computes for input
M\in\mathbb{N}
a rational number
\widetilde{x}_{n}(M)=\mathrm{TM}_{n}(M)
such that

\left|\widehat{x}_{n}-\widetilde{x}_{n}(M)\right|<2^{-M}
for every
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then it follows that the sequence
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(M)\right\}_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(M)=(\widetilde{x}_{1}(M),\dots,\widetilde{x}_{m}(M))
satisfies

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(M)\right\|_{2}<\sqrt{m}\,2^{-M}\,,\quad\text{for all}\ M\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Thus
\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}(M)\right\}_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
converges effectively to the global optimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
,
and so we have found an effective numerical procedure to compute the global optimizer
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
.
So for both functions, there exists a numerical algorithm for computing the global minimizer of (
1
).
However, such an algorithm is based on a global optimization strategy. Iterative algorithms that apply a local (i.e. a coordinate-wise) optimization strategy cannot effectively solve (
1
).

Our results are also relevant for cases where a global optimization is hard to implement or where it is even impossible to implement such a global optimization (cf., e.g.,
[
22
,
20
]
).
As an example consider a decentralized communication system where it is impossible to collect global information about the whole network at a central point, but where the optimization has to performed locally, based on only restricted knowledge on the network.
Our results are relevant for these scenarios since they correspond to a local (decentralized) optimization, discussed in this paper.

We would also like to point out that our findings are in sharp contrast to the common heuristic.
By this heuristic, it is assumed that a global, jointly optimization over all variables (i.e. over all degrees of freedoms) is too complex, but it is less complex to optimize locally over some (or even a single) dimensions while keeping the other coordinates fixed and to iterate over all dimensions.
However, as discussed in this paper, the local optimization strategy needs to solve iteratively the
\arg\min
-operation dependent on some parameters (the fixed coordinates).
We have shown that the parameter dependent minimizers are uniquely determined, but even for very simple functions in two variables, the mapping from the parameters to the unique minimizer is generally not Turing computable (cf.
Theorem

4.1
).
Thus the unique minimizer can generally not be computed effectively from the parameters.
In other words, the computation of the local minimizers of the iterative algorithm is even too complex for a digital computer.
Nevertheless, the function
f_{1}
from
Theorem

4.1
has very simple properties that allows one to determine a global minimizer by standard joint optimization.
So this result is contrary to the common heuristic that an iterative local optimization is less complex than a joint global optimization.

Appendix A
An auxiliary function

In the proof of
Theorem

5.1
we need a particular auxiliary function
g_{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
This appendix defines
g_{*}
and proves properties of
g_{*}
needed in the proof of
Theorem

5.1
.
To this end, we first choose an arbitrary number
\xi_{*}\in(0,1)
that is the limit of a computable sequence
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{Q}
of strictly monotonically decreasing rational numbers, i.e.

(22)

\xi_{n+1}<\xi_{n}\,,\ \text{for all}\ n\in\mathbb{N}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi_{n}=\xi_{*}\,.

Remark 11
.

It is important to notice that we require only that
\xi_{*}
is the limit of a monotonic decreasing sequence. So there might exist no monotonically increasing sequence that converges to
\xi_{*}
.
In such a case
\xi_{*}
would not be a computable number. So in other words, we explicitly allow
\xi_{*}
to be non-computable.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define a continuously differentiable function
g_{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
by

g_{n}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}(x+\xi_{n})^{2}&:&x<-\xi_{n}\\
0&:&-\xi_{n}\leq x\leq\xi_{n}\\
(x-\xi_{n})^{2}&:&x>\xi_{n}\end{array}\right..

Therewith, we define finally the function

(23)

g_{*}(x)=\textstyle\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}2^{-n}\,g_{n}(x)\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\,.

The function
g_{*}
depends on the chosen
\xi_{*}
and on the chosen sequence
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
However, the important properties of
g_{*}
that are needed in this paper are independent of the choice of
\xi_{*}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.

Figure

3
illustrates the shape of
g_{*}
for some values of
\xi_{*}
, and
the next lemma collects the properties of
g_{*}
that are needed in the proof
Theorem

5.1
.

Illustration of the function
g_{*}
defined in (
23
) for
\xi_{*}=1/20
(blue),
\xi_{*}=1/2
(red), and

(green).
In all three cases, the sequence
\xi_{n}=\xi_{*}+2^{-n}
,

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\xi_{*}\in(0,1)
be arbitrary and let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=\left\{\xi_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{Q}
be a sequence that satisfies (
22
).
Then the function
g_{*}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined in (
23
) has the following properties:

(1)

g_{*}
is even on
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
g_{*}(-x)=g_{*}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

g_{*}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(3)

g_{*}(x)=0
for all
x\in[-\xi_{*},\xi_{*}]
.

(4)

g_{*}
is continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
.

(5)

The first derivative
g^{\prime}_{*}
is piecewise linear and
g^{\prime}_{*}(x_{1})-g^{\prime}_{*}(\widetilde{x}_{1})\leq 2\left(x_{1}-\widetilde{x}_{1}\right)
for all
x_{1},\widetilde{x}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.

(6)

g_{*}
is convex.

(7)

g_{*}
is a computable continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Properties 1 – 3 are obvious from the definition.
Property 4 follows because the series (
23
) that defines
g_{*}
as well as the corresponding series for
g^{\prime}_{*}
are absolute convergent.

To verify Property 5, we define for
n=1,2,3,\dots
, the intervals

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{I}_{-1}=(-\infty,-\xi_{1})&\text{and}&\mathcal{I}_{1}=(\xi_{1},\infty)\\[3.01385pt]
\mathcal{I}_{-n}=[-\xi_{n-1},-\xi_{n})&\text{and}&\mathcal{I}_{n}=(\xi_{n},\xi_{n-1}]\\[3.01385pt]
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}=[-\xi_{*},\xi_{*}]\,.\end{array}

Then (
23
) can be written as

g_{*}(x)=\sum^{\infty}_{k=n}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\left(x-\xi_{k}\right)^{2}\,,\quad\text{for}\ x\in\mathcal{I}_{n}

and
n=1,2,\dots
, and similarly for
x\in\mathcal{I}_{-n}
.
Differentiating term by term within the sum yields

(24)

g_{*}^{\prime}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}c_{1}(n)\,x+c_{0}(n)&:&x\in\mathcal{I}_{-n}\\
0&:&x\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}\\
c_{1}(n)\,x-c_{0}(n)&:&x\in\mathcal{I}_{n}\end{array}\right.,

with positive constants
c_{0}(n)
and
c_{1}(n)
that satisfy

(25)

\displaystyle 0<

\displaystyle c_{1}(n)=\frac{1}{2^{n-2}}\leq 2\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\ c_{1}(n)\,\xi_{*}\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}(n)=\sum^{\infty}_{k=n}\frac{\xi_{k}}{2^{k-1}}\leq c_{1}(n)\,\xi_{n}\,.

This shows that
g^{\prime}_{*}
is linear on all intervals
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and one can easily verify (directly) that
g^{\prime}_{*}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
.
Property 5 follows from the observation that the slopes of the linear pieces are given by
c_{1}(n)
which satisfy (
25
).

The convexity, i.e., Property 6, follows from (
24
). It shows that
g^{\prime}_{*}
is continuous and monotonically increasing because the sequence
\left\{c_{1}(n)\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is monotonically decreasing.
The computability of
g_{*}
follows from the observation that
g_{*}
is a polynomial on every interval
\mathcal{I}_{n}
and that the boundary points
\xi_{n}
of these intervals are rational numbers.
