On Runge-Kutta convolution quadrature based fractional variational integrators
Khaled Hariz Belgacem, Sina Ober-Blöbaum, Fernando Jiménez

Abstract
. Lagrangian systems subject to fractional damping can be incorporated into a variational formalism. The construction can be made by doubling the state variables and introducing fractional derivatives [ 33 ] . The main objective of this paper is to use the Runge–Kutta convolution quadrature (RKCQ) method for approximating fractional derivatives, combined with higher order Galerkin methods in order to derive fractional variational integrators (FVIs). We are specially interested in the CQ based on Lobatto IIIC. Preservation properties such as energy decay as well as convergence properties are investigated numerically and proved for 2nd order schemes. The presented schemes reach 2nd, 4th and 6th accuracy order. A brief discussion on the midpoint fractional integrator is also included.

Introduction

Differential equations are extensively used to model many phenomena in science and engineering, however, they often might not be capable in describing systems having long-term memory property due to their local nature. For instance, phenomena like anomalous diffusion, deformations, viscoelastic materials

Their nonlocal nature allows the current behavior of a system to depend not only on the present but also on its entire past history, leading to more accurate and realistic representations of complex phenomena.
We refer to

for a comprehensive overview of the fractional calculus theory. Although fractional calculus has many applications, developing analytical solutions for fractional differential equations remains challenging. Some modern applications of fractional calculus to physical systems, complex media, quantum dynamics and more can be found in
[

. Applications in mechanics such as non-local elasticity,
viscoelasticity models, heat conduction diffusion problems are treated in
[

]
.
See also
[

,

for applications in control, engineering, life and social sciences.

Recently, fractional calculus has emerged as a powerful tool in the study of dynamical systems. Its applications extend across a wide range of models, including dissipative and conservative systems as well as constrained dynamical systems
[

,

,

,

,

. Of particular interest in this work are fractional variational problems, which extend the classical variational framework to the fractional setting. Such problems naturally give rise to fractional equations of motion, namely the fractional Euler–Lagrange equations, which generalize their classical counterparts.

Classical variational principles fail to capture dissipative systems
[

]
, fractional calculus provides a natural extension of the variational framework, enabling the dissipation to be fully treated within the framework of variational principles.
This topic has been discussed by several authors

[

]

At the discrete level, the restricted Hamilton’s principle gives rise to Fractional Variational Integrators by employing two different approximations: one for the conservative part and the other for the fractional one. A well known discretization for the conservative Lagrangian can be found in

In this context, FVIs have been derived in

2.
Discrete Lagrangian Mechanics

In this section, we give a brief summary of the Lagrangian mechanical system from both continuous and discrete points of view.

2.1.
Lagrangian Hamilton’s Principle

Consider mechanical system with a
d
-dimensional configuration manifold
Q
, whose motion is described by a trajectory in the tangent bundle
TQ
associated to
Q
, parameterized by positions

q(t)\in Q
and velocities
\dot{q}(t)\in T_{q(t)}Q
. The Lagrangian system is characterized by a
C^{2}
-Lagrangian
L:TQ\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, commonly given by
L(q,\dot{q})=T(q,\dot{q})-V(q)
, where
T
and
U
denote the kinetic and potential energies, respectively. Consider
the action
\mathcal{L}:C^{2}([0,T],Q)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of a mechanical system, given by

\mathcal{L}(q)=\int_{0}^{T}L(q(t),\dot{q}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

(1)

In Lagrangian mechanics, the trajectory is characterized by Hamilton’s principle. That is, the trajectory
q
, with fixed points
q(0)
and
q(T)
, is determined by saying the action is stationary, namely

\delta\mathcal{L}(q)=0.

for all arbitrary variations that satisfy
\delta q(0)=\delta q(T)=0
. Necessary and sufficient conditions for the extremals lead to the Euler-Lagrange equations

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}(q(t),\dot{q}(t))\right)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}(q(t),\dot{q}(t))=0.

(2)

These are the equations of motion for conservative system. See
[
1
,
44
,
45
]
for more details.

2.2.
Discrete Lagrangian Hamilton’s Principle

Discrete mechanics is essentially a discrete formulation of geometric mechanics that preserves its underlying structure within a numerical framework, leading to what are also called
structure-preserving methods
. Variational integrators (VIs) are a discrete formulation of the Euler–Lagrange equation (
2
) and belong to a family of structure-preserving methods. Unlike standard discretizations of differential equations, the construction of VIs starts by discretizing the state space
TQ
and the action
\mathcal{L}

[
44
]
. More concretely, consider a time discretization
t_{k}=\left\{kh\right\}_{k=0}^{N}
of the interval
[0,T]
where
h=T/N
is the time step. One approximates the continuous trajectory
q(t)
by a discrete sequence
q_{d}:=\left\{q_{k}\right\}_{0:N}\in Q^{N+1}
at times
t_{k}
, i.e.,
q_{k}\approx q(t_{k})
. The discrete Lagrangian
L_{d}
has to be defined in such a way as to approximate the action over a short time interval. Thus, define
L_{d}:Q\times Q\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
based on two neighboring configurations
q_{k}
and
q_{k+1}
as

L_{d}(q_{k},q_{k+1})\approx\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}L(q(t),\dot{q}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

(3)

Furthermore, the discrete action sum
\mathcal{L}_{d}:Q^{N+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
approximates the continuous one (
1
) as

\mathcal{L}_{d}(q_{d})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{d}(q_{k},q_{k+1}).

The discrete variational principle is designed to mirror the continuous one. Namely, the trajectory
q_{d}=\{q_{k}\}_{k=0}^{N}
, with fixed points
q_{0}
and
q_{N}
, is determined by saying the action sum is stationary

\delta\mathcal{L}_{d}(q_{d})=0,

for arbitrary variations
\delta q_{0}=\delta q_{N}=0
, leading to the discrete Euler-Lagrange (DEL) equation

D_{2}L_{d}(q_{k-1},q_{k})+D_{1}L_{d}(q_{k},q_{k+1})=0,\qquad k=1,\ldots,N-1,

(4)

where
D_{i}
refers to the partial derivative with respect to the
i
-th argument. This scheme is called variational integrator and its flow is defined implicitly by the map
\Phi_{h}:Q\times Q\rightarrow Q\times Q
,
(q_{k-1},q_{k})\mapsto(q_{k},q_{k+1})
.
The resulting integrator (
4
) belongs to the class of structure-preserving methods
[
27
,
22
,
16
]
, meaning that it preserves, or nearly preserves, the invariants of the underlying continuous system such as, the symplectic structure, the total energy and, the momentum in presence of symmetries. Despite this advantage, this construction yields at most a second-order accurate scheme. The purpose of the following paragraph is to introduce a family of variational methods of arbitrary order.

2.3.
Galerkin Variational Methods

We now consider another class of methods to generate variational integrators, known as Galerkin methods. This approach involves locally interpolating the trajectories on a finite-dimensional basis and evaluating the action integral using a suitable quadrature method
[
29
,
44
,
46
]
.

(1) Trajectories space

We replace the infinite-dimensional function space

\mathcal{C}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],Q)=\left\{q:[t_{k},t_{k+1}]\rightarrow Q\,|\,q(t_{k})=q_{k},\,q(t_{k+1})=q_{k+1}\right\},

by a finite-dimensional one
\mathcal{C}^{s}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],Q)\subset\mathcal{C}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],Q)
, where
\mathcal{C}^{s}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],Q)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree
s
. Given
s+1
control points
0=d_{0}<d_{1}<\cdots<d_{s-1}<d_{s}=1
and
s+1
configurations
q_{k}=(q_{k}^{0},q_{k}^{1},\ldots,q_{k}^{s-1},q_{k}^{s})
, which satisfy
q_{k}^{0}=q_{k}
and
q_{k}^{s}=q_{k+1}
. Thus, there exists a basis
\{\ell_{\nu}\}_{\nu=0}^{s}
, e.g. the Lagrange polynomials, for which an element
q_{d}\in\mathcal{C}^{s}([t_{k},t_{k+1}],Q)
can be uniquely written as a linear combination of
\{\ell_{\nu}\}_{\nu=0}^{s}

q_{d}(t;k):=\sum_{\nu=0}^{s}q_{k}^{\nu}\,\ell_{\nu}\left(\frac{t}{h}\right),

(5)

such that
\ell_{\nu}(d_{i})=\delta_{\nu i}
and therefore
q_{d}(d_{\nu}h;k)=q_{k}^{\nu}
according to (
5
). A trajectory
q_{d}:[0,T]\to Q
, defined over the entire interval, is expressed on each sub-interval using the local interpolation
q_{d}
. This gives, for all
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]

q_{d}|_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(t)=q_{d}(t;k).

To ensure the continuity of
q_{d}
at the main nodes, we write for all
0<k<N
the condition

q_{d}(t_{k},k)=q_{d}(t_{k},k-1)=q_{k}.

(2) Quadrature for the action integral

We first replace the curve
q(t)
and the velocity
\dot{q}(t)
by their polynomial counterparts
q_{d}(t;k)
,
\dot{q}_{d}(t;k)
on the action as

\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}L(q_{d}(t;k),\dot{q}_{d}(t;k))\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad k=0,\ldots,N-1.

(6)

Then, a quadrature rule
(b_{i},c_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
with
c_{i}\in[0,1]
is applied to (
6
) on the same time interval yields

L_{d}(\{q_{k}\})\equiv L_{d}(q_{k}^{0},\ldots,q_{k}^{s}):=h\sum_{i=1}^{r}b_{i}L(q_{d}(c_{i}\,h;k),\dot{q}_{d}(c_{i}\,h;k))\approx\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}L(q(t),\dot{q}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t.

(7)

Following §
2.2
, the action sum can be defined from (
7
) by

\mathcal{L}_{d}(q_{d})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}L_{d}(\{q_{k}\}),

and hence the discrete Hamilton’s principle leads to the discrete Euler-Lagrange equations

\begin{split}D_{s+1}L_{d}(q_{k-1}^{0},\ldots,q_{k-1}^{s})+D_{1}L_{d}(q_{k}^{0},\ldots,q_{k}^{s})=0,\\
D_{i}L_{d}(q_{k}^{0},\ldots,q_{k}^{s})=0,\quad\forall\,i=2,\ldots,s;\end{split}

for
k=1,\ldots,N-1
where
D_{i}L_{d}(q_{k}^{0},\ldots,q_{k}^{s}):=\frac{\partial L_{d}(\{q_{k}\})}{\partial q_{k}^{i-1}}
. See
[
44
,
46
]
for further details.

Variational integrators have been widely used for conservative systems
[
29
,
36
,
46
,
18
]
, nonconservative systems
[
47
,
55
]
, optimal control problems
[
19
]
and multirate systems
[
48
,
58
,
37
]
.

The discrete variational principle will automatically share the accuracy of the Lagrangian to the resulting DEL equation by means of what is called
local variational order

[
44
]
, that is,
to construct a geometric integrator of order
r
, focus on creating an approximation to the action integral of order
r+1
. Depending on the degree of the interpolating polynomial and choice of the quadrature rule, one obtained VIs of different computational complexity and accuracy. Galerkin variational methods with polynomial interpolation of degree
s
and quadrature of order
s
were shown to converge with order at least
s

[
29
]
and the optimal order is generally unknown. Numerical studies
[
46
]
indicate convergence of order
\min(2s,r)
for Lobatto and Gauss quadrature rules.

3.2.
Runge-Kutta Convolution Quadratures

The theory of convolution quadrature methods (CQ) was developed in
[
40
,
38
,
39
]
. It provides a systematic framework to compute the convolution integral

\left(\kappa\ast f\right)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\kappa(t-\tau)f(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\quad t>0

(13)

efficiently without evaluating the convolution kernel
\kappa
, but instead through its Laplace transform
K(s)
by combining quadrature rules with linear multistep methods. A particular case of convolution is the
retarded
fractional integral (
8a
) where the kernel
\kappa
is given by

\kappa(t)=\kappa^{(\alpha)}(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(14)

with the corresponding Laplace transform of the form

K^{(\alpha)}(s):=\mathscr{L}(\kappa)(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}e^{-st}\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{s^{\alpha}}.

(15)

Building on this theory, subsequent extensions have applied CQ with Runge-Kutta methods
[
41
,
12
,
11
]
, which enable higher-order accuracy. In the present work, we adopt this Runge-Kutta-based approach in order to construct higher-order fractional variational integrators in §
4
.

3.2.1.
Runge-Kutta methods

We first recall some notations of Runge-Kutta (RK) methods which will be used throughout the following sections. We refer to
[
28
,
22
]
. Consider a
r
-stage RK method described by the coefficient matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, the vector of weights
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{r})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
and the vector of abcissae
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{r})^{\top}\in[0,1]^{r}
, or by means of the standard Butcher tableau

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\mathbf{c}&A\\
\hline\cr&\mathbf{b}^{\top}\end{array}.

Let the time step
h=T/N
, and the grid points
t_{j}=jh
, an
r
-stage RK method applied to ordinary differential equations

u^{\prime}(t)=f(t,u(t)),\quad u(0)=u_{0}

is given by

\displaystyle U_{ki}

\displaystyle=z_{k}+h\sum_{j=1}^{r}a_{ij}f(t_{k}+c_{j}h,U_{kj}),\quad j=1,\ldots,r

(16a)

\displaystyle u_{k+1}

\displaystyle=z_{k}+h\sum_{j=1}^{r}b_{j}f(t_{k}+c_{j}h,U_{kj}).

(16b)

The method (
16
) has

p\geq 1
and

q\leq p
, respectively, if for sufficiently smooth

(see

]

\left|u_{k+1}-u_{k}\right|=\mathcal{O}(h^{p+1})\quad\text{and}\quad\left|U_{kj}-u({t_{k}+c_{j}h})\right|=\mathcal{O}(h^{q+1}),\quad i=1,\ldots,r.

Let
I
be the identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
and
\mathds{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
,
the corresponding stability function is given by

R(z)=1+z\mathbf{b}^{\top}(I-zA)^{-1}\mathds{1}.

(17)

Assumption 3.1
.

From now on, we make the following assumption:

(1)

The matrix

(2)

R(iy)\leq 1

The two last assumptions guarantee

Important examples of RK methods satisfying these assumptions are Radau IIA (with order

4.2.

Remark 4.1
.

In the formulation of the discrete restricted Hamilton’s principle, all variables must be treated consistently within the discrete framework. Therefore, the function

f
should be represented by its discrete approximation

f_{d}=\{\mathbf{f}_{k}\}_{0:N}
, defined at the discrete time. Accordingly, the convolution quadratures (
18

at grid time

With these ingredients, we can establish the asymmetric integration by parts for the RKCQ which we need to derive the restricted discrete dynamics.

Lemma 4.1

Consider two discrete series
\left\{{\bf f}_{k}\right\}_{0:N},\left\{{\bf g}_{k}\right\}_{0:N}

(1)

(2)

(27)

Proof.

From this last expression, extracting the

\begin{split}&\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{n=0}^{k}(g_{k})_{i}\left[W_{n}^{(\alpha)}\right]_{j}^{i}f_{k-n}^{j}=^{1}\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{k=n}^{N}(g_{k})_{i}\left[W_{n}^{(\alpha)}\right]_{j}^{i}f_{k-n}^{j}\\

+&\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{i=2}^{s}\left(D_{i}L_{d}(y_{k})-h\rho\,\left[\mathcal{D}^{(2\alpha)}_{+}{\bf By}_{k}\right]_{i}\right)\delta x_{k}^{i}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(D_{s+1}L_{d}(y_{k-1})+D_{1}L_{d}(y_{k})-h\rho\,\left[\mathcal{D}^{(2\alpha)}_{+}{\bf By}_{k}\right]_{1}\right)\delta x_{k}^{1},\end{split}

where it is taken into account that
\delta x_{0}^{1}=0
and that
D_{s+1}L_{d}(x_{k-1})=D_{1}L_{d}(x_{k}).
Now, given that
\delta x_{k}^{i}
are arbitrary for
k=0,\ldots,N-1
,
i=1,\ldots,s+1
(except
\delta x_{0}
), we obtain the system (
31
) from the last equality and the theorem follows.
∎

Approximation Order

The restricted Hamiltonian system associated to the FEL equation (

) is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\begin{cases}\dot{x}&=\phantom{-}p\\
\dot{p}&=-\nabla U(x)-\rho D_{-}^{(2\alpha)}x.\end{cases}\end{split}

(33)

The error analysis for FVI (
31a
)-(
31b
) requires the definition of discrete momenta, and it can be done by applying the discrete Legendre transform. In this section, we focus on the
2
-stage Lobatto IIIC method.

\displaystyle p_{k}^{-}

\displaystyle=-D_{1}L_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})+\frac{\rho h}{2}\left[\mathcal{D}^{(2\alpha)}_{-}{\bf x}_{k}\right]^{1}

(34)

\displaystyle p_{k+1}^{+}

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}D_{2}L_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})-\frac{\rho h}{2}\left[\mathcal{D}^{(2\alpha)}_{-}{\bf x}_{k}\right]^{2}.

(35)

For the mechanical Lagrangian
L(q,\dot{q})=\dot{q}^{\top}\dot{q}-U(q)
, the associated approximation to the
2
-stage Lobatto IIIC method reads

\displaystyle L_{d}(x_{k},x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{2}L\left(x_{k},\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{h}\right)+\frac{h}{2}L\left(x_{k+1},\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{h}\right)

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2h}\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)^{\top}\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k+1})

(37)

With
\alpha=1/2
, we have only two non-vanishing weights, given by

W_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad W_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then, the approximation (
21
) becomes

\mathcal{D}^{(2\alpha)}_{-}{\bf x}_{k}=\frac{1}{h}\begin{bmatrix}x_{k+1}-x_{k}\\
x_{k+1}-x_{k}\end{bmatrix}

Now, we want to define the
qp
-formula, i.e., the map
(x_{k},p_{k})\mapsto(x_{k+1},p_{k+1})
to (
25a
). We compute explicitly the discrete Legendre transform as

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{k}^{-}&=\frac{1}{h}\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)+\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})+\frac{\rho}{2}(x_{k+1}-x_{k})\\
p_{k+1}^{+}&=\frac{1}{h}\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k+1})-\frac{\rho}{2}(x_{k+1}-x_{k}).\end{split}

(38)

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\alpha=1/2
with RKCQ associated to the
2
-stage Lobatto IIIC. The FVI with the corresponding scheme (
38
) applied to the system (
33
) is a second-order accurate method for both
x
and
p
variables. This means that their local errors are proportional to
O(h^{3})
.

Proof.

By a simple computation, solving the two equations in (
38
) with respect to
x_{k+1}
and
p_{k+1}
, respectively, gives

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\frac{2h}{2+\rho h}\left(p_{k}-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})\right)

(39)

\displaystyle p_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2-\rho h}{2+\rho h}\left(p_{k}-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})\right)-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k+1}),

(40)

Expand, for a small
h
, in Taylor series the exact position and momentum
x_{\text{ex}}(t+h)
and
p_{\text{ex}}(t+h)
gives

\displaystyle x_{\text{ex}}(t+h)

\displaystyle=x+hp+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\dot{p}+\frac{h^{3}}{6}\ddot{p}+O(h^{4})

\displaystyle=x+hp-\frac{h^{2}}{2}(\nabla U(x)+\rho p)+\frac{h^{3}}{6}\ddot{p}+O(h^{4})

(41)

\displaystyle p_{\text{ex}}(t+h)

\displaystyle=p+h\dot{p}+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\ddot{p}+O(h^{4})

\displaystyle=p-h(\nabla U(x)+\rho p)+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\left(-\frac{d}{dt}\nabla U(x)+\rho\nabla U(x)+\rho^{2}p\right)+O(h^{3}),

(42)

here
x=x_{\text{ex}}(t)
and
p=p_{\text{ex}}(t)
. Similarly, expanding the numerical update
x_{k+1}
yields

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\frac{2h}{2+\rho h}\left(p_{k}-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})\right)

\displaystyle=x_{k}+h\left(1-\frac{\rho h}{2}+\frac{\rho^{2}h^{2}}{4}+O(h^{3})\right)\left(p_{k}-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})\right)

\displaystyle=x_{k}+hp_{k}-\frac{h^{2}}{2}(\nabla U(x)+\rho p_{k})+\frac{\rho h^{3}}{4}(\nabla U(x_{k})+\rho p_{k})+O(h^{4}).

(43)

For the update
p_{k+1}
, we have for a small
h
that
\frac{2-\rho h}{2+\rho h}=1-\rho h+\rho^{2}h^{2}/2+O(h^{3})
and
\nabla U(x_{k+1})=U(x_{k})+h\frac{d}{dt}\nabla U(x_{k})+O(h^{2})
, so that

\displaystyle p_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\rho h+\rho^{2}h^{2}/2+O(h^{3})\right)\left(p_{k}-\frac{h}{2}\nabla U(x_{k})\right)-\frac{h}{2}\left(U(x_{k})+h\frac{d}{dt}\nabla U(x_{k})+O(h^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=p_{k}-h\left(\nabla U(x_{k})-\rho p_{k}\right)+\frac{h^{2}}{2}\left(-\frac{d}{dt}\nabla U(x_{k})+\rho\nabla U(x_{k})+\rho^{2}p_{k}\right)+O(h^{3}).

(44)

Comparing (
41
) with (
43
) and (
42
) with (
44
), we thus have the local truncation errors for
x
and
p
, that is

\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\text{ex}}(t_{k+1})\right\|=O(h^{3}),\quad\left\|p_{k+1}-p_{\text{ex}}(t_{k+1})\right\|=O(h^{3}).

Hence, the global error is
O(h^{2})
for both
x
and
p
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The definition of RKCQ (
18
) based on the evaluation of the function
f
at the RK nodes
\left\{t_{k}+c_{i}h\right\}_{i=1}^{r}
. On the other hand, the variations
\delta x_{k}^{i}
appearing in (
32
) also involve the main nodes, i.e., with
c_{1}=0
and
c_{r}=1
, which must coincide with the variations of Lemma
4.3
. This is precisely why we employ Lobatto IIIC in the discrete action (
29
). For Radau or Gauss methods, the main nodes are not directly included. Thus, one would need to interpolate the function
f
.

In particular, with the midpoint
\begin{array}[]{c|c}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\\
\hline\cr&1\end{array},

the CQ generating function is given by

\gamma_{\text{mid}}(z)=2\frac{1-z}{1+z},

which coincide the generating function of the trapezoidal rule, a method for which convolution quadrature theory is already well established in
[
24
]
and its theoretical order is
2
at the midpoint stage, see also
[
14
]
for more details.
We can define a modified midpoint CQ method (MIDCQ) using a local linear interpolation
\tilde{f}(t)=f_{j}+(t-t_{j})(f_{j+1}-f_{j})/h
on each subinterval
[t_{j},t_{j+1}]
, that is

\mathcal{D}_{-}^{\alpha}{f}_{k}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{k}w_{k-j}^{(\alpha)}\tilde{f}\left(t_{j}+h/2\right)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}w_{k-j}^{(\alpha)}\left(\frac{f_{j}+f_{j+1}}{2}\right),

where
w_{k-j}^{(\alpha)}
is the Taylor expansion of
(\gamma_{\text{mid}}(z)/h)^{\alpha}
. This allows to approximate the fractional derivatives at
t_{k}+h/2
and use the variation at the main nodes which are needed to derive the midpoint scheme.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a discrete setting of the restricted Hamilton’s principle using two different approaches for the continuous actions (
23
). One for conservative part
\mathcal{Lc}^{\text{con}}
[
29
,
44
,
46
]
and the other for the fractional part
\mathcal{L}^{\text{fra}}
by means of a convolution quadrature based on Lobatto IIIC Runge-Kutta methods
[
41
,
12
,
11
]
. In addition, we developed a midpoint-based fractional variational integrator, which achieves second-order accuracy.

Unlike the backward-difference-based method
[
30
]
, which is limited to second-order accuracy due to saturation effects, the Runge–Kutta–based FVI avoids limitations and can achieve higher-order accuracy. In the case of
\alpha=1/2
, the theoretical accuracy for Lobatto IIIC coincides with the result given by
[
47
]
. We also test our scheme for the fractional Bagley–Torvik equation (
46
).

While Lagrangian variational error analysis
[
44
]
indicates that the order of variational integrators depends on the accuracy of the discrete Lagrangian,
a full theoretical analysis of the variational error in the fractional setting remains to be developed. This might not be trivial, since the Lagrangian depends on more than just the tangent bundle variables.

Future work will focus on establishing a rigorous fractional variational error analysis, determining the theoretical convergence order for fractional Bagley–Torvik equation, and developing fractional variational integrators based on Gauss-type RKCQ.
