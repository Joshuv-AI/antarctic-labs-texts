Abstract
The paper considers function-valued tensors, viewed as multidimensional arrays with entries in an abstract Hilbert space. Despite the absence of the algebraic structure of a field, the geometric inner-product structure suffices to introduce the Tucker rank, higher-order SVD, and Tucker-cross decomposition for function-valued tensors. An adaptive cross-approximation algorithm is developed to compute low-rank approximations of such tensors. The framework is motivated by, and applied to, model order reduction of the parameter-to-solution map for a parametric PDE. The resulting reduced-order model can be interpreted as an encoder-decoder scheme with a nonlinear encoder and a multilinear decoder. The performance of the proposed non-intrusive approximation method is demonstrated in numerical examples for two nonlinear parametric PDE systems.

Introduction

The need for solving a parametric PDE for varying parameter values arises frequently in inverse problems, optimal control, uncertainty quantification, and digital-twin applications
[
49
,
8
,
50
]
.

Reduced-order modeling
(ROM) aims to extract the similarity of the PDE instances corresponding to distinct parameter values and to exploit it for reducing the cost of solving the PDE.
A wide spectrum of ROM techniques exists, spanning from physics-based projection methods, such as POD-Galerkin and reduced basis approaches
[
6
,
31
,
43
]
, to fully data-driven models enabled by machine learning and nonlinear regression techniques
[
36
,
32
,
53
]
.

This paper applies methods of multilinear (tensor) algebra to develop a method for the data-driven ROM of parametric PDEs.
The proposed ROM method approximates the map from the parameter domain to a
quantity of interest
(QoI), which may be either a (vector-valued) functional of the PDE solution or the solution itself.
The key dimension-reduction technique underlying the proposed method is a low-rank cross approximation of function-valued tensors, which leads to a data-driven ROM based on the non-intrusive sparse sampling of the parametric solution manifold.

Function-valued tensors are currently not as well understood as their scalar-valued counterparts, possibly due to the absence of the algebraic structure available to tensors over a field.
In this paper, we show that arrays with values in a Hilbert (or Euclidean) space admit the generalization of basic notions and techniques of low-rank tensor approximation: we introduce the Tucker rank, the higher-order singular value decomposition (HOSVD), and the Tucker-cross decomposition for such function-valued tensors.
We also present an adaptive cross-approximation algorithm for computing low-rank approximations of such tensors based on subsets of their entries.

Together with the novel notions and techniques of low-rank approximation for function-valued tensors, we present herein their application to model-order reduction for parametric PDEs.
In particular, we show that the resulting ROM method can be interpreted as an encoder-decoder scheme with a
nonlinear encoder
and a
multilinear decoder
.
This can be contrasted with the well-known POD-Galerkin and reduced basis (RB) approaches, which are based on the classical low-rank matrix approximation and can be viewed as linear encoder-decoder schemes performing projection onto linear latent spaces.
At the same time, in comparison with autoencoder-based ROM methods based on neural networks, the tensor-based methods remain tractable, and their interpolation properties appear amenable to rigorous analysis.

Outline

Since function-valued tensors do not routinely appear in the literature, and the ideas behind their application to ROM can be rather intricate, the exposition in this paper begins with the matrix case in
Section

2
.
We first recall a few well-known results on the cross approximation of real-valued matrices and then proceed to matrices over Hilbert spaces.
Understanding the notions of rank, cross decomposition, and their application to ROM in the matrix setting makes their subsequent extension to function-valued tensors in
Section

3
more straightforward.

Section

4
introduces an algorithm for adaptive function-valued cross approximation in the Tucker format, which in the present paper serves as the main model-reduction technique.
Finally,
Section

illustrates the approach with numerical experiments, in which the proposed ROM method is applied for the approximation of parameter-to-solution maps:
a system describing the slow motion of a non-Newtonian incompressible fluid with parametrized rheology and boundary conditions, and a parametric Monge–Ampère equation.

1.2
Related work on function-valued tensors

Vectors, matrices, and tensors with function-valued entries have been considered previously, though in settings different from ours.
In particular, tensors with entries from a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS) were studied in
[
51
,
46
,
37
,
48
,
30
]
.
A distinctive property of RKHS functions is that their pointwise evaluation is well-defined.
Consequently, what we regard as an order-
d
function-valued tensor over an RKHS would be treated in these works as an order-
(d+1)
real-valued tensor with one continuous dimension (
mode
).
The low-rank approximation methods of
[
37
,
48
,
30
]
and the cross approximation of function-valued vectors in
[
51
]
rely on the point evaluation of function-valued entries, which is not generally feasible in typical PDE settings.
In contrast, the framework we develop herein is based on accessing entries of a tensor exclusively as elements of the underlying Hilbert space, which is not required to allow for point evaluation and can therefore be arbitrary.
As a result, the framework applies to more general function-valued tensors and is suitable for typical PDE settings.

For a Hilbert space
\mathrm{H}
and
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
, the space
\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
of tensors of size
n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}
with values in
\mathrm{H}
is isometrically isomorphic to a tensor-product Hilbert space
\mathrm{H}\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\mathbin{\otimes}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}
.
As such, it is covered by the treatment of tensor products of arbitrary Banach spaces given in
[
28
]
;
in particular, the HOSVD approximation naturally generalizes to tensor products of arbitrary Hilbert spaces
[
28
, § 10]
.
In contrast, our particular setting of
\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}\simeq\mathrm{H}\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}\mathbin{\otimes}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes}\mathbb{R}^{n_{d}}
allows for generalizing not only the HOSVD approximation but also the Tucker-cross approximation.

1.3
Related work on tensor methods for parametric PDEs

The
low-rank tensor decomposition
(LRTD) Galerkin ROM for parametric dynamical systems was introduced in

]
and further developed and analyzed in

,

.
In these works, the POD component of the Galerkin ROM was replaced by the LRTD to provide a parameter-specific local basis for approximating the system dynamics in a reduced latent space.
Reduced-order models for parametric PDEs based on the low-rank completion of tensors sampled by slices was studied in
[

.
That approach can also be interpreted as finding a low-rank approximation of a vector-valued tensor from sparse sampling; however, it does not exploit the Hilbert structure of the space to which the tensor entries belong.

For the approximation of scalar-valued QoI of parametric PDEs, cross approximation was used in
[
3
]
.
Similar approaches were developed in
[
9
,
27
,
47
]
.

Parameter-to-solution maps can be expanded with respect to fixed bases in the parameter and spatial domains, with the resulting coefficient tensor approximated with LRTD
[
34
]
.
Following this line of research:
(i) Richardson iteration with adaptive rank truncation was proposed in
[
35
,
2
]
to obtain an approximate least-squares solution of the parametric PDE
eq.

1
below, whose coefficient tensor is represented in a low-rank hierarchical format;
(ii) the tensor-train (TT) and the hierarchical tensor representations were employed in
[
33
,
35
,
21
,
20
]
, where discretization of
eq.

1
, with collocation in the parameter space, yields block-diagonal linear systems with low-rank structure across the blocks, the TT structure being imposed analytically in
[
33
,
35
,

]
and via the TT cross approximation of the parametric coefficient in
[

]
;
(iii) a stochastic version of
eq.

1
was studied in
[
18
]
, where the Karhunen–Loève expansion of the stochastic diffusion coefficient, followed by block TT cross approximation, was used for a stochastic formulation of the problem.
The methods described above rely on the linearity of
eq.

1
, as they focus on assembling and solving tensor-structured linear systems; in contrast, our approach also applies to nonlinear PDEs.

The non-intrusive method of
[
23
,
24
]
treats the parametric PDE as a black box, making no assumptions about its structure and relying solely on sampled solutions—much like our approach.
Given a set of Monte Carlo samples,
[
23
,
24
]
fits a low-rank TT decomposition of the coefficient tensor to these data in a least-squares sense using alternating steepest descent.
Our method differs in two essential aspects:
(i) adaptive cross approximation is an “active-learning” technique that
generates samples
, whereas
[
23
,
24
]
performs “passive learning” on
given samples
;
(ii) our approach is based on
interpolation
, while that of
[
23
,
24
]
relies on
least-squares approximation
.

A block TT-cross algorithm was developed in
[
18
]
to construct a block TT decomposition from
N
-blocked samples.
From a vector-valued perspective, this algorithm implicitly equips the space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with the standard inner product.
Hence, following our results in
Section

3
, the resulting approximation can be regarded as a “mathematically correct” cross approximation for that particular Hilbert space.
The associated inner product is, however, not suitable in the context of a parametric PDE.
In the present paper, the formalism of function-valued tensors incorporates the Hilbert-space structure into all computations and can therefore be viewed as a way to extend the block TT-cross algorithm to parameter-to-solution maps.
Its use in
[
18
]
is limited to assembling the linear system.

2
Low-rank cross approximation as reduced-order modeling

Our motivating example is a diffusion equation with a coefficient depending on two parameters
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
:

\mathrm{div}\left(a(\mathsf{x};\alpha,\beta)\nabla u(\mathsf{x};\alpha,\beta)\right)=f(\mathsf{x}),\quad\mathsf{x}\in\Omega,\quad\left.u\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(1)

Equation
eq.

1
defines a parametric family of mathematical models.
In numerical optimization or inverse engineering, one often seeks to select a single model from this family according to certain criteria, i.e., by minimizing a cost functional.

These criteria may depend not on the solution of
eq.

1
itself, but on a QoI computed from it.
In the simplest case, the QoI is a scalar-valued function
\eta:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, such as the spatial average of the solution:

\eta(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{\Omega}u(\mathsf{x};\alpha,\beta)d\mathsf{x}.

In an optimization loop, the QoI may need to be evaluated at many parameter values, requiring repeated numerical solutions of
eq.

1
and leading to high computational cost.
A common strategy to mitigate this is to construct a ROM, i.e., to approximate
\eta
in a data-driven fashion.

There exist many data-driven approximation methods, including sparse grids
[
13
]
, kernel methods
[
52
]
, sparse polynomial approximation
[
1
]
, and neural networks
[
17
]
.
Here, we focus on low-rank ROMs, constructed for example as follows:

1.

Evaluate
\eta
on a Cartesian
m\times n
grid in the parameter domain and use these values to define an interpolant of
\eta
.

2.

Compute a rank-
r
approximation of the matrix
\mathsf{A}=[\eta(\alpha_{i},\beta_{j})]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
via truncated singular value decomposition (SVD).

This yields an “approximate interpolant” of the QoI.
The low-rank approximation
compresses
the exact interpolant from
mn
parameters to
O(mr+nr)
.
Its main drawback is that the full matrix
\mathsf{A}
must be evaluated and stored, which is prohibitive when
mn
is large and the PDE is expensive to solve.
Since the resulting low-rank ROM is characterized by
O(mr+nr)
parameters, one may seek to construct it using only
O(mr+nr)
function evaluations (i.e., PDE solves).
In the case of two parameters and a scalar QoI, the matrix cross approximation may serve this purpose.

2.1
Matrix cross approximation

The following
interpolation identity
can serve as the basis for an efficient low-rank ROM.
Every matrix
\mathsf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
of rank
r
contains at least one invertible submatrix
\mathsf{A}(I_{A},J_{A})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, and any such a submatrix allows for exactly recovering
\mathsf{A}
from its
(m+n-r)r
entries by
cross interpolation
:

\mathsf{A}=\mathsf{A}(:,J_{A})\,\mathsf{A}(I_{A},J_{A})^{-1}\,\mathsf{A}(I_{A},:).

(2)

The right-hand side of
eq.

2
extends naturally to arbitrary nonempty index sets
I\subseteq[m]
and
J\subseteq[n]
at the cost of possibly losing equality, resulting in
cross approximation
by cross interpolation.
Furthermore, the invertibility requirement can be relaxed by replacing the inverse with the pseudoinverse:

\mathrm{cross}(\mathsf{A},I,J)=\mathsf{A}(:,J)\,\mathsf{A}(I,J)^{\dagger}\,\mathsf{A}(I,:)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.

(3)

The cross approximation
\mathsf{B}=\mathrm{cross}(\mathsf{A},I,J)
inherits the interpolatory property of
eq.

2
: it satisfies
\mathsf{B}(I,J)=\mathsf{A}(I,J)
and exactly recovers
\mathsf{A}
whenever
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{A}(I,J))=\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{A})
; see, e.g.,
[
29
]
.

When
\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{A}(I,J))<\mathrm{rank}(\mathsf{A})
, the approximation quality of
eq.

3
depends on the choice of
I
and
J
.
The
adaptive cross approximation
(ACA) algorithm
[
5
]
constructs these sets iteratively, selecting new index pairs based on the largest residual entries.
Although ACA offers only weak theoretical guarantees
[
15
]
, it performs very well in practice.
Stronger approximation bounds hold when
\mathsf{A}(I,J)
has maximum
volume
—defined as the product of singular values—among all
|I|\times|J|
submatrices
[
26
]
.

A cross approximation computed by ACA can be used to define a low-rank ROM for a scalar-valued QoI.
When ACA employs rook pivoting to select index pairs, it requires access to
O(m+n)
matrix entries per iteration—equivalently, this many PDE solves in our setting.
Hence,
O(mr+nr)
QoI samples suffice to construct a rank-
r
ROM, as desired.

2.2
Bi-parametric PDE with vector- and function-valued QoI

Let us return to the parametric PDE
eq.

1
and consider a vector-valued QoI.
For example, this can be a vector-function
\eta:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
that computes the first
k
moments of the solution.
The “ultimate” vector-valued QoI is the full-order-model solution of the PDE itself with
k
being the number of degrees of freedom.
Even more generally, we can consider a QoI with values in an abstract function space.
Under standard regularity assumptions on the diffusion coefficient and the right-hand side, the weak solution of
eq.

1
belongs to the Sobolev space
\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
; see
[
11
]
.
If these assumptions are satisfied for every parameter value, one can consider the
parameter-to-solution
map
\eta:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathrm{H}
as the function-valued QoI.

Attempting to construct a ROM of the parameter-to-solution map
\eta
along the same lines as described above, one ends up with an
m\times n
array whose entries are elements of
\mathrm{H}
.
Below, we give meaning to low-rank approximation and, more importantly, cross approximation for such arrays—leading to a sample-efficient low-rank ROM for vector-valued QoIs such as the parameter-to-solution map.

2.3
Function-valued matrix cross approximation

Consider a general setting where
\mathrm{H}
is a Hilbert space over
\mathbb{F}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}
.
Two-dimensional arrays
\bm{\mathsf{A}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
, referred to as
Bochner matrices
, were studied in
[
12
]
.
They can be transposed
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\intercal}\in\mathrm{H}^{n\times m}
and multiplied with matrices
\mathsf{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times m}
and
\mathsf{C}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times l}
according to the standard formulas to give
\mathsf{B}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\mathsf{C}\in\mathrm{H}^{k\times l}
, but two function-valued matrices cannot be multiplied even if they have conforming sizes.
The
adjoint
of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is defined as a linear operator
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}:\mathrm{H}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
,

\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{b}}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\bm{\mathsf{b}},\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,1)\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}&\cdots&\langle\bm{\mathsf{b}},\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,n)\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal},\quad\bm{\mathsf{b}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m},

where
\langle\bm{\mathsf{b}},\bm{\mathsf{a}}\rangle_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\langle\bm{\mathsf{b}}(i),\bm{\mathsf{a}}(i)\rangle_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}
is an inner product in
\mathrm{H}^{m}
.
By convention, the adjoint is applied columnwise to
\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times k}
, so that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{B}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
.
A function-valued matrix
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times n}
is said to have orthonormal columns if
\bm{\mathsf{Q}}^{\ast}\bm{\mathsf{Q}}=\mathsf{I}_{n}
.

The
column-rank
of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
,
r=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
, is defined as the number of its linearly independent columns.
Function-valued matrices admit an SVD,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
, where
\bm{\mathsf{U}}\in\mathrm{H}^{m\times r}
is a function-valued matrix with orthonormal columns,
\mathsf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix with positive nonincreasing entries, and
\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times r}
is a matrix with orthonormal columns.
Such an SVD can be computed via a QR decomposition of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
followed by computing an SVD of the scalar-valued triangular factor.
The
pseudoinverse
of a nonzero function-valued matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is a linear operator
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}:\mathrm{H}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
given by
\bm{\mathsf{A}}^{\dagger}=\mathsf{V}\mathsf{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\mathsf{U}}^{\ast}
,
and it is zero when
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
is zero.

We have introduced the necessary definitions and proceed to the cross approximation of function-valued matrices.
The interpolation identity
eq.

2
extends as follows:

\bm{\mathsf{A}}=\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)}_{\mathrm{H}^{m\times|J|}}\cdot\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,:)}_{\mathbb{F}^{|J|\times n}}

(4)

if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J))=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}})
.
Since
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\dagger}
is a linear operator, it cannot be left as a stand-alone factor in the decomposition, so the size of the index set
|I|
ends up being “hidden.”
This is the first distinction from
eq.

2
and
eq.

3
.

The second one arises as we consider minimal
J
so that the columns of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)
are linearly independent.
When
\mathrm{H}=\mathbb{F}
, it is not possible to further compress these columns.
But for general function-valued matrices, the
row-rank
(the column-rank of the transpose) is not necessarily equal to the column-rank, so we can apply
eq.

4
to
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)^{\intercal}
.
This observation leads to the definition of cross approximation for function-valued matrices:

\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},I,J)=\underbrace{\Big\{[\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\intercal}]^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(:,J)^{\intercal}\Big\}^{\intercal}}_{\mathbb{F}^{m\times|I|}}\cdot\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)}_{\mathrm{H}^{|I|\times|J|}}\cdot\underbrace{\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,:)}_{\mathbb{F}^{|J|\times n}}.

(5)

Note that transposition and pseudoinversion do not commute for function-valued matrices, hence the form of the left factor.
The cross approximation
\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\mathrm{cross}(\bm{\mathsf{A}},I,J)
enjoys interpolation properties similar to
eq.

3
: the submatrix
\bm{\mathsf{B}}(I,J)=\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)
is always preserved, and
\bm{\mathsf{B}}=\bm{\mathsf{A}}
whenever the column-rank and row-rank of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)
coincide with those of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
.

The ACA algorithm was extended to function-valued matrices in
[
12
]
, providing a sample-efficient way to select the index sets
I
and
J
for
eq.

5
.

Remark 1
.

If we fix
I
and
J
and change the inner product, the cross approximation
eq.

5
will change.
This property highlights that function-valued cross approximation takes the geometry of the Hilbert space into account.

2.4
Reduced-order modeling

The discussion of function-valued cross approximation has been purely algebraic so far; let us now view it from a different perspective.
For both parameters, consider one-dimensional grids with nodes
{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}}
and
{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}}
, and fix interpolation basis functions
{\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}}
and
{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}}
satisfying
\varphi_{i}(\alpha_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j\in[m]
and
\psi_{i}(\beta_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j\in[n]
.
The first (implicit) step in constructing the low-rank ROM is interpolation:

\eta(\alpha,\beta)\approx\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\eta(\alpha_{i},\beta_{j})\varphi_{i}(\alpha)\psi_{j}(\beta),

which we can rewrite using the function-valued matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}
of samples as

\eta(\alpha,\beta)\approx\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{1}(\alpha)&\cdots&\varphi_{m}(\alpha)\end{bmatrix}\bm{\mathsf{A}}\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}(\beta)&\cdots&\psi_{n}(\beta)\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.

Once the grid nodes and basis functions have been chosen, the offline (“learning”) phase of ROM is carried out with an ACA-like algorithm.
This results in a cross approximation of the form

\bm{\mathsf{A}}\approx\mathsf{F}\cdot\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)\cdot\mathsf{P}^{\intercal},\quad\mathsf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times|I|},\quad\mathsf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|J|},

and in the associated cross-approximation-based ROM given by

\hat{\eta}(\alpha,\beta)=\bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{1}(\alpha)&\cdots&\varphi_{m}(\alpha)\end{bmatrix}\mathsf{F}\bigg\}\cdot\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)\cdot\bigg\{\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}(\beta)&\cdots&\psi_{n}(\beta)\end{bmatrix}\mathsf{P}\bigg\}^{\intercal}.

(6)

Encoder-decoder scheme

In the fields of ROM and operator learning, the respective models are often represented as compositions of two building blocks
[
7
]
:

1.

an encoder that maps the input into its latent representation;

2.

a decoder that maps the latent representation to the output space.

For the low-rank ROM
eq.

6
, the
encoder
is given by the interpolation step

(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\{\mathsf{\upphi},~\mathsf{\uppsi}\}:=\{\varphi_{1}(\alpha)\cdots\varphi_{m}(\alpha),~\psi_{1}(\beta)\cdots\psi_{n}(\beta)\}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}

followed by the transformation

\{\mathsf{\upphi},~\mathsf{\uppsi}\}\mapsto\{\mathsf{\upphi^{\intercal}F},~\mathsf{\uppsi^{\intercal}P}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|+|J|}.

This order-reduction transformation is “learned” by the ACA-like algorithm: the matrices
\mathsf{F}
and
\mathsf{P}
of the cross component
eq.

5
depend on the index sets
I
and
J
, and also on the inner product of the Hilbert space.

The
decoder
computes the output via

\{\mathsf{\upphi^{\intercal}F},~\mathsf{\uppsi^{\intercal}P}\}\mapsto\mathsf{\upphi^{\intercal}F}\cdot\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)\cdot(\mathsf{\uppsi^{\intercal}P})^{\intercal}\in\mathrm{H}.

This mapping is also “learned” by the ACA-like algorithm and depends on the selected index sets (their choice, in turn, depends on the inner product).

The resulting low-rank ROM
eq.

6
consists of a
nonlinear
encoder and a multilinear decoder.

Interpolation on a subgrid

The interpolation properties of function-valued cross approximations
eq.

5
guarantee that the low-rank ROM
eq.

6
is exact on a subgrid:

\hat{\eta}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{j})=\eta(\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}),\quad i\in I,\quad j\in J.

This property suggests that the “learning” phase can be interpreted as an adaptive search of an “important” coarse subgrid within a fine grid and the coarsening of the interpolation basis.
Both steps depend on the specific inner product of the Hilbert space (
Remark

1
).

Comparison with the reduced-basis method

The RB method in ROM
[
43
]
seeks to approximate the parametric solution manifold by a low-dimensional linear subspace.
However, it does not provide an explicit formula for projecting the solution onto this subspace; instead, one must solve a Galerkin-projected PDE.
In the case of a linear PDE, both the encoder and decoder of the RB method are linear.

The low-rank ROM
eq.

6
also identifies a linear subspace of
\mathrm{H}
spanned by the entries of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}(I,J)
, and directly provides the coefficients of the corresponding linear combination.
Note that these coefficients do not arise from a projection onto this subspace but are obtained through a nonlinear encoding step.
It remains possible within our framework to subsequently solve a projected PDE, which makes the encoder in our approach physics-informed.
This is realized, for example, in the interpolatory tensor ROMs developed in
[
38
]
, which, however, employ a full tensor HOSVD for the reduction step.

3
Cross approximation of function-valued tensors

In this section, we extend the previous discussion from two parameters and function-valued matrices to
d\geq 2
parameters and
function-valued tensors
.
This constitutes the main theoretical and conceptual contribution of the article.

3.1
Tucker decomposition

Let
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
.
The
mode-
k
unfolding
of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
is a function-valued matrix
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}
of size
n_{k}\times\prod_{l\neq k}n_{l}
whose columns are the mode-
k
fibers of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
arranged as

\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}(i_{k},\overline{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{d}})=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}).

By
\overline{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},i_{k+1},\ldots,i_{d}}
, we denote a “long” index written in the big-endian notation. Let
\mathsf{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{m_{k}\times n_{k}}
.
The
mode-
k
product
of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
with
\mathsf{B}_{k}
is a function-valued tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{C}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{k}\mathsf{B}_{k}
with the mode-
k
unfolding given by
\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{(k)}=\mathsf{B}_{k}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}
.
This product satisfies the standard commutativity property

(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{k}\mathsf{B}_{k})\times_{l}\mathsf{B}_{l}=(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{l}\mathsf{B}_{l})\times_{k}\mathsf{B}_{l},\quad l\neq k.

The mode-
k
unfolding of
\bm{{\mathscr{C}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{1}\mathsf{B}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{B}_{d}
can be represented as
1
1

1

The order of Kronecker products would be reversed if little-endian notation was used.

\bm{\mathsf{C}}_{(k)}=\mathsf{B}_{k}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}(\mathsf{B}_{1}\mathbin{\otimes}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes}\mathsf{B}_{k-1}\mathbin{\otimes}\mathsf{B}_{k+1}\mathbin{\otimes}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes}\mathsf{B}_{d})^{\intercal}.

(7)

We define the
Tucker rank
of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
as the tuple of row-ranks of its mode unfoldings,

\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})=(\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(1)}),\ldots,\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(d)})),

which is consistent with the Tucker rank of “usual” tensors when
\mathrm{H}=\mathbb{F}
.
Clearly, a componentwise inequality
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})\preccurlyeq(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
holds.
We say that a product

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d}

(8)

is a
Tucker decomposition
of the function-valued tensor
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
with core
\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}\in\mathrm{H}^{r_{1}\times\cdots\times r_{d}}
, factors
\mathsf{F}_{s}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{s}\times r_{s}}
, and rank
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
.
When the core of a Tucker decomposition is a subtensor of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
, we will call this decomposition
Tucker-cross
.
There is a simple relationship between the Tucker rank of a function-valued tensor and the rank of its Tucker decomposition.

Proposition 3
.

If
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
admits a Tucker decomposition
eq.

8
, then
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})\preccurlyeq\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{G}}})
. If this is a Tucker-cross decomposition, then
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{G}}})
.

Proof.

The inequality follows from
eq.

7
and
Lemma

2
. If
\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}
is a subtensor of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
, then
Lemma

2
applied to every mode unfolding yields
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{G}}})\preccurlyeq\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})
, so the Tucker ranks coincide.

The following result shows that there always exists a
minimal
Tucker-cross decomposition, i.e., whose rank is equal to
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})
.
We assume further throughout this section that
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\neq 0
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
and
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
. There exist index sets
{I}_{k}\subseteq[n_{k}]
of cardinality
|{I}_{k}|=r_{k}
and matrices
\mathsf{F}_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{k}\times r_{k}}
of full column-rank such that

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d}.

Proof.

For every
k
,
Lemma

2
shows that there exist
\mathsf{F}_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{k}\times r_{k}}
of rank
r_{k}
and
{I}_{k}\subseteq[n_{k}]
of cardinality
|{I}_{k}|=r_{k}
such that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}=\mathsf{F}_{k}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}({I}_{k},:)
. Note that
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}({I}_{k},:)=\mathsf{S}_{k}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}
, where
\mathsf{S}_{k}=\mathsf{I}_{n_{k}}({I}_{k},:)
is a row-selection matrix, so
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{k}\mathsf{F}_{k}\mathsf{S}_{k}
. Since
k
is arbitrary,

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\mathsf{S}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d}\mathsf{S}_{d}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d}.

3.2
Tucker-cross approximation

Now, we extend the cross approximation
eq.

5
to function-valued tensors.
Consider
\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})
at index sets
{I}_{k}\subseteq[n_{k}]
.
For every
k
, we introduce a function-valued matrix

\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}=\mathrm{rows}_{k}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\big[\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{k-1},:,{I}_{k+1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\big]_{(k)}^{\intercal}

(9)

of size
\prod_{l\neq k}|{I}_{l}|\times n_{k}
, whose rows consist of mode-
k
fibers.
We then define the
Tucker-cross approximation
of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
at
({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})
as

\mathrm{cross}_{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}\times_{1}\Big[\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(1)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{1}\Big]^{\intercal}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\Big[\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(d)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{d}\Big]^{\intercal}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}.

(10)

When
d=2
, this is exactly the cross approximation
eq.

5
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}=\mathrm{cross}_{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})
. Then

1.

\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}
and
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{B}}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{G}}})
;

2.

\mathrm{rows}_{k}(\bm{{\mathscr{B}}},{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k})
;

3.

\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{G}}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})
.

Proof.

\bm{1.}
Note that
\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}(:,{I}_{k})=\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}
and

\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}\times_{1}\Big[\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(1)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(1)}^{\intercal}\Big]^{\intercal}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\Big[\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(d)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(d)}^{\intercal}\Big]^{\intercal}.

The equality
\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}
follows from
eq.

7
and the definition of the pseudoinverse. Then
\mathrm{cross}_{\mathrm{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})
is a Tucker-cross decomposition of
\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}
, so
Proposition

3
applies.

\bm{2.}
By definition
eq.

10
,
\mathrm{rows}_{k}(\bm{{\mathscr{B}}},{I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})=\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}
. By
Lemma

2
, it holds
\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}=\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank_{r}}(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)})=\mathrm{rank_{c}}(\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k})
.

\bm{3.}
If
\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
, see part one. If
\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{G}}})=\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})
, then
Lemma

2
gives for every
k
that

\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}=\Big[\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}\Big]^{\intercal}\mathsf{S}_{k}\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)},\quad\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{k}\Big[\big(\bm{\mathsf{G}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}\big)^{\dagger}\bm{\mathsf{R}}_{k}\Big]^{\intercal}\mathsf{S}_{k},

where
\mathsf{S}_{k}=\mathsf{I}_{n_{k}}({I}_{k},:)
. Repeat this for each
k
to finish the proof.

Theorem

5
describes the interpolation properties of
eq.

10
.
A Tucker-cross approximation of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
recovers it exactly and it coincides with Tucker-cross decomposition whenever
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})
preserves the Tucker rank.
Otherwise, the quality of Tucker-cross approximation depends on the choice of the index sets.

3.3
Higher-order SVD

For comparison and evaluation of the Tucker-cross approximation quality, it is important to have a reference approximant.
For ordinary tensors, the HOSVD algorithm
[
16
]
computes a low-rank Tucker decomposition that provides a
quasi-optimal
approximation in the Frobenius norm.
For function-valued tensors, we use the generalization of the Frobenius norm:

\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}=\left(\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{n_{d}}\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\|_{\mathrm{\mathrm{H}}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Suppose that the approximation rank satisfies
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\preccurlyeq\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})
.
For every
k
, let
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}_{k}\mathsf{\Sigma}_{k}\mathsf{V}_{k}^{\ast}
be an SVD of the transposed mode-
k
unfolding.
Denote by
\mathsf{V}_{k,r_{k}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n_{k}\times r_{k}}
the first
r_{k}
columns of
\mathsf{V}_{k}
and by
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{k,r_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}\times r_{k}}
the upper left block of
\mathsf{\Sigma}_{r_{k}}
.
We define a HOSVD of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
as a Tucker decomposition of rank
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{hosvd}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})

\displaystyle=\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}\times_{1}\overline{\mathsf{V}}_{1,r_{1}}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\overline{\mathsf{V}}_{d,r_{d}},

\displaystyle\bm{{\mathscr{G}}}

\displaystyle=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{1}\mathsf{V}_{1,r_{1}}^{\intercal}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{V}_{d,r_{d}}^{\intercal}.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\preccurlyeq\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}})
. Let
\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}=\mathrm{hosvd}(\bm{{\mathscr{A}}},r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})
and denote

\mathbb{M}=\{\bm{{\mathscr{C}}}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}:\mathrm{rank_{T}}(\bm{{\mathscr{C}}})\preccurlyeq(r_{1},\ldots,r_{d})\}.

Then

\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}-\bm{{\mathscr{B}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}\leq\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\|\mathsf{\Sigma}_{k}-\mathsf{\Sigma}_{k,r_{k}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{d}\min_{\bm{{\mathscr{C}}}\in\mathbb{M}}\|\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}-\bm{{\mathscr{C}}}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}(\mathrm{H})}}.

Proof.

The proof is a direct extension of the
d=2
case from
[
12
, Thm. 5.16]
.

3.4
Reduced-order modeling

We use Tucker-cross approximations
eq.

10
to extend the low-rank ROM
eq.

6
from two to multiple parameters.
The setup of
Section

2.4
is repeated almost verbatim.
Consider a parameter-to-solution map
\eta:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathrm{H}
, introduce one-dimensional grids
\alpha_{k,1},\ldots,\alpha_{k,n_{k}}
along each parametric dimension, and choose the corresponding interpolation basis functions
\varphi_{k,1},\ldots,\varphi_{k,n_{k}}
.
The initial, “implicit” interpolation of
\eta
is given by

\displaystyle\eta(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{n_{1}}\cdots\sum_{i_{d}=1}^{n_{d}}\eta(\alpha_{1,i_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{d,i_{d}})\prod_{k=1}^{d}\varphi_{k,i_{k}}(\alpha_{k})

\displaystyle=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\times_{1}\mathsf{\upphi}_{1}(\alpha_{1})^{\intercal}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{\upphi}_{d}(\alpha_{d})^{\intercal}

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}
and
\mathsf{\upphi}_{k}(\alpha_{k})=[\varphi_{k,1}(\alpha_{k})~\cdots~\varphi_{k,n_{k}}(\alpha_{k})]^{\intercal}
.
The Tucker-cross approximation (
10
) of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
gives

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\approx\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d},\quad\mathsf{F}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{k}\times|I_{k}|},

which leads to the low-rank ROM

\hat{\eta}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})=\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\times_{1}[\mathsf{\upphi}_{1}(\alpha_{1})^{\intercal}\mathsf{F}_{1}]\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}[\mathsf{\upphi}_{d}(\alpha_{d})^{\intercal}\mathsf{F}_{d}].

Theorem

5
implies that the ROM is interpolatory on a
I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{d}
subgrid in the sense that

\hat{\eta}(\alpha_{1,i_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{d,i_{d}})=\eta(\alpha_{1,i_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{d,i_{d}}),\quad(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{d}.

The algorithm we propose extends the algorithm of

from scalar-valued tensors to function-valued tensors.
Specifically, we extend the ACA-like algorithm for function-valued matrices proposed in

to function-valued tensors.
Its first ingredient is a
rook-pivoting
procedure, which finds a large entry in a function-valued matrix by scanning its rows and columns (

Rook pivoting is de facto the standard way to select a new index pair in ACA

]
.

5.1
Parametric nonlinear Stokes equations

Consider the two-dimensional Stokes equations in
\Omega\subset[0,1]^{2}
, which describe a stationary flow of a non-Newtonian fluid with dominating viscous forces. Let
\mathsf{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the velocity and
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be the pressure.
When there are no external forces, the Stokes equations read

-\mathrm{div}(2\nu\epsilon(\mathsf{u})-p\mathsf{I}_{2})=0,~\mathrm{div}(\mathsf{u})=0~\text{ in }~\Omega,

(11)

where
\epsilon(\mathsf{u})=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\mathsf{u}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla\mathsf{u})^{\intercal}
and
\nu
is the viscosity.
The viscosity law

\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathsf{u})=\nu_{0}+\alpha\|\nabla\mathsf{u}\|_{\mathrm{\ell_{2}}}^{\beta}.

involves the rheological parameters
\alpha\in[0,1]
(consistency coefficient) and
\beta
(flow index).
We consider a shear thickening fluid with
\beta\in[0,1]
.

The boundary is partitioned as
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega_{D}\cup\partial\Omega_{N}
with the no-slip and pressure-drop boundary conditions:

\mathsf{u}=0~\text{ on }~\partial\Omega_{D},\qquad-(2\nu\epsilon(\mathsf{u})-p\mathsf{I}_{2})\mathsf{n}=p_{0}\mathsf{n}~\text{ on }~\partial\Omega_{N},

(12)

where
\mathsf{n}:\partial\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the outward normal, and
p_{0}:\partial\Omega_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
defines the pressure drop that drives the flow.
We set

p_{0}=\gamma(1-x)~\text{ with }~\gamma\in[1,5].

We consider the flow domain
\Omega
and its triangulation from
[
44
]
(cf.
Figure

1
).
The system
eqs.

11
to
12
is discretized using Taylor–Hood finite elements on a locally refined, regular triangulation, resulting in
6495
degrees of freedom.
The mesh and the corresponding solution for
\nu_{0}=0.01
,

, and

To gather sufficient statistics in a reasonable amount of time, we perform a preliminary projection of the PDE solutions onto a 100-dimensional reduced basis in
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
, so that the resulting function-valued tensor is
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{100})^{64\times 64\times 64}
with the standard inner product in
\mathrm{H}=\mathbb{R}^{100}
.

The main hyperparameters of TuckerABC are the number of rook-pivoting rounds
n_{\mathrm{rook}}
and the initial auxiliary index sets
\{{I}_{k}^{\prime}\}
, i.e., their cardinalities and content.
In
Figure

2
(left), we vary
n_{\mathrm{rook}}\in\{0,1\}
and the cardinalities
|{I}_{k}^{\prime}|\in\{2,8\}
, selecting the index sets
\{{I}_{k}^{\prime}\}
uniformly at random.
We observe that

•

larger index sets
\{{I}_{k}^{\prime}\}
do not lead to reduced approximation errors,

•

rook pivoting is critical for good performance of the algorithm. At the same time, taking
n_{\mathrm{rook}}>1
did not improve the approximation (we do not present the corresponding results).

Next, we look into
how
the auxiliary index sets
\{{I}_{k}^{\prime}\}
are formed and change the probability distribution from which the
{I}_{k}^{\prime}
are sampled at random; specifically, we consider the (approximate)
leverage scores
of the unfoldings
[
22
]
.
The leverage scores of a matrix
\mathsf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
with orthonormal columns are defined as the vector of squared row-norms

\mathsf{p}=\tfrac{1}{r}\begin{bmatrix}\|\mathsf{Q}(1,:)\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}&\cdots&\|\mathsf{Q}(n,:)\|_{\mathrm{2}}^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{\intercal}.

The vector
\mathsf{p}
is nonnegative and satisfies
\|\mathsf{p}\|_{\mathrm{1}}=1
, so it represents a probability distribution on the set of indices
[n]
.
For an unfolding
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}\in\mathrm{H}^{n_{k}\times\prod_{l\neq k}n_{l}}
, we can take the SVD of its transpose,
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathsf{U}}\mathsf{\Sigma}\mathsf{V}^{\ast}
, and the leverage scores of
\mathsf{V}
.
To approximate the leverage scores, we select
|{I}_{k}^{\prime}|
columns of
\bm{\mathsf{A}}_{(k)}
uniformly at random and compute the SVD of the smaller
|{I}_{k}^{\prime}|\times n_{k}
function-valued matrix.
These approximate leverage scores are then used to draw
\{{I}_{k}^{\prime}\}
.
The results in
Figure

2
(right) show that the use of leverage scores can lead to lower approximation errors in TuckerABC, at the cost of computing
\sum_{k=1}^{d}n_{k}|{I}_{k}^{\prime}|
additional entries of
\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}
.

5.2
Parametric Monge–Ampère equation

The second example we study is a Monge–Ampère equation in a square
\Omega=[-1,1]^{2}
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition and parametric right-hand side:

\det(\nabla^{2}u)=f(\mathsf{x};\alpha,\beta,\gamma),\quad\mathsf{x}\in\Omega,\quad\left.u\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

(13)

Here,
\nabla^{2}u
denotes the
2\times 2
Hessian of the function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.
The highly nonlinear nature of
eq.

13
necessitates special techniques for its numerical solution.
We use the vanishing moment method
[
25
]
that consists in approximating
eq.

13
with a fourth-order quasilinear equation with a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
:

-\varepsilon\Delta^{2}u+\det(\nabla^{2}u)=f(\mathsf{x};\alpha,\beta,\gamma),\quad\mathsf{x}\in\Omega,\quad\left.u\right|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\left.\mathsf{n}^{\intercal}(\nabla^{2}u)\mathsf{n}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\varepsilon,

(14)

where
\Delta^{2}
is the biharmonic operator and
\mathsf{n}:\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the outward normal.
For every strictly positive
f
, there exists a unique strictly convex solution of
eq.

14
.

Following
[
25
]
, we discretize
eq.

14
using Hermann–Miyoshi-type mixed finite elements, so that the solution
u
is approximated with second-order finite elements.
We consider a family of Gaussian functions as the right-hand side,

f(\mathsf{x};\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\exp\left(-\frac{(x_{1}-\alpha)^{2}+(x_{2}-\beta)^{2}}{\gamma}\right),

with parameters
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\in[-0.8,0.8]\times[-0.8,0.8]\times[0.001,0.1]
.
A uniform triangulation of the square domain
\Omega
with 1250 finite elements is used, yielding
2601
degrees of freedom for the numerical solution
u
.
The mesh and the solution for
\varepsilon=0.01
,
\alpha=0.7
,
\beta=0.6
, and
\gamma=0.01
are plotted in
Figure

3
.

50\times 50\times 50

A simple idea follows from the observation made above.
To make the offline phase more efficient, one can construct the low-rank ROM of the parameter-to-solution map using a coarser mesh,

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}_{\mathrm{coarse}}\approx\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}_{\mathrm{coarse}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d},

and reuse the selected indices and computed scalar factors (encoder) for finer meshes:

\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}_{\mathrm{fine}}\approx\bm{{\mathscr{A}}}_{\mathrm{fine}}({I}_{1},\ldots,{I}_{d})\times_{1}\mathsf{F}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{d}\mathsf{F}_{d}.

Only
\prod_{k}|{I}_{k}|
fine-mesh PDE solutions need to be evaluated in this case, and no extra computations are required.
We compare the effect of the coarse-mesh size on the resulting error corresponding to the mesh of size 2048.
The results in
Table

5
suggest that once a mesh is fine enough to resolve the features of PDE solutions, it can be used to build a low-rank ROM that remains suitable for further mesh refinements.
