A brief history of the human liver peroxisome
Authors: Dirk De Craemer, Christiane Van den Branden, Frank Roels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human liver peroxisome deserves a special review because it differs from peroxisomes in laboratory animals and cultured cells. We provide an overview of the historical stepping stones, with special emphasis on electron microscopy, enzyme- and immunocytochemistry, and morphometry. The contributions from several hundreds of patients, children with inherited diseases and adults with acquired illnesses are summarized. Intriguing findings such as liver peroxisomes of a patient changing over time, or the patient series with peroxisome mosaics in the liver, are highlighted. We compare our own data with the more recent discoveries, in particular the endoplasmic reticulum–peroxisome membrane contact sites and their inherited deficiencies: retinal dystrophy with leukodystrophy (RDLD). For complete diagnosis, expanded phenotyping and understanding the diseases, we urge the examination of human liver tissue.

Introduction

The human liver peroxisome deserves a special review. Indeed most studies and publications about peroxisomes used laboratory animals, even plants and yeasts, or cultured cells: skin fibroblasts, hepatocellular carcinoma cells, and cell lines such as Chinese hamster ovary cells. The DNA sequences of patients are analyzed, but these differ from their expression in organs. In an extensive review Wanders et al. (2023) concluded that “the current state of knowledge about peroxisomes, especially in humans, is still immature. Only very few different human cell types have been studied so far because of the restricted access to different human cell types other than fibroblasts and blood cells”. Peroxisomes, and the contribution of microscopy, were even not mentioned in a recent review of hereditary metabolic diseases (Saudubray and Schiff 2025).

Attention for human liver peroxisomes increased as data were obtained about patients with often very grave peroxisomal diseases. The livers of patients have revealed unexpected alterations that are still largely unexplained. The diversity of peroxisomes has been highlighted (Islinger et al. 2010).

From microbodies to peroxisomes

Electron microscopists were the first to describe the organelles in mouse kidney proximal epithelium (Rhodin 1954) and in rat liver where they contain a dense core shown in many images (Bernhard and Rouiller 1956). Rhodin called these organelles “microbodies”. The core consists of urate oxidase in rat liver (Hruban and Swift 1964), but the early researchers had no knowledge of the organelle’s enzyme content. Tissue fractionation studies started in the early 1950s, but led to uncertainty about which microscopic structures contained the many different enzyme activities measured (Schein et al. 1951; de Duve et al. 1955, 1960; de Duve and Baudhuin 1966).

The logic for the introduction of the name “peroxisome” is quite evident: three enzymes that metabolize hydrogen peroxide (catalase, uricase and d-amino acid oxidase) sediment together during tissue fractionation. They are visibly co-localized in the microbodies in rat and mouse.

But who launched the novel denomination? After studying brilliant electron micrographs of cell fractions that contain the “microbodies”, Baudhuin et al. (1965) commented that a novel denomination would not be useful. The same year, however, Christian de Duve (1965) coined the name “peroxisome” in a communication at the American Society of Cell Biology. In that communication he mentions rat liver and kidney, and Tetrahymena, but not human tissues.

Peroxisomes in human liver: the early years

Who first observed human peroxisomes? In 1966 de Duve and Baudhuin wrote that, using electron microscopy, they observed many microbodies in human liver without a core. Human liver has no urate oxidase. This explains uric acid storage in gout. So how does one recognize peroxisomes that show no crystalloid core? It required an experienced pathologist (Sidney Goldfischer) using electron microscopy, to detect the absence of peroxisomes in the liver parenchymal cells of a child with the cerebro-hepatic-renal syndrome (Zellweger syndrome) and a most experienced paediatrician (Lawrence Gartner) who diagnosed the syndrome. Their seminal paper also shows peroxisomes in a control liver of an 11-month-old girl (Fig. 1 in Goldfischer et al. 1973). The cerebro-hepatic-renal syndrome belongs to the Zellweger spectrum disorders and is today assigned to the group of peroxisome biogenesis disorders (Wanders et al. 2017).

Later the morphology of human peroxisomes was detailed by Sternlieb and Quintana (1977) in the livers of 16 adult patients. Some peroxisomes had a core, a marginal plate or other variations of shape. The nature of the cores and marginal plates is not explained by the authors. Marginal plates are characteristic for peroxisomes in the human kidney (Roels and Goldfischer 1979); in rat, sheep and cow this inclusion contains alpha-OH acid oxidase, one of the peroxide-producing enzymes (Yokota et al. 1985; Zaar and Fahimi 1991).

Crystalloid tubular, core-like arrays in the hepatic microbodies of a unique patient were found by Biempica (1966), in two subsequent biopsies. One might speculate whether a familiar drug such as aspirin could be the cause (Yokota et al. 1995a).

Cytochemical staining allows for peroxisome detection by light and electron microscopy

The major advance in studies of peroxisomes in human livers was due to the use of cytochemical stains for peroxisomal enzymes, starting with catalase (by diaminobenzidine oxidation (DAB), or by immune staining), and alanine glyoxylate aminotransferase (AGT) (by immune staining). Staining of peroxisomes by DAB required the differentiation between catalase and peroxidase. Indeed, DAB had been introduced to visualize horse radish peroxidase activity (Graham and Karnovsky 1966). Native catalase, on the other hand, did not stain with DAB. A subsequent publication reported detailed evidence that prior fixation in aldehydes, pH and temperature of the DAB medium, and H2O2 concentration determine whether catalase or peroxidase produce a visible stain (Roels et al. 1975). Endothelial cells and, in the liver, the Kupffer resident macrophages have peroxidase in their endoplasmic reticulum (ER), but no visible peroxisomes. This peroxidase is inhibited by fixation.

DAB staining of peroxisomes in rat liver was pioneered by Fahimi (1969) and in rat liver and kidney by Novikoff and Goldfischer (1969).

Peroxisomal staining in humans was first shown in the seminal paper by Goldfischer et al. (1973), and in a single case by Novikoff et al. (1973). For human peroxisomes the DAB technique was improved several times, as reported by Roels and Goldfischer (1979): formaldehyde–Ca2+ prefixation and post-osmication enhances contrast for light microscopy. The optimized recipe was detailed in the Journal of Inherited Metabolic Disease (Roels et al. 1995).

Catalase and AGT localization outside peroxisomes

In rhesus monkey, guinea pig and sheep liver, catalase activity is normally localized in the cytosol as well as in peroxisomes. This contrasts with mouse where hepatic catalase is exclusively peroxisomal (Roels 1976; Roels et al. 1977). In normal human liver, cytosolic catalase could not be visualized; but it is visible in livers of patients with a Zellweger spectrum disorder, also in nuclei (Roels et al. 1986, 1993; Espeel et al. 1993b, 1995a). In primary hyperoxaluria type 1, hepatic AGT either is absent or it is mistargeted to mitochondria and cytosol instead of to peroxisomes (Danpure et al. 1989, 1994a). Because AGT is absent in peroxisomes, oxalic acid formation is not prevented. It should be reminded that in humans, this crucial enzyme is normally expressed only in liver peroxisomes, different from other species such as cat, rat and guinea pig (Danpure et al. 1994b). The second enzyme that prevents the formation of oxalate, glyoxylate reductase/hydroxypyruvate reductase, was recently localized in the mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions of a human liver sample (Garrelfs et al. 2024). Fluorescence microscopy of HepG2 and HeLa cells confirmed this. It is absent in the peroxisomal fraction (Garrelfs et al. 2024). This enzyme is not liver-specific; it is deficient in patients with primary hyperoxaluria type 2 (Mistry et al. 1988).

Peroxisomes are not continuous with other organelles

Novikoff et al. (1973) argued that peroxisomes and ER cisternae are connected,1 a viewpoint that one of us (Roels 1991) has questioned—notwithstanding Novikoff’s excellent electron micrographs—because the catalase reaction is never continuous into the ER. Even after OsO4-only fixation and without catalase stain, the peroxisomal matrix (in rat liver) is more dense than the lumen of tubular extensions (which the authors consider to be smooth ER) (Planche X, Figs. d and e, and Planche XI, Figs. a and b in Novikoff and Shin 1964). That ER and peroxisomes are separate compartments was elegantly demonstrated by Shio and Lazarow (1981) using cytochemical localization of glucose-6-phosphatase compared to catalase.

The presence of connections between peroxisomes and ER was again concluded from brilliant pulse-chase fluorescence imaging on cultured mammalian cells by Kim et al. (2006); what the authors actually show is the consecutive appearance of the peroxisomal protein PEX16 in the ER, and then in the peroxisomes. When they block protein export from the ER by using brefeldin, PEX16 nevertheless appears in peroxisomes, which apparently have their own import mechanism.

At the ultrastructural level one often observes peroxisomes that are very close to the ER. In our own work, adjacency of ER and the peroxisomal membrane is shown in adult liver of Bufo marinus (Figs. 11–13 in Roels et al. 1970), in many human livers, for example in livers of patients with viral hepatitis (Figs. 3 and 4) (Figs. 5 and 7 in De Craemer et al. 1991a), with drug-induced hepatitis (Figs. 7 and 8 in De Craemer et al. 1991b) and with liver metastases (Figs. 2 and 4 in De Craemer et al. 1993a) and it is stressed by Espeel et al. (1993b) in the liver of a child with atypical chondrodysplasia punctata caused by a PEX7 mutation (Fallatah et al. 2021). This adjacency is also shown in cultured HEK293 cells (Fig. 3 in Costello et al. 2017 and Fig. 1G in Costello et al. 2023), in cultured COS-7 cells (Fig. 1 in Hacker et al. 2023) and in the liver of mice (Figs. 1A, 2A and 2B in Ilacqua et al. 2022) and yellow catfish (Fig. 1F in Zhong et al. 2025). The membranes of both subcellular organelles are never continuous nor touching each other: a narrow space between both membranes can still be observed. Kartenbeck and Franke (1974) were among the first to draw attention to these narrow spaces between peroxisomes and ER in hepatocytes and Morris hepatoma cells in situ and described their proteinaceous nature at the morphological level. Recent fluorescence microscopy of cell cultures has shown that an exchange of molecules between the ER and the peroxisome occurs at these sites via interactions between proteins present in the ER membrane (the protein VAPB, vesicle-associated membrane protein-associated protein B) and in the peroxisomal membrane (the proteins ACBD4 and ACBD5, acyl-coenzyme A-binding domain protein 4 and 5) (Costello et al. 2017; Hua et al. 2017; Kumar et al. 2024). The authors state that the two organelles are “tethered” to one another. Cali et al. (2025) gave an overview of the proteins involved in contact site formation and the roles of intracellular membrane contact sites (including ER–peroxisome contact sites) in cellular function, in many types of cells, but not in liver. Darwisch et al. (2020) suggested that ACBD5, in addition to its role as an acyl-CoA binding protein, also fulfils an important regulatory role in the very long chain fatty acid (VLCFA) metabolism by facilitating peroxisome–ER membrane contact sites in mouse liver. They investigated ACBD5 null mice, and on 3-D reconstructions of a liver cell measured the distance between peroxisomes and ER or mitochondria (Figs. 6h–j in Darwisch et al. 2020). By eliminating the ACBD5 gene the distance between peroxisomes and ER is increased from approx. 25 nm to 106 nm; this is accompanied by a rise in VLCFA and serious defects in cerebellum and several organs.

On the basis of our own electron microscopy (EM) observations, it is our balanced opinion that exchange of molecules between ER and peroxisomes also takes place in the liver, in conditions of growth, cell division and division of peroxisomes. Even in adult liver (in animals), there is a fast turnover of peroxisomal components, and probably a (slower) turnover of the peroxisomes as structures (Geerts and Roels 1981; Geerts et al. 1984).

The importance for human health of the ACBD5 protein and the transfer processes between ER and peroxisomes is clearly demonstrated by a novel group of disorders called retinal dystrophy with leukodystrophy (RDLD). Fourteen patients are reviewed by Dawes et al. (2025). A first patient was described by Abu-Safieh et al (2013). Biochemical characterization of the deficiency in this patient was reported by Yagita et al (2017) and in other patients by Ferdinandusse et al. (2017) and Herzog et al (2018). Caused by mutations in the ACBD5 gene, the biochemical parameters in serum and cultured fibroblasts are either abnormal or not (VLCFAs, docosahexaenoid acid (DHA), ether phospholipids). The clinical phenotypes also vary: inability to walk (data from 13 patients), microcephaly (data from 10 patients), delayed speech (data from 9 patients) are severe or in the normal range. All patients have vision loss (with variable onset; data from 13 patients), nystagmus, spasticity (data from 10 patients) and psychomotor regression (data from 12 patients). On MRI the areas with leukodystrophy vary; the retina is involved in all 14 patients, the cerebellum in 2. However, the patients’ survival is better than the “classical” peroxisomal disorders (such as Zellweger spectrum disorder and D-bifunctional enzyme deficiency): their age at present is 5 to 48 years. Dawes et al. (2025) point to the dual role of the ACBD5 protein: it facilitates the import of VLCFA into the peroxisome, but also mediates the exchange between ER and peroxisome. Two ACBD5-deficient mouse models have been described recently (Darwisch et al. 2020; Granadeiro et al. 2024), but it would be most instructive to examine also the liver of each human patient.

Peroxisomes are not interconnected

That individual human liver peroxisomes have different catalase concentrations (Singh et al. 1984; Roels and Cornelis 1989; Roels 1991; Espeel et al. 1993a) refutes the model that peroxisomes are interconnected to form a peroxisomal reticulum (Lazarow 1983). Activities of d-amino acid oxidase also differ between individual peroxisomes in rat liver and kidney (Angermüller and Fahimi 1988), as is the case for polyamine oxidase, as shown by electron microscopy in rat liver (Yamamoto and Fahimi 1987) and in fetal rat liver (Fig. 6 in Depreter et al. 1998; Fig. 6D in Depreter et al. 2002).

Proliferation of peroxisomes through division

In regenerating human and rat liver and in cells in culture, peroxisomes show membrane extensions suggesting dividing peroxisomes (Roels et al. 1996; Carmichael et al. 2023) (Fig. 4).

Peroxisome proliferators elicit spectacular images of dividing peroxisomes: phytol (in mouse intestinal epithelium, liver, myocardium) and clofibrate (in rat liver) (Van den Branden et al. 1986; Figs. 9 and 10 in Roels 1991). Daughter and mother peroxisomes show different activities of catalase and d-amino oxidase.

Environmental factors modify peroxisomes

That liver peroxisomes change in different environments was well demonstrated by culturing hepatocytes under a variety of conditions (Depreter et al. 2002, 2003b). It was shown that catalase activity and fatty acid (FA) oxidation in primary rodent hepatocyte cultures reduced to less than 10% when compared to the activities measured in liver. Peroxisomal size, shape and catalase activity differed between rat hepatocytes cultured as monolayers or collagen gel sandwich cultures or spheroid cultures (Depreter et al. 2003b). Also in living animals hepatic peroxisomes are altered, by hormones and by diet (Christiansen et al. 1981; Van den Branden et al. 1986; De Craemer et al. 1994).

That many environmental factors elicit changes in peroxisomes calls for caution when results and conclusions from cultured cells and skin fibroblasts are applied to organs in vivo and to patients. This dilemma is discussed by Depreter et al. (2003b). Earlier Wiemer et al. (1991) called it “phenotypic variability” when catalase particles were shown in cultured cells from a patient with infantile Refsum disease without hepatic peroxisomes. They recall Arias et al. (1985) who saw catalase particles in fibroblasts from a patient with Zellweger spectrum disorder. Moreover, cell lines differ with respect to metabolic capacities (Maeda et al. 1978). Phytanic acid induces phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase in some cell lines, and not in others (Zomer et al. 2000).

Discrepancies between liver and cultured cells could also be due to the status of cell division. Cells in culture are growing and dividing, and so must organelles including peroxisomes. Liver (and kidney), on the other hand, shows no cell divisions, except in conditions of compensatory hypertrophy (partial hepatectomy), of pathological cell death, and in very young individuals. As early as 1964, Novikoff and Shin remarked that tubular extensions of liver peroxisomes (that they considered as smooth ER) “are common” after partial hepatectomy and “were encountered relatively infrequently” in untreated rats. Differentiated neurons do not divide (except in the hippocampus). So, in many organs, peroxisomes can be stable structures, different from peroxisomes in cells growing in culture. This is not contradictory to the fast turnover of a number of peroxisomal components. Catalase has a half-life of 1.35 days in adult rat liver, later corrected to 3.3 days, and 3.35 days in guinea pig liver. But catalase is asynchronous with many other peroxisome components (see Roels (1991) for literature and discussion). In contrast, the microscopic visible structure of the liver peroxisomes with its membrane and matrix has a much longer life: Geerts et al. (1984), applying the catalase synthesis inhibitor allylisopropylacetamide, calculated a half-life of 10 days in guinea pig liver in one series of experiments. In another experiment (Geerts and Roels 1981), turnover of peroxisome structures could not be demonstrated because the number of stained peroxisomes did not diminish during synthesis inhibition. Recently, Xiong et al. (2026) used a tandem fluorescent protein paired with peroxisome-lumen-targeting sequence, SKL, or peroxisome-membrane-targeting sequence, PEX3, and studied the turnover of these constructs in the liver of developing PO-TRG mice. The authors observed an increase in turnover between day 14 and day 28 postnatal and interpreted this as an increase in pexophagy rate. However, the difference between a single peroxisomal protein and the peroxisome structure was not made.

Peroxisomes are not the only stable structure whose components have a continuous turnover: the half-life of nuclear proteins in rat liver is 5.2 days (Glaumann et al. 1981).

In the duodenum, a tissue greatly differing from adult liver, the epithelial cells have a fast turnover. Its peroxisome population is peculiar and diverse; but images of budding were not observed (Roels et al. 1991b).

Direct demonstration of function

Direct demonstration of function comes from the following experiments in animals. In the unanesthetized mouse functioning hepatic catalase was demonstrated by measuring its inhibition by 3-aminotriazole which only binds to the catalase–H2O2 complex. H2O2 production and its binding to catalase is increased by offering substrates: fatty acids (by fasting), glycolate or urate (injected) (Van den Branden et al. 1984) or aminopyrine, which is metabolized by the P450 cytochrome complex that produces H2O2 (Premereur et al. 1985, 1986). H2O2 is also produced by superoxide dismutase (SOD) (as shown by its inhibition with diethyldithiocarbamate), providing proof of free radical production in normal healthy liver (Geerts and Roels 1982). This last experiment was carried out in the guinea pig which has a large amount of cytosolic catalase in the hepatocyte. SOD production was also demonstrated in normal functioning myocardium. In contrast to liver, myocardial H2O2 production was not raised by fatty acids (increased by fasting or mechanical load), nor did monoamine oxidase (inhibited by phenelzine) (Kerckaert and Roels 1986). Renal H2O2 metabolism was increased in the remnant rat kidney (Van den Branden et al. 1994).

Demonstration of functioning hepatic catalase was pioneered by Sies et al. (1973) who used isolated perfused rat liver.

Peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation and immunocytochemistry

That liver peroxisomes perform FA oxidation was discovered by Lazarow and de Duve (1976), in rats treated with the peroxisome proliferator clofibrate. This drug has only minor effects on human liver peroxisomes (Hanefeld et al. 1983; Blaauboer et al. 1990; Goll et al. 1999; Ammerschlaeger et al. 2004); however, in a single patient with the peroxisomal disorder X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (X-ALD) peroxisomal volume density (Vv) was increased fivefold after 2 years of treatment with clofibrate, and it remained above the pretreatment value in subsequent biopsies (Sepulveda et al. 2000).

Peroxisomal FA oxidation differs from the mitochondrial FA oxidation (Knoop 1904 in Houten and Wanders 2010; Wanders and Waterham 2006). It leads to H2O2 formation (H2O2 is then neutralized by peroxisomal catalase), and is resistant to cyanide. That human peroxisomes also perform FA oxidation in vivo was demonstrated (1) by Bronfman et al. showing the presence of fatty acyl-CoA oxidase, fatty acyl-CoA synthetase, fatty acyl-CoA oxidase, D-bifunctional protein (with enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase) and 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase in the peroxisomal fraction of human livers (Bronfman et al. 1979, 1984); (2) by Moser et al. (1984) who found that children who have no peroxisomes in liver and kidney (Zellweger spectrum disorder) accumulate VLCFAs (C26) in their plasma; subsequently, (3) by immunocytochemistry of human liver sections, the three FA oxidation enzymes (i.e. fatty acyl-CoA oxidase, D-bifunctional protein (with enoyl-CoA hydratase and 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase) and 3-oxoacyl-CoA thiolase) were visualized in human liver peroxisomes (Litwin et al. 1987, 1988; De Craemer et al. 1990; Espeel et al. 1990a, b, 1991). These results also benefited from the work of Takashi Hashimoto and co-workers who purified the three enzymes from rat liver (Osumi and Hashimoto 1978, 1979; Miyazawa et al. 1980), and prepared antibodies that reacted with the human proteins and which they offered to researchers who asked for them.

Species differences in FA oxidation (human vs rat) were analyzed in detail by Van Veldhoven (1998).

Because patients with X-ALD accumulate VLCFA, Singh et al. (1984) measured oxidation of C24 fatty acid by peroxisomal homogenates from cultured fibroblasts, in the presence of CN: oxidation was reduced by three-quarters. These authors also used electron microscopy to observe the liver of patient with X-ALD after staining for catalase activity: they show normal peroxisomes. Later the mutations found in DNA of patients with X-ALD led to the ABCD1 gene, encoding ABCD1 protein (previously called adrenoleukodystrophy protein or ALD protein), localized in the peroxisomal membrane. This protein has a transport function (Kawaguchi et al. 2021). Its role in peroxisomal oxidation of VLCFA was analyzed by Wiesinger et al. (2013) in human fibroblasts; they compared patients with X-ALD and Zellweger spectrum disorder to controls, and also inactivated this protein with a specific antibody. Oxidation of free C26 fatty acid as well as its CoA ester was impaired. Wiesinger et al. (2013) explain why their results differ from those described by van Roermund et al. (2011) in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, who proposed that acyl-CoAs are hydrolyzed to free FA prior to peroxisome import via the yeast ABCD1 orthologues Pxa1p-Pxa2p and subsequently re-esterified to acyl-CoA in the peroxisomal matrix. The ATPase activity of human ABCD1 when stimulated by VLCFA was measured, and its configuration at the molecular level was visualized by Le et al. (2022) using high resolution cryo-electron microscopy; but they only used CoA esters, not the free FAs, avoiding the question of the activation.

In some peroxisomal patients, there is also a shortage of metabolites normally synthesized by peroxisomes: ether phospholipids or plasmalogens (due to a defective DHAPAT), the polyunsaturated DHA, and normal bile acids. The last two metabolites require peroxisomal FA oxidation (Sprecher 2002). Patients with peroxisomal deficiency can also accumulate abnormal bile acids, phytanic acid, pristine acid, pipecolic acid, and oxalic acid. For the biochemistry of peroxisomal disorders of FA oxidation, see Van Veldhoven (2010). Deficiencies of peroxisomes lead to liver cirrhosis, with increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma, to adrenocortical and testicular failure, disturbance of bone formation, and several distinct diseases of the central nervous system and retina.

Patients series: morphometry, intracellular distribution, altered catalase activity

The use of the DAB stain resulted in the generation of data from large series of patients. The results on peroxisomes in 85 liver biopsies of adults were reported in Roels et al. (1983). A detailed analysis of peroxisomes in 167 liver samples of babies and children was reported by Depreter et al. (2003a). De Craemer et al. performed morphometry of liver peroxisomes in 155 adults with different conditions (steatosis, viral and drug-induced hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, cirrhosis, cholestasis, cancer, among others) (De Craemer et al. 1991a, b, 1993a, b, 1996a, 1998). Alterations of peroxisomal size, number and shape parameters were found. Recently Wisse et al. (2022) described a specific form of liver injury in the form of single parenchymal cell steatonecrosis. It would be interesting to perform morphological and morphometric studies of the peroxisomes in these injured human livers.

Peroxisomal morphometry in a single case of pyruvate dehydrogenase deficiency was reported by De Craemer (1995, in Table 1). The ultrastructure of microbodies in archival liver blocks of seven patients (neonatal adrenoleukodystrophy (one of the Zellweger spectrum disorders (Wanders et al. 2017)) and X-ALD) revealed their variability and difficulty of identification without specific stain. One patient had elongated liver peroxisomes in close contact with lipid droplets; in another patient some peroxisomes showed a marginal plate, or a dark core apparently unstructured (Roels et al. 1988). Finger-like invaginations of the peroxisomal membrane, and dark cores were documented in the liver of several peroxisomal patients (Espeel et al. 1991; Roels et al. 1991a; De Craemer et al. 1992).

More recently the livers of 14 patients affected by a peroxisome biogenesis disorder belonging to the Zellweger spectrum disorders were described by Berendse et al. (2019).

Peroxisomal defects and altered morphology

“Pseudo-Zellweger”

Goldfischer et al. (1986) diagnosed an infant with symptoms similar to Zellweger spectrum disorder (defective brain development). When the DAB stain for catalase was applied, the liver showed normal peroxisomes, also by electron microscopy. This apparent contradiction was resolved when several peroxisomal enzyme activities could be measured or localized by immunocytochemistry: the deficiency of D-bifunctional enzyme (D-BFP)—which performs a step in FA oxidation—results in a clinical syndrome very similar to total absence of peroxisomes. Subsequently such cases were recognized more often by paediatric neurologists.

Very large peroxisomes

Three related patients lacking the peroxisomal fatty acyl-CoA oxidase had very peculiar microscopy images of the liver: the peroxisomes stained for catalase activity were unusually large, as was already observed by light microscopy (Poll-The et al. 1988). Since this finding, morphometry of peroxisomes was carried out regularly in patients, and alterations were discovered in their number, size and shape (Kerckaert et al. 1995; De Craemer et al. 1996b). Enlarged liver peroxisomes were repeatedly observed in patients with the D-bifunctional enzyme defect (Fig. 1b) or with rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata (RCDP) (De Craemer et al. 1991c; Hughes et al. 1992). RCDP is an autosomal recessive peroxisomal disorder resulting in an impairment of the peroxisomal steps of plasmalogen (PL) biosynthesis. A causal relationship has been suggested between import defects and increased peroxisomal size (Roels 1991; Roels et al. 1988; Roels and Cornelis 1989; De Craemer et al. 1991c). More detailed biochemical, clinical and genetical patient data were reported by Smeitink et al. (1992), Espeel et al. (1993b) and more recently by Fallatah et al. (2021).

Rat liver peroxisomes become larger in hypothyroidism (Riede et al. 1978) and smaller when stimulated by thyroid hormone (Fringes and Reith 1982; Kerckaert et al. 1989).

Peroxisomal import defects

Immunolocalization by electron microscopy of the 43-kDa peroxisomal membrane protein showed rare empty vesicles of different types (Espeel et al. 1995b). Such “empty” structures had been called “peroxisomal ghosts” in cultured skin fibroblasts of patients with a Zellweger spectrum disorder (Wiemer et al. 1989). This suggested that defective import into vesicles (called assembly impairment by Tager et al. 1990) explained the absence of complete peroxisomal structures. But in some patients’ livers without catalase- or AGT-stainable peroxisome structures, membrane vesicles were not observed (Roels et al. 1993; Espeel et al. 1995b). The cytochemical localization of peroxisomal proteins revealed another novel finding: in patients without visible hepatic peroxisomal structures, catalase activity, AGT protein and peroxisomal membrane proteins all were observed in the cytosol and in some nuclei, as already detected by light microscopy (Espeel et al. 1993b, 1995a; Roels et al. 1986, 1993, 2003c; Berendse et al. 2019).

In the cytosol of livers without peroxisomes, the AGT protein often formed crystal-like fibrillar structures, observed already by using light microscopy (Fig. 2).

Unexpectedly, in an 8-year-old girl with atypical RCDP (Smeitink et al. 1992), the import defect of catalase was accompanied by strongly enlarged organelles (Fig. 6) (Espeel et al. 1993b). Because they were poor in catalase they were called peroxisomal “ghosts”; but they contained the three FA oxidation enzymes. Catalase was in the cytosol. Peroxisomal mosaics in the liver of patients

Stainable peroxisomes in only a small number of liver parenchymal cells, next to large areas where catalase and AGT are present in the cytosol and nuclei, is a constellation found in 10 patients with inherited defects (Mandel et al. 1994; Espeel et al. 1995a; Giros et al. 1996; Pineda et al. 1999; Roels et al. 2003a, b, c) (Fig. 8).Fig. 8Light microscopy of the liver of a patient with a peroxisome mosaic. In one of them, catalase was observed in both the cytoplasm and peroxisomes, in the same hepatocyte (Espeel et al. 1995a). The mechanism creating the mosaic has not been fully explained. Since two of the patients are brothers and their parents are consanguineous, an inherited factor seemed likely. In addition, an interaction between several gene products, and temperature sensitivity, was proposed, based on experiments with cultured fibroblasts: “normal peroxisome biogenesis can be restored when compensatory proteins are high (PEX1, PEX5, PEX10, PEX26, PMP70/ABCD3) and when temperature is low” (Roels et al. 2003a, b, c; figures available online3).

Electron microscopy of the livers of 14 patients affected by a peroxisome biogenesis disorder belonging to the Zellweger spectrum disorders revealed that while the majority of patients, who had mutations in PEX1 or PEX26, did not exhibit visible peroxisomes in hepatocytes, a subset presented with peroxisomal mosaicism associated with longer survival and more benign clinical course (Berendse et al. 2019).

Peroxisomal mosaics were observed more recently in cultured fibroblasts by several research teams (Zeharia et al. 2007; Berendse et al. 2013; Matsunami et al. 2016; Nava et al. 2022; Takashima et al. 2022). A study of prolonged survival of 31 patients with a Zellweger spectrum disorder concluded that “next to the PEX1 genotype other yet unknown factors determine the ultimate phenotype”; the presence of peroxisomal mosaics was not considered (Poll-The et al. 2004), although the authors were acquainted with two published mosaic cases (Barth et al. 2004).

Takashima et al. (2022) mutated PEX3 gene in HEK293 cells and observed a mutant clone showing peroxisomal mosaicism. Using time-lapse imaging and a long-term culture experiment, peroxisome biogenesis in this mutant clone oscillated over a span of days. The metabolic activity of peroxisomes was maintained in the cells with many peroxisomes while it depleted in the cells without peroxisomes.

Trilamellar, polarizing inclusions

Trilamellar, polarizing inclusions are instrumental in diagnosis. They are observed in many patients with peroxisomal deficiency, in macrophage lysosomes. In the electron microscopy images the lysosomes are called “angulate”. The inclusions consist of two dark straight and perfectly equidistant lines separated by a transparent space; their width is 6–14 nm as observed by electron microscopy (images and earlier references in Kerckaert et al. 1988; Roels et al. 1993, 1995) (Fig. 9). In the light microscopy the inclusions are birefringent in polarized light (Roels et al. 1986). They are needle-shaped and do not stain with any histological technique; but they are often surrounded by PAS-positive material and by acid phosphatase reaction product (Roels et al. 1993).Fig. 9Detail of a macrophage in the liver of a patient with infantile Refsum disease, stained for acid phosphatase activity using cerium ions as capturing agent. Trilamellar inclusions are invariably observed in liver macrophages of patients with a Zellweger spectrum disorder and the D-trifunctional enzyme defect; however they seem to accumulate with age and may be missed in very young babies. Remarkably, the livers of patients with X-ALD never show typical trilamellar inclusions but their adrenal cortex and brain do contain PAS-positive macrophages with polarizing inclusions. The kidney lysosomes of a patient with D-BFP deficiency, and of a lung macrophage of a patient with Zellweger spectrum disorder, also showed typical inclusions by electron microscopy (figures available online4).

Trilamellar inclusions were not observed in patients with rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata; nor in patients with isolated peroxisomal acyl-CoA oxidase deficiency (Poll-The et al. 1987; Kerckaert et al. 1988; De Craemer et al. 1991c).

It has been suggested that the inclusions constitute VLCFA (that accumulate in these patients); but this was not confirmed by an attempt to dissolve the structures in acetone and n-hexane (Poll-The et al. 1987; Kerckaert et al. 1988; Roels et al. 1995). Because the inclusions survive paraffin embedding, archival samples also are useful, even for electron microscopy after post-osmication. The real nature of these trilamellar inclusions remains to be elucidated.

Brown formaldehyde crystals should not be mistaken for typical trilamellar inclusions; they are formed when tissue stays weeks or longer in the fixative (Figs. 3, 5 and 6 in Kerckaert et al. 1988). Lamellar, birefringent inclusions are also observed in the liver of patients with erythropoietic protoporphyria (ferrochelatase deficiency) but their ultrastructure differs from those in peroxisomal disorders, and the macrophages contain orange pigment (Casanova-González et al. 2010).

Summary and conclusion

Since the first description of peroxisomes (microbodies) in 1954, our knowledge of these cell organelles in the human liver has grown exponentially. The number of identified peroxisomal matrix proteins and membrane proteins is steadily increasing, and the unravelling of their functions increasingly confirms the essential role of peroxisomes in diverse physiological and biochemical processes. Light and electron microscopy were essential to localize enzymatic activities and the exchange of substrates and products in and near peroxisomes, but also contributed to the understanding of the mechanisms of molecular exchange between peroxisomes and other cell organelles. Human peroxisomal disorders (cerebro-hepato-renal (Zellweger) syndrome, rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata and other disorders of peroxisome biogenesis, and X-linked adrenoleukodsytrophy, D-bifunctional enzyme defect, acyl-CoA oxidase deficiency, Refsum disease, hyperoxaluria type 1 and other disorders of peroxisome function) have been crucial to reveal and understand peroxisomal function and morphology.

Electron microscopy has shown that human liver peroxisomes are individual organelles that are not in open connection to each other and to other cell organelles. Literature indicates that differences in peroxisomal morphology and metabolism between different types of cells, tissues, and organs, as well as between humans and other organisms, must be taken into account. Interspecies variation must be considered when studying peroxisomes in experimental animals and cell cultures to explain symptoms exhibited by patients with hereditary peroxisomal diseases.

Caution is also required because environmental factors, both in vitro and in vivo, can influence peroxisomes. This is an important point of attention in in vitro-based studies and is also evident from morphometric analysis of peroxisomes in the liver of patients with common liver disease.

Provided the above points of attention are taken into account, morphological, morphometric, and cytochemical studies of the peroxisomal compartment in various types of cells in tissues and organs constitute an essential addition to our understanding of the human liver peroxisomes and their function in specific circumstances and diseases.
