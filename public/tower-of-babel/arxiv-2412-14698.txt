Geometrical optics for the fractional Helmholtz equation and applications to inverse problems
Giovanni Covi, Maarten de Hoop, Mikko Salo

Abstract
. In this paper we construct a parametrix for the fractional Helmholtz equation ( ( − Δ ) s − τ 2 ​ s ​ r ​ ( x ) 2 ​ s + q ⁡ ( x ) ) ​ u = 0 ((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s}+q(x))u=0 making use of geometrical optics solutions. We show that the associated eikonal equation is the same as in the classical case, while in the first transport equation the effect of nonlocality is only visible in the zero-th order term, which depends on s s . Moreover, we show that the approximate geometrical optics solutions present different behaviors in the regimes s ∈ ( 0 , 1 2 ) s\in(0,\frac{1}{2}) and s ∈ [ 1 2 , 1 ) s\in[\frac{1}{2},1) . While the latter case is quite similar to the classical one, which corresponds to s = 1 s=1 , in the former case we find that the potential is a strong perturbation, which changes the propagation of singularities. As an application, we study the inverse problem consisting in recovering the potential q q from Cauchy data when the refraction index r r is fixed and simple. Using our parametrix based on the construction of approximate geometrical optics solutions, we prove that Hölder stability holds for this problem. This is a substantial improvement over the state of the art for fractional wave equations, for which the usual Runge approximation argument can provide only logarithmic stability. Besides its mathematical novelty, this study is motivated by envisioned applications in nonlocal elasticity models emerging from the geophysical sciences.



1.
Introduction

In this article we study the fractional Helmholtz operator

L=((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s}+q(x))

and the fractional Helmholtz equation

Lu=0\text{ in $\Omega$}.

As we will explain below, this is a scalar model for equations appearing in nonlocal elasticity. We assume that
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary,
r\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a positive function (index of refraction),
q\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is a potential, and
\tau>0
is a (large) frequency. The operator
(-\Delta)^{s}
is the fractional Laplacian, defined via the Fourier multiplier
|\xi|^{2s}
where
0<s<1
. We consider functions
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
that solve
Lu=0
in
\Omega
in the sense of distributions.

In the classical case where
s=1
, the above equation is the standard Helmholtz equation. If the index of refraction
r(x)
is sufficiently nice (e.g. the Riemannian metric
g_{jk}(x)=r(x)^{2}\delta_{jk}
in
\overline{\Omega}
is simple, meaning that it is simply connected with strictly convex boundary and no conjugate points
[
41
]
), then the equation
(-\Delta-\tau^{2}r^{2}+q)u=0
admits geometrical optics solutions that concentrate along geodesics of the metric
g
when
\tau\to\infty
. These solutions have the form

u=e^{i\tau\varphi}(a_{0}+\tau^{-1}a_{1}+\tau^{-2}a_{2}+\ldots)

where the phase function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
is a real valued solution of the eikonal equation

|\nabla\varphi|=r,

and the principal amplitude
a_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
solves the transport equation

2\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla a_{0}+(\Delta\varphi)a_{0}=0.

Moreover, if we write
h=1/\tau
and let
P=h^{2}r^{-2}L=-h^{2}r^{-2}\Delta-1+h^{2}r^{-2}q
, then semiclassical singularities of solutions of
Pu=0
propagate along the bicharacteristics of the semiclassical operator
P

[
57
, Section 12.3]
. The spatial projections of these bicharacteristics are the geodesics of the metric
g
.

We wish to obtain analogous results in the nonlocal case where
0<s<1
. Our first result shows that for
1/2\leq s<1
the fractional Helmholtz equation, even though it is nonlocal, admits geometrical optics solutions that are very similar to the classical case
s=1
.

Theorem 1.1

(Case
1/2\leq s<1
)
.

Suppose that
\varphi\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
solves the eikonal equation

|\nabla\varphi|=r\text{ in $\Omega$},

and
a_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
solves the transport equation

\left.\begin{array}[]{rl}2\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla a_{0}+b_{s}a_{0}=0\text{ in $\Omega$},&\text{if $1/2<s<1$,}\\[5.0pt]
2\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla a_{0}+b_{s}a_{0}=q\text{ in $\Omega$},&\text{if $s=1/2$,}\end{array}\right.

where
b_{s}:=\Delta\varphi+(2s-2)\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\varphi}{\left\lvert\nabla\varphi\right\rvert^{2}}
.

Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there is a solution of
Lu=0
in
\Omega
having the form

u=e^{i\tau\varphi}(a_{0}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}a_{1}+\ldots+\tau^{-\alpha_{N}}a_{N})+R_{N}

where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
are independent of
\tau
, and
R_{N}\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfies

\left\lVert R_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}=O(\tau^{-C_{s}N})

for a fixed constant
C_{s}>0
depending only on
s
. Above,
(\alpha_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is a strictly increasing sequence depending on
s
such that
\alpha_{j}\to\infty
as
j\to\infty
.

We make the following remarks:

•

The eikonal equation for
1/2\leq s<1
is exactly the same as for
s=1
, which indicates that singularities propagate along geodesics.

•

The first transport equation for
1/2<s<1
is almost the same as for
s=1
. The effect of nonlocality is only visible in the zeroth order term
b_{s}
that depends on
s
. Thus
a_{0}
will contain an exponential
s
-dependent attenuation factor.

•

For
s=1/2
the first transport equation also contains the potential
q
, which can be considered as part of the subprincipal symbol (the principal part is of order
1
and
q
is of order
0
). This is analogous to the case of first order perturbations of the Laplacian.

•

The functions
\varphi
and
a_{j}
need to be defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to have
u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Their values outside
\overline{\Omega}
will affect
R_{N}
, but not the asymptotics in
\tau
.

We also note that for
1/2\leq s<1
, one could formally apply the semiclassical propagation of singularities theorem in
[
57
, Section 12.3]
to conclude that singularities propagate along geodesics. This is not quite rigorous since the symbol of
h^{2s}(-\Delta)^{s}
is not smooth at
\xi=0
. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on stationary phase expansions that deal with this nonsmoothness.

Next we state a result for
0<s<1/2
. In this case the potential
q
is a strong perturbation (the principal part has order
2s<1
and
q
is of order
0
), which changes the propagation of singularities. Similar phenomena appear in long range scattering theory
[
30
, Volume 4]
. In the geometrical optics construction this will be visible in the presence of lower order phase functions that give rise to exponential factors oscillating at different frequencies.

Theorem 1.2

(Case
0<s<1/2
)
.

Suppose that
\varphi_{0}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
solves the eikonal equation

|\nabla\varphi_{0}|=r\text{ in $\Omega$},

and
a_{0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
solves the transport equation

2\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla a_{0}+b_{s}a_{0}=0\text{ in $\Omega$}

where
b_{s}:=\Delta\varphi+(2s-2)\frac{\varphi^{\prime\prime}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\varphi}{\left\lvert\nabla\varphi\right\rvert^{2}}
.

Then for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
M\in\mathbb{N}
and a solution of
Lu=0
in
\Omega
having the form

u=e^{i\tau(\varphi_{0}+\tau^{-2s}\varphi_{1}+\ldots+\tau^{-2Ms}\varphi_{M})}(a_{0}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}a_{1}+\ldots+\tau^{-\alpha_{M}}a_{M})+R_{N}

where
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
a_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
are independent of
\tau
, and
R_{N}\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfies

\left\lVert R_{N}\right\rVert_{H^{s}}=O(\tau^{-C_{s}N})

for a fixed constant
C_{s}>0
depending only on
s
.

Note that in both Theorem
1.1
and
1.2
, if
x_{0}\notin\overline{\Omega}
and if
(\rho,\theta)
are Riemannian normal coordinates for the metric
g_{jk}=r(x)^{2}\delta_{jk}
centered at
x_{0}
, then choosing
\varphi(x)=\varphi_{0}(x)=\mathrm{dist}_{g}(x,x_{0})=\rho
and
a_{0}(\rho,\theta)=e^{F(\rho,\theta)}b(\theta)
, where
F
is a certain fixed function depending on
r
and
q
, while
b
is any function supported near
\theta_{0}
, yields solutions that concentrate near the geodesic
\rho\mapsto(\rho,\theta_{0})
. See the proof of Theorem
1.3
for more details.

As an application of the geometrical optics construction, we consider the inverse problem consisting in recovering the potential
q
from Cauchy data in the case of fixed, but unknown, refraction index
r
. In particular, we have the following Hölder stability result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
,
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open and bounded. Assume that the refraction index
r\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is positive with
r\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, and that the manifold
(\overline{\Omega},g)
with
g_{jk}=r(x)^{2}\delta_{jk}
is simple. Moreover, let
k,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and assume that
q_{1},q_{2}\in H^{k}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
are such that
\|q_{1}\|_{H^{k}},\|q_{2}\|_{H^{k}}\leq N
, with
q_{1}=q_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Then there exist constants
\tau_{0},\gamma,C>0
, with
\gamma
depending only on
s
, such that

\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left[\sup_{\tau\geq\tau_{0}}\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau})\right]^{\gamma}.

The symbol
C_{r,q_{j}}^{\tau}
indicates the Cauchy data set associated to the operator

P_{j}:=(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q_{j},

while the function
\delta
measures the distance between the Cauchy data sets. In the case that
\Omega
is Lipschitz and the exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map
\Lambda_{P_{j}}
is well defined, we have

C_{r,q_{j}}^{\tau}:=\{(f,\Lambda_{P_{j}}f)\in H^{s}(\Omega_{e})\times H^{-s}(\Omega_{e})\}.

In the same case, we can define the distance
\delta
as

\displaystyle\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau}):

\displaystyle=\max_{j\neq k}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f_{1}\in H^{s}(\Omega_{e}):\\
\|f_{1}\|=1\end{subarray}}\inf_{f_{2}\in H^{s}(\Omega_{e})}\left(\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{e})}+\|\Lambda_{P_{j}}f_{1}-\Lambda_{P_{k}}f_{2}\|_{H^{-s}(\Omega_{e})}\right).

We will rigorously define both the Cauchy data and the distance function
\delta
in greater generality in Section
4
. Note that existing stability results for fixed frequency have a logarithmic modulus
[
46
]
and this is optimal
[
48
]
. The fact that we use many frequencies leads to the improved Hölder stability result in Theorem
1.3
.

Because of the particular nature of the geometrical optics solutions in the regime
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, which differs substantially from the classical case
s=1
, we can apply the method in Theorem
1.3
only in the regime
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
. A precise account of this observation is given in Remark
4.1
.

1.1.
Motivation

The main motivation of our study on the wave properties of fractional Helmholtz equations comes from the field of nonlocal elasticity.

Local (or classical) continuum theories of elasticity, while in perfect agreement with experiments involving only measurements at scales which are large in comparison to the intermolecular distances of the elastic material, are unable to predict physical phenomena at the smallest scales, where the intrinsic granularity of the examined material becomes important. As observed by Eringen in
[
21
]
(see also
[
37
]
and
[
24
]
for a recent overview), one of the most critical failures of the local theory can be identified in the predicted value of the stress field at a sharp crack tip, which produces a singularity, clearly contradicting experimental measurements. The local theory also wrongly predicts that phase velocities should be independent of wavelength. This problematic effect is seen also in the acoustic (or scalar) case. As discussed in
[
42
]
, the solutions of the Dirichlet problem for the Laplacian in a domain with a polyhedral boundary present so-called
corner singularities
, and as such do not have the regularity which would be expected of solutions of elliptic equations in regular domains. When the domain is an infinite wedge, a polyhedral cone, or a polygon in the 2D case, the solutions exhibit a similar behaviour.

While many strategies have been proposed to correct these problematic observations, the most elegant is the one by Eringen, Speziale and Kim (
[
23
]
), which does not need to modify the ordinary hypothesis of rupture based on the maximum stress hypothesis as in the work by Griffith and collaborators (see e.g.
[
29
]
). By incorporating elements of the lattice structure of the elastic material into the model, the theory of
nonlocal elasticity
introduced in
[
23
]
produces predictions in complete accordance with experimental data in the sharp crack tip case, and reduces to the classical theory in the limit of vanishing atomic scale.

Since the publication of the cited seminal papers by Eringen
[
20
,
22
]
, the theory of nonlocal elasticity has been widely adopted by the scientific community and extended in various directions. A variational formulation of nonlocal elasticity has been the object of the works of Mindlin
[
39
,
40
]
. Time-fractional wave equations have been used by Zorica and Oparnica
[
56
]
for modeling viscoelastic behavior. Nonlocal elasticity was also studied in relation to inverse problems by Askes and Aifantis
[
1
]
. Other examples include the recent studies
[
55
,
54
]
, which have considered aspects of the theory concerning the relation between elastic stress and the equilibrium corner angle, as well as rigid inclusions in nonlocal elastic materials. The overview by Jin and Rundell
[
31
]
contains a multitude of references on fractional derivative equations and related inverse problems.

In our work
[
16
]
, we considered a model of nonlocal elasticity related to the fractional linear gradient-elasticity model in
[
4
,
52
]
, in which the nonlocal stress is defined as the Riesz fractional integral of the strain field. In that occasion, we have proved uniqueness in the inverse problem of determining the Lamé parameters of the isotropic material from nonlocal DN data, under the additional assumption that the Poisson ratio is fixed a priori. This has shown the intrinsic limits of the nonlocal technique we used to obtain such result. In this paper, we shall generalize the well known geometrical optics technique, which is classically available for the
local
case, to the inverse problem for the
fractional
Helmholtz equation. We understand the present article, which considers the acoustic (scalar) case, as a first step towards the wider goal of studying geometrical optics for the fractional elastic (vector) operator we introduced in
[
16
]
. This will be the object of future work.

1.2.
Connection to the literature

The fractional Calderón problem is a prototypical inverse problem for a nonlocal operator, which in recent years has attracted the attention of many authors. Introduced in the seminal paper
[
28
]
, it asks to recover the potential
q
in the fractional Schrödinger equation

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u+qu=0,&\Omega\\
u=f,&\Omega_{e}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega}\end{cases}

from partial data given in the form of a nonlocal Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map
\Lambda_{q}
, which is defined as

\Lambda_{q}:H^{s}(\Omega_{e})\ni f\mapsto(-\Delta)^{s}u|_{\Omega_{e}}\in H^{s}(\Omega_{e})^{*}.

Here
s\in(0,1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Omega
is a bounded open set, and
(-\Delta)^{s}
is the fractional Laplace operator, which can be defined via the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
as

(-\Delta)^{s}u=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(|\xi|^{2s}\mathcal{F}u(\xi)),

or equivalently as the singular integral

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x):=c_{n,s}PV\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}dy,

for a fixed constant
c_{n,s}:=\frac{4^{s}\Gamma(n/2+s)}{\pi^{n/2}|\Gamma(-s)|}
. The main technique available for the solution of this inverse problem was already given in
[
28
]
, where it was shown that a potential
q\in L^{\infty}
can be uniquely recovered. The method was based on the strong unique continuation property of the fractional Laplacian, which can be shown to be equivalent to the Runge approximation property, and on the Alessandrini identity.

The uniqueness result was later improved in
[
49
]
to potentials in
q\in L^{n/2s}
. In the paper
[
27
]
, the authors further showed uniqueness and reconstruction even in the case of a single measurement. The stability and instability properties of the fractional Calderón problem were also explored. The works
[
46
,
49
,
47
]
proved exponential stability, in accordance to the results known in the classical case. In the case of finite dimension, Lipschitz stability was obtained in
[
50
]
. As for instability, the general technique of
[
32
]
based on the study of entropy numbers was recently applied to the fractional Calderón problem in
[
2
]
. The fractional Schrödinger equation has been studied in many variations, including with general local (
[
15
,
5
,
13
]
), magnetic (
[
7
,
35
,
36
]
) and quasilocal perturbations (
[
9
]
). The case of closed and complete Riemannian manifolds was also considered (
[
25
,
6
]
). Many more related nonlocal operators were the objects of study of
[
3
,
14
,
26
,
33
,
34
]
, among others.

The conductivity formulation of the fractional Calderón problem was introduced in
[
8
]
, making use of the nonlocal vector calculus of
[
18
,
19
]
. For this formulation, one considers the fractional gradient
\nabla^{s}
defined as

\nabla^{s}u(x,y):=\frac{c^{1/2}_{n,s}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{u(y)-u(x)}{|x-y|^{n/2+s+1}}(x-y),

using which one can pose the fractional conductivity equation

\begin{cases}\mathbf{C}^{s}_{\gamma}u:=(\nabla\cdot)^{s}(\gamma^{1/2}(x)\gamma^{1/2}(y)\nabla^{s}u)=0,&\Omega\\
u=f,&\Omega_{e}\end{cases}.

Here
\gamma
is a scalar conductivity function, while
(\nabla\cdot)^{s}:=(\nabla^{s})^{*}
is the fractional divergence, i.e. the adjoint of the fractional gradient. Observe that
(\nabla\cdot)^{s}\nabla^{s}=(-\Delta)^{s}
. The inverse problem for the fractional conductivity equation consists in finding
\gamma
from a nonlocal DN map. It was shown in
[
8
]
to be solvable by means of the fractional Liouville reduction, a transformation which reformulates the problem in fractional Schrödinger form. The paper
[
8
]
also shows how the fractional conductivity operator naturally arises as a limit of a random walk with long jumps on a square lattice (see also
[
53
]
). This interpretation is extended to a general graph
G
in the recent work
[
12
]
, where the inverse problem consisting in recovering both the edge set of
G
and the conductivity
\gamma
from partial random walk data is solved up to natural constraints by means of a new algebraic method. In
[
45
]
the results of
[
8
]
are improved to conductivities in
H^{s,n/s}
. This inverse problem was also considered in the higher order regime (
[
13
]
), anisotropic (
[
10
]
) and global cases (
[
43
,
44
]
). Stability and instability results for the fractional conductivity formulation are the objects of
[
17
,
2
]
.

In particular, a fractional isotropic elasticity operator was studied by the authors of this paper in
[
16
]
, where uniqueness was proved to hold under strong assumptions on the Poisson ratio. Improving the results of this study with new powerful techniques borrowed from the theory of inverse problems for local operators is one of the main motivations of this paper (see Section
1.1
).

We refer to the surveys
[
51
,
11
]
for more references on the fractional Calderón problem in both the Schrödinger and conductivity formulations.

1.3.
Organization of the article

The rest of the article is organized as follows. Section
2
is dedicated to the construction of approximate geometrical optics solutions for the fractional Helmholtz equation. We first consider the case of constant coefficients, and then prove the result for variable refraction index and potential. In Section
3
we employ a resolvent estimate to upgrade the approximate geometrical optics solutions from the previous section to exact solutions, thus proving Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Applications to inverse problems are considered in Section
4
, where the stability result in Theorem
1.3
is proved. Finally, in the Appendix we show how to compute the transport equation for the principal amplitude of the geometrical optics solutions in polar normal coordinates.

2.
Geometrical optics solutions for the fractional Helmholtz equation

In this section we construct geometrical optics solutions for different fractional equations. We start in section
2.1
with the fractional Helmholtz equation with constant coefficients, for which we construct geometrical optics solutions directly by using the Fourier transform. Next, we consider the construction of approximate geometrical optics solutions for the case of variable refraction index and potential (section
2.2
). In the coming section
3
we shall upgrade these solutions from approximate to exact.

2.1.
The case of constant coefficients

As a warmup, we will construct an approximate geometrical optics solution to the equation

((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s})u=0\text{ in $\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$}.

Proposition 2.1
.

For any
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a positive constant
C_{M}
and amplitude functions
\{a_{l}\}_{l=0}^{M}
such that the function

u(x):=e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}a(x),\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad a(x):=\sum_{l=0}^{M}\tau^{-l}a_{l}(x)

and
\alpha\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, verifies

\|((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s})u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{M}\tau^{-M}.

Moreover, the principal amplitude
a_{0}
solves the transport equation
\alpha\cdot\nabla a_{0}=0
.

Proof.

We start by computing

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}|\xi|^{2s}\mathcal{F}(e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}a)d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}|\xi|^{2s}\hat{a}(\xi-\tau\alpha)d\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}|\xi+\tau\alpha|^{2s}\hat{a}(\xi)d\xi.

Assume that
\alpha\cdot\xi\neq 0
. Then using the formula for the binomial series
(1+x)^{s}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{j}x^{j}
we compute

\displaystyle|\xi+\tau\alpha|^{2s}

\displaystyle=(|\xi|^{2}+\tau^{2}+2\tau\alpha\cdot\xi)^{s}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}\left(1+2\tau^{-1}(\alpha\cdot\xi)\left(1+2^{-1}\tau^{-1}|\xi|^{2}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{-1}\right)\right)^{s}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{j}2^{j}\tau^{-j}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j}\left(1+2^{-1}\tau^{-1}|\xi|^{2}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{-1}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}+\tau^{2s}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{s}{j}2^{j}\tau^{-j}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j}\left(1+2^{-1}\tau^{-1}|\xi|^{2}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{-1}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}+\tau^{2s}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{s}{j}2^{j}\tau^{-j}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\binom{j}{k}2^{-k}\tau^{-k}|\xi|^{2k}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{-k}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{j}c^{\prime}_{s,j,k}\tau^{2s-j-k}|\xi|^{2k}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j-k},

where
c^{\prime}_{s,j,k}:=\binom{s}{j}\binom{j}{k}2^{j-k}
. By the assumption
a(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{M}a_{l}(x)\tau^{-l}
, we have
\hat{a}(\xi)=\sum_{l=0}^{M}\hat{a}_{l}(\xi)\tau^{-l}
. Then, because for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
the set of
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
orthogonal to
\alpha
has vanishing measure, we have

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\tau^{2s}\hat{a}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{j}c^{\prime}_{s,j,k}\tau^{2s-j-k}|\xi|^{2k}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j-k}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{M}\hat{a}_{l}(\xi)\tau^{-l}\right)d\xi

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\tau^{2s}a+\frac{e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}}{(2\pi)^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\sum_{l=0}^{M}c^{\prime}_{s,j,k}\tau^{2s-(j+k+l)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}|\xi|^{2k}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j-k}\hat{a}_{l}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}u+e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{j}\sum_{l=0}^{M}c^{\prime}_{s,j,k}\tau^{2s-(j+k+l)}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[|\xi|^{2k}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j-k}\hat{a}_{l}(\xi)\right](x)

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}u+e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\tau^{2s-\nu}\sum_{l=0}^{\min\{\nu-1,M\}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\psi_{\nu,l}(\xi)\hat{a}_{l}(\xi)\}(x)

\displaystyle=\tau^{2s}u+e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\sum_{\nu=1}^{\infty}\tau^{2s-\nu}\sum_{l=0}^{\min\{\nu-1,M\}}\Psi_{\nu,l}a_{l},

where
\Psi_{\nu,l}
is the operator whose symbol
\psi_{\nu,l}
is given by

\psi_{\nu,l}(\xi):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in{\mathbb{N}}_{+},k\in\{0,...,j\}:\\
\nu-l=j+k\end{subarray}}c^{\prime}_{s,j,k}|\xi|^{2k}(\alpha\cdot\xi)^{j-k}.

Observe that
\psi_{l+1,l}(\xi)=2s\alpha\cdot\xi
for all
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We assume that the coefficient of
\tau^{2s-1}
vanishes, that is

0=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\psi_{1,0}(\xi)\hat{a}_{0}(\xi)\}=2s\alpha\cdot\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\xi\hat{a}_{0}(\xi)\}=-2is\alpha\cdot\nabla a_{0}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\alpha\cdot\nabla a_{0}=0.

Thus we see that
a_{0}
solves the time-independent transport equation. Now we have

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u-\tau^{2s}u

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\sum_{\nu=2}^{\infty}\tau^{2s-\nu}\sum_{l=0}^{\min\{\nu-1,M\}}\Psi_{\nu,l}a_{l}

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\tau^{2s-2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\nu}\sum_{l=0}^{\min\{\nu+1,M\}}\Psi_{\nu+2,l}a_{l}

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\tau^{2s-2}\left(\sum_{\nu=0}^{M-1}\tau^{-\nu}\sum_{l=0}^{\nu+1}\Psi_{\nu+2,l}a_{l}+\sum_{\nu\geq M}\tau^{-\nu}\sum_{l=0}^{M}\Psi_{\nu+2,l}a_{l}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\alpha\cdot x}\tau^{2s-2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{M-1}\tau^{-\nu}\left(\Psi_{\nu+2,\nu+1}a_{\nu+1}+\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}\Psi_{\nu+2,l}a_{l}\right)+O(\tau^{-M}).

Requiring that the terms in parenthesis vanish, for all
\nu<M
we get

\alpha\cdot\nabla a_{\nu+1}=\frac{i}{2s}\Psi_{\nu+2,\nu+1}a_{\nu+1}=-\frac{i}{2s}\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}\Psi_{\nu+2,l}a_{l},

that is
a_{\nu+1}
solves an inhomogeneous time-independent transport equation, in which the right hand side is computed recursively. This gives the required estimate and completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
The case of variable refraction index and potential

In this section we will construct an approximate geometrical optics solution to the equation

(2.1)

((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s}+q(x))u=0\text{ in $\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}$},

where
r(x),q(x)
are smooth positive functions (index of refraction and potential).

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
M,\beta\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exist two constants
C_{s},C_{M,\beta}>0
such that

u_{M}(x):=e^{i\tau\varphi(x)}a(x)

verifies

\|((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s}+q(x))u_{M}(x)\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}\leq C_{M,\beta}\tau^{-M},

where the phase and amplitude functions
\varphi,a
are given by

\varphi(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{F_{M}}\tau^{-2sj}\varphi_{j}(x),\qquad a(x):=\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{-\alpha_{l}}a_{l}(x)

with
A_{M}=F_{M}:=C_{s}M\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

\mathcal{A}:=\{\alpha_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathbb{N}}+(2s-\lfloor 2s\rfloor){\mathbb{N}},\qquad\alpha_{l}<\alpha_{l+1}\quad\mbox{for all}\quad l\in{\mathbb{N}},

for two suitable sequences
\{a_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}},\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
independent of
M
. Moreover, if
s\in[1/2,1)
then
\varphi_{j}
can be chosen to vanish for all
j\neq 0
.

Remark 2.3
.

Observe that when
s=1/2
we simply have
\{\alpha_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover, the powers of
\tau
appearing in the definition of the phase function
\varphi
emerge naturally from the proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Proof.

Step 1.

(Stationary-nonstationary phase)
We let
\tilde{a}(x):=e^{i\tau\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\tau^{-2sj}\varphi_{j}(x)}a(x)
, so that
u_{0}(x)=e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}(x)}\tilde{a}(x)
. Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
satisfy
0\leq\chi\leq 1
,
\chi(\xi)=1
for
|\xi|\leq 1/2
, and
\chi(\xi)=0
for
|\xi|\geq 1
. We will use
\chi
to give a precise meaning to the oscillatory integral below. We compute

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{n}(-\Delta)^{s}(e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}}\tilde{a})(x)=\int e^{ix\cdot\xi}|\xi|^{2s}(e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}}\tilde{a})\hat{\phantom{a}}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int e^{ix\cdot\xi}|\xi|^{2s}\chi(\varepsilon\xi)(e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}}\tilde{a})\hat{\phantom{a}}(\xi)\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\iint e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi+i\tau\varphi_{0}(y)}|\xi|^{2s}\chi(\varepsilon\xi)\tilde{a}(y)\,dy\,d\xi

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tau^{2s+n}\iint e^{i\tau[(x-y)\cdot\xi+\varphi_{0}(y)]}|\xi|^{2s}\chi(\varepsilon\xi)\tilde{a}(y)\,dy\,d\xi.

We wish to use the method of stationary phase to compute the asymptotics of this integral as
\tau\to\infty
. To deal with the minor problem that
|\xi|^{2s}
is not smooth near
\xi=0
, write
|\xi|^{2s}=\chi(\tau^{\alpha}\xi)|\xi|^{2s}+(1-\chi(\tau^{\alpha}\xi))|\xi|^{2s}
where
0<\alpha<1
. Observe that
\int e^{i\tau(x-y)\cdot\xi}\chi(\tau^{\alpha}\xi)|\xi|^{2s}\,d\xi=\tau^{-(n+2s)\alpha}\eta(\tau^{1-\alpha}(x-y))
, where
\eta
is smooth and all of its derivatives are bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Thus

\iint e^{i\tau[(x-y)\cdot\xi+\varphi_{0}(y)]}\chi(\tau^{\alpha}\xi)|\xi|^{2s}\tilde{a}(y)\,dy\,d\xi=\tau^{-\alpha(n+2s)}\int e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}(y)}\eta(\tau^{1-\alpha}(x-y))\tilde{a}(y)\,dy.

Since
\nabla\varphi_{0}
is nonvanishing near
\mathrm{supp(\tilde{a})}
, nonstationary phase
[
30
, Theorem 7.7.1]
implies that the last integral is
O(\tau^{-\alpha N})
for any
N\geq 0
.

We now consider the expression

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\tau^{2s+n}\iint e^{i\tau\Phi}(1-\chi(\tau^{\alpha}\xi))|\xi|^{2s}\chi(\varepsilon\xi)\tilde{a}(y)\,dy\,d\xi

where
\Phi(y,\xi;x):=(x-y)\cdot\xi+\varphi_{0}(y)
. In order to deal with the part where
\xi
is large, we note that there is
R>0
such that
|\nabla\varphi_{0}|\leq R/4
in
\mathrm{supp}(\tilde{a})
. We further write
|\xi|^{2s}=\chi(\xi/R)|\xi|^{2s}+(1-\chi(\xi/R))|\xi|^{2s}
. Then in
\mathrm{supp}(1-\chi(\xi/R))
we have
|\xi-\nabla\varphi_{0}(y)|\geq|\xi|/2\geq R/4
and also
|\nabla\Phi|\geq|\xi|/2\geq R/4
. Applying nonstationary phase
[
30
, Theorem 7.7.1]
, we have

\iint e^{i\tau\Phi}(1-\chi(\xi/R))|\xi|^{2s}\chi(\varepsilon\xi)\tilde{a}(y)\,dy\,d\xi=O(\tau^{-N})

for any
N\geq 0
, uniformly over
0<\varepsilon<1
.

It remains to consider the expression

\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\tau^{2s+n}\iint e^{i\tau\Phi}u(y,\xi)\,dy\,d\xi,

where
u(y,\xi)=u_{\tau,\varepsilon}(y,\xi):=\tilde{a}(y)(1-\chi(\tau^{\alpha}\xi))|\xi|^{2s}\chi(\xi/R)
. Note that
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{2n})
. We write
x_{0}
instead of
x
and rewrite the integral as

\iint e^{i\tau((x_{0}-y)\cdot\xi+\varphi_{0}(y))}u(y,\xi)\,dy\,d\xi=\iint e^{i\tau(\varphi_{0}(x+x_{0})-x\cdot\xi)}u(x+x_{0},\xi)\,dx\,d\xi.

We now apply stationary phase
[
30
, Theorem 7.7.7]
with
f(x,x_{0})=\varphi_{0}(x+x_{0})
to obtain that

\displaystyle\iint e^{i\tau(f(x,x_{0})-x\cdot\xi)}u(x+x_{0},\xi)\,dx\,d\xi

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}(x_{0})}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}\right)^{n}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N}\frac{1}{\nu!}\langle iD_{x}/\tau,D_{\xi}\rangle^{\nu}(e^{i\tau r_{x_{0}}(x)}u(x+x_{0},\xi))|_{(x,\xi)=(0,\nabla\varphi_{0}(x_{0}))}+O(\tau^{-n-N/2})

\displaystyle=:e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}(x_{0})}\left(\frac{2\pi}{\tau}\right)^{n}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N}\frac{S_{\nu}}{\nu!}+O(\tau^{-n-N/2})

where
r_{x_{0}}(x):=\varphi_{0}(x_{0}+x)-\varphi_{0}(x_{0})-\nabla\varphi_{0}(x_{0})\cdot x
.Observe that this implies
r_{x_{0}}(0)=\nabla r_{x_{0}}(0)=0
.

Step 2.

(High order terms)
In this step we clarify the dependency on
\tau
of the higher order terms appearing in
(-\Delta)^{s}u_{0}
. By letting

\displaystyle S_{\nu}

\displaystyle=:i^{\nu}\tau^{-\nu}\sum_{|\beta|=\nu}D^{\beta}_{\xi}(|\xi|^{2s})|_{\xi=\nabla\varphi_{0}(y)}S_{\nu,\beta},

we have that

\displaystyle S_{\nu,\beta}=e^{i\tau(\varphi-\varphi_{0})(y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j=0}^{F_{M}}t_{j}\leq|\beta|\end{subarray}}\tau^{\sum_{j=0}^{F_{M}}t_{j}(1-2sj)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\leq\beta\\
|\delta|\leq|\beta|-\sum_{j=0}^{F_{M}}t_{j}\end{subarray}}D^{\delta}a(y)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\eta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}^{n}:\\
\sum_{j=0}^{F_{M}}\eta_{j}=\beta-\delta,\;|\eta_{j}|\geq t_{j}\end{subarray}}R^{\beta,\delta,\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{T}}(y),

where we let
\mathcal{N}:=\{\eta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{F_{M}}
,
\mathcal{T}:=\{t_{j}\}_{j=0}^{F_{M}}
,
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}:=\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}
and

R^{\beta,\delta,\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{T}}(y):=\binom{\beta}{\delta}\binom{\beta-\delta}{\beta-\delta-\eta_{0}}I_{\eta_{0},t_{0},r_{y}}(0)\prod_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\binom{\beta-\delta-\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\eta_{i}}{\beta-\delta-\sum_{i=0}^{j}\eta_{i}}I_{\eta_{j},t_{j},\varphi_{j}(\cdot+y)}(0).

In the above computation we used the fact that
r_{y}(0)=0
and

\displaystyle e^{-i\tau^{\sigma}v(x)}D^{\alpha}_{x}(e^{i\tau^{\sigma}v(x)})=\sum_{j\leq|\alpha|}i^{j}\tau^{j\sigma}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho_{1},...,\rho_{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}_{>0}^{n}:\\
\rho_{1}+...+\rho_{j}=\alpha\end{subarray}}D^{\rho_{1}}_{x}v(x)\,...\,D^{\rho_{j}}_{x}v(x)=:\sum_{j=0}^{|\alpha|}\tau^{j\sigma}I_{\alpha,j,v}(x).

Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu=0}^{N}\frac{S_{\nu}}{\nu!}=e^{i\tau(\varphi-\varphi_{0})(y)}\sum_{\mu=0}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}\leq\mu\end{subarray}}\tau^{-\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}(1-2sj)}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a(y),

where the differential operator
L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
of order
\mu-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}
is defined as

(2.2)

L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}f(y):=\sum_{|\delta|\leq\mu-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}}D^{\delta}f(y)\sum_{t_{0}=0}^{N-\mu}\frac{i^{\mu+t_{0}}}{({\mu+t_{0}})!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta\geq\delta\\
|\beta|={\mu+t_{0}}\end{subarray}}D^{\beta}_{\xi}(|\xi|^{2s})|_{\xi=\nabla\varphi_{0}(y)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{\eta_{j}\}_{j=0}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}^{n}:\\
\sum_{j=0}^{F_{M}}\eta_{j}=\beta-\delta,\\
|\eta_{j}|\geq t_{j}\end{subarray}}R^{\beta,\delta,\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{T}}(y).

Therefore we obtain

(2.3)

\displaystyle(-\Delta)^{s}u_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{i\tau\varphi(x)}\sum_{\mu=0}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}\leq\mu\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s-\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}(1-2sj)}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).

Step 3.

(The eikonal equation)
If
\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}=\mu
holds, then there exists a function
\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
depending on the phase functions
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(y)f(y)=\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(y)f(y).

Using the fact that
I_{\alpha,|\alpha|,r_{y}}(0)=0
for all
\alpha\neq 0
, and
I_{0,0,v}(0)=1
for all
v
, we compute in particular the special cases

\lambda_{0;0,...}(y)=|\nabla\varphi_{0}(y)|^{2s}

and, for all
j\geq 1
,

\lambda_{1;\mathcal{T}_{j}}(y)=-2s|\nabla\varphi_{0}(y)|^{2s-2}\nabla\varphi_{0}(y)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}(y),

where
\{\mathcal{T}_{j,i}\}_{i=1}^{F_{M}}:=\delta_{i,j}
.
Therefore by assuming that the eikonal equation
|\nabla\varphi_{0}(x)|=r(x)
holds we are left with

(2.4)

\begin{split}&e^{-i\tau\varphi(x)}((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&\qquad=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}\leq\mu\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s-\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}(1-2sj)}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a(x)+q(x)a(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).\end{split}

Step 4.

(Transport equations,
s\in[1/2,1)
)
Let us consider first
s\neq 1/2
. In this case it suffices to assume
\varphi_{j}\equiv 0
for all
j\neq 0
, which immediately implies
L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\equiv 0
for all
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\neq 0
. In these assumptions equation (
2.4
) becomes

(2.5)

\begin{split}&e^{-i\tau\varphi(x)}((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&\qquad=\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s-1-\alpha_{l}}L_{1;0}a_{l}(x)+\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s-\mu-\alpha_{l}}L_{\mu;0}a_{l}(x)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{-\alpha_{l}}q(x)a_{l}(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}),\end{split}

where we used the assumption on the amplitude
a
. Observe that whenever
l,l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
are such that

(2.6)

2s-1-\alpha_{l}=-\alpha_{l^{\prime}}\qquad\mbox{ or }\qquad 2s-1-\alpha_{l}=2s-\mu-\alpha_{l^{\prime}},

then it must be
l^{\prime}<l
, since
0<2s-1
and
2s-\mu<2s-1
for all
\mu\in\{2,..,N\}
. In order to ensure that for each
l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
verifying either of the conditions in (
2.6
), we need to verify that

2s-1+\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\mu-1+\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A}

for all
\mu\in\{2,...,N\}
, which is indeed the case for
\{a_{l}\}_{l\in{\mathbb{N}}}={\mathbb{N}}+(2s-1){\mathbb{N}}
as in the statement. Therefore, all the summands in the second and third term on the right-hand side of (
2.5
) (up to indices
A^{\prime}_{M},A^{\prime\prime}_{M}
, respectively) can be absorbed by the ones in the first term, because the corresponding powers of
\tau
coincide.
By assuming that the coefficients associated to each power of
\tau
vanish, we obtain a sequence of differential equations in the unknowns
\{a_{l}\}_{l=0}^{A_{M}}
. Because of our previous discussion, these are all transport equations for the amplitude functions
a_{l}
whose source terms depend only on
\{a_{j}\}_{j<l}
. We are therefore able to obtain the amplitudes recursively, which leaves us with

(2.7)

\begin{split}e^{-i\tau\varphi(x)}&((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&=\sum_{l=A^{\prime}_{M}}^{A_{M}}\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\tau^{2s-\mu-\alpha_{l}}L_{\mu;0}a_{l}(x)+\sum_{l=A^{\prime\prime}_{M}}^{A_{M}}\tau^{-\alpha_{l}}q(x)a_{l}(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).\end{split}

Consider now
s=1/2
. In this case the right-hand side of equation (
2.5
) becomes

\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\tau^{-\alpha_{l}}(L_{1;0}+q(x))a_{l}(x)+\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\tau^{1-\mu-\alpha_{l}}L_{\mu;0}a_{l}(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}),

and thus we can repeat the same procedure as before using the new first order operator
L_{1;0}+q(x)
in place of
L_{1;0}
.

Step 5.

(Transport equations,
s\in(0,1/2)
)
In this case we write

\begin{split}&e^{-i\tau\varphi(x)}((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&\qquad=\tau^{2s-1}L_{1;0}a(x)+\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}\leq\mu\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s-\mu+\sum_{j}t_{j}(1-2sj)}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a(x)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sum_{j=2}^{F_{M}}\tau^{2s(1-j)}L_{1;\mathcal{T}_{j}}a(x)+L_{1;\mathcal{T}_{1}}a(x)+q(x)a(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).\end{split}

By solving the first transport equation

(2.8)

q(x)-2s|\nabla\varphi_{0}(x)|^{2s-2}\nabla\varphi_{0}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{1}(x)=q(x)+\lambda_{1;\mathcal{T}_{1}}(x)=0

for the phase function
\varphi_{1}
, we are left with

\begin{split}&e^{-i\tau\varphi(x)}((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&\qquad=\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}<\mu\end{subarray}}\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s(1-\sum_{j}jt_{j})-(\mu-\sum_{j}t_{j})-\alpha_{l}}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a_{l}(x)\\
&\qquad\quad+\left(\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}=\mu\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s(1-\sum_{j}jt_{j})}\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(x)+\sum_{j=2}^{F_{M}}\tau^{2s(1-j)}\lambda_{1;\mathcal{T}_{j}}(x)\right)a(x)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s-1-\alpha_{l}}L_{1;0}a_{l}(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}),\end{split}

where all the powers of
\tau
are negative. The term in parentheses can be rewritten as

\displaystyle S_{1}+S_{2}:

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{F_{M}}\tau^{2s(1-k)}\left(\lambda_{1;\mathcal{T}_{k}}(x)+\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}=\mu,\;\sum_{j}jt_{j}=k\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}=\mu,\;\sum_{j}jt_{j}>F_{M}\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s(1-\sum_{j}jt_{j})}\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(x),

and we see that
S_{1}
vanishes as soon as

(2.9)

2s|\nabla\varphi_{0}(x)|^{2s-2}\nabla\varphi_{0}(x)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{k}(x)=\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}=\mu,\;\sum_{j}jt_{j}=k\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(x)

holds for all
k\in\{2,...,F_{M}\}
. This is a sequence of transport equations for the phase functions
\varphi_{k}
, where the right-hand sides only depend on the functions
\{\varphi_{j}\}_{j<k}
. This follows from the fact that, since
\mu\geq 2
, necessarily the largest
j
for which
t_{j}
does not vanish must be strictly smaller than
k
. Having already determined both
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{1}
, equation (
2.9
) allows us to compute all the remaining phase functions recursively. Eventually, we are left with

(2.10)

\begin{split}&e^{-ik\varphi(x)}((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&\qquad=\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}<\mu\end{subarray}}\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s(1-\sum_{j}t_{j}j)-(\mu-\sum_{j}t_{j})-\alpha_{l}}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a_{l}(x)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sum_{l=0}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s-1-\alpha_{l}}L_{1;0}a_{l}(x)+S_{2}a+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).\end{split}

Observe that we have
2s(1-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}j)-(\mu-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j})<2s-1
for all choices of
\mu,\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
as in the first term on the right-hand side. This implies that if
l,l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
are such that

(2.11)

2s(1-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}j)-(\mu-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j})-\alpha_{l}=2s-1-\alpha_{l^{\prime}},

then necessarily
l<l^{\prime}
. The fact that for all
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a
l^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that (
2.11
) holds follows from

2s\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}j+(\mu-\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}-1)+\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{A},

which holds for
\{\alpha_{j}:j\in{\mathbb{N}}\}={\mathbb{N}}+2s{\mathbb{N}}
. Therefore, all the summands in the first term on the right-hand side of (
2.10
) (up to a certain index
A^{\prime}_{M}
) can be absorbed by the ones of the second term, and by comparing the powers of
\tau
we obtain a sequence of transport equations for the amplitude functions
a_{l}
, where the source terms depend only on
\{a_{j}\}_{j<l}
. This allows us to compute all the amplitude functions recursively, which leaves us with

(2.12)

\begin{split}&e^{-i\tau\varphi(x)}((-\Delta)^{s}+q(x)-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s})u_{0}(x)=\\
&\qquad=\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}<\mu\end{subarray}}\sum_{l=A^{\prime}_{M}}^{A_{M}}\tau^{2s(1-\sum_{j}t_{j}j)-(\mu-\sum_{j}t_{j})-\alpha_{l}}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a_{l}(x)\\
&\qquad\quad+\sum_{\mu=2}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}=\mu,\;\sum_{j}jt_{j}>F_{M}\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s(1-\sum_{j}jt_{j})}\lambda_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(x)a(x)+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).\end{split}

Step 6.

(Final estimates)
We will now use equations (
2.7
) and (
2.12
) to show that there exists
C_{M,\beta}>0
such that

\|((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q)u_{0}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}\leq C_{M,\beta}\tau^{-M}.

If
s\geq 1/2
, then

\displaystyle\|((-\Delta)^{s}

\displaystyle-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q)u_{0}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\leq c_{N}\tau^{-n-N/2}+\tau^{2s-2-\alpha_{A^{\prime}_{M}}}\|L_{2;0}a_{A^{\prime}_{M}}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}+\tau^{-\alpha_{A^{\prime\prime}_{M}}}\|qa_{A^{\prime\prime}_{M}}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}.

The wanted result is obtained by choosing
N=2M
and
A^{\prime}_{M},A^{\prime\prime}_{M}
so large that
M\leq\min\{\alpha_{A^{\prime}_{M}},\alpha_{A^{\prime\prime}_{M}}\}
, which is possible by choosing
A_{M}
large enough. Similarly, if
s<1/2
we have

\displaystyle\|((-\Delta)^{s}

\displaystyle-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q)u_{0}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\leq c_{N}\tau^{-n-N/2}+\tau^{2s(1-F_{M})}\|L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}+\tau^{2s-\alpha_{A^{\prime}_{M}}}\|L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a_{A^{\prime}_{M}}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)},

which implies the result for
N=2M
and
A_{M},F_{M}
large enough. Using the relation

\alpha_{K_{s}h}\geq h\quad\mbox{for all }h\in{\mathbb{N}},\qquad K_{s}:=\begin{cases}(2s-\lfloor 2s\rfloor)^{-1},&s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\cup(\frac{1}{2},1)\\
1,&s=\frac{1}{2}\end{cases},

it is easy to see that choosing
A_{M},F_{M}=\lceil 3K_{s}\rceil M
suffices in both regimes. Finally, the constant
C_{M,\beta}
is obtained by observing that all the
L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
are local differential operators, and all the amplitudes
a_{l}
belong to
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
by construction, which implies that all the quantities of the kind
\|L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}a_{l}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}
are bounded.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Observe that the geometrical optics solutions constructed in Proposition
2.2
for the two regimes
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
differ substantially. While in the former case there are many phase function associated to both positive and negative powers of
\tau
, in the latter case we have a unique phase function associated to
\tau
. This difference is due to the computations in Step 4: if we were to assume a unique phase function also in the regime
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, which would lead to equation (
2.5
), then because
2s-1<0
we would obtain
l<l^{\prime}
when equation (
2.6
.1) holds. This in turn would imply that in the transport equation for
a_{l}
there would appear terms depending on
a_{j}
for
j>l
, which would impede the recursion argument. Thus we have shown that it is impossible to get geometrical optics with a single phase function in the regime
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. This fact will have important consequences in Section
4
, where we study applications to inverse problems.

We complement the results of Proposition
2.2
with the following Corollary:

Corollary 2.5
.

Making use of the fractional Liouville reduction formula

\mathbf{C}^{s}_{\gamma}u=\gamma^{1/2}\left((-\Delta)^{s}(\gamma^{1/2}u)-u(-\Delta)^{s}\gamma^{1/2}\right)

(see
[
7
]
), we can compute

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}^{s}_{\gamma}u-\tau^{2s}u=\gamma^{1/2}\left((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}\gamma^{-1}-\gamma^{-1/2}(-\Delta)^{s}\gamma^{1/2}\right)(\gamma^{1/2}u),

which we recognize to be an operator of the kind considered in the above Proposition, with
r(x):=\gamma^{-1/2s}(x)
and
q(x):=-\gamma^{-1/2}(x)(-\Delta)^{s}\gamma^{1/2}(x)
. In particular, we deduce that it is possible to find an approximate solution of the form
u(x)=e^{i\tau\varphi(x)}\gamma^{-1/2}(x)a(x)
, with
\varphi(x)
and
a(x)
determined by the eikonal and transport equations as above.

3.
Upgrading approximate solutions to exact solutions

In this section we show how to construct exact solutions to the problem
Lu=0
in
\Omega
starting from approximate geometrical optics solutions
u_{M}
verifying the property

\|Lu_{M}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{M}\tau^{-M},

where
L:=(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q
. In order to produce an exact solution
u=u_{M}+v_{M}
, it is enough to solve the equation
Lv_{M}=-Lu_{M}
with suitable estimates. To state the solvability result, we will consider the small parameter
h:=\tau^{-1}
. The semiclassical Sobolev norm is defined by

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}:=\left\lVert\langle hD\rangle^{\alpha}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

where
\langle\xi\rangle=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}
and
D=-i\nabla
. The
H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)
norm is defined by restriction.

The solvability results are as follows. In order to have good high frequency estimates for all large
\tau
in the case of the operator
(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q
, we need to assume that the refraction index
r(x)
is
nontrapping
in
\overline{\Omega}
, i.e. that any geodesic of the Riemannian metric
g_{jk}(x)=r(x)^{2}\delta_{jk}
reaches
\partial\Omega
in finite time in both directions.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open and bounded,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
s\in(0,1)
. Let
L:=(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q
, with
r,q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
,
r
positive and nontrapping. There are
C,h_{0}>0
such that for any
f\in H^{\alpha}(\Omega)
the equation

Lu=f\text{ in $\Omega$}

has a solution
u\in H^{2s-1+\alpha}(\Omega)
satisfying

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1+\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{2s-1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)}

whenever
0<h<h_{0}
.

The above result will follow by duality from the next a priori estimate.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be open and bounded,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
s\in(0,1)
. Let
L:=(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q
, with
r,q\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
,
r
positive and nontrapping. There are
C,h_{0}>0
such that the estimate

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1+\alpha}_{scl}}\leq Ch^{-1}\left\lVert h^{2s}Lu\right\rVert_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}}

holds whenever
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
and
0<h<h_{0}
.

The estimate in Proposition
3.2
is a high frequency resolvent type estimate, but it is stated in a bounded domain and therefore one does not need to pay attention to behavior at infinity. We will prove the estimate by a positive commutator argument as in
[
38
]
, which considered the case
s=1
. The main difference in our case is that the symbol of the fractional Laplacian is not smooth at
\xi=0
, so one cannot use
C^{\infty}
pseudodifferential calculus. We will prove this case by a direct argument based on freezing coefficients.

We let
P:=(-h^{2}\Delta)^{s}-r^{2s}+h^{2s}q
with semiclassical principal symbol

p(x,\xi):=|\xi|^{2s}-r(x)^{2s},

and we define
\tilde{P}
as the operator generated by
p(x,\xi)
. Observe that
\|(P-\tilde{P})u\|\lesssim h^{2s}\|u\|
for all
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. Choose
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with
\chi=1
when
|\xi|\leq\frac{1}{4}c_{0}
and
\chi=0
when
|\xi|\geq\frac{1}{2}c_{0}
, where the constant
c_{0}>0
is such that
r(x)\geq c_{0}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We write

B_{0}u:=\chi(hD)u,\qquad Bu:=(I-B_{0})u.

Note that
B_{0}u
is supported in phase space away from
p^{-1}(0)
.

The proof of Proposition
3.2
is based on the following two lemmas, which consider the case
\alpha=1
. Below we will write
A\lesssim B
if
A\leq CB
where
C
is independent of
h\in(0,h_{0})
and of
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
. The first lemma is an estimate in the elliptic region, and comes with a power of
h
that is better than in the main estimate.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there is
h_{0}>0
such that

\left\lVert B_{0}u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\lesssim\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

when
0<h<h_{0}
.

The next lemma requires the assumption that
r
is nontrapping, and uses a positive commutator argument to give an estimate away from
\xi=0
.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there are
h_{0},C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\left\lVert Bu\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\lesssim\varepsilon\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+C_{\varepsilon}h^{-1}\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert B_{0}u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}.

when
0<h<h_{0}
. Here the implied constant is independent of
\varepsilon
.

Assuming these lemmas, the proof of Proposition
3.2
is given as follows.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Let first
\alpha=0
. Adding the estimates in Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
yields

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert B_{0}u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+\left\lVert Bu\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\varepsilon\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+C_{\varepsilon}h^{-1}\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{L^{2}}+\left\lVert B_{0}u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+C_{\varepsilon}h^{-1}\left\lVert Pu\right\rVert_{L^{2}}.

Choosing
\varepsilon
small enough allows us to absorb the first term on the right, which proves the estimate when
\alpha=0
. The same estimate is also true in a slightly larger domain
\Omega_{1}
. We apply the estimate in
\Omega_{1}
to
\psi(1-h^{2}\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u
where
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{1})
is a cutoff function with
\psi=1
near
\overline{\Omega}
. Using pseudolocal estimates for the commutator
[P,\psi(1-h^{2}\Delta)^{\alpha/2}]
and the fact that
\mathrm{supp}(u)\subset\Omega
gives the estimate for general
\alpha
.
∎

Next, we prove Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We first let
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
and consider the frozen coefficient operator
P_{x_{0}}
of semiclassical symbol
p(x_{0},\xi)
. Since
r(x)\geq c_{0}
and
\mathrm{supp}(\chi)\subset\{\left\lvert\xi\right\rvert\leq c_{0}/2\}
, we see that

\displaystyle\|B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle=\|\langle h\xi\rangle^{2s-1}\chi(h\xi)\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}=\left\|\frac{\langle h\xi\rangle^{2s-1}\chi(h\xi)}{p(x_{0},h\xi)}p(x_{0},h\xi)\hat{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\langle h\xi\rangle^{2s-1}\chi(h\xi)}{p(x_{0},h\xi)}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\|p(x_{0},h\xi)\hat{u}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|P_{x_{0}}u\|_{L^{2}}.

The implied constants are independent of
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
.

In order to obtain an argument for variable coefficients, first fix
\delta>0
, and then cover
\overline{\Omega}
with
N_{\delta}
balls
U_{j}:=B(x_{j},\rho)
in such way that
|r(x)^{2s}-r(x_{j})^{2s}|\leq\delta
for all
x\in\overline{B}_{j}
. Let now
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with
0\leq\chi_{j}\leq 1
be a partition of unity subordinate to the cover
\{U_{j}\}
such that
\sum\chi_{j}^{2}=1
near
\overline{\Omega}
. We can also arrange so that
\left\lVert\partial^{\gamma}\chi_{j}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq C_{\delta,M}
for
\left\lvert\gamma\right\rvert\leq M
, uniformly over
j
. Then for any fixed
j
, one has

\displaystyle\|B_{0}(\chi_{j}u)\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|P_{x_{j}}(\chi_{j}u)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\tilde{P}(\chi_{j}u)\|_{L^{2}}+\|(r(x_{j})^{2s}-r(x)^{2s})(\chi_{j}u)\|_{L^{2}}

(3.1)

\displaystyle\leq\|\chi_{j}\tilde{P}u\|_{L^{2}}+\|[\tilde{P},\chi_{j}]u\|_{L^{2}}+\delta\|\chi_{j}u\|_{L^{2}}.

On the left hand side, we also estimate

\|B_{0}(\chi_{j}u)\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\geq\|\chi_{j}B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-\|[\chi_{j},B_{0}]u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}.

The last commutator satisfies the semiclassical estimate

\|[\chi_{j},B_{0}]u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\leq C_{\delta}h\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}.

For the other commutator term, we use the fact that
\chi(D)
is smoothing and
(1-\chi(D))\tilde{P}
is a classical pseudodifferential operator to estimate

\|[\chi_{j},\tilde{P}]u\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|[\chi_{j},\chi(D)\tilde{P}]u\|_{L^{2}}+\|[\chi_{j},(1-\chi(D))\tilde{P}]u\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{\delta}h\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}}.

Thus, after squaring the estimate (
3.1
) we get

\|\chi_{j}B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}^{2}\lesssim\|\chi_{j}\tilde{P}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{\delta}h^{2}\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}^{2}+\delta^{2}\|\chi_{j}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Adding these estimates and writing
\psi=\sum\chi_{j}^{2}
gives

(3.2)

\|\psi B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}^{2}\lesssim\|\tilde{P}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{\delta}h^{2}\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}^{2}+\delta^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Since
\psi=1
near
\overline{\Omega}
and
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, pseudolocal estimates give
\left\lVert(1-\psi)B_{0}u\right\rVert_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\lesssim h\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}
. Thus taking square roots in (
3.2
) gives

(3.3)

\|B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\lesssim\|Pu\|_{L^{2}}+C_{\delta}h\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+(\delta+h^{2s})\|u\|_{L^{2}}.

Here we have used the fact that
\|(P-\tilde{P})u\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim h^{2s}\|u\|_{L^{2}}
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, we first choose
\delta
and
h_{0}
so that
\delta+h^{2s}\leq\varepsilon/2
for
h<h_{0}
. Then we choose
h_{0}
possibly even smaller so that
C_{\delta}h\leq\varepsilon/2
for
h<h_{0}
. After replacing
\varepsilon
by
\varepsilon/C
where
C
is the implied constant, we obtain the required result.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Define the operator
P^{\prime}:=r(x)^{-2s}P
. Since
r\geq c_{0}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, it is enough to prove the estimate for
P^{\prime}
instead than
P
. We shall use the
positive commutator method
, which requires the construction of a self-adjoint semiclassical pseudodifferential operator
A
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
of order
2s-1
(called a
commutant
) such that the following properties hold for all
u\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
:

(1)

\|Au\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}
,

(2)

(i[P^{\prime},A]u,u)_{L^{2}}\geq ch\|Bu\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-Ch\|B_{0}u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}
.

If such operator
A
can be constructed, then we have

\displaystyle ch\|Bu\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-Ch\|B_{0}u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\leq(i[P^{\prime},A]u,u)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\|Au\|_{L^{2}}\,\|P^{\prime}u\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}h\|Au\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+(\varepsilon^{2}h)^{-1}\|P^{\prime}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\varepsilon^{2}h\|u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+(\varepsilon^{2}h)^{-1}\|P^{\prime}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

and therefore

\|Bu\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+\|B_{0}u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+\varepsilon^{-2}h^{-2}\|P^{\prime}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}}.

This proves the required estimate.

In order to prove the existence of a suitable commutant
A
, we follow the same method as in the proof of
[
38
, Lemma 3.2]
. Consider the simple manifold
(\Omega,g)
, where the metric
g
is given by

g|_{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}:=r(x)^{-2}\delta_{ij}dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j},\qquad|g|:=\det g_{ij}=r(x)^{-2n},

\langle X,Y\rangle_{g}:=r(x)^{-2}X\cdot Y,\qquad|X|_{g}:=r(x)^{-1}|X|.

Let

p^{\prime}(x,\xi):=r(x)^{-2s}h^{2s}|\xi|^{2s}=h^{2s}|\xi|_{g}^{2s}

be the semiclassical principal symbol of
P^{\prime}
, let
H_{p^{\prime}}
be its Hamiltonian vector field, and let
a\in C^{\infty}(S^{*}\Omega)
be a function such that
H_{p^{\prime}}a>0
in
S^{*}\Omega
. Observe that such function exists, because
(\Omega,g)
is nontrapping. If
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{2}
, we can extend
a
smoothly to
T^{*}\Omega_{2}
as a symbol that is homogeneous of degree
2s-1
for
|\xi|_{g}\geq 1
, and such that

H_{p^{\prime}}a(x,\xi)\gtrsim|\xi|_{g}^{2s},\qquad\xi\in T^{*}\Omega_{1},\;|\xi|_{g}\sim 1,

for some
\Omega_{1}
such that
\Omega\Subset\Omega_{1}\Subset\Omega_{2}
.

The semiclassical differential operator
A
of order
2s-1
is now obtained by Weyl quantization of its real valued symbol
a
. Because
p^{\prime}
is not smooth, we are not allowed to use the semiclassical estimate for the commutator
[P^{\prime},A]
. However, recall that by definition
B_{0}:=\chi(hD)
and
B:=I-B_{0}
, with

\chi(\xi)=1\quad\mbox{ if }\,|\xi|\leq\frac{c_{0}}{4},\qquad\chi(\xi)=0\quad\mbox{ if }\,|\xi|\geq\frac{c_{0}}{2}.

Let us similarly define the operators
\tilde{B}_{0}:=\tilde{\chi}(hD)
and
\tilde{B}:=I-\tilde{B}_{0}
, with

\tilde{\chi}(\xi)=1\quad\mbox{ if }\,|\xi|\leq\frac{c_{0}}{16},\qquad\tilde{\chi}(\xi)=0\quad\mbox{ if }\,|\xi|\geq\frac{c_{0}}{8}.

Then we can compute

\displaystyle[P^{\prime},A]B

\displaystyle=[\tilde{B}P^{\prime},A]B+[\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime},A]B

\displaystyle=[\tilde{B}P^{\prime},A]B+\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime}AB-A\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime}B

\displaystyle=[\tilde{B}P^{\prime},A]B+\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime}[A,B]+\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime}BA-A[\tilde{B}_{0},P^{\prime}B]

\displaystyle=[\tilde{B}P^{\prime},A]B+\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime}[A,B]+[\tilde{B}_{0},P^{\prime}B]A-A[\tilde{B}_{0},P^{\prime}B].

Here we used that
\tilde{B}_{0}P^{\prime}B=[\tilde{B}_{0},P^{\prime}B]
, which holds because
B\tilde{B}_{0}=0
. Observe that the operators appearing in the commutators on the right-hand side have smooth symbols, and so we can use the semiclassical estimate to deduce

\|[P^{\prime},A]Bu\|_{H^{1-2s}_{scl}}\lesssim h\|Bu\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}.

Moreover, using a freezing coefficients argument followed by partition of unity as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we are able to deduce that

\displaystyle\|\chi_{j}P^{\prime}AB_{0}u\|

\displaystyle\leq\|P^{\prime}A\chi_{j}B_{0}u\|+\|[\chi_{j},P^{\prime}A]B_{0}u\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\|P^{\prime}_{x_{0}}A_{x_{0}}\chi_{j}B_{0}u\|+\|\left(r(x)^{2s}a(x,\xi)-r(x_{0})^{2s}a(x_{0},\xi)\right)\chi_{j}B_{0}u\|+C_{\delta}\|B_{0}u\|

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\delta)\|\chi_{j}B_{0}u\|+C_{\delta}\|B_{0}u\|,

which reveals that
\|P^{\prime}AB_{0}u\|\lesssim h\|B_{0}u\|
. Because a similar estimate holds for
AP^{\prime}B_{0}u
as well, we deduce that

\|[P^{\prime},A]B_{0}u\|_{H^{1-2s}_{scl}}\lesssim h\|B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}.

It now follows from the semiclassical Gårding inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with
\varepsilon
that

\displaystyle(i[P^{\prime},A]u,u)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=(i[P^{\prime},A]Bu,Bu)_{L^{2}}+(i[P^{\prime},A]Bu,B_{0}u)_{L^{2}}+(i[P^{\prime},A]B_{0}u,u)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}h\|Bu\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-\|i[P^{\prime},A]Bu\|_{H^{1-2s}_{scl}}\|B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\quad-\|i[P^{\prime},A]B_{0}u\|_{H^{1-2s}_{scl}}\left(\|Bu\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}+\|B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq c^{\prime}h\|Bu\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-2h\|Bu\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}\|B_{0}u\|_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-h\|B_{0}u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}

\displaystyle\geq(c^{\prime}-\varepsilon)h\|Bu\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}-C_{\varepsilon}h\|B_{0}u\|^{2}_{H^{2s-1}_{scl}}.

This proves the second commutator inequality.
∎

Finally, we prove Proposition
3.1
in light of Proposition
3.2
:

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and consider
f\in H^{\alpha}(\Omega)
. Define the subspace
E:=L(C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega))
of
H^{1-2s-\alpha}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where
L:=(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q
. Finally, define on
E
the functional
T
given by

T(Lv):=\langle f,v\rangle,\qquad\mbox{ for all }v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

Observe that
T
is well-posed by the linearity of
L
and the estimate in Proposition
3.2
. The same inequality also implies that
T
is bounded, since
(H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega))^{*}=H^{-\alpha}_{scl,\overline{\Omega}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and therefore

\displaystyle|T(Lv)|

\displaystyle=|\langle f,v\rangle|\leq\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{(H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega))^{*}}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)}h^{2s-1}\|Lv\|_{H_{scl}^{1-2s-\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

By the Hahn-Banach theorem, it is possible to find an extension
\tilde{T}
of
T
which is a bounded functional on
H^{1-2s-\alpha}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with norm at most
\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)}h^{2s-1}
. Using the Riesz representation theorem, we find the unique
\tilde{u}\in(H^{1-2s-\alpha}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))^{*}=H^{2s+\alpha-1}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
such that

\tilde{T}(w)=\langle\tilde{u},w\rangle,\qquad\mbox{ for all }w\in H^{1-2s-\alpha}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

If now
u:=\tilde{u}|_{\Omega}
, we have

\|u\|_{H^{2s+\alpha-1}_{scl}(\Omega)}\leq\|\tilde{u}\|_{H^{2s+\alpha-1}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\lesssim h^{2s-1}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)},

and for all
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
we have

\langle f,v\rangle=T(Lv)=\tilde{T}(Lv)=\langle\tilde{u},Lv\rangle=\langle L\tilde{u},v\rangle.

∎

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
now follow by combining Proposition
3.1
with each of the two cases of Proposition
2.2
.

4.
Applications to inverse problems

We begin by considering the inverse problem for the operator

(4.1)

(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r(x)^{2s}+q(x)

consisting in recovering the refraction index
r\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and potential
q\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
from DN data. The proof of well-posedness for the Dirichlet problem associated to the above operator in the space
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
does not differ substantially to the one obtained in
[
28
]
for the case
r\equiv 0
. We shall always assume that
0
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue for said problem, which ensures the well-definedness of the DN map
\Lambda_{\tau,r,q}:H^{s}(\Omega_{e})\rightarrow H^{s}(\Omega_{e})^{*}
via the associated bilinear form
B_{\tau,r,q}
:

\langle\Lambda_{\tau,r,q}[f],[g]\rangle:=B_{\tau,r,q}(u_{f},g)=\langle(-\Delta)^{s/2}u_{f},(-\Delta)^{s/2}g\rangle+\langle(q-\tau^{2s}r^{2s})r_{\Omega}u_{f},r_{\Omega}g\rangle_{\Omega}.

Using the above definition, it is easy to obtain an Alessandrini identity. For
j=1,2
, let
f_{j}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega_{e})
be an exterior value, and assume that
u_{j}\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is the corresponding solution in the Dirichlet problem for the operator (
4.1
) with coefficients
r_{j},q_{j}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Let also
\Lambda_{\tau,j}:=\Lambda_{\tau,r_{j},q_{j}}
. Then it holds

(4.2)

\langle(\Lambda_{\tau,1}-\Lambda_{\tau,2})[f_{1}],[f_{2}]\rangle=\langle((q_{1}-q_{2})+\tau^{2s}(r_{2}^{2s}-r_{1}^{2s}))r_{\Omega}u_{1},r_{\Omega}u_{2}\rangle_{\Omega},

from which we can easily obtain a uniqueness result for fixed frequencies using the Runge approximation technique from
[
28
]
. However, the stability associated to this method is at most logarithmic. In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.3
, thus showing that Hölder stability for the inverse problem in the regime
s\geq 1/2
can be achieved by using high frequencies. We begin by rigorously defining the Cauchy data
C_{r,q_{j}}^{\tau}
associated to the operator

P_{j}:=(-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q_{j}

and the distance function
\delta
measuring the distance between the Cauchy data for the potentials
q_{1},q_{2}
.

The operator
P_{j}
is associated to the symmetric bilinear form

B_{j}(u,v):=((-\Delta)^{s/2}u,(-\Delta)^{s/2}v)_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}-\tau^{2s}(r^{2s}u|_{\Omega},v|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}+(q_{j}u|_{\Omega},v|_{\Omega})_{\Omega},

defined for all
u,v\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Let
S_{j}
be the linear subspace of
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
consisting of weak solutions to
P_{j}u=0
in
\Omega
, that is

S_{j}:=\{u\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}):P_{j}u=0\mbox{ in }\Omega\},

and consider the linear operator
T_{j}:S_{j}\rightarrow X^{*}
defined for all
v\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
by

\langle T_{j}u,[v]\rangle:=B_{j}(u,v).

We have to check that
T_{j}
is actually well-defined. Recall that
X
is given by

X:=H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})/\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega),

to which we associate the usual quotient norm. Thus
T_{j}
will be well-defined as soon as
B_{j}(u,v)=B_{j}(u,v+\varphi)
for all
u\in S_{j}
,
v\in H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. This is true because
u\in S_{j}
implies
B_{j}(u,\varphi)=0
for all
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
. The operator
T_{j}
is bounded, since for all
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
we have

\displaystyle\langle T_{j}u,[v]\rangle=B_{j}(u,v+\varphi)\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\|v+\varphi\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

and thus, by taking infimum with respect to
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)
we get

\displaystyle\|T_{j}u\|_{X^{*}}=\sup_{\|[v]\|_{X}=1}\langle T_{j}u,[v]\rangle\lesssim\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\sup_{\|[v]\|_{X}=1}\inf_{\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)}\|v+\varphi\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}.

It is known that if
\Omega
is Lipschitz, then
X
can be naturally identified with
H^{s}(\Omega_{e})
; however, recall that while objects in
X
are equivalence classes with representatives in
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, the elements of
H^{s}(\Omega_{e})
are restrictions of
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
functions to
\Omega_{e}
. As a consequence,
X^{*}
can be identified with
(H^{s}(\Omega_{e}))^{*}
, and therefore with
H^{-s}_{\overline{\Omega}_{e}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, which is the set of
H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
functions with support in
\overline{\Omega}_{e}
. Finally, only for
s<1/2
we can identify
H^{-s}_{\overline{\Omega}_{e}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
with
H^{-s}(\Omega_{e})
. With these distinctions in mind, we define the following Cauchy data

C_{r,q_{j}}^{\tau}:=\{([u],T_{j}u)\in X\times X^{*}:u\in S_{j}\}

and the distance

\displaystyle\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau}):

\displaystyle=\max_{j\neq k}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{u\in S_{k}:\|[u]\|_{X}=1\}\end{subarray}}\inf_{v\in S_{j}}\|([v],T_{j}v)-([u],T_{k}u)\|_{X\times X^{*}}

\displaystyle=\max_{j\neq k}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{u\in S_{k}:\|[u]\|_{X}=1\}\end{subarray}}\inf_{v\in S_{j}}\left(\|[v-u]\|_{X}+\|T_{j}v-T_{k}u\|_{X^{*}}\right).

Observe that when
\Omega
is Lipschitz both spaces
X,X^{*}
can be identified with spaces of functions on
\Omega_{e}
, and thus the Cauchy data given above is exterior. If
s<1/2
, both the Cauchy data and the distance function coincide with the usual ones.

We can now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Step 1.
Using the symbols introduced in the above discussion, let
u_{1}\in S_{1}
and
u_{2},\tilde{u}_{2}\in S_{2}
. We compute

\displaystyle((q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}|_{\Omega},u_{2}|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=B_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})-B_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle=B_{1}(u_{1},u_{2})-B_{2}(\tilde{u}_{2},u_{2})+B_{2}(u_{2},\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1})

\displaystyle=\langle T_{1}u_{1}-T_{2}\tilde{u}_{2},[u_{2}]\rangle+\langle T_{2}u_{2},[\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1}]\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{1}u_{1}-T_{2}\tilde{u}_{2}\|_{X^{*}}\|[u_{2}]\|_{X}+\|T_{2}u_{2}\|_{X^{*}}\|[\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1}]\|_{X}.

By the boundedness of
T_{2}
and the well-posedness estimate for the problem relative to operator
P_{2}
, we have for all
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega)

\|T_{2}u_{2}\|_{X^{*}}\lesssim\|u_{2}\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\lesssim\|u_{2}+\varphi\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})},

and thus by taking the infimum with respect to
\varphi
we get
\|T_{2}u_{2}\|_{X^{*}}\lesssim\|[u_{2}]\|_{X}
. Eventually, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle((q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}|_{\Omega},u_{2}|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|T_{1}u_{1}-T_{2}\tilde{u}_{2}\|_{X^{*}}+\|[\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{1}]\|_{X}\right)\|[u_{2}]\|_{X}

\displaystyle=\left(\|T_{1}v_{1}-T_{2}\tilde{v}_{2}\|_{X^{*}}+\|[\tilde{v}_{2}-v_{1}]\|_{X}\right)\|[u_{1}]\|_{X}\|[u_{2}]\|_{X},

where we normalized
v_{1}:=u_{1}/\|[u_{1}]\|_{X}
and
\tilde{v}_{2}:=\tilde{u}_{2}/\|[u_{1}]\|_{X}
, in order to have
\|[v_{1}]\|_{X}=1
. Taking on both sides the infimum with respect to
\tilde{v}_{2}\in S_{2}
and then the supremum with respect to
v_{1}\in S_{1}
with
\|[v_{1}]\|_{X}=1
, we finally get

\displaystyle|((q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}|_{\Omega},u_{2}|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}|\lesssim\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau})\|[u_{1}]\|_{X}\|[u_{2}]\|_{X}.

If
\Omega
is Lipschitz, we can alternatively restate the above inequality as

(4.3)

|((q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}|_{\Omega},u_{2}|_{\Omega})_{\Omega}|\lesssim\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau})\|u_{1}|_{\Omega_{e}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{e})}\|u_{2}|_{\Omega_{e}}\|_{H^{s}(\Omega_{e})}.

Step 2.
Next, we will show that
\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega_{e})}\leq C\tau^{s}
. To this end, let
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and let
u_{M}:=e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}}a
be the approximate solution constructed in Section
2
. The phase
\varphi_{0}
and the amplitude
a
are obtained by solving eikonal and transport equations near
\overline{\Omega}
. Given an open set
\Omega^{\prime}\supset\overline{\Omega}
, define a smooth cutoff function
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega^{\prime})
that equals
1
near
\overline{\Omega}
, and let
u^{\prime}_{M}:=\chi u_{M}=e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}}\chi a
. After a normalization we can fix
\|u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=1
, which implies

\|\nabla u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\tau\|\nabla\varphi_{0}u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+O(1)=O(\tau),

and therefore
\|u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}=O(\tau)
. It now follows by interpolation that

\|u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\leq\|u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{1-s}\|u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}^{s}=O(\tau^{s}).

The approximate solution
u^{\prime}_{M}
can be upgraded to a solution
u=u^{\prime}_{M}+r_{M}
to the problem
Lu=0
in
\Omega
, where by Proposition
3.1
for all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
the error
r_{M}
verifies

\|r_{M}\|_{H^{2s-1+\alpha}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}\lesssim\tau^{1-2s}\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{\alpha}_{scl}(\Omega)}.

Since by fixing
\alpha=1-s
we can compute for large
\tau

\displaystyle\|r_{M}\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{s}\|r_{M}\|_{H^{s}_{scl}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{1-s}\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{1-s}_{scl}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{1-s}\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{s}\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{1}_{scl}(\Omega)}^{1-s},

in order to obtain the wanted estimate for
\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}
we only need to estimate
Lu^{\prime}_{M}=L(\chi u_{M})
in the norm of
H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)
, for
\beta=1,2
. Recall that we have already obtained similar estimates for
Lu_{M}
as part of the construction of geometrical optics approximate solutions. These were derived from formula (
2.4
), which for
u^{\prime}_{M}
implies

\begin{split}&e^{-i\tau\varphi}Lu^{\prime}_{M}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{t_{j}\}_{j=1}^{F_{M}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}:\\
\sum_{j}t_{j}\leq\mu\end{subarray}}\tau^{2s-\mu+\sum_{j=1}^{F_{M}}t_{j}(1-2sj)}L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}(\chi a)+q\chi a+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}).\end{split}

We obtained the components
a_{l}
of the amplitude
a
by solving transport equations close to
\overline{\Omega}
for all the distinct powers of
\tau
, up to a fixed
\tau^{-A_{M}}
. Given the definition of the cutoff function
\chi
and the fact that all the operators
L_{\mu;\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
are local, we see that for
x\in\Omega

Lu^{\prime}_{M}=Lu_{M}+O(\tau^{-n-N/2}),

and thus we have the same estimates for
\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}
as for
\|Lu_{M}\|_{H^{\beta}_{scl}(\Omega)}
. Eventually

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}+\|r_{M}\|_{H^{s}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{1-s}\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{s}\|Lu^{\prime}_{M}\|_{H^{1}_{scl}(\Omega)}^{1-s}+O(\tau^{s})

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{1-s}\|Lu_{M}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{s}\|Lu_{M}\|_{H^{1}_{scl}(\Omega)}^{1-s}+O(\tau^{s})

\displaystyle\lesssim\tau^{1-s-M}+O(\tau^{s})=O(\tau^{s}).

The argument above holds for both
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, so we have obtained in particular
\left\lVert u_{j}\right\rVert_{H^{s}(\Omega_{e})}\lesssim\tau^{s}
for
j=1,2
. Coming back to equation (
4.3
), we get that the inequality

(4.4)

\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}\overline{u_{2}}\,dx\lesssim\left[\sup_{\tau\geq\tau_{0}}\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau})\right]\tau^{2s}

holds for all
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
. For simplicity of notation, we will let
\delta:=\sup_{\tau\geq\tau_{0}}\delta(C_{r,q_{1}}^{\tau},C_{r,q_{2}}^{\tau})
.

Step 3.
We now argue as in Section 4 of
[
38
]
. Consider the simple manifold
(\Omega,g)
, where the metric
g
is given by

g|_{(x_{1},...,x_{n})}:=r(x)^{-2}\delta_{ij}dx^{i}\otimes dx^{j},\qquad|g|:=\det g_{ij}=r(x)^{-2n},

\langle X,Y\rangle_{g}:=r(x)^{-2}X\cdot Y,\qquad|X|_{g}:=r(x)^{-1}|X|.

We can choose an open set
\Omega^{\prime}\supset\overline{\Omega}
such that
(\Omega^{\prime},g)
is also simple (recall that
r\equiv 1
outside of
\Omega
). If
p
is any point such that
p\in\partial\Omega^{\prime}
, by the properties of simple manifolds each
x\in\Omega
can be expressed as
x=\exp_{p}(\rho\theta)
, where
\rho>0
and
\theta\in S^{n-1}
. This defines the
polar normal coordinates
on
\Omega
, which allow us to write the metric
g
as

g|_{(\rho,\theta)}=d\rho^{2}+g_{0}(\rho,\theta)d\theta\otimes d\theta,\qquad|g|=|g_{0}|.

We observe that the functions
\varphi_{0}(\rho,\theta)=\pm\rho
solve the eikonal equation. In fact, since the
\varphi_{0}
defined above do not depend on
\theta
, by the expression of the metric in polar normal coordinates we get

r(x)^{-2}\nabla\varphi_{0}\cdot\nabla\psi=\langle\nabla\varphi_{0},\nabla\psi\rangle_{g}=\pm\partial_{\rho}\psi,\qquad|\nabla\varphi_{0}|=r(x)|\nabla\varphi_{0}|_{g}=r(x).

Step 4.
Having solved the eikonal equation and found the phase function
\varphi_{0}
, we now turn to the transport equation for the principal amplitude
a_{0}
. We see that
a_{0}
solves

\begin{cases}L_{1;0}a_{0}=0,&\mbox{ if }s\in(1/2,1),\\
(L_{1;0}+q)a_{0}=0,&\mbox{ if }s=1/2,\end{cases}

and thus we need to compute the operator
L_{1;0}
. Let us write the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{g}
in Euclidean and polar normal coordinates. We have

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle=|g|^{-1/2}\partial_{i}(|g|^{1/2}g^{ij}\partial_{j}\varphi_{0})=r^{n}\nabla\cdot(r^{2-n}\nabla\varphi_{0})

\displaystyle=r^{2}\Delta\varphi_{0}+(2-n)r\nabla r\cdot\nabla\varphi_{0}=r^{2}\Delta\varphi_{0}\pm(2-n)r^{3}\partial_{\rho}r

and

\Delta_{g}\varphi_{0}=|g|^{-1/2}\partial_{\rho}(|g|^{1/2}\partial_{\rho}\varphi_{0})=\pm r^{n}\partial_{\rho}(r^{-n})=\mp nr^{-1}\partial_{\rho}r,

so that

r^{2}\Delta\varphi_{0}=\mp\left(nr^{-1}+(2-n)r^{3}\right)\partial_{\rho}r,

or equivalently

\mp r^{-2}\Delta\varphi_{0}=\left(nr^{-5}+(2-n)r^{-1}\right)\partial_{\rho}r.

As observed in the Appendix, we can write the operator
L_{1;0}
as

\displaystyle L_{1;0}=\pm 2sir^{2s}\left(\partial_{\rho}\mp\frac{r^{-2}\Delta\varphi_{0}}{2}+(1-s)r^{-1}\partial_{\rho}r\right),

where the sign changes are due to the fact that
L_{1;0}
depends on the phase function
\varphi_{0}
. Thus we get

\displaystyle L_{1;0}

\displaystyle=\pm 2sir^{2s}\left(\partial_{\rho}+\left(\frac{n}{2}r^{-5}+\left(2-\frac{n}{2}-s\right)r^{-1}\right)\partial_{\rho}r\right)=\pm 2sir^{2s}\left(\partial_{\rho}+\partial_{\rho}f\right),

where
f(r):=\left(2-\frac{n}{2}-s\right)\log r-\frac{n}{8}r^{-4}
, and the principal amplitude
a_{0}
must solve

\begin{cases}\partial_{\rho}a_{0}+a_{0}\partial_{\rho}f=0,&\mbox{ if }s\in(1/2,1),\\
\partial_{\rho}a_{0}+a_{0}\left(\partial_{\rho}f\mp iqr^{-1}\right)=0,&\mbox{ if }s=1/2.\end{cases}

Therefore, we obtain
a_{0}(\rho,\theta)=b(\theta)e^{-f(\rho,\theta)+iJ(q(\rho,\theta))}
, where
b
is any function depending only on
\theta
, and

J(q(\rho,\theta)):=\begin{cases}0,&\mbox{ if }s\in(1/2,1),\\
\pm\int_{0}^{\rho}q(t,\theta)r(t,\theta)^{-1}dt,&\mbox{ if }s=1/2.\end{cases}

In polar normal coordinates, an approximate solution (of order
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
) corresponding to a fixed potential
q
will take the form

u_{M}(\rho,\theta)=e^{\pm i\tau\rho}a_{0}+v_{M},

where the error
v_{M}
depending on
b
and
q
is defined as

v_{M}:=e^{i\tau\varphi_{0}}\sum_{l=1}^{A_{M}}\tau^{-\alpha_{l}}a_{l}.

It also follows from the construction of approximate geometrical optics solutions that for all
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
the amplitude
a_{j+1}
is obtained by solving the transport equation

L_{1;0}a_{j+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}\tilde{L}_{ij}(a_{i}),

where the differential operator
\tilde{L}_{ij}
has order at most
N
, and does not depend on the function
b
. Thus we can estimate
\|a_{j+1}\|_{H^{\beta}}\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{j}\|a_{i}\|_{H^{\beta+N}}
, and ultimately using induction we obtain that there exists a
t_{M}\in{\mathbb{N}}
so large that

\|a_{j}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|a_{0}\|_{H^{t_{M}}},\qquad\mbox{for all }j\in\{1,...,A_{M}\}.

This allows us to estimate

\|v_{M}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|a_{0}\|_{H^{t_{M}}},

where the implied factor depends on
M
, but not on the function
b
. The exponent
\alpha_{1}
in the above formula is as in the statement of Proposition
2.2
, and thus it is the smallest real number in the set
({\mathbb{N}}+(2s-\lfloor 2s\rfloor){\mathbb{N}})\setminus\{0\}
. As such, we have

\alpha_{1}=\begin{cases}2s-1&\mbox{ if }s\in(1/2,1),\\
1&\mbox{ if }s=1/2.\end{cases}

Step 5.
Let now
u_{1}
be the exact solution of equation
((-\Delta)^{s}-\tau^{2s}r^{2s}+q_{1})u_{1}=0
given by

u_{1}=u_{M,1}+r_{M,1}=e^{\pm i\tau\rho}b_{1}e^{-f+iJ(q_{1})}+v_{M,1}+r_{M,1},

where
b_{1},q_{1}
are a fixed smooth function and potential. We are indicating by
r_{M,1}
the error due to the approximation to order
M\in{\mathbb{N}}
from approximate geometrical optics to exact solution, while by
v_{M,1}
we are indicating the error due to the non-principal amplitudes, as defined above. We can do a similar construction for
u_{2}
, which corresponds to potential
q_{2}
and function
b_{2}
. This gives

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})u_{M,1}\overline{u_{M,2}}dx\right|\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}\overline{u_{2}}dx\right|+O(\tau^{1-M}),

where the small error of order
\tau^{1-M}
is independent of the functions
b_{1},b_{2}
and is due to the error terms
r_{M,1},r_{M,2}
, which we estimated as above. We also compute

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})e^{-2f+iJ(q_{1}-q_{2})}b_{1}b_{2}\;dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})u_{M,1}\overline{u_{M,2}}dx\right|

\displaystyle\quad+C\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|a_{0,1}\|_{H^{t_{M}}}\|a_{0,2}\|_{H^{t_{M}}},

where the error term of order
\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}
is obtained from the estimates for
v_{M,1},v_{M,2}
and the constant
C
is independent of
b_{1},b_{2}
.

For the sake of simplicity, let us define

\displaystyle Q:=(q_{1}-q_{2})e^{-2f+iJ(q_{1}-q_{2})}r^{-n}.

Using the last two inequalities and (
4.4
), we now obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\Omega}Qr^{n}b_{1}b_{2}\;dx\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|a_{0,1}\|_{H^{t_{M}}}\|a_{0,2}\|_{H^{t_{M}}},

where the implied constant is independent of
b_{1},b_{2}
.
Since
dx=|g|^{1/2}d\rho d\theta=r^{-n}d\rho d\theta
, using the fact that
q_{1}=q_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\setminus\Omega
we have

(4.5)

\int_{\Omega}Qr^{n}b_{1}b_{2}\;dx=\int_{\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime}}b_{1}(\theta)b_{2}(\theta)\int_{0}^{\tau_{\Omega^{\prime}}(p,\theta)}Q(\rho,\theta)d\rho d\theta=\int_{\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime}}b_{1}b_{2}IQ(p,\cdot)d\theta,

where
I
is the geodesic ray transform on
\Omega^{\prime}
. This gives us the estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime}}b_{1}b_{2}IQ(p,\cdot)d\theta\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|a_{0,1}\|_{H^{t_{M}}}\|a_{0,2}\|_{H^{t_{M}}},

which holds for every choice of
b_{1},b_{2}
with a uniform constant independent of the point
p\in\partial\Omega^{\prime}
. Therefore, integrating over
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
gives

\displaystyle|\int_{\partial_{+}S\Omega^{\prime}}

\displaystyle b_{1}(\theta)b_{2}(\theta)IQ(p,\theta)d(\partial S\Omega)|\leq\int_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}|\int_{\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime}}b_{1}(\theta)b_{2}(\theta)IQ(p,\theta)d\theta|dp

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}\|a_{0,1}\|_{H^{t_{M}}}\|a_{0,2}\|_{H^{t_{M}}}dp

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}\|b_{1}\|_{H^{t_{M}}(\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime})}\|b_{2}\|_{H^{t_{M}}(\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime})}dp.

Let us choose
b_{1}(\theta):=I(I^{*}IQ)\nu_{p}\cdot\theta
, where
\nu_{p}
is the normal to
\partial\Omega^{\prime}
at
p
, and
b_{2}(\theta):=1
. By the boundedness of
I,I^{*}I
, this gives

\displaystyle\|I^{*}IQ\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\int_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}\|I(I^{*}IQ)\|_{H^{t_{M}}(\partial_{+}S_{p}\Omega^{\prime})}dp

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|Q\|_{H^{t_{M}}(\Omega)}.

Using a stability estimate for
I^{*}I
on simple manifolds, we get

\displaystyle\|Q\|^{2}_{H^{-1}}\lesssim\|I^{*}IQ\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|Q\|_{H^{t_{M}}(\Omega)}.

Using the fact that
r
is bounded away from
0
and the interpolation inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|^{2+\frac{2}{t_{M}}}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|Q\|^{\frac{2(t_{M}+1)}{t_{M}}}_{L^{2}}\leq\|Q\|^{2}_{H^{-1}}\|Q\|^{\frac{2}{t_{M}}}_{H^{t_{M}}}\lesssim\left(\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}\|Q\|_{H^{t_{M}}}\right)\|Q\|^{\frac{2}{t_{M}}}_{H^{t_{M}}}.

Observe now that
Q
is independent of
\tau
, and therefore using the high order Sobolev estimates for
q_{1},q_{2}
we get
\|Q\|_{H^{t_{M}}}\lesssim 1
. As a result,

\displaystyle\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|^{2+\frac{2}{t_{M}}}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta\tau^{2s}+\tau^{-\alpha_{1}}.

Because
\alpha_{1}>0
, the above inequality can be optimized with respect to
\tau
: choosing
\tau=\delta^{-\frac{1}{2s+\alpha_{1}}}
, we finally get the stability estimate

\displaystyle\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{4s+2\alpha_{1}}\frac{t_{M}}{t_{M}+1}}.

Here
t_{M}
can be taken arbitrarily large, and so the exponent
\gamma
can approach
\frac{\alpha_{1}}{4s+2\alpha_{1}}
. However, this implies that we need to assume higher order Sobolev estimates for
q_{1},q_{2}
.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

As discussed in Remark
2.4
, the geometrical optics solutions constructed in Proposition
2.2
for the two regimes
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
differ substantially. Having now completely solved the eikonal and transport equations in polar normal coordinates, we see that in both cases the principal phase function
\varphi_{0}
oscillates very rapidly for large
\tau
in the direction of the geodesics of the simple refraction index
r
, while it is constant in the orthogonal direction
\theta
. If there are no other phase functions, which is the case for
s\in[\frac{1}{2},1)
, this behaviour allows us to interpret the integral
\int_{\Omega}(q_{1}-q_{2})u_{1}\overline{u_{2}}dx
in terms of the geodesic ray transform, as in equation (
4.5
). This in turn is our main tool in the final Step 5 of the proof.

However, in the regime
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
the geometrical optics construction produces a sequence of non vanishing phase functions
\varphi_{j}
, associated to both positive and negative powers of
\tau
. These additional phase functions do not follow the geodesics of
r
, and also they do not necessarily oscillate rapidly for
\tau\rightarrow\infty
, which makes our method inapplicable in the case
s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Heuristically, one can reason that geometrical optics solutions with ”classical” behaviour only arise when the fractional parameter
s
is close enough to
1
(and thus the main part of the operator is close to the Laplacian), while for
s<\frac{1}{2}
they do not exist due to the fact that the main part of the operator has order less than
1
, and is thus very different from the Laplacian.
