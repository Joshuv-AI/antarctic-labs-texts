Molecular Genetic Analysis and Prenatal Diagnosis of a Family With MECP2 Duplication Syndrome
Authors: FengQiu Lin, DingGuo Chen, ZhengLin Lu, Yu Huang, ChunFang Wang, JuHua Liang, XueJuan Nong, ZhengYi Chang, FuBo Wei, GuiDan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to conduct a molecular genetic analysis and prenatal diagnosis for a family with MECP2 duplication syndrome (MDS), with the goal of improving diagnostic accuracy and enabling informed reproductive decisions to reduce the incidence of this condition.MethodsThe study involved a proband (a 15‐week‐old fetus), his symptomatic older brother, and their parents. Genomic DNA from chorionic villus and peripheral blood samples was analyzed using high‐throughput sequencing to detect chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs). To confirm the findings, multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA) was specifically employed.ResultsHigh‐throughput sequencing identified a 0.35 Mb duplication at Xq28 in the fetus and his mother, and a 0.4 Mb duplication in the brother. The duplicated region contained the critical MECP2 gene. MLPA analysis confirmed the MECP2 gene duplication in the fetus, leading to a definitive diagnosis of MDS. The mother was identified as an asymptomatic carrier.ConclusionBoth the proband and his brother were diagnosed with MDS, having inherited the duplication from their asymptomatic mother. The combined use of high‐throughput sequencing and MLPA proved to be an effective strategy for accurate pedigree analysis and prenatal diagnosis of MDS, providing a crucial basis for genetic counseling.

Introduction

MECP2 duplication syndrome (MDS) comprises a spectrum of clinical manifestations caused by duplication of the MECP2 gene located in the Xq28 region of the X chromosome. It is an X‐linked recessive neurodevelopmental disorder and accounts for approximately 1% of cases of X‐linked intellectual disability [1]. Most affected individuals are male, with an estimated live‐birth incidence of approximately 1 in 150,000. Approximately 50% of patients die before 25 years of age, primarily because of severe infections and recurrent epileptic seizures [2, 3]. The MECP2 gene encodes methyl‐CpG‐binding protein 2, a critical epigenetic regulator in neurodevelopment that plays an essential role in neuronal maturation, synapse formation, and gene expression regulation. Duplication of this gene results in overexpression of the MECP2 protein, with expression levels approximately 2‐ to 6‐fold higher than normal, thereby disrupting normal neural network function and leading to intellectual disability, hypotonia, and other related symptoms. MDS follows a typical X‐linked recessive inheritance pattern, with affected males showing complete penetrance and severe phenotypes. It accounts for approximately 2% of cases in males with intellectual disability accompanied by severe progressive neurological symptoms [4]. Female carriers are often asymptomatic because of skewed X‐chromosome inactivation, but they can transmit the pathogenic variant to their offspring [5]. Carrier mothers have a 50% probability of transmitting the mutation to their sons, who will be affected, and a 50% probability of transmitting it to their daughters, who will become carriers. Given the lack of effective treatment for this disorder, pedigree analysis and prenatal diagnosis have become essential strategies for preventing the birth of affected children. As MDS is a rare disease with limited clinical reports, this study aimed to perform molecular genetic analysis and prenatal diagnosis in a family affected by MDS in order to improve early diagnostic capability, enhance disease management, and optimize clinical outcomes.

Subjects and Methods

Subjects

The proband was a 15‐week‐old fetus evaluated at Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities. The proband's mother was 37 years old, representing advanced maternal age, and had a history of adverse pregnancy outcomes. She had a normal phenotype, no history of consanguinity, and one living child. The proband's older brother presented with severe intellectual disability, emotional instability, typical autistic behaviors, motor developmental delay, abnormal muscle tone, limb rigidity, occasional epileptic seizures, and immunodeficiency. Based on the mother's adverse obstetric history and the clinical manifestations observed in the older sibling, the fetus underwent combined high‐throughput sequencing and multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA). In addition, peripheral blood samples were collected from the proband's parents and older brother for high‐throughput sequencing to confirm the diagnosis and perform pedigree analysis.

Methods

Genomic DNA Extraction

After written informed consent was obtained, genomic DNA was extracted from the fetus and family members using a genomic DNA extraction kit (Guangzhou Darui Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA purity and concentration were measured using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and the dsDNA HS Assay Kit. The extracted DNA samples were stored at −20°C until further analysis.

High‐Throughput Sequencing for Chromosomal Copy Number Variation (CNV) Analysis

Constructed DNA libraries were sequenced using the DA8600 high‐throughput sequencer (Guangzhou Darui Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) with an average sequencing depth of 0.2× and coverage of ≥ 99%. The sequencing data were analyzed bioinformatically by aligning each read to the reference genome (GRCh37/hg19), followed by standardized Z‐score analysis to detect chromosomal abnormalities. A Z‐score threshold of ±3 was used to define significant copy number changes. Public databases, including DECIPHER, UCSC, OMIM, ClinGen, ClinVar, DGV, and PubMed, were consulted to assess the clinical significance of the detected CNVs. In the present study, the 0.2× CNV‐seq result was used primarily for CNV screening and family segregation analysis rather than definitive breakpoint resolution. The fetal MECP2 duplication was independently confirmed by MLPA. Interpretation of CNVs was performed in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines for CNVs.

MLPA Testing and Result Analysis

MLPA analysis was performed using the SALSA MLPA Probemix P015 MECP2 kit (MRC‐Holland, Amsterdam, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer's standard protocol. This probe mix contains 24 probes targeting the X chromosome, including four probes covering exons 1–4 of the MECP2 gene, and eight reference probes targeting autosomal genes (including TUBB3, GAPDH, and others). Raw data were analyzed using Coffalyser. Net software. The signal intensity of each target probe was normalized to the average signal intensity of the reference probes. The copy number cut‐off values were defined as follows: < 0.7 indicated a deletion, 0.7–1.3 indicated a normal copy number, and > 1.3 indicated a duplication.

Results

Whole‐Genome CNV Analysis by High‐Throughput Sequencing

High‐throughput sequencing of chorionic villus samples from the pregnant woman revealed a 0.35 Mb duplication at chrX: 153,000,000–153,350,050 (GRCh37/hg19), indicating the presence of an Xq28 duplication on the fetal X chromosome (Figure 1A and Table 1). The identified duplicated fragment showed substantial overlap with a ClinGen dosage‐sensitive region with a triplosensitivity score of three. This region corresponds to Xq28, encompassing MECP2, located at chrX:153,273,980–153,375,749 in GRCh37/hg19 (Figure 2). Given the ultra‐low sequencing depth (0.2×), the reported CNV boundaries and size should be regarded as approximate rather than definitive. The reported duplicated region involved 15 protein‐coding genes/loci, including MECP2 (OMIM300005), IRAK1 (OMIM300283), L1CAM, NAA10, AVPR2, ABCD1, SSR4, and HCFC1. Peripheral blood analysis of the mother revealed an approximately 0.35 Mb pathogenic duplication in the same Xq28 region, consistent with the chorionic villus results, suggesting maternal inheritance of the fetal duplication (Figure 1B and Table 1). Peripheral blood analysis of the proband's brother also identified a pathogenic 0.4 Mb duplication at chrX:152,949,977–153,350,520 (GRCh37/hg19) (Figure 1C and Table 1). No pathogenic CNV was detected in the father (Figure 1D and Table 1). MLPA Results

MLPA analysis using the P015 probe mix confirmed that the signal intensities of the MECP2 gene probes in the fetal Xq28 region were all > 1.3 (range, 1.35–1.52). These findings indicate increased copy numbers of exons 1–4 of the MECP2 gene and its flanking regions in the chorionic villus sample, consistent with a duplication. Combined with the high‐throughput sequencing results, these findings supported a diagnosis of MDS in the fetus (Figure 3). Based on these results, the family pedigree is shown in Figure 4.

Variant Interpretation

The Xq28 duplication involving MECP2 was classified as pathogenic according to ACMG/ClinGen principles for CNV interpretation. Supporting evidence included its location within a known dosage‐sensitive region (ClinGen triplosensitivity score = 3), segregation with the affected male siblings in this family, absence of the duplication in the unaffected father, and the established association between MECP2 duplication and MDS. The fetal MECP2 duplication was independently confirmed by MLPA. Because the sequencing was performed at 0.2× depth, the precise CNV breakpoints were not considered definitively established.

Discussion

In this study, high‐throughput sequencing identified an Xq28 duplication in the fetus, the mother, and the proband's older brother. All reported duplicated segments involved the MECP2 locus, which is the principal pathogenic gene responsible for MDS [14, 15]. MECP2 dosage imbalance can disrupt neuronal development and synaptic function, and experimental studies have shown that MECP2 overexpression is associated with abnormalities in synaptic plasticity and increased oxidative stress [16, 17, 18, 19]. The Xq28 region also contains repetitive sequences that may contribute to structural variation and differences in breakpoint mapping [20]. In the present family, the combination of CNV‐seq screening, MLPA confirmation, and pedigree analysis provided molecular evidence for prenatal diagnosis and maternal inheritance.

A key methodological limitation is the use of ultra‐low‐depth sequencing (0.2×) for a CNV of approximately 0.35–0.4 Mb. Published evidence indicates that some low‐coverage analytical methods can detect CNVs larger than 300 kb at approximately 0.2× depth [21], and low‐pass whole‐genome sequencing has also been clinically evaluated for CNVs > 50 kb [22]. However, comparative studies also show that ultra‐low‐coverage approaches can produce false positives for smaller CNVs and show variable performance on sex chromosomes [23]. Thus, the present CNV‐seq results should be interpreted as screening‐level evidence rather than definitive breakpoint characterization. The fetal MECP2 duplication was independently confirmed by MLPA, which supports the presence of the pathogenic MECP2 copy‐number gain.

The estimated duplication size differed slightly between the fetus (approximately 0.35 Mb) and the older brother (approximately 0.4 Mb). Because the sequencing depth was low and Xq28 contains repetitive and segmentally duplicated sequences, these coordinates and size estimates should be interpreted cautiously. The difference may reflect the resolution and boundary‐calling characteristics of low‐depth sequencing; low‐level mosaicism cannot be completely excluded, although no mosaicism assay was performed. Importantly, both CNV‐seq calls involved the MECP2 locus, and MLPA independently confirmed increased copy numbers of MECP2 exons 1–4 in the fetal sample, supporting the clinically relevant MECP2 copy‐number gain.

The mother was clinically asymptomatic despite carrying the Xq28 duplication. Although skewed X‐chromosome inactivation has been reported in female carriers of MECP2 duplications [24], no X‐inactivation assay was performed in this family. Therefore, we cannot determine whether skewed X‐chromosome inactivation explains the mother's asymptomatic phenotype. This remains an important limitation and should be addressed by X‐inactivation analysis in future studies. The reported immune abnormalities in some individuals with MDS [25] were not specifically evaluated in this family and therefore cannot be used to explain the mother's phenotype.

From a clinical perspective, this case illustrates the value of combining low‐pass CNV screening with a locus‐specific confirmatory assay for prenatal diagnosis and family testing. For families with a known MECP2 duplication, molecular confirmation can facilitate genetic counseling and reproductive decision‐making. Clinical management remains largely supportive, including early rehabilitation and treatment of complications such as recurrent infections [26, 27], but these aspects are beyond the scope of the present case report.
