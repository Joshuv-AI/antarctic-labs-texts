Cross-linguistic comparison of color word learning in German and Japanese children shows universal and language-specific processes
Authors: Noburo Saji, Henrik Saalbach, Gerlind Große, Cornelia Schulze, Mutsumi Imai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines how German- and Japanese-speaking children acquire the meanings of color words, focusing on two theoretically distinct processes: centroid mapping and boundary delineation. While previous cross-linguistic studies have typically addressed these aspects in isolation, this paper explicitly investigates both processes simultaneously, assessing language-specific and perceptual factors contributing to lexical acquisition. Using previously collected Japanese data (re-analyzed here for comparative purposes) and newly collected German data, we explore how input frequency, naming inconsistency, category size, and perceptual saliency influence these processes. Our findings show that centroid mapping in both languages is primarily shaped by input frequency, that is, children learn prototypical meanings by hearing the respective color words often. In contrast, boundary delineation is predicted by naming consistency in adult speakers, that is, children’s differentiation of color categories is based on the consistency with which adults refer to the same color with the same word. The structural characteristics of each language’s lexicon affect these qualitative aspects, highlighting an interplay between perceptual constraints and language-specific lexical structures. This comparative approach provides novel insights into lexical and conceptual development, contributing to broader theoretical debates on continuity and discontinuity in semantic acquisition and informing computational models of lexical development.Subject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

To use a word properly in a diverse range of contexts, speakers must understand its meaning in relation to other words with similar meanings. Lexical acquisition, therefore, involves knowledge of other words in the same lexical domain and an understanding of how a target word is differentiated from surrounding lexical items1–5. Researchers of lexical development have described the process of semantic reorganization in certain lexical domains, including spatial terms6, prepositions7, container labels1 and verbs used for carrying actions8. However, it remains unclear whether the process of lexical reorganization in a given lexical domain is driven by the same mechanisms with the same factors across different languages. The present study addresses this question by comparing the process of color word acquisition in German and Japanese children.

The color lexicon provides a particularly interesting case for understanding how children develop adult-like representations of a lexical domain as an interconnected system. A longstanding debate concerns whether perceptual or linguistic factors primarily influence the acquisition process. Some researchers have argued that color terms are learned in a systematic, pre-fixed order regardless of the native language, assuming that the process of color word learning is biologically constrained and hence should be homogeneous across different languages. This idea was originally introduced in Eleanor Rosch’s prototype theory, in which she emphasized that certain perceptually salient colors (focal colors) serve as natural prototypes of color perception9. Perceptual salience of this kind has frequently been considered to be an main (if not sole) factor behind the cross-linguistic universality observed in the process of color term acquisition. The theoretical framework of focal colors has been subsequently employed by many researchers (for discussions on the relationship between perceptual salience and universality, see10,11. For critical examination of this view, see12. We acknowledge that while multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain the universality of color term acquisition, perceptual salience is one of the leading candidates. Importantly, numerous studies have demonstrated the existence of color categories in pre-linguistic infants, which strengthens the view that the developmental trends of color word acquisition can be mainly explained by perceptual factors and hence the trends should be more or less universally observed13–17.

However, other researchers have also convincingly demonstrated that the structure of the color lexicon varies considerably across languages and is strongly influenced by linguistic and cultural factors18–22. For instance, Roberson et al.20 argued that category boundaries of colors in Berinmo language were linguistically determined, disagreed the view that there are universal, perceptually pre-determined color categories that serve as the “building blocks” for learning color naming. Roberson et al.23 conducted a longitudinal study comparing the color word acquisition in Himba-learning and English-learning children. They found that focal color categorization was language-dependent and that there was no common pattern of color word acquisition across these two languages that can be explained by perceptual focal colors. Expanding the number of languages, Forbes and Plunkett18 analyzed data from 11 languages using the Oxford Communicative Development Inventory to investigate what factors contribute the order of color word acquisition in each language and in what degree the factors affecting the order is common across the 11 languages. They found substantial variation in the order of color word acquisition, with input frequency and syllabic complexity strongly predicting the timing of color word acquisition across these languages. These studies underscore the importance of language-specific factors as driving forces of color word acquisition from early stages of lexical development.

Thus, various factors including perceptual, linguistic, or cultural factors have been proposed to explain the mechanism of color word acquisition and there have been disagreements on what factors should be more important than others.

Here, it is worth noting that researchers use widely different meanings (or definitions) of “acquisition” in their discussing the mechanism of it For instance, Yang et al.17 defined acquisition based on infants’ early perceptual discrimination abilities, emphasizing biological constraints, while being silent about how these perceptual categories transform into culturally specific linguistic meanings. Forbes and Plunkett, on the other hand, used the word “acquisition,” when the given word is identified to be included in the child’s vocabulary; they, too, did not consider whether children understand the boundary of the lexical category of the word18.

The issue of when and how “acquisition of a word is defined leads to further critical questions concerning lexical acquisition: how are word meanings represented in children’s lexicon and which processes contribute to their acquisition? On what bases should the word meaning acquisition be indexed? Specifically, how can we properly capture the representation of word meanings in young children? Importantly, Clark4 outlined a two-stage process model to address this issue: initially, in a process termed as centroid mapping, children associate words with their typical reference, relying on socio-cognitive skills and perceptual/conceptual biases; subsequently, as children accumulate clusters of words, they proceed to the stage of boundary delineation, whereby the clusters are integrated into a structured lexical system, differentiating words from similar ones within the same domain4. This two-process theory of lexical acquisition is supported by studies demonstrating that initial broad conceptual mappings gradually give way to finer distinctions24–28. Regarding the domain of color words, Wagner et al.22 observed that children initially overextend color terms (e.g., calling orange ‘red’) but refine these categories as their lexical knowledge expands. Saji et al.5 extended this research, explicitly distinguishing two processes in color word acquisition: centroid mapping (learning prototypical meanings) and boundary delineation (differentiating color categories based on linguistic structure). Based on the results, these authors suggested that the two processes operate distinctly, influenced by separate factors such as input frequency and naming consistency.

An important question needing further clarification is whether identical factors contributing the centroid mapping process and the boundary delineation process would be the same, and these factors would be the same across different languages. As reviewed, cross-linguistic research indicates significant variability in lexical structures even within similar semantic domains20,29. For example, Japanese contains a distinct term mizuiro, which refers to colors falling into English sense of ‘blue’ with high brightness (e.g., light blue)30. There are languages which have two distinct (basic level) terms for English-equivalent ‘blue’ colors along with the brightness dimension (e.g., Japanese, Russian, Greek, etc.). However, this lexical distinction is not common across the languages of the world; it is not present at a basic level in English or German. To fully understand the mechanism of lexical acquisition, a theory should address whether differences in the lexical structure of languages, even subtle ones such as the fine distinctions in “blue”, affect the process of lexical acquisition, both for the centroid mapping and boundary delineation.

The current study aims to investigate how the two-process theory of lexical acquisition applies across languages. We tested whether the factors in the previous studies affect the ease/difficulty on both the centroid mapping and the boundary delineation processes in German and Japanese in the same way5. Here, we considered four factors based on prior studies8,22,31: (1) input frequency (how often a color term appears in child-directed speech); (2) category size (the range of referents covered by each word); (3) category ambiguity (the level of disagreement among speakers in naming a color); and (4) hue uniqueness (the perceptual salience of a color). We hypothesize that, while the same factors influence color word acquisition across languages, the order of acquisition differs due to variations in input frequency and language-specific properties such as the structure of the lexicon in a given language18,20. By finely comparing German-learning and Japanese-learning children, we aim to provide new insights into the interaction between perceptual and language-specific forces in lexical development.

Experiment 1

Experiment 1 aims to characterize the structure and semantic properties of the color lexicon among adult German speakers to establish a baseline for comparison with previously collected Japanese data5. Understanding of adult lexical organization in details is crucial for investigating children’s lexical acquisition, particularly concerning the centroid mapping and the boundary delineation processes. Prior research emphasizes that lexical acquisition is significantly influenced by adult lexical characteristics, including naming consistency, lexical category boundaries, and frequency of word usage1,4,5.

In Experiment 1 we wished to identify the following properties of the German color lexicon: First, we wished to identify ‘basic’ color words within the German lexicon, those which are used consistently and dominantly by adult native speakers to label prototypical color exemplars. Subsequently, we obtained statistics for the ‘basic’ terms, specifically input frequency (the occurrence of color words in child-directed speech), category size (the breadth of color referents covered by each term), and naming inconsistency (the degree of variability or disagreement among adult speakers when labeling color exemplars), as these factors have been found to affect children’s ability to acquire lexical categories in previous studies22,31.

Critically, this research is methodologically consistent with the Saji et al.’s5 prior research with Japanese speakers. Thus, the experiment used the same set of stimuli (93 color swatches) and identical procedures as those used in previous research on Japanese adults5. The Japanese data, originally collected to analyze the structure of the Japanese color lexicon, are re-analyzed here for direct cross-linguistic comparisons with the German data collected in this experiment.

All experiments reported in this paper were approved by the ethics committees of the faculty of medicine at University Leipzig (number: 286/17-ek). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki and the standards of the local ethics committee of the University of Leipzig, Germany. The children’s parents had agreed to their children participating in studies on child development. Method

Participants

Participants included 26 native German-speaking adults (undergraduate and graduate students) from Leipzig University, who provided informed consent before participating.

Stimuli

Stimuli were ninety-three color swatches selected from the Practical Color Coordinate System (PCCS) developed by the Japan Color Research Institute, which was used by Saji et al.5. PCCS consisted of 14 ‘tone’ categories, each of which has 24 hues. Tone is a combination of lightness and metric chroma32. Out of the 14 tones, we used colors from seven tones (‘light,’ ‘bright,’ ‘soft,’ ‘vivid,’ ‘dull,’ ‘deep,’ and ‘dark’), which varied in lightness and chroma, so that the stimulus colors covered the entire color spectrum. To reduce the number of stimuli, only half of the 24 hues of each tone were (with every other even number) used. In addition to these 84 chromatic colors (7 tones x 12 hues), nine achromatic colors were included (black, white, and five different grays; see Fig. 1 for the complete set of stimulus colors.

Procedure

Participants were tested individually under controlled lighting conditions replicating natural daylight (D55 standard lighting provided by Solax XC-100 AF (Seric Ltd.). Each participant was presented sequentially with the 93 color swatches on a neutral gray background, one at a time in a randomized order. Participants were asked, “Welche Farbe hat die Karte?” (“What color is this swatch?“). They were instructed to use common, everyday German color terms and avoid specialized or rarely used terms. The responses were recorded verbatim for subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

We first identified basic color words based on participants’ dominant responses to each swatch. Following this identification, three key semantic variables were computed for each identified basic color word: (1) Input Frequency: The frequency with which each color word appeared in German child-directed speech, obtained from the CHILDES database; (2) Category Size: The total number of swatches dominantly labeled with each basic color term; (3) Naming Inconsistency: The degree of agreement or variability among adult participants when labeling each color swatch, calculated as the average Euclidean distance of labeled swatches from the centroid of each identified color category. We restricted our analysis to 84 chromatic colors. This decision was made to match the number of stimuli used in the Japanese study by Saji et al.5, which included only 84 chromatic colors.

Results

Identification of basic color words

Although the definition of ‘basic’ color words has been controversial in the literature29,30, we defined them operationally in this research in the same way as in Saji et al.5: we determined a word form to be ‘basic’ if the word is applied most dominantly (most frequently) for at least one of the 86 color swatches1. For example, because rot (‘red’) was most frequently produced for two swatches, it was regarded as a ‘basic’ word. By contrast, although some participants produced violett (‘purple’) for several purple swatches, lila was produced more dominantly (more frequently) for these swatches. We thus determined lila, but not violett, as the ‘basic’ term for ‘purple.’ In counting the frequency of each word, compound color names such as dunkelgrün (‘dark green’) and hautfarbe (‘skin color’) were considered as different word types from monomorphemic words such as grün (‘green’) and orange (‘orange’). Finally, fourteen basic color terms were identified: gelb (‘yellow’), blau (‘blue’), grün (‘green’), rot (‘red’), orange (‘orange’), braun (‘brown’), lila (‘purple’), rosa (‘reddish-pink’), pink (‘pink’), grau (‘gray’), hautfarbe (‘light orange’), dunkelgrün (‘dark green’), hellblau (‘light blue’), and türkis (‘turquoise blue’). The swatch that received the highest agreement for each word was considered the centroid of the word category. Coincidentally, the number of basic color words in German is the same as that in Japanese.

Semantic characteristics of the German color lexicon

In the second step, we computed the previously mentioned three values that have been considered to predict the ease or difficulty of acquisition: input frequency, category size, and naming inconsistency for each color word. The input frequency values for each word were obtained from the word production counts by German caretakers in CHILDES33. All tokens were log transformed31,34. The category size score of a given word was quantified by counting the number of swatches labeled by the word most dominant among the participants in Experiment 1. For example, since two swatches were dominantly labeled as rot (‘red’), the value for category size for rot was ‘2.’ Finally, naming inconsistency represents a cross-individual agreement in applying a given word to a target color swatch (see next section).

Structure of the German color lexicon

To calculate the naming inconsistency value for each basic-level word and visualize the structure of the German color lexicon, we conducted the multi-dimensional scaling (MDS), following the same procedure as Saji et al.5. We created the MDS input as an 84 × 84 matrix, where rows and columns represent the stimulus color swatches for the input, and each cell contained the number of times participants named the swatch pairs were with the same name by35. A two-dimensional solution was adopted because the stress value (a goodness-of-fit index that represents how well the distances between plots in the reduced dimensional space) for this solution was satisfactory (s = 0.10) (see Fig. 2). The points in Fig. 2 represent the 84 stimulus swatches. The labels indicate the dominant color name and swatches labeled with the same dominant color word are outlined by solid lines. Thus, the size of the clusters on the MDS plane reflects the degree of individual inconsistency in color word naming. For example, on the MDS plane the cluster of lila (‘purple’) is larger than that of rot (‘red’). This suggests that among adult native German speakers, agreement on the category boundary of lila (‘purple’) is lower than their agreement on rot (‘red’). For each word cluster, we average the Euclidean distances between the centroid and each cluster member. This score was used as the index of naming inconsistency; a higher naming inconsistency score indicated that swatches around the category boundary tended to be named using multiple words. Dunkelgrün (‘dark green’), hautfarbe (‘light orange’), and rosa (‘reddish pink’) were dominantly named for only one patch and hence the naming inconsistency scores cannot be calculated. For this reason, these three words were excluded from the analysis. (The Japanese words kon (‘deep blue’) and oudo (‘ocher’) were also excluded for the same reason in the Saji et al.5 analysis).

Tables 1 and 2 also show that the semantic properties of the words for unique-hue colors are similar between the two languages in terms of category size and naming inconsistency. The words that are applied for (the English sense of) red swatches cover relatively narrow ranges (rot in German and aka in Japanese) and are cleanly separated from other swatches, indicating that these colors are clearly distinguished from other colors by name. The coverages of the words for (the English sense of) blue and green colors tend to be broad in both languages (lila (‘purple’), blau (‘blue’) and grün (‘green’) in German and murasaki (‘purple’), ao (‘blue’) and midori (‘green’) in Japanese, respectively), similar to what was reported by Yurovsky et al.31 for English.

Interestingly, cross-linguistic differences are observed for non-unique-hue colors. Even when a word in one language has a morphologically equivalent counterpart in the other, German speakers divided (English-sense of) pink colors by two different words, pink (‘pink’) and rosa (‘reddish-pink’), whereas Japanese speakers’ pinku (‘pink’) corresponded more closely to rosa (‘reddish-pink’) than pink (‘pink’) in German. Japanese has the terms midori (“green”) and kimidori (“yellowish-green”) for green, while the same colors were named by a single term grün (‘green’) in German.

Taken together, cross-linguistically shared semantic properties are observed in color words for unique-hue colors, whereas language-specific properties were observed in words denoting non-unique hue colors.

Discussion of experiment 1

Experiment 1 provided insights into the semantic organization of the German adult color lexicon, setting an essential foundation for comparative analyses with previously collected Japanese data. Consistent with expectations derived from prior cross-linguistic studies, we found that German-speaking adults utilize a similarly diverse array of basic color terms compared to Japanese speakers.

Importantly, the results demonstrated clear distinctions between unique-hue and non-unique-hue color terms in terms of input frequency, category size, and naming inconsistency. Unique-hue colors (rot (‘red’), gelb (‘yellow’), grün (‘green’), blau (‘blue’)) showed higher input frequency and more stable semantic boundaries, suggesting they may form more robust early lexical entries for children due to frequent exposure and clear categorical delineation. In contrast, non-unique hues exhibited broader category sizes and greater naming inconsistency, potentially posing greater challenges for lexical acquisition.

Experiment 2

Experiment 2 investigated whether the two-process theory of lexical acquisition is supported in the domain of color terms not only in Japanese-learning children but also in German-learning ones. Consistent with Saji et al.5 and Experiment 1, 3-year-old and 5-year-old children were asked to name each of the 93 color swatches. The analyses proceeded in three main steps. First, we assessed children’s centroid mapping accuracy, identifying how well German-speaking children labeled category centroids (Analysis 1). Second, we quantified children’s boundary delineation, determining how closely children’s use of color terms matched adult-defined lexical boundaries (Analysis 2). Finally, we evaluated whether the semantic properties identified in Experiment 1—input frequency, category size, naming inconsistency, and perceptual saliency— predicted lexical acquisition similarly across German and Japanese children (Analysis 3).

Method

Participants

Participants were 52 monolingual German-speaking children recruited from local preschools, comprising two age groups: 26 three-year-olds (M age = 42.8 months, SD = 3.7) and 26 five-year-olds (M age = 65.8 months, SD = 5.3). All parents signed an informed consent for their children to participate in the study. Gender distribution was balanced across groups. All children participated in the experiment with parental consent. The results from German-speaking children were compared with those from 39 native Japanese-speaking children (20 three-year-olds: 36–47 months old and 19 five-year-olds: 60–71 months old) by Saji et al.5.

Stimuli and procedure

The stimuli and procedure were identical to those in Experiment 1 with one exception. Before the main test, children received warm-up trials designed to familiarize them with the response format. During this warm-up, they were shown six pictures—three different cats, two different dogs, and one rabbit—and asked to name each picture. This phase ensured that children understood they could use the same label more than once across different trials. Children were then presented individually with the same 93 color swatches under controlled lighting conditions (D55 standard) in randomized order and were asked to name each swatch spontaneously. Responses were recorded verbatim by experimenters for analysis.

Data analysis

The extraction of color words from children’s responses was carried out in the same way as in Experiment 1 (i.e., compound color names were counted as different word types). The color words produced were analyzed for accuracy in centroid mapping and boundary delineation. Concerning centroid mapping accuracy, the proportion of children correctly applying adult-defined basic color terms to prototypical color swatches was calculated. To assess the scores of boundary delineation, F-measure scores were calculated for each color word, reflecting children’s precision and recall in applying color terms within adult-defined semantic boundaries. Statistical analyses using R software involved logistic and linear mixed-effects modeling e, examining the contributions of input frequency, category size, naming inconsistency, and perceptual saliency (hue uniqueness), as identified in Experiment 1, to children’s lexical accuracy and precision.

Results

Analysis 1: naming accuracy for the category centroids

We examined how well the children applied basic color words in their native languages to category centroids. Table 3 shows the proportion of German 3- and 5-year-olds who produced the appropriate basic color words for the swatches. Table 3 also shows the similarity between German and Japanese children’s patterns of successfully and poorly labeled words. The Spearman’s rank correlations between the German and Japanese patterns of producing the nine common basic colors are high (0.78 for 3-year-olds and 0.69 for 5-year-olds), suggesting that the centroid mapping is comparable across the two languages. In both languages, the words corresponding to English equivalents of ‘red,’ ‘green,’ ‘yellow,’ and ‘blue’ were named correctly even by 3-year-olds. As noted earlier, these colors are perceptually salient ‘unique hue’ colors, which cannot be described in terms of any other hues (for the biological foundation for unique hues, see Hurvich & Jameson37,). In contrast, both German and Japanese 3- and 5-year-olds named language-specific words, such as hautfarbe (‘light orange’), dunkelgrün (‘dark green’), hellblau (‘light blue’) and türkis (‘turquoise blue’) in German and mizu-iro (‘light blue’), kimidori (‘yellowish green’), hada-iro (‘peach’), kon (‘deep blue’), and ōdo (‘ochre’) in Japanese less well.

Analysis 2: boundary delineation

To quantify how closely children acquired adult-equivalent word boundaries, an f-measure score was calculated for each word. This measure represents the degree to which each child assigns a given color term to the swatches in an adult-like manner, without over- or under-extending the word meaning2 (Powers38; see Saji et al.5, for the specific calculation process with the naming dataset).

Figure 3 shows the average f-measure scores for each word in German (3 A) and Japanese (3B). As was the case with the centroid mapping, in both German and Japanese, cross-linguistically-shared words (i.e., ‘red,’ ‘yellow,’ ‘green,’ ‘blue,’ ‘brown,’ ‘purple,’ ‘pink,’ and ‘orange’) were delineated earlier than language-specific words, and language-specific words (e.g., hellblau (‘light blue’) and türkis (‘turquoise blue’) in German and kimidori (‘yellowish-orange’), mizuiro (‘light-blue’) and hada (‘light orange’) in Japanese) converged on the adult usage with age in both languages. Importantly, however, how fast the speed with which children converged to adult-like uses of non-basic color words differ in the two languages, despite this common language pattern.

f-measure scores in German and Japanese. The f-measure scores reflect the degree to which children had achieved an adult-equivalent boundary delineation for each color word. The scores were calculated for terms for which the scores of input frequency, category size, and naming inconsistency could be calculated (10 colors in German and 11 colors in Japanese).

In Saji et al.5, Japanese children were found to have difficulty identifying color word boundaries when the coverage of cross-linguistically shared (i.e., basic) color words overlapped with that of language-specific (i.e., non-basic) color words. For example, Japanese-speaking children showed delayed acquisition of the boundary for midori (‘green’), presumably because delineating the category boundary of this word involves finding the boundary between two language-specific words, kimidori (yellowish green) and mizuiro (light blue).

If we could draw an analogy from the Japanese pattern, we would expect German-speaking children to have difficulty distinguishing pink (‘pink’) from rosa (‘reddish pink’) as they both describe a reddish color. Consistent with the expectation, it is rosa that covers a range similar to ‘pink’ in English and Japanese (pinku). Indeed, the f-measure values for rosa and pink (‘pink’) in German-speaking children were lower than those for pinku in Japanese-speaking children (see Fig. 3A and B, respectively).

Analysis 3: factors affecting the ease of acquisition

Next, a series of model analyses were conducted to examine the factors that affect color word acquisition in German and Japanese. In examining the centroid mapping process, the naming response of each child was coded binomially for each word (1 if the child’s response agreed with the most dominant names identified in Experiment 1; otherwise 0). To analyze the boundary delineation process, the individual f-measure score for each word was adopted for each participant.

The contributions of the following six factors were examined: (a) hue uniqueness, (b) input frequency, (c) category size, (d) naming inconsistency, (e) age, and (f) the interactions between age and other factors. On the hue-uniqueness factor, the unique hue colors (i.e., aka/rot (‘red’), ao/blau (‘blue’), kiiro/gelb (‘yellow’), and midori/grün (‘green’)) and non-unique hue colors (the other seven colors) were treated as binomial variables (unique hue colors: 0.5; other colors: − 0.5). The values for input frequency, category size, and naming inconsistency for each word were drawn from adult data in Experiment 1 (Tables 2 and 3). Finally, to examine the effect of age, the age group information was coded categorically over the dummy variables. All models reported in the following analyses were estimated using the lme4 package in R39.

Centroid mapping. A logistic mixed-effects model was used to predict naming accuracy for centroid mapping, with six fixed effects using the glmer function in lme4. The effects of the individual participants3 were considered by including this information as a random factor.

Discussion of experiment 2

The results of Experiment 2 uncovered different sets of factors contributing to the ease of acquisition at the centroid mapping and the boundary delineation in German-learning children, as well as in their Japanese-learning age peers. This finding support Clark’s two-process theory, highlighting that centroid mapping and boundary delineation are distinct yet interrelated developmental processes. German-speaking children’s lexical acquisition aligned closely with predictions based on the semantic properties identified in Experiment 1, confirming cross-linguistic generalizability for certain factors (e.g., input frequency) but also underscoring important language-specific differences (e.g., naming inconsistency effects).

General discussion

Two-process theory of lexical acquisition

This study tested the cross-linguistic applicability of the two-process theory of lexical acquisition2–4 by analyzing color word acquisition in German- and Japanese-speaking children. The results support the two-process of lexical acquisition theory in the domain of color lexicon in German learning children. The results also expand on the finding already demonstrated for Japanese-learning children that have been reported in Saji et al.5.

The role of linguistic input in lexical acquisition has been widely acknowledged, particularly in studies emphasizing quantitative aspects such as input frequency26,41–43. More recently, researchers have begun to explore the role of the structure of the lexicon as another crucial factor in lexical development1–4,40,43. Our findings highlight that both input frequency and lexical structure influence lexical acquisition, but they play different roles at different stages of development: Early in acquisition, input frequency may bootstrap word learning via general cognitive mechanisms40,41. As the lexicon expands, the contribution of the lexical structure in an ambient language becomes greater for semantic differentiation within a given lexical domain1,4.

Language-specific factors affecting ease of acquisition

Despite shared developmental patterns, we observed important language-specific differences. In German, both input frequency and category size predicted success in centroid mapping, while in Japanese, only frequency played a significant role. Similarly, category size influenced boundary delineation in German but not in Japanese. These divergences likely reflect cross-linguistic differences in lexical category density and organization. For example, Japanese divides the English-German sense of ‘blue’ into two basic terms—ao (‘blue’) and mizuiro (‘light blue’)—while German uses blau (‘blue’) and hellblau (‘light blue’) with less categorical separation. The degree of semantic overlap between terms, as captured by naming inconsistency, was particularly relevant for boundary delineation. Children found it more difficult to establish category boundaries when adult usage was inconsistent. Thus, the ease or difficulty of acquiring specific terms is tied not only to perceptual or cognitive salience but also to language-specific lexical mappings. These findings support the notion that lexical acquisition follows principles of category learning31, modulated by the structure of the language being learned5. The two-process theory of lexical development offers a coherent explanation for the cross-linguistically shared processes at a general level, while accounting for cross-linguistically different orders of color word acquisition18.

Biological and cultural/linguistic constraints on color word acquisition

Our findings contribute to the long-standing debate about whether lexical acquisition is driven primarily by biological perceptual factors or by language-specific and cultural input. The present study suggests that both perspectives capture part of the picture, but their influence varies across developmental stages and acquisition processes. If acquisition is defined in terms of centroid mapping (Analysis 1), perceptual factors have the impact on the ease of learning, as children in both languages accurately labeled prototypical exemplars of unique-hue colors (e.g., red, green, yellow, blue) early on. However, the more complex process of boundary delineation clearly reflects linguistic and cultural learning, requiring integration of multiple category distinctions shaped by the ambient language2–4.

Interestingly, perceptual saliency on its own did not predict acquisition contribute to the ease of acquisition of words in our statistical models. This is not to say that saliency is unimportant (see Malt et al.27 on the importance of perceptual factors in lexical acquisition). In fact, as we reported in the qualitative analysis of the centroid mapping, the colors corresponding to ‘red,’ ‘green,’ ‘yellow,’ and ‘blue’ colors in English, which were all unique-hue colors, were successfully named by 3-year-olds in both German and Japanese. In contrast, the words 3- and 5-year-olds failed to name were language-specific words denoting non-unique-hue colors, such as hellblau (‘light blue’) in German and mizuiro (light blue) in Japanese. It is more likely that perceptual saliency operates indirectly through its correlation with input frequency. Caregivers may more frequently refer to perceptually salient colors, reinforcing their early acquisition36. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that linguistic experience—especially in terms of frequency and lexical structure—plays a more critical role in shaping acquisition order, particularly beyond the earliest stages of learning.

Implications and future directions

This study provides strong empirical support for a dual-process model of lexical acquisition and highlights the value of cross-linguistic comparison in testing developmental theories. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Sample sizes were modest, and our production-based tasks may have underestimated children’s receptive knowledge. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and comprehension measures to track developmental change more fully. In addition, computational modeling approaches should be leveraged to simulate acquisition trajectories using real-world input data11,44.

Finally, extending this work to other lexical domains—such as spatial terms or emotion words—would allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how perceptual, linguistic, and cultural constraints interact across semantic fields. By refining our models of lexical development, we move closer to understanding the mechanisms that allow children to build language-specific yet cognitively grounded semantic systems.

Conclusion

A full theory of lexical acquisition should explain the process of how children acquire the meanings of words as a connected system, and in doing so, not only the mechanism of centroid mapping but also that of boundary delineation should be considered. Previous studies on lexical acquisition have typically focused exclusively on either centroid mapping or boundary delineation, often neglecting the latter. Our research, however, highlights the significant role of linguistic input in both processes. We established that the quantitative side (i.e., frequency) of the linguistic input is more important for centroid mapping whereas the qualitative side (i.e., the range of hue to which a color word can be applied) is more important for boundary delineation. The language-specific structure of the lexicon affects the qualitative aspect of linguistic input; hence, the order of acquisition of individual words can vary across languages. More cross-linguistic research is needed to further specify the relationships between lexical structure, quantitative and qualitative sides of linguistic input, and order of acquisition.
