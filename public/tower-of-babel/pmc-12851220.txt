Climate change health communication and its association with awareness and behaviors in South Korea
Authors: Hyeyun Kim, Sumi Chae
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn a climate crisis where climate change poses serious risks, there is still a lack of awareness of climate change and its health impacts, and the awareness does not translate into health adaptation behaviors. Communication for health adaptation to climate change is not just about conveying information or changing awareness, but requires a strategy that considers the factors that drive individual behavioral change. This study aimed to confirm whether communication experiences are associated with awareness and behaviors related to climate change and health.MethodsThe online survey was conducted among a nationally representative sample of 2,000 adults aged 19-64. Climate change communication was measured in terms of information seeking experience and climate communication experience. Mean differences in climate change health awareness and behavior between subgroups were identified and regression analyses were conducted to determine associations.ResultsThe experiences of climate communication were associated with lower perceived likelihood of susceptibility to health risks. However, experiences of information seeking and climate communication had a positive association with pro-environmental and health adaptive behavior.ConclusionsCurrent communication approaches have not been effective enough in combining information and communication experiences about climate change and health to build accurate awareness. Protecting and promoting health in the context of the climate crisis requires a public demand-driven approach and a systematic communication strategy that leads to the right awareness and action.Keywords: Climate crisis, Health communication, Awareness, Health behavior, Adaptive behavior

Introduction

Climate change triggers diverse weather phenomena such as heatwaves and deteriorating air quality, which cause acute and chronic diseases and increase the risk of premature death [1]. Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) reported that heat waves have substantially increased cases of heat-related illness, resulting in surges in emergency department visits, hospitalizations, and deaths, as well as excess admissions for cardiovascular and acute kidney diseases [2]. The health impacts are more severe for socially vulnerable populations, resulting in the exacerbation of existing health inequalities [3].

In a climate crisis where climate change poses serious risks, previous studies have shown that in many countries, public awareness of the health impacts of climate change remains relatively low [4,5]. It has become clear that not only the general public but also health professionals have insufficient knowledge about the relationship between climate change and health [6,7]. Although climate change and its effects are scientifically proven, the assimilation of these findings and subsequent action can be obstructed by complex psychological factors. While climate anxiety is often fostered by increased awareness and can motivate deeper engagement [8], it can also become a significant barrier to adaptation when exacerbated by information overload and a burdensome sense of responsibility [9].

Collective efforts to respond to the health threats of climate change are rooted in the awareness and actions of each individual [10]. The initial step in driving individual action is a clear perception of health risks [6,11,12], with advice from medical professionals serving as an effective motivational tool [6]. Nevertheless, for perception to translate into health behavior, it should be supported by a sense of self-efficacy-the belief that one can make a difference-and by social norms that affirm a collective community effort [8]. It is at this juncture that the role of climate crisis health adaptation communication becomes pivotal. Effective communication is defined not merely as a strategy for raising awareness by disseminating risk information, but as one that simultaneously reinforces individual self-efficacy and positive social norms. It can thus be characterized as a multifaceted and strategic communication activity that builds social consensus, fosters policy support, and ultimately induces tangible behavioral change [4,13,14].

Government agencies in South Korea, such as KDCA and the Ministry of Environment, are making efforts to inform the public about the health risks of climate change and to provide information on response measures. However, despite these efforts, current communication methods are not fully addressing the public's needs. According to a 2022 survey, only about 30 % of the public felt they had sufficient information [15], and a majority (69.1 %) rated the government's explanations as insufficient [4]. Nevertheless, despite unmet public demand for information and policies on the health impacts of climate change in South Korea, research on the factors that influence and mediate changes in public awareness and behavior remains limited. This study therefore examined the extent to which the public engages in information seeking and communication experiences regarding climate change. Furthermore, it aimed to confirm whether communication correlated with awareness and behaviors related to climate change and health.

Methods

Survey participants and data collection methods

This study utilized a cross-sectional study design. The survey was conducted among a nationally representative sample of adults aged 19–64. The survey was age-restricted to exclude children, teenagers, and seniors, who are less likely to be able to access and engage online. The target sample size was 2000, with a confidence level of 95 % and a sampling error of ±2.19 percentage points. The total sample was drawn based on the resident population as of August 2024 from Statistics Korea, reflecting the population structure by gender, 10-year age group, and region. The online survey was conducted from September 2 to September 8, 2024. The survey was outsourced to a specialized survey agency and the survey URL was sent to the online panel established by the agency. The survey was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Institute of Health and Social Research (No. 2024–85).

Measurement tools and analysis methods

In this study, "information seeking experience" refers to the frequency of searching for information on the health impacts of climate change through various sources (e.g., news media, government websites, social media). Items were adapted from Lehrer et al. [16] and Chae et al. [17], with responses rated on a 5-point scale and summed (Cronbach’s alpha=0.931).

“Climate communication experience” refers to the frequency of engagement in various climate-related activities (e.g., seminars, lifestyle changes, online engagement, activism, interpersonal communication). Items were adapted from Lehrer et al. [16] and Maran & Begotti [18] and translated into Korean, with some items having been paraphrased to fit the Korean context. Responses were rated on a 5-point scale and summed (Cronbach’s alpha=0.939).

“Susceptibility to the health risks of climate change” was assessed by summing two 5-point scale items on perceived impacts on mortality and disease [19]. To compare susceptibility to health risks, we asked people if they think climate change is harming their lives overall [20].

“Pro-environmental behavior” was adapted from previous studies [21,22] and consists of 9 items (Cronbach’s alpha=0.759). “Health adaption behavior” consists of 18 items about how people respond to climate change-related health problems. For the health behavior, the items were developed based on the contents of guidelines distributed by KDCA and Ministry of the Interior and Safety. Each item was scored on a 5-point scale, with the average value used as the score (Cronbach’s alpha=0.906).

“Climate change-related disaster experience” was measured by examining experience within the past five years for eight weather events [17]. The impact of climate disasters was coded as "yes" if there was a "significant impact" on any of the eight types of disasters. “Climate self-efficacy” was measured using Doran's [23] Perceived Climate Self-Efficacy Scale with a 4-point scale (Cronbach’s alpha=0.820). “Climate social norm” measured how important family and friends think it is to reduce the effects of climate change and how committed they are to taking action to do so with a 4-point scale. The survey items are presented in Appendix 2.

To address the research questions regarding (1) sociodemographic differences in awareness and behaviors, (2) variation in information-seeking and communication experiences by perceived awareness of climate-related health risks, and (3) the associations of communication exposure with awareness and behavior, we conducted a series of statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics, t-tests, and ANOVA were used to examine group differences in awareness and behavior. Awareness was classified as low when participants disagreed with either the mortality or morbidity item. Frequency analyses assessed differences in information seeking and communication experiences by awareness level. Lastly, regression analyses were employed to determine whether information seeking and communication experiences were associated with higher levels of risk awareness and climate-related behaviors.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and their differences in awareness and behaviors

Respondents were highly educated, with many having graduated from college or graduate school (83.4 %), and most had a spouse (53.2 %) or children (49.8 %). 28.6 % reported living in disaster-prone areas. Susceptibility to climate change health risks was significantly higher among women (p < 0.001), older age (p = 0.004), having a spouse (p = 0.019), and having a progressive political orientation (p < 0.001). Behavioral practices showed more significant differences between groups, with those with higher levels of awareness, correlating with taking more action, as well as having children (p < 0.001) and living in disaster-prone areas (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Association between climate change communication and related awareness

Two communication experiences did not significantly change susceptibility to climate change risk. On the other hand, more information seeking experience was associated with lower perceived likelihood of death and illness due to climate change (β=0.−102, p < 0.001). The same association was found when experience with climate communication was included in the analysis (β=0.−205, p < 0.001). The association between health risks susceptibility and disaster impacts experience, climate self-efficacy, and climate social norm were positively and significantly associated, with self-efficacy having the greatest effect on health risk perception (Fig. 3, Appendix Table 1).

Association between climate change communication and related behavioral practice

It was confirmed that pro-environmental behavior increases as information seeking experience (β=0.214, p < 0.001) and climate communication experience (β=0.164, p < 0.001) increase. The effect of information seeking experience seems to be slightly stronger. Climate communication experience, self-efficacy, and social norm seem to be positively correlated with pro-environmental behavior, suggesting that they have a positive effect on behavior. Similarly, it was found that more information seeking (β=0.214, p < 0.001) and climate communication experience (β=0.132, p < 0.001) increased health adaptation behavior. Climate change-related self-efficacy and social norm were also positively associated with health adaptation behaviors (Fig. 4, Appendix Table 2).

Discussion

Linking information seeking experiences to awareness and behaviors

It was found that communication is not associated with susceptibility to climate change risks. In particular, the experience of climate communication was not directly related to susceptibility to health risks, likely because it is not specific to climate change and health. Vrselja et al. [24] found effectiveness of cognitive aspects in influencing pro-environmental behavior varied by media. For information received through traditional media such as TV and radio, higher levels of concern were found to promote pro-environmental behavior. Conversely, an indirect effect of cognitive factors on pro-environmental behavior was not found significant for information received through Social Networking Services (SNS) and YouTube. Amplification effects of information are greater with more trustworthy information sources [25]. Despite being exposed to substantial information, individuals with a low level of trust are unlikely to experience the full impact of the risks highlighted in the media. Consequently, it is difficult to bring about a change in awareness and behavior. Therefore, the sources of information on climate change and their amount of information encountered may increase the perceived risk, so it is necessary to identify both.

However, there were no significant group differences in awareness of information-seeking experiences by source. This suggests that information seeking may not be associated with the acquisition of adequate knowledge and understanding of the health risks of climate change. The experiences captured in this survey came from a variety of sources and it is difficult to assess their contents and accuracy. In the field of climate change and health, information is not yet systematized, and it seems that “awareness” alone does not distinguish between sources of information.

On the other hand, this study found that pro-environmental and health adaptation behaviors increased as information seeking increased. Baek and Kim [26] found that among climate change-related experiences, information acquisition from experiences had the greatest impact on high school students. This suggests that acquiring information can lead to behavioral intentions and actual behavioral changes. On the other hand, those with low behavioral intentions had lower information seeking, which may be due to their lack of access to information [16].

Higher levels of physical, mental, and financial impact from disasters, as well as higher levels of self-efficacy and social norm about climate change, were associated with higher levels of susceptibility to climate change health risks and higher levels of adaptive behavioral practices. A study of nurses in Korea found that more optimistic attitudes toward climate change action, more frequent experiences with climate change-related extreme weather events, and more sources of exposure to climate change-related information were associated with higher levels of climate and health awareness and behavior. In addition, those who were concerned about the occurrence of natural disasters, concerned about the spread of infectious diseases, achieving social justice and reducing inequality, and preventing property loss as motivating factors, had significantly higher levels of climate- and health-related cognitive behaviors than those who did not perceive these motivating factors [27]. In addition, Akompab et al. [28] found that a high level of perceived benefits and action triggers, among heat wave risk perceptions, significantly predicted adaptive behavior. Furthermore, Kim and Cooke [29] found that environmental beliefs and media attention positively influenced behavioral intentions.

Linking climate communication experiences to awareness and behaviors

Communication experience appears to be associated with positive behavioral change in the general public, as the more people communicate about climate change, the more they adopt health-adaptative or pro-environmental behaviors. In this study, those with higher levels of awareness about climate change and health were relatively more likely to vote during the communication experience. However, because the information is not being delivered through specific sources, and the communication experiences are not focused on health, the differences between groups are not as pronounced. Communication about the health risks of climate change is known to positively change individual health-related behaviors, increase awareness of health, environmental justice, and other issues, and can lead to actions that demand government policies [30]. A national survey of Canadians in 2021 suggested that approaching climate change communication in terms of health risks can help increase public awareness and perceived seriousness [31].

It has also been found that education about the risks of climate change can increase intentions to act, and that lack of trust, self-efficacy, and frustration with politics may have contributed to low levels of political engagement [16]. Depending on what the health impacts of climate change are, knowing or believing about them has been found to significantly increase intentions to act [32]. Because knowledge and perceived beliefs act independently but have the potential to influence behavioral intentions, addressing cognitive factors in combination may increase the likelihood of acting.

Communication strategies to improve awareness and behaviors

Much of the research on improving health behavior has applied the Health Belief Model, which suggests that perceived awareness of a disease can lead to changing health behaviors. Results that are not explained by this theoretical model, i.e., positive changes in behavior without improvements in awareness, are interpreted as indicating that climate communication may have compelled people to act, but failed to improve understanding and awareness of why behavior change is necessary. Under these circumstances, it is difficult to expect that the current behavioral changes will be sustained or expanded.

While most studies have focused primarily on the health impacts of climate change, Maibach and colleagues [33] have emphasized the benefits of presenting health-related alternatives alongside climate change. Risk information is known to have a greater impact on behavioral change when it is presented alongside efficacy information or information about the existence and effectiveness of alternatives [34]. Kotcher and colleagues [35] showed that information about climate change and health was most influential when it included health consequences of climate change, health benefits of climate change action, and call-to-action [35]. Suggesting the health benefits of climate change solutions was more persuasive than others, it can strengthen the intentions and commitment of policy advocates and the public [36].

In addition, positive messages, such as the health and economic benefits of climate action, have been shown to be more effective than negative ones, such as the potential impacts of the climate crisis or the costs of addressing climate change [32,37]. It has shown that moderate levels of anxiety can evoke positive emotions and encourage people to practice pro-environmental behaviors. On the other hand, some studies have reported that exposure to information about climate change decreases self-efficacy and increases climate anxiety. This suggests that indiscriminate exposure to information or emphasizing only the risks of global warming may increase anxiety and lead to inaction. Therefore, it is necessary to provide media coverage of good examples of pro-environmental behavior at both the individual and societal levels [17,18].

The findings of this study suggest that to foster positive emotions and engagement, communication should strategically focus on solutions rather than solely on risks. However, our results also highlight that for such solution-oriented messaging to be perceived as credible and effective, it should be supported by tangible, proactive national-level actions that the public can witness. Furthermore, this study indicates effective communication should move beyond abstract warnings to provide specific, actionable missions for various stakeholders from policymakers and healthcare professionals to local communities and individuals. It is also crucial to establish specific guidelines and create tailored content that directs how each group should communicate its respective mission.

Limitations and recommendations for further research

The cross-sectional design of this research precludes the establishment of a clear causal relationship. The association between communication and behavior could be bidirectional, where existing behaviors might also drive communication experiences. Given the lack of sufficient tools to measure communication, items were adapted from existing scales to fit the current study's context. This approach makes it difficult to establish a direct association between communication and health-specific awareness. Therefore, future research would be strengthened by employing longitudinal designs to clarify causality and by developing and validating a scale specific to climate-health communication in the Korean context.

Conclusion

Climate change communication experiences were significantly associated with climate change response behaviors, but not with health awareness. This suggests that current communication approaches have not been effective enough in combining information and communication experiences about climate change and health to build accurate awareness. Protecting and promoting health in the context of the climate crisis requires a public demand-driven approach and a systematic communication strategy that leads to the right awareness and action.
