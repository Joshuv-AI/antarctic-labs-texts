The role of 1400 plasma metabolites in gastric cancer: A bidirectional Mendelian randomization study and metabolic pathway analysis
Authors: Yihao He, Peiyuan Cai, Anchi Hu, Jiali Li, Xuan Li, Yini Dang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
While observational studies have illustrated correlations between plasma metabolites and gastric cancer (GC), the causal association between the 2 is still unclear. Our study aims to delineate the bidirectional relationship between plasma metabolites and GC and find potential metabolic pathways. We undertook a bidirectional 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to investigate the causal relationship, specificity, and direction of association between 1400 plasma metabolites and GC. The GWAS data for metabolites was obtained from a cohort of 8299 European individuals. And the GC’s GWAS data was from FinnGen Consortium with 2384 European individuals, and the GWAS catalog with 1029 European ancestry cases for validation. Causal estimates were primarily calculated by the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method. To ensure robustness, we performed comprehensive sensitivity analyses to assess heterogeneity and address concerns regarding horizontal pleiotropy. We validated the forward relationship between metabolites and GC from another database and implemented meta-analysis. Furthermore, we conducted metabolic enrichment and pathway analysis of these causal metabolites using MetaboAnalyst5.0/6.0 with the database of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes. All statistical analysis was carried out using R software. Metabolites like 2s, 3R-dihydroxybutyrate, 4-acetamidobutanoate, ferulic acid 4-sulfate and methyl indole-3-acetate was proven positively linked with the development of GC. Asparagine, glucose to maltose ratio, glycohyocholate, Gulonate levels, linoleoyl ethanolamide and Spermidine to (N(1) + N(8))-acetylspermidine ratio was proven to be negatively associated with GC. Moreover, linoleic acid, histidine, glutamine, bilirubin, Succinate to proline ratio were found to be potentially linked to the development of GC. Furthermore, our analysis identified 18 significant metabolic pathways, including Arginine and proline metabolism (P < .009) and Valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis (P < .031). Our findings offer evidence supporting potential casual relations between multiple plasma metabolites and GC. These findings may offer great potential for future application of these biomarkers in GC screening and clinical prevention strategies.

1. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a disease of significant global impact, with over 1 million new cases recorded each year, making it the 5th most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide. Tragically, due to its frequent diagnosis at advanced stages, GC carries a high mortality rate, placing it as the third cancer-related cause of death.[1] Currently, the mainstream treatment for GC is endoscopic resection for early GC and chemotherapy for advanced GC.[1] In addition to this, some new treatments for stomach cancer have recently surfaced in the field of Chinese medicine.[2] The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate for patients with early GC can exceed 90 percent.[1] However, since the diagnosis of stomach cancer relies on complex gastroscopy and the experience of doctors. It cannot be applied on a large scale where resources are limited. Even in the United States, more than 60% of patients with GC have local or distant metastases at the time of diagnosis.

Given these alarming statistics, there is an urgent need for early detection and preventive measures to combat GC. Emerging biomarker blood tests have the advantage of being easy to perform in order to facilitate widespread early diagnosis of GC.[3] Previous studies have provided sufficient evidence of some common risk factors for GC, such as smoking,[4] gut microbiota,[5] hepatitis B infection,[6] etc. However, there remains to be more research investigating metabolic changes in GC. Despite its global significance, limited attention has been given to unraveling the intricate metabolic alterations associated with this disease.

Over the past few decades, growing evidence has firmly established the crucial role of metabolic reprogramming and energy metabolism in driving the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells.[7,8] Cancer cells independently change some metabolic pathways to facilitate survival and proliferation, and it was also found that reprogramming cellular metabolism assists tumorigenesis, progression.[9,10] Specific metabolic pathways are involved in transformation and tumor progression straightforward. Interdict these pathways or rebuilding altered metabolic pathways have demonstrated to be promising target strategies for treatment.[11,12] Among the notable breakthroughs in cancer treatment, the targeted regulation of metabolism has gained significant attention, with dichloroacetate (DCA) serving as a remarkable example.[13–15] Furthermore, modulating cellular metabolism has shown promise in increasing the sensitivity of cancer cells to treatment.[16]

There has been a recent surge in observational studies focusing on the potential association between metabolites and GC. These studies have successfully identified biomarkers and developed prediction models for GC occurrence.[17] In a multicenter study involving a retrospective cohort of 1944 participants from 7 centers and a prospective cohort of 264 participants, they constructed and identified 21 metabolites for the diagnosis of GC. Moreover, scholars conducted untargeted metabolomics analysis on plasma metabolites from 218 patients with gastric adenocarcinoma to explore their prognostic significance in GC patients.[17] However, establishing causality between metabolites and GC remains challenging due to the absence of prospective studies. Conventional observational studies are prone to design weaknesses, such as altered metabolite levels resulting from deliberate lifestyle changes after cancer diagnosis or the use of antitumor drugs.[18] Conversely, implementing rigorous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) is hindered by ethical considerations, financial constraints, and logistical limitations.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a widely used method in genetic epidemiology study design, exploring causal inference between exposures and outcomes.[19] MR allows for the assessment of causality between specific exposures and desired outcomes by employing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as IVs. This approach helps address confounding factors often encountered in conventional observational studies.[20] In our research, in order to acquire comprehensive insights into the role of plasma metabolites in GC, a bidirectional 2-sample MR analysis and pathway analysis were utilized to systematically explore potential suggestive associations between plasma metabolites and the occurrence of GC.

2. Material and methods

2.1. Research design

The bidirectional MR analysis in this study is presented in the flow chart below Figure 1. We conducted a bidirectional 2-sample MR analysis with genetic variation as instrumental variables (IVs) to investigate the causal relationship between plasma metabolites and GC. To validate the MR analysis, 3 fundamental hypotheses need to be satisfied: The IVs should exhibit a high correlation with exposure; There should be no relationship or correlation between the IVs and any potential confounding factors; The IVs can only influence the results through their impact on exposure.[21]

The overview of the study process. The study includes 4 aspects: (1) Acquisition of statistically significant IVs (P < 5e-6, r2 < 0.001, LD = 10000kb, F > 10). (2) MR analysis using the IVW method, MR-Egger, simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode. (3) Sensitive analysis using Cochran Q test, MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO, and leave-one-out analysis. (4) Metabolic pathway analysis using MetOrigin web tool (KEGG). IVs = instrumental variables, IVW = inverse-variance weighted, KEGG = Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, LD = linkage disequilibrium, MR = Mendelian randomization.

2.2. GWAS data used in the research

2.2.1. GWAS data for human plasma metabolites

We acquired the latest genetic information regarding plasma metabolites from a genome-wide association study (GWAS) conducted by Chen Y et al.[22] This GWAS included 1091 metabolites and 309 metabolic ratios, evaluated in a cohort of 8299 European individuals from the Canadian Longitudinal Study on Aging (CLSA). Approximately 15 million SNPs were examined for their associations with plasma metabolites. The study accession in the GWAS catalog was GCST90199621-GCST90204063. All data can be acquired from the GWAS catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) for free without ethical problems.

2.2.2. GWAS data for GC

Primarily, we got GC’s GWAS summary statistics from the FinnGen Consortium R9 release (https://www.finngen.fi/en). In this study, the phenotype of interest was defined as “Malignant neoplasm of stomach (controls excluding all cancers).” The dataset comprised 2384 European individuals with 1307 cases and 287,137 controls.

The GC’s GWAS summary statistics for validation were acquired from GWAS catalog with the accession of GCST90018849. The dataset comprised 1029 European ancestry cases and 475,087 European ancestry controls.

2.2.4. Selection of IVs

In this study, we implemented a rigorous selection process for IVs by focusing on SNPs with P-values below the level (5e-6) in the initial analysis. This threshold allows for insufficient SNPs if we set it as 5e-8. A similar disposition was conducted in previous articles.[23] This approach ensured comprehensive results and enhanced sensitivity to IVs. Additionally, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed on all IVs using a threshold of r2 = 0.001 and a distance of 10,000kb to eliminate the potential chain ambiguity.

To evaluate the power of each instrument, we calculated the F-statistic [R2(N-2)/(1-R2)], where R2 refers to the percentage of variance that can be explained by a genetic instrument, and N is the adequate sample size of the GWAS.[24] Selected SNPs for subsequent MR analysis had F-statistic exceeding 10, ensuring robust genetic variation estimation.[25]

Moreover, to ensure data integrity, we excluded palindromic SNPs[26] from our study due to their unclear effective allele, thereby improving the reliability and validity of our MR analysis.

Furthermore, the PhenoScanner (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) was used to remove potential confounding factors to satisfy 1 of the 3 fundamental hypotheses: “The IVs can only influence the results through their impact on exposure”

2.3. MR analysis

A bidirectional MR analysis was employed to explore the causal relationship between the level of plasma metabolites and the risk of GC. We utilize several complementary MR methods to ensure accurate causal effects assessment, including the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, MR-Egger, simple mode, weighted median, and weighted mode.[27–29] The IVW method was utilized as our primary MR analysis, which has been widely recognized for its robustness and efficiency in estimating causal effects.[30]

Traditionally, a P-value threshold of <.05 indicated a nominal association. To address the challenge of false positives in multiple testing, we could employ False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. By applying FDR correction, we can control false positives’ inflation and establish more stringent criteria for identifying truly significant associations. However, considering the large number of types of plasma metabolites and the strict threshold that we set for the selection of IVs compared to similar studies.[31–35] We decided to deregulate FDR appropriately, as used in some other articles,[23,36–38] so as to explore more possibly suggestive associations between plasma metabolites and GC.

2.4. Sensitive analysis

We employed additional sensitivity analysis methods to strengthen the robustness of our results: Cochran Q test was used with IVW and MR-Egger methods to detect possible heterogeneity violations between IVs, with a threshold of 0.05; MR-Egger was employed to estimate the horizontal pleiotropy according to its intercept; The MR-PRESSO method was utilized to identify any anomalies and adjust for horizontal pleiotropy.[39]

Moreover, we further conducted leave-one-out analyses to evaluate the reliability of associations discovered by individual SNP drivers. These analyses systematically removed 1 SNP at a time to assess their impact on the overall causal estimates and ensure the stability of our findings.

All analyses were employed using R Version 4.3.2. MR analyses were performed by the Mendelian randomization package. MR-PRESSO was performed by the MR-PRESSO package.

2.5. Meta-analysis of cross-validation

As for the positive results in the forward MR analysis, we performed a validation analysis in a new database and conducted meta-analysis. The GC’s GWAS summary statistics for validation were acquired from GWAS catalog with the accession of GCST90018849.

2.6. Metabolic pathway analysis

For the metabolites mentioned above that may have potential causal effects on GC, the web-based MetaboAnalyst 5.0/6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) was used for metabolic pathway analysis.[40] The metabolites that arise from the positive phenotypes could be matched with online analysis tools. Information on these metabolites that can be matched is presented in the Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O47. It includes new metabolites that were added to our further cross-validated analysis due to the updates of the MetaboAnalyst. Furthermore, we conducted functional enrichment analyses and pathway analyses online to investigate the metabolic pathways and their metabolites that may be associated with GC. We applied these analyses in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes databases,[41] resulting in a total of 18 metabolic pathways. Detailed results are in the results section as well as in the Supplementary Materials. It is crucial to highlight that this study focused on metabolites that yielded a positive result using the IVW method (P < .05).

3. Results

3.1. Selected IVs

As described in the method section, we preprocessed the data by the LD threshold (r2 < 0.001 and a distance of 10000kb) to guarantee the reliability of selected IVs. The F-statistics for the IVs indicated a reduced likelihood of experiencing weak instrumental bias, thereby enhancing the reliability of the estimates (F > 10). The total data of SNPs were displayed in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, (sheet 1 and sheet 2), http://links.lww.com/MD/O40 which respectively demonstrate the SNP-related information going through the processing above in the forward and reverse MR analysis. Furthermore, the PhenoScanner database was used to identify established GC risk factors (such as alcohol consumption, ever smoked and Helicobocton Pylon) to examine and remove associated confounding SNPs. The removed SNPs associated with confounders are displayed in Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O41.

To analyze the effects of plasma metabolites on GC, We used a set of carefully selected SNPs, ranging from 5 to 28, as IVs to represent the plasma metabolites in our study. To analyze the effects of GC on plasma metabolites, we selected 12 SNPs for GC. All IVs were selected according to the criteria previously mentioned in 3.3 Selection of IVs.

The IVW method was utilized to examine the association, accounting for potential weak instrument bias. A comprehensive sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure robustness. Notably, no existence of heterogeneity and pleiotropy were found in the sensitivity analysis (P > .05) (Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O42 and Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O44.). In addition, neither the MR-PRESSO (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O43 and Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O45) nor the leave-one-out analyses indicated any significant outliers. The F-statistics estimated for all variables were consistently above 10, indicating the exclusion of weak IVs. 4. Discussion

Our research was the first endeavor to comprehensively explore the causal relationships between 1400 plasma metabolites and GC with a bidirectional MR approach. Our research contains a variety of metabolites that came from the latest database on metabolites released in 2023. Then, we analyzed the metabolic pathways through metabolites that originated from the outcome of our forward MR analysis.

It is noteworthy that plasma metabolites mirror endogenous and exogenous processes of disease occurrence.[42] Metabolomics technology acts as a bridge between genotype and phenotype, allowing researchers to gain a deeper understanding of the disease at a more profound level.[35]

In our forward analyses through cross-validation, 2s, 3R-dihydroxybutyrate, 4-acetamidobutanoate, ferulic acid 4-sulfate and methyl indole-3-acetate was proven positively linked with the development of GC. Asparagine, glucose to maltose ratio, glycohyocholate, Gulonate levels, linoleoyl ethanolamide and Spermidine to (N(1) + N(8))-acetylspermidine ratio was proven negatively associated with GC.

In the reversed MR analysis, some metabolites were also proven to be potentially linked with GC.

Over the past few decades, a growing body of evidence has established the crucial role of metabolic reprogramming and energy metabolism in driving the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells. According to existing literature, linoleic acid (18:2n6) has been suggested to promote the formation of lipid peroxides, induce cancer cell apoptosis by increasing ROS production, and disrupt mitochondrial function.[43] As an essential fatty acid, linoleic acid inhibits the growth of gastric tumor cells, thereby impeding the progression of gastric lesions. Furthermore, previous studies have reported a significant inverse association between linoleic and the risk of overall GC.[44] In our research, through reverse MR analysis, we confirmed a reverse causal relationship between linoleic acid and GC (P = 2.82e-02, OR = 0.957, 95% CI = 0.957–0.920), indicating its potential utility as a diagnostic biomarker for GC.

Emerging evidence highlights the significance of alterations in plasma histidine metabolism as a diagnostic marker for GC.[45,46] Building upon this knowledge, our study provides further compelling evidence through reverse causality analysis, revealing a robust association between GC and plasma histidine concentration (P = 2.04e-02, OR = 1.049, 95% CI = 1.007–1.093). These findings firmly establish that GC indeed triggers changes in plasma histidine levels, emphasizing its possibility as a valuable biomarker for diagnosing GC.

Experimental evidence has provided compelling insights into the influence of glutamine on signaling pathways associated with tumor growth.[47] Specifically, the addition of glutamine to cell lines has been shown to rapidly enhance the expression of pro-proliferative factors like c-jun and c-myc while concurrently suppressing the expression of growth-inhibitory and pro-apoptotic factors, including CHOP, GADD45, Fas, and ATF5. This intricate interplay promotes tumor cell survival, proliferation, and ultimately contributes to tumor development. Our study further strengthens the existing correlation between glutamine and GC by establishing a causal relationship between plasma glutamine levels and GC (P = 2.35e-02, OR = 1.049, 95% CI = 1.006–1.093). These findings underscore the significant role of glutamine in GC pathogenesis and its potential as a promising therapeutic target.

Previous studies have developed a Metabolic Score (MS).[48] Bilirubin, as a component of the MS, contributes significantly to the prognostic assessment of GC patients in an inverse manner. Specifically, the research reveals that patients with elevated levels of bilirubin (i.e., high MS status) tend to have a poorer prognosis, manifested by lower 3-year overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates. This suggests that elevated bilirubin levels may be associated with tumor progression and adverse outcomes. Consequently, bilirubin plays a negative indicative role in the MS. By calculating the MS, physicians can more accurately predict the long-term survival of GC patients and tailor personalized treatment and monitoring strategies accordingly. The MS calculation formula can be referenced in the original text at the following URL: https://quesijin.shinyapps.io/Mscaculator/. However, in our study, bilirubin demonstrates a negative impact on GC. (P = .039, OR = 0.958, 95% CI = 0.919–0.998) The variance in conclusions could stem from factors such as the selection of study subjects, methods employed for data collection, or the presence of other potential variables.

In a study published in Cancer Biology and Translational Studies, it is demonstrated that the excessive expression of PIN1 (peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase) is closely linked to advanced tumor staging, poor response to chemotherapy, and shorter RFS in patients with diffuse-type GC.[49] Additionally, the deficiency of Succinate leads to energy metabolism defects, inhibiting the growth of GC.[50] Taken together, these 2 points suggest a positive correlation between the Succinate to proline ratio (P = .048, OR = 0.960, 95% CI = 0.922–0.999) and GC.

In addition to the metabolites mentioned above, some other positive metabolites that we discovered in our MR analysis were not involved in the previous observational and experimental studies. This may provide ideas and directions for subsequent research.

After investigating the relationship between individual metabolites and GC, we analyzed metabolic pathways. Our pathway analysis has shown that the metabolism of Arginine and proline, as well as the biosynthesis of Valine, leucine, and isoleucine, play a protective role in the occurrence and development of GC. Arginine undergoes various metabolic pathways in our body. Under the action of arginase, Arginine is metabolized to ornithine and urea, forming the urea cycle.[51] It can also be converted to nitric oxide (NO) through the catalysis of NO synthase (NOS).[52] NO is a short-lived signaling molecule generated by the enzyme family known as NOS. This enzyme catalyzes the synthesis of NO from Arginine, playing a crucial role in various biological processes. The dysregulation of NO has been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple diseases, including cancer. Extensive research has demonstrated the broad involvement of NO in cancer, influencing both its initiation and progression. Additionally, NO, or NO, plays an important role in preventing the spread and growth of cancer cells, while also promoting immune system responses against tumors. The precise control of NO levels is crucial to its function in various cellular processes, which involves regulation at multiple levels such as transcriptional, posttranslational and metabolic. Cancer cells often exhibit enhanced proliferation in hypoxic environments, where hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are activated. NO serves as a crucial regulator of HIF-1, a downstream effector involved in the expression of genes related to angiogenesis, glucose metabolism and hematopoiesis under hypoxic conditions. HIF-1α, which is a subunit of HIF, is normally degraded in normoxic conditions but is stabilized and dimerizes with HIF-1β in hypoxic environments, forming the active HIF-1 complex.[53] It is necessary to gather more evidence in order to fully understand the impact of NO on the regulation of HIF-1. The p53 protein, which is responsible for suppressing tumors, plays a crucial role in regulating the cell cycle, DNA repair, metabolism, and cell proliferation. Cancer cells that have a normal, or wild-type, p53 protein are more sensitive to cytotoxic effects caused by NO than those with mutated p53 proteins.[54] NO is also associated with the inhibition of angiogenesis signaling pathways. The NO-mediated inhibition of NF-κB renders tumor cells more susceptible to chemotherapy.[55] Furthermore, an elevation in local NO concentration diminishes angiogenesis and induces cell death by inhibiting the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathways. Despite the established contribution of NO to the mechanisms underlying cancer initiation and progression, the implementation of NO-related cancer therapies remains constrained. Both in vitro and clinical data from cancer patients suggest that supplementation with Arginine mediates immune-stimulatory effects, triggering macrophage phagocytic activity, activation of NK cells, and lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cell cytotoxicity. These effects may potentially impede the dissemination of tumor cells.[56] In specific tumor models, supplementation with Arginine has been shown to enhance both specific and nonspecific antitumor functions, retarding tumor progression and extending patient survival.[57] Therefore, gaining a better understanding of how Arginine affects the production of NO in cancer is crucial in developing anticancer drugs that target this important metabolic pathway, as well as other pathways involved in generating NO.

The biosynthesis and degradation of proline Involve a shared Intermediate, Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C), which is formed through a reversible nonenzymatic reaction to generate L-glutamate-γ-semialdehyde (GSAL). Proline is synthesized from glutamate or ornithine via GSAL/P5C and reduced back to proline by P5C reductase (PYCR) in an NAD(P)H-dependent reaction.[58] Existing studies have demonstrated the crucial regulatory role of the proline metabolic axis in the occurrence and development of cancer.[59] Proline oxidase (POX), also known as proline dehydrogenase (PRODH), is 1 of the few genes rapidly and strongly induced by the tumor suppressor P53. Under the control of the tet-off promoter, ectopic expression of POX triggers mitochondrial apoptosis. The mechanism activated by POX involves the mediation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Research suggests that, in comparison to normal tissues, 61% of tumors exhibit decreased expression of POX. In the case of gastrointestinal and renal tumors, 78% of tumors demonstrate a significantly reduced expression of POX, indicating downregulation of POX inhibitory activity.[60] Therefore, focusing on the mechanisms underlying the downregulation of POX expression in GC is advantageous for gaining insights into the specific role of proline metabolism in the initiation and progression of GC, as well as providing novel perspectives for its therapeutic interventions.

Valine, leucine, and isoleucine are all BCAAs that not only directly participate in protein synthesis but also provide a nitrogen source for the synthesis of large molecules such as nucleic acids. Valine is an amino acid that is essential for the growth and metabolism of tumor cells. It has been discovered that it plays a role in protein synthesis of tumor cells, which can promote their growth. Nevertheless, a catabolic inhibitor has also been found that can control the progression of advanced colorectal cancers.[46] Metabolomic studies consistently detected elevated levels of Valine in GC patients’ tissue, plasma, serum, and urine samples using gas chromatography.[61] Additionally, BCAAs can induce mTORC1 activation, contributing to cell growth and proliferation. The breakdown metabolism of BCAAs collectively leads to chemotherapy resistance, upregulation of SOX2, induction of USP1, BCAT2 deubiquitination, glutamine synthesis, tumor cell growth, proliferation, migration, and metastasis.[62] Studies indicate that alterations in BCAA metabolism can impact tumor proliferation. Elevated levels of BCAAs are associated with growth inhibition in tumors, disrupting the migration and invasive effects of tumor cells on surrounding tissue and organs. Conversely, a deficiency in BCAA promotes the metastasis of tumors to different organs. This disruption is associated with tumor cell adhesion, angiogenesis, metastasis, and invasion.[63] Moreover, BCAAs act as nitrogen donors and play a significant role in the synthesis of macromolecules, including proteins and nucleotides. These macromolecules are essential for the growth of cancer cells. Therefore, the impact of BCAAs on cancer growth or inhibition is dependent on different conditions and concentrations, making their role in cancer dual. The results of this study demonstrate a protective effect of the branched-chain amino acid metabolic pathway in GC. Further experimental investigations are required to elucidate the specific mechanisms involved. Modulating the accumulation of BCAAs in vivo can regulate tumor cell proliferation, tumor burden, and overall survival. The dietary intake of BCAAs is also associated with the development of certain malignant tumors. Therapeutic interventions targeting the reduction of branched-chain amino acid catabolism may potentially exert inhibitory effects on the initiation and progression of GC.[64] Based on current research, enzymes that break down branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), like BCAT1, BCAT2, and BCKDK, could be used as potential targets for cancer treatment. Therefore, it is recommended to explore targeted therapies and dietary interventions that focus on BCAAs for GC treatment in the future.

Our study has several notable strengths. Firstly, a significant strength lies in the extensive range of genetic variables we examined to assess the correlation between plasma metabolites and GC. More specifically, our study encompassed a total of 1400 metabolites after excluding those that remained unidentified. Moreover, by leveraging these 2 GWAS datasets, we have conducted a relatively systematic analysis of the metabolic level associated with the development of GC. Secondly, employing the MR design, our study performed thorough sensitivity analyses to rule out potential confounding factors arising from variable polymorphisms effectively.

Indeed, it should be acknowledged that there are limitations in our study. Firstly, due to the vast amount of plasma metabolite data analyzed, we did not apply a correction for multiple tests in this exploratory study. This approach was chosen to maximize the identification of potentially positive relationships. However, it is important to interpret these findings with caution and replicate them in future studies. Secondly, the data on metabolites primarily originated from individuals of European ancestry, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations. Further investigations supported by extensive GWAS data involving diverse ethnic groups are warranted to validate and extend our results. Thirdly, due to the reliance on existing GWAS data, the variety of metabolites included may not be sufficiently complete, which may result in some key metabolic causality not being detected. Last but not least, while our study encompassed a relatively comprehensive range of metabolites, the exact mechanisms by which specific metabolites are associated with disease are still not fully understood. The interpretation of the results from this MR analysis may be limited by this lack of knowledge. In summary, while our study has strengths, it is important to consider its limitations. Future research should address these concerns to better understand the relationship between plasma metabolites and GC.

5. Conclusion

Our study is the first systematic MR analysis and metabolic pathway analysis to evaluate the causal relationship between plasma metabolites and GC. Metabolites like 2s, 3R-dihydroxybutyrate, 4-acetamidobutanoate, ferulic acid 4-sulfate and methyl indole-3-acetate was proven positively linked with the development of GC. Asparagine, glucose to maltose ratio, glycohyocholate, Gulonate levels, linoleoyl ethanolamide and Spermidine to (N(1) + N(8))-acetylspermidine ratio was proven to be negatively associated with GC. Linoleic acid, histidine, glutamine, bilirubin, and Succinate to proline ratio etc were found to be potentially linked to the development of GC. Meanwhile, metabolic pathways such as Arginine and proline metabolism and Valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis. Were proven to play a crucial role in GC. By identifying and characterizing these metabolites, researchers can potentially develop novel diagnostic tools and therapeutic interventions that are more precise and tailored to individual patients. This breakthrough knowledge has the potential to revolutionize the field of GC research and improve patient care by enabling personalized approaches to diagnosis and treatment.
