Frequency of chronic diseases, health behaviors, and quality of life among physicians: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Defne Kalayci, Sule Ozturk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe health of the physician workforce is a critical issue, as it directly affects both occupational health and the effectiveness of healthcare delivery. This study evaluated physicians’ health status and quality of life in relation to occupational factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted among actively practicing physicians in Samsun, Türkiye, via an online survey. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, occupational features, health status and behaviors, and quality of life-via Short Form-36 (SF-36)-were collected. ResultsA total of 207 physicians participated, 57.4% of whom were women. At least one chronic disease was reported by 45.9% of the participants, whereas 29.9% reported more than one. The most frequently reported problems were musculoskeletal disorders. Among those with chronic diseases, 78.9% utilized healthcare services, and 22.1% reported taking sick leave in the past 12 months. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, age (OR = 1.09, 95% CI:1.019–1.167) and economic status (OR = 0.313, 95% CI:0.151–0.649) were significantly associated with the presence of chronic disease. After Bonferroni correction, physicians in internal medicine specialties had significantly higher mental health scores than those in surgical specialties (p = 0.006). Physicians with chronic diseases scored lower in physical functioning, role-physical and emotional, social functioning, bodily pain, and general perception of health compared with those without chronic diseases (p < 0.05). Correlation analysis revealed a weak but statistically significant positive association between duration of professional experience and both vitality (r = 0.14, p = 0.044) and social functioning scores (r = 0.164, p = 0.019). Conversely, longer weekly working hours and a higher number of monthly night shifts were associated with lower scores across most SF-36 domains (p < 0.05).ConclusionChronic diseases were common among physicians. Sex, the presence of chronic disease, and occupational variables contributed to differences across multiple domains of quality of life. Strategies to protect and improve physician health should be supported by comprehensive studies that assess work-related risk factors via standardized measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26039-z.

Introduction

Healthcare is a fundamental human right essential for protecting the health and safety of societies [1]. Owing to their central role in the delivery of healthcare, physicians are widely regarded as leaders in improving health services [2]. This perspective, which also refers to their guidance role, establishes physician health as a multidimensional field of inquiry.

Given their medical knowledge, physicians are expected to adopt healthier lifestyle behaviors and have a lower prevalence of certain chronic diseases than populations matched for age, socioeconomic status, and education [3]. There are no studies on the general health status of doctors in Türkiye, but in one study examining a group of doctors, participants reported the most common conditions of hyperlipidemia (23%), hypertension (18%), and diabetes mellitus (8%) [4]. The most frequently reported health problems among physicians in Bahrain showed a similar pattern (hyperlipidemia (26%), hypertension (20%), diabetes (11%) [5]. Among primary care physicians in Swiss, the most common problems were depression/anxiety (21%), burnout (21%), dyslipidemia (19%) and hypertension (17%) [6]. It is an expected result that the prevalence of recorded health problems among physicians will vary in accordance with the prevalent diseases in the general population. However, healthcare professionals face long-term and simultaneous exposure to multiple occupational risk factors, including physical, chemical, biological, psychosocial, and ergonomic hazards [7]. Such exposures may contribute to the development of various diseases or accelerate their onset. For example, a study conducted in Taiwan revealed that healthcare professionals had a higher risk of systemic diseases than nonmedical professionals did and that physicians’ susceptibility to infectious, parasitic, and gastrointestinal diseases was likely related to the specific nature of their work [8]. Furthermore, evidence indicating that long working hours and surgical specialties are associated with increased risks for unhealthy lifestyle behaviors highlights the need to identify vulnerable groups for targeted interventions [9, 10].

As a natural consequence of their key role in the functioning of the healthcare system, physicians have been reported to continue working while ill without taking sick leave and often tend to self-medicate. When physician patients delay seeking appropriate medical care, their condition may progress, resulting in greater functional impairment [11]. Another important dimension of this issue is the potential impact of physicians’ illnesses on their clinical practice [12]. When physicians’ well-being is compromised, the effectiveness and safety of the care they provide may also be affected [13].

Traditionally, biomedical outcomes have been the primary endpoints of research in medicine and healthcare. However, in recent years, increasing attention has been given to patients’ quality of life. Quality of life is influenced by multiple factors, including expectations, standards, diseases, and functional status. As such, it plays a critical role both in identifying the spectrum of problems that affect patients and in evaluating treatment outcomes [14]. In the literature on physician health, mental health has long been a major focus of investigation [15, 16], whereas studies addressing physical and social health remain relatively limited [3, 6, 17, 18]. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine the frequency of chronic diseases, health behaviors, and quality of life among physicians in relation to occupational factors. Specifically, this study aimed to assess these outcomes and their variation by occupational characteristics.

Methods

Study design, participants, and data collection

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Samsun, the largest city in the Central Black Sea Region of Türkiye. The study population consisted of 3,400 registered physicians in Samsun [19]. The inclusion criteria were being employed in Samsun, aged 65 or younger, and not being retired. Physicians who were not actively working and those older than 65 years were excluded.

To calculate the minimum required sample size, the formula for known populations “n = Nt²pq / [d²(N − 1) + t²pq]” was applied [20]. In this formula, p represents the estimated prevalence of the condition (17.3%) [21], and q = 1 − p (82.7%). Based on these parameters (t = 1.96, d = 0.05, N = 3,400), the minimum sample size was calculated to be 206. Data were collected via an online survey. The survey was piloted with physicians working outside Samsun, and necessary revisions were made on the basis of their feedback. The final version was shared through social media platforms (WhatsApp, Facebook, and Twitter) accessible to physicians. Data collection commenced on September 15, 2023, with reminder posts shared on days 7, 14, and 21. The data collection process was completed on October 15, 2023, upon reaching the targeted sample size. Only responses from volunteers who met the inclusion criteria and provided online informed consent within this period were analyzed. Data from the pilot test were not included in the study.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Samsun University (decision number: 2023/15/10).

Data collection tool

The survey consisted of four sections: (A) Sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle habits, (B) Occupational features, (C) Health status and behaviors, and (D) Quality of life. Socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed based on participants’ self-reported monthly household income and perceived economic status, categorized as low, moderate, or high according to national income distribution standards. Alcohol consumption was defined as drinking more than one standard glass of any alcoholic beverage. Participants who had not consumed cigarettes or alcohol for at least one year were classified as “former users.” For physical activity, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation was adopted: at least 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, or 75–150 min of vigorous intensity aerobic activity, or an equivalent combination of moderate- and vigorous intensity aerobic activities [22]. In the final section of the survey, the Short Form-36 (SF-36) was used to assess quality of life. This instrument evaluates an individual’s health status without being disease or age specific, covering physical, social, and mental domains. It consists of 36 items grouped into eight domains: physical functioning, role-physical, role-emotional, vitality, mental health, social functioning, bodily pain, and general perception of health. In the Turkish validity and reliability study by Koçyiğit et al., Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for each domain were reported to range between 0.73 and 0.76. Each domain is scored on a scale from 0 (worst) to 100 (best), with higher scores indicating better quality of life [23, 24].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed via SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test was applied to assess normality. Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) (minimum–maximum) or medians (25th–75th percentiles; (Q1, Q3), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%). For comparisons between two independent groups, Student’s t test or the Mann‒Whitney U test was used, depending on the distribution of the data. For comparisons among three or more groups, the Kruskal‒Wallis test was applied. When a statistically significant difference was found in the Kruskal–Wallis test, pairwise post hoc comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni-adjusted Mann–Whitney U test. The Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the conventional significance level (0.05) by the number of pairwise comparisons (n = 3), resulting in an adjusted p threshold of < 0.017 to control for type I error. The Pearson chi-square test was used for categorical variables. To determine the factors associated with the presence of chronic disease, multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted. Correlations between variables and scale scores were assessed via Spearman’s correlation coefficient. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 207 physicians participated in the study, including 119 women (57.4%) and 88 men (42.6%). The median age of the participants was 43 years, and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.47 ± 3.67 kg/m². Regarding lifestyle habits, approximately one in five participants reported smoking, and one in four reported alcohol consumption. Seven out of ten physicians did not exercise regularly. Compared with women, male physicians were significantly older (p < 0.001), had a higher BMI (p < 0.001), and smoked more frequently (p = 0.003) (Supplementary Table 1).

Physicians from 30 different fields participated. The three most common groups were family medicine physicians, general practitioners, and anesthesiologists (Supplementary Table 2). Nearly half of the participants (46.9%) were specialists. Overall, 44% reported working night shifts. The median number of monthly night shifts was 4 with no significant difference between men and women (p = 0.854). The median weekly working hours for the overall sample was 40, with no significant sex difference (p = 0.054). (Supplementary Table 3).

Nearly half of the physicians (n = 95, 45.9%) reported having at least one physician-diagnosed chronic disease, whereas 29.9% (n = 62) had multiple chronic diseases. The most frequently reported conditions were musculoskeletal disorders (lumbar region: 27.1%, cervical region: 10.6%), followed by autoimmune diseases (11.1%), hypertension (10.6%), and allergic diseases (8.2%) (Table 1).

Nearly one in five physicians with a chronic disease had taken sick leave, which was significantly more common among female physicians (p = 0.002). The median duration of sick leave was 5 days. With respect to chronic disease follow-up, most participants reported seeking professional medical consultation, followed by consulting a physician colleague or self-medicating. Physicians’ lifestyle habits, presence of chronic disease, and SF-36 scores were compared according to various work-related characteristics. When physicians working in internal medical sciences, surgical sciences, and basic medical sciences were compared in terms of smoking, alcohol consumption, regular exercise, and frequency of chronic disease, no statistically significant differences were observed among the groups (p = 0.158; 0.228; 0.333; 0.180, respectively). Table 4 presents the distribution of chronic diseases according to certain descriptive characteristics of the physicians. The median age (p < 0.001), mean BMI (p = 0.038), and median years of professional practice (p < 0.001) were significantly higher among physicians with chronic diseases. The percentage of participants who perceived their economic status as “good” was 69.7% among those without chronic diseases, whereas it was 30.3% among those with chronic diseases; this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.002). In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, the independent effects of BMI (1.077 [95% CI 0.986–1.177]) and duration of professional practice (0.984 [95% CI 0.920–1.051]) were not statistically significant, whereas age (OR = 1.09 [95% CI 1.019–1.167]) and perceived economic status remained significantly associated with chronic disease. Specifically, participants who perceived their economic status as “good” had a significantly lower likelihood of having a chronic disease (OR = 0.313 [95% CI 0.151–0.649]). However, those with a “moderate” perception of economic status were not significantly different (OR = 0.633 [95% CI 0.166–2.418]). The independent effects of other variables were not statistically significant in the multivariable analysis (p > 0.05).

The SF-36 domain scores were compared across medical fields (internal medicine, surgical, and basic sciences). The mental health scores differed significantly among the three groups (internal medicine: 60.0 (44.0, 76.0), surgical: 52.0 (44.0, 60.0), basic sciences: 56.0 (54.0, 80.0); p = 0.012). According to pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction, physicians in internal medicine specialties had significantly higher mental health scores than did those in surgical specialties (p = 0.006). Although physicians in basic sciences also scored higher than those in surgical specialties did, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.044). No significant difference was observed between internal medicine specialties and basic sciences (p = 0.583). For the other SF-36 domains median scores were comparable across medical fields, with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05).

When participants were compared according to the presence of chronic disease, those with chronic conditions had significantly lower scores in SF-36 domain median scores than those without chronic disease. Only, vitality and mental health scores did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Table 5).

Discussion

According to the WHO Constitution, health is defined not only as the absence of disease or infirmity but also as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being [25]. Yet, are physicians who play a frontline role in safeguarding and promoting public health from this holistic and ideal perspective themselves truly healthy? In this study, physicians’ health and quality of life were examined through the lens of occupational health and safety. Although full comparability with other studies is limited due to differences in participants, data collection methods, and measurements, the results were interpreted in light of the literature.

Approximately half of the physicians in this study reported having at least one chronic condition. This frequency was comparable to that reported among Swiss primary care physicians but lower than that reported among Polish physicians [6, 18]. Geographic and individual factors, along with countries’ levels of development, health systems, and working conditions, may act as potential confounding elements in such comparisons.

The most frequently reported group of conditions was musculoskeletal disorders. Although musculoskeletal disorders are rarely investigated among physicians, they represent one of the leading occupational diseases across all workers. Their association with non-ergonomic working conditions and psychosocial factors highlights them as a major concern for physicians [26]. Another commonly reported condition, consistent with the literature, was hypertension [3, 5, 7, 18, 27]. A study conducted in Spain demonstrated a high prevalence of masked hypertension among healthcare professionals and recommended the inclusion of 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring in routine occupational health examinations [28]. The medical profession is characterized by numerous occupational stressors, such as workload, effort–reward imbalance, low autonomy, work–family conflict, workplace violence, and insufficient peer support [29]. Occupational stress is frequently cited as a factor in the etiology of hypertension [30]; however, the evidence regarding this association remains complex [31].

In the present study, autoimmune diseases, allergic disorders, and gastrointestinal complaints were also among the frequently reported conditions. Approximately one decade ago, in a Turkish physician cohort with a similar mean age to the present sample, the most commonly reported problems were hyperlipidemia and hypertension, while the frequencies of diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and asthma were nearly identical to those reported in this study [4]. Large-scale, population-based studies employing standardized questionnaires may provide valuable insights into the occupational patterns underlying chronic diseases.

Although the literature provides well-established evidence for risk factors for chronic diseases, such as smoking, alcohol consumption, high BMI, unhealthy diet, and insufficient physical activity [32], these associations were not identified in the present study. In the multiple regression analyses, only age and socioeconomic status were found to be significantly associated with the presence of chronic disease. The increase in chronic disease incidence with age is a well-documented phenomenon. Socioeconomic status, while often conferring protective effects such as improved living conditions and better access to healthcare, has also been noted to exert bidirectional influences on the prevalence of chronic diseases [33]. In a study by Garg et al., the frequency of diabetes and hypertension was higher among surgeons than among physicians from internal medicine specialties [34]. In the present study, however, no significant differences in the frequency of chronic diseases were observed across specialties or according to work-related characteristics. It can be assumed that physicians with significant health problems would be less likely to remain in the workforce. The healthy worker effect, which describes this phenomenon, along with the cross-sectional design of the study may have obscured some associations. Longitudinal studies would be more informative for identifying relevant determinants.

Previous research has reported that physicians often tend to self-manage their illnesses [26, 35]. In contrast, participants in the present study with chronic conditions indicated that they sought professional medical care or consulted a physician colleague. This finding was further supported by the finding that the majority of physicians with chronic diseases had accessed healthcare services within the past 12 months. Only one-fifth of those with chronic conditions had taken sick leave. It is plausible that the clinical course and severity of disease influence such health behaviors. Moreover, in Turkey, salary systems based on working days and performance-based incentives may also have shaped these tendencies. Although no differences were found in the overall duration of leave, consistent with the results of Gustafsson Sendén et al., female physicians were more likely to take sick leave than their male counterparts were [36]. Similar to prior studies, it was also observed that many physicians continued working despite illness. Factors contributing to this behavior may include concern about increasing colleagues’ workload, patient scheduling pressures, the accumulation of tasks, and the stigma associated with acknowledging illness. However, presenteeism may lead to negative consequences such as disease progression, medical errors, and reduced productivity, underscoring the importance of further investigation into this issue [6, 35, 36].

In the SF-36 assessment of self-perceived health status, male physicians scored higher than female physicians in the domains of role-emotional, vitality, and social functioning. Similarly, a cross-sectional study among Norwegian physicians reported that male physicians obtained higher scores than females across various domain combinations, including physical functioning, role-physical, bodily pain, vitality, and mental health [17]. In the present study, physicians from internal medicine specialties had higher mental health scores compared with those in surgical fields. Demanding work schedules, constant exposure to life-and-death situations, and personal sacrifices may negatively influence the mental health of surgeons [37]. A meta-analysis identified female physicians as being particularly vulnerable to suicide risk and highlighted that certain specialties, such as anesthesiology, psychiatry, general practice, and general surgery, may also carry elevated risk [16]. In recent years, psychosocial workplace risk factors, including excessive workload, poor work–life balance, and long working hours, have become increasingly evident, while protective factors such as high professional autonomy, social support, and societal respect have diminished. Therefore, priority should be given to interventions that protect and promote physicians’ mental health [38].

The results also revealed that participants with chronic diseases reported lower quality of life across most domains than did their counterparts without chronic conditions. Consistent with this, a study among Iranian farmers revealed significant negative associations between chronic diseases and several quality-of-life domains [39]. Considering the high morbidity and mortality burden of chronic diseases, future research should address both patient prognosis and the evaluation of quality of life [40].

Additionally, correlation analyses indicated that length of professional experience was positively correlated with vitality and social functioning scores. Similarly, a study conducted in Norway reported that older physicians scored higher than their younger colleagues in vitality, social functioning, role-emotional, and mental health domains [17]. This result suggests that increased professional experience with age may enhance physicians’ ability to cope with challenges, thereby contributing to better outcomes. In contrast, longer weekly working hours and higher numbers of monthly night shifts were associated with lower scores across most SF-36 domains. Consistently, a study among residents revealed that increased hospital working hours led to significant decreases in nearly all SF-36 subscales, including general health, mental health, physical functioning, role-emotional, and social functioning [41]. Another study of hospital physicians reported that greater control over work schedules mitigated the negative impact of night shifts on sleep [42]. These results underscore the importance of examining the effects of work arrangements, such as on-call systems and working hours, on physicians’ quality of life through both quantitative and qualitative research in the future.

This study provides a holistic perspective on physician health by integrating individual and occupational characteristics and represents an initial step for future research. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the data were collected through a survey distributed via social media platforms, which may have introduced selection bias. Although an adequate sample size was achieved, subgroup analyses, for instance, by specialty, could not be performed. Furthermore, as a cross-sectional study conducted in a single province, causal inferences cannot be made, and generalizability is limited. A limitation of this study is that economic status was assessed solely based on participants’ self-reported household income and perceived financial standing, without accounting for other indicators of socioeconomic status such as education level, occupation, or personal assets. Therefore, economic status in this study may not fully represent participants’ broader socioeconomic circumstances.

Conclusion

This study revealed that physicians, as key actors in the healthcare system, are also vulnerable to chronic diseases, with musculoskeletal disorders, autoimmune diseases, and hypertension being the most common. The majority of physicians with chronic conditions sought professional medical care, whereas only a minority relied solely on self-medication. Chronic diseases were associated with age and socioeconomic status, and there were significant differences in quality-of-life domains across sex, the presence of disease, and medical field. The quality of life improved with increasing years of experience but declined with increasing weekly working hours and increasing numbers of night shifts. These results underscore the importance of recognizing physicians’ health as a critical occupational health concern. Systems should be developed to monitor the overall health status of physicians, including their quality of life, in conjunction with workplace risk factors and conditions. Identifying risk factors and high-risk groups, and integrating evidence-based interventions into occupational health and safety programs could serve as an effective roadmap for protecting and promoting physicians’ well-being, ultimately contributing to a more sustainable healthcare system.
