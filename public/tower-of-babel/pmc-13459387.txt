Data driven student support: utilize the LASSI to improve academic performance
Authors: Yuanyuan Zhou, Amber Sechelski, Brent Mowdy, Tess Johnson, Nikea Vernardo, Courtney A. West
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical student academic success is dependent not only on aptitude but also on the implementation and utilization of effective learning strategies. The Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI) measures ten strategies that may impact academic performance. Since LASSI research in medical education is somewhat limited, this study examined LASSI subscale scores and multiple outcomes of academic performance.MethodsFour cohorts of medical students (N = 423) completed the LASSI during orientation. Academic outcomes included first-semester GPA, pre-clerkship GPA, COMLEX-USA Level 2, and COMLEX-USA Level 3 scores. Correlational analyses, Pearson’s r and Spearman’s ρ, were used to explore relationships between LASSI subscales and performance measures. Partial correlation analysis was utilized to control for gender, MCAT scores, and Undergraduate Science GPA (USGPA).ResultsAcross cohorts, Time Management demonstrated small but statistically significant correlations with first-semester GPA (r = 0.11; p=0.028) and pre-clerkship GPA (r=0.10; p=0.036). Selecting Main Ideas correlated with COMLEX-USA Level 2 scores (r = 0.16; p=0.007), and Information Processing correlated with COMLEX-USA Level 3 scores (r=0.40; p=0.014). Between cohorts, analyses yielded variability suggesting that learning strategies may differ across classes. Subscales with minimal variance, such as Attitude, Motivation, and Test Taking Strategies, did not predict outcomes, which differ from prior meta-analysis findings in other populations.ConclusionsFindings support the use of the LASSI as a tool for guiding academic support in medical education. Targeting interventions focused on Information Processing, Selecting Main Ideas, and Time Management may enhance performance on both internal exams and board exams. Future research should explore longitudinal patterns and the impact of learning strategy instruction on LASSI scores and academic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09519-7.

Introduction

Successful applicants to medical school must demonstrate they possess strong academic aptitude. Admissions committees consider several factors, including candidates’ undergraduate grade-point averages (UGPA) and medical school entrance exam scores. UGPA has been associated with medical students’ academic success [1] and progress through medical school [2]. Researchers have observed similar associations between entrance exam scores and student success [3] and retention [2, 3]. Medical College Admission Test (MCAT) scores specifically have been linked to several important academic outcomes for medical students, including academic performance during the preclinical [1, 2, 4, 5] and clinical years [2], as well as performance on board exams [2, 4]. However, once successful medical school applicants have matriculated, they must employ the behaviors and skills that will help them become successful medical students. For example, West and Sadoski concluded that first-year medical students’ perceptions of their time management and self-testing skills-as measured by their scores in the Time Management and Self-Testing subscales of the Learning and Study Strategies Inventory (LASSI)-were stronger predictors of success during their first semester than their UGPA or MCAT scores [5]. As a result of these findings, the researchers implemented workshops for students who scored lower in those areas and provided individualized consultations to scaffold students’ utilization of time management strategies and self-testing techniques.

Some researchers have documented the importance of behaviors and skills delineated by the LASSI subscales without administering the LASSI itself. For example, researchers have confirmed through various methods that medical students who devote an adequate amount of time to studying consistently, without resorting to cramming, tend to be more successful than medical students who do not engage in this behavior [6–9]. Other researchers have administered the LASSI, a 60-item diagnostic assessment initially designed by Claire Winstein and her colleagues in 1988 to examine college students’ awareness of and use of learning and study skill strategies related to skill, will, and self-regulation components of strategic learning [10], to determine medical students’ areas for improvement (see Table I. LASSI Components, Scales, Descriptions, Sample Items, and Statistics). For example, medical students have had challenges with Concentration, Motivation, and Selecting Main Ideas [11], as well as with Self-Testing [12] and Using Academic Resources [11–13]. In addition, researchers have discovered that men and women enrolled in medical school might not consistently share the same struggles, exhibiting significant differences in their LASSI subscale scores in Anxiety [13], Attitude [12, 13], Test Strategies [13], Information Processing [13], Using Academic Resources (formerly Study Aids) [13], Selecting Main Ideas [12, 13], and Self-Testing [12, 13]. Similarly, second-year students’ perceptions of their academic challenges have differed from those of first-year students, evidenced by declining LASSI subscale scores [1, 14].

Researchers have also examined relationships specifically between students’ LASSI subscale scores and various academic performance measures. Medical students’ scores in various LASSI subscales have been associated with performance in biomedical science courses [14, 15], preclinical academic performance [1, 5, 13], and underperformance on specific shelf exams [16]. Importantly, several researchers have linked board or board-style exam performance and medical (or similar health-profession) students’ scores in nine of the ten LASSI subscales: Anxiety [13–15, 17, 18], Concentration [13, 17–19], Information Processing [13–15, 17], Motivation [13, 15, 17], Selecting Main Ideas [13–15, 17, 18], Self-Testing [13, 17], Test Strategies [13–15, 17, 18], Time Management [13, 17], and Using Academic Resources (formerly Study Aids) [14].

Although students’ demonstration of academic aptitude prior to matriculating into medical school has been linked to their future success, clearly, medical students’ behaviors and skills also play a role. LASSI scores and subscale scores have been positively associated with most primary, secondary, and postsecondary academic outcomes [20], but findings regarding associations between LASSI scores and subscale scores of medical students and their academic outcomes are still emerging. This study adds to the literature describing relationships between medical students’ LASSI subscale scores and their academic performance, further informing academic support and intervention efforts to promote their success.

Methods

Students who volunteered completed the LASSI during medical school orientation held late July to August each year. To examine the associations between LASSI subscales and academic performance, multiple academic outcomes were collected, including first-semester GPA, pre-clerkship GPA, and COMLEX-USA Level 1, 2, and 3 scores. Additional pre–medical school variables, such as gender, MCAT scores, and USGPA, were collected as control variables to account for variation in the sample. Because COMLEX-USA Level 1 is reported as a categorical (pass/fail) outcome with a very high pass rate, it lacked sufficient variance for statistical analysis and was excluded. COMLEX-USA Level 3 is typically taken after graduation and was available only for Cohort 2024 and a small subset of Cohort 2025. COMLEX-USA Level 2, generally completed at the end of the third year of medical school, was available for Cohorts 2024–2026.

Correlational analyses using Pearson’s r were conducted to assess the relationships between raw scores on LASSI subscales and each academic outcome. Additional correlational analyses using Spearman’s Rho were performed for ranked variables such as GPAs, to evaluate whether the use of Pearson’s r with these variables was appropriate. To account for pre-medical school factors, including Gender, MCAT scores, and Undergraduate Science GPA (USGPA), Partial correlations were calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.1.

Results

The dataset included 423 participants across four cohorts (Cohort 2024: 65; Cohort 2025: 90; Cohort 2026: 129; Cohort 2027: 139), with an average participation rate of 84%. The percentage of female students ranged from 43% to 61%, and mean MCAT scores ranged from 504.7 to 506.3 across the four cohorts. GPA and COMLEX outcomes were derived from different subsamples (GPA Cohort 2024–2027 n = 423; COMLEX-USA Level 2 Cohort 2024–2026 n = 278; COMLEX-USA Level 3 n = 62). (See Table II. Correlations between LASSI subscales and academic indices). Overall, participants demonstrated the highest scores with the least variability in Attitude and Motivation. For Attitude, our sample had a mean of 25.7 and an SD of 2.9, compared with the LASSI 3rd Edition scale norms of 23.7 and 4.2, respectively. For Motivation, our sample had a mean of 25.5 and an SD of 3.0, compared with the LASSI norms of 23.6 and 4.0, respectively. Scores on Test Strategies were also relatively high, with smaller variation than the other LASSI subscales, compared with the LASSI norms: 23.0 and 3.2 versus 20.8 and 4.3. These results on the LASSI, which has not been normed with medical students, suggest that participants were generally highly motivated, held positive attitudes toward their chosen field, and possessed strong testing strategies. For the remaining seven subscales, mean scores were lower and showed greater variability, with slight differences observed across the four cohorts.

When examining the associations between LASSI subscales and the early academic outcomes—first-semester GPA and pre-clerkship GPA —across all four cohorts (Cohorts 2024–2027), Time Management was the only subscale that showed a statistically significant correlation with First-semester GPA (r = 0.11, p = 0.028, n = 423) and pre-clerkship GPA (r = 0.10, p = 0.036, n = 423).

When examining the associations between LASSI subscales and COMLEX-USA Level 2 and Level 3 scores using Pearson’s r, stronger linear associations were observed for different subscales. Statistically significant correlations were found between Selecting Main Ideas and COMLEX-USA Level 2 (r = 0.16, p = 0.007, n = 278); and Information Processing and COMLEX-USA Level 3 (r = 0.40, p = 0.014, n = 62). However, results for GPA and COMLEX-USA data are not directly comparable because the samples included in each analysis differed (see Table II. Correlations between LASSI subscales and academic indices). Such correlation coefficients were generally small to moderate in magnitude, based on Cohen’s conventional benchmarks, in which Pearson’s r values of approximately 0.10 and 0.30 are interpreted as small and medium effects, respectively [21, 22].

To examine whether differences in sample composition influenced the findings, additional analyses were conducted by cohort. The results revealed substantial variability across cohorts, suggesting that the strength and consistency of the associations were highly dependent on the samples included in the correlational analyses.

Associations by cohorts

For Cohort 2024, several subscales showed relatively consistent associations across academic outcomes. In particular, Information Processing demonstrated small to medium correlations with multiple outcomes (First-Semester GPA: r = 0.26, p = 0.037, n = 65; pre-clerkship GPA: r = 0.31, p = 0.011, n = 65; COMLEX-USA Level 2: r = 0.24, p = 0.059, n = 64; COMLEX-USA Level 3: r = 0.39, p = 0.003, n = 54).

In contrast, for Cohort 2025, no LASSI subscale demonstrated a consistent association with any of the four academic outcomes. For Cohort 2026, two subscales exhibited small to medium correlations: Selecting Main Ideas (First-Semester GPA: r = 0.13, p = 0.132, n = 129; pre-clerkship GPA: r = 0.17, p = 0.050, n = 129; COMLEX-USA Level 2: r = 0.20, p = 0.026, n = 125) and Time Management (First-Semester GPA: r = 0.22, p = 0.014, n = 129; pre-clerkship GPA: r = 0.18, p = 0.039, n = 129; COMLEX-USA Level 2: r = 0.14, p = 0.110, n = 125). For Cohort 2027, no LASSI subscale showed consistent correlations with either first-semester GPA or pre-clerkship GPA.

When Spearman’s ρ was used to assess the associations between LASSI subscales and GPA outcomes (first-semester GPA and pre-clerkship GPA), which are theoretically more appropriate for ranked data, the results were largely consistent with those obtained using Pearson’s r.

Accounting for pre-medical school factors

To examine factors that might contribute to variation in the associations between LASSI subscales and each academic outcome across cohorts, several pre–medical school variables were included as control variables in partial correlation analyses. Based on prior literature, these factors—gender, MCAT score, and USGPA—have been shown to potentially influence academic outcomes.

The gender composition varied somewhat across Cohorts 2024–2027 (see Table III. Demographics and LASSI subscale means by cohort). However, gender was not significantly correlated with any of the four academic outcomes, either when the data were analyzed across all cohorts combined (rs = − 0.058 to − 0.020; ps = 0.233 to 0.876) or when examined separately by cohort (rs: − 0.19 to 0.01; ps = 0.075–0.981). After controlling for gender, the partial correlations between the four academic outcomes and the LASSI subscales changed only minimally. Analyses repeated separately for each cohort yielded consistent results, indicating that for our data, gender did not significantly influence the correlation between LASSI subscales and academic outcomes.

MCAT Scores. The MCAT scores were statistically significantly correlated with all four academic outcomes (Pearson’s rs = 0.15–0.28, ps = 0.000–0.026). After controlling for MCAT scores, the partial correlations between the LASSI subscales and the four academic outcomes changed slightly relative to the original Pearson’s rs: some became stronger (e.g., Time Management with first-semester GPA increased from 0.11, p = 0.028, to 0.13, p = 0.021, and with pre-clerkship GPA from 0.10, p = 0.036, to 0.13, p = 0.009), whereas others became weaker (e.g., the partial correlation between Selecting Main Ideas and COMLEX-USA Level 2 decreased from 0.16, p = 0.007, to 0.14, p = 0.016).

When examined separately by cohort, the relationships between MCAT scores and the four academic outcomes showed greater variability across both cohorts and academic outcomes, with some correlations reaching statistical significance and others not (Cohort 2024: rs = 0.00–0.14, ps = 0.328–0.980; Cohort 2025: rs = 0.00–0.29, ps = 0.19–0.55; Cohort 2026: rs = 0.12–0.22, ps = 0.013–0.186; Cohort 2027: rs = 0.18–0.19, ps = 0.023–0.035), indicating a more complex pattern of associations between MCAT scores and academic outcomes (see Table IV. Associations between LASSI subscales and academic indicators, grouped by cohort).

After controlling for MCAT scores, the partial correlations between the LASSI subscales and the four academic outcomes changed slightly in most cases, with some associations becoming stronger and others weaker. For example, in Cohort 2026, the correlations between Time Management and all four academic outcomes increased after controlling for MCAT (first-semester GPA: r from 0.22, p = 0.014 to 0.27, p = 0.002; pre-clerkship GPA: r from 0.18, p = 0.039 to 0.22, p = 0.011); COMLEX-USA Level 2: r from 0.14, p = 0.110 to 0.20, p = 0.028). Similarly, in Cohort 2027, the partial correlations between Motivation and GPA were higher than the original Pearson’s rs (first-semester GPA: r from 0.15, p = 0.070 to 0.17, p = 0.047; pre-clerkship GPA: r from 0.15, p = 0.083 to 0.16, p = 0.059).

USGPA. USGPA was significantly correlated with first-semester GPA (r = 0.20, p = 0.000), pre-clerkship GPA (r = 0.24, p = 0.000), and COMLEX-USA Level 2 (r = 0.15, p = 0.014), but not with COMLEX-USA Level 3 (r = 0.06, p = 0.674). After controlling for USGPA, correlations between some LASSI subscales and academic outcomes became stronger. For example, Information Processing with first-semester GPA increased from 0.07 (p = 0.177) to 0.10 (p = 0.050), and with pre-clerkship GPA from 0.09 (p = 0.081) to 0.12 (p = 0.012). Similarly, Selecting Main Ideas increased with first-semester GPA from 0.09 (p = 0.070) to 0.11 (p = 0.022), with pre-clerkship GPA from 0.11 (p = 0.020) to 0.14 (p = 0.003), and with COMLEX-USA Level 2 from 0.16 (p = 0.007) to 0.19 (p = 0.002). In contrast, some correlations weakened; for example, Time Management with first-semester GPA decreased from 0.11 (p = 0.028) to 0.08 (p = 0.085), and with pre-clerkship GPA from 0.10 (p = 0.036) to 0.08 (p = 0.120).

When examined separately by cohort, the partial correlations between the LASSI subscales and the four academic outcomes controlling for USGPA changed slightly in most cases, with some showing substantial increases. For example, in Cohort 2026, the partial correlation for Information Processing increased from 0.05 (p = 0.539) to 0.16 (p = 0.067) with first-semester GPA, from 0.07 (p = 0.444) to 0.20 (p = 0.027) with pre-clerkship GPA, and from 0.04 (p = 0.625) to 0.12 (p = 0.197) with COMLEX-USA Level 2. Similarly, the partial correlation for Selecting Main Ideas increased from 0.13 (p = 0.132) to 0.21 (p = 0.019) with first-semester GPA, from 0.17 (p = 0.050) to 0.27 (p = 0.002) with pre-clerkship GPA, and from 0.20 (p = 0.026) to 0.26 (p = 0.004) with COMLEX-USA Level 2.

Discussion

Sample homogeneity

The homogeneity of the sample may have reduced the observed correlations between LASSI subscales and academic outcomes. None of the three subscales with the smallest variances—Attitude, Motivation, and Test Strategies—showed statistically significant associations with any academic outcomes. This finding contrasts with meta-analytic evidence from 158 studies indicating that Motivation and Test Strategies typically demonstrate the strongest associations with academic outcomes [20].

Internal grades vs. standardized exam scores

Although prior research has reported varying strengths of association between LASSI subscales and academic performance depending on whether performance was defined using internal grades (e.g., course grades or GPA) [1, 5, 13–15] or standardized examination scores (e.g., shelf exams or licensure examinations) [5, 13–19], the current study revealed relatively consistent patterns across different academic outcomes. Certain subscales—specifically Information Processing, Selecting Main Ideas, and Time Management—showed stronger correlations across all four academic performance measures used in this study. These results suggest that learning strategies influence the academic performance of medical students regardless of whether outcomes are represented by internal grades or standardized assessments.

Pre-medical school factors

Based on the data examined, gender does not appear to influence the associations between LASSI subscales and academic outcomes. In contrast, MCAT scores and USGPA can affect these associations in some cases, although the effects vary by cohort and by specific LASSI subscales. MCAT may either strengthen or weaken the relationships, whereas USGPA more often strengthens the associations for certain LASSI subscales. These findings indicate that pre-matriculation academic performance can influence the observed correlations between LASSI subscales and academic performance in medical school. In general, after accounting for pre-matriculation academic performance, learning skills such as Information Processing and Selecting Main Ideas, and self-regulation such as Time Management show stronger associations with medical school performance.

Additional factors

Even after controlling for pre-medical school factors such as gender, MCAT scores, and undergraduate science GPA (USGPA), the results continued to vary by cohort. This suggests that differences between cohorts cannot be fully explained by these factors alone and that additional variables may also influence the observed associations. Several factors may contribute to this variability.

Timing of administration. The one-time administration of the LASSI is a study limitation. The LASSI was administered during orientation for each cohort, but the timing varied somewhat from year to year, and students did not complete the LASSI again later in medical school to assess changes in their study strategies. As a result, differences in the timing of LASSI administration may partially account for variability across cohorts.

Cohort characteristics. The cohort-specific analyses were exploratory. Cohort variability reflects contextual factors such as cohort composition, curriculum structure, and instructional approach. For example, Cohort 2024 experienced a fully virtual curriculum during their first year of medical school, which may have influenced how students reflected on their learning strategies when completing the LASSI survey.

Statistical methods. The statistical methods used to examine these associations may also affect the results. Prior studies have employed a range of analytic approaches, including ANOVA, Pearson’s correlations, and multiple regression analyses, which can lead to differences in conclusions and interpretations.

Normative data. Finally, because the LASSI has not yet been normed on medical students, there is no established baseline profile for this population, which further limits interpretation.

Conclusions and future research implications

Results from this study support established evidence in medical education research indicating that pre-matriculation academic metrics, including MCAT scores and undergraduate science GPA, are predictive of medical school and licensure exam performance [1–4, 5]. The findings highlight the importance of using the LASSI as a formative, practical tool to inform proactive, programmatic planning for academic support and intervention. The LASSI appears to be most beneficial when interpreted by learning specialists at both the individual and cohort levels, rather than for individual-level predictions of academic performance, such as determining whether students are at risk. The consistent associations observed for Selecting Main Ideas, Information Processing, and Time Management indicate that these learning strategies domains warrant focused instructional attention, as prior studies have linked these subscales to preclinical performance [5] and board or board-style examination outcomes among medical and health-profession students [13–15, 17, 18]. Rather than relying primarily on remediation after academic difficulty occurs, practitioners can use early LASSI data to inform targeted workshops, individualized coaching, and course-embedded instruction that explicitly teaches students how to process large volumes of information, prioritize core concepts, and structure study time effectively, aligning with evidence that self-regulated learning behaviors and study habits are central to medical student achievement [6, 7, 9]. The cohort-level variability observed in this study further suggests that academic support programming should remain adaptive, with aggregated LASSI profiles guiding the focus of orientation programming and ongoing support initiatives rather than assuming uniform learning needs across classes [1]. Evidence that formal instruction in learning strategies has been associated with stronger performance on LASSI subscales reinforces the importance of intentionally teaching these skills within the curriculum rather than assuming students will develop them independently [12]. The LASSI does not appear to yield an exact pattern of subscale outcome correlations. Instead, the findings highlight the role of LASSI-informed practice in delivering timely, targeted, adaptive, and scalable academic support.
