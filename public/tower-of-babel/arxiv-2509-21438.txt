Holographic codes and bulk RG flows
Xi Dong, Donald Marolf, and Pratik Rath

Abstract
We consider the coarse-graining of holographic quantum error correcting codes under a generalized notion of bulk renormalization-group flow. In particular, we study the renormalization under this flow of the A / 4 ​ G A/4G term in the Faulkner-Lewkowycz-Maldacena formula and in its Rényi generalization. This provides a general quantum code perspective on the arguments of Susskind and Uglum. Specifically, given a ‘UV’ code with two-sided recovery and appropriately flat entanglement spectrum together with a set of ‘seed’ states in the UV code, we explicitly construct an ‘IR’ code with corresponding properties which contains the given seed states and is of minimal size in a sense we describe.

Introduction

It is now well-established that an appropriate semiclassical bulk limit of the AdS/CFT bulk-to-boundary map can be thought of as a quantum error correcting code
Almheiri:2014lwa
; see e.g.
Jafferis:2015del
;
Dong:2016eik
;
Harlow:2016vwg
;
Kamal:2019skn
. This idea has thus become an integral part of modern discussions of holography, and in fact forms the basis of certain proposals
Akers:2022qdl
;
Harlow:2025pvj
for understanding the physics of black hole interiors and closed cosmologies.

Here we explore the behavior of holographic codes under renormalization-group-like transformations in which a bulk Hilbert space is first defined at some ultraviolet (UV) scale and then truncated to a smaller Hilbert space that might, for example, allow rather general infrared (IR) behavior while requiring the UV degrees of freedom to be in a local vacuum state. Susskind and Uglum
Susskind:1994sm
proposed that changes in renormalization-group scale are associated with changes in both Newton’s constant
G
and the von Neumann entropy
S_{vN}
of certain subsystems such that the generalized entropy
S_{gen}=\frac{A}{4G}+S_{vN}
remains invariant, where
A
is the area of an appropriate surface bounding the desired subsystem. Related issues regarding quantum error correction in AdS/CFT were recently explored in
Gesteau:2023hbq
, which demonstrated a setup where the boundary entropy computed using the Faulkner-Lewkowycz-Maldacena (FLM) formula
Faulkner:2013ana
is invariant under renormalization-group (RG) flow.
1
1

1

See also
Furuya:2020tzv
for discussion of the renormalization group as an error correcting code in contexts without complementary recovery.

Our analysis will go beyond
Gesteau:2023hbq
in several ways. The first is that we will analyze settings where the bulk algebras recovered by our codes have non-trivial centers in both the UV and the IR. This allows us to treat the area
A
in the FLM formula as an operator as is natural in gravitational theories
Faulkner:2013ana
;
Harlow:2016vwg
. In general, we will find these centers to change significantly under our RG flow. Secondly, a special feature of holographic codes is that they possess an (approximately) flat entanglement spectrum so that they satisfy a Rényi generalization of the FLM formula
Dong:2018seb
;
Akers:2018fow
;
Dong:2019piw
. Our RG flow is designed to ensure that, when this property holds in the UV, it also remains true in the IR. Finally, whereas
Gesteau:2023hbq
assumed the existence of a sequence of codes that are related by RG flow, we will explicitly construct such codes given a set of UV ‘seed’ states that are required to remain in the IR Hilbert space.

Since we will largely work from a code perspective, it is natural to consider very general contexts in which the coarse-graining that defines the IR degrees of freedom need not be strictly local. As a result, we will think of the resulting flow as a renormalization of the full geometric entropy operator
\sigma=\frac{A}{4G}+\cdots
(where dots include higher-derivative corrections), rather than simply renormalizing the local coupling
G
.

The above goals are motivated in part because we wish to use our RG transformation in forthcoming works
JLMS
;
ModFlow
to study modular flow while avoiding the kind of failures of the Jafferis-Lewkowycz-Maldacena-Suh (JLMS) formula
Jafferis:2015del
found in
Kudler-Flam:2022jwd
. As will be explained in
JLMS
, such failures are fundamentally associated with small eigenvalues for the density matrix of a bulk subregion. Because UV Hilbert spaces tend to be very large, small eigenvalues are difficult to avoid in a UV description. Flowing to an IR description in terms of a smaller Hilbert space will thus make such issues easier to control.

We will treat the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
of our UV holographic code as being finite-dimensional. This means that we impose both UV and IR cutoffs in the bulk, and that we also impose a cutoff on the amplitude of any (bosonic) excitation about some reference state (perhaps a classical background). We will use the term ‘holographic code’ to mean a quantum error-correcting code with complementary recovery (which we will also sometimes call ‘two-sided recovery’)
Harlow:2016vwg
and flat entanglement spectrum in the sense of
Dong:2018seb
;
Akers:2018fow
;
Dong:2019piw
.
For simplicity, up until section
5
we will assume these two properties to be exact in our UV holographic code.
Of course, at finite
G
the AdS/CFT dictionary is known to only approximately have these two properties. But it will simplify our presentation to postpone explicit treatment of such approximations until the final discussion in section
5
. By that time it will be clear that our treatment of the exact case carries over verbatim. This is because our main construction takes as input only the UV bulk Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{\text{UV}}
(and a set of ‘seed’ states therein) and then constructs an IR bulk Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
whose embedding into
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
satisfies exact two-sided recovery. As a result, the IR bulk-to-boundary map simply inherits any small deviations from exact two-sided recovery in the UV bulk-to-boundary map. Similar statements will apply to approximate flatness of the entanglement spectrum.

We begin in section
1.1
with a more technical overview of our results and an outline of our methods. We then describe our construction in the particularly simple case in which we specify only a single seed state. This is done in section
2
. As we will see, choosing this state to be the only state in
{\cal H}_{\text{IR}}
does not generally lead to a code with an approximate flat entanglement spectrum; additional states will typically need to be added as well, which we will do by chopping the seed state into pieces defined by eigenvalue windows of its boundary modular Hamiltonian.

We then proceed in section
3
to the general construction where we choose an arbitrary set of seed states to be included in the IR code. We will first construct the smallest possible space that contains the given seed states and whose embedding into
{\cal H}_{\text{UV}}
defines a code with perfect two-sided recovery. The states in that space will again be further chopped into pieces defined by appropriate eigenvalue windows, constructing our desired IR code with a degree of flatness for its entanglement spectrum set by the sizes of these windows (and which becomes very flat when the windows become small).

Section
4
provides illustrative examples showing both how our construction can lead to a small
{\cal H}_{\text{IR}}
and how, for certain choices of seed states, no coarse-graining of the UV code will preserve exact two-sided recovery. In particular, in the second example the smallest allowed IR code will in fact have
{\cal H}_{\text{IR}}={\cal H}_{\text{UV}}
.
We then close with some final discussion in section
5
.

A simple example of the IR code

Here we consider a simple example where we choose only a single seed
|\chi\rangle
that we require to be included in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
.
One may think of this example as modeling the case where we follow the bulk RG flow to the deep IR, so that almost all quantum fluctuations have been integrated out. As stated in the introduction, here and in section
3

5

Our goal is to find what is, in some sense, the ‘smallest’
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
that contains
|\chi\rangle
and satisfies an exact FLM formula (
4
) as well as an approximate version of its Rényi generalizations (
7

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
to be the one-dimensional Hilbert space containing only
|\chi\rangle

4

is the reduced density operator of
|\chi\rangle
. However, we would then generally have difficulty satisfying the Rényi FLM formula (
7
) since the entanglement spectrum of
\chi_{u}
generically fails to be approximately flat.

To proceed, we note that, as a state in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}{\mathcal{H}}_{u}^{\alpha}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{u}}^{\alpha}
, our

has a Schmidt decomposition of the form

|\chi\rangle=\bigoplus_{\alpha}\sum_{i}c_{\alpha i}|i\rangle_{u}^{\alpha}|i\rangle_{\bar{u}}^{\alpha},

(8)

where
c_{\alpha i}\neq 0
by convention (i.e., we keep only nonzero terms in the sum), and
\{|i\rangle_{u}^{\alpha}\}
,
\{|i\rangle_{\bar{u}}^{\alpha}\}
are orthonormal sets in
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}^{\alpha}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{u}}^{\alpha}
, respectively. The corresponding reduced density operator
\chi_{u}
in
{\mathcal{A}}_{u}
is

\chi_{u}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}\sum_{i}|c_{\alpha i}|^{2}|i\rangle_{u}^{\alpha}\langle i|_{u}^{\alpha},

(9)

and we define the associated UV bulk modular Hamiltonian to be
K_{\chi_{u}}:=-\log\chi_{u}
. On the other hand, since
A_{\text{UV}}
is an operator in the center
{\mathcal{Z}}_{u}
of
{\mathcal{A}}_{u}

A_{\text{UV}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}\mathbbm{1}^{\alpha},

(10)

where

is a c-number and

is the identity operator on the
\alpha

\chi_{u}
(viewed as a subspace of

A_{\text{UV}}+K_{\chi_{u}}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}\sum_{i}\lambda_{\alpha i}|i\rangle_{u}^{\alpha}\langle i|_{u}^{\alpha},

(11)

\lambda_{\alpha i}:=A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}-\log|c_{\alpha i}|^{2}.

A helpful way of understanding these

We now chop the state

2.1
FLM formula

It remains to show that the FLM and Rényi FLM formulas hold in the IR with some appropriate

Let us first consider the simple case where there is only one

Since

We may thus compensate for the change of the bulk entropy by ‘renormalizing’ the area operator via

In the more general case with multiple

3
A general construction

In the previous section, we considered the simple example of starting with a single state in the IR. We might call it the ‘seed’ state which we used to construct

Now we provide a general construction of

As in section

The construction has two steps, which we now outline briefly:

Step 1:

Construct a
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}^{\text{pre}}\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
that contains the seed states, has a decomposition

(43)

and satisfies “complementary recovery” with respect to
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
in the sense that the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}_{r}^{\text{pre}}:=\bigoplus_{\beta}{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{r}^{\beta})
can be recovered exactly from
{\mathcal{A}}_{u}
(meaning that every operator

{\mathcal{O}}_{u}\in{\mathcal{A}}_{u}

{\mathcal{A}}_{\bar{u}}

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}^{\text{pre}}

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}^{\text{pre}}

Step 2:

Construct
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
as an extension of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}^{\text{pre}}
by including appropriate “fixed-area” truncations of states therein.

The above two steps will be described in detail in subsections

3.2

We now construct the desired extension

Let us first note that, for each
\beta

{\mathcal{H}}_{o{\bar{o}}}^{\beta}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}{\mathcal{H}}_{o}^{\alpha\beta}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{o}}^{\alpha\beta},

{\mathcal{H}}_{o}^{\alpha\beta}

\chi_{o}^{\beta}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}\sum_{i}|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}\,|i\rangle_{o}^{\alpha\beta}\langle i|_{\bar{o}}^{\alpha\beta},

Recall that

For each

3.4
Rényi FLM formula

Recall that the Rényi FLM formula in the UV takes the general form (
23
), which we repeat here for the convenience of the reader:

e^{(1-n)S_{n}(\widetilde{\rho}_{B})}=\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}^{n}e^{(1-n)\left[A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}+S_{n}(\rho_{u}^{\alpha})\right]},

(104)

where again
p_{\alpha}
,
\rho_{u}^{\alpha}
are defined by
\rho_{u}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\rho_{u}^{\alpha}
and
\operatorname{tr}\rho_{u}^{\alpha}=1
. As noted earlier, this section assumes (
104
) to be exact in the UV code.

We now show that the IR Rényi FLM formula (
25
) holds approximately for any IR state
\rho
:

e^{(1-n)S_{n}(\widetilde{\rho}_{B})}=\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}^{n}e^{(1-n)\left[A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}+S_{n}(\rho_{r}^{\mu})\right]}\left[1+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right].

(105)

Here we used the simpler notation
\epsilon_{\mu}:=\epsilon_{\gamma}^{\beta}
, and
p_{\mu}
,
\rho_{r}^{\mu}
are again defined by
\rho_{r}=\bigoplus_{\mu}p_{\mu}\rho_{r}^{\mu}
and
\operatorname{tr}\rho_{r}^{\mu}=1
.

To proceed, we wish to show that for any state
\rho
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
,

\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}^{n}e^{(1-n)\left[A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}+S_{n}(\rho_{u}^{\alpha})\right]}=\sum_{\mu}p_{\mu}^{n}e^{(1-n)\left[A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}+S_{n}(\rho_{r}^{\mu})\right]}\left[1+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right],

(106)

which is equivalent to

\sum_{\alpha}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\operatorname{tr}_{u}\left(p_{\alpha}\rho_{u}^{\alpha}\right)^{n}=\sum_{\mu}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}}\operatorname{tr}_{r}\left(p_{\mu}\rho_{r}^{\mu}\right)^{n}\left[1+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right].

(107)

To establish (
107
), we first note that the density operator
\chi_{o}^{\mu}
as an element of
{\mathcal{A}}_{o}^{\mu}:=\bigoplus_{\alpha}{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{o}^{\alpha\mu})
can be decomposed as
\chi_{o}^{\mu}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}
, where
\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}
does not generally have trace
1
. Thus for any state
\rho
in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
, its UV reduced state (
84
) can be written as

\rho_{u}=\bigoplus_{\alpha,\mu}p_{\mu}\rho_{r}^{\mu}\otimes\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}.

(108)

Comparing this with
\rho_{u}=\bigoplus_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}\rho_{u}^{\alpha}
, we find

p_{\alpha}\rho_{u}^{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{\mu}p_{\mu}\rho_{r}^{\mu}\otimes\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}.

(109)

As a result, we find an exact equality:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\operatorname{tr}_{u}\left(p_{\alpha}\rho_{u}^{\alpha}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\operatorname{tr}_{u}\left(\bigoplus_{\mu}p_{\mu}\rho_{r}^{\mu}\otimes\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}\right)^{n}

(110)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha,\mu}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\operatorname{tr}_{o}\left(\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}\right)^{n}\operatorname{tr}_{r}\left(p_{\mu}\rho_{r}^{\mu}\right)^{n}.

(111)

To complete the derivation of (
107
), we need only show

\sum_{\alpha}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\operatorname{tr}_{o}\left(\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}\right)^{n}=e^{(1-n)A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}}\left[1+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right].

(112)

We proceed by using (
75
) to find an explicit expression for
\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}
:

\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}=\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\beta\gamma}=\frac{1}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}\sum_{i:\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\in I_{\gamma}^{\beta}}|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}\,|i\rangle_{o}^{\alpha\beta}\langle i|_{o}^{\alpha\beta}.

(113)

Using this result, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\operatorname{tr}_{o}\left(\hat{\chi}_{o}^{\alpha\mu}\right)^{n}

(114)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i:\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\in I_{\gamma}^{\beta}}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{UV}}^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}\right)^{n}

(115)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i:\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\in I_{\gamma}^{\beta}}\frac{|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}\left(\frac{e^{-\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}}}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}\right)^{n-1}

(116)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i:\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\in I_{\gamma}^{\beta}}\frac{|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}e^{(1-n)A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}}e^{(n-1)\left(A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}-\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}-\log|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)}

(117)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e^{(1-n)A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i:\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\in I_{\gamma}^{\beta}}\frac{|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}\left[1+(n-1)\left(A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}-\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}-\log|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}\right)+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right]

(118)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e^{(1-n)A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{i:\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}\in I_{\gamma}^{\beta}}\frac{|c_{\alpha i}^{\beta}|^{2}}{|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}}\left[1+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right]

(119)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e^{(1-n)A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}}\left[1+O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}\right)\right],

(120)

where on the third line we used (
74
), on the fifth line we kept the linear term exact and for higher-order terms we used
A_{\text{IR}}^{\mu}-\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}-\log|N_{\gamma}^{\beta}|^{2}=O(\epsilon_{\mu})
due to
\lambda_{\alpha i}^{\beta}=\lambda_{\gamma}^{\beta}+O(\epsilon_{\mu})
and (
100
), on the sixth line we used (
98
) and (
76
) to remove the linear term, and on the final line we used (
76
) again. Here we have kept
|n-1|^{2}
in the error bound because at the moment we allow
n
to be an arbitrary complex number.

We have thus established (
107
). Our desired IR Rényi FLM formula (
105
) then holds to the extent that the UV version (
104
) holds (i.e., the IR formula inherits any additional errors in the UV formula if it is approximate). The relative errors shown in (
105
) are of order
|n-1|^{2}\epsilon_{\mu}^{2}
for each
\mu
term; these errors to the Rényi FLM were introduced because the entanglement spectra within the eigenvalue windows are only approximately flat. So long as all window sizes
\epsilon_{\mu}
are chosen to be upper bounded by some
\epsilon
(and
n
is real
16
16

16

For complex
n
, there can be cancellations among the formally-leading terms for different values of
\mu
so that the final relative error is larger. However, such cancellations require fine tuning. The relative error will thus remain
O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon^{2}\right)
for generic cases; e.g., in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
with the IR state held fixed and with
n
fixed to a generic complex value.
), we find that (
105
) holds up to an
O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon^{2}\right)
relative error. In such cases, upon taking the logarithm of (
105

S_{n}(\widetilde{\rho}_{B})
up to an additive error of size
\frac{1}{|n-1|}\,O\left(|n-1|^{2}\epsilon^{2}\right)

As in section
2

) that we derived in the previous subsection.

4
Examples

Here we apply the general construction of section
3
to two examples. They are more nontrivial than the single-seed-state example of section
2
and in particular will demonstrate cases where Step 1 of the construction is nontrivial and produces an
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{pre}}_{\text{IR}}
that is strictly larger than
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}
.

Before proceeding, we prove the following lemma about
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
to simplify some of our analysis later.

Lemma 3
.

Assume as before that
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
is finite-dimensional. For any two density operators
\rho
,
\check{\rho}
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}
with
\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{u})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})
, the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
defined by (
50
) contains
\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}
. The same statement holds for
\rho=|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|
with any two potentially distinct states
|\psi_{1}\rangle
,
|\psi_{2}\rangle
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}
(but where
\check{\rho}
remains Hermitian).
Here again the inverse of a Hermitian operator
{\mathcal{O}}
is defined to be the standard inverse on
\operatorname{supp}({\mathcal{O}})
and to annihilate its kernel, so that
{\mathcal{O}}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}={\mathcal{O}}{\mathcal{O}}^{-1}=P_{\operatorname{supp}({\mathcal{O}})}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho
be either a density operator on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}
, or
|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|
with any two states
|\psi_{1}\rangle
,
|\psi_{2}\rangle
in
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}
. In both cases, it satisfies
\rho_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
(where
\sigma
is the maximally mixed state on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}
). In the latter case, this is because
|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{2}|
can be written as a linear combination of density operators.

Let
{{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}}^{\prime}
be the commutant of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
in
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}})
. By the von Neumann bicommutant theorem, it suffices to prove that
\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}
commutes with every
{\mathcal{O}}\in{{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}}^{\prime}
.
To do so, we note that such an
{\mathcal{O}}
by definition commutes with
\rho_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1},\check{\rho}_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
and find

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{O}}\rho_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}\sigma_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}=\rho_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}\sigma_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}=\rho_{u}P_{\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})}\sigma_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}\sigma_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}

(121)

\displaystyle=\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}\check{\rho}_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}\sigma_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}=\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}\check{\rho}_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}\sigma_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}=\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}\check{\rho}_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}

(122)

\displaystyle=\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}}P_{\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})}=\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}P_{\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})}{\mathcal{O}}=\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}{\mathcal{O}},

(123)

where in the last step of the first line we used
\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{u})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})
, and in going to the last line we used
P_{\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
(and thus it commutes with
{\mathcal{O}}
); to see this, note that
\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})
is the range of
\check{\rho}_{u}
, which is the range of
\check{\rho}_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}
(because
\operatorname{supp}(\rho_{u})\subset\operatorname{supp}(\check{\rho}_{u})
), which in turn is the range of a Hermitian operator
\check{\rho}_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1}(\check{\rho}_{u}\sigma_{u}^{-1})^{\dagger}
in
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
, and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
must contain the projection onto this range (as it contains all spectral projections).
∎

We will now choose two seed states in

(125)

where the first (second)

{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
factor in

(

If we were to choose the second seed state as

4.2
Two seed states without a common
|\chi\rangle

We now choose two seed states that do not precisely contain any common
|\chi\rangle

(and thus also
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}

{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{u}}

Before proceeding, we prove another useful lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
be two
N
-dimensional diagonal matrices with real, non-degenerate, but otherwise arbitrary diagonal elements. Let
U
be any
N
-dimensional unitary matrix whose matrix elements are all nonzero. Let
{\mathcal{O}}=D_{1}+iUD_{2}U^{\dagger}
. Then

\operatorname{vN}\{{\mathcal{O}}\}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N}).

(131)

Proof.

The von Neumann algebra
\operatorname{vN}\{{\mathcal{O}}\}
generated by
{\mathcal{O}}
obviously contains
D_{1}
and
UD_{2}U^{\dagger}
. Since these are both Hermitian with non-degenerate eigenvalues,
\operatorname{vN}\{{\mathcal{O}}\}
must contain the projection onto each of their eigenvectors (as it contains all spectral projections). Let
\{|j\rangle\}
be the orthonormal basis in which
D_{1}
is diagonal. Then
\{|\widetilde{k}\rangle=U|k\rangle\}
is an orthonormal basis that diagonalizes
UD_{2}U^{\dagger}
. Thus
\operatorname{vN}\{{\mathcal{O}}\}
contains all projections
|j\rangle\langle j|
and
|\widetilde{k}\rangle\langle\widetilde{k}|
. It must therefore also contain

|j\rangle\langle j|\widetilde{k}\rangle\langle\widetilde{k}|j^{\prime}\rangle\langle j^{\prime}|=U_{jk}(U_{j^{\prime}k})^{*}|j\rangle\langle j^{\prime}|,

(132)

and thus also contain
|j\rangle\langle j^{\prime}|
for all
j
,
j^{\prime}
(as the matrix elements of
U
are all nonzero). These operators obviously generate the entire matrix algebra
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathbb{C}}^{N})
.
∎

A special choice of a unitary matrix
U
without any zero matrix elements is the discrete Fourier transform matrix:

U_{jk}=\frac{e^{-2\pi ijk/N}}{\sqrt{N}},

(133)

although there are many other such matrices (and in fact they are generic among all unitary matrices).

We now return to presenting our example. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}={\mathcal{H}}_{u}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{u}}
where
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{u}}
have the same (arbitrary) dimension
N
. We choose the first seed state
|\psi_{1}\rangle
to be any state in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
whose reduced density operator
\check{\rho}_{u}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
has full rank. We choose the second seed state to be

|\psi_{2}\rangle=\left[\left({\mathbbm{1}}_{u}+\epsilon{\mathcal{O}}\right)\otimes{\mathbbm{1}}_{\bar{u}}\right]|\psi_{1}\rangle,

(134)

where
\epsilon
is any nonzero complex number and
{\mathcal{O}}
is an operator on
{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
whose matrix representation in some chosen orthonormal basis is given by
{\mathcal{O}}=D_{1}+iUD_{2}U^{\dagger}
with matrices
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
,
U
as in Lemma
4
. Using
Lemma
3
with
\rho=|\psi_{2}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|
,
\check{\rho}=|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|
tells us that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
contains

\rho_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}=(\mathbbm{1}_{u}+\epsilon{\mathcal{O}})\check{\rho}_{u}\check{\rho}_{u}^{-1}=\mathbbm{1}_{u}+\epsilon{\mathcal{O}}.

(135)

According to Lemma
4
, this operator must generate the entire
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{u})
, giving immediately

{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{u}).

(136)

Acting with this
{\mathcal{A}}^{\text{seed}}_{u}
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{seed}}

\check{\rho}_{u}
having full rank means that
|\psi_{1}\rangle
is a so-called cyclic vector for
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{u})
, it is then a standard result that this action gives

{\mathcal{H}}^{\text{pre}}_{\text{IR}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}},

(137)

and thus
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
as well. Our construction did not find any common
|\chi\rangle
part among the seed states to integrate out, so it simply gives back the original
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
. Note that this is true even if we take the two seed states to be very close to each other (e.g., by making

{\mathcal{H}}_{u}
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\bar{u}}
have arbitrarily large dimensions.

5
Discussion

Our work above provided an explicit construction taking as input a ‘UV’ code with exact two-sided recovery and flat entanglement spectrum, together with a set of seed states. The output of our construction was then an ‘IR’ code with two-sided recovery and (to some given approximation) flat entanglement spectrum. We think of this as modeling the action of bulk renormalization-group flow on holographic codes. However, in the limit in which the UV cutoff is removed one may also think of our RG flow as directly describing the relation between the full underlying dual CFT and the bulk effective field theory at an appropriate IR scale. Our construction identified the smallest possible subspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
which allows two-sided recovery and then ‘chopped’ those states into pieces associated with small windows of eigenvalues for appropriately defined code modular Hamiltonians (see footnote
5
), with the size of the windows controlling the approximation to which the resulting IR code has flat entanglement spectrum.

As advertised in the introduction, our construction allows non-trivial centers in both the UV and the IR. This is more general than the framework considered in
Gesteau:2023hbq
. Furthermore, in our construction the IR centers generally have no simple relation to those in the UV. A possible alternative construction is to bin the eigenvalues separately within each
\alpha
-sector of the UV code. In that case, one would obtain an RG flow where there is a natural inclusion of the UV central operators in the center of the IR algebra.

As noted previously, another important way in which our work differs from
Gesteau:2023hbq
is that we provided an algorithmic construction of an IR code starting from the UV code given a set of states preserved by the RG flow, whereas
Gesteau:2023hbq
assumed the existence of such a sequence of nested codes. Given such a sequence, one could, of course, use our idea of binning eigenvalues to construct a truly holographic code with an approximately flat entanglement spectrum. However, we also note that
Gesteau:2023hbq
generalized their result to allow infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, whereas we worked with finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces for simplicity. It would be useful to attempt to merge these approaches in the future to extend our results to infinite-dimensional cases.

It is natural to use our construction to help control analyses in contexts where small eigenvalues of reduced density matrices are problematic. In particular, as pointed out in
Kudler-Flam:2022jwd
, the JLMS relation can fail dramatically in such contexts. Recall then that on a UV Hilbert space of dimension
D_{\text{UV}}
, the smallest eigenvalues of a density matrix is necessarily of size
1/D_{\text{UV}}
or smaller, which can indeed be very small in the far UV. For states that can be described by an IR Hilbert space of dimension
D_{\text{IR}}\ll D_{\text{UV}}
, the associated issues are then much reduced. In general, this will reflect the fact that while small eigenvalues
\lambda
may lead to large effects (perhaps through factors of
\log\lambda
or
\lambda^{-1}
), these effects can then be canceled by additional factors of
\lambda
that may arise when a given eigenvalue is common to
all
states being studied (e.g., to all states that appear in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
).

However, as illustrated by the second example in section
4
, some choices of seed states can in fact preclude any reduction in the size of the code Hilbert space (no matter how large the eigenvalue windows are). In such cases, our construction gives
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{UV}}
and thus offers little help in ameliorating the above concerns. One may also be concerned that, even when it provides small code Hilbert spaces of small dimension (where a small code means a superselection sector in the IR), the construction given here can lead to a very large number of IR superselection sectors (especially when the eigenvalue windows are taken to be very small so that each small code has very flat entanglement spectrum). It seems likely that our construction can be further improved (in the sense of reducing the size of
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{IR}}
and, in particular, the number of IR superselection sectors) by allowing additional errors in the IR FLM and Rényi-FLM formulas and, in particular, by allowing two-sided recovery (of the IR-to-UV map) to hold only in an approximate sense. Indeed, we will show in
ModFlow
that such improvements are quite useful when studying the exponentiated JLMS relation between bulk and boundary modular flows for semiclassical states. Nevertheless, we leave for future work any thorough study of this idea and of the associated trade-offs between code size and code precision.

On the other hand, it is important to comment here on settings where we are given a UV code that satisfies two-sided reconstruction and flatness of the entanglement spectrum only up to some approximation. In particular, in AdS/CFT we expect two-sided recovery to become imperfect at the order in the bulk Newton’s constant
G
where one can no longer neglect quantum fluctuations in the relevant quantum extremal surface. In addition, taking the entanglement-spectrum of the UV code to be exactly flat appears to require projecting onto an exact eigenvalue of the area operator, which would then involve physics far outside the regime of semiclassical control; see related discussions in e.g.
Marolf:2018ldl
;
Dong:2019piw
;
Dong:2022ilf
.

Let us therefore suppose that the UV FLM formula (
2
) and its Rényi analogue hold only up to some error bounds
\epsilon_{FLM}
and
\epsilon_{FLM}(n)
.
Recall that our construction proceeded entirely in the bulk, so that we were able to both define the IR code and to relate it to the UV code without relying on the UV (Rényi) FLM formulas for any Rényi index
n
. Indeed, the final result was simply to equate the right-hand-side of the UV FLM formula with the right-hand-side of the IR FLM formula (and similarly for the Rényi generalizations up to an error set by the widths of our eigenvalue windows). Repeating the arguments in the presence of non-zero
\epsilon_{FLM}
and
\epsilon_{FLM}(n)
then immediately gives an IR FLM formula with the same error bound
\epsilon_{FLM}
, as well as
an IR Rényi FLM formula up to error bounds that are increased by
\epsilon_{FLM}(n)
relative to those in section
3
. With this understanding, our analysis can be applied directly to the holographic bulk-to-boundary map at finite UV cutoff with finite-but-small bulk Newton’s constant
G
. We will make use of this as part of our study of modular flows in AdS/CFT in
ModFlow
.
