The impact of monetary incentives and gamification on glucose levels: real-world assessment in people with diabetes
Authors: Mohammed E Al-Sofiani, Sahar K Alharthi, Saud M Albalawi, Erfan M Allababidi, Olayan Alharbi, Amir Raouf, Suliman Alblowi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractKaraz is a mobile health solution designed to leverage real-time monitoring, monetary incentives, and gamification to improve health outcomes in people with diabetes (PWD). Data generated by wearables are all displayed on a unified interface for users and healthcare professionals. Karaz app rewards users for engaging in health-promoting behaviors by offering points redeemable for monetary value. To evaluate the changes in continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics, level of physical activity, and sleep duration over three months among PWD who used Karaz app for ≥ 3 months. Data from 384 Karaz app users who had diabetes, were wearing CGM, and used Karaz app for ≥ 3 months were retrospectively analyzed. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify potential predictors of improvement in time in range (TIR) after using Karaz app. The average age of the study participants was 24 years, 57% women, and 78% had type 2 diabetes. In PWD who had baseline TIR ≤ 60%, CGM metrics improved from baseline to month 3: TIR (43.1 to 45.89%, p < 0.01), time above range (TAR) > 250 mg/dL (24.66 to 22.5%, p < 0.01), TAR > 180 mg/dL (51.83 to 48.79%, p < 0.01), glycemia risk index (GRI) (87 to 84%, p < 0.01), and time in tight range (TITR) 70–140 mg/dL (25.54 to 27.84%, p < 0.01). Whereas, in PWD with baseline TIR > 60%, glucose levels changed from baseline to month 3 as follows: TIR (74.88 to 73.53%, p < 0.01), TAR > 250 (4.03 to 4.81%, p = 0.03), TAR > 180 (20.19 to 21.24%, p = 0.07), TITR (52.63 to 52.04%, p = 0.29). Compared to PWD with baseline TIR > 70%, those with baseline TIR 40–70% and TIR < 40% were 10 and 8 times, respectively, as likely to experience a significant improvement in TIR, by > 5%, after adjusting for age, gender, baseline TBR, and daily steps. Use of Karaz app, with monetary incentives and gamification, was associated with significant improvements in CGM metrics in PWD who had suboptimal glucose control at baseline. Introduction

Diabetes is associated with staggering and growing health and economic burden throughout the world; and it remains a leading cause of death particularly in developing countries1–3. The Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region has the highest prevalence of people with diabetes (PWD) worldwide; with approximately 73 million individuals living with diabetes within the region. The regional prevalence of diabetes is projected to further increase to 95 million by 2030 and 136 million by 2045. Saudi Arabia, the second largest country in the MENA region, has one of the highest prevalence rates of diabetes in adults aged 20–79 years (17.7%)3. Unfortunately, most people living with diabetes in the MENA region, and globally, have glucose levels that are above target1,4–6. Such high prevalence of poorly controlled glucose has led to significant health and economic burden. Diabetes-related health expenditures are not limited to the costs of medications, medical supplies, clinic visits, and treatment of diabetes complications; but also include indirect costs (such as increased absenteeism, premature mortality, family burden, etc.)1,2,5.

The role of behaviors and diabetes self-management is critical in diabetes management and often determines the long-term outcomes of any therapeutic interventions. According to the science of behavioral economics, individual decision-making is often influenced by economical and psychological factors. People tend to favor immediate costs (i.e. behaviors) and reward (i.e. benefits) over those that may occur in the future. In the context of diabetes management, the “present-biased” preferences may make PWD adopt more appealing, yet unhealthy behaviors, despite knowing their negative long-term consequences7. This phenomenon reflects the human tendency to prioritize immediate gratification over delayed potential benefits. Therefore, linking healthy behaviors with short-term rewards can be an effective strategy to overcome this phenomenon. Such an approach to diabetes management is theoretically promising but needs to be studied in the real-world setting8.

Mobile health (mHealth) emerged as a potential tool to empower PWD and support them with real-time monitoring, personalized education, and easy access to healthcare professionals (HCPs), especially when complemented with telehealth9,10. However, the patients’ behavioral fatigue and drop-off remain some of the major barriers to wide utilization of mHealth in diabetes management. Without consistent motivation and perceived value, users often stop using the mHealth tool after a while11,12. Karaz app is a comprehensive smart health solution designed to leverage the power of real-time monitoring, short-term rewards, and tiny habit changes by utilizing the integration of wearable-generated health data [e.g. continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics, insulin doses, daily steps, sleep duration, body weight and composition, etc.] into a unified interface for users and HCPs. Moreover, Karaz app rewards users for health-promoting behaviors with cherries (i.e. Karaz points) that are convertible to currency in the form of discount vouchers. In addition, it offers gamification experience for users as they compete within the Karaz community to collect the highest number of cherries and have their accomplishment celebrated on the app homepage at the end of each day. To maximize users’ engagement and tiny habit changes, Karaz points are rewarded on daily basis.

Up to our knowledge, the association between monetary intervention using mHealth and comprehensive glucose evaluation using CGM has not been examined in PWD. In this study, we aim to examine a) the association between instant financial incentives for health-promoting behaviors and changes in CGM metrics over three months and b) identify predictors of improvement in glycemic control with such approach using the Karaz app in PWD in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a retrospective secondary analysis of existing data from 384 Karaz app users who had diabetes, consented to share their data for research purposes, and were already using CGM for at least three months during the year 2024. A total of 856 Karaz app users who had any logged data during the first quarter of 2024 were initially identified. Of these, 151 users were excluded due to the absence of any glucose data recorded during that period. Of the remaining 705 users, additional 321 users were excluded because they were using blood glucose monitors rather than CGM devices. This resulted in a final analytic sample of 384 users who utilized CGM and logged data consistently for three consecutive months in 2024 (Fig. 1). All data were passively collected through the Karaz app via users’ connected wearables, such as CGM devices, and stored securely on the platform’s encrypted servers. Only de-identified data from users who had previously consented to research use were included in this analysis. Karaz app users were asked if they wanted to share their Karaz data for research purposes when they signed up to use the app. Only those who agreed to share their data for research purposes were included in this study. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of College of Medicine at King Saud University.

Karaz gamification and monetary incentives

Karaz app aims to improve the glucose control and overall health of PWD via different approaches including a) gamification experience and b) positive monetary interventions. Karaz app rewards users with Karaz points for health-promoting behaviors that they adopt and for diabetes-related health goals that they accomplish on a daily basis. These cherries are convertible to currency in the form of discount vouchers and sometimes cash. The vouchers can be then used to buy healthy snacks, diabetes digital solutions, and other products offered through the Karaz online store. For example, Karaz users are rewarded with Karaz points when they: a) achieve target glucose levels (e.g. 5 points when glucose is 70–120 mg/dL and 3 points when glucose is 121–180 mg/dL); b) walk more (e.g. 250 points for ≥ 10,000 steps/day); c) sleep more (e.g. 50 points for sleep ≥ 8 h/day); d) drink more water (e.g. 50 points for drinking ≥ 10 cups of water/day); e) or log in their food intake (e.g. 50 points for each meal logged in) and/or medications (e.g. 50 points for each medication logged in). Karaz points are rewarded on a daily basis to stimulate positive reinforcement of tiny healthy habits and maximize the user’s adherence to health-promoting behaviors.

Moreover, Karaz users earn a Karaz badge when they meet any of the listed goals for seven consecutive days. The badge is displayed on the user’s profile and is visible to all members in Karaz community. Furthermore, Karaz users compete with one another to collect the highest number of cherries per day. The top three scorers are announced every day, and their usernames are displayed on the “Karaz Leader Board” on the app’s homepage.

Outcomes and covariates

We analyzed sociodemographic data (age and gender), health-related data (type of diabetes and CGM metrics), and behavioral data (average daily steps, sleep duration, and number of cherries “reward points” gained). The following CGM metrics were evaluated in all the study participants both at baseline and month 3: Average glucose, coefficient of variation (CV), glucose management indicator (GMI), TIR (time in range 70–180 mg/dL), TITR (time in tight range 70–140 mg/dL), TAR > 180 (time above range > 180 mg/dL), TAR > 250 (time above range > 250 mg/dL), TBR < 70 (time below range < 70 mg/dL), TBR < 54 (time below range < 54 mg/dL), and GRI (glycemia risk index). The GRI is a relatively recent composite metric derived from CGM data13. It places greater emphasis on extreme hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia compared with other CGM metrics. It yields a single score from 0 to 100 that reflects the overall quality of glycemic control. Higher GRI values indicate greater glycemic risk. In contrast, GMI offers an estimate of average glucose, expressed in the same units as hemoglobin A1C (%), and approximates long-term glycemic control in real time.

In addition, we analyzed the data stratified by baseline TIR (> 60% vs ≤ 60%) and examined the changes in CGM metrics and other outcomes from baseline to month 3 within each group. The 60% TIR threshold was chosen because it is the baseline median TIR in our study. This approach allows us to evaluate differences in outcomes relative to the central tendency of our study population and ensures sufficient group sizes for comparative analyses.

Statistical analysis

All significance testing was 2-tailed with α of 0.05, and data were analyzed using Stata Statistical Software (release 15). Variable distributions were examined using Shapiro–Wilk test and visual examination of histograms. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs) and were compared between groups using student t-test. Whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages; and were compared between groups using contingency table arrays and the χ2 statistic. The changes in CGM metrics within each study subgroup over the three months period were examined using paired t-test. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify potential predictors of achieving clinically significant improvement in TIR of > 5%. The associations between the predictors and outcomes are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We present the ORs before and after adjusting for potential confounders including age, gender, baseline TBR and TIR, and baseline average daily steps. To isolate the impact of app engagement on glycemic outcomes, we controlled for baseline CGM metrics that are known predictors of follow-up glycemic control. Specifically, baseline TIR and baseline TBR were included as covariates in the regression models. We also controlled for average daily step count as a proxy for physical activity, given its known association with glycemic control and its consistent availability across Karaz app users. Other behavioral variables such as sleep duration or water intake were not included in the regression model due to incomplete or insufficient data availability in our dataset.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 856 Karaz users who had any logged data during the first quarter of 2024, 384 users met the study inclusion criteria (detailed in the Methods section) and were included in the final analysis. Approximately 57% of the study participants were women and 78% had type 2 diabetes. The average (SD) age of the study participants was 23.64 (9.85) years. At baseline, the average TIR was 58.74%, TBR < 70 was 5%, TBR < 54 was 1.95%, TAR > 180 was 36.26%, TAR > 250 was 14.51%, TITR was 38.88%, GRI was 63, and glucose CV was 38.12%. The baseline average daily steps were 3274 steps and average daily sleep duration was 368.07 min (i.e., 6 h and 13 min). When stratified by baseline TIR (> 60% vs ≤ 60%), those with higher TIR were slightly older and had significantly lower TBR < 54; higher TITR; and lower TAR > 180, TAR > 250, GRI, and glucose CV. Moreover, those with higher baseline TIR had greater average of daily steps, albeit, not statistically significant, sleep duration, and number of cherries earned per day compared to those with lower TIR at baseline (Table 1).

Changes in CGM metrics and other health outcomes over three months

Karaz users with baseline TIR ≤ 60% had statistically significant improvements in the following CGM metrics from baseline to month 3: [TIR: 43.10 to 45.89% (p < 0.01), TAR > 180: 51.83 to 48.79% (p < 0.01), TAR > 250: 24.66 to 22.50% (p < 0.01), TITR: 25.54 to 27.84% (p < 0.01), and GRI: 87 to 84 (p < 0.01)]. In contrast, those with baseline TIR > 60% did not experience statistically significant changes in CGM metrics from baseline to month 3 except for the following: [TIR: 74.88 to 73.53% (p = 0.01), TAR > 250: 4.03 to 4.81% (p = 0.03), and GRI: 39 to 42 (p < 0.01)]. No significant changes were noted in the average number of daily steps nor sleep duration from baseline to month 3 in either group (Table 2). Both groups, however, had a significant increase in the number of cherries earned per day from baseline to month 3.

Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses

In the unadjusted model, Karaz users with baseline TIR < 40% were eight times as likely to achieve the clinically meaningful improvement in TIR by > 5% over three months compared to those with baseline TIR > 70% [OR, 95% CI: 7.61 (3.36 – 17.21)]. This association remained the same even after adjusting for age, gender, baseline TBR, and baseline number of steps per day. Similarly, those with baseline TIR of 40–70% were four times as likely to achieve a significant improvement in TIR over three months compared to those with baseline TIR > 70% [OR, 95% CI: 4.09 (1.93 – 8.70)]. This association became stronger after adjusting for potential confounders [OR, 95% CI: 10.25 (2.78 – 37.86)] (Table 4).

Discussion

Among the 384 individuals with diabetes who used Karaz app for at least 3 months, significant improvements in CGM metrics were noted in those with baseline suboptimal glucose control (i.e. baseline TIR ≤ 60% and/or average glucose > 183 mg/dL). The improvements in CGM metrics included improvements in TIR, TITR, TAR > 180, TAR > 250, GRI; but not TBR nor glucose CV. The improvement in these metrics were both clinically and statistically significant and may reflect the impact of positive monetary reinforcers (Karaz points) and gamification experience (The Karaz highest scorer competition). Users’ characteristics independently associated with clinically meaningful improvement in TIR included baseline TIR < 70%, which represents the level of glucose control in most people with diabetes in Saudi Arabia and worldwide14,15. While it is less clear how to interpret the slight worsening of TAR > 250 and GRI in those with baseline TIR > 60%, this group was generally more physically active at baseline and month 3 than those with TIR ≤ 60%. It is possible that those with higher baseline TIR may have intentionally chosen to keep their glucose levels at slightly higher levels than those less physically active to minimize risk of hypoglycemia, maximize TIR, and earn more cherries for staying longer within TIR.

Previous studies exploring the impact of monetary incentives on glucose control among people with diabetes are few and limited to monitoring hemoglobin A1C as the glycemic metric of interest. Our study is the first to examine the impact of monetary incentives and gamification on CGM metrics. A recent systematic review of 13 studies that examined the effect of monetary incentives on hemoglobin A1C in people with type 2 diabetes revealed that 10, out of the 13, studies showed significant and clinically meaningful improvement in A1C after implementing monetary incentives8. However, most of these studies incorporated multicomponent interventions alongside monetary incentives such as skill training, diabetes education, telemonitoring, and/or text messaging. In contrast, our study did not provide any education, training, or telemonitoring by HCPs beyond what users may have already received from their treating HCPs. The role of Karaz app was limited to gamification and monetary incentives aimed at encouraging health-promoting behaviors and achieving health-related goals. It is possible that a multicomponent intervention strategy might have resulted in greater improvement in glycemic control.

The differentiated impact of Karaz app on glucose control by baseline glucose levels is similar to that commonly seen with other therapeutic interventions. For instance, among CGM users, the improvement in glucose levels is often more remarkable in those with lower baseline TIR than those with higher TIR16,17. Likewise, higher hemoglobin A1C at baseline is a predictor of greater improvement in glucose levels in those starting new glucose-lowering medications such as insulin, GLP-1RA, or others. This can be explained by regression to the mean and by having more room for glucose improvement in those with high baseline glucose. Moreover, the average number of daily steps was inversely associated with the average glucose levels at baseline. However, the number of daily steps did not significantly change from baseline to month 3 in any of the groups. Furthermore, all groups had a significant increase in the number of cherries earned per day regardless of the baseline glucose. This pattern reflects the high level of engagement with the app and suggests that reward-seeking behaviors may have played a central role in the observed improvement in glucose control in our study. Moreover, this finding highlights the promising role of mHealth, when combined with monetary incentives and gamification, in improving patients’ adherence to the use of diabetes digital tools.

Limitations

Our study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. We did not have data on the level of education, income, and other sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants. As a result, we were unable to assess how social and economic characteristics may have influenced the users’ engagement with the app nor their response to incentives. Furthermore, we did not have data about the glucose-lowering medications used by the study participants and whether those medications were changed during the study period. In addition, our study participants were self-selected users of the Karaz app who were using CGM and were actively engaged with the platform and earning cherries throughout the study. As a result, there is a potential for selection bias and survivorship bias that may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of PWD including those with less motivation and lower digital engagement. Furthermore, the app’s gamification features, such as rewards and engagement incentives, may have influenced user behavior and adherence in ways that differ from other platforms or traditional care settings. Therefore, our findings may not be directly applicable to non-gamified digital health interventions.

Nonetheless, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the impact of an mHealth tool, that utilizes monetary incentives and gamification to improve users’ engagement, on CGM metrics and other health-related behaviors among PWD. Future studies are needed to examine the impact of Karaz app use on glucose control in those who do not use CGM. In addition, studies with richer sociodemographic data are needed to further investigate the interaction between sociodemographic characteristics and impact of monetary incentives on behaviors and glycemic control.

Conclusion

Despite advances in diabetes therapeutics and digital health, most people with diabetes have suboptimal glucose levels. This has further increased the health and economic burden of diabetes in the MENA region and worldwide. The utilization of mHealth tools along with monetary incentives and gamification to encourage health-promoting behaviors and achievement of diabetes-related goals is an innovative and promising approach to diabetes management. Our findings suggest that adding monetary incentives and gamification experience to mHealth tools improve glucose control in PWD who have suboptimal glucose control. Further studies are needed to determine the long-term outcomes of such approach to diabetes management.
