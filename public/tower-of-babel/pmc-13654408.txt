The Thrombus We Leave Behind
Authors: Bahjat Ghazzal, Ziyad Ghazzal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: aspiration thrombectomy, intracoronary imaging, optical coherence tomography, primary percutaneous coronary intervention, residual thrombus, risk stratification, ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, thrombus aspiration

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remains the cornerstone of reperfusion therapy in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), aiming at restoring patency of the infarct-related artery. However, reestablishing epicardial flow is not equivalent to eliminating culprit thrombus, which can still embolize distally and injure the microcirculation. Although manual aspiration thrombectomy initially generated enthusiasm for its potential to improve epicardial flow and myocardial reperfusion, subsequent large randomized trials tempered routine use because of inconsistent clinical benefit1,2 and, in TOTAL (A Trial of routine aspiration ThrOmbecTomy with PCI vs. PCI ALone in patients with STEMI), a signal for harm in the form of stroke.2,3 Routine aspiration is accordingly no longer recommended,4 and thrombectomy is now reserved for selective use, particularly in patients with a high thrombus burden—even though a prespecified subgroup analysis of TOTAL found no benefit in that population, while confirming that high thrombus burden itself remains an important predictor of outcome.5 In that setting, the key question is no longer whether aspiration should be performed, but whether it meaningfully reduces residual thrombotic burden enough to improve downstream outcomes.

Crucially, performing aspiration and achieving complete thrombus clearance are not synonymous. Data from a post hoc analysis of TOTAL showed that a high residual thrombus burden after thrombectomy is directly associated with an increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including cardiovascular death.6 In that study, however, residual thrombus burden was quantified primarily by 2-dimensional angiographic assessment using the standard TIMI thrombus grade. Whether precise, high-resolution volumetric assessment of residual thrombus independently determines prognosis remains an open question.

The Present Study

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Endo et al7 address this clinically relevant and underexplored question by using optical coherence tomography to quantify residual thrombus after aspiration thrombectomy in patients with STEMI undergoing primary PCI. In this single-center retrospective study, 233 patients with STEMI treated within 12 hours of symptom onset underwent manual aspiration thrombectomy followed by optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging before stent implantation. Residual thrombus volume was quantified at 1.0-mm intervals along the culprit segment, and the optimal cutoff for predicting major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) was derived using maximally selected log-rank statistics. The primary endpoint was a composite of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or hospitalization for heart failure, assessed over a median follow-up of 2.5 years.

MACE occurred in 46 patients (19.7%), and the optimal cutoff for residual thrombus volume predicting MACE was 4.14 mm3. Patients with residual thrombus volume of at least 4.14 mm3 had a significantly higher cumulative incidence of MACE than those below this threshold (32.9% vs 13.8%; log-rank P = 0.0018). This association persisted after multivariable adjustment, both as a continuous variable (adjusted HR: 1.014 per 1 mm3 increase; 95% CI: 1.001-1.027; P = 0.031) and as a categorical variable using the 4.14 mm3 cutoff (adjusted HR: 2.95; 95% CI: 1.51-5.74; P = 0.002). Patients with a high residual thrombus burden also had worse myocardial blush grade, suggesting impaired microvascular perfusion despite successful epicardial recanalization.8,9 Plaque morphology, by contrast, did not differ between the high- and low-thrombus groups, suggesting that the prognostic signal was not simply a reflection of underlying plaque characteristics.

Why Residual Thrombus May Matter

The contribution of this work is 2-fold. First, it provides a quantitative, imaging-based framework for assessing the adequacy of thrombectomy rather than relying on angiographic appearance alone. Second, it reinforces the concept that residual thrombus is not inert. Mechanistically, thrombus remaining in the infarct-related artery can embolize distally during wiring, ballooning, or stent implantation, obstructing the microcirculation and contributing to no-reflow, infarct extension, and adverse remodeling.9,10 Residual thrombus also may sustain local platelet activation, thrombin generation, and inflammatory signaling at the culprit lesion, creating a prothrombotic milieu that could plausibly increase recurrent ischemic events.9 These pathways provide a biologically coherent explanation for the observed association with MACE.

Several strengths of the analysis deserve emphasis in this context. The study uses OCT, which offers high spatial resolution and allows more precise characterization and quantification of residual thrombus than angiography or conventional clinical judgment. The authors also performed multivariable adjustment and internal validation, and thrombus measurement reproducibility was good (intraclass correlation coefficient: 0.88). Most importantly, the findings are clinically intuitive and consistent with previous observations that thrombus burden and impaired reperfusion are linked to worse outcomes.6,8,10, 11, 12 The data help bridge the gap between procedural imaging findings and prognosis, strengthening the argument that residual thrombus has adverse clinical consequences.

What the Study Cannot Tell Us

At the same time, the study has important limitations. Its findings are primarily limited by the retrospective, single-center design, which introduces potential selection bias by restricting the cohort to patients who successfully underwent both aspiration thrombectomy and high-quality OCT imaging. In addition, the 4.14 mm3 residual thrombus cutoff was derived mathematically from a relatively small cohort (N = 233), making it exploratory until validated in prospective external studies. Finally, although residual thrombus volume was an independent predictor of MACE, adding it to established clinical risk factors yielded only a minimal improvement in overall prognostic discrimination (Harrell's C-index: 0.728-0.731).

More importantly, we do not know how patients in the study were managed after OCT. Were adjunctive pharmacotherapies intensified? Was additional aspiration performed in some cases? Were glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, potent P2Y12 inhibition, anticoagulation adjustments, low-dose intracoronary thrombolytics, intracoronary vasodilators, or deferred strategies used selectively?13 Without this information, the optimal response to residual thrombus in the catheterization laboratory remains uncertain. Nor can the study determine whether residual thrombus is a causal mediator of adverse events or merely a marker of more severe thrombotic biology, larger infarcts, or more complex lesions.6

From Observation to Action

From a practical standpoint, these findings support a more deliberate intraprocedural strategy when substantial residual thrombus is present. Options may include reassessing lesion preparation, considering repeat or more extensive aspiration in selected cases, optimizing antithrombotic therapy, and paying meticulous attention to distal embolization risk during stent deployment. In carefully selected high-thrombus cases, operators may also consider deferred stenting or adjunctive imaging-guided decision-making, although definitive evidence for these approaches remains limited. The broader message is that residual thrombus should prompt active management rather than passive acceptance.

Confirming that message will require prospective, multicenter studies that define imaging-guided thresholds for action and test whether thrombus reduction translates into improved clinical outcomes. Randomized strategies could evaluate intensified antithrombotic regimens, novel catheter-based thrombectomy systems, embolic protection approaches, or hybrid pharmaco-mechanical protocols in patients with persistent thrombus after initial aspiration.13 In addition, serial intracoronary imaging may help clarify whether residual thrombus resolves spontaneously, persists despite therapy, or predicts late thrombotic complications. Ultimately, the field needs to move from describing residual thrombus to proving which interventions, if any, can safely and effectively modify its prognostic impact.

Endo et al7 provide an important imaging-based signal that residual thrombus after aspiration thrombectomy is prognostically relevant in STEMI. Their study does not revive the era of routine aspiration, but it does sharpen our focus on the real target: not aspiration as a procedure, but effective thrombus removal as a biologic and clinical goal. For interventionalists confronting thrombotic STEMI, the study is a timely reminder that what remains behind may matter as much as what is removed.
