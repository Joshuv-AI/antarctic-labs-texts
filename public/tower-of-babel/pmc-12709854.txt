The experience of social isolation among community-dwelling older adults: a qualitative study
Authors: Mengfan He, Shitong Guo, Xiang Sun, Yi Yu, Ling Li, Jinhua Zhang, Shiguang Wang, Shuya Li, Peng Wang, Lin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial isolation is a major problem for older adults. Increasing evidence suggests that perceived social isolation is linked to adverse physical and mental health outcomes among older adults. There is still a lack of qualitative interviews with Chinese community-dwelling older adults regarding social isolation. Guided by the Social Ecosystem Theory, this study aimed to explore community-dwelling older adults’ experiences of social isolation, the reasons why social isolation occurred, and the help they wished to receive.MethodsThis research was a descriptive qualitative study based on semi-structured face-to-face interviews, conducted in Zhengzhou, China. Used maximum variation and purposive sampling, 16 older adults were recruited from five communities. The qualitative study was guided by Husserl’s descriptive phenomenology and followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines. Interviews were transcribed verbatim, and data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method with the support of the qualitative data analysis software NVivo 12.0.ResultsA total of 16 interviews were completed. Five themes were identified: 1) Experiences of Social Isolation; 2)Micro systems: Personal factors that produce social isolation; 3)Mezzo systems: Social support network factors that produce social isolation; 4)Macro systems: Environmental factors that produce social isolation; 5)Desired assistance.ConclusionsCommunity-dwelling older adults faced various challenges associated with social isolation. Social isolation in community-dwelling older adults stems from interconnected personal, familial, and environmental factors, often leading to negative emotional experiences. Multistakeholder collaboration—engaging individuals, families, communities, and policymakers—can help reduce social isolation and support emotional well-being.Keywords: Social isolation, Community-dwelling older adults, Qualitative, Community

Background

The international aging process is accelerating, and it is estimated that by 2030, there will be more than one billion people over 65 years of age [1]. Population aging has become a worldwide phenomenon, and China has one of the highest aging rates. China’s seventh population census shows that there are 190 million elderly people aged 65 and above, accounting for 13.5% of the total population [2]. It is predicted that by 2050, China’s population above 60 years of age will reach 498 million. Since the announcement of the Healthy China 2030 policy by the Chinese government in 2016, promoting healthy aging has become one of the most important goals [3].

Social isolation is defined as the objective state of having few social relationships or infrequent social contact with others [4, 5]. According to research based on the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) database, 34.8% of community-dwelling older adults were reported to experience significant social isolation [6]. Influenced by traditional Chinese culture, older Chinese adults prefer to live in their own homes and neighbourhoods rather than in institutions that provide services for older adults [7]. However, rapid urbanization and legacy policies such as the one-child policy were reported to [8]. Factors such as living alone and empty nesting are associated with a higher likelihood of social isolation among older adults [9, 10]. International agencies such as the World Health Organization (WHO) have identified social isolation as a key social and policy issue in aging [11].

Social isolation significantly impacts the physical and mental health, as well as the quality of life, of older adults. Research suggests that social isolation is associated with the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system. This physiological state is further linked to increased vascular tension and processes such as oxidative stress, which may promote atherosclerosis. These pathways are considered potential mechanisms in the development of cardiovascular conditions, including coronary heart disease and stroke [12, 13]. In addition, social isolation is associated with a higher risk of depression, cognitive decline, and various chronic diseases such as dementia and cancer among older adults [14, 15]. It also correlated with slower recovery from illness and higher rates of comorbidities, disability, and rehospitalization [16, 17]. In conclusion, social isolation has emerged as a critical global public health issue with implications for the physical and mental well-being of older adults.

While social isolation represents a universal risk in aging societies, its manifestations and contributing factors are shaped by socio-cultural context [18]. In China, a distinctive feature is the contrast between enduring cultural expectations of family cohesion and the increasing prevalence of older adults living apart from their children [19, 20]. This situation may create a particular psychological and social dissonance for older adults. Furthermore, community life is largely structured by a state-led governance model. The presence of state-supported community centers and the involvement of neighborhood committees in organizing social activities establish a distinct environment for social interaction [21]. The functions of state-led community organizations, such as residents’ committees, and the characteristics of community-based care in China differ from Western models of social services, resulting in different frameworks for intervention and support [21]. Currently, research on social isolation among older persons is dominated by quantitative studies, which often focus on prevalence and associated factors [16, 22, 23]. Compared with quantitative research, qualitative research can provide a clearer picture of what older people really think and feel, to better analyse and understand the phenomenon of social isolation and loneliness of older people in specific cultural contexts. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct qualitative research on social isolation among community-dwelling older adults in China.

The “Healthy China 2030” initiative emphasizes coordinated efforts at the individual, community, and societal levels to form synergies in maintaining and promoting health, to provide systematic and continuous health services, and to achieve higher health standards for all citizens. Social Ecosystems Theory(SET) is a multidisciplinary theory that studies how individual behaviour is influenced by the social environment and explores the interactions between the individual and the social environment. With full consideration of the proactive agency of community-dwelling older adults, the relevant social ecosystems can be categorized into three types: micro systems, which include biological and psychological factors of older adults; mezzo systems, encompassing family conditions and peer groups; and macro systems, referring to various social support systems along with sociocultural and policy elements. These three systems interact and influence one another. Three systems are interconnected, constrained, and influenced by each other [24]. Currently, this theory has been widely applied in the fields of chronic disease management and health education [25, 26], offering a theoretical framework for understanding individual health behaviors across different levels.

Methods

Aim

Guided by the SET, this study aimed to explore community-dwelling older adults’ experiences of social isolation, the reasons why social isolation occurred, and the help they wished to receive.

Study design and setting

This qualitative study was guided by Husserl’s descriptive phenomenology [27] and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [28]. It explored experiences of social isolation among community-dwelling older adults in Zhengzhou, China (July–October 2023). Phenomenology was chosen to prioritize participants’ subjective experiences through their own narratives, aligning with naturalism [29]. The COREQ guidelines were followed to ensure methodological rigor and reporting transparency. Ethical approval was obtained by Zhengzhou University (ZZUIRB2022-45). And written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Participants

The study was conducted in five communities in Zhengzhou, Henan Province, China. Purposive sampling combined with a maximum variation sampling approach was utilized to ensure maximum diversity in social and demographic status. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 60 years or older; 2) normal reading, comprehension, and expression ability, as evidenced by a score of 8–10 (out of a total of 10) on the 10-item Simple Psychological State Questionnaire (SPMSQ) [30, 31]; 3) living in the community; 4) experiencing social isolation, as evidenced by a score of 0–12(out of a total of 30) on the Lubben social network scale-6 (LSNS-6) [32, 33]. Exclusions were as follows: 1)people with communication difficulties such as impaired consciousness and severe hearing impairment; (2) people with other serious life-threatening diseases; 3)people with physical disability; 4)unwillingness to participate or to complete the interview; 5) a disease in a terminal stage.

Data collection

Semi-structured face-to-face interviews were conducted at community health centers (CHCs) or in participants’ homes. Eligible subjects were initially referred by community nurses. Following referral, potential subjects were contacted and informed about the study’s purpose and confidentiality measures. Subsequently, those who agreed to participate were screened against the pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria. Private interviews were conducted with enrolled participants. If they agreed to participate and allowed us to record them, they were asked to sign an informed consent form. Eligible participants who declined to participate did not have their existing services affected. No non-participants were present during the interviews; no duplicate interviews were conducted. A semi-structured interview guide (Table 1) was used to collect information through face-to-face interviews. Interviews included participants’ demographic characteristics (i.e., gender, age, living status) and social isolation experiences. Each interview lasted between 30 and 45 min, and comprehensive notes were maintained by the interviewers throughout the process.

Interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were then checked against the original recordings to verify accuracy. Sampling continued until data saturation was reached [34, 35]. Following the principle of data saturation, the lead researcher, in consultation with peer researchers, concluded that no new themes or concepts emerged after two consecutive interviews. This resulted in a final sample of 16 community-dwelling older adults.

Date analyses

Transcripts were anonymized (coded N1–N16) and stored securely to ensure confidentiality. Two researchers (the first author and the second author) independently analyzed and coded the same textual data using Colaizzi’s seven-step method, supported by the qualitative data analysis software NVivo 12.0 [36]. Relevant content was systematically coded, categorized, and then simplified. Through iterative discussions, codes were consolidated into categories and refined into emergent themes [37]. These themes were returned to interviewees for feedback before final definition. Any discrepancies in coding were resolved through consensus, and a senior researcher reviewed thematic interpretations for credibility. The lead researcher, in consultation with peer researchers, determined that no new themes or ideas emerged after two consecutive interviews; saturation was confirmed [38].

Reflexivity

Two female investigators with medical backgrounds were trained in qualitative methods. Throughout the study, they maintained reflective awareness of how their backgrounds and ages might influence responses. Following each interview, the researchers conducted a systematic debriefing session to discuss challenges encountered and develop strategies for improvement. Each investigator held master’s-level expertise in qualitative data collection and analysis. Whether interviews took place in homes or healthcare settings, the methodology followed standardized procedures, including uniform interview protocols, qualified interviewers, and consistent ethical principles to minimize potential methodological bias resulting from contextual differences. Any adjustments in interview location were made to accommodate the physical and psychological needs of the older adults or environmental constraints, with no changes to the research protocol or interview content.

Themes

Based on the SET and Colaizzi’s seven-step method, five main themes and fifteen sub-themes were identified (Fig. 1).

Thematic map of themes and sub-themes

Experiences of social isolation

Loneliness

Respondents experiencing social isolation often reported having few friends to converse with, expressing a desire for companionship and close relationships, and describing feelings of social loneliness [39].

“Every night when I am by myself, I feel lonely because I have no one to talk to.“(N1).

“We are really getting old, watching people around us leave one by one, and former friends contacting less and less. It’s sad when I think about it, especially when I’m alone.“(N2).

“After my partner left, I’m now all alone in the house, without even someone to talk to (sigh).“(N3).

The overwhelming sense of having no one to talk to emerges as a central cause of their profound social loneliness.

Inner emptiness

After retirement, the social role of older adults changed. Respondents transitioned from previously busy professional roles to more leisurely family roles, and from a busy working state to a life of inactivity. This made them feel a sense of discrepancy, a sense of inner emptiness, a decrease in social activities, and a decline in willful activities.

“I used to be busy working, and I felt it was nice and fulfilling just to be busy at work. Now, as soon as I’m retired, it’s a bit of an adjustment.“(N1).

“Usually at home watching TV, and there’s not much I want to do, and I don’t know what I can do. Sometimes it really feels like there’s no point in going about the day.“(N5).

“My partner has also left, my legs are not easy, so I don’t want to go and do any activities, I just stay inside my room.“(N7).

The absence of routine and meaningful engagement fosters a pervasive sense of purposelessness, revealing how role dislocation can erode an individual’s sense of self and lead to a state of existential emptiness [40].

Worthlessness

Most respondents described their existence as worthless and meaningless, and they had no choice but to get by, and had negative emotional experiences such as boredom and meaninglessness.

“Now just beg the body do not have any major problems, never drag children …… I’m old and useless, and they don’t tell me what’s going on. “(N2).

“In the past, you might have thought you had to do something for the family. But now that you’re old, who needs you to do anything? Just mind your own business and try not to bother them.“(N6).

“I have this disease, I can’t do anything. What’s the point of living a really old life, like a parasite, it’s not useful at all.“(N9).

“I have been wondering for a long time whether I am too old to be useful. (Emotional)“(N12).

The transition from being viewed as a family pillar to being perceived as a potential burden contributed to a sense of obsolescence among participants. This diminished sense of identity, where their wisdom and care were described as no longer being sought, suggests a potential connection between perceived usefulness and psychological well-being in later life [41].

Micro systems: personal factors that produce social isolation

Anxiety about diseases

The majority of respondents reported having multiple chronic conditions. The decline in physiological function and deficiencies or weaknesses in self-care abilities were associated with reduced social participation and higher levels of social isolation [41]. In addition, the recurring nature of chronic illnesses and extended treatment durations were frequently accompanied by frustration and negative emotions among participants.

“I also have some minor health problems and often have knee pain. I went to see a doctor and said it was rheumatoid arthritis, and sometimes my back hurts when I cook, and I get very anxious when it hurts.“(N1).

“I also have a lot of problems in my body now. I had a brain attack some years ago, and I also have high blood pressure. Every time I think of these, my mood is very bad. I’m still under quite a lot of psychological stress because of my illness, and I need someone to look after me.“(N9).

“I’m too old to go out and participate in many activities because of my age, my legs, and my heart condition. I have to take medicine every day, and the doctor says I have to go to the hospital for a review.“(N12).

These statements collectively highlighted how physical ailments not only imposed direct psychological burdens such as stress and anxiety but also structurally constrained social involvement.

Lack of knowledge and perception of social isolation

Most respondents viewed health primarily in physical terms, with less consideration given to emotional and psychological well-being. They further lacked social isolation awareness and related knowledge resources.

“Not being sick and being able to eat and sleep well is healthy.“(N1).

“How does social isolation affect health? … Being healthy and not sick, and being able to look after yourself.“(N2).

“Haven’t heard of any social isolation, not sure what you mean by that …. I don’t know what happens when social isolation occurs.“(N4).

The narrow conceptualization of health as merely the absence of physical illness, combined with the unfamiliarity regarding social isolation’s impact, suggested that many respondents remained unaware of how psychosocial factors contributed to overall well-being.

Lack of hobbies and interests

Some respondents had altered their lifestyles after retirement. Their lack of hobbies/interests and diminished life fulfillment typically led to social isolation.

“I don’t have any hobbies, and I don’t know what I can do.“(N2).

“I don’t really have any hobbies, I usually just watch TV at home and go out for a walk once in a while.“(N10).

The lack of purposeful activities limited opportunities for social connection. This absence of fulfilling pursuits was associated with feelings of emptiness and was linked to patterns of social withdrawal and isolation [42].

Unwilling to be a burden

A prevalent theme among respondents was the avoidance of being perceived as a burden. Chinese parents prioritized their children’s needs over their own, and often sacrificed activities to avoid inconveniencing families. They similarly resisted becoming societal burdens [43].

“I don’t want my kids to worry about me anymore.“(N2).

“As long as I can go to the hospital by myself, I won’t call my son so as not to affect his work. “(N3).

“My children are quite dutiful, but they are busy, they have to go to work, and they also have their own families, and I don’t want to trouble them.“(N15).

“Just don’t give people a hard time …… embarrassed to let others help, get on the bus, young people give me their seats, I feel especially embarrassed.“(N16).

This self-imposed isolation, motivated by a desire to protect their families and avoid societal inconvenience, paradoxically reinforces their social withdrawal and exacerbates social isolation [44].

Mezzo systems: social support network factors that produce social isolation

Insufficient family support

With socioeconomic development and the trend toward shrinking family structures, intergenerational support resources for older persons gradually weakened. Older adults often lacked companionship and often described feelings of mental emptiness. Additionally, factors included adult children’s frequent absence, infrequent visits, and older adults’ limited proficiency with social media platforms, which resulted in ineffective family support [45].

“I don’t want anything else, I don’t want to talk to my kids about this (feeling unaccompanied), don’t try to influence my kids. “(N2).

“I live at home alone now, and sometimes I miss them terribly, but what can I do? They all have jobs. “(N3).

“My children have all gone away to work, and they are busy with their work. My partner and I usually just watch TV at home and take care of ourselves.“(N4).

The older participants consistently prioritized their children’s well-being and careers over their own emotional and physical needs. This self-imposed silence, rooted in deep-seated cultural values of sacrifice and non-interference, not only perpetuated their loneliness but also masked the severity of their isolation.

Weakened relationships and reduced social interactions

Respondents’ social networks and social interactions diminished during the aging process in conjunction with factors such as illness and environmental constraints. This led to an increased risk of social isolation.

“I used to have a lot of friends, but everyone is getting older and their health is failing; some have passed away, and some are in nursing homes. So there’s no way to communicate with them about a lot of things, and sometimes it’s still a bit lonely when I think about it.“(N14).

The cumulative loss of peers due to health decline, relocation, or death corresponded with a progressively narrowing circle of contacts, which weakened social connectedness and contributed to feelings of loneliness [43].

Macro systems: environmental factors that produce social isolation

Residential environment

A good residential environment was a critical infrastructure for community activities and enhanced mobility for respondents, which mitigated risks of their social isolation.

“There is no lift where I live, so it is not very convenient to go up and down the stairs and go out for activities.” (N15).

“There is no activity centre for the older adults, the greenery downstairs is not good, there is no shade.“(N16).

The absence of essential amenities transforms the living environment from a potential facilitator to a constraint on social engagement, directly contributing to isolation among vulnerable older residents [46].

Public facilities issues

Public facilities were public goods or equipment provided by the government for public enjoyment or use. However, these facilities proved inadequate in facilitating older adults’ social participation and preventing their isolation [47]. Many respondents expressed concerns about crossing roads due to excessive crossing distances and rapid signal changes.

“Crossing the road is a big problem, a lot of roads are very wide, and now there are a lot of cars. You also have to walk faster; the green light is very short. We old people walk slowly, and sometimes when we walk in the middle of the road, the light suddenly turns red, and it’s a problem, and it’s quite dangerous.“(N12).

“The park gate is on the other side of the road, and I am afraid to cross the road. There are a lot of cars, and the drivers drive fast, but I can’t walk fast enough, I can’t walk well.“(N16).

These accounts revealed how urban infrastructure that failed to accommodate aging needs created tangible barriers to social participation. By making basic mobility challenging and unsafe, such design limitations hinder older adults’ access to community life and indirectly contribute to their social isolation.

Desired assistance

Wish to learn about electronic technology products

A cognitively advanced subgroup of respondents valued ongoing education, stressing continuous learning [48]. Interview data further demonstrated their reliance on digital platforms to sustain kinship networks and social ties.

“Now that technology has advanced, I wish they would teach me how to use these smartphones and stuff. But a lot of functions I do not know, I can’t remember when people taught me once, so sometimes there is no way.“(N10).

“I wish I could call more often, send video messages, and make it easier to talk about what’s going on. It’s not as good as meeting and nagging, I guess, but it can ease a bit (of loneliness).“(N4).

The gap between their willingness to adopt digital tools and their actual ability to use them effectively represents a critical challenge in addressing social isolation among older adults.

Need for family support

Family support historically modulated older adults’ psychological states. Participants acknowledged infrequent family visits and consciously avoided imposing burdens while desiring emotional support [49]. The transition into retirement led them to focus more on family life. This was evident in their increased attention to the day-to-day activities within their household and a stronger desire to maintain family bonds.

“Communicate with my children usually mainly through the phone, call only when something happens, sometimes it’s still a bit lonely when I’m alone …… But I care about them here too, they tell me what they are doing and I am happy to know.“(N13).

“I sometimes envy others, you see other people’s children, who will often take the initiative to call their parents. My daughter and I call occasionally, we can’t say more than a few words before hanging up, and I feel bad every time.“(N7).

These accounts revealed the complex emotional dilemma faced by older adults: while deeply desiring meaningful family connection, they simultaneously restrain themselves from expressing this need fully.

Enriching community activities and reconstructing social networks

Post-retirement, older adults frequently experience a reduction in pre-existing social ties and reduced communal engagement [50]. Interview data revealed that respondents primarily engaged in leisure activities alone in their daily lives. However, they also expressed openness to third-party interventions that could encourage social engagement and help rebuild their social networks.

“I hope that the community will organize more activities that people are willing to take part in, and that we will get to know some like-minded people and talk to them.“(N13).

“My community doesn’t have a senior centre and doesn’t hold any activities.“(N14).

“I hope there will be activities that older adults can take part in.“(N12).

These responses suggested a clear readiness among respondents to participate in community activities. The gap between their willingness to participate and the current lack of organized opportunities underscored the importance of developing targeted social interventions to address isolation in later life.

Provision of psychological counselling

Training addressing older adults’ mental health needs had been limited, with healthcare providers predominantly focused on managing somatic symptoms [51]. Respondents expressed a desire for professionals to alleviate their negative emotions and support sustained psychological well-being.

“When people get old, they mainly want to have a psychological relief. My health is not as good as theirs, and when I’m not feeling well, I’m not in a good mood. It would be nice to have someone to talk to at this time.“(N7).

“Sometimes when I feel like I’m going through the day with no fun, I’m prone to ruminating and being in a bad mood, and it would be nice to have someone to help alleviate that.” (N10).

“It feels like the services provided by the occasional community health centre clinic are just measuring blood pressure and blood sugar and things like that, and there’s no kind of psychological counselling.“(N16).

These responses revealed a significant gap between the current predominantly physical healthcare services and the psychological support needs of older adults. The expressed desire for professional emotional assistance underscores the importance of developing dedicated mental health services.

Discussion

The study findings illustrated that social isolation among community-dwelling older adults was closely associated with loneliness, inner emptiness, and worthlessness, which might be linked to further mental health challenges [52–56]. This decline in psychological well-being could further reinforce social isolation among older adults, creating a vicious cycle that increases suicide risk [55, 56]. As reported by Liu L J et al. [57], most older adults were reported to experience depression, anxiety, emptiness, vulnerability, and physical symptoms, including fatigue and insomnia. Existentialist philosophy described social isolation as a significant dimension of human existence [58]. However, within Asian cultural contexts, older adults tend to suppress emotions rather than express them openly, as disclosing problems or seeking help is often viewed as “feeling embarrassed” or as potentially disrupting group harmony [59, 60]. Therefore, older adults should be guided to actively reposition themselves by engaging in low-pressure, health‐appropriate social or volunteer roles. It is essential to encourage older adults to adopt a more open attitude toward acknowledging social isolation and its accompanying psychological distress, while promoting timely expression of needs and active help-seeking behavior.

At the micro system level, it was indicated that poorer self-rated health was associated with higher levels of social isolation among older adults, which might be related to older adults’ negative attitude towards self-aging due to poor health [61] and their poor understanding of their own value [62]; these factors made them reluctant to interact with others. Multiple contributing factors were identified in the qualitative interviews. The burden of chronic diseases, particularly the physical and psychological strain of managing incurable conditions, was observed to affect older adults’ ability to maintain social relationships [63, 64]. Simultaneously, limited awareness of social isolation was reflected in a predominant focus on physical health needs, with less attention to psychosocial well-being, potentially limiting recognition and addressing of isolation risks [16]. Additionally, consistent with other studies on Chinese families, many older adults expressed reluctance to be a burden to their children and hesitated to ask for help unless absolutely necessary-even when ill [65]. When combined with reduced participation in social activities-partly due to health limitations and partly to declining personal interests, elements created self-reinforcing cycles of isolation [66]. Targeted education on loneliness risks should therefore be promoted to encourage active involvement among the elderly. Additionally, community-based initiatives need to be developed around their varied interests. Through sustained participation in these tailored activities and conscious efforts to sustain close relationships, older adults can foster emotional openness, realize meaningful social integration, and finally cultivate a sense of self-worth in their later years.

At the mezzo system level, the findings of this study were consistent with existing literature regarding the role of family factors in social isolation among older adults in the Chinese context [67]. In this cultural setting, where family support remains a primary source of care, factors such as limited family support, weakened intergenerational ties, and the increasing prevalence of empty-nest households were associated with reduced intergenerational interaction. Additionally, some older adults reported concealing difficulties from their children to avoid being perceived as a burden, which was similarly linked to increased social isolation [68]. In contrast, older adults who received robust familial care demonstrated heightened ability to perceive and utilize support systems. Their enhanced support perception increased the likelihood of physical and psychological needs being met, thereby mitigating social isolation risks [69]. Furthermore, existing research demonstrated that peer friendships held significant protective value against social isolation, as higher perceived friend support helped alleviate disease-related anxiety and negative emotional states [70–72]. These findings suggested that building a resilient support network will require intentional effort from both older adults and their social circles. Family members and friends will prioritize consistent, meaningful engagement—such as regular visits or shared activities—while creating inclusive opportunities for involvement in social and familial life. Older adults will be encouraged to express their needs openly, demonstrate receptiveness to intergenerational exchange, and proactively initiate contact. This reciprocal approach will foster emotional bonds within both family and friendship networks, reinforcing a sense of belonging and contributing to reduced social isolation.

At the macro system level, degradation of the community environment was identified as a risk factor for social isolation among older adults [73]. A well-maintained community environment, quality community services, and comprehensive community facilities were associated with a harmonious community atmosphere and were considered to provide a platform for social activity participation among older adults, which could facilitate expanded social relationships and lower levels of social isolation. The planning of appropriate physical environments and the development of age-friendly neighborhoods and societies were identified as important factors in fostering social networks and addressing social isolation risks [74]. Therefore, governments should prioritize older adults’ psychosocial well-being by developing age-friendly policies that encourage social participation. This should include mandating age-friendly community renovations—such as installing elevators, building ramps, and creating accessible public spaces—to facilitate mobility and engagement. Additionally, public initiatives should promote intergenerational interaction and establish diverse activity venues, providing tangible platforms for older adults to maintain social connections and community involvement.

It was indicated that older adults who used the Internet showed lower levels of social isolation, suggesting a potential association between the use of digital communication technologies and reduced social isolation [75]. In addition, some older adults expressed interest in participating in online social activities with guidance, describing such opportunities as addressing certain psychosocial needs and potentially mitigating isolation [76]. Moreover, participants reported a need for psychological counseling services, where professionals could help them manage negative emotions and maintain emotional well-being. However, community-based services tend to focus on the physical aspects of care rather than the social or emotional aspects [51]. Therefore, integrating isolation checks into routine health visits, offering regular health talks, and forming community activity teams to organize peer events and hobby groups. Additionally, training young and older volunteers to visit isolated older adults, providing coaching on friendship-building, and digital skills. Finally, setting up mobile mental health stations where professionals offer accessible counseling in neighborhood settings.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative study provided valuable insights into social isolation by directly capturing the lived experiences, perceived causes, and desired support of community-dwelling older adults in China, where qualitative evidence regarding social isolation remains limited. Guided by the SET, the collection of firsthand accounts provided authentic data that not only deepened the theoretical understanding of isolation within its socio-cultural context but also provided authentic data that established a research foundation for developing culturally tailored interventions for this population.

Although this study contributes to research on social isolation among older adults, several limitations should be noted. The participant sampling and referral strategy may have limited the study’s scope. By recruiting through community and healthcare channels, we likely excluded the most isolated individuals—those without service contact, with severe mobility or cognitive limitations, or with strong institutional distrust. This could result in an underrepresentation of the most vulnerable populations and limit the transferability of findings to the full spectrum of socially isolated older adults. Future studies should employ diversified recruitment strategies, including non-institutional channels, to reach the most isolated older adults who were not reached by this study. Due to the relationship between time and energy, the study was limited to one city in central China, so it has certain limitations. In the future, we can expand the research area to further enrich the results.

Conclusion

Social isolation represents a significant recognized public health concern among older adults, with unique manifestations in the Chinese context that warrant further investigation. This study reveals that social isolation in community-dwelling older adults stems from interconnected personal, familial, and environmental factors, often leading to negative emotional experiences. To address this issue, multistakeholder collaboration-involving individuals, families, communities, and policymakers essential to mitigate isolation and promote emotional well-being. The findings provide a foundation for developing culturally tailored interventions to improve the quality of life for older adults in China.
