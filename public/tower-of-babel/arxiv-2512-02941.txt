Abstract
. While low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes are near capacity-achieving when paired with iterative decoders, these decoders may not output a codeword due to the existence of pseudocodewords. Thus, pseudocodewords have been studied to give insight into the performance of modern decoders including iterative and linear programming decoders. These pseudocodewords are found to be dependent on the parity-check matrix of the code and the particular decoding algorithm used. In this paper, we consider LP decoding, which has been linked to graph cover decoding, providing functions which capture these pseudocodewords. In particular, we analyze the underlying structure of pseudocodewords from quantum stabilizer codes that arise from LP decoding, quasi-cyclic LDPC codes, and spatially-coupled LDPC codes.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin by introducing some standard notation we will use throughout. The set of nonnegative integers is denoted by
\mathbb{N}
. Given a positive integer
t
,
[t]:=\left\{1,\dots,t\right\}
.
\mathbb{F}_{2}
denotes the finite field with two elements, and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
denotes the set of
m\times n
matrices with entries from
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. The all-zero matrix of size
m\times n
is denoted by
{0}_{m\times n}
. For
j\in[m]
,
Row_{j}(A)
denotes the
j^{th}
row of the matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
. Vectors are denoted in boldface, i.e.
\mathbf{x}
. The support of
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is
\text{Supp}({\mathbf{x}})=\left\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq 0\right\}
. A graph is a pair
(V;E)
where
V
is the set of vertices and
E
is the set of edges. Vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
are adjacent, or connected by an edge, if
(v_{1},v_{2})\in E
.

The codes we consider are binary linear codes, which we now define.

Definition 1
.

Let
k
and
n
be positive integers where
k\leq n
. A
binary linear code

C
of length
n
and dimension
k
is a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of dimension
k
. The
minimum distance

d
of
C
is the minimum support size of a nonzero vector in
C
, i.e.

d=\min\{|\text{Supp}(\mathbf{c})|\mid\mathbf{c}\in C\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}\}.

If
n
,
k
, and
d
are known for a code
C
, we call
C
an
[n,k,d]
code.

A matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times{}n}
, where
r\geq n-k
, is called a
parity-check matrix
of
C
if
C
is the null space of
H
. Since a parity-check matrix of a code is not unique, we write
C
as
C(H)
to specifically designate
C
to the matrix
H
. This makes

C(H)=\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:H\mathbf{c}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\}.

The code
C(H)
is called a
low-density parity-check (LDPC) code
if
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
has row weight
O(1)
, meaning few non-zero entries. One may also consider
moderate-density parity-check (MDPC) codes

C(H)
which are given by matrices
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
with row weight
O(\sqrt{n})
.

In this paper, we consider the LP decoder for both classical and quantum codes, which also gives insight into other iterative decoders, most notably graph-based iterative decoders.
Viewing codewords as elements of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the codeword polytope of a binary linear code C is the convex hull of the codewords:

\text{poly}(C)=\left\{\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in C}\lambda_{\mathbf{c}}\mathbf{c}:\lambda_{\mathbf{c}}\in[0,1],\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in C}\lambda_{\mathbf{c}}=1\right\}\subseteq[0,1]^{n},

which depends on the set of codewords (as opposed to the representation of the code). We note that the vertices of
\text{poly}(C)
are precisely the codewords of
C
.

Maximum likelihood (ML) decoding may be described using the codeword polytope as follows; for more details, see
[
20
]
. Given a received word
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
i\in[n]
, let

\gamma_{i}:=-\log\left(\frac{Pr(w_{i}\mid 1)}{Pr(w_{i}\mid 0)}\right)

denote the log likelihood ratio (LLR) associated with position
i
.
Then the ML decoding problem is to find
\mathbf{y}\in C
such that the cost function
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}y_{i}
is minimized, which can be stated as

(1)

\min\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}y_{i}\textnormal{ subject to }y\in\text{poly}(C).

Generally, the solution to the LP in Equation (
1
) will be a vertex of
\text{poly}(C)
and the vertices of
\text{poly}(C)
are precisely the codewords of
C
, so the solution will be a codeword of
C
as desired. However, in general, the description of
\text{poly}(C)
is exponential in
n

[
20
]
. It is this complexity that motivates the relaxed polytope, which in turn invites non-codeword pseudocodewords.

To obtain a more tractable representation, the codeword polytope is relaxed by fixing a particular parity-check matrix
[
20
]
. Let
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}
be a parity-check matrix for the binary linear code
C(H)
. For
j\in[r]
, each row of
H
defines a
codeword polytope of a supercode of
C(H)
:
\text{poly}(C(\text{Row}_{j}(H)))
. Noting that
C=\bigcap_{j=1}^{r}C\left(\text{Row}_{j}(H)\right)
, the relaxed polytope of
C(H)
is

R(H):=\bigcap_{j\in[r]}\text{poly}(C(\text{Row}_{j}(H)))\subseteq[0,1]^{n}

as defined in
[
20
]
. Unlike the codeword polytope
\text{poly}(C)
, the relaxed polytope depends on the choice of parity-check matrix representation. The associated LP, sometimes called the Linear Code Linear Program, may be stated as

(2)

\min\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}y_{i}\textnormal{ subject to }\mathbf{y}\in R(H).

It has been shown that if the optimal solution
\mathbf{y}
satisfies
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then
\mathbf{y}
is the ML codeword
[
20
, Proposition 2]
. Moreover, the description of the relaxed polytope
R(H)
is polynomial in the code length
n

[
20
, Appendix II]
, rather than exponential as is the case for
\text{poly}(C)
.
However, an optimal solution to the LP in (
2
) is a vertex of
R(H)
which might not be a vertex of
\text{poly}(C(H))
, meaning a noncodeword.
Indeed, note that in general

\text{poly}(C(H))\subsetneqq R(H)

inviting the possibility that noncodewords may appear as outputs of the linear program in (
2
) optimizing over
R(H)
. This situation motivates the following definition.

Definition 2
.

An LP pseudocodeword of the code
C(H)
is a vertex of the relaxed polytope
R(H)
.

The original setting for LP decoding using the Linear Code Linear Program is classical. More recently, it was demonstrated in
[
22
]
that there are analogous results for quantum codes through the use of label codes. Among them are the fact that this polytope is the correct one for blockwise MAP decoding in the setting they consider, yet it is intractable. More details will be provided in Section
4.1
.

Alternatively, pseudocodewords can be defined from a graph cover perspective and made precise with the definition of the fundamental cone. A graph cover is defined over a bipartite graph called
Tanner graph
, which is used for iterative decoding algorithms on parity-check codes.

Definition 3
.

Let
C(H)
be the code defined by parity-check matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times n}
. The
Tanner graph

T(H)
of
C
corresponding to
H
is the bipartite graph
T(H)=(V,W;E)
with vertex set
V
corresponding to the
n
columns of
H
and vertex set
W
corresponding to the
m
rows of
H
such that there is an edge between
v_{i}\in V
and
w_{j}\in W
if and only if
H_{j,i}=1
.

From a graphical perspective, the Tanner graph encodes codewords as follows: a vector is assigned to the vertex set
V
. This vector is in
C
if and only if the sum at each node
w\in W
of all assignments of the neighbors of
w
in
V
is equal to
0
modulo
2
.

Iterative decoders based on message-passing algorithms act locally on a Tanner graph in order to find a global solution. This aspect of iterative decoders delivers a great strength – speed – at the cost of optimality. Loosely speaking, iterative graph decoding operates in the following way: a received message (
0
’s and
1
’s, or their likelihoods) from the channel is put onto the variable nodes (sometimes along with a reliability value). Each variable node then sends information to its neighboring check nodes. Each check node now calculates new estimates for the assignments to its neighboring variable nodes and sends this information back. This process continues until either a codeword is obtained or a certain predetermined number of iterations have occurred.

Generally speaking, these algorithms converge to a solution fairly quickly, but the structure of the Tanner graph can sometimes cause iterative decoders to fail or converge to non-optimal solutions. This is caused by the existence of
graph cover pseudocodewords
which, loosely speaking, are solutions to some finite lift of a code’s Tanner graph representation.

Definition 4
.

A finite graph cover of
T(H)
is a bipartite graph
(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup\ldots\cup V_{t},W_{1}\cup W_{2}\cup\ldots\cup W_{t};E)
where
V_{k}
and
W_{k}
are disjoint copies of
V
and
W
respectively for all
k\in[t]
. If there is an edge between
v_{i}\in V
and
w_{j}\in W
(i.e.,
H_{j,i}=1
.), then each copy of
v_{i}
will be adjacent to exactly one copy of
w_{j}
.

Example 5
.

Let the following matrix be a parity-check matrix for a code
C(H)
.

H=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0&1&0&0\\
0&1&1&0&1&0\\
0&0&0&1&1&1\end{bmatrix}

Then the corresponding Tanner graph of the code,
T(H)
, is given in Figure
1
. Check nodes, which correspond to rows in
H
, are denoted by squares, and variable nodes, which correspond to columns of
H
, are denoted by circles, as is convention. Additionally, Figure
2
gives an example of a graph cover of
T(H)
.

.

Now, a graph cover itself can act as a Tanner graph, and graph cover pseudocodewords are essentially codewords of these graph covers. For more background on graph cover pseudocodewords, we refer the interested reader to
[
19
]
or
[
17
]
. For a code
C(H)
with parity-check matrix
H
, we denote the associated set of graph cover pseudocodewords by
\mathcal{P}(H)
. It was shown in
[
17
]
that rational elements of the relaxed polytope,
R(H)\cap\mathbb{Q}^{n}
, are scaled graph cover pseudocodewords. The associated fundamental cone has proven useful in the analysis of these outputs.

Definition 6
.

The fundamental cone of
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}
is

\mathcal{K}(H)=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2h_{ji}v_{i}\ \forall i\in[n],\forall j\in[r]\right\}.

The relationship between the fundamental cone and the set of graph cover pseudocodewords is given in the following result.

Theorem 7
.

[
19
, Theorem 4.4]

Let
H\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}
and
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Then
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
if and only if
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{K}(H)
and
H\mathbf{p}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\mod 2
.

Throughout, we drop the “graph cover” adjective on pseudocodewords when it is clear from the context. We will make use of the fundamental cone and this equivalence to give insights into graph cover pseudocodewords.

3.
Properties of the fundamental cone for general codes

In this section, we develop the key properties of the fundamental cone that will be applied to three classes of parity-check codes in Section
4
and provide key observations on the fundamental cone which will be useful in studying the set
\mathcal{\mathcal{}}{P}(H)
of pseudocodewords of a binary code
C(H)
. We will consider associated generating functions, a direction motivated by the work of Koetter, Li, Vontobel, and Walker
[
19
]
concerning
cycle codes, meaning those in which all bit nodes in the associated Tanner graph
T(H)
have degree
2
. Beginning in
[
30
]
, they associate a normal graph with the parity-check matrix for such a code and then consider the Hashimoto edge zeta function of this normal graph.
This allows for the pseudocodewords of a cycle code to be read off from
the monomials appearing in the power series expansion of an associated rational function. This point of view was carried on in
[
18
]
and
[
31
]
to study the set of pseudocodewords via their generating functions. In particular, the set of pseudocodewords
\mathcal{P}(H)
of a code
C(H)
with parity-check matrix
H
can be captured via the
n
-variate generating function
f_{H}
:

f_{H}({\bf{x}}):=\sum_{\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H)}{\bf{x}}^{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}],

where
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{p}}:=x_{1}^{p_{1}}\dotsm x_{n}^{p_{n}}
for
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}]
.

The following handful of results (Lemma
8
, Theorem
9
, and Theorem
10
) all consider natural ways of combining collections of block matrices into larger parity-check matrices.

Lemma 8
.

Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{t}
be a collection of matrices formed by the rows of
H
, where
H_{\ell}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r_{\ell}\times n}
and
r:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}r_{\ell}
.
The fundamental cone of

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}\\
H_{2}\\
\vdots\\
H_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}

is

\mathcal{K}(H)=\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{t}\mathcal{K}(H_{\ell}).

Proof.

The result follows almost immediately from the definition, since

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(H)

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2h_{ji}v_{i}\ \forall i\in[n],\forall j\in[r]\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{t}\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}\text{Row}_{j}(H_{\ell})\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2(H_{\ell})_{ji}v_{i}\\
\forall i\in[n],\forall j\in[r_{\ell}]\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{\ell=1}^{t}\mathcal{K}(H_{\ell}).

∎

Theorem 9
.

Let

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&0&\dotsm&0\\
0&H_{2}&&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\dotsm&H_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}.

Then:

(1)

R(H)=R(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times R(H_{t})
;

(2)

\mathcal{K}(H)=\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t})
;

(3)

\mathcal{P}(H)=\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})
; and

(4)

the generating function of the pseudocodewords of
C(H)
,
\mathcal{P}(H)
, is given by

f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{x}_{t})=\prod_{k=1}^{t}f_{H_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

Proof.

Assume
H_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r_{i}\times n_{i}}
so that
r=\sum_{i=1}^{t}r_{i}
and
n=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_{i}
. We define
L_{k}
to be the
k
th block row of
H
. Set
n_{k}^{(0)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}n_{\ell}
and
n_{k}^{(1)}=\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{t}n_{\ell}
. Then we can write
L_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{r_{k}\times n_{k}^{(0)}}&H_{k}&0_{r_{k}\times n_{k}^{(1)}}\end{bmatrix}
.
Now,

R(H)=\bigcap_{k\in[t]}\bigcap_{j\in[r_{k}]}\text{poly}(\text{Row}_{j}(L_{k})).

Since
\bigcap_{j\in[r_{k}]}\text{poly}(\text{Row}_{j}(L_{k}))=\mathbf{0}_{n_{k}^{(0)}}\times R(H_{k})\times\mathbf{0}_{n_{k}^{(1)}}
, 1) readily follows. We can also see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(L_{k})

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\text{Row}_{j}(L_{k})\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2(L_{k})_{ji}v_{i}\ \forall i\in[n],\forall j\in[r_{k}]\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n}:\begin{array}[]{l}\text{Row}_{j}(0_{r_{i}\times n_{k}^{(0)}})\cdot(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n_{k}^{(0)}})+\\
\text{Row}_{j}(H_{k})\cdot(v_{n_{k}^{(0)}+1},\dots,v_{n_{k}^{(0)}+n_{k}})\\
+\text{Row}_{j}(0_{r_{i}\times n_{k}^{(1)}})\cdot(v_{n_{k}^{(0)}+n_{k}+1},\dotsc,v_{n})\geq 2(L_{k})_{ji}v_{i}\\
\forall i\in[n],\forall j\in[r_{k}]\end{array}\right\}

Because
\text{Row}_{j}(0_{r_{i}\times n_{k}^{(0)}})\cdot(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n_{k}^{(0)}})=0
and
\text{Row}_{j}(0_{r_{i}\times n_{k}^{(1)}})\cdot(v_{n_{k}^{(0)}+n_{k}+1},\dotsc,v_{n})=0
for all
j
and for any choices of
(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n_{k}^{(0)}})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{k}^{(0)}}
and
(v_{n_{k}^{(0)}+n_{k}+1},\dotsc,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{k}^{(1)}}
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(L_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{k}^{(0)}}\times\mathcal{K}(H_{k})\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{k}^{(1)}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{1}}\times\dotsm\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{k-1}}\times\mathcal{K}(H_{k})\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{k+1}}\times\dotsm\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n_{t}}.

By Lemma
8
, 2) holds since

\mathcal{K}(H)=\bigcap_{k=1}^{t}\mathcal{K}(L_{k})=\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t}).

Let
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
. Then using 2) and Theorem
7
,
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{K}(H)=\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t})
and
H\mathbf{p}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\mod 2
. We can write
\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{p}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{p}_{t})
where
\mathbf{p}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{r_{k}}
. From this decomposition, it is clear that
\mathbf{p}_{k}\in\mathcal{K}(H_{k})
. Because
H\mathbf{p}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\mod 2
, we conclude that
H_{k}\mathbf{p}_{k}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\mod 2
. Hence
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})
. To see that
\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(H)
, let
\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{p}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{p}_{t})\in\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})
. Again using (1) and Theorem
7
,
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{K}(H)=\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t})
,
\mathbf{p}_{k}\in\mathcal{K}(H_{k})
, and
H_{k}\mathbf{p}_{k}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\mod 2
for all
k\in[t]
. Thus,
H\mathbf{p}^{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}H_{k}\mathbf{p}_{k}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\mod 2
, and so
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
. Hence
\mathcal{P}(H)=\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})
, proving 3).

To prove 4), let
f_{H_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
be the generating function of the pseudocodewords of
C(H_{k})
. Then

f_{H_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{k})}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathbf{p}_{k}}=\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{k}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{k})}x_{k1}^{p_{k1}}\dotsm x_{kn_{k}}^{p_{kn_{k}}}.

By 2),
\mathcal{P}(H)=\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})
. Therefore, 4) holds as

\displaystyle f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\mathbf{p}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{p}_{t})\in\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\mathbf{p}_{1}}\dotsm\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{p}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{1})}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\mathbf{p}_{1}}\right)\dotsm\left(\sum_{\mathbf{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(H_{t})}\mathbf{x}_{t}^{\mathbf{p}_{t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{t}f_{H_{k}}(\mathbf{x}_{k}).

∎

Theorem 10
.

Let
H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&H_{2}&\dotsm&H_{t}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}
.
Then

(1)

\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{K}(H)
;

(2)

\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(H)
; and

(3)

f_{H}(\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0})=f_{H_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.

Proof.

Suppose
H_{k}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n_{k}}
so that
n=\sum_{k=1}^{t}n_{k}
. Let
\mathbf{w}=(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{t})\in\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t})
.
Because
\mathbf{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{K}(H_{k})
for all
k\in[t]
, for all
j\in[r]
and for all
i\in[n_{k}]
,

\text{Row}_{j}(H_{k})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{k}\geq 2(H_{k})_{ji}v_{ki}.

For all
i\in[n]
,
j\in[r]
, and for any
k\in[t]
,

\displaystyle\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{1}+\dots+\text{Row}_{j}(H_{t})\cdot\mathbf{v}_{t}

\displaystyle\geq 2(H_{k})_{ji}v_{ki}.

Hence,
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{K}(H)
. It is an immediate consequence that
\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(H_{t})\subseteq\mathcal{K}(H)
, proving 1).

Let
\mathbf{p}=(\mathbf{p}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{p}_{t})\in\mathcal{P}(H_{1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{P}(H_{t})
. Using 1) and Theorem
7
,
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathcal{K}(H_{i})
and
H_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\pmod{2}
for all
i\in[t]
. Thus,
H\mathbf{p}^{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}H_{k}\mathbf{p}_{k}^{T}=\mathbf{0}\pmod{2}
and so
\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P}(H)
, proving 2).

To prove 3), let
i\in[t]
. It is clear from 2) that
f_{H}(\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0})
contains all the terms from
f_{H_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. Let
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathbf{p}_{i}}
be a term of
f_{H_{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
. Because
(\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{p}_{i},\mathbf{0},\dotsc\mathbf{0})\in\mathcal{P}(H)
,
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathbf{p}_{i}}
is also a term of
f_{H}(\mathbf{x})
. In particular,
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{p_{i}}
has no entries from
\mathbf{x}_{1},\dotsc,\mathbf{x}_{i-1},\mathbf{x}_{i+1},\dotsc,\mathbf{x}_{t}
, and so
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathbf{p}_{i}}
is a term of
f_{H}(\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{0},\dotsc,\mathbf{0})
, proving 3).
∎

When the matrices

in Theorem

Theorem 11
.

Let

,
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{K}(H)
,
H^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}H&\dotsm&H\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times tn}
, and

\mathbf{w}=(w_{11},w_{12},\dotsc,w_{1n},w_{21},\dotsc,w_{2n},\dotsc,w_{t1},\dotsc,w_{tn})\in\mathbb{R}^{tn}_{\geq 0}.

If
w_{ki}\leq v_{i}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{t}w_{ji}
for all
i\in[n]
and
k\in[t]
, then
\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{K}(H^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Because
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{K}(H)
, for all
j\in[r]
and all
i\in[n]
,

\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2h_{ji}v_{i}.

We can rewrite:

\displaystyle\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\leq\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{t}w_{k1},\dotsc,\sum_{k=1}^{t}w_{kn}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot(w_{11},\dotsc,w_{1n})+\dotsm+\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot(w_{t1},\dotsc,w_{tn})

\displaystyle=\text{Row}_{j}(H^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{w}

Putting everything together,

\text{Row}_{j}(H^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{w}\geq\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2h_{ji}v_{i}\geq 2h_{ji}w_{ki}

for all
i\in[n]
,
j\in[r]
, and

.
∎

The next result provides a restriction on the fundamental cone of the code from a parity-check matrix which has been augmented by a single additional column.

Proposition 12
.

Let
H_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times n}
,
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}
,
\text{Supp}(\mathbf{s})=S\subseteq[r]
, and
H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&\mathbf{s}^{T}\end{bmatrix}
. Then

\left\{(\mathbf{v},w)\in\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}:w\leq\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}\ \forall j\in S\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{K}(H).

Moreover, for
H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&J\end{bmatrix}
where
J\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r\times r}
is a permutation matrix given by
\sigma\in S_{r}
such that

J_{ji}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sigma(j)=i,\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

we have

\hskip-2.0pt\left\{(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})\in\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{r}\!:\!w_{\sigma(j)}\leq\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}\ \forall j\in[r]\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{K}(H).

Proof.

Let
(\mathbf{v},w)\in\mathcal{K}(H_{1})\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
where
w\leq\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}
for all
j\in S
.
For all
j\in[r]\setminus S
and
i\in[n]
,

\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot(\mathbf{v},w)=\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}+0\cdot w\geq 2(H_{1})_{ji}v_{i}=2(H)_{ji}v_{i},

and for
i=n+1
,

\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot(\mathbf{v},w)=\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}+0\cdot w\geq 0=2(H)_{ji}w.

Similarly, for all
j\in S
and
i\in[n]
,

\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot(\mathbf{v},w)\geq\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}\geq 2(H_{1})_{ji}v_{i}+1\cdot w\geq 2(H)_{ji}v_{i},

and for

,

\text{Row}_{j}(H)\cdot(\mathbf{v},w)=\text{Row}_{j}(H_{1})\cdot\mathbf{v}+1\cdot w\geq 2w=2(H)_{ji}w,

showing that

.

The result for

follows from applying the previous argument repeatedly, since each column and row of

has weight
1
.
∎

We begin by outlining the necessary concepts to understand quantum stabilizer codes and refer readers to

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,0)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle I

\begin{array}[]{lccc}\phi_{Z}:&\mathcal{G}_{n}&\rightarrow&\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\

4.2.
Pseudocodewords of quasi-cyclic codes

In this subsection, we first provide general results on pseudocodewords of classical quasi-cyclic codes. At the end of the section, we additionally use results from Section
4.1
and apply these results to quasi-cyclic CSS codes.

A linear code
C
is called a
cyclic code
if every cyclic shift of a codeword in
C
is also a codeword in
C
. Given
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})
,

\mathbf{x}^{i}=(x_{n-i+1},x_{n-i},\dotsc,x_{n},x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n-i})
is its cyclic shift
i
positions to the right. A linear code is
quasi-cyclic with shifting constraint
n_{0}
if cyclically shifting a codeword a fixed number
n_{0}
of symbol positions results in another codeword. Cyclic codes are quasi-cyclic codes with shifting constraint
n_{0}=1
.

Given shifting constraint
n_{0}
and positive integers
c,t
with
c<t
, let
H_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{c\times n_{0}}
for
i\in[t]
. The nullspace of the matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{tc\times tn_{0}}
in Equation
4
, which is said to be in block circulant form, is a quasi-cyclic parity-check code.

(4)

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{1}&H_{2}&\dotsm&H_{t}\\
H_{t}&H_{1}&\dotsm&H_{t-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
H_{2}&H_{3}&\dotsm&H_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(5)

H=\begin{bmatrix}J_{1,1}&J_{1,2}&\dotsm&J_{1,n_{0}}\\
J_{2,1}&J_{2,2}&\dotsm&J_{2,n_{0}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
J_{c,1}&J_{c,2}&\dotsm&J_{c,n_{0}}\end{bmatrix}

Such a matrix is often represented as a matrix consisting of circulant matrices which is permutation equivalent to
H
. In this case,
H
is a
c\times n_{0}
block matrix composed of
t\times t
circulants, as in Equation
5
.

The next corollary follows immediately from Theorem
10
as well as Lemma
8
.

Corollary 18
.

Let
H
be the parity-check matrix of a quasi-cyclic parity-check code in circulant form, as in Equation
5
. Then

\bigcap_{j=1}^{c}\left(\mathcal{K}(J_{j,1})\times\dotsm\times\mathcal{K}(J_{j,n_{0}})\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n_{0}}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{c}\mathcal{K}(J_{j,i})\right)\subseteq\mathcal{K}(H).

We can make use of the highly structured nature of cyclic and quasi-cyclic parity-check codes in decoding algorithms.

Definition 19
.

A parity-check matrix
H
(resp., polytope
\mathscr{P}
) is called quasi-cyclic with shifting constraint
n_{0}
if every quasi-cyclic shift by
n_{0}
of a row of
H
(resp., vertex of
\mathscr{P}
) is a row of
H
(resp., vertex of
\mathscr{P}
).

As mentioned in
[
40
]
, given a cyclic code with a parity-check matrix that does not consist of all possible cyclic shifts of its rows, all cyclic shifts of integer vertices of the fundamental polytope are also vertices. However, this is not necessarily the case for non-integer vertices. In
[
40
]
, it is shown that if a parity-check matrix of a cyclic code contains all possible cyclic shifts of its rows, non-integer vertices are also vertices of the fundamental polytope. We prove analogous results for quasi-cyclic parity-check codes.

Theorem 20
.

A polytope
\text{poly}(H)
is quasi-cyclic with shifting constraint
n_{0}
if the matrix
H
is quasi-cyclic with shifting constraint
n_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
H
be a quasi-cyclic parity-check matrix with shifting constraint
n_{0}
. It is clear that the set of inequalities given by
\mathcal{K}(H)
in Definition
6
is both completely determined by and completely determines
H
. Let
H^{n_{0}i}
be
H
with its rows all cyclically shifted
n_{0}i
positions to the right. Because
H
is quasi-cyclic,
H^{n_{0}i}
for
i\in\mathbb{Z}
is the same as
H
up to row permutations, and so the set of inequalities in
\mathcal{K}(H)
is the same as those in
\mathcal{K}(H^{n_{0}i})
, i.e.
\mathcal{K}(H)=\mathcal{K}(H^{n_{0}i})
.

Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{K}(H)
. Note that
H(\mathbf{x}^{n_{0}i})^{T}=H^{n_{0}i}\mathbf{x}^{T}
, so
\mathbf{x}^{n_{0}i}\in\mathcal{K}(H)
. The fundamental cone is quasi-cyclic if
H
and
H^{n_{0}i}
have the same fundamental cone, which is satisfied if
H
contains all quasi-cyclic shifts of its rows. Using Theorem
7
, we conclude this is also true of the polytopes of
H
and
H^{n_{0}i}
.
∎

A crucial property of LP decoder performance on a code with quasi-cyclic parity-check matrix is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 21
.

If the parity-check matrix is quasi-cyclic with shifting constraint
n_{0}
, then over the binary symmetric channel, a shift of the error vector by
n_{0}i
for any
i\in\mathbb{Z}
does not affect the success or failure of the LP decoder.

Proof.

The probability that the LP decoder fails is independent of the codeword which was transmitted
[
20
]
, so we can assume that the transmitted codeword is the all zero vector. Suppose
\mathbf{e}
is the error vector, and hence
\mathbf{e}
is also our received vector. If the cost of vertex
\mathbf{v}
is minimum, then because of the quasi-cyclic property of the polytope, the cost of the vertex
\mathbf{v}^{n_{0}i}
is also minimum for the error pattern
\mathbf{e}^{n_{0}i}
. Hence, when error vectors are quasi-cyclic shifts of each other, the corresponding LP decoder outputs are also quasi-cyclic shifts of each other. This means that if the output of the LP decoder for some vector
\mathbf{v}
is integer (or non-integer), then the LP decoder outputs for
\mathbf{v}^{n_{0}i}
are also integer (or non-integer).
∎

The performance of an LP decoder is greatly dependent on the parity-check matrix representation of the code. As shown in e.g.
[
20
,
41
]
, introducing redundancy to a parity-check matrix by adding additional rows can improve LP decoder performance. However, adding these redundant rows randomly is unlikely to have significant positive impact. Theorems
20
and
21
suggest an algorithmic approach to finding “good” redundant rows to improve LP decoder performance. The algorithm, based on that for cyclic codes in
[
40
]
, is as follows:

(1)

Identify a low weight codeword among the codewords of the dual code that are not rows of the parity-check matrix,
H
.

(2)

Add this codeword and all its quasi-cyclic shifts to
H
.

(3)

Examine the LP decoder performance. If it achieves the required performance, stop. Otherwise, return to 1).

4.3.
Pseudocodewords of spatially-coupled codes

We conclude this section with a relatively short subsection on spatially-coupled LDPC (SC-LDPC) codes, which are a special class of LDPC codes originally introduced in
[
44
]
. These codes have highly repetitive structures and exhibit excellent iterative decoder performance
[
45
]
.

SC-LDPC codes have parity-check matrices with
L
block columns, each block column containing
m
smaller parity-check matrices
H_{i}
with
i\in[m]
, where each
H_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n_{c}\times n_{r}}
. There are two types of SC-LDPC codes: terminated and tailbiting. If
C
is terminated, its parity-check matrix
H
has form as in Eq.
6
, and if
C
is tailbiting,
H
has form as in Eq.
7
.

(6)

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{0}&&&&&&\\
H_{1}&H_{0}&&&&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&&&\\
H_{m}&H_{m-1}&\dots&H_{0}&&&\\
&H_{m}&\dots&H_{1}&H_{0}&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&&H_{m}&\dots&H_{1}&H_{0}\\
&&&&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&&&&&H_{m}&H_{m-1}\\
&&&&&&H_{m}\end{bmatrix}

(7)

H=\begin{bmatrix}H_{0}&&&&H_{m}&\dots&H_{1}\\
H_{1}&H_{0}&&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&&&&H_{m}\\
H_{m}&H_{m-1}&\dots&H_{0}&&&\\
&H_{m}&\dots&H_{1}&H_{0}&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&&H_{m}&\dots&H_{1}&H_{0}\end{bmatrix}

Corollary 25
.

Let
C
be an SC-LDPC code with parity-check matrix
H
as in either Eq.
6
or Eq.
7
. Then

\underbrace{\left(\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}\mathcal{K}(H_{j})\right)\times\dotsm\times\left(\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}\mathcal{K}(H_{j})\right)}_{L\text{ times}}\subseteq\mathcal{K}(H).

Proof.

In both Eq.
6
and Eq.
7
, each
H_{j}
appears exactly one time in each block column, and all other block matrices are zero matrices. So, by Lemma
8
, the fundamental cone of a block column of
H
is
\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}\mathcal{K}(H_{j})
. By Theorem
10
, taking each block column of
H
as an individual block matrix, the result follows.
∎

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we determined the pseudocodewords of codes defined using component codes. We applied these ideas to quantum stabilizer codes from the CSS construction, quasi-cyclic parity-check codes, and spatially-coupled LDPC codes. It remains to apply these results to the MDPC codes used in BIKE as well as other commonly considered families of quantum LDPC codes.
