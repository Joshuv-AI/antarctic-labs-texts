Role of AI-supported case-based learning in medical education: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Syed Hani Abidi, Joseph Almazan, Fatin Zehra, Olaoluwa Fabiyi, Muhammad Tariq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGenerative artificial intelligence (AI) tools are rapidly transforming case-based learning (CBL) within medical education. Despite growing interest, the literature is heterogeneous, fragmented and inconsistent in terminology and methodology. This scoping review aims to systematically map and synthesise evidence on the integration of generative AI in CBL, identifying key themes, educational outcomes, challenges and research gaps to guide future investigations, curricular innovation and policy development.Method and analysisA comprehensive search strategy, developed with a health sciences librarian, will be implemented across multidisciplinary databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, ERIC, Scopus, Web of Science, EMBASE and CINAHL, covering publications from 2019 to 2025. Two independent reviewers will conduct title/abstract and full-text screening using predefined eligibility criteria. Data extraction will use standardised charting forms capturing study characteristics, AI applications, educational contexts, outcomes and user perceptions. Data synthesis will involve descriptive statistics and inductive thematic analysis to create an evidence map of generative AI-supported CBL in medical education.Ethics and disseminationNo ethics approval is required, as the review synthesises published literature. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conferences and stakeholder networks to inform educators, researchers and policymakers.

Introduction

Medical education is a structured programme designed to equip learners with the foundational biomedical knowledge, clinical skills and ethical grounding needed for safe patient care.1 Despite this, much of the curriculum remains tied to the traditional Flexner model, where students progress from preclinical instruction in basic sciences to observational learning during clinical rotations.2 These approaches rely heavily on passive delivery, memorisation and written assessments that emphasise recall rather than deeper understanding.3 As a result, they offer limited opportunities for active learning, critical thinking, problem-solving or the development of adaptive clinical judgement required in contemporary healthcare.4

Over the past decade, medical education has shifted towards active, student-centred learning that involves the development of problem-solving skills, critical thinking and other essential abilities for real-world medicine. The common methods include simulation-based learning, problem-based learning and case-based learning (CBL).5 CBL is especially valuable because it helps students connect foundational science with clinical reasoning by working through real patient cases. Additionally, CBL directly reflects the clinical reasoning processes that generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools are now designed to simulate or assist with.6 This application of CBL fosters profound understanding of complex cases and effective communication skills that are indispensable for healthcare professionals. For example, a study by McLean showed that CBL, at both graduate and undergraduate levels, effectively bridges the gap between clinical theories and real-world practice and can also improve patient care.7 Similarly, a study by Ali et al using the CBL approach found a more than 70% increase in student engagement and self-learning.8 However, generating high-quality and clinically diverse cases is time-consuming and resource-intensive, often requiring input from multiple disciplines. Because CBL depends on a continuous supply of varied cases, this challenge presents a clear opportunity for generative AI to enhance case delivery.

GenAI directly addresses core CBL demands by generating realistic cases, modifying case complexity and supporting clinical reasoning dialogues that mirror tutor–student interactions. There is a growing focus on integrating CBL with AI models to innovate teaching and learning processes in medicine. GenAI can be trained on complex datasets to produce human-like responses to prompts and questions. In medical education, AI has enhanced CBL by offering personalised tutoring, creating complex case studies and automatically generating assessments based on these cases. The GenAI-CBL merger holds significant importance in medical education, but it remains an underexplored domain in empirical studies. There are a few studies that have explored this area, for instance, the use of ChatGPT3 for generating complex, dynamic clinical cases or the use of AI to explore healthcare professionalism through simulated real-world clinical scenarios.

The AI-CBL merger also aligns with the assumptions of the cognitive load theory, which suggests that learning is most effective when cognitive demand on the student’s working memory is optimised.9 Since CBL can be intrinsically demanding and has a high cognitive load, GenAI can be effectively used to manage and optimise this load. For example, GenAI can be used to clarify jargon, break down complex cases into more manageable components, or understand the link between different case components, thereby freeing up mental space for deep/critical analysis, clinical reasoning and knowledge assimilation.10

A systematic review could, in principle, synthesise evidence on the use of generative AI within CBL in medical education. However, the existing literature is still limited, conceptually inconsistent and dominated by descriptive reports, editorials and early exploratory studies that lack comparable outcome measures or rigorous evaluative designs. These characteristics limit the feasibility of conducting a meaningful systematic review and prevent quantitative synthesis. In this context, a scoping review is more suitable, as it enables comprehensive mapping of available evidence, clarification of key concepts and identification of emerging patterns in the integration of GenAI and CBL into medical education.11 Scoping reviews are particularly useful for rapidly evolving or understudied fields where the evidence base is diffuse and requires structured exploration rather than formal effectiveness evaluation.12

We aim to answer the following question: What is known about the use of generative AI (Concept) to support CBL in medical education settings (Context) among medical students and educators (Population)?

Methods

This scoping review will follow the methodological framework established by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI)13 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols14 to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting. Likewise, it will also follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist, as developed by Tricco et al,15 to guide reporting and ensure methodological rigour throughout the review process. The duration of the scoping review extends from September 2025 to February 2026.

Protocol registration

The study protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework (OSF) under DOI https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/28E3G.

Information sources

This scoping review will employ a comprehensive search strategy across multiple bibliographic databases to capture the breadth and depth of the existing literature on the integration of generative AI into CBL within medical education. The selected databases include PubMed/MEDLINE, providing extensive biomedical and health sciences literature; ERIC, covering educational research pertinent to pedagogical methods and AI applications; Scopus and Web of Science, which offer broad multidisciplinary indexing with citation tracking to identify key studies and emerging trends; EMBASE, complementing MEDLINE with a wider European biomedical focus; and CINAHL, capturing allied health and interprofessional education literature that overlaps with medical education.

Selection of sources of evidence

All records identified through the comprehensive database and grey literature searches will be imported into robust citation management software such as Zotero16 or EndNote to facilitate systematic organisation and efficient de-duplication of references throughout the selection process. The curated set of citations will subsequently be uploaded to dedicated screening and review platforms such as the JBI SUMARI Reviewer’s software, which supports blinded, structured and auditable screening workflows.

Two independent reviewers with expertise in scoping review methodology and medical education will conduct the initial screening of titles and abstracts against predefined eligibility criteria. This dual-reviewer process safeguards against the inadvertent exclusion of pertinent studies and enhances the validity of study identification. Before full screening, two reviewers will independently pilot-test the eligibility criteria on a small sample of studies (approximately 5–10). Inter-rater agreement (Cohen’s kappa) will be calculated to assess consistency and refine the criteria as needed. Full texts of potentially relevant records will then be retrieved and independently assessed by the same reviewers to confirm eligibility for inclusion. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text review stage will be meticulously documented to support transparency and reproducibility.

Any discrepancies or conflicts between the primary reviewers at any stage will be addressed through discussion and consensus; persisting disagreements will be resolved by a third senior reviewer with extensive experience in evidence synthesis and the topic area. This triage structure ensures that the study selection process remains impartial, thorough and defensible.

A PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) flow diagram17 will be used to comprehensively document the selection process, including the number of records identified, screened, excluded (with reasons) and ultimately included in the scoping review. Adoption of this transparent reporting mechanism enhances the reproducibility and credibility of the overall evidence synthesis and provides readers with a clear audit trail of study inclusion and exclusion decisions.

This rigorous, multistage approach anchored by independent review, consensus resolution and best-practice reporting will ensure the integrity, validity and reliability of the study selection phase in this scoping review of generative AI-supported CBLS within medical education.

Data charting process

Data extraction will be performed systematically using a standardised charting form developed a priori, aligned with the review’s objectives and key research questions. This form will undergo pilot testing and iterative refinement based on a preliminary sample of included studies (typically 5–10) to ensure clarity, comprehensiveness and uniform interpretation across reviewers. Calibration exercises will be conducted to harmonise understanding of the data items and reduce variability in charting.

Two independent reviewers with expertise in medical education and scoping review methodology will extract data in duplicate to enhance reliability and minimise errors. Extracted data will encompass bibliographic details, study design and setting, participant characteristics, types of generative AI tools applied, features of CBL interventions, measured educational outcomes and any other relevant thematic or contextual information pertinent to generative AI integration within medical education. Methodological nuances and study limitations will also be documented where applicable.

Following independent extraction, the reviewers will compare results, discuss discrepancies and reach a consensus. Persistent disagreements will be resolved by a third or fourth reviewer with substantial experience in evidence synthesis. Where information is unclear or incomplete, study authors may be contacted to obtain or confirm data, with all correspondence recorded.

Extracted data will be managed securely in Excel to facilitate organisation, quality control and subsequent analysis. This rigorous, transparent and collaborative data charting process will underpin the reliability and validity of the evidence map, providing a robust foundation for comprehensively synthesising the current state of generative AI-supported CBL in medical education.

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

A systematic critical appraisal of included sources will be undertaken in this protocol to enhance interpretability and contextualise the strength of the evidence base. Recognising the heterogeneity and nascent nature of research at the intersection of generative AI and CBL in medical education, this appraisal seeks to elucidate methodological quality, highlight common limitations and foster nuanced evidence synthesis.

Two independent reviewers will assess the methodological quality of each included study using the appropriate JBI critical appraisal tool checklist for each study design, such as cross-sectional, randomised control trial.18 Reviews, or grey literature not amenable to formal appraisal, will be described narratively, with transparency regarding their evidentiary contribution and limitations.

Any disagreements between reviewers will be resolved via discussion and, if necessary, adjudication by a third, experienced reviewer to ensure rigorous consensus and minimise subjectivity. To increase consistency and reliability, the review team will undertake calibration exercises prior to appraisal and periodically review concordance.

Importantly, findings from the critical appraisal will not be used to exclude studies but will be presented descriptively in summary tables and in a narrative synthesis to inform readers about the methodological landscape of the included literature. Appraisal outcomes will be integrated into the synthesis process, allowing for interpretive commentary stratified by study quality and, where appropriate, sensitivity analyses. Where possible, limitations of critical appraisal tools in capturing the unique methodological issues of this rapidly evolving, interdisciplinary field will be explicitly acknowledged and scoring checklists or appraisal details will be made available in online supplemental materials to maximise transparency and reproducibility. By embedding methodological quality assessment as a core component, this review upholds the highest standards of evidence synthesis, accommodates the complexity of emerging scholarship and enables a sophisticated, contextually aware interpretation of the evolving literature on generative AI-supported CBL in medical education.

Synthesis of results

The synthesis of results will follow a systematic, transparent and descriptive approach aligned with the overarching objectives of the scoping review to comprehensively map the evidence on generative AI-supported CBL in medical education. Quantitative and qualitative data extracted during the data charting phase will be collated, organised and presented in a narrative and tabular format to facilitate clear interpretation and identification of key themes, trends and knowledge gaps.

Initially, extracted quantitative data (eg, study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details and outcome measures) will be tabulated descriptively using summary statistics such as frequencies, proportions and ranges to provide a broad overview of the included literature. Visual representations, including charts or graphs, may be employed to illustrate distributions across variables such as publication trends over time, geographical regions and types of generative AI tools used.

Qualitative data, including user perceptions, experiences, reported challenges and recommendations, will be synthesised using a thematic analysis approach.19 This process will involve iterative coding by the review team to identify recurrent patterns, salient themes and conceptual categories shaping the discourse around AI-enhanced CBL. Thematic findings will be integrated narratively alongside quantitative summaries to provide a rich, multidimensional understanding of the field. Where applicable, the results of the critical appraisal will be incorporated descriptively to contextualise the quality of evidence supporting key findings and highlight areas where methodological limitations may influence interpretations. The synthesis will explicitly consider heterogeneity in study designs, contexts and outcomes without attempting meta-analysis or other quantitative aggregation techniques inappropriate for the scoping review’s aims.

Patient and public involvement

This scoping review protocol did not involve patients or members of the public in the design, conduct or reporting of the study, as it synthesises existing published literature. Given the methodological nature of the review and its focus on educational interventions in medical settings, direct patient or public involvement was not applicable.

Ethics and dissemination

As this scoping review involves the synthesis of existing published literature without the collection of primary data from human participants, formal ethics approval is not required. The review will adhere to ethical standards by ensuring accurate representation, acknowledgement of original sources and transparent reporting.

Findings from this review will be disseminated widely to maximise impact and knowledge translation within medical education and AI research communities. Dissemination strategies will include submission of manuscripts to peer-reviewed, high-impact journals, presentations at relevant national and international conferences and sharing through professional and stakeholder networks engaged in medical education, educational technology and AI. Additionally, summary reports or knowledge briefs may be developed to facilitate uptake among educators and policymakers interested in integrating generative AI tools in CBL curricula.
