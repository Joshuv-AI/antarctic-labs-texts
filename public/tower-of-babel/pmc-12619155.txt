A new biological enhancement therapy for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction: the preclinical proof of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with tendon graft reseeded with autologous anterior cruciate ligament-derived cells
Authors: Jinsung Park, Hyunsoo Soh, Yoon-Kwan Jang, Young Seok Lee, Myung-Kyu Lee, Hyunsung Kim, Tae-Hwan Kim, Khalid Alkhelaifi, Tae-Jin Kim, Jin Kyu Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe current standard of care to treat a ruptured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is ACL reconstruction (ACLR), which involves replacing the torn ligament with a tendon graft. The implanted tendon graft undergoes a graft-maturation process known as ligamentization. However, the synthesis of type III collagen, which is typically found in weaker scar tissue, continues at higher concentrations, and the heterogeneous composition of collagen fibers of varying diameters of native ACL is never restored. Culture-expanded ACL-derived cells reseeded to tendon graft have shown the potential for enhanced ligamentization. We performed in vitro and in vivo studies of the outcomes of ACLR with decellularized tendon grafts reseeded with ACL-derived cells combined with acellularized cruciate ligament matrix (ACLM) powder as a biological scaffold. We hypothesized that this tissue-engineered construct would enhance the graft maturation process and mechanical properties of the graft following ACLR.MethodsACL-derived cells were harvested and isolated from remnants of ruptured ACLs within 4 weeks of injury in patients who had undergone ACLR. An ACLM was prepared from human ACL and posterior cruciate ligament tissues. Forty-five Sprague Dawley rats were randomly divided into 3 groups: a standard allograft group, an ACLM powder-only injection group, and an ACL-derived cells and ACLM powder co-injection group (e.g., target group). ACL-derived cells were mixed with ACLM powder and then injected into the decellularized tendon graft before ACLR.ResultsIn vitro experiments found that combining ACLM powder and ACL-derived cells improved survival and integration of reseeded cells in the tendon extracellular matrix, resulting in the successful transplantation of ACL-derived cells. Animal ACLR experiments confirmed histologically improved structural maturation in cellularity, metaplasia, and restoration of collagen crimping in the graft reseeded with ACL-derived cells and ACLM powder. Enhanced gene expression of type I collagen resulted in superior mechanical properties of the tendon graft compared with the control groups.ConclusionImplantation of a tendon graft reseeded with culture-expanded ACL-derived cells, and ACLM powder enhanced the tendon graft’s maturation process and mechanical properties. Background

Spontaneous healing of a ruptured anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) often fails due to a ruptured synovial sheath and direct contact between the ruptured ligament and intra-articular synovial fluid [1, 2]. The current standard of care for treating a ruptured ACL is anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR), which involves replacing the torn ligament with a tendon graft. Autografts and allografts are both viable options when considering graft options for ACLR. However, the mechanical properties of the implanted graft, regardless of graft type, do not return to normal after ACLR surgery [3–5]. Although the implanted graft reportedly undergoes remodeling after 1 year, restoration of the mechanical strength of an intact ACL is not achieved even after 2 years [3, 4]. Animal studies have shown that the healing graft’s mechanical properties (e.g., failure load) remain less than 50% to 60% of those of the native ACL [6–8]. A human case study that retrieved a patellar tendon autograft found that a significant portion of the implanted graft sustained necrosis and acellularity 18 months after index surgery [4].

A tendon graft implanted during ACLR undergoes a maturation process known as ligamentization [9, 10]. The influx of host fibroblasts into the necrotic area of the graft reportedly occurs early in the healing phase, followed by remodeling of the tendon graft. However, the predominant synthesis of type III collagen, which is typically found in weaker scar tissue, continues at higher concentrations, and the heterogeneous composition of collagen fibers of varying diameters of native ACL is never restored [5, 11, 12].

To overcome such a time-consuming healing process, which leads to weaker reparative scar tissue, tissue-engineering strategies that involve fibroblasts or stem cell transplantation, gene regulation, and bioengineering have been attempted to enhance ACLR outcomes [13–18]. Several studies have reported that ruptured human ACL tissue possesses numerous cells promoting healing and multilineage differentiation [19–21]. In a recent in vitro study, we demonstrated that reseeding culture-expanded ACL-derived cells to a decellularized tendon graft enhanced the graft’s ability to survive and integrate into the tendon’s extracellular matrix (ECM), resulting in a higher gene expression level of type I collagen compared with mesenchymal stem cells derived from adipose tissue, which may lead to the normal ligamentization process [22]. However, it has been suggested that most transplanted stem cells die within 1 week of implantation due to harsh conditions, such as hypoxia, poor nutrition, inflammation, and lack of biophysical protection in the host tissue [23]. The ECM provides a physical structure that affects cell fate by adjusting cell-matrix interactions, and studies have confirmed the efficacy of the tissue-derived acellular matrix as a cell carrier or biological scaffold. Xu et al. reported that applying an acellular cartilage matrix on bone marrow stem cells in an animal model efficiently repaired defects in articular cartilage by promoting chondrogenic differentiation [24]. Mao et al. found that applying an acellularized matrix derived from chondrocytes promoted the rapid expansion of primary chondrocytes and reduced in vitro dedifferentiation [25]. However, the effect of the acellularized cruciate ligament matrix (ACLM) on ACL-derived cells, particularly in the tendon microenvironment, has yet to be tested. In this study, we conducted in vitro and in vivo experiments to evaluate the outcomes of ACLR with a decellularized tendon graft reseeded with ACL-derived cells combined with ACLM powder as a biological scaffold. We hypothesized that this stem cell transplantation therapy combined with a biological scaffold would enhance the graft maturation process and improve the mechanical properties of the tendon graft following ACLR.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study followed institutional guidelines and was approved by the Ethics Committee of OO Hospital. All patients provided written informed consent before participating (IRB-2022-01-049). Between August 2022 and December 2023, ACL-derived cells from remnant tissues of ruptured ACLs in patients who had undergone primary ACLR within 4 weeks of injury were collected from 15 patients (12 males and 3 females) with an average age of 29.3 ± 2.2 years (ranging from 17 to 41 years). ACLM powder was extracted from human ACLs and posterior cruciate ligaments (PCLs) harvested from 2 osteoarthritic patients (60 and 62 years old) during total knee arthroplasty.

Isolation and preparation of ACL-derived cells

The procedure for isolating ACL-derived cells and characterizing culture-expanded ACL-derived cells was previously described by Park et al. [26]. ACL remnant tissues were dissected into segments < 1 cm in length using scissors. These tissue segments were washed twice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and enzymatically digested using collagenase type I (Gibco, 17100-017; Sigma, C0130) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in serum-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher, 15140122), followed by 3 to 4 h incubation at 37 °C with shaking. The cell suspensions were filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (SPL, 93070), and cells were isolated from the pellet after discarding the supernatant following centrifugation at 1400 rpm for 3 min. The isolated ACL-derived cells were cultured until passage 2 with medium changes every 2 to 3 days. After that, the isolated ACL-derived cells were preserved in a deep freezer at − 80 ℃ and subsequently stored in liquid nitrogen for long-term preservation. All experiments utilized ACL-derived cells in the third passage (P3). A detailed cell characterization of culture-expanded ACL-derived cells was previously published [22].

Preparation of ACLM powders and determination of concentration

The ACLM was prepared using a modified protocol based on previous studies [27, 28]. Human ACL and PCL tissues were collected from donors following aseptic procedures. The harvested ACL and PCL tissues were cut into pieces < 1 cm in size and frozen at − 80 °C. The frozen tissue was ground into a coarse powder and agitated in a 1% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulfate solution in PBS for 72 h to facilitate decellularization. The samples were treated with a 0.1% (w/v) ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid solution in PBS for 24 h and then immersed in a large volume of deionized water overnight to remove residual chemicals. After thorough washing, the resulting ACLM was frozen at − 80 °C and lyophilized for 3 days to ensure complete dehydration. The ACLM was subsequently milled into a fine powder to allow an easy mixture of cells. Low-dose (25 kGy) gamma irradiation, which is commonly used for effective sterilization by damaging microbial DNA and rendering pathogens inactive, was applied to the lyophilized ACLM for sterilization, and the ACLM was stored at − 20 °C until further use.

The seeding efficiency was determined using previously reported studies [29–31]. Briefly, 3.75 × 105 ACL-derived cells (Passage 3) were mixed with ACLM powders at concentrations of 10, 30, and 60 mg/mL and injected into each decellularized rat Achilles tendon graft. After incubation for 24 h, the supernatant and the cells at the bottom of the well were collected, and the non-adherent cells were counted using a hemocytometer. The seeding efficiency was calculated using the formula: seeding efficiency = (initial cells − non-adherent cells)/initial cells × 100%.

Tendon graft preparation

For in vitro experiments, we used 12 strips of donated human decellularized tibialis tendon allografts provided by the Korea Public Tissue Bank. All tendon allografts were processed for decellularization and sterilization following standard protocols. Initially, tendon allografts were stripped of muscle tissue and washed with sterile distilled water for 5 min. Next, the tendon grafts were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 5 min and 70% ethyl alcohol for 15 min. All washing procedures were repeated 3 times. Low-dose (25 Gy) gamma irradiation was applied for sterilization, and the tendon allografts were frozen at − 80 °C.

For the animal experiments, Achilles tendon grafts (n = 46) were procured from adult Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (n = 23; age = 10 weeks) that were being used for other nonmusculoskeletal experiments at the time of sacrifice. The tendon allograft was obtained by making a lateral skin incision to the Achilles tendon, and the tendon was transected transversely, typically at the calcaneal insertion region. After trimming the adjacent tissues, the tendon graft was cut transversely at the proximal end to obtain the entire Achilles tendon graft. All grafts were wrapped immediately with sterile gauze and stored in a − 80 °C freezer until further use.

In vitro experiments

The in vitro experiments were randomly conducted in 3 groups: a standard allograft group, an ACL-derived cells–only injection group, and an ACL-derived cells and ACLM powder co-injection (target) group. Human tibialis allografts were thawed at room temperature for 15 min and sectioned into segments 1.5 cm long. Each target set used tissue from the same donor for consistency. In the target group, 1 × 106 ACL-derived cells (P3) were mixed with ACLM powder to create a 150 µL cell suspension and then injected into the tendon segment using a 25-gauge needle. For the control groups, a 150 µL suspension of PBS or ACL-derived cells was injected into the tendon graft without a mixture of ACLM powder. A single injection into the core of the graft was performed, with careful monitoring to detect any leakage. The reseeded tendon segments were incubated in a 5% CO2 environment for 20 min after injection. The segments were then transferred to high-glucose DMEM (SH30243.01, Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The DMEM growth medium was refreshed every 3 days. On the designated harvest days (1, 7, and 14 days post-seeding), the tendon segments were fully embedded in a Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (4583, Sakura, Japan) and stored at − 80 °C. The frozen tissue blocks were cryosectioned longitudinally into slices 6 μm thick. These sections were prepared for histological and immunohistochemical analyses and stored on glass slides in a deep freezer at − 80 °C.

In vivo ACL reconstruction

The Animal Experiments Ethics Committee of OO reviewed and approved the rat protocol (2023–0095 A). SD rats were obtained from KOATECH (Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea). Only animals free of specific pathogens were selected, and any abnormal or dying animals were excluded during the acclimatization period prior to the experiment. Forty-five skeletally mature male SD rats 10 weeks old at the time of surgery were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 15 rats per group) (Fig. 5A): a standard allograft group, an ACLM powder-only injection group, and an ACL-derived cells and ACLM powder co-injection group. The sample size for both the target and control groups was determined based on prior research related to ACL reconstruction [32–34]. Frozen rat Achilles tendon grafts were thawed at room temperature for 15 min before experiments. The average length of the rat Achilles tendon grafts was 12.8 (± 0.554) mm, and the average diameter was 1.71 (± 0.018) mm. Grafts were tested to determine the appropriate injection volume, and no leakage was observed with 15 µL of PBS solution (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Based on seeding efficiency experiments, 3.75 × 105 ACL-derived cells (P3) were mixed with ACLM powder (30 mg/mL), and DMEM was added to a total suspension volume of 15 µL. Graft injection was performed with a 31-gauge, 0.3 mL insulin needle (BD, 328822), and the samples were incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator.

The SD rats were anesthetized by isoflurane inhalation (Med-Vet International, RXISO-100). After the fur from the left hind leg was removed, the skin was disinfected with ethanol and a povidone-iodine solution. An incision 2 cm long was made in the skin at the anteromedial aspect of the knee joint, and the patella was laterally dislocated to expose the joint. The ACL was completely transected with a sharp scalpel. Achilles tendon graft composites previously prepared were used for ACL reconstruction purposes. A bone tunnel (2.0 mm) was created in the femur and tibia, as appropriate, and grafts were secured to the periosteum and surrounding soft tissues overlaying the lateral femoral condyle or medial tibial tuberosity (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Postoperatively, the animals were allowed free movement without any restrictions. The animal laboratory was kept at a temperature of 22 °C, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Each cage housed 3 rats, which were identified using ear tags. Fifteen rats per group were euthanized at 4 (n = 4; histology only) or 8 (n = 11) weeks postoperatively. For animal euthanasia was used carbon dioxide (CO2). The native and reconstructed ACLs were harvested with attached bones on both ends. After then, the following parameters were assessed: histological analysis (n = 4), mRNA analysis (n = 3), mechanical test, and micro-CT analysis (n = 4). Samples for histological analysis were fixed in 10% formalin at room temperature for 24 h, washed three times with 1× PBS, and decalcified in 10% formic acid at room temperature for approximately one week. Subsequently, paraffin blocks were prepared. Single blinding was applied to the researchers conducting the animal experiments and analyzing the histopathology. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Histological analysis

Cryosectioned slides were cut at a thickness of 6 μm and paraffin-section slides at 3 μm for histological analysis. The staining procedures followed the standard protocols indicated in the manufacturer’s instructions. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain was used for histological and cytological evaluations, while Picrosirius red (PSR; Abcam, ab150681) was chosen to analyze types I and III collagen. All stained slides were mounted using a permanent medium (Vector Labs, H-5000). Cellular images within the tendon allografts were observed under bright-field microscopy at 200× magnification, and cell counts were conducted across 5 consecutive slides. All measurements were analyzed by 2 independent observers blinded to each other.

Graft cellularity, necrosis, and collagen crimping were analyzed to determine the degree of graft maturation. Cellularity was based on the predominant nuclear shape as described by Dong et al. [35]. Five grades were given as follows (light microscopy, ×200): grade 0, round nuclei were the most common; grade 1, spindle nuclei were most common, followed by round nuclei; grade 2, spindle nuclei were the most common, followed by oblong nuclei; grade 3, spindle nuclei were the most common, followed by linear nuclei; and grade 4, linear nuclei were the most common, followed by spindle nuclei. Graft necrosis was based on the degree of grafts with metaplasia (acellular, avascular, and irregularly oriented collagen bundles) described by Falconiero et al. [9]. Five grades were assigned as follows (light microscopy, ×200): grade 0, no metaplasia; grade 1, minimal metaplasia; grade 2, focal area of metaplasia; grade 3, bubbly area of metaplasia; and grade 4, large area of metaplasia. Picorosirius red staining of longitudinal slides was used under linear polarized light microscopy to evaluate the organization of collagen fibers, which were classified according to criteria described by Zhao et al. [36]. Substantial crimping was characterized by consistent banding with a visible waveform. Intermediate crimping was characterized by a reduced waveform, missing banding, and partially straightened collagen fibers. Minimal crimping involved an extensive area of straightened collagen fibers.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical staining was performed as previously described [22]. Briefly, slides were deparaffinized using Neo-Clear, rehydrated with a series of ethanol solutions (100% to 50% ethanol), and washed 3 times with distilled water and 1× PBS. Slides were then incubated with 1× PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by incubation with BLOXALL (SP-6000, Vector Lab) for 30 min. The slides were immunostained with primary antibodies at a 1:200 dilution in an antibody diluent (S3022, DAKO, USA) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by 1 h of incubation with biotinylated anti-rabbit immunoglobin G secondary antibodies (BA-1000, Vector Lab) mixed with normal horse serum (S-2012, Vector Lab). Next, the slides were incubated with ABC kit components (PK-6102, Vector Lab) for 30 min following the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by a DAB substrate kit (SK-4100, Vector Lab) for 1 to 5 min. Finally, the slides were washed 5 times with distilled water, counterstained with hematoxylin (1.05174.0500, Merck, Germany) for 10 s, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions (50% to 100% ethanol), and mounted in a permanent mounting medium (H-5000, Vector Lab). Images of stained cells were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti-U microscope. The antibodies used for immunohistochemistry were COL1A1 (Santa, sc-8784; dilution 1:200) and COL3 (ABclonal Technology, a3795; dilution 1:200). Both positive and negative controls were prepared using normal samples. Positive controls were processed under the same staining conditions as the experimental samples, while negative controls were treated identically except that the primary antibody was omitted during the staining procedure. The intensity of immunohistochemistry images was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Regions of interest (ROI) were selected, and the mean gray value was measured for each sample to evaluate collagen expression levels.

Immunofluorescence

In vitro experiments, the tissue slides underwent fixation in 100% cold acetone for 10 min, followed by a 10 min water wash to eliminate the OCT compound (SAKURA, 4583). For the animal experiments, they were deparaffinized using Neo-Clear, rehydrated with a series of ethanol solutions (100% to 50% ethanol), and washed 3 times with distilled water and 1× PBS. The slides were washed 3 times with 1× PBS, treated with 1× PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked using BLOXALL (Vector Lab, SP-6000) for 30 min at room temperature. Tissue slides were stained with primary antibodies (diluted 1:100 in antibody diluent, DAKO, S3022) overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation, the slides were washed 3 times with 1× PBST, incubated with Alexa 488 or Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted to 1:100 in an antibody diluent) for 1 h at room temperature, and mounted using a medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vectorlabs, H-2000). Images were obtained using a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems). The primary antibodies used were Ki-67 (Invitrogen, 14-5698-82), type I collagen (Santa, sc-8784), or YAP (ABclonal, A1002). Both positive and negative controls were prepared using normal samples. Positive controls were processed under the same staining conditions as the experimental samples, while negative controls were treated identically except that the primary antibody was omitted during the staining procedure.

An anti-human nuclei antibody (Ku-80, Cell Signaling, 2180 S) was used for in vivo tracking of cells after transplantation [37, 38]. At 4 and 8 weeks after transplantation, co-immunofluorescence staining for Ku-80 and type I collagen (Santa, sc-293182) was conducted, and images were obtained using a confocal microscope.

Immunoblotting

Proteins were extracted from ACLM using standard procedures involving a 1× RIPA buffer containing a phosphatase inhibitor (Cell Signaling, 5870 S) and protease inhibitor (Calbiochem, 535140). Quantification of the extracted proteins was carried out using a Bradford assay. Subsequently, 2, 4, and 8 µg of proteins were used for immunoblotting. The primary antibody in the immunoblotting process was type I collagen (Santa, sc-8784).

RT-qPCR

In vitro experiments, the tissue slides were scraped using a cell scraper to gather material for mRNA analysis. The reconstructed tendon grafts were gathered at sacrificed rat knee tissues for mRNA analysis for the animal experiments. RNA extraction followed standard procedures using a TRI reagent. For quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) amplification, 1 µg of RNA was used to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). The obtained cDNA served as the template for RT-qPCR. cDNA was used as the template for RT-qPCR. Primers for RT-qPCR are in Table 1.

Biosensor DNA construct

The Talin-TS (plasmid #83376) and Lyn-FAK (plasmid #78299) biosensors, which utilize fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET, a technique that detects changes in distance between two fluorescent molecules to monitor molecular interactions or structural shifts) to identify biomolecules or monitor environmental changes, were obtained from Addgene. In brief, Talin-TS incorporates a tension module housing two fluorescent proteins that gauge the tension between the talin head domain, which is responsible for binding to the integrin β tail, and the rod domain that encompasses actin-binding sites and vinculin binding sites [39]. Talin is a key cytoskeletal protein that connects integrins—cell surface receptors—to the internal actin cytoskeleton, facilitating cell adhesion and mechanical signal transmission. As actin filaments apply tension to the Talin-TS, a nano spring within the tension module extends, increasing the distance between the 2 fluorescent proteins. The Lyn-FAK biosensor’s core element is the FAK substrate sequence, which is intricately connected by a flexible linker to the SH2 domain of c-Src [40]. FAK, or focal adhesion kinase, is a signaling protein that helps transmit mechanical and chemical signals involved in cell movement, attachment, and survival. Activation of FAK prompts the phosphorylated substrate peptide to bind with the SH2 domain, instigating a conformational shift that results in reduced fluorescence resonance energy transfer. This functional component is fused with a sequence that targets the detergent-resistant membrane, which is sensitive to cell adhesion and the surrounding environment [41].

Transfection

Before transfection, ACL-derived cells were seeded onto a 6-well plate (SPL, 30006). Transfections with plasmid DNAs were carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) following the manufacturer’s guidelines, and the cells were incubated for 24 h.

FRET image acquisition and calculation

Experiments involving FRET ratios with the Talin-TS and Lyn-FAK biosensors were conducted using a Leica DMI8 microscope equipped with a digital camera (Leica-K5-14401252) and a 40×/1.30 NA oil objective. FRET ratio calculations employed the DFT51010 filter cube (Leica, 11525418) and the CYR71010 filter cube (Leica, 11525416). Background subtraction and the lightning-and-thunder process were applied to all fluorescence images, which were captured utilizing LAS X software. Basic image processing was conducted by Fiji (National Institutes of Health), and pseudocolor images were generated using LAS X software. The FRET ratio of Talin-TS was determined by dividing the acceptor image by the FRET image in cells (tagRFP/FRET). Similarly, the FRET ratio of the Lyn-FAK sensor was calculated by dividing the donor image by the FRET image (ECFP/FRET). The intensity values for the FRET ratio calculations were obtained using LAS X software. These calculations match the changes in talin tension and FAK activity to warm or cold colors.

Mechanical analysis

Mechanical analysis was performed at 8 weeks following ACL reconstruction based on reported studies [32, 42, 43]. Briefly, the soft tissues surrounding the tendon graft were carefully removed, and the femur and tibia were placed into a 3D-printed mold (2 cm × 2 cm × 4 cm). Subsequently, a Kirschner wire (SOLCO, 81-61622) was used to secure the bone, and the 3D-printed mold and bone cement (Heraeus, Palacos R + G) were applied to prevent bony slippage (Fig. 7A, B). Following preconditioning with 5 load cycles up to 0.5 N at a speed of 0.37 mm/s, using a materials-testing machine (TESTONE, TO-101), the specimens were loaded to failure at an elongation rate of 20 mm/min, and the ultimate failure loads were recorded. Stiffness was calculated using the load-deformation curve.

Micro-CT

Micro-CT analysis was performed at 8 weeks following ACL reconstruction surgery using methods adapted from previous studies [44, 45]. The micro-CT scan was set perpendicular to the long axis of the bony tunnel using the SkyScan 1272 micro-CT device (Bruker micro-CT, Belgium). The parameters for the micro-CT were a resolution of 9.00 μm, an X-ray source voltage of 60 kV, and an X-ray source current of 166 µA. The average cross-sectional areas of the femoral and tibial tunnels were calculated using CTAn software to assess new bone growth within the bone tunnels. For the bone volume to total volume ratio (BV/TV), a cylindrical region of interest with a diameter of 2 mm and a height of 3 mm was analyzed, focusing on the proximal end of the tibial tunnel and the distal end of the femoral tunnel.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative and statistical analyses of the graph data were conducted using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software. A 2-tailed paired t-test and 1-way and 2-way analysis of variance were used to compare the data from the target and control groups at each time point. The results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of a minimum of 3 independent experiments. The asterisks represent the level of statistical significance (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

The ACLM assumed the form of a fine white powder after freeze-drying and milling (Fig. 1A). Immunoblotting analysis was positive for type I collagen in the ACLM powder (Fig. 1B). The seeding efficiency of the 3 concentrations of ACLM powders was tested, with the 30 mg/mL group exhibiting a significantly (p < 0.001) higher cell seeding efficiency compared with the 10 and 60 mg/mL groups (Fig. 1C).

In vitro histologic features

Histologic features observed by H&E staining in the control (standard allograft) group showed that no cells were retained by the tendon allograft ECM. On day 1, a cluster of reseeded cells was noticeable along the intrafascicular opening, where the ACL-derived cells were injected. In the target group, a large number of elongated cells that integrated into the tendon ECM was evident on days 7 and 14; however, in the ACL-derived cell-only injection group, few cells were evident on day 14 (Fig. 2A). A new collagen matrix appeared to form around the ACL-derived cells at days 7 and 14 after ACLM powder was co-injected. The number of cells that survived and integrated into the tendon ECM was counted on day 14, and a significantly (p < 0.001) higher number of cells was counted in the target group compared with the ACL-derived cell-only injection group (Fig. 2B). Picrosirius red staining for collagen types I and III showed additional evidence of collagen deposition in the target group compared with the ACL-derived cell-only injection group (Fig. 2C).

In vitro matrix protein contents and gene expression

Immunofluorescence staining was observed on day 14 for Ki-67, and type I collagen was associated with stronger staining for type I collagen in Ki-67-positive ACL-derived cells in the target group compared with the ACL-derived cell-only injection group (Fig. 2D, E). An mRNA expression analysis confirmed significantly (p < 0.01) higher gene expression levels of type I collagen in the target group compared with the ACL-derived cell-only injection group. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups for type III collagen and other ligament-related markers, including tenascin C and smooth muscle A (Fig. 2F).

YAP translocation, talin, and FAK activity

Immunofluorescence staining observed on day 14 for Yes-associated protein (YAP) showed a significantly (p < 0.01) higher number of cells, indicating translocation of YAP to the nucleus in the target group compared with the ACL-derived cell-only injection group (Fig. 3A, B). We employed biosensors to visually assess the impact of the ACLM on the tension regeneration of ACL-derived cells. Specifically, two biosensors were utilized: the talin tension sensor (Talin-TS), which uses focal adhesions and actin filaments [39] to detect tension, and the Lyn-FAK sensor, which measures changes in focal adhesion kinase (FAK) activity in response to heightened tension [40, 46]. The fluorescence intensity of the reseeded ACL-derived cells was notably higher than that of the surrounding cells (Fig. 4A). ACL-derived cells co-injected with ACLM powder displayed a significant (p < 0.001) increase in tension on talin compared with those without ACLM (Fig. 4B). Additionally, ACL-derived cells transfected with the Lyn-FAK sensor exhibited a significant (p < 0.05) increase in FAK activity upon the addition of the ACLM (Fig. 4C, D). These findings strongly suggest that the addition of ACLM powder enhanced the adhesion of ACL-derived cells within the tendon microenvironment.

Histological features in a rat ACL reconstruction model

H&E staining performed at 4 weeks revealed the presence of focal (grade 2) to large (grade 4) areas of metaplasia within the tendon grafts of the control groups; however, minimal (grade 1) to no (grade 0) metaplasia was found in the tendon graft of the target group, indicating superior graft maturation (Fig. 5B). H&E staining performed at 8 weeks revealed increased cellularity without large areas of necrosis in the control groups; however, cells with spindle (grade 1) to oblong (grade 2) nuclei were predominant in the control groups, whereas cells with linear (grade 4) to spindle (grade 3) nuclei were predominant in the target group, indicating superior graft maturation (Fig. 5C). Polarized microscopy at 8 weeks showed consistent banding with a visible wave (substantial crimping) in the target group compared with an extensive area of straight collagen fibers (minimal crimping) in the control groups (Fig. 5D) indicating better collagen crimp restoration in the target group.

Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression of type I and III collagen. Immunofluorescence staining at 4 weeks and 8 weeks for type I collagen and Ku-80 (anti-human nuclei antibody) revealed more pronounced evidence of Ku-80 in type I collagen-positive cells, demonstrating successful transplantation of reseeded cells in the early period (Fig. 6D).

Mechanical strength

The ultimate failure load (N) values of the standard allograft, ACLM powder-only injection, and target groups were 13.125 ± 0.431, 18.400 ± 0.782, and 20.400 ± 0.949, respectively. There was an average increase of 40.2% and 55.4% in the failure loads of the ACLM powder-only injection (p = 0.002) and target groups (p < 0.001), respectively, compared with the standard allograft groups (Fig. 7C). The stiffness values (N/mm) of the standard allograft, ACLM powder-only injection, and target groups were 3.410 ± 0.942, 7.918 ± 1.060, and 9.508 ± 1.020, respectively. There was an average increase of 132.2% and 178.8% in the stiffness of the respective ACLM powder-only injection (p = 0.028) and target groups (p = 0.005) compared with the standard allograft groups (Fig. 7D).

Discussion

A tendon graft is often used to perform ACLR; however, the graft undergoes necrosis early in the healing process, and deterioration occurs through the proliferation of the extrinsic fibroblasts in place of graft necrosis, resulting in overexpression of type III collagen [3, 11]. Our in vitro experiments found that combining ACLM powders with ACL-derived cells improved survival and integration of reseeded cells in the tendon ECM microenvironment, resulting in successful transplantation of ACL-derived cells. Our animal ACLR experiments confirmed the improvement in histological structural maturation in terms of cellularity, metaplasia, and restoration of collagen crimping in grafts reseeded with both ACL-derived cells and ACLM powder. Enhanced gene expression of type I collagen resulted in superior mechanical properties of the tendon graft, supporting the potential use of autologous ACL-derived cell implantation as a biological enhancement therapy for ACL reconstruction.

Various cell types, including tenocytes, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), adipose-derived MSCs, and umbilical cord-derived MSCs, have been tested for their potential to regenerate tendons [15, 16, 47–49]. Burk et al. reported that adipose-derived MSC can positively influence tendon matrix reorganization (e.g., expression of type I collagen) more effectively than MSCs from other tissue sources [50]. Ruptured ACL tissue possesses numerous cells with high expansion and multilineage differentiation potentials [19, 51, 52]. Park et al. reported that an average of 9.5 × 105 primary (CD 34 negative) ACL cells can be obtained from an acute rupture of ACL remnant tissue, and these cells showed similar laboratory expansion and proliferation rates to adipose-derived MSCs. They also demonstrated that cultured-expanded ACL-derived cells within the tendon microenvironment achieved earlier and superior survival and integration rates in the tendon ECM, resulting in higher type I collagen gene expression compared with adipose-derived MSCs [22]. This supports the clinical use of ACL-derived cells as a source of autologous stem cell therapy to enhance ligamentization following ACL reconstruction. However, the condition of the ruptured ACL tissue may differ between individuals, leading to inconsistent yield of ACL-derived cells. Therefore, there may be a challenge in identifying the ideal patients for applying such autologous tissue-derived cell therapy in clinical settings.

During the healing process of implanted tendon grafts following ACL reconstruction, graft necrosis occurs early in the process, leading to the invasion of extrinsic fibroblasts. Over the years, fibroblast proliferation forms reparative scar tissue; however, an endpoint of graft maturation has yet to be detected [4, 53, 54]. To overcome such a time-consuming tendon regeneration process, reseeding of autologous fibroblasts or bone marrow-derived MSCs into the tendon graft has been attempted in the ACLR model [13, 14, 16, 55]. Lu et al. [56] performed ACLR in a rabbit model using a decellularized allogenic tendon reseeded with bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells. They reported improved histology results regarding fibroblast infiltration and blood vessel formation. Ingram et al. [57] performed ACLR in a xenogenic model using an acellularized tendon graft reseeded with tenocytes. They reported that reseeded tenocytes remained detectable on the ECM for several weeks. They found reseeding the tendon ECM challenging as the dense tendon ECM does not allow sufficient cell adhesion and infiltration [58, 59]. However, prior studies did not assess the improvement of the ligamentization process regarding type I collagen synthesis. In the current study, co-injection of ACLM powder and ACL-derived cells improved survival and integration of cells in tendon ECM, leading to successful transplantation of reseeded cells and higher gene expression levels of type I collagen. ACL-derived cells transfected with the Lyn-FAK and TalinTS biosensors showed increased activity with a combination of ACLM powders, supporting a tension-induced cellular adhesion mechanism within the tendon microenvironment. Cellular adhesion strength is often modulated by integrin binding to ECM ligands, which is mediated by actin-talin coupling and focal adhesions [60, 61]. Our findings point toward the co-injection of ACLM as a catalyst for enhancing tension on talin and amplified FAK activity within ACL-derived cells. Once introduced into the tendon microenvironment, these cells establish connections with the ECM through focal adhesion, allowing them to interpret their surroundings without external triggers and altering their intracellular physiological responses. Cells often translate mechanical cues from the ECM into signals that affect the nucleus and prompt chemical reactions that lead to nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ, as confirmed in the current study.

The ligament healing mechanism in this study may be associated with the paracrine action of ACL-derived cells. The ACL-derived cells have shown multilineage differentiation potential in vitro and a strong tendency for ligamentous differentiation [19]. Besides ligamentous differentiation of ACL-derived cells, an unidentified paracrine action between transplanted ACL-derived cells and the host cells may have enhanced ligament-specific ECM synthesis [22, 62]. In this study, transplanted cells gradually disappeared 4 weeks after transplantation, supporting the paracrine interaction between reseeded ACL-derived cells and host progenitor cells, which may play an essential role in ligament healing.

Our efforts focused on assessing the intra-articular portion of the tendon graft in terms of graft maturation. Excessive graft necrosis observed in animal studies reportedly occurs in much smaller areas of the graft in human ACL grafts [63–65]. However, several human biopsy studies have confirmed a similar remodeling cascade of graft necrosis (although to a smaller extent), recellularization, revascularization, and changes in collagen composition during the healing of the tendon graft [5, 9, 10, 66]. Janssen et al., in their retrieval biopsy studies of 67 human ACL grafts, reported a substantial increase in myofibroblast and vessel density up to 24 months, confirming that collagen composition and orientation did not return to normal in the study period [5]. In the current study, 4-week biopsies in a standard tendon graft group revealed a large area of necrosis and a substantial increase of cellularity comprising oblong to spindle-shaped immature cells at 8 weeks. This is consistent with previous animal studies that reported excessive graft necrosis in the early phase, followed by an influx of fibroblasts [53, 67, 68]. However, a minimal area of graft necrosis was found in 4-week biopsies in grafts reseeded with ACL-derived cells and ACLM powder, and predominant cell types comprising linear to spindle-shaped cells were evident in 8-week biopsies, demonstrating superior cell maturation. Human-antibody tracking analysis confirmed that reseeded ACL-derived cells successfully survived for 4 weeks in the tendon ECM, suggesting that the early graft necrosis phase could be omitted by transplantation of ACL-derived cells combined with ACLM powder.

This study has several limitations. First, as homogeneous injection of cells into the whole tendon graft was not possible, the results may not represent findings across the entire graft tissue. Second, because the ACL donors were mainly young males, the findings may not be generalized to all patient populations. Third, only the results of the early time points (within 8 weeks) were analyzed, and we cannot assume that the complete ligament healing process would be enhanced in the long term. Fourth, the sample size at each time point was small, and the study period was relatively short to determine the mechanical properties of the healing grafts. Finally, small animal models (e.g., Sprague Dawley rats) have different joint biomechanics compared to humans, and response to human-originated cells may vary between species. Therefore, the findings of the current experiment can not be generalized to humans in the context of ligament regeneration.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first report of biological enhancement therapy for ACLR that adopts the concept of autologous ACL-derived cell transplantation. We provided in vitro and in vivo proof of concept that reseeding a tendon graft with ACL-derived cells, and ACLM powder as a biological scaffold for ACL reconstruction holds promise for clinical applications. In conclusion, a tendon graft reseeded with culture-expanded ACL-derived cells and ACLM powders can enhance the graft maturation process and its mechanical properties.
