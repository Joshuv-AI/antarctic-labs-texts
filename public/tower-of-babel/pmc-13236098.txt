Engaging with the All My Relations Advisory Board in a healthy food incentive randomized controlled trial: a qualitative process evaluation
Authors: Yun Yun Lee, Anika Sehgal, Kienan Williams, Sara Scott, Kari Meneen, Patrick Lightning, Jackie Bromley, Laura White, Britt Voaklander, Chelsea Crowshoe, Dana Lee Olstad, David J.T. Campbell, Eldon Spackman, Reed F. Beall, Sharlette Dunn, Teagan Mauthe, Richard T. Oster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The FoodRx randomized controlled trial (RCT) studied the impact of a healthy food incentive program among adults with type 2 diabetes and food insecurity (including an Indigenous subset), and engaged an advisory board (All My Relations [AMR]) to advise on aspects relevant to Indigenous Peoples. We sought to explore the evolution of the board’s role and its contribution and influence on the RCT.Methods:We conducted a qualitative process evaluation within community-based participatory research. We conducted semistructured interviews with AMR board members and FoodRx academic members, and sourced AMR board meeting notes. We analyzed these data iteratively using directed content analysis and continuous engagement with the AMR board.Results:Seven AMR board members, 3 FoodRx academic members, and 4 AMR board members and FoodRx academics participated in interviews. Our analysis showed 4 key interconnected themes: Culture and ceremony (celebrating the importance of Indigenous languages, naming ceremonies, and spiritual practices fostering inclusivity and connection); Transformative environment (emphasizing a sacred gathering space where voices are equal, trust thrives, and personal growth is cultivated); Indigenous Ways of Knowing (embracing the application of Indigenous wise practices, challenging Western research ideals, and advocating for RCT design equity); and Equitable partnership (affirming the centrality of relationship building, acknowledging Indigenous knowledge, redistributing decision-making power, and increasing transparency).Interpretation:Our findings emphasized the importance of authentic involvement of Indigenous community representatives in the conduct of research. Research involving Indigenous participants requires a commitment to respect for Indigenous values and incorporating Indigenous Ways of Knowing, as well as involving Indigenous partners early in the planning of the project.

High-quality and ethical health research with Indigenous Peoples (referred to in a global sense in our introduction, with distinction-based identities specific to this study provided in the Methods section) should be grounded in the notion of relationships and reciprocity to promote safe, meaningful, community-driven, respectful, and culturally appropriate research activities.1–3 Historically, health research involving Indigenous Peoples has largely overlooked their self-determined needs and priorities and has failed to establish the lasting authentic relationships that are essential for ethical research engagement.4–6 Instead, research has often been paternalistic, devoid of meaningful involvement of Indigenous Peoples, and focused on deficits — producing findings with little to no benefit to the Indigenous communities or individuals involved, reinforcing a pattern of extraction, exploitation, and misrepresentation rather than reciprocity. 7,8 This disconnect also reflects an epistemic injustice, as Western scientific paradigms often devalue or dismiss Indigenous Ways of Knowing.9,10

From a Western research perspective, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are generally considered a gold standard study design that generate high-quality evidence to show cause-and-effect relationships between health interventions and outcomes. 11 Randomized controlled trials typically reinforce dominant Western social and economic norms, values, and meanings of health.12 These approaches are often incongruent with Indigenous principles of equity, holism, and empowerment; tend to devalue other types of evidence and knowledge; and have contributed to historical harms perpetrated within Indigenous communities.13–15 In contrast, community-based participatory research (CBPR) approaches are more closely aligned with Indigenous perspectives, principles, and practices.16,17 This includes study designs and objectives that benefit communities, minimize hierarchical relationships, and prioritize building long-term partnerships — centring Indigenous communities’ priorities and needs.16,17 Moreover, Indigenous communities globally often share similar understandings of well-being that are socially, culturally, and environmentally collective in nature and rich with intergenerational thinking — rather than a binary state, wellness for many Indigenous communities is understood as a spiritual, emotional, mental, and physical process.4,18–21 Unsurprisingly, these conceptions clash with Western research paradigms — a tension that has rarely been articulated or acknowledged in Western research12 but has been described by Indigenous scholars for decades.22

In the province of Alberta, the FoodRx RCT — a collaborative effort between academics from the University of Calgary and the University of Alberta — examined a healthy food incentive program among adults with type 2 diabetes who were experiencing food insecurity and persistent hyperglycemia.23 Prior to RCT deployment, we established an advisory board (All My Relations [AMR]) to advise, as a subset of RCT participants identified as Indigenous. We sought to understand the principles, practices, approaches, and outcomes that were essential to the board’s attempts at moving beyond mere “cultural sensitivity” to meaningfully incorporating Indigenous Ways of Knowing into a complex RCT.

Methods

We conducted this qualitative study in Alberta, Canada, concurrently with the FoodRx RCT.

All My Relations Advisory Board

In 2020, before the implementation of the FoodRx trial, the advisory board was developed with the shared aim of informing the research processes within the RCT to help enhance the safety of Indigenous participants and to uphold principles such as relevance, reciprocity, respect, and equity throughout. Members included a Cree Elder and person with lived experience with type 2 diabetes, a Blackfoot Elder and person with lived experience with type 2 diabetes, an Anishnawbek scholar, a Cree/Métis/settler health care provider and scholar, a Cree health care provider, a Blackfoot scholar and health systems professional, a Métis scholar, and 5 non-Indigenous health care professionals or researchers. The First Nations Principles of OCAP (ownership, control, access, and possession of data)24 and the Principles of Ethical Métis Research25 helped inform how the board worked together and treated one another, as well as the board’s sovereignty over their discussions and ideas. After careful consideration, involved Elders chose an appropriate name for the board: Niiksokowaaks (Blackfoot), Wahkohmakanahk (Cree), and All My Relations (English). This name has multiple spiritual meanings from Cree, Blackfoot, and Métis perspectives and is considered to have a strong inter-Nation Indigenous ethic to contribute to fostering connections among members from diverse backgrounds.26 The AMR board functioned through recurring online meetings (around every 6 weeks) and made regular recommendations for the FoodRx academics to improve the cultural safety of the RCT. At least 1 in-person meeting was held each year of the project. The decision-making power of the board, and its position within the organizational structure of FoodRx, was not well-defined. In hindsight, this was an oversight. That said, recommendations of the AMR board led to numerous changes to the RCT protocol and processes. Further details on the development and functioning of the AMR board can be found in Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250735/tab-related-content.

Study design, setting, and participant recruitment

We used qualitative process evaluation27 within a CBPR approach. 28–34 Within RCTs, qualitative process evaluations are becoming increasingly common as researchers grasp the importance of context and environment for understanding the success of interventions.35–37 We based our qualitative process evaluation on guidance from the AMR board. We shaped our method further based on a recent qualitative process evaluation that included Indigenous participants and prioritized establishing genuine partnerships rooted in an understanding of Indigenous self-determination, ownership, governance, and data sovereignty.38 Specifically, Beks and colleagues38 inspired decisions around approaches to data generation, data analysis, and rigour. We used CBPR principles with Indigenous communities as an overarching guiding framework acknowledging community members as experts in their own context;28–34 thus, control was situated within, and the research was facilitated through, ongoing close partnerships of AMR board members.

All My Relations board members resided across Treaty Areas 6, 7, and 8 of Alberta. We performed total population sampling to recruit participants, including all AMR board members, who attended meetings regularly. Similarly, we recruited FoodRx academics (individuals with leadership roles in the RCT — namely, the study coordinator and co–principal investigators). We approached potential participants by telephone and email. Potential participants included 8 AMR board members, 3 FoodRx academics, and 4 individuals who were considered both AMR board members and FoodRx academics.

Data collection

Data for this study were generated between September 2020 and January 2023, which aligned approximately with the grant proposal development phase to the final completion of data collection phases of the FoodRx lifecycle. We used records of meeting notes from AMR board meetings (September 2020 to January 2023), and transcripts of semistructured interviews with AMR board members and FoodRx academics (collected August 2022 to January 2023). Authors K.W., R.T.O., and Y.Y.L. developed 2 semi-structured interview guides specific to each participant group that were iteratively revised through discussion with the AMR board to explore concepts emerging from the data (Appendix 2, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250735/tab-related-content). We pilot tested interview guides. Questions specifically addressed the process by which the AMR board functioned, how it informed and shaped the RCT, and what facilitators and barriers were apparent. Author Y.Y.L. conducted the interviews; at the time of the study, she was hired as a research assistant, having experience and training in conducting qualitative interviews. She was not involved in the implementation of the FoodRx RCT or the initial recruitment of the AMR board. However, in keeping with a CBPR approach and to facilitate interviews, she sought to develop genuine relationships and be transparent about her positionality. Participants were given the option of having a virtual or face-to-face interview. Interviews were audio-recorded. All research participants provided written informed consent. We used Otter software version 2 to transcribe interviews and NVivo 12 to code the transcripts and meeting notes.39

Data analysis

The AMR board collaboratively agreed on its purpose and approach, and led all stages of the research. At the requirement of the AMR board, the board members co-owned all generated data, with secure storage at the University of Alberta.

Data analysis was an iterative and interactive process, concurrent with data collection and occurring between August 2022 and April 2024. We used directed content analysis in line with principles of CBPR as an overarching framework to analyze the data.30,40 For example, board members and participants had equal say in how data were interpreted, the final analysis was meant to directly inform action for intervention planning including Indigenous peoples, and transparency about the authors’ own identities (positionality) formed the basis for analyzing and interpreting the data to safeguard against potential researcher bias and stigma. Author Y.Y.L. coded the first 3 interviews and first 3 meeting notes. Authors Y.Y.L. and R.T.O. subsequently discussed possible emerging concepts and varying perspectives of the coded data, after which the codebook was finalized. Once the codebook was finalized, Y.Y.L. coded the remaining interviews and meeting notes, organized and analyzed codes, and generated subthemes and themes, which were further developed and refined after discussion with R.T.O. and AMR board members. Participants were provided with the initial findings and the opportunity to provide feedback; this was done through a combination of written format (over email) and a series of oral presentations. Data saturation was discussed by Y.Y.L. and R.T.O., as well as with the AMR board. The AMR board considered it was important to interview all potential participants and give everyone a voice, even if data saturation was reached.

Strategies to enhance rigour included maintaining reflexive notes, recording field notes after each interview, and keeping a detailed audit trail throughout the research process. Investigator triangulation with the research team (when initial findings were defined), regular debriefing meetings with R.T.O. during data generation and analysis, and a discussion of the initial findings with the AMR board was conducted. Rigour from an Indigenous perspective was enhanced by careful adherence to the First Nations Principles of OCAP,24 the Principles of Ethical Métis Research,25 and CBPR processes,28,33 and by frequently seeking advice from the AMR board.

Reflexivity

The research team was made up of a mix of AMR board members, FoodRx academics, and hired research staff. To further foster transparency, respect, and reciprocity within the research process, the individual positionality of authors is provided in Appendix 1. Reflexivity was an individual and collective process in which the research team critically and continuously examined their own beliefs, biases, backgrounds, and assumptions, and how these influence the entire research process. Authors Y.Y.L. and R.T.O. carried out most of the data generation and analysis and centred the reflexive process on their own cultural background, assumptions, beliefs, biases, and lived experiences; their role within the historical and ongoing context of colonialization; personal and professional reasons for conducting research with Indigenous Peoples; and the role of researchers on the nature of dialogue. We specifically sought to contribute to the Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada’s42 Call to Action number 20: “[W]e call upon the federal government to recognize, respect, and address the distinct health needs of the Métis, Inuit, and off-reserve Aboriginal peoples.”

Ethics approval

The University of Calgary’s Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB19–0344) granted ethics approval for this study. The ethics board adheres to the Tri-Council Policy Statement 2, Chapter 9: Research Involving the First Nations, Inuit, and Métis Peoples of Canada.41

Results

Out of the 15 potential participants, 14 took part in interviews (including 7 AMR board members, 3 FoodRx academics, and 4 people who were both AMR board members and FoodRx academics). Seven participants self-identified as Indigenous (2 Cree, 2 Blackfoot, 1 Anishnawbek, 1 Métis, and 1 Cree/Métis/settler), 2 of whom were Elders. We conducted most interviews virtually, with a single face-to-face interview. Interviews lasted 30 to 60 minutes. We analyzed meeting notes from 22 AMR board meetings.

We generated 4 key themes and subthemes describing the principles, practices, approaches, and outcomes that were deemed essential from the perspective of the AMR board. The first theme was Culture and ceremony, which described the profound role of language and how experiences of Indigenous community fostered relational connection and mutual respect between the board and FoodRx academics. The second theme was Transformative environment and reflected how the AMR board prioritized cultivation of a sacred gathering space that was absent of hierarchy, and nurtured open dialogue and mutual learning and unlearning, thereby fostering personal growth for all board members and those working with the board. The third theme was Indigenous Ways of Knowing, which emphasized Indigenous wise practices in conducting research that aligns with Indigenous values, ethics, and sovereignty, and equity in research by advocating for ethical research methodologies. The final theme was Equitable partnership, which outlined the board’s charge to affirm the centrality of relationship building in research with Indigenous partners, acknowledging Indigenous knowledge, redistributing decision-making power, and increasing transparency for more collaborative decision-making.

Language matters

Members of the AMR board indicated that the languages they spoke to one another and the ways people or things were identified were a form of cultivating inclusivity and connection. Once the board was formed, AMR board members collectively agreed to replace what was perceived as a scientific, technical name for the group (“Indigenous Subcommittee”) with a name that represented their aim of working together in harmony between Indigenous Peoples and Western researchers. Through ongoing discussions, and wisdom and guidance provided by Elders on the board, the board was renamed: Niiksokowaaks (Blackfoot), Wahkohmakanahk (Cree), and All My Relations (English). All My Relations carries multiple spiritual meanings from both tribes’ perspectives. The Elders’ intentions for selecting this name were to instill the notion that all of those involved in the RCT — study participants, AMR board members, and FoodRx academics — are interconnected and look after one another in a familial way. This was further fostered in meetings and in-person gatherings where all members were encouraged to speak their languages. Elders often spoke and prayed in Cree and Blackfoot, providing translations for other board members where appropriate. The concept of inclusivity continued to resonate throughout the work of the AMR board, including this manuscript.

Experiencing community

Members of the AMR board and several FoodRx academics were invited to participate and experience community through Cree and Blackfoot practices regularly at each meeting. The Elders and Indigenous members of the AMR board offered unique ways for all members to experience community during online meetings, such as Elders encouraging members to light their own smudge in their offices during opening prayers, traditional singing by Elders, Elders sharing oral teachings, and everyone on the board regularly reciting the group’s name in Cree and Blackfoot at each online meeting. Moreover, about half of each online meeting was dedicated to strengthening relationships through group members sharing personal reflections, “catching up” with one another personally, and joking and teasing. What were perceived, at first, as ritual-based practices that were performed as part of a routine became perceived as ceremony-based practices. According to the Elders, when traditional practices become ceremony, it deepens one’s connection with others and oneself. Participants expressed feeling grounded by prayer, smudging ceremonies, traditional singing by Elders, Elder oral teachings, and reciting the group’s name in Cree and Blackfoot. Group reflections were viewed by meeting attendees as opportunities to establish heart-to-heart connections that extended beyond formal meetings. Informal gatherings outside of “work” included invitations to participate in cultural experiences, such as Sundance and Sweat Lodge ceremonies. Many participants conveyed that these informal and formal gatherings demonstrated ways in which research is as much a social and emotional involvement as it is academic.

Sacred gathering space

Members of the AMR board often described the meeting atmosphere as equalizing and having a sense of personal connection at meetings. Participants felt that their input was equally valued regardless of their position in academia, work, or society. Participants described feeling safe contributing their thoughts and opinions without fear or shame, because they did not perceive hierarchy within the group. Participants often contrasted this group dynamic with other work-related meetings in which discussion was centred on accomplishing the task at hand and only speaking when asked to.

Personal growth

Both AMR board members and FoodRx academics described experiencing personal growth connected to the board, particularly from the wisdom they received from the Elders. Elders shared their life experiences, lived experiences in their communities, and knowledge and stories passed down from ancestors. Participants expressed feeling humbled by their journey with the board and noted learning and unlearning of personal biases and knowledge, particularly about Indigenous Peoples’ culture, history, and Ways of Knowing.

Wise practices

Many participants expressed that the Elders played a vital role in sharing their knowledge of past harms and hurt from mistreatment in research, which offered opportunities for non-Indigenous members and academics to learn, understand, and reflect. In contrast to best practices in RCT research, which aims to establish universality or standardization, wise practices emphasize holistic approaches that are grounded in culturally appropriate, ethical, and collaborative approaches that uphold Indigenous knowledge systems and sovereignty. Wise practices are approaches rooted in Indigenous Ways of Knowing, values and traditions, recognizing that each Indigenous community holds unique wisdom, cultural practices, languages, and world views.

The AMR board’s guidance, rooted in the wisdom of the Elders, was essential to ensure cultural safety and respect, and to establish meaningful participant engagement in the FoodRx RCT. The board conducted an extensive review of FoodRx participant-facing materials and consent processes to ensure strengths-based framing, cultural relevance, inclusion of Cree and Blackfoot language where appropriate, and sensitivity to intergenerational trauma related to research. Critical input from the board on the RCT’s intervention processes helped avoid the use of triggering language and images in study documents and protocols, and identify potentially shame-inducing moments Indigenous participants might encounter and proposed strategies to mitigate them. Participants (non-Indigenous and Indigenous) acknowledged that implementing wise practices focused their approach on humanizing the study, often by redirecting conversations.

Advocating for equity

Discussions among AMR board members and FoodRx academics during meetings highlighted the contrasting methodologies of conducting research through an Indigenous lens compared with a Western research lens. Indigenous approaches to research emphasize ethical considerations that position RCTs as contrary to Indigenous world views, cultural values, and beliefs. Randomization to a control group in the RCT was a key ethical challenge for the AMR board because potentially beneficial interventions may be withheld from participants. In FoodRx, the study design stipulated that food insecure households in the control group did not receive a subsidy designed to enhance household purchasing power to afford more food, while those in the intervention group did. The board perceived this as an inequitable and potentially harmful practice, particularly when the intervention may reduce food insecurity.

Members approached differing world views as opportunities to bridge gaps in understanding and misunderstanding through open and respectful discussion. Being able to engage in “peaceful discussions” invited opportunities to discuss Indigenous cultural practices and the misrepresentation of Indigenous research approaches by Western researchers (e.g., that Indigenous research approaches are “fluffy” or not as rigorous).

Relationship building

Elders on the AMR board taught the group that Indigenous world views often share a common perspective of bringing together people for the collective purpose of bettering our shared world. Once assembled, board meeting facilitators prioritized relationship building as foundational to conducting research in a good way to foster trust and a sense of collective responsibility when working together. Meeting attendees expressed looking forward to participating in AMR board meetings and valuing informal conversations at meetings.

A mark of reconciliation

Study participants expressed that they appreciated their experiences of understanding, respect, and collaboration were reconciliatory and created opportunities for deep learning. They perceived that the next step toward reconciliation was the need to equalize the value of Indigenous knowledge with Western research by recognizing the value of Elders and integrating them as part of the research team. Some AMR board members challenged the thinking that Indigenous knowledge, which has been passed down for centuries or more, is less credible than Western scientific methods.

Transparency

Most AMR board members wished that there had been more clarity as to what aspects of the RCT process they were expected to have a say in and perceived that areas of the study they could consult on were delegated by FoodRx academics. Members expressed that this top–down approach felt perpetuated when the final decision to integrate AMR board recommendations resided with FoodRx academics, as opposed to collective decision-making. In this way, some AMR board members described that the board’s existence, in some instances, felt like tokenism. Several AMR board members suggested increasing interactions between AMR and the FoodRx team to mitigate potential notions of a top–down approach. Importantly, many participants felt that, in future RCTs of this nature, developing and integrating a board, such as AMR, should be done as early as possible in the research process.

Interpretation

We found 4 key themes that characterized the priorities of an advisory board (of Indigenous individuals with lived experience of type 2 diabetes, Indigenous scholars, health care providers with lived experience working with Indigenous patients with type 2 diabetes, and non-Indigenous allies within Indigenous health) formed as part of the FoodRx RCT, which studied the impact of a healthy food incentive program among adults with type 2 diabetes and food insecurity. The board was formed with the aim of fostering reconciliation with Indigenous Peoples throughout the course of the FoodRx RCT, and our qualitative study highlights invaluable insights into what is important to ensure a community-driven and community-led approach to such research. We found that integrated culture and ceremony helped to create a transformative environment, heightened respect for Indigenous Ways of Knowing, and emphasized a need for more equitable partnership. Our study also showed that structural and procedural limitations existed in interactions between the AMR board and FoodRx team.

In Western epistemology, RCTs are commonly regarded as the pinnacle of scientific rigour and reliability.43,44 Nevertheless, no RCT has proven suitable for evaluating all dimensions of intervention effectiveness.45 Within Indigenous frameworks, the RCT diverges considerably from traditional perceptions of physical, social, and emotional well-being.46,47 Our findings demonstrate support for approaches to conducting RCTs that include Indigenous participants in a “good way”48 — that is, when studies are community-driven, relevant, and reciprocal. The AMR board aligned itself with the Calls to Action for Truth and Reconciliation, 42 integrating Indigenous voices and wisdom, fostering genuine relationships between Indigenous community representatives and researchers, and challenging traditional power dynamics that have often targeted Indigenous communities.

The integration of Indigenous languages into the AMR board’s activities supported the centring of Indigenous knowledge systems and community-driven education. Language is a powerful medium to promote both inclusivity and connection. In Indigenous world views, it is said that “language is land and land is language,” whereby language is inherently tied to the way in which one understands and relates to the world.49 Language is a tool for preserving and transmitting essential cultural knowledge and can help to foster cultural diversity.50 Indigenous languages in postcolonial contexts continue to face suppression owing to structural and systemic barriers.51,52

Incorporating traditional practices of board members transformed research into ceremony, creating a sense of community within the board but also inviting all involved to participate in personal, communal, cultural, and spiritual experiences. The importance of ceremony is multifaceted and highlights the emotional involvement, heart-to-heart connections, and trust inherent in research processes done in a good way.53 Furthermore, the concept of a sacred gathering space within meetings reflects a climate in which every participant’s input is valued. In contrast with research settings commonly found in Western research methodologies, our findings underscore that the emphasis on relationship building and sharing of wisdom by Elders can allow for the unlearning of biases within this transformative environment. 54 The focus on wise practices, unique to each Indigenous community, rather than a one-size-fits-all “best practice” approach, underscores a commitment to acknowledging diverse types of Indigenous knowledge. The incorporation of Cree and Blackfoot perspectives into the study protocols and materials reflected a conscious effort to advance reconciliation and help ensure cultural safety.

The board members’ emphasis on transparency, valuing Indigenous knowledge, and authentic relationship building served as a foundation for fostering more inclusive and respectful collaborations. By embracing Indigenous Ways of Knowing, research endeavours can better serve Indigenous Peoples and communities, ensuring that research processes are not only culturally sensitive but also mutually beneficial.55,56 The successes of the AMR board in helping to foster collaboration reflects the importance of combining lived experience expertise with learned expertise.57 The advocacy for equity within research designs, as evidenced by the AMR board’s discussions around RCTs, should prompt a re-evaluation of established methodologies, encouraging researchers to consider alternative approaches that align with Indigenous values.

The AMR board was not a complete success in incorporating Indigenous Ways of Knowing into the FoodRx trial. Our evaluation revealed some frustration expressed by AMR board members regarding tokenism and suggestions that the board would have functioned better had its inception occurred earlier in the RCT process, with increased interactions between board and research team, the development of a collective decision-making process, and co-determination of organizational structures. Uncertainty and obscurity of the board’s positioning within the organizational structure of the RCT and the decision-making process for incorporating the board’s recommendations contributed to feelings of being tokenized and at the behest of hierarchical approaches. Our evaluation would support a commitment for future trials that include Indigenous populations to involve Indigenous communities and partners meaningfully and transparently, as early as possible in the research process and throughout, with clear terms of reference from the outset and equal and explicit decision-making power. Such proactive engagement can help cultivate a sense of ownership and empowerment, fostering a more collaborative and reciprocal relationship between researchers and the community.54 An ongoing commitment to transparency, where Indigenous voices have a more substantial influence in decision-making processes, can mitigate concerns of tokenism and ensure that research truly reflects the needs and aspirations of the community.34

Limitations

Although efforts were made to ensure diverse representation within the AMR board, we did not have equal representation from all nations and groups within the province, which could have affected the applicability of findings to other regions or populations. Moreover, unique distinctions between Indigenous Peoples across Canada and globally limit the transferability of our results to other work with Indigenous Peoples. Potential biases associated with authors’ involvement in the FoodRx trial likely exist that would affect the current evaluation of the AMR board. However, CBPR approaches often embrace the reality of bias among Western researchers with respect to Indigenous groups as inherent, and authors engaged in self-reflective positionality regarding their identity and worldview and its influence on the research process (Appendix 1). Despite these limitations, the study underscores the importance of community-driven research approaches and can offer valuable insights for future endeavours in fostering inclusive and respectful collaborations with Indigenous Peoples and communities.

Conclusion

We assessed the extent to which an advisory board associated with an RCT conducted in Alberta contributed to meaningful collaboration with Indigenous communities in the conduct of the trial. Our findings emphasized the importance of culture and ceremony, transformative environment, Indigenous Ways of Knowing, and becoming equitable partners. Such endeavours should never be perceived as a “checkbox,” but rather a deep commitment and journey requiring humility and mutual respect, and an investment of time and effort, involving Indigenous partners early in the planning of research and committing to equity in valuing knowledge.
