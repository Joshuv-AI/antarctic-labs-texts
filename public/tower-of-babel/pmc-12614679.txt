Health System Factors Influencing Availability and Access to Comprehensive Care for Cleft Lip, Palate, and Craniofacial Anomalies in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania
Authors: Adelaida Mghase, Meshack Brighton, Hussein Cheyo, Edwin Mrema, Ibrahim Mkoma, Vincent Were, Martin Bundi, Elizabeth Matey, Seif Wibonela, Laurean Rwanyuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Cleft lip and palate are common craniofacial anomalies. In low- and middle-income countries, patients with these conditions face barriers to comprehensive care. In Tanzania, despite some hospitals performing surgical procedures, the health system factors affecting access to full cleft care remain unclear. This study aimed to assess the health system factors influencing the availability and access to comprehensive care for cleft lip, palate, and associated craniofacial anomalies in Tanzania.Methods:A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across 37 health facilities selected based on their capacity to provide cleft care services. Facility readiness, availability of specialized personnel, infrastructure, and financing were assessed through structured questionnaires administered to health facility managers. Data were analyzed descriptively, and cross-tabulations were performed to determine predictors of cleft care availability and accessibility.Results:Among the 37 facilities surveyed, 89.7% (33 of 37) could diagnose cleft lip and palate; however, only 24% (9 of 37) provided comprehensive management. There was a severe shortage of specialized personnel required for comprehensive cleft care. Plastic surgeons were available in only 3 out of 37 (8.1%) hospitals surveyed. Speech therapists were available in only 2 facilities (5.4%), and only 1 public hospital offered speech therapy services. Limited financial resources and inadequate equipment were major barriers to providing comprehensive care.Conclusions:Although many Tanzanian health facilities can diagnose cleft lip and palate, few provide comprehensive care. Addressing these gaps requires targeted government funding, expanded local surgeon training programs, improved retention strategies, structured referral pathways, and investment in a multidisciplinary approach for cleft care services.

Takeaways

Question: What health system barriers affect access to comprehensive cleft care in Tanzania?

Findings: Most facilities can diagnose cleft lip and palate, but very few provide comprehensive care due to a lack of specialists, inadequate equipment, and limited funding.

Meaning: Strengthening referral pathways and investing in local training programs and multidisciplinary care are critical to improving cleft services in low-resource settings.

INTRODUCTION

Clefts of the lip, palate, or both are among the most common congenital craniofacial anomalies, accounting for approximately 65% of all head and neck anomalies.1 The prevalence of cleft lip and palate (CLP) is higher among Asians and Native Americans compared with Africans.2 Some patients may present with craniofacial anomalies alongside cleft lip and/or palate, such as Pierre Robin sequence, craniofacial microsomia, midface hypoplasia, hemifacial microsomia, and Treacher Collins syndrome.3 Comprehensive care for cleft lip, palate, and associated anomalies demands a well-organized multidisciplinary team that includes surgeons, speech therapists, orthodontists, and other specialists to address the complex functional and aesthetic needs of affected individuals.3–6 Given the limited understanding of health system factors affecting comprehensive cleft care in Tanzania, this research is significant, as it represents the first multicenter assessment of CLP care in Tanzania, aiming to provide a broader perspective on the challenges affecting access to comprehensive services within the local context. To achieve this objective, a cross-sectional study was conducted across 37 health facilities in Dar es Salaam, assessing facility readiness, availability of specialized personnel, infrastructure, and financing. This targeted approach enabled the evaluation of selected health system factors most relevant to cleft care delivery. Specifically, the study selectively applied 4 of the 6 World Health Organization (WHO) health system building blocks: service delivery, health workforce, health financing, and medical products/infrastructure. Leadership/governance and health information systems were beyond the scope of this study due to data limitations. These building blocks, as emphasized by the WHO in addressing the Sustainable Development Goals, are essential for equitable healthcare access.7–9 CLP services are scantily available in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and in some places where they do exist, they are provided by nonprofit organizations, private facilities, and tertiary public hospitals. Delivery of comprehensive cleft care (cleft lip, palate, and associated anomalies), particularly in LMICs, experiences substantial health system barriers.10–12 In sub-Saharan Africa, children born with CLP undergo surgery late due to inefficient health systems and significant challenges in public hospitals.13

The lack of comprehensive cleft care, which may require up to 15 years of treatment, contributes to challenges such as high patient dropout rates due to travel and hospital costs.14 In many LMICs, CLP and craniofacial anomaly management are often underprioritized, relying heavily on the availability of donor funding for resources, healthcare provider training, community awareness programs, and surgical materials.6

Although there have been some improvements in the availability of CLP services, their equitable accessibility to many people remains limited. Health system factors are posited as a significant contributor to this situation.2 In Tanzania, some hospitals perform CLP surgery, but little is known about the health system factors affecting access to comprehensive care for these craniofacial anomalies.1,2,5 Therefore, this study aimed to assess the health system factors influencing access to comprehensive cleft care in public and private health facilities in Dar es Salaam.

METHODOLOGY

Study Design

The cross-sectional study used a quantitative approach to assess health system factors affecting access to comprehensive cleft care in public and private health facilities in Dar es Salaam.

Study Settings

The study was conducted in selected public and private health facilities in Dar es Salaam, chosen for their capacity, level of care, and geographic location, to represent facilities offering comprehensive cleft care. The study population consisted of all health facility managers.

Inclusion Criteria

Public and private health facilities in the Dar es Salaam region that are considered capable of providing cleft care services were included.

Exclusion Criteria

Newly established health facilities and facilities that did not provide consent to participate in the study were excluded.

Sampling Technique

The study used a convenience sampling method to select health facilities in Dar es Salaam. Data were collected using questionnaires with structured questions administered to health facility managers.

Data Collection Tools and Techniques

Data collection involved a survey tool adapted from WHO health system tools, administered by the research assistant to the health facility managers. This survey aimed to gather comprehensive data on the availability and readiness of facilities for comprehensive cleft care services by assessing service delivery and measuring improvements in health outcomes.

Data Management and Analysis

Field-collected information was stored on a password-protected computer and analyzed using Stata version 15, with descriptive analysis presented in frequency tables. The availability of services (surgical, dental, speech therapy) and specialized staff in public and private health facilities was summarized using descriptive statistics, whereas linear regression and factor analysis were used to determine variable associations. Chi-square tests or t tests compared service and resource proportions, with significance set at a P value of 0.05. Survey data were analyzed for the frequency and percentage of patients using various cleft care services, and comparative analysis assessed usage rates between public and private facilities. Logistic regression identified significant predictors of service usage, focusing on accessibility, affordability, and surgeon availability. Descriptive statistics calculated readiness and responsiveness scores based on infrastructure, equipment, staffing, and protocols, with comparisons made between facility types. Gap analysis compared these scores with benchmarks, highlighting areas for improvement through comparative charts and tables.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from Muhimbili National Hospital and the National Institute for Medical Research institutional review boards (approval number: NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol.IX/4355). Informed consent was sought from the administrative leaders of the relevant departments of hospitals included in the study.

RESULTS

A total of 37 health facilities (6 public and 31 private) were included in the study. Regarding the availability of comprehensive care for cleft lip, palate, and associated anomalies, 33 out of 37 (89.2%) facilities reported the ability to diagnose CLP (5 public and 28 private facilities). Meanwhile, 32 facilities reported diagnosing congenital anomalies, with 6 out of 32 (18.8%) public and 26 out of 32 (81.3%) private.

All public facilities and 26 out of 31 (33.8%) private facilities had cases of congenital anomalies. Among the included facilities, 9 manage CLP cases, of which 2 are public and 7 are private facilities. Two private and 1 public facility offer speech therapy services.

Only 3 out of the 37 facilities have access to a plastic surgeon: 2 private facilities and 1 public facility. All 37 facilities have functioning theaters and provide pediatric surgery services. Furthermore, 36 hospitals reported improved availability and functionality of essential equipment. Use of Comprehensive Cleft Care Service Offered by Public and Private Health Facilities

The analysis of service usage across facilities revealed notable trends. For diagnosing CLP, 15.2% of facilities with the service used it, compared with none without (P = 0.403), showing no significant difference. Similarly, 15.6% of facilities with the service diagnosed associated congenital anomalies, whereas none without did (P = 0.342). In contrast, 60.0% of facilities offering genetic counseling used the service, versus only 6.3% without (P = 0.001), indicating a significant difference. Plastic surgeon availability showed a stark contrast, with 100% use in facilities offering the service compared with 5.9% without (P < 0.001). Additionally, inpatient management, speech therapy, and cleft surgery had 100% usage in facilities with these services, whereas those without had significantly lower rates (P < 0.001). Some facilities lacking these services reported outsourcing care, highlighting a reliance on external support (Table 1).

Health System Factors Affecting the Use of Comprehensive Care of CLP

Among factors influencing the usage of comprehensive cleft/lip and palate care, Regional referral hospitals were significantly more likely to use these services compared with district-level facilities (odds ratio [OR] = 3.0; 95% CI, 2.14–4.21; P = 0.012). Tertiary facilities had a higher OR of 3.2 (95% CI, 2.1–10.3; P = 0.998), but this was not significant. No significant differences were found between private and public facilities (OR = 1.35; 95% CI, 0.12–14.7; P = 0.806), the presence of a plastic surgeon (OR = 2.5; 95% CI, 2.32–23.9; P = 0.999), readiness and responsiveness (OR = 1.1; 95% CI, 0.16–7.6; P = 0.915), or government financial support (OR = 4.5; 95% CI, 0.63–31.9; P = 0.133). Additionally, facilities funded by the government (OR = 0.19; 95% CI, 0.02–1.7; P = 0.138) or donors (OR = 0.07; 95% CI, 0.03–1.7; P = 0.107) did not differ significantly from self-funded facilities (Table 2).

Readiness and Responsiveness of Public and Private Health Facilities to Provide Comprehensive Care for CLP and Associated Anomalies

The readiness and responsiveness of public and private health facilities to provide comprehensive care for cleft lip, palate, and associated anomalies were assessed. Ambulance availability showed a significant difference (P = 0.037), with 26.1% of public facilities (6 out of 23) and 73.9% of private facilities (17 out of 23) reporting availability. Access to an ambulance elsewhere was similar across public (17.1%, 6 out of 35) and private (82.9%, 29 out of 35) facilities, with no significant difference (P = 0.522). The availability of a children’s blood pressure machine approached significance (P = 0.087), with 26.3% of public facilities (5 out of 19) and 73.7% of private facilities (14 out of 19) having one. Oxygen concentrators were available in 16.7% of public facilities (6 out of 36) and 83.3% of private facilities (30 out of 36), with no significant difference (P = 0.656). Fuel and generators were available in all facilities, and all reported good participation in community activities (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The study assessed the availability, use, and factors influencing access to comprehensive care for cleft lip, palate, and associated craniofacial anomalies in public and private health facilities. Results showed that most facilities could diagnose CLP, with a notable disparity between public and private facilities in diagnosing congenital craniofacial anomalies. Only 9 facilities managed CLP cases, with a limited number offering speech therapy and plastic surgery services. Usage rates of services such as genetic counseling, plastic surgery, and inpatient management were significantly higher in facilities that offered these services. Factors influencing service use included facility type, with regional and tertiary referral hospitals using comprehensive care more than district-level facilities. The presence of a plastic surgeon was also crucial for service use, but financial and governance factors showed no significant differences. The study highlighted the readiness of private facilities to provide comprehensive care, especially in terms of equipment and staffing, compared with public ones.

In sub-Saharan Africa, healthcare infrastructure and access to specialized services vary considerably across countries. The study’s findings from the surveyed region reveal a relatively high capacity for diagnosing CLP. Similar observations were reported in studies conducted in Uganda, Nigeria, and Kenya.15–17 In this study, approximately 89% of surveyed institutions could diagnose CLP conditions. This finding was higher compared with a study conducted in South Africa, which found that only about 30% of facilities could diagnose these conditions. The discrepancy between the diagnostic capacity in the current study (urban-based) and the South African study may be attributed to differences in sample size, geographic context, and healthcare infrastructure. The challenges highlighted include a lack of trained personnel, financial constraints, and a lack of diagnostic equipment, particularly in rural areas.17,18

When comparing these results to high-income countries, significant disparities become evident. In developed countries, the availability of comprehensive care for CLP, including diagnostic services, surgical management, and postoperative care, is typically much higher.18 For instance, in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Germany, specialized cleft care centers are prevalent, and almost all hospitals can diagnose and treat cleft conditions.19

The current study revealed a notable shortage of plastic surgeons and other allied health specialists, such as speech therapists and nutritionists. Our findings show that no facility reported having a dedicated cleft care coordinator. Introducing cleft care coordinators could enhance patient tracking, streamline referrals, and improve continuity of care. This issue reflects broader challenges faced across low- and middle-income countries, including regions in East, Southern, and Western Africa, as well as East Asia and South America.19–21 Within these areas, significant disparities in healthcare availability are evident. For example, in South Africa, although there is a higher concentration of specialized healthcare professionals compared with other regions, access to these services remains uneven.17 Urban centers tend to have greater availability of specialized professionals, whereas rural areas are often underserved, leading to significant healthcare disparities within the country.19

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

This study represented the first multicenter assessment of CLP care in Tanzania, offering a broader perspective on access and availability across both public and private health facilities. However, its generalizability is limited due to the use of convenience sampling and a relatively small sample size, which may affect the robustness of the findings. Although the study was guided by the WHO health system framework, only 4 of the 6 building blocks were assessed in detail: service delivery, workforce, financing, and infrastructure. Leadership/governance and health information systems were not evaluated due to the lack of access to relevant administrative and digital data at the facility level.

In addition, practical operational indicators such as the number of surgical beds, operating theaters, and distance traveled by patients to access care were not included in the survey. These variables are important for clinical decision-making and may be explored in future studies to enhance the relevance of findings to surgical professionals.

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

This study highlighted significant disparities in the diagnosis and management of CLP in Tanzania, including limited equipment, financial constraints, and shortages of specialized professionals. These challenges slow comprehensive care, particularly in rural areas. Addressing the identified gaps requires a diverse approach. It is essential to enhance training for local surgeons, speech therapists, nutritionists, orthodontists, and other specialists.

Further research is needed to identify specific gaps in the availability of a multidisciplinary team. Expanding outreach services and telemedicine could help bridge access in underserved areas, particularly given the urban concentration of existing services. Addressing financial constraints through targeted government funding is needed for healthcare infrastructure and specialist training.
