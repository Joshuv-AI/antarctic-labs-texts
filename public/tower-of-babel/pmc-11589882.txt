Volatile metabolomics and metagenomics reveal the effects of lactic acid bacteria on alfalfa silage quality, microbial communities, and volatile organic compounds
Authors: Yichao Liu, Shuai Du, Lin Sun, Yuyu Li, Mingjian Liu, Pengbo Sun, Baochao Bai, Gentu Ge, Yushan Jia, Zhijun Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Lactic acid bacteria metabolism affects the composition of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in alfalfa silage, which results in differences of odor and quality. The aim of this study was to reveal the effects of commercial Lactobacillus plantarum (CL), screened Lactobacillus plantarum (LP), and screened Pediococcus pentosaceus (PP) on quality, microbial community, and VOCs of alfalfa silage based on volatile metabolomics and metagenomics. The results showed that the LP and PP groups had higher sensory and quality grades, and the dominant bacteria were Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus. The main VOCs in alfalfa silage were terpenoids (25.29%), esters (17.08%), and heterocyclic compounds (14.43%), and esters such as methyl benzoate, ethyl benzoate, and ethyl salicylate were significantly increased in the LP and PP groups (P < 0.05). Correlation analysis showed that terpenoids, esters, and alcohols with aromatic odors were positively correlated with Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus. Microbial functions in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and degradation of aromatic compounds were significantly enriched. In conclusion, the addition of lactic acid bacteria can increase the aromatic substances in silage and further improve silage odor and quality.

Introduction

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.) is one of the most commonly cultivated forage plants in the world and is widely used in animal husbandry. Silage is a feed preservation method in which plants are harvested, chopped, and stored in a sealed enclosure, and the quality of silage has a direct impact on livestock performance and health1. The odor of silage is one of the most important indicators of its quality, with good-quality alfalfa silage usually having a strong acidic aroma, which indicates better retention of nutrients. Conversely, alfalfa silage that smells moldy or has an off flavor may indicate a problem during storage, resulting in reduced forage quality and the production of toxic and harmful substances2. The smell of silage comes from VOCs, and a sour aroma can stimulate the appetite of the livestock and promote active consumption, while a musty or foul odor can reduce the livestock feed intake. Harmful substances associated with a foul odor can not only affect the growth of cows and the quality of livestock products but also pose a threat to human health. Scherer et al.3 found that ruminants’ preference for forage at the start of feeding changed depending on the odor emitted by the silage. The VOCs in silage not only directly affect the feeding behavior of livestock but also affect the growth and production performance of livestock and the quality of livestock products through metabolism. Manzocchi et al.4 found that feeding cows on pasture with different hay, silage, and fresh feed affects the sensory properties and chemical composition of milk, cheeses from cows fed fresh herbage had more intense barnyard and dry fruit flavors, were perceived as creamier, and had less lactic odor. Therefore, improving the smell of silage is of great significance for feed quality and livestock production.

Lactic acid bacteria are a common additive to improve the quality of silage because lactic acid bacteria are the main microorganisms that convert soluble sugars in raw materials into lactic acid during fermentation. The production of lactic acid can reduce the pH value of silage and make it acidic, thus inhibiting the growth of other harmful microorganisms and maintaining the quality and nutritional value of silage5. Many studies have found that lactic acid bacteria will produce a large number of VOCs such as acids, alcohols, and esters during the process of growth and metabolism, and these substances are the main contributors to flavor. Liu et al.6 found that adding lactic acid bacteria had a positive effect on the sensory quality of pickles, with the flavor substances produced in pickles samples mainly being acids, alcohols, and ketones. Dongmo et al.7 found that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum was associated with fruit aroma, producing acids, esters, and aldehydes that improved the flavor of malt beverages. A study by Zhang et al.8 showed that the microbial community has a significant influence on the volatile metabolites of silage, and the use of lactobacillus additives can change the odor of silage by changing the microbial community. Therefore, the volatile metabolites and metabolic pathways associated with lactic acid bacteria during silage fermentation are the key to regulating and improving the silage odor.

Metagenomics can reveal changes in the species and relative abundance of microorganisms and the expression of functional genes in biomes at different fermentation stages. Volatile metabolomics can be used to identify and quantitatively analyze a variety of VOCs produced in silage, which enables the evaluation of silage quality and odor characteristics, and can reveal the relationships between microbial communities and the production of VOCs through combined analysis with metagenomics. At present, the research of silage and lactic acid bacteria is mainly focused on improving the nutritional quality of silage. However, as an important evaluation index of silage, there are few related studies. Therefore, the aim of this study was to reveal the effects of lactic acid bacteria on alfalfa silage quality, microbial communities, and VOCs through the exogenous addition of commercial and screened lactic acid bacteria and to identify the substances, functions, and metabolic pathways that improve silage odor, thus further improving feed quality and utilization.

Materials and methods

Silage preparation

The alfalfa (WL319 variety) was planted in an experimental field at Tumetzuo Banner, Hohhot City, Inner Mongolia (E40°64’, N111°17’). The raw material was collected in the first flowering period (June 28, 2023). The moisture content of alfalfa was adjusted to 60% by drying and then cut into 2 cm with a guillotine. Silage raw materials were inoculated with commercial Lactobacillus plantarum (CL, 1 × 106 CFU/g fresh matter), screened Lactobacillus plantarum (LP, 1 × 106 CFU/g fresh matter), and screened Pediococcus pentosaceus (PP, 1 × 106 CFU/g fresh matter), and an equal amount of distilled water was added as the control group (CK). CL was provided by Inner Mongolia Hemei Kesheng Biotechnology Co. Ltd., while LP and PP were strains screened from alfalfa, provided by the Key Laboratory of Forage Cultivation, Processing and High Efficient Utilization, Ministry of Agriculture, and the viable count of lactic acid bacteria was 1 × 108 cfu/g. The raw materials were evenly mixed, and 400 g of each treatment was weighed before being put into vacuum sealed silage bags (each 25 × 36 cm in size, provided by Shijiazhuang Youlang Trading Co. Ltd., China). Six replicates were set up for each treatment, and samples were taken after 60 days of ensilage at room temperature (20–30 °C) to determine silage quality, microbial community, and VOCs.

Sensory evaluation and quality analysis

The sensory evaluation is carried out immediately when silage is opened, 100 g fresh samples are taken and evaluated using the sensory evaluation approach established by the German Agricultural Society (DLG)9, and the grading criteria are presented in Supplementary Data 1. Six experts assessed the quality of the silage by evaluating the odor, texture and appearance of the silage. The DLG score was calculated as the aggregate of all individual scores. The DLG score distinguishes five quality classes based on the overall scoring: very good (18–20), good (14–17), fair (10–13), poor (5–9) and corrupted (0–4).

Samples were dried at 65 °C for 72 h to determine their dry matter (DM) content and then ground with a mill for chemical composition analyses. Crude protein was measured using the Dumas nitrogen determination method (Model Dumatherm 7700, Gerhardt Analytical Instruments Co. Ltd., Germany). An ANKOM fiber analyzer (Model A2000i, ANKOM Technology, Macedon, NY, USA) was employed to quantify acid detergent fiber (ADF) and neutral detergent fiber (NDF). Crude fat analysis was performed utilizing an ANKOM fat analyzer (Model XT15i, Beijing ANKOM Technology Co. Ltd., China). Water-soluble carbohydrates (WSC) were determined by anthrone colorimetry10.

10 g of fresh samples were added to 90 ml of distilled water, and then the silage extract was obtained by flapping and filtering, which was used for fermentation quality analysis. The pH was measured using an acidity meter (Model LEICI pH S-3C, Shanghai Yida Scientific Instruments Co. Ltd., China), and lactic acid (LA), acetic acid (AA), propionate (PA), and butyric acid (BA) were identified by high-performance liquid chromatography (Model e2695, Waters Technology, Milford, MA, USA). In addition, ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N) was measured by phenol-hypochlorite colorimetry11.

Microbial metagenomic analysis

DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing were conducted at Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Total microbial genomic DNA was extracted by a DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were examined using a Qubit™ 4 fluorometer (Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer: Q33238; Qubit™ Assay Tubes: Q32856; Qubit™ 1X dsDNA HS Assay Kit: Q33231, Invitrogen, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. To construct metagenome shotgun sequencing libraries with insert sizes of 400 bp, the extracted microbial DNA was processed using the Illumina TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Preparation Kit. Each library was sequenced with the PE150 strategy of the Illumina NovaSeq Platform (Illumina, USA), and sequencing was performed by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw sequence reads were processed for high-quality filtered reads, and metagenomics sequence reads were categorized for each sample using Kraken2 based on the RefSeq database, with a data volume of 10G for metagenomic sequencing. The functions of genes were obtained using mmseqs2 and CAZy, eggNOG, and KEGG databases for annotation12.

Volatile organic compounds analysis

Fresh samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen when the silage was opened and stored at −80 °C. Samples were pressed into powder with liquid nitrogen, and 500 mg of the powder was mixed into 20 ml vials. A saturated NaCl solution was added to avoid enzyme reactions, and the vials were secured with Agilent headspace silica gel caps. Each vial was heated for 5 min at 60 °C for solid-phase microextraction and then inserted into the Agilent fiber optic in the overhead vial and placed for 15 min at 60 °C to absorb the VOCs in the sample13. After sampling, desorption of the VOCs from the fiber coating was carried out in the injection port of the GC apparatus (Model 8890, Agilent) at 250 °C for 5 min in the splitless mode. The identification and quantification of VOCs was carried out using an Agilent Model 8890 GC and a 7000D mass spectrometer (Agilent), equipped with a 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 um DB-5MS (5% phenyl-polymethylsiloxane) capillary column, with 3-hexadone (CAS: 24588-54-3) as the internal standard. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a linear velocity of 1.2 mL/min. The oven was programmed to start at 40 °C for 3.5 min, then rise by 10 °C per minute until 100 °C was reached, followed by a 7 °C per minute increase up to 180 °C, then a 25 °C per minute increase up to 280 °C, and finally sustaining that temperature for 5 min. Mass spectra were obtained using the electron impact (EI) ionization technique at 70 electron volts (eV). The temperatures of the quadrupole mass detector, ion source, and transmission lines were 150 °C, 230 °C, and 280 °C, respectively. Identification and quantification of substances used the selective ion monitoring (SIM) technique. Integration and calibration correction procedures were performed using MassHunter quantitative software. The descriptions of material aromas followed http://www.thegoodscentscompany.com, http://perflavory.com/, or http://www.odour.org.uk/odour/index.

Statistical analysis

The statistical software SAS 9.2 was used to conduct an analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was observed when the p-value was less than 0.05. Tables were produced using Excel 2010, and images were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 and R version 3.6.3. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to draw a correlation heat map. The identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG compound database, and the annotated metabolites were mapped to the KEGG pathway database.

Differences in VOCs composition of silage

Differences in the relative content of VOCs in alfalfa silage are shown in Fig. 2A. A total of 749 VOCs were detected (Supplementary Data 5). The VOCs in each group were mainly composed of terpenes (19.25%), esters (17.51%), heterocyclic compounds (18.85%), alcohols (7.22%), aldehydes (6.68%), and ketones (6.68%). Among treatments, the CK group had the highest content of terpenes and alcohols, followed by the CL, LP, and PP groups. The ester content of the PP group was the highest, followed by the LP, CL, and CK groups. The content of heterocyclic compounds and aldehydes in the CL group was the highest, followed by the CK, LP, and PP groups.

Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) results showed a clear separation of the groups, indicating that the VOCs from the different alfalfa silages were independent of each other and well differentiated overall (Fig. 2B). Among treatments, the CK group was significantly different from the additive groups, with the PP group being the most different from the CK group, and the CL group being the least different from the CK group, while the LP group was in between. A violin plot shows the differential volatile metabolites with VIP ≥ 1 and the top 50 contents (Fig. 2C). Among them, terpenoids (19), esters (13), hydrocarbons (5), heterocyclic compounds (3), and alcohols (3) were the main substances. The contents of terpenes, such as geranyl acetate, levomenthol, and β-ocimene, were higher in the LP and PP groups than in other groups, but β-ionone was higher in the CL group. The contents of esters, such as methyl benzoate, ethyl benzoate, and ethyl salicylate, were higher in the LP and PP groups than in other groups, while p-tolyl acetate was higher in the CK group. The content of 3-octanol was higher in the additive groups than in the CK group, and the content of 2-acetyl-3-ethylpyrazine was higher in the LP and PP groups than in the other groups.

Correlation analysis of differential VOCs with microorganisms

Discussion

The results of sensory evaluation and quality grade showed that the sensory and quality class of alfalfa silage with added lactic acid bacteria was better than that of the CK group. This can be attributed to the fact that lactic acid bacteria produce lactic acid through fermentation, which reduces the acidity of silage, inhibits the growth of harmful bacteria, and reduces the loss of nutrients14. At the same time, lactic acid bacteria produce VOCs during the ensiling process, releasing acidity and aroma to improve the odor of the silage. Among the additive groups, the LP and PP groups had higher grades than the CL group, which was due to the fact that the self-screened Lactobacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus had better adaptability and survivability in the alfalfa silage environment. These strains can rapidly produce lactic acid, reduce pH value, ensure the smooth progress of silage fermentation, protect protein from degradation, and reduce the production of undesirable odors. Through rapid acidification and production of bacteriostatic substances (such as hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocin), the self-screening strains effectively inhibited the growth of mold and yeast, reducing spoilage and musty odor, and produced more organic acids and aromas, thereby improved the sensory quality of silage, which was consistent with the research results of Na et al.15 and Zhao et al.16 Among the treatments, the odor score of the PP group was slightly better than that of the LP group, probably due to the fact that Pediococcus pentosaceus was able to rapidly convert soluble sugars in the raw materials into lactic acid in the early stage of ensiling, inhibit the growth of harmful microorganisms, reduce the production of malodorous VOCs, and at the same time increase the content of aromatic volatile substances17. The stray bacteria in the ensiling process may produce odors and harmful substances that cause feed deterioration and color darkening. Lactobacillus inhibits the growth of stray bacteria through rapid acid reduction and creates an anaerobic environment to reduce the oxidation reaction rate, so as to maintain the good structure and color of the silage.

The results of the analysis of the nutritional and fermentation quality of silage showed that the silage quality of the additive group was better than that of the CK group, which was consistent with the results of sensory evaluation. This is due to the fact that the organic acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid produced by the fermentation of lactic acid bacteria can reduce the pH value of the feed, thus inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria. Wang et al.18 showed that the pH value of Moringa oleifera silage decreased from 6.5 to 4.2 during fermentation, which effectively inhibited the propagation of harmful bacteria, such as Escherichia coli. Lactobacillus fermentation was able to reduce dry matter, crude protein, and crude fat losses in silage. Costa et al.19 showed that silage supplemented with Lactobacillus reduced dry matter losses by 20% and crude protein and crude fat by 15% and 10%, respectively, during 60 days of fermentation. The fermentation of lactic acid bacteria can also inhibit the heterogeneous decomposition of proteins, which in turn reduces the generation of ammoniacal nitrogen20. The fermentation process can also partially degrade the fiber in the feed and generate soluble sugar as a substrate, further improving the fermentation effect of alfalfa silage21. The fermentation quality of the LP group was better than that of other groups because the self-screening bacteria have strong adaptability and a good matching relationship to the raw materials and the silage environment and can quickly occupy ecological niches, form the dominant flora, and inhibit the growth of other undesirable fermentation microorganisms. Lactic acid bacteria can quickly form an acidic anaerobic environment through lactic acid fermentation, prevent oxidation reactions and metabolic decomposition of hybrid bacteria, and retain nutrients in raw materials22.

The results of the bacterial community alpha diversity analysis showed that the LP and PP groups had low diversity, which could be attributed to their faster fermentation rate, which led to a rapid decrease in oxygen content and pH in the silage environment beyond the tolerance range of some microorganisms, resulting in a reduction of bacterial community species23. Chen et al.24 found that the pH of corn silage supplemented with lactic acid bacteria dropped below 4.0 within 3 days, and the number of aerobic and acid-sensitive bacteria was significantly reduced, ultimately leading to a decrease in the alpha diversity of the bacterial community. The Simpson of CL group was lower and the community diversity was higher, which may be caused by the weak competition effect of commercial lactic acid bacteria in silage. The number of species and the diversity of the community were higher in the CK group, which might be due to the lactic acid bacteria inhibiting the proliferation of other microorganisms through acid production action and competition for nutrients that inhibited the proliferation of other microorganisms. By contrast, the lower number of lactic acid bacteria in the CK group was less effective in inhibiting other microorganisms, resulting in a more complex microbial composition25.

The results of the analysis of bacterial composition and differences in alfalfa silage at the genus and species level showed that the dominant genus in the CK group was Enterococcus, while the dominant genus in the additive groups was Lactiplantibacillus. This was due to competition between the added lactic acid bacteria and bacteria attached to the raw material, which altered the composition of the microbial community, as was also found in a study by Liu et al.26 Enterococcus are relatively poorly adapted to acidic environments, while Lactiplantibacillus are able to grow and reproduce at lower pH and produce large quantities of organic acids. At the same time, Lactiplantibacillus has a strong ability to inhibit other microorganisms, thus reducing the chances of survival and reproduction of Enterococcus. The dominant species in the CK group was Enterococcus mundtii, while the dominant species in the additive groups was Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. Enterococcus mundtii mainly produces lactic acid through lactic acid fermentation, lowers the pH value, and at the same time produces phenyl propionic acid and its derivatives, which have certain antioxidant and bacteriostatic abilities27. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum produces a large amount of lactic acid and other organic acids during silage fermentation, as well as enzymes, extracellular polysaccharides, and antimicrobial substances with stronger antimicrobial capacity28. These different dominant fermenting strains may be the reason for the significant differences in silage quality between groups. Moreover, Enterococcus mundtii produces volatiles such as acids, ketones, and alcohols during fermentation, whereas Lactiplantibacillus plantarum produces volatiles such as esters, alcohols, and aldehydes, which also affect the odor of silage. Lu et al.29 found that inoculation with Lactobacillus plantarum promoted the production of esters and increased sweetness and fruity flavors in fermented vegetables. Lactobacillus plantarum produces intermediates such as aldehydes, acetic acid, and ethanol during glycolysis, which are further reacted to produce esters. At the same time, Lactobacillus plantarum can also combine fatty acids with alcohols through the fatty acid metabolism pathway to produce the corresponding esters.

The results indicate that the VOCs in alfalfa silage are dominated by terpenoids, esters, and heterocyclic compounds, which are capable of imparting rich odors to alfalfa silage and also contain aroma molecules that are attractive to humans and animals. For example, terpenoids may contribute herbal and woody odors, esters may contribute fruit and floral scents, and heterocyclic compounds may contribute characteristic aromatic odors30,31. The combination and concentration of these compounds will collectively affect the odor characteristics of alfalfa silage. Among treatments, the CK group had higher terpene and alcohol content, retained the odor of alfalfa herbage, and had a fair odor sensation. The LP and PP groups had higher ester content, a strong sour and fruity aroma, and a good odor sensation. The CL group had a higher content of heterocyclic compounds and aldehydes, a partially aromatic odor, and a moderate odor sensation. This may be due to the differences in the microbial species dominating the fermentation process, which is consistent with the findings of ref. 32 Different lactic acid bacteria contain different types and activities of enzymes that catalyze different chemical reactions during fermentation, resulting in different odors. For example, dehydrogenase affects the production of alcohols and ketones, esterase and lipase catalyze the production of esters, and decarboxylase is involved in the conversion of organic acids, which affects the sour taste and odor.

Among the terpenes in the LP and PP groups, geranyl acetate has a sweet floral odor similar to that of flowers such as roses, lavender, or geranium, with some citrus and peach notes. β-ocimene is often found in flowers and has a citrusy and floral flavor. Levomenthol has a cool minty odor that manifests as fresh and refreshing, with a hint of sweetness33. During the ensiling process, rupture of alfalfa plant cells and microbial metabolic activity may result in the release of these terpenoids. Among the esters in the LP and PP groups, methyl benzoate has a stronger odor and is sometimes described as fruity or similar to citrus fruits. Ethyl benzoate has a pear or peach aroma and vanilla or spice notes, with some sweetness and fruit aroma. Ethyl salicylate has a strong odor similar to that of the sweet fruity aroma of wintergreen oil and sweet cherry, sometimes with a slight herbal note34. During the ensiling process, microorganisms decompose sugars and amino acids in alfalfa, producing a series of metabolites such as benzoic acid and alcohols, which react under acidic conditions to form benzoate esters that increase the flavor of silage. Ethyl benzoate is often found in fermented fruit juices and wines with sweet, fruity, and floral aromas and can significantly enhance the aromatic properties of juice wines and increase their sensory appeal. During fermentation, lactic acid bacteria and yeast convert benzoic acid and ethanol to ethyl benzoate through esterase and esterification reactions. β-ionone has a soft, floral odor and is usually found in violet flowers and other flowers, with higher levels associated with first-flowering alfalfa feedstock. P-tolyl acetate is often described as having an odor similar to fructose or sweet perfumed water. Alcohols produced in alfalfa silage are esterified with acids by microbial metabolic catalysis, and this esterification usually occurs under fermentation conditions35. 3-octanol, which has a strong oleaginous, nutty, and herbal aroma, is produced by microbial decomposition of fatty acids in the plant cell during fermentation of alfalfa silage36. Fatty acids in plants mainly include linoleic acid, linolenic acid, oleic acid, and palmitic acid, which have energy storage, cell membrane structure, and signal transduction functions. 2-acetyl-3-ethylpyrazine has a roasted nut and caramel odor and is produced by the microbial metabolism of amino acids and ketones in alfalfa37. High levels of these substances with distinctive odors work together to increase the flavor of alfalfa silage.

The correlation analysis showed that Enterococcus had the opposite correlations to Lactiplantibacillus and Pediococcus. Enterococcus was positively associated with benzenemethanethiol, benzaldehyde, n-methylenevinylamine, 2-methylisoborneol, and other substances with unpleasant odors. Among them, benzenemethanethiol has a smell similar to garlic or onion, benzaldehyde has a strong odor of bitter almonds, n-methylenevinylamine has a pungent and unpleasant odor, and 2-methylisoborneol has a rancid, dirt, or sewage odor38,39. The dominant genus in the CK group was Enterococcus, which may explain the poor sensory evaluation of the CK group. Lactiplantibacillus plantarum showed a highly significant positive correlation with esters such as benzoate and salicylate, which have strong sweet, fruity flavors, and are commonly found in flowers and fruits, and are the main substances conferring flavor to alfalfa silage during the metabolism of lactic acid bacteria40. Among the substances positively associated with Pediococcus pentosaceus, in addition to esters with aromatic odors, 3-octanol has a strong greasy, mushroom, and cheese flavor, and benzothiazole has a baked or nutty flavor, while levomenthol has cool, minty, and refreshing odors41, which enriched the odor composition of the PP group. Analysis of the correlations between bacterial communities and VOCs and the analysis of aroma and flavor compounds generated by different microorganisms during the fermentation process can help to develop bacterial agents related to silage odor improvement so as to optimize the quality and aroma of the product by regulating the fermentation process.

GT is related to the biosynthesis of oligosaccharides and polysaccharides, GH is an important class of hydrolases related to starch liquefaction, saccharification, and cellulose degradation, and CBM promotes the binding of enzymes and substrates contributing to the degradation of polysaccharides such as starch and cellulose42, which have important roles in silage quality and odor formation. The higher abundance of GT in the CK and CL groups indicates that these groups are more capable of producing oligosaccharides and polysaccharides. The higher abundance of GH and CBM in the LP and PP groups indicates that large amounts of polysaccharides, such as starch and cellulose, were degraded during the ensiling process and converted into organic acids and aromatic volatiles, improving the flavor of alfalfa silage.

The L functions include microbial replication, genetic recombination, and DNA repair, which enhance microbial adaptation in silage and enable lactic acid bacteria to grow, multiply, and occupy ecological niches rapidly, thus helping to establish dominant flora and inhibit the growth of spoilage microorganisms in the early stages of fermentation17. The K functions involve the biosynthesis and maintenance of cell walls and cell membranes, and strong cell walls and cell membranes help microorganisms to resist low pH and high organic acid concentrations and improve their survival rate. This allows beneficial microorganisms to remain active during the silage process, continuing fermentation and production of beneficial metabolites43. G and E are associated with carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, and their higher abundance in the LP group indicated that the screened lactic acid bacteria were able to better utilize sugars and amino acids from alfalfa to optimize silage quality and odor. The E function involves the transport and metabolism of amino acids and promotes the efficient utilization of amino acids in feed by Lactobacillus plantarum. This not only contributes to the growth and reproduction of the microorganism itself but also to the metabolic generation of a range of beneficial metabolites, such as small-molecule peptides and volatile compounds such as esters, aldehydes, and alcohols, which contribute directly to the flavor and aroma of the silage. The G function involves the transport and metabolism of carbohydrates and promotes the rapid fermentation of sugars in the feed by lactic acid bacteria, generating large amounts of lactic acid and other organic acids. The process of carbohydrate metabolism also generates a variety of VOCs, such as acetic acid and ethyl lactate, which can enhance the overall aroma and flavor of silage44.

The results of analysis of the functional composition of bacteria showed that at the L2 level, the groups were mainly carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins. The functional abundance of the additive groups was higher than that in the control group, except for carbohydrates metabolism in the LP group. The growth and metabolism of many microorganisms in the CK group promoted the hydrolysis and utilization of carbohydrates, thus improving the level of carbohydrate metabolism. Lactic acid bacteria utilize carbohydrates for fermentation metabolism to produce volatile fatty acids and other organic acids, which promote the growth of beneficial bacteria and the inhibition of unfavorable bacteria to maintain good fermentation conditions in silage. At the same time, the products metabolized by lactic acid bacteria through phenylalanine and tyrosine can further react with alcohols to produce aromatic esters, and these newly produced esters usually have a strong fruity, floral aroma, which improves the odor of silage. Coenzymes work in the electron transport chain to help maintain cellular energy metabolism, thereby supporting the activity of beneficial microorganisms that help convert sugars into lactic acid. Vitamins are cofactors in many enzyme reactions during silage and are involved in energy metabolism and amino acid metabolism45. At the L3 level, the groups were mainly starch and sucrose metabolism and peptidoglycan biosynthesis, and the functional abundance was higher in the LP group than in the CK group. Among these processes, microorganisms decompose starch and sucrose in silage into monosaccharides by amylase and glucosidase as carbon sources for metabolism, and produce organic acids and VOCs. At the same time, peptidoglycan is an important component of the bacterial cell wall, and microorganisms biosynthesize structural proteins, which can improve the protein content of feed and increase the nutritional value of silage, which is consistent with the results of ref. 46 The cell walls of lactic acid bacteria are mainly composed of peptidoglycan, which makes them more stable and resistant to external stresses in acidic environments, enabling continuous lactic acid fermentation, lowering the pH of silage, and inhibiting the growth of proteolytic bacteria. The higher abundance of these functions in the LP group indicated more relevant metabolism and accordingly better silage results. Analysis of bacterial differential functions showed that the biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites and penicillin and cephalosporin biosynthesis were more significant in the LP group, while amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism were more significant in the PP group. This suggests that microorganisms in the LP group produce a greater variety of secondary metabolites and antibiotics during the ensiling process, such as penicillin, phenols, polyphenols, and other substances with antioxidant and bacteriostatic functions, as well as various types of VOCs, which are able to improve the stability and odor of silage. In the PP group, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolic pathways enhance the stability of microbial cell structure, promote the survival and reproduction of microorganisms, and ensure continuous fermentation activity and high-quality metabolite generation during ensiling. Different microorganisms may participate in the metabolism of amino and nucleotide sugars through synergistic effects, which together promote silage fermentation and improve feed quality25.

The results of the annotation and enrichment analyses indicate that microbial production of VOCs during silage fermentation is mainly realized through microbial metabolism in diverse environments, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and degradation of aromatic compounds pathways. Among these pathways, silage odor is mainly related to the metabolic activities of microorganisms during the ensiling process. Through fermentation activities and metabolites, lactic acid bacteria not only produce lactic acid and other organic acids to give the feed an acidic aroma but also enrich the aroma level of the feed by generating a variety of VOCs. In addition, the rapid acidification ability of lactic acid bacteria effectively inhibits the growth of spoilage microorganisms and prevents the production of undesirable odors, which ensures the high quality and good sensory characteristics of silage. Yeast produces ethanol and acetic acid under aerobic conditions, and these substances impart odors similar to those of alcohol and vinegar. Molds produce a variety of organic acids, alcohols, amines, and sulfides under anoxic and high humidity conditions, and these metabolites impart a putrid odor47. At the same time, microorganisms usually produce various secondary metabolites during fermentation and metabolism, and these metabolites may include compounds with specific odors, such as aromatic volatile acids, esters, and phenolic compounds, which can directly affect the odor of silage, as has been shown in the analysis of the metabolite synthesis of corn silage by Su et al.48 Aromatic compounds include benzene, toluene, xylene, ethylbenzene, and other compounds with aromatic properties, which can be broken down by microorganisms into simpler substances such as phenols, ketones, aldehydes, and acids through the aromatic compound degradation pathway, and these degradation products release specific odors in silage49. This is similar to the results of the microbial functional analysis, which demonstrated that the odor of silage can be improved by altering microbial composition prior to ensiling, which affects the metabolic activities related to volatiles.

Conclusion

This study based on volatile metabolomics and metagenomics revealed that the main VOCs in alfalfa silage were terpenoids, esters, and heterocyclic compounds. Among these compounds, esters, such as methyl benzoate, ethyl benzoate, and ethyl salicylate, were the main source of aroma. LP and PP groups had higher sensory and quality grades, and the dominant bacteria were Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Pediococcus pentosaceus, and the functions in carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and degradation of aromatic compounds metabolic pathways were significantly enriched. The composition of VOCs can be changed through the regulation of the microbial community and metabolic pathways, which provide new ideas to improve silage odor and quality.
