MET (c-Met) protein overexpression is an emerging protein biomarker in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Ming-Sound Tsao, Lynette Sholl, Michelle Shiller, Peter Illei, Ignacio I Wistuba, Mary Beth Beasley, Kurt A Schalper, Archana Simmons, Peter Ansell, Sue Beruti, Mari Mino-Kenudson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMET protein (also known as c-Met protein) overexpression (OE) is an emerging clinically relevant biomarker that is targetable with antibody-drug conjugates. c-Met protein’s prevalence varies, depending on the different OE thresholds, antibodies, and detection methodologies used. Unlike MET genomic aberrations, MET protein OE is a protein biomarker requiring immunohistochemistry (IHC)-based testing. Herein, we summarize the current evidence on MET protein OE, including prognostic value, heterogeneity, and stability. We also provide key considerations for enabling optimal real-world testing of this IHC-based biomarker.Subject terms: Non-small-cell lung cancer, Predictive markers

Introduction

MET protein (also known as c-Met protein) is a tyrosine kinase receptor encoded by the MET protooncogene. Under normal physiologic conditions, the binding of its ligand, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), to MET results in the activation of multiple intracellular pathways that regulate cellular functions, including proliferation and protection from apoptosis1–3. The MET/HGF signaling pathway is dysregulated in cancer, and this has been associated with tumor invasion, progression, and metastasis4–6. MET protein overexpression (OE) and MET amplification and mutations are reported in various malignancies and contribute to this dysregulation7,8. MET protein OE has also been linked to poor clinical outcomes9 and is currently under investigation as a biomarker for MET-targeted therapies in various cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)9.

A role for the MET pathway in lung cancer pathogenesis was first reported in the 1990s10–12 and it has since been recognized as a primary oncogenic driver. MET activation is also a resistance mechanism to various targeted therapies13. The most common MET alterations associated with NSCLC tumorigenesis are MET protein OE, MET amplification, and splice-site mutations leading to the skipping of exon 14 (MET exon 14 skipping mutations), and their presence has been associated with poor clinical outcomes in NSCLC14–17. Hence, detection of these aberrations could help inform prognosis and treatment options. MET amplification (when rigorously defined) and MET exon 14 skipping mutations, both of which lead to oncogenic addiction, are actionable biomarkers in NSCLC, and their activity can be modified by MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). MET protein OE, which is more common in NSCLC than the other two MET aberrations, does not lead to oncogenic addiction in the majority of cases18–22. MET protein OE may occur independently of MET alterations, although overlap alongside MET amplification and, in some cases, MET exon 14 skipping mutations has been observed17,23–27. Despite this, it should be noted that MET protein OE should not be considered a surrogate marker for the other aberration or vice versa. Consequently, an avenue has opened up for therapeutics that specifically and directly target the MET protein versus the signaling pathway28.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are one such class of therapeutics that typically comprise a specific cell-surface tumor antigen-directed monoclonal antibody linked to a cytotoxic “payload”29. The precise targeting of cell-surface protein enables clinical actionability for aberrant protein OE. A number of MET-targeted ADCs, including telisotuzumab adizutecan (Temab-A, ABBV-400), REGN5093, SHR-A1403, and TR1801, are currently in development for solid tumors such as NSCLC, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer30–33 (Table 134–48). On May 14, 2025, telisotuzumab vedotin-tllv was granted accelerated approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of adult patients with locally advanced or metastatic, non-squamous NSCLC with high MET protein OE who have received a prior systemic therapy. High MET protein OE is defined as ≥50% of tumor cells with strong (3+) staining as determined by an FDA-approved test49. Teliso-V is a MET-directed ADC comprising the monoclonal antibody telisotuzumab and the microtubule polymerization inhibitor monomethyl auristatin E. Unlike MET TKIs, which block the activation of the MET receptor, thereby inhibiting the downstream signaling cascade, Teliso-V binds to the MET protein and delivers a cytotoxic drug into the cancer cell, killing it. Given the mode of action of this drug and similar ADCs, MET protein OE has emerged as a potentially actionable biomarker that may be used to select patients most likely to benefit from therapeutic interventions. The phase II LUMINOSITY trial (NCT03539536) identified patients who had MET protein-overexpressing epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC as those most likely to benefit from treatment with Teliso-V monotherapy. Durable responses were seen, with an overall response rate (ORR) of 29%. The response was particularly enriched in patients who had high MET protein OE (ORR = 35%)50.

Herein, we review the available evidence on MET protein OE as an emerging biomarker with potential actionability in NSCLC, including its prognostic value, prevalence, overlap with other MET aberrations, and methodology for assessment. In addition, we review key lessons learned from the pathologist’s perspective on establishing testing for programmed cell death protein 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) and human epidermal growth factor receptor (HER2) as immunohistochemistry (IHC) biomarkers in NSCLC. Potential learnings to enable clinical implementation of emerging IHC biomarkers such as MET protein OE are also discussed.

Prevalence of MET protein OE in NSCLC

MET protein OE is prevalent in NSCLC. Reports suggest a point prevalence between 15% and 75%11,18,21,22,51,52; one recent study reported a prevalence of 25% in patients with EGFR-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC50. The variation in reported rates reflects the previous lack of standardization in MET protein expression assessment by IHC and the different protocols that feature a variety of antibody clones, as well as the different criteria for intensities (or definition of each intensity) and positive-threshold cutoff values53 (Table 211,18,21,22,50,52,54–56). MET gene amplification and MET exon 14 skipping mutations, which are common therapeutic targets in NSCLC, have a much lower prevalence (Table 311,18,20–22,51–53,57–60). MET amplification is reported in 2–5% of NSCLC cases23,53,61,62, with the exact prevalence dependent on the definition of amplification and method of detection. Rates of 1.7% were reported when next-generation sequencing (NGS) was employed vs higher rates of 4.5–6% observed using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-based testing, where MET positivity was defined as MET/CEP7 ≥ 563,64. MET splicing mutations leading to exon 14 skipping have an approximate prevalence of 3–4%51,65–67. MET exon 14 point mutations (D1028N, Y1003C/F/N/S and D1010H/N/Y)65,66,68–70, various missense mutations in the MET first juxtamembrane domain (R988C, T1010I, S1058P) and Sema domain (E168D, L299F, S323G, and N375S)71,72, and the MET exon 14 deletion mutation72,73 have also been observed at low frequencies in NSCLC, with several different point mutations emerging following treatment and progression or development of resistance to targeted therapies74–76. MET amplification and MET exon 14 skipping mutations have arisen as clinically actionable biomarkers in NSCLC, with various therapies approved. MET protein OE is a prevalent and distinct biomarker compared with other MET genomic aberrations, although MET protein OE has been observed in some patients with MET exon 14-altered cancers24,25,66, and there are reports of overlap between MET amplification and MET protein expression17,23,24,26,27. For example, Fig. 1 illustrates the observed prevalence of these MET alterations in the ongoing retrospective U.S. real-world METPRO study17. These data, while currently only published in an abstract, reflect the prevalence observed in the 148 patients who had EGFR-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC, of a total of 500 accrued patients with NSCLC. However, the exact prevalence and overlap of MET mutations, MET amplification, and MET protein OE vary across the literature due to the varying methodologies used between different studies.

Various mechanisms can drive MET protein OE, including MET amplification, MET exon 14 skipping mutations, and activation of other oncogenic pathways. MET exon 14 skipping mutations result in the loss of Y1003, a Cbl binding site that promotes MET protein degradation, leading to decreased ubiquitination and degradation of MET, and increased MET protein levels73. Gene amplification leads to increased mRNA levels, which could translate to higher MET expression via enhanced protein translation, and studies have shown that MET amplification can correlate with MET protein OE23,26. Emerging evidence suggests that MET mRNA levels may correlate with protein expression, indicating a potential complementary role for RNA-based testing. Although RNA assays are primarily designed to detect MET exon 14 skipping and fusions, they often yield quantitative transcript data that may inform MET status. Preliminary findings from a meta-analysis across clinical and real-world data showed good concordance between MET RNA and protein expression in NSCLC27,77,78. Similarly, Ingale et al. reported significant positive correlations between RNA expression and protein OE probabilities in lung adenocarcinoma (Pearson r ≈ 0.40–0.43, P < 0.0001)79. Consistent with these observations, MET protein OE has been shown to correlate with increased gene copy number, although not all protein-overexpressing cases were amplified, suggesting multiple mechanisms may drive elevated transcript and protein levels27,80. In the absence of MET amplification or the MET exon 14 skipping mutation, MET protein expression is thought to be driven by transcriptional upregulation, cell-matrix adhesion, and possibly enhanced by the presence of hypoxia81–87. This may explain its increased prevalence compared with MET amplifications or MET exon 14 skipping mutations. The activation of other oncogenic pathways, such as EGFR, that may directly transactivate MET or alter MET transcription, translation, or degradation, could also drive increased MET protein OE in NSCLC88.

The relatively high prevalence of MET protein OE indicates that a substantial proportion of patients could benefit from the therapeutic actionability of drugs that target MET protein OE, and as previously mentioned, several MET protein-targeted agents are currently being evaluated in various solid tumors, including NSCLC.

Testing modalities for MET protein OE

Unlike the methodologies used to detect MET amplifications (NGS, FISH) and MET mutations (DNA- or RNA-based NGS, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction, and Sanger sequencing), the principal method for detecting MET protein OE is IHC54,89. This technique uses high-specificity antibodies that can recognize and enable quantification of the cell-surface protein. IHC is generally performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue21,90 and is a widely used and familiar clinical testing methodology in pathology.

IHC and genomic methods each have their own unique considerations and factors that impact test performance. For IHC, similar to NGS, the key variables are how the tissue samples are obtained, fixed, and processed91–93. IHC results may also be affected by the differences in reagents, temperatures for antigen retrieval and antibody incubation steps, as well as fixation durations across institutions and laboratories during the multistep process of formalin fixation and paraffin embedding. Fixation delays of 2, 3, or 12 h have been shown to progressively decrease the percentage change in MET H-scores vs a 1-h fixation delay. Meanwhile, extended time in formalin fixation (72 h) resulted in a 23% reduction in MET H-scores and 51% probability of a reduction in staining intensity for MET compared with a 12-h fixation time91. If cytology cell blocks are to be used to detect MET expression, consideration should be given to the potential effects that different fixation methods can have on MET protein detection.

Other potential issues include the difficulty of precise quantification18 and the subjective nature of IHC interpretation, which may lead to inter- and intraobserver variation. This was highlighted by Boyle et al.94, who reported interobserver variation in MET IHC evaluation. The Lung Cancer Mutation Consortium (LCMC) 2.0 pathologist group reported that the variation in MET protein expression positivity for 150 LCMC samples at four sites ranged from 27 to 83%. The variation was significantly reduced following an interactive round-robin training program, which resulted in an improvement of the intraclass correlation coefficient (which compares the consistency of scoring) from 0.5 (95% CI, 0.37–0.64) in the first scoring round to 0.74 (95% CI, 0.64–0.83) in the second round. Challenges with data interpretation may also arise from the heterogeneity of the signal.

Accurate assessment of MET protein expression is further complicated by the various MET antibody clones and scoring systems that are used to define OE (Table 211,18,21,22,50,52,54–56). The most common scoring systems are based on an H-score that ranges from 0 to 300, with protein OE arbitrarily defined as a score of 20095, or a scale of 0–3+ intensity where the cutoff for OE is 2+ in 50% of cells96. However, there is no universally accepted and clear cutoff to define MET protein OE. Different studies adopt their own criteria, which contributes to the reported variability in MET protein OE and consequently its prevalence. The recent LUMINOSITY trial that evaluated Teliso-V in patients with MET-overexpressing NSCLC used a cutoff for protein OE of ≥25% tumor cells with 3+ staining, with high OE defined as ≥50% of cells with 3+ staining50.

The anti-MET SP44 clone (Roche Diagnostics) has been used in various clinical trials, although the definition of MET positivity varies between studies. In the OAM4558g trial assessing the combination of erlotinib and onartuzumab in patients with advanced NSCLC, patients were considered MET positive if they had moderate or strong levels of MET in ≥50% of cells (clinically relevant IHC score of ≥2+). The scoring criteria used in this study96 are shown in Table 496. This assay has since been used in the phase II LUMINOSITY study to select for patients with MET protein OE. In this trial OE is defined as ≥25% of tumor cells with membrane staining at 3+ intensity (MET high, ≥50% of tumor cells with 3+ intensity; MET intermediate, ≥25 to <50% of tumor cells with 3+ intensity)50 (Fig. 2). Similarly, different cutoffs were used in the ongoing phase II SAVANNAH trial in patients with EGFR-mutant, MET-overexpressed and/or -amplified NSCLC, where MET positivity was defined as ≥90% of cells with 3+ intensity and/or MET copy number ≥1097.

Heterogeneity of MET protein expression in NSCLC

There is limited evidence regarding the heterogeneity of MET protein expression in NSCLC, although data suggest that MET expression changes as cells progress from normal to cancerous and during tumor progression. While MET is expressed at a very low level in normal lung tissues72, its OE in NSCLC has been documented since the early 1990s10,98. MET protein OE has been seen in lung tumors compared with adjacent normal lung tissue99, and MET activation by HGF has resulted in enhanced cell motility, migration, and invasion, leading to early metastasis100,101. MET protein OE has been associated with higher tumor grade in adenocarcinomas, and increased MET immunoreactivity has been observed at the advancing edge of tumors, supporting a role for MET protein OE in lung cancer cell invasion18. MET protein OE has been shown to correlate with a higher pathologic tumor stage in NSCLC11. One study demonstrated an association between MET protein OE and the pN and M variables102. Spatial heterogeneity of MET expression within tumors has also been reported94,103, and has been associated with interobserver disagreement104.

Impact of therapeutic intervention on MET protein expression

There is currently limited direct evidence that MET protein expression changes with lines of therapy in EGFR-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC, despite the functional cross-talk between the MET/HGF axis and various other signaling pathways in cancer105–107. A small analysis of 28 paired archival and postprogression tumor biopsies from patients in the EGFR-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC cohort of the phase II LUMINOSITY trial demonstrated that MET protein status remained the same for 75% (n = 21) of patients, while 7% (n = 2) of patients changed from MET positive to negative and 18% (n = 5) changed from MET negative to positive. The changes may potentially be due to prior immuno-oncology/chemotherapy treatments50. Meanwhile, an increase in MET protein OE from baseline to progression has been observed in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC following treatment with EGFR TKIs108.

Various studies in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC indirectly support the concept that MET expression may change following treatment. One mechanism through which tumors acquire resistance to first-generation TKIs is aberrant MET activity. Between 5 and 20% of acquired resistance to EGFR TKIs is associated with MET amplification109,110 and given that MET amplification may contribute to MET protein OE26,111, one could postulate that MET protein levels may change following exposure to EGFR TKIs. Evidence also suggests that increased MET expression contributes to acquired insensitivity to radiation therapy in NSCLC112 and chemotherapy113. Such data could indicate that MET expression changes over lines of therapy, particularly in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC, although this needs to be prospectively validated. The results of METEXPRESS, a retrospective, single-center, non-interventional study, support this premise. MET protein OE status was seen to increase following treatment in patients with EGFR-mutated non-squamous NSCLC, which was in contrast to those patients who had EGFR-wildtype NSCLC, where minimal change in MET protein OE was observed114.

Overlap of MET protein OE with other driver mutations

A number of studies have indicated that MET protein OE is not mutually exclusive and can occur in a context of other oncogenic driver alterations. EGFR alterations, including mutations and OE, have been significantly correlated with MET protein expression115,116. MET protein OE can be seen in many different contexts, including, but not limited to, patients who have anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) rearrangement and/or positivity117–119. PD-L1 expression has also been shown to positively correlate with MET amplification23,120 and MET protein expression in patients with NSCLC23,121, although at least one study122 found no significant association between expression patterns of the two proteins, so further investigation is required to confirm any relationship between the two signaling axes. The purported cross-talk between these pathways may have implications for treatment sequencing and testing sequencing in patients with NSCLC.

MET protein OE as a prognostic biomarker

MET protein expression was first reported to correlate with higher pathologic tumor stage and worse outcomes in 199611. Since then, a number of studies have supported a potential role for MET as a prognostic biomarker (Table 511,21,23,55,57,90,111,123). The prognostic value of MET protein OE has predominantly been explored in patients with surgically resected NSCLC, where it has been seen to be a negative indicator of survival. One meta-analysis of eight studies (n = 1254 patients with surgically resected NSCLC) concluded that high MET protein OE was significantly associated with worse overall survival (OS; hazard ratio [HR] 2.18, 95% CI 1.60–2.97, P < 0.001)124; however, this analysis was heavily biased toward the inclusion of Asian cohorts and no association with poorer OS was observed in the non-Asian population. Two other meta-analyses, which also predominantly included studies undertaken in Asian countries, corroborated this finding and found MET protein OE to be an indicator of shorter OS in patients who had surgically resected NSCLC125,126. Data from a randomized phase II trial evaluating erlotinib with or without onartuzumab in pretreated patients with advanced NSCLC also indicated that MET was associated with poorer clinical outcomes; the prognosis, assessed in patients assigned to placebo plus erlotinib, was worse in MET-positive vs MET-negative patients for both progression-free survival (PFS; HR 1.71; P = 0.06) and OS (HR 2.61; P = 0.004)127. Conversely, results from the European Thoracic Oncology Platform Lungscape project of surgically resected, stage I–III NSCLC cases from 17 centers found no correlation between MET protein OE and OS/prognosis128. Furthermore, at least one publication has reported no impact of MET protein expression on the survival of Japanese patients with resected NSCLCs54, either in the whole patient cohort or when grouping patients according to histology.

Interestingly, recent emerging data from real-world studies have also demonstrated associations between MET protein expression and clinical outcomes. In a real-world cohort, patients with stage IV non-squamous metastatic MET protein-overexpressing NSCLC had a worse prognosis vs those whose tumors did not have MET protein OE129. Furthermore, an interim analysis of the METPRO retrospective study of US patients with non-squamous NSCLC reported that high MET protein OE was associated with a shorter time to next treatment or death vs low or no MET expression and may be associated with poor OS17. Finally, MET protein OE correlated with significantly longer PFS (P = 0.028) compared with MET negativity in a retrospective study of 213 patients with NSCLC23. Since studies to date have been mostly restricted to Asian populations or in earlier-stage settings, the prognostic impact of MET protein OE in broader populations of patients with NSCLC and those within the metastatic setting remains unclear and needs further evaluation.

Procedures and considerations for diagnostic IHC in patients with NSCLC

Several guidelines are available regarding molecular testing in patients with NSCLC. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network’s guidelines58 recommend testing for the MET exon 14 skipping mutation at diagnosis, and for high-level MET amplification in the context of acquired resistance to targeted therapy (EGFR TKIs in particular), as do the European Society for Medical Oncology’s130. Conversely, the 2018 College of American Pathologists, the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, and the Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines59, which are also endorsed by the American Society of Clinical Oncology, do not recommend MET molecular testing as a routine stand-alone assay outside of a clinical trial, but do indicate that such testing would be appropriate as part of larger testing panels performed either initially or when routine EGFR, ALK, and ROS proto-oncogene 1, receptor tyrosine kinase (ROS1) tests are negative. An updated version of these guidelines is currently in development and should be available in 2025.

In the majority of patients with NSCLC, samples for protein biomarker testing are obtained through minimally invasive biopsy procedures, as the disease is too advanced for surgical intervention. Techniques include core needle biopsies via the transthoracic approach or from metastatic sites, bronchoscopic forceps biopsy, and transbronchial fine needle aspiration with or without endobronchial ultrasound guidance. The method of choice is highly dependent on the site of disease58,131, and biopsies are often challenging due to small or inaccessible lesions. The different methods can affect the sample available for staining132; for example, use of forceps during bronchoscopy can potentially result in tissue-crushing artifacts due to pinching133, while fine needle aspiration and biopsy may result in a limited amount of tissue material133. Cryobiopsy, which uses a cryoprobe to cool and freeze tissue for extraction, may allow for the collection of larger samples134 without compromising tissue integrity. Indeed, a comparison between cryobiopsy and forceps biopsy has shown good agreement for PD-L1 and HER2 expression, indicating that the freezing and thawing during cryobiopsy should not be an issue in sampling for IHC staining132. To maximize ancillary testing, a clinical practice guideline has been developed with 16 formal recommendations and expert consensus opinions on how to handle and process small biopsy specimens133. However, limited tissue availability often impedes the ability to test for all biomarkers. In such instances, a testing algorithm based on multidisciplinary input should be applied135.

The choice of antibody clone used for the test is critical and needs careful consideration. The sensitivity and specificity of the antibody are key, as both can impact results and potentially lead to interobserver variability. Special attention should be paid to whether the assay has been fully clinically validated or evaluated as a laboratory-developed test136 because this may affect the reproducibility of the data in future studies. The challenges faced in the adoption of PD-L1 and HER2 assays to inform therapy selection highlight the issues surrounding the choice of clone and assay. Clinical testing for both biomarkers was initially hampered by the different commercially available assays and platforms that were used137–140. Since multiple clones were used in the assays, it was necessary to establish concordance between the clones and assess whether the tests could be used interchangeably, which made implementation of assays in the real world difficult141. Subsequently, guidelines were developed for both biomarkers140,142,143 to assist pathologists in navigating the various assays, platforms, and thresholds that were available. A similar approach could be considered for MET protein OE testing by IHC.

Another key consideration for MET protein OE testing is the time of testing and testing sequencing. Key considerations regarding whether MET protein OE is consistent from diagnosis through treatment may inform the testing paradigm and help assess if testing should be considered at diagnosis alongside other clinically relevant biomarkers. The phase II LUMINOSITY trial data demonstrated that MET status was unchanged between paired archival and postprogression tumor biopsies in the majority of patients with EGFR-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC50. Similarly, MET protein OE status was consistent between the first sample and posttreatment samples in 81% of patients with EGFR-wildtype non-squamous NSCLC from the non-interventional METEXPRESS study114. These data could indicate that MET protein OE could be tested as early as diagnosis and that the results could reasonably be extrapolated and may be relevant throughout disease progression, although more studies will be required to confirm the optimal MET testing strategy for MET-directed therapies.

Future perspectives

MET protein OE is an emerging biomarker in NSCLC and one that will gain more prominence as therapeutics that actively target the MET protein proceed through development. Evidence suggests that MET protein OE may have a negative prognostic value, although this requires further investigation. Given its higher prevalence vs other MET biomarkers, such as MET amplification and MET exon 14 skipping mutations, MET protein OE could identify a larger patient population that could benefit from targeted therapies. However, additional data will be required to confirm the clinical validity of the MET protein as a biomarker in NSCLC.

Moving forward, accrual of clinical and real-world data will enable further optimization of the most predictive MET cutoff score for clinical practice. In addition, the development of clinically validated assays along with clear guidelines on MET testing (such as those established for PD-L1 testing) will be requisite to ensure optimal reproducibility and accuracy across laboratories in the real-world and clinical trial settings. Standardization of specimen-handling protocols for all diagnosed malignancies will also be necessary to ensure quality and decrease the negative impact of variable pre-analytic tissue handling. The application of tools that allow for increased sensitivity and precision of protein-based scoring will also be key. Given the move toward digital pathology processes being implemented in current practices, the reproducibility of scoring between microscope vs virtual slide assessments will need to be assessed and confirmed. Early work suggests that artificial intelligence (AI)-based image analysis may further enhance scoring consistency. For example, Rajan et al. applied convolutional and graph neural network models to hematoxylin and eosin-stained NSCLC samples to predict MET OE, while Ingale et al. used AI-assisted quantification of MET IHC to reduce interobserver variability80,144. Although promising, these approaches remain in early development and require validation before clinical adoption.
