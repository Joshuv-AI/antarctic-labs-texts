Sex differences in the associations between lifestyle, intestinal permeability and brain health in middle-aged adults
Authors: R Tume, G Cattaneo, F Carmona-Pontaque, J Solana-Sánchez, L Iglesias-Vejar, M Cubedo-Cullere, M Rabassa, M Cabello-Toscano, A Sanchez-Pla, A Guadall, M Martínez-Huélamo, A Pascual-Leone, C Andrés-Lacueva, D Bartrés-Faz, T Meroño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence suggests that brain health is related to intestinal health. Increased intestinal permeability (IP) biomarkers as risk factors for reduced brain health, considering sex differences in the gut-brain axis, in middle-aged adults has not been studied.AimTo evaluate the associations between IP biomarkers and brain health in a middle-aged cohort of healthy women and men.Methods827 participants from the Barcelona Brain Health Initiative (BBHI) study with a complete neuropsychological evaluation, neurofilament light-chain (NfL), and brain-MRI data were studied (median age: 52y, 427 males). Analyses were adjusted for clinical variables, physical exercise, sleep, nutrition, social interactions, and vital plan. IP was evaluated by levels of lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP), zonulin and soluble-CD14. Integrative analyses included multiple factor analysis. Diagnoses of neurological, psychiatric and a 19-diseases composite-endpoint score during a 4-year period were also analyzed.ResultsAssociations between individual IP biomarkers and brain health were minor in fully adjusted models in both women and men. In women, IP biomarkers were integrated with age, hypertension and hypercholesterolemia in dimension 2, which was inversely associated with cortical and white-matter volumes and positively associated with plasma NfL (all p < 0.05). In men, IP biomakers were combined with high alcohol consumption in dimension 3, which was associated with reduced hippocampal and cortical volumes, as well as higher plasma NfL (all p < 0.05). Only in women, dimensions including IP biomakers were associated with the diagnosis of neurological (OR, 95%CI: 2.3, 1.5-3.6) and other (OR, 95%CI: 1.3, 1.1-1.6) diseases.ConclusionThe association of IP biomarkers with brain health may vary according to sex, and clinical and lifestyle factors. In women, more so than men, IP biomarkers were associated with brain health in mid-life.Keywords: Intestinal permeability, Gut–brain axis, Aging, Brain health, Neurodegeneration, Zonulin, Lipopolysaccharide-binding protein, sCD14, Cortical volume, Hippocampal volume

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), brain health is the state of brain functioning across cognitive, sensory, social-emotional, behavioural and motor domains, allowing persons to realize their full potential over the life course (World Health Organization, 2022). Thus, beyond the individual benefit, brain health is also related to brain capital, as a resource needed for the economic and digital transition foreseen for the coming years and to generate disaster-resilient societies (Eyre et al., 2021). Indeed, neurodegenerative and mental health diseases are major health concerns. Between 2010 and 2021, anxiety and depressive disorders were among the main causes of increasing age-standardised disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs), meaning years of healthy life lost due to a disease or condition (Ferrari et al., 2024). For brain diseases, approaches based on diet and lifestyle changes remain as one of the most valuable options for prevention and/or to be combined with other treatment modalities (Barbera et al., 2023).

Novel evidence shows that many brain disorders are associated with alterations in intestinal barrier function underscoring the role of the gut-brain axis in brain health (Pellegrini et al., 2023). The gut-brain axis is a bidirectional communication network between the gut and the brain including, but not limited to, the action of gut microbial metabolites, endocrine mediators (mainly the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis) and immunological and neural connections (Ashique et al., 2024). Increased intestinal permeability (IP) is characterized by a low-grade systemic inflammation sustained by the continuous diffusion of bacterial factors to the bloodstream, which may impact neurological health via the gut–brain axis (Ghosh et al., 2020). Among the risk factors for “leaky gut”, some studies have reported an increase in IP in individuals over 50 years of age (Ghosh et al., 2020). Moreover, medications, radiation treatment, alcohol consumption, hyperglycemia, and even autonomic nervous system dysfunction after stroke have been associated with increased IP (Camilleri, 2019; Fine et al., 2020). Last, gut microbiota composition and its metabolic activity are also involved in the regulation of IP (Barbara et al., 2021). In consequence, there is a three-point relationship between gut microbiota composition, IP, and brain health disorders (Camilleri, 2019; Grover et al., 2025), which holds the potential to open new treatment modalities for brain health maintenance during aging. Of major interest, gut-brain axis differences between females and males have been proposed to play a role in the observed divergences in the epidemiology of brain diseases during the life course (Jaggar et al., 2020).

IP has been described as the consequence of loss in intestinal tight junctions and increased paracellular translocation of bacterial factors from the gut lumen to the circulation. Unfortunately, non-invasive biomarkers for evaluation of IP are not easily interpretated, and ambiguous roles for these markers have been described (Kitchens and Thompson, 2005). Some of the most commonly used biomarkers are zonulin, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) and soluble CD14 (sCD14). These molecules evaluate different aspects of IP (Grover et al., 2025) and may, in combination, reflect the pathophysiological processes related to intestinal barrier integrity and a chronic low-grade inflammation. Regarding the association of these biomarkers with brain diseases, previous studies have shown an association between serum levels of LBP and increased odds of developing Alzheimer's Disease over 12 years in a nested case-control study (André et al., 2019). Furthermore, some studies have observed that higher zonulin levels were associated with major depressive disorder (Stevens et al., 2018), as well as increased sCD14 levels with higher risk for incident dementia and brain atrophy (Pase et al., 2020). Nonetheless, these studies were carried out in older adults aged 65 years or more.

The importance of the exposure to risk factors in mid-life to shape brain health trajectories during late life has been recently highlighted (Dohm et al., 2024). The life-course approach posits that late-life phenotype is the consequence of exposure to risk factors during the entire lifespan and emphasizes the importance of attempts to identify critical time windows of life in which specific exposures have the greatest effect on health outcomes. In middle-aged adults, there are scarce reports linking IP biomarkers with brain health. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the association between IP biomarkers and brain health measurements and to describe their interaction with lifestyle determinants of brain health in healthy middle-aged women and men (Wang et al., 2019).

Methods

Participants and measures

The Barcelona Brain Health Initiative (BBHI) is a longitudinal cohort study launched in 2017 with the aim of identifying and characterizing the biological, behavioral, and environmental determinants associated with the maintenance of brain health in middle-aged adults. The initial cohort included 5468 participants aged 40 to 65 years (33.2% male), without psychiatric or neurological diagnoses at the time of recruitment. Sex was self-reported at baseline using a binary questionnaire item and was analyzed as sex. Participants were community-dwelling individuals who, compared with the general population, tended to show higher educational levels and healthier lifestyles (Roca-Ventura et al., 2024; Cattaneo et al., 2018).

Participants completed various validated online questionnaires to collect information on lifestyle, psychological and emotional well-being, and perceived general and mental health. The self-administered questionnaires assessed nine components of modifiable healthy lifestyles, six of which are the pillars of the BBHI: cognitive activity, physical activity, sleep, nutrition, vital plan, and socialization. The other three refer to risk factors such as alcohol consumption, smoking, and Body Mass Index (BMI). At each annual wave during follow-up, participants were asked to report any new diagnoses, provide an assessment of their perceived general and mental health, and specify health indicators such as diagnosis of hypercholesterolemia and hypertension. Additional information was also collected using questionnaires assessing quality of life, social networks, and emotional states (Roca-Ventura et al., 2024; Cattaneo et al., 2020).

A subgroup of 1000 participants underwent a comprehensive in-person assessment that included medical assessment, cognitive testing, high-resolution brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and blood extraction. The criteria for inviting these participants were the extreme scores on the PROMIS “Mental Health” subscale, as previously described (Cattaneo et al., 2018). For the present study, we included all participants who had blood extraction at baseline and MRI data, and whose data was validated after preprocessing. The final sample included 827 subjects (427 male) aged 40–65 years, mean ± s.d.: 53 ± 7 years.

The study protocols were approved by the Comitè Ètic d’Investigació (CEIm) de la Fundació Unió Catalana d’Hospitals (ref. CEIC 17/06), and all participants gave their written informed consent to participate in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Intestinal permeability markers

Levels of zonulin (IDK Zonulin, Immundiagnostik AG, Bensheim, Germany), and of LBP and sCD14 (both from Hycult biotech, Uden, Netherlands) were measured in plasma samples by ELISA kits following the manufacturers’ instructions. Values below the limits of detection were left missing. The number of missing values for zonulin, LBP and sCD14 were: 4, 36 and 17, respectively. Intra- and inter-assay coefficient of variations were in all cases <8% and <15%, respectively.

Brain health assessment

Cognitive health. During a separate visit, participants underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation administered by a trained clinical neuropsychologist. The battery of tests included standardized instruments designed to assess a broad range of cognitive domains (Cattaneo et al., 2022). Based on the individual test scores, a global composite index of cognitive function was calculated, together with five domain-specific measures corresponding to processing speed, working memory, episodic memory, cognitive flexibility, and reasoning (See supplementary material for more information). It is important to note that these composite metrics were generated using the entire BBHI cohort as the normative reference. Consequently, the scores for the subgroup analyzed in this study (n = 827) are expressed in relation to the distribution of the whole BBHI cohort. As a result, they are not centered at zero within this subsample but rather indicate whether individual performance was above (positive values) or below (negative values) the average of the overall BBHI population.

Psychological health. Psychological status was assessed using the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21), a validated self-report instrument that measures symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress across three independent subscales. The instrument was administered online at the visit of the blood extraction (Zhou et al., 2023).

Brain MRI scans. MRI data was acquired at the Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (Unitat d’Imatge per Ressonància Magnètica, IDIBAPS) using a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner with a 32-channel head coil. For all participants, high-resolution structural T1-and T2-weighted sequences were obtained. All scans were reviewed by a senior neuroradiologist to exclude clinically relevant abnormalities and were visually inspected by trained technicians for quality control of motion and metallic artifacts (Mulet et al., 2024). For the present analyses, we used pre-processed volumetric measures provided by the BBHI dataset. These included estimated total intracranial volume, left and right hippocampal volumes, total white matter volume, and cortical volume, all expressed in cubic millimeters (mm3). All volumetric measurements were corrected by total intracranial volume. Brain MRI was performed in a visit close to the time of blood extraction.

Plasma neurodegeneration biomarker. NfL is an axonal protein released into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood in response to neuroaxonal injury and thus serves as a nonspecific marker of neuronal integrity; in the general population, higher levels are also associated with brain atrophy and cognitive decline (Khalil et al., 2018; Redondo-et al., 2022). NfL concentrations were determined within the BBHI biobank. Blood samples were collected in EDTA tubes during the medical assessments, processed to obtain plasma aliquots, and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Quantification of NfL was carried out using ultrasensitive single-molecule array (Simoa) technology with the NF-light Advantage assay on an HD-X analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA). Analytical sensitivity of the assay was below 1 pg/mL, with a lower limit of quantification of 2.7 pg/mL. Quality control procedures indicated coefficients of variation below 7% across low and high concentration ranges. All determinations were performed at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory (University of Gothenburg) by certified staff blinded to participants’ clinical and lifestyle data (Cattaneo et al., 2022).

Evaluation of brain health determinants

Physical activity. It was assessed using the self-reported International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which records the frequency, duration, and intensity of physical activity in leisure time, work, and transportation during the previous week. Participants were provided with examples of light, moderate, and vigorous intensity activities, along with physiological references related to breathing and heart rate, to facilitate identification of the corresponding intensity. Based on these responses, Metabolic Equivalents of Task (METs) were calculated, and activities were classified as light, moderate, or vigorous, in accordance with the IPAQ guidelines (Craig et al., 2003).

Social support networks. This item was assessed using the revised version of the Lubben Social Network Scale, an instrument designed to evaluate social isolation in older adults by measuring the perceived support from family and non-family members, considering network size, frequency of contact, and closeness. The scale consists of 12 items grouped into two subscales (family and non-family support), which yield a global score ranging from 0 to 60. Higher values indicate greater social integration, whereas scores ≤20 are considered indicative of social isolation, with intermediate categories distinguishing low, moderate, or high risk (Lubben et al., 2002; Lubben, 1988).

Nutrition. Adherence to the Mediterranean diet was assessed using the validated MEDAS-14 questionnaire. The instrument consists of 14 items, 12 of which address the frequency of consumption of key foods (such as fruits, vegetables, fish, nuts, legumes, and olive oil), while 2 refer to dietary practices characteristic of the Spanish Mediterranean diet (use of sofrito and wine consumption). Each item is scored with 0 or 1, and the total sum yields an adherence index ranging from 0 to 14 points. Higher scores indicate greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet (Schröder et al., 2011).

Vital plan. This domain was assessed using the Personal Growth and Purpose in Life subscales of Ryff's Psychological Well-Being Scale (Ryff, 1995). This is a brief but robust instrument that evaluates the eudaimonic dimension of well-being, addressing aspects such as life meaning, self-realization, and continuous personal development, among others. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (6). Each of these subscales include 7 items, resulting in a total score ranging from 14 to 84, with higher scores reflecting greater perceived psychological well-being in terms of personal growth and life purpose.

Meaning in life. This construct was assessed with the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ), which consists of 10 items distributed across two subscales: Presence of Meaning and Search for Meaning (5 items each). Responses were recorded on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = completely false, 7 = completely true). Sum scores were computed for each subscale (range 5–35), with higher scores indicating greater presence or search for meaning.

Sleep quality. The Jenkins Sleep Scale, a brief self-report questionnaire of four items, was used to evaluate the frequency of common sleep problems during the past four weeks (Jenkins et al., 1988; Tibubos et al., 2020). It addresses difficulty falling asleep, frequent nocturnal awakenings, problems maintaining sleep or waking too early, as well as fatigue and daytime sleepiness despite having slept. Responses are given on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 5 (”22–31 days”), producing a total score between 0 and 20. Higher scores indicate greater sleep disturbances and poorer perceived sleep quality.

Alcohol Consumption: The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), a 10-item instrument developed by the WHO was used to assess drinking patterns. It evaluates alcohol consumption, symptoms of dependence, and alcohol-related problems. Total scores range from 0 to 40, with cut-offs defining four levels: low risk (0–7 points), hazardous use (8–15 points), harmful use (16–19 points), and probable dependence (≥20 points) (Conigrave et al., 1995).

Smoking: Smoking status was self-reported based on the question “Do you smoke?“. Participants were classified into three categories: current smokers (“Yes”), former smokers (“I smoked, but I left it”), and never smokers (“No”).

Nutritional status. Body Mass Index (BMI) was used as an anthropometric indicator of nutritional status, calculated at each visit by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m2).

Socioeconomic status. For the analysis, socioeconomic status (SES) was addressed as a group of two indicators obtained from the sociodemographic questionnaires: the highest educational level attained (Education) and the number of rooms in the house. The education variable was categorized into three levels (1 = primary, 2 = secondary, 3 = higher education). Similarly, according to the number of rooms in the house three levels were established: level 1 = between 0 and 2 rooms, level 2 = 3 rooms, and level 3 > 3 rooms. Both indicators have been previously employed as markers of SES in epidemiological studies, as they capture both cultural capital and material living conditions (Galobardes et al., 2006).

Disease diagnoses

At each annual online follow-up, participants were asked to indicate whether they had received any new physician-confirmed medical diagnoses during the previous year. To focus on incident conditions, individuals who reported a history of disease at baseline were excluded from the analyses, and only new diagnoses during the study period were considered. The reported medical conditions were categorized into three main groups (Roca-Ventura et al., 2024). Psychiatric disorders comprised depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and other psychotic disorders. Neurological diseases included epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, other neurodegenerative conditions, mild cognitive impairment, dementia, stroke, transient ischemic attack, brain tumor and traumatic brain injury. Cardiovascular diseases encompassed myocardial infarction, angina, heart failure, arrhythmia, peripheral vascular disease and stroke of vascular origin. In addition, other clinically relevant conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease, diabetes and cancer were also recorded. For the purposes of analysis, all these categories were considered together under the construct “Total Diagnoses”, while highly prevalent risk factors like hypertension and hypercholesterolemia were tracked independently and excluded from this grouping.

Statistical analysis

Lifestyle trajectories in participants of the BBHI study were shown to remain stable during a 4-year follow-up period (Roca-Ventura et al., 2024). For this reason, variables with missing values in lifestyle variables (10% of the data) at the time of the blood extraction were imputed with values from subsequent waves (next annual observation carried backward). Imputed lifestyle data was 4%, the other 6% were left as missing. Based on biological assumptions regarding sex-related differences in the gut-brain axis (Jaggar et al., 2020), all the statistical analyses were stratified by sex.

Data of continuous variables are shown as median (Q1-Q3). For categorical variables, data is reported as number of participants and percentages. Differences between IP markers by sex were tested using Mann-Whitney U test. Correlations were assessed using Spearman's correlations test.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to reduce the dimensionality and integrate the information from IP markers. Values of principal components (PCs) were next used in association analyses. To evaluate the associations of IP markers and PCs with brain health, linear regression models were employed. In all the models the following covariates were included: age, BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking, education, house rooms, physical activity, MEDAS, social network, Jenkins sleep scale, MLQ, Ryff, hypercholesterolemia and hypertension. For psychological tests (coming from DASS evaluation), censored regression using Tobit models was employed.

Multiple Factor Analysis (MFA) with the R package FactoMineR (Lê et al., 2008) was used to comprehensively integrate clinical, lifestyle, and IP markers to obtain dimensions (conceptually like PCs). Groups of variables in this analysis were: age; BMI; lifestyle (including physical activity, alcohol and smoking); SES (education and house rooms); metabolic (hypertension and hypercholesterolemia); vital plan (Ryff's test subscales and MLQ); diet (MEDAS); sleep (Jenkins); social (Lubben); and IP (zonulin, LBP and sCD14). Dimensions were then associated with brain MRI and plasma NfL data using linear regression. In these analyses, the models included the first 5 dimensions of the MFA as independent variables. Covariates (such as age, BMI, etc.) were not included in the regression models since the dimensions were constructed based on these variables and their inclusion in the models would generate overfitting, biasing the interpretation of the results. Last, logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between dimensions of the MFA and diseases diagnoses during follow-up.

Due to the exploratory nature of the study, p-values <0.05 were considered as statistically significant, and p-values between 0.05 and 0.10 were considered as trends. No correction for multiple testing was conducted based on the aim and exploratory nature of the study. For all the statistical analyses R statistical software (Version 4.4.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna) was employed.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The clinical characteristics of the population are shown in Table 1. As already reported (Cattaneo et al., 2020) the studied population consisted of middle-aged individuals with a relatively high educational level and SES. The overall prevalence of hypertension and hypercholesterolemia was 20% and 33%, respectively. As expected, BMI, alcohol consumption, and diagnosis of hypertension were higher in men than in women (Table 1). Conversely, men presented lower Jenkins sleep scale (lower sleep complaints) and Lubben Social Scale (lower level of social connections) than women (Table 1). None of the participants reported a diagnosis of gastrointestinal disease.

Intestinal permeability markers

Fig. 1 shows the distribution of plasma concentrations of IP markers in women and men. Women showed higher sCD14 levels than men (p < 0.001), while no other statistical differences were observed in the concentrations of LBP and zonulin (Fig. 1).

Concentrations of intestinal permeability markers in women (n = 400) and men (n = 427). LBP and sCD14 concentrations are in μg/mL and Zonulin concentration is in ng/mL. A statistically significant difference was only noticed for sCD14 between women and men (p < 0.001).

PCA was carried out to combine these features into components that could be more representative of IP. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows biplots for women (panel a) and men (panel b). As it can be seen, PC1 had diverse, but consistent, contributions from all biomarkers (higher values indicating higher IP), but PC2 was the resultant of an inverse relationship between LBP and zonulin in women, and between LBP and sCD14 in men.

Association between intestinal permeability biomarkers and brain health measurements

Associations between IP markers, principal components (PCs) and brain health are shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Table 2, respectively. In women, PC1 did not show any association with brain health measurements. Conversely, PC2 (which combined LBP and zonulin concentrations in inverse directions) was positively associated with some cognitive scores and inversely with the anxiety scale of DASS evaluation. Intriguingly, PC2 was inversely associated with cortical volume and positively with NfL levels. In men, PC1 was inversely associated with processing speed and left hippocampal volume. Similarly, a trend towards an inverse association was observed between PC1 and global cognition, and episodic memory scores (Table 2). On the other hand, PC2 (which combined LBP and sCD14 in inverse directions) was positively associated with working memory and inversely with plasma NfL concentration.

Regarding the associations of individual IP biomarkers, in women, zonulin was inversely associated with some cognitive evaluations, but intriguingly it was positively associated with cortical volume and negatively with NfL plasma concentrations. Furthermore, higher LBP levels were associated with lower anxiety, stress and total DASS scores. No statistically significant associations between sCD14 and any of the brain health evaluations were observed (Supplementary Table 1). Among men, zonulin was directly associated with working memory. LBP was inversely associated with several cognitive evaluations, as well as a statistical trend towards an inverse association with left hippocampus volume. Lastly, sCD14 levels showed a statistical trend towards an inverse association with some cognitive evaluations, and a positive one with NfL levels (Supplementary Table 1).

Overall, although statistically significant, the standardized Beta coefficients were rather small (∼|0.10|), attesting for marginal associations between IP biomarkers and brain health measurements in fully-adjusted models. The only exception was the association between PC2 and anxiety scores in women with a standardized Beta coefficient of −0.26. When medication use for hypertension and hypercholesterolemia were added to the linear models, results remained the same (data not shown).

Integrative analysis combining intestinal permeability biomarkers and brain health determinants

Correlations between IP biomarkers and brain health determinants are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. In women, PC1 was positively correlated with age and BMI, while negatively with education. PC2 was only slightly correlated with BMI. In men, only PC1 was positively correlated with age and alcohol intake. Remarkably, individually all the IP markers were significantly correlated with age among women, while in men none (Supplementary Fig. 2).

A comprehensive analysis was carried out to integrate IP biomakers with brain health determinants (lifestyle factors influencing brain health) using Multiple Factor Analysis. Contributions of IP to the top three dimensions for women and men were calculated and plotted in a heatmap (Fig. 2). As it can be seen in women IP has a mid-contribution to Dimensions 2 and 3 (18 and 10%, respectively); while in men IP has a relatively high contribution only to Dimension 3 (37%). Higher dimension 2 in women was related with older age, higher BMI, and cardiometabolic risk conditions (hypertension and hypercholesterolemia), apart from higher IP markers. In contrast, higher Dimension 3 was related with lifestyle (low physical activity, high alcohol consumption and current smoking status), intermediate educational level, higher sleep disturbances and MEDAS score and lower LBP levels. In men, Dimension 3, apart from increased IP biomarkers, was also related with higher alcohol consumption and MEDAS score.

Contributions of the different groups of variables to the first three dimensions derived by the Multiple Factor Analysis in women (n = 350) and men (n = 356). Only participants with complete data in all the variables were included. BMI, body mass index; SES, socioeconomical status; IP, intestinal permeability.

Next, we evaluated the associations of Dimension 2 and 3 in women, and Dimension 3 in men, with brain MRI measurements and NfL levels (Table 3). In women, Dimension 2, which included age, BMI, cardiometabolic risk conditions and IP biomarkers, showed inverse associations with cortical and white matter volume and a direct association with plasma NfL. Dimension 3, which combined contributions from physical activity, alcohol and tobacco consumption, higher sleep complaints, indicators of SES, and lower LBP concentrations, was also directly associated with NfL concentration. In men, Dimension 3, which was mainly composed of higher IP biomarkers, high alcohol consumption and higher MEDAS, was inversely correlated with right and left hippocampal volume, and with brain cortical volume. Accordingly, the association of Dimension 3 with plasma NfL levels in men was positive (Table 3).

Discussion

Up to our knowledge, this is the first study in healthy middle-aged individuals assessing the associations of IP biomarkers with clinical and lifestyle factors, and brain health measurements underscoring the presence of sex differences. While in women age was strongly correlated with IP biomarkers, in men these correlations were not observed. Analysis integrating IP biomarkers with clinical and lifestyle showed that in middle-aged women, a dimension combining older age, and diagnoses of hypertension and hypercholesterolemia, together with increased IP biomarkers, was inversely associated with cortical and white matter volume, and positively with plasma NfL. In men, a dimension integrating elevated IP biomarkers and high consumption of alcohol was associated to reduced brain hippocampal and cortical volume, as well as higher plasma NfL. In agreement with the brain health concept, dimensions including IP biomarkers in women were associated with increased odds for disease diagnosis in a 4-year period, but not in men. As dimension 2 in women included non-modifiable risk factors, such as age, and diagnosis of hypertension and hypercholesterolemia, our findings suggest the possibility that in women, mid-life may represent a critical time window in which interventions targeting IP may be of importance for brain health maintenance. The associative evidence provided by this exploratory study is hypothesis generating and translational implications need to be further studied.

In both women and men, PC1 was related to the traditional concept of IP correlated with older age, increased concentrations of IP biomarkers and a possible trigger of low-grade chronic inflammation. In women, PC1 was not associated with any evaluated measure of brain health, probably because regression models were adjusted for age. It was only after integration with clinical and lifestyle data, that the contribution of IP biomarkers was evident. As such, these results underscore that aging (Man et al., 2015; Quin et al., 2024) and menopause transition (Shieh et al., 2020) are associated with increased IP in women, and that the impact of these on brain health can not be isolated one from the other. Indeed, Dimension 2 (combining age, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia and IP) was inversely associated with brain MRI data and increased odds for disease diagnoses during the 4-year follow-up period. Conversely, in men, there were some inverse associations between PC1 and cognitive tests, and with left hippocampal volume. After integration with lifestyle variables in men, the dimension combining IP biomarkers and alcohol consumption showed inverse correlations with brain MRI measures, especially hippocampal volume. Human studies have shown that alcohol can impair intestinal barrier integrity and increase IP, which may help explain why alcohol was integrated with IP biomarkers in the MFA analysis in men (Leclercq et al., 2014; Donnadieu-Rigole et al., 2018). These results are relevant and suggest that higher IP biomarkers in middle-aged men (in a context of elevated alcohol consumption) could contribute to reductions in hippocampal volume, an early sign in the development of brain diseases (Achterberg et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2020). A longer follow-up may determine whether this association translates into clinically relevant diagnoses among men in this study.

Analysis of PC2 evidenced the sexual dimorphism regarding the physiological roles of LBP and sCD14 in middle-aged women and men. While, in women, LBP and sCD14 concentrations were closely related, and contributed to PC2 in a positive direction; in men, LBP and sCD14 showed opposite contributions to PC2. Furthermore, in women, PC2 was associated with reduced anxiety with one of the largest standardized Beta coefficients in the study (−0.26). This result in part reflects the observed inverse association between LBP and anxiety. This result may seem contradictory to the canonical role of LBP as a marker for higher IP and endotoxemia. However, LBP concentration in middle-aged women could reflect estrogenic action (Chisamore et al., 2012). In our study, higher scores of PC2 represents a condition not correlated with age (as reported in Supplementary Fig. 2) in which LBP levels are high and zonulin ones are low. This case may represent women with physiological estrogenic action, in which high LBP may be a biomarker for the protective action of estrogen against depressive symptoms during perimenopause (Gordon et al., 2018). Indeed, higher LBP levels were associated with lower Dimension 3 values, which was associated with increased risk for neurological and psychiatric diagnoses during follow-up. It has already been reported that menopause leads to higher LBP levels and other IP markers (Shieh et al., 2020). Therefore, it is in the context of postmenopausal women with older ages that increased LBP concentration may be considered as a biomarker for increased bacterial translocation as consequence of both decreased estrogenic action and aging. More studies in women at different stages of their reproductive life are needed to confirm these assumptions.

On the other hand, in men, PC2 was inversely associated with NfL levels and showed a trend towards an association with reduced stress. Most, likely these results reflect the contribution of low sCD14 concentration to higher PC2 scores. Among middle-aged men, in contrast with middle-aged women, higher sCD14 may represent a biomarker of immune activation of TLR4 in response to bacterial translocation. Supporting our interpretation, higher sCD14 levels have been previously associated with higher IL-6 levels (Kiecolt et al., 2018) and atherosclerosis (Reiner, 2013), even though women showed higher levels of sCD14 than men. This finding could be explained by the effects of estrogens in upregulating the cell surface expression of CD14 in immune cells (Rettew et al., 2009). In this sense, higher expression of CD14 in women due to estrogenic action could obscure the straight-forward interpretation of sCD14 as a marker of IP. In fact, differences by sex have been reported in the association between the inflammatory response to LPS and depressive symptoms in middle-aged adults (Knight et al., 2020). A correlation between lower ex-vivo inflammatory responses to LPS and higher depressive symptoms in women, and the opposite in men, was observed (Knight et al., 2020). In this regard, women physiologically respond with a heightened inflammatory response upon exposure to LPS, which is more rapidly terminated by increased cortisol secretion (Wegner et al., 2017). Therefore, these observations are partially compatible with our results, in which higher PC2 (combining higher LBP and sCD14, while lower zonulin, representative of a ready-state higher activation level combined with low IP) was inversely associated with depression in women. However, in men there exists controversy because higher LBP/sCD14 ratios (almost equivalent to higher PC2 in our study) were associated with marital distress and depressive symptomatology (Kiecolt et al., 2018). Longitudinal measurements of IP markers and brain health evaluations are needed to solve these controversies.

Some of the limitations of the study are inherent to the use of biomarkers for evaluation of IP. In fact, the association between individual IP biomarkers with brain health has been ambiguous, and dual biological roles have been described for LBP, as well as for sCD14. On one side, both biomarkers could either facilitate bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) presentation to TLR4 receptors (ultimately triggering inflammatory pathways) or contribute to LPS clearance through lipoproteins as well (Kitchens and Thompson, 2005). Furthermore, the study cohort included apparently healthy participants, and therefore only subtle changes in IP biomarkers were evaluated. It has been confirmed that non-infectious gastrointestinal disorders may increase the risk for Alzheimer's Disease (Shafieinouri et al., 2025). Therefore, another sample including participants with overt diagnosis of gastrointestinal diseases may provide different estimates between IP markers and brain health. In this aspect, it remains to be determined if in populations from different SES and higher comorbidity burden (older adults, people with obesity, or other preexistent chronic diseases, such as depression, type 2 diabetes or chronic kidney disease, among others), IP biomarkers may have a higher influence in brain health. The standardized Beta coefficients of the associations between IP biomarkers (as well as their PCs) and brain health measurements were rather small in fully adjusted models (Betast ∼ |0.1|) indicating marginal associations when not integrated with clinical and lifestyle factors. Indeed, IP may be influenced by lifestyle and many clinical conditions; thus, the potential impact in brain health of approaches targeting IP, like a polyphenol-rich diet (Peron et al., 2021), remains to be determined. The exploratory nature of the study provides the rationale for testing further this hypothesis but do not identify IP as a modifiable pathway towards brain health impairment. Among the strengths of the study, we comprehensively included several lifestyle factors, including vital plan, sleep quality, and social network, which are often overlooked in other studies. Last, brain health was assessed through multiple domains using a validated battery of neuropsychological tests, data from MRI scans and a validated biomarker for neurodegeneration (Hofmann et al., 2024).

In conclusion, associations of IP biomarkers with brain health measurements differed by sex and they relied strongly in their integration with clinical and lifestyle factors in healthy, middle-aged individuals. In women, the increase in IP biomarkers may be a primary consequence of aging and the menopausal transition; and thus, mid-life could represent a critical window in women's life in which IP may represent a modifiable risk factor for maintaining brain health. In men, higher levels of IP biomarkers in combination with higher alcohol consumption were associated with reduced brain hippocampal and cortical volume; however, these were not translated into increased risk for disease diagnoses. More specific and unambiguous biomarkers for IP are needed to estimate the real impact of IP on brain health in middle-aged women and men.
