Cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury in lupus-prone NZB/W F1 mice: a potential experimental model of acute kidney injury superimposed on chronic kidney disease
Authors: Eun Wha CHOI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease frequently complicated by immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis that can progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD). Acute kidney injury (AKI) superimposed on CKD (AKI on CKD) is associated with a markedly poor prognosis; however, experimental models that accurately reflect this condition in SLE are limited. This study aimed to establish and characterize an AKI on CKD model using SLE-prone mice with pre-existing immune complex-mediated glomerulonephritis. Female NZB/W F1 mice with established glomerulonephritis at 32 weeks of age were administered cisplatin (10 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) to induce AKI. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin (NGAL), urinary NGAL, and the urinary NGAL-to-creatinine ratio were evaluated as clinicopathological indicators of renal injury. Following cisplatin administration, BUN and serum NGAL concentrations increased significantly. Urinary NGAL concentrations and the urinary NGAL-to-creatinine ratio were also significantly elevated on days 1–4 after AKI induction compared with baseline, whereas urinary protein concentration, urinary creatinine concentration, and the urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio showed no significant changes. NGAL is synthesized by macrophages, glial cells, and epithelial cells under inflammatory conditions and is an established biomarker of tubular injury in AKI. The observed increases in BUN and NGAL parameters confirm successful induction of AKI in SLE mice with pre-existing CKD. This cisplatin-induced AKI model in NZB/W F1 mice may serve as a useful AKI on CKD model for investigating disease mechanisms and developing therapeutic strategies for AKI in patients with SLE and CKD.Keywords: acute kidney injury on chronic kidney disease, lupus nephritis, neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin, (NZB×NZW) F1 mice, systemic lupus erythematosus

INTRODUCTION

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease of unknown etiology. It is thought to result from a complex interplay of genetic factors, immunological abnormalities, hormonal influences, and environmental factors. Antinuclear antibodies, including anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies, are generated, leading to the formation of antigen-antibody immune complexes. These immune complexes deposit in small blood vessels, including the basement membranes of the skin and kidneys, thereby inducing inflammation and tissue damage in multiple organs [23].

Female (NZB ×NZW) F1 mice (NZB/W F1) exhibit a spontaneous lupus-like disease characterized by progressive proteinuria and increasing anti-dsDNA antibody titers beginning at approximately 5 weeks of age. Owing to the development of immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis, up to 93% of untreated NZB/W F1 mice reportedly die by 10 months of age, making this strain a well-established experimental model of human SLE-associated lupus nephritis [26].

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined as an abrupt decline in renal function occurring over hours to days and is clinically diagnosed based on increases in serum creatinine concentration and/or reductions in urine output [2]. According to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, AKI encompasses a spectrum of renal injury ranging from mild functional impairment to severe renal failure requiring renal replacement therapy [15]. AKI is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, and its impact is particularly pronounced in individuals with underlying chronic kidney disease (CKD), in whom AKI accelerates renal functional decline and markedly worsens overall prognosis. This clinical entity, commonly referred to as acute kidney injury on chronic kidney disease (AKI on CKD), is now recognized as a distinct and clinically important condition associated with poorer outcomes compared with AKI occurring in previously normal kidneys [4, 8]. Traditional markers of renal function, such as serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), have limited sensitivity for early tubular injury and often increase only after substantial renal damage has occurred [9, 16]. Consequently, there has been growing interest in the identification of early and sensitive biomarkers for AKI. Neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin (NGAL), also known as lipocalin-2, is a 25-kDa protein originally identified in neutrophils and subsequently shown to be highly upregulated in renal tubular epithelial cells following ischemic or nephrotoxic injury [12, 18]. Both serum and urinary NGAL concentrations increase within hours of AKI onset and have been extensively validated as early biomarkers of tubular injury in experimental models and clinical studies [9, 14, 17].

In addition to its diagnostic utility, NGAL plays important roles in innate immune responses and inflammatory processes through iron sequestration and modulation of cellular survival pathways [10, 12]. These immunological functions are particularly relevant in autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), supporting the use of NGAL as a clinicopathological marker for evaluating acute tubular injury superimposed on immune complex–mediated chronic kidney disease [4, 9].

Based on these observations, we hypothesized that cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury in lupus-prone NZB/W F1 mice with pre-existing immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis would result in exaggerated tubular injury, accompanied by a marked increase in NGAL expression, thereby recapitulating the clinicopathological features of AKI on CKD.

The aim of this study was to induce acute kidney injury using cisplatin in NZB/W F1 mice with established immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis and proteinuria, and to evaluate, using clinicopathological parameters, whether this experimental system can serve as a reproducible and clinically relevant model of AKI on CKD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals

Four-week-old female NZB/W F1 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and acclimated for 2 weeks before the experiment. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of CHA University (CE2019158). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Animal Welfare Act of the Republic of Korea and with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Induction of acute kidney injury

In SLE mice with established glomerulonephritis at ≥32 weeks of age, eight mice were treated with cisplatin (10 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally on Day 0. Cisplatin was freshly prepared in saline at a concentration of 1 mg/mL immediately before administration, and food and water were withheld for 6 hr prior to injection [24]. Fresh urine samples were collected by gentle abdominal massage before cisplatin administration (d0) and on days 1, 2, 3, and 4 after administration (d1–d4). Blood samples were collected before cisplatin administration (d0) and 4 days after administration (d4); serum was separated and stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer until analysis. One mouse died the day after cisplatin administration and was excluded from further analysis. Therefore, serial blood and urine samples were obtained from the remaining seven mice.

Measurement of blood urea nitrogen (BUN)

BUN concentrations were measured using a urea nitrogen colorimetric detection kit (EIABUN, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of neutrophil gelatinase–associated lipocalin (NGAL) in serum and urine

Serum and urine NGAL concentrations were measured using a mouse Lipocalin-2/NGAL immunoassay (MLCN20, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Samples were diluted with Calibrator Diluent RD5-24, and concentrations were calculated from a standard curve generated using serial dilutions of the supplied standard.

Anti-dsDNA antibody concentrations

Anti-dsDNA antibody concentrations were measured using a mouse Anti-dsDNA ELISA kit (Shibayagi Co., Ltd., Shibukawa, Japan).

Measurement of urine protein

Urine protein was measured using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method, as described in a previous study [6].

Measurement of urine creatinine

Urine samples were stored at −80°C until analysis. Prior to measurement, samples were thawed and centrifuged at 4°C for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected. Urine samples were diluted 1:20 with distilled water and analyzed using a commercial creatinine assay kit (KGE005, R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio and NGAL-to-creatinine ratio were subsequently calculated.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), and Masson’s trichrome staining

Kidneys were collected from 32-week-old female NZB/W F1 mice at day 4 following cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury superimposed on pre-existing lupus nephritis. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and humanely euthanized by cervical dislocation before organ harvesting. For light microscopic evaluation, tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin using standard histological procedures. PAS staining was performed to evaluate tubular injury, including cast formation, and Masson’s trichrome staining was used to assess fibrotic changes.

Renal histopathological changes were evaluated using semi-quantitative scoring systems. Nine random cortical fields per section were examined under light microscopy at ×200 magnification by a blinded observer. AKI–related tubular damage was assessed based on tubular necrosis and cast formation. Tubular necrosis was graded on a scale of 0 to 4 according to the percentage of affected tubules showing epithelial degeneration, necrosis, or tubular dilation (0, normal; 1, <10% of tubules affected; 2, 10–25%; 3, 25–50%; 4, >50%). Cast formation was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scale from 0 to 3 (0, none; 1, rare casts; 2, moderate casts; 3, frequent casts).

CKD-related changes were evaluated based on inflammatory cell infiltration, mesangial proliferation, tubular dilation, and interstitial fibrosis. Inflammatory cell infiltration was graded on a scale of 0 to 3 (0, none; 1, mild focal infiltration; 2, moderate multifocal infiltration; 3, severe diffuse infiltration). Mesangial proliferation was scored from 0 to 3 according to the degree of mesangial cell proliferation and mesangial matrix expansion (0, normal; 1, mild proliferation; 2, moderate mesangial expansion and hypercellularity; 3, marked mesangial proliferation). Tubular dilation was graded on a scale of 0 to 3 (0, none; 1, mild dilation; 2, moderate dilation; 3, severe dilation). Interstitial fibrosis was assessed primarily using Masson’s trichrome staining and scored from 0 to 3 (0, none; 1, mild fibrosis; 2, moderate fibrosis; 3, severe fibrosis).

Immunofluorescence staining for immunoglobulin G (IgG) and complement component 3 (C3)

Immunofluorescence staining was carried out using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200; Millipore, AP308F) or FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse C3 (1:100; Cappel, West Chester, PA, USA). Slides were mounted with a DAPI-containing mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Southfield, MI, USA) and analyzed using an LSM 880 laser-scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss GmbH, Germany). Glomerular deposition of IgG and C3 was semi-quantitatively scored on a scale of 0–4 (0, none; 1, mild; 2, moderate; 3, strong; 4, very strong) based on fluorescence intensity.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) in the text and are shown as dot plots with box-and-whisker plots in the figures. Prior to statistical analysis, the normality of the paired differences was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the assumption of normality was not consistently satisfied and the sample size was small, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for comparisons between day 0 and subsequent time points. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationships between biochemical parameters and renal injury severity scores, as well as between AKI and CKD histopathologic scores. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

One of the eight NZB/W F1 mice died the day after cisplatin administration and was excluded from the analysis. Therefore, the following results are based on the remaining seven mice. The excluded mouse exhibited markedly elevated renal injury biomarkers at baseline and severe chronic renal lesions at necropsy.

BUN, serum NGAL, and anti-dsDNA antibody levels

In 32-week-old female NZB/W F1 mice with established glomerulonephritis, induction of acute kidney injury using cisplatin resulted in a significant increase in BUN and serum NGAL concentrations; BUN levels were 31.0 (29.7) [median (IQR)] mg/dL prior to AKI induction and increased significantly to 173.2 (284.9) mg/dL following AKI induction (P=0.043; Fig. 1A). Serum NGAL levels were 248.2 (181.7) ng/mL prior to AKI induction and increased significantly to 1335.5 (2922.3) ng/mL following AKI induction (P=0.028; Fig. 1B). For reference, serum NGAL concentrations in 6-week-old NZB/W F1 mice, prior to the development of glomerulonephritis, were 112.7 (31.0) ng/mL, representing near-normal baseline levels. In contrast, anti-dsDNA antibody levels were 1,640.0 (500.2) U/mL prior to AKI induction and decreased significantly to 613.4 (584.1) U/mL following AKI induction (P=0.018; Fig. 1C).

Urine protein, urine creatinine and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio

Urine protein concentration, urine creatinine concentration, and the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio did not differ significantly before and after AKI induction (Fig. 2C–E).

Histopathological examination of kidneys

Histopathological examination of kidneys collected on day 4 after cisplatin administration revealed prominent features of acute tubular injury, including tubular epithelial necrosis, intraluminal cast formation, tubular dilation, and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration. These acute tubular lesions were observed in the background of pre-existing immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis, characterized by mesangial expansion and glomerular structural alterations, consistent with an AKI on CKD phenotype (Fig. 3A–C).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we successfully established an experimental model of acute kidney injury superimposed on chronic kidney disease (AKI on CKD) in lupus-prone NZB/W F1 mice by inducing cisplatin nephrotoxicity in animals with pre-existing immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis. The model was characterized by significant increases in BUN, serum NGAL, urinary NGAL, and the urinary NGAL-to-creatinine ratio, together with histopathological evidence of acute tubular injury.

In this study, 32-week-old NZB/W F1 mice were selected because lupus nephritis with clinically detectable renal dysfunction is typically established at this age. Our longitudinal observations demonstrated progressive increases in BUN levels and proteinuria beginning around 28–32 weeks, indicating the development of chronic kidney disease [5]. Previous studies have similarly reported that histopathological renal damage becomes evident in NZB/W F1 mice at approximately 28–30 weeks of age [3]. Consistent with these observations, the mice used in the present study also exhibited detectable proteinuria prior to cisplatin administration, supporting the presence of underlying lupus nephritis–associated renal injury at baseline. Furthermore, renal histopathology performed after AKI induction revealed characteristic features of lupus nephritis, including mesangial proliferation and immune complex deposition, suggesting the presence of pre-existing chronic glomerular injury.

Cisplatin-induced AKI is a well-characterized experimental model that predominantly affects proximal tubular epithelial cells through mechanisms involving DNA damage, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory signaling pathways [19, 20]. In the present study, typical histopathological hallmarks of cisplatin nephrotoxicity—tubular epithelial necrosis, intraluminal cast formation, tubular dilation, and interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration—were observed on day 4 after cisplatin administration. Importantly, these acute tubular lesions were clearly distinguishable from the chronic glomerular pathology intrinsic to NZB/W F1 mice, supporting the interpretation that acute tubular injury was superimposed on established chronic kidney disease.

NGAL is rapidly induced in renal tubular epithelial cells in response to ischemic and nephrotoxic injury and has been extensively validated as a sensitive and early biomarker of AKI [9, 14, 17]. In this study, both serum and urinary NGAL concentrations increased markedly within 24 hr after cisplatin administration and remained elevated for several days, whereas conventional markers of renal function, such as urinary protein and creatinine concentrations, did not show significant changes. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that NGAL reflects acute tubular injury rather than glomerular damage and may detect AKI earlier than traditional renal biomarkers [21].

The pronounced elevation of NGAL in this model is particularly relevant in the context of systemic lupus erythematosus, as it not only reflects tubular injury but also participates in innate immune responses and inflammatory processes. Given the heightened inflammatory milieu characteristic of SLE, the observed NGAL response may reflect both acute tubular damage and amplified inflammatory signaling.

Notably, baseline NGAL levels at 32 weeks of age (day 0) were already higher than those observed in 6-week-old NZB/W F1 mice, suggesting the presence of subclinical tubular stress associated with established lupus nephritis; serum NGAL levels showed an age-dependent increase in NZB/W F1 mice, rising from 112.7 (31.0) ng/mL at 6 weeks to 248.2 (181.7) ng/mL at 32 weeks prior to AKI induction. Following cisplatin-induced AKI, NGAL levels increased markedly to 1,335.5 (181.7) ng/mL at day 4. For comparison, NZB/W F1 mice of the same birth date, untreated at 42 weeks of age (n=6), exhibited NGAL levels of 1,068.1 (830.7) ng/mL [5], demonstrating that the combination of pre-existing chronic renal pathology and acute tubular injury results in a pronounced NGAL response.

While the inflammatory environment associated with lupus nephritis in NZB/W F1 mice may contribute to tubular susceptibility, our results suggest that inflammatory cell infiltration alone was not strongly correlated with AKI severity. Instead, AKI scores showed stronger associations with structural features of chronic kidney disease, including mesangial proliferation, tubular dilation, and fibrosis. Consequently, the composite CKD score, which integrates inflammatory cell infiltration with mesangial proliferation, tubular dilation, and fibrosis, was strongly correlated with AKI severity (ρ=0.843, P=0.009). This finding indicates that the overall burden of chronic renal pathology, rather than inflammation alone, influences the susceptibility of renal tubules to nephrotoxic injury.

Baseline BUN concentrations measured prior to AKI induction were also positively correlated with both CKD severity and subsequent AKI severity. Mice with higher baseline BUN levels exhibited more advanced chronic renal lesions and were more susceptible to cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury. These findings suggest that baseline BUN not only reflects the pre-existing burden of chronic renal pathology but may also serve as a predictor of vulnerability to nephrotoxic injury. In contrast, other baseline biomarkers, including serum NGAL, urinary NGAL, anti-dsDNA antibody levels, and urinary protein markers, did not show significant correlations with CKD or AKI scores, indicating that they may primarily reflect ongoing tubular injury or systemic autoimmunity rather than pre-existing chronic renal damage.

Clinically, AKI occurring in patients with pre-existing CKD is associated with accelerated progression to end-stage renal disease and increased mortality compared with AKI in patients without underlying renal disease [2, 4]. Despite its clinical importance, experimental models that faithfully recapitulate AKI on CKD in the setting of autoimmune disease remain limited. The present model addresses this gap by combining spontaneous immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis with superimposed nephrotoxic AKI, thereby providing a platform for investigating pathophysiological mechanisms and therapeutic interventions relevant to patients with SLE. From a translational perspective, this model may reflect clinically relevant scenarios in which patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and pre-existing immune complex–mediated chronic kidney disease are exposed to additional acute renal insults. In clinical practice, patients with SLE may develop acute kidney injury following exposure to nephrotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, such as cisplatin, during cancer treatment, or as a postoperative complication after major surgeries. In these settings, the presence of underlying lupus nephritis substantially increases susceptibility to AKI and is associated with worse renal and overall outcomes. The kidneys of lupus-prone NZB/W F1 mice are characterized by immune complex deposition, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cytokine production associated with lupus nephritis [1]. This inflammatory microenvironment may enhance the susceptibility of renal tubular cells to nephrotoxic injury. Cisplatin is known to induce acute tubular damage through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage [22]. Therefore, the pre-existing inflammatory milieu in lupus nephritis may interact with cisplatin-induced toxicity and amplify tubular injury responses, potentially leading to increased expression of injury markers such as NGAL [17]. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when extrapolating cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity to the multifactorial mechanisms of human AKI on CKD. Despite this limitation, this model may provide a useful experimental platform for studying acute renal injury in the context of chronic inflammatory kidney disease.

Interestingly, anti-dsDNA antibody levels were significantly decreased following cisplatin administration in this study. Although cisplatin is widely used to induce experimental AKI through tubular toxicity, several studies have suggested that platinum-based chemotherapeutic agents may also exert immunomodulatory effects. Notably, a clinical case report described complete remission of lupus nephritis following chemoradiotherapy including cisplatin in a patient with cervical cancer [11]. Although the precise mechanism remains unclear and both chemotherapy and radiotherapy were administered, the specific contribution of cisplatin to the observed immunological effect remains uncertain. Therefore, the potential immunological effects of cisplatin should be considered when interpreting the long-term disease course in this AKI-on-CKD model.

This study has several limitations. Although serum creatinine is widely used as a standard indicator of renal function, it was not included in the present analysis. In murine lupus models, serum creatinine often exhibits limited sensitivity compared with BUN, as it is strongly influenced by muscle mass [13]. In systemic lupus erythematosus, disease-associated weight loss and reduced muscle mass may result in lower baseline creatinine levels, thereby further limiting its sensitivity. In addition, serum creatinine may remain within the normal range despite underlying renal impairment in lupus nephritis [25]. Accordingly, several experimental lupus nephritis studies preferentially utilize BUN as a biochemical marker of renal impairment [7, 27]. Nevertheless, serum creatinine remains an important complementary parameter, and its inclusion in future studies would provide a more comprehensive assessment of renal function.

Histopathological comparisons with non–cisplatin-treated control kidneys were not performed. However, the tubular lesions observed are well-established hallmarks of cisplatin-induced AKI and are distinct from the chronic glomerular changes typically observed in NZB/W F1 mice [17, 18]. In addition, the absence of a vehicle-treated age-matched SLE control group limits direct histological confirmation of baseline CKD prior to AKI induction, although biochemical findings and the presence of proteinuria support underlying lupus nephritis–associated renal injury in the animals used in this study.

Longer-term outcomes following AKI induction were not evaluated; therefore, studies assessing recovery or progression of renal injury would further enhance the translational value of this model. In addition, long-term observational studies are needed to further evaluate the impact of cisplatin on autoimmune responses and disease progression in this AKI-on-CKD model.

Some inter-individual variability in AKI severity was observed following cisplatin administration. In one mouse, biochemical markers such as BUN and serum NGAL did not increase despite histological evidence of mild tubular injury, a pattern consistent with subclinical AKI. This variability may reflect differences in individual susceptibility to cisplatin and is a known feature of experimental cisplatin-induced AKI models. The cisplatin dose used in this study (10 mg/kg) was selected based on previously established murine AKI protocols and has been reported to reliably induce tubular injury within several days. Nevertheless, variability in AKI severity may occur under these conditions. Future studies may further optimize dosing conditions to improve the reproducibility of this AKI-on-CKD model. Furthermore, the correlation analyses performed in this study should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, and further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

In conclusion, cisplatin-induced AKI in lupus-prone NZB/W F1 mice with established immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis represents a reproducible and clinically relevant experimental model of AKI on CKD. This model may be useful for elucidating disease mechanisms and for preclinical evaluation of therapeutic strategies targeting acute tubular injury in patients with SLE and chronic kidney disease.
