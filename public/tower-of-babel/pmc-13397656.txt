Usefulness of chest compression landmarks for improving the quality of infant cardiopulmonary resuscitation: a simulation study
Authors: Da Hyun Kim, Seung Jun Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to determine whether using a visible landmark could improve the quality of infant cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).MethodsIn this matched, randomized, simulation-based study conducted at a tertiary academic hospital, PALS-certified nurses who provided informed consent performed three cycles of infant chest compressions on a Laerdal Little Baby manikin equipped with a QCPR sensor. Each participant completed compressions under one of three randomized conditions: correct landmark, incorrect landmark, and no landmark. The primary outcome was compression accuracy, and the secondary outcome was hand position consistency across cycles.ResultsTwenty-seven PALS-certified nurses were enrolled and evenly randomized into the three groups. Mean compression accuracy was significantly higher in the correct-landmark group compared with the incorrect- and no-landmark groups (99.6% vs. 46.9% and 68.1%, respectively; p < 0.001). Compression depth and rate did not differ among groups, with all within the PALS-recommended range. Cycle-to-cycle spatial variability across cycles was also greatest in the correct-landmark group, demonstrating enhanced precision and reproducibility of infant chest compressions.ConclusionIn this simulation-based study, placing a visible landmark at the correct anatomical site significantly improved the accuracy and consistency of infant chest compressions. Further studies in clinical settings are warranted to validate these findings.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01625-5.

Introduction

In North America, the annual incidence of pediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is 8.04 per 100,000 person-years, and approximately 15,200 in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) events occur each year—both markedly lower than in adults [1–3]. Although cardiac arrest in children is relatively uncommon, it is often associated with poor survival and substantial neurological sequelae [4–6]. Therefore, the delivery of high-quality chest compressions during resuscitation is critically important.

The 2025 American Heart Association Guidelines emphasize maintaining rate and depth, along with full chest recoil to ensure high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) [7]. All of these are factors that ultimately contribute to maintaining a high cardiac output; although correct hand placement is not directly included among them, accurate compression at the proper anatomical site is equally critical, as it maximizes left ventricular outflow and perfusion [8–10].

Although experienced healthcare providers can generally identify the appropriate hand position recommended in current guidelines, consistency may vary when multiple rescuers alternate compressions, particularly among those with differing experience levels. A previous study in adults using a visible Heartsaver sticker demonstrated that such visual guidance can improve CPR quality [11]. However, this approach has not been evaluated in infants.

We hypothesized that, because infant CPR is performed using two fingers or two thumbs on a much smaller surface area, even minor deviations in the compression site could substantially affect cardiac output. Therefore, we speculated that providing a visible landmark could help rescuers maintain a more accurate and consistent compression position, ultimately serving as a simple, effective tool to enhance the quality of infant CPR.

Methods

Participants

This study recruited pediatric emergency department (ED) nurses from a tertiary academic referral hospital. Of 30 volunteers, three were excluded due to pregnancy or health conditions that precluded performing chest compressions, resulting in a final cohort of 27 nurses. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Ulsan College of Medicine (No. 2025–0820), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All participants had prior experience with pediatric CPR and had completed Pediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) training.

Landmark

Simulations were conducted using a Laerdal Little Baby™ manikin equipped with a QCPR sensor (Laerdal Medical, Stavanger, Norway), provided by Asan Medical Center Simulation Center. The manikin contains an electronic sensor precisely 1 cm below the intermammary line, enabling real-time measurement of compression accuracy via the QCPR application and SkillGuide device (Supplementary File 1).

The visible landmark was represented by a circular red sticker (12 mm in diameter). In a preliminary pilot test, this site corresponded to the point of 100% compression accuracy on the manikin. The correct landmark was defined as the center of the sticker positioned 1 cm below the intermammary line, corresponding to this validated anatomical site. The incorrect landmark was defined as the placement of the sticker 2 cm below the intermammary line.

Simulation protocol

Participants were stratified according to years of clinical experience (0–4, 5–9, and ≥10 years). Within each stratum, allocation to Group A, B, or C was determined by drawing lots from sealed, identical envelopes, ensuring balanced distribution across groups and equal group sizes (n = 9 per group); Group A (correct landmark), Group B (incorrect landmark), and Group C (no landmark). The allocation process was conducted by a researcher not involved in outcome assessment. The study followed a single-blind design: participants were unaware of their group assignment and were not informed of the meaning or purpose of the landmark.

Each participant performed three cycles of infant chest compressions using either the two-finger or the two-thumb encircling hands technique, as instructed by current PALS guidelines. A one-minute rest period was provided between cycles. Compression accuracy for each attempt was automatically recorded via the QCPR application, and the percentage of correct compressions per cycle was calculated.

After completing all cycles, participants completed a brief questionnaire (Supplementary File 2). The primary outcome was the accuracy of hand position, and the secondary outcome was the consistency of hand position across cycles.

Accuracy was defined as the percentage of compressions performed within the correct anatomical location as detected by the QCPR sensor. For each participant, the mean accuracy (%) per cycle was calculated automatically by the QCPR system. Consistency refers to the reproducibility of hand positioning across cycles. It reflects the stability of compression location within each participant over repeated cycles, rather than the proximity to the correct anatomical target.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences in the accuracy of correct hand positioning among the three groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Bar graphs and line charts were used to visualize group differences and trends in accuracy across cycles. The simulation was conducted using a method validated through post-hoc analysis. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Questionnaire responses

After completing the simulation, participants in Groups A and B completed a brief 13-item questionnaire (Table 3), which required less than 5 minutes to complete. All participants reported noticing the sticker on the manikin during chest compressions. Three participants stated that the sticker position differed from their initially intended compression site; two of these participants were in Group B. Both indicated that they ultimately performed compressions at the stickered location. More than 90% of participants reported that the presence of the sticker influenced their choice of compression site and that they continued compressing at the marked location during subsequent cycles. Discussion

In this simulation study evaluating the usefulness of a visible landmark for infant chest compressions, participants who performed compressions at the precisely marked location demonstrated significantly higher accuracy and greater consistency compared with those in other groups.

These findings are consistent with a previous study that assessed the utility of a Heartsaver sticker in adult simulations. The study was conducted among 51 emergency medical technician trainees and demonstrated that the use of the sticker enabled all participants to identify the correct compression site within six minutes, with consistently accurate performance thereafter [11].

In contrast to adults or older children, infants have a much smaller thoracic surface area, so even minor deviations in hand placement may lead to relatively large differences in cardiac output. Additionally, infant CPR—performed using the two-finger or two-thumb encircling technique—requires a precise and stable compression point to ensure optimal efficacy [12]. This necessity was clearly reflected in our results: although all participants were PALS-certified nurses, those without a landmark exhibited marked variability in hand placement accuracy across cycles.

Notably, when the landmark was deliberately positioned incorrectly (Group B), compression accuracy and consistency declined substantially compared with the correct landmark group. Despite participants in Group B reporting that they followed the sticker position, their performance suggested partial reversion to the hand placement habitually reinforced during PALS training. This discrepancy highlights the influence of prior training and muscle memory, which may override visual cues if they contradict learned practice.

Interestingly, although both accuracy and consistency were significantly higher in Group A, the mean compression rate was slightly greater in Group C (Table 2). However, all three groups maintained compression rates within the AHA-recommended range of 100–120 per minute, so these differences are unlikely to be clinically relevant.

From the perspective of cognitive ergonomics, reducing cognitive load and providing clear visual cues can facilitate task performance under stress. During cardiopulmonary resuscitation, rescuers must simultaneously process multiple pieces of information, including compression depth, rate, and recoil. A visible landmark may function as an external attentional guide, reducing the cognitive demand associated with identifying the correct compression site and thereby promoting more accurate hand positioning. In addition, principles of motor learning suggest that consistent spatial referencing enhances movement reproducibility. Repeated compressions performed relative to a stable visual cue may help reinforce motor patterns and reduce variability across cycles. In this context, a visible landmark can be interpreted not merely as a design element, but as a cognitive and motor aid that supports precision and consistency during infant chest compressions [13].

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, with only nine participants per group, which limited the statistical power to detect subtle differences. Second, because this was a manikin-based simulation, the results may not fully reflect real-world variability in infant anatomy. For example, accurate landmark identification may be more difficult in infants with structural anomalies such as pectus excavatum. Third, the exclusion of the two-finger compression technique from the updated AHA guidelines was not reflected in this study because the study protocol had been designed prior to the publication of the 2025 AHA guidelines. In addition, we considered the two-finger technique to be more suitable for evaluating accuracy, as it allows for more precise identification and targeting of the intended compression point. Nevertheless, in accordance with the updated guidelines, further studies are warranted to evaluate accuracy and consistency using the one-hand compression technique.

Despite the limited sample size, our findings suggest that a correctly positioned landmark substantially improves both compression accuracy and consistency during infant CPR. Notably, the benefit depends on correct placement: an incorrectly positioned landmark led to confusion and poorer performance, underscoring the importance of accurate initial positioning by the CPR leader when applying this approach in practice. Based on our simulation findings, a simple, visually guided sticker may serve as a supportive tool to improve the precision of infant chest compressions among trained providers, although its effectiveness in real-world clinical settings requires further investigation.

In conclusion, this simulation study demonstrated that applying a visible landmark at the anatomically correct site significantly enhanced the accuracy and consistency of infant chest compressions. Future research in real-world clinical settings is warranted to confirm these findings and explore their impact on resuscitation outcomes.
