The interrelationship between periodontitis and pancreatic cancer: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ya-Hsin Li, Sin-Tong Lan, Kuan-Ju Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeriodontitis and pancreatic cancer are major public health concerns with increasing prevalence worldwide, including in Taiwan. Although previous studies have suggested a potential association between periodontitis and pancreatic cancer, findings remain inconclusive, particularly in Taiwanese populations. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between periodontitis and pancreatic cancer.MethodsThe study conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) between 2000 and 2019. Patients diagnosed with periodontitis were compared with matched controls without periodontitis. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software. Beyond descriptive statistics, the study conducted bivariate analyses to examine differences between patients with and without pancreatic cancer, followed by Cox regression to further evaluate the the risk of pancreatic cancer, adjusting for demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic factors.ResultsA total of 428,814 patients with periodontitis and matched controls were included. Overall, no significant increase in pancreatic cancer risk was observed among periodontitis patients compared with controls (aHR = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.96–1.22). However, subgroup analyses indicated higher risks in specific populations, including males (aHR = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.01–1.34), individuals aged 45–64 years (aHR = 4.43, 95% CI: 3.68–5.33), and those aged ≥ 65 years (aHR = 9.18, 95% CI: 7.43–11.30). Additionally, low annual expenditures for periodontitis care (≤ USD 11) and higher outpatient visit frequencies (> 0.7 per year) were associated with increased pancreatic cancer risk.ConclusionThe study findings underscore the potential role of periodontitis as a risk factor for pancreatic cancer in specific subgroups. Effective management of periodontitis, particularly among high-risk populations, may help mitigate systemic inflammatory effects linked to pancreatic cancer. Further studies are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms and inform targeted prevention strategies.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer represents a major and escalating public health challenge globally and in Taiwan, characterized by high mortality, aggressive disease progression, and limited effective early detection strategies. In 2022, it was the seventh leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with incidence rates continuing to rise steadily [1]. In Taiwan, pancreatic cancer similarly ranks seventh in cancer mortality, exhibiting a growth rate higher than most other malignancies. Its poor prognosis, coupled with a tendency for late-stage diagnosis, underscores the urgent need to identify modifiable risk factors and develop targeted preventive strategies.

Concurrently, periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease of the supporting structures of the teeth, is highly prevalent worldwide and affects up to 80% of adults in Taiwan [2]. Periodontitis is characterized by persistent local inflammation, alveolar bone loss, and progressive destruction of periodontal ligaments, often leading to tooth loss if untreated. Increasing evidence suggests that systemic inflammation originating from periodontitis contributes to the pathogenesis of various chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancers [3–6]. Mechanistically, chronic low-grade inflammation may promote tumor initiation and progression by inducing DNA damage, supporting tumor microenvironment remodeling, and altering immune surveillance [7].

Despite this biologically plausible link, epidemiological studies specifically examining the association between periodontitis and pancreatic cancer have reported inconsistent findings [8]. While some studies report a positive correlation, suggesting that periodontitis may increase pancreatic cancer risk, others fail to demonstrate a significant relationship, leaving an important knowledge gap [3–6, 9, 10]. This uncertainty hampers the development of evidence-based public health policies and targeted preventive strategies.

To address this gap, the present study utilized a nationwide, population-based database from Taiwan covering nearly two decades (2000–2019) to investigate the long-term association between periodontitis and the subsequent risk of pancreatic cancer. The study aimed to clarify whether periodontitis constitutes an independent risk factor for pancreatic cancer while accounting for demographic, socioeconomic, and comorbidity-related variables, thereby providing insights into potential avenues for prevention and early intervention.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data were derived from the Health and Welfare Data Science Center (HWDSC), incorporating the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) and the national death registry from 2000 to 2019. The NHIRD provides comprehensive data on demographic characteristics (birth date, gender, income), outpatient (OPD) and inpatient (IPD) claims, and hospital-level information for nearly all Taiwanese residents. Linkage of datasets was achieved using encrypted identification codes, ensuring patient privacy while allowing longitudinal tracking. Disease classification followed ICD-9 prior to 2016 and ICD-10 between 2016 and 2019, providing standardized diagnostic coding across the study period. The HWDSC database, managed by the Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW), has been extensively used in population-based epidemiologic research due to its high coverage (> 99%) and reliability.

Study subjects

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (CS2-22206). We initially identified 624,201 patients diagnosed with periodontitis from the HWDSC database between 2001 and 2018. Patients with a history of cancer (N = 9,559) and those younger than 21 years (N = 131,157) were excluded. After these exclusions, 483,485 patients were included in the periodontitis cohort.

Controls were selected from individuals without a history of periodontitis or cancer and who were aged 21 years or older. Each patient in the periodontitis cohort was matched 1:1 with a control based on propensity scores (PS), which were estimated using multivariable logistic regression. Matching variables included age, gender, and monthly income. Outcome and risk factor

All study subjects were followed from index date until to the date of pancreas cancer occurred, death, or the end of 2019. The risk factors included subject information, CCI score, and medical information. Subject information contained age (21–44, 45–64 and 65 + years), gender, income (< 880, 881–1110, 1111–1400, and > 1400 USD), and urban (level 1–7). CCI score was counted based on the weight of disease: weight of “1” included myocardial infract, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, connective tissue disease, ulcer disease, mild liver disease, and diabetes; weight of “2” included hemiplegia, moderate or severe renal disease, diabetes with end organ damage, any tumor, leukemia, and lymphoma; weight of “3” included moderate or severe liver disease; weight of “6” included metastatic solid tumor and ADIS. Medical information contained annual periodontitis medical benefit (≤ 11, 12–27, and > 27 USD), annual periodontitis OPD frequency (≤ 0.3, 0.4–0.7, and > 0.7), hospital area (northern, medium, southern, and eastern and island), and hospital type (non-public and public hospital).

Statistical analysis

Number and percentage were presented for categorical variables. Mean and standard deviation (SD) was presented with age. Beyond descriptive statistics, the study conducted bivariate analyses to examine differences between patients with and without pancreatic cancer. In addition, the study used Cox proportional hazard regression model to estimate the hazard ratio (HR) of pancreas cancer occurred in periodontitis cohort compared to control cohort after controlling age, gender, income, CCI score, and urban. We also assessed the risk of pancreas cancer stratified by age, gender, income, CCI score, and urban. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05. All analyses were used SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between periodontitis and pancreatic cancer, building on prior evidence suggesting that periodontitis may promote carcinogenesis through systemic inflammation or oral carcinogens [11]. Our analysis specifically focused on identifying key risk factors within a periodontitis population.

Our Cox proportional hazards regression analysis confirmed that males and older individuals within the periodontitis cohort exhibited a significantly higher incidence of pancreatic cancer. The elevated risk for males aligns with global epidemiological data demonstrating higher incidence and mortality rates among men [1, 12]. Age emerged as a dominant factor: compared to the 21–44 age group, the adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for patients aged 45–64 and ≥ 65 years were 4.43 (95% CI = 3.68–5.33) and 9.18 (95% CI = 7.43–11.3), respectively. This pronounced age-dependent risk is consistent with findings from Chang et al., who reported a similar positive correlation (HR = 1.55) primarily in individuals over 65 [13], as well as another Taiwanese study in which pancreatic cancer risk was markedly higher in patients over 60 [14].

Socioeconomic status presented a nuanced picture. While income level was not a significant factor within the periodontitis group, periodontitis patients with a monthly income below USD $880 demonstrated a significantly higher cancer risk than controls without periodontitis (aHR = 1.16, 95% CI = 1.01–1.34). This suggests that low income may act as a contributing risk factor, consistent with research linking lower socioeconomic status to poorer cancer prognosis [15, 16].

Additionally, comorbidity burden, assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), was a significant predictor. Patients with a CCI score of ≥ 1 exhibited an increased pancreatic cancer risk (aHR = 1.56, 95% CI = 1.23–1.98), echoing previous findings that higher CCI scores correlate with elevated cancer risk [17]. In this study, periodontitis patients with a CCI score ≥ 1 also showed a significantly increased risk of pancreatic cancer compared to those with a CCI score of 0, underscoring the potential role of comorbidity burden in influencing pancreatic cancer risk.

This study also revealed a significant association between healthcare utilization and cancer risk. The risk increased with the frequency of yearly outpatient department (OPD) visits, with an aHR of 4.83 (95% CI = 3.57–6.55) for patients with >0.7 visits per year. This finding contrasts with a prior study by Chang et al., which reported no such association [13], suggesting that visit frequency may be an important, previously under-recognized factor.

Similarly, patients with low annual health insurance reimbursement for periodontitis treatment (≤ USD $11) had a significantly higher cancer risk (aHR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.03–1.79) compared to those receiving higher reimbursement. This may indicate that inadequate treatment leads to persistent systemic inflammation, a recognized contributor to pancreatic cancer [18], or serves as a proxy for limited health awareness [19, 20]. These findings align with evidence that untreated periodontitis is associated with higher overall cancer risk [21].

Unlike studies in other countries identifying geographical location as a risk factor [21], our analysis found no significant association with urban–rural classification or hospital location. This likely reflects Taiwan’s National Health Insurance (NHI) system, which provides comprehensive and accessible care to over 99% of the population, mitigating the effects of geographical and socioeconomic disparities on healthcare access [19]. Regardless of individual economic status or regional distribution of medical resources, the NHI ensures that most patients can access necessary medical services promptly, likely contributing to the absence of significant associations in our analysis.

Our findings highlight the clinical importance of recognizing under-treated periodontitis as a potential contributor to pancreatic cancer risk, particularly among elderly male patients with multiple comorbidities and frequent outpatient visits that may signal early, non-specific symptoms. The synergistic effect of periodontitis with low socioeconomic status emphasizes the need to address both biological and social determinants of health in cancer prevention. In Taiwan, the universal NHI system offers opportunities for early detection and management of periodontal disease; integrating routine dental care and timely periodontal treatment into preventive strategies may represent a practical and effective approach to reducing long-term pancreatic cancer risk.

Limitations

This study leveraged the Health and Welfare Data Science Center (HWDSC) database to perform a retrospective analysis, benefiting from its extensive dataset and long-term follow-up. Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, as the database primarily consists of insurance claims, it lacks detailed information on patients’ lifestyles, dietary habits, and family medical histories. Key factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, and obesity—each shown to elevate pancreatic cancer risk [13, 22–24]—were unavailable. Second, diagnoses were based solely on International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes, which may be incomplete or subject to coding errors. Third, because the study is restricted to Taiwanese data, external validity may be limited. Moreover, the observational design precludes causal inference; the findings reflect associations rather than definitive causal relationships. Finally, while propensity score matching was applied to mitigate confounding, residual unmeasured confounders cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that periodontitis is significantly associated with an increased risk of pancreatic cancer. After adjusting for age, sex, annual medical insurance benefits, number of periodontal outpatient visits, and CCI scores, patients with periodontitis exhibited a higher risk of pancreatic cancer. These findings underscore the importance of prioritizing treatment resources and educational interventions for high-risk groups within public health strategies. While this study provides valuable insights, its observational nature and reliance on insurance claims limit causal interpretation.

Future investigations should emphasize well-designed prospective cohort studies that closely monitor patients with periodontitis, capturing detailed data on treatment, lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, diet), and systemic inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein to clarify causal relationships. In parallel, mechanistic studies examining the oral microbiome in high-risk individuals, particularly focusing on pathogenic species such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, are warranted to elucidate potential pathways in pancreatic carcinogenesis.
