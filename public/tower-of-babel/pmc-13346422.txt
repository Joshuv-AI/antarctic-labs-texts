Leveraging AI and multi-source satellite imagery for multi-hazard monitoring and susceptibility mapping in urban areas
Authors: Raffaele Lafortezza, Francesco Giordano, Domenico Capolongo, Alberto Refice, Francesco P Lovergine, Mario Elia, Davide D’Alò, Nicola Amoroso, Raffaele Nutricato, Marina Zingaro, Alessandro Parisi, Giacomo Caporusso, Floriana Rizzo, Alessandra Costantino, Alessandro Ursi, Patrizia Sacco, Maria Virelli, Deodato Tapete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing frequency and severity of natural hazards, such as floods, wildfires, land degradation, and ground displacement, pose significant challenges to the protection of urban areas worldwide. While traditional monitoring approaches based on a single-source satellite sensor have proved to be reliable, they often fail to provide a holistic representation of the complexity, scale, and rapid evolution of these phenomena. The recent advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), coupled with the unprecedented availability of multi-source satellite imagery, offers new perspectives for enhancing natural hazard monitoring and susceptibility mapping. In this study, we present a novel approach that leverages state-of-the-art Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, particularly SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), to analyze multi-source satellite imagery for natural hazard monitoring and assessment in urban areas. The framework utilizes globally available, open-source satellite data (Sentinel-1/2, COSMO-SkyMed, SAOCOM) to ensure inherent scalability and transferability. XAI is chosen to move beyond black-box prediction, providing transparent attribution of susceptibility to underlying environmental and infrastructural parameters, which is essential for informed intervention. This interpretability is critical for building stakeholder trust and ensuring that automated predictions align with domain knowledge before deployment. Our approach was developed, applied, and validated in two distinct sites located in the Puglia region, southern Italy: the densely populated Bari Urban Region (BUR) and the diverse settlements and land uses within the Gargano Urban Region (GUR). We combined XAI-based models with optical imagery from Sentinel-2, SAR data from Sentinel-1, COSMO-SkyMed, and SAOCOM to extract the key features explaining the occurrence and magnitude of the following hazards: (1) sediment connectivity; (2) land displacement; (3) urban floods; and (4) urban wildfires. Our results demonstrate that the integration of multi-source satellite imagery through AI not only significantly enhances the accuracy and reliability of hazard detection (e.g., F1 scores consistently above 67.5% for three of the four hazards, and high Recall across all modules) but also enables the identification of subtle spatial patterns and crucial interrelationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52139-w.Keywords: Multi-hazard monitoring, Susceptibility mapping, XAI, Flood, Land displacement, Sediment connectivity, Urban wildfiresSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Geography, Geography, Natural hazards

Introduction

Natural hazards such as floods, wildfires, land degradation, and land displacement pose a significant threat to urban areas worldwide1–8. These hazards often cause substantial economic losses, infrastructure damage, and human casualties. The increasing frequency and severity of these events, exacerbated by climate change and rapid urbanization, require the development and implementation of advanced monitoring and assessment strategies to effectively mitigate their impacts and enhance urban resilience9.

Methods for monitoring natural hazards are increasingly shifting from single source to multi-source remote sensing data10,11. This approach overcomes limitations tied to specific technologies, such as limited spatial or temporal coverage and susceptibility to atmospheric conditions like cloud cover in optical imagery. However, these multi-source approaches are still primarily applied within a single-hazard framework. This fails to capture the intricate interplay between natural hazards, which often occur simultaneously or in a cascading sequence. A growing body of literature recognizes that natural hazards are frequently interconnected, thus exacerbating the impacts of concurrent hazards12–14. These interactions can lead to multifaceted disaster scenarios that single-hazard assessments are unable to capture, thus resulting in an underestimation of the overall susceptibility (i.e., areas prone to multiple hazards) and ineffective management strategies.

The recent advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), coupled with the unprecedented availability of multi-source satellite imagery, offers new perspectives and tools for enhancing natural hazard monitoring and susceptibility mapping15–17. AI techniques, such as machine learning and deep learning, are powerful tools for analyzing the complex natural and anthropogenic factors affecting the magnitude, frequency, and spatial distribution of hazardous events within urbanized areas18–20. Multi-source satellite imagery provides critical information on cities, land cover, and infrastructure networks, which can be used to assess exposure and vulnerability to a wide range of hazards. For example, SAR data (Sentinel-1, COSMO-SkyMed) is prioritized for measuring land deformation and water extent due to its cloud-independent nature, while optical data (Sentinel-2) is utilized for land cover, fuel load, and environmental parameters that precondition wildfire and erosion. By combining AI and multi-source satellite imagery, it becomes possible to develop more accurate and efficient methodologies for multi-hazard monitoring.

Recent studies have shown the potential of AI techniques in various remote sensing applications, from land cover classification to change detection21. For example, Rodríguez-Antuñano et al. (2024a, 2024b) have demonstrated the potential of combining optical and SAR data through deep learning for urban resilience and climate-induced vulnerability modeling62,63. However, the integration of AI with multi-source satellite images for natural hazard monitoring remains relatively unexplored, particularly in terms of combining multiple datasets to enhance prediction accuracy and reliability.

The overarching goal of this paper is to explore the potential of leveraging AI and multi-source satellite imagery to support multi-hazard monitoring and assessment within urban areas. We combined the latest advancements in AI techniques, such as eXplainable AI (XAI) with optical imagery from Sentinel-2 and SAR data from Sentinel-1, COSMO-Sky-Med and SAOCOM, to extract the key input features explaining the spatial co-occurrence and magnitude of the following hazards: (1) sediment connectivity; (2) land displacement; (3) urban floods; and (4) urban wildfires. Our study focused on two main test sites located in the Puglia region, southern Italy presenting historical records and distinct challenges related to the selected hazards. Our results suggest that the integration of multi-source satellite imagery through advanced XAI techniques not only significantly enhances the accuracy and reliability of individual hazard detection and mapping but also enables the identification of subtle spatial patterns and local interrelationships between different hazards that might remain undetected by traditional, single-sensor, or single-hazard-focused methodologies.

The insights from this study are important for several reasons. First, they provide a strong example for AI-based decision-making, offering a data-driven way to approach multi-hazard monitoring. Second, these findings are critical for shaping prevention strategies aimed at reducing multi-hazard occurrence. Finally, this work contributes to creating more resilient urban environments by improving our ability to anticipate, prepare for, and mitigate the impact of natural hazard threats22.

Materials and methods

The integration of AI with multi-source satellite images is a complex task which requires a dedicated workflow encompassing (i) data acquisition and pre-processing of satellite data; (ii) hazard-specific feature extraction; and (iii) multiple hazards analysis using AI techniques; the subsequent assessment of multiple hazards across the study areas through geo-analytics and spatial metrics (Fig. 1). To ensure the scientific rigor and interpretability of the analysis, we designed a workflow that combines AI capabilities with specific domain knowledge and scientific expertise. This expertise, relevant to each hazard type under investigation - such as sediment connectivity, land displacement, urban floods, and urban wildfires - guided the selection of appropriate satellite and input data. It also informed the development, parameterization, and interpretation of the eXplainable AI (XAI) models and final results (see Fig. 1).

Workflow describing the main steps used in this study. To ensure the scientific rigor and interpretability of the analysis, we designed a workflow that combines XAI capabilities with specific domain knowledge and scientific expertise.

For instance, monitoring land displacement required us to work closely with experts in this type of hazard. We relied on Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data, specifically from the Sentinel-1 mission, which uses C-band SAR interferometry. This technology is highly sensitive to even subtle ground deformation, allowing us to detect and measure land shifts with millimeter-level accuracy. For the scope, we used advanced Differential Interferometric SAR (DInSAR) techniques to create interferograms and coherence maps, which we then analyzed to pinpoint areas that were experiencing significant ground movement23,25–27. Additionally, high-resolution SAR data from COSMO-SkyMed offered valuable complementary information that we used to validate and fine-tune the land displacement maps originally derived from Sentinel-128–30.

For urban flooding, we utilized time series analysis techniques and employed Bayesian statistical methods applied to multi-source, and, where available, multi-polarization SAR and InSAR data to accurately separate flooded areas from non-flooded regions31.

The core of our methodology lies in the application of XAI models to enhance the explainability power of the machine learning techniques. This involved employing methods such as feature importance analysis and visualization techniques to understand which features were the most influential in the model’s predictions for each hazard. This not only increases the global and local interpretability of the results but also provides valuable insights into the underlying bio-physical features driving the occurrence and magnitude of these hazardous events32–34.

Study areas

The above-described workflow was developed, applied and validated in two distinct sites located in the Puglia region, southern Italy: the densely populated Bari Urban Region (BUR) and the diverse settlements and land uses within the Gargano Urban Region (GUR) (Fig. 2). These sites have been selected as testbeds due to their specific geographical and environmental characteristics as well as their historical susceptibility to the selected natural hazards. The potential impacts of these natural hazards on populations and infrastructures, coupled with the availability of validation datasets, make these regions ideal for developing and testing the proposed approach. The subsequent sections will delve into the specific implementation details and results obtained for each hazard in these study areas.

Selection of the target layers

The selection of target sets of data is a critical step to support the application the XAI method. For the scope, four main layers were drawn from existing maps or published studies (see Table 1). In the case of “urban floods”, for example, the target layer was provided by the local authorities in charge of monitoring and managing this type of hazard in BUR and GUR35, while for the “land displacement” the training layer included a combination of existing maps and new data derived from satellite images. For sediment connectivity36–39, Sediment flow connectivity Index (SfCI) maps were generated using the algorithm developed by Zingaro et al. 201940. The connectivity map may be regarded as a hazard proxy, as it captures dynamics that are strongly linked to flood events, wildfires, and land displacement41–43,58. For urban wildfires, the target layers were obtained from previous studies. All the target layers were computed using a 100 × 100 m (i.e., 1 ha) grid of cells. This grid size was selected to ensure our framework remains computationally efficient and highly scalable for future national-level hazard monitoring65. Each cell served as a single observation for training the XAI model. Specific information and details related to the pre-processing and subsequent analysis of the four target layers are provided in the supplementary material. All geospatial data processing, spatial analyses, and final map products were generated using QGIS (Version 3.34 ‘Prizren’; https://qgis.org/).

Extraction of the input features

Just as critical as the definition of target layers is the selection of a comprehensive and relevant set of input features, which form the foundation of the model’s learning process. An extended set of features was selected from existing dataset and satellite images to capture a comprehensive range of climatic, geomorphological, and anthropogenic factors known from previous studies to influence the occurrence of the four natural hazards.

Table 2 provides a summary of the features that have been used as input variables (i.e., predictors) in the Random Forest classification process. More information on the extraction of these features, including those derived from satellite images, are provided in the supplementary materials. Since positive linear correlations emerged between multiple features, hierarchical clustering algorithm with distance was applied to reduce the redundancy of features in our dataset with distance: , where is the Pearson correlation between feature i and feature j. Features with Pearson > 0.9 were grouped together in the model. Feature grouping was performed using the agglomerative hierarchical clustering module within the SciPy library60, ensuring a statistically sound selection of independent predictors. This ensured that the final input features set (n = 45) remained statistically independent while preserving maximum environmental information. The retained features were selected as the most physically representative from each high-correlation cluster and were used for training the RF classifier (Table 2).

Random forest classification of multiple hazards

This final set of input features was used to train four separate instances of the Random Forest (RF) classifier, each performing a binary classification of one of the four target layers.

For each study area, we used a separate set of sampling cells (100 × 100 m):

GUR = 284,937 cells; BUR = 382,269 cells. RF classifiers were configured with unrestricted tree depth and a maximum feature subset size = √(n), where n is the number of features. The number of trees was fixed to 100 as preliminary sensitivity tests indicated that further increases yielded negligible gains (< 1% F1 score) at a significant computational effort. A 5-fold cross-validation was employed to run the RF classification, considering 80% of the cells for training and the remaining 20% for validation. To reduce the effects of spatial autocorrelation between neighboring cells, we partitioned each study area into multiple clusters made by 1000 grid cells and selected the training and validation cells from different clusters. Spatial leakage was minimized by ensuring that adjacent border cells comprised less than 2% of the total dataset, thereby maintaining the integrity of the geographically independent validation folds.

Figure 3 shows an example of the spatial distribution of training (white) and test (grey) clusters for a single cross-validation. As described in the supplementary material, we have re-classified our target layers into a binary format: class “0” (no hazard presence); class “1” (hazard presence). Model training relied on validated historical occurrence records documented between 2010 and 2023, providing an empirical basis for susceptibility mapping rather than a purely probabilistic estimation.

Example of the spatial distribution of training (white) and test (grey) clusters for a single fold validation. Maps were generated by the authors using QGIS v3.34 (https://qgis.org/).

In the case of the flood hazard in BUR, there was a clear imbalance between the two classes. To overcome this bias, we used a random sub-sampling of the samples during all the training process. For a robust estimation of the RF predictions, the cross-validation procedure was repeated 100 times. Numerical experiments were performed on high-performance computing (HPC) infrastructure61, utilizing compute nodes equipped with AMD EPYC 7702P processors (64 cores, 128 threads) and 32 GB of allocated RAM.

XAI-based assessment of multiple hazards

Traditional AI models often function as “black boxes,” offering high predictive accuracy but little insight into the underlying physical drivers of multiple hazards. To address this, our study integrates Explainable AI (XAI), specifically SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), to ensure transparency and interpretability Unlike traditional black-box models, the proposed XAI framework ensures that the classification of the areas prone to multiple hazards is driven by relevant features rather than model artifacts. The SHAP framework, developed by Lundberg44,45 measures the contribution of each feature to the model decision by computing the marginal impact on the classification when a feature is removed or included from a feature subset. The SHAP value Φ quantifies the influence of each feature to a model’s prediction. SHAP provides a cell-based (local) interpretability, which allows understanding the effect of each feature (i) in a specific location x. SHAP were computed separately for each of the four instances of the RF classifier for GUR and BUR46–48. To accelerate model interpretability, SHAP value estimations were parallelized across 128 concurrent threads, enabling the distributed processing of the dataset followed by the systematic aggregation of local explanations.

In the BUR, the flood module exhibits lower precision, reflecting a tendency to generate false positives. In contrast, the land displacement, sediment connectivity, and wildfire modules achieve significantly higher precision. Importantly, the consistently high Recall values across all four hazard types demonstrate the framework’s strong capability to effectively detect most actual positive cases.

The F1 score, which balances precision and recall, reveals further insights. In the BUR, the extreme class imbalance inherent to flood susceptibility skews the F1 score downwards. However, for the other three hazard types, the F1 score consistently remains above 58%, providing a reliable quantitative benchmark for prediction accuracy compared to baseline statistical models.

Regarding the lower precision observed for floods (20.3% in GUR and 53.2% in BUR), this is a direct consequence of the extreme rarity of high-hazard zones (Class 1) within the study area - a common challenge in imbalanced hazard mapping. In the context of disaster reduction and early warning, minimizing False Negatives (i.e., ensuring no true hazard zone is missed) is the primary objective, often prioritizing safety over the minimization of False Positives. Given this operational priority, the consistently high Recall values (e.g., 69.1% in GUR and 82.7% in BUR) confirm the model’s utility. By erring on the side of caution, the framework identifies a broad range of susceptible areas for further inspection, ensuring that critical vulnerabilities are not overlooked. The remaining three hazard modules (Land Displacement, Sediment Connectivity, Wildfires) all exhibit strong F1 scores well above 58%, confirming the overall reliability of the multi-hazard assessment framework.

To quantify the added value of our integrated framework, we performed a benchmarking analysis comparing the proposed multi-hazard approach against traditional single-hazard models. We trained independent Random Forest classifiers for each hazard using only their specific, domain-standard predictors (detailed in the Supplementary Materials – Table S2), maintaining the same 5-fold cross-validation protocol.

During each iteration, optimal hyperparameters were determined via a systematic grid-search under a repeated stratified cross-validation scheme (number of trees = 100, 200,300; maximum depth = 10, 20, 80) and fixing the maximum feature subset size = √(n).

This tuning ensured that performance comparisons between multi-source and single-source models were equitable and robust. Final hyperparameter configuration was selected based on the aggregated performance across 10 repetitions. The selected model was then used to generate out-of-fold predictions for all observations using the same cross-validation strategy as the multi-hazard model.

The results (Table 3) reveal a substantial performance gap, providing strong empirical evidence for the advantage of leveraging AI and the multi-source satellite imagery. The multi-hazard integration consistently outperforms the single-hazard baselines, with the most significant improvements observed in complex, interdependent phenomena. Most notably, the F1 score for Land Displacement surged by approximately 20% in GUR (76.9% vs. 56.3%) and over 32% in BUR (58.7% vs. 26.8%). This striking difference underscores that land instability in complex urban regions cannot be accurately monitored using InSAR displacement data alone; it requires the holistic context of anthropogenic pressures (e.g., road proximity) and geomorphological pre-conditions provided by other features. Similarly, Flood detection improved significantly (approximately + 7–8% in F1 score), validating the hypothesis that integrating geomorphic features (HAND, sediment connectivity) with SAR-based flood probability enhances detection in complex terrain.

Statistical validation using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirms these improvements are significant (p < 0.01) for Land Displacement, Floods, and Sediment Connectivity. The sole exception is the Wildfire hazard, where performance remained statistically equivalent. These results corroborate the potential role of wildfires as critical ‘initiators’ within complex hazard cascades, where early-stage landscape alterations create environmental conditions conducive to secondary threats such as flooding and erosion13,39., driven primarily by direct factors like fuel load and climate, which are well-captured by the single hazard set, rather than being conditioned by other features. In summary, these results demonstrate that the multi-hazard framework consistently outperforms the single hazard baseline, offering superior reliability and predictive power across complex urban environments.

Model interpretation

Global and local interpretation

To gain an understanding on how our input features affects multiple hazards in GUR and BUR, we used a combination of SHAP-value plots and SHAP-value maps. A significant advantage of employing SHAP values over traditional variable importance metrics lies in their bidirectional nature; SHAP values are not restricted to positive magnitudes but can range from negative to positive. The magnitude of a SHAP value directly indicates the influence of an individual feature on the target hazard, while the sign of the SHAP value reveals the direction of this influence: a positive sign signifies that the given feature contributes to increasing the probability estimate of the hazard, while a negative sign indicates a contribution to decreasing it. To this end, we used SHAP-value (bee swarm) plots to illustrate the contribution of the ten most influential features in explaining the four hazards in GUR (Fig. 4a) and BUR (Fig. 4b).

SHAP-value (bee swarm) plots for the four hazards in (a) GUR and (b) BUR.

Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND), Digital Elevation Model (DEM) slope, soil thickness, and the rainfall index were the main features explaining floods’ occurrence and distribution across GUR and BUR. Satellite-based layers such as flood “probability” also contributed to the model’s accuracy in both regions, underscoring the value of direct satellite-based observations. For “land displacement” the bee swarm plots show a different pattern in the two study areas: in GUR, slope and absolute elevation were the most significant features affecting landslide hazards: the presence of steep terrains are critical factors for mass movements in such environments; in BUR, degree of urbanization, proximity to the road networks, and soil stability were the most influential suggesting a general wider impact of human activities on landslides in this area. Results for sediment connectivity indicate a slightly different pattern in the two areas. In BUR, soil stability index emerged as the most influential feature reflecting its role in controlling sediment mobilization in this context. However, in GUR the same index ranked only eighth in importance, suggesting that other features such as rainfall, soil thickness, slope, and elevation may play a stronger role in affecting hydrological and geomorphological processes. For “urban wildfires” the bee swarm plots suggest that fuel availability, land cover types (e.g., grassland and forest), and the fire climate index were the most significant features affecting wildfire hazards in both areas. These results highlight the critical role of fuel load and local climatic conditions in determining wildfire occurrence and spread in urban regions.

We then extended our analysis from a global to a local interpretation of the results to gain a local knowledge of the features explaining each single hazard. Local interpretation of feature importance provides a detailed feature contribution for each individual mapping unit (i.e., grid cell). For this purpose, we generated SHAP-value maps for the most influential features of each instance of the RF classifier, as identified from the bee swarm plots (see Fig. 5a, b).

SHAP-value maps for the four most influential features across the four hazard classifiers - Floods, Land displacements, Sediment connectivity, and Wildfires. (a) GUR; (b) BUR.

The feature “HAND” consistently demonstrated a strong positive contribution to flood predictions, particularly along established drainage networks, unequivocally confirming its fundamental role in the local hydrological system in both GUR and BUR (see Fig. 5a; flood prediction maps #1 and 2). Similarly, fuel load exhibited a consistent and robust positive influence on wildfire predictions across both regions, highlighting its pivotal role in the model’s hazard classification logic (Fig. 5a, b; wildfire map #1). For land displacement and sediment connectivity, the RF classifiers identified distinct dominant features in GUR compared to BUR. This difference is a direct consequence of the unique geomorphological processes and environmental characteristics that define each region’s susceptibility to these specific hazards (Fig. 5a, b, land displacement and sediment connectivity maps #1 and #2).

Several observations emerge from the spatial patterns displayed in these SHAP-value maps. As already explained for the flood hazard, HAND and elevation consistently demonstrate the strongest positive contributions, particularly in areas situated higher above stream networks. This highlights how both overall elevation and the local relief shape profoundly influence the potential of floods to occur by controlling water accumulation and flow pathways. Notably, the flood probability features derived from Sentinel-1 (S1) data exert a significant positive influence, particularly within river valleys in both regions. This outcome highlights the substantial value of integrating remote sensing observations, as they provide a unique capability to detect the physical and hydrological conditions of landscapes, thereby improving the accuracy of flood susceptibility assessment in areas susceptible to inundation.

Interestingly, soil thickness also emerges as an influential factor in the BUR area of study, suggesting that shallow soils may reduce infiltration, promote surface runoff, and facilitate more direct flood pathways (Fig. 5b; floods and sediment connectivity maps #3).

For land displacements, SHAP-value maps reveal distinct drivers across the two regions. In GUR, slope, elevation, and notably the Fire Climate Index correlate positively with instability (Fig. 5a; see land displacement maps #1, #2, #3). This suggests that steeper relief and areas recently impacted by wildfires amplify soil displacement, likely due to reduced vegetation cover and altered soil properties post-fire. This result is consistent with our expectations, our previous studies and with findings from other studies49–51.

Conversely, an unanticipated variable, Distance to Roads, emerges as a significant predictor in BUR. Here, land displacements appear more likely in closer proximity to road networks, which strongly indicates an anthropogenic trigger. This could be due to construction activities and altered drainage patterns (Fig. 5b; land displacement map #1).

For sediment connectivity and accumulation, the rainfall index consistently drives enhanced connectivity across both study sites, as indicated by its positive SHAP values. Interestingly, steeper slopes appear to somewhat reduce connectivity, potentially by promoting the fragmentation or interruption of sediment supply pathways (Fig. 5a; sediment connectivity map #1; Fig. 5b; sediment connectivity map #2).

The Fire Climate Index emerges as a positive contributor in GUR, providing clear evidence of increased erosion and sediment transport in post-fire landscapes due to altered ground conditions. In the BUR, a high “Soil Stability Index” significantly reduces sediment connectivity, acting as a resistance factor against soil mobilization (Fig. 5b; sediment connectivity map #4).

For wildfires, fuel availability and specific land cover types (cropland, grassland, forest) are the dominant drivers across both study areas; a greater presence of fuel material consistently increases the susceptibility to wildfire throughout the regions. In GUR, slope amplifies fire spread potential, as reflected by its increased SHAP values, indicating that heat and fire propagate faster uphill in rugged terrain. Surprisingly, high soil elevation shows a modest positive effect, ranking 4th in GUR and 5th in BUR. This hints at potential elevation-linked factors such as fuel continuity or increased dryness contributing to wildfire susceptibility in both geographical contexts.

Multi-hazard monitoring and susceptibility assessment

Leveraging the localized insights from the XAI analysis, we generated multi-hazard susceptibility maps for both study areas. Each 100 × 100 m cell was assigned a code representing the spatial co-occurrence and potential interactions of hazards at that location (Fig. 6).

Gargano Urban Region (GUR): A critical finding is the pronounced concentration of three-hazard overlaps along the steep, dissected slopes of the central and eastern promontory. These high- susceptibility zones (Fig. 6 - dark blue areas) face simultaneous threats from landslides, intense sediment transport, and wildfires, underscoring their extreme vulnerability and the urgent need for integrated mitigation strategies. While the coastal plain and urban centers generally exhibit lower overall susceptibility, isolated hotspots emerge where anthropogenic pressures - such as dense road networks and settlements - interact with specific site conditions. This reinforces the necessity for cross-hazard early warning systems and land-use planning that explicitly accounts for these interdependencies. Bari Urban Region (BUR): The multi-hazard landscape here contrasts sharply with the GUR. Although the overall hazard intensity is lower than in the mountainous GUR, these findings highlight a crucial link between anthropogenic activities and multi-hazard vulnerabilities in this flatter, highly urbanized region.

Discussion

The application of SHAP-based explainable modeling has provided a crucial opportunity for detailed exploration of the spatial drivers behind multiple hazards and their intricate interactions within two distinct urbanized landscapes in southern Italy. This advanced interpretability approach not only reveals where hazards are likely to occur but, critically, elucidates the complex mechanisms and feedback among them, offering a spatial understanding of the multi-hazard dynamics that is essential for developing truly integrated management strategies.

XAI: decoding hazard mechanisms and feature interactions

One of the key methodological strengths demonstrated by our framework is the ability of SHAP values to move beyond simple feature ranking to reveal specific environmental and anthropogenic mechanisms, including complex feature interactions, driving multiple hazards.

The observed relationships consistently link different hazards, for example, one of the most striking relationships uncovered by our model is the link between wildfires and subsequent soil instability. This interaction - rooted in post-fire reductions in vegetation cover - illustrates how one hazard can directly increase susceptibility to another, particularly in steep, erosion-prone areas like the Gargano Urban Region (GUR). This confirms that post-fire soil degradation may act as a dynamic trigger, undermining soils that would otherwise be considered stable. Traditional linear regression or methods based on single hazard would fail to capture this conditional dependency, leading to an underestimation of susceptibility in steep, post-fire landscapes.

The Fire Climate Index (FCI) consistently emerged as a top feature in the SHAP analysis for both land displacements and sediment connectivity models, particularly pronounced in the GUR. This strong positive correlation supports our findings that wildfires can significantly increase the likelihood of landslides and debris flows by reducing protective vegetation cover, altering soil structure (e.g., through the formation of water-repellent layers), and enhancing surface runoff generation52,53. The XAI analysis effectively quantified this interaction, showing that the influence of high slope (static factor) on land displacement is significantly amplified (synergistic effect) in cells also characterized by a high FCI (dynamic factor).

Similarly, the close relationship between flood and sediment connectivity hazards is underscored by common hazard hotspots concentrated in low-relief, rainfall-prone areas. This spatial overlap directly aligns with hydrological evidence indicating that flash floods act as primary drivers of sediment transport in Mediterranean catchments. Valley-floor areas that are connected to upstream sediment sources can receive substantial sediment inputs, leading to geomorphic variations in the channel and altering its discharge capacity24. These processes are particularly pronounced in the karst environments of Puglia, where high-intensity flooding events can trigger significant sediment mobilization across shallow basins54. Our findings reinforce the critical need to address floods and sediment connectivity together as a single, combined geomorphic hazard.

The role of multi-source data and anthropogenic drivers

The multi-source satellite data played a crucial role in delivering the necessary feature diversity. SAR data (Sentinel-1, COSMO-SkyMed) were essential for capturing subtle, cloud-independent features like flood probability and land displacement acceleration, providing direct physical evidence of hazard mechanisms. Simultaneously, optical data (Sentinel-2) enabled accurate mapping of vegetation cover and urban density, which are critical precondition factors for wildfires and exposure assessment.

Beyond climatic and topographic factors, our SHAP analysis also revealed significant human influences on multiple hazards. Proximity to transportation infrastructure may also serve as a proxy for human disturbances. Specifically, the “Distance to Roads” feature was a significant factor in predicting land displacement in the BUR. This demonstrates a feature interaction where the positive contribution of road proximity (anthropogenic stress) outweighs the negative stabilizing effect of the Soil Stability Index in specific urban fringe zones. This suggests a clear link between landslides and human activity in this region, which may be due to factors like construction, altered drainage patterns, or improper excavation, and reinforces the importance of integrating anthropogenic features into multi-hazard susceptibility models55,56.

The influence of soil thickness on floods, as revealed by positive SHAP values in the BUR, offers an unexpected yet insightful observation. While deeper soils are generally understood to enhance infiltration, our model suggests that, under specific land use patterns and lithological settings (e.g., urbanized areas overlying specific soil types), deeper soils may also retain water to saturation or direct subsurface flows toward vulnerable areas, thus paradoxically increasing flood occurrence in certain contexts. This phenomenon may be particularly relevant in this area of karst terrains where characteristic red soils overlie fractured limestone, influencing the complex interaction between surface and subsurface hydrology and potentially leading to localized flooding57. This unexpected insight demonstrates the power of XAI in uncovering non-obvious relationships within complex environmental systems that traditional, linear statistical models might overlook.

While the study primarily addresses urban and semi-urban environments, our approach effectively bridges the urban-rural continuum. By treating population density as a continuous variable, the model accounts for varying degrees of anthropogenic pressure, showing how feature importance shifts from infrastructure-driven variables in dense urban cores to geomorphological and land use factors in the rural fringe.

Actionable guidance for urban and regional planning

The successful application of this multi-hazard methodology is subject on its direct utility for decision-makers. The quantitative multi-hazard maps (Fig. 6) provide immediate, spatially explicit guidance, delineating priority zones for intervention by stakeholders such as the Puglia regional authorities and municipal urban planners.

Actionable policy guidance here mandates integrated fuel reduction strategies combined with soil stabilization measures (e.g., biotechnical engineering) to interrupt the wildfire-erosion-landslide interaction. Moreover, multi-hazard maps can serve as a zoning tool for Civil Protection agencies to prioritize, for example, fuel-break construction and fuel load reduction to prevent wildfire spread and potential mobilization of sediment into downstream urban centers.

In addition, the consistent mapping of co-occurring flood and sediment connectivity allows water management authorities to shift from single-issue flood defense (dams/levees) to integrated catchment management, focusing on upstream erosion control to reduce sediment input and preserve the capacity of hydrological pathways. To ensure that our approach can be effectively integrated into institutional workflows and operational decision-making processes (e.g., Civil Protection agencies, local and regional authorities), maps should be delivered through cloud-based geoinformation platforms capable of providing scalable access, continuous updates, and advanced spatial analytics59. This technical infrastructure facilitates interoperability with existing decision-support systems and establishes the foundation for next-generation geospatial services aimed at enhancing our spatial understanding of multiple hazards. Finally, the ability to directly link hazard outcomes to their underlying drivers provides reliable and streamlined tools that can directly inform better decisions, guide prevention efforts, and help build safer, more sustainable cities.

Limitations and future directions for global transferability and scalability

While the proposed framework marks a significant advancement in multi-hazard monitoring, its current application to two regional test sites warrants discussion regarding global transferability and scalability64.

Scalability: The selection of GUR and BUR as study areas provided a diverse experimental setup, encompassing contrasting geomorphological and anthropogenic settings, from complex hilly terrains to dense coastal urban cores. This evaluation confirms that the framework is not site-specific but robust across different hazard regimes. Furthermore, because our methodology relies exclusively on standardized, high-revisit satellite constellations (Sentinel-1/2) and globally available geospatial layers, it is designed for high scalability. The core strength lies not merely in the regional results, but in the framework’s ability to automatically ingest diverse, global data streams and use XAI to identify the dominant local hazard mechanisms, enabling its robust application across different climatic and geological contexts globally. The framework’s reliance exclusively on global, open-access satellite data (Sentinel-1/2, DEM, open-source climate indices) ensures that the core methodology can be efficiently replicated across any large region worldwide without requiring proprietary or locally restricted data sets. This addresses major challenges related to cost and data access in developing global monitoring systems.

Transferability: The use of XAI (SHAP) is the key enabling factor for transferability. Because the model automatically derives local feature weights - assigning high importance to slope in mountainous GUR and to anthropogenic features (roads) in flat BUR - the framework is designed to identify the dominant local hazard mechanisms, enabling its robust application across different climatic and geological contexts globally. This inherent transferability.

minimizes the need for extensive adaptation when applying the approach to different hazard regimes or geographical contexts. Scaling this framework to a national level is therefore computationally feasible and would offer a standardized, multi-hazard decision-support tool for large-scale urban planning and multi-hazard mitigation.

Limitations and Future Work: The accuracy of hazard classification is heavily dependent on the quality and precision of the reference (ground truth) layers. As noted in Sect. "Performance of the RF classifiers", the low Precision for the Flood module is a direct consequence of the extreme rarity of high-hazard zones in the target map; however, the consistently high Recall confirms the model’s ability to minimize critical failures (False Negatives), making it valuable for prevention-focused planning. Second, while XAI provides crucial interpretability, it does not fully replace the need for causal, process-based modeling, especially for understanding and predicting dynamic hazard interactions that evolve and involve complex physical phenomena. Expanding the number of hazards considered and broadening the remote sensing datasets analyzed, for example, by incorporating very high-resolution commercial imagery, represents a clear direction for enhancing the comprehensiveness of our assessment. However, the feasibility of these advancements must be carefully considered.

Conclusion

In this study, we explored the potential of combining cutting-edge AI with multi-source satellite imagery to support multi-hazard monitoring and susceptibility assessment within urban areas. Our approach leveraged a diverse and powerful remote sensing dataset, including optical imagery from Sentinel-2 and Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data from Sentinel-1, COSMO-Sky-Med, and SAOCOM. By employing Explainable AI (XAI) models, we were not only able to predict the spatial occurrence and magnitude of four primary hazards (with F1 scores consistently above 58% for three of the four modules, and Recall maintained above 69% for the most imbalanced flood module) but also to effectively interpret the key features driving them. Our XAI-driven framework revealed distinct, and at times surprising, hazard drivers across the study areas.

For instance, in the Gargano Urban Region (GUR), our models confirmed that steep slopes and post-wildfire conditions are primary contributors to land displacement and sediment connectivity. XAI specifically quantified the interrelationship where a high Fire Climate Index amplifies erosion and mass wasting, directly increasing the land displacement hazard. For the Bari Urban Region (BUR), our models revealed an unexpected link between land displacement and proximity to the road network. This finding points to the critical role human factors play in this flatter, more urbanized landscape. The multi-hazard susceptibility maps we generated from this approach represent a tool for visualizing the spatial co-occurrence and potential interactions between hazards. This reinforces the idea that we need integrated, not isolated, strategies for managing multiple hazads. Ultimately, this work offers a solid foundation for creating more robust and flexible urban monitoring systems. The quantitative multi-hazard maps (Fig. 6) generated for BUR and GUR are immediately actionable: they delineate priority zones for regulatory enforcement (e.g., limiting construction near road networks in BUR) and targeted environmental management (e.g., concentrated fuel reduction and erosion control in the GUR uplands). Ultimately, by delivering transparent, physically explainable assessments, this framework bridges the critical gap between advanced earth observation and operational civil protection, providing tools that can directly inform better decisions, guide prevention efforts, and help build safer, more sustainable cities.
