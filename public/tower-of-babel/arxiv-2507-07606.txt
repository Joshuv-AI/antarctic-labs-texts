Ramsey-like theorems for separable permutations
Quentin Le Houérou, Ludovic Patey

Abstract
We conduct a computability-theoretic study of Ramsey-like theorems of the form “Every coloring of the edges of an infinite clique admits an infinite sub-clique avoiding some pattern”, with a particular focus on transitive patterns. As it turns out, the patterns corresponding to separable permutations play an important role in the computational features of the statement. We prove that the avoidance of any separable permutation is equivalent to the existence of an infinite homogeneous set in standard models, while this property fails for any other pattern. For this, we develop a novel argument for relativized diagonal non-computation.

Introduction

Ramsey’s theorem for pairs and
k

\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k}
) is a foundational theorem from combinatorics, stating in its graph-theoretic form that every edge-coloring of an infinite clique using
k

[
39
,
15

In this article, we pursue the computability-theoretic study of weakenings of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs by considering infinite sub-cliques avoiding some finite coloring patterns instead of monochromatic sub-cliques. For instance, the weakening of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
to cliques avoiding the following two patterns yields the Erdős-Moser theorem (
\mathsf{EM}
), a statement from graph theory on infinite tournaments.

(1)

We also consider dual weakenings of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs by restricting the colorings to those avoiding some pattern. For instance,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
for colorings avoiding (
1
) yields the Ascending Descending Sequence theorem (
\mathsf{ADS}
), stating that every infinite linear order admits an infinite ascending or descending sequence. The statements
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
,
\mathsf{EM}
and

have been extensively studied in reverse mathematics
[

,

We give a particular focus on transitive forbidden patterns, that is, patterns which do not include (

introduced by Bose, Buss and Lubiw
[

1.1
Reverse mathematics

Reverse mathematics is a meta-mathematical program founded by Harvey Friedman, whose goal is to find optimal axioms to prove ordinary theorems. It uses the framework of subsystems of second-order arithmetic, with a base theory,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, capturing
computable mathematics
. More precisely, the
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension scheme
is the following scheme, for every
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula
\varphi(x)
and every
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-formula
\psi(x)
:

\forall x(\varphi(x)\leftrightarrow\psi(x))\to\exists A\forall x(x\in A\leftrightarrow\varphi(x))

The
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-induction scheme
is the following scheme, for every
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-formula
\varphi(x)
:

\varphi(0)\wedge\forall x(\varphi(x)\to\varphi(x+1))\to\forall y\varphi(y)

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
consists of Robinson arithmetic (in other words, Peano arithmetic without induction), together with the
\Delta^{0}_{1}
-comprehension scheme and the
\Sigma^{0}_{1}
-induction scheme. See Simpson
[
39
]
for an extensive study of early reverse mathematics, and Dzhafarov and Mummert
[
12
]
for a modern presentation with a particular focus on combinatorics.

A structure in the language of second-order arithmetic is of the form

(M,S,0,1,+,\cdot,<)

where
M
denotes the set of integers of the model,
S\subseteq\mathcal{P}(M)
is a collection of sets of integers representing the second-order part,
0,1\in M
are distinguished constants, and
+
,
\cdot
and
<
represent the addition, multiplication and order, respectively. We are particularly interested in
\omega
-structures
, that is, structures where
M=\omega
are the standard integers, and
0,1,+,\cdot,<
have the usual interpretation. An
\omega
-structure is therefore fully specified by its second-order part
S
.

\omega
-models of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
admit a nice characterization in terms of Turing ideals. A
Turing ideal

\mathcal{I}
is a non-empty class of sets which is closed downward under the Turing reduction (if
X\in\mathcal{I}
and
Y\leq_{T}X
, then
Y\in\mathcal{I}
), and closed under the effective join (if
X,Y\in\mathcal{I}
, then
X\oplus Y=\{2n:n\in X\}\cup\{2n+1:n\in Y\}\in\mathcal{I}
). An
\omega
-structure is a model of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
if and only if its second-order part is a Turing ideal. We shall exclusively consider
\omega
-structures which are models of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, and simply call them
\omega
-models
.

1.2
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
-patterns

Given a finite or infinite set

(Ramsey’s theorem for pairs)

\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{k}

An

-pattern

Fix a function

f
-realizes

. We say that

f
-avoids

if no subset of

-realizes

. When

f

avoids

. We are primarily interested in the following two families of statement:

Statement 1.2
.

Given a pattern
p

\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
is the statement “For every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
, there exists an infinite set
f
-avoiding

.”

Statement 1.3
.

Given a pattern

,
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
is the statement “For every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
avoiding
p

Note that if the pattern

\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
is combinatorially false, as witnessed by the function

By Ramsey’s theorem for pairs,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
is true for any other pattern

.
The statements
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
and
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
play a dual role with respect to

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}\leftrightarrow\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)+\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for any non-constant pattern

. At first sight, the following Erdős-Moser theorem cannot be cast as one of the previous statements, as it requires to avoid multiple patterns simultaneously.

Statement 1.4

(Erdős-Moser theorem)
.

\mathsf{EM}
is the statement “For every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
, there exists an infinite set
f
-avoiding both non-transitivity patterns (see (
1
))”.

Every pattern
p:[\ell]^{2}\to 2
admits a
dual pattern

\overline{p}:[\ell]^{2}\to 2
defined by
\bar{p}(x,y)=1-p(x,y)
. In particular, the non-transitivity patterns (
1
) are dual. Over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
and
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(\bar{p})
are equivalent, as given a coloring

\bar{f}:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
and apply
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(\bar{p})
. This enables to define the Erdős-Moser theorem as a Ramsey-like statement: Over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}

\mathsf{EM}
is equivalent to the statement
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
, where

is any of (
1
).

1.3
Permutations

A
permutation
on
\ell
is a bijection
\pi:\ell\to\ell

\mathcal{L}_{\pi}=(\ell,<_{\pi})
defined for every

\pi(x)<_{\mathbb{N}}\pi(y)

There is therefore a one-to-one correspondence between permutations on

1

Indeed, given a permutation
\pi
on
\ell

p_{\pi}:[\ell]^{2}\to 2

coding the graph of
<_{\pi}
, that is, for
x<_{\mathbb{N}}y<_{\mathbb{N}}\ell
,
p_{\pi}(x,y)=0
if
x<_{\pi}y
and
p_{\pi}(x,y)=1
otherwise. Thanks to this correspondence, we shall consider permutations as particular
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
-patterns, and will use either representation when most convenient. Permutations enjoy a simple notation as their range sequence. For instance, the sequence
2031
denotes the permutation
\pi
on
4
defined by
\pi(0)=2
,
\pi(1)=0
,
\pi(2)=3
and
\pi(3)=1
. Under the pattern representation, this corresponds to

[colsep=1.5cm,rowsep=1.5cm,mnode=circle]a&bcd\everypsbox{}_{1}{}_{0}{}_{1}{}_{0}{}_{0}{}_{1}

(2)

Permutations enjoy two natural associative operators, namely, the skew sum
\ominus
and the direct sum
\oplus
, combining shorter permutations into longer ones. They are defined as follows: for
\pi
and
\sigma
two permutations of size
m
and

(\pi\ominus\sigma)(x)=\begin{cases}\pi(x)+n&\text{for }x<m,\\
\sigma(x-m)&\text{for }m\leq x<m+n,\end{cases}

(\pi\oplus\sigma)(x)=\begin{cases}\pi(x)&\text{for }x<m,\\
\sigma(x-m)+m&\text{for }m\leq x<m+n.\end{cases}

These sums enable to define the class of separable permutations, which will play a central role in this article.

Definition 1.5
.

A permutation is
separable
if it can be obtained from the trivial permutation 0 by direct sums and skew sums.

Bose, Buss and Lubiw
[
4
]
studied separable permutations, and gave a very nice characterization in terms of pattern avoidance, that we shall study in
Section

2
.
As it turns out, many lower bounds on the strength of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for separable permutations are uniform in
p
, motivating the following definition.

Statement 1.6
.

\mathsf{Sep}\mbox{-}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
is the statement “For every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
, there exists an infinite set
f
-avoiding some separable pattern.”

In particular, for every separable permutation
p
,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)\to\mathsf{Sep}\mbox{-}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
. Moreover,

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash(\mathsf{Sep}\mbox{-}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}\wedge\forall p\mbox{ separable permutation }\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p))\to\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
.
Note that in non-standard models, separable permutations might be of non-standard size, so the quantification is internal.

We prove that
\mathsf{Sep}\mbox{-}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
implies
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
over
\omega
-models, and combine this result with a theorem from Mimouni and Patey
[
31
]
to deduce the following characterization.

Main Theorem 1.7
.

For every pattern
p
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
implies
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
over
\omega
-models if and only if
p
is a separable permutation.

2
Irreducible and convergent patterns

Bose, Buss and Lubiw
[
4
]
introduced the notion of separable permutation as a natural family for which multiple problems (sub-pattern decision, longest common sub-pattern) admit a polynomial time procedure.
The goal of this section is to state multiple (existing and new) combinatorial characterizations of the class of separable permutations. We then derive some immediate computability-theoretic consequences which will be useful for the remainder of this article. Separable permutations admit the following historical characterization:

Theorem 2.1

(Bose, Buss and Lubiw
[
4
]
)
.

A permutation is separable if and only if it does not contain the permutations
1302
and
2031
as sub-patterns.

The main interest of this characterization in this article is the existence of a finite number of patterns (the two non-transitivity patterns and the permutations

and
2031
) such that every coloring avoiding all of them also avoids all non-separable patterns.
Among the consequences, the following corollary states that over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}

is sufficient to satisfy
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for any other

-pattern

and

to a statement of the form

First, note that

Let

be a coloring. By

Proposition 2.7
.

A permutation is separable if and only if it does not contain any sub-pattern which is simultaneously diverging and irreducible.

Proof.

It is clear that the permutations

are diverging and irreducible.
Thus, every permutation without a diverging irreducible sub-pattern is separable.

For the other inclusion, it is sufficient to show that any diverging irreducible permutation pattern admits

and assume by symmetry that

(the case

yields the dual sub-pattern). Since this permutation is diverging, there exists

and consider the biggest such

such that

is the smallest possible.

As the permutation is irreducible, it cannot be written as the join of
\{x_{0},\dots,\allowbreak x_{i},x_{i+1}\}
and
\{x_{i+1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, meaning that there exists some element

for

that is bigger than one element of

and smaller than one other, thus
x_{j}<x_{k}<x_{n}
(by definition of

,

is the biggest element of that set). It is not possible to have

, as

, thus

and the permutation type of

is

∎

A

3
Separable permutations and minimal covers

The statements
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
and
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
were studied both classically and from a reverse mathematical viewpoint for small patterns
p
. For instance,
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(120)
corresponds the restriction of the Chain-Antichain principle to trees
[
5
]
. However, for large patterns, the situation is less clear, as the corresponding statements were not studied previously, and the induced combinatorial objects are therefore new and seem out of reach of intuition.

To guide their computability-theoretic study, it is therefore natural to consider the known properties common to all small patterns, and try to generalize them to all sizes. Let us consider separable permutations of small size. By
Theorem

2.1
, every permutation of size at most 3 is separable. The only permutations of size 2 are
01
and
10
, and correspond to constant patterns. Recall that the statement
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for a constant pattern is combinatorially false, so the statements
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(01)
and
\mathsf{RT}(10)
are of no interest. The Ramsey-like statements for permutations of size 3 were studied by multiple authors, and summarized in Cervelle, Gaudelier and Patey
[
5
, Section 1.2]
.
There are 6 permutations of size 3, which can be grouped by duals: the permutations
012=01\uplus 01
and
210=10\uplus 10
which correspond to constant patterns, the permutations
021=01\uplus 10
and
201=10\uplus 01
which are reducible and diverging, and the permutations
120
and
102
, which are irreducible and converging.

By Mimouni and Patey
[
31
]
, the statement
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p\uplus q)
is equivalent to the existence, for every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
, of an infinite set which
f
-avoids
p
or
q
. Therefore,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
is computably reducible to
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for every reducible permutation of size 3, and the main cases of interest are for the irreducible permutations
120
and
102

Since
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(120\uplus 102)
is equivalent to the statement “for every coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
, there is an infinite set

-avoiding either

or

\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(120\uplus 102)\wedge\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(120)\wedge\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(102)\to\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
.
Cervelle, Gaudelier and Patey
[
5
]
proved that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(120\uplus 102)\to\mathsf{ADS}
, and that
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{ADS}\to(\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(120)\wedge\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(102))
. Thus
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(120\uplus 102)\to\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
. To summarize, all the statements
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for separable permutations

of size at most 3 are known to imply
\mathsf{ADS}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
with only one application.

We now prove that
\mathsf{Sep}\mbox{-}\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}

\mathsf{ADS}
over
\omega

The known proofs of the Erdős-Moser theorem are by many means very similar to the ones of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs and two colors, apart from the disjunctive nature of

Basis for separable permutations

The separable permutations form a relatively large family. In this section, we define canonical sub-families for separable permutations, the
k
-fractals for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, which enjoy some universality properties: for every
k\geq 2
, every separable permutation is a sub-pattern of a
k
-fractal. These families will play an essential role in
Section

5
and
Section

6
to prove our main theorem.

Definition 4.1
.

A family
\mathcal{P}
of patterns is a
basis
for another family
\mathcal{Q}
of patterns
if every pattern
q\in\mathcal{Q}
is a sub-pattern of some pattern
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a basis for
\mathcal{Q}
. Then the statement “For every
p\in\mathcal{P}
, every coloring avoiding
p
satisfies a property” implies “For every
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, every coloring avoiding
q
satisfies the same property”.
Indeed, if a coloring does not satisfy the property, then every pattern in
\mathcal{P}
appears in it, and as
\mathcal{P}
is a basis for
\mathcal{Q}
, every pattern in
\mathcal{Q}
also appears.

Definition 4.2
.

The family
\mathcal{F}_{k}
of
k
-fractals
is defined inductively as follows:

•

The
k
-fractal of dimension 0 is the trivial permutation
p:[1]^{2}\to 2
.

•

Let

be the
k
-fractal of dimension
n
. The

-fractal of dimension

is the permutation

defined as the direct sum
p_{n}\oplus\dots\oplus p_{n}
of

p_{n}
if
n
is even, and as the skew sum
p_{n}\ominus\dots\ominus p_{n}
of
k

p_{n}
if
n
is odd.

Note that since the skew sum and the direct sum are associative, the previous definition is well-defined.

Remark 4.3
.

For a fixed coloring

, by a small abuse of notation, we shall simply call

-fractal of dimension

-realizing the

-fractal of dimension

Let

6
Separable permutations and
2\mbox{-}\mathsf{DNC}

We now refine the probabilistic argument of
Section

5
to obtain better bounds in terms of relativized diagonal non-computation. Intuitively, it would be sufficient to have a partial choice function instead of a total one, to find good elements in the blocks of sets and obtain an infinite homogeneous set. One therefore does not need to have access to all the bits of the 2-random sequence. It is therefore natural to consider the computational power corresponding to finding infinitely many bits of a random sequence, which is exactly diagonal non-computation.

.

A function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is
diagonally non-
Z
-computable
or
Z
-DNC if for every
e
,
f(e)\neq\Phi^{Z}_{e}(e)
. A Turing degree is
Z
-DNC
if it bounds a
Z
-DNC function.

The diagonally non-computable degrees have many characterizations, using various paradigms, such as fixpoint-free functions (
[
17
]
), infinite subsets of random sequences (
[
22
,
13
]
, see
[
11
, Theorem 8.10.2]
) sequences of high Kolmogorov complexity (
[
23
]
). The following characterization, in terms of uniform avoidance of finite
\Sigma^{0}_{1}(Z)
sets with bounded size, is arguably the most useful:

Definition 6.2
.

A function
g:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
is
Z
-escaping
if for every
e,k\in\mathbb{N}
, if
\operatorname{card}W_{e}^{Z}\leq k
,
then
g(e,k)\not\in W_{e}^{Z}
.

The following lemma can be considered as folklore. We reprove it for the sake of completion.

Lemma 6.3

(Folklore (see
[
3
, Lemma 3.3]
))
.

Fix a set
Z
and a Turing degree
\mathbf{d}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbf{d}
bounds a
Z
-DNC function;

(2)

\mathbf{d}
bounds a
Z
-escaping function.

Proof.

(1)\to(2)
. Given a
Z
-DNC function
f
and
e,k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
g(e,k)
as follows:
For every
i\leq k
, let
\Phi_{a_{i}}^{Z}
be the function which, on every input, searches for the
i
th element
x_{i}
of
W_{e}^{Z}
in order of apparition, if it exists, and interprets
x_{i}
as a
(k+1)
-tuple
\langle x^{0}_{i},\dots,x^{k}_{i}\rangle
and outputs
x^{i}_{i}
. Let
g(e,k)=\langle f(a_{0}),\dots,f(a_{k})\rangle
.
Suppose for the contradiction that
\operatorname{card}W_{e}^{Z}\leq k
but
g(e,k)=x_{i}\in W_{e}^{Z}
for some
i\leq k
. Then
\Phi_{a_{i}}^{Z}(a_{i})=x_{i}^{i}=f(a_{i})
, contradicting the fact that
f
is
Z
-DNC.

(2)\to(1)
. Given a
Z
-escaping function
g
, and
e\in\mathbb{N}
, define
f(e)
as follows:
Let
W_{a}^{Z}
be the
Z
-c.e. set whose unique element is
\Phi^{Z}_{e}(e)
, if it exists, and
W_{a}^{Z}=\emptyset
otherwise. Let
f(e)=g(a,1)
. By construction,
\operatorname{card}W_{a}^{Z}\leq 1
, so

, hence

∎

One can refine the previous lemma to require the
Z
-escaping function to output its value within an infinite computable set
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, by transforming any
Z
-c.e. set
W_{e}^{Z}\subseteq X
into the set of positions of its elements in

.

Computably bounded escaping functions
.
The situation becomes more problematic if one wants the
Z
-escaping function
g
to output its value within a finite set
F\subseteq\mathbb{N}
. One would require
g
to be
h
-bounded, for a computable function
h
. By an analysis of the proof of
Lemma

6.3
, the existence of a computably bounded
Z
-escaping function is equivalent to the existence of a computably bounded
Z
-DNC function (with a different computable bound). Such functions are still probabilistic objects for sufficiently fast-growing bounds, in that every
Z
-random computes a computably bounded
Z
-DNC function. They have been studied by multiple authors
[
2
,
14
]
, and in particular by Khan and Miller
[
21
]
, who proved the existence, for every
Z
, of
Z
-DNC functions which do not compute any computably bounded DNC function, and a fortiori any 2-random set.

Although strictly weaker than 2-randomness, computing solutions from any computably bounded escaping function is not satisfactory for the following reason: The underlying goal is to prove that for every separable permutation
p
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
implies
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
over
\omega
-models, to deduce that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
implies
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
. Mimouni and Patey
[
31
]
constructed a computable coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
such that any infinite set
f
-avoiding any pattern
p
(not only separable permutations) computes a
\emptyset^{\prime}
-DNC function. It follows that for every pattern
p
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
implies the existence of a
\emptyset^{\prime}
-DNC function. There is however no hope of improving this bound by showing that any pattern avoiding
p
computes a computably bounded
\emptyset^{\prime}
-DNC function, as Liu
[
29
]
proved that every computable coloring
f:[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to 2
admits an infinite
f
-homogeneous set which does not compute any computably bounded DNC function.

Partial escaping functions.

Thankfully, we shall see that any
\emptyset^{\prime}
-DNC function (or equivalently any
\emptyset^{\prime}
-escaping function) computes a partial, computably bounded
\emptyset^{\prime}
-escaping function, in the following sense:

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
set of “bad elements”,
(F_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a computable family of subsets of
\mathbb{N}
and

such that:
\operatorname{card}F_{i}\cap B\leq k
and
\operatorname{card}F_{i}\geq i
for every

. Then, every
\emptyset^{\prime}
-DNC degree
\mathbf{d}
computes an infinite subset of
\bigcup_{i}F_{i}\setminus B
.

Proof.

Fix such
B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,
(F_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbf{d}
. We have two cases.

Case 1:

\mathbf{d}\geq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
. Let
\ell\leq k
be the largest integer such that
\operatorname{card}F_{i}\cap B=\ell
for infinitely many
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Let

be such that for every
i\geq t
,
\operatorname{card}F_{i}\cap B\leq\ell
. As

is
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
, one can
\emptyset^{\prime}
-computably find infinitely many

such that
\operatorname{card}F_{i}\cap B\geq\ell
, hence such that
\operatorname{card}F_{i}\cap B=\ell
, and pick an element in
F_{i}\setminus B
.

Case 2:

\mathbf{d}\not\geq\emptyset^{\prime}
. Let
g\leq_{T}\mathbf{d}
be the function of
Lemma

6.3
(2). Let
\mu_{s}
be the standard left-c.e. approximation of the modulus
\mu
of
\emptyset^{\prime}
, that is,
\mu_{s}(x)
is the least

such that
\emptyset^{\prime}_{t}\upharpoonright x=\emptyset^{\prime}_{s}\upharpoonright x
. For every
x\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
(\mu_{s}(x))_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
can only change values at most
x
times (every time a program with Turing index less than
x
stops), and let
m_{0}<\dots<m_{i}(=\mu(x))
be those values for some

. Then, consider the
\emptyset^{\prime}
-enumerable set

containing
\{0,\dots,x-1\}
and the position in

of the elements of
F_{m_{j}}\cap B
for every

.

Note that
\operatorname{card}W^{\emptyset^{\prime}}_{e(x)}\leq x\times(k+1)
, hence, by hypothesis on
g
, letting
p_{x}=g(e(x),x\times(k+1))
, the sequence
(p_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{N}}
is such that
p_{x}\notin W^{\emptyset^{\prime}}_{e(x)}
for every

. In particular,
p_{x}\geq x
for every

as
\{0,\dots,x-1\}\subseteq W^{\emptyset^{\prime}}_{e(x)}
. Since
\mathbf{d}\not\geq\emptyset^{\prime}
, the sequence
(p_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{N}}
does not dominate

, hence, for infinitely many

, we can computably find some stage

such that
\mu_{s}(x)\geq p_{x}
and if

is chosen to be minimal, this ensures that the element in position
p_{x}
of

does not belong to

. Let

be this set of elements. Then

, and since

, then

is infinite.
∎

Consider for instance a stable computable version of
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(120)

Remark 6.6
.

The argument in
Lemma

6.4
admits many degrees of freedom, opening the door to many generalizations.
First, the notion of “bad element” is absolute, in the sense that it does not depend on the previous choices of elements, while with non-stable colorings, one uses the notion of
X
-bad which depends on the reservoir
X
, which itself depends on the choices of the previous elements.
Second,
Lemma

6.4
only states the existence of an infinite subset
S
of
\bigcup_{i}F_{i}\setminus B
, but one could require that for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the set
S
intersects simultaneously
F_{i},F_{i+1},\dots,F_{i+k}
for infinitely many
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
We shall use both generalizations when dealing with arbitrary separable permutations, through
Proposition

6.11
.

General case.
We now turn to the study of
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for arbitrary separable permutations

.
As in
Section

5
, we shall first prove the theorem for fractals, and deduce the general case since they form a basis for separable permutations.
By
Proposition

3.3
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
implies
\mathsf{COH}
for every separable permutation

, so it would be sufficient to consider only stable instances of
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)

. We however prove it for every computable instance of

It follows that

is computably reducible to

, that is, every

, such that for every infinite

7

In

5

6

is a separable permutation, and gave computability-theoretic upper bounds in terms of relativized diagonal non-computability. In this section, we prove the tightness of

by proving that if

is a pattern which is not a separable permutation, then
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
admits no probabilistic solutions, in the sense that there is a computable instance such that the measure of oracles computing a solution is 0. For this, we use some interactions between computable linear orders and measure.

Given a linear order
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{N},<_{\mathcal{L}})
of order type
\omega+\omega^{*}
.
We write
A(\mathcal{L})
for the
\omega
-part of
\mathcal{L}
, that is, the set
\{x\in\mathbb{N}:\forall^{\infty}y\ x<_{\mathcal{L}}y\}

A sequence is
\mathcal{L}
-monotonous
if it is
\mathcal{L}
-ascending or
\mathcal{L}
-descending.

The following proposition is a consequence of
[
18

. Indeed, for every computable linear order
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{N},<_{\mathcal{L}})
of order type
\omega+\omega^{*}
, by
[

, Theorem 4.1]

A(\mathcal{L})
and

are semirecursive. If furthermore
\mathcal{L}
has no infinite computable
\mathcal{L}
-monotonous sequence, then
A(\mathcal{L})
and
\overline{A}(\mathcal{L})
are both immune, hence by
[
18
, Theorem 4.2]
they are both hyperimmune.
We give a more direct proof without using the notion of semirecursive set.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{N},<_{\mathcal{L}})
be a computable linear order of order type
\omega+\omega^{*}
, with no computable infinite
\mathcal{L}
-monotonous sequence. Then
A(\mathcal{L})
is bi-hyperimmune.

Proof.

Suppose for the contradiction that
A(\mathcal{L})
is not hyperimmune.
Let
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a c.e. array such that
F_{n}\cap\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{L})\neq\emptyset
for every

.
Then, the set
H=\{\min_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{n}):n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is an infinite c.e. subset of

. The set
H
contains a computable infinite
\mathcal{L}
-ascending sequence, contradicting the hypothesis.
By a dual argument,
\overline{A}(\mathcal{L})
is hyperimmune since there is no computable infinite
\mathcal{L}
-descending sequence.
∎

Mileti
[
30
, Theorem 5.2.6]
proved that for every infinite hyperimmune set

, the measure of oracles computing an infinite subset of

is 0. It follows that if
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{N},<_{\mathcal{L}})
is a computable instance of
\mathsf{SADS}
with no computable solution, the measure of oracles computing a solution is 0. We prove it directly for the sake of containment and simplicity.

Proposition 7.2

Let
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{N},<_{\mathcal{L}})
be a computable linear order of order type

, with no computable infinite
\mathcal{L}
-monotonous sequence. The measure of oracles computing an infinite
\mathcal{L}
-monotonous sequence is 0.

Assume by contradiction that this measure is non-zero. There are countably many functionals

\mathcal{L}
-ascending sequences for every oracle.

By Lebesgue’s density lemma, there exists a finite binary sequence
\sigma

is an infinite

-ascending sequence is greater than half the measure of

For every

-smaller than

\mathcal{L}
-ascending sequence (in particular those outputted by

-ascending sequence whose last element is

-bigger than

is an infinite
\mathcal{L}
-ascending sequence, as we cannot have two disjoint sets of measure greater than

in a set of measure

Theorem 7.8
.

There exists a computable linear ordering
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq)

Proof.

for

an infinite set, as well as a functional that outputs an infinite
\sqsubseteq_{A}
-increasing sequence on a set of measure
>\frac{1}{4}

For that, we will consider four functionals
\Gamma_{inc}
,
\Gamma_{dec}
,
\Delta_{inc}
and
\Delta_{dec}
with the following properties:

•

For
A=\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots\}
a set and

\Gamma^{A}_{inc}(e)
and
\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
will compute a linear order
(B,\sqsubseteq_{B})
for some

. If
A
is infinite, then so will be
B
.

•

For every sets
A
and
C
,
\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{inc}(e)
(resp
\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{dec}(e)
) will compute an increasing sequence of
\Gamma^{A}_{inc}(e)
(resp
\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
). This sequence will also be increasing for the standard ordering
<
of the integers.

If
A
is infinite, then the measure of oracles
C
such that
\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{inc}(e)
(resp
\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{dec}(e)
) is an infinite increasing sequence will be at least
\Pi_{\ell>e}(1-\frac{1}{2^{\ell}})

\Pi_{\ell>0}(1-\frac{1}{2^{\ell}})>\frac{1}{4}
.

•

No

for

will be an infinite monotone sequence of
\Gamma^{A}_{inc}(e)
or
\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
. Furthermore,

will not be an infinite monotone
sequence of
\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
and will not be an infinite decreasing sequence of

.

These four functionals will construct their ordering step by step, with the position of

.
\Gamma_{inc}
and

will call each other recursively as follows: For every infinite oracle

\Gamma^{B}_{dec}(1)
for multiple

Let

be a set and

be a Turing index.

Definition of
\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
.

\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
computes the order

defined as follows:

.

\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{dec}(e)
.

.

in the ordering, a finite amount of recursive calls are needed. This is due to the fact that

immediately fixes the position of

which then call

, and so on for the next elements.

This definition of

ensures that

will not be an infinite increasing sequence. Indeed, otherwise, there would be some

such that after a certain stage

are in

(and since the sequence is increasing, no elements of

are in

), this would disable

is finite and contradicting our assumption.

Definition of
\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{inc}(e)
.

Notice that in the construction of the order

, exactly one of the blocks

will end up disabled, thus, for all the other blocks

, an infinite increasing sequence of

will output an infinite increasing sequence of

by removing the finite amount of elements that are not in

. Hence,

can be defined to act as follows: using the first

, then

will be the smallest element of the increasing sequence outputted, and it will then recursively call

and remove the elements of the sequence that are not in

to find the rest of the sequence.

\Delta^{C\oplus A}_{inc}(e)
will output an infinite increasing sequence if it never picks the wrong block that end up disabled during the recursive calls. This happens with probability

.

So far, we only proved that
\Gamma^{A}_{dec}(e)
(resp
\Gamma^{A}_{inc}(e)
) ensures that

will not be an infinite decreasing (resp increasing) sequence of the computed order. Due to the recursive nature of the functionals, we can go further than that and obtain the same property for every

. Indeed, for every

, all the elements ordered by
\Gamma^{A}_{inc}(e)
, except for a finite amount, have been ordered by one of the (finitely many) recursive call to some
\Gamma^{A_{i}}_{inc}(e^{\prime})
for some

. If

was an infinite increasing sequence, then this would yield an infinite decreasing subsequence for one of the

Thus, taking
\Gamma_{dec}^{\mathbb{N}}(0)
to be the ordering and

to be the functional yields the desired result.

Corollary 7.9
.

There exists a computable linear ordering
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq^{\prime})
with no computable infinite monotone sequence,
such that the measure of oracles computing both an infinite increasing and an infinite decreasing sequence is one.

Proof.

Let
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq)
be the ordering obtained in
Theorem

7.8
and let
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq^{\prime})
be defined as follows:
a\sqsubseteq^{\prime}b
holds if either
a=2a^{\prime}
,
b=2b^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime}\sqsubseteq b^{\prime}
, or
a=2a^{\prime}
and
b=2b^{\prime}+1
, or
a=2a^{\prime}+1
,

and

.

The ordering
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq^{\prime})
corresponds to two copies of

put one next to each other, with the second copy flipped. It is still a computable ordering with no infinite computable monotone sequence, as such a sequence would eventually stays in one of the copy an yield an infinite computable

Remark 7.10

This can be solved by noticing that the proof of

allows us to uniformly compute an infinite sequence
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of linear orders with no computable infinite monotone sequence, as well as an infinite list of functionals
\Phi_{e(n)}
computing an infinite increasing sequence for a set of oracles of measure
>1-\frac{1}{n+1}
. To do so, notice that the probability
\Pi_{\ell>0}(1-\frac{1}{2^{\ell}})>\frac{1}{4}
obtained in the proof could have been made arbitrarily close to
1
by splitting into
2^{e+n+1}
blocks instead of
2^{e+1}
at stage
e
of the construction.

We can then combine the orderings
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
into a single ordering
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq)
as follows: let
\langle a,b\rangle\sqsubseteq\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rangle
if either
b\sqsubset_{0}b^{\prime}
or if
b=b^{\prime}
and
a\sqsubseteq_{b}a^{\prime}
(the order
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq)
correspond to the union of the all the orders
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
placed relative to each other according to
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq_{0})
).

The order
(\mathbb{N},\sqsubseteq)
is computable and contains no infinite computable monotone sequence
(\langle a_{n},b_{n}\rangle)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Indeed, if such a sequence existed, either the value of
b_{n}
would change infinitely many time, yielding an infinite computable
\sqsubseteq_{0}
-monotone sequence, or eventually
b_{n}
stays constant to some value
b
after some stage
k
, and
(a_{n})_{n>k}
would be an infinite computable
\sqsubseteq_{b}
-monotone sequence.

Finally, we obtain a single functional
\Phi^{A}(n)
, taking a parameter
n
and running
\Phi^{A}_{e(n)}
on the order induced on
\{\langle a,n\rangle:a\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Thus, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the set of oracles
A
such that
\Phi^{A}(n)
is an infinite increasing sequence if of measure at least
1-\frac{1}{n+1}
.

8
Separable permutations and first-order part

The
first-order part
of a second-order system is the set of its first-order consequences, that is, the set of theorems in the language of first-order arithmetic. Characterizing the first-order part of a theorem is of fundamental importance in understanding its reverse mathematical strength. To this end, one usually proves that a second-order theorem
T_{0}
is a
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative extension of another theorem
T_{1}
for which the first-order part is already known. If so, then their first-order parts coincide.

The characterization of the first-order part of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs and two colors is one of the major remaining open questions around the reverse mathematics of combinatorial theorems. See Kołodziejczyk and Yokoyama
[
24
]
for a survey on the subject. The first-order part of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
is known to follow strictly from
\Sigma_{2}
-induction (
\mathsf{I}\Sigma_{2}
) and to imply the
\Sigma_{2}
-bounding scheme (
\mathsf{B}\Sigma_{2}
). It is currently unknown whether it coincides with
\mathsf{B}\Sigma_{2}
. The goal of this section is to relate the first-order parts of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
and
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
for any separable permutation
p
.

By
Proposition

3.3
,
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)\to\mathsf{ADS}
for every separable permutation
p
.
It follows from Hirschfeldt and Shore
[
16
]
that

implies the
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
-bounding scheme over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
. We now generalize this to every permutation via a direct proof.

In the quest for characterizing the first-order part of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs, Patey and Yokoyama

n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
H_{n}
has some
\mathcal{L}
-maximal element
x
, let
H_{n+1}=H_{n}\setminus\{x\}
, otherwise let
H_{n+1}=H_{n}
. Every
H_{n}
is an infinite set, as we only remove at most one element each time.

By definition of a largeness notion, for every
n
, there exists some
\mathbb{L}
-large subset
F\subseteq H_{n}
. Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\min_{\mathcal{L}}(F)=x_{\ell}
, then
\max_{L}(F_{k-1}^{\ell})<_{\mathcal{L}}\min_{\mathcal{L}}(F)
and there are at least
n
elements

that are
\mathcal{L}
-bigger than
\max_{\mathcal{L}}F
by definition of
H_{n}
(if
H_{n}
has no maximal element, then this is obvious, and is
H_{n}
has some maximal element, then there are
n
elements bigger than it in
H_{0}
). Hence the property is true for
k+1
.
∎

Note that by
Theorem

8.8
, the previous lemma can be proven over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
for
\omega^{n}
-largeness. Indeed, any
\omega^{n+1}
-large
\mathcal{L}
-homogeneous set

can be decomposed into
\min F
many

-large sets with the desired property.

The following proposition uses the same proof structure as in

Proposition 8.11
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a computable largeness notion and
\mathcal{L}=(\mathbb{N},<_{\mathcal{L}})
a computable linear order. Then, every
\emptyset^{\prime}
-DNC degree
\mathbf{d}
computes an infinite
\mathbb{L}
-grouping.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{d}\geq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
, then we are done, as every computable linear order admits a
\Pi^{0}_{1}
infinite
\mathcal{L}
-monotonous sequence (Manaster; see Downey
[
10
, Section 5]
). The degree
\mathbf{d}
can therefore compute such a sequence, and from it, any infinite sequence of
\mathbb{L}
-large subsets forms an
\mathbb{L}
-grouping.

If
\mathbf{d}\not\geq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
, then, let
g\leq_{T}\mathbf{d}
be the function of
Lemma

6.3
(2). Let
\mu_{s}
be the standard left-c.e. approximation of the modulus
\mu
of
\emptyset^{\prime}
, that is,
\mu_{s}(x)
is the least
t\leq s
such that
\emptyset^{\prime}_{t}\upharpoonright x=\emptyset^{\prime}_{s}\upharpoonright x
. For every
x\in\mathbb{N}
, the sequence
(\mu_{s}(x))_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
can only change values at most
x
times (every time a program with Turing index less than
x
stops), and let
m^{x}_{0}<\dots<m^{x}_{i_{x}}(=\mu(x))
be those values for some
i_{x}<x
.

Every set
F\subseteq\mathbb{N}
defines an interval comprised of all the elements
x
such that
\min_{\mathcal{L}}F<_{\mathcal{L}}x<\max_{\mathcal{L}}F
. For
H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}
a family of finite sets, let
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
be set of all the elements bigger (for the standard order on
\mathbb{N}
) than
\max H_{s-1}
and not inside one of the interval defined by the
H_{i}
for
i<s
.

A finite set
F
will be said to be
good
in
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
if there are infinitely many
x\in X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
not in the interval determined by
F
, otherwise
F
is said to be
bad
in
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
. Notice that being bad is a
\Sigma_{2}^{0}
property, and that if
F
is good in
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
, then
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1},F\rangle}
is infinite.

Given some infinite set
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
and some sequence
F_{0},\dots,F_{k-1}
of finite sets such that
\max_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i})<_{\mathcal{L}}\min_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i+1})
for every
i<k-1
, at most one

can be bad in
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
. Indeed, under our assumption, the intervals defined by the
F_{i}
are disjoints, hence no two of them can have a finite complement in
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
.

We can
\mathbf{d}
-computably build an infinite sequence of finite sets
H_{0}<H_{1}<\dots
satisfying that:

•

X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
is infinite for every
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}
forms an
\mathbb{L}
-grouping for every
s\in\mathbb{N}
.

Suppose
H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}
have been defined for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Since they form an
\mathbb{L}
-grouping, their corresponding intervals are disjoints and
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
can be partitioned into
s+1
many blocks
X^{0},\dots,X^{s}
with
X^{i}
containing all the
x\in X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
such that
\max_{\mathcal{L}}H_{j}<_{\mathcal{L}}x
for exactly
i
values of

.

For every
x\in\mathbb{N}
, let
W_{e(x,\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle)}^{\emptyset^{\prime}}
be the
\emptyset^{\prime}
-c.e. set which, for every
j<i_{x}
, searches for some
\ell\leq s
and some sequence
F_{0},\dots,F_{m_{j}^{x}}\subseteq X^{\ell}
of
\mathbb{L}
-large sets, such that
\max_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i})<_{\mathcal{L}}\min_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i+1})
for every
i<m_{j}^{x}
and then list the index
i\leq m_{j}^{x}
such that
F_{i}
is bad in
X_{\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle}
if there is one.

Note that
\operatorname{card}W_{e(x,\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle)}\leq x
for every
x\in\mathbb{N}
and let
h(x)=g(e(x,\langle H_{0},\dots,H_{s-1}\rangle),x)
. Since
\mathbf{d}\not\geq_{T}\emptyset^{\prime}
and
h
is
\mathbf{d}
-computable, we have
h(x)<\mu(x)
for infinitely many
x
, hence we can wait and find some
x
and

such that
h(x)<m_{j}^{x}
.

Computably search for some
\ell\leq s
and some sequence
F_{0},\dots,F_{m_{j}^{x}}\subseteq X^{\ell}
of
\mathbb{L}
-large sets such that
\max_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i})<_{\mathcal{L}}\min_{\mathcal{L}}(F_{i+1})
for every

. Such a sequence exists by

8.10
as one of the

is infinite. Then, by definition of

,
F_{h(x)}
will be good in

. Pick

. By our assumption that

∎

Theorem 8.12
.

\mathsf{EM}
implies
\mathsf{GP}^{2}_{2}
in
\omega
-models.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}=(\mathbb{N},S)
be an
\omega
-model of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{EM}
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be an
X
-computable largeness notion for some set
X\in S
and let

Y
-computable coloring for some set

. Using

9
Open questions

The systematic study of Ramsey-like statements is relatively new, so many questions remain open. The most fundamental question is about the general structure of this partial order.

Question 9.1
.

What is the structure of the Ramsey-like statements, ordered by implication over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
? over
\omega
-models? over computable reduction?

We showed in particular in
Corollary

3.7
that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
forms a minimal cover of
\mathsf{EM}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
in the set of Ramsey-like statements, and
a fortiori
over
\omega
-models. It remains unknown whether this is actually a strong minimal cover.

Question 9.2
.

Is
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
a strong minimal cover of
\mathsf{EM}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
? over
\omega
-models? over computable reduction?

In particular, over
\omega
-models, if
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
is strictly weaker than
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
, then by
1.7
,
p
is not a separable permutation, so either it is not a permutation, in which case
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
follows from
\mathsf{EM}
, or it is a permutation, but not separable, and by
Theorem

2.1
it follows from
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(1302)
. By Mimouni and Patey
[
31
]
,
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(1302)
does not imply
\mathsf{EM}
over
\omega
-models, but the other direction is unknown.

Question 9.3
.

Does
\mathsf{EM}
imply
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(1302)
over
\omega
-models?

The main theorem of this article states that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
implies
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
over
\omega
-models whenever
p
is a separable permutation. The proof however seems to make a strong use of
\omega
-models, in particular with
Lemma

5.2
. We shall prove in a later paper that
Lemma

5.2
is not provable over
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
.

Question 9.4
.

For every separable permutation
p
, does
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}\vdash\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)\to\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
?

Moreover, the proof uses two applications of
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
. In particular, 120 and 102 are the two smallest separable permutations for which the following question is open.

Question 9.5
.

For every separable permutation
p
, is
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
computably reducible to
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}(p)
?

To prove our main theorem, we show that for every computable instance of
\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
, every 2-random computes a solution. However, the proof seems to make multiple essential uses of
\Sigma^{0}_{2}
-induction.

Question 9.6
.

For every separable permutation
p
, does
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}\vdash 2\mbox{-}\mathsf{RAN}\to\mathsf{coRT}^{2}_{2}(p)
?

The statement
2\mbox{-}\mathsf{RAN}
was studied by various authors in reverse mathematics
[
8
,
33
,
1
]
. In particular, Conidis and Slaman
[
8
]
showed that
\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}+2\mbox{-}\mathsf{RAN}
is equivalent to
2\mbox{-}\mathsf{WWKL}
over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, and is
\Pi^{1}_{1}
-conservative over
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}+\mathsf{B}\Sigma^{0}_{2}
, where
2\mbox{-}\mathsf{WWKL}
is weak König’s lemma for
\Delta^{0}_{2}
trees of positive measure.
