A nanofiber-based biocatalytic system: catalase immobilization on an electrospun PVP/GA carrier platform for hydrogen peroxide decomposition
Authors: Ceyhun Işık, Özgün Vatansever, Mustafa Teke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is widely used in industrial and environmental processes; however, excessive accumulation of H2O2 may cause oxidative damage and environmental hazards. Catalase (CAT) is an efficient enzyme capable of decomposing H2O2 into water and oxygen, but its practical application is limited by low stability and lack of reusability in its free form. In this study, CAT was immobilized onto electrospun polyvinylpyrrolidone/gum arabic (PVP/GA) nanofibers via glutaraldehyde-mediated covalent cross-linking to develop a stable and reusable biocatalytic system. The effects of key immobilization parameters, including CAT concentration, nanofiber amount, cross-linking time, and glutaraldehyde concentration, were systematically optimized, and a maximum immobilization efficiency of 81.2% was achieved under optimal conditions. The immobilized enzyme exhibited improved thermal stability, broader pH tolerance, enhanced pH stability, and superior storage stability compared to the free enzyme. Kinetic analysis revealed an increase in both Km and Vmax values after immobilization, indicating enhanced catalytic capacity despite partial diffusion limitations. Application experiments demonstrated that the immobilized CAT achieved complete degradation of H2O2 at concentrations up to 100 mM within 25 min, while maintaining significant catalytic performance during repeated operational cycles. Moreover, the immobilized enzyme retained 52% of its initial activity after 30 days of storage, highlighting its improved stability. These findings demonstrate that PVP/GA nanofiber supported CAT represents a promising and reusable biocatalytic system for efficient H2O2 detoxification in environmental and industrial applications.

Introduction

H2O2 is a widely used oxidizing agent in various industrial processes such as textile bleaching, pulp and paper production, food sterilization, and wastewater treatment.1 Despite its utility, the excessive release of H2O2 into the environment poses ecological and health concerns due to its oxidative toxicity.2 Therefore, the efficient and controlled removal of residual H2O2 from industrial effluents is of significant importance to prevent environmental contamination and to meet regulatory discharge limits.3 The environmental relevance of residual H2O2 is further supported by its large-scale industrial use, particularly in the pulp and paper, textile, chemical processing, and water treatment sectors.4 According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, maintaining free H2O2 residual concentrations within a controlled range is important in water treatment applications to ensure effective microbial control while avoiding adverse environmental and operational effects.5 Therefore, efficient post-treatment removal of excess H2O2 remains an important consideration before discharge or reuse of treated waters.6

To address this issue, several physical and chemical methods have been employed for H2O2 degradation, including catalytic reduction using transition metals, advanced oxidation processes, UV irradiation, and activated carbon adsorption.7,8 While these techniques can be effective, they often suffer from drawbacks such as high energy demand, incomplete degradation, secondary pollution, or the need for expensive reagents and equipment.9 In contrast, enzymatic degradation of H2O2 has emerged as a green, specific, and efficient alternative. Among the enzymes capable of H2O2 decomposition, CAT stands out due to its exceptional catalytic efficiency in converting H2O2 into harmless water and oxygen, without generating toxic by-products.10 Compared with conventional H2O2 removal technologies, enzymatic degradation using CAT offers several advantages, including high substrate specificity, operation under mild conditions, and the absence of secondary pollutants.11 While advanced oxidation processes and metal-based catalytic systems may achieve rapid H2O2 conversion, they often require significant energy input, chemical reagents, or generate secondary waste streams.12 In contrast, CAT catalyzes the direct decomposition of H2O2 into water and oxygen, making it an environmentally benign alternative for peroxide detoxification applications.

CAT (EC 1.11.1.6) is a heme-containing oxidoreductase that catalyzes the disproportionation of H2O2 into water and molecular oxygen. This reaction is crucial in protecting cells from oxidative damage caused by reactive oxygen species.13 Due to its exceptionally high turnover number capable of decomposing millions of H2O2 molecules per second CAT has gained widespread attention for biotechnological and environmental applications involving peroxide removal.11,14 Although CAT exhibits remarkable catalytic efficiency, its free form is often unstable under industrial or environmental conditions due to factors such as thermal inactivation, pH sensitivity, and susceptibility to proteolytic degradation. Moreover, the inability to recover and reuse free enzymes limits their practical and economic viability in continuous or large-scale operations.15,16 Enzyme immobilization offers a promising strategy to overcome these challenges by confining the enzyme onto or within solid supports, thereby enhancing its stability, reusability, and operational lifetime.17

Among the various supports used for enzyme immobilization, carriers such as natural polymers, inorganic materials, nanoparticles, hydrogels, and nanofibers have been widely explored.18 The ideal immobilization matrix is expected to offer a high surface area, biocompatibility, chemical functionality, and mechanical stability, while also preserving the native conformation and activity of the enzyme. These properties contribute to enhanced enzyme retention, improved reaction efficiency, and increased resistance to harsh operational conditions.19,20

In recent years, nanofibrous materials produced via electrospinning have emerged as particularly attractive supports for enzyme immobilization.21 Compared to other matrices, electrospun nanofibers offer a unique combination of advantages, including an exceptionally high surface area to volume ratio, tunable porosity, high fiber interconnectivity, and versatile surface modifiability.22 These structural features create a highly accessible and favorable microenvironment for enzyme binding, promoting not only effective enzyme loading but also facilitating substrate diffusion and enhancing overall catalytic performance.23 Moreover, the mild processing conditions and adaptability to various polymer systems make electrospinning a convenient and scalable technique for fabricating functional immobilization platforms.24 In this context, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and gum arabic (GA) represent two polymeric materials with complementary properties, making them suitable candidates for nanofiber-based immobilization systems. PVP is a synthetic, hydrophilic, and biocompatible polymer widely used in biomedical and pharmaceutical applications. It offers excellent solubility and film-forming ability, which aids in the formation of uniform nanofibers with mechanical stability.25 On the other hand, GA is a natural, water-soluble polysaccharide composed mainly of arabinogalactan and glycoproteins. It contains numerous functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which can participate in cross-linking reactions and enhance enzyme attachment through covalent bonding.26 Furthermore, GA contributes bioadhesive properties and structural flexibility, which can improve the immobilization matrix's interaction with the enzyme and the substrate.27 The combination of PVP and GA thus enables the formation of hybrid nanofibers with favorable physicochemical characteristics, supporting enzyme immobilization while maintaining high catalytic activity and structural integrity under operational conditions. Compared with many conventional immobilization supports, the combination of PVP and GA offers a complementary balance of structural and functional properties. PVP contributes excellent electrospinnability, hydrophilicity, and mechanical integrity, whereas GA provides abundant hydroxyl and carboxyl groups that can facilitate enzyme attachment and improve the biocompatibility of the support matrix. The integration of these synthetic and natural polymers enables the fabrication of a highly porous nanofibrous platform with favorable characteristics for enzyme immobilization. Furthermore, reports on CAT immobilization using electrospun PVP/GA hybrid nanofibers remain limited. Therefore, the present study provides additional insight into the feasibility of utilizing PVP/GA nanofibrous supports for the development of stable and reusable CAT-based biocatalytic systems for H2O2 detoxification.

Although CAT immobilization has been extensively investigated using various supports, including nanoparticles, hydrogels, membranes, and electrospun fibers, challenges such as enzyme leaching, limited operational stability, and reduced reusability still remain. Therefore, there is continued interest in developing immobilization platforms that combine high enzyme loading, structural stability, and operational durability. In this context, the present study investigates electrospun PVP/GA nanofibers as a support matrix for CAT immobilization through glutaraldehyde-mediated covalent cross-linking to develop a stable and reusable biocatalytic system for H2O2 detoxification. The effects of key immobilization parameters, including enzyme concentration, nanofiber amount, cross-linking time, and glutaraldehyde concentration, were systematically optimized. The catalytic properties of the immobilized enzyme were subsequently evaluated in terms of kinetic behavior, optimum pH and temperature, thermal stability, pH stability, storage stability, and reusability. Furthermore, the practical applicability of the developed system was assessed through H2O2 degradation experiments under varying substrate concentrations, reaction times, and operational cycles. A schematic representation of the preparation of PVP/GA nanofibers via electrospinning and the subsequent CAT immobilization process is presented in Fig. 1. Overall, this study provides insight into the catalytic performance and operational durability of CAT immobilized on electrospun PVP/GA nanofibers and demonstrates the potential of this hybrid nanofibrous platform for efficient and reusable H2O2 detoxification applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

PVP (Mw ≈ 1 300 000 g mol−1) and GA (food grade) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). CAT from bovine liver (EC 1.11.1.6, ≥2000 units per mg protein), glutaraldehyde solution (25% v/v), acetic acid (glacial, ≥99%), and H2O2 (30% w/w) were also purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. All aqueous solutions were prepared using ultrapure deionized water unless otherwise stated.

Preparation of PVP/GA nanofibers

To prepare the electrospinning solution, 16% (w/v) PVP and 2% (w/v) GA were dissolved in a 1 : 1 (v/v) mixture of deionized water and glacial acetic acid. The polymer blend was magnetically stirred at room temperature for 12 h until a clear, homogeneous solution was obtained. The viscosity and conductivity of the resulting solution were visually assessed to ensure suitability for electrospinning. Electrospinning was performed using a laboratory-scale electrospinning device equipped with a high-voltage power supply and a syringe pump. The prepared solution was loaded into a 10 mL plastic syringe fitted with a stainless-steel needle (inner diameter: 0.6 mm). The process parameters were set as follows: an applied voltage of 13 kV, a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm, and a flow rate of 1.0 mL h−1. Nanofibers were collected onto a stationary aluminum foil-covered flat collector under ambient conditions (22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity ∼ 40–45%). The as-spun nanofibers were left to dry at room temperature overnight to remove any residual solvent. It should be noted that PVP/GA nanofibers are water-soluble, and therefore cannot be directly used for enzyme immobilization in aqueous systems. As such, a cross-linking step was required to stabilize the nanofibers, and they were stored in a desiccator until the cross-linking process was performed prior to enzyme immobilization.

Cross-linking of PVP/GA nanofibers

Due to the inherent hydrophilicity of PVP and GA, the electrospun nanofibrous mats exhibited limited stability in aqueous environments. To improve water resistance and maintain structural integrity during subsequent immobilization and application studies, the nanofibers were chemically cross-linked prior to enzyme immobilization. For the cross-linking process, an aqueous glutaraldehyde solution was freshly prepared by diluting a commercial 25% (v/v) glutaraldehyde stock solution with deionized water to obtain the desired working concentration. The electrospun PVP/GA nanofibers were fully immersed in this cross-linking solution, ensuring uniform contact between the fibers and the cross-linking medium. The treatment was carried out under ambient conditions, allowing glutaraldehyde molecules to react with functional groups present on the polymer chains and form covalent inter-chain linkages within the nanofibrous matrix. Following the cross-linking treatment, the nanofibers were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual glutaraldehyde and any unreacted species. The cross-linked nanofibers were then air-dried at room temperature and stored in a desiccator until further use.

Characterization of PVP/GA nanofibers

The physicochemical, morphological, and thermal properties of the electrospun PVP/GA nanofibers were systematically characterized using multiple analytical techniques. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was employed to identify the characteristic functional groups of the polymer components and to examine possible interactions within the nanofibrous structure. FTIR spectra were recorded using an ATR-FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS-5) over the spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Thermal properties of the nanofibers were investigated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) using a PerkinElmer TGA 4000 system. Approximately 5–10 mg of sample was heated from 50 °C to 650 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere to evaluate thermal stability and decomposition behavior. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements were carried out using a PerkinElmer Jade DSC instrument to determine thermal transition temperatures, including glass transition and melting behavior. Samples were heated in the temperature range of 30–350 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under an argon purge gas flow of 200 mL min−1. The resulting thermograms were analyzed using Pyris software.

The surface morphology of the electrospun nanofiber mats before and after enzyme immobilization was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JEOL JSM-7600F), located at the Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University Advanced Technologies Application and Research Center. Prior to SEM imaging, nanofiber samples were thermally dried at 60 °C for 48 h to remove residual solvent and moisture. The dried samples were subsequently stored in a desiccator for 24 h to minimize humidity-related artifacts during imaging. After preparation, the samples were mounted onto aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance electrical conductivity. SEM micrographs were obtained at identical magnification levels (×5000) from five randomly selected regions of each nanofiber mat in order to ensure representative sampling of the fibrous structure. The average fiber diameter was determined by manually measuring 100 randomly selected fibers from SEM micrographs using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Diameter distribution histograms and mean diameter values were subsequently generated using OriginPro 2019 software. The apparent surface area and porosity of the nanofiber mats were estimated by analyzing the SEM images using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Each SEM image was first converted to an 8 bit grayscale format and subsequently binarized using an appropriate threshold to distinguish fibers from pore regions. Following binarization, the “Analyze Particles” function in ImageJ was applied to quantify the pore area fraction. The porosity of the nanofiber mats was calculated as the ratio of the pore area to the total image area, expressed as a percentage. Surface area-related morphological characteristics were evaluated based on the distribution and proportion of the fiber and pore regions obtained from the processed images. For each sample, five independent SEM images were analyzed, and the reported values represent the mean ± standard deviation of these measurements.

Immobilization and optimization of CAT on PVP/GA nanofibers

CAT was immobilized onto cross-linked PVP/GA nanofibers via direct glutaraldehyde-mediated covalent attachment. In a typical immobilization experiment, a known amount of cross-linked PVP/GA nanofiber (10–30 mg) was incubated with 1 mL of CAT solution prepared at different concentrations (0.5–2.5 mg mL−1). Glutaraldehyde was added directly to the enzyme nanofiber system to initiate covalent coupling between aldehyde groups of the cross-linker and amino groups present on both the enzyme molecules and the polymeric matrix. The immobilization mechanism is expected to involve the reaction of glutaraldehyde-derived aldehyde groups with surface-accessible amino groups of CAT, primarily the ε-amino groups of lysine residues. These reactions lead to the formation of Schiff-base linkages between the enzyme molecules and the cross-linked PVP/GA nanofiber matrix, resulting in stable covalent attachment. Since lysine residues are generally distributed on the external surface of CAT, covalent coupling is expected to occur predominantly away from the catalytic heme center, thereby preserving enzymatic functionality. The immobilization reaction was carried out at room temperature under gentle agitation for varying cross-linking durations (30–150 min) using glutaraldehyde concentrations in the range of 1–5% (v/v). After completion of the immobilization process, the nanofibers were separated from the reaction medium and washed thoroughly with buffer solution to remove unbound enzyme molecules and residual glutaraldehyde. Each immobilization experiment was performed independently under identical conditions to ensure reproducibility.

Determination of CAT activity

The catalytic activity of CAT was determined spectrophotometrically by monitoring the decomposition of H2O2 at 240 nm, based on the decrease in absorbance associated with H2O2 consumption. All activity measurements were carried out in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) at 25 °C. For free CAT, the reaction mixture consisted of 1.25 mL of 50 mM H2O2 solution prepared in phosphate buffer and 10 µL of CAT solution at an appropriate concentration. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 2 min, after which it was terminated by the addition of 250 µL of 1 M HCl. The absorbance of the resulting solution was then recorded at 240 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer.28 For immobilized CAT, the activity assay was performed under identical conditions, except that CAT solution was replaced by a known amount of CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofibers. After the reaction period, the nanofibers were removed from the reaction medium, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 240 nm. Blank experiments containing all reaction components except CAT were conducted inparallel to account for non-enzymatic decomposition of H2O2 and any potential interference arising from reagent addition. The absorbance values obtained from the blanks were subtracted from those of the test samples. One unit (U) of CAT activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to decompose 1 mmol of H2O2 per minute under the assay conditions.

Determination of protein content

The protein content before and after immobilization was quantitatively determined using the Bradford assay. Protein measurements were carried out spectrophotometrically at 595 nm according to the Bradford method.29 Bovine serum albumin was used as the standard protein for the construction of the calibration curve. The amount of immobilized protein was calculated based on the difference between the initial protein content and the residual protein detected in the supernatant after immobilization.

Characterization of enzymatic properties of immobilized CAT

The effect of temperature on the activity of free and immobilized CAT was investigated by performing activity assays at temperatures ranging from 20 °C to 60 °C under standard assay conditions. The temperature corresponding to the highest relative activity was considered as the optimum temperature. Thermal stability was evaluated by incubating free and immobilized CAT at different temperatures (4–70 °C) for 1 h in the absence of substrate. After incubation, the samples were cooled to room temperature, and the residual enzyme activity was determined using the standard CAT activity assay.

The influence of pH on the activity of free and immobilized CAT was examined using substrate solutions prepared in buffer systems covering a pH range of 3.0 to 10.0. Enzyme activity was measured under standard assay conditions, and the pH corresponding to the maximum activity was identified as the optimum pH. To assess pH stability, free and immobilized CAT were incubated in buffer solutions with pH values ranging from 4.0 to 9.0 for 1 h at room temperature. Following incubation, the residual activity of the enzymes was measured, and the remaining activity was expressed as a percentage of the initial activity.

The apparent kinetic behavior of free and immobilized CAT was evaluated by measuring enzyme activity at different H2O2 concentrations ranging from 2.5 to 50 mM. The apparent Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) and maximum reaction rate (Vmax) were determined by plotting the reciprocal of reaction velocity against the reciprocal of substrate concentration according to the Lineweaver–Burk method, based on the Michaelis–Menten equation.

The operational reusability of CAT immobilized on PVP/GA nanofibers was examined through repeated catalytic cycles. After each activity measurement, the immobilized enzyme was removed from the reaction medium, thoroughly washed with distilled water, and reused under identical assay conditions. To avoid potential activity loss due to exposure to the reaction-stopping reagent, the immobilized nanofibers were removed from the reaction mixture prior to termination of the reaction. Fresh substrate solution was subsequently added for each new cycle, and the activity was determined.

The storage stability of free and immobilized CAT was assessed by storing the enzyme samples at 4 °C for a period of 30 days. Enzyme activity was measured at predetermined time intervals, and the residual activity was calculated relative to the initial activity to evaluate stability during storage.

H2O2 removal by immobilized CAT

The practical applicability of CAT immobilized on PVP/GA nanofibers was evaluated through H2O2 removal experiments under optimized reaction conditions. All application studies were conducted using the optimum pH and temperature values determined for the immobilized enzyme, and the removal efficiency was calculated based on the decrease in H2O2 concentration after the reaction period. To investigate the effect of substrate concentration on the catalytic performance of immobilized CAT, H2O2 solutions with initial concentrations of 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, and 150 mM were prepared in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). A fixed amount of CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofibers was added to each solution, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min under optimal conditions. At the end of the reaction period, the immobilized nanofibers were removed from the reaction medium, and the remaining H2O2 concentration was determined. The removal efficiency was calculated as a percentage relative to the initial H2O2 concentration. The influence of reaction time on H2O2 degradation was evaluated using a fixed substrate concentration of 100 mM H2O2. The immobilized CAT was incubated with the substrate solution for different reaction times (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min) under optimal conditions. At the end of each time interval, the nanofibers were removed from the reaction mixture, and the residual H2O2 concentration was measured to determine the degradation efficiency as a function of time. The operational stability and reusability of immobilized CAT in H2O2 removal were assessed through repeated application cycles. For this purpose, 100 mM H2O2 was treated with CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofibers for a fixed reaction time of 25 min under optimal conditions. After each cycle, the immobilized nanofibers were removed from the reaction medium, thoroughly washed with distilled water, and reused for the subsequent cycle. This procedure was repeated for multiple cycles, and the H2O2 removal efficiency was determined after each run to evaluate the retained catalytic performance of the immobilized enzyme. Control experiments were performed under identical reaction conditions in the absence of immobilized CAT to evaluate the possible abiotic degradation of H2O2. The H2O2 removal efficiencies reported in this study were corrected by subtracting the background H2O2 loss observed in the control experiments.

Results and discussion

Optimization of electrospinning parameters for PVP/GA nanofibers

The morphology and structural integrity of electrospun nanofibers are strongly influenced by the selected processing parameters. In this study, PVP/GA nanofibers were fabricated via electrospinning and utilized as support matrices for CAT immobilization. Achieving uniform, bead-free fibers with sufficient mechanical stability was essential to ensure effective enzyme attachment and subsequent catalytic performance. The electrospinning solution was optimized to contain 8% (w/v) PVP and 1% (w/v) GA, which provided adequate polymer chain entanglement and suitable viscosity for stable jet formation. Lower polymer concentrations resulted in bead formation and discontinuous fibers, while higher concentrations increased solution viscosity and led to irregular fiber deposition. The electrospinning process was carried out using an applied voltage of 13 kV, a solution flow rate of 1.0 mL h−1, and a tip-to-collector distance of 15 cm. These parameters enabled stable Taylor cone formation and continuous fiber generation without droplet formation or jet instability. The selected flow rate ensured controlled polymer supply, preventing bead defects, while the optimized voltage provided sufficient electrostatic force for uniform jet stretching. The chosen tip-to-collector distance allowed adequate solvent evaporation before fiber deposition. Under these optimized conditions, smooth, continuous, and bead-free PVP/GA nanofibers were obtained. The resulting nanofibers exhibited sufficient structural robustness to withstand subsequent cross-linking and enzyme immobilization processes. These findings highlight the importance of carefully tuning electrospinning parameters to obtain nanostructured supports suitable for enzyme immobilization applications.

Although the operational temperature range of CAT is considerably lower than the decomposition temperatures observed for the PVP/GA nanofibers, thermal characterization of the support material remains important for evaluating its structural integrity during processing, storage, sterilization, and potential industrial handling. The TGA and DSC analyses primarily confirm that the nanofiber matrix possesses sufficient thermal robustness and does not undergo significant structural degradation within the temperature range relevant to enzyme immobilization and application. Therefore, these analyses were intended to characterize the physicochemical stability of the support material rather than to define the operational temperature limits of the immobilized enzyme system.

Optimization studies of immobilization of CAT on PVP/GA

The amount of CAT used during immobilization is a key factor influencing the enzyme loading capacity and catalytic performance of the PVP/GA nanofiber support. The effects of CAT concentration on the specific activity and immobilization efficiency of the immobilized enzyme are presented in Fig. 4a, while the complete numerical data are provided in Table S1. Varying the CAT concentration between 0.5 and 2.5 mg mL−1 led to noticeable differences in enzyme activity, specific activity, and immobilization efficiency. The highest immobilization efficiency (65.1%) and specific activity (319 U per mg protein) were obtained at a CAT concentration of 1.5 mg mL−1, where the activity of immobilized CAT reached 143 U. At lower enzyme concentrations, both the immobilization efficiency and catalytic activity were significantly reduced, most likely due to the limited availability of enzyme molecules for effective interaction with the functional groups on the nanofiber surface. For example, at 0.5 mg mL−1, the immobilization efficiency decreased to 41.2%, while the measured activity was 102 U, indicating incomplete utilization of the available binding sites on the nanofiber matrix. Further increases in CAT concentration beyond 1.5 mg mL−1 did not enhance immobilization performance. Instead, both enzyme activity and immobilization efficiency gradually declined, reaching 53.4% and 45.2%, respectively, at higher concentrations. This decrease can be attributed to possible enzyme aggregation at elevated concentrations, which may hinder effective cross-linking and reduce the accessibility of active sites. In addition, excessive enzyme loading may lead to surface saturation of the nanofiber support, resulting in steric hindrance and weaker or non-specific interactions between the enzyme and the support material.

To better understand the influence of enzyme concentration on immobilization performance, the obtained results were compared with previously reported studies. The optimal CAT concentration has been shown to vary considerably depending on the physicochemical characteristics of the support material. For example, CAT immobilized on p(HEMA-CTS)-Cu nanospheres exhibited its highest activity at an enzyme concentration of 3.0 mg mL−1.47 In another study, the optimal concentration for CAT immobilization on an eggshell membrane was reported to be 1.0 mg mL−1.48 Such variations are mainly attributed to differences in surface chemistry, available binding sites, and the overall enzyme support interaction mechanisms of the carrier materials. In the present study, the optimal CAT concentration was determined as 1.5 mg mL−1, which is consistent with the concentration range reported for many immobilized CAT systems.

The amount of nanofiber support plays a critical role in determining the efficiency and capacity of enzyme immobilization. In this study, the amount of PVP/GA nanofibers used for CAT immobilization was varied between 10 and 30 mg, and its effect on enzyme activity and immobilization efficiency was evaluated (Fig. 4b). As the amount of nanofiber increased from 10 mg to 25 mg, a significant improvement in enzyme activity and immobilization efficiency was observed. The highest catalytic activity (168 U) and immobilization efficiency (70.4%) were obtained when 25 mg of nanofibers were used, along with the maximum specific activity of 345 U per mg protein. This enhancement can be attributed to the increased surface area and availability of functional groups provided by the larger nanofiber mass, which facilitates more effective enzyme attachment and stabilization. At lower support amounts, particularly 10 mg, the immobilization efficiency decreased to 52.8%, likely due to the limited surface area and insufficient binding sites available for enzyme molecules. As a result, incomplete enzyme immobilization and reduced catalytic activity were observed. Although increasing the nanofiber amount from 10 to 25 mg produced a positive trend in immobilization performance, further increasing the support amount to 30 mg did not lead to additional improvement. Instead, a slight decrease in enzyme activity (151 U) and immobilization efficiency (66.9%) was observed.

Cross-linking time is an important parameter in enzyme immobilization processes, particularly when glutaraldehyde is employed as a cross-linking agent to establish covalent bonds between the enzyme and the nanofiber surface. In this study, cross-linking durations ranging from 30 to 150 min were investigated to determine the optimal exposure time for CAT immobilization onto PVP/GA nanofibers (Fig. 4c). The results revealed that enzyme activity and immobilization efficiency increased with increasing cross-linking time up to a certain point. The highest immobilized activity (186 U), specific activity (376 U per mg protein), and immobilization efficiency (76.7%) were obtained at a cross-linking time of 90 min, indicating this duration as the optimal condition for effective enzyme attachment and stabilization. Shorter cross-linking times, such as 30 and 60 min, resulted in comparatively lower immobilization efficiencies (69.1% and 71.8%, respectively). These reduced values may be attributed to insufficient formation of covalent bonds between the enzyme molecules and the support material, which can lead to incomplete immobilization and potential enzyme leaching during washing or catalytic reactions. In contrast, extending the cross-linking time beyond 90 min did not further improve immobilization performance. At 120 and 150 min, a slight decrease in both enzyme activity and immobilization efficiency was observed. This decline may be associated with excessive cross-linking, which can induce structural rigidity or conformational alterations in the enzyme. Such modifications may restrict enzyme flexibility and hinder substrate accessibility to the active site, ultimately leading to reduced catalytic performance.

A comparable tendency has also been documented in previous studies. For example, in a study examining CAT immobilization on hyaluronic acid/chitosan and hyaluronic acid/chitin carriers, immobilization experiments conducted over a time interval of 2–10 h showed that the specific activity increased progressively up to 6 h, after which a decline in activity was observed. Consequently, the optimal immobilization time for both carrier systems was reported as 6 h.49 These findings suggest that sufficient cross-linking time is necessary to achieve stable enzyme attachment, whereas excessively prolonged exposure may lead to structural alterations that negatively affect enzymatic activity.

Glutaraldehyde concentration is a critical parameter in enzyme immobilization processes, as it directly affects the degree of cross-linking between enzyme molecules and the nanofiber support. To evaluate this effect, glutaraldehyde concentrations ranging from 1% to 5% were investigated (Fig. 4d). The results indicated that increasing the glutaraldehyde concentration enhanced immobilization efficiency and enzyme activity up to an optimal level. The highest immobilized activity (196 U) and specific activity (397 U per mg protein) were obtained at a glutaraldehyde concentration of 4%, with the maximum immobilization efficiency reaching 81.2%. These results indicate that 4% glutaraldehyde provides sufficient aldehyde groups to promote effective covalent bonding between the enzyme and the nanofiber matrix, leading to improved immobilization performance. At lower glutaraldehyde concentrations (1–2%), immobilization efficiencies ranged between 72.2% and 73.8%, suggesting that cross-linking was initiated but not fully optimized due to the limited availability of reactive aldehyde groups. Although moderate immobilization efficiencies were achieved under these conditions, the resulting enzyme activity remained below the optimal level, which may be attributed to partial enzyme attachment or weaker enzyme–support interactions. When the glutaraldehyde concentration was further increased to 5%, a decline in both enzyme activity (159 U) and immobilization efficiency (69.8%) was observed. This decrease may be attributed to excessive cross-linking, which can lead to structural rigidification of the enzyme or the formation of inter and intramolecular cross-links. Such structural constraints may limit the conformational flexibility of the enzyme and hinder substrate access to the active site, thereby reducing catalytic performance. Based on these findings, a glutaraldehyde concentration of 4% was identified as the optimal condition for CAT immobilization on PVP/GA nanofibers, providing strong enzyme support interactions while preserving enzymatic activity.

Comparable observations have been reported in previous studies. For instance, CAT immobilization on hyaluronic acid/chitosan and hyaluronic acid/chitin carriers reported an optimal glutaraldehyde concentration of 100 mM within a tested range of 50–150 mM.49 Similarly, another study involving CAT immobilized on an eggshell membrane exhibited an optimal glutaraldehyde concentration of 4%, while higher concentrations resulted in a decline in enzymatic activity due to excessive cross-linking that adversely affected enzyme structure.48 These findings highlight that the optimal cross-linker concentration strongly depends on the physicochemical characteristics of the support material and the immobilization strategy employed.

Effect of temperature on CAT activity

Temperature is one of the most important factors influencing enzyme activity, as it affects both enzyme conformation and the kinetic energy of substrate molecules. The relative activities of free and immobilized CAT were evaluated over a temperature range of 20–60 °C, and the results are presented in Fig. 6a. As illustrated in the figure, the optimal temperature for free CAT was determined to be 35 °C, at which the enzyme exhibited 100% relative activity. However, beyond this temperature, a sharp decrease in activity was observed. At 60 °C, free CAT retained only 30% of its initial activity, indicating a high susceptibility of the native enzyme to thermal denaturation. This rapid loss of activity can be attributed to structural destabilization of the enzyme at elevated temperatures, which may involve partial unfolding of its tertiary structure and consequent loss of catalytic functionality. In contrast, immobilized CAT displayed a broader temperature tolerance and maintained 100% relative activity within the range of 35–45 °C. Even at 50 °C, the immobilized enzyme preserved 83% of its initial activity, and 56% at 60 °C, demonstrating a considerable enhancement in thermal resistance compared with the free enzyme. The improved thermal behavior of immobilized CAT can be attributed to the protective microenvironment provided by the PVP/GA nanofiber matrix, which restricts conformational mobility and helps maintain the structural integrity of the enzyme. Additionally, covalent cross-linking through glutaraldehyde may introduce structural rigidity, thereby reducing the likelihood of thermal unfolding and deactivation. The ability of immobilized CAT to retain higher catalytic activity at elevated temperatures highlights the stabilizing effect of the nanofiber support and suggests its potential applicability in industrial processes, where operational conditions often involve elevated or fluctuating temperatures. Overall, the immobilization of CAT onto PVP/GA nanofibers significantly enhanced the thermal tolerance of the enzyme, resulting in a broader operational temperature range and improved catalytic stability.

The temperature profiles (a) and thermal stabilities profiles (b) of free CAT and CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofiber.

Comparable improvements in temperature tolerance following enzyme immobilization have been reported in earlier studies. For example, CAT immobilized on chitin, chitosan, hyaluronic acid, hyaluronic acid/chitin, and hyaluronic acid/chitosan carriers exhibited enhanced thermal behavior compared with the free enzyme. While the optimum temperature for free CAT was reported as 30 °C, the immobilized forms demonstrated improved thermal stability, with the hyaluronic acid/chitosan carrier showing an optimum temperature of 40 °C, whereas the other carrier systems displayed maximum activity at approximately 35 °C.49 Similarly, CAT immobilized on hollow silica nanospheres showed a shift in optimum temperature from 30 °C for the free enzyme to 40 °C after immobilization, indicating improved thermal resistance due to the stabilizing effect of the support material.54 The differences in optimum temperature reported for immobilized CAT systems are largely dependent on the physicochemical characteristics of the support material and the nature of enzyme–support interactions. Factors such as hydrophilicity, surface functionality, matrix rigidity, and the degree of covalent attachment can influence the conformational flexibility of the enzyme and its resistance to thermal denaturation. In the present study, the hydrophilic nature of PVP together with the functional groups provided by GA may contribute to the formation of a favorable microenvironment around the enzyme, thereby enhancing its thermal tolerance over a broader temperature range.

Thermal stability is a crucial property of enzymes, particularly for industrial applications where catalytic processes frequently occur at elevated temperatures. In the present study, the thermal stability of free and immobilized CAT was evaluated by incubating both enzyme forms at temperatures ranging from 4 to 70 °C, followed by the determination of their residual activities (Fig. 6b). At lower temperatures, both free and immobilized CAT retained relatively high levels of activity, with the maximum observed at 35 °C for both enzyme forms. However, notable differences in stability became evident as the incubation temperature increased beyond this point. Free CAT exhibited a rapid decline in residual activity, retaining only 63% of its initial activity at 45 °C, which further decreased to 26% at 60 °C and 12% at 70 °C. In contrast, the immobilized enzyme demonstrated substantially higher thermal stability across the same temperature range. Immobilized CAT maintained 94% of its activity at 45 °C, 76% at 60 °C, and 59% even at 70 °C, clearly indicating enhanced resistance to thermal inactivation compared with the free enzyme. The improved thermal stability observed after immobilization can be attributed to the stabilizing effect of the PVP/GA nanofiber matrix. Covalent attachment of the enzyme to the support likely restricts conformational mobility, thereby helping preserve the native structure of the enzyme under thermal stress. In addition, the microenvironment provided by the nanofiber network may reduce the probability of thermal unfolding and subsequent deactivation. Overall, these findings demonstrate that immobilization of CAT onto PVP/GA nanofibers significantly enhances its thermal stability, allowing the enzyme to maintain higher catalytic activity at elevated temperatures.

Comparable improvements in thermal stability following enzyme immobilization have also been reported in previous studies. For example, CAT immobilized on CaCO3 microspheres demonstrated significantly greater thermal resistance than the free enzyme across the temperature range of 50–70 °C. At 70 °C, the free enzyme lost nearly 90% of its catalytic activity, whereas the immobilized form was able to retain approximately 40% of its activity.55 These findings further support the general observation that immobilization can effectively enhance enzyme stability under thermal stress by providing structural support and restricting conformational changes. Compared with previously reported CAT immobilization systems, the PVP/GA nanofiber platform provided comparable or superior thermal stability, suggesting that the hybrid nanofibrous structure effectively preserves enzyme conformation under thermal stress.

Effect of pH on CAT activity

The catalytic performance of enzymes is highly sensitive to pH, as variations in proton concentration can influence the ionization state of amino acid residues involved in substrate binding and catalytic activity. The relative activity profiles of free and immobilized CAT within a pH range of 3.0–9.5 are presented in Fig. 7a. The optimal pH for free CAT was determined to be pH 7.0, at which the enzyme exhibited 100% relative activity. However, the free enzyme displayed a relatively narrow pH tolerance range, with a pronounced decrease in activity under both acidic and alkaline conditions. For instance, at pH 5.0 and pH 9.0, the enzyme retained only 29% and 26% of its maximum activity, respectively. This sharp decline indicates that free CAT is highly susceptible to structural alterations or partial denaturation when exposed to pH values outside its optimal range. In contrast, immobilized CAT exhibited a broader and more stable pH activity profile, maintaining approximately 85% relative activity between pH 6.0 and 8.0. The maximum activity was observed at pH 7.5, indicating a slight shift in the optimal pH toward more alkaline conditions after immobilization. Even at pH 5.0 and pH 9.0, the immobilized enzyme retained 56% and 62% of its activity, respectively more than double that of the free enzyme under the same conditions. The improved pH tolerance of immobilized CAT can be attributed to the protective microenvironment created by the PVP/GA nanofiber matrix, which may buffer local pH fluctuations and limit conformational changes that could lead to enzyme inactivation. Moreover, the covalent attachment of the enzyme to the support likely contributes to preserving the structural integrity of the active site across a wider pH range. These findings demonstrate that immobilization not only slightly shifted the optimal pH of CAT toward alkaline conditions but also significantly enhanced its operational stability across a broader pH spectrum.

The pH profiles (a) and pH stabilities (b) of free CAT and CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofiber.

Comparable observations regarding the influence of immobilization on the pH behavior of CAT have been reported in previous studies. For instance, in a study examining CAT immobilization on chitosan beads and chitosan–bentonite beads, the optimal pH of the enzyme shifted from 7.5 for the free form to pH 8 after immobilization.56 In addition, CAT immobilized on PCL/PEI–Cu2+ nanofibers retained relatively high activity over a wide pH range between pH 5.0 and 8.5, whereas the free enzyme displayed a narrower pH stability range.57 Such observations indicate that immobilization onto nanostructured supports can significantly improve enzyme tolerance to pH variations by providing a protective microenvironment around the catalytic structure.

The stability of enzymes under varying pH conditions is a crucial factor for their practical application, particularly in processes where fluctuations in pH are common. To assess the pH stability of CAT, both free and immobilized forms of the enzyme were incubated in buffer solutions with pH values ranging from 4.0 to 9.0, and their residual activities were subsequently determined (Fig. 7b). The results clearly demonstrate that immobilization significantly enhanced the pH stability of CAT. Although both free and immobilized enzymes exhibited their highest stability near neutral pH, the immobilized enzyme maintained substantially higher relative activity across the entire tested pH range. For instance, after incubation at pH 4.0, immobilized CAT retained 52% of its initial activity, whereas the free enzyme preserved only 35%. Similarly, at pH 9.0, immobilized CAT still retained 72% residual activity, while the activity of free CAT declined sharply to 39%. The enhanced stability observed for immobilized CAT can be attributed to the protective environment provided by the PVP/GA nanofiber matrix. Covalent attachment of the enzyme to the support likely restricts pH-induced conformational changes and helps maintain the structural integrity of the catalytic site. Furthermore, the immobilization matrix may buffer localized pH fluctuations around the enzyme molecules, thereby reducing the risk of denaturation under both acidic and alkaline conditions. A particularly notable observation was that the immobilized enzyme preserved high catalytic activity after exposure to suboptimal pH conditions. Within the pH range of 6.0–8.0, immobilized CAT maintained more than 80% of its initial activity, whereas the free enzyme exhibited a much narrower stability window and showed a rapid decline in activity outside its optimal pH range. The broader pH tolerance observed in the present study further highlights the ability of the PVP/GA nanofiber matrix to provide a favorable microenvironment for CAT, supporting its potential use under variable operational conditions.

Storage stability and reusability proporties

Storage stability is an important parameter for evaluating the long-term usability of enzymatic systems, particularly in industrial and biomedical applications where extended shelf-life is required. In the present study, the relative activities of free and immobilized CAT were monitored over a 30 days storage period at 4 °C to evaluate their residual activity during storage (Fig. 8a). The results clearly demonstrate that immobilization significantly improved the storage stability of CAT. Both enzyme forms initially exhibited 100% activity; however, their stability profiles diverged considerably during storage. The free enzyme showed a rapid decline in activity, retaining 82% of its initial activity after 3 days, which further decreased to 44% by day 15, and ultimately dropped to only 12% after 30 days. This substantial loss of activity likely reflects the intrinsic instability of the enzyme in solution, where it is more susceptible to structural denaturation, aggregation, or autolytic degradation over time. In contrast, immobilized CAT retained significantly higher activity throughout the entire storage period. After 15 days, the immobilized enzyme still preserved 76% of its initial activity, and even after 30 days, it maintained 52% activity, which is more than four times higher than that of the free enzyme at the same time point. The enhanced storage stability of immobilized CAT can be attributed to the protective microenvironment provided by the PVP/GA nanofiber matrix. Covalent attachment of the enzyme to the support likely restricts conformational mobility and helps maintain the structural integrity of the catalytic site. Additionally, immobilization may prevent enzyme aggregation and reduce susceptibility to structural degradation during prolonged storage. Overall, these results demonstrate that CAT immobilized on PVP/GA nanofibers exhibits significantly improved storage stability compared with the free enzyme.

The storage stability of free CAT and CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofiber (a) and reusability of CAT immobilized PVP/GA nanofiber (b).

Comparable improvements in storage stability after enzyme immobilization have also been reported in the literature. For example, CAT immobilized on CaCO3 microspheres retained approximately 90% of its activity after 14 days of storage, whereas the free enzyme lost nearly all of its activity over the same period.55 Similarly CAT chitosan exhibited enhanced storage stability, preserving about 70% of its initial activity after 15 days, while the free enzyme lost nearly 80% of its activity under identical storage conditions.58 In another study, CAT immobilized on hollow silica nanospheres maintained 64% of its activity after 10 days of storage, whereas the free enzyme completely lost its activity during the same storage period.54 These results collectively demonstrate that enzyme immobilization can significantly enhance storage stability by minimizing structural denaturation and preserving catalytic activity over time.

One of the primary advantages of enzyme immobilization is the possibility of reusing the biocatalyst in multiple reaction cycles while maintaining catalytic activity. The operational reusability of immobilized CAT was evaluated by performing successive catalytic cycles under standard assay conditions and determining the residual activity after each cycle (Fig. 8b). The immobilized enzyme retained 95% of its initial activity after the second cycle and 92% after the third cycle, indicating excellent operational stability during the initial reuse stages. Although a gradual decrease in activity was observed with increasing cycle numbers, the immobilized enzyme maintained over 80% of its activity up to the 8th cycle and more than 70% through the 11th cycle. Even after 16 consecutive reaction cycles, the immobilized CAT preserved 44% of its original activity, demonstrating substantial long-term reusability. The gradual decline in activity during repeated cycles may be attributed to several factors, including partial enzyme desorption from the support surface, structural fatigue resulting from repeated reaction and washing steps, or minor structural alterations in the nanofiber matrix over time. Despite this progressive decrease, the retention of nearly half of the initial activity after multiple reuse cycles highlights the robustness of the immobilization strategy and the stabilizing effect of the PVP/GA nanofiber support. Such reusability is particularly advantageous for industrial and environmental applications, where enzyme recovery and repeated use play a critical role in reducing operational costs and improving process sustainability. Overall, the results confirm that CAT immobilized on PVP/GA nanofibers exhibits high operational stability and represents a promising system for repeated catalytic applications.

Comparable reusability behavior has been reported for CAT immobilized on different support materials. For example, CAT immobilized on gelatin alginate hydrogel scaffolds maintained approximately 52% of its original activity after 20 consecutive reaction cycles, demonstrating relatively high operational stability.55 In another study, CAT immobilized on chitosan/ZnO/Fe2O3 nanocomposites retained 45% of its initial activity after 10 catalytic cycles, whereas the system based on chitosan/ZnO preserved only 14% of its activity under the same conditions.59 Similarly, in a nanofiber-based immobilization system, CAT immobilized on PCL/PEI–Cu2+ nanofibers exhibited a gradual decline in activity during repeated use, with the residual activity dropping below 50% after the sixth catalytic cycle.57 These observations indicate that the operational stability and reusability of immobilized CAT strongly depend on the nature of the carrier material, enzyme–support interactions, and the immobilization strategy employed. The combination of enhanced storage stability and prolonged operational reusability demonstrates the practical potential of the developed immobilization platform and supports its applicability in repeated catalytic processes where enzyme recovery and reuse are essential.

Although the reusability performance observed in the present study is comparable to that reported for several previously developed CAT immobilization systems, the significance of this work lies in the overall combination of properties achieved using the electrospun PVP/GA platform. In addition to maintaining catalytic activity over repeated cycles, the immobilized enzyme exhibited high immobilization efficiency, enhanced thermal and pH stability, improved storage stability, and effective H2O2 degradation performance. These results demonstrate that the PVP/GA nanofibrous matrix provides a balanced immobilization platform capable of simultaneously improving multiple operational characteristics that are important for practical biocatalytic applications.

Kinetic parameters of free and immobilized CAT

The kinetic behavior of free and immobilized CAT was investigated by determining the Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) and the maximum reaction rate (Vmax) using H2O2 as the substrate. These parameters provide valuable information regarding the enzyme's substrate affinity and catalytic efficiency under substrate-saturated conditions. For free CAT, the Km value was calculated as 22.52 mM, indicating a relatively high affinity toward H2O2. The corresponding Vmax was determined to be 12.26 µmol H2O2 per min, representing the maximum catalytic rate achieved under the assay conditions. In contrast, immobilized CAT exhibited a higher Km value of 47.18 mM, suggesting a decrease in substrate affinity compared with the free enzyme. Such an increase in Km is frequently observed in immobilized enzyme systems and is generally attributed to mass transfer limitations or diffusion barriers that restrict substrate access to the enzyme's active sites. In the present case, the nanofiber matrix may partially hinder substrate diffusion or create spatial constraints around the immobilized enzyme molecules. In addition to diffusional limitations, the observed increase in Km may also be associated with the covalent immobilization mechanism. CAT contains several surface-exposed lysine residues that can participate in glutaraldehyde-mediated coupling reactions. Although these residues are generally located on the outer surface of the enzyme rather than directly within the catalytic heme-containing active site, covalent attachment may impose local conformational constraints or influence the orientation of enzyme molecules on the nanofiber surface. As a consequence, substrate accessibility to the active site may be partially reduced, contributing to the increase in the apparent Km value. Nevertheless, the substantial catalytic activity retained after immobilization and the increase in Vmax suggest that the immobilization procedure did not significantly disrupt the catalytic machinery of CAT and that the enzyme remained in a catalytically favorable conformation. Despite the reduced substrate affinity, the immobilized enzyme displayed a significantly higher Vmax value of 21.87 µmol H2O2 per min, indicating an enhanced maximum catalytic capacity. This increase in Vmax may be associated with the improved structural stability of the enzyme after immobilization, which can reduce conformational fluctuations and maintain the enzyme in a catalytically favorable state during the reaction. Furthermore, the localized concentration of enzyme molecules on the nanofiber surface may facilitate more efficient catalytic turnover under substrate-saturated conditions.

Comparable effects of immobilization on the kinetic parameters of CAT have been reported in previous studies. For instance, CAT immobilized on iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles displayed a notable increase in the Km value from 30.5 mM for the free enzyme to 69.7 mM for the immobilized form, while the Vmax decreased from 2124 µmol mL−1 to 872 µmol mL−1. This behavior was attributed to diffusional limitations and steric hindrance arising from the immobilization process, which restricted substrate accessibility to the enzyme's active site.60 In contrast, a different trend was observed in a study where CAT was immobilized on hyaluronic acid/chitosan carriers. In that system, the Km value of the immobilized enzyme (3.10 mM) was slightly lower than that of the free enzyme (3.18 mM), suggesting a minor improvement in substrate affinity. Additionally, the Vmax value increased from 5.32 U mg−1 to 6.68 U mg−1 of sample, indicating enhanced catalytic efficiency following immobilization.49 These examples highlight that the impact of immobilization on enzyme kinetics largely depends on the characteristics of the support material and the immobilization strategy employed. In the present study, the simultaneous increase in both Vmax and Km suggests that although substrate diffusion to the active sites may be partially restricted, immobilization of CAT onto PVP/GA nanofibers provides a stabilizing microenvironment that enhances catalytic turnover and preserves the enzyme in a catalytically favorable conformation.

Application study: H2O2 removal efficiency by immobilized CAT

To evaluate the practical applicability of the immobilized CAT, its ability to remove H2O2 from aqueous solutions was investigated under the previously determined optimal pH and temperature conditions. Different substrate concentrations ranging from 25 to 150 mM were tested, and the enzymatic reaction was allowed to proceed for 30 min. The removal efficiency of H2O2 was subsequently calculated, and the results are presented in Fig. 9a. Control experiments conducted in the absence of immobilized CAT showed negligible abiotic H2O2 degradation under the applied reaction conditions. Therefore, the observed decrease in H2O2 concentration can be primarily attributed to the catalytic activity of immobilized CAT. The removal efficiencies reported in Fig. 8 and 9 were corrected for the background degradation determined from the corresponding control experiments.

Effect of H2O2 amount (a) and reaction time (b) on the removal efficiency of H2O2.

The obtained results showed that immobilized CAT was able to achieve complete removal of H2O2 at concentrations up to 100 mM, demonstrating excellent catalytic performance under moderate substrate loading conditions. However, when the substrate concentration exceeded this level, a gradual decrease in removal efficiency was observed. Specifically, the removal efficiency decreased to 86% at 125 mM and further to 72% at 150 mM. The decline in removal efficiency at higher substrate concentrations may be attributed to substrate saturation of the enzyme active sites as well as possible oxidative inactivation caused by excessive H2O2 levels. Although CAT is highly efficient in decomposing H2O2 into water and oxygen, exposure to elevated peroxide concentrations may promote the formation of reactive intermediates that can partially inhibit enzymatic activity. Despite this decrease at higher substrate levels, the immobilized enzyme maintained high catalytic performance across a wide concentration range, indicating its strong potential for practical applications such as bioremediation or industrial detoxification of hydrogen peroxide-containing effluents. The ability to achieve complete removal of H2O2 at concentrations up to 100 mM highlights the suitability of the immobilized CAT system for treating environments where relatively high peroxide levels may be encountered, including textile bleaching effluents and disinfection-related wastewater streams.

To investigate the time-dependent catalytic performance of immobilized CAT, the degradation of 100 mM H2O2 was monitored at different reaction times ranging from 5 to 30 minutes under the previously determined optimal pH and temperature conditions (Fig. 9b). The results revealed a clear time-dependent increase in H2O2 removal efficiency. The degradation efficiency increased from 25% after 5 min to 86% after 20 min, eventually reaching complete removal at 25 min, which remained unchanged up to 30 min. This progressive increase in removal efficiency reflects the continuous catalytic activity of the immobilized enzyme, which effectively decomposes H2O2 over time without noticeable loss of activity. The achievement of full degradation within 25 min highlights the high catalytic efficiency and stability of the immobilized CAT under the applied reaction conditions. Moreover, the plateau observed after 25 min indicates that the substrate was completely converted within this period. The persistence of 100% removal at 30 min further suggests that the immobilized enzyme remained catalytically active even after substrate depletion. These findings demonstrate that the immobilized CAT system is capable of rapid and efficient H2O2 degradation, highlighting its potential applicability in time-sensitive environmental processes, such as wastewater treatment or on-site peroxide detoxification.

To further evaluate the operational durability of immobilized CAT under practical application conditions, the reusability of the enzyme system was investigated through repeated cycles of 100 mM H2O2 removal under the previously optimized conditions, with a fixed reaction time of 25 min per cycle (Fig. 10). The immobilized enzyme exhibited 100% removal efficiency during the initial cycle, demonstrating the full catalytic capacity of the freshly prepared immobilized system. In subsequent cycles, a gradual decrease in removal efficiency was observed. After the third reuse, the immobilized enzyme still retained 86% of its initial removal efficiency, and 73% after the fifth cycle, indicating relatively high catalytic stability during the early stages of reuse. However, continued operation resulted in further reductions in catalytic performance, with the removal efficiency decreasing to 54% by the eighth cycle and 42% by the ninth cycle. The observed decline in catalytic performance during repeated use may be attributed to several factors, including partial enzyme leaching, gradual enzyme deactivation, or structural fatigue of the immobilized enzyme system during repeated reaction and washing steps. In addition, the possible accumulation of residual H2O2 or reaction by-products on the nanofiber surface may partially hinder substrate diffusion and enzyme accessibility over time. Despite the gradual decrease in activity, the immobilized CAT retained over 40% removal efficiency even after nine consecutive cycles, demonstrating a considerable degree of operational stability. These results indicate that the immobilized enzyme system possesses sufficient durability for repeated catalytic applications, highlighting its potential use in batch-wise treatment processes or wastewater detoxification systems, where enzyme reusability plays a critical role in improving process sustainability and cost-effectiveness. The ability to achieve complete degradation of H2O2 at concentrations up to 100 mM within a short reaction time, while maintaining catalytic performance during repeated cycles, demonstrates the practical applicability of the developed biocatalytic system and highlights its potential for peroxide detoxification processes.

The operational durability of immobilized CAT under practical application conditions.

The H2O2 degradation performance obtained in the present study compares favorably with previously reported immobilized CAT systems. For example, CAT immobilized on glutaraldehyde-activated chitosan achieved more than 96% removal of H2O2 at concentrations up to 24 mM after 2 h of treatment, whereas the present PVP/GA-supported CAT system achieved complete degradation of 100 mM H2O2 within 25 min.58 Similarly, CAT immobilized on PAES-C carriers operated in a continuous-flow reactor exhibited approximately 90% H2O2 conversion after 1 h, while electrospun PVA/PA6–Cu(ii)-based enzyme membrane reactors reported conversion efficiencies between 87% and 92% under continuous operation.62 In addition, chitosan-based immobilized CAT systems have been successfully applied for continuous peroxide removal over extended operating periods, demonstrating the practical applicability of immobilized CAT platforms for wastewater treatment.63 Although direct comparisons should be made cautiously due to differences in reactor configurations, substrate concentrations, enzyme loadings, and operational conditions, these studies collectively support the effectiveness of immobilized CAT systems for peroxide detoxification. The results obtained in the present study further demonstrate that electrospun PVP/GA nanofibers can serve as an efficient support for CAT immobilization, enabling rapid H2O2 degradation at relatively high peroxide loadings while maintaining operational stability and reusability.

Although the present study demonstrated effective H2O2 removal under controlled laboratory conditions, real industrial effluents may contain additional components such as metal ions, surfactants, dyes, and various organic contaminants that could influence enzyme activity and operational stability. Previous studies have shown that certain wastewater constituents may inhibit CAT activity through active–site interactions, oxidative stress, or fouling of the immobilization matrix.64,65 Therefore, while the developed immobilized CAT system exhibited promising performance under model conditions, further investigations using real wastewater matrices will be necessary to evaluate its robustness, resistance to potential inhibitors, and practical applicability under complex environmental conditions.

Conclusion

CAT was successfully immobilized onto electrospun PVP/GA nanofibers through glutaraldehyde-mediated covalent cross-linking, and the catalytic performance and stability of the immobilized system were comprehensively investigated. The immobilization parameters were systematically optimized, and the optimal conditions were determined as 1.5 mg per mL CAT concentration, 25 mg nanofiber amount, 90 min cross-linking time, and 4% glutaraldehyde concentration, yielding high immobilization efficiency and catalytic activity. The immobilized CAT exhibited enhanced biochemical properties compared to the free enzyme. In particular, the immobilized enzyme demonstrated improved thermal stability, broader pH tolerance, enhanced pH stability, and superior storage stability. Kinetic analysis indicated an increase in both Km and Vmax values after immobilization, suggesting partial diffusion limitations but improved catalytic capacity and structural stabilization of the enzyme. Additionally, the immobilized CAT retained significant activity over multiple reuse cycles, highlighting the operational robustness of the developed nanofiber-based biocatalytic system. Application experiments further confirmed the practical potential of the immobilized enzyme. The system achieved complete degradation of H2O2 at concentrations up to 100 mM within a short reaction time and maintained considerable removal efficiency during repeated operational cycles. These findings demonstrate that PVP/GA nanofiber-supported CAT represents an efficient and reusable biocatalyst with promising potential for environmental and industrial applications, particularly for the detoxification of H2O2 in wastewater treatment and related oxidative processes.
