Comparative Analysis of Regularised Logistic Regression and Random Forest Models for In-hospital or 30-day Post-Discharge Mortality Prediction within the Hospital Standardised Mortality Ratio Framework
Authors: Mohd Kamarulariffin Kamarudin, Sarbhan Singh Lakha Singh, Nuur Hafizah Md Iderus, Sumarni Mohd Ghazali, Lonny Chen Rong Qi Ahmad, Nur’ain Mohd Ghazali, Mohamad Nadzmi Md Nadzri, Asrul Anuar Zulkifli, Mei Cheng Lim, Chien Huey Teh, Mohd Azahadi Omar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe hospital standardised mortality ratio (HSMR) is the ratio of the observed number of hospital deaths to the expected number of deaths, with the latter estimated using statistical models that adjust for available case-mix factors. This study aimed to develop and validate in-hospital or 30-day post-discharge mortality prediction models for 40 diagnosis groups within the HSMR framework, using penalized logistic regression (pLR) and random forest (RF), and to compare the performance of these two approaches.MethodsWe analysed 1,144,890 hospital admissions from 14 Malaysian state hospitals between 2012 and 2016. Separate models were developed for each diagnosis group using nine administrative features, including age, comorbidities, and admission category. Model performance was evaluated using mean Brier scores and C-statistics across multiple bootstrapped datasets to obtain less biased performance estimates. Aggregate expected mortality counts were also compared with observed counts.ResultsThe overall observed mortality rate was 10.2%. The pLR models consistently showed better discrimination and calibration than the RF models, with lower Brier scores and higher C-statistics across the 40 diagnosis groups. On average, the C-statistic for pLR exceeded that for RF by 0.062. Although the RF model produced aggregate mortality predictions that were numerically closer to the observed counts, it showed high variance and poorer probabilistic calibration than pLR.ConclusionsThe pLR model tended to underestimate mortality more than RF but still demonstrated better calibration and discrimination, making it the preferable model for HSMR analysis in this dataset.

Introduction

Monitoring hospital outcomes is a central component of healthcare quality management. Statistical models can predict expected mortality counts across common causes of hospital admission for comparison with observed deaths, yielding the hospital standardised mortality ratio (HSMR), a numerical indicator of hospital mortality performance [1].

Mortality prediction models are often trained using administrative data. Although administrative data offer a practical and economical data source, their clinical utility has been questioned because nonclinical databases may contain missing values and may lack sufficient clinical detail [2]. Consequently, methodological debates have emerged regarding the selection of risk-adjustment variables and the most appropriate modelling approach [3,4].

Mortality prediction in health-related research has traditionally relied on logistic regression (LR) [5]. However, the assumption of linear relationships between predictors and outcomes on the log-odds scale may be restrictive when the underlying data-generating process is complex or nonlinear. Nonetheless, LR models without nonlinear transformations have been used to generate parsimonious predictive models for HSMR monitoring. Scotland departed from this approach in 2009 by adopting a decision tree model with predictive accuracy comparable to that of LR [6].

In this study, we developed and evaluated random forest (RF) models for 30-day mortality prediction after hospitalisation. We compared their performance with that of LR and L2-penalized logistic regression (pLR). RF can model complex nonlinear relationships and interactions that may not be captured by a linear model. Its flexibility may also help manage bias–variance trade-offs through model configuration and aggregation across trees.

Background of the Study Area: Malaysia

In Malaysia, the disease burden is increasingly driven by non-communicable diseases. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and pneumonia consistently rank among the leading causes of mortality. Between 2009 and 2010, for example, patients with CVD accounted for approximately 6.91% of all hospital admissions and 24.5% of deaths in Malaysian public hospitals [7].

Malaysia’s public healthcare system is predominantly government-funded. HSMR can help administrators detect special-cause variation when a hospital’s observed mortality substantially exceeds its predicted mortality. Such findings may prompt clinical review and quality-improvement activities, including protocol refinement and staffing adjustments during high-risk periods.

HSMR had not been formally measured or validated in the Malaysian public healthcare context before this study. Establishing baseline models is therefore a critical first step toward creating a standardised benchmark. Malaysia’s multiethnic population and distinct clinical case mix also support the need for localised mortality models.

Methods

Data Sources

This study used data from the Malaysian Health Data Warehouse (MyHDW) and the National Registration Department (JPN) death certificate database. The JPN database provided mortality-status information for patients who were discharged alive from hospital and died within 30 days after discharge. MyHDW data were collected through an online web application and included records from 145 public hospitals, comprising 41 major specialist hospitals and 14 state hospitals, as well as 2,863 primary care facilities and 262 private hospitals across Malaysia since 2011. The MyHDW includes outpatient, inpatient, and day-care encounters. The database contains diagnostic and procedural information, along with demographic variables such as age, ethnic group, and sex. MyHDW recorded the principal diagnosis or cause of death associated with each admission according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). These codes were recategorized into mutually exclusive and clinically homogeneous Summary Hospital-level Mortality Indicator (SHMI) categories [8]. Details of the My-HDW database have been reported elsewhere [9].

Study Population

The analysis included hospitalisation data from 14 state hospitals between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2016, across 40 diagnosis groups. These groups accounted for 80% of inhospital deaths in state hospitals. This period was selected because it provided a comprehensive and stable MyHDW dataset after national rollout. In addition, 2012 marked the start of several key Ministry of Health strategic initiatives, providing a baseline for evaluating hospital performance trends before subsequent healthcare reforms and the disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic [10].

Each hospitalisation record represented one admission to a single hospital, ending at discharge or transfer. For interhospital transfers, separate records were maintained for the sending and receiving hospitals. For example, a patient transferred from Hospital A to Hospital B and then to Hospital C would have three admission records. When patients were transferred, any in-hospital or 30 days post-discharge mortality from the final hospital was also attributed to the originating hospital.

Additional variables, including length of stay (LOS) and discharge status at the end of each episode of care, were extracted. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 1 month, were discharged within 24 hours after admission or more than 365 days after admission, or received care considered palliative. Cases with inconsistent data, including out-of-range values or missing data, were also excluded. Of 1,278,552 admissions across 14 hospitals, 1,144,890 hospitalisations, representing 89.5% of the dataset, were eligible after application of the exclusion criteria.

Design Matrix

The training dataset comprised hospitalisation data from 2012 to 2015. Nine input features were included: age, sex, LOS, ethnic group, admission category (emergency vs. non-emergency), admission frequency within the previous year, year of discharge, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and transfer-out status. The CCI score was calculated using a previously described method to ensure consistent assessment of comorbidity burden [11]. Variable selection was based on prior knowledge and evidence from relevant literature showing associations between these variables and hospital mortality [12,13]. Most variables were categorical, except for age, CCI score, and LOS, which were continuous. No data transformation was applied. The dependent variable was coded as 1 if the patient died during admission or within 30 days after discharge and as 0 otherwise.

Model Development and Hyperparameter Tuning

Mortality risk prediction models were prespecified and developed for the 40 selected diagnosis groups. Each model included all nine variables. RF models for 30-day mortality prediction were fitted using the “randomForest” package in R, with ntree, the number of trees, fixed at 1,000 and other parameters left at their default settings. Each decision tree was grown fully without pruning. The tuning parameter mtry, defined as the number of variables randomly sampled as candidates at each split, was selected through a grid search from 2 to 9 within a repeated k-fold cross-validation framework with 3 repeats and 10 folds. The grid search was optimised by maximising the F1-score.

The optimal penalty parameter, λ, for pLR was selected using the “pentrace” function in the “rms” package through a grid search from 0 to 5 for simple terms [14]. This grid search was conducted by optimising Hurvich and Tsai’s corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc). The LR and pLR models did not include interaction terms.

The tuning objectives were selected to align with the mathematical framework of each model. RF hyperparameters were optimised using the F1-score to balance sensitivity and precision, whereas pLR was optimised using AICc to obtain a parsimonious likelihood-based model fit. Although different tuning criteria were used, model comparisons were performed using independent standardised evaluation metrics, namely C-statistics and Brier scores, to support methodologically consistent comparison.

Model Generalisation Performance on the Held-out Test Dataset

To obtain less biased and more conservative estimates of model performance, 3,000 models were pretrained for RF, LR, and pLR using bootstrapped training datasets. Each pretrained model was then evaluated on a randomly generated test dataset. The test dataset was created by sampling 70% of the 2016 dataset without replacement while preserving the same distribution of positive and negative cases as in the original dataset. This approach provided temporal hold-out validation, allowing model generalisability to be assessed for their ability to generalise across different time periods. It also allowed detection of unusual changes in mortality that could affect the stability of model performance over time. C-statistics and Brier scores were calculated for each surrogate model and then averaged to provide the final performance estimates.

Model Fairness

Model fairness was evaluated to assess whether risk adjustment performed equitably across Malaysia’s major ethnic groups. For each diagnosis group, the dataset was stratified into three primary ethnic subgroups: Bumiputera, Chinese, and Indian. An LR model using the same input variables, excluding ethnicity, was then fitted so that any observed differences in model predictions between ethnic groups could not be attributed to ethnicity being explicitly included as a predictor. Mortality risk, expressed as a percentage, was calculated for a defined reference patient profile within each ethnic stratum, together with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Predictions were considered comparable when the 95% CIs overlapped across ethnic groups. This analysis was based on the assumption that, within a standardised HSMR framework, mortality predictions for the same reference patient should not differ substantially by ethnicity.

Expected Mortality Calculation

The final pLR and RF models were trained on the complete 2012–2015 dataset using the optimal hyperparameters identified during model tuning. Each model generated a predicted probability of death during hospitalisation or within 30 days after discharge. For RF, predicted probabilities were derived as the proportion of decision trees in the ensemble that classified an observation as a mortality event. These probabilities were extracted directly from the model without transformation or post hoc calibration. For pLR, predicted probabilities were calculated from the logistic function. Probabilities were then summed across all admissions for each of the 40 diagnosis groups within each hospital in 2016. For each diagnosis group, this cumulative probability represented the expected number of deaths in that hospital. The average disparity, average Edelta, between observed mortality (O) and predicted mortality (E) across diagnosis groups was calculated for both RF and pLR using the following formula: average Edelta=∑(O − E)/40. This calculation was performed for all 14 state hospitals.

Ethical Statement

This study received ethical approval from the National Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC) of the Ministry of Health (Approval No. KKM/NIHSEC/P20-505(7)) on March 17, 2021. This is an MREC-approved retrospective study; all patient information was de-identified, and patient consent was not required. Patient data will not be shared with third parties.

Results

The analysis used a discharge dataset comprising 40 diagnosis groups and 1,144,890 records. Overall, 10.2% of records represented mortality events during 2012–2016. Mortality distributions by diagnosis group are summarised in Table 1.

HSMR Model Quality Metrics

C-statistics (Table 2) and Brier scores (Table 3) were calculated for each diagnosis group. Across all models, pLR achieved the lowest Brier scores compared with LR and RF. The C-statistics for pLR were also equivalent to or higher than those for LR. In nearly all diagnosis groups, pLR outperformed RF in C-statistics, with differences ranging from 0.03 to 0.14. The average difference was 0.062 (t = 327.32, df = 2999, p < 0.001). The largest difference was observed in the “Superficial injury; contusion” diagnosis group, in which the C-statistic for pLR exceeded that for RF by 20.6%. Both pLR and RF demonstrated moderate discrimination overall, defined as C-statistics between 0.6 and 0.8, although RF showed poor discrimination, defined as C-statistics <0.6, for several diagnosis groups. Despite this moderate discrimination, RF models consistently produced higher Brier scores, suggesting poorer calibration of predicted probabilities, as shown in the representative calibration plots in Supplementary Figure S1.

HSMR Model Aggregate Accuracy

Figure 1 shows the disparity, Edelta, between mortality counts predicted by RF and pLR and the mortality counts observed in state hospitals. For RF, Edelta varied across hospitals from 3 to 38, with a median of 21. For pLR, Edelta ranged from 4 to 42, with a median of 25 (Wilcoxon signed-rank exact test, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that RF-predicted mortality counts were numerically closer to observed mortality counts in all 14 hospitals.

HSMR Model Fairness Analysis

The fairness analysis showed that predicted mortality probabilities for the reference patient profile were either comparable across Bumiputera, Chinese, and Indian groups, with overlapping 95% CIs, or showed no systematic bias toward any particular ethnic group across the 40 diagnosis groups. Discussion

Numerous mortality risk models used for HSMR have traditionally relied on LR. However, this study applied algorithmic modelling to mortality risk prediction. Among the evaluated models, pLR showed the best overall performance and appeared less prone to overfitting. Because pLR estimates model parameters through likelihood-based optimisation with penalisation, it was expected to produce better-calibrated predictions than RF. In addition, pLR reduces prediction variance relative to unpenalized LR by limiting the influence of extreme coefficients [15]. These features may improve predictive performance.

RF may outperform other models for mortality prediction in specific diagnostic contexts [16,17]. In contrast, our analysis across 40 diagnosis groups showed that pLR consistently outperformed RF. Thus, a model should be selected based on the characteristics of the dataset and the intended use of the prediction model [18]. In some clinical contexts, simpler models, such as LR with transformations, can provide predictive performance comparable to that of more complex and computationally intensive machine-learning approaches [19–21].

In healthcare quality studies, data source is often emphasised, and existing administrative databases are commonly preferred due to their practicality and cost-effectiveness. Adding clinical variables can improve mortality prediction and provide a clearer understanding of how predictors relate to outcomes [22]. In the HSMR context, however, the primary objective is to estimate mortality probability. Variable inclusion is therefore often guided by data availability rather than model explainability. Accordingly, this study included only relevant variables available in the existing administrative database.

Despite lower individual-level predictive performance, RF models produced expected mortality counts that were numerically closer to the observed hospital-level counts. However, this aggregate agreement should be distinguished from true model calibration. The total expected mortality count was obtained by summing predicted risks across all admissions. In RF models, high variance among the ensemble’s base learners can pull individual predictions away from the extremes; survivors may receive inflated risk estimates, whereas nonsurvivors may receive deflated estimates [23]. Because survivors substantially outnumber nonsurvivors, upward bias in the larger group and downward bias in the smaller group can offset each other arithmetically when predictions are summed. However, this numerical agreement does not necessarily indicate accurate individual risk estimation. By contrast, pLR’s superior Brier scores and C-statistics indicate more precise individual-level risk adjustment, which is a primary requirement for a robust and fair HSMR monitoring system.

Both models consistently underestimated mortality across all hospitals, as indicated by uniformly positive Edelta values. This suggests systematic bias. One possible explanation is reliance on administrative data, which may not fully capture clinical severity and may therefore lead to underadjustment of mortality risk. Both modelling approaches also incorporate mechanisms that favour conservative predictions: regularisation in pLR and averaging across trees in RF may shrink predicted probabilities toward lower values. Temporal differences between the training period, 2012–2015, and the test year, 2016, may also have contributed to this discrepancy. However, this bias does not alter the relative comparison between models, because both were evaluated under the same conditions.

The apparent discrepancy between moderate discrimination, as reflected by C-statistics, and substantially higher Brier scores in RF models highlights a limitation of tree-based ensemble methods. Although RF can rank patients according to risk, its predicted probabilities are not inherently well calibrated. This can produce probability estimates that are biased toward intermediate values and deviate from observed event frequencies. In contrast, pLR operates within a probabilistic framework that directly optimises likelihood.

Moreover, use of an administrative database for model training may have omitted important predictors, potentially reducing the performance of less assumption-dependent algorithms such as RF. Nonetheless, RF can accommodate nonlinear variables and nonadditive interactions [24]. The algorithm operates without prespecified model assumptions, and its nonlinear structure may capture complexity relevant to mortality prediction. It also uses random combinations of inputs at each node when growing each tree, which can help reduce overfitting. RF therefore offers a potential route for further refinement of mortality models, although more extensive parameter tuning or recalibration of model outputs may be needed.

In this study, partitioning the data into training and test datasets within a bootstrap cross-validation framework was intended to produce more conservative model performance estimates while accounting for the longitudinal nature of HSMR monitoring. This approach used models trained on earlier data to generate predictions for subsequent-year data. Using the 2012–2016 baseline provides a longitudinal anchor for the Malaysian healthcare system. Although clinical practices and disease burdens change over time, the core risk-adjustment variables used in the pLR model, including comorbidities and age, remain standard predictors of mortality. The superior calibration of pLR suggests that it is well suited for long-term monitoring because it is less prone to the high variance observed in algorithmic models such as RF. Validating this framework enables future researchers to measure drift in mortality rates through 2030 and beyond, providing a consistent metric for evaluating the impact of national health strategic plans. Future iterations of the model can be periodically recalibrated with contemporary data while maintaining the same validated methodological structure.

Although pLR demonstrated superior performance metrics in this study, the models were developed without nonlinear terms or interaction effects. This choice was made to maintain model parsimony and ensure comparability across the 40 diagnosis groups within a standardised framework. However, the absence of these terms may limit the ability of regression-based models to capture the same level of data complexity that RF can accommodate through its ensemble structure. Future studies could improve pLR predictive accuracy by incorporating higher-order terms, nonlinear terms, or interaction effects tailored to the complexity of HSMR mortality prediction [19]. In addition, this study used temporal validation within the same set of hospitals rather than external validation. Further evaluation in independent hospital settings is therefore needed to assess external generalisability. Additional model adjustments, such as incorporating comorbidity and disease severity scores, may address current limitations and improve predictive ability.

Prediction models are widely used in healthcare quality management and clinical decision support. As healthcare systems face increasing pressure to manage medical expenditures, accurate predictive models for in-hospital mortality have become increasingly important. This study systematically compared three modelling approaches—LR, pLR, and RF—for predicting mortality associated with hospitalisation. The findings indicate that, for HSMR calculation, pLR outperformed the comparator models in predictive discrimination and calibration. Using data from more than 1.1 million admissions, this study established the first validated HSMR framework in Malaysia and provides an evidence-based early warning approach that can help hospital administrators identify higher-than-expected mortality after adjustment for patient case mix. Although pLR appears to be the most robust approach for routine monitoring in this setting, a combined strategy that uses alternative algorithmic models in selected analytical contexts may offer additional opportunities to refine mortality prediction for better hospital performance monitoring.
