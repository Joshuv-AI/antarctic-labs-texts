Impact of postoperative surveillance via computed tomography after radical resection for thymoma
Authors: Natsumi Maru, Haruaki Hino, Takahiro Utsumi, Aki Kobayashi, Kento Fukumoto, Hiroshi Matsui, Yohei Taniguchi, Tomohito Saito, Tomohiro Murakawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutpatient follow-up using computed tomography (CT) after thymoma resection is recommended to evaluate tumor recurrence and detect second malignancies. However, the optimal interval for CT surveillance remains unclear. We aimed to investigate the association between CT surveillance intervals and postoperative survival after thymoma resection.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 143 patients who underwent periodic CT scans after complete thymoma resection at a single institution between 2006 and 2022. Patients were stratified into three groups based on CT interval: half-, 1-, and 2-year groups. CT intervals were stratified according to the attending physician’s clinical judgment. Overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), tumor recurrence rates, and post-recurrence treatments were compared among the three interval groups. A multivariate analysis was performed to evaluate the independent prognostic impact of CT intervals.ResultsAmong the 143 patients included, 82 were assigned to the half-year group, 33 to the 1-year group, and 28 to the 2-year group. The 7-year OS rates were 96.1% in the half-year group, 100% in the 1-year group, and 100% in the 2-year group (P=0.34). The 7-year RFS rates were 85.2%, 92.3%, and 88.1% in the half-, 1-, and 2-year groups, respectively (P=0.37). No significant differences were found in overall and RFS among the three groups. Tumor recurrence rates were 11.0% in the half-year group, 3.0% in the 1-year group, and 7.1% in the 2-year group (P=0.38). No significant differences were noted in recurrence detection modalities and treatment approaches among the three groups (P>0.99, 0.99). In the multivariate analysis, CT follow-up interval was not significantly associated with OS.ConclusionsPostoperative CT follow-up at intervals longer than 1 year may be acceptable and feasible for patients after thymoma resection.Keywords: Computed tomography surveillance (CT surveillance), thymoma, postoperative follow-ups, second malignancy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Computed tomography (CT) surveillance at intervals of up to 2 years after complete thymoma resection demonstrated equivalent efficacy to follow-up at biannual intervals for detecting recurrence and secondary malignancies.

What is known and what is new?

• Following thymoma surgery, CT follow-up is generally recommended in guidelines such as those from the European Society for Medical Oncology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network; however, limited evidence exists to support the interval. CT surveillance is primarily performed to detect recurrence and second malignancies. Surgical resection of recurrent lesions can contribute to an improved prognosis, and aggressive treatment after recurrence is considered acceptable. Additionally, a previous study estimated that the risk of second malignancies after thymoma surgery is approximately twice as high as that of the general population. Therefore, recognizing this ‘intrinsic risk’ implies the need for appropriate clinical surveillance with lifelong follow-up.

• However, considering the drawbacks of CT scans, such as radiation exposure and medical costs, longer intervals are preferable. Besides, thymomas are relatively indolent, and previously reported recurrence-free periods exceeding 3 years, then the currently recommended close postoperative CT follow-up intervals may be excessive. CT follow-up at intervals of approximately half-year to 1 year is recommended after thymoma surgery; however, our findings indicate that longer-term CT follow-up is feasible following complete resection of the thymoma.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• No studies have compared outcomes between short- and long-term CT intervals. Our findings may provide options about the optimal postoperative CT follow-up interval after radical resection for thymoma.

Introduction

Thymomas, among the most common thymic epithelial tumors located in the anterior mediastinum, have relatively favorable survival rates after complete resection. According to European and United States guidelines, regular contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) surveillance, at intervals of at least once every 6–12 months, is recommended for detecting recurrence or second malignancies. Contrast-enhanced CT with a delayed phase is generally preferred to achieve optimal delineation of anterior mediastinal lesions. Magnetic resonance imaging may be considered in specific clinical situations, such as intolerance to contrast or in young patients to minimize radiation exposure during long-term follow-up (1,2). We hypothesized that curable thymoma recurrences and second malignancies could be identified within a reasonable timeframe, and that earlier detection of these events may improve survival outcomes. Conversely, extending surveillance intervals may reduce healthcare costs and minimize radiation exposure. We previously conducted a study evaluating optimal CT intervals after surgery for advanced lung cancer surgery and concluded that annual CT surveillance was a feasible alternative to biannual imaging for detecting recurrence, guiding treatment, and maintaining postoperative survival (3). However, evidence regarding optimal postoperative CT surveillance intervals after thymoma resection has not been reported thus far. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the impact of CT frequency after thymoma resection on postoperative survival and elucidate the optimal postoperative surveillance interval. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://med.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/med-25-41/rc).

Methods

Study design

We performed a retrospective cohort study to compare overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) among patients who underwent complete resection for thymoma and CT surveillance at various intervals. Both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced CT scans were considered acceptable. Additionally, we conducted a descriptive analysis of recurrence to examine recurrence patterns, detection modalities, treatment approaches, and the development of second malignancies.

Patients

We included patients who underwent complete resection for thymoma between January 2006 and December 2022, and those who underwent periodic postoperative CT surveillance at Kansai Medical University Hospital. Clinical data were collected through a retrospective review of a maintained database. Variables included age, sex, body mass index, myasthenia gravis, age-adjusted Charlson comorbidity index (4), pathological tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) and Masaoka stages, type of procedure (extended thymectomy or tumor resection), surgical approach (thoracoscopy), histological type, tumor size (clinical or pathological), receipt of postoperative additional therapy, and presence of second malignancies. No age cut-off criterion was applied in this study. Thymoma was suspected on imaging and was histologically confirmed after complete resection. Histological tumor types were classified according to the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors (5). Tumor staging was determined based on the 9th edition of the Union for International Cancer Control (UICC) for TNM and Masaoka classifications (6,7). For patients staged using a previous TNM edition, restaging was conducted to align with the current 9th edition. Recurrence was defined radiologically. The modality by which recurrence was first detected was noted. Detection methods included chest X-rays and CT of the chest and abdomen, along with assessment of symptom presence as well as surgical biopsy. Recurrence was categorized as either local, defined as lesions within the mediastinal operative field, or distant, which included tumor recurrence in distant lymph nodes, pleural dissemination, or metastases to distant organs. Treatment modalities for recurrence comprised surgical resection, high-dose radiotherapy for locoregional recurrence, and systemic therapies, such as chemotherapy and chemoradiation, for advanced disease. Best supportive care was also considered a treatment option.

Surveillance intensity stratified by CT interval

Follow-up intervals of 6 months and 1 year aligned with major guideline recommendations (1,2). Given the favorable prognosis after complete resection of thymoma, longer CT intervals were considered feasible. Consequently, biennial CT scans were implemented. The CT interval groups were defined as follows: the half-year interval group underwent scans between 3–9 months postoperatively; the 1-year group, between 10–14 months; and the 2-year group, between 21–26 months. All three groups received identical follow-up procedures aside from the CT schedule. These procedures included physical examinations, chest X-rays, and blood tests conducted 2–3 weeks after surgery and every 6–12 months thereafter. Intervals of CT scanning varied among five consultants from biannually to 2 years, and were determined based on the preferences rather than disease staging or strict adherence to institutional guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean [standard deviation (SD)] for variables with approximately normal distributions, median [interquartile range (IQR) or range] for non-normally distributed variables, and count (percentage) for categorical variables. Continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical data were assessed using Fisher’s exact test. All P values were two-tailed, with values <0.05 considered statistically significant. OS was defined as the time from surgery to death or the last follow-up. RFS was calculated from the date of surgery to recurrence, death, or last follow-up. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to assess and compare survival curves, and the log-rank test was performed to evaluate the statistical significance of differences among the three groups at all follow-up intervals. The Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the effect of CT interval on survival, while adjusting for other related factors. Candidate covariates of interest in the Cox proportional hazards model for OS were determined based on the literature, including age, sex, Masaoka stage, histological type (type A, AB, B1/B2, B3), and CT intervals. Since the study period was 16 years, calendar time bias was a concern. The year in which surgery was performed was included as a covariate. All analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (8).

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Kansai Medical University Ethics Board (No. 2024148). Kansai Medical University Hospital is an affiliated institution of Kansai Medical University, and clinical research conducted at the hospital is reviewed and approved by Kansai Medical University Ethics Board. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 2006 and 2022, 185 patients with anterior mediastinal tumors underwent surgery. Exclusion criteria included incomplete resections (n=7), histological types other than thymoma, such as thymic carcinoma (n=18), thymic cyst (n=10), and absence of routine CT after surgery (n=7). A total of 143 patients who underwent surgery for pathologically diagnosed thymomas at our institution and were monitored through periodic CT surveillance according to defined intervals were included in the study (Figure 1). Among them, 82 patients were assigned to the half-year interval group, 33 to the 1-year interval group, and 28 to the 2-year interval group. Median CT follow-up intervals for each group were 182, 367, and 735 days, respectively. There was a bias in the allocation of patients to each group depending on the year. From 2006 to 2014, most cases underwent CT follow-up at half-year intervals. In 2015, a change in the department leadership influenced the postoperative surveillance strategy. From 2015 onwards, CT follow-up at 1- or 2-year intervals became an option (Figure S1). Of the 143 postoperative surveillance CT examinations included in this study, only 13 (9.1%) were performed with contrast enhancement. Of these, 9 patients were in the half-year interval group, 3 were in the 1-year interval group, and 1 was in the 2-year interval group; there was no statistically significant difference among the groups (P=0.61). The clinical characteristics of patients stratified by CT interval are listed in Table 1. No significant differences were observed among the three groups regarding age, sex, body mass index, prevalence of myasthenia gravis, age-adjusted Charlson comorbidity index, pathological stage based on the Masaoka classification and the 9th edition of the TNM classification, histological type, tumor size, or postoperative therapy. After 2015, a change in the department leadership led to a revision in surgical strategy. The department implemented a policy of performing total thymectomy and thoracoscopic surgeries whenever feasible. Therefore, extended thymectomies and thoracoscopic surgeries were more frequently performed in the 1- and 2-year interval groups than in the half-year group (P<0.001 and 0.004, respectively). The most extended follow-up periods were 6,779 days in the half-year interval group, 6,320 days in the 1-year interval group, and 2,913 days in the 2-year interval group.

Prognosis in three CT interval groups

During the entire follow-up period, four patients (4.9%; 4/82) in the half-year CT interval group died due to pneumonia (2), deterioration of myasthenia gravis (1) and unknown cause of death (1), whereas no deaths occurred in the 1- and 2-year CT interval groups (P=0.39). Recurrence was observed in nine patients (11.0%; 9/82) in the half-year CT interval group, one patient (3.0%; 1/33) in the 1-year group, and two patients (7.1%; 2/28) in the 2-year group (P=0.38). The 7-year OS rate was 96.1% [95% confidence interval (CI): 88.5–98.7%] in the half-year CT interval group and 100% in both the 1- and 2-year CT interval groups, showing no statistically significant difference (P=0.34) (Figure 2A). The 7-year RFS rate was 85.2% (95% CI: 74.8–91.6%) in the half-year CT interval group, 92.3% (95% CI: 56.6–98.9%) in the 1-year CT interval group, and 88.1% (95% CI: 58.8–97.0%) in the 2-year CT interval group, also without significant differences (P=0.37) (Figure 2B). The median time to recurrence in each group was as follows: 3.4 years in the half-year interval group, 4.9 years in the 1-year interval group, and 4.6 years in the 2-year interval group. The cumulative incidence of recurrence plateaued between 5 and 10 years after surgery (Figure 3A), and the cumulative incidence of overall death gradually increased over 15 years after surgery (Figure 3B). The results of multivariate analysis are shown in Table 2. There was no significant association between CT intervals and prognosis.

Recurrence details

Recurrence details stratified by surveillance intensity are listed in Table 3. A total of 12 recurrences were identified: nine in the half-year interval group, one in the 1-year interval group, and two in the 2-year interval group. All three groups included patients with early-stage thymoma recurrence. The median RFS was 3.7 years for stage I (range, 2.1–4.9 years), 6 years for stage II (range, 3.4–7.2 years), and 2.3 years for stage III (range, 1.1–5.2 years). Although the recurrence-free interval of stage III thymoma was considerably shorter, the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.09) (Figure 4A). Histological differences were noted in recurrent cases among the three groups. The half-year interval group had more recurrences of types B2 and B3, the 1-year interval group had a recurrence of type AB, and the 2-year interval group had a recurrence of type B1. The median recurrence-free intervals were as follows: A: none, AB: 4.9 years, B1: 3.5 years (range, 2.1–6.0 years), B2: 3.4 years (range, 1.1–6.0 years), and B3: 3.9 years (range, 3.4–7.2 years). No relationship was found between histological type and recurrence-free interval (P=0.84) (Figure 4B). In terms of recurrence patterns, seven were locoregional (mediastinal) and five were distant metastases. Among the seven local recurrences, five occurred in the half-year interval group, 1 in the 1-year interval group, and 1 in the 2-year interval group. Among the five distant metastasis recurrences, pleural dissemination was detected in four, with all cases occurring in the half-year interval group. One recurrence in the supraclavicular lymph nodes was recorded in the 2-year interval group. Nearly all recurrences (11/12, 91.6%) were detected using CT, and one case was diagnosed by surgical biopsy. Only one case was identified using chest X-ray in the half-year interval group. Surgical resection was performed in most recurrent cases (9/12, 75%) in all groups. Six of nine patients in the half-year interval group, one patient in the 1-year interval group, and two patients in the 2-year interval group underwent surgery for recurrence. In the half-year interval group, complete resection of the recurrence was achieved in two of six cases. Among patients with a CT interval of 1 year or more, complete resection was considered feasible in all cases. No significant differences were observed in the post-recurrence treatment approach among the three groups (P>0.99). In the half-year interval group, five of the nine recurrent cases experienced a second recurrence. Among these, three patients underwent surgical resection again, one received chemotherapy, and one received radiotherapy.

Second malignancy

A second malignancy other than thymoma was detected in 12 of 143 patients (8.4%): 8 of 82 (9.8%) in the half-year interval group, 2 of 33 (6.1%) in the 1-year interval group, and 2 of 28 (7.1%) in the 2-year interval group. Routine follow-up CT after surgery identified 8 of 12 total cases detecting second malignancy (66.7%), distributed as follows: 5 of 8 in the half-year interval group, 1 of 2 in the 1-year interval group, and all two cases in the 2-year interval group. The second malignancies included laryngeal (1 case), prostate (1 case), bladder (1 case), gastric (1 case), thyroid (2 cases), and lung cancers (6 cases). No deaths were attributed to these second malignancies.

Discussion

No significant association was found between the interval of postoperative CT after radical thymoma resection and postoperative survival. Recurrences were generally detected using CT, and surgical resection was actively performed following previous reports (9,10). In our cohort, surveillance was predominantly performed using non-contrast CT. This low utilization likely reflects the routine surveillance practice in our institution during the study period, when concerns about renal function, previous contrast allergy, and logistical constraints frequently often resulted in the use of non-contrast CT. During the entire observation period, second malignancies were identified in 8.4% (12/143) of patients, the majority of which were detected through routine postoperative periodic CT at relatively early stage. These findings suggest that postoperative CT surveillance is effective for detecting postoperative recurrence and second malignancies, regardless of imaging interval, given the comparable postoperative survival rates among the three groups. Based on these results, CT follow-up interval may have a limited influence on postoperative survival in patients undergoing thymoma resection.

This may be attributed to the biological behavior of thymoma, which typically progresses slowly and is associated with prolonged survival. Previous studies have reported 10-year survival rates of approximately 90%. Recurrence rates remain low in early-stage disease, with 1% for stage I and 4% for stage II, increasing to approximately 30% in patients with stage III and above (11-13). In cases of recurrence, procedures such as debulking surgery, including resection of pleural dissemination, may prolong survival, although treatment indications and procedures remain subjects of debate (10,11,14-16). In our cohort, we selected operable recurrence cases and surgical timing based on those prior publications.

In addition, no differences in the treatment approach after recurrence were observed among the groups with varying CT intervals. Complete surgical resection of the recurrence site was possible even in the group with longer CT intervals. In particular, frequent CT may be useful for early detection of recurrence in cases with shorter recurrence-free intervals, such as advanced-stage thymoma or tumors with B2/B3 histopathology (17,18). Interleukin-8 has been recently reported as a potential biomarker for thymoma recurrence. Given that thymoma recurrence is often asymptomatic, blood-based biomarkers could serve as a useful screening tool, as seen in other malignancies, and may enable more personalized CT intervals (19). Adjusting the interval of CT surveillance according to individual recurrence risk may represent an optimal follow-up strategy. However, several studies have reported a median recurrence-free period of approximately 2–5 years, with the present study observing a median of 4.0 years (10,18,20,21). Although recurrence risk and recurrence-free intervals vary depending on specific factors, performing a CT scan within 2 years after surgery was considered feasible for detecting recurrences early, at a stage amenable to complete resection.

To date, no studies have directly examined the impact of CT surveillance intervals on survival outcomes after thymoma surgery. Existing guidelines recommend a 12-month interval for patients with R0 resection and stage I–II thymomas, and a shorter 6-month interval for stage III or more advanced R1–2 thymomas. However, these recommendations are not well supported by evidence, except for our findings (1,2). By contrast, follow-up strategies for tumors in other organs have been debated. For instance, previous studies in lung cancer have shown no relationship between postoperative CT interval and survival (3,22,23). Although no definitive conclusions could be drawn regarding postoperative CT surveillance in cases of other malignant tumors, such as colorectal and liver cancers, few reports have demonstrated a significant relationship between high-frequency CT and prognosis (24,25). These studies suggest that the timing of detecting asymptomatic recurrence does not influence treatment approach and may introduce lead time bias (26). Therefore, early detection of recurrence via CT may not have a positive effect on prognosis, particularly in low-grade malignancies such as thymoma. Future nationwide, large-scale cohort studies are needed to clarify optimal CT surveillance intervals.

Second malignancies identified during the observation period are considered an additional factor affecting postoperative survival. This is particularly relevant in children and young adults, where thymomas have a favorable prognosis. In such cases, second malignancies may play a more significant prognostic role, warranting more proactive screening strategies (27). Previous studies have reported the incidence of second malignancies after thymoma surgery to range from 3 to 22%, and the incidence observed in this study was 8.4% (12/143) overall (28). All second malignancies were detected using postoperative CT before symptom onset, suggesting that early detection may have been facilitated by imaging surveillance. However, no deaths related to second malignancies occurred in this cohort, and the small number of patients makes it difficult to establish a definitive association between CT scan intervals and outcomes related to second malignancies. Although the number of cases was limited, the impact of second malignancy is considered as another important aspect of CT follow-ups for survival of patients with postoperative thymoma.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single institution, with a relatively small cohort. CT intervals were determined by the attending outpatient staff without standardized consideration of thymoma stage or WHO histological classification, leading to an imbalanced patient population. Second, the follow-up period for the 2-year CT interval group was short. Third, leadership changes during the target period affected surgical procedures and postoperative surveillance policies.

Fourth, considering the generally indolent nature of thymoma, recurrence and mortality rates remained low, potentially limiting the statistical power to assess the impact of CT intervals on survival outcomes.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that longer CT intervals may be a feasible and cost-effective follow-up strategy after complete thymoma resection, potentially minimizing healthcare costs and radiation exposure without compromising survival outcomes. However, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size and retrospective study design. Validation in larger, multi-institutional prospective studies is warranted.

Conclusions

Surveillance CT at 1- or 2-year intervals after complete thymoma resection was comparable with that at half-year interval regarding detection of recurrence, identification of second malignancies, and maintenance of postoperative survival, based on outcomes from this single-institution study.
