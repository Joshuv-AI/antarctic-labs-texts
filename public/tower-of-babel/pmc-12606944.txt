Conversion of elite bread wheat cultivars HD3086 and HD2932 into cytoplasmic male sterile (CMS) lines and their genetic assessment to develop CMS-based hybrids

Background

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most prominent sources of food grain, essential for global food and nutritional security. However, to meet the growing needs of the population, it is crucial to enhance wheat productivity with limited inputs. Hybrid technology is a promising strategy in crop plants, leveraging hybrid vigour to achieve higher yields. Hybrid breeding has already revolutionized the production of cross-pollinated crops, such as maize, pearl millet and sorghum [1, 2]. However, productivity enhancement through hybrid development in wheat remains a significant challenge due to its predominantly self-pollinating nature and limited heterosis [3].

Cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) based controlled pollination systems serve as an effective tool for accelerating hybrid development in self-pollinated cereals like wheat. CMS systems eliminate the need for manual emasculation by inhibiting pollen production in the female parent, thereby facilitate controlled cross-pollination [4, 5]. In wheat, a three-line hybrid development system comprising a CMS line (A-line), its fertile maintainer (B-line), and a fertility restorer (R-line) offers a practical framework for hybrid seed production [6]. However, the success of such a system hinges on the availability of elite parental lines that combine agronomic desirability with genetic compatibility for CMS and fertility restoration.

The evaluation of wheat lines for their combining ability plays a crucial role in selecting and identifying promising parental lines and heterotic combinations [7–9]. The line × tester mating design has been successfully used for identifying and selecting promising parents and their hybrids in wheat [9–11]. The analysis of general combining ability (GCA) and specific combining ability (SCA) estimates provides lines that combine well with tester lines and the best specific cross combination that outperforms the other genotypes for target traits [12]. GCA helps to identify parental lines that influence favourable additive gene effects for the target trait. Whereas the SCA gives a superior hybrid combination that exploits non-additive gene effects [13]. Additionally, deciphering GCA and SCA contributions provides essential insights into the genetic control of traits, which aids in the development of practical breeding approaches [10].

The development of hybrid wheat varieties with wide adaptability is persistently challenged by genotype × environment interactions (GEI), which result in variable performance of lines across different environments. This variability is a significant barrier for wheat breeders aiming to create superior cytoplasmic male sterile (CMS)-based hybrids that can thrive under diverse agronomic conditions [14]. Under such conditions, an estimate of hybrid performance in multi-environment trials is a key factor. To address this challenge, several statistical tools for both parametric and non-parametric analysis have been developed. Among these, the Genotype and Genotype × Environment Interaction (GGE) biplot model is widely exploited for dissecting stability and broad adaptability of wheat hybrids [15–17].

Therefore, the current study describes the conversion of widely grown wheat cultivars into CMS lines and their practical application in hybrid development for multi-environment trials. In our breeding program at ICAR-Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI), New Delhi, we have developed a series of CMS lines (A-lines) with varied genetic backgrounds, employing a backcross breeding strategy [18]. To date, no CMS-based commercial hybrids have been developed and released in many of the South Asian countries, including India. Therefore, the present investigation was focused on the following objectives: (i) to convert widely growing elite wheat cultivars HD3086 and HD2932 into cytoplasmic male sterile lines using A-GW365 as the male sterile cytoplasm donor through backcross breeding, (ii) to estimate the combining abilities of fertility restorer lines (R-lines) and male-sterile lines (A-lines) for grain yield, and (iii) to analyze the genotype × environment interaction and adaptability of CMS-based hybrids under multi-environment conditions.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The widely adapted bread wheat mega cultivars HD3086 (Pusa Gautami) and HD2932 were used for the conversion to CMS lines. Both cultivars have agronomic superiority and have wide adaptability across diverse agro-climatic zones of India. Wheat cultivar HD3086 has been released for commercial cultivation under timely sown irrigated conditions in the North Western Plains Zone (NWPZ) and North Eastern Plains Zone (NEPZ) of India by ICAR-IARI, New Delhi [19]. HD2932 was released for the central zone of India under late sown conditions [20]. The CMS line (A-GW365) used as a donor parent in the present investigation possesses sterile cytoplasm derived from the AAGG Triticum genome species. It exhibited complete male sterility during the CMS introgression program [18]. The two converted A-lines from the current investigation and the donor A-line were crossed with six fertility restorer lines (R-lines) through a line × tester mating design. The pedigree details and source of the genetic materials used in line × tester analysis is presented in Table S1.

Development of backcross progenies

The independent conversion program was initiated by crossing the CMS line A-GW365 as female parent with the elite wheat cultivars HD3086 and HD2932 as pollen parents. The F1 plants were raised and were backcrossed to respective recurrent parents (HD3086 and HD2932) to generate sufficient BC1F1 seeds. One hundred BC1F1 plants were raised as space planting at 10 cm plant to plant and 25 cm row to row distance. The best five BC1F1 plants were selected based on pollen sterility and phenotypic similarity to the recurrent parent. The five selected BC1F1 plants were backcrossed to respective recurrent parents (HD3086 and HD2932) to generate the BC2F1 seeds. Again, one hundred BC2F1 progenies were raised by bulking an equal number of seeds from five selected and backcrossed BC1F1 plants. The similar selection and generation advancement criteria were practised till BC4F₁ generation. The phenotypically selected best-performing plants from BC4F1 were advanced to the BC5F₁ generation in a plant-to-progeny row. The most uniform row was selected and used for further background analysis, cytological study for pollen sterility and seed multiplication. The details of the scheme followed in the present study to develop the CMS/A line are presented in Fig. S1.

Pollen staining analysis

To ensure successful hybrid development, CMS lines must exhibit complete pollen sterility. For the staining analysis, pollen was randomly collected from three florets positioned at the lower, middle, and upper portions of the main spike from both the male sterile line and the male fertile maintainer parents. These spikes were preserved in 70% ethanol. Anthers were then stained using a 2% iodine-potassium iodide (I₂-KI) solution for two minutes and gently crushed with a pestle to release the pollen grains [21], which were subsequently examined under a light microscope (Nikon Y-TV55, Japan). Digital imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse H600L microscope camera system (Nikon, Japan). For each sample, a minimum of three biological replicates were analysed. Fertile pollen grains were identified as fully rounded and deeply stained, whereas sterile grains appeared either unstained or deformed and faintly stained.

Morphological and agronomic evaluation of newly developed CMS lines

To measure the agro-morphological characteristics based on DUS (Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability) characterisation, the final converted A-lines (BC5F₁ generation) were grown alongside the recurrent parents HD3086 and HD2932, which serve as the B-line to maintain the A-line. The designated phenotypes were recorded at the appropriate stage for each trait (https://plantauthority.gov.in/crop-dus-guidelines).

Parental polymorphism analysis and genotyping for background recovery

To determine the extent of nuclear genome recovery of the converted A-line, a total of 628 SSR markers distributed across all 21 wheat chromosomes were used to detect polymorphisms among the donor parent A-GW365 and the recurrent parents (HD3086 and HD2932). Of these, 152 markers for HD3086/A-GW365 and 145 markers in HD2932/A-GW365 were identified as polymorphic and were used for background analysis of the selected BC5F₁ converted lines. Primer sequences and their corresponding chromosomal locations were retrieved from the GrainGenes database (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/GG3/). Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the parents and selected BC5F₁ lines using the CTAB method [22]. The quality and concentration were assessed via 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis and a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. PCR amplification for SSR markers was performed in a 10 µL reaction volume containing 4 µL of 2X GoTaq PCR Master Mix (Promega, #M7122), 1 µL of each primer (5 pmol/µL), 2 µL of nuclease-free water, and 2 µL of genomic DNA (25 ng/µL, total 50 ng). Amplification was carried out using an Eppendorf thermal cycler under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50–60 °C for 30 s (depending on the primer), and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were resolved on a 3.5% agarose gel and visualized using a Gel Documentation System (G: Box, Syngene).

A total of 152 and 145 polymorphic SSR markers distributed across chromosomes were used for background analysis of A-HD3086 and A-HD2932, respectively. The genotypic data of the polymorphic marker were scored, and the RPG recovery was calculated using the formula:

The background recovery was scored as “A” for the number of homozygous loci matching the recurrent parents, “B” for the number of homozygous loci matching the donor and “H” for the number of heterozygous loci [20]. The recurrent parent genome (RPG) recovery of backcrossed derived lines was graphically illustrated using Graphical Genotype (GGT2) software [23].

Hybrid development in line × tester mode and evaluation

A set of six restorer lines (R-lines) was crossed with two newly developed A-lines (A-HD3086 and A-HD2932) and a donor A-line (A-GW365) in a line × tester design. For hybrid seed production, isolation plays a vital role in stopping contamination from unwanted plant pollen. The physical barriers, as caged crossing blocks with curtains, were employed in the hybrid seed production. The CMS line (A) and Restorer line (R) are planted in a specific ratio (2:1) to ensure sufficient pollen availability. The developed CMS-based wheat hybrids were evaluated in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) under three environmental conditions, viz., timely sown winter season of 2021-22 and 2022-23, and late sown season of 2022-23 at IARI, New Delhi. Standard agronomic practices were followed to ensure proper crop growth in a plot size of 6 row at 22.5 cm row to row spacing and 6 m length. The grain yield data were recorded and expressed in quintals per hectare (q/ha).

Estimation of heterosis, combining ability of parental lines and stability of hybrids

The average and better heterosis for grain yield of newly synthesized wheat hybrids were calculated as per Fonseca and Patterson [24]. The analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for grain yield to analyse the significant differences among genotypes, parents, crosses, lines, testers, and lines × testers. The combining ability of parents was estimated using lines × testers mating design described by Kempthorne [25] and implemented by Singh and Chaudhary [26], which splits the variance of crosses into GCA and SCA. These estimates helped to identify superior lines, testers and heterotic cross combinations for wheat hybrid breeding. The GCA estimates of the lines and testers, as well as the SCA effects of the hybrids, were estimated using the agricolae package of R [27]. For stability and adaptability analysis under multi-environmental trials, the Plant Breeding Tool (http://bbi.irri.org/products) was used by applying the GGE biplot models [28]. For GGE biplot analysis, the grain yields of hybrids were centred by the environmental mean, and principal components (PC1 and PC2) were used to build a GGE biplot.

Result

Development of cytoplasmic male sterile (CMS) lines

Development of HD3086 and HD2932-based backcross progenies

The initial cross between A-GW365 (CMS donor line) and recurrent parents (HD3086 and HD2932) resulted in F₁ plants that exhibited male sterility due to the sterile cytoplasm inherited maternally from A-GW365. Five F₁ plants were backcrossed with their recurrent parents to initiate the recovery of the recurrent parent genome. A total of five successive backcrosses were carried out using five selected backcross progenies, progressing through BC₁F₁ to BC5F₁ generations. In the backcross generations, 100 plants were raised, and five were selected based on two key criteria: (i) complete pollen sterility confirmed by 2% iodine potassium iodide (I₂-KI) staining, (ii) phenotypic resemblance to the recurrent parents. This selection process was repeated for each backcrossed generation using bulked seed from selected plants at each stage. The selected and backcrossed seed from BC4F₁ progenies were planted in a plant-to-progeny row. In the best five BC5F1 plant to progeny rows, the most uniform and most similar line compared to recurrent parents HD3086 and HD2932 was selected and referred to as the A-HD3086 and A-HD2932 lines, respectively. The backcross breeding program based on selection for pollen sterility and phenotypic selection led to the development of CMS lines with high genetic fidelity to HD3086 and HD2932 while retaining stable cytoplasmic sterility suitable for hybrid breeding programs.

Background analysis of newly developed CMS lines

The SSRs are well-established in wheat backcross breeding for background genome recovery, offering multi-allelic polymorphisms and broad genome coverage besides cost-effective and accessible. In the current investigation a total of 628 SSR markers distributed across all 21 wheat chromosomes were used to perform a parental polymorphism survey between the donor A-GW365 and recurrent parents HD3086 and HD2932. From 628 SSR markers, a total of 152 and 145 polymorphic SSRs identified in the parental polymorphism survey were used for background analysis of A-HD3086 and A-HD2932, respectively. The number of polymorphic SSRs used for background selection varied from 6 to 15 per chromosome across the crosses. The selected A-HD3086 and A-HD2932 lines exhibited 95.72% and 97.24% recovery of the RPG, respectively, indicating a high level of genomic similarity with HD3086 and HD2932 (Figs. 1, 2 and 3).

Evaluation of developed CMS lines for agronomic and morphological characters

The agro-morphological evaluation of the CMS lines (A-HD3086 and A-HD2932) revealed a near-complete morphological similarity with the recurrent parents (HD3086 and HD2932) across 36 DUS traits (Table S2). Both lines recorded identical scores with respective recurrent parents for traits such as plant growth habit, flag leaf attitude, spike emergence, spike shape, grain characteristics, and seasonal type. Minor variations were observed in attributes such as awn colour and attitude in A-HD3086, which remained within acceptable limits. Similarly, colouration at the base of the leaf was present in HD2932, while it was absent in A-HD2932. Additionally, all the CMS lines exhibited some degree of open spikelets compared to the recurrent parents because of sterility.

Cytogenetic analysis for pollen sterility

Cytogenetic analysis of pollen sterility with I₂-KI staining in the CMS lines (A-HD3086 and A-HD2932) and their original versions (HD3086 and HD2932) shown that the newly developed CMS lines, along with the donor (A-GW365), exhibited predominantly shrivelled, lightly stained, or unstained pollen grains, indicating complete male sterility. In contrast, the B-GW365 (maintainer line of A-GW365) and recurrent parents exhibited round, well-stained pollen, confirming normal fertility. The full frequency of sterile pollen in newly developed CMS lines validates its complete male sterility and its potential to be used in hybrid seed production. (Fig. 4).

Analysis of variance for grain yield

The ANOVA revealed highly significant mean squares for genotypes, parents, crosses, testers, and line × tester interactions for grain yield in timely sowing environments across years (Table 1). However, only the testers showed a significant mean square under the late sowing environment. The significant differences among genotypes, parents, crosses, testers, and line × tester interactions indicate sufficient genetic variation for potential improvement. Ratio of σ2a/σ2d (additive/dominance) for the timely sowing of 2023, and sowing-2022 was 0.41 and 0.12, respectively. Whereas for late sowing of 2023, a slight predominance of additivity was observed with a σ2a/σ2d ratio of 1.19. Thus, dominance effects contributed significantly to heterotic effects under the timely sowing condition (Table S3). The genotypes predominantly explained the total variance in yield, contributing 81.00%, 84.30%, and 62.90% to the variance under timely sowing 2023, timely sowing 2022, and late sowing 2023, respectively.

Combining ability estimates for grain yield

The general combining ability (GCA) effects for grain yield ranged from − 4.30 to 7.22 for lines and − 8.06 to 9.10 for testers across the multi-environmental trials (Table 2). Among the lines, 955R exhibited the positive GCA effects across three environments, viz., timely sown 2023 (7.22), timely sown 2022 (4.53) and late sown 2023 (1.23) environmental conditions, indicating its strong potential as a parent for grain yield improvement. In contrast, 942R showed the most negative GCA effects under timely sown 2023 (−3.89) and timely sown 2022 conditions, while 958R showed highly negative GCA (−3.08) under the late sowing condition. Negative GCA of 942R and 958R suggested limited potential in these environmental trials. Among the testers, A-HD3086 recorded the highest positive GCA estimates under all three environments, namely timely sown 2023 (9.10), timely sown 2022 (8.80) and late sowing of 2023 (6.21) conditions, whereas A-HD2932 had the most negative GCA effects of −5.86 (timely sown 2023), −8.06 (timely sown 2022) and − 6.60 (late sown 2023). Thus, 955R (among the lines) and A-HD3086 (among the testers) were identified as the most promising parents for hybrid development (Table 2).

Heterosis of wheat test-cross hybrids

All hybrids produced by crossing the A-GW365, A-HD3086 and A-HD2932 with restorer (R) lines showed complete seed setting in spikes, indicating that the R lines carried effective Rf genes. The analysis of 18 wheat test crosses revealed significant and broader variation for both mid-parent heterosis (MPH) and better-parent heterosis (BPH) values, indicating genetic variations and differences in the combining abilities of the tested genotypes. MPH was from − 16.09 (A-HD2932 × 1752R) to 24.54 (A-HD3086 × 955R), with a total average of 0.47%. Whereas BPH ranged from − 19.41 (A-HD2932 × 1752R) to 11.68 (A-HD3086 × 955R) with an average of −5.41%. Additionally, donor A-GW365 exhibited a positive average (20.13%) and better (10.56%) heterosis compared to 1752R. The highest and positive average, as well as better heterosis, of A-HD3086 × 955R and A-GW365 × 1752R, make these lines effective targets in hybrid wheat breeding (Fig. 5).

Performance of CMS -based hybrids

The CMS-based hybrids developed through a line × tester mating design were evaluated across three environments. The mean grain yield of all the CMS-based hybrids was 59.71 q/ha under the environmental conditions (Table 4). Among the experimental hybrids, five hybrids recorded yields greater than 70 q/ha, while five hybrids had grain yields between 60 and 70 q/ha across timely sowing environments (Table 4). Among the CMS-based hybrids, A-HD3086 × 955R (82.2 q/ha) exhibited the highest grain yield, followed by A-HD3086 × 917R (75.2 q/ha), A-HD3086 × 958R (72.0 q/ha), A-HD3086 × 908-3R (71.0 q/ha) and A-GW365 × 1752R (70.6 q/ha) under the timely sowing environment (2023 and 2022 timely sown). During the late sowing condition, A-HD3086 × 955R (69.7 q/ha) exhibited the highest grain yield, followed by A-GW365 × 1752R (66.8 q/ha), and A-HD3086 × 917R (61.8 q/ha) (Table 4).

Genotype × environmental interaction for identification of stable hybrids

The GGE biplot analysis for grain yield revealed the principal component 1 (PC1) and 2 (PC2) accounted with 54.50% and 41.60% of the total variation, respectively. Together with PC1 and PC2, the GGE biplot captured 96.10% of the Genotype × Environment Interaction (GEI) variation for grain yield (Fig. 6). The ‘which-won-where’ biplot (Fig. 6) summarised the effects of wheat hybrids and their interactions with environmental factors. In the biplot, E1 (timely sowing 2023), E2 (timely sowing 2022), and E3 (late sowing 2023) represent the three tested environments. The polygon view of the GGE Biplot connects seven outermost hybrids (G3, G5, G6, G10, G13, G15, and G18). The vertex genotype within each sector is considered the “winner” for environments in that sector. Among the Genotypes, G13 and G5 are good performers in environments E1 and E2, respectively. In contrast, G1, G2, G4, and G12 are performing stably across the environmental trials (Fig. 6). Considering all aspects, hybrid G4 and G12 were identified as the best-performing genotypes in terms of both yield and stability.

Discussion

Conversion of HD3086 and HD2932 into CMS lines

In India, HD3086 and HD2932 are widely cultivated wheat varieties, valued for their high yield potential and adaptability across multiple agro-climatic zones [29]. Its conversion into a CMS line not only preserves these desirable agronomic attributes but also enables its integration into hybrid breeding pipelines, thereby addressing yield stagnation issues observed in traditional breeding systems [3, 30]. The sterile cytoplasm, via donor line A-GW365, was instrumental in ensuring complete and stable male sterility across generations. This demonstrates the efficacy of sterile cytoplasm from AAGG genome based Triticum species such as T. timopheevii cytoplasm in inducing non-reversible pollen sterility and its compatibility with the nuclear genomes of elite wheat cultivars [18, 31]. The cytological analysis of pollen sterility in A-HD3086 and A-HD2932 genotypes, using I₂-KI staining, revealed a complete absence of fertile pollen grains, a prerequisite for reliable hybrid seed production.

A critical aspect of CMS line development lies in achieving maximum recovery of the recurrent parent genome while retaining cytoplasmic sterility. However, we have achieved ≥ 95% genome coverage recoveries in both lines using 152 and 145 polymorphic SSR markers. This high recovery rate is comparable to earlier efforts by CMS conversion of elite genotypes, such as A-HD3209, where over 96% recovery of progeny was achieved using similar methodologies [18, 20]. This is especially relevant in complex genomes, such as hexaploid wheat, where linkage drag and cryptic introgressions may compromise breeding outcomes if not critically monitored [32].

Notably, the agro-morphological evaluation of newly developed CMS lines revealed near-complete phenotypic similarity with the recurrent parents across 36 DUS traits. This level of conformity ensures that the CMS line retains the adaptation potential, phenology, and plant architecture of its recurrent parents, HD3086 and HD2932, making it suitable for deployment in their respective adaptive environments. Minimal deviations in awn-related traits were within acceptable breeding limits and did not affect the overall integrity of the line. These findings are aligned with earlier reports on phenotypic stability in CMS lines developed through rigorous backcrossing and selection [6]. The successful development of the CMS lines A-HD3086 and A-HD2932 from the elite wheat cultivars (HD3086 and HD2932) demonstrates the feasibility and promise of employing cytoplasmic male sterility systems to advance hybrid wheat breeding in India and South Asia.

Heterosis and genetic analysis for grain yield

In comparison to other cereals, such as maize, sorghum, and rice, the extent of heterosis in wheat is relatively low, and sometimes the heat-tolerant hybrids derived from well-yielding parents do show negative heterosis [3]. One of the main reasons is the lack of additional beneficial alleles between the parental lines [33]. Additionally, unfavourable epistatic interactions lead to negative dominance effects and dominance-by-dominance interactions, which reduce the grain yields compared to parental lines [34]. The current study revealed broad variation in mid-parent heterosis (MPH) and better-parent heterosis (BPH) among the wheat test hybrids, reflecting the underlying genetic diversity and parental genomic interactions. Although many of the crosses demonstrated low or negative heterotic responses, few hybrids, A-HD3086 × 955R (MPH: 24.54%; BPH: 11.68%) and A-GW365 × 1752R (MPH: 20.13%; BPH: 10.56%), showed high positive heterosis. These contrasting heterotic responses of the tested hybrids are consistent with previous findings, which suggests that heterosis in wheat is mainly genotype-specific and subject to both additive and non-additive genetic effects [35, 36]. Furthermore, the result of undesirable specific combining ability (SCA), negative epistasis, or disturbance of favourable homozygous gene blocks and non-complementary gene interactions may result in negative heterosis as observed in the cross A-HD2932 × 1752R (MPH: −16.08%, BPH: −19.41%) [37]. Additive genetic effects mainly govern many yield-related traits in wheat; hybrids derived from genetically similar or non-divergent parents may fail to generate sufficient non-additive interactions, resulting in reduced heterotic expression or even hybrid depression. This underscores the importance of strategically selecting parental lines with adequate genetic distance and complementary allele combinations to achieve desirable heterotic effects. The narrow cytoplasmic diversity in most CMS-based wheat systems further restricts the ability to tap into broader heterotic pools [38]. Moreover, the CMS-restorer system in wheat is still under optimisation, which adds a layer of complexity to hybrid development.

The significance of the mean square for line × tester is indicative of the presence of non-additive variance. In contrast, the same value for line and tester depicts the importance of additive genetic variance [39]. In the current study, non-additive gene action was predominant for grain yield in both timely sowing conditions, while additive gene action was predominant in late sowing. Several research groups have also mentioned the importance of both additive and non-additive gene action for grain yield in wheat germplasm [11, 13, 40]. The moderate heritability for yield components in the present study also supports the importance of non-additive gene action. In wheat, the preponderance of additive gene action was observed for grain yield under late sowing conditions [9, 40, 41]. Among the R-lines and testers, 955R (R-line) and A-HD3086 (tester) exhibited a positive GCA effect on grain yield across the three environmental trials. These lines possess favourable genes for grain yield; thus, they can be further utilised to accumulate favourable alleles through a recombination and selection process [42]. It is also evident that these parents were part of hybrids which had a desirable SCA effect coupled with high grain yield. The other authors also identify superior parents and hybrid combinations for developing high-yielding wheat varieties [9–11].

Stability and adaptability analysis across the environments

Understanding the stability and adaptability analysis of yield across environmental trials is vital for developing effective and high-yielding cultivars in crops. The GGE biplot model offers valuable insights into the GEI and stability analysis for the identification of suitable hybrids. The represented biplot “which-won-where” identifies mega-environment disparity for environmental conditions suitable for the wheat hybrids [43]. The biplot indicated that the first two principal components explained 96.10% of the G + G × E interaction variation for yield, the GEI accounting for 41.60% is important from a wheat breeder’s perspective (Fig. 6). In current study, few wheat hybrids were well adapted in each environment and confirmed the presence of interaction differentiation between wheat hybrids and environments conditions. The identified mega-environment for yield allows us to select the best hybrids in the particular environment. The biplot analysis identified four hybrids, viz., G1, G4, G8, and G12, which are clustered near the origin and remained stable across different environments. In contrast, G5 and G13 were positioned farther from the origin, indicating stronger interactions with the environments. The influence of genotype × environment interaction on yield has also been reported by several authors [16, 17, 44]. Hybrids identified as adaptable across various environments can thus be effectively utilised for commercialization.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the development of CMS lines (A-HD3086 and A-HD2932) by employing sterile cytoplasm through backcross breeding. The CMS lines exhibited complete pollen sterility, with ≥ 95% recovery of the RPG and showed high phenotypic similarity to the recurrent parent. The developed A-line can now serve as a crucial component in three-line hybrid wheat systems, facilitating the identification of superior hybrids with diverse restorer lines. Further line × tester analysis identified a promising line (955R) and tester (A-HD3086) that were part of the promising CMS-based hybrids. The G × E interaction analysis revealed that the two superior CMS-based hybrids (G4 and G12) were stable with high yields across diverse environments. These findings also highlight the importance of environmental factors in ensuring accurate and meaningful genotype evaluations. These findings will contribute to the development of resilient wheat varieties that can thrive under diverse agro-climatic conditions.
